# Identification and validation of microtubule depolymerizing agent, CYT997, as a potential drug candidate for hepatocellular carcinoma


## Background and Aims:
  Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a typically fatal malignancy with limited treatment options and poor survival rates, despite recent FDA approvals of newer treatment options. We aim to address this unmet need by using a proprietary computational drug discovery platform that identifies drug candidates with the potential to advance rapidly and successfully through preclinical studies.


## Methods:
  We generated an in silico model of HCC biology to identify the top 10 small molecules with predicted efficacy. The most promising candidate, CYT997, was tested for its in vitro effects on cell viability and cell death, colony formation, cell cycle changes, and cell migration/invasion in HCC cells. We used an HCC patient-derived xenograft (PDX) mouse model to assess its in vivo efficacy.


## Results:
  CYT997 was significantly more cytotoxic against HCC cells than against primary human hepatocytes, and sensitized HCC cells to sorafenib. It arrested cell cycle at the G2/M phase with associated up-regulations of p21, p-MEK1/2, p-ERK, and down-regulation of cyclin B1. Cell apoptosis and senescence-like morphology were also observed. CYT997 inhibited HCC cell migration and invasion, and down-regulated the expressions of acetylated tubulins, β-tubulin, glypican-3 (GPC3), β-catenin, and c-Myc. In vivo, CYT997 (20 mg/kg, three times weekly by oral gavage) significantly inhibited PDX growth, while being non-toxic to mice. Immunohistochemistry confirmed the down-regulation of GPC3, c-Myc, and Ki-67, supporting its anti-proliferative effect.


## Conclusion:
  CYT997 is a potentially efficacious and non-toxic drug candidate for HCC therapy. Its ability to down-regulate GPC3, β-catenin, and c-Myc highlights a novel mechanism of action.


## INTRODUCTION
  Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the major form of adult primary liver cancer.1 Its mortality rate continues to increase in many parts of the globe, due to the lack of effective treatment options.2 Treatment strategies depend largely on, and are confounded by many factors, such as tumour stage, and overall liver function.3 Systemic therapy with first line therapies, such as sorafenib, lenvatinib, and bevacizumab remains the standard of care for advanced stage HCC.4 However, these therapies are limited by their lack of significant improvement in the overall survival in patients, and their poorly tolerated side effects.5 Therefore, there remains an urgent and unmet need in developing new therapies with improved efficacy and safety for HCC treatment.

  The use of artificial intelligence (AI) technology empowers high-throughput processing and analysis of large volumes of data,6 and has proven valuable in diverse areas of drug discovery and diagnosis of multiple diseases such as cancer.7 Drug discovery has benefitted significantly from the use of AI.8 For example, the platform BANDIT (Bayesian ANalysis to determine Drug Interaction Targets) integrates diverse data encompassing drug structures, drug efficacies, and post-treatment responses to predict molecular targets for known compounds for which targets have not been well identified.9 The small molecule ONC201 was thus identified as a selective antagonist of the dopamine receptor D2 (DRD2), and was entered into clinical trial for paediatric gliomas.10 Machine learning via the random forest model also helped identify drugs that are potent against specific cancer cell types based on the mutational states of about 50 signature oncogenes.11 Similarly, Aria Pharmaceuticals has developed an AI-driven drug discovery approach which first builds an in silico disease model using complex patient-derived biological data combined with clinical health record data. The disease model was then interrogated against transcriptomic profiles and pharmacology data of diverse drug molecules, to uncover novel drug candidates for the disease of interest. This approach identified CYT997 as a top drug candidate for HCC.

  CYT997 is a tubulin-binding agent reported to be a potent antineoplastic agent for multiple cancers including myeloma,12 head and neck carcinoma,13 and gastric cancer.14 Crystallography study demonstrated that CYT997 primarily binds at V236 on helix H7, βD249 on loop T7, and αT179 of the tubulin protein,15 and stabilizes microtubules to ultimately alter microtubule dynamics. Microtubules are made up of αβ-tubulin heterodimers; α-tubulins are involved in various cellular processes, such as maintenance of cell shape, cell motility, and segregation of chromosomes during mitosis and meiosis.16 In various cancers, different tubulins isotypes have been reported to be associated with aggressiveness, poor prognosis, and resistance to cancer treatment.17 Tubulin TUBA1C was reported to be involved in enhancing proliferation and migration of HCC cells; its down-regulation significantly reduced these processes both in vitro and in vivo.18

  To date, various microtubule targeting agents have been reported with promising preclinical results.19 Mechanistically, CYT997 induces endoplasmic stress, cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, and autophagy in cancer cells.14,20 Cancer cells treated with CYT997 showed alterations in the expression of apoptotic markers, such as caspases, PARP, BCL-2,20,21 and in major signalling components that are commonly deregulated in cancers, such as Akt /Stat/JNK.14,22 The underlying mechanism of how CYT997 exerts its potential anti-tumour effect in HCC has yet to be fully understood. Herein, we validated our AI-enabled discovery of CYT997 as a promising drug candidate for HCC, both in vitro and in vivo, and further studied its mechanism of anti-tumour action in HCC.


## MATERIALS AND METHODS
  Identification of drug candidates, and selected cell-based assays are presented as Supplemental Materials.


### Cell cycle analysis
  Briefly, HepG2, Huh-7, Hep3B, and SNU-449 were treated with 0.01, 0.1, or 1 μM of CYT997 for 24 h, after which cells were harvested and resuspended in PBS and fixed in 70% cold ethanol at 4°C overnight. Cells were then pelleted by centrifugation at 2000 rpm. Cell pellets were washed twice with cold PBS and resuspended with 100 μg/mL RNAase (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) and 50 μg/mL Propidium Iodide (PI; Sigma-Aldrich), and incubated at room temperature for 45 min. Flow cytometry was performed (10 000 events) using FACS LSRII.UV (BD, Franklin Lakes) and data analysed using FlowJo software (Ashland).


### Apoptosis analysis
  Briefly, HepG2, Huh-7, Hep3B, and SNU-449 cells (2 × 105) were cultured on 6-well plates and treated with the indicated concentration of CYT997 for 48 h. Cells were then harvested and washed in cold PBS, followed by staining with APC-Annexin V and propidium iodide (Biolegend) in 1X annexin buffer for 25 min in the dark at room temperature, per the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were analysed using FACS LSRII.UV for percentages of cells in the early or late apoptotic phases, or necrosis.


### Wound healing assay
  HepG2, Huh-7, Hep3B, and SNU-449 cells (~ 0.5 million) were cultured in 6-well plates. After cell confluency reached 100%, a scratch was made in each well, using a 200 μL tip. Closure of the scratched area was monitored and images were taken at 0 and 48 h using phase contrast inverted microscope at 10X magnification (Olympus, Centre Valley).


### Transwell migration and invasion assay
  Migration assay was done using Transwell inserts (pore size, 8 μm, Corning) in 24-well plates. Briefly, 4 × 104 HepG2, Huh-7, Hep3B, or SNU-449 cells were seeded on the upper chamber in 200 μL serum-free media. The lower chamber was filled with 650 μL of media containing 10% FBS and desired concentrations of CYT997 (0.02 or 0.04 μM). After 48 h of incubation, cells that did not migrate to the lower surface of the inserts were removed using cotton swabs, whereas migrated cells were fixed in 70% ethanol (Koptec, King of Prussia) and stained with 0.4% crystal violet (Sigma) and were counted in four random fields using inverted microscope at 20X magnification (Olympus, Center Valley). For invasion assay, Transwell inserts were coated with matrigel (Corning) followed by similar procedure.


### Senescence-associated β-Galactosidase staining assay
  Senescence-associated β-Galactosidase activity was evaluated using Senescence β-Galactosidase Staining Kit following manufacturer’s protocol (Cell signalling). Briefly, 1 × 105 HepG2, Huh-7, Hep3B, or SNU-449 cells were cultured in 12-well plates and treated with indicated concentrations of CYT997 for 48 h. Thereafter, cells were washed with PBS and fixed using 1X fixative solution for 10 min at room temperature followed by washing with PBS and laying over with β-Galactosidase Staining Solution (pH -6.0). Plates were incubated overnight at 37°C in the absence of CO2. Senescent cells with characteristic blue staining were observed using inverted microscope (Olympus, Centre Valley) at 40X magnification and counted for six random fields.


### Western Blotting
  Total protein from untreated, and CYT997-treated HepG2, Huh-7, Hep3B, and SNU-449 cells were extracted using T-PER Tissue Protein Extraction Reagent (Pierce) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). Equal amounts of protein (30 μg; estimated by BCA protein assay kit (Pierce)) were mixed with loading buffer and resolved on 4%–12% precast sodium dodecyl sulphate-polyacrylamide gels (Invitrogen), then electro-transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Biorad). Membranes were then blocked for 2 h at room temperature in 5% non-fat milk (Chem Cruz), then incubated with primary antibodies p-MEK1/2 (9154), p-ERK1/2(4370), p21 (2947), Acetyl-α-Tubulin (3971S), β-Tubulin (5346), caspase-3 (9662S), β-catenin, (8480), Cyclin B1 (D5C10) GAPDH (5174) from Cell Signalling (Danvers, MA); Glypican-3 (1G12) (BioMosaics), and Lamin B1 (12987–1-AP) (Proteintech); c-Myc (13–2500) (Invitrogen) overnight at 4°C. After incubation with IRDYe 800CW secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit 926–32 211; goat anti-mouse 926–32 350) for 1 h at room temperature (LI-COR), images were taken using Odyssey IR imaging system (LI-COR).

  The proteasomal inhibitor MG132 (Cell Signalling) was co-incubated (at the indicated concentrations) with CYT997 to determine whether tubulin expressions were regulated through the proteasomal degradation system.


### Determination of the maximum tolerated dose of CYT997
  Animal studies were carried out in compliance with all federal and local institutional rules for the conduct of animal experiments. We used a total of 20 BALB/cJ male mice (approximately 6 weeks of age; Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbour)), randomized into four groups (n = 5 each): vehicle control (0.5% methyl cellulose, 10% DMSO), and three treatment groups at 20, 40, or 60 mg/kg. CYT997 was prepared as an 80 mg/mL stock solution in 0.5% methyl cellulose and 10% DMSO. Mice were dosed via oral gavage (PO) with the vehicle or desired doses of CYT997, for 42 days. Clinical signs were recorded daily, and body weight measured weekly. On day 42, blood was drawn for the measurement of metabolic and blood parameters, and mice were sacrificed.


### Establishment of orthotopic HCC patient-derived xenografts and treatment with CYT997
  The in vivo efficacy of CYT997 was evaluated in orthotopic HCC patient-derived xenografts (PDX). Written informed consent from HCC patients was obtained before liver resection. The use of human subjects and execution of animal studies were approved and were in accordance with the Institutional Review Board and Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC), respectively, at Stanford University.

  Male (4–6 weeks old) NOD.Cg-PrkdcscidIl2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ (Nod-SCID-Gamma; NSG) mice were used for this study, purchased from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbour, MA). Single-cell suspensions of two HCC patient specimens, generating PDX1 and PDX2, were labelled with luciferase reporter gene using lenti-virus as described earlier.23 Subsequently, donor mice for both PDX were prepared as previously described.24 Once subcutaneous tumours in the donor mice reached the desired diameter of ~1 cm, tumours were harvested and dissected into ~2 mm3 fragments for immediate surgical implantation onto the left lobe of the liver in another cohort of NSG mice. Dosing of mice was initiated within 1 week of tumour implantation. Each of the PDX1 and PDX2 cohorts were randomized into two groups (n = 9 and n = 7 respectively), and were dosed via oral gavage with vehicle (control; 0.5% methyl cellulose, 10% DMSO) or CYT997 (20 mg/kg) three times weekly. A separate PDX2 cohort, (n = 6) was dosed with sorafenib in water via oral gavage (50 mg/kg) 5 times per week. Body weight and tumour growth were monitored weekly. After 3 weeks of treatment, mice were sacrificed and tumours and normal livers harvested. Tumour size was measured with digital callipers and tumour volume was calculated using the formula π/6 × larger diameter × [smaller diameter].2


### Immunohistochemistry
  At the end of the treatment period, mice were sacrificed and PDX and normal liver harvested, fixed in formalin, and embedded in paraffin. The embedded tissues were sectioned and subjected to haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. For immunohistochemistry, tissues were deparaffinized by submerging in xylene twice for 10 min each followed by rehydration in decreasing concentrations of ethanol 100% twice and once in 90% and 75% for 5 min each. Antigen retrieval was carried out using 1X citrate buffer; pH 6.0 in IHC-TekTM Epitope Retrieval Steamer Set for 45 min. Slides were washed in 1X TBS (3 times, 5 min each), then incubated with 3% H2O2 for 15 min followed by washing with 1X TBS (3 times, 3 min each), and blocking with 5% normal goat serum for 1 h. Anti-GPC3 antibody (Biomosaic, 1G12) at 1:50 dilution; anti-c-Myc antibody (Invitogen, 13–2500) at 1:100 dilution was incubated with slides at 4°C overnight in a humidified chamber. Slides were then washed in 1X TBS-T for 3 times and incubated with HRP conjugated secondary antibodies; 1:100 (Santa Cruz) (1 h, room temperature). After washing, sections were developed using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) and were counterstained with haematoxylin (Dako). and Ki-67 (Biocare, API3156AA), staining was done by Histo-Tec Laboratory using antibody dilutions of 1:100. Images were obtained using NanoZoomer whole slide scanner (Hamamatsu, Hamamatsu City, Japan).


### Statistical analyses
  All quantitative data from cell-based experiment were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Each experiment was repeated at least twice, and representative images are shown. The student’s t-test and 2-way ANOVA were used where applicable to calculate the statistical differences between control and the treated group. p values of <0.05 were considered significant.


## RESULTS

### CYT997 selectively inhibited the proliferation of HCC cell lines but not of primary human hepatocytes
  CYT997 exerted anti-proliferative effects against three human HCC cell lines with epithelial phenotype (HepG2, Huh-7, and Hep3B), with IC50 values of 0.08, 0.05, and 0.04 μM, respectively (Figure 1A, left graph), and three HCC cell lines with mesenchymal phenotype (SNU-449, SNU-398, and SNU-423), with IC50 values of 0.05, 0.02, and 0.03 μM, respectively (Figure 1A, right graph). We confirmed the anti-proliferative effect of CYT997 in HCC cell lines using the colony formation assay. Compared with vehicle (DMSO)-treated control, HCC cells treated with CYT997-treated HCC showed marked reductions in both colony number and colony size (Figure 1B,C; p = 0.001). The IC50 values of CYT997 in three batches of primary human hepatocytes (HU8264 HU8216, and HU167; see Table S1 for donor characteristics) were markedly higher at 31.57, 12.81, and 12.32 μM (Figure 1D). Thus, CYT997 preferentially inhibited the proliferation of HCC cells while sparing the normal liver cells.

  When co-treated with sorafenib, CYT997 reduced the IC50 of sorafenib from 22.81 to 7.47 μM in HepG2 cells, 11.45 to 4.85 μM in Huh-7 cells. 12.02 μM to 8.23 in Hep3B, 29.45 to 1.23 μM in SNU-449 cells, 19.72 to 12.91 μM in SNU-398 cells and 35.69 μM to 1.56 in SNU-423 cells (Figure S1). Among these cell lines, CYT997 significantly sensitized HepG2, Huh-7, SNU-449, and SNU-423 to sorafenib treatment.


### CYT997-induced G2/M cell cycle arrest in HCC cells via activation of MEK1/2
  Treatment of HCC cells with CYT997 at 0.1 μM and 1 μM caused a significant arrest of HCC cells in the G2/M phase (Figure 2A), shown by a significant increase in the population of cells in G2/M phase compared with untreated control. The percentage of cell population in control, 0.1 μM, or 1 μM CYT997-treated respective HCC cells were as follows: HepG2 (Control-15.0 ± 1.2%, 0.1 μM- 44.5 ± 0.4%, and 1 μM 45.2 ± 1.6%), Huh-7 (Control-18.5 ± 1.8%; 0.1 μM-46.3 ± 5.2%; and 1 μM 53.4 ± 6.2%), aHep3B (Control-21.7 ± 3.7%; 0.1 μM- 77.3 ± 3.3%, and 1 μM 79.1 ± 0.5%), and SNU-449 (Control-30.3 ± 0.8%; 0.1 μM-80.7 ± 2.5%, and 1 μM-84.7 ± 4.8%). Correspondingly, we observed up-regulation of proteins that are associated with cell cycle regulation, such as, p-MEK1/2, p-ERK, and p21, and down-regulation of Cyclin B1 (Figure 2B).

  Pretreatment of HCC cells with MEK inhibitor U0126 significantly reduced the percentage of cells arrested in the G2/M phase. In Hep3B cells treated with 0.1 μM CYT997, the percentage of cells in the G2 phase was reduced from 71.23% to 48.46% when cells were pretreated with 20 μM of U0126 (p = 0.05; Figure 2C upper panel). Similarly, in SNU-449 cells treated with 0.1 μM CYT997, the percentage of cells in the G2 phase was reduced from 63.85% to 51.9% after pretreatment with 20 μM U0126 (p = 0.01; Figure 2C lower panel). Our data suggest that inhibition of MEK attenuated the G2/M arrest caused by CYT997.


### CYT997 differentially induced senescence-like morphology in HCC cells
  We next evaluated whether the G2/M arrest and induction of p21 by CYT997 in HCC cells (HepG2, Huh-7, Hep3B and SNU-449) can lead to senescence in these cells. We observed that HepG2, Huh-7, and Hep3B cells had significantly higher numbers of SA β-galstained cells in at least one of the two concentrations of CYT997 used (Figure 3A); however, no significant difference was observed in SNU-449 cells. In all four cell lines, Lamin B1 expression was unaffected by CYT997 treatment (Figure 3B); some cells were observed to be enlarged in size, which is one of the characteristic morphological features of senescent cells.


### CYT997-induced apoptosis in HCC cells
  Cell cycle arrest may lead to apoptosis if damage is unrepairable. We observed that CYT997 caused apoptosis of HepG2, Huh-7, Hep3B, and SNU-449 cells. Significant increases were observed in the early apoptotic (Q3 quadrant), late apoptotic (Q2 quadrant) and necrotic population (Q1 quadrant) of HCC cells (HepG2, Huh-7, Hep3B, and SNU-449) treated with CYT997, with respect to vehicle-treated controls (Figure 4A). Next, we evaluated the expression of caspase-3 (using antibody capable of detecting both pro- and cleaved caspase-3), which is important for the execution of apoptosis. However, we did not observe any decrease in the levels of pro-caspase-3 except for Hep3B (Figure 4B), suggesting that the mechanism of CYT997-mediated apoptosis may occur via both caspase-3-dependent and independent pathways in HCC cells.


### CYT997 reduced the migration and invasion potential of HCC cells
  Since microtubules play an important role in cell motility, we studied the effects of CYT997 on HCC cell motility. In the wound healing assay, HepG2, Huh-7, Hep3B, and SNU-449 cells treated with CYT997 (0.02, or 0.04 μM) for 48 h showed significantly greater wound gap compared with their respective untreated controls (Figure 5A). The percentage of gap area in control, 0.02 μM, or 0.04 μM CYT997-treated HCC cells were: HepG2 (Control-44.6 ± 2.4%; 0.02 μM – 42.65 ± 6.5%; and 0.04 μM 66.8 ± 6.2%), Huh-7 (Control-3.5 ± 1.4%; 0.02 μM – 2.0 ± 0.6%; and 0.04 μM – 12.7 ± 1.1%), Hep3B (Control-13.7 ± 3.0%; 0.02 μM- 16.9 ± 2.1%; and 0.04 μM – 52.7 ± 4.6%), and SNU-449 (Control-9.5 ± 0.12%; 0.02 μM- 25.10 ± 2.88%; and 0.04 μM – 60.73 ± 1.54%). This observation suggests that CYT997 inhibited the motility of HCC cells (Figure 5A).

  In the Transwell migration assay, treatment with CYT997 significantly reduced migration of HepG2, Huh-7, Hep3B, and SNU-449 cells to the lower chamber as compared with vehicle-treated controls. Cell invasion assay through Matrigel consistently showed significant reduction in the number of invaded cells in HCC cells treated with CYT997 compared with vehicle-treated controls (Figure 5B).


### CYT997 down-regulated expression of tubulins, GPC3, β-catenin, and c-Myc
  Next, we investigated the probable mechanism of anti-tumour action of CYT997 in HCC cells. Since CYT997 is a microtubule depolymerizing agent, we first assessed its effect on tubulin expression in HCC cells. Immunoblotting showed that CYT997 treatment led to destabilization of microtubules as evident by the down-regulation of acetylated-α-tubulin and β-tubulins (Figure 6A). The down-regulation of β-tubulins by CYT997 was substantially rescued when HepG2 cells were pretreated with proteasome inhibitor MG132, indicating that CYT997 down-regulated β-tubulins through the proteasomal degradation system (Figure 6B). Because of the association of glypican-3 (GPC3) with acetylated tubulin,25 we also studied the effect of CYT997 on GPC3 expression in HCC cells. Immunoblotting showed that treatment with CYT997 down-regulated the expression of GPC3 (except in SNU449 cells which are GPC3 negative), as well as that of β-catenin, and c-Myc (Figure 6A).


### CYT997 inhibited the growth of HCC in PDX mouse models
  We used orthotopic HCC PDX as a mouse model to study the in vivo effects of CYT997. Limited toxicity studies in NSG mice suggested the maximum tolerate dose was 20 mg/kg for CYT997 (Table S2); this dose was used for subsequent treatment of mice bearing PDX.

  Data for PDX1 are shown in Figure 7A,B; PDX2 in Figure 7C,D. Based on weekly monitoring of tumour luciferase signals, CYT997 significantly inhibited growth of PDX1 and PDX2 (compared with vehicle-treated controls) at the end of the respective treatment period (Day 25 for PDX1 and Day 42 for PDX2) (Figure 7A,C, left graphs). At the end of the treatment period, region of interest (ROI) analysis showed that the tumour volumes of CYT997-treated animals were significantly smaller than that in vehicle-treated animals, respectively for PDX1 and PDX2 (Figure 7B,D; bioluminescence images on left) (p ≤ 0.001, 2-way ANOVA). Consistent with imaging data, CYT997 caused significant reductions in tumour volumes of both PDX1 and PDX2 (Figure 7B,D; quantification of tumour volumes on right), compared with vehicle-treated groups. In the PDX2 group, CYT997 caused a greater reduction in tumour volume than sorafenib (Figure 7D).

  In vivo toxicology analysis revealed that animals bearing the PDX1 tumour showed a non-significant, but notable body weight loss (p > 0.05; 26% vs. 18% in vehicle controls vs. CYT997-treated) by Day 21, compared with Day 0 (Figure 7A, right graph). Due to the decrease in body weight and large tumour size, these mice were sacrificed on Day 25. Animals bearing PDX2 and treated with vehicle, 20 mg/kg CYT997, or 50 mg/kg sorafenib showed a small increase (2%–5%) in body weight (p > 0.05) (Figure 7C, right graph). These results indicate that the severe body weight loss observed in PDX1 study was the result of tumour growth and not due to CYT997 toxicity. In addition, there were no significant changes in blood parameters and liver function tests (Table S3).

  IHC staining of harvested PDX tissue revealed a decrease in the proliferation marker Ki-67, in CYT997-treated tissues compared with untreated tissues, suggesting that the anti-tumour effect of CYT997 manifests as a decrease in cell proliferation together with enhanced apoptosis. Additionally, we observed the down-regulation of GPC3 and c-Myc in CYT997-treated PDX tissues compared with untreated control tissues (Figure 8 and Figure S2), consistent with our immunoblot data.


## DISCUSSION
  Rapid advances in AI have led to its enhanced applications in the field of drug discovery, especially for areas with unmet needs such as oncology. In this study, we used a proprietary, integrative computational platform (that links human disease models to pharmacological properties of FDA-approved drugs) to identify an orally available microtubule targeting agent, CYT997, for the treatment of HCC. This discovery was successfully validated by our preclinical experiments, demonstrating the robustness of our approach.

  CYT997 is a microtubule depolymerizing agent that has been shown to exert cytotoxic and vascular disrupting properties in multiple types of cancers.13,14 Consistently, we observed selective inhibition of HCC cell proliferation, but not that of normal primary hepatocytes, suggesting a favourable therapeutic index and safety profile. In clinical trials for other solid tumours, it has proven to be well tolerated.26,27 CYT997 sensitized HCC cells to the effect of sorafenib, suggesting that combination therapy may be clinically beneficial. The inhibition of HCC cell growth by CYT997 was associated with a significant arrest of HCC cells in the G2/M phase of the cell cycle, along with up-regulation of p21. These observations are consistent with previous studies on CYT997 and other microtubule inhibitors.14,28 Cell senescence can result from arrests in both the G1,29 and G2/M phases.30 Indeed, we observed senescence-associated phenotypes such as higher positive staining for β-gal, and flat, enlarged cell sizes in CYT997 treated HCC cells without any change in the expression of Lamin B1, a widely reported as senescence marker.31 Consistent with reports that CYT997 can induce apoptosis in different cancer types,14,20 we also observed that CYT997 caused significant induction of apoptosis in HCC cells.

  Microtubules facilitate the protrusion of cells during migration, which play key roles in normal physiological, as well as pathological states like cancer metastasis.32 When the integrity of microtubules is impaired, so is cell migration. We observed that CYT997 down-regulated acetylated α-tubulin, which was associated with the inhibition of migration and invasion abilities of HCC cells. Acetylated α-tubulin has been reported to be associated with stabilizing microtubules,33 while promoting directional cell migration.34 In HCC, increased levels of acetylated α-tubulin is predictive of a high rate of recurrence and reduced overall survival.35 CYT997 reduced the expression of acetylated α-tubulin which suggests a destabilizing effect of CYT997 on the microtubules of HCC cells, thereby reducing their viability. Additionally, CYT997 down-regulated β-tubulin; consistent with reported effects of tubulin-binding agent Withaferin which caused proteasomal degradation of tubulin.36 We observed that the down-regulation of β-tubulin by CYT997 was partially rescued by a proteasomal inhibitor, suggesting that it occurs to some extent through the proteasomal degradation pathway.

  Schlaepfer et al. reported the co-localization of glypican-3 (GPC3) and acetylated tubulin in oral squamous carcinoma cells.25 We observed correlated down-regulation of both GPC3 and acetylated α-tubulin in three GPC3 expressing HCC cell lines treated with CYT997. Liu et al. reported that Dactolisib (PI3K and mTOR inhibitor) and paclitaxel (a tubulin polymerizing agent) alone reduced the expression of GPC3, and combination treatment significantly reduced the expression of GPC3 in HCC in vivo.37 GPC3 has been reported as a transcriptional target of c-Myc; the expression of c-Myc is also regulated by GPC3 under feedback mechanism.38 Indeed, we observed down-regulation of c-Myc in HCC cells treated with CYT997, albeit to a lesser extent than GPC3, suggesting that CYT997 likely regulates GPC3 and c-Myc via independent mechanisms. Colchicine derivatives (tubulin destabilizers) have similarly been shown to down-regulate 20%–60% of c-Myc at the transcriptional level.39

  The expressions of both GPC3 and c-Myc are critical in the development and progression of HCC. GPC3 is overexpressed in more than 70% of HCC,40 and has been associated with poor prognosis in HCC patients.41 Functionally, GPC3 has been reported to regulate the Wnt/β-catenin signalling pathway which is hyperactivated in HCC.42 In this study, we found CYT997 to inhibit the expression of β-catenin in all four cell lines studied, suggesting that CYT997 may potentially interfere with the GPC3/Wnt signalling pathway to result in HCC cell growth inhibition. In a recent meta-analysis report, over-expression of c-Myc has been reported in 30% of HCC samples.43 The over-expression of c-Myc could predict the poor overall survival in HCC patients.44 Wang et al. reported that the over-expression of transforming growth factor-beta (TGFβ) promotes metastasis, thereby enhancing HCC progression driven by c-Myc.45 When c-Myc was over-expressed (and P53 inactivated) in transgenic mouse models, 40% of these mice developed HCC.44 Therefore, the down-regulation of GPC3, β-catenin, and c-Myc by CYT997 potentially contributes towards its cytotoxic effect in HCC.

  Microtubule-binding agents including nocodazole, CYT997, and colchicine have been reported to exert modulating effects on the activated form of mitogen-activated protein kinase/ERK kinase (MEK) and extracellular-signal-regulated kinase (ERK) signalling cascade,46,47 leading to cell cycle arrest or apoptosis-inducing effect in different cancer cells. ERK signalling of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway phosphorylates various intracellular targets including cytoskeletal proteins, and regulates processes, such as proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis.48 However, elevated level of ERK in response to DNA damage or metabolic stress (in cell-dependent manner) and overactivation of MEK1/2 can lead to cell cycle arrest by up-regulating CDK inhibitor such as p21.49,50 Consistently, we observed an up-regulation of p-MEK, p-ERK, and p21 and down-regulation of cyclin B1, together with the arrest of cells in the G2/M phase of cell cycle. Pretreatment with MEK inhibitor U0126 attenuated the cell cycle effect of CYT997.

  In vivo, CYT997 significantly reduced the tumour growth and tumour volumes of two HCC PDX models. Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining supported this observation, showing marked inhibition in the expression of proliferation marker Ki-67. This is consistent with the study by Teng et al., which reported that CYT997 reduced the metastatic nodules on pulmonary and hepatic surface.47 Consistent with Western blot data on HCC cells treated with CYT997, IHC staining revealed down-regulation of GPC3 and c-Myc in PDX tissues treated with CYT997. Toxicology studies of CYT997 in mice indicated its favourable safety profile, similar to our observations in primary hepatocytes.


## CONCLUSION
  In conclusion, we have successfully identified a potential drug candidate, CYT997, using a proprietary AI drug discovery platform, and provided experimental validation of its anti-tumour activity in HCC cell lines and PDX mouse models. Anti-tumour activity of CYT997 was associated with significant cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, inhibitions of HCC cell migration and invasion potentials, as well as down-regulation of tubulins and the oncofetoprotein GPC3, an established therapeutic target for HCC. The therapeutic activity of CYT997, along with its relative safety profiles and proven mechanisms of action, suggest that our AI technology is able to effectively identify highly relevant drug candidates for HCC, holding promise for drug discovery in other cancers and diseases.
