# Structural and Dynamic Changes of Nucleosome upon GATA3 Binding


## Abstract
  Pioneer factors, which can directly bind to nucleosomes, have been considered to change chromatin conformations. However, the binding impact on the nucleosome is little known. Here, we show how the pioneer factor GATA3 binds to nucleosomal DNA and affects the conformation and dynamics of nucleosomes by using a combination of SAXS, molecular modeling, and molecular dynamics simulations. Our structural models, consistent with the SAXS data, indicate that only one of the two DNA binding domains, N- and C-fingers, of GATA3 binds to an end of the DNA in solution. Our MD simulations further showed that the other unbound end of the DNA increases the fluctuation and enhances the DNA dissociation from the histone core when the N-finger binds to a DNA end, a site near the entry or exit of the nucleosome. However, this was not true for the binding of the C-finger that binds to a location about 15 base pairs distant from the DNA end. In this case, DNA dissociation occurred on the bound end. Taken together, we suggest that the N-finger and C-finger bindings of GATA3 commonly enhance DNA dissociation at one of the two DNA ends (the bound end for the C-finger binding and the unbound end for the N-finger binding), leading to triggering a conformational change in the chromatin.


## Introduction
  Chromatin conformation and its posttranslational modification are strongly associated with gene expression [1, 2]. In the condensed chromatin state, DNA accessibility of transcription factors is low, and gene expression is generally inactive. Nucleosome positioning analysis reveals that most genes have at the upstream of the transcription start site nucleosome depleted region to which many transcription regulatory proteins bind [3]. Among such proteins, some transcription factors, so-called pioneer transcription factors (PTFs) such as GATA3/4 and FoxA1/H1, can recognize their DNA motifs and directly bind to nucleosomal DNA. FoxH1 is, for instance, an essential maternal PTF during embryonic development that binds to specific DNA target sequences[4] and participates in the coordination of zygotic genome activation[5]. The PTFs are thus considered to initiate the opening of closed chromatin, which enables transcription factors to access genetic information, leading to activated gene expression. However, for most of the PTFs, the complex structures bound to nucleosomes have yet to be available, remaining that the impact of PTF’s binding to nucleosomes is unknown.

  To study the effect of PTF binding, much effort has been devoted to elucidating nucleosome-protein complex structures. The individual structures of SOX2 and SOX11 PTF-nucleosome complexes show DNA distortion at the SHL +2 binding site, where the minor groove was widened, and the DNA was slightly pulled away from the histone core [6]. In addition, both ends of the DNA were invisible in the cryo-EM density maps. Interestingly, the alternative structure of the SOX11-nucleosome in the same study showed that the DNA end distant from the binding site is dissociated while the DNA close to the binding site is well-wrapped. Two OCT-4-SOX2-nucleosome ternary structures in which the two proteins were bound to the DNA in a different orientation show that the proteins’ bindings at SHL −6 and SHL+6 individually cause dissociation of the DNA at the end and SHL +5, respectively, from the histone core, demonstrating the binding orientation gives the different effect on the nucleosome structure [7]. Another PTF, GATA3-bound nucleosome structure, shows that GATA3 does not cause significant structural changes in either nucleosomal DNA or histone octamer [8]. Collectively, the effect of PTF binding to the nucleosome remains unclear.

  Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation has been one of the tools used to study the dynamics of biomolecules and applied for elucidating the stability and dynamics of nucleosomes, especially for the behavior of histone tails that are invisible experimentally [9]. Before any PTF-bound nucleosome structure was available, examination of OCT4-binding motifs in a context of nucleosome structure by an MD study showed that the extent of flexibility and the motions such as nucleosomal DNA breathing, twisting, or local twisting were correlated with the number of motifs in the nucleosomal DNA, indicating that such features are encoded in DNA sequences [10].

  This study aims to reveal the dynamic evolution of the previously described complex and help understand how this PTF’s binding might contribute to nucleosome opening. To this end, the structure and dynamics of the GATA3-bound nucleosome were examined using SAXS, molecular modeling, and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. We first searched for conformations that matched the SAXS profile and found that these were always conformations in which only one of the two DNA binding domains of GATA3 binds to the nucleosome. Furthermore, MD simulations showed that when GATA3 binds to the entry/exit site, the nucleosome increases the flexibility of the DNA end at the other unbound entry/exit site. This indicates that suppression of the dynamics of one DNA end upon protein binding induces the increase in dynamics of the other DNA end, which may compensate for the loss in entropy at one end from a physical point of view.


## Results
  GATA3 has two finger domains, the N-terminal Zinc finger (N-finger) and the C-terminal Zinc finger (C-finger), which bind to specific DNA sequences. GATA3 – nucleosome complex structure [8] was reported, but the Cryo-EM structural analysis did not show sufficient electron density for one of the two fingers of GATA3 and histone tails. We first built the entire GATA3-bound nucleosome structure using the crystal structures of the GATA3 – DNA complex and the free nucleosome (see Methods). The missing histone tails were modeled with the molecular modeling tool Modeller [11], as described before [12]. We then fit the structure to the EM density. Starting with the EM-density fit structures, we searched for the GATA3-bound nucleosome conformations consistent with SAXS experiments. We finally examined the stability and dynamics of the GATA3-bound nucleosome.


### MD simulations of GATA3 – nucleosome complex to equilibrate the system
  Using the nine selected conformations as the initial conformations, MD simulations of the nucleosome-GATA3 complex were carried out at a constant pressure of 1 bar and a temperature of 300 K to equilibrate the system. Heavy atoms in GATA3 and the nucleosome without histone tails were fixed with decreasing restraints for 100 ns, and water molecules and ions were allowed to move. The system was then equilibrated for 900 ns at a constant pressure of one bar and a temperature of 300 K without these restraints. GATA3 maintained the bound state of both fingers for 900 ns in three of the nine simulations, while in the remaining six simulations, the C-finger domain of GATA3 was slightly displaced from the major groove of the DNA at ~ 300 ns.

  We computed the SAXS curves for each conformation in the three MD simulations in which both fingers of GATA3 were stably bound to the nucleosome. SAXS measurement was carried out for GATA3-bound nucleosome and GATA3-free nucleosome (i.e., nucleosome). We derived the two χ2 values, χ2GATA3 for the GATA-bound nucleosome and χ2dGATA3 for the GATA-free nucleosome—the latter prepared structures by removing the GATA3 from the GATA3-bound nucleosome trajectories. The distribution of the conformations was analyzed concerning χ2GATA3 and χ2dGATA3 in the three simulations, as shown in Figure S1. The minimum values of χ2GATA3 and χ2dGATA3 in all three simulations were 23.9 and 14.3, respectively (the SAXS profiles with the minimum χ2GATA3 and χ2dGATA3 are shown in Figures S2 and S3). This large χ2GATA3 value indicates that both fingers-bound conformations obtained by the MD simulations did not reproduce the SAXS curve. Therefore, it was speculated that both finger domains of GATA3 may not bind to the nucleosome under the conditions of the SAXS experiment. It should be noted that a clear correlation between χ2GATA3 and χ2dGATA3 (Figure S1) suggests that appropriately modeled conformation of the nucleosome, particularly that of the DNA, is crucial for achieving a small χ2GATA3 value. As a result, we considered a variety of conformations, including those in which either of the GATA3 fingers was dissociated from the nucleosome and ones in which the DNA end was open, to search for conformations that better match the experimental SAXS data.


### ABMD simulations of the system to generate conformations where GATA3 is partially dissociated from the nucleosome
  To generate a variety of the GATA3-partially dissociated conformations of the GATA3 – nucleosome complex, we employed an adaptively biased molecular dynamics (ABMD) simulation [13–19], which has been recently implemented in GENESIS [20, 21]. From each of the three simulations where both fingers of the GATA3 stably maintained the bound state, we selected six conformations in the trajectory from 400 to 900 ns at intervals of 100 ns (18 conformations were selected in total) as initial conformations for the following ABMD simulations.

  The ABMD simulation of each system was carried out for up to 200 ns to observe whether the N-finger or C-finger of GATA3 dissociated from the nucleosome. Two distances, dN, and dC, were monitored, which correspond to the distance between the center of mass of the histone core and the center of mass of the N- or C-finger. Figure 1(a) shows that three types of partial dissociation of GATA3 from the nucleosome were observed: Type 1: Detachment of the N-finger (along the dN axis), Type 2: Detachment of the C-finger (along the dC axis), and Type 3: Detachment of both the N- and C-fingers accompanied by the unwrapping of the DNA (the diagonal direction). It should be noted that the DNA end at the GATA3-bound side (referred to as “B-end”) unwraps as the N-finger moves outward. As a result, db (defined as the distance between the center of mass of the third base pair from B-end and the center of mass of the histone core without histone tails) becomes large (Figure. 1(b)).

  We found that the conformations in which only one of the two fingers is bound to nucleosomal DNA give small χ2 values (Figures 2(a) and (b)). We also noticed a difference in the DNA conformations between N-finger and C-finger bound complexes, as depicted in Figure 2(c). In the N-finger bound complexes with small χ2 values, the DNA at the unbound side of GATA3 (U-end) was partially unwrapped while the B-end was well wrapped. Conversely, the DNA wrapping state was opposite in the C-finger bound complexes with small χ2 values.


### Stability and dynamics of N-finger and C-finger bound nucleosomes
  We carried out MD simulations for the N-finger and C-finger bound nucleosomes to investigate if they differ in stability and dynamics. For each bound form, 16 independent 600 ns-long MD simulations were conducted with different initial atomic velocities to obtain conformational ensembles (Table I). We also ran 12 independent MD simulations of a single nucleosome (without GATA3) as a reference (Table I).

  We examined the last 500 ns trajectories for each type of form and computed both χ2GATA3 and χ2dGATA3 for each conformation. The distributions of the conformations concerning χ2GATA3 and χ2dGATA3 for each type are shown in Figure S2. The minimum values of χ2GATA3 and χ2dGATA3 were 5.5 and 9.6 for the C-finger bound form, respectively, and 4.0 and 4.5 for the N-finger bound form, respectively (the SAXS profiles with the minimum χ2GATA3 and χ2dGATA3 are shown in Figures S2 and S3). Although the minimum value of χ2GATA3 for the C-finger bound is small enough to match the experimental SAXS curve, the distribution was wide, indicating that the χ2GATA3 did not stay at low values during the simulations. On the other hand, in the N-finger bound form, the χ2GATA3 maintained low values, suggesting that this form is more stable. Moreover, in this form, the χ2dGATA3 value was also kept low, indicating that the nucleosome conformation of the N-finger bound nucleosome is more similar to that of the free nucleosome.

  Figures 3(a) and (b) show the population distribution for the N-finger bound and C-finger bound GATA3 – nucleosome complex, respectively, plotted against two distances, du and db, from the center of mass of the histone core to either of the U and B-ends. The U-end had a wide distribution in the N-finger bound form while not in the C-finger bound form. In contrast, the B-end had a wide distribution in the C-finger bound form, while not in the N-finger form. These results show a distinct impact of N-finger and C-finger bindings on the nucleosome. Still, it is also considered due to the initial conformations of the H3 histone tail. We carried out MD simulations to rule out this possibility by removing GATA3 from the structure. Figures 3(c) and (d) show the population distribution for the GATA3-free nucleosome. In the N-finger bound form, the magnitude of the unwrapping of the U-end (du) was larger than that in the GATA3-free nucleosome, indicating that the binding of the N-finger facilitates the unwrapping of the U-end. However, there was little difference in db, indicating that the binding of the N-finger has little effect on the B-end. In the C-finger bound form, the magnitude of the unwrapping of the B-end (db) tended to be larger than that in the GATA-free nucleosome, indicating that the binding of the C-finger facilitates the unwrapping of the B-end. du appears to be slightly larger in the C-finger bound form than that in the GATA3-free nucleosome (Figures 3(b) and (d)). However, the difference in du is negligibly minor compared to the N-finger bound form, indicating that the unwrapping of the U-end is not facilitated in the C-finger bound form.

  We also examined the impact of the GATA3 binding to the nucleosomal DNA other than the DNA ends. The change in the atomic fluctuation of the DNA was remarkable at both DNA ends, while they were small for the other region, suggesting that the GATA3 binding impact appears at either end of the DNA (Figure 4).

  In summary, the N-finger domain of GATA3 bound to the nucleosome prevented the B-end from unwrapping while promoting the unwrapping of the U-end. The binding of the C-finger domain of GATA3 to the nucleosome did not prevent the unwrapping of the B-end (because the N-finger domain was dissociated from the B-end) and did not cause the unwrapping of the U-end.


## Discussion
  From the MD simulations of the free nucleosome and the analysis of the χ2 values (χ2free) for the SAXS profile, it became apparent that one end of the DNA in the free nucleosome was wrapped around the histone core, and the other end was unwrapped in solution. Moreover, we found that the nucleosome conformations in the GATA3 – nucleosome complex were like those of the free nucleosome when the χ2GATA3 values were small (Figure S4). Corresponding to the asymmetrical unwrapping of DNA in the free nucleosome, there are two types of conformations in the complex when the χ2GATA3 value is small. One is that the N-finger-bound end of the DNA wraps, and the unbound end partially unwraps. The other is that the C-finger-bound end of the DNA, which is the same end as the N-finger-bound end, partially unwraps, and the unbound end wraps (Figure 2).

  Tanaka et al. [8] demonstrated that when the GATA3-binding sequences (5-GAT-3) are tandemly present in both SHL+5.5 and SHL+6.5 of the nucleosome (SHL5e), GATA3 effectively binds to the nucleosome. However, the binding significantly weakens when the sequence is present in only one of these positions. Consequently, in nucleosomes with such sequences, it is considered that both fingers are involved in binding to the nucleosome. However, our results indicate that both fingers do not bind to the nucleosome simultaneously. Instead, one of them binds to the nucleosome. Considering all these findings together, it implies that when GATA3 binds to the nucleosome, two stable binding states exist, with transitions occurring between them via an unstable state where both fingers are bound, which is likely unfavorable regarding entropy.

  The impact of GATA3 binding on the DNA wrapping state at the B- and U-ends can be explained in terms of free energy. In this study, we found that the conformations with one of the two ends of the DNA partially unwrapped gives small χ2. When the N-finger domain of GATA3 binds to nucleosomal DNA, the end of the DNA on the bound side tends to be tightly wrapped because the attached N-finger domain of GATA3 prevents the B-end from dissociating from the histone core. However, the U-end is facilitated to unwrap, possibly to compensate for the conformational entropy loss of the B-end upon N-finger binding. We previously showed that the asymmetric unwrapping of nucleosomal DNA is more likely to occur than the symmetric unwrapping [18]. Taken together, this asymmetrical unwrapping of the two DNA ends upon GATA3 binding would be enhanced.-Takaku et al. showed that GATA3 binding brings about two scenarios dependent on the binding positions [22]. One scenario is nucleosome eviction when GATA3 binds to SHL+2. The other is nucleosome sliding, after which GATA3 binding motif appears at SHL+5 after sliding [8]. Indeed, the observed asymmetrical unwrapping of the DNA ends upon GATA3 binding would facilitate the nucleosome sliding.

  Some limitations and future directions should be noted. First, our GATA3 model includes only the DNA binding domains and lacks the transactivation domains necessary for nucleosome eviction and sliding [22]. Intact GATA3, as well as recruited co-factors, will show more interesting features of the GATA3 binding. Second, we used the Widom 601-based DNA sequence, mainly used for experimental studies because of the nature of the well-defined positioning. It will also be interesting to see the binding effect on native DNA sequences. Last, we studied the GATA3 binding at SHL+5 based on the Cryo-EM density map [8]. The previous research suggests that GATA3 can also bind to the DNA at SHL+2 [8], indicating its various interaction manners to be further explored.


## Methods

### Sample preparation for the SAXS experiment
  Recombinant human core histone proteins (H2A, H2B, H3.1, and H4) were expressed and purified as described previously [23]. Histone octamer was reconstituted and purified using Superdex200 (Cytive) gel filtration column chromatography as described previously [23]. The nucleosome was reconstituted with 145 bp DNA containing the GATA3 target motif (SHL5e) and was purified as described previously [8]. Recombinant human GATA3znf was purified as described previously [8].

  To reconstitute the GATA3 – nucleosome complex, the purified nucleosome and GATA3znf were mixed in reaction buffer containing 13.3 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, 6.7% glycerol, 1.3 mM DTT, 0.67 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, and 0.3 μM ZnSO4. The reaction mixture was incubated for 1hr at 25 °C. After the incubation, the sample was loaded on a 5–20% sucrose density gradient, containing 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 20 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 1 μM ZnSO4, and was centrifugated at 27,000 r.p.m for 16 h, using SW 41 Ti rotor (Beckman Coulter). After the ultracentrifugation, fractions (0.6 ml each) were collected from the top of the gradient and were analyzed by native PAGE (Figure S5). The fractions containing GATA3 – nucleosome complex were combined and desalted using a PD-10 column (Cytive) in 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5) buffer containing 1 mM DTT and 1μM ZnSO4. The sample was then concentrated with an Amicon Ultra centrifugal filter unit (Merck, 30,000 MWCO) and stored on ice. For the SAXS experiment, all samples were dialyzed against 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5) buffer containing 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, and 1 μM ZnSO4.


### SAXS experiment
  SAXS measurement was performed with a laboratory-based instrument, NANOPIX (Rigaku, Japan), equipped with a high-brilliance point-focused generator of a Cu-Kα source (wavelength = 1.54 Å). Scattered X-rays were counted with a two-dimensional semi-conductor detector HyPix-6000 (Rigaku, Japan). The sample-to-detector distances were set to be 1330 and 300 mm, which covers the scattering vector (Q)-range of 0.007 Å−1 ≤ Q ≤ 0.7 Å−1. The measurement was carried out at 25 °C. A two-dimensional scattering pattern was converted to a one-dimensional scattering profile with the SAngler software[24]. After correction by the transmittance and subtraction of the buffer scattering, the absolute scattering intensity was obtained using the standard scattering intensity of pure water (1.632 × 10−2 cm−1).

  To eliminate the effect of aggregates in the experimental scattering profile, we conducted the analytical ultracentrifugation (AUC)-SAXS treatment [25]. AUC measurement was performed with ProteomeLab XL-I (Beckman Coulter, USA). The sedimentation data was acquired using Rayleigh interference optics at the rotor speed of 40,000 rpm. The weight fractions of the main component and its aggregates were derived from the sedimentation data with SEDFIT software[26]. The AUC-SAXS treatment finally offered the aggregation-free scattering profile[27], which was used to compare with the MD simulation data.


### Building the initial GATA3-nucleosome complex model for EM-fitting
  The initial GATA3 – nucleosome complex structure was modeled using an EM structure (EMD-0783) [8], an X-ray crystal structure of a nucleosome with the Widom 601 DNA sequence of 145 bp DNA (PDB code: 3lz0) [28], and X-ray crystal structures of the DNA and GATA3 complex (PDB codes: 4hca and 4hc7). The two GATA3 fingers in 4hca were initially placed by structural alignment of the DNA in 4hc7 and the corresponding DNA in EMD-0783. Note that the linker connecting the two fingers was initially deleted and was later modeled in the presence of the nucleosome using Modeller [11, 29] with default parameters. The missing parts of histones were also modeled using Modeller, as described before [12]. Finally, the atomic model of the complex was composed of two of H3 (135 residues), H4 (102 residues), H2A (129 residues), and H2B (125 residues),145 base pairs of DNA, and the two fingers of GATA3 (110 residues). We defined residues 5 to 41 and 59 to 95 as the N-finger and C-finger domains, respectively.


### Intact complex structural model construction by Cryo-EM fitting simulation
  Starting with the initial entire structure of the GATA3-bound nucleosome, we carried out cryo-EM fitting simulations [30] to fit the structure into the EM density map of the GATA3 – nucleosome complex. The cryo-EM fitting simulation was carried out using GENENSIS [20, 21] at a constant temperature of 300 K for 1,000 ps.

  The cryo-EM data used was EMD-0783 from the electron microscopy database at the European Bioinformatics Institute (resolution: 3.15 Å, voxel size: 1.05 Å) [8]. The energy term for the cryo-EM fitting is,  U=Uatom+WEM×(1-c.c.)  where Uatom is the energy function for atoms and c.c. is the correlation coefficient between the theoretical and experimental density maps,  c.c.=∑ijkρexp(i,j,k)⋅ρsim(i,j,k)∑ijkρexp2(i,j,k)∑ijkρsim2(i,j,k)  where (i,j,k) is the index of a voxel in the density map, and ρexp and ρsim are the experimental and simulated EM densities, respectively. The simulated EM density map was generated at 3.15 Å resolution with 1.05 Å voxel size to match the experimental data. The atoms in the histone tails were not included in the calculation for the c.c. because they are not visible in the cryo-EM density map. WEM is the weight for UEM.WEM should be large enough to move the atomic model into an EM density map, while at the same time, it should also be small to avoid the overfit to the density map with structural distortion. We set the weight of the EM-fitting force to WEM=5.0×103,1.0×104,1.5×104,2.0×104,5.0×104, and 1.0 × 105. Figure S6 shows the c.c. increased as the EM-fitting weight increased; however, distortion of protein structure and DNA became noticeable.

  For each model, ten conformations extracted at every 100 ps in the simulation were analyzed using the MolProbity web service [31–33], as shown in Figure S7. The conformations were assessed by Ramachandran, CaBLAM, and CA Geometry outliers. CaBLAM and CA Geometry outliers are good indicators to analyze the local backbone for 2.5 – 4 Å resolution. Ramachandran validation is sensitive to small dihedral changes and is a powerful, high-resolution tool. CaBLAM is a validation tool of a protein backbone that is more robust in the 2.5 – 4.0 Å regime than Ramachandran analysis. CaBLAM is most helpful in validating secondary structure elements in low-resolution models. Figure S7 shows that the CaBLAM outliers did not increase up to WEM=2.0×104, while at WEM>2.0×104, the CaBLAM outliers increased. With the above results, nine conformations from the trajectory with WEM=2.0×104 were selected as the fitted conformations into the cryo-EM density map.


### The systems in water
  Simulations (Table I) were carried out using an MD simulation program called GENESIS [20, 21] and AMBER 20 [34] with the AMBER ff14SB [35], bsc1 [36], and ff99ions08 [37] force fields for the histone core, DNA and ions, respectively. The zinc AMBER force field (ZAFF) was used for the 4-coordinated zinc metal centers in the two zinc finger domains of GATA3 [38].

  The modeled structure of GATA3 – nucleosome complex was placed in a cubic box of ~ 160 Å × 160 Å × 160 Å. About 119,000 TIP3P water molecules [39] were added to surround the system. To neutralize the charges of the system, sodium ions were placed in the box, and then additional sodium and chloride ions were added at a concentration of 0.15M NaCl. In total, the system comprised ~ 384,000 atoms.

  The dielectric constant used was 1.0, and the van der Waals interactions were evaluated with a cut-off radius of 9 Å. The PME method [40, 41] was used for the electrostatic interactions for the direct space cutoff of 9 Å. The Langevin dynamics algorithm was utilized to control the temperature and pressure of the system. The temperature and pressure control coupling times were set at 2 ps−1. The SHAKE algorithm [42, 43] was used to constrain all the bond lengths involving hydrogen atoms. The leap-frog algorithm with a time step of 2 fs was used throughout the simulation to integrate the equations of motion.


### ABMD conditions
  In the ABMD, the reaction coordinate was set to be the distance between the center of mass of the two finger domains of GATA3 and the center of mass of the histone core (excluding histone tails). The range of the reaction coordinate for the ABMD simulations was set at 0≤d≤150Å, while the resolution of the reaction coordinate (Δd) and the flooding time scale (τ) were set at 1.5 Å and 400 ps, respectively.


### Measures used for characterizing nucleosome conformation
  We used the following four distances for characterizing the nucleosome conformation. du (and db) is the distance between the center of mass of the third base pair from the U-end (and B-end) and the center of mass of the histone core (without histone tails). dN (and dC) is the distance between the center of mass of the histone core (without histone tails) and the center of mass of the N-finger (and C-finger) domain of GATA3.


### MD simulations for the N-finger and C-finger bound complexes.
  The MD simulations were carried out using AMBER20 [34], and a conformation with the smallest χ2 value for the SAXS profile in the ABMD simulation was used as the initial conformation for each MD simulation. The distances of the initial conformation between the center of mass of the histone core and U-end (du), B-end (db), N-finger (dN) and C-finger (dC) were respectively 56.6 Å, 53.0 Å, 59.1 Å, and 75.2 Å for the N-finger bound form and 52.7 Å, 59.4 Å, 82.1 Å, and 54.6 Å for the C-finger bound form. The complex was placed in a larger box of (192 Å × 192 Å × 192 Å) so that the molecules in the complex were at least 20 Å away from the box boundary. The concentration of NaCl was set at 0.15 M. The system comprised ~ 668,000 atoms with ~ 213,000 TIP3P water molecules [39]. The MD simulations were carried out at a constant pressure of 1 bar and a temperature of 300 K for 600 ns (100 and 500 ns with and without the positional restraints, respectively).

  For reference, 12 independent MD simulations of a single nucleosome (without GATA3) were also carried out for 600 ns (Table I). The initial conformations were prepared by simply removing the GATA3 molecules from the 12 complex structures with the smallest χ2GATA-values in the ensembles for the N-finger or C-finger bound forms. The ensembles used for analysis were taken from the 16 independent 600 ns-long simulations for the N-finger bound and C-finger bound complexes described above.


### Selection of candidate models by SAXS profiles
  The MD simulations generated a considerable number of conformations of the nucleosome bound with GATA3 and of the free nucleosome. To select appropriate conformations that well reproduced the experimentally obtained scattering curves, we computed the χ2 value defined as, where Np is the number of experimental points. IeQi and IcQi are the  χ2=1Np∑i=1Np (IeQi-cIcQi+aσQi)2  experimental and computed scattering intensities at a scattering angle, Qi, respectively. σQi is the experimental error, “a” is the offset that accounts for possible systematic a errors due to mismatched buffers in the experimental data, and c is a scale factor given by[44]  c=∑i=1Np IeQiIcQiσQi2/∑i=1Np IcQi2σQi2.

  The scattering intensities IcQi were computed using CRYSOL3 [45] with default parameters for the fitting (using -def option). Smaller χ2 values indicate a better fit to the experimental curve.

  We obtained experimental scattering curves for the nucleosome bound with GATA3 IeGATA3Qi and free IefreeQi. Similarly, we computed scattering curves IcGATA3Qi and IcfreeQi from the conformations obtained by the MD simulations and derived χ2 values for the corresponding experimental curves. We named χ2 of IcGATA3Qi to IeGATA3Qi as χGATA32 and those of IcfreeQi to IefreeQi as χfree2. Moreover, to see the effect of the binding of GATA3, we removed GATA3 from the nucleosome complex and computed the SAXS curves IcdGATA3Qi to derive the χ2 values for IefreeQi, which was named as χdGATA32.
