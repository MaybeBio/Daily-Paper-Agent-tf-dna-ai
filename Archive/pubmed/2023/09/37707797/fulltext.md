# Interpretable predictive models of genome-wide aryl hydrocarbon receptor-DNA binding reveal tissue-specific binding determinants


## Abstract
  The aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) is an inducible transcription factor whose ligands include the potent environmental contaminant 2,3,7,8-tetrachlorodibenzo-p-dioxin (TCDD). Ligand-activated AhR binds to DNA at dioxin response elements (DREs) containing the core motif 5′-GCGTG-3′. However, AhR binding is highly tissue specific. Most DREs in accessible chromatin are not bound by TCDD-activated AhR, and DREs accessible in multiple tissues can be bound in some and unbound in others. As such, AhR functions similarly to many nuclear receptors. Given that AhR possesses a strong core motif, it is suited for a motif-centered analysis of its binding. We developed interpretable machine learning models predicting the AhR binding status of DREs in MCF-7, GM17212, and HepG2 cells, as well as primary human hepatocytes. Cross-tissue models predicting transcription factor (TF)-DNA binding generally perform poorly. However, reasons for the low performance remain unexplored. By interpreting the results of individual within-tissue models and by examining the features leading to low cross-tissue performance, we identified sequence and chromatin context patterns correlated with AhR binding. We conclude that AhR binding is driven by a complex interplay of tissue-agnostic DRE flanking DNA sequence and tissue-specific local chromatin context. Additionally, we demonstrate that interpretable machine learning models can provide novel and experimentally testable mechanistic insights into DNA binding by inducible TFs.


## Materials and methods

### 

#### 

##### Reference genome
  The reference genome used for sequence alignment was the human genome assembly version hg19. We opted for hg19 due to availability of data on ChIP-seq and DNase-seq data repositories such as GEO Datasets (Barrett et al., 2013), ChIP-Atlas (Oki et al., 2018), and ENCODE (Davis et al., 2018; Dunham et al., 2012). Likewise, most other TF binding prediction tools available at this time were trained on hg19. The cross-tissue model trained on MCF-7 data and used to predict T-47D data was trained on data aligned to hg38 reference genome.


##### Cell culture and treatment
  Primary human cryopreserved hepatocytes were obtained from Triangle Research Labs (Research Triangle Park, North Carolina). Cells were plated on rat collagen I-coated plates and Geltrex was added for a final concentration of 0.25 mg/ml approximately 6 h after plating. Cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere in William E medium supplemented with cell maintenance cocktail B (Invitrogen). TCDD was obtained from Dow Chemicals. Cells were treated for 24 h with a final concentration of 1 nM TCDD in 0.1% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Cells treated with DMSO (0.1%) were used as vehicle control.


##### Chromatin immunoprecipitation
  Human hepatocytes incubated with 1 nM TCDD or DMSO for 24 h were fixed with 1% formaldehyde at room temperature for 15 min and quenched with 0.125 M glycine and shipped to active motif (Carlsbad, California) for ChIP-seq analysis. A single sample was collected for both conditions and pooled input DNA was used a control experiment. Chromatin was isolated by adding lysis buffer, followed by disruption with a Dounce homogenizer. Lysates were sonicated using a Misonix Sonicator 3000 equipped with a microtip to shear the DNA to an average length of 300–500 bp. Lysates were cleared by centrifugation and stored at −80°C. Genomic DNA was prepared by treating aliquots of chromatin with RNase, proteinase K, and heat for de-crosslinking, followed by phenol/chloroform extraction and ethanol precipitation. Purified DNA was quantified on a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. Extrapolation to the original chromatin volume allowed quantification of the total chromatin yield. For each ChIP reaction, 30 μg of chromatin was precleared with protein A agarose beads (Invitrogen). ChIP reactions were set up using precleared chromatin and a rabbit polyclonal antibody against AhR (ENZO Lifesciences, BML-SA210, Lot. no. 01031447) and incubated overnight at 4°C. Protein A agarose beads were added and incubation at 4°C was continued for another 3 h. Immune complexes were washed 2 times each with a series of buffers consisting of the deoxycholate sonication buffer, high salt buffer, LiCl buffer, and TE buffer. Immune complexes were eluted from the beads with SDS buffer and subjected to RNase treatment and proteinase K treatment. Crosslinks were reversed by incubation overnight at 65°C, and ChIP DNA was purified by phenol-chloroform extraction and ethanol precipitation.


##### ChIP sequencing
  ChIP and input DNAs were prepared for amplification by converting overhangs into phosphorylated blunt ends and adding an adenine to the 3'-ends. Illumina genomic adapters were ligated, and the sample was size fractionated (200–250 bp) on a 2% agarose gel. After a final PCR amplification step (18 cycles), the resulting DNA libraries were quantified and sequenced on HiSeq 2000. Sequences (50 nucleotide reads, single end) were aligned to the human genome (hg19) using the BWA algorithm (Li and Durbin, 2009). Aligns were extended in silico at their 3'-ends to a length of 150 bp, which is the average genomic fragment length in the size-selected library and assigned to 32-nt bins along the genome. Peak locations were determined using the MACS algorithm (v1.4.2) with a cutoff of p = 10−7 (Zhang et al., 2008). Signal maps and peak locations were used as input data to active motif's proprietary analysis program, which creates excel tables containing detailed information on sample comparison, peak metrics, peak locations, and gene annotations.


##### Visualization of ChIP-seq signal
  Bigwig files were used as inputs to deepTools version 3.5.1 (Ramírez et al., 2014) for visualization. DeepTools plotHeatmap function was used to create visualizations of ChIP-seq signal fold enrichment within a −1.5 to +1.5 kb region around the bound and unbound DREs, as well as to generate average profiles for ChIP-seq enrichment in the same region.


##### DREs in open chromatin
  We obtained DNase-seq experiments for all relevant cell lines (MCF-7, T-47D, primary hepatocytes, HepG2, GM12878) from ENCODE: https://encodeproject.org/. We downloaded the broadPeak DNase-seq files for the hg19 genome assembly, and if there were multiple replicates, found the intersection of all replicates. Any DRE found under the peaks of DNase-seq intersection was considered to be in the open chromatin of the corresponding cell line and was used in the determination of bound and unbound DREs for the purposes of model training. DREs occurring in ENCODE blacklisted regions, namely the merged consensus blacklist (wgEncodeHg19ConsensusSignalArtifactRegions.bed.gz) and exclusion list regions (accession ENCFF001TDO) were ignored in downstream analyses. A list of all ENCODE accession numbers for DNase-seq broadPeak files is given in Supplementary Table 2.


##### AhR-bound and unbound DREs
  Firstly, we assembled a list of all DREs in the human genome by searching the hg19 human reference genome sequence for the occurrences of the core DRE sequence 5′-GCGTG-3′ on either strand of the DNA. Only DREs in open chromatin, that is, DREs overlapping the DNase-seq broadPeaks from an ENCODE experiment for a given cell line, were considered for training. Additionally, bound DREs in closed chromatin were also considered for testing purposes. Secondly, we obtained the AhR ChIP-seq bed and bigwig files either from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets or from ChIP-Atlas (Oki et al., 2018), where the original sequencing files have been processed uniformly following a standard processing pipeline. Originally, AhR ChIP-seq data were generated in the following independent experiments: (1) AhR and ARNT ChIP-seq of MCF-7 cells treated with 10 nM TCDD for 45 min (Lo and Matthews, 2012)—the binding data were obtained from GEO datasets—accession GSE41820, (2) AhR and AhRR ChIP-seq of MCF-7 cells treated with 10 nM TCDD for 45 min and 24 h (Yang et al., 2018)—the binding data were obtained from GEO datasets—accession GSE90550, (3) AhR ChIP-chip of T-47D cells treated with 1 µM 3-MC or 10 nM TCDD for 1 h (Ahmed et al., 2009; Pansoy et al., 2010)—the binding data were obtained from their respective publications and converted from hg18 to hg19 using the liftOver tool (Navarro Gonzalez et al., 2021), 4) AhR ChIP-seq of primary hepatocytes treated with 1 nM of TCDD for 24 h (this publication)—accessible through GEO datasets—accession GSE205502, (5) AhR (3xFLAG tagged AhR) ChIP-seq of untreated HepG2 cells from ENCODE—accession ENCSR412ZDC (Davis et al., 2018; Dunham et al., 2012), and (6) AhR ChIP-seq of GM17212 cells treated with 1 µM 3-MC for 24 h—accessible through GEO datasets—accession GSE116632; however, the binding data were obtained from ChIP-Atlas—accessions SRX4342282, SRX4342283, SRX4342285, and SRX4342286. Details of these experiments are summarized in Table 1 and all other data sources are summarized in Supplementary Table 2. Bound DREs for the purposes of model training were determined as DREs found in open chromatin and under AhR peaks where only 1 DRE was present under the AhR peak (referred to as singleton DREs). Unbound DREs are DREs in open chromatin found at least 500 bp away from the boundary of any AhR peak, as well as 100 bp away from any other DRE, in order to minimize confounding of DRE contribution to binding. All other DREs in open chromatin were considered ambiguous and were not used in model training.


##### Alternative AhR binding motifs
  We assembled a list of all nonconsensus XREs (NC-XREs) and AHRE-II binding motifs in the human genome by searching the hg19 human reference genome sequence for the occurrences of the (1) 5′-GGGAGGGA-3′ (NC-XRE x2), (2) 5′-GGGAGGGAGGGA-3′ (NC-XRE x3), and (3) 5′-CATG N6 C(A/T)TG-3′ (AHRE-II) on either strand of the DNA. We calculated the proportion of 0-DRE AhR peaks containing either one of the investigated alternative AhR binding motifs.


##### Promoters and enhancers
  We obtained all annotated transcription start sites from Ensembl 105 BioMart (human genes; GRCh38.p13) (Howe et al., 2021) and considered regions ±200 and ±1500 bp around the transcription start site as stringent and relaxed promoters, respectively. We obtained all computationally predicted enhancers from ChromHMM (Ernst and Kellis, 2012) for samples that had ChromHMM data available—HepG2 and GM12878 (ENCODE) and MCF-7 (GEO datasets—accession GSE57498). We did not predict enhancers in the primary hepatocytes due to the lack of available data needed to construct a ChromHMM model. Both weak and strong enhancers (ChromHMM states 4 through 7) were considered as valid enhancers.


##### Sequence and genomic signal features
  For each DRE in the human genome, we obtained the genomic sequence of 7 nucleotides 5′ upstream and 3′ downstream from the DRE (5′-GCGTG-3′). These nucleotides were 1-hot encoded and used as features in our machine learning models. In total, there were around 1.6 million DREs spread across the human genome. However, only a small fraction of them fulfilled the criteria for bound and unbound DREs used in training and testing. DNase-seq, as well as all available histone modification and TF ChIP-seq genomic signal (bigwig) files were downloaded for MCF-7, T-47D, primary hepatocytes, HepG2, GM12878 (as the closest match to GM17212 where AhR was ChIP-ed) from the ENCODE consortium. List of all used experiments and files is provided in Supplementary Table 2. For each bound and unbound DRE and each genomic signal (bigwig) file, we extracted the value of the genomic signal 740 bp up- and 740 bp down-stream from the DRE, for a total of 1485 bp of signal (DRE width is 5 bp). The extracted signal was split into 15 bins of equal, 99-bp size and the signal within each bin was averaged to produce 15 features corresponding to the particular DRE-genomic signal combination. During averaging, any areas of missing signal were replaced with zeros.


##### Model architecture and training
  For each cell line and all the bound and unbound DREs appearing in open chromatin of that particular cell line, we have created sequence, as well as genomic signal features for all available DNase-seq, histone modification, and TF ChIP-seq experiments. We have then performed hyperparameter tuning of an XGBoost model through a grid search of the hyperparameter space with the following values—max_depth = {3, 4, 5, 6, 7}, min_child_weight = {3, 4, 5, 6, 7}, subsample = {1.0, 0.9, 0.8, 0.7}, colsample_by_tree = {1.0, 0.9, 0.8, 0.7}, and eta = {0.05, 0.075, 0.1, 0.125, 0.15, 0.2, 0.3}. We reported the average performances over all 5 folds for the best performing models in terms of hyperparameter selection.


##### Model evaluation
  In addition to evaluating the models through 5-fold cross-validation, we also evaluated model performance on predicting the binding status of DREs that occurred under multi-DRE AhR peaks both in open and closed chromatin. For each such peak and each DRE under the peak, we used the AhR binding prediction model to make a prediction regarding whether the DRE is bound or not. If at least one DRE under the peak was predicted as bound, the peak was considered recovered, and the total fraction of recovered peaks was reported. Similarly, we evaluated the model performance on predicting the binding status of AhR peaks without DREs. Briefly, for each 0-DRE peak, we simulated 5 dummy DREs. These dummy DREs are not actually present in the genomic sequence and only represent the genomic location that was used as reference for the calculation of all nonsequence model input features. The center of the first dummy DRE is aligned to the center of the AhR peak and the other 4 dummy DREs are positioned −100, −50, +50, +100 bp relative to the center point of the first dummy DRE. A zero-DRE peak is considered partially recovered if at least 1 of the 5 dummy DREs is predicted as bound. The peak is considered centrally recovered if the central DRE is predicted as bound.


##### Model performance metrics
  To calculate the area under receiver operating characteristic (auROC) and area under precision recall (auPRC) curves, we used the output of the XGBoost algorithm in the form of probabilities of each particular observation (DRE) belonging to a particular output class (bound or unbound). By using different thresholds for these probabilities above which the model predicts a DRE as bound, we obtain the number of true and false positives for each threshold, as well as true and false negatives relative to the ground truth of DRE binding obtained from the corresponding AhR ChIP-seq experiment. Each threshold produces a point on the ROC and PRC curves; the area under the curve was calculated using a line interpolated through all the points.


##### Statistical analysis
  Statistical analysis was carried out in Python 3, using the scipy 1.8.0 package (Virtanen et al., 2020). ChIP-seq signals were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test and post hoc analysis performed with the Wilcoxon test for each pair. Results were considered significant if p value was <.01.


##### Machine learning model development code
  The code used to download, prepare, and preprocess the data, as well as to create, optimize and evaluate the machine learning models is available at https://github.com/BhattacharyaLab/AhR_binding_prediction.


## Results

### 

#### AhR binding profile is tissue specific
  To determine the role of the core 5′-GCGTG-3′ AhR binding motif (DRE) and the tissue-specific determinants of AhR binding, we compared AhR binding data across several publicly available human ChIP-seq and ChIP-chip experiments, and the primary human hepatocyte ChIP-seq experiment conducted for this study. Each experiment was performed with cells of a specific cell line (MCF-7, HepG2, GM17212, T-47D) or primary cell type (primary hepatocytes). The cells in these experiments were treated with either an AhR agonist (TCDD or 3-MC) or vehicle control—DMSO for a duration of 45 min, 1 h, or 24 h (for the full list of experiments, see Table 1).

  In developing our machine learning models, we used the data from 4 AhR ChIP-seq binding experiments, namely (1) MCF-7 cells treated with 10 nM TCDD for 24 h (referred to MCF-7 or MCF-7 24 h), (2) primary hepatocytes treated with 1 nM TCDD for 24 h (referred to as primary hepatocytes), (3) HepG2 cells without AhR agonist treatment (referred to as HepG2), and (4) GM17212 cells treated with 1 µM 3-MC for 24 h (referred to as GM17212). Some additional analyses were based on the remaining 3 AhR ChIP-seq and ChIP-chip binding experiments—(1) MCF-7 cells treated with 10 nM TCDD for 45 min (referred to as MCF-7 45 m), (2) T-47D cells treated with 10 nM TCDD for 1 h (referred to as T-47D TCDD), and (3) T-47D cells treated with 1 µM 3-MC for 1 h (referred to as T-47D 3-MC). AhR binding in T-47D cells was originally investigated using the ChIP-chip.

  We found close to 1.6 million DREs in the human reference genome (version hg19), and identified the intersection of these DREs with the genomic locations of AhR peaks, obtaining the number of DREs under each AhR peak. AhR peaks overlapped either none (0-DRE peaks), exactly 1 (singleton peaks), or more than 1 (multi-DRE peaks) DREs (Figure 1A). We calculated the percentage of 0-DRE, singleton, and multi-DRE AhR peaks within each AhR binding dataset listed in Table 1 (Figure 1B). All datasets have a similar percentage of singleton AhR peaks ranging between 22.3% and 33%. However, liver cells (HepG2 and primary hepatocytes) exhibited a larger proportion of multi-DRE peaks than other cell types (Figure 1B). Interestingly, HepG2 cells were not treated with an AhR agonist, yet the binding experiment resulted in 12 164 AhR peaks. These results suggest a level of basal induction of AhR in HepG2 and potentially other cells in culture conditions. Next, we investigated the distributions of singleton DRE locations relative to the midpoint of their corresponding AhR peak. If these DREs are functional in determining AhR binding, we would expect them to be enriched at 1 central or 2 centrally symmetrical points, depending on the physical position of the DNA binding domain within the AhR relative to the protein’s 3D spatial configuration. In all binding experiments, the majority of singleton DREs are situated near the midpoint of the corresponding AhR peak, that is, they are centrally enriched (Figure 1C andSupplementary Figure 1). For example, in MCF-7 cells, the DREs of about 50% and 80% of singleton peaks were found within 100 and 200 bp up-/down-stream from the midpoint of the peak, respectively (Figure 1C). However, in HepG2 cells, even though singleton DREs are centrally enriched, they are markedly less centrally enriched than singleton DREs in MCF-7, primary hepatocytes and GM17212 cells (Supplementary Figure 1).

  Cell-type specificity of binding for many TFs is influenced by chromatin accessibility—that is, TF binding peaks most often overlap with DNase hypersensitive sites (DHS) (Dunham et al., 2012). To examine the contribution of chromatin accessibility in determining tissue specificity of AhR binding, we examined DNase-seq broadPeak files from the ENCODE database most closely corresponding to the appropriate cell line or primary cell type used in the AhR binding experiment (Davis et al., 2018; Dunham et al., 2012). We determined that the majority of AhR peaks in all binding experiments are found in open chromatin (Supplementary Table 1). Due to the lack of DNase-seq or other chromatin accessibility experiments performed on cells treated with AhR agonists, all DNase-seq experiments used here represent the chromatin accessibility state prior to AhR activation (ie, nontreated state). Further analysis revealed that the proportions and exact identities of unbound and bound DREs found in open chromatin are tissue specific. For example, out of nearly 8000 DREs found in open chromatin in all 4 relevant binding experiments, about half are bound in at least 1 experiment, whereas only 14 DREs are bound in all 4 experiments (Figure 1D). On the other hand, about half of these ubiquitously accessible DREs are unbound in all 4 experiments (Figure 1E). Even when comparing 2 breast cancer cell lines, MCF-7, and T-47D, with similar treatment conditions (45 min of 10 nM TCDD and 1 h of 10 nM TCDD treatment, respectively), most accessible DREs were bound by AhR in only 1 of the 2 cell lines. However, upon closer inspection at least half of the peaks unique to T-47D cells might be subthreshold peaks in MCF-7 cells (Supplementary Figure 2). Nonetheless, these results suggest that a DRE that lies within open chromatin of 2 different tissues can be bound in 1 and unbound in the other, implying the existence of AhR binding determinants beyond the accessibility of chromatin.

  On the other hand, AhR peaks that appear in common in 2 or more binding experiments have a higher percentage of singleton and multi-DRE peaks than AhR peaks that are unique to a single binding experiment (Figure 1F). Thus, AhR peaks with DREs are more likely to appear in more than 1 tissue than peaks with 0-DREs. Additionally, quantification of AhR ChIP-seq signal within a binding experiment revealed significant differences between average signal strength of 0-DRE, singleton, and multi-DRE peaks. Namely, the more DREs an AhR peak had, the higher the average ChIP-seq signal was under the peak (Figure 1G and Supplementary Figure 3). Collectively, these results suggest that DREs are functional in determining AhR binding and that a DRE-centric approach to the investigation of tissue specificity of AhR binding could reveal determinants and potential mechanisms of AhR binding to DNA.

  The AhR has been shown to be capable of binding DNA motifs other than the DRE. The identity of the DNA sequence of 2 alternative AhR binding motifs has been described and investigated in some detail. These are the (1) NC-XRE (Huang and Elferink, 2012) and (2) AHRE-II (Sogawa et al., 2004). To investigate the potential usefulness of integrating AhR motifs other than the DRE into our machine learning models, we investigated the prevalence of NC-XRE and AHRE-II motifs under AhR peaks without DREs (0-DRE AhR peaks). The identity of the NC-XRE sequence has not been fully characterized. However, it is believed that the 5′-GGGA-3′ tetrameric repeat is the identifying characteristic of NC-XREs (Huang and Elferink, 2012). We investigated the prevalence of double—NC-XRE (x2) and triple tetrameric repeats—NC-XRE (3x) under 0-DRE AhR peaks. On the other hand, the sequence of the AHRE-II binding motif has been identified as 5′-CATG N6 C(A/T)TG-3′ (Sogawa et al., 2004). We observed that an average of only about 5% of 0-DRE AhR peaks possess a NC-XRE motif and only about 3% of 0-DRE AhR peaks possess the AHRE-II motif across different cell lines/types (Supplementary Figure 4). Consequently, these motifs could not be used as the basis of additional machine learning models due to the low number of AhR peaks containing these alternative AhR binding. Therefore, only DREs were considered in subsequent analyses.


#### Machine learning models accurately predict AhR binding
  To better understand the tissue-specific determinants of AhR binding beyond the core DRE motif and chromatin accessibility, we developed a series of interpretable machine learning classifier models focused on the prediction of binary binding status (bound or unbound) of DREs in open chromatin. To this end, we developed models with different combinations of input features for each individual binding experiment. We trained the models on DREs occurring under singleton, that is, 1-DRE, peaks only. These DREs represent about one-third of all AhR peaks, across all binding experiments. Multi-DRE peaks were not used for training because it is impossible to determine which specific DRE among the cluster of DREs was responsible for AhR binding. However, we used the multi-DRE peaks for model validation. All our machine learning models were based on the gradient boosted tree algorithm of the XGBoost family of algorithms (Elith et al., 2008). These algorithms also provide metrics of feature importance, that is, the contribution of individual input features that contribute to the model performance (Gregorutti et al., 2017).

  Our models use local chromatin features as inputs and are trained and validated to predict the binding status of singleton bound and isolated unbound DREs (see Materials and Methods), limited to DREs found in open chromatin of a particular cell line or cell type. Validation is performed using a 5-fold cross-validation procedure (see Materials and Methods for details) (Figure 2A). The local chromatin input features that were used include: (1) the DNA sequence immediately flanking the DRE. We included the flanking sequence of up to 7 nucleotides directly up- and down-stream from the DRE—previously proposed to be involved in AhR binding (Sun et al., 2004). These sequences were 1-hot encoded prior to being used as model inputs; (2) binned mean values of bigWig signals of the cell line or type most closely corresponding to the one used in the AhR binding experiment. For example, for primary hepatocytes we used bigWig signals from experiments done in hepatocytes originated from H9 human embryonic stem cells, and for GM17212, we used bigWig signals from experiments done in GM12878. We used the bigWig files corresponding to (i) DNase-seq (as representative of chromatin accessibility), (ii) histone modification, and (iii) TF ChIP-seq signals from ENCODE—see Materials and Methods for details (Davis et al., 2018; Dunham et al., 2012). For each bigWig signal and each DRE, we created 15 bins of width 99 bp. Each bin was assigned a value representing the average bigWig signal across the width of that bin. The midpoint of the central bin was positioned at the middle nucleotide of the 5-bp DRE; (3) indicator variables of whether the DRE is found in a strict (±200 bp away from a transcription start site) or loose (±1500 bp away from the transcription start site) definition of a promoter.

  To produce the best performing models, we conducted an extensive hyperparameter search for each binding experiment and input feature set (see Materials and Methods) and chose the model that produced the highest performance. In all instances, unless otherwise stated, model performance was reported as the auROC and auPRC curves, averaged over 5 folds using the 5-fold cross-validation procedure. For class imbalanced datasets such as the ones used here, where unbound DREs far outnumber the bound (Supplementary Table 1), the auPRC is considered a more appropriate metric of model performance. Therefore, the model producing the highest auPRC was selected as the best performing model. However, the area under the ROC curve (auROC) was still a useful metric to distinguish between poorly and well performing models when comparing between binding experiments (see Model Performance Metrics).

  To investigate model performance as a function of the input feature set, we developed and validated models with feature sets consisting of the following features: (1) DNase-seq only, (2) flanking sequence only, (3) flanking sequence and DNase-seq, (4) flanking sequence, DNase-seq, and histone modifications, and (5) flanking sequence, DNase-seq, histone modifications, and TF binding (referred to as the full model). For most cell types, the performance of each successive model improved, with the exception of the primary hepatocyte and HepG2 models where the performance of the sequence-only model was low overall and also lower than the performance of the DNase-seq model. However, in both binding experiments, the performance of the sequence and DNase-seq model was higher than the DNase-seq only model. These results suggest that even though the flanking sequence by itself provides little useful information regarding AhR binding in these binding experiments, when combined with detailed chromatin accessibility information, the flanking sequence does provide additional useful information (Figure 2B and Supplementary Figure 5). Additionally, most models exhibited overall high performance, except the primary hepatocyte models (Supplementary Figure 5).

  To understand how specific chromatin context features contribute to the full model performance, we trained the full model on all available training data with the model hyperparameters previously optimized to produce the best performance for the binding experiment of interest. Subsequently, after model training, we used the information gain metric generated by XGBoost to determine, for each binding experiment, the total feature importance of nonsequence (ie, TF binding and epigenomic) features (Figure 2C), relative feature importance of sequence features per flanking sequence nucleotide position (Figure 2D), and relative importance of individual bins of nonsequence features (Figure 2E). Further examination of nonsequence feature importance scores revealed that the specific models are predominantly learning and making AhR-DRE binding predictions by relying on different sets of features across different binding experiments (Figure 2C). For instance, within each binding experiment, we observed 3–6 bigWig signals with feature importance 2–5 times higher than that of any other signal. These were (1) in MCF-7 cells—H3K4me1, H3K4me3, GATA3, CTCF, ZNF217, and FOXA1; (2) in primary hepatocytes—DNase-seq, SP1, H3K27ac, and RXRA; (3) in HepG2 cells—MXI1, MAX, NR2F2, ZEB1; and (4) in GM17212 cells—SP1, POLR2A, TAF1, H3K27ac, and CREB1. Admittedly, certain features were ranked with high importance across binding experiments, such as the binding of CTCF, Rad21, SP1, FOXA1, MAX and MAX-related factors MAZ, and MXI1; as well as histone modification H3K27ac. However, we determined that the relative level of importance of these features varied widely across different binding experiments—for example, CTCF ranked fifth in MCF-7 cells and 20th in primary hepatocytes (Figure 2C). Additionally, the distribution of relative importance scores across the bins were markedly different—for example, the central bins of H3K27ac in GM17212 cells exhibited the highest importance for this feature, whereas in MCF-7 cells, the central bins were not used by the model and were, consequently, not assigned an importance score (Figure 2E). To verify the reproducibility of the features with highest importance in Figure 2C, we generated lists of the most important features for each of the 5 folds in the 5-fold cross validation. For each feature, we reported the average feature and the variability of its rank across folds. The highest-ranking features had low variability, and feature rank variability increased with decreasing feature importance (Supplementary Figure 6).

  To investigate the contribution of DNA sequence immediately flanking the DRE, we examined the importance scores of nucleotides flanking the DRE produced by (1) flanking sequence-only models, and (2) full models (inclusive of flanking sequence, DNase-seq, histone modification, and TF features). We determined that the importance scores produced by the sequence-only models were highly variable between binding experiments. On the other hand, the importance scores of nucleotides flanking the DRE produced by the full models revealed similar profiles of nucleotide importance across binding experiments. Mainly, the thymine residue at the flanking position directly 5′ of the DRE (labelled as the −1 position) exhibited the highest feature importance out of all nucleotides that could appear at that position in 3 out of 4 examined binding experiments (Figure 2D). Additionally, for 2 out of 4 binding experiments, the thymine at position −2 and cytosine and guanine at position +1 also exhibited high feature importance (Figure 2D).

  To further examine the relation of individual TF binding with AhR binding within the same experiment, we developed models using only binding of a single TF as input. Upon sorting these models by performance, we determined that the relative ordering of TFs (Figure 2F) was decidedly different when compared with the feature importance ordering of the full models (Figure 2C). Notably, for MCF-7 cells (Figure 2F), EP300 was the factor resulting in the second-best performing model, whereas in the corresponding full model, EP300 did not appear among the top 20 features with highest importance (Figure 2C). On the other hand, GATA3 was the most predictive factor both in the full model and individually (Figures 2C and 2F). These results point to a high likelihood of redundancy between binding of different TFs.

  In summary, our DRE occupancy models generalized well within the binding experiment they were trained on when evaluated on a subset of bound singleton DREs and unbound isolated DREs left out from the training dataset (ie, a single fold in a 5-fold cross-validation). Additionally, these models revealed highly variable chromatin context specificity between different binding experiments, except the DNA sequence immediately flanking the DRE, where similarities between binding experiments were observed, potentially pointing to a common flanking sequence grammar.


#### Singleton peak-trained models predict multi-DRE and 0-DRE AhR peak binding within the same tissue
  To assess whether the high performance of our full and sequence-only models could be a result of chance due to overtraining, we performed feature selection on each of these models and compared the performance of the reduced models to the original full models. Because we use thousands of features in our full models (exact number of features depends on the cell line or cell type investigated), we created models with a subset of top N features with highest importance scores in the full model (where N = 10, 25, 50, 75, 100, 200, 300). In the MCF-7, the performance plateaued already at only 100 top features. The performance of the model using only the top 50 features, although slightly lower on average, was not significantly different than the performance of the full model (Figure 3A). To investigate the influence of the number of included flanking nucleotides on model performance, we relied on a previous study that indicated, based on computational analysis of 13 experimentally verified DREs, that up to 7 up- and down-stream DRE-flanking nucleotides influenced AhR binding (Sun et al., 2004). However, when creating a series of models with each consecutive model including an increasing number of flanking nucleotides, the sequence-only model performance plateaued at 4 flanking nucleotides (Figure 3B). We therefore used only 4 flanking nucleotides up-/down-stream of the DRE in all our models. Additionally, because the DRE is not a palindromic binding motif and the orientation of the DRE on the genome might direct the orientation of AhR binding and its interactions with other TFs, we investigated whether correcting the orientation of local chromatin context features influences model performance. For DREs found on the forward strand, all features were left as-is, and for DREs found on the reverse strand, the bins of all features were flipped around the central bin (the bin containing the DRE) to match the DRE orientation. We found no difference between our original and strand-corrected models (Figure 3C).

  To further examine how well the models generalize outside of their training data, we evaluated the full models for their ability to predict multi-DRE peaks. We examined all multi-DRE peaks, regardless of how close the DREs were to each other. For each multi-DRE peak, we predicted the bound/unbound status of every single DRE under that peak. If at least 1 DRE was predicted as bound, the entire peak was considered as generally recovered (blue bars in Figs. 3D–F). If the DRE closest to the midpoint of the peak was predicted as bound, the entire peak was considered as centrally recovered (orange bars in Figs. 3D–F). Recovery of multi-DRE peaks in open chromatin resulted in true positive rates between 80% and 100% (Figure 3D). Peaks containing more than 4 DREs were poorly recovered (true positive rate of about 40%) by the MCF-7 model only, suggesting a different mechanism underlying binding in areas of high DRE density in MCF-7 cells, possibly through cooperative binding (Stefan et al., 2013). In the hg19 human reference genome approximately 1% of all DREs occur in one of these high DRE density areas, defined as 5 or more DREs within a 500-bp region (Supplementary Figure 7). Similarly, multi-DRE peaks in closed chromatin are recovered at a much lower and variable rate of 25%–60% (Figure 3E). This result is not surprising, considering that our models are trained on DREs occurring in open chromatin. However, we found no correlation between the normalized binding strength of DNase-seq signal and DRE binding status prediction probabilities (Supplementary Figure 8), suggesting that the poor performance of our models when predicting the binding status of bound DREs in closed chromatin was not due to lower DNase-seq signal alone.

  Additionally, we evaluated the false positive rates of our models when predicting the binding status of (1) multi-DRE DNase-seq peaks, (2) 1+-DRE peaks of the best performing TF in open chromatin, and (3) 1+-DRE peaks of the best performing TF in closed chromatin (Supplementary Figs. 9A–C). “Best performing TF” refers to the TF that was ranked the highest in importance in Figure 2C, namely GATA3, MXI1, SP1 for MCF-7, HepG2, and GM17212 cells, respectively. The false positive rates generally do not exceed 20% and were the lowest for multi-DRE DNase-seq peaks.

  As expected, the central recovery rate in Figures 3D–F was slightly lower or equal to the general recovery rate. Furthermore, we did not observe a drastic drop in performance of central recovery with increasing number of DREs, contrary to the expectation that the more DREs a peak contains, the higher the likelihood of that peak being recovered by pure chance due to the multiple testing problem. These results suggest that our models are generalizing well, and that multiple testing is not a significant problem.

  Similarly, we evaluated the performance of models trained on all but the DRE flanking sequence features in predicting the binding status of 0-DRE peaks, that is, AhR peaks with no DREs. All nonsequence input features were calculated with the DRE genomic location as a reference. To create input features for model evaluation with these 0-DRE peaks, we had to select positions within a peak to center our input features. Guided by our results in Figure 1C and Supplementary Figure 1, we reasoned that because most singleton DREs occur within ±100 bp from the midpoint of AhR peaks, it would be sensible to test our models at several points within that range, with the most sensible position being the midpoint of the peak, because the majority of singleton DREs occur at this position. We also decided to use 4 other positions relative to the midpoint of the peak to evaluate how including these additional evaluation points would affect model performance. We refer to all these positions as dummy DREs. Unlike actual DREs, these dummy DREs are not present in the genomic sequence and only represent the genomic location to be used as reference for the calculation of input features. The center of the first dummy DRE was aligned to the midpoint of the AhR peak and the other 4 dummy DREs were positioned at −100, −50, +50, and +100 bp relative to the midpoint of the peak. After setting up the dummy DREs, we applied the same procedure as described for predicting multi-DRE peaks. Namely, a 0-DRE peak was considered generally recovered if at least 1 of the 5 dummy DREs was predicted as bound. Additionally, the peak was considered centrally recovered if the central dummy DRE was predicted as bound. Overall, the central recovery rate of 0-DRE AhR peaks in open chromatin ranged from 63.1% to 91% for GM17212 and MCF-7 cells, respectively. Central recovery rate was 3%–11% lower than general recovery rate (Figure 3F). On the other hand, the central recovery rate of 0-DRE AhR peaks in closed chromatin was considerably lower—between 11.7% and 41.8% for GM17212 and MCF-7, respectively (Supplementary Figs. 10A and 11B). Additionally, we evaluated the false positive rates of our models when predicting the binding status of (1) 0-DRE DNase-seq peaks, (2) 0-DRE peaks of best performing TF in open chromatin, and (3) 0-DRE peaks of best performing TF in closed chromatin (Supplementary Figs. 10B–D and 11C–E). Central false positive rate for 0-DRE DNase-seq was lower than 1.3% (Supplementary Figs. 10B and 11C). However, central false positive rate for 0-DRE best performing TF peaks in open chromatin ranged from 8.6% to 45% for HepG2 and MCF-7 cells, respectively (Supplementary Figs. 10C and 11D).


#### Cross-tissue models provide insights into tissue specificity of AhR binding
  We developed additional models to investigate whether our full models for multiple binding experiments would still learn from different features even in scenarios of deliberately constrained input or output data. Specifically, we examined whether full feature models learn from different features simply because different features were available for different cell lines or cell types. To make a fair comparison between models predicting AhR binding in different cell types, we created models with input features limited to DNase-seq, and only those TF and histone modification features for which ChIP-seq experiments were available in all cell lines or types. We still found a highly variable set of the most important features determining AhR binding within different cell lines or cell types (Figure 4A). To further investigate whether there are enhancer-specific binding rules and whether these rules might be similar between cell lines or cell types, we created full models predicting the occupancy of singleton bound and isolated unbound DREs found only in enhancers. Once again, we found highly variable sets of the most important features for each cell line/type. However, feature importance for the EP300 transcriptional coactivator increased for all cell lines/types (Supplementary Figure 12 and Figure 2C). Enhancers were determined using ChromHMM and both weak and strong enhancers were considered equally in the construction of the models.

  SP1 and MAX TF binding had the highest feature importance in the full feature models for GM17212 and HepG2 cells, respectively. However, in MCF-7 cells, neither of these factors appeared within the top 20 factors with highest feature importance (Figure 2C). To evaluate whether the feature importance of these factors within MCF-7 cells was low due to potential redundancy with other factors, we examined the discriminative power of a single feature derived from these factors. Namely, for both SP1 and MAX, we calculated the maximal normalized signal in a 100-bp region surrounding the singleton bound DRE and isolated unbound DREs. We then evaluated the optimal threshold that produced the highest F1 score for each of these features. Both SP1 and MAX produced much lower optimal F1 scores in MCF-7 cells when compared with HepG2 and GM17212 cells—7% versus 35% and 60%, respectively (Figure 4B).

  These results suggest that SP1 and MAX are not at all predictive of AhR binding in the MCF-7 cells, whereas they were the factors most predictive of AhR binding in GM17212 and HepG2 cells. Similarly, GATA3 was the single factor most predictive of AhR binding in MCF-7 cells. However, GATA3 is either very lowly expressed or not expressed at all in other cell types, making it hard to investigate whether the AhR binding dependence on GATA3 in MCF-7 cells was specific to that cell line. To further understand why including certain TF information as model inputs results in high performing models in 1 tissue but not in another, we focused on the case of the TF MAX. We observed that MAX binding also appeared qualitatively different between bound and unbound DREs when comparing MCF-7 and HepG2 cells (Figure 4C). Together, these results could explain the increased utility of MAX in the HepG2 and GM17212 models.

  To investigate whether models specific to 1 cell line/type can predict AhR binding in others, we evaluated the cross-cell performance of models with similar treatments or within similar cell lines. The sequence-only model trained on MCF-7 cells and evaluated on primary hepatocytes did not perform any better than random. Conversely, the sequence-only model trained on primary hepatocytes performed worse when evaluated within primary hepatocytes than when evaluated cross-tissue in MCF-7 cells (Figure 4D). Even though the cells in these 2 binding experiments were both treated with TCDD for 24 h, the concentration of TCDD was different: 10 nM and 1 nM in MCF-7 cells and primary hepatocytes, respectively. These results indicate that cells with a smaller number of bound and unbound DREs in open chromatin (such as MCF-7) might result in somewhat overfitted sequence models, especially when a large number of flanking nucleotides is used in the model. On the other hand, (1) sequence-only, (2) GATA3-only, and (3) sequence and GATA3 models trained on the MCF-7 cells exhibit high performance within tissue, with model performance increasing with each successive model (Supplementary Figure 13). However, when we evaluated these models on all bound DREs within T-47D cells treated with either 3-MC or TCDD for 1 h (after excluding any DREs that were bound in both MCF-7 and T-47D cells, to avoid using training data in the testing phase), it was the sequence-only model that produced the highest accuracy of 90.67%. Performance of the sequence-only model trained on MCF-7 cells evaluated separately in T-47D cells for 3 groups of AhR peaks appearing in (1) TCDD treatment only, (2) 3-MC treatment only, and (3) both TCDD and 3-MC treatment, yielded accuracies of 90.48%, 89.47%, and 92.86%, respectively, showing that the sequence-only model was unable to distinguish between AhR binding resulting from activation by different AhR ligands (Figure 4E). Also, AhR peaks in T-47D cells common to both ligands were slightly more predictable by the MCF-7 sequence-only model. These results collectively suggest that cross tissue model predictions might be more accurate for binding experiments with similar cell lines/types (like MCF-7 and T-47D, both of which are breast carcinoma cell lines) and treatments, as opposed to binding experiments with dissimilar cell types (like MCF-7 and primary hepatocytes) but similar treatments.


#### AhR binding models reveal positive and negative regulators of AhR binding
  To examine the decisions, the AhR binding models make when predicting the binding status of each DRE, we used ELI5—https://eli5.readthedocs.io/, an algorithm that summarizes the decision-making process underlying individual model predictions. For each prediction, ELI5 assigns a numerical weight to each feature the model used to make that prediction. These weights represent a measure of how much a particular feature contributed to the final prediction across all decision trees used by the XGBoost model. The higher the assigned weight the more the feature contributed to the model prediction. An example of the top 10 positively and negatively weighted features used in a single DRE binding status prediction in the HepG2 model is shown in Figure 5A. In this example, the high average bigWig signal value within bin 0 of MAX binding is assigned the highest weight and contributes most to the model predicting the corresponding DRE as bound.

  First, we analyzed the weights assigned to features that are the individual bins of bigWig signals originating from DNase-seq, histone modification, or TF ChIP-seq experiments. When examining the weights assigned to a single feature, for example bin 0 of GATA3 binding, across all correctly classified bound DREs (true positives), we observed model features whose weights increase with increasing feature values (positive regulators, Figure 5B), as well as features whose weights decrease with increasing feature values (negative regulators, Figure 5C). To examine whether the direction of regulation (positive or negative) of individual features is preserved between models, we investigated features in common to each combination of 2 models. In total, we found 102, 46, and 59 features used in common by (1) MCF-7 and HepG2, (2) MCF-7 and GM17212, and (3) HepG2 and GM17212 models, respectively. For each model combination, approximately 41%–62% of features were positive regulators in 1 model and negative in another (Figure 5D). Additionally, out of 15 features used by all 3 models, only 3 features have the same direction of regulation in all 3 models—bin 1 of MAZ, bin 1 of MAX, bin 3 of H3K27ac. All 3 of these features are positive regulators in all 3 models (Supplementary Figure 14A). Even for a single TF, such as CTCF in HepG2 cells, some bins can be positive regulators, whereas others can be negative regulators or not be used by the model at all (Figure 5E). These results indicate that even though tissue-specific models primarily learn from entirely different features, a small subset of those features shows similar patterns across tissues.

  Our models also provide weights for the DRE-flanking DNA sequence features. These features are binary and indicate whether a certain nucleotide is found at a certain position relative to the DRE, for example, whether the nucleotide at position −1 is a thymine or not. These flanking sequence features can also be seen as positive or negative regulators, as the model produces either positive or negative weights when a nucleotide is present. The classical representation of sequence features across many binding sites is the sequence logo (Schneider and Stephens, 1990). These representations indicate how informative the presence of a certain nucleotide at a particular position is in determining whether the sequence represents a putative binding site. The logo generated by our models is a combination of 2 motifs—one that describes bound DREs, and another that describes the unbound DREs (the upper and lower motifs in Figure 5F, respectively). Additionally, when comparing the direction of regulation for DNA flanking sequence features between MCF-7 and primary hepatocytes, all 3 nucleotide features (position −2 is thymine, −1 is thymine, and 1 is guanine) that are used by both models have the same direction of regulation (Supplementary Figure 14B).


## Discussion
  Traditionally, ligand-activated AhR has been assumed to bind to the 5′-GCGTG-3′ DNA sequence, referred to as the DRE. However, in vivo studies have shown that DREs are not necessary for AhR binding (Lo and Matthews, 2012; Yang et al., 2018). Our results show that (1) the number of DREs under AhR peaks relative to peak widths is higher than expected by chance (Figure 1B). Secondly, DREs occurring under singleton AhR peaks are centrally enriched relative to the midpoint of their respective peaks (Figure 1C), and AhR peaks with a higher number of DREs under the peak have a statistically higher average bigWig signal than AhR peaks with a lower number of DREs (Figure 1G). These results suggest that DREs, although not necessary, are functional in determining the strength of AhR binding.

  Interestingly, our singleton DRE-centered models of AhR binding were able to predict the binding of 0-DRE AhR peaks with high accuracy. One possible explanation is that there is a high level of indirect binding of AhR to DNA within singleton and 0-DRE AhR peaks, possibly due to tethering to other TFs that are directly bound to DNA (Lonard and O’Malley, 2006). The existence of a substantial number of 0-DRE AhR peaks suggests that even some DREs occurring under AhR peaks might not actually be bound by AhR at all, but instead might be occurring by chance. We refer to these DREs as “not directly bound DREs.” If the number of not directly bound DREs used in the model training phase were high enough to capture sufficient information about tethering complexes or chromatin context surrounding such complexes, then the model would be learning how to identify indirect AhR binding. The model could, therefore, predict the binding of 0-DRE peaks, which are likely a result of indirect AhR binding, with high accuracy.

  Another likely explanation is that a portion of bound AhR might be part of chromatin loops, with AhR being directly bound to 1 or more DREs, in at least 1 loop anchor, and the imprint of AhR binding appearing in other anchors due to their proximity. Our results show that EP300 is one of the factors most predictive of AhR binding in enhancers (Supplementary Figure 12). EP300 has been implicated in the formation of chromatin loops, specifically as a marker of preestablished enhancer anchors that appear in enhancer-promoter loops formed after glucocorticoid receptor (GR) activation (McDowell et al., 2018). Additionally, EP300, H3K4me1, and H3K27ac jointly mark active enhancers (Creyghton et al., 2010). In this scenario, the directly bound AhR molecule could leave an impression of a 0-DRE AhR peak in another anchor of the same loop, due to their physical proximity (Liang et al., 2014). Any TF or chromatin complexes established in the formation of these loops would then be associated with both singleton and 0-DRE peaks. In that case, our models would be learning how to identify direct AhR binding.

  Most likely, AhR binding peaks result from a balance of both direct and indirect binding. When this balance is heavily tipped toward indirect binding, machine learning models based on bound and unbound DREs might not be the best approach to study the binding of AhR. This could be the case for the HepG2 AhR ChIP-seq experiment where singleton DREs are not as centrally enriched as they are in other binding experiments. Additionally, our models indicate that 0-DRE peaks highly resemble DRE-containing peaks. Therefore, 0-DRE peaks might not necessarily be functionally distinct from DRE-containing peaks. We propose that factors and chromatin context determinants interacting with AhR are likely very similar, regardless of whether AhR is bound to DRE or not. Therefore, the functional consequences might also be very similar. Consequently, this implies that the presence or absence of DREs at gene promoters is not sufficient to claim potential gene regulation by the activated AhR.

  Several different methods and models have recently been developed to address the problem of cross-tissue prediction of TF-DNA binding. For instance, in 2017 the DREAM-ENCODE “in vivo TF binding site prediction challenge” was conceived to specifically tackle this problem and produced 2 top-performing methods (Keilwagen et al., 2019; Li et al., 2019). Depending on the TF under investigation these methods have had varying degrees of success. Building on the success of these models, we focused on model interpretation to derive potential mechanistic insights. Interestingly, we found that the chromatin context determinants differ vastly between tissues (Figs. 2C and 2E and 5D), with 1 or 2 distinct TFs dominating as the most important contributors to model performance in each model. We propose that these factors either: (1) play a significant role in determining cell identity, or (2) are a common AhR cofactor in that specific tissue. As an example, GATA3 is mutated in MCF-7 cells, but not in T-47D cells. The MCF-7 mutation of GATA3 is heterozygous, and results in a copy of the GATA3 protein that is more stable and resistant to turnover, and binds to DNA more strongly than its wildtype counterpart (Adomas et al., 2014; Takaku et al., 2020). We propose that it is precisely this increase in binding activity of GATA3 in MCF-7 cells that makes GATA3 the most predictive factor of AhR binding in those cells. On the other hand, AhR peaks in T-47D cells are still correlated with GATA3 binding but to a lesser extent. These results indicate that wildtype GATA3 might still play a role in determining AhR binding. Additionally, synergistic effects of GATA3 and AhR binding on the expression of the GPR15 gene have been reported in human CD4+ T cells (Swaminathan et al., 2021), suggesting that AhR-GATA3 interactions might not be confined to breast cancer cells.

  In contrast to pioneering factors like GATA3, certain TFs known to be functionally associated with AhR have not been ranked highly by our models. For example, ARNT is the prototypical dimerization partner of AhR. However, our HepG2 and GM17212 models rank ARNT features very lowly (Figure 2C). Admittedly, for the GM17212 model, the ARNT binding experiment comes from a related but different cell line compared with the AhR experiment (GM12878). Both are lymphoblastoid cell lines but have been generated from different donors. Additionally, the ARNT binding experiment in GM12878 cells has no AhR ligand treatment, whereas the AhR binding experiment is based on treatment with 3-MC. Collectively, these factors might contribute to the observed discrepancy between AhR and ARNT binding signals. On the other hand, HepG2 cells were not treated with an AhR ligand. However, a photoproduct of tryptophan which is present in cell culture media has been shown to be an AhR agonist and could explain the basal AhR activity in HepG2 cells (Öberg et al., 2005). In this case, the ARNT experiment would have received the same treatment as the AhR experiment. However, ARNT features were not used by the HepG2 model at all. We suspect that the tryptophan derivative-induced activation of AhR is similar to endogenous activation of AhR and does not result in prominent levels of AhR-ARNT dimerization.

  Both AhR and ARNT have biological activities distinct from those associated with gene expression induced by the AHR-ARNT heterodimer responding to exogenous ligands. Some of these activities are likely mediated through AhR multimerization with its other known partners, such as KLF6, RELA, or RB1. These dimers or multimers seem to bind to DNA motifs distinct from DREs (Wilson et al., 2013). Surprisingly, even though KLF6 and RELA binding were used as features in the HepG2 model, they did not rank highly in feature importance. This result might be due to the fact that we had used a DRE-centered approach and AhR-KLF6 dimers do not appear to directly bind DREs, binding to nonconsesus XREs (NC-XRE) motifs instead (Huang and Elferink, 2012). Our results indicate a higher prevalence of NC-XREs under AhR peaks in HepG2 cells, compared with other cell lines (Supplementary Figure 4). However, the prevalence of NC-XRE motifs under AhR peaks is still relatively low and likely cannot explain all of AhR binding in HepG2 cells. Still, this could make our selection of bound and unbound DREs in open chromatin of HepG2 cells noninformative for the machine learning models.

  Additionally, we demonstrated that features based on the binding signal strength of TFs are a much stronger predictor of AhR binding than features based on the motifs or peaks of TFs (Supplementary Figure 15), suggesting that the intensity of cofactor binding is much more important than simply knowing if a cofactor was bound or not. It has been suggested that the DNA sequence immediately flanking the DRE forms an extended AhR binding motif and thus influences its binding (Sun et al., 2004). Even though our models do suggest the existence of a common flanking sequence grammar across tissues, flanking sequence features have higher feature weights in MCF-7 cells than in any other tissue. Therefore, it appears that certain AhR binding sites rely more on the DRE flanking sequence than others. Additionally, the number of these flanking sequence-dependent sites varies across tissues.

  To minimize the bias in our models potentially introduced by differences in AhR agonist treatment between different AhR ChIP-seq experiments, we have made efforts to analyze the binding of AhR across tissues only for cell lines/types with similar AhR agonist treatment. However, it has to be noted that some bias in the models might still exist due to slight variations in the treatment, differences in the sex, and the use of data from cancerous cell lines. Additionally, even though the cell types used here represent the predominant cell types of their respective tissues, that is, epithelial cells for breast tissue and hepatocytes for liver tissue, they do not necessarily capture the complexities of AhR-DNA binding dynamics across all cell types, especially any immune cells present in the tissue.

  In summary, we have developed highly accurate and robust predictive models of within-tissue AhR binding for several distinct cell lines and 1 primary cell type. Our models dissected the tissue specificity of AhR binding and strongly suggested that tissue-specific AhR binding is driven by a complex interplay of tissue-agnostic DNA sequence immediately flanking the DRE, and a highly tissue-specific local chromatin context. Particularly, the flanking sequence of the DRE can explain a portion of AhR binding in a tissue-agnostic way, however, the degree of AhR binding attributable to the flanking sequence varies from tissue to tissue.
