# Role of Dynamics and Mutations in Interactions of a Zinc Finger Antiviral Protein with CG-rich Viral RNA


## Abstract
  Zinc finger antiviral protein (ZAP) is a host antiviral factor that selectively inhibits the replication of a variety of viruses. ZAP recognizes the CG-enriched RNA sequences and activates the viral RNA degradation machinery. In this work, we investigated the dynamics of a ZAP/RNA complex and computed the energetics of mutations in ZAP that affect its binding to the viral RNA. The crystal structure of a mouse-ZAP/RNA complex showed that RNA interacts with the zinc finger 2 (ZF2) and ZF3 domains. However, we found that due to the dynamic behavior of the single stranded RNA, the terminal nucleotides C1 and G2 of RNA change their positions from the ZF3 to ZF1 domain. Moreover, the electrostatic interactions between the zinc ions and the viral RNA provide further stability to the ZAP/RNA complex. We also provide structural and thermodynamic evidence for seven residue pairs (C1-Arg74, C1-Arg179, G2-Arg74, U3-Lys76, C4-Lys76, G5-Arg95, and U6-Glu204) that show favorable ZAP/RNA interactions although these interactions were not observed in the ZAP/RNA crystal structure. Consistent with the observations from the mouse-ZAP/RNA crystal structure, we found that four residue pairs (C4-Lys89, C4-Leu90, C4-Tyr108, and G5-Lys107) maintained stable interactions in MD simulations. Based on experimental mutagenesis studies and our residue-level interaction analysis, we chose seven residues (Arg74, Lys76, Lys89, Arg95, Lys107, Tyr108, and Arg179) for individual alanine mutations. In addition, we studied mutations in those residues that are only observed in the crystal structures as interacting with RNA (Tyr98, Glu148, and Arg170). Out of these 10 mutations, we found that the Ala mutation in each of the five residues Arg74, Lys76, Lys89, Lys107, and Glu148 significantly reduced the binding affinity of ZAP to RNA.


## Introduction
  Antiviral restriction factors are cellular proteins that limit viral replication by contributing to the fundamental immune response underlying the severity of viral infections.1 The Zinc finger antiviral protein (ZAP) is an antiviral restriction protein, which is present in the cytoplasm and specifically targets the replication of retroviruses (e.g., HIV-1),2 filoviruses (e.g., Ebola virus),3 and a wide array of other viruses (e.g., alphaviruses,4,5 hepatitis B virus,6 and influenza A virus).7 ZAP was first identified as restricting the murine leukemia virus from replicating in rat cells.8 ZAP directly binds to specific viral mRNA sequences and recruits RNA processing molecular machinery to degrade target RNAs.2 Moreover, ZAP also recruits the decapping complex via its cofactor RNA helicase P72 to degrade the viral mRNA.9,10 Importantly, ZAP is also known to selectively bind RNA sequences rich in CG-dinucleotides.11–13

  At a structural level, ZAP consists of four tandem Cys-Cys-Cys-His (CCCH)-type zinc finger (ZF) motifs in the N-terminal domain (labeled ZF1, ZF2, ZF3, and ZF4 in Fig. 1A).13 Moreover, the N-terminal domain is the primary functional domain for binding target RNAs and recruiting the RNA-degrading cellular machinery. The “tractor-like” structure (Fig. 1B) of the first 227 residues in the N-terminal domain is composed of seven α-helices (α1-α7), three β-strands (β1-β3), two short 310-helices (η1-η2), and several loops. The structure is further classified into three layers. The first 72 residues form the ‘cockpit’ layer, which exhibits an αβ framework along with a twisted plane consisting of three short antiparallel β-strands, that are surrounded by three α-helices on top. The bottom layer is made up of four ‘wheels’ (Fig. 1B), where each ‘wheel’ represents a ZF motif, situated at each of the four corners of a twisted rectangle. One zinc ion coordinates with one histidine (labeled as H; Fig. 1B) and three cysteine residues (labeled as C; Fig. 1B), and is located at the center of each zinc finger domain (Fig. 1C). The overall folds of the ZF domains and their sequences are different from each other (Fig. 1D). Lastly, the ‘wheel guards’ in the middle serve as a link for the top and the bottom layers via four helices. Each ZF motif in the bottom layer interacts with each of the four helices (α4, α5, α6, and α7), where α4 interacts with ZF1, α5 with ZF2, α6 with ZF3, and α7 with ZF4. This aids in strengthening the detached structure of the ZF motifs so that no significant change in the conformation is sustained.11

  In 2012, Chen et al.11 determined the crystal structure (PDB: 3U9G) of the apo N-terminal domain of the rat ZAP (r-ZAP) (Fig. S1A). This structure revealed the arrangement for each of the four ZF domains. In 2019, Meagher et al.12 reported the crystal structure (PDB: 6UEJ) of the complex of human ZAP (h-ZAP) with RNA (three nucleotides), where it was observed that the ZF2 domain selectively recognizes the CG dinucleotide bases (Fig. S1B). In 2020, a crystal structure (PDB: 6L1W) of the complex of mouse ZAP (m-ZAP) with RNA was reported,13 where ZAP binds with a six nucleotide long single-stranded-RNA (ss-RNA) (C1GUCGU6). In this structure, they also identified that the CG-dinucleotides specifically bind with the ZF2 domain and a guanine (G2) nucleotide residue of the viral RNA interacts with the ZF3 domain (Fig. S1C). Considering these differing observations from various crystal structures,11–13 we first aligned the sequences of various ZF domains (Fig. S2A) which revealed that the sequences of the ZF domains from the rat and mouse species have a higher sequence conservation in comparison to the human ZAP. We also aligned the r-ZAP, m-ZAP, and h-ZAP structures to understand structural differences (Fig. S2B). An RMSD-based comparison (Fig. S2C) showed that the RMSD of m-ZAP with reference to r-ZAP (apo-form) is 1.05 Å, and relative to h-ZAP is 0.59 Å, which indicates highly similar structural folds. Moreover, the side-chains of the residues involved in recognition of the CG-rich RNA are oriented similarly in all structures, except the ZF2 residue Tyr98, which has a slightly different side-chain orientation (Fig. S2D).

  Based on antiviral activity studies, Chen et al.11 further showed that the ZF1 residues (Arg74, Arg75, and Lys76), the ZF2 residues (Lys89, Arg95, and Lys107), and the ZF4 residues (Arg179 and Arg189) (Fig. S1A), likely play a role in recognizing the CG-rich viral RNA. Using a mutagenesis approach, Meagher et al.12 also suggested that the ZF1 residues (Arg74, Arg75, and Lys76) may affect binding of RNA. However, in their crystal structure (PDB: 6UEJ), no direct interactions of RNA were observed with the suggested residues in the ZF1 domain. Additionally, they suggested a role of three new ZF2 residues (Tyr98, Tyr108, and Phe144) in RNA recognition (Fig. S1B). Luo et al.13 also studied the cellular antiviral activity of alanine (Ala) mutations in each of the ZF1 residues (Arg74, Arg75, and Lys76), the ZF2 residues (Cys96, Tyr98, Lys107, and Tyr108), and the ZF3 residues (Glu148, Lys151 and Arg170), and found that the mutations in these residues reduce the binding affinity as well as the cellular antiviral activity of the viral RNA. However, in their crystal structure of the m-ZAP/RNA complex (PDB: 6L1W) as well, the residues from the ZF1 domain did not directly participate in RNA binding.

  While these experimental structures of various ZAP/RNA complexes provide molecular details of some ZAP/RNA contacts, the conflicting observations from different experimental structures suggest a role of conformational dynamics in RNA recognition. Moreover, the energetic contributions and structural perturbations due to mutations known to affect RNA binding are unresolved. We address these questions via all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) based methods combined with the free-energy calculations, which have also proven useful in studies of other protein/RNA complexes.14–17


## Methods

### Simulation Setup
  Among three crystal structures of ZAP (m-ZAP, r-ZAP, and h-ZAP), r-ZAP is the apo form of ZAP, while the m-ZAP and h-ZAP are complexes with RNA, where the RNA orientations near the ZF2 domains are similar (Fig. S1B,C). However, in the m-ZAP/RNA complex, the viral CG-rich RNA also interacts with the ZF3 domain, which is not observed in the h-ZAP/RNA complex. Thus, we conducted several all-atom MD simulations using the complex of m-ZAP and RNA (C1GUCGU6) structure. The initial coordinates of the complex were obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) entry 6L1W.13 The MODELLER software v10.218 was used for modeling the missing residues SM1TD3, T54GDQAGI60 and R226N227 in the crystallographic structure. The m-ZAP/RNA complex was then solvated using water molecules based on the TIP3P19 water model and neutralized using NaCl (150 mM concentration). The solvated and neutralized system contained 43496 total atoms.

  At first, the system was minimized for 10000 steps by applying the steepest descent method for the first 5000 steps, followed by the conjugate gradient method for the remaining steps. Then, the system was gradually heated from 0 to 300 K in the NVT ensemble for 20 ps. Afterward, an equilibration step was conducted for 200 ps in the NVT ensemble at 300 K. The atoms in RNA and ZAP as well as the Zn+2 ions were restrained with a force constant of 100 kcal mol−1 Å−2 in all of the above steps. Then, a similar series of minimization and equilibration was conducted by slowly reducing the positional restraints to 25, 20, 15, 10, 5, and finally to 0 kcal mol−1 Å−2. Finally, an NPT equilibration was carried out for 10 ns, which was followed by a production simulation for 2 μs at 1 atm physical pressure and 300 K. Three independent MD simulations were conducted for the m-ZAP/RNA complex.

  In all MD simulations, the temperature was controlled using the Langevin thermostat with a collision frequency of 5 ps−1 and the pressure was maintained via the Berendsen barostat20 with a pressure relaxation time of 2 ps. To restrain the bonds involving the hydrogen atoms, the SHAKE algorithm with a tolerance of 10−5 Å was applied. An integration time-step of 2 fs was implemented. For all non-bonded short-ranged interactions, a cut-off radius of 12 Å was applied, and the particle mesh Ewald method was utilized for the long-ranged electrostatic interactions. In all MD simulations, the ff14SB21,22 (protein) and RNA.OL323,24 (RNA) force fields were used. The Joung-Cheatham parameters25 for Na+ and Cl− ions, and the zinc AMBER force field (ZAFF)26–28 for modeling the zinc coordination with the Cys (C) and His (H) residues were used. All MD simulations were conducted using the AMBER18 package.29


### Relative Binding Free Energy Calculations
  Alchemical free energy simulations have been widely used in estimating the binding free energies in various biomolecular systems.15,30 Among different methods of computing free energies, thermodynamic integration (TI) is a widely-used method to calculate the free-energy changes due to alchemical transformations.31

  In this work, we have computed the relative binding free energies for ZAP mutations within the ZAP/RNA complex, by considering a thermodynamic cycle involving a wild type amino-acid residue converted into a mutated amino-acid residue (Fig. S3). In this thermodynamic cycle, the vertical arms represent the RNA-bound forms of ZAP, and the horizontal arms represent an alchemical transformation in ZAP that cannot be experimentally achieved. Considering the free energy change along the horizontal arms of the cycle (ΔGbind and ΔGfree), we estimated the relative binding free energy as:  (1) ΔΔGbind=ΔGcomp−ΔGfree=ΔGmutant−ΔGwild–type

  The TI calculations are based on the following equation:  (2) ΔG=(A→B)=∫01〈∂U(λ,X)∂λ〉λdλ  where λ is a coupling parameter between two chemical states A and B. X corresponds to the spatial coordinates of the wild-type and the mutated proteins. Here, 0 and 1 indicate the starting and the end states respectively. 〈…〉λ is the ensemble averaged value at a particular λ point. U is the potential energy function defined by a force field. For the common case of linear scaling of the potential between two end states, the potential energies UA and UB can be combined as:  (3) U=λUB(X)+(1−λ)UA(X)  and, the derivative of U with respect to λ is UB(X)−UA(X), thus Eq. 2 can be expressed as:  (4) ΔG=(A→B)=∫01〈UB(X)−UA(X)〉λdλ

  Considering previously successful studies,14,32–38 a one-step protocol was used in TI calculations. In this protocol, a Lennard-Jones transformation of the targeted regions between the chemical states A and B occurs in the same step. The following steps were considered for each λ to perform TI calculations: each starting structure was initially minimized for 5000 steps via the steepest descent method followed by an equal number of steps via the conjugate gradient method. The system was then gradually heated from 0 to 300 K in the NVT ensemble followed by a 1 ns equilibration in the NPT ensemble. Then, a 2 ns long MD simulation in the NPT ensemble was performed at 300 K and at 1 atm pressure to accumulate the estimates of the derivative of the potential energy (∂U/∂λ), using 25 equally-spaced λ windows. To get the ΔG value, a Gaussian quadrature approximation was employed in the numerical integration step.

  Each alchemical transformation was carried out only in the forward direction (residue X to Alanine, where X is the mutated residue) and replicated three times because the free energy calculations in the backward direction (when a shorter sidechain is converted to a longer sidechain) require larger amounts of sampling time to converge, causing hysteresis in the estimates of the free energy. In such cases, obtaining multiple independent estimates of the free energy from forward alchemical transformations, as carried out here, is a viable approach, which has also been successfully utilized in other systems.37,38 Therefore, the data for the variation in the potential energy gradient (∂U/∂λ) and ΔG for different λ values (see Supporting Information) from three independent simulations were used to compute the standard deviations of the relative binding energy calculations, which varied between 0.10 and 1.16 kcal/mol (Table S1), indicating a good convergence in TI calculations. Previous benchmarking studies39 have suggested that TI gives similar free energy values as other methods for sufficient number of intermediate λ values.


### Conformational Analyses Metrics
  All trajectories were analyzed using the CPPTRAJ40 toolkit of the AMBER18 package and using the Visual Molecular Dynamics (VMD) package.41


#### Root mean squared deviation/fluctuation (RMSD/RMSF).
  To characterize the conformational changes in the ZAP/RNA complex, RMSD and RMSF analyses were performed by considering all heavy atoms of the backbone of ZAP and RNA. Here, the crystal structure of the m-ZAP/RNA complex was considered as a reference.


#### Buried surface area (BSA).
  BSA was calculated between RNA and ZAP as well as separately between RNA and each of the individual ZF domains (ZF1, ZF2, ZF3, and ZF4). BSA was estimated using the following equation:  (5) BSA=SASAP+SASAR−SASAP−R  where SASAP represents the solvent accessible surface area (SASA) of ZAP, SASAR represents the SASA of RNA, and SASAP−R represents the combined SASA of the ZAP/RNA complex. Furthermore, to estimate the differences in the BSA computed via simulations, and the BSA observed in the crystal structure, we computed ΔBSA:  (6) ΔBSA=BSAsim−BSAcrystal  where, BSAsim is the average BSA computed based on data from three independent MD simulations, and BSAcrystal is the initial BSA value based on the crystal structure.


#### Interaction energy (electrostatic and vdW).
  The NAMDEnergy plugin in VMD41 was used to estimate the electrostatic and vdW interaction energies between all-atoms of the ZAP protein (or individual domains) and RNA.


#### Hydrogen bonds.
  A hydrogen bond was considered to form when the donor-acceptor distance was within 3.5 Å and the donor-acceptor-hydrogen angle is within 45°.42–44


#### Cluster analysis.
  To classify conformations sampled in MD simulations into individual populations, the Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (DBSCAN)45 clustering algorithm was employed. The heavy atoms of the ZAP/RNA complex were included in the cluster analysis. In the DBSCAN-based clustering, a minimum number of points of 4 and a cut-off distance of 0.6 Å were used.


#### Fractional occupancy (FO).
  This metric indicates the consistency of interactions between specific residue pairs. For these calculations, the distance between the heavy atoms of the specific residues was set to 3.5 Å. The frames from an MD simulation where the residue-pairs were within or outside the cut-off distance were designated as 1 or 0. The count of frames designated as 1 out of total frames is the value of the metric for a given residue pair.


#### Binding free energy (ΔGbind).
  The binding free energy of the residues in ZAP with the nucleotides in RNA was calculated using Molecular Mechanics/Generalized Born Surface Area (MM/GBSA) approach,46–48 using a Python script (MMPBSA.py).49 The binding energy (ΔGbind) is made up of several components:  (7) ΔGbind=ΔEvac+ΔGsolv   (8) ΔEvac=ΔEele+ΔEvdW   (9) ΔGsolv=ΔGGB+ΔGNP   (10) ΔGNP=γ(SASA)+β

  ΔEvac is the energy in vacuum (gas phase), and ΔEele and ΔEvdW represent the electrostatic and van der Waals energies, respectively. ΔGsolv is the solvation free energy consisting of the polar solvation energy (ΔGGB) and nonpolar solvation energy (ΔGNP).ΔGGB is obtained by the GB model, and ΔGNP was calculated by using the SASA with the surface tension of the solvent (γ) as 0.00720 kcal.Å−2 and the fitting parameter (β) as 0.0.


## Results
  In this work, we have focused on characterizing the conformational dynamics of the m-ZAP/RNA complex, and studying the effects of mutations in ZAP on its interactions with RNA. At first, we studied the structural changes in ZAP and RNA to understand the driving forces for RNA recognition by ZAP. Finally, we quantified the effects of mutations in key residues that significantly contribute to the formation of the ZAP/RNA complex.


### Dynamics of the ZAP/RNA Complex
  To monitor the conformational dynamics of the ZAP/RNA complex, we first assessed the backbone RMSD of the ZAP/RNA complex (Fig. 2A). The converged RMSD values between 3.5 and 4.0 Å indicate that the ZAP/RNA complex is conformationally stable in each of the three MD simulations. To probe the origins of deviations in the structure of the complex, we also estimated the RMSD for the isolated ZAP (Fig. 2B) and for RNA (Fig. 2C), which showed lower values for ZAP (2.5-3.5 Å) than for RNA (3.4-4.0 Å), thereby suggesting a higher flexibility in the RNA structure than in ZAP. To check the residue-level/nucleotide-level flexibility in ZAP and RNA, we further calculated the RMSF of residues in each zinc finger domain (Fig. 2E) as well as of each nucleotide in viral RNA (Fig. 2F). Among four ZF domains (ZF1, ZF2, ZF3, and ZF4), the residues of the ZF2 and ZF3 domains fluctuate more in comparison to those in the ZF1 and ZF4 domains. Specifically, the residues Gln100 through Arg103 of ZF2, and the residues Gly155 through Gly157 as well as the residues Gln164 through Gln166 of ZF3 showed higher fluctuations relative to the crystal structure. The residues Thr54 through Ile60 in a loop connecting the β2 and β3 strands (outside of the ZF domains) also showed higher fluctuations, although these residues do not directly participate in RNA recognition. Among six nulceotides in RNA, the C1 and G2 nucleotides of viral RNA showed higher flexibility, and the C4 and C5 showed lower flexibility relative to the crystal structure.

  To assess the ZAP/RNA interface, we estimated the differences in the averaged buried surface area (ΔBSA) between the BSA of complex or individual ZF domains measured from simulations, and the BSA observed in the crystal structure. A positive or a negative ΔBSA value (Fig. 2F) indicates that the interaction surface increased or decreased relative to the interaction surface observed in the crystal structure. Based on the ΔBSA values, we observed that the overall interfacial area between ZAP and the viral RNA marginally increases by ~88 Å2. Among four ZF domains, two domains showed positive ΔBSA values (+128 Å2, ZF1; and +29 Å2, ZF4), thereby indicating that each of these domains (ZF1 and ZF4) domains had a small increase in the interaction surface with RNA. However, we also noticed that the other two domains (ZF2 and ZF3) showed negative ΔBSA values (−43 Å2, ZF2; −35 Å2, ZF3), thereby indicating a decrease in the interaction surface of these domains with RNA. During MD simulations, we noticed that the C1 and G2 nucleotides of RNA changed their interactions from the ZF3 domain to the ZF1 domain due to the dynamic behavior of RNA. This conformational rearrangement resulted in a decrease in the ΔBSA value of the ZF3 domain and an increase the ΔBSA value of the ZF1 domain. Moreover, during the movement of RNA from ZF3 to ZF1, the C1 and G2 nucleotides also interacted with the ZF4 domain, which contributed to an increase in the ΔBSA value for the ZF4 domain. Also, the terminal nucleotide (U3) changed its orientation from the ZF2 domain to the linker domain, which acts as a connector between the ZF3 and ZF4 domains. This led to the negative ΔBSA value for the ZF2 domain.


### Interaction Energies Between Zinc Finger Domains and RNA
  To understand the interactions between ZAP and RNA, we calculated the non-bonded (van der Waals and electrostatic) interaction energies between all atoms of each of the ZF domains with the viral RNA (Fig. 3A), as well as between all atoms of each nucleotide in RNA and ZAP (Fig. 3B). The interaction energies of different ZF domains with RNA are: −227.56 kcal/mol (ZF1), −336.29 kcal/mol (ZF2), −104 kcal/mol (ZF3), and −5.96 kcal/mol (ZF4). These results suggest that the ZF2 domain has strongest interactions with the CG-rich RNA, followed by the ZF1 and ZF3 domains, and the weakest interactions with ZF4. Furthermore, among individual RNA nucleotides, the interaction energies with ZAP are: C1 (−64.75 kcal/mol), G2 (−117.12 kcal/mol), U3 (−120.74 kcal/mol), C4 (−110.45 kcal/mol), G5 (−98.18 kcal/mol), and U6 (−27.56 kcal/mol). These data revealed that the nucleotides G2 through G5 have stronger interactions than the terminal nucleotides (C1 and U6), and the interactions are dominated by the electrostatic contributions.

  We also estimated the number of hydrogen bonds (NH) between each ZF domain and the CG-rich RNA (Fig. 3C) and each nucleotide in RNA with ZAP (Fig. 3D). These analyses showed that the ZF2 domain had more hydrogen bonds with RNA than the other ZF domains (Fig. 3C). Moreover, among six nucleotides of the viral RNA, the C4 nucleotide followed by the G5 nucleotide interact with the RNA via hydrogen bonding interactions (Fig. 3D). Overall, the hydrogen bonds between ZAP and RNA also contribute to the formation of this protein/RNA complex.

  In Fig. S4A,B, we further show the non-bonded interaction energies of each zinc ion with ZAP and the RNA nucleotides. These data (Fig. S4A) suggest that the zinc ion in the ZF2 domain has significantly stronger interactions with ZAP (−57.8 kcal/mol) than the zinc ion in ZF1 (−31.51 kcal/mol), followed by the zinc ion in ZF3 (−27.8 kcal/mol) and the zinc ion in ZF4 (−8.98 kcal/mol). Moreover, among six nucleotides of the CG-rich RNA, the C4 (−36.48 kcal/mol) and U3 (−25.2 kcal/mol) nucleotides strongly interact with the zinc ion located in the ZF2 domain (Fig. S4B). Collectively, these data showed that the ZF2 domain followed by the ZF1 domain interact strongly with the CG-rich RNA via electrostatic and hydrogen bonding interactions, and the zinc ions also play a key role in providing additional stability to the ZAP/RNA complex.


### Residue-level Interactions Between ZAP and RNA
  In Fig. 4, we show the fractional occupancy (FO) of a nucleotide-amino-acid interaction in the ZAP/RNA complex (Fig. 4A, upper panel), the pair-wise decomposition of the residue-level binding free energy (Fig. 4A, lower panel), and the snapshots representing the residues involved in these protein/RNA interactions (Fig. 4B). The FO data showed that a large number of residue-nucleotide interactions in the RNA/ZAP interface have ~25% occupancy (C1-Arg170, C1-Lys151, C1-Arg179, G2-Lys151, U3-Arg75, U3-148, U6-His97, and U6-Tyr98), some interactions have 25-50% occupancy (C1-Arg74, G2-Arg74, G2-Glu148, U3-Lys89, C4-Lys76, G5-leu90, G5-Tyr98, and G5-Lys107), and a few have higher than 50% occupancy (U3-Arg74, U3-Lys76, C4-Leu87, C4-Lys89, C4-Leu90, C4-Tyr108, G5-Arg95, U6-Arg95, and U6-Glu204).

  The ΔGbind results (Fig. 4A, lower panel) indicate several favorable interactions (a more negative value indicates a more favorable interaction between the amino-acid-nucleotide pair) in the ZAP/RNA interface. Specifically, several interactions have ΔGbind under −2.5 kcal/mol (C1-Arg170, C1-Lys151, C1-Arg179, G2-Glu148, G2-Lys151, U3-Arg74, U3-Arg75, U3-Lys89, U3-Glu148, C4-Leu87, G5-Leu90, G5-Tyr98, G5-Lys107, U6-Arg95), some interactions have between −2.5 kcal/mol and −5 kcal/mol (C1-Arg74, G2-Arg74, C4-Lys76, C4-Leu90, C4-Tyr108, and G5-Lys107), and a few have values lower than −5 kcal/mol (U3-Lys76, C4-Lys89, and G5-Arg95).

  Based on the ΔGbind data, we identified the following critical residue-pairs (Fig. 4B): C1-Arg74, G2-Arg74, U3-Lys76, C4-Lys76, C4-Lys89, C4-Leu90, C4-Tyr108, G5-Arg95, G5-Lys107, and U6-Glu204. Most of these residue-pairs have a hydrogen bonding interaction, except the pairs G2-Arg74, C4-Lys76, and G5-Arg95, where salt-bridging interactions, and the pair C4-Tyr108, where π-π stacking interactions were observed. Additional interactions between other residue pairs are shown in Fig. S5. We also note that during the movement of RNA from ZF3 to ZF1, we observed that the C1 nucleotide also interacted with the residue Arg179 of the ZF4 domain (Fig. S5, panel iii).


### Relative Binding Free Energies (ΔΔGbind) and Structural Changes on Mutations
  Based on previous studies11–13 and our MM/GBSA analysis (Fig. 4), we found that the residues Arg74 and Lys76 of the ZF1 domain, and the residues Lys89, Leu90, Arg95, Tyr108, and Lys107 of the ZF2 domain, and Arg179 of the ZF4 domain (only residue in the ZF4 domain showing a negative ΔGbind) are primarily involved in RNA recognition. Among these residues, most of the residues interact with the RNA via side-chain interactions except the residue Leu90, which has backbone interactions. Thus, to estimate the relative binding free energy on individual mutations in these residues, we considered all residues except for the residue Leu90.

  Apart from the residues mentioned above, the m-ZAP crystal structure showed that other residues including Tyr98, Glu148, Ile149, Lys151, and Arg170 of ZAP also directly participate in binding to RNA. However, among these five residues, the side-chains of the residues Glu148 and Arg170 interact with the G2 nucleotide and the residue Tyr98 interacts with the G5 nucleotide. Thus, from the m-ZAP crystal structure, Tyr98, Glu148, and Arg170 were considered for mutations in our study. Therefore, we computed the changes in binding free energies on Ala mutations in ten key interacting residues that were chosen based: (i) only on the m-ZAP crystal structure (Tyr98, Glu148, and Arg170); (ii) only on MD simulations (Arg74, Lys76, Arg95, and Arg179); and (iii) on consistency among the crystal structure (PDB: 6L1W) and MD simulations (Lys89, Lys107, and Tyr108).

  In Fig. 5, we show the change in the binding free energy (ΔΔGbind) after each mutation (Fig. 5A) and the representative snapshots of each interaction before and after the mutation (Fig. 5B). For each mutation, the free energy values from three independent simulations are reported in Table S1, and the gradient of the potential energy as well as the free energy changes as a function of the coupling coordinate (λ) are shown in Figs. S6, S7, and S8. For mutations in residues identified based only on the crystal structure, the most unfavorable energetic penalty (~7.93 kcal/mol) was observed for Glu148, while the energetic penalty for Tyr108 and Arg170 are significantly lower and comparable. For mutations in residues identified based solely on MD simulations, the energetically unfavorable mutations ranging from the most to least deleterious were Arg74, Lys76, Arg95, and Arg179 where Arg179 had a lower energetic penalty. For residues identified as critical to interaction both in the crystal structure and MD simulations, Lys89 and Lys107 were significantly unfavorable (~7.24 kcal/mol and ~5.93 kcal/mol), while Tyr108 was marginally unfavorable (~1.88 kcal/mol).

  On Ala mutations for the residues Arg74, Arg95, Tyr98, Lys107, Tyr108, Glu148, and Arg179, we observed that the hydrogen-bonding and/or stacking interactions were disrupted in all of them. However, mutations in the residues Lys76 and Lys89 led to disruption of the side-chain interactions but the backbone interactions remained intact (Fig. 5B). Here, it is worth mentioning that when we mutated the residue Arg170 to Ala170, we noticed that the residue Lys151 forms a new hydrogen bond with the G2 nucleoside base. Overall, from these results, we observed that among ten crucial residues that we identified, the residues Arg74, Lys76, Lys89, Lys107, and Glu148 play a prominent role in recognition of the viral RNA.


## Discussion
  ZAP secures cells from viral infection by preferentially detecting and depleting viral RNAs with a higher frequency of CG dinucleotides than the host messenger RNAs. In our work, we have reported simulation studies on the conformational dynamics of a ZAP/RNA complex and studied the energetic and structural effects of mutations in ZAP on its interactions with RNA.

  The crystal structures of the h-ZAP (PDB: 6UEJ)12 and m-ZAP (PDB: 6L1W)13 showed that the ZF2 domain specifically interacts with the CG dinuleotides. From MD simulations, the interaction energies and the number of hydrogen bonds between each ZF domain with RNA also suggest that among four ZF domains, ZF2 domain contributes most to the binding of the CG-rich viral RNA (Fig. 3). Moreover, in the m-ZAP crystal structure, the guanine (G2) nucleotide also interacts with the residues Glu148, Ile149, Lys151, and Arg170 of the ZF3 domain. However, in MD simulations, due to the dynamic behavior of the single stranded RNA, the C1 and G2 nucleotides shift their positions from the ZF3 domain to the ZF1 domain, and primarily interact with the residues Arg74 and Lys76 (ZF1).

  The interaction energy between the zinc ions and the viral RNA further suggested that the zinc ion situated in the ZF2 domain significantly interacts with the RNA (Fig. S4). Among other three zinc ions of the ZF motifs, the zinc ion present in the ZF1 domain followed by the one located in the ZF3 domain, also contributed to interactions with RNA. These interaction energies between the zinc ions and the viral RNA indicate that the zinc ions also facilitate recognition of the CG-rich RNA by ZAP.

  To identify which residue-pairs contributed most to the ZAP/RNA complex, we performed the MM/GBSA analysis.48 Based on the ΔGbind data (Fig. 4A), we identified eleven crucial interacting residue-pairs. Among these eleven residue-pairs, seven residue-pairs (C1-Arg74, C1-Arg179, G2-Arg74, U3-Lys76, C4-Lys76, G5-Arg95, and U6-Glu204) are only observed in MD simulations, but not in the m-ZAP crystal structure (PDB: 6L1W). The other four interactions (C4-Lys89, C4-Leu90, C4-Tyr108, and G5-Lys107) remained intact in MD simulations (Fig. 4A). Most of these residue-pairs have hydrogen bonding interactions except for the C4-Lys76 (salt bridging interaction), the C4-Tyr108 pair (π-π interaction), and the C1-Arg179 pair (NH-π interaction). Among these pairs, C4-Lys89 followed by G5-Arg95 and U3-Lys76 contributed significantly to the ZAP/RNA stability.

  Based on the biological activity studies for the r-ZAP,11 h-ZAP,12 and m-ZAP,13 it was suggested Ala mutations of the residues Arg74, Lys76, Lys89, Arg95, Tyr98, Lys107, Tyr108, Glu148, Arg 170, and Arg179 reduced the cellular antiviral activity. Moreover, based on our MM/GBSA analysis (Fig. 4), we chose seven residues for mutations: Arg74 and Lys76 (ZF1), and Lys89, Arg95, Lys107, and Tyr108 (ZF2), and Arg179 (ZF4). We also considered the residues Tyr98, Glu148, and Arg170 for mutations primarily because those residues interact with the guanine G2/G5 nucleotides of RNA. Thus, we estimated the relative binding free energies upon alchemical transformations of these residues. Our ΔΔGbind results revealed that five of these residues (Arg74, Lys76, Lys89, Lys107, and Glu148) significantly affect the binding affinity of ZAP to RNA. Moreover, upon mutations in each of these residues, their interactions with the RNA nucleotides are disrupted. However, on mutation of Arg170 to Ala170, Lys151 forms new hydrogen bonds with the G2 nucleotide which were not observed in the m-ZAP crystal structure. Overall, our calculations showed the energetically unfavorable effects of mutations in several residues (Arg74, Lys76, Lys107, Tyr108, Glu148, and Arg170) that have been suggested to play a key role in the ZAP/RNA interaction.10,12 Moreover, we identified new residues (Lys89, Arg95, Tyr98, and Arg179) and showed that their mutations also adversely affect the ZAP/RNA interaction (Fig. 5).

  The effects of mutations in other biomolecular complexes have been previously studied using ΔΔG calculations and found to be in good agreement with the experimentally observed trends. For example, Serra et al.50 studied the protein-protein interaction in the CDC42/PAK1 complex. In their work, they estimated the computed relative binding free energy (ΔΔG) values for 16 mutations among which 12 single-point transformations were in remarkable agreement with the experimental values (correlation coefficient of 0.91 and a mean error of 0.4 kcal/mol). Clark et al.51 estimated the binding free energy in the protein-protein interfaces involving antibodies and glycoproteins, and found excellent agreement (error within 0.68 kcal/mol) with the experimental values. Moreover, the relative binding free energies for mutations in the protein/RNA interfaces were found to have trends similar to the experimental values.14


## Conclusion
  In this work, we reported results on the conformational dynamics of a ZAP/RNA complex and investigated the energetics of mutations in the ZAP protein that affect its binding to RNA. We found that among four ZF domains (ZF1, ZF2, ZF3, and ZF4), the ZF2 domain followed by the ZF1 and ZF3 domains specifically recognize the viral RNA. While it is not observed in the crystal structures of the ZAP/RNA complexes (PDBs: 6UEJ, 6L1W), we observed RNA interactions with the ZF1 domain. Specifically, we found that the residues Arg74 and Lys76 of the ZF1 domain form stable interactions with the C1 and G2 nucleotides in MD simulations although no such interactions are observed in the crystal structures. From the biological activity studies by various experimental groups,11–13 they reported that the Ala mutations of the residues Arg74, Lys76, Lys89, Arg95, Tyr98, Lys107, Tyr108, Glu148, Arg 170, and Arg179 reduced the cellular antiviral activity. To probe for the structural evidence of the effects of these mutations, we also performed mutational studies of these residues by applying the thermodynamic integration method. Among these residues, we found that the mutations in five residues (Arg74, Lys76, Lys89, Lys107, and Glu148) significantly reduced the binding affinity of ZAP to RNA.
