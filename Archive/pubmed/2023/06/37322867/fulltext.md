# Assembly of JAZ–JAZ and JAZ–NINJA complexes in jasmonate signaling


## Abstract
  Jasmonates (JAs) are plant hormones with crucial roles in development and stress resilience. They activate MYC transcription factors by mediating the proteolysis of MYC inhibitors called JAZ proteins. In the absence of JA, JAZ proteins bind and inhibit MYC through the assembly of MYC–JAZ–Novel Interactor of JAZ (NINJA)–TPL repressor complexes. However, JAZ and NINJA are predicted to be largely intrinsically unstructured, which has precluded their experimental structure determination. Through a combination of biochemical, mutational, and biophysical analyses and AlphaFold-derived ColabFold modeling, we characterized JAZ–JAZ and JAZ–NINJA interactions and generated models with detailed, high-confidence domain interfaces. We demonstrate that JAZ, NINJA, and MYC interface domains are dynamic in isolation and become stabilized in a stepwise order upon complex assembly. By contrast, most JAZ and NINJA regions outside of the interfaces remain highly dynamic and cannot be modeled in a single conformation. Our data indicate that the small JAZ Zinc finger expressed in Inflorescence Meristem (ZIM) motif mediates JAZ–JAZ and JAZ–NINJA interactions through separate surfaces, and our data further suggest that NINJA modulates JAZ dimerization. This study advances our understanding of JA signaling by providing insights into the dynamics, interactions, and structure of the JAZ–NINJA core of the JA repressor complex.


## Introduction
  Jasmonates (JAs) are hormones with crucial roles in biotic and abiotic stress responses, as well as in many developmental programs, most importantly in reproduction, growth inhibition, and senescence. JAs are synthesized from membrane lipids in response to environmental cues and wounding by herbivorous insects, necrotrophic pathogens, and abiotic stresses (Howe et al., 2018; Wasternack and Feussner, 2018). Increased JA levels exert their effects by a large-scale reprogramming of gene expression to protect plants against these stresses and to reallocate resources between defense and growth (Guo et al., 2018). Understanding JA signaling at the structural and mechanistic level thus has great potential for improving the protection of crop plants against biotic stresses and modulating resource allocation for growth optimization.

  JA ZIM (Zinc finger expressed in Inflorescence Meristem)-DOMAIN (JAZ) transcriptional proteins mediate the switch-like activation and repression of JA responses (Chini et al., 2007; Thines et al., 2007). In the absence of stress, JAZ proteins bind and inhibit the activity of diverse transcription factors, including the basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) proteins MYC2, MYC3, and MYC4 (Fernandez-Calvo et al., 2011; Howe et al., 2018; Zander et al., 2020). At increased levels of JA, bioactive forms of the hormone (e.g., jasmonoyl-l-isoleucine [JA-Ile]) mediate formation of ternary complexes between the hormone, JAZ, and the COI1 F-box subunit of the Skp–Cullin–F-box (SCF) ubiquitin ligase (Thines et al., 2007; Katsir et al., 2008; Fonseca et al., 2009). The induced proximity between JAZ and SCFCOI1 leads to ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of JAZ (An et al., 2017; Howe et al., 2018). This in turn relieves repression of JAZ-controlled transcription factors and allows them to induce expression of a large number of JA-responsive genes. Recent results have revealed that in addition to promoting JAZ degradation, JA also destabilizes the acetylation-dependent association of the TOPLESS (TPL) corepressor protein through induction of the deacetylase HDA6 (An et al., 2022).

  Although the mechanism of JA-Ile sensing through SCFCOI1 is understood at the atomic level (Sheard et al., 2010), much less is known about the architecture and assembly of complexes that repress the activity of JA-responsive transcription factors. In Arabidopsis, the prototypical repressor complex consists of at least four subunits: an MYC transcription factor (MYC2, MYC3, MYC4, or MYC5), a JAZ repressor (1 of 13 JAZ proteins [JAZ1–JAZ13]), the adaptor protein Novel Interactor of JAZ (NINJA), and one of five TPL/TPL-related (TPR) corepressor proteins (TPL and TPR1–TPR4) (Howe et al., 2018). These MYC–JAZ–NINJA–TPL complexes are the principal repressors of JA-mediated defense and root growth inhibition responses (Pauwels et al., 2010; Acosta et al., 2013). In addition, a subset of JAZ family members directly interact with TPL proteins (Shyu et al., 2012; Thireault et al., 2015), histone deacetylases (Long et al., 2006; Zhu et al., 2011; An et al., 2017), chromatin-modifying Polycomb proteins (Li et al., 2021), and the alternative adaptor protein ECAP (Li et al., 2020).

  Interactions between the components of the MYC–JAZ–NINJA–TPL complex are mediated by distinct domains and peptide motifs (Howe et al., 2018). The MYC N-terminal JAZ-interaction/transcriptional activation domain (NTD) binds JAZ to mediate transcriptional repression and also competitively binds the MED25 subunit of the Mediator complex to activate transcription (Zhang et al., 2015). JAZ proteins have two MYC-interaction motifs. The first is the highly conserved Jas motif that competitively interacts with both the MYC NTD in the absence of JA-Ile and with COI1 in the presence of JA-Ile (Melotto et al., 2008). The other is the cryptic MYC-interaction domain (CMID), which occurs in a subset of JAZ proteins such as Arabidopsis JAZ1, JAZ2, JAZ5, JAZ6, and JAZ10 (Moreno et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2017). Because the CMID does not bind COI1 to promote JAZ degradation, the main function of the CMID is to desensitize cells to the JA signal. A hallmark of all JAZ proteins is the presence of a ZIM domain that harbors a conserved TIFY sequence motif (Vanholme et al., 2007). The ZIM domain mediates homo- and hetero-dimerization among JAZ proteins and also interacts with the NINJA adaptor protein (Chini et al., 2009; Chung and Howe, 2009; Pauwels et al., 2010). Finally, the ERF-associated amphiphilic repression (EAR) motif, which is found in NINJA and a subset of JAZ proteins, binds the TOPLESS domain of TPL corepressors to modulate transcription through changes in chromatin structure (Pauwels et al., 2010; Howe et al., 2018).

  We previously determined crystal structures of the MYC NTD and TPL TOPLESS domain (TPD), as well as their complexes with the JAZ Jas motif, JAZ CMID, and NINJA EAR motif (Ke et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2015, 2017). However, despite these efforts, the structures of the JAZ ZIM domain, the NINJA ZIM-binding domain (ZBD), and JAZ–JAZ and JAZ–NINJA complexes have proven recalcitrant to experimental structure determination. Moreover, the architecture and subunit stoichiometry of the repressor complex, the mechanism of its assembly, and whether ZIM–ZIM and ZIM–NINJA interactions can occur simultaneously or are competitive have remained unclear.

### AlphaFold/RoseTTAFold models predict that JAZ and NINJA are largely disordered in isolation

A recent large-scale analysis demonstrated that AlphaFold predictions are, on average, more accurate than experimentally determined NMR structures (Fowler and Williamson, 2022) but less accurate than crystal structures at a resolution of 3.5 Å or better (Shao et al., 2022). ### The NINJA ZBD consists of a conserved structured core and a NINJA-specific N-terminal loop
  Previous yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) experiments with Arabidopsis thaliana (At) proteins identified a 40-aa region of JAZ1 (116–155) and a 104-aa region (322–425) of NINJA as sufficient for JAZ–NINJA interaction (Pauwels et al., 2010). We used AlphaScreen luminescence proximity assays to fine-map the interaction between purified His6-AtNINJA fragments and a biotinylated MBP-AtJAZ2 ZIM domain fragment (JAZ2 residues 109–148). As shown in Figure 1B, the smallest NINJA fragment that is sufficient for JAZ2 ZIM binding, independent of the presence or absence of the affinity tag, spans G342–S406. We named this region of NINJA the ZBD.

  The AlphaFold structure model (Figure 1A) and sequence alignment (Supplemental Figure 2) predict that the ZBD consists of two parts: a four-stranded β sheet followed by an α helix (the “core”) and a loop N-terminal to the core, referred to here as the “N-terminal extension region (NTE)” of the ZBD. The core ZBD sequence is conserved in >3000 diverse plant proteins in the NCBI database. Seventy proteins in Arabidopsis contain the core sequence, including the abscisic acid–signaling proteins AFP1–AFP4 (Supplemental Figure 2). By contrast, the NTE, which is required for JAZ binding, is conserved in NINJA orthologs but is not present in AFP1–AFP4 or other NINJA family proteins (Supplemental Figure 2).

  Using AlphaScreen homologous competition binding assays, we determined IC50 values (the concentration of an inhibitor that causes 50% inhibition) for the interaction between the biotinylated MBP-JAZ2 ZIM domain and different NINJA fragments. For N-terminally tagged full-length NINJA and the C-terminal half of NINJA, the IC50 value was approximately 0.19 μM, whereas it was 0.22 μM for the minimal ZBD directly N-terminally fused to a His6Sumo tag (Figure 1C) (see supplemental information, discussion of AlphaScreen competition data). The slopes of both competition curves were very steep (Hill slope of ∼−3), indicating negative cooperativity in the competition (see supplemental information, discussion of AlphaScreen competition data).


### Differential ZIM determinants for JAZ–JAZ and JAZ–NINJA interactions
  Besides mediating JAZ homodimerization and the interaction with NINJA, the ZIM domain also mediates JAZ heterodimerization with other members of the JAZ family. Among the Arabidopsis JAZ proteins, JAZ3 heterodimerizes with JAZ4 and several other AtJAZ family members in Y2H assays (Chini et al., 2009; Chung and Howe, 2009). We therefore compared the determinants of interactions of AtJAZ3 ZIM with (1) AtNINJA ZBD, (2) AtJAZ3 ZIM (ZIM homodimerization), and (3) AtJAZ4 ZIM (ZIM heterodimerization).

  A 30-aa ZIM region is highly conserved and predicted to form a structural domain consisting of a two-stranded β sheet followed by an α helix (Supplemental Figure 3). Y2H assays show that the 30-aa AtJAZ3 ZIM region from residues 178 to 207 was sufficient for JAZ3 interaction with the full-length JAZ3, JAZ4, and NINJA proteins (Figure 2). Next, we deleted either the highly conserved N-terminal TIFY motif (residues 180–185, corresponding to part of the modeled N-terminal β strand and the β turn connecting the two strands), the middle region of the ZIM fragment (residues 186–195, corresponding to the modeled C-terminal β strand), or the C-terminal fragment (residues 196–207, corresponding to the modeled α helix) (Figure 2A). Deletion of the TIFY motif abolished all three JAZ3 interactions. By contrast, deletion of either the middle or the C-terminal ZIM fragment strongly reduced the AtJAZ3–AtJAZ3 interaction and reduced the AtJAZ3–AtJAZ4 interaction weakly, if at all; the combined deletion of both fragments (JAZΔM/C) abolished both interactions (Figures 2B and 2C).   In contrast to JAZ dimerization, deletion of the middle ZIM fragment strongly reduced NINJA interaction, but deletion of the C-terminal fragment failed to noticeably affect NINJA binding (Figure 2D). The JAZ interaction with NINJA is therefore dependent on the predicted C-terminal β strand but not on the ZIM α helix. This suggests that the 18-aa JAZ3 (178–195) fragment is sufficient for NINJA binding and that the relative contributions of the predicted ZIM C-terminal β strand versus the α helix differentiate the JAZ–JAZ and JAZ–NINJA interactions.

  We next tested alanine substitutions of blocks of three or four amino acids within the middle and C-terminal ZIM motifs. With the exception of the IML→AAA substitution in the predicted ZIM α helix, which strongly reduced the JAZ3–JAZ3 interaction, the remaining amino acid replacements had comparatively mild effects on interactions of JAZ3, JAZ4, and NINJA (Supplemental Figure 4).


### The isoleucine and phenylalanine residues of the TIFY motif are specificity determinants for ZIM–ZIM and ZIM–ZBD interactions
  Next, we generated alanine substitutions in the highly conserved TIFYXG motif of AtJAZ10.4 and tested their effects using Y2H, pull-down, and in planta assays (Figure 3A–3C). JAZ10.4 is a dominant-negative JAZ10 splice variant that has an MYC-binding CMID but lacks the COI1-binding Jas motif. It therefore confers strong insensitivity to JA (i.e., lack of de-repression) when overexpressed in Arabidopsis (Yan et al., 2007; Chung and Howe, 2009).   In Y2H assays, the TIFY I107A mutation abolished AtJAZ10.4 homodimerization, as reported previously (Chung and Howe, 2009), but had only a mild effect on the interaction with AtNINJA. Conversely, TIFY F108A strongly attenuated the JAZ10.4 interaction with NINJA but had only a mild effect on JAZ10.4 homodimerization. The corresponding TIFY mutations in JAZ3 similarly discriminated JAZ3 interaction with JAZ10.4 and NINJA (Figure 3A). We confirmed the selectivity of the I107A and F108A mutations by in vitro pull-down assays. MBPHis6-JAZ10.4I107A pulled down HA-NINJA but not HA-JAZ10.4, whereas MBP-JAZ10.4F108A pulled down HA-JAZ10.4 but only very weakly pulled down HA-NINJA (Figure 3B). Together with the selective requirement of the ZIM α helix (Figure 2B–2D), these results provide further evidence that JAZ10.4 and NINJA interact with distinct surfaces of the ZIM domain.

  To test the effect of these point mutations in vivo, we generated transgenic Arabidopsis plants in which wild-type (WT) and mutant JAZ10.4 were expressed from the Cauliflower Mosaic Virus 35S promoter, then tested their sensitivity to JA in root growth inhibition and fertility assays. Overexpression of the WT repressor protein (JAZ10.4WT) strongly suppressed JA-induced root shortening (Figure 3C and Supplemental Figure 5). All 35S::JAZ10.4WT transgenic lines were sterile, as previously reported (Chung and Howe, 2009), but all 35S::JAZ10.4I107A and 35S::JAZ10.4F108A lines were fertile (at least eight independent lines tested per construct). Importantly, however, root growth assays revealed that I107A largely eliminated the JA insensitivity of 35S::JAZ10.4WT lines, whereas F108A only partially affected this phenotype (Figure 3C and Supplemental Figure 5). These data indicate that both JAZ dimerization (defective in the I107A mutant) and JAZ–NINJA binding (defective in the F108A mutant) are important for repression of JA responses in Arabidopsis, and they further suggest that the contribution of I107 to JAZ-mediated repression is greater than that of F108.


### ColabFold models illustrate different ZIM surfaces for ZIM dimerization and NINJA interaction
  To understand the molecular basis of the differential ZIM–ZBD and ZIM–ZIM interactions, we used AlphaFold2-based ColabFold open source code (Mirdita et al., 2022) to generate unbiased structure models of (JAZ3)2 (Figure 3D and Supplemental Figure 6), JAZ3–JAZ4 (Supplemental Figures 7 and 8), NINJA–JAZ3 (Figure 3E and Supplemental Figure 9), and NINJA–(JAZ3)2 complexes (Figure 3F and Supplemental Figure 10). As expected, these models contain large regions with predicted local Distance Difference Test (lDDT) scores <50, which are indicative of disordered regions rather than a specific structure (Jumper et al., 2021). By contrast, the ZIM domains and the ZIM–ZIM interfaces in the binary (JAZ3)2 and JAZ3–JAZ4 complexes and the ternary NINJA–(JAZ3)2 complex were modeled with very high confidence and predicted precision and high consistency among different independently generated models. Similarly, the ZBD domain and ZBD–ZIM interface of the NINJA–(JAZ3)2 complex were modeled with high confidence and predicted precision and high consistency among different independently generated models (Supplemental Figure 10). However, the ZIM domain and ZIM–ZBD interface in binary NINJA–JAZ3 complex models had only moderate confidence and precision, and the interface could not be modeled in a single conformation (Supplemental Figure 9). A binary NINJA–JAZ3 interaction may therefore be unstable or dynamic.

  In the (JAZ3)2 and JAZ3–JAZ4 models, the ZIM–ZIM interaction is mediated through formation of an extended, four-stranded β sheet mainly through TIFY–TIFY main-chain interactions and through interactions of all four strands with the two opposing ZIM α helices (Figure 3D and Supplemental Figure 8). By contrast, in the NINJA–(JAZ3)2 model, the ZBD β sheet interacts with the extended ZIM β sheet through side-chain interactions without formation of an intermolecular β sheet (Figure 3F). Furthermore, the ZBD NTE loop undergoes a conformational change (Supplemental Figure 11) to bind both the C terminus of the TIFY strand and the β turn that connects the two β strands (Figure 3F). The different ZIM β-sheet surfaces allow simultaneous interaction of the two ZIM strands with each other, as well as with the NINJA ZBD. Neither the core ZBD nor the ZBD NTE interact with the ZIM α helix (Figures 3E and 3F). The ColabFold models are therefore consistent with, and provide a structural rationale for, our experimentally determined lack of a requirement of the JAZ3 ZIM helix for NINJA interaction, requirement of the ZBD NTE for ZIM binding, and partial redundancy of the central and C-terminal ZIM fragments for ZIM–ZIM interaction.

  The models also provide structural insight into the highly selective effects of the TIFY point mutations. Consistent with the in vivo mutational analysis (Figures 3A–3C), the TIFY isoleucine makes multiple interactions with the ZIM domain in the model but makes no interactions with the ZBD (Figures 3G–3I). Thus, the model provides a structural rationale for why the corresponding JAZ3 I181A and JAZ10.4 I107A mutations disrupt JAZ–JAZ interaction but have a weaker effect on JAZ–NINJA interaction. Conversely, the TIFY phenylalanine interacts with both the ZBD core and the ZBD NTE (Figures 3H and 3I), but it makes no interactions with the ZIM helix (Figure 3G), explaining why its alanine replacement has a greater disruptive effect on JAZ–NINJA compared with JAZ–JAZ interaction.

  Alanine replacements of the remaining three TIFYXG residues had milder effects on homodimerization, JAZ10.4–JAZ3 heterodimerization, and NINJA interaction (Figure 3A). Consistent with this result, in the model, JAZ3 T180 makes only one interaction each with ZIM and ZBD and is not required for either interaction; Y183 does not bind the ZBD but makes four predicted van der Waals interactions with the ZIM motif of the other monomer and is weakly required for ZIM binding; and G185 stabilizes the β turn that connects the ZIM β strands and is partially required for ZIM binding.


### NINJA modulates JAZ dimerization
  In the ternary complex, the NINJA ZBD core and NTE bind and stabilize the ZIM–ZIM β sheet interface from both ends and interact with the strands from both monomers (Figure 3F and Supplemental Figures 10C and 11). Furthermore, an additional β strand C-terminal to ZIM (JAZ3 213–215) forms, which binds the β sheet of the opposing monomer to extend and further stabilize the intermolecular ZIM β sheet (Figure 3F and Supplemental Figure 11). The model therefore predicts that NINJA binding stabilizes JAZ3 dimerization.

  We used Y2H assays to experimentally test this prediction. As shown in Figure 4, NINJA indeed increased the homodimerization signal for JAZ3.   By contrast, the homodimerization capacity of JAZ proteins that do not (JAZ7 and JAZ8, clade IV) or only weakly (JAZ11 and JAZ12, clade II) interact with NINJA (Pauwels et al., 2010) and did not (or only very weakly) homodimerize in our assay was unaffected by the presence of NINJA. Unexpectedly, NINJA co-expression had little or no effect on the homodimerization signal of clade I JAZ1 and clade III JAZ10 and reduced the homodimerization signals of the clade I JAZ proteins JAZ2, JAZ5, and JAZ6, suggesting that NINJA differentially modulates homodimerization of JAZ proteins from different clades. Notably, JAZ1, JAZ2, JAZ5, JAZ6, and JAZ10 are the five JAZ proteins that appear to use a CMID for repression of JA responses (Moreno et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2017).

  To gain structural insight into NINJA effects on clade I JAZ proteins, we generated ColabFold models of binary and ternary NINJA complexes with JAZ5 (Supplemental Figure 13) and JAZ1 (Supplemental Figure 14). For all four complexes, the ZBD and ZIM domains were modeled with high confidence. The predicted JAZ5–NINJA and JAZ1–NINJA interfaces were modeled with low-to-moderate (JAZ5) and moderate-to-high (JAZ1) precision with side-chain interactions between the ZIM β strands and ZBD β sheet, as well as between the ZIM β turn and the ZBD NTE (Supplemental Figures 13 and 14, left panels), very similar to the JAZ3–NINJA interaction. However, in contrast to the JAZ3 model, these ColabFold models failed to identify stable (JAZ5)2–NINJA and (JAZ1)2–NINJA interfaces (Supplemental Figures 13 and 14, right panels), consistent with the inability of NINJA to stabilize JAZ5 and JAZ1 homodimerization.

  In the (JAZ3)2–NINJA model, each JAZ3 subunit directly interacts with NINJA through eight residues: the five NINJA-facing ZIM β strand residues and the three NINJA-facing residues that directly follow the induced β strand in the ZIM-Jas linker and that clamp the NINJA ZBD core from two sides (Supplemental Figure 15A). The NINJA-facing ZIM β sheet residues are identical between JAZ3 and JAZ5 and adopt very similar conformations in a structural alignment (Supplemental Figure 15A). However, the residues of the induced β strand and those C-terminal to the β strand (JAZ3 213–218) are not conserved. The corresponding region is missing in the shorter ZIM-Jas linkers of CMID-containing JAZ proteins (Supplemental Figure 15B), and the JAZ5 and JAZ1 linkers do not form β strands and do not interact with NINJA in the ternary complex models (Supplemental Figures 13 and 14). The models therefore suggest that interactions between NINJA and JAZ homodimers are unstable or cannot be accurately modeled in the absence of ZBD-clamping linkers.


### Reconstitution of MYC–JAZ–NINJA and MYC–JAZ–TPD repression complexes
  From among the 13 known Arabidopsis JAZ proteins, we initially focused on JAZ5 for protein complex purification because it contains all four known JAZ-interaction modules: Jas, ZIM, CMID, and EAR. JAZ5 therefore has the potential to interact both directly with the TPD of TPL/TPR proteins through its own EAR motif and indirectly through the EAR motif of NINJA. Moreover, the comparatively high stability of purified JAZ5 and JAZ1 made them more suitable than JAZ3 for biochemical and structural analysis. We further focused on the JAZ-binding AtMYC3 NTD and the TPD of OsTPR2 because we previously purified these proteins and determined their high-resolution crystal structures (Ke et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2015). Full-length, bacterially produced AtMYC3, AtJAZ5, AtNINJA, and OsTPR2 formed aggregates and could not be purified in isolation. However, we did purify all components of the repressor complex as MBP fusion proteins when expressed in Sf9 insect cells. We also purified full-length, Escherichia coli–expressed AtJAZ5 when it was co-expressed with the JAZ-binding AtMYC3 NTD, indicating that MYC binding stabilizes JAZ5. JAZ5 and the MYC3 NTD co-eluted as a monodisperse complex from a size exclusion chromatography (SEC) column with an apparent 2:2 stoichiometry (Supplemental Figure 16A and 16B). Pre-incubation of the NTDMYC3–JAZ5 complex with either AtNINJA or the OsTPR2 TPD enabled us to purify the ternary NTDMYC3–JAZ–NINJA and NTDMYC3–JAZ–TPDTPR2 complexes with estimated stoichiometries, based on SEC and molecular weight–adjusted subunit staining, of 2:(1–2):(1–2) for NTDMYC3:JAZ:NINJA and 2:2:4 for NTDMYC3:JAZ:TPDTPR2 (Supplemental Figure 16A and 16B).

  In a JAZ5-containing MYC–JAZ–NINJA–TPD complex, the TPD may interact with either the JAZ5 EAR motif and or the NINJA EAR motif. We found that heterogeneous, higher-order complexes formed when we incubated WT AtNTDMYC3, AtJAZ5, AtNINJA, and OsTPDTPR2. By contrast, when we used AtJAZ5 with a mutated EAR motif in which the three leucine residues of the LxLxL EAR motif were replaced with alanine residues (270-LDLRL-274 to 270-ADARA-274), two overlapping complexes formed with estimated stoichiometries of 2:(1–2):(1–2):4 and 2:(1–2):(1–2):2 for NTDMYC3:JAZ:NINJA:TPDTPR2 (Supplemental Figure 14C). This indicates that both JAZ5 and the NINJA EAR motifs are active and that each one has the potential to interact with the OsTPR2 TPD.


### Analysis of the NINJA conformational landscape by hydrogen/deuterium exchange mass spectrometry
  Purification of the proteins and complexes enabled us to analyze their conformational landscapes in solution using hydrogen/deuterium exchange mass spectrometry (HDX-MS). We first analyzed AtNINJA(233–425) in the presence and absence of AtNTDMYC3–AtJAZ5 (Figure 5). Backbone amide hydrogens rapidly exchange against deuterium in solution in flexible, solvent-exposed regions of a protein, whereas they are comparatively stable in buried regions and when engaged in hydrogen bonds such as those formed in stable helices and β sheets. As shown in Figure 5A, outside the ZBD, AtNINJA(233–425) is highly flexible with near-maximum hydrogen exchange. By contrast, the predicted α helix of the ZBD is highly stable, and the preceding β sheet and NTE loop are moderately stable, consistent with the secondary structure prediction of the AlphaFold model (Figure 1A) and the high sequence conservation of the ZBD (Supplemental Figure 2).Figure 5HDX-MS analysis of NINJA.(A) NINJA(233–425) HDX heatmap. Secondary structure elements are indicated by open arrows (β strands), cylinders (α helices), and thick lines (loops). % HDX is indicated by the heatmap color bar (0–100% HDX).(B) ΔHDX of NINJA −/+ JAZ/MYC.   The NTE shows the highest hydrogen exchange within the ZBD, consistent with it being relatively dynamic in isolation. However, this region became selectively and very strongly stabilized upon AtJAZ5 binding, as shown by the change in deuterium exchange (ΔHDX-MS or HDX-MS perturbation map; Figure 5B). This result provides direct experimental support for a JAZ-induced ZBD NTE conformational change and ZIM interaction as shown in the ColabFold models (Figure 3F and Supplemental Figure 11) and is consistent with a function of the NINJA-specific NTE as a ZIM specificity determinant.


### MYC3 NTD, JAZ ZIM, and NINJA ZBD are dynamic in isolation but become stabilized upon complex formation
  Next, we used ΔHDX-MS to map sites in AtJAZ5 that become selectively stabilized upon binding of AtNINJA. Addition of AtNINJA to the AtJAZ5–NTDMYC3 complex induced strong protection of the ZIM β sheet (Figure 6A). Because deuterium exchange occurs at backbone amide hydrogens, which predominantly mediate formation of the JAZ β sheet but not of the interaction with NINJA, this indicates that the β sheet of the ZIM domain is dynamic in NINJA-free JAZ5 and becomes strongly stabilized when bound to NINJA.Figure 6MYC–JAZ interaction mapping by HDX-MS.(A) ΔHDX of JAZ5–MYC3 +/− NINJA.(B) ΔHDX of MYC3(5–242) +/− JAZ5. Relative change in HDX is indicated by the heatmap color code bar at the bottom, with colors corresponding to −50% to +50%. Gray: <5% change. JID, TAD, and secondary structure elements are indicated.

  Finally, we measured ΔHDX-MS of the comparatively stable AtNTDMYC3 in the presence and absence of full-length AtJAZ5. MYC interacts with JAZ through its NTD (Zhang et al., 2015). Based on Y2H experiments, the MYC NTD was subdivided into a JAZ-interaction domain (JID) (Cevik et al., 2012; Chen et al., 2012) and a transcriptional activation domain (TAD) (Fernandez-Calvo et al., 2011; Chen et al., 2012), both of which directly bind the JAZ Jas motif (Zhang et al., 2015). Upon JAZ binding, the TAD α4 and the JID β1-β2 linker were selectively but moderately stabilized (reduced deuterium exchange), whereas the TAD α5, which is distant from JAZ, was mildly destabilized (Figure 6B).

  Together, the ΔHDX data demonstrate that the ZBD NTE, as well as the JAZ ZIM β sheet and regions of the AtMYC3 JID and TAD, are dynamic in the context of the isolated subunits but form stable secondary structures upon binding of their interacting subunits, indicating stepwise subunit stabilizations with increasing assembly of the MYC–JAZ–NINJA repression complex, as schematically summarized in Supplemental Figure 17.


### Negative-stain electron microscopy analysis of an MYC–JAZ–NINJA complex
  We purified a monodisperse ternary complex of His8MBP-tagged AtMYC3 NTD (MYC3 residues 49–238), His16-tagged AtNINJA (Figure 7A and 7B), and untagged AtJAZ1, and we analyzed the complex by negative-stain electron microscopy (EM). The EM density enabled us to build a 3D model of the complex at a resolution of ∼15 Å (Figure 7C and 7D). Even though our biochemical analysis identified larger complexes at higher concentration in solution (Figure 7B and Supplemental Figure 16), under negative-stain EM conditions with a low concentration of complex, the EM density indicated the presence of a monomeric ternary complex. Because many protein complexes partially or fully dissociate at the low protein concentration required for negative-stain EM, we believe that the 1:1:1 complex is either not relevant under physiological conditions or only relevant at low concentrations. We could model into the density the known structures of MBP and MYC3(49–238), as well as I-TASSER models of JAZ and NINJA (Figure 7D). We used the less powerful I-TASSER models (Zhang, 2008; Roy et al., 2010; Yang et al., 2015) for density fitting because the inconsistent “floating spaghetti strands” of the AlphaFold models do not present meaningful structure coordinate predictions (Necci et al., 2021; Ruff and Pappu, 2021; Shao et al., 2022) and cannot be fitted into the density. Importantly, although the resolution is too low for mechanistic analysis, the negative-stain EM density provides clear structure constraints for the AlphaFold model low-confidence regions in the context of the ternary complex. ## Discussion
  Despite the biological importance of JA signaling and its practical impact on agronomic traits, the architecture and subunit interactions of the JA transcriptional repression complex are poorly understood. That is especially the case for JAZ and NINJA, proteins that are predicted to be largely unstructured in isolation, which we have experimentally validated for NINJA by HDX-MS. Our mutational and biochemical analyses of JAZ–JAZ and JAZ–NINJA interactions and the generation of ColabFold complex models have filled major knowledge gaps in the structure and architecture of the complex.

  Repressor complexes consisting of transcriptional activators, hormone-regulated master transcriptional repressors, and TPL corepressors also play key regulatory roles for several other plant hormones, including auxin, gibberellin, and strigolactone, but there is a lack of structural information for all of these complexes. The JA repressor complex provides the first structural details of a plant hormone repressor complex and may be generally relevant beyond JA signaling.


### Strengths and limitations of AlphaFold modeling
  We used AlphaFold-based ColabFold to generate models for JAZ and NINJA complexes because ColabFold enables high-quality predictions of homomeric and heteromeric protein complexes (Mirdita et al., 2022). As expected, the low-complexity regions of JAZ and NINJA and their complexes have very low predicted local distance difference test (plDDT) values (plDDT < 30). The structure illustrations of these regions are not meaningful coordinate predictions but rather indicate the protein disorder typical of intrinsically disordered regions (Necci et al., 2021; Ruff and Pappu, 2021; Shao et al., 2022). Accordingly, these regions showed maximal predicted alignment errors, exhibited no consistencies among independently generated models, and could not be fitted into our negative-stain EM densities. By contrast, the structured ZIM and ZBD domains, the ZIM–ZIM modules of all modeled complexes, and—with lower precision—the ZBD–(JAZ3 ZIM)2 complex were modeled with high confidence. Large-scale validation (Fowler and Williamson, 2022) has suggested that AlphaFold-generated models can be accepted as validated structures when the models have high-confidence and clearly fit the experimental data. That is the case for JAZ3 ZIM, NINJA ZBD, and their (ZIM)2 and ZBD–(ZIM)2 complexes but not for the regions that separate these domains and that could not be fit into the 3D negative-stain EM density. These complex models, which were generated without input from our experimental data, are consistent with the results of our follow-up experiments: (1) the structural core–preceding NTE loop is required for NINJA–ZIM interaction; (2) the predicted ZIM helix is important for ZIM–ZIM, but not ZIM–ZBD, binding; (3) the predicted ZIM helix and C-terminal strand are partially redundant for ZIM–ZIM interaction; (4) alanine replacements of the TIFY isoleucine and phenylalanine selectively disrupt JAZ–JAZ and JAZ–NINJA interactions, respectively; (5) ZIM–ZIM and ZIM–NINJA interactions are mediated through different ZIM surfaces; (6) NINJA binding increases JAZ3 dimerization; and (7) JAZ–NINJA binding selectively reduces HDX of the NTE and ZIM β sheet.

  In contrast to the ternary NINJA–(JAZ3)2 complex interface, all binary ZBD–ZIM interfaces could be modeled with only comparatively moderate precision, and no ternary NINJA–(JAZ)2 interface could be modeled for clade I JAZ1 or JAZ5. Although the binary complex interfaces could be modeled with only moderate precision, they were all very similar to each other, and the JAZ3–NINJA model provided a good fit to our experimental interaction data. We suggest that the binary NINJA–JAZ3 interaction may be less stable or more dynamic than the ternary NINJA–(JAZ3)2 interaction and that JAZ3 binds NINJA predominantly as a ternary complex. This is consistent with the predicted strong NINJA-mediated JAZ3–JAZ3 stabilization that was confirmed by Y2H assay. We note that the ZIM-Jas linker region that interacts with NINJA in the ternary NINJA-(JAZ3)2 complex model (Supplemental Figure 15) was not required for Y2H NINJA–JAZ3 interaction (Figure 2). The Y2H interaction signal may therefore be caused by the fraction that forms the binary NINJA–JAZ3 complex. Assuming that the ability of NINJA to form repressive complexes with JAZ3–JAZ3 homodimers extends to JAZ3–JAZ heterodimers, our findings have implications for understanding how truncated forms of JAZ3 lacking the Jas motif repress JA responses without the ability to directly bind MYCs (Chini et al., 2007; Browse, 2009).


### The NINJA-specific ZBD NTE makes key ZIM interactions
  A homology search and sequence alignment identified a large number of NINJA-related proteins that share sequence homology over the predicted ZBD β sheet/α-helical core domain but lack homology outside of this fold. By contrast, NINJA orthologs share additional homology in the N-terminal NTE loop, which we showed is required for NINJA–JAZ interaction (Figure 2 and Supplemental Figure 2). This requirement was structurally rationalized by our ColabFold models, which show a tight interaction of the NTE with the ZIM β sheet. This interaction was further supported by the HDX-MS perturbation data, which demonstrate a JAZ binding–induced strong and selective stabilization of the N-terminal half of the NTE (Figure 5B) and of the ZIM β sheet, especially around the β turn that connects the ZIM β strands (Figure 6A). Based on these data, we propose that the NTE functions as a ZIM-specificity determinant that was co-opted by a common fold.


### NINJA binding modulates ZIM dimerization
  A long-standing question has been whether ZIM–ZIM and ZIM–NINJA interactions are mutually exclusive or occur independently. The ZIM internal deletion and TIFY point mutant data clearly show differential requirements for the ZIM–ZIM and ZIM–ZBD interactions, which the JAZ3 ColabFold models can explain at the structural level. Moreover, the NINJA–(JAZ3)2 model implies not only that these interactions occur independently but also that NINJA binding stabilizes JAZ3 dimerization. This prediction is consistent with the Y2H assays of the strong NINJA-interacting JAZ3, JAZ4, and JAZ9 proteins, which form clade V of the JAZ family. Our data therefore suggest that NINJA binding may boost the dimerization strength of clade V proteins under physiological conditions. JAZ proteins that do not (JAZ7/8/13) or only weakly (JAZ11/12) interact with NINJA did not homodimerize in this assay, independent of the presence or absence of NINJA.

  By contrast, the five CMID-containing JAZ proteins (JAZ1, JAZ2, JAZ5, JAZ6, and JAZ10) both homodimerize (Figure 4) and interact with NINJA (Pauwels et al., 2010). NINJA had only relatively small effects on the homodimerization signals of the strongly homodimerizing JAZ1, JAZ2, and JAZ10 proteins but clearly decreased the signal of the weakly homodimerizing JAZ5 and JAZ6 proteins (Figure 4). The CMID-containing JAZ proteins JAZ5 and JAZ6 did not form ZBD-binding ZIM-Jas linker fragments in their ColabFold models (Supplemental Figures 13 and 14) and may not be able to form stable ternary NINJA–(JAZ)2 complexes. They may therefore only form binary complexes with NINJA (or potentially complexes that are larger than the ternary complexes). If that is indeed the case, then their homodimerization signals would have derived only or predominantly from NINJA-free JAZ dimers.

  We also note that these proteins may form alternative repressor complexes. For instance, JAZ5 and JAZ6 have C-terminal EAR motifs that allow them to bind TPL both directly and through NINJA; JAZ6 and JAZ8, and possibly more weakly JAZ1/2, JAZ10, and JAZ11/12, can bind the alternative adaptor ECAP to form repressor complexes that contain a bHLH/WD-repeat/MYB TF complex, JAZ, ECAP, and TPR2 (Li et al., 2020); and several JAZ proteins form complexes with MYB, NINJA, and components of the polycomb complex (Li et al., 2021). Clearly, more work is needed to understand the architecture of these complexes.

  Our mapping and functional analyses were performed with clade V JAZ3, which robustly homodimerizes and heterodimerizes and for which we have the most precise ColabFold complex models. Because JAZ3 was insufficiently stable for EM and HDX-MS analysis, we used clade I JAZ1 and JAZ5 for EM and HDX-MS analysis, even though we have only moderately precise NINJA interaction models for JAZ1 and JAZ5. This raises the question of which results apply to JAZ proteins of the other clade. Our results indicate that the binary JAZ3 ZIM–ZBD and especially JAZ3 ZIM–ZIM interface residues and models likely apply to both clade V and clade I proteins (see structural alignments [Supplemental Figure 8] and predicted alignment error plots [Supplemental Figures 6, 7, 13, and 14]), whereas the ternary NINJA–(JAZ3)2 model likely applies only to clade V JAZ proteins. Given the overall similarities of the interactions, the stepwise interface stabilization determined by HDX-MS (Figures 5 and 6) and the residual structural constraints in disordered regions identified by negative-stain EM (Figure 7) likely also apply to other JAZ proteins.


### JAZ dimerization through the ZIM domain is crucial for repressor function
  The physiological role of JAZ homodimerization has been enigmatic. Using Y2H and pull-down assays, we show in this study that a single point mutation, alanine replacement of the TIFY isoleucine, in JAZ10.4 or JAZ3 abolished JAZ dimerization but had a much weaker effect on NINJA binding. Compared with the strong repressive activity of JAZ10.4 on JA responses in planta, JAZ10.4I107A largely lost its repressor function in both fertility and root growth inhibition assays. These findings support and extend our previous studies (Chung and Howe, 2009) showing that JAZ10.4 dimerization is crucial for JAZ10.4 repressor function. JAZ10.4 directly interacts with two proteins that have key functions in JA-mediated root inhibition: NINJA and MYC (Moreno et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2017). The capacity of the JAZ10.4 dimerization mutant to interact with NINJA raises the intriguing possibility that JAZ dimerization may be required for a stronger (higher-avidity) MYC dimer interaction under physiological conditions, in which JAZ and MED25 compete for MYC binding.

  NINJA plays a major role as a repressor of JA signaling in roots (Pauwels et al., 2010; Acosta et al., 2013). We found that mutation of the TIFY phenylalanine in JAZ10.4 strongly disrupted the JAZ10.4–NINJA interaction in vitro. Interestingly, this mutation prevented JAZ10.4-mediated sterility in 35S::JAZ10.4F108A plants but had only a partial negative effect on JA-induced root growth inhibition. These findings suggest that a critical threshold of JAZ–NINJA affinity is required for effective repression of JA responses in vivo, or that, as previously proposed (Acosta et al., 2013), the requirement for NINJA in JAZ-mediated repression differs among tissue types. The remaining repressive activity of JAZ10.4F108A may be attributed to such NINJA-independent mechanisms, including inhibition of MYC through blocking of MED25 access (Zhang et al., 2015) or through interaction with other adaptor proteins or repressors such as ECAP (Li et al., 2020) and polycomb (Li et al., 2021).


### Repressor complex assembly stabilizes subunit interfaces
  The ability to generate a distinct MBP-MYC3–JAZ1–NINJA complex EM density map at low resolution demonstrates the presence of structural constraints in JAZ and NINJA beyond their folded core domains. Although the complex was still too heterogeneous for higher-resolution cryo-EM imaging, the presence of structural elements may reflect the stepwise, increased stabilization around the complex interfaces with addition of each subunit as seen in our binary ΔHDX-MS maps. Partial stabilization of intrinsically disordered proteins upon formation of different complexes is considered a hallmark of intrinsically disordered proteins (Dyson and Wright, 2005).

  In summary, the work presented here led us to propose a detailed structural and mechanistic model for JAZ3–JAZ3 and JAZ3–NINJA interactions. Together with our previous structural work on MYC–JAZ and NINJA EAR–TPL interactions, this represents the first comprehensive view of a transcriptional repression complex in a plant hormone pathway. This structural framework should facilitate further testing and refinement of our models and may inform biotechnological approaches to fine-tune the strength of JA responses for improving crop performance.


## Methods

### Protein complex expression and purification
  Proteins were expressed from Sf9 insect cells using the baculovirus system (ThermoFisher) and constructs described in the supplemental experimental procedures. For purification of the His8-SUMOstar-AtMYC3(44–238)–AtNINJA–AtJAZ5(mutant) complex, cell pellets from 500-ml culture were thawed at room temperature and lysed by Dounce homogenization in 50 ml lysis buffer (50 mM Tris [pH 7.8], 200 mM NaCl, 50 mM imidazole, 10% glycerol, 0.1 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP), and 0.5 Complete Protease Inhibitor tablet [Roche]). The solubilized fraction was isolated by centrifugation at 100 000 g for 45 min, and the supernatant was transferred to a gravity column containing 3 ml Nickel-NTA resin (Qiagen) pre-equilibrated with lysis buffer and incubated for 2 h at 4°C. The resin was washed with 10 column volumes of lysis buffer, followed by 5 column volumes of wash buffer A1 (50 mM Tris [pH 7.8], 500 mM NaCl, 80 mM imidazole, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM TCEP), 5 column volumes of wash buffer A2 (50 mM Tris [pH 7.8], 1 M NaCl, 80 mM imidazole, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM TCEP), and 10 column volumes of wash buffer A3 (50 mM Tris [pH 7.8], 200 mM NaCl, 100 mM imidazole, 10% glycerol, 1 mM TCEP). The complex was eluted with 5 column volumes of elution buffer (50 mM Tris [pH 7.8], 200 mM NaCl, 500 mM imidazole, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM TCEP). The eluted sample was concentrated to 0.5 ml using centrifugal filter units with a molecular weight cutoff of 100 kDa (MilliporeSigma) and loaded onto a Superdex 200 10/300 GL Increase column (GE Healthcare) pre-equilibrated with buffer containing 25 mM HEPES (pH 7.8) and 150 mM NaCl. The peak fractions were collected and concentrated.

  The purification process for the AtMYC3(49–238)–AtNINJA–JAZ1 complex was the same as that for the AtMYC3(44–238)–AtNINJA–AtJAZ5 (mutant) complex, and the peak fractions were collected and concentrated. Then 0.45 ml purified AtMYC3(49–238)–AtNINJA–AtJAZ1 complex at a concentration of 0.28 mg ml−1 was crosslinked with 0.05% glutaraldehyde and incubated on ice for 5 min, and 50 mM Tris (pH 7.8) was added. Then the crosslinked complex sample was concentrated to 0.5 ml and loaded onto a Superdex 200 10/300 GL Increase column (GE Healthcare) pre-equilibrated with buffer containing 25 mM HEPES (pH 7.8) and 150 mM NaCl. The peak fractions of the complex were collected and concentrated for negative-stain transmission EM experiments.


### ColabFold modeling
  ColabFold was developed from AlphaFold2 for accelerated structure prediction of proteins and their homomeric and heteromeric complexes (Mirdita et al., 2022), and it matches AlphaFold2 and AlphaFold Multimer in prediction quality of tested datasets (Mirdita et al., 2022). ColabFold structures were generated through Google ColabFold (https://colab.research.google.com/github/sokrypton/ColabFold/blob/main/AlphaFold2.ipynb) with MSA mode MMseqs2 (UniRef+Environmental), pair mode unpaired+paired, and other default settings.


### Y2H assays
  Y2H assays were performed using the Clontech Matchmaker System (Takara) (Figure 3 and Supplemental Figure 4). Details of Y2H vector construction and interaction assays are provided in the supplemental experimental procedures.


### Immunoblot analysis
  Single colonies growing on SD/−2 were selected and cultured in SD/−2 liquid medium. Cells were collected, resuspended in 0.1 M NaOH, then held on ice for 5 min, pelleted, and resuspended in 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 0.1 mM EDTA. Samples were denatured by boiling for 5 min in SDS–PAGE loading buffer and separated by SDS–PAGE. The proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes, and anti-myc antibody was used for immunoblot analysis.


### Pull-down assay
  In vitro pull-down assays were performed as described previously (Shyu et al., 2012; Moreno et al., 2013). In brief, 25 μg purified JAZ10.4-MBPHis6 fusion protein (WT, I107A, or F108A) was mixed with 600 μg protein extract from yeast cells expressing either JAZ10.4 or NINJA as human influenza hemagglutinin (HA)-tagged derivatives. After incubation at 4°C for 30 min, protein complexes were isolated by nickel affinity chromatography on Ni-NTA resin. Protein complexes were recovered in 30 μl elution buffer, and portions of this eluant were used for immunoblot analysis with anti-HA antibody (Convance) or for analysis on 10% SDS–PAGE gels followed by staining with Coomassie blue.


### Root inhibition assay
  JAZ10.4 expression constructs (supplemental experimental procedures) were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 and subsequently transformed into Col-0 Arabidopsis plants using the floral dip method (Clough and Bent, 1998). Transformed seedlings were screened on agar medium for phosphinothricin (BASTA) resistance followed by monitoring of yellow fluorescent protein (YFP) expression with a fluorescence dissecting microscope (Leica). JA-induced root growth inhibition assays were performed as described previously (Shyu et al., 2012) using homozygous lines.


### HDX detected by MS
  Differential HDX-MS experiments were conducted as described previously (Chalmers et al., 2006) with a few modifications detailed in the supplemental experimental procedures.


### Negative-stain transmission EM grid preparation and data acquisition
  For negative-stain EM grid preparation, a 0.02 mg ml−1 His8-MBP–AtMYC3(49–238)–AtNINJA–AtJAZ1 complex sample was prepared by depositing 3.5 μl on glow-discharged 200 mesh carbon grids (Ted Pella). After 60 s, 5 μl of water was added, the grids were carefully aspirated using the edge of a filter paper, and the procedure was repeated once. The sample was stained with 5 μl of a 2% (w/v) uranyl formate solution for 4 s and aspirated, then stained again with 5 μl of a uranyl formate solution for 60 s and aspirated. Images were recorded with an accelerating voltage of 120 kV using a Tecnai Spirit G2 BioTWIN Transmission Electron Microscope equipped with a Gatan Orius 830 CCD camera.


### EM data processing
  A total of 158 negative-stain EM images were processed using cryoSPARC v3.3.1 (Punjani et al., 2017). A total of 29 618 particles were used for ab initio 3D reconstitution. The volume map of about 10.4 Å3 from 3991 particles showed features consistent with the MBP and MYC3 crystal structure models and the JAZ1 and NINJA I-TASSER models. The MBP, MYC3, JAZ1, and NINJA models were docked in the map in UCSF Chimera (Pettersen et al., 2004), and rigid body refinement was performed using Phenix software (Liebschner et al., 2019).


### AlphaScreen assay
  The assay was performed as described previously (Zhang et al., 2015). The principle of the assay, experimental details, and interpretations are provided in the supplemental experimental procedures.
