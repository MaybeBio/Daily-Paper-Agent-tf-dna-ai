# Proanthocyanidin B2 derived metabolites may be ligands for bile acid receptors S1PR2, PXR and CAR: an in silico approach


## Abstract
  Bile acids (BAs) act as signaling molecules via their interactions with various nuclear (FXR, VDR, PXR and CAR) and G-protein coupled (TGR5, M3R, S1PR2) BA receptors. Stimulation of these BA receptors influences several processes, including inflammatory responses and glucose and xenobiotic metabolism. BA profiles and BA receptor activity are deregulated in cardiometabolic diseases; however, dietary polyphenols were shown to alter BA profile and signaling in association with improved metabolic phenotypes. We previously reported that supplementing mice with a proanthocyanidin (PAC)-rich grape polyphenol (GP) extract attenuated symptoms of glucose intolerance in association with changes to BA profiles, BA receptor gene expression, and/or downstream markers of BA receptor activity. Exact mechanisms by which polyphenols modulate BA signaling are not known, but some hypotheses include modulation of the BA profile via changes to gut bacteria, or alteration of ligand-availability via BA sequestration. Herein, we used an in silico approach to investigate putative binding affinities of proanthocyanidin B2 (PACB2) and PACB2 metabolites to nuclear and G-protein coupled BA receptors. Molecular docking and dynamics simulations revealed that certain PACB2 metabolites had stable binding affinities to S1PR2, PXR and CAR, comparable to that of known natural and synthetic BA ligands. These findings suggest PACB2 metabolites may be novel ligands of S1PR2, CAR, and PXR receptors.


## Introduction
  Primary bile acids (PBAs) required for digesting and solubilizing lipophilic compounds are synthesized in the liver from cholesterol and further biotransformed by the gut microbiota to secondary BAs (SBAs) (Ridlon et al., 2006; Wan & Sheng, 2018; Zheng et al., 2021). Both PBAs and SBAs have been implicated in regulation of cardiometabolic health and altered profiles have been associated with diabetes (T2D), non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), and cardiovascular diseases (CVD) (Callender et al., 2022; Chávez-Talavera et al., 2019; Gottlieb & Canbay, 2019; Kuipers et al., 2007; Lefebvre et al., 2009; T. Li & Chiang, 2014; Thomas, Pellicciari, et al., 2008).

  BAs can regulate metabolism as signaling molecules for G-protein coupled receptors, including Takeda G-protein coupled receptor-5 (TGR5), muscarinic acetylcholine receptor 3 (M3R), and sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor 2 (S1PR2), as well as the nuclear receptors farnesoid X receptor (FXR), vitamin D receptor (VDR), pregnane X receptor (PXR), and constitutive androstane receptor (CAR) (Schaap et al., 2014). TGR5, FXR, PXR, and CAR are canonically classified as orphan receptors (Lehmann et al., 1998; Maloney et al., 2000; Pascussi et al., 2003; Pols et al., 2011) and M3R, S1PR2, and VDR are receptors for many non-BA ligands. BA interactions with TGR5 and FXR are known to activate or inhibit pathways regulating BA, cholesterol, lipid, and carbohydrate metabolism (Chiang et al., 2017; Claudel et al., 2005; Keitel et al., 2007; Thomas, Auwerx, et al., 2008; Thomas, Pellicciari, et al., 2008; Watanabe et al., 2006). Both FXR and TGR5 have been targeted to alleviate symptoms in murine models of colitis, nonalcoholic steatohepatitis, obesity, and type-2 diabetes (Guo et al., 2016; Shi et al., 2020; Vavassori et al., 2009). The activity and downstream effects of other BA receptors, M3R, S1PR2, VDR, PXR, and CAR is less understood. Emerging data suggests that M3R, S1PR2, VDR, PXR, and CAR signaling pathways can regulate inflammatory and immune responses (Fiorucci et al., 2018; McMillin et al., 2017), xenobiotic processing (Wagner et al., 2005), and cellular proliferation (Cheng & Raufman, 2005). Activation of M3R and S1PR was shown to induce cell proliferation in the intestine and liver (T. Chen et al., 2017; Cheng & Raufman, 2005; Y. Wang et al., 2017) suggesting they are protective against cell damage from injury associated with colitis or cholestasis (Nagahashi et al., 2015; Y. Wang et al., 2017). VDR is known for its receptor activity for vitamin D3 to regulate bone metabolism and intestinal absorption of calcium and phosphorus (Jurutka et al., 2007). BAs have also been found to act as VDR ligands (Makishima et al., 2002), to regulate their detoxification and synthesis via Fgf15 (T. Li & Chiang, 2013; Makishima et al., 2002). In addition to detoxification of endotoxins and xenotoxins, PXR and CAR are regulators of intestinal and hepatic BA transporters (Gao & Xie, 2012; Wagner et al., 2005).

  Polyphenols are widely distributed defense compounds occurring in a variety of plant foods (i.e., fruits, vegetables, nuts, seeds, grains, herbs, spices) with variable bioavailability depending on structure, size, and food matrix (Manach et al., 2004; Serra et al., 2010). Bacteria along the gastrointestinal tract can metabolize dietary polyphenols into smaller phenolic acids with greater bioavailability (Marín et al., 2015). Absorbed polyphenols and metabolites may be methylated, sulfated, or glucuronidated by the liver and are found at low concentrations in circulation in their derivative forms or unchanged (Williamson & Clifford, 2017).

  Consumption of dietary polyphenols was shown to alter the gut microbiota and BA profiles of mice in association with improved glucose metabolism, increased energy expenditure, and reduced fatty liver (Anhê et al., 2019; D. Li et al., 2021; Tveter et al., 2020). Polyphenol supplementation was reported to affect PBA synthesis through cytochrome P450 family 7 subfamily A member 1 (CYP7A1) (Anhê et al., 2019; D. Li et al., 2021; Tveter et al., 2020), whose activity can be controlled by TGR5, S1PR2, FXR, PXR, and CAR (Chambers et al., 2019; T. Li & Chiang, 2005; Liao et al., 2019). Metabolic improvements from grape and apple polyphenols were hypothesized to be due to reduced FXR activation (D. Li et al., 2021), possibly due to reduced abundance of bacteria producing SBAs agonistic to FXR (Tveter et al., 2020). Alternatively, camu camu-associated metabolic improvements were proposed to be due to microbial promotion of a BA profile that activated Tgr5 in white adipose tissue (Anhê et al., 2019). It has been proposed that dietary polyphenols alter BA signaling by altering the BA profile, but another possibility is that polyphenols are direct ligands to BA receptors. A recent study showed that Kaempferol-7-O-rhamnoside, a common dietary polyphenol, exhibited high affinity for FXR using in silico and in vitro approaches (K. Liu et al., 2022). Other studies have demonstrated that different polyphenols can influence gene expressions of BA receptors in vitro as well in vivo (Avior et al., 2013; Bartik et al., 2010; S. Chen et al., 2021; Lacroix et al., 2018; Pustylnyak et al., 2020; Y. Zhang et al., 2015; Zhao et al., 2019).

  We hypothesized that polyphenols and their metabolites may be putative ligands for BA receptors and this direct modulation of BA signaling may contribute to polyphenol-induced metabolic improvements. Herein we utilized an in silico approach to determine the binding affinities and molecular dynamics between BA receptors (FXR, TGR5, PXR, CAR, S1PR2, M3R, and VDR) and PACB2 as well as 35 conjugated and unconjugated metabolites of PACB2 that were previously identified in mice (Xiao et al., 2017). Putative polyphenol interactions were also compared to binding energies of 32 BA species and 4 synthetic antagonists.


## Material and Methods

### Ligand preparation
  The PACB2 metabolites used in this study were previously detected in the circulation and small intestine of mice orally administered PACB2 (800 mg/kg) for 3 days (Xiao et al., 2017). MarvinSketch (version 21.16, ChemAxon) was used to create 3D structures of PACB2 metabolites, which were saved as .sdf files. Babel.exe algorithm (version 1.5.7, MGLTools, the Scripps Research Institute) was used to convert .sdf files into .pdbqt files for docking studies with MMFF94 molecular optimization. PACB2 and 35 PACB2 metabolites used as ligands were numbered as L1 through L36 (Supplementary Figure 1). PACB2 metabolites are indicated as ligands with their common and IUPAC names (generated from MarvinSketch) in Supplementary Table 1, respectively. For comparison, a panel of 32 BAs were used for docking experiments. At the time of the study there were no known BA antagonists for TGR5, S1PR2, PXR, and CAR, therefore 4 synthetic non-BA antagonists for these receptors (SBI115, JTE013, SPA80, and CINPA1, respectively) were used for docking experiments. BA structures and 4 synthetic antagonists were retrieved from PubChem (pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) and are presented with their common names and abbreviations in the Supplementary Table 2.


### Protein Structure Preparation
  The 3D structures of BA receptors were collected from the Research Collaboratory for Structural Bioinformatics Protein Data Bank (RCSB PDB). The artificial-intelligence-assisted homology model of S1PR2, collected from the AlphaFold Protein Structure BIOVA Discovery Studio 2021 Client (Dassault Systems), was used to predict the binding cavity of S1PR2. The protein identifications are presented in Supplementary Table 3.


### Molecular Docking
  Prior to molecular docking, PyMol was used to remove existing ligands and water molecules from the 3D BA receptor structures. AutoDockTools was used to add polar charges, hydrogen ions, and Kollman charges to the BA receptors, as well as to determine the grid box coordinates (Supplementary Table 3) that encapsulate the binding sites of the ligands. AutoDock Vina (version 1.1.2, the Scripps Research Institute) (Trott & Olson, 2010) was used to perform the molecular docking between BA receptors and BA as well as PACB2 metabolite ligands. The exhaustiveness was set to 100 for enhanced accuracy. LigPlot+v.2.2 (EMBL-EBI) was used to analyze docking interactions. Based on docking results, PACB2 metabolites with the strongest binding affinities were compared to reported binding affinities of synthetic and natural BAs to the BA receptors


### Molecular Dynamics Simulations
  YASARA Dynamics (Version 21.8.27) (Land & Humble, 2018) was used for molecular dynamics simulations with previously reported optimization process, solvent properties, and configurations (Hoang, 2023). The script md_run.mcr was applied towards the simulations of PXR and CAR, producing 100-nanosecond dynamics simulations with 501 snapshots. Since S1PR2 is a GPCR, the script md_runmembrane.mcr was applied towards the simulations of S1PR2, producing 100-nano second dynamics simulations with 501 snapshots with the protein being attached to a cellular membrane. In addition, md_analyzebindingenergy.mcr script was employed to calculate the binding energy derived from molecular mechanics combined with Poisson-Boltzmann surface area (MM/PBSA) for every 4 nanoseconds of the simulations from 0th ns to 100th ns with the corresponding AMBER14 forcefield and temperature of 298K. MM/PBSA binding energy is calculated with the following premise/equation:  BindingEnergy(BE)=-PotentialEnergyoftheReceptor(EpotReceptor)-SolvationEnergyoftheReceptor(EsolvReceptor)-PotentialEnergyoftheLigand(EpotLigand)-SolvationEnergyoftheLigand(EsolvLigand)+PotentialEnergyoftheComplex(EpotComplex)+SolvationEnergyoftheComplex(kJ/mol)

  The more negative binding energies indicate better binding due to lower energy cost, while positive energies suggest weaker binding. Notably, for S1PR2 complexes, the reported MM/PBSA calculations only account for energies from 80ns to 100ns due to the introduction of the bilayer membranes in the simulations of S1PR2 complexes, which could confer significant instability in the early phases of the simulations.


## Results

### PACB2 metabolites are putative ligands for S1PR2, PXR, and CAR.
  PACB2 and 35 PACB2 metabolites were tested as ligands for TGR5, M3R, S1PR2, FXR, VDR, PXR, and CAR. Names of test ligands were abbreviated L1 - L36 (Supplementary Figure 1, Supplementary Table 1). Ligands included compounds with a PACB2, epicatechin, or phenolic backbone conjugated to a sulfate, methyl, or glucuronide moiety. Phenolic metabolites are products of gut bacterial metabolism and were considered due to their higher bioavailability compared to parent compounds (Marín et al., 2015). The binding affinities of BA receptors with PACB2 metabolites and BA ligands are summarized in Figure 1. None of the tested PACB2 metabolites had stronger binding affinities to TGR5, M3R, FXR, and VDR than known agonist or antagonist ligands (Figure 1A). The three polyphenols with the strongest binding affinity (lowest ΔG) for BA receptors are presented along with the binding affinity for known BA or synthetic ligands (Table 1). Notably, several polyphenol metabolites interacted with S1PR2, PXR, or CAR receptors with higher binding affinities than known agonist or antagonist ligands (Table 1, Figure 1A). The three PACB2-derived metabolites with the strongest binding affinities for S1PR2, PXR, and CAR were considered further as putative ligands for BA receptors. Identified putative polyphenol ligands for S1PR2 (L13, L16, L20), PXR (L2, L6, L15), and CAR (L18, L20, L21) were previously detected in murine plasma, intestine, heart, kidney, spleen, and/or lung (Xiao et al., 2017) and may therefore be bioactive across several organs (Figure 2A–C).

  S1PR2 showed preferential binding affinities for both PACB2-backbone and (-)-epicatechin backbone metabolites (Figure 1A, S1PR2 column), PXR showed preferential binding affinities for only PACB2-backbone (Figure 1A, PXR column), and CAR showed preferential binding affinities for only (-)-epicatechin backbone (Figure 1A, CAR column). Phenolic metabolites exhibited weak binding affinities for all BA receptors (Figure 1A, rows L22-L36). Based on docking results, BA species had the strongest affinity for FXR when compared to other BA-BA receptor interactions (Figure 1B). In contrast, BAs exhibited weak binding affinities towards CAR (Figure 1B, CAR column). Additionally, some ligands with PACB2 backbone had stronger binding affinities to PXR than several of the tested BA (Figure 1, PXR column).

  From docking results, L13 and L16 (ΔG of −9.5 and −9.3 kcal/mol, respectively) showed stronger binding affinities to S1PR2 than GDCA, a natural BA agonist, and the synthetic antagonist, JTE013 (with ΔG of −9.1 kcal/mol each) while L20 showed less affinity (ΔG of −9.0 kcal/mol; Table 1). L2, L6, and L15 (ΔG of −10.7, −10.6 and −10.6 kcal/mol, respectively) exhibited stronger binding affinities towards PXR than the BA ligand LCA and synthetic antagonist SPA70 (with ΔGs of −9.6 kcal/mol each; Table 1). L18, L20, and L21 (ΔG of −9.6, −9.9 and −9.9 kcal/mol, respectively) exhibited stronger binding affinities towards CAR when compared to LCA and a synthetic antagonist, CINPA1 (with ΔG of −8.8 and −8.4 kcal/mol, respectively; Table 1). Although PACB2 metabolites do not exhibit stronger binding energies than established BA ligands for FXR, TGR5, M3R, and VDR, there may be noteworthy interactions between PACB2 metabolites and these receptors that are not investigated in this study.

  Analysis of interactions showed that GDCA, JTE013, L13, L16 and L20 occupied a similar binding pocket at S1PR2 (Figure 3A). GDCA formed 7 hydrogen bonds with residues Lys22, Ser37, Thr90, Ser93, Val182, and His271 and hydrophobic interactions with 9 residues (Arg33, Asn89, Glu109, Gly94, Leu267, Tyr268, Lys269, Ala270, Phe274; Figure 3B). JTE013 formed a hydrogen bond with residue Glu109 and hydrophobic interactions with 9 residues (Phe86, Asn89, Ser93, Glu109, Ala112, Phe113, Trp246, His271, and Phe274; Figure 3C). L13 formed 4 hydrogen bonds with residues Lys22, Glu26, Arg33, and His271 and hydrophobic interactions with 16 residues (Tyr18, Leu25, Ile40, Ser83, Phe86, Asn89, Thr90, Ser93, Arg108, Glu109, Val182, Leu183, Leu267, Tyr268, Lys269, and Ala270; Figure 3D). L16 formed 6 hydrogen bonds with residues Arg33, Asn89, Arg108, Leu267, His271 and hydrophobic interactions with 11 residues (Lys22, Leu25, Ser37, Ile40, Phe86, Thr90, Gly94, Thr97, Val182, Tyr268, and Ala270; Figure 3E). L20 formed 5 hydrogen bonds with Asn89, Thr97, Arg108, and Thr278 and hydrophobic interactions with 13 residues (Tyr18, Lys22, Leu25, Ile40, Phe86, Ser93, Gly94, Glu109, Val182, Leu183, His271, Phe274, and Ala275; Figure 3F). Despite differences or similarities in occupied residues in silico, further machine-learning-assisted analysis on atomic level would be needed to predict functions of putative ligands (Plante et al., 2019).

  Analysis of interactions showed that LCA, SPA70, L2, L6, L15 occupied a similar binding pocket at PXR (Figure 4A). LCA formed 2 hydrogen bonds with residues, His407 and Lys210, and hydrophobic interactions with 10 residues (Leu206, Leu209, Val211, Leu239, Leu240, Met243, Phe281, Leu411, Ile414, and Phe420; Figure 4B). SPA701 formed a hydrogen bond with residue Ser247 and hydrophobic interactions with 12 residues (Leu206, Leu209, Leu240, Met246, Phe281, Gln285, Phe288, Trp299, Tyr306, His407, Arg410, and Ile414; Figure 4C). L2 formed 3 hydrogen bonds with residues His407, Met243, and Gln285 and hydrophobic interactions with 13 residues (Leu209, Ala244, Met246, Ser247, Phe281, Phe288, Trp299, Tyr306, Met323, Leu324, Thr408, Leu411, and Phe429; Figure 4D). Similarly, L6 formed 2 hydrogen bonds with residues Met243 and Gln285, and hydrophobic interactions with 14 residues (Leu209, Ala244, Met246, Ser247, Phe281, Phe288, Trp299, Tyr306, Met323, Leu324, His407, Thr408, Leu411, and Phe429; Figure 4E). L15 formed 4 hydrogen bonds with residues Met243, His407, Gln285, and Ser247 and hydrophobic interactions with 16 residues (Leu206, Leu209, Leu240, Ala244, Met246, Phe281, Cys284, Phe288, Trp299, Tyr306, Leu324, Thr408, Leu411, Ile414, Met425, and Phe429; Figure 4F).

  Analysis of interactions showed that LCA, CINPA1, L18, L20, L21 occupied a similar binding pocket at CAR (Figure 5A). LCA formed a hydrogen bond with residue Lys235 and hydrophobic interactions with 13 residues (Phe171, Asn175, Leu216, Phe227, Tyr234, Phe244, Phe248, Leu249, Ile252, Tyr336, Glu339, Leu340, and Leu346; Figure 5B). CINPA1 formed hydrophobic interactions with 19 residues (Phe142, Phe171, Ala172, Ile174, Asn175, Met178, Leu216, Phe227, Tyr234, Lys235, Ala239, Phe244, Phe248, Ile252, Tyr336, Leu340, Leu346, Thr350, and Leu353; Figure 5C). L18 formed 4 hydrogen bonds with residues Asn175, Leu249, and Tyr336 and hydrophobic interactions with 9 residues (Phe171, Phe227, Met236, Ala239, Phe244, Phe248, Ile252, Leu340, and Leu353; Figure 5D). Similarly, L20 formed 4 hydrogen bonds with residues Asn175, Leu249, Tyr336, and hydrophobic interactions with 10 residues (Phe171, Ala172, Phe227, Met236, Ala239, Phe244, Phe248, Ile252, Leu340, and Leu353; Figure 5E). L21 formed a hydrogen bond with residue Lys235, and hydrophobic interactions with 10 residues (Phe171, Asn175, Phe227, Tyr234, Met236, Ala239, Phe244, Phe248, Tyr336, and Leu346; Figure 5F).


### Molecular Dynamics Simulations reveal PACB2 metabolites interactions with S1PR2, PXR, and CAR are stable.
  The mean RMSD values obtained from the analysis of the movement of the S1PR2 Cα-backbones (i.e., carbons that connect carboxyl, amino groups, and side chains) for S1PR2-GDCA, S1PR2-JTE013, S1PR2-L13, S1PR2-L16, and S1PR2-L20 complexes were 2.0 ± 0.24, 1.77 ± 0.19, 1.88 ± 0.20, 1.76 ± 0.19, and 2.1 ± 0.19 Å, respectively (Figure 6A). Analysis of ligand movement throughout 100 ns simulations with S1PR2 showed that L13 (3.1 ± 0.72 Å) and L16 (2.3 ± 0.52 Å) were more stable in their initial binding conformation compared to GDCA (5.3 ± 0.40 Å) and JTE013 (3.8 ± 0.41 Å) (Figure 6B). Analysis of ligand movement showed that L20 moved the most from its initial binding site (6.1 ± 0.93 Å) however, starting from 10 ns, L20 returned closer to its original docking site (Figure 6B). MM/PBSA binding energy calculations validated these findings with comparable mean values (due to substantial standard deviations): BES1PR2-GDCA = −97.91 ± 56.87 kJ/mol; BES1PR2-TE013 = −72.98 ± 44.00 kJ/mol; BES1PR2-L13 = −30.31 ± 32.99 kJ/mol; BES1PR2-L16 = −55.18 ± 52.88 kJ/mol; BES1PR2-L20 = −67.9 ± 39.52 kJ/mol (Table 2).

  Analysis of the PXR Cα-backbones demonstrated that their ligand-protein complexes were comparable in term of stability, as the mean RMSD values of PXR-LCA, PXR-SPA70, PXR-L2, PXR-L6, PXR-L15 complexes were 3.1 ± 0.20, 3.7 ± 0.42, 3.3 ± 0.26, 2.74 ± 0.23, and 3.0 ± 2.3 Å, respectively (Figure 6C). Analysis of ligand movement throughout 100 ns simulations at PXR showed that L2, L6, and L15 possessed comparable stabilities to those of LCA and SPA70 (Figure 6D). Particularly, L2 and L15 reached stability sooner at the 7th ns with little displacement (1.6 ± 0.38 Å and 1.6 ± 0.24 Å, respectively, Figure 6D). Meanwhile, LCA, SPA70, and L6 reached stability at approximately the 30th, 32nd, 40th ns and were displaced similarly with 2.4 ± 0.56 Å, 3.2 ± 0.40 Å, ± 2.3 ± 0.80 Å, respectively. MM/PBSA binding energy calculations validate these findings with comparable mean values (due to substantial standard deviations): BEPXR-LCA = −167.6 ± 67 kJ/mol; BEPXR-SPA70 = 131.1 ± 68.4 kJ/mol; BEPXR-L2 =103.8 ± 84.7 kJ/mol; BEPXR-L6 = 103.4 ± 105.6 kJ/mol; BEPXR-L20 = 77.4 ± 88.3 kJ/mol (Table 2).

  Assessment of the CAR Cα-backbones demonstrated the ligand-protein complexes exhibited comparable stability, with RMSD mean values of CAR-LCA, CAR-CINPA1, CAR-L18, CAR-L20, CAR-L21 complexes of 1.5 ± 0.11, 1.8 ± 0.26, 1.88 ± 0.22, 1.69 ± 0.27, and 1.5 ± 0.18 Å, respectively (Figure 6E). Analysis of ligand movement throughout 100 ns simulations at CAR showed that L18, L20, L21 possessed comparable stabilities to those of LCA and CINPA1 (Figure 6F). Particularly, LCA, L18, and L21 all reached stability before the 10th ns with comparable displacements of 2.2 ± 0.28 Å, 2.6 ± 0.41 Å, and 1.8 ± 0.27 Å, respectively (Figure 6F). CINPA1 did not reach stability until the 19th ns with a displacement of 3.3 ± 0.89 Å, whereas L20 reached stability immediately with a comparable displacement of 3.4 ± 0.17 Å (Figure 6F). MM/PBSA binding energy calculations validate these findings with comparable mean values (due to substantial standard deviations): BECAR-LCA = −203 ± 75.3 kJ/mol; BECAR-SPA70 = −195.7 ± 81.9 kJ/mol; BECAR-L18 = −178.1 ± 52.8 kJ/mol; BECAR-L20 = −154.5 ± 81.7 kJ/mol; BECAR-L21 = −169 ± 59.4 kJ/mol (Table 2).


## Discussion
  Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation results suggested that PACB2 and derived metabolites may influence BA signaling through direct interactions with S1PR2, PXR, and CAR. S1PR2 exhibited preferential affinity to metabolites with (-)-epicatechin- and PACB2-backbones, PXR showed preferential binding affinity to PACB2-backbone metabolites, and CAR towards PACB2 metabolites with epicatechin-backbones. These data suggest that metabolism of PACB2 generates a diverse pool of PACB2 metabolite ligands that may modulate S1PR2, CAR, and PXR signaling. It should be noted that these PACB2 metabolites may be moderate modulators of these receptors, due to the MM/PBSA binding energy calculation which suggested slightly weaker binding affinities compared to known BA and synthetic antagonist ligands.

  Proanthocyanidin compounds undergo chemical modification by host and bacterial enzymes to yield an extensive array of metabolites. The novelty of the present investigation resides in consideration of polyphenol metabolites rather than parent compounds, which are limited in circulation. This aspect is of particular significance, as slight variations in structurally similar compounds can result in different receptor responses. In a luciferase assay with a CYP2B6 reporter gene, (-)-epicatechin was identified as a non-binder and a weak agonist of human and mouse CARs, respectively (Yao et al., 2010). In contrast, (-)-epicatechin gallate, which possesses an additional gallate moiety at the 3-position of (-)-epicatechin, elicited more potent responses from both human and mouse CARs (Yao et al., 2010). The flavone morin was demonstrated to be an agonist of PXR in a study utilizing a luciferase assay with a CYP3A4 reporter gene (Y.-H. Liu et al., 2011) while the flavone apigenin, which lacks hydroxyl groups at the 3- and 3’-positions, exhibited weak binding affinity toward PXR in a time-resolved fluorescence resonance energy transfer PXR competitive binding assay (Dong et al., 2010). To the best of our knowledge, this investigation represents one of the few studies establishing a link between polyphenolic compounds and S1PR2. S1PR2 ligands can encompass a wide variety of moieties or functional groups (D. Luo et al., 2022; Z. Luo et al., 2018, 2019).

  Dietary polyphenols are xenobiotics (Z. Liu & Hu, 2007; Vidjaya Letchoumy et al., 2008), which is consistent with PACB2 and PACB2 metabolites being putative ligands for xenobiotic regulators such as PXR and CAR. PACB2 metabolite interactions with PXR and/or CAR may influence xenobiotic sensing and metabolism pathways, such as hydroxylation, oxidation, or conjugation of toxic compounds to promote their excretion through upregulating ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters or solute carriers (Y.-M. Wang et al., 2012). In addition, emerging preclinical and clinical data suggest PXR and CAR are regulators of glucose and lipid metabolism and may be important therapeutic targets for metabolic diseases such as type 2 diabetes, obesity, and atherosclerosis (Gao & Xie, 2012; Wagner et al., 2005). Activation of hepatic CAR suppressed lipogenesis through its inhibitory effect on SREBP1 and LXR as well as gluconeogenesis through its inhibitory effect on FoxO1, HNF4α, and PGC1α (Gao & Xie, 2012). PXR activation suppressed gluconeogenesis and inhibited inflammatory responses of the intestine and immune cells through decreased expression of NF-kB and TNFα (Gao & Xie, 2012). Agonists of PXR in clinical trials have demonstrated adverse interactions where acute heart transplant rejection or loss of anti-HIV drug was observed (Mani et al., 2013). CINPA1, a potent CAR antagonist, attenuated multidrug resistance in cancers in vivo (Cherian et al., 2015).

  Previous studies have shown that conjugated BA might be beneficial for metabolic health through both activation and inhibition of S1PR2 in different tissue compartments. In murine studies, inhibition at S1PR2 by JTE-013 reverted the effect of agonism by the bile acid TCA, thus S1PR2 inhibition is investigated as a strategy to alleviate liver injury (Y. Wang et al., 2017). In a model of high glucose-induced dysfunction of human glomerular endothelial cells, JTE-013, a potent S1PR2 antagonist, decreased mitochondrial fragmentation, generation of reactive oxygen species, and calcium overload while increasing ATP production, indicating that JTE-013 reverted the effects of high glucose concentration (W. Chen et al., 2019). In contrast, indirectly increasing the level of gastrocnemius muscle sphingosphine-1-phosphate (an endogenous agonist of S1PR2) via inhibition of sphingosine phosphate lyase was demonstrated to attenuate muscle dystrophy in mdx murine model through a S1PR2/STAT3 signaling pathway (Loh et al., 2012).

  This in silico study provides preliminary evidence for that PACB2 metabolites are ligands for S1PR2, PXR, and CAR. To our knowledge, this is the first investigation of dietary polyphenols as putative ligands to multiple BA receptors. Comparison to known agonists and antagonists in this study supported the potential of the tested polyphenols as candidate ligands due to comparable binding stability or energies. While this work suggests PACB2 metabolites have pharmacological activity at S1PR2, PXR, and CAR receptors, Biochemical binding experiments with purified ligands and receptors or transcription reporter assays are needed to validate findings and investigate antagonistic or agonistic activity. Further work using relevant in vivo models could be conducted to confirm tissue-specific inhibition or activation of receptors to further delineate receptor functions. Promising candidate therapeutic compounds should undergo in vivo pharmacological characterization (i.e., ADMET). Deeper knowledge of polyphenol metabolite interactions with S1PR2, PXR, and CAR may allow better guidance of dietary approaches for maintaining metabolic health or development of novel pharmacological agents.
