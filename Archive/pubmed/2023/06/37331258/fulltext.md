# Exploring the interactions of antihistamine with retinoic acid receptor beta (RARB) by molecular dynamics simulations and genome-wide meta-analysis


## Abstract
  Kaposi sarcoma (KS) is one of the most common AIDS-related malignant neoplasms, which can leave lesions on the skin among HIV patients. These lesions can be treated with 9-cis-retinoic acid (9-cis-RA), an endogenous ligand of retinoic acid receptors that has been FDA-approved for treatment of KS. However, topical application of 9-cis-RA can induce several unpleasant side effects, like headache, hyperlipidemia, and nausea. Hence, alternative therapeutics with less side effects are desirable. There are case reports associating over-the-counter antihistamine usage with regression of KS. Antihistamines competitively bind to H1 receptor and block the action of histamine, best known for being released in response to allergens. Furthermore, there are already dozens of antihistamines that are FDA-approved with less side effects than 9-cis-RA. This led our team to conduct a series of in-silico assays to determine whether antihistamines can activate retinoic acid receptors. First, we utilized high-throughput virtual screening and molecular dynamics simulations to model high-affinity interactions between antihistamines and retinoic acid receptor beta (RARβ). We then performed systems genetics analysis to identify a genetic association between H1 receptor itself and molecular pathways involved in KS. Together, these findings advocate for exploration of antihistamines against KS, starting with our two promising hit compounds, bepotastine and hydroxyzine, for experimental validation study in the future.


## Introduction
  Kaposi sarcoma (KS) is one of the most common malignant neoplasms associated with acquired immunodeficiency syndromes (AIDS) (1, 2). KS lesions on the skin serve as a visible sign of HIV infection, hence treatment is desired by HIV-patients even when the extent of lesion is not life-threatening (3). Current treatments, such as radiation therapy, intralesional vinblastine sulfate, and laser ablation, require administration by physicians and may result in scarring, ulceration, or unwanted pigmentation changes (4-8). On the other hand, topical application of retinoic acid and its derivatives have been used successfully to treat cutaneous KS lesions (9, 10). These compounds exert their effects by binding to retinoic acid receptors, which are nuclear receptors that regulate the expression of target genes involved in cell differentiation, proliferation, and apoptosis (11-13). There are six known retinoic acid receptors: α-, β-, and γ-retinoic acid receptors (RARα, RARβ, RARγ), and α-, β-, and γ-retinoic X receptors (RXRα, RXRβ, RXRγ) (14). Of the six retinoic acid receptors, the anti-cancer effects of RARβ agonism and RARγ antagonism are well-documented, where structure-activity relationship (SAR) studies have been established to guide development of new retinoid-derivatives against cancer (15-21). 9-cis-retinoic acid (9-cis-RA) is a pan-agonist for all six types of retinoic acid receptors and has been FDA-approved to treat skin lesions associated with Kaposi sarcoma (14). However, there are reports that topical application of 9-cis-RA can cause several unpleasant side effects, like headache, hyperlipidemia, and nausea (22, 23). Specifically, 9-cis-RA can lead to retinoid-induced dermatitis that can last up to 3 months on patient skin. Hence, this calls for novel therapeutics that are effective against KS but induce less side effects.

  Another class of compounds that has been recently explored in cancer therapy is antihistamine. Antihistamines competitively bind to H1 receptor and block the action of histamine, best known for being released in response to allergens (24). Histamine expression is known to increase in cancer cells and believed to contribute to cancer cell proliferation and angiogenesis (25-28). This led to a retrospective study looking at patients receiving immunotherapy together with any of 40 over-the-counter antihistamine drugs, where researchers found increased survival rates among melanoma patients taking fexofenadine, loratadine, and cetirizine (29). Furthermore, there are case reports of rapid regression of KS among patients taking over-the-counter antihistamines, like cetirizine (30, 31). While the effects of antihistamines are often attributed to H1 receptor antagonism, antihistamines are known to bind to other proteins. For example, propiomazine, an antihistamine used as sedatives to treat insomnia, has been reported to antagonize muscarinic acetylcholine receptor, alpha-1A adrenergic receptor, and dopamine D2 receptor (32-35). Considering the similarities in chemical structures between several antihistamines and 9-cis-RA, it may be possible that antihistamines bind to retinoic acid receptors and exert similar effects to skin cancers as 9-cis-RA (Fig. 1).

  In drug discovery and lead optimization, in-silico assays have proven to be effective tools (36-40). The term “in-silico” often refers to experiments performed on a computer, and this definition includes molecular modeling. Specifically, high-throughput virtual screening (HTVS) allows mass-docking of compounds to analyze their binding modes in different proteins (41-43). In addition, molecular dynamics (MD) simulation considers the flexibility of protein structures based on Newtonian principles and is used to study the protein-ligand interaction collected during the simulation (44-47). Molecular mechanics and generalised Born surface area (MM/GBSA) method can also be used to predict the binding free energy of a compound interacting with macromolecules (48-50). MM/GBSA based on MD simulation can then be used to analyze the stability of a protein-ligand complex and predict the sensitivity of a ligand caused by local changes in protein structures (51). These results may also be utilized for compound optimization.

  Another type of in-silico assay that is becoming more popular in drug discovery is systems genetics, which is an extension of complex trait analysis that examines large sets of genotypes and phenotypes to investigate the genetic basis of disease traits (52-55). The BXD family is an admixture of C57BL/6J and DBA/2J mice genomes, and the family are segregating for roughly 5.5 million sequence variants (52-55). It’s also the biggest and best characterized mouse genetic reference population (56). Over the past few decades, the BXD family has become a valuable resource in translational research because they have been phenotyped so extensively—from messenger RNA (mRNA) to cancer growth (57-59). By visiting Gene Network 2.0 (http://www.genenetwork.org/), researchers can freely access BXD genome databases and conduct genetic meta-analysis to investigate their genes of interest (60).

  In this study, the crystal structures of ligand binding domains (LBD) of different retinoic acid receptors were obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank. We then docked 9-cis-RA to all the retinoic acid receptors and compared their binding modes to published structures to test the fidelity of our docking method. Subsequently, we performed high-throughput virtual screening (HTVS) of 204 antihistamines against retinoic acid receptors to analyze differences in their binding modes. Using the pharmacophore features of RARβ agonists, we filtered potential RARβ agonists from antagonists and performed MD simulations with the top scoring antihistamines to confirm structural changes associated with RARβ agonism. We then conducted genome-wide meta-analysis of our target genes, RARB and H1HR, in mice to explore a possibility that H1HR antagonism itself may be associated with molecular pathways of Kaposi Sarcoma. Molecular docking analysis coupled with MD simulation successfully modeled two FDA-approved antihistamines (bepotastine and hydroxyzine) inside the LBD of RARβ with higher affinity than 9-cis-RA. Structural analysis revealed that conformational changes in the LBD of RARβ are consistent with their published structures in active vs inactive state. Together, these findings advocate for testing antihistamines against KS, starting with our two promising hit compounds, bepotastine and hydroxyzine, for experimental validation studies in the future.


## Materials and Methods

### Preparation of retinoic acid receptors for molecular docking
  The crystal structures of the ligand binding domain (LBD) of wild-type human retinoic acid receptors with the lowest resolution (as of January of 2023) were obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank. RARα (PDB: 3KMR), RARβ (4JYI), RARγ (1FCY), RXRα (7A77), RXRβ (7A78), and RXRγ (7A79) had resolutions of 1.80, 1.90, 1.30, 1.50, 1.72, and 2.05 Å, respectively. After removing any co-crystal structures present in the original PDB files, bond orders and hydrogenation for the complex structures were assigned using Protein Preparation in Maestro. The ionization states within pH 7.0 ± 2.0 were generated using Epik in Maestro (61). Hydrogen bond optimization was performed using PROPKA in Maestro (62). Energy minimization was done with OPLS3e force field setting (63). Loop structures were refined using Loop Refinement feature in Maestro. The SDF files of 204 antihistamines were imported from DrugBanks database into Maestro and were prepared for molecular docking using LigPrep. The ligand ionization states within pH 7.0 ± 2.0 were generated using Epik with OPLS3e force field setting. In terms of stereoisomer generation, chiralities were determined from input 3D structure, generating up to 32 stereoisomers per ligand. In total, LigPrep generated 301 compounds with unique stereochemistry, which equates to roughly 1.5 stereoisomers generated per ligand. Once the LigPrep output files were generated in mae.gz file, high-throughput virtual screening was performed against prepared protein structures using flexible docking in Glide via Maestro. First, receptor grids were generated at the canonical binding site of each retinoic acid receptor using “Receptor Grid Generation” tool in Maestro with box lengths of X, Y, and Z dimensions at 12 Å, 12 Å, and 12 Å. Once receptor grid text files were generated, retinoic acid receptors were prepared for HTVS using “Ligand Docking” tool in Glide. In the setting tab, Glide-XP (extra precision) was selected for precision setting, and flexible docking was selected. For “Bias Sampling of Torsions” setting, “Amides-only” was selected prior to running the HTVS. No intermolecular force constraints were applied. Finally, superposition and RMSD calculations for ligands, proteins, and protein-ligand complexes were performed using “Structure Alignment” tool in Maestro.


### MD simulation
  MD simulations for interaction analysis and binding free energy calculation were performed in triplicates using Desmond (Schrödinger Inc., USA). All systems were set up using “System Setup” tool in Maestro. The protein-ligand complexes were first placed in the orthorhombic box with a buffer distance of 10 Å to create a hydration model using the SCP water model. The following parameters were set: cut-of radius for van der Waals interaction, time step, initial temperature, and pressure of the system set to 9 Å, 2.0 fs, 300 K, and 1.01325 bar, respectively. The sampling interval during the simulation was set to 100 ps. Finally, we performed each MD simulation under the NPT ensemble for 100 nanoseconds.


### Interaction analysis and trajectory clustering for MM/GBSA
  Simulation Interactions Diagram tool in Maestro was used to conduct interaction analysis. Trajectory Frame Clustering function in Maestro was used to estimate the most populated representative structure during each simulation. In MD simulation, free energy calculations generate quantitative calculation of protein-ligand binding energies. The binding energy (ΔGbind) was obtained using Equation [1]:  (1) ΔGbind=GR+L−(GR+GL)  where GR+L represents the RARβ in complex with ligands, while GR and GL represent the optimized free RARβ and optimized free ligand Gibbs energies, respectively (64).

  In the generalised Born surface area (MM/GBSA) approach (48), each free energy term in Equation [1] was generated using Equation [2].  (2) G=Gcoulomb+GvDW+Gcovalent+Gsolv+Gself−contact+GH−bond+Glipo+Gpacking  where Gcoulomb represents coulombic energy, GvDW Van der Waals energy, Gcovalent covalent binding energy, Gsolv Generalised Born electrostatic solvation energy, Gself−contact self-contact energy, GH−bond hydrogen-bonding energy, Glipo lipophilic energy, and Gpacking π-π packing energy.

  The MM/GBSA (Molecular Mechanics/Generalized Born Surface Area) method has the advantage of excluding solvent-solute entropic terms in the free energy calculation. This allows for faster prediction of relative binding free energy magnitudes compared to methods like free energy perturbation (FEP) or FEP-MD. However, the drawback is that the absence of conformational sampling of entropic terms can lead to significant errors, especially if the global minimum conformations of the protein-ligand complex differ greatly from their bioactive conformations in the crystal structures (65). Despite this limitation MM/GBSA is widely employed in in-silico studies. One study reported linear correlation coefficients (r) and Spearman correlation coefficients (rs) of 0.66 and 0.63, respectively, between the predicted binding free energies and experimental values (66). Hence, it is crucial to note that the MM/GBSA binding free energy can be interpreted to compare the relative binding free energy of a protein-ligand complex. However, it may not be reliably used to predict the absolute binding free energy of a protein-ligand complex or its experimental dissociation constant (Kd). The performance of the MM/GBSA algorithm is based on the specificity of the force field and ligand partial charges, the specificity of protein–ligand complex, inner dielectric constant, and the docking pose number based on top scoring (67). The VSGB solvation model (68) and OPLS3e force field were set for the calculation via MM/GBSA tool in Maestro.


### Gene expression profiling studies
  We collected data from BXD mouse mRNA datasets extracted from GENE NETWORK 2.0 web-based server (60). For each dataset, we then performed sample correlation analysis in Gene Network 2.0 to identify up to 1,500 genes, whose expressions are correlated with our target genes, RARB and H1HR (p-value < 0.05), for each database. For example, sample correlation analysis of lung mRNA dataset (HZI Lung M430v2 (Apr08) RMA) produced two separate lists, consisting of 1,282 unique annotated genes correlated with RARβ expression and 1,153 genes correlated with H1HR expression. This was repeated for the rest of the other 21 mRNA datasets (each representing a different organ of the body): Adipose mRNA (EPFL/LISP BXD CD Brown Adipose Affy Mouse Gene 2.0 ST Gene Level (Octl3) RMA), Adrenal gland mRNA (INIA Adrenal Affy MoGene 1.0ST (Jun12) RMA), Amygdala mRNA (INIA Amygdala Cohort Affy MoGene 1.0 ST (Mar11) RMA), Bone femur mRNA (UCLA GSE27483 BXD Bone Femur ILM Mouse WG-6 v1.1 (Jan13) RSN), Brain mRNA (UTHSC Mouse BXD Whole Brain RNA Sequence (Nov12) RPKM Untrimmed), Cartilage mRNA (UCLA BXD and BXH Cartilage v2), Cerebellum mRNA (GE-NIAAA Cerebellum mRNA M430v2 (May05) RMA), Eye mRNA (UTHSC BXD Old Aged Eye RNA-Seq (Apr22) DESeq2 rlog2), Gastrointestinal mRNA (Data Set Group: UTHSC Mouse BXD Gastrointestinal Affy MoGene 1.0 ST Gene and Exon Level (Apr14) RMA), Heart mRNA (NHLBI BXD Young Adult Heart CD RNA-Seq (Nov20) TMP Log2), Hematopoietic cell mRNA (UMCG Stem Cells ILM6v1.1 (Apr09) original), Hippocampus mRNA (Hippocampus Consortium M430v2 (Jun06) PDNN), Hypothalamus mRNA (INIA Hypothalamus Affy MoGene 1.0 ST (Nov10)), Kidney mRNA (Mouse kidney M430v2 Female (Aug06) RMA), Leukocytes mRNA (UWA Illumina PBL (Nov08) RSN), Muscle mRNA (EPFL/LISP BXD CD+HFD Muscle Affy Mouse Gene 1.0 ST (Dec11) RMA), Pituitary gland mRNA (INIA Pituitary Affy MoGene 1.0ST (Jun12) RMA), Retina mRNA (DoD Retina Normal Affy MoGene 2.0 ST (May15) RMA Gene Level), Spleen mRNA (UTHSC Affy MoGene 1.0 ST Spleen (Dec10) RMA), and Thymus mRNA (UWA Illumina Thymus (Nov08) RSN). Note that the mRNA database for skin is not available, hence the need for systems genetics meta-analysis derived from 22 unique mRNA datasets derived from other organs.


### Construction of 3D protein-protein interaction network and functional enrichment analysis
  Network analyst is a web-based server that uses a comprehensive PPI (protein-protein interaction) database obtained from InnateDB database (69). It also contains manually curated interaction data from published literature and experimental data from different PPI databases, including IntAct, MINT, DIP, BIND and BioGRID (70-74). Our gene lists obtained from gene expression profiling studies were pooled and mapped onto the protein interaction network tool in NetworkAnalyst. As the total nodes were >1,000, the network was minimized to “Minimum Network” setting, composed only of the protein nodes, seed nodes, and the edges that interconnect them (75). Visualization and functional analyses were performed. Finally, functional modules from the PPI network were identified, and the ‘Function Explorer’ tool in NetworkAnalyst was utilized to perform functional enrichment analysis based on gene ontology (GO) terms or pathways according to the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) or Reactome databases. The following statistical settings were applied for the functional enrichment analysis: significance threshold of 0.05 for adjusted P value, 0.10 for adjusted false-discovery-rate, at least two genes from the input list in the enriched category, and the whole genome as the reference background.


## Result

### Molecular docking of 9-cis-retinoic acid
  The crystal structures of LBD of retinoic acid receptors were obtained from RCSB Protein Data Bank (Fig. 2) and were prepared for docking using Protein Preparation in Maestro (Schrödinger Inc., USA). Readers who are not familiar with the nuclear receptor binding pocket are referred to Supplemental Video 1 for general viewing of its location. All the protein structures were subjected to energy minimization to relax their 3D conformations from the crystallized state. The relaxed structures were first docked with 9-cis-RA, a known pan-agonist of retinoic acid receptors. Then, the predicted binding modes were compared to their actual binding modes to check the fidelity of our docking method. The following crystal structures were used for comparison: PDB - 5UAN for RARβ-(9-cis-RA) complex, PDB - 3LBD for RARγ-(9-cis-RA) complex, and PDB - 1FBY for RXRα-(9-cis-RA) complex. Note that there are no crystal structures available for RARα, RXRβ, and RXRγ in complex with 9-cis-RA.

  The binding pocket appeared to be conserved across RARs and RXRs with presence of key binding residues consistent with the literature (16, 76-79), specifically the presence of hydrogen bond network between arginine and/or serine residues and the carboxyl functional group of 9-cis-RA (Fig. 3). Superposition studies with root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) analyses were conducted to verify the alignment of the three-dimensional (3D) structures of the retinoic acid receptor subtypes, including their canonical binding site (Supplemental Fig. 1). Despite the all-atom RMSD values being greater than 2.0 Å, which can be attributed to the inherent flexibility of side chain atoms, the backbone RMSD values were less than 2.0 Å. This indicates that the secondary structures of the receptor subtypes are positioned in a similar topology, despite some variations in the positions of side chain atoms (80). When we analyzed their docking scores, they ranged from −8.758 to −10.409 kcal/mol. Furthermore, when we compared the predicted binding mode of 9-cis-RA in RXRα to its solved structure (PDB: 1FBY), they both displayed hydrogen bonding with arginine and alanine residues (ARG 316 and ALA 327), validating the importance of this interaction in retinoic acid receptor activation (77). Similarly, the 3D binding modes of 9-cis-RA with other types of retinoic acid receptors were nearly identical with their published crystal structures (PDB: 5UAN for RARβ, 3LBD for RARγ) (81, 82). The ligand RMSD values between the docked 9-cis-RA and the 9-cis-RA present in the crystal structures were computed. We observed RMSD values of 0.577 Å, 0.733 Å, and 0.399 Å when comparing to 5UAN for RARβ, 3LBD for RARγ, and 1FBY for RXRα, respectively. It is important to note that ligand RMSD values below 2.0 Å are considered desirable when validating molecular docking results (83). Additionally, through multiple sequence alignment studies, we discovered the presence of a shared motif among the binding sites of retinoic acid receptor alpha, beta, and gamma. This motif, observed as ISO-CYS-THR-ARG-TYR or ICTRY motif, includes ARG276, ARG269, and ARG278 for each receptor subtype, respectively. On the other hand, the ICTRY motif was replaced by a different motif observed as PHE-SER-HIS-ARG-SER or FSHRS motif in the binding sites of retinoic acid receptor X alpha, beta, and gamma. These binding sites contain ARG316, ARG387, and ARG317, respectively. While the specific binding residue motif housing the key arginine residue varies across the six models analyzed, it can be concluded that these motifs are conserved within their respective receptor families. The retinoic acid receptor family exhibits the ICTRY motif, while the retinoic acid receptor X family displays the FSHRS motif. Together, the predicted affinities and binding modes suggest that our molecular docking method can predict protein-ligand interaction in retinoic acid receptors with reasonable accuracy.


### High-throughput virtual screening (HTVS) of antihistamines against retinoic acid receptors
  Out of the 204 antihistamines analyzed from the Drug Banks database, none of them showed predicted affinities higher than 9-cis-RA in RARγ, RXRα, RXRβ, and RXRγ. However, when examining their affinities with respect to RARβ, it was found that 20 antihistamines displayed higher predicted affinities than 9-cis-RA, as shown in Table 1. Notably, almost all of these antihistamines maintained the correct stereochemistry consistent with their published structures in the ZINC catalog for FDA-approved drugs. One interesting exception was rupatadine, which had two stereoisomers successfully docked in the binding site of RARβ. The IUPAC names of these stereoisomers are as follows: 13-chloro-2-{1-[(5-methylpyridin-3-yl)methyl]piperidin-4-ylidene}-4-azatricyclo[9.4.0.03.8]pentadeca-1(11),3(8),4,6,12,14-hexaene and [3-(2-chloro-10H-phenothiazin-10-yl)propyl]dimethylamine. Among these two compounds, only the former stereoisomer showed a docking score of −9.721 kcal/mol with respect to RARβ, as presented in Table 1. In contrast, the latter stereoisomer had a docking score of −5.882 kcal/mol. Furthermore, it is worth noting that the rupatadine stereoisomer with the docking score of −9.721 kcal/mol maintained the correct stereochemistry consistent with its published structure in the ZINC catalog for FDA-approved drugs. As a result, this specific structure was chosen for further molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to study its real-time behavior. Meanwhile, in RARα, there were two antihistamines with higher predicted affinities than 9-cis-RA. In the context of this study, we are focusing exclusively on discovering antihistamines that may interact with RARβ because the anti-neoplastic action of RARβ agonism has been documented more extensively than RARα (15-19). Furthermore, propiomazine and acepromazine, which displayed high predicted affinities in both RARα and RARβ, were excluded from subsequent modeling studies to prevent potential cross-reactivities between the two receptors.

  Using the ePharmacophores tool in Maestro, the following structural motifs associated with 9-cis-RA in complex with RARβ were first identified: capacity to form hydrogen bonds with ARG269 and/or SER280, capacity to form hydrophobic forces with PHE295, and absence of steric clash with helix 12. These protein-ligand interactions are present in known experimental agonists of RARβ, like alitretinoin, BMS453, UVI2007, and arotinoid acid (TTNPB) (15-19). Subsequently, the binding modes of antihistamines with higher predicted affinities than 9-cis-RA were compared to the pharmacophore features of RARβ agonism (Table 2). Specifically, loratadine had an ester moiety capable of forming hydrogen bonds with ARG269 and SER280 as well as aromatic structures near PHE295 for possible π-π stacking exchange (Fig. 4). Residues of helix 12, specifically ILE403 and MET407, were seen far from the ligand, indicating absence of steric clash with helix 12. Similar analysis was done with the rest of the antihistamines. Of the 20 antihistamines, there were four compounds that shared structural motifs associated with RARβ agonism (loratadine, bepotastine, dimetotiazine, and hydroxyzine) and subsequently subjected to MD simulations.


### MD simulation for interaction analysis
  To analyze additional interactions, we conducted MD simulations using docked complexes with loratadine, bepotastine, dimetotiazine, and hydroxyzine in parallel with 9-cis-RA as agonist control. Then, protein-ligand conformations with the highest population were extracted for binding free energy calculation and structural comparisons using Desmond (Schrödinger Inc., USA). The video analyses of MD simulations for 9-cis-RA-RARβ, loratadine- RARβ, bepotastine-RARβ, dimetotiazine-RARβ, and hydroxyzine-RARβ complexes can be found in Supplemental Video 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5, respectively. The root mean square distance (RMSD) analyses of RARβ in complex with our ligands were performed, where lower the RMSD indicates how stable the protein-ligand complex is in the applied system (84) (Fig. 5). The following ligand RMSD values were obtained: 9-cis-RA (1.5 Å), loratadine (1.3 Å), bepotastine (1.4 Å), dimetotiazine (2.2 Å), and hydroxyzine (1.7 Å). All the ligands, except dimetotiazine, equilibrated to ligand RMSD values lower than the protein RMSD. On the other hand, with the exception of bepotastine-RARβ complex theprotein RMSD equilibrated to values ranging from 1.5 to 2.1 Å, which implies the protein assumed a stable conformation towards the end of the simulation. Curiously, although bepotastine protein RMSD appears to be fluctuating with greater extent than other protein-ligand complexes, its ligand RMSD remains equilibrated for most of the MD simulation at a value even lower than that of 9-cis-RA (bepotastine: 1.4 Å VS 9-cis-RA: 1.5 Å). To analyze amino acid side chain stability, root mean square fluctuations (RMSF) analysis was performed, where higher the RMSF values indicates higher fluctuation of amino acid side chain. In RMSF analysis of 9-cis-RA-RARβ complex, none of the amino acid side chains exceeded RMSF value of 4.0 Å. Similar pattern was observed in the RMSF analysis of loratadine-RARβ complex. In fact, the RMSF plots of RARβ-9-cis-RA and RARβ-loratadine complexes were nearly identical, which suggest the structural changes in RARβ are nearly identical between 9-cis-RA and loratadine. Similar pattern was observed in the RMSF plots of bepotastine-RARβ, dimetotiazine-RARβ, and hydroxyzine-RARβ complexes.

  Interaction fraction analyses were also performed, where an interaction fraction value of 1.0 means the ligand made contact with a binding residue during 100% time of the simulation. Values greater than 1.0 indicate more than one binding forces. For example, in the interaction fraction analysis of 9-cis-RA-RARβ (Fig. 6), SER280 has an interaction fraction value of 1.849 and contacts 9-cis-RA via hydrogen bonds and water bridges. The top three contributors to the binding interaction for 9-cis-RA were ARG265, ARG269, and SER280 with scores of 0.779, 1.581, and 1.849, respectively. In the interaction fraction analysis of bepotastine-RARβ, SER280 was also seen intimately associating with the carboxylic acid group via hydrogen bond networks with an interaction fraction value of 1.885. In fact, the interaction fraction plot for bepotastine was similar to that for 9-cis-RA. The top three contributors to the binding interaction with bepotastine were PHE221, ARG265, and SER280 with scores of 1.323, 0.663, and 1.885, respectively. Similarly, in the interaction fraction analysis of hydroxyzine-RARβ, SER280 was seen forming hydrogen bond network with hydroxyzine’s ethanol group with an interaction fraction value of 1.123. The top three contributors to the binding interaction with hydroxyzine were TRP218, SER280, and PHE295 with scores of 0.729, 1.123, and 0.735, respectively. On the other hand, the interaction fraction plots of loratadine-RARβ and dimetotiazine-RARβ displayed mainly hydrophobic forces, where none of the residues exceeded values greater than 1.0. The top three contributors to the binding interaction with loratadine were TRP218, PHE295, and VAL388 with scores of 0.675, 0.875, and 0.428, respectively. Meanwhile, dimetotiazine had the weakest binding profile, whose interaction fraction values did not even reach 0.50. The top three contributors to the binding interaction with dimetotiazine were PHE221, PHE295, and LEU391 with scores of 0.356, 0.364, and 0.413, respectively. Together, these data revealed that bepotastine and hydroxyzine have similar binding modes as 9-cis-RA, including the presence of extensive hydrogen bond network with SER280.

  Finally, through structural comparisons, we were able to visualize RARβ interacting with 9-cis-RA and the antihistamines (Fig. 7). Upon superimposition, we identified overlay of secondary structures, except loop structures that are known to fluctuate more than α-helices and β-sheets. This pattern was conserved across the four antihistamines. However, when we zoomed into helix 12 of RARβ, we identified a 2.3 Å shift in RARβ-loratadine and RARβ-dimetotiazine due to steric hindrance from their three-membered aromatic rings. Meanwhile, 9-cis-RA, bepotastine, and hydroxyzine were seen intimately associating with residues of helix 12, where we observed a minor angle change to shorten the distance and promote hydrophobic interaction via MET407. This absence of steric clash with helix 12 is one of the structural motifs associated with agonism of RARβ (15-19). Together, our structural analysis suggests that bepotastine and hydroxyzine have similar binding profiles as 9-cis-RA and are promising candidates for future in-vitro or in-vivo experiments to determine their capacity to agonize RARβ.


### Binding free energy analysis by MM/GBSA
  To compare the stability of protein-ligand complex between 9-cis-RA and antihistamines, we used the MM/GBSA method to obtain the binding free energy (ΔGbind). Table 3 lists the ΔGbind of RARβ-9-cis-RA and RARβ-antihistamine complexes, whose overall predicted binding free energies were as follows: 9-cis-RA (−71.25 kcal/mol), loratadine (−90.08 kcal/mol), bepotastine (−89.34 kcal/mol), dimetotiazine (−73.1 kcal/mol), and hydroxyzine (−101.88 kcal/mol). It should be noted that the MM/GBSA binding free energy of hydroxyzine (−101.88 kcal/mol) appears to correspond to a dissociation constant (Kd) that is remarkably low near the picomolar range. This may seem implausible since it suggests a binding force even stronger than covalent bonds. However, it is important to emphasize that the interpretation of MM/GBSA binding free energy is primarily useful for comparing the relative binding energies of different protein-ligand complexes. It is not considered a reliable method for predicting the absolute binding free energy or the Kd of a protein-ligand complex. For instance, in the case of hydroxyzine, its lower MM/GBSA binding free energy compared to that of 9-cis-RA suggests that the RARβ-hydroxyzine complex may be more stable than the RARβ-9-cis-RA complex. This relative comparison can be made based on the MM/GBSA results. However, caution should be exercised when interpreting the absolute values of the binding free energy and relating them directly to Kd values. Overall, these results suggest that antihistamines form more stable complexes with RARβ than 9-cis-RA, which is consistent with the results of HTVS. It’s interesting to note that loratadine had lower binding free energy than 9-cis-RA despite its lack of hydrogen bonding with SER280. This is likely due to the cumulative effects of hydrophobic forces that we discovered in the interaction fraction analysis. For similar reasons, dimetotiazine had lower binding free energy than 9-cis-RA despite its lack of hydrogen bonding. Our final protein-ligand complexes predicted by MM/GBSA method can be accessed as supplemental PDB files.


### 3D protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis
  While our modeling studies suggest that the anti-neoplastic action of antihistamines in KS may be attributed to their off-target binding to RARβ, it may also be possible that antagonism of their primary target, histamine receptor 1 (HRH1), itself may be associated with regression of KS. To explore this possibility, a genome-wide meta-analysis was conducted to explore a genetic association between RARβ expression and molecular pathways associated with KS. Then, this was repeated with HRH1 to compare its PPI network to that of RARβ.

  First, the visualization of PPI network of RARβ in mice contained 512 nodes, 818 edges, and 293 seed genes (Fig. 8). Within the network, we analyzed enrichment of several proteins associated with mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling because RARβ agonism is believed to be intimately associated with this pathway (85, 86). In the PPI network of RARβ, the MAPK-associated proteins were identified as follow: Mapk14, Jun, Fgfr1, Irak1, Mapk8, Egfr, Sos1, Tgfb1, Jund, Srf, Kitl, Tgfbr1, Fos, Mapk1, Mapk11, Mapkapk2, Atf2, Flna, Mapk9, Hgf, Myc, Trp53, Map3k7, Mapk8ip3, Dusp2, Traf2, Traf6, Mknk1, Dusp3, Crk, Tnf, Grb2, Rac1, Rela, Hspa8, Map2k5, Kit, Myd88, Map2k4, Ppp3cc, and Ecsit. Similarly, proteins directly associated with Kaposi sarcoma were also identified as follow: Mapk14, Jun, Il6st, Stat3, Mapk8, Lyn, Fos, Mapk1, Mapk11, Mapkapk2, Mapk9, Syk, Src, Gsk3b, Myc, Trp53, Ctnnb1, Rb1, Traf2, Rac1, Rela, Tbk1, Map2k4, Ppp3cc, Ptgs2, Nfkb2.

  Similarly, the PPI network of HRH1 in mice contained 397 nodes, 588 edges, and 233 seed genes. Within the network, the enrichment of several proteins associated with MAPK signaling were found as follow: Mapk14, Kit, Ikbkb, Jun, Nfkb2, Map3k8, Tgfbr1, Vegfa, Il1r1, Rela, Rps6ka5, Il1b, Ntrk2, Mapk9, Map2k5, Traf6, Egfr, Map3k7, Mapk1, Dusp2, Myc, Grb2, Nfkb1, Mknk1, Trp53, Tgfb1, Rac1, Mapk8ip3, Fgf4, Bdnf Myd88, Ppm1b, and Rap1a. Meanwhile, proteins associated with Kaposi sarcoma were revealed as follow: Mapk14, Ikbkb, Jun, Cxcl1, Gnb1, E2f1, Vegfa, Rela, Casp8, Ccnd1, Mapk9, Ctnnb1, Creb1, Pik3r1, Mapk1, Myc, Src, Rb1, Nfkb1, Trp53, Rac1, Hck, Stat3, E2f2, and Nfatc2. In order to assess the statistical significance of these gene enrichments, pathway analyses were subsequently performed on both PPI networks.


### Functional enrichment analysis of PPI network
  KEGG pathway analysis of PPI network of RARβ revealed enrichment of several types of cancers, including pathways related to KS that was enriched by a ratio of 2.97 compared to reference background population (p-value = 9.67 X 10−7 & FDR = 6.46 X 10−6) (Table 4). Similarly, KEGG pathway analysis of PPI network of HRH1 also produced enrichment of pathways related to KS that was enriched by a ratio of 3.68 compared to reference background population (p-value = 1.47 X 10−8 & FDR = 1.23 X 10−7) (Table 5) Together, these data suggest that HRH1 expression promotes upregulation of pathways involved in a variety of cancers, including KS. This further supports testing of HRH1 antagonism via antihistamines in experimental models of cancer.


## Discussion
  Preliminary docking of 9-cis-RA and the comparison of its binding mode to published structures confirmed the fidelity of our docking method. There were variations in the docking scores across different retinoic acid receptors with respect to 9-cis-RA. Specifically, 9-cis-RA had the lowest predicted affinity of −8.758 kcal/mol in RARβ, and its flexible docking with Glide failed to capture presence of hydrogen bond between the carboxylic acid group of 9-cis-RA and ARG269 or SER280. This could be attributed to the limitation of molecular docking: it assumes rigid models despite the dynamic nature of proteins. Furthermore, the protein structure was relaxed from the original crystal structure prior to docking, hence it’s possible that the guanidino group of ARG269 and hydroxyl group of SER280 weren’t oriented in a conformation favorable to promote hydrogen bonding. Cross-docking with another crystal structure of RARβ is encouraged, but this technique was not employed for this study because we were able to restore hydrogen bonding with ARG269 and SER280 via more advanced technique, molecular dynamics simulation.

  On the interaction fraction analysis of loratadine-RARβ complex, the hydroxyl group of SER280 can be seen forming a water bridge with loratadine’s ethyl moiety. However, this interaction was present only 22% of the simulation time, suggesting this hydrogen bond network is a minor binding force. In fact, none of the binding residues exceeded interaction fraction values greater than 1.0. Curiously, loratadine had higher affinity to RARβ than 9-cis-RA according to its RMSD and binding free energy value. This increased stability may be attributed to the combined van der Waals forces by hydrophobic residues in the binding pocket. In fact, the MM/GBSA analysis revealed that the van der Waals force was the strongest contributor to the binding free energy of loratadine-RARβ complex, whose ΔGvdW is roughly −9 kcal/mol lower than ΔGvdW in 9-cis-RA-RARβ complex. Despite differences in their predicted binding affinity, there were no major changes in the overall structure of RARβ between 9-cis-RA and loratadine. However, when we observed helix 12 of RARβ, we discovered a 2.3 Å shift, where loratadine’s three-membered ring can be seen sterically hindering helix 12. This shift is not likely due to chance because it’s outside the RMSF value of residues found in helix 12 (Table 6). Similarly, on the interaction fraction analysis of dimetotiazine-RARβ complex, none of the binding residues exceeded values greater than 0.5. This suggests presence of ligand mobility within the binding pocket. Structural analysis revealed that dimetotiazine diffused away from the original binding site predicted by molecular docking and ended up finding a new conformation near helix 12. However, like loratadine-RARβ complex, we discovered a 2.3 Å shift in helix 12 likely due to steric hindrance from dimetotiazine’s three-membered ring. Helix 12 of RARβ is known to shift during RARβ antagonism, which subsequently exposes co-repressor binding site and promotes transcriptional repression (87). Likewise, loratadine and dimetotiazine may not be agonists of RARβ, despite exhibiting MM/GBSA binding free energies lower than 9-cis-RA.

  The interaction fraction plot of bepotastine-RARβ complex looked similar to that of 9-cis-RA-RARβ complex, including the interaction value of roughly 1.80 with SER280. Ligand interaction analysis revealed preservation of hydrogen bond network with the carboxylic acid group via the guanidino group of ARG265 and hydroxyl group of SER280. Similar observation was made in the interaction fraction analysis of hydroxyzine-RARβ complex with an ethanol group instead of a carboxylic acid moiety. The binding free energies of bepotastine-RARβ and hydroxyzine-RARβ were much lower than 9-cis-RA-RARβ, suggesting these two compounds form a more stable protein-ligand complex than 9-cis-RA. This result is consistent with the result of molecular docking, which also revealed that bepotastine and hydroxyzine had lower docking scores than 9-cis-RA. Structural analyses of both complexes revealed a slight angle change in helix 12 to bring the alanine group of MET407 closer to aromatic rings. It’s interesting to note that bepotastine and hydroxyzine have 2-membered aromatic rings, which are smaller than the 3-membered aromatic rings of loratadine or dimetotiazine. This is consistent with the literature that ligands with bulkier aromatic structures promote antagonism of RARβ by causing steric hindrance with helix 12 (16). Likewise, bepotastine and hydroxyzine may agonize RARβ with even higher affinities than 9-cis-RA.

  Bepotastine has been used to treat allergic rhinitis and urticaria in Japan and in the US to treat allergic conjunctivitis (88). Its therapeutic potential in cancer treatment has not been published yet. Hydroxyzine is used to relieve itching caused by allergic reactions and to help control anxiety (89). While oral medication of hydroxyzine in cancer patients is reported, it was to relieve chronic cancer pain rather than to treat cancers (90). None of the literatures associating antihistamine to cancer therapy mentions their potential mechanism via retinoic acid receptors. Another notable characteristic of antihistamines is their lipophilicity, which is important for topical delivery not only to the skin but also to nuclear receptors. Log P values were obtained from PubChem database, where higher log P value indicates higher lipophilicity (91). Bepotastine and hydroxyzine have experimental log P values of 1.0 and 3.7, respectively. In context, 9-cis-RA has an experimental log P value of 6.3. While bepotastine and hydroxyzine may not be as lipophilic as 9-cis-RA, there are experimental techniques to improve permeability through the skin, such as lipid nanoparticle delivery systems (92, 93).

  In addition to modeling, PPI network and functional enrichment analysis were performed to explore the possibility that antagonism of their primary target, histamine receptor 1, itself may be associated with regression of KS. The 3D PPI network of RARβ revealed enrichment of several proteins associated with MAPK signaling, which is expected because RARβ activation is believed to be intimately associated with this pathway (85, 86). In fact, some of the proteins associated with MAPK signaling were also found to be associated with KS, specifically the gene products of Mapk14, Jun, Mapk8, Fos, Mapk1, Mapk11, Mapkapk2, Mapk9, Syk, Myc, Trp53, Rac1, Rela, Map2k4, and Ppp3cc. Of these genes, Jun, Fos, Myc, and Rela are known proto-oncogenes or oncogenes (94-97). Likewise, it may be possible that the anti-cancer action of RARβ agonism may be modulated by directly altering the expression/activity of these genes in KS. Similarly, the 3D PPI network of HRH1 revealed enrichment of several proteins associated with MAPK signaling, although its number was not as high as those seen in RARβ: 33 genes VS 41 genes, respectively. Several of those proteins associated with MAPK signaling pathway were also found to be associated with pathways in KS, specifically the gene products of Mapk14, Ikbkb, Jun, Vegfa, Rela, Mapk1, Myc, Nfkb1, Trp53, and Rac1. Of these genes, Jun, Fos, Rela and Myc are known proto-oncogenes or oncogenes, while Ikbkb, Vegfa, and Nfkb1 are associated with pathogenesis of cancer (98-100). Together, the PPI networks of RARβ and HRH1 share several proteins encoded by proto-oncogenes or oncogenes as well as proteins associated with MAPK signaling pathway, which is known to play a role in cancerous cell growth (101, 102).

  Functional enrichment analysis of both PPI networks revealed enrichment of pathways related to KS. For RARβ, the Reactome pathway analysis revealed enrichment of loss of Function of FBXW7 in Cancer and NOTCH1 signaling by a ratio of 19.2 (p-value = 2.30 X 10−4 & FDR = 3.22 X 10−3). This is consistent with the literatures citing the potential roles of FBXW7 and NOTCH1 signaling in Kaposi sarcoma (103, 104). Other notable pathways included the activation of Nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) signaling and interleukin pathways. The gene ontology cellular compartment analysis identified the nuclear compartment of the cell as most likely impacted by expression of RARβ. Within the nucleus, the gene ontology biological process analysis predicted enrichment of regulation in chromosomal organization and positive regulation of cell cycle. Meanwhile, the gene ontology molecular functions analysis revealed enrichment of MAPK signaling pathway as well as NF-κB signaling, which is consistent with the results of our KEGG and Reactome pathway analysis. For H1R, the Reactome pathway analysis revealed enrichment of two pathways both by a ratio of 15.8: inhibition of replication initiation of damaged DNA by RB1/E2F1 complex (p-value = 5.83 X 10−4 & FDR = 7.32 X 10−3) and RIP-mediated NF-κB activation (p-value = 6.27 X 10−6 & FDR = 1.95 X 10−4), both of which are known to impact cancer growth (105). The gene ontology cellular compartment analysis also identified the nuclear compartment as most likely impacted by expression of HRH1. Within the nucleus, the gene ontology biological process analysis predicted enrichment of positive regulation of cell cycle and positive regulation of sequence-specific DNA binding transcription factor activity. Finally, the gene ontology molecular functions analysis revealed enrichment of Insulin-like growth factor receptor binding as well as histone deacetylase binding, both of which are positive regulators of cell cycle progression in cancer pathogenesis (106, 107). Together, the functional enrichment analysis suggests that the expression of HRH1 may influence development of Kaposi sarcoma via several pathways related to cancer growth, specifically the activation of NF-κB signaling pathway. This further supports the investigation of antihistamines in the context of not only KS but also potentially a variety of other cancers discovered in the systems genetics analysis.


## Conclusion
  Molecular modeling discovered two antihistamines that might potently activate RARβ to suppress cancer growth. Both drugs have already been FDA-approved. Genetic analysis also revealed that HRH1 expression is associated with pathways leading to KS and that its antagonism may be therapeutically exploited to target cancers. Hence, if these findings translate to mice and eventually humans, bepotastine and hydroxyzine may be potent anti-cancer drugs through dual mechanisms: agonism of RARβ and antagonism of HRH1. Together, these findings advocate for exploration of antihistamines against KS, starting with our two promising hit compounds, bepotastine and hydroxyzine, for experimental validation studies in the future.
