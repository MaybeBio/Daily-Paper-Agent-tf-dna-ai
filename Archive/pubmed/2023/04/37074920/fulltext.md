# A new high-throughput screen discovers novel ligands of full-length nuclear receptor LRH-1


## Abstract
  The nuclear receptor Liver Receptor Homolog-1 (LRH-1, NR5A2) is a lipid-regulated transcription factor and an important drug target for several liver diseases. Advances toward LRH-1 therapeutics have been driven recently by structural biology, with fewer contributions from compound screening. Standard LRH-1 screens detect compound-induced interaction between LRH-1 and a transcriptional coregulator peptide, an approach that excludes compounds that regulate LRH-1 through alternative mechanisms. Here, we developed a FRET-based LRH-1 screen that simply detects compound binding to LRH-1, applying it to discover 58 new compounds that bind the canonical ligand binding site in LRH-1 (2.5% hit rate), also supported by computational docking. Four independent functional screens identified 15 of these 58 compounds to also regulate LRH-1 function in vitro or in living cells. Although one of these 15 compounds, abamectin, directly binds LRH-1 and regulates full-length LRH-1 in cells, abamectin failed to regulate the isolated ligand binding domain in standard coregulator peptide recruitment assays using PGC1∝, DAX-1 or SHP. Abamectin treatment of human liver HepG2 cells selectively regulated endogenous LRH-1 ChIP-seq target genes and pathways associated with known LRH-1 functions in bile acid and cholesterol metabolism. Thus, the screen reported here can discover compounds not likely to have been identified in standard LRH-1 compound screens, but which bind and regulate full-length LRH-1 in cells.


## Introduction:
  The nuclear receptor superfamily is a group of transcription factors regulated by hydrophobic ligands such as cholesterol-based steroids1, fatty acids2,3 and phospholipids4. The 48 human nuclear receptors have important functions in metabolism, development, homeostasis, signaling and reproduction and are conserved throughout all metazoans5,6. The lipids and synthetic small molecules that regulate nuclear receptors bind a well-established cleft in the ligand binding domain (LBD), an event which alters LBD conformation to either enhance or discourage interaction with various transcriptional co-regulator proteins bearing the “LXXLL” motif7. This standard model of nuclear receptor regulation has been validated in many receptors through structural and functional studies7–11 and represents a rigorously tested, well-accepted regulatory model for this group of transcription factors. Based on this standard model, high throughput compound screens have demonstrated outstanding effectiveness12–14. These screens monitor the physical interaction between a nuclear receptor ligand binding domain and an LXXLL-motif containing peptide from biologically relevant transcriptional coregulators 12,15–17. Drugs developed with the aid of these standard screens remain first line therapies in human diseases such as cancer14, obesity and diabetes18,19.

  Liver Receptor Homolog-1 (LRH-1, NR5A2) is a nuclear receptor expressed mainly in the liver20–24, pancreas20,25,26 and gut27–32 of adult mammals. A wide variety of chemical, genetic and physiological studies have provided pre-clinical evidence that LRH-1 is a drug target with high potential therapeutic value in Type 2 Diabetes, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH)33–35, as well as pancreatic cancer and colorectal cancer8,25,36,37, all diseases with substantial unmet clinical need. Early LRH-1 compounds38 were developed into useful chemical tools17, and there has been great recent progress using structure-activity relationships based on structural biology to optimize these lead compounds into potential therapeutics for the clinic 39,40. Notwithstanding this progress, no FDA-approved drugs currently target LRH-1, so there remains a continued need for approaches that aid in the development of LRH-1 compounds.

  The standard model of compound-induced coregulator recruitment is the basis of current LRH-1 drug screening strategies, so any compound that might regulate LRH-1 through an alternative mechanism is unlikely to be identified in these standard screens. Indeed, several lines of evidence suggest LRH-1 and the close homolog Steroidogenic Factor-1 (SF-1, NR5A1) can be regulated by endogenous ligands through non-canonical mechanisms. SF-1 binds the same range of phospholipid ligands as LRH-1 41,42 which regulate SF-1 transcriptional activity43,44 but do not change SF-1 structure45. In the context of full-length LRH-1, binding events in the ligand binding domain (LBD) alter the DNA affinity of full-length LRH-1, in addition to physical interactions between the LBD and the DNA-binding domain46. The Prox-1 coregulator for LRH-147 interacts with nuclear receptors dependent on ligand, but independent of the Prox-1 LXXLL-motif, again suggesting a non-canonical mechanism of regulation48. These data only underscore the formal possibility that LRH-1 harbors unknown ligand-dependent regulatory mechanisms. Any unknown mechanism provides unexplored potential for small molecule regulation of LRH-1 that is independent of the assumptions inherent to the standard model of nuclear receptor regulation.

  Thus, we set out to validate a screen that makes minimal assumptions about how compounds might regulate LRH-1, only requiring compounds bind the canonical ligand-binding site in LRH-1. We then used multiple secondary screens to determine functional regulation. Accordingly, we designed, scaled and validated a FRET-based high throughput screen, using only commercially available reagents to maximize accessibility to smaller academic labs, based on previously validated high throughput FRET platforms49,50 and lower throughput assays51. We scaled up to screen the 2,322-compound Discovery Spectrum library and identified 58 new compounds that bind directly to the single ligand-binding site in LRH-1, also supported by computational docking. We then show 15 of these compounds regulate LRH-1 function in at least one of four independent secondary functional screens. Unexpectedly, we present data suggesting that one of the 15 hit compounds (abamectin, also known as Avermectin B1) was able to regulates full-length LRH-1 in cells but was unable to regulate coregulator peptide binding to the isolated LBD in any assay we tested, despite clear evidence abamectin binds the canonical regulatory site in LRH-1. This hit compound therefore regulates LRH-1 dependent upon either 1) an unknown factor present in cells and/or 2) the full length LRH-1. Perhaps more importantly, the data demonstrate the screen reported here can identify LRH-1 regulatory compounds that bind the canonical ligand binding site, but which would not have been identified in standard LRH-1 compound screens.


## Results & Discussion:

### FRET occurs in small scale assays between labeled LRH-1 and a rhodamine phospholipid probe.
  Previous studies taking a direct binding approach to LRH-1 compound development have required resource-intensive, custom synthesis of a fluorescent probe, a barrier to broader access52. Thus, we designed a FRET-based assay using only commercially available reagents that would similarly identify compounds that specifically bind to the canonical ligand binding pocket of the human LRH-1 ligand binding domain, guided by previously validated high-throughput FRET screens49–51 and lower throughput assays51. Both the LRH-1 protein and a commercially available phospholipid-ligand probe bear fluorophores allowing FRET to occur, thus a compound that displaces the phospholipid-ligand probe from the LRH-1 orthosteric site would result in loss of the FRET signal. Reactive thiol groups in cysteine residues of the bacterially expressed human LRH-1 were labeled using fluorophore-functionalized maleimide as the FRET-donor, while the FRET-acceptor was the fluorophore-functionalized lipid ligand (Figure 1A). A survey of commercially available fluorophores and phospholipid ligands 51 using a highly sensitive but lower-throughput single cuvette fluorimeter (see methods) led to two suitable donor-acceptor FRET pairs, LRH-1-Alexa488 (LRH-1-488) with Cy5-phosphatidylethanolamine (Cy5PE) or Rhodamine-phosphatidylethanolamine (Rh-PE) (Figure 1A, Figure S1A). Due to higher overlap in the emission spectra of Alexa488 with Rh-PE compared to that of Cy5-PE, we selected dioleoyl (18:1/18:1) Rhodamine-phosphatidylethanolamine (Rh-PE) as the phospholipid FRET-acceptor and LRH-1–488 as the FRET donor for all analyses. Fluorescence emission spectra of LRH-1-488 in the presence of increasing concentrations of Rh-PE showed the expected decrease in donor signal along with concomitant sensitization of phospholipid ligand FRET acceptor (Figure 1B, Supplemental Figure S1B–C). To determine binding specificity of Rh-PE, fluorescence emission scans were obtained in the presence of increasing concentrations of Rh-PE and in the presence of a single concentration of 5uM dilauroylphosphatidylcholine (DLPC), a known ligand of LRH-1 that binds the canonical ligand binding pocket34,42 (Figure 1C, Supplemental Figure S1D). To determine the suitability of the assay in a high-throughput format, we collected FRET data in a 384-well format plate reader (see methods), showing that increasing concentrations of DLPC attenuated the FRET signal (Supplemental Figure S1E). These data confirm that displacement of the Rh-PE probe by a known chemical competitor decreases FRET, as expected. Consistent with wet lab observations, PyRx computational docking of Rh-PE to LRH-1 suggested Rh-PE binds at the expected single canonical site in LRH-1, with the hydrophobic acyl chains buried deep within the ligand binding pocket and the Rhodamine-labeled head group solvent exposed on the surface of the LRH-1 protein (Supplemental Figure S1A). This mode of binding is consistent with many published crystal structures of LRH-1 bound to a range of phospholipids bearing a variety of headgroups51,53–57. The decrease in fluorescence intensities as a function of increasing concentration of Rh-PE measured in the 384-well format were fitted to a one-site total binding model (Figure 1D) suggesting Rh-PE binds to a single site in LRH-1. The data could not be reasonably fit to a two-site binding model. The FRET efficiency of wild type LRH-1-488/Rh-PE pair produced maximal FRET efficiency of 84% and exhibited fully saturable Rh-PE concentration dependence with an apparent Kd 10.2 ±1.7nM of Rh-PE for LRH-1 (Figure 1E). Together, these data suggest a known LRH-1 ligand (DLPC) can competitively displace a Rh-PE probe from LRH-1 labeled with Alexa488 on thiol groups, as detected by loss of FRET between the two fluorophores.


### FRET efficiency of wild type vs. Cys-lite mutants is similar.
  The maleimide labeling chemistry using thiol groups of cysteine residues might require generating a Cys-lite mutant of LRH-1, and mutants could alter results of the screen. As there are five cysteine thiol groups available, we mutated these residues to determine if multiple reactive groups would affect FRET efficiency. Two of the five cysteine residues in LRH-1 (C381 and C450) are not solvent exposed and form part of the hydrophobic core (Figure 2A–B). The remaining three cysteines (C311, C346 and C487) are more solvent exposed on the surface (Figure 2A–B). FRET efficiency analyses of these double-Cysteine to Serine mutants at these residues (Cys-lite) examining C311, C346 and C487 shows that although absolute fluorescence intensities differed slightly, all mutants exhibited comparable maximal FRET efficiencies ranging between 0.76 and 0.87 (Figure 2C–E, Supplemental Figure S2). The FRET efficiency of the wild type LRH-1-488/Rh-PE pair exhibited comparable FRET efficiency to any of the tested mutants (Figure 1E). We chose the wild type, unmutated LRH-1 for further analyses. Together, these observations suggest wild-type LRH-1 can be effectively labeled with maleimide-Alexa488 for use in this FRET assay, with only a slight decrease in FRET efficiency compared to double mutant Cys-lite versions of LRH-1. These observations will inform future applications of the screening platform by others.


### The primary FRET screen identified novel 58 compounds that directly bind LRH-1.
  Our strategy used the primary FRET screen to identify compounds that bind the orthosteric site of LRH-1, then four secondary screens to measure LRH-1 function (Figure 3A). For the primary FRET screen, we scaled up to the 2,322-compound Spectrum library, which contains a blend of natural products, pharmacophores and bioactive molecules (Supplemental Spreadsheet 1). We executed the screen using standard HTS protocol detailed in methods, note all aspects of the screen proceeded as expected. Assay performance was assessed using z’-factor determined from whole plates containing wells in the absence and presence of the known ligand PIP3 for LRH-1 as a positive control53 and was calculated to be 0.458,59. We defined hit compounds as any compound that decreased FRET by greater than 3 standard deviations from the mean of the DMSO control (Figure 3B), typically corresponding to inhibition of FRET by log2FC ≈ −1 compared to DMSO controls. DMSO vehicle at 5% did not affect any detectable aspect of the FRET assay (Figure 3B). Applying these criteria, the primary screen identified 58 unique compounds (Supplemental Spreadsheet 2). These data suggest that from the 2,322 compound Spectrum library, 58 compounds displaced the Rh-PE probe from LRH-1, corresponding to a hit rate of 2.5%.


### The FRET-based screen is more selective than computational docking.
  We next used PyRx to computationally dock the identical compound library into the orthosteric site of the crystal structure of LRH-1, defined as a 22.7Å X 25.2Å X 26.1Å (~15,000 Å3) box centered on the canonical ligand binding site in the LRH-1 crystal structure (PDB ID: 6OQX). Docking produced binding positions and computed binding energies for each of the 2,322 compounds in the library (Supplemental Spreadsheet 3). We defined hits in the PyRx docking screen as any compound with a computed binding energy lower than that for LRH-1 compounds “5N” and “6N”, previously shown to be the highest-affinity ligands ever synthesized for LRH-139, thus representing a stringent but empirical standard from which to evaluate compound binding. Computed binding energies for 5N and 6N compounds were both computed to be −9.0kcal/mol, and 172 compounds had binding energies below this value (Supplemental Spreadsheet 3), corresponding to a hit rate for the docking screen of ~7%. All 58 hit of the compounds identified from the primary FRET screen docked with favorable computed binding energies to LRH-1 at the canonical ligand binding site (Supplemental Figure S3A). To permit direct comparison of the wet-lab FRET screen (Figure 3B) to the computational PyRx screen (Figure 3C), both datasets were normalized to fold change relative to the average of all compounds in each screen, showing a broad, qualitative difference in output from the two screens (Figure 3D). Simple contingency analyses suggest the FRET screen was significantly more selective than the computational screen (Supplemental Fig S3B, p=0.0102, Fisher Exact; p≈0.009, Chi-square). The docked positions of all 58 hit compounds (Figure 3E) and all compounds with negative computed binding energies (Figure 3F) are shown superposed bound to LRH-1 LBD, all clustered to the canonical binding site45 established by other studies54,60. More detailed analyses of the predicted binding positions suggests that from a total of 113 predicted polar interactions between the ensemble of compounds and LRH-1, 42 were backbone interactions and 71 were side chain interactions. Interactions overrepresented in the docking bound to the side chains of H390 (11 polar interactions) and R393 (44 polar interactions) (Supplemental Figure S3C). These two side chains accounted for 49% of all the predicted polar interactions between LRH-1 and the entire ensemble of hit compounds (Figure 3F, Supplemental Figure S3C). This result supports previous studies suggesting these same two residues are important for ligand-mediated regulation of LRH-161. Additional docking studies that did not specify an LRH-1 binding site predict 51 of the 58 hit compounds to have low-energy docked binding positions inside the canonical ligand binding site (Supplemental Spreadsheet 4). This was a high percentage (88%) given the limitations of rigid body docking and that LRH-1 is known to require thermal flexibility to accommodate binding of ligands17,45,62. Together, these computational docking data identify 58 compounds from a FRET screen that can bind the canonical ligand binding site in LRH-1 as suggested by their negative computed binding energies. We next tested these 58 hit compounds in four high-throughput wet lab screens evaluating LRH-1 function.


### A secondary high-throughput functional screen identifies 5 compounds that regulate LRH-1 interaction with the PGC1α coactivator peptide.
  The standard model of ligand regulation of LRH-1 function is that small molecule binding allosterically changes LRH-1 conformation, altering the ability of LRH-1 to recruit an LXXLL-motif containing transcriptional coregulator protein7. Functional assays that measure changes to compound induced LXXLL peptide recruitment are well validated and remain the gold standard for nuclear receptor compound development13,15. Thus, we used this type of transcriptional coactivator peptide recruitment screen as a secondary functional screen, applied to 50 of the 58 primary screen hit compounds in a fluorescence anisotropy screen (8 compounds had high inherent fluorescence that did not provide data above background and were thus excluded from analysis). This high-throughput secondary screen uses fluorescence anisotropy / polarization to measure compound-induced interaction of a FAM-labeled LXXLL-peptide representing PGC1α, a well-established transcriptional coregulator of LRH-1, with the isolated LBD of LRH-1, at single concentrations of compound (10uM), receptor (1.0uM) and FAM-peptide (50nM) (Figure 4A). We observed five of the 50 compounds to significantly alter peptide recruitment to the isolated LBD of LRH-1, which also resulted in ≥0.5 log2 fold change (L2FC) of the peptide recruitment compared to DMSO control (Supplemental Spreadsheet 5), after Dunnett’s correction for multiple comparisons following ordinary one-way ANOVA (padj≤0.05). These data suggest that 10% of compounds identified to directly bind the canonical ligand binding site in LRH-1 were also able to regulate PGC1α peptide recruitment to the isolated ligand binding domain, and further that 90% of hit compounds identified by our screen would not have been identified in a standard PGC1α nuclear receptor screen.


### Three additional secondary high-throughput functional screens in mammalian cells identify 10 distinct compounds that regulate LRH-1 function.
  To probe for compounds that regulate full length LRH-1 in living cells, we performed three additional high-throughput secondary screens of the 58 hit compounds using cell-based luciferase reporters in mammalian cells. The first screen was a commercially available LRH-1 drug screening platform in Chinese Hamster Ovary (CHO) cells, which stably express a chimeric human LRH-1 ligand binding domain genetically fused to the Gal4 DBD, along with a luciferase reporter driven by the Gal4 promoter (Figure 4B). This screen executed at 10uM each compound yielded five hits with padj≤0.05 (Dunnett’s corrected ordinary ANOVA) and a log2 fold change (L2FC) of at least ±0.5 compared to DMSO controls (Figure 4B), the same statistical threshold used in all four high-throughput secondary screens (Figures 4A–E). Native full-length human LRH-1 was used for the third screen (Figure 4C) in an HEK293T cell line co-expressing a luciferase reporter driven by the CYP17A1 promoter, a known target promoter of LRH-1 (Figure 4C). This assay yielded six hit compounds according to the same criteria (Dunnett’s corrected ordinary ANOVA, padj≤0.05 and L2FC ±0.5). The fourth independent screen also used native full-length LRH-1, but the luciferase reporter was driven by a different promoter (CYP8B1), also a well-established target promoter of LRH-1 (Figure 4D). This screen yielded six hit compounds, using the same criteria as applied all the other screens (Dunnett’s corrected ordinary ANOVA, padj≤0.05 and L2FC ±0.5). Thus 15 compounds regulated LRH-1 in at least one of the 4 secondary screens (Supplemental Spreadsheet 6). Only four compounds regulated LRH-1 across multiple secondary screens and no compound regulated LRH-1 in all four of secondary screens (Supplemental Figure S4A). These data suggest that 15 of 58 hit compounds, or about one-quarter of all compounds that bind LRH-1 from the primary screen, were able to regulate LRH-1 activity (Dunnett’s corrected ordinary ANOVA, padj≤0.05 and L2FC ±0.5) in at least one of the four secondary functional screens. Importantly, we also note that there was no overlap in the hit compounds that regulated the isolated ligand binding domain (Figure 4A) vs. those compounds that regulated full-length LRH-1 in any of the cell-based assays we attempted (Figure 4C–E).


### Compounds that regulated the isolated LBD of LRH-1 did not regulate full-length LRH-1 in cells.
  Although these 15 hits from the secondary screens all have potential utility interrogating LRH-1 functions, no one compound regulated LRH-1 in all the secondary screens (Supplement Figure S4A). We immediately noted that no compounds that regulated the isolated ligand binding domain also regulated full-length LRH-1 in any of the assays we attempted (Supplemental Figure S4A). To investigate this further, we applied cluster analysis to determine which groups of compounds had similar patterns of LRH-1 regulation, which yielded 3 clusters of compounds (Figure 4E). Whereas only 1 of 11 compounds from Clusters 1 and 2 regulated LRH-1 in multiple assays (Supplemental Figure S4B), 3 of the 5 compounds in Cluster 3 regulated LRH-1 in multiple assays (Supplemental Figure S4C), and 2 compounds regulated LRH-1 in all three cell-based screens (Supplemental Figure S4D–E). Further, the docked binding positions of compounds to the canonical site in LRH-1 in the three clusters had notable differences (Supplemental Figure S5A–C). All 11 compounds in Clusters 1 and 2 are predicted to bind deep in the canonical ligand binding pocket of LRH-1 (Supplemental Figure S5D–E) but in Cluster 3, two of the five compounds were predicted to bind more towards the solvent-exposed surface of LRH-1, at the mouth of the ligand binding pocket (Supplemental Figure S6A). A recent study showed positive structure-activity relationships for compounds designed to engage residues at this site in LRH-154. The two compounds were Abamectin (VU0243218, also known as Avermectin B1) and Doramectin (VU0656021, Supplemental Figure S6B), both these Ivermectin-related compounds regulated LRH-1 in all screens that used full-length LRH-1 (Figure 4C–E) but failed to regulate the isolated LBD in the standard peptide interaction screen (Figure 4A). Together, these data suggest that the screen reported here identified hit compounds that would not have been identified in one standard coregulator peptide binding screen (Figure 4A), yet directly bind the canonical site in LRH-1 (Figure 3B) and regulated full-length LRH-1 in cells (Figure 4C–E). We next investigated if the most interesting candidate, abamectin, was able to regulate the isolated LBD of LRH-1 in more detailed, lower-throughput studies to confirm the above results from the high-throughput screens.


### Abamectin does not regulate dissociation constants of the isolated LRH-1 LBD with transcriptional coregulator peptides of PGC1∝, SHP or DAX1.
  The studies above suggest abamectin (VU0243218) was able to regulate native full-length LRH-1 activity in cell-based screens (Figure 4C–D) but did not affect PGC1∝ coregulator peptide binding in high-throughput screens (Figure 4A), despite that abamectin was identified based on its ability to directly bind the regulatory site in LRH-1 (Figure 3, Supplemental Figures S5). We therefore used lower-throughput studies to more closely examine if abamectin might regulate the dissociation constant (Kd) between LRH-1 and the PGC1∝ coregulator peptide. In parallel, we also examined LXXLL-containing peptides representing the well-known LRH-1 coregulators SHP and DAX1 (Figure 5A). Again, abamectin failed to significantly change LRH-1 binding to any of these three coregulator peptides we tested in these more detailed, lower throughput assays (Figure 5A). To confirm abamectin binds LRH-1 in an assay other than FRET (Figure 3B), we determined IC50s for abamectin and two other hit compounds by differential scanning fluorimetry, confirming abamectin binds purified LRH-1 with a fully saturable curve and an IC50 of 9.4±1.4uM (Supplemental Figure S7). Three other hit compounds from the screen (Figure 5A) all selectively regulated LRH-1 interaction with coregulator peptides (Figure 5B–C), demonstrating that other hit compounds identified in the screen were able to alter coregulator recruitment to LRH-1. Indeed, mebendazole (VU0239647) increased affinity of LRH-1 for DAX and SHP peptides but did not change Kd for the PGC1α peptide. 3αDOG (VU0656679) increased LRH-1 affinity for DAX1 and SHP without effect on PGC1α. Puromycin (VU0244504) slightly decreased affinity of LRH-1 for PGC1α but had no effect on SHP or DAX. These data suggest that although abamectin was identified in the primary screen to bind the canonical site in LRH-1 (Figure 3B) and to regulate full-length LRH-1 in cells (Figure 4C–E) and binds purified LRH-1 ligand binding domain with a saturable binding curve (Supplemental Figure S7), abamectin does not alter interaction between LRH-1 and any of the three coregulators we tested in this assay (Figure 5). In the context of LRH-1 drug screening, abamectin represents a compound that would likely have been missed in a standard peptide-interaction screen but was discovered in our screen.


### Abamectin selective regulates endogenous LRH-1 target gene expression.
  The data above suggest LRH-1 binds abamectin and LRH-1 activity is regulated by abamectin in luciferase reporter gene assays. We therefore asked if abamectin (Figure 6A) might regulate endogenous LRH-1 target genes in human liver HepG2 cells. We treated HepG2 cells with 10uM abamectin for 24 hours and performed RNA-seq, which revealed 1,601 genes regulated by abamectin (padj>0.05 and Log2FC±1.0), including several well established LRH-1 target genes such as CYP7B1, CYP19A1, CYP24A1 and GSTA1 (Figure 6B), genes also confirmed to be direct LRH-1 ChIP-seq target genes in HepG2 cells63. We next determined the extent to which abamectin-regulated genes were enriched in these ChIP-seq LRH-1 target genes. Abamectin regulated 1,601 genes out of all 28,636 quantifiable genes in the transcriptome (5.6% of all genes), however abamectin regulated 9.9% of all quantifiable LRH-1 ChIP-seq target genes (557 abamectin-regulated genes out of 5,646 LRH-1 ChIP-seq target genes), a significant enrichment (p<0.0001, Fisher exact test, Figure 6C). If only the padj>0.05 cutoff is applied without excluding genes based on Log2 fold-change, abamectin-regulated genes are still significantly enriched among all quantifiable LRH-1 ChIP-seq target genes (p<0.0001, Fisher exact test, Supplemental Figure S8). Thus, in addition to discovering several well-known direct LRH-1 target genes regulated by abamectin (Figure 6B), the abamectin-regulated transcriptome is enriched in LRH-1 target genes (Figure 6C, Supplemental Figure S8), supporting that abamectin regulates LRH-1 in HepG2 cells. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of the abamectin-regulated transcriptome revealed significant enrichment of cholesterol homeostasis and bile acid metabolism genes, consistent with known LRH-1 functions in liver metabolism (Supplemental Spreadsheet 8). We next asked if a specific chemical competitor of LRH-1 could attenuate the abamectin-induced response in HepG2 cells. The small molecule RJW100 binds and displaces ligands from the canonical ligand binding site of LRH-117,64. We pre-treated HepG2 cells with 20uM RJW100 competitor compound, then immediately with 10uM abamectin, for 24 hours. RT-qPCR analysis of the established LRH-1 ChIP-seq target gene CYP11A1 suggests excess RJW100 attenuated the abamectin response significantly (Figure 6E). At the equally well established endogenous LRH-1 ChIP-seq target gene CYP8B1, RJW100 also significantly attenuated the response to topotecan (Figure 6F). These data suggest abamectin selectively regulates LRH-1 ChIP-seq target genes in human HepG2 cells, and that response can be attenuated by a chemical competitor of LRH-1. Although complete mechanistic characterization of how abamectin regulates LRH-1 is outside the scope of this study, here we demonstrate the FRET screen reported here (Figure 1) identified at least one compound (Figure 2–3) that is unlikely to have been discovered in a standard nuclear receptor screen using PGC1∝, SHP or DAX1 (Figure 4–5), but which regulates full-length LRH-1 (Figure 4–6), suggesting the screen can discover unique compounds other screens would likely miss.

  Classic nuclear receptor compound screens often measure compound-induced recruitment of a transcriptional coregulator peptide18. It would be difficult to overstate how effective and important these canonical screens have been in developing compounds that regulate nuclear receptors in general. However, functional screens require a compound to both bind the target and regulate its function, thus a simpler binding screen permits more chemical space in a library to be explored for non-canonical modes of regulation. A direct binding assay was recently developed using an innovative fluorescent LRH-1 probe52, however the complex synthesis of this probe presents a barrier to smaller academic labs. We addressed this by developing a FRET-based screen that uses only commercially available reagents and validated the screen by identifying 58 hit compounds that bind the canonical ligand binding site in LRH-1. Four independent secondary functional screens revealed 15 of these 58 hit compounds regulate LRH-1 function in at least one of the secondary screens, suggesting the effectiveness of the screen and utility of the new compounds in studying LRH-1 function.

  Although no single compound regulated LRH-1 across all four high-throughput platforms, a more detailed in vitro analysis of Abamectin (VU0243218) suggests it does not regulate recruitment of PGC1∝, SHP or DAX1 coregulator peptides to the isolated LBD of LRH-1 (Figure 5), but abamectin does regulate full-length LRH-1 activity in cells (Figure 4, Figure 6). The simplest interpretation of these data is that in a complex living cell, abamectin indirectly activates LRH-1 through an indirect pathway. However many lines of evidence suggest abamectin directly binds LRH-1 to mediate activation. First, abamectin was discovered in the FRET-screen based on its ability to compete with a FRET-probe installed at the canonical regulatory site in LRH-1 (Figure 1A, Figure 3B), confirmed in a second assay independent of FRET to bind LRH-1 in a saturable manner (Supplemental Figure S7), as positive controls we should other compounds identified in the screen regulate LRH-1 through the standard mechanism, altering interactions with PGC1∝, SHP or DAX1 coregulator peptides (Figure 5B). Second, computational docking predicts the abamectin binding site is at the opening of the ligand binding pocket, more towards the surface of LRH-1 (Figure S6B). At this position, abamectin atoms are unable to contact LRH-1 amino acids deep in the pocket, such as H390 and R393, important residues that translate compound binding to the well-known site of coregulator peptide interaction60. Third, doramectin and abamectin are chemically similar, both predicted to bind similarly to LRH-1 (Figure S7B) and both have a similar pattern of regulating LRH-1 activity (Figure 4E), consistent with both compounds binding LRH-1. Fourth, while abamectin decreased LRH-1 activity in the Gal4 screen (Figure 4B), abamectin increased LRH-1 activity in the CYP17A1 screen (Figure 4E), suggesting abamectin is not generally inhibiting or activating transcription in these assays, but rather has specific effects at these different promoters (Figure 4B–E). Fifth, abamectin selectively regulated endogenous LRH-1 ChIP-seq target genes in human HepG2 cells (Figure 6B). Finally, a chemical competitor of LRH-1 attenuated the abamectin response by RT-qPCR in HepG2 cells (Figure 6E). Together, the data are most consistent with abamectin regulating LRH-1 directly, and while it remains unclear how abamectin achieves this regulation, the discovery of this compound validates the logic of the LRH-1 screen reported here. Thus, the screening approach reported here can discover unique LRH-1 compounds, which we hope will be applied to ongoing efforts targeting LRH-1 in therapeutic development.


## Conclusions:
  We developed and scaled a new LRH-1 compound screen that identified 58 new compounds that bind LRH-1. We found 15 of those 58 compounds regulated LRH-1 function in at least one of four high-throughput secondary screens. One of these 15 compounds (abamectin) was not identified in a standard LRH-1 coregulator interaction screen, yet our data suggest abamectin binds LRH-1 directly and regulates full-length LRH-1 in cells. Thus, our screen can identify unique hit compounds likely to be missed in standard nuclear receptor coregulator peptide recruitment screens that use the isolated ligand binding domain, by making fewer assumptions about how compounds might regulate full-length LRH-1. Together, the screening tool presented here may hasten discovery of unique small molecules that regulate full-length LRH-1.

### LRH-1 protein expression and purification.
  A wild type human LRH-1 (NR5A2) ligand-binding domain (LBD) construct (residues 266–541, UniProt ID O00482) and Cys-lite mutants (C311/346S), (C311/487S) and (C346/487S) were cloned into isopropyl 1-thio-b-galactopyranoside (IPTG)-inducible pET vector with an N-terminal His-tag and expressed in E. coli BL21 dE3 using standard methods, briefly E. coli BL21 dE3 were transformed with an ampicillin-resistant LRH-1 expression plasmid, grown at 37°C until OD600 = 0.8 and induced with 1mM IPTG overnight at 15°C. Cells were collected by centrifuge at 3000×g at 4°C for 20 minutes, the pellet resuspended in lysis buffer (20mM Tris (7.5), 150mM NaCl, 2mM CHAPS, 1X Peirce/Roche protease inhibitors), transferred to a 50mL conical and stored at −80°C. Cell pellets were thawed on ice, lysed by probe sonication and LRH-1 purified using TALON metal affinity chromatography, followed by TEV cleavage of the 6X-His tag.


### DLPC competitive binding with the Rh-PE FRET probe.
  To further validate specificity of the FRET-LRH-1 binding assay in the presence of increasing concentrations of DLPC, a known small molecular competitor ligand of LRH-142, a competitive binding assay (Supplemental Figure S1E) was performed. Constant and equimolar concentrations of donor LRH-1-488 (100nM) and acceptor Rh-PE (100nM) were incubated with various concentrations of DLPC (0–50uM), for 60 minutes at room temperature, fluorescence intensities of the donor at λemm=520 nm were then measured following excitation at λexc=470 nm in a 384-well format using a Biotek Neo multimodal plate reader. FRET efficiency was calculated using equation 2 in Supplemental Methods.


### Peptide binding secondary screen.
  A secondary functional screen was carried out to determine the effect of the hit compounds on binding of PGC1α peptide motif to LRH-1 LBD using fluorescence polarization/anisotropy. Assay plates were prepared by adding 80nL of each hit compound identified in the FRET screen in quadruplicates into 384-well black plates, with the first and last two columns loaded with equal volume of DMSO vehicle as no-compound controls. To assess the effect of the compounds to the PGC1α peptide fluorescent probe in the absence of LRH-1 LBD, assay buffer (20mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150mM NaCl) with or without LRH-1 LBD was then added to duplicate wells for each compound using Agilent Bravo Liquid Handler to a concentration of 10uM in the final assay, followed by incubation of the plates overnight at room temperature. PGC1α peptide labeled with Fam at the N-terminal was then added to the plate to a final concentration of 50nM. Plates were incubated at room temperature for an hour and spun at 1000×g for 5 minutes. Fluorescence polarization was then measured with excitation at 480nm and emission at 535nm using Biotek Neo multimodal plate reader. For each compound, the fractional change in FP(ΔFPf) as a result of binding of the LXXLL peptide representing PGC1α (5Fam-EEPSLLKKLLLAPA-OH) to LRH-1 was calculated using equation 1:  (1) ΔFPf=FP+LRH1−FP−LRH1/FP−LRH1  where FP+LRH1 is the FP in the presence of LRH-1 LBD and FP−LRH1 in the absence of LRH-1 LBD. Any compound that results in either an increase or decrease in log2 ΔFPf compared to DMSO controls were considered for further validation, with padj≤0.05 (Dunnett’s corrected for multiple comparisons) and a log2 fold change (L2FC) of at least ±0.5 compared to DMSO controls. Results of the peptide binding screen are in Supplemental Spreadsheet 5.


### HepG2 cells stably expressing SLCO1B1.
  Polyclonal, stable human HepG2 cells expressing the human SLCO1B1 organic anion transporter were used for all endogenous gene expression analyses. The SLCO1B1 (OATP1B1) transmembrane transporter enhances HepG2 cell uptake of anionic small molecules from cell culture media76–78, these cells were therefore chosen to maximize uptake of hit compounds from the cell culture media. Polyclonal stable cells were generated by transfecting standard human HepG2 cells (ATCC HB-8065) with human SLCO1B1 (NCBI accession: 10599) cDNA in a pPB plasmid (VectorBuilder) or the empty pPB vector using Lipofectamine 3000 with a piggyback transposase. After 48 hours the cells were selected with 750ug/ml hygromycin, chosen based on a hygromycin kill-curve executed on these HepG2 cells that were mock transfected with Lipofectamine 3000. Stable transformants were selected for three weeks and pooled to create a polyclonal, stable cell line used in all HepG2 cell-based studies, this stable cell line is available on request.


### HepG2 cell transcriptome analysis.
  Human HepG2 polyclonal cells stably expressing SLCO1B1 (see above) were seeded at 0.5X106 cells in 3ml L-Glucose DMEM media supplemented with 1mg/mL glutathione, 10% FBS, 1% Pen/Strep and 250ug/mL hygromycin in a 6-well plate overnight at 37°C, then treated with 10uM abamectin (Spectrum Discovery) in 3ml media or an equal volume of DMSO vehicle control. Cells were incubated for 24hrs at 37°C, 5% CO2 in a cell culture incubator, and total RNA prepared using Quick-RNA MiniPrep (Zymo Research) according to manufacturer recommendations. DNase-treated total RNA was then used to generate PolyA-selected RNA-seq libraries, which passed quality control (QC) and were sequenced by the Vanderbilt Advanced Technologies for Advanced Genomics core on a NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina), using 150bp paired-end sequencing with a minimum coverage of 50 million reads per sample. Actual number of reads was at least 64 million per sample. QC of raw reads were tested using FastQC (0.11.9), low quality reads were removed, and Illumina adapter sequences trimmed using bbDuk (https://jgi.doe.gov/data-and-tools/bbtools/bb-tools-user-guide/bbduk-guide/). Trimmed sequences were then mapped to hg38 using HiSat279. Poorly mapped and unaligned sequences were removed using Hisat2 and SamTools80. Mapped reads were counted using feature counts81, raw read counts were used to assess differential expression using DESEQ2 with Apeglm LFCShrinkage in the R Bioconductor package. Differentially expressed gene sets were subjected to Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) using the fgsea package in R using the Molecular signatures database (version 7.4) as the reference. Contingency analyses to determine enrichment of abamectin-regulated transcripts in previously published LRH-1 ChIP-seq target gene sets63 from PMID: 26653140 are available in Supplemental Spreadsheet 7, all Fisher exact tests were computed in GraphPad Prism. The quantifiable transcriptome from RNA-seq was defined as all genes with any reported p value (28,636 genes qualified). Quantifiable LRH-1 ChIP-seq genes were defined as any ChIP-chromatin locus in HepG2 cells that was reported by Suzawa, et al.63 and which was present in the quantifiable RNA-seq transcriptome (5,646 LRH-1 ChIP-seq genes qualified, all excluded loci and included genes are reported in “Quantifiable ChIP” tab of Supplemental Spreadsheet 7). All original FASTQ files are available on request.


### RJW100-competitive reverse-transcriptase quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR):
  RJW100 is an LRH-1 competitive synthetic small molecule that binds the canonical ligand-binding site of LRH-116,64 which has been demonstrated to competitively displace other ligands from the LRH-1 canonical ligand binding site17,82. HepG2 cells used for transcriptome studies above stably expressing the SLCO1B1 organic anion transporter (to aid in compound uptake from the media) were seeded in 6 well plate overnight, cells were treated with combinations of DMSO vehicle, 20uM RJW100, 10uM abamectin or 10uM topotecan. First strand cDNA was synthesized from 2ug total RNA using random-primed reverse transcription (Verso cDNA Synthesis, Thermo-Scientific) using a thermal cycler according to manufacturer’s instructions. First strand cDNA synthesis was done at 42°C for 30min followed by 2 minutes inactivation at 95°C. Quadruple biological replicates with 2 technical replicates were used for each RT-qPCR condition. GAPDH was the reference gene for normalization, and all primers were validated at serially diluted cDNA concentrations to produce a single PCR product in the dissociation curve. Primer sequences were: GAPDH Forward: 5’-CAAGGTCATCCATGACAACTTTG-3’; GAPDH Reverse: 5’-GGCCATCCACAGTCTTCTGG-3’; CYP11A1 Forward: 5’-GGGTCGCCTATCACCAGTATT-3’; CYP11A1 Reverse: 5’-GCTGCCGACTTCTTCAACAG-3’; CYP8B1 Forward: 5’-TGGTGCTGAAGGTATTTGGA-3’; CYP8B1 reverse: 5’-TGGTGCTGGCTGAGTGTATC-3’. A CFX96 Real Time qPCR instrument (BioRad, Hercules, CA) was used for real time data acquisition using the following cycling parameters: pre-denaturation 95°C for 5 minutes, denaturation 95°C for 10 sec, annealing and extension 60°C for 30 sec for a total of 40 cycles. A melt curve step at 65°C for 5 sec and 56°C for 50 sec in every run further validated the specificity of primers in every run to produce only one PCR product. The ΔΔCt method using reference gene GAPDH subtracted from the Ct of the target gene of interest was used to normalize transcript levels for each cDNA sample. The data were analyzed in GraphPad Prism by one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s correction for multiple comparisons from combined quadruplicate biological samples and technical RT-qPCR duplicates. All raw RT-qPCR data are available on request.
