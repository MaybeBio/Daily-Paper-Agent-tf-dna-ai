# Quantitative model for genome-wide cyclic AMP receptor protein binding site identification and characteristic analysis


## Abstract
  Cyclic AMP receptor proteins (CRPs) are important transcription regulators in many species. The prediction of CRP-binding sites was mainly based on position-weighted matrixes (PWMs). Traditional prediction methods only considered known binding motifs, and their ability to discover inflexible binding patterns was limited. Thus, a novel CRP-binding site prediction model called CRPBSFinder was developed in this research, which combined the hidden Markov model, knowledge-based PWMs and structure-based binding affinity matrixes. We trained this model using validated CRP-binding data from Escherichia coli and evaluated it with computational and experimental methods. The result shows that the model not only can provide higher prediction performance than a classic method but also quantitatively indicates the binding affinity of transcription factor binding sites by prediction scores. The prediction result included not only the most knowns regulated genes but also 1089 novel CRP-regulated genes. The major regulatory roles of CRPs were divided into four classes: carbohydrate metabolism, organic acid metabolism, nitrogen compound metabolism and cellular transport. Several novel functions were also discovered, including heterocycle metabolic and response to stimulus. Based on the functional similarity of homologous CRPs, we applied the model to 35 other species. The prediction tool and the prediction results are online and are available at: https://awi.cuhk.edu.cn/∼CRPBSFinder.


## INTRODUCTION
  Cyclic AMP receptor proteins (CRPs), also known as catabolite gene activators, are important transcription factors (TF) in Escherichia coli and other species. As the first purified [1] and crystallized [2] TF in E. coli, CRPs have been extensively studied. CRPs belong to the CRP/FNR TF superfamily [3]. The CRP dimer is activated after the binding of two cAMP molecules to the effector-binding domain of each subunit. This bond causes a conformational change in DNA-binding domains, allowing each to bind to half of a specific pseudo-palindromic DNA sequence in the promoters of genes [4]. Upon binding, a CRP interacts with the C-terminal domain of the alpha subunit of an RNA polymerase, affecting the binding of the RNA polymerase to a promoter and leading to a change in the transcription initiation rate of a target gene [5–9]. The mechanism of CRP regulation is mainly divided into three classes. The CRP is classified as Class I when its binding site is located at position −61.5 of a promoter [8, 10, 11]. If the binding site of CRP is located at position −41.5 of the promoter, this CRP is classified as Class II [8, 10, 11]. If more than one CRP regulates the same promoter, the CRPs are classified as Class III [8, 11]. These well-known class models are collectively called the three-class rule [10]. However, further studies on CRPs have shown that the three-class rule is not appropriate for defining the regulatory distance from a CRP-binding site to a promoter.

  Through decades of research, numerous CRPs with regulatory roles in E. coli have been identified. Experimental results were collected from several literature-based databases on E. coli, such as RegulonDB [12] and EcoCyc [13]. In RegulonDB, 280 CRP-binding sites and 400 transcription units have been experimentally identified in E. coli [12]. Biological processes under the regulation of CRP are highly diverse, including energy metabolism [14, 15], cell division and development [16], toxin production [17] and stress regulation [18].

  In recent years, genome-wide TF-binding site detection technology (experiment) has proposed the analysis of CRPs in E. coli. For example, previous research improved the systematic evolution of ligands by exponential enrichment (SELEX) system, scanned the genome of E. coli, and identified at least 183 novel regulatory target operons. It proposed a small regulatory network of CRPs. Further, it discussed the key regulatory role of CRPs in the selective transport of carbon sources and the switch of glycolysis and gluconeogenesis to metabolism downstream of glycolysis [19]. Other research groups conducted a similar analysis and used Chip-exo [20]. However, these experiments were defective because of inaccuracy caused by experimental bias and low sensitivity in detecting weak binding sites.

  Some recent bioinformatics research also contributed to the CRP research field. Our previous study used machine learning methods to build an accurate method called PredCRP for predicting and analyzing the regulatory roles (activation and repression) of CRP from sequences of CRP-binding sites. [21]. One previous research predicted and analyzed the CRP-binding sites and regulons in 12 sequenced cyanobacterial genomes using a modified PWM-based method [22]. Another research analyzed the regulons of E. coli based on experimental data. They found seven global regulatory TFs in E. coli, including CRP [23]. However, these prediction methods merely provided some improvements based on position-weighted matrix methods, which were limited to discovering flexible binding sites. A comprehensive and systematic genome-scale method for predicting potential CRP-binding sites and their binding affinity still needs to be improved.

  CRP-binding site identification is a special case of the protein–ligand binding prediction task. Machine learning and deep learning promoted protein–ligand prediction in recent years [24]. A great many of these works utilized machine learning methods and energy functions to solve the protein–ligand binding prediction task [25, 26]. In this research, we developed a novel CRP-binding site prediction model called CRPBSFinder, which combined the hidden Markov model (HMM), knowledge-based position-weighted matrixes (PWMs) and structure-based binding affinity matrixes. We utilized this model to identify the CRP-binding sites across 36 species including E. coli. Furthermore, we systematically analyzed the regulatory role and regulatory mechanism of CRP in E. coli. By applying this model, we not only provide abundant cross-species CRP-binding site prediction information but also summarize changes in the CRPs’ regulatory role according to their binding affinity in E. coli. In future research, this prediction model can be extended to more TFs in other species.


## RESULTS

### Overview of the study
  An overview of this study is shown in Figure 1, which includes four parts: model construction, model evaluation, comprehensive analysis of CRPs in E. coli and an online database or tool containing the model and CRP-binding site prediction results of 36 species.


### Evaluation of the quantitative model
  CRPBSFinder can identify CRP-binding sites with quantitative affinity scores. Binding scores between 5.5 and 6.0 are considered low affinity and doubtful identification results, and binding scores over 6.0 are considered trustful binding sites that regulate nearby genes. We predicted 5091 confident CRP-binding sites with a score of ≥6.0. Among these CRP-binding sites, there are 868 binding sites with a score of ≥7.0, which are considered high-confidence and high-affinity binding sites (Table 1). The prediction results were compared with experimentally validated CRP-binding sites from the RegulonDB and SELEX systems [19]. A total of 280 CRP-binding sites were explored from RegulonDB, which were divided into 151 strong-evidence and 129 weak-evidence CRP-binding sites. Approximately 71.4% of all the CRP-binding sites were included in our results. The true positive rates (TPRs) for the prediction of strong- or weak-evidence CRP-binding sites were 80.8% and 60.5%, respectively. According to RegulonDB, weak evidence represents binding sites that have single evidence with more ambiguous conclusions, where alternative explanations, indirect effects or potential false positives are prevalent. And strong evidence means binding sites with direct physical interaction or solid genetic evidence with a low probability for alternative explanations. So, weak evidence data has a higher chance of being low affinity binding sites. Thus, the TPRs of our model for strong- and weak-evidence datasets indicated the ability to detect high-affinity binding sites. Furthermore, the SELEX system detected 280 CRP-binding sites, and 85 of them were included in RegulonDB. The TPR for predicting detected CRP-binding sites in the SELEX system was 87.8%. Our predicted CRP-binding sites overlapped 98.8% of 85 CRP-binding sites included in RegulonDB, whereas 83% with those of 195 CRP-binding sites that were not included in RegulonDB.

  We further compared the model performance with that of other computational prediction methods. According to previous research, FIMO and MAST were effective tools for identifying single transcription factor binding sites (TFBS) among PWM-based tools [27, 28]. Therefore, FIMO and MAST were trained using the training dataset of CRPBSFinder. In recent years, transcription factor identification methods based on deep learning have also been hot spots. These deep-learning-based methods take ChIP-seq data as input and consider the TFBS prediction a binary classification task. DeepRAM is a flexible deep-learning-based TFBS prediction framework that can change the model structure and mimic other prediction methods [29]. HOCNN is another deep-learning-based TFBS prediction framework that employs a high-order encoding method to build high-order dependencies among nucleotides, and a multi-scale convolutional layer to capture the motif features of different lengths [30]. We constructed two deepRAM models with convolutional neural network (CNN) and recurrent neural network (RNN), and a HOCNN model for predicting CRP-binding sites, respectively. We trained and tested all models as described in the methods. The result was shown in Table 2 and Supplementary File 1. MAST achieved the best positive predicted value (PPV) but also got relatively low sensitivity (Sn) and geometric accuracy (ACCg). Similarly, FIMO performed good PPV but got the worst Sn and ACCg. The performance of deepRAM with CNN structure was better than RNN structure. And the performance of HOCNN was slightly lower than deepRAM with CNN structure. CRPBSFinder, with a score cut-off of 5.5, achieved the best Sn. The combination of CRPBSFinder and deepRAM can further improve performance. For those doubtful prediction results with a score between 5.5 and 6.0, we utilized the deepRAM prediction result to prove them further. The combined model reached the best ACCg performance. Overall, CRPBSFinder can perform better than FIMO and RNN-based deepRAM. The combination of CRPBSFinder and CNN-based deepRAM can further improve computational performance.

  In addition to good prediction performance, a significant strength of CRPBSFinder was the quantitative score represented the binding affinity. Thus, to further verify the correlation between the prediction score and the binding affinity of CRP-binding sites, electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSAs) were designed (Figure 2). At the same CRP concentration, if the number of CRPs successfully bound to DNA increases, CRP-binding sites can be considered to have a high binding affinity. To verify the correlation between predicted scores and combining ability and ensure the rationality of the score cut-off selection, we selected 10 CRP-binding sites from 10 genes based on the predicted score distribution. The experiment result showed that the number of CRP–cAMP complexes can bind to DNA fragments carrying predicted CRP-binding sequences with low CRP concentrations as the prediction score of CRPBSFinder increases. However, the prediction scores of deepRAM cannot map to the experiment result. The experiment result proved the correlation between the CRPBSFinder prediction score and the binding affinity of the CRP-binding sites. According to the experiment result, we kept the model predicted result with a score over 6.0 as trustful binding sites for further bioinformatics analysis.


### Consensus recognition sequences of CRPs in differential datasets
  The consensus recognition sequences of CRPs have been proposed after sequence analysis of the hitherto identified targets [31]. Weblogo [32] was used in visualizing motifs. We identified a 22 bp sequence CRP motif for all 5179 CRP-binding sites (Figure 3A). The motif of a CRP was 5'-N3-TGTGA-N6-TCACA-N3-3'. The G at position 8 and C at position 16 were the most conserved positions. The motif logos of CRP-binding sequences in RegulonDB (Figure 3B) and from the SELEX system (Figure 3C) were also determined. The conservation level of each position in the conserved ranges had the same tendency as our motif, indicating the accuracy of our quantitative model. We were also interested in the logos of other CRP-binding sequence sets, such as the 48 CRP-binding sites that had weak evidence in RegulonDB but were not included in the model. All these motif logos are provided in the Supplementary Figure S1) along with their sequences (Supplementary File 2). The distribution of CRP-binding sites in the genome was also discussed in Supplementary Data and Figure S2.


### Functional classification of CRP regulons in E. coli
  To elucidate the regulatory roles of CRP, we generated 101 biological pathways regulated by CRP in E. coli (Figure 4). The pathways were divided into 41 novel biological pathways represented by yellow and 60 known biological pathways represented by green. All genes related to novel biological pathways were predicted to be regulated by CRP generated from CRPBSFinder and not included in RegulonDB. Furthermore, 66 of 101 biological pathways were classified into seven functional groups: Group 1, which regulated transmembrane transport, was separated from other functions, and contained 11 biological pathways. Groups 2–7 formed a network with the functions of the carboxylic acid catabolic process, carbohydrate metabolic process, alpha-amino acid metabolic process, organonitrogen compound biosynthetic process, heterocycle metabolic process and response to a stimulus, respectively; Groups 1–4 mainly contained known biological pathways, considered as known functions; and Groups 5–7 mainly contained novel biological pathways. A Supplementary Figure S3 shows a subgraph providing details about several novel CRP-regulated pathways and genes. Details about the global regulatory network and CRP-regulated genes are provided in Supplementary Files 3 and 4.


### Regulatory roles of CRPs vary with their binding affinity scores
  The major regulatory role of CRPs was summarized using different prediction score cut-offs (Figure 5). The CRP-binding sites with scores of over nine were considered top-rank high-affinity binding sites. CRPs can easily bind to the binding sites, which have low CRP and cAMP concentrations. The genes regulated by these binding sites are mainly involved in carbohydrate and nitrogen compound metabolic processes. These two functions were considered the most essential regulatory roles of the CRP. As scores decreased, two other important functions of CRP regulation also emerged: the metabolism of organic acid and the process related to cell transport. We also found that CRP regulates the phosphorus metabolic process and response to stimulus, which were not known to be the primary regulatory role of CRP. The subgraph networks for each score cutoff and RegulonDB, as provided in Supplementary File 5. We also discussed the co-regulation machines of CRP with other factors in Supplementary Data. CRPs co-regulated with sigma 70, sigma 32 and sigma 24. The distribution of CRP-binding sites with different scores was highly related to these 3 sigma factors (Figures S4 and S5). The main functions of sigma 24 and sigma 32 corresponded to several novel CRP-regulated roles in this research. We also found that CRPs co-regulated with FNR, Fis, ArcA, Fur, IHF, NarL and Lrp (Figures S6 and S7). Particularly, we summarized five regulatory modules of CRP and FNR (Table S1).


### Experiment validation of genes of interest
  To prove the accuracy of the CRP-binding sites predicted by our quantitative model and determine the predicted regulatory relation between CRPs and genes, we selected some predicted CRP-regulated genes, identified the CRP-binding interaction on the predicted regions of these genes, and validated the regulatory relation between CRPs and these genes. We selected htpG, purH and entF as our genes of interest, which participated in the cellular response to DNA damage stimulus, de novo IMP biosynthetic process and enterobactin biosynthetic process, respectively. All the CRP-binding sites of these genes have not been experimentally identified until now.

  Electrophoretic mobility shift assays were performed first to test the interaction between CRPs and sequential fragments located from a defined range relative to the transcription start sites of the genes of interest. In a typical procedure, fragments carrying the predicted CRP-binding regions of the genes of interest were synthesized for the confirmation of the binding ability of the CRPs. Retarded migrating bands became evident with increasing CRP concentrations (Figure S8). All the CRP-binding sites were significantly confirmed by experimental results. The corresponding relationship between the experimental results and the prediction score further proved the ability of the model to identify the binding affinity. Real-time PCR was performed to validate the regulatory relation between CRPs and genes of interest and measure the mRNA expression levels of htpG, purH and entF in the wild-type and CRP-deletion mutant cells. We found that the expression levels of htpG, purH and entF were repressed by CRPs. The abundance of purH and entF transcript increased twofold, whereas that of htpG increased fourfold in the absence of CRPs. These results further confirmed the accuracy of the quantitative model.


### Prediction of CRP-binding sites on 35 species and construction of online tools and databases
  After systematically analyzing CRP-binding sites in E. coli, we applied the model to 35 species with highly conserved CRP function domains. Previous research shows that CRP-binding site identification model can be extended to other species with orthologous CRP [22]. All these 35 species have orthologous CRPs with E. coli according to the Database of Clusters of Orthologous Genes and Conserved Domains Database [33, 34]. The statistic of the prediction results in these species is provided in Supplementary File 6. Nineteen of the 35 species were considered common pathogens. The genome length, the total number of genes, and predicted CRP-binding sites under different score cutoffs were calculated. The result shows that CRP-binding sites appeared in different species. However, the regulatory ability of CRPs is diverse in different species. If we assume that the regulatory regions of CRPs in all the species are similar to those in E. coli, the percentage of a predicted regulated gene ranges from 16% to 65%. Nearly half of the genes in E. coli were predicted to be regulated by CRPs. All the prediction results are available online in the genome browser. The CRPBSFinder model was also online available on the same website (Figure 6).


## DISCUSSION
  Classical PWM-based motif discovery methods have been used for decades, and novel methods have been developed. Hidden Markov model-based methods are next-generation motif discovery methods [35], which have better performance than classical methods and can predict binding affinity. In this research, our model not only included HMMs but also included PWMs and the binding affinity energy matrix, which can further increase the ability of the model to predict binding affinity, as shown in the experiment result in Figure 2.

  In the evaluation of the quantitative model shown in the result, several CRP-binding sites in RegulonDB were not included in our model. To determine such inconsistency, we plotted the motif logos of 26 unpredicted CRP-binding sites with strong evidence (Figure S1b) and 48 CRP-binding sites with weak evidence (Figure S1d). The conservation level of the conserved range in the strong-evidence motif logo was extremely weak, and the T at position 15 disappeared. As for the weak-evidence motif logo, no conserved range was found. Thus, these unpredicted CRP-binding sites in RegulonDB failed to comply with the traditional CRP-binding motif, indicating that these CRP-binding sites had a too-low affinity.

  Although our predicted CRP logo was similar to the logo of a CRP detected by the SELEX system, the inconsistency between the results of our model and the SELEX system remains to be discussed. First, we plotted the motif logo of the predicted high-affinity CRP-binding sites with scores of ≥7.0 (Figure S1e). The logo was extremely similar to the logo of CRP-binding sequences detected by the SELEX system. However, 33 CRPs were detected by the SELEX system that was not included in our model. The logo of these CRP-binding sequences (Figure S1g) differed from traditional motif logos. The T at position 4 and G at position 5 disappeared, and the T at position 15 and the A at position 17 were extremely small. However, the G at position 7 and the C at position 16 were highly conserved. This phenomenon indicated that the G at position 7 and the C at position 16 played a key role in the binding of CRPs, as suggested by a previous study [19]. Thus, we further adjusted our model to recognize these CRP targets.

  Except for the binding features of CRPs, this research attempted to comprehensively describe the biological pathways regulated by CRPs in E. coli. We proposed seven major functions regulated by CRPs in E. coli. CRPs mainly act as the regulators of energy utilization in E. coli [5, 10, 19, 36, 37]. The known function from Groups 1–4 mainly represents the energy utilization function. For example, Group 1 contains the transport of energy molecules. Moreover, CRPs can regulate ptsG [38], which encodes the major glucose transporter IICB in E. coli [39]. We found that CRPs can regulate the cation transmembrane transport in E. coli by regulating seven genes, namely, brnQ, nhaA, livK, mgtA, leuE, livF and trkA. Another research proposed several major CRP-regulated energy metabolism biological pathways, including the transport of carbon sources, carbon metabolism, aerobic respiration, glycolysis regulation and gluconeogenesis [19]. All these functions were included in our global regulatory function network (Figure 4), and the genes reported in previous research were also covered by our network (Supplementary File 5). Thus, these previous studies proved that our model and research were comprehensive enough to cover known CRP-regulated functions.

  We discovered three classes of novel functions regulated by CRPs: organonitrogen compound biosynthesis, cellular nitrogen compound metabolism and response to a stimulus. Most biological pathways included in these functional classes have not been systematically researched. One research analyzed nitration assimilation in E. coli by placing molecular data in a system perspective [40]. They found that CRPs play a key role in nitrogen assimilation. Some studies found a relation between CRPs and response to stress. The transcriptomic analysis of CRP-dependent catabolite control in E. coli unexpectedly showed that CRPs can regulate response to various types of stress, including several cold and heat shock proteins [18]. This research confirmed these novel biological pathways. The novel regulated genes were also selected to be confirmed by experiments. The three selected genes of interest, htpG, purH and entF, are regulated by CRPs, as indicated by real-time PCR and EMSA results. Thus, our quantitative model and analysis can accurately identify all genes regulated by CRPs and their functions. The biological pathways in E. coli were comprehensively analyzed, and some potential genes can be located for further study.

  The regulatory roles of CRPs in E. coli change with CRP and cAMP concentrations. At low CRP and cAMP concentrations, the cAMP–CRP complex can only bind to high-affinity CRP-binding sites. The functions of genes regulated by these CRPs can be considered the essential regulatory roles of CRPs in E. coli. As shown in Figure 5 and Supplementary File 5, CRPs mainly regulate carbohydrate and nitrogen compound metabolic processes. Carbohydrate and galactose transport is considered a core regulatory role of CRPs. If the normal situation in E. coli is considered, CRPs mainly regulate cellular carbohydrate, monosaccharide, organic acid and nitrogen compound metabolic processes and carbohydrate or transmembrane transport. As for low-affinity CRP-binding sites, they may only be bound at high cAMP and CRP concentrations. In this situation, CRPs may enable E. coli to respond to stimuli and generate precursor metabolites and energy.

  Novel CRP regulatory mechanism hypotheses can be generated by analyzing the prediction results of CRP-binding sites in E. coli. For instance, lacZ is a well-known gene in E. coli regulated by CRPs. A CRP-binding site located at −61.5 to lacZp1 was recognized as the main regulator of lacZ. This binding site was predicted as a high-affinity binding site with a score of 9.22. However, in recent years, another CRP-binding site located at 11.5 to lacZp1 was accepted and collected by Ecocyc and RegulonDB. This binding site was predicted as a low-affinity binding site with a score of 6.06, indicating the reliability of the low-affinity prediction result. Based on the prediction result, this dual regulation mode of CRPs might be widely present in E. coli. Escherichia coli might have more complex self-regulatory and adaptation mechanisms than we expected.

  Finally, the prediction model was extended to 36 species, which contain CRP homologous functional domains. Although CRP-binding sites wildly exist in the genomes of all the species, the ratios of the total number of binding sites to genome length are diverse in different species. Fission yeast was predicted to have the most abundant CRP-binding sites, which indicates that CRPs may play an important role in fission yeast. The percentages of genes regulated by CRPs are diverse in different species. Staphylococcus aureus was predicted to have the most number of genes regulated by CRPs. All the prediction results were collected to construct an online database and prepared for further analysis.


## CONCLUSIONS
  This research constructed a quantitative CRP-binding site identification model and provided a trusted dataset of identified CRP-binding sites with binding affinity scores in 36 species that contain CRP proteins. The model predicted 5179 high-quality CRP-binding sites in E. coli, and model performance was evaluated with computational and experimental methods. The evaluation result shows that the model not only can provide higher prediction performance than a classic method but also indicates the binding affinity of the TFBSs based on prediction scores. Based on the prediction result of E. coli, the major regulatory roles and novel functions of CRPs were discussed according to the different binding affinity cutoffs. The co-regulation mechanisms of CRPs with other biological components were also discussed. Thus, the quantitative prediction model and comprehensive analysis in this paper can enhance understanding of the role and mechanism of CRPs in E. coli. The model was further applied to 35 other species containing homologous CRPs. All the prediction data and the prediction model are available online. The trust dataset can be further analyzed, and the model construction method can be applied to TFs in other species.


## METHODS

### Materials
  The experimental datasets were collected from RegulonDB (release: 10.6.2) [12]. The collected datasets included TF Binding Sites, which included all the TF binding sites’ names, positions, sequences, regulated promoters and experiment confidence levels; E. coli K-12 genome sequence; All Promoters, which included all the promoters’ names, positions, and related sigma factors; and Transcription Units, which included corresponding promoters, transcription units (TU), and genes included in the TUs. For the TF-Binding Sites dataset, we collected CRP-binding site sequences in RegulonDB (release: 6.3) as a training dataset and kept the rest as a testing dataset. The CRP dataset from SELEX screening results was used as another training dataset [19]. All 17 double-strand DNA binding CRP crystallization structures in E. coli were retrieved from Protein Data Bank (PDB) [41]. The PDB ids were 5CIZ, 3N4M, 3MZH, 1ZRF (forward and reverse stand separately), 1ZRE, 1ZRC, 1RUO, 1RUN, 1O3T, 1O3S, 1O3R, 1O3Q, 1LB2, 1J59 and 1CPG.


### Construction of the CRP-binding site prediction model and annotation of the genomic information with predicted CRPs in E. coli
  We presented a prediction ensemble based on the HMM, PWM and structure-based energy matrix (Figure 7). Four training datasets were generated to construct the HMM models [42] and the MATCH models [43]. Two 22mer CRP-binding site datasets were extracted from RegulonDB (release: 6.3) and SELEX screening results. These datasets were further aligned with CLUSTALW [44] and trimmed or removed excess or low-quality sequences to form the other two datasets. Due to model characteristics, aligned datasets were only used to train HMMs.

### Evaluation of the model
  The model was evaluated using dry and wet laboratory methods. To estimate the TPR of the model, we calculated the true positive (TP) of the model on the datasets from RegulonDB with weak evidence, RegulonDB with strong evidence, and previous SELEX experiment result [19].

  Then, the performance of CRPBSFinder was compared with FIMO, MAST, HOCNN and deepRAM. All models were trained with a similar training dataset. FIMO and MAST were trained by default parameters. The parameter of deepRAM with CNN structure was: ‘python deepRAM.py --train_data crp/train.gz --test_data crp/test.gz --data_type DNA --train True --evaluate_performance True --model_path cnn_DeepBind.pkl --out_file crp/prediction.txt --Embedding False --Conv Ture --RNN False.’ And the parameter of deepRAM with RNN structure was: ‘python deepRAM.py --train_data crp/train.gz --test_data crp/test.gz --data_type DNA --train True --evaluate_performance True --model_path cnn_DeepBind.pkl --out_file crp/prediction2.txt --Embedding False --Conv False --RNN True.’ And the command of HOCNN was: ‘python HOCNN.py train.tsv train_target.tsv train.h5 -k 2’ We created a testing dataset that contains 379 sequences with 101 bases in length. The positive samples were collected from the testing dataset split from RegulonDB, and the negative samples were randomly generated. We evaluated the performance of different models on the test dataset. ### Analysis of biological pathways regulated by CRPs
  The regulatory roles of CRPs in E. coli should be understood. Based on the predicted CRP set, a comprehensive regulatory network of CRPs was generated.

  Gene ontology (GO) [50] and DAVID Bioinformatics Resources [51] were used as gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) tools for the analysis of biological pathways regulated by CRPs. We analyzed the predicted CRP-regulated gene sets and experimentally confirmed CRP-regulated gene sets and compared the GO terms obtained from each set. The genes corresponding to common GO terms were called known genes, and the genes corresponding to GO terms only in the predicted set were called novel genes. We further applied GSEA on known and novel genes. The enrichment statistics method was the Benjamini procedure, and the threshold of EASE score or P-value was set at 0.1. The enriched GO terms were collected and included in known and novel function sets to represent the major biological pathways regulated by CRPs in E. coli.

  Furthermore, we classified these GO terms to obtain a general view of biological pathways regulated by CRPs in E. coli. The GO terms with the same ancestor were included in the same group. We used Quick GO [52] to trace back the ancestors of each GO term in each function set. Given the balance between the specificity of biological significance and coverage of total GO terms, we selected level 3 or 4 GO terms as the target class terms. We then built a graph visualized by Cytoscape [53] to connect selected ancestor GO terms to their children’s GO terms. The detailed information on the graph can be found in Supplementary File 3.

  To further determine the details of the CRP regulatory role in the novel functions, we used the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) [54] and DAVID as GSEA tools and applied similar analysis on novel genes. We then generated a graph that connected KEGG pathways and genes by Cytoscape (Figure S3).


### Extension of the model to 35 species and construction of the online webserver
  Based on the functional similarities of homologous proteins, we applied the model to 35 other species with highly conserved CRP function domains from National Center for Biotechnology Information. The prediction results were performed on the online genome browser. We then constructed an online version of the prediction tool, which allowed users to input their sequences of interest in FASTA form and provided prediction results.
