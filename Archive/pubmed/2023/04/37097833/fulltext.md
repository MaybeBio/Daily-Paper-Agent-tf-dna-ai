# Stapled Peptides
as Direct Inhibitors of Nrf2-sMAF
Transcription Factors


## Abstract
  

  Nuclear factor erythroid-related 2-factor 2 (Nrf2) is a transcription factor traditionally thought of as a cellular protector. However, in many cancers, Nrf2 is constitutively activated and correlated with therapeutic resistance. Nrf2 heterodimerizes with small musculoaponeurotic fibrosarcoma Maf (sMAF) transcription factors, allowing binding to the antioxidant responsive element (ARE) and induction of transcription of Nrf2 target genes. While transcription factors are historically challenging to target, stapled peptides have shown great promise for inhibiting these protein–protein interactions. Herein, we describe the first direct cell-permeable inhibitor of Nrf2/sMAF heterodimerization. N1S is a stapled peptide designed based on AlphaFold predictions of the interactions between Nrf2 and sMAF MafG. A cell-based reporter assay combined with in vitro biophysical assays demonstrates that N1S directly inhibits Nrf2/MafG heterodimerization. N1S treatment decreases the transcription of Nrf2-dependent genes and sensitizes Nrf2-dependent cancer cells to cisplatin. Overall, N1S is a promising lead for the sensitization of Nrf2-addicted cancers.


## Introduction
  The Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1/nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Keap1/Nrf2) system is a chemical sensor that controls the cellular response to oxidative stress.1−3 When cells are not stressed, Keap1 binds to Nrf2, resulting in the ubiquitination and degradation of Nrf2. When electrophiles or reactive oxygen species are present, cysteines in Keap1 are modified, resulting in the disruption of the DLGex Keap1 interaction,4,5 stabilization of Nrf2, translocation of Nrf2 to the nucleus, and heterodimerization of Nrf2 with transcription factor small musculoaponeurotic fibrosarcoma Maf (sMAF) proteins (i.e., MafG, MafK, or MafF).6−8 The complex binds the antioxidant response element (ARE) sequence and induces transcription of genes including detoxification enzymes, antioxidant proteins, and genes that promote aerobic glycolysis.7−11 In many lung cancers, as well as head-and-neck,12 pancreatic,13 and breast cancers, there is constitutive activation of Nrf2 via mutations in Keap1, mutations in Nrf2, or other mechanisms.7,14−22 Constitutively high levels of Nrf2 result in enhanced chemotherapeutic resistance of cancer cells as well as re-engineering of metabolic pathways for increased cancer cell proliferation.11 Due to these advantages, cancers with constitutive activation of Nrf2 often develop Nrf2 addiction. Patients bearing Nrf2-addicted tumors face poorer prognoses and intrinsic resistance to chemotherapy, targeted anticancer agents, and radiation.7,15,17,23,24 The genetic knockdown of Nrf2, both in vitro and in vivo, results in reduced cancer cell proliferation and increased sensitivity to anticancer agents, suggesting that inhibitors of Nrf2 would be effective anticancer agents.7,25,26

  Nrf2 forms heterodimers with sMAF proteins in the nucleus via their α-helical basic leucine zipper (bZIP) regions.27 This heterodimeric complex is responsible for the transcription of Nrf2-dependent genes like antioxidant proteins and oncogenes.28,29 A few Nrf2 inhibitors have been reported, including the small molecules brusatol,30 AEM1,31 and ML385.32 While these molecules suppress gene expression in cell culture, they do not directly inhibit the Nrf2-sMAF interaction.33−35 Two peptide-based inhibitors, an Nrf2 stapled peptide for inhibiting the Nrf2/DNA interaction36 and a heterodimeric peptide inhibitor of the NRF2-MAFG-DNA ternary complex,37 have been developed. These have either not been tested for activity in cells or are not cell permeable. The discovery of a nontoxic, direct inhibitor of the Nrf2/MafG interaction would enable the development of cancer treatments that are more efficient with reduced chemotherapeutic drug resistance.

  Stapled peptides (short α-helical peptides stabilized by a hydrocarbon staple) have shown significant promise for specifically inhibiting protein–protein interactions, with two peptides currently in clinical trials.38−42 For example, ALRN-6924, a p53-MDM2/MDMX dual inhibitor stapled peptide, has completed Phase 1 clinical trials for advanced solid tumors and lymphomas, where it showed good tolerability and promising efficacy.43 Stapled peptides are better able to target protein–protein interactions compared to small molecules due in part to their larger size. Additionally, they have improved cell permeability and oral bioavailability when compared to biologics.39−42 In the current study, we developed N1S, a potent and direct hydrocarbon stapled peptide inhibitor of the Nrf2 and MafG interaction and demonstrated its ability to sensitize Nrf2-addicted cancer to cisplatin.


## Results and Discussion

### Design of Stapled Peptide Nrf2 Inhibitors
  Nrf2 interacts with sMAFs (MafG, MafK, and MafF) via their α-helical bZIP prior to binding the ARE. Specifically, the NESzip Neh2 domain of Nrf2 (1L2L3N4L5L6) interacts with the bZIP domain of sMAFs (L1L2L3M4L5L6 for MafG, Figure 1A).27 We aimed to design stapled peptides based on Nrf2 sequences, with the goal of identifying direct inhibitors of Nrf2-sMAF heterodimerization. We chose to focus on Nrf2 peptides rather than MafG peptides because Nrf2 is known to be unstructured when not bound to either Keap1 or sMAFs. Unfortunately, no crystal structures of the Nrf2/MafG interaction exist to guide the design of the Nrf2 peptides. Therefore, we utilized AlphaFold to predict the structure of the Nrf2/MafG heterodimer (Figure 1B). The structure predicted by AlphaFold agreed well with previous studies that demonstrated experimentally the necessity of the bZIP domains for the interaction of Nrf2 with sMAFs such as MafG.28 We used the AlphaFold model to determine the best region to target for inhibition. Specifically, we designed three 16 amino acid peptides that cover the predicted binding domain. The length of the peptides (16 amino acids) was based on other cell-permeable stapled peptides.39,42,44−46 We also used the model to determine the best locations for staples, specifically focusing on locations unlikely to interrupt the binding of the Nrf2 peptides to MafG (Figures 1B,C and S1). We then synthesized these peptides using standard Fmoc-based solid-phase peptide synthesis followed by Grubbs metathesis to generate the stapled peptides. Circular dichroism measurements indicated that all stapled peptides had higher α-helicity compared to their corresponding unstapled peptides (Figure S2A). Additionally, they had greatly increased protease stability compared to the unstapled peptides (Figure S2B). Among the stapled peptides, N3S is the most stable, with no significant degradation for up to 24 h, followed by N1S, showing some degradation at 24 h. N2S is the least stable stapled peptide showing significant degradation starting at 40 min.


### Screening Peptides and Derivatives for Activity
  The peptides were screened for Nrf2 inhibition using a previously developed dual-luciferase assay.30 Of all of the peptides tested, N1S showed the most potent inhibition of Nrf2 activity while showing no toxicity to the cells (Figures S2A−C and S3A). Unstapled N1 showed no significant activity, likely due to its lower helicity, increased rate of proteolysis, and its lower cell permeability, which was determined using fluorophore-labeled versions of these peptides and confocal microscopy to determine the abilities of these peptides to enter cells (Figure 2A–C). Interestingly, neither the stapled nor unstapled versions of N2 or N3 showed significant activity. In the case of N3S, this was likely due, at least in part, to poor cell permeability. Both N1S and N2S showed good cellular and nuclear permeability. Given the differences in activity between N1S and N2S, this suggests that N2S may not engage MafG as efficiently as N1S. Because N1S showed the most promise, we evaluated it for the dose response (Figure 2B). It showed a classical dose response for Nrf2 inhibition without showing toxicity. Excitingly, the luciferase response shown by N1S was much stronger than the response shown by ML385,32 one of the few reported Nrf2 inhibitors to date. With these data in hand, we decided to explore derivatives of N1S. We specifically explored 16 derivatives with varying staple positions. We also developed an N1S derivative where the binding residues expected to be key for binding (bold residues in Figure 1A) were substituted with alanines (N1SM). These residues have previously been proposed to be important for the binding of MafG to Nrf2,27 suggesting that replacement with alanines should decrease the binding affinity of the stapled peptide for MafG and thus lower activity in the luciferase reporter assay.

  Several of the N1S derivatives showed activity, but none of them had better activity than N1S (Figure S3B,C). As expected, N1SM showed no significant inhibition at all concentrations tested (Figure S3D). With this data in hand, we went forward with exploring the mechanism of N1S.


### Validation that N1S Directly Targets MafG
  To determine whether N1S directly inhibits the NRF2-sMAF-ARE association, we performed a fluorescence polarization assay with Nrf2, MafG, and fluorophore-labeled ARE (Figure S4A). N1S decreased the binding of the heterodimer to DNA at concentrations as low as 0.2 μM, suggesting that the decrease in luciferase signal seen in the cell-based luciferase assay is due to the direct disruption of the Nrf2-MafG-DNA ARE complex. Based on our design, we expect that N1S directly inhibits the heterodimerization of Nrf2 with sMAFs including MafG. A split-luciferase assay confirmed this, with N1S inhibiting dimerization of Nrf2 with MafG in vitro in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 3A). To show the direct interaction between N1S and MafG, an immunoprecipitation assay was carried out with His-tagged purified MafG and the biotin-labeled N1S peptide (Figure S4B,C). N1S-biotin successfully interacted with MafG, and this interaction could be completed by adding a 20-fold unlabeled N1S peptide, providing strong evidence that MafG is specifically binding to the N1S peptide. Biotin-labeled N2S and N3S were also explored to determine whether they were capable of interacting with MafG (Figure S4B,C). Interestingly, no binding to MafG was observed with either N2S or N3S, suggesting that their lack of activity in the reporter assay was due to their inability to bind MafG. To determine the affinity of binding between MafG and N1S, surface plasmon resonance (SPR) was performed with biotin-labeled N1S and His-MafG. Using a one-site binding model, the dissociation constant was found to be 337 nM (Figures 3B and S4D). To further validate the biotin pull-down results, SPR was carried out using biotin-labeled N3S and N1SM with His-MafG, as well as biotin-labeled N1S with bovine serum albumin (Figure S4E–G). No binding between MafG and biotin-labeled N3S was observed at all tested concentrations of MafG, confirming that the lack of activity of N3S is due to its inability to bind to MafG. For N1SM, we saw a significantly lower binding affinity compared to N1S (>3-fold increase in KD), further supporting the importance of the bolded residues in Figure 1A for the interaction with MafG bZIP. Finally, no binding was observed between biotin-labeled N1S and bovine serum albumin, providing evidence that N1S does not bind nonspecifically to proteins. Collectively, this data highly suggests that N1S directly inhibits Nrf2/sMAF heterodimerization by directly binding the sMAF proteins.

  After confirming the in vitro activity of N1S, we chose to further explore its mechanism in a cellular context. A549 cells treated with N1S for 72 h showed a significant decrease in mRNA levels for multiple Nrf2-regulated genes including the multidrug resistance-associated protein (MRP1) gene ABCC1, the thioredoxin gene TXN, the quinone reductase gene NQO2, the heme oxygenase 1 (HO-1) gene HMOX1, and the glutamate-cysteine ligase catalytic subunit gene GCLC (Figure 3C). Western blots were carried out in the A549 non-small-cell lung cancer cell line to show the effects of N1S inhibition on the protein expression of the Nrf2-regulated protein, thioredoxin reductase 1 (TrxR1) (Figure 3D). As expected, a decrease in expression levels was observed. To further ensure the biological relevance of these results, we quantified glutathione (GSH) levels, which are largely controlled by Nrf2-inducing transcription of proteins such as γ-glutamyl cysteine ligase and the Xc-system. In N1S-treated cells, significant reductions in glutathione levels were observed after 24 h of treatment (Figure 3E). The decrease in GSH observed was similar to the decrease observed when A549 cells were treated with AEM1.47 Overall, these data are in strong support that N1S is directly inhibiting the Nrf2/MafG interaction and their downstream activation of transcription of their gene targets.


### Sensitization of Cancer Cells to Chemotherapeutics
  Given the ability of N1S to inhibit Nrf2 transcription of target genes, we would expect them to increase sensitivity to chemotherapeutics. To explore this, we chose to examine the growth of A549 non-small-cell lung cancer cells treated with cisplatin alone, N1S alone, or in combination. Growth was assessed using an MTT assay, and synergy was determined using the Chou–Talalay method (Figure 4A,B). The data points suggest that synergy is observed with relatively low concentrations of cisplatin and varying concentrations of N1S. These data were further confirmed using live-cell analysis, which showed similar results with 6 μM cisplatin in combination with 50 μM N1S showing greatly reduced levels of growth compared to either treatment alone. (Figure 4C). Overall, these results demonstrate that N1S can greatly sensitize cancer cell lines to chemotherapeutics. Gratifyingly, N1S also shows no toxicity to red blood cells (0.3 ± 1% hemolysis at 100 μM), providing further support for it as a lead to sensitize Nrf2-dependent cancers to chemotherapeutics.


## Conclusions
  Approximately 90% of failures in chemotherapy treatments are due to drug resistance.48 While many are exploring the development of novel chemotherapeutics to overcome this resistance, another more direct method is to inhibit mechanisms of resistance. Nrf2 is often constitutively activated in solid tumors and many of its downstream genes are responsible for chemotherapy resistance. Therefore, Nrf2 is a good target for resistance. However, as with other transcription factors, it is challenging to target with traditional small molecules. Herein, we developed the first direct inhibitor of heterodimerization of Nrf2 with its partner MafG. Specifically, we used AlphaFold models to design stapled peptides to inhibit this heterodimerization and found a promising lead (N1S) that can enter cells and inhibit Nrf2 in luciferase reporter assays. In vitro analysis including fluorescence polarization, split luciferase, immunoprecipitation, and SPR demonstrates that N1S directly binds to MafG and inhibits its dimerization with Nrf2. Finally, studies of N1S in combination with cisplatin demonstrate strong synergy against A549 non-small-cell lung cancer cells. Overall, this work demonstrates the great promise of utilizing N1S as an inhibitor of Nrf2/MafG signaling to sensitize cancers to traditional chemotherapeutics.


## Experimental Section

### Instrumentation
  A PS3 peptide synthesizer (Gyros Protein Technologies) or a liberty blue 2.0 microwave peptide synthesizer (CEM) was used for peptide synthesis. An Agilent Technologies 1260 Infinity II preparative HPLC system was used for peptide purification. An Advion Expression CMS single quadrupole mass spectrometer and an RDa Waters UHPLC-mass spectrometer were used for peptide confirmation. A Jasco J-1500 Circular Dichroism spectrophotometer was used for circular dichroism experiments, and a Nikon A1 Confocal was used for imaging fluorescent peptides for cell permeability assays.


### AlphaFold Modeling
  Protein models were generated using Alphafold2 and Alphafold2-multimer. Sequence alignments were generated using MMseq. 2 and HHsearch.49 Query sequences were submitted to the server using colabFold, allowing for quick alignment and modeling.

### Cell Lines
  Cell lines A549 and Hek-293T were purchased from American Type Culture Collection. Hek-293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum, stable l-glutamine, and 1× nonessential amino acids. A549 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum and l-glutamine. Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2.


### Stapled Peptide and Linear Peptide Synthesis
  Fmoc-protected α-amino acids (other than olefinic amino acid Fmoc-(S)-2-amino-2-methyl-hept-6-enoic acid ((Fmoc-S5-OH)), which was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich) were purchased from ChemImpex. Rink Amide MBHA resin was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Dimethylformamide (DMF), N,N-diisopropylethylamine (DIEA), acetic anhydride, trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), dichloromethane (DCM), anisole, 1,2-dichloroethane (DCE), triisopropylsilane (TIPS), fluorescein isothiocyanate isomer 1 (FITC), biotin-OSu, and piperidine were purchased from Millipore Sigma and used as supplied. The Grubbs first-generation catalyst was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich.


### General Procedure for Resin Loading for Linear Peptide Synthesis
  Rink amide MBHA resin (1 g, 0.68 mmol g–1, 0.68 mmol, 100–200 mesh) was swelled in DMF for 30 min, drained, and treated with a solution of Fmoc-protected amino acid (2 mmol) and N,N′-dicyclohexylcarbodiimide (1 mmol) in DMF (11 mL). The resulting mixture was gently agitated for 2 h, after which the resin was filtered and washed with DMF (2 mL × 5 mL). The remaining unreacted amide groups were capped by agitating the resin with acetic anhydride and DIEA in 4.5 mL of DMF (0.5:0.5:9) for 20 min. The resin was filtered and washed with DCM (3 mL × 5 mL) and MeOH (3 mL × 5 mL) and dried under vacuum for 1 h. The resin loading was determined by treating an aliquot (1–3 mg) of the dried resin with piperidine-DMF (1:4) and observing the UV absorbance of the piperidine-dibenzofulvene adduct at 301 nm (ε = 7800 M–1 cm–1).50


### General Procedure for Automated Linear Peptide Synthesis
  Preloaded rink amide resin was used for all synthesis. Synthesis was performed as previously described.50


### General Procedure for Automated Stapled Peptide Synthesis
  Liberty blue 2.0 (CEM) was used for making the linear precursor along with performing ring-closing metathesis on the peptide while still on the rink amide solid support. For a 0.05 M scale, 0.2 M amino acids, 0.5 M DIC, 0.5 Oxyma, and 20% piperidine in DMF were used for synthesis. After the amino acids were assembled, the N-terminal amine was acetylated using a 10% solution of acetic anhydride in DMF. The peptide resin was cyclized in the presence of a 10 mM Grubbs catalyst in 1,2-dichloroethane. The ring-closing metathesis (RCM) reaction was verified by cleaving a small portion (∼1 mg) of the peptide from the resin and performing liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS). The resin-bound peptide was washed three times with DMF and three times with DCM and dried under vacuum.


### Cycles for Automated Peptide Synthesis on Liberty Blue
  The resin was swelled in DMF for 5 min, followed by Fmoc deprotection with 20% piperidine in DMF for 1 min at 90 °C. The first Fmoc amino acid was loaded on the resin with DIC (0.5 M) in DMF and Oxyma (0.5 M) in DMF, reacting for 2 min at 90 °C, followed by Fmoc deprotection with 20% piperidine in DMF and reacting for 1 min at 90 °C. The following Fmoc-protected amino acids (0.2 M) were coupled using DIC (0.5 M) in DMF and Oxyma (0.5 M) in DMF and deprotected with 20% piperidine in DMF following the same reaction conditions. Following final Fmoc deprotection, the N-terminus was acetylated using 20% Ac2O in DMF, reacting for 2 min at 60 °C. All Fmoc-Arg-OH and amino acids coupled following the stapling residues Fmoc-(S)-2-(4-pentenyl)alanine-OH were double-coupled (0.2 M × 2) at 90 °C. Ring-closing metathesis (RCM) was performed using the Grubbs I catalyst (10 mM) in DCE, reacting for 5 min at 90 °C. This was carried out twice. DMF and DCM washes were performed following each coupling, deprotection, acetylation, and RCM step.51

  Fmoc-Ahx-OH was coupled as a spacer to peptides subjected to FITC or biotin modification on the N-terminus instead of acetyl capping. FITC labeling was performed with a 4-fold excess of FITC and 8 equiv DIPEA in (3:1) DMF/DCM overnight. Biotin was coupled using 2 equiv biotin-OSu and 8 equiv DIPEA in (3:1) DMF/DCM overnight.


### General Procedure for Peptide Cleavage
  Peptides were deprotected and cleaved from the solid support by treatment with TFA/H2O/TIS (95:2.5:2.5 vol/vol) for 2 h at room temperature. After filtration of the resin, the TFA solution was evaporated, the peptide was precipitated by the addition of a cold diethyl methyl ether mixture and centrifuged, and the supernatant was removed. The pellet was then frozen and lyophilized to yield the desired product as a solid.50

  HPLC purification was performed on an Agilent Technologies 1260 Infinity II preparative HPLC system using a 1260 variable wavelength detector (measuring at 214 and 254 nm). A Luna Mega Polar C18, 5 μm, 150 mm × 21.2 mm column (Phenomenex) was used for purification. Solvent A: water with 0.05% TFA and solvent B: acetonitrile with 0.05% TFA. For the preparatory HPLC runs, two different methods were employed.

  Method A1 (purification of stapled and nonstapled peptides without FITC or biotin): (A:B, 20 mL min–1): 100:0, 0 min; 100:0, 3 min; 50:50, 25 min; 100:0, 25.01 min; 100:0, 30 min.

  Method A2 (purification of stapled and nonstapled peptides conjugated to biotin or FITC): (A:B, 20 mL min–1): 100:0, 0 min; 100:0, 3 min; 35:65, 25 min; 100:0, 25.01 min; 100:0, 30 min.

  Mass spectra (MS) were recorded on an Advion Expression CMS single quadrupole mass spectrometer using electrospray ionization (ESI). A Waters UPLC attached to an RDa detector was used for purity analysis. For purity analysis, a Luna Mega Polar C18, 1.6 μm, 50 mm × 2.1 mm column (Phenomenex) and the following gradient was used: A: water with 0.1% formic acid and solvent B: acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. (A:B, 0.5 mL min–1): 100:0, 0 min; 100:0, 1 min; 5:95, 6 min; 100:0, 6.1 min; 100:0, 8 min. The purity of peptides is >95%, as measured by the integration of all peaks in the 214 nm absorbance spectrum. Waters_connect (UNIFI) software was used for the integrations.


### Circular Dichroism
  The α-helicity of stapled and unstapled peptides was evaluated using circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy obtained on a Jasco J-1500 Circular Dichroism spectrophotometer. Measurement parameters included temperature (37 °C), wavelength (190–260 nm), step resolution (0.5 nm), speed (20 nm/s), accumulations (3), response (1 s), bandwidth (1 nm), and path length (0.2 cm). The peptide was dissolved in deionized water at a concentration of 100 μM. The percentage helicity was calculated from the absorbance at 222 nm using helical models as previously reported.52


### Peptide Permeability Assay
  A549 cells at 9.5 × 104 cells/mL in 600 μL of media were plated on 10 mm glass bottom 24-well plates (Chemglass Life Sciences Cell Culture Plate, 24-Well, Fisher Scientific) and cultured for 24 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2 in complete growth medium (RPMI supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum, l-glutamine, and nonessential amino acids). The next day, cells were treated with fresh prewarmed complete growth medium supplemented with the desired FITC-labeled peptide or DMSO vehicle control at a final concentration of the 100 μM FITC-labeled peptide and 1% DMSO. After 90 min treatment, cells were washed three times in warm phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove the excess peptide from the cell surface and left in the modified Krebs–Ringer buffer solution [KRBH: 134 mM NaCl, 3.5 mM KCl, 1.2 mM KH2PO4, 0.5 mM MgSO4, 1.5 mM CaCl2, 5 mM NaHCO3, 10 mM HEPES (pH 7.4)] containing the Hoechst stain at 1 μg/mL. Cells were immediately analyzed under a Nikon A1 confocal microscope (Resonant). The images were taken at 20× magnification with fluorescence excitation wavelengths at 409 for Hoechst and 488 for FITC. Images were processed using NIS-Elements AR.53,54


### Hemolysis Assay
  Hemolysis assays were based on a previously described protocol.50 Peptides were tested at a final concentration of 100 μM.


### Peptide Stability Assay
  Peptide stability assays were performed as previously described.55 Briefly, a 55 μM peptide was incubated with 10 μg/mL proteinase K for the indicated time points. At the end of each time point, 50 μL of the reaction mixture was quenched with 100 μL of a 1:1 mixture of acetonitrile/water + 1% formic acid. Samples were injected and analyzed on an Agilent Technologies 1200 Series attached to an Agilent Technologies 6460 Triple Quad LC/MS. Analysis was performed with a ZORBAX XDB-C18 3.5 mm, 2.1 mm × 50 mm column (Agilent). Eluent A: H2O with 0.1% formic acid. Eluent B: MeCN with 0.1% formic acid. Purity analysis utilized the gradient as follows: (A:B, 0.3 mL/min): 95:5, 0 min; 95:5, 1 min; 5: 95, 5 min; 5:95, 8 min; 95:5, 8.5 min; 95:5, 12.5 min.


### Dual-Luciferase Assay
  HEK-293T cells stably expressing the ARE-firefly luciferase and TK-Renilla luciferase were used. The cells were treated with different concentrations of the compound for 16 h. The cells were lysed in 1× Passive Lysis Buffer, and both firefly luciferase and Renilla luciferase activities were measured using a Promega Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System and a luminometer (Turner Biosystems 20/20n Luminometer). The samples are in triplicate for all concentrations of compounds (n = 3).


### Fluorescence Polarization Assay
  A 96-well plate (VWR Solid Bottom Assay Plates, 96-well, black) was used to encapsulate each fluorescence polarization reaction. Unless otherwise stated (i.e., ARE only, ARE + MafG), each 100 μL reaction contained 200 nM purified recombinant His-MBP-Nrf2, 200 nM purified recombinant His-MafG, various concentrations of the molecule, and 20 nM 5′ FAM-labeled ARE all together in 1× PBS solution. First, Nrf2 was incubated with the molecule for 5 min with shaking, followed by the addition of MafG for another 5 min incubation. Finally, the ARE was added and the MafG-Nrf2-ARE system was incubated for 10 min with shaking. After incubation, the plate was placed into a plate reader (Molecular Devices SpectraMax iD5 Multi-Mode Microplate Reader). The fluorescence polarization measurement was performed at excitation/emission values of 470:525 nm, respectively. The samples are in triplicate (n = 3).


### Cloning, Expression, and Purification of Human Nrf2 and MafG
  The Nrf2 sequence was obtained via PCR from the human cDNA library. The insert was cloned into pMalc2 with BamHI and XbaI. After transformation into Rosetta (DE3), the cells were grown in 2XYT media to OD600 = 0.3 at 37 °C, and then protein expression was induced by the addition of 1 mM IPTG at 37 °C for 2.5 h. After collection of the cell pellet by centrifugation, the pellet was lysed by a microfluidizer in 1× CB buffer (10 mM Tris buffer with 200 mM NaCl, pH 7.4) with 2% Triton X-100, 1 mM DTT, and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF). The target protein was incubated in and bound to Amylose resin and then washed with CB buffer. The target protein was eluted with 10 mM maltose in 1× CB buffer. The eluted protein was dialyzed against 1× CB buffer with 20% glycerol and then stored at −80 °C.

  The MafG sequence was obtained via PCR from the human cDNA library. The insert was cloned into pET15B with NdeI and BamHI. After transformation into Rosetta (DE3) cells, the cells were grown in 2XYT media to OD600 = 0.3 at 37 °C, and then protein expression was induced by the addition of 1 mM IPTG at 37 °C for 2.5 h. After collection of the cell pellet by centrifugation, the pellet was lysed by a microfluidizer in 1× CB buffer with 2% Triton X-100, 1 mM DTT, and 1 mM PMSF. The target protein was incubated in and bound to Ni-NTA resin and then washed with 50 mM imidazole in CB buffer. The target protein was eluted with 250 mM imidazole in CB buffer. The eluted protein was dialyzed against 1× CB buffer with 20% glycerol and then stored at −80 °C.


### Biotin Pull-Down Assay
  The Pierce Biotinylated Protein Interaction Pull-Down Kit was purchased from Thermofisher and used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Purified His-tagged MafG was used as the prey and biotin-tagged peptides were used as the bait. For competition experiments, 20× unlabeled N1S was added after 1 h incubation with MafG.


### SPR
  A Biacore100-Instrument (Biacore-GE) was used for all SPR-binding measurements. Binding between MafG and biotin-labeled peptides was performed by first immobilizing the biotin peptide on a SA chip (Cytiva). The binding buffer used was 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 1 mM MgCl2, 1 mM TCEP, and 0.05% P-20. Biotin peptide (1 μM) was injected at a rate of 10 μL min–1 for 10 min, immobilizing approximately 500–1000 response units. Increasing concentrations of MafG (two-fold dilutions between 4 and 0.100 μM, including two blanks) were subsequently passed over the biotin-N1S functionalized chip as well as a streptavidin reference surface. Multiple cycle analysis was carried out without regenerating the chip between different MafG concentrations. Binding data were reference normalized and processed using GraphPad Prism 9 software. A one-site binding model was applied to the processed data set to determine the binding parameters of MafG binding to the peptide.


### Western Blot
  A549 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a concentration of 2 × 105 cells/mL in 2.5 mL. Cells were treated either with the peptide at desired concentration or DMSO the next day. After a 72 h incubation, cells were collected by trypsinization and centrifugation. Pellets were washed with PBS three times before the pellets were resuspended in 250 μL of RIPA buffer containing a protease inhibitor (1 μM pepstatin A) and kept on ice for 30 min with an intermittent vortex. The cells were then spun at 16,000g for 5 min and the supernatant was collected. The protein concentration of the lysate was normalized with the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermofisher). Equal volumes of lysate was mixed with 2× Laemmli sample buffer before separating on 4–15% Mini Protean TGX SDS-PAGE (Bio-Rad).

  Protein content was transferred to PVDF membranes and blocked for 1 h with a 5% nonfat dry milk in the TBST solution. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C and washed 3 times for 5 min each with TBST. HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were then added for 2 h at room temperature. Membranes were washed with TBST an additional 3 times for 5 min each before visualizing with a SuperSignal West Pico Plus chemiluminescent substrate (ThermoFisher Scientific) on a ChemiDoc (Bio-Rad). ImageJ was used for the quantification of all bands. Primary antibodies: TrxR1, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-28321, 1:100 dilution (∼55 kDa band analyzed); β-Actin, Cell signaling Technology, #3700, 1:1000. Secondary antibodies: mouse IgG Fc binding protein (m-IgG Fc BP) conjugated to Horseradish Peroxidase (HRP), Santa Cruz Biotechnology, 525409, 1:500 used with the TrxR1 primary antibody and Anti-mouse IgG, #7076, Cell signaling Technology, 1:5000 was used with the β-Actin primary antibody.31


### qPCR
  A549 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a concentration of 0.5 × 105 cells/mL in 3 mL. The day after plating, cells were treated either with peptide at a desired concentration or the vehicle (final 1% DMSO). After a 72 h incubation, cells were collected by trypsinization and centrifugation. Total RNA was isolated using the Genejet RNA purification kit (Thermofisher), and total RNA concentrations were measured using a NanoDrop instrument. Turbo DNase1 treatment was carried out, and RNA was repurified using the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA (1 μg) was then reverse transcribed to generate complimentary cDNAs using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (ThermoFisher). Quantitative RT-PCR reactions were performed with a PowerUP SYBR Green Master Mix according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Thermofisher) and performed on a QuantStudio 3 (ThermoFisher). Gene-specific primers were based on previously used sequences.31 All reactions were normalized to endogenous RPLPO levels. Relative transcript abundance was calculated using the typical comparative Ct method.


### Incucyte Studies/MTT Assay
  A549 cells were seeded into a Corning 96-Well Cell Culture Plate and incubated for 24 h. The cells were treated with various doses of compounds. Immediately following the treatment, the cells were placed into an Incucyte S3 Live-Cell Analysis Instrument (Sartorius) and imaged every 2 h for 3 days. The Incucyte S3’s analytical software was used to calculate cell confluency at each time point. Additionally, the MTT assay was performed on the plate of cells from the Incucyte experiment, and a separate, identical plate of cells was incubated for 24 h. 20 μL of the MTT reagent (5 mM stock) was added to each well and incubated for 3 h at 37 °C. Following incubation, the plate was spun down at 2500 RPM for 5 min at room temperature, and then the media was removed via aspiration. 100 μL of isopropanol/HCl (53 μL concentrated HCl in 50 mL isopropanol) was added into each well, and the plate was placed on a shaker at room temperature for 10 min. The absorbance of each well was measured at 570 nm using a plate reader (Molecular Devices SpectraMax iD5 Multi-Mode Microplate Reader). Each treatment group was plated in triplicate (n = 3).


### GSH Assay
  The GSH-Glo Glutathione Assay from Promega was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were plated at a density of 20,000 cells/mL and treated with various concentrations of N1S for 24 h.
