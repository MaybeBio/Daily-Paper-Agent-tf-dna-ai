# A combinatorial approach to uncover an additional Integrator subunit


## SUMMARY
  RNA polymerase II (RNAPII) controls expression of all protein-coding genes and most noncoding loci in higher eukaryotes. Calibrating RNAPII activity requires an assortment of polymerase-associated factors that are recruited at sites of active transcription. The Integrator complex is one of the most elusive transcriptional regulators in metazoans, deemed to be recruited after initiation to help establish and modulate paused RNAPII. Integrator is known to be composed of 14 subunits that assemble and operate in a modular fashion. We employed proteomics and machine-learning structure prediction (AlphaFold2) to identify an additional Integrator subunit, INTS15. We report that INTS15 assembles primarily with the INTS13/14/10 module and interfaces with the Int-PP2A module. Functional genomics analysis further reveals a role for INTS15 in modulating RNAPII pausing at a subset of genes. Our study shows that omics approaches combined with AlphaFold2-based predictions provide additional insights into the molecular architecture of large and dynamic multiprotein complexes.


## INTRODUCTION
  Expression of protein-coding genes in eukaryotes is carried out by RNA polymerase II (RNAPII). The core RNAPII machinery bears the catalytic activity but is not sufficient to initiate and properly effect transcription in vivo.1 General transcription factors (such as TFIID) and Mediator are essential to recruit and position RNAPII to promoters and enhancer elements.1–3 After initiation, additional protein complexes bind and regulate RNAPII activity through termination.4 One such complex, found across all metazoans, is the Integrator complex.5,6

  Integrator is a 14-subunit (INTS1–INTS14) modular complex that associates with the C-terminal domain (CTD) of RNAPII and was originally discovered as the 3′-end processing machinery of the spliceosomal U-rich small nuclear RNAs (U snRNAs).7 More recently, Integrator has been shown to target a much broader set of transcripts including the noncoding enhancer RNAs (eRNAs) that arise from distal cis-regulatory elements, thereby modulating enhancer-promoter looping,8 as well as several herpesvirus transcripts.9 In addition, Integrator is recruited to protein-coding genes and regulates RNAPII dynamic turnover by endonucleolytic cleavage of the nascent messenger RNA, resulting in either suppression or activation of transcription.10–14

  Proteomic and biochemical studies revealed the multifaceted nature of the Integrator complex, which can be dissected into a set of functional modules. The cleavage module comprises INTS4, INTS9, and the MBL/β-CASP family endonuclease INTS11,15 which is responsible for Integrator’s endonucleolytic activity.16–18 The INTS5/8 heterodimer, together with INTS6, recruits components of the PP2A phosphatase complex to form the Integrator-PP2A (Int-PP2A) module,19–21 which endows Integrator with a second catalytic activity. The Int-PP2A module is recruited to all protein-coding loci and is capable of dephosphorylating serine residues in the RNAPII CTD, negatively regulating polymerase pause-release.19 Three other subunits—INTS13, INTS14, and INTS10—form a distinct, biochemically stable module22,23 that is devoid of enzymatic activity but has been shown to interact with the cleavage module22 as well as to independently bind poised enhancer loci across the genome.23

  Recent cryoelectron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of the Int-PP2A complex and Integrator-bound paused elongation complex (PEC) elucidated how Integrator’s functional modules are assembled onto a central backbone structure20,24 and revealed the articulate way in which Integrator interfaces with the RNAPII holoenzyme.24,25 While these data provide groundbreaking insight into the mechanism of Integrator’s recruitment to paused promoters and the activation of the endonucleolytic module, many Integrator subunits remain not fully characterized, raising questions about their roles within the complex. For instance, while INTS13/14/10 are stoichiometrically associated with core Integrator subunits, their structures could not be resolved within the cryo-EM reconstructions of the fully assembled complex.20,24 The INTS13 and INTS14 subunits were crystallized as a heterodimer,22 but it is unclear how they interact with INTS10 or potentially crosstalk with other Integrator components.

  Tackling the architecture of large and dynamic multiprotein complexes is a major challenge that limits our ability to understand their full biological scope. A recent leap forward in computational approaches, driven by machine-learning algorithms, such as AlphaFold2, has the potential to overcome some of the current technical bottlenecks and accelerate the structure determination process. AlphaFold2 allows accurate prediction of 3D protein structures directly from their primary sequences.26,27 Although the method was originally intended to be used for single protein chains, it was shown to be able to predict also inter-protein interfaces, holding great promise for future modeling of protein complexes.28–30 While some of the individual interfaces can be predicted with great precision, modeling of large and complex macromolecular assemblies still requires additional experimental data.31,32 Ongoing efforts aim to benchmark and validate these methods to assess their usefulness in structural analysis of large molecular machines.

  Here, we identified C7ORF26 as an additional subunit of the Integrator complex (INTS15) and show that it interacts directly with INTS10 and forms a tetrameric complex with INTS13/14/10. INTS15 co-localizes with INTS11 at protein-coding genes and is involved in modulation of their expression. We used AlphaFold2 modeling to search for putative interfaces between INTS15 and all other known Integrator subunits, confirming its interaction with INTS10 and identifying INTS5 as a direct interactor. This finding, together with functional genomics data, biochemistry, and proteomics analyses, establishes INTS15 as the 15th Integrator subunit, provides insights into its architecture, and suggests its function in bridging different Integrator modules.


## RESULTS
  The Integrator complex was originally purified as a stable nuclear protein complex composed of 12 subunits (named INTS1 to INTS12).7 A functional screening in Drosophila melanogaster suggested that 2 additional proteins (Asunder and the von Wille-brand factor A domain containing 9 [VWA9]) may associate with Integrator.33 Subsequent biochemical, structural, and genetic evidence demonstrated that INTS13 (Asunder) and INTS14 (VWA9) are in fact Integrator subunits, forming part of a modular extension of the core complex, along with INTS10.16,22,23 We reasoned that additional subunits of the complex may have escaped biochemical identification in Homo sapiens; therefore, we performed affinity purification of endogenous INTS13 from the nuclear fraction of induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) and HeLa cells, followed by in-gel trypsin digestion and peptide identification by liquid chromatography-coupled tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). In addition, we had previously assessed INTS13-associated proteins in the promyelocytic leukemia cell line HL-60.23 We reasoned that comparing the interactomes of INTS13 across a diverse array of cell types would increase the chance of identifying specific protein partners of Integrator while decreasing noise from common nuclear contaminants. We calculated iBAQ enrichment scores using MaxQuant and charted normalized intensity values of all identified proteins in a 3D scatterplot (Figures 1A and 1B). Endogenous INTS13 interacts with stoichiometric amounts of INTS14, in accordance with the heterodimeric assembly and reciprocal stabilization of these subunits. INTS10 was previously suggested as an additional component of the INTS13/14 module and emerges as the third highest enriched Integrator subunit, while all remaining subunits of the complex segregate at lower stoichiometry. Interestingly, the uncharacterized chromosome 7 open reading frame 26 (C7ORF26) pairs up with INTS10, as both proteins boast near-identical normalized intensities (Figures 1A, 1B, S1A, and S1B), suggesting that C7ORF26, a 449 aa polypeptide, may operate as an additional subunit (INTS15) of the INTS13/14/10 module.

  To test this hypothesis, we took advantage of AlphaFold2 (AF2), a machine-learning algorithm originally designed to predict 3D protein structures from their primary sequence.27 The neural network model of AF2 can also be harnessed to predict structures of multiprotein complexes.28–31 Therefore, we designed an experimental pipeline to perform an exhaustive search for interactions of INTS15 with all other known components of the Integrator complex (including the PP2A module) using the ColabFold implementation of AF234 (see STAR Methods). Notably, most of the existing Integrator structures were not released when the AF2 network was trained; therefore, predictions obtained in this screen are largely unbiased.

  Through AF2 predictions, we identified putative interfaces between INTS15 and three other Integrator subunits: INTS10, INTS5, and INTS14 (Figures 1C and S1C–S1K). In the case of INTS14/INTS15 interaction, the interface is incompatible with the known INTS13/14 structure,22 and therefore it was excluded from further consideration. In all other combinations, AF2 did not predict any reliable inter-protein interfaces. To validate and benchmark this approach, we executed this pipeline for all known interactions within the Int-PP2A complex.20 AF2 predicted with high confidence multiple known interfaces within Integrator, all of which were in striking agreement with the experimental structures (Figure S2). Additionally, we compared the results obtained with ColabFold34 and AF2 multimer methods,29 both of which differ in their predictive capabilities for protein complexes (Figure S2A). For the successfully predicted interactions, we observed almost no differences locally at the predicted interfaces, but globally some structures differed in their long-range geometry.

  Taken together, analysis of MS/MS data combined with machine-learning structure prediction approaches suggest that INTS15 is an additional component of the Integrator complex that is related to the INTS13/14/10 module.


### INTS15 stably associates with Integrator
  To validate our prediction, we generated HEK-293T clones stably expressing tagged INTS15 and performed affinity purification. INTS15-FLAG co-precipitates along with several Integrator subunits and RNAPII as revealed by immunoblot (Figure 2A). We further analyzed INTS15 complexes by silver staining and identified proteins by LC-MS/MS, retrieving all Integrator subunits (Figures 2B, 2C, and S3A). Next, to assess the stability of the INTS15-Integrator association, we performed large-scale affinity purification from nuclear extracts and subjected the eluate to fractionation analysis by glycerol gradient (Figure 2D). Strikingly, the majority of nonmonomeric INTS15 associates with the smaller INTS13/14/10 module of Integrator, suggesting that INTS15 is a stoichiometric component of this modular extension of the complex (Figures 2E and S3B). Next, to assess the stability of INTS15 association to Integrator and RNAPII, we performed affinity purification of INTS15-FLAG under increasing salt concentrations up to 750 mM KCl (Figure 2F). INTS15 association with INTS10, INTS13, and INTS5 is unchanged under high-salt conditions. To further establish that endogenous INTS15 associates with Integrator, we developed rabbit polyclonal antibodies raised against the full-length recombinant protein. We used serum collected from two different animals to confirm that endogenous INTS15 associates with INTS13, INTS3, and INTS1 (Figure 2G). We subjected eluates from serum 1 to LC-MS/MS and identified all Integrator subunits and most RNAPII core components (Figure S3C), similar to the analysis performed with INTS15-FLAG eluates (Figure S3D). To further establish that assembly of an INTS15-containing module along with INTS13/14/10 also occurs when INTS15 is present at endogenous levels, we isolated nuclear extract from wild-type HEK-293F cells and subject it to glycerol gradient centrifugation. INTS15 appears to form a ~200 kDa complex consistent with the size of the INTS13/14/10 module but also co-localizes with INTS1 in high molecular weight fractions (Figure S3E). Altogether, these data establish that INTS15 associates robustly with the INTS13/14/10 module and further associates with all known Integrator subunits into a fully assembled complex.


### INTS15 is part of a tetrameric Arm module connected to the core Integrator complex
  Proteomics and pull-down experiments from HEK-293T cells show that INTS10 and INTS15 co-purify with one another, consistent with the AF2 prediction. To confirm this interaction, we expressed the INTS10/15 putative heterodimer in insect cells and purified a stable, monodisperse complex as analyzed by size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) (Figures 3A and 3B).

  Previous work has shown that INTS13/14/10 constitute a module of Integrator,16,22 but it is not clear if the interactions between INTS10 and INTS15 are compatible or mutually exclusive with INTS13/14 binding. To address this question, we expressed a quaternary complex of INTS13/14/10/15 in insect cells and analyzed it by SEC. All four proteins form a stable, monodisperse complex with expected retention volume (Figure S3F), suggesting that INTS15 is a missing component of the previously identified INTS13/14/10 heterotrimer.

  In order to find the direct interacting partner of INTS15 responsible for its incorporation into the fully assembled Integrator, we performed in vitro reconstitution of a series of recombinant subcomplexes. SEC analysis shows that mixing INTS13/14/10/15 with INTS5/8 results in a new, high molecular weight peak, which contains all 6 proteins (Figures 3C, 3D, and S3F). When the same experiment is performed with INTS13/14/10 (missing INTS15), no shift is observed, pointing at INTS15 as the critical component of this interaction (Figure 3C). Similarly, INTS10/15 forms a quaternary complex with INTS5/8 (Figures 3A and 3B). These observations are in agreement with the AF2 screen results, which predict a high-confidence interface between INTS5 and INTS15 (Figure S3G). Taken together, our results reveal a putative function of INTS15 in tethering INTS13/14/10 to the core of the Integrator complex (Figure 3E). We refer to INTS13/14/10/15 as the Integrator Arm module due to its predicted elongated structure and direct contacts with the previously described Shoulder module (INTS5/8) in the Int-PP2A structure.20


### Architecture of the Integrator Arm module based on integrative modeling
  There is no structural information available for INTS10 and INTS15 or for any components of the Arm module in the context of the Int-PP2A. AF2 generates high-confidence predictions for both proteins as assessed by predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT) score >9026 for the major parts of both proteins (Table S1). Prediction of the INTS10/INTS15 complex structure identifies an interface with low (<4 Å) intermolecular predicted align error (PAE) crosspeaks,26 indicating a well-defined relative orientation of the two proteins. In the resulting model, the first N-terminal helical repeat of INTS10 interacts with the C-terminal helices of INTS15 in a head-to-tail arrangement (Figure 4A), adopting an L-shaped structure. This arrangement has been confirmed by negative-stain EM (Figures 4B and S4A–S4C) and BS3 crosslinking followed by MS (XL-MS) (Figures S4D–S4G). To further validate this model, we mutated residues at the predicted interface and assessed effects of these mutations on the pull-down efficiency in HEK-293T cells. Removal of the first helical repeat of INTS10 (residues 1–37) or helix-breaking point mutations (W28P/L29P) results in a nearly complete loss of INTS15 co-purification (Figures 4C and S5A). Similarly, in the reverse experiment, INTS15 mutations (L384A/L387A) result in a loss of INTS10 binding (Figures 4D and S5B). Taken together, AF2 prediction, EM, XL-MS, and mutagenesis allow integrative modeling of the approximate structure of the INTS10/15 dimer.

  Interestingly, a shorter splicing isoform of C7ORF26/INTS15 is annotated and found across a variety of tissues and cell types. The short INTS15 variant (INTS15s) encodes for a 352 aa polypeptide (Figure S5C) that can be stably overexpressed in HEK-293T cells. INTS15s loses the ability to interact with Integrator as revealed by immunoprecipitation and LC-MS/MS analysis (Figures S5D–S5F). AF2 structure prediction of INTS15s shows that some of the α helices that are important for the INTS10-INTS15 interaction are missing in this variant, explaining its inability to recruit other Integrator components (Figure S5G).

  The structure of INTS13/14 has been previously determined, and the VWA domain of INTS14 was shown to be involved in INTS10 binding.22 However, the structural basis of INTS10/INTS14 recognition remains unclear. To address this question, we used data from the AF2 screen and identified an interface between INTS10 and INTS14 that involves the C-terminal region of the INTS10 helical repeats (Figure 4E). In spite of structural similarity between INTS14 and INTS13, the latter is not predicted to interact with INTS10. To validate the predicted interface, we performed immunoprecipitations with different variants of INTS10, INTS13, INTS14, and INTS15 co-expressed in HEK-293T cells. Wild-type (WT) INTS10 can efficiently enrich INTS14 but not INTS13, consistent with the predicted model (Figures 4F and S5H). Mutations of INTS10 residues in the C-terminal region located at the putative INTS10/INTS14 interface result in loss of INTS14 recruitment, which is particularly pronounced for INTS10E633A and INTS10E633A/E634A (Figure 4F), supporting the in-silico-generated model. Noteworthy, the MIDAS pocket of the INTS14 VWA domain has been proposed to be implicated in INTS10 recruitment22 based on its similarity to other proteins such as Integrins35 and the conservation of the magnesium binding site. Mutations in INTS14, which were shown to disrupt binding with INTS10 (i.e., D8A/S10A/S12A and L11E/R15A),22 are indeed located at the interface predicted in this study (Figure 4E).

  Next, we performed differential quantitative proteomics of affinity-purified INTS10, either WT or mutant, transiently overexpressed in HEK-293T cells (Figures 4G and 4H). While INTS10WT efficiently co-precipitates endogenous INTS15, the INTS10W28A/L29A mutant loses this interaction while retaining INTS13/14 (Figure 4G), consistent with the predicted INTS10/15 interface (Figures 4A, 4C, and 4D). Similarly, the INTS10E633A/E634A mutant is deficient in INTS13/14 recruitment, while the INTS10/15 interaction is unaffected (Figure 4H). Furthermore, disruption of the INTS13/14/10/15 module by mutating either interface of INTS10 affects recruitment of the cleavage module (Figures 4G and 4H), in agreement with the previously reported interaction between INTS13/14/10 and INTS4/9/11.16,22 Additional proteins appear to be affected by disrupting INTS14/INTS10, such as the uncharacterized ZNF655 (Figure 4H), suggesting that INTS13/14 may engage additional DNA-binding proteins on chromatin. Taken together, these data allow construction of a composite structural model of the Integrator Arm module (Figure 4I).


### Genome-wide analysis of INTS15
  Our data indicate that INTS15 is structurally and biochemically associated with the core Integrator complex, prompting us to determine its genome-wide recruitment. First, we interrogated the promyelocytic HL-60 cell line. Chromatin immuneprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) analysis of endogenous INTS15 shows a broad distribution across active promoter and enhancer regions (Figures 5A and S6A), suggesting that INTS15 associates, like the core Integrator, with transcriptionally engaged RNAPII. In fact, INTS15 co-localizes with RNAPII, INTS10, INTS13, and INTS11 at all transcriptionally active RNAPII sites (Figure 5B). Correlation analysis of INTS15 ChIP-seq coverage with INTS10, INTS13, or INTS11 coverage demonstrates that INTS15 is recruited to chromatin alongside the Integrator complex (Figures 5C, 5D, and S6B–S6G). Looking further, we next analyzed INTS15 recruitment to sites of stimulus-dependent nascent transcription. First, we serum starved HeLa cells to reduce transcription to basal levels and then induced gene activation through the addition of epidermal growth factor (EGF).8,14 After treatment, we observed increased recruitment of INTS15 utilizing both a commercially available INTS15 antibody (899) as well as a house-made INTS15 antibody (serum 1). We find a strong correlation in the coverage of INTS15 using both antibodies (Figure S6H). Additionally, we demonstrate that INTS15 is recruited alongside RNAPII, INTS10, and INTS11 at a subset of the 50 EGF-responsive genes (Figures 5E and 5F). These data further demonstrate that INTS15 is a functional component of the Integrator complex and that it associates to RNAPII along with Integrator during active transcription.

  We previously showed that the INTS13 subunit of the Arm module is able to bind poised enhancers in myeloid cells, independent of the core Integrator subunits, owing to its ability to engage EGR1/NAB2.23 We asked whether INTS15 was recruited alongside INTS13 to poised developmental enhancers. In HL-60 cells, differentiation into monocytes/macrophages can be induced through stimulation with PMA (phorbol myristate acetate). After 16 h of treatment, INTS13 is recruited to a set of 4,274 monocytic enhancers,23 of which nearly half are poised. INTS15, like INTS10, appears to localize at active enhancers but not at poised enhancers (Figures S6I–S6K). Furthermore, we can identify a distinct set of cis-regulatory genomic loci (n = 2,600) that recruit INTS15 in the absence of INTS10, INTS13, INTS11, and RNAPII (Figures S6L and S6M). Motif analysis of these loci reveals enrichment for zinc finger transcription factors, which may contribute to Integrator-independent recruitment of INTS15 at such loci (Figure S6N). INTS15 is also found at loci encoding U snRNAs (Figure S6O), which utilize the endonucleolytic module of Integrator for 3′ processing.7 However, INTS15 is not required for biogenesis of U snRNAs, as its depletion does not impact maturation of their nascent transcripts (Figure S6P).


### Depletion of INTS15 leads to select gene mis-regulation and elongation defects
  INTS15 is robustly recruited at the transcriptional start site and gene body of EGF target genes upon stimulation. Therefore, we sought to determine its requirement in EGF-dependent transcriptional activation. To do this, we depleted protein levels of INTS15 using short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) (Figures S7A and S7B) and performed total RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis across three independent replicates in EGF-treated HeLa cells. The expression of 50 EGF genes remains unaltered after loss of INTS15 (Figure S7C). Furthermore, ChIP-seq analysis of RNAPII upon INTS15 depletion revealed that the traveling ratio of RNAPII (the ratio between RNAPII occupancy at the promoter-proximal region and the gene body) across the gene body of all EGF genes remained, in fact, unchanged (Figure S7D).

  Since EGF-dependent activation is not modulated by INTS15, we next looked at differentially expressed genes genome-wide. We identified 1,717 differentially expressed genes after loss of INTS15. Of these, 351 genes were downregulated and 1,366 were upregulated compared with the shRNA for luciferase (shLUC) control (Figure 6A). Integrator has previously been shown to work as both a positive and negative modulator of transcription. To tease apart direct versus indirect modulation of gene expression by INTS15, we examined RNAPII occupancy at differentially regulated genes. First, we noticed that upregulated genes were far less transcribed than downregulated genes in the control shLUC condition (Figure 6B). Next, we gauged Integrator occupancy at each gene subset through INTS11 and INTS15. We find that baseline levels of both INTS11 and INTS15 are undetectable at genes that are upregulated by INTS15 depletion (Figures 6C, 6D, S7E, and S7F), suggesting that their upregulation upon INTS15 loss in HeLa cells is indirect and/or driven by an Integrator-independent mechanism. Conversely, downregulated genes are occupied by Integrator in normal conditions (Figures 6C–6E, S7E, and S7F). Therefore, INTS15 may directly modulate RNAPII pausing and processivity at this subset of genes. To test this hypothesis, we assessed RNAPII levels using spike-in normalized ChIP-seq of total Rpb1 (POLR2A) to assess what happens to RNAPII dynamics upon INTS15 depletion. Our analysis suggests that with INTS15 depletion, RNAPII levels are drastically reduced across the gene body of all downregulated genes compared with the control (Figure 6F). We calculated RNAPII traveling ratio values and found that INTS15 loss results in a significant decrease of elongating RNAPII (Figures 6G and 6H) at this gene subset.


## DISCUSSION
  In this work, we characterize C7ORF26 as an additional INTS15 Integrator subunit, in line with recent evidence from extensive human proteome data mining36 and genome-wide functional CRISPR screens.37–39 INTS15 associates with INTS10, INTS13, and INTS14 to form an extended Arm module and is critical to bridge the INTS13/INTS14 heterodimer to the core Integrator complex. We show that depletion of INTS15 leads to downregulation of a set of genes via defects in polymerase pause-release.

  Structures of large protein complexes, such as Integrator, are difficult to discern due to their inherent dynamics and complexity. Recent reports have revealed the structure of Integrator both in isolation and bound to the paused RNAPII complex.20,24 While this brings about important insight into the overall architecture of the complex, including the central backbone, the cleavage module, and the newly identified phosphatase module, multiple subunits were not resolved in these cryo-EM reconstructions. Importantly, the entire INTS13/14/10 module could not be visualized, leaving many questions unanswered as to how this module fits into the larger structure and interfaces with the core of the Integrator complex. To better understand how Integrator modules associate with one another, we used an innovative combination of proteomics and AF2 structure prediction.27

  AF2 was originally designed to predict 3D structures of proteins from their primary sequence, achieving accuracy comparable to experimental methods.27 Although the neural network-based model of the original AF2 algorithm was not trained on protein complexes, it proved capable of successfully predicting inter-protein interfaces, pointing at the generalized properties of the structural information contained within the network.28–30 This property was further improved by extending the network training dataset to known protein complexes.29 Several high-throughput studies reported structure predictions of large sets of binary protein complexes using AF2 or RoseTTAfold.32,40 Although these studies provide excellent resources, they have several major shortcomings. Firstly, the RoseTTAfold dataset is restricted to yeast, which is a simplified model organism missing some of the protein complexes found in humans (i.e., the Integrator complex). Secondly, these predictions are performed for pre-selected targets and do not sample the search space exhaustively for all possible interactions. Finally, predicted interactions are not validated experimentally. To address this issue, we designed a pipeline to perform an exhaustive search for binary interactions on a focused target (i.e., INTS15) followed by experimental validation.

  Through AF2, we identified putative protein-protein interfaces within the Integrator Arm module, which were further validated by site-directed mutagenesis, XL-MS, and EM. This, together with benchmark predictions for known structures, provides a proof of concept that our screening approach is suitable for studying the architecture of large and dynamic protein complexes in the absence of sufficient experimental information. We further corroborated that INTS15 interacts with the fully assembled Integrator in three different cell types by LC-MS/MS, and we used SEC to validate INTS15-interacting partners within the INTS13/14/10 module. We propose that INTS15 anchors INTS13/14/10, as an extended Arm module, to Integrator via INTS5 and the Int-PP2A phosphatase module.

  The functional implications of INTS15 may tie into Integrator’s various roles in genome regulation. Co-transcriptional recruitment of Integrator by RNAPII regulates the biogenesis of several noncoding RNAs by endonucleolytic cleavage via its dedicated cleavage module composed of INTS4, INTS11, and INTS9.7,8,17,41 Additionally, the cleavage module targets a broad spectrum of mRNAs at the pausing site, promoting early termination. Cleavage activity at protein-coding genes leads to divergent outcomes according to the gene set and the model system considered. Integrator may promote transcription activation by patrolling the activity of RNAPII and selectively terminating poorly processive holoenzymes.11,14 Alternatively, cleavage by Integrator may overall dampen paused polymerase levels and operate as a transcriptional attenuator at large.10,13 Importantly, our study did not find evidence that INTS15 is directly required to support activity of the cleavage module. Conversely, we hypothesize that INTS15-dependent effects on RNAPII processivity may be dependent on its crosstalk with the Int-PP2A phosphatase module. The recently discovered PP2A module of Integrator endows the complex with the ability to dephosphorylate the RNAPII CTD as well as DSIF (DRB Sensitivity Inducing Factor), tipping the phosphorylation balance in favor of pausing rather than elongation.19–21 We predicted and validated the interaction between INTS15 and INTS5. Along with INTS8 and INTS6, INTS5 recruits PP2A heterodimers and anchors them to the Integrator core complex.19–21 Upon depletion of INTS15, a subset of downregulated genes display increased pausing and impaired elongation. Therefore, we propose that INTS15 may operate as a negative modulator of Int-PP2A activity at pausing sites.

  We previously demonstrated that the INTS13/14/10 “enhancer module” is independently recruited to poised developmental cis-regulatory elements and that it regulates their activation.23 We could not observe INTS15 recruitment at these poised enhancers in myeloid cells. Moreover, INTS15 is found at active enhancers along with the fully assembled Integrator, but its depletion does not impair enhancer-dependent gene activation in our model system. Therefore, it appears that the INTS13/14/10 “enhancer module” may also function independently of INTS15. Conversely, we identified a subset of cis-regulatory elements that recruit INST15 independent of INTS13. Therefore, we cannot exclude a tissue- or context-dependent role of INTS15 in regulating specific enhancers.

  Taken together, our data demonstrate the existence of an additional Integrator complex subunit, INTS15, which is involved in the modulation of RNAPII pause-release on protein-coding genes. We characterized this subunit as a bridge between the INTS13/14/10 module and the core Integrator complex and modeled the structure of the now-defined tetrameric Integrator Arm module. Our approach exemplifies the use of proteomics and machine learning to gain structural functional information on large multisubunit complexes that have previously evaded a comprehensive structural dissection.


### Limitations of the study
  This study has several limitations that may require further analysis. Viral mediated shRNA depletion of INTS15 assesses the effects on RNA and RNAPII after 72 h loss of INTS15, which could be convoluted with the indirect effects driven by changes in the expression of other transcription regulators upon Integrator depletion. Future studies would focus on utilizing a degron system to allow for a more acute depletion of the target proteins. Additionally, analysis of the functional role of INTS15 should be performed in additional cell lines to obtain a comprehensive overview of INTS15 targets and potential INTS15 cell-type specificity. Although the AF2 models used to generate working hypotheses in this study were carefully examined and experimentally validated, the integrative structure of the Arm module does not capture its potential dynamics, and alternative conformations of this complex might exist. Further experimental structural analysis of the INTS15-containing complexes could provide insights into this aspect.

### EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND SUBJECT DETAILS

#### Cell lines
  HEK-293T cells and HeLa cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) that was supplemented with 10% super calf serum (GEMcell) and 2mM L-glutamine (Corning). HL-60 cells were purchased from ATCC and maintained in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640 medium that was supplemented with super calf serum (GEMcell, 10% v/v) and 2mM of L-glutamine (Corning). Inducible pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) (SV20) were obtained from the iPSC Core at the University of Pennsylvania and were cultured in IPS Brew XF (Miltenyi) supplemented with B-27 supplement added (Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco) and grown on plates coated with Geltrex (ThermoFisher).

  For the in vitro differentiation of HL-60 cells, cells were treated with 100nM Phorbal 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) in growing medium for 16 hours.


#### Creation of INTS15-FLAG HEK-293T cell lines
  For full length INTS15, the pcDNA3.1+/C-(k)DYK-C7orf26 plasmid was obtained from Genscript (Cat #: OHu71729D) (pcDNA3.1-INTS15-FLAG). ### METHOD DETAILS

#### Nuclear fractionation
  Cells were collected and washed twice with cold 1X PBS. The cell pellet was then resuspended in 5 packed cell volumes (PCV) of Buffer A (10mM Tris pH 7.9, 1.5mM MgCl2, 10mM KCl, 0.5mM DTT, and 1mg/ml each of protease inhibitors aprotinin, leupeptin, and pepstatin). Following resuspension, cells were incubated at 4°C for 10 minutes, rotating. The cells were pelleted at 2000rpm 10 minutes at 4°C. The pellet was then resuspended in 2 PCV’s of Buffer A and homogenized with a dounce homogenizer. The cells were spun down again, and the supernatant was kept as the cytoplasmic extract. To isolate the nuclear extract, the pellet was resuspended in 1 PCV of Buffer C (20mM Tris pH 8.0, 1.5mM MgCl2, 0.42M NaCl, 25% glycerol, 0.2mM EDTA, 0.5mM DTT, protease inhibitors) and homogenized with a dounce homogenizer. The extract was then incubated at 4°C for 30 minutes, rotating and then spun down at 20,000xg for 30 minutes at 4°C. The supernatant was kept as a nuclear extract. Dialysis was done on all extracts overnight in BC80 (20mM Tris pH 8.0, 80mM KCl, 0.2mM EDTA, 10% glycerol, 1mM B-mercaptoethanol, 0.2mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF)). Following dialysis, the extracts were cleared at 20,000xg for 20 minutes, 4°C, and stored at −80°C.


#### Immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS)
  For endogenous Co-IP’s, 1.5mg nuclear extract was used per IP and diluted in Co-IP buffer (20mM Tris pH 8.0, 100mM NaCl, 0.1% NP-40, protease inhibitors). For each IP, 4ug of antibody (or 10uL of serum) was used and 30uL of Dynabeads Protein A or Protein G. The IPs were incubated at 4°C for 2 hours, rotating, and then washed three times with Co-IP buffer. The IPs were then washed with 0.05% NP-40 in 1X PBS. 0.1M glycine pH 3.0 was used to elute, and 1M Tris base pH 11.0 was added to neutralize the eluate pH. The eluates were either analyzed by SDS-PAGE followed by Western blot, or by LC-MS/MS, described below.

  Antibodies used for endogenous Co-IP’s include anti-INTS13 (Bethyl A303-575A) and anti-Rabbit IgG (Cell Signaling 2729). INTS15 antibodies Serum 1 and Serum 2 were raised against recombinant full-length INTS15 in rabbits for this paper.

  For FLAG IP’s, 1.5mg nuclear extract was used per IP. The extract was diluted in Co-IP buffer and combined with 20uL of anti-FLAG M2 Magnetic Beads (Sigma). The IP was rotated at 4°C for 2 hours, washed 3 times with BC500, 1 wash with BC250 + 0.1% NP-40, and eluted with FLAG peptide (Sigma).

  For the salt titration FLAG IP’s, 1.5mg nuclear extract was used per IP. Extracts were diluted in BC100, BC250, BC500, or BC750 buffer (20mM Tris-HCl pH 7, 0.2mM EDTA, 10% glycerol, and either 100mM, 250mM, 500mM, or 750mM KCl). 20uL of anti-FLAG M2 magnetic beads (Sigma) were used and the IP’s were incubated at 4°C for 2 hours. The IP’s were washed 3 times with their respective salt buffers (i.e. the IP diluted in BC100 was washed 3 times with BC100, the IP diluted in BC500 was washed 3 times with BC500, etc) and then eluted with FLAG peptide (sigma).

  Eluates were prepared for SDS-PAGE and run on a Novex WedgeWell 10% Tris-Glycine Gel (Invitrogen) with Tris-Glycine-SDS buffer (BioRad). The samples were run at 110V for 10 minutes to allow the samples to enter the gel. Following, the gel was stained overnight with the Colloidal Blue staining kit (Invitrogen). Samples were then processed at the Wistar Institute proteomics facility for MS.

  Each sample was cut from the gel. Following excision, samples were reduced with TCEP, and alkylated with iodoacetamide. The samples were then digested with trypsin and run on a Q-Exactive Plus mass spectrometer and analyzed by LC-MS/MS with the standard 90-minute LC gradient. Following, the MS/MS spectra were searched against the UniProt human database (10/10/2019) with full tryptic specificity with MaxQuant 1.6.15.0. False discovery rates for protein and peptide identification were set to 1%. To visualize, the RStudio package ggplot2 (v3.3.0) was used and a value of 10 was added to raw iBAQ values to allow log10 transformation.


#### Western blot
  For HeLa whole cell lysates, cells were collected and washed with 1X PBS three times. The cell pellet was then resuspended in ChIP lysis buffer (150mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 0.7% SDS, 6500mM DTT, 10mM Tris-HCl, 5mM EDTA, and protease inhibitors aprotinin, leupeptin, pepstatin at 1ug/mL). Following cell lysis, the samples were incubated on ice for 20 minutes and spun down at 20,000xg for 20 minutes. The supernatant was kept as the whole cell lysate. Samples were prepared for SDS-PAGE with the Bolt LDS Sample (Thermo Fisher) and Bolt Sample Reducing Agent (Thermo Fisher) and boiled at 95°C for 10 minutes. Samples were then loaded onto Bolt 4-12% Bis-Tris Plus gels (Thermo Fisher) and separated through gel electrophoresis with Bolt MES running buffer (Thermo Fisher). Following separation, proteins were transferred to ImmunBlot PVDF membranes (BioRad) with a wet transfer in Tris-Glycine transfer buffer (BioRad). The membranes were then blocked with 10% BSA in TBST for 1 hour at room temperature. Then, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies diluted in 5% BSA in 1X TBST for 2 hours at room temperature or overnight at 4°C. Following, the membranes were washed in 1XTBST for 10 minutes, twice. The secondary antibodies were diluted 1:10,000 in 5% BSA diluted in 1X TBST. The membranes were incubated in secondary antibody for 1 hour at room temperature and washed with 1X TBST following incubation. To visualize, Clarity Western ECL substrate (BioRad) was used and imaged with a Fujifilm LAS-3000 Imager (Fujifilm).

  The following antibodies were used for detection of HeLa and INTS15-FLAG HEK-293T cell lines: anti-INTS1 (Millipore MABS1984), anti-INTS3 (Proteintech 16620-1-AP), anti-INTS5 (Proteintech 14069-1-AP), anti-INTS6 (Santa Cruz sc-676524), anti-INTS8 (Sigma HPA057299), anti-FLAG M2 (Sigma F1804), anti-INTS13 (Bethyl A303-575A), anti-INTS10 (Proteintech 15271-1-AP), anti-C7ORF26 (INTS15) (Sigma HPA042899), anti-Rabbit IgG (Cell Signaling 2729), anti-GAPDH (Cell Signaling 2118), anti-RNA Polymerase II (Santa Cruz sc-56767), anti-INTS14 (Proteintech 25227-1-AP). INTS15 antibodies serum 1 and serum 2 were raised against recombinant full-length INTS15 in rabbits for this paper. All primary antibodies were used at a dilution of 1:1000 in 5% BSA (in TBST). The secondary antibodies used for this study include anti-mouse IgG, HRP-linked (Cell Signaling 7076) and anti-rabbit IgG, HRP-linked (Cell Signaling 7074).

The supernatant was incubated for 2h with 5% (v/v) of affinity resin (HA- or FLAG-agarose) to capture the bait protein. Affinity resin was washed 3 times with 10 resin volumes of Buffer3 (150 mM KCl and 20 mM K-HEPES pH 7.8) and subsequently resuspended in SDS sample buffer and heated up to 95°C for 5 min to release bound proteins. Input and elution fractions were analyzed by western blotting.

  For the differential quantitative proteomics experiments of INTS10 variants, cells were grown in 10 cm dishes in triplicates and proteins were purified as above. Label-free TMT-10plex mass spectrometry experiments were performed as previously described.16

  A PVDF membrane (Merck) was activated for 5 min in 100% EtOH and incubated for 5 min in the transfer buffer (1xTris-Glycine, 20%EtOH). A wet transfer was performed for 60-90min at 30V in an Invitrogen XCell II Blot Module. The membrane was blocked with 5% milk in PBS supplemented with 0.2% Tween 20 (PBST) for 1h at room temperature.

#### Silver stain
  Eluates from an IP or inputs were prepared for SDS-PAGE as described above. The samples were boiled at 95°C for 10 minutes and then loaded onto a Bolt 4-12% Gradient Bis-Tris gel (Thermo Fisher). The proteins were separated at 140V with Bolt MES running buffer (Thermo Fisher). Following separation of the proteins, the gel was stained with the SilverQuest Silver Staining Kit (Thermo Fisher) following manufacturer’s instructions. For LC-MS/MS analysis of bands, bands were excised and processed for LC-MS/MS as described above.


#### qRT-PCR
  Cells were lysed in TRI-reagent and RNA was purified using the Direct-zol RNA MiniPrep kit (Zymo research). Using the Revertaid first strand cDNA synthesis kit, 900ng of RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA (Thermo Scientific). 15ug of cDNA was used for real-time quantitative PCR along with 0.4mM primers, 10uL iQ SYBR Green Supermix (BioRAD) for a total volume of 20uL with a CFK96 real-time system (BioRAD). The parameters for thermal cycling are: 3 minutes at 95°C, 40 cycles of 10 seconds at 95°C, 20 seconds at 63°C, and 30 seconds at 72°C. qRT-PCRs were done in triplicate and normalized to GAPDH RNA.


#### Glycerol gradient ultracentrifugation
  For the INTS15-FLAG HEK-293T cell lines, glycerol gradients were prepared with 1X HEMG buffer (200mM KCl, 30mM MgCl2, 0.2mM EDTA, 50mM HEPES (pH 7.9), 0.5mM DTT, and 1mg/mL each of the protease inhibitors aprotinin, leupeptin, and pepstatin) and glycerol on ice. The glycerol fractions increased by 3% ranging from 11% to 50%. To assemble the gradients, 320uL 1X-HEMG-glycerol (50% glycerol) was layered first into an ultracentrifuge tube (Beckman Coulter 344057). Additional fractions of glycerol were layered onto the 50% fraction until the 1X HEMG-glycerol (11%) was layered on top. The elution of a 15mg FLAG-IP was layered on top of the gradient. The gradient was then placed into a swinging-bucket rotor and ultracentrifuged at 48,000rpm (282,897xg) for 16 hours (accel = 8, decel = 8). Following centrifugation, the bottom of the ultracentrifuge tube was pierced and 320uL fractions were collected. Each fraction was then precipitated by adding 1/10th volume trichloroacetic acid (TCA, Sigma-Aldrich)and incubated overnight. Following incubation, precipitated protein pellets were washed with 1 volume cold acetone (Sigma-Aldrich) and resuspended in 1X Bolt LDS sample buffer (Invitrogen). Samples were then boiled at 95°C for 10 minutes, vortexed, and spun down for 5 minutes at 20,000xg. Samples were analyzed by SDS-PAGE.

  For HEK-293F cells, cells were grown to a density of 2x106 cells/ml and harvested by centrifugation. Cells were washed with cold 1X PBS and then scraped from the plate. Cells were spun at 1000xg for 5 minutes and washed with 1X PBS. Cells were resuspended in 3 packed cell volumes of hypotonic buffer A (10mM Tris pH 7.9, 1.5mM MgCl2, 10mM KCl, 0.5mM DTT), and swelled for 10 minutes on ice. Cells were homogenized with a Dounce homogenizer with a type-B pestle. Cells were pelleted at 2000xrpm, 15 minutes, at 4°C. The supernatant was kept. 0.11volume of 10X S100 buffer was added (0.3M Tris-HCl, pH 7.9, 1.4M KCl, 0.03M MgCl2) and this was spun at 100,000xg, 4°C for 1 hour. The supernatant was kept as the cytoplasmic extract. To the pellet, 0.5 volume of low-salt extraction buffer B (20mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.9, 25% (v/v) glycerol, 1.5mM EDTA, 20mM KCl) was used to resuspend. While mixing, 0.5 times the packed pellet volume of high-salt extract buffer C (20mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.9, 25% (v/v) glycerol, 1.5mM MgCl2, , 0.2mM EDTA, 1.2M KCl) was added. The nuclei were rotated for 30 minutes at 4°C. The extract was cleared at 10,000rpm, 30 minutes, 4°C. the supernatant was kept as the nuclear extract. Both the cytoplasmic and nuclear extracts were dialyzed in 100 volumes of dialysis buffer D (20mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.9, 20% (v/v) glycerol, 100mM KCl, 0.2mM EDTA, 1mM DTT) for 4 hours at 4°C. The extracts were cleared at 10,000xg for 20minutes at 4°C.53 To reduce the high glycerol content from the nuclear extract preparation, the sample was diluted 3-fold in 150mMKCl and 20 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.8. Subsequently the diluted sample was carefully loaded on top of a 10-30% glycerol gradient and centrifuged for 14 h at 30,000 rpm at 4 °C (SW 60 Ti). The gradient was fractionated manually in 150 μl aliquots, pooled and subjected to TCA precipitation as following. 1 ml of pooled gradient fractions, was mixed with 250 μl TCA and incubated for 10 min on ice. The tube was spun for 5 min at 14.000 rpm and 4 °C in a table top centrifuge. The supernatant was discarded and the pellet washed 3 times with 200 μl acetone. Subsequently the acetone was removed, the pellet dried in a heat block at 95 °C and resuspended in 150 μl SDS sample buffer and stored at −20 °C until further use. The content of the fractions was analyzed by SDS-PAGE followed by immunodetection.

#### Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq)
  For wild-type HeLa cells, the media was removed, cells were washed with 1X PBS, and the cells were serum starved with 0.3% SCS for 72 hours. After 72 hours, nascent transcription was induced through the addition of recombinant EGF (0.1ug/mL, GIBCO) or PBS as a control for 15 minutes. For shRNA infected HeLa cells, cells were incubated with puromycin as described above for 48 hours. The night prior to harvesting, cells were switched to 0.3% SCS-containing media and incubated overnight. Then, nascent transcription was induced as described above.

  All ChIPs performed in HeLa cells were done utilizing D. melanogaster specific tandem ChIP-seq (Egan et al, 2016). Briefly, 2x107 cells were collected and fixed with 1% formaldehyde for 5 minutes at room temperature. The cells were washed with 1X PBS twice. The pellet was lysed in ChIP buffer (150mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 0.7% SDS, 500mM DTT, 10mM Tris-HCl, 5mM EDTA). Sonication was performed with a Covaris S220 Ultrasonicator to shear the chromatin to a size of 200-400bp. The sheared chromatin was then diluted with ChIP lysis buffer, and 15ug of human antibody, 0.5ug Drosophila spike in antibody, Drosophila spike-in chromatin, and Protein A or Protein G Dynabeads were added. The ChIP was incubated overnight at 4°C rotating. The following day, the beads were washed twice with a series of buffers: Mixed Micelle Buffer (150mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 0.2% SDS, 20mM Tris-HCl, 5mM EDTA, 65% sucrose), Buffer 500 (500mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% Na-deoxycholate, 25mM HEPES, 10mM Tris-HCl, 1mM EDTA), and LiCl Detergent Buffer (250mM LiCl, 0.5% Na-deoxycholate, 0.5% NP-40, 10mM Tris-HCl, 1mM EDTA). The ChIPs were washed a final time with cold 1X TE. Following, the chromatin was eluted with 1X TE + 1% SDS for 10 minutes at 65°C. The elution was repeated, the eluates combined, and incubated overnight at 65°C to reverse crosslink. The following day, 0.5mg/ml proteinase K was added and incubated for 1 hour. The DNA was then purified with the Zymo ChIP DNA Clean and Concentrator Kit (Zymo Research) and quantified with QUBIT. Libraries were prepared with the NEB Ultra II DNA Library Prep kit for Illumina and sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 500 or NextSeq 2000 with 40 base pair paired-end reads. DNA sequences were aligned with Burrows Wheeler Alignment tool (BWA) to the human reference hg19 and D. melanogaster dm5 genomes with the MEM algorithm.44 Aligned reads were filtered based on mapping quality (MAPQ >10) using Samtools and rmdup was used to remove PCR duplicates.42 For spike in normalization, the number of hg19 filtered reads was divided by the lowest number of dm5 filtered reads for the set of experiments to create a down-sampling factor. This downsampling factor was then used to normalize BAM files using samtools -s and normalized bigwig files were generated with bamCoverage -binSize 10 -extendReads 150.42 MACS2 (2.1.2) was used to call peaks using the broad peak option.45 The UCSC Genome Browser was used to visualize data (http://genome.ucsc.edu/) seqMINER 1.3.4. was used to calculate the average read density across genomic intervals (left and right extensions = 5.0 kb, internal bins = 160, flanking region bins = 20).44,49 Read density matrices were exported to calculate average profile plots in RStudio with ggplot2 (v4.1.2). Traveling ratio was calculated by taking the of the coverage at the TSS (−150bp/+50bp) and comparing it to the coverage at the gene body (+50bp through TES). computeMatrix from deeptools was used to generate heatmaps from the normalized bigwig files.43 RStudio with ggplot2 was used to visualize all other data. Motif analysis was performed with Meme-ChIP54 using standard parameters. The E value is the statistical significance of the motif, as calculated and defined by MEME-ChIP.

  Antibodies used for ChIP-seq are anti-INTS10 (Proteintech 15271-1-AP), anti-C7ORF26 (INTS15) (Sigma HPA042899), anti-RNA Polymerase II (anti-RNA Polymerase II (raised against the N-terminus of subunit B122).


#### Total RNA-seq
  Total RNA was extracted using the Zymo Direct-Zol RNA miniprep kit (Zymo research) with in-column DNase treatment. The quality of the RNA was assessed by the RNA Integrity Number (RIN) with the Agilent Bioanalyzer. All RNA samples used for total RNA-sequencing had a RIN >8. 1ug of total RNA were ribosome depleted using NEBNext rRNA depletion kit (New England Biolabs). This rRNA-depleted RNA was then purified with the miRNEasy kit (QIAGEN). Barcoded RNA sequencing libraries were produced with the NEBNext Ultra Directional RNA Library Prep kit (New England Biolabs). RNA was sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 500 with 50bp paired end reads.

  The resulting reads were aligned to the hg19 genome using STAR v2.5 in 2-pass mode (-quantMode TranscriptomeSAM -outFilterMultimapNmax 10 -outFilterMismatchNmax 10 -outFilterMismatchNoverLmax 0.3 – alignIntronMin 21 – alignIntronMax 0 -align-MatesGapMax 0 -alignSJoverhangMin 5 -runThreadN 12 -twopassMode Basic -twopass1readsN 60000000 -sjdbOverhang v0.1.1.19).46 Bam files were filtered based on alignment quality (q = 10) with Samtools v0.1.19.42 The latest annotations from Ensembl were used to build reference indexes for STAR alignment.46 FeatureCounts was used to count reads mapping for each gene.55 RSEM was used to obtain FPKM (Fragment per Kilobase of exon per Million fragments mapped.47 DESeq2 was used to analyze differential gene expression levels using read counts normalized by feature length (RStudio 4.1.2,48).


#### AlphaFold2-based interaction screen
  We used ColabFold implementation34 of the AlphaFold2 algorithm27 to perform structure prediction calculation locally on a workstation equipped with Nvidia RTX3090 GPU. Initially, for predicting structures of protein complexes, we utilized the so-called “linker” method, where the sequences of two proteins are connected with a long linker ([Gly-Ser]x50) and treated as a single chain afterward. The calculations were subsequently repeated with the Multimer version of AlphaFold2, when it became available. Proteins with long sequences (<1000AA) were fragmented into 2 or 3 non-overlapping parts to avoid exhausting GPU memory. Typical ColabFold run parameters included 5 models and 3–12 rounds of recycling. The results of the AF2 prediction were inspected manually. The outcome of interaction prediction was considered as positive when the Predicted Aligned Error (PAE) plots showed inter-molecular crosspeaks with values better than 5Å for at least 2 out of 5 predicted models. The prediction was performed for INTS10, INTS13, INTS14 and INTS15, paired with all other components of the integrator.

  For the benchmark dataset as well as for the INTS10/15; INTS5/15 and INTS10/14, predictions were also performed with Deepmind’s original implementation of AlphaFold Multimer.29 No major differences were observed, except for some minor distortions in individual proteins.

  Buried Surface Area (BSA) was calculated with PDBePISA. Interface pLDDT was calculated as an average pLDDT value for all residues with non-zero BSA that are involved in the interface formation.


#### Protein expression and purification
  Open reading frames (ORFs) of Integrator subunits were cloned and assembled in different combinations (see key resources table) into expression vectors using the biGBac system.56 Baculoviruses were generated in SF21 cells grown in SF- 900 II SFM medium, as previously described.57 Hi5 cells, used for protein production, were grown in Express Five™ SFM media and infected at a density of 1x106 with 1% volume of the SF21 pre-culture and incubated for 72 h at 27°C.

  Cell pellets were thawed in 5-10 volumes of Buffer1 (150 mM KCl; 20 mM K-HEPES pH 7.8; 30 mM Imidazole) and sonicated (4 times 1 min, 30% Amplitude, 10 s ON/OFF cycle). The lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 20 000 rpm for 45 min at 4°C (JA-25.50) and the supernatant was incubated with 5-10% (v/v) of Ni-NTA agarose resin (QIAGEN) at 4°C for 2 h on a turning wheel. The resin was transferred to Poly-Prep Chromatography Columns (Bio-Rad) washed 3 times with 1 resin volume (RV) of Buffer1 and eluted 5 times with 1 RV of Buffer2 (150 mM KCl; 20 mM K-HEPES pH 7.8; 250 mM Imidazole). The elution fractions were pooled together and incubated with 5-10% (v/v) of Streptavidin agarose FastFlow resin for 2h at 4°C on a turning wheel. The resin was washed 3 times with 5 RV volumes of Buffer3 (150 mM KCl; 20 mM K-HEPES pH 7.8) and eluted 8 times with 0.5 RV of Buffer4 (150 mM KCl; 20 mM K-HEPES pH 7.8; 10 mM desthiobiotin). Elution fractions containing proteins of interest were pooled together, aliquoted, and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen.


#### In vitro reconstitution and size exclusion chromatography (SEC)
  Recombinant sub-complexes of the Integrator were mixed in 1:1 molar ratio, incubated on ice for 30-60 min and centrifuged for 5 min at 13 000 rpm before SEC. Samples were injected into Superose 6 3.2/300 Increase gel filtration column equilibrated with Buffer3 and operated on the Akta Micro system (GE).


#### Cross-linking and mass spectrometry
  INTS10/15 complex at 0.5 mg/ml was incubated with 2 mM BS3 at room temperature for 30 min and the cross-linking reaction was quenched by the addition of Tris-Cl pH 8.0 to the final concentration of 50 mM. Samples were then mixed with 0.05 (v/v) of RapiGest and after addition of 10mM DTT, incubated at 50°C for 30 min. 2-chloroacetamide was added to 50 mM final concentration and samples were incubated at room temperature for 30 min, protected from direct light. Proteins were digested with 1:50 (m/m ratio) of trypsin and 1:100 of LysC for 4h at 37°C and subsequently acidified with 0.5% (v/v) of trifluoroacetic acid to stop the digestion.

  Digested peptides were concentrated and desalted using an OASIS® HLB μElution Plate (Waters) according to manufacturer instructions. Crosslinked peptides were enriched using size exclusion chromatography.58 In brief, desalted peptides were reconstituted with SEC buffer (30% (v/v) ACN in 0.1% (v/v) TFA) and fractionated using a Superdex Peptide PC 3.2/30 column (GE) on a 1200 Infinity HPLC system (Agilent) at a flow rate of 50 ml/min. Fractions eluting between 50-70 ul were evaporated to dryness and reconstituted in 30 μl 4% (v/v) ACN in 1% (v/v) FA.

  Collected fractions were analyzed by liquid chromatography (LC) -coupled tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) using an UltiMate 3000 RSLC nano LC system (Dionex) fitted with a trapping cartridge (μ-Precolumn C18 PepMap 100, 5μm, 300 μm i.d. x 5 mm, 100 Å) and an analytical column (nanoEase™ M/Z HSST3 column 75 μm x 250 mm C18, 1.8 μm, 100 Å, Waters). Trapping was carried out with a constant flow of trapping solvent (0.05% trifluoroacetic acid in water) at 30 μL/min onto the trapping column for 6 minutes. Subsequently, peptides were eluted and separated on the analytical column using a gradient composed of Solvent A ((3% DMSO, 0.1% formic acid in water) and solvent B (3% DMSO, 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile) with a constant flow of 0.3 μL/min. The outlet of the analytical column was coupled directly to an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos (Thermo Scientific, SanJose) mass spectrometer using the nanoFlex source.

  The peptides were introduced into the Orbitrap Fusion Lumos via a Pico-Tip Emitter 360 μm OD x 20 mm ID; 10 μm tip (CoAnn Technologies) and an applied spray voltage of 2.1 kV, instrument was operated in positive mode. The capillary temperature was set at 275°C. Only charge states of 4-8 were included. The dynamic exclusion was set to 30 sec. and the intensity threshold was 5e4. Full mass scans were acquired for a mass range 350-1300 m/z in profile mode in the orbitrap with resolution of 120000. The AGC target was set to Standard and the injection time mode was set to auto. The instrument was operated in data dependent acquisition (DDA) mode with a cycle time of 3 sec between master scans and MSMS scans were acquired in the Orbitrap with a resolution of 30000, with a fill time of up to 100 ms and a limitation of 2e5 ions (AGC target). A normalized collision energy of 32 was applied. MS2 data were acquired in profile mode.

  All data were analyzed using the cross-linking module in Mass Spec Studio v2.4.0.3524 (www.msstudio.ca,50). Parameters were set as follows: Trypsin (K/R only), charge states 4–8, peptide length 7–60, percent Evalue threshold = 50, MS mass tolerance = 10 ppm, MS/MS mass tolerance = 10 ppm, elution width = 0.5 min. BS3 cross-links residue pairs were constrained to K on one end and one of KSTY on the other. Identifications were manually validated, and cross-links with an E-value corresponding to <0.01% FDR were rejected.


#### Negative stain EM
  Purified proteins were cross-linked with 0.5 mM BS3 for 30min on ice and quenched with Tris-Cl pH 8.0 added to the final concentration of 20 mM. Carbon-coated Quantifoil grids were glow-discharged in Pelco EasyGlow device operated at 25 mA, 0.4 mbar for 30 s. 3 μl of the sample was applied to the grids, incubated for 60 s, blotted away with whatmann filter paper and stained with 2% Uranyl acetate for 20 s. Grids were imaged on a Tecnai G2 Spirit BT microscope (Thermo Fisher) operated at 120 kV at the magnification of 49 000x (2.05 Å/pixel) with ~20 e/Å2 using CETA camera. 600 particles were picked manually and used to generate an initial model via stochastic gradient descent algorithm and subsequently refined in Relion 4.0.51 The resulting map was visualized in Chimera.52
