# BLEACH&STAIN 15-marker Multiplexed Imaging in 3,098 Human Carcinomas Reveals Six Major PD-L1–driven Immune Phenotypes with Distinct Spatial Orchestration


## Implications:
  The development of an easy-to-use high-throughput 15+1 multiplex fluorescence approach facilitates the in-depth understanding of the immune tumor microenvironment (TME) and enables to study the prognostic relevance of more than 130 immune cell subpopulations.


## Introduction
  Given that a major proportion of patients in most tumor entities is still not responding to immune checkpoint blockade, substantial effort is currently put into understanding the immune tumor microenvironment (iTME) to predict response to anti–PD-1/PD-L1/CTLA-4 immune checkpoint–therapy. Although the FDA-approved biomarkers – microsatellite instability and the PD-L1 status for response to therapy are increasing the likelihood of treatment response, 62% to 78% of PD-L1+ patients were lacking an overall response across different tumor entities (1–5). This was either due to the poor interobserver reproducibility of manual PD-L1 assessment by pathologists (6) or due to a bystander role of PD-L1 in the complex iTME that is characterized by the interplay between tumor cells and its adjacent immune cells, stromal, and extracellular components (7). Spatial orchestration of these cellular components and the immune checkpoint expression level regulates the preexisting antitumor–immune reaction (7) and drives the response to immune checkpoint blockade (8–10).

  Reproduceable and thus automated cell type–specific as well as spatial PD-L1 assessment in the iTME is thus of topical interest. However, PD-L1 assessment is mostly performed by conventional brightfield IHC that shows a lower predictive performance as compared with PD-L1 quantification by multiplex fluorescence IHC (mfIHC; ref. 11). It is, therefore, expected that mfIHC will play a pivotal role for PD-L1 quantification on tumor cells (different tumor markers) and immune cells (macrophages and T-cell markers), as well as its corresponding immune checkpoint receptor PD-1 in the iTME. For instance, this comprehensive PD-L1 quantification, however, requires the simultaneous analysis of more than six biomarkers which in turn requires a high-throughput, multiplexed imaging technology, both of which do not currently exist as a combined technology. Hence, previous studies on the PD-L1 expression in human tissues have either been limited to a small patient cohort (12–15) or to a maximum of six costained biomarkers (16). Taken together, the current 30+ marker mfIHC approaches are rather methods for deep profiling of a few small tissue samples than ones appropriate for high-throughput tissue microarray (TMA) analysis due to the cumbersome and time-consuming instrumentation, TMA-spot-harming reagents, or the limitation to a small total tissue size (e.g., 15 mm × 15 mm as the total tissue as well as scan area per slide; refs. 12–15, 17–19).

  In response to these issues, we developed a BLEACH&STAIN mfIHC approach and a deep-learning–based image analysis framework that facilitates staining and image analysis of 15 biomarkers on more than 3,000 different carcinoma samples within less than one week. This technology enables a fast and automated PD-L1, PD-1, and CTLA-4 quantification on tumor cells as well as different subsets of immune cells for translational research studies.


## Materials and Methods

### Patients and tissues
  The study included multitumor TMAs with tissue spots measuring 0.6 mm in diameter that was made from a total of 3,536 primary tumors from 44 different carcinoma entities, operated between 1993 and 2019 at the University Medical Center Hamburg- Eppendorf and Clinical Center Osnabrück (Hamburg, Germany). This cohort was not treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors. Detailed histopathologic data on grade, pathologic tumor stage (pT), or pathologic lymph node status (pN) were available from up to 3,109 tumors (cancers of the colon, bladder, ovarian, endometrium, lung, stomach, breast, and kidney tumors). Clinical follow-up data were available from 682 breast cancer with a median follow-up time of 43 months (95% confidence interval; CI, 40–45). All samples came from the archives of the Institutes of Pathology at the University Hospital of Hamburg (Hamburg, Germany) and Clinical Center Osnabrück (Osnabrück, Germany). The use of archived remnants of diagnostic tissues for manufacturing of TMAs and their analysis for research purposes, as well as patient data analysis, have been approved by local laws (HmbKHG, §12) and by the local ethics committee (Ethics commission Hamburg, WF-049/09, January 25, 2010). All work has been carried out in compliance with the Helsinki Declaration. (for patient's characteristics, see Supplementary Table S1 in the Supplementary Appendix)


### BLEACH&STAIN mfIHC
  Several primary antibody clones for a single target were compared in preliminary experiments as described earlier (20). The optimal dilution as well as pH for every antibody clone, composed in the 15+1 marker BLEACH&STAIN panel, were evaluated on a test-TMA using conventional brightfield IHC. Freshly cut 4-μm consecutive tissue sections were used on X-tra glass slides (catalog no. 3800204AE, Leica), with a positively charged surface, for mfIHC staining. Slides were initially boiled in an autoclave (30 minutes at 100°C –120°C and 2 bar) for antigen retrieval. The staining of a single biomarker was performed using AKOYA's OPAL fluorophore dyes that are implemented in a tyramide signal amplification (TSA)-based mfIHC assay.

  The 15+1 marker BLEACH&STAIN mfIHC was conducted in five sequential staining and imaging rounds. Each round comprised the staining of three biomarkers, counterstaining with 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), imaging and a bleaching step. A three-biomarker staining of the first round was achieved by three sequential staining rounds that were performed largely according to the manufacturer's instructions (AKOYA) including the following optimizations to reduce tissue damage and preserve tissue integrity for successful restaining:

  Each of the 15 staining cycles (5 rounds of 3 cycles) of antibody staining included peroxidase blocking, application of the primary antibody, detection with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody, TSA-based fluorophore detection (OPAL system, AKOYA), and removal of the bound antibodies by a short microwave treatment (5 minutes at 100°C and 5 minutes at a mean temperature of 93°C). This cycle was repeated two times for the remaining antibodies for the first three biomarker stainings (first round). Slides were subsequently counterstained with DAPI and mounted in antifade solution. The fluorescent dyes OPAL 520 (catalog no. FP1487001KT, AKOYA Biosciences), OPAL 570 (catalog no. FP1488001KT, AKOYA Biosciences), and OPAL 650 (catalog no. #FP1496001KT, AKOYA Biosciences) were used. Details on the used primary antibodies, antibody retrieval and OPAL dyes are given in Supplementary Table S2.

  After every 3 + 1 staining (3 biomarker + DAPI staining), digital images (qpTIFF and TIF format) of mfIHC slides were acquired with a Leica Aperio VERSA 8 automated epifluorescence microscope and a Zeiss Axio Scan.Z1 slide scanner.

  The bleaching step after every sequential 3 + 1 marker staining consisted of photobleaching (metal-halide lamp with 1,600 W), slide incubation in 3% hydrogen peroxide for chemical inactivation of fluorochromes, and cooling (4°C) of the slides at the same time. The bleaching was stopped as soon as the fluorescence signal of all fluorophores was vanished (exposure time > 600 ms). This staining round was repeated until five imaging rounds were completed including 15 staining cycles of individual biomarkers and 15 cycles of microwave-based antibody stripping.

  Finally, the five sequential digital images were aligned according to the DAPI signal of every staining round and thus merged into a single 15+1 mfIHC image using a custom software written in the Python programming language (21). Autofluorescence was imaged at the filter OPAL 480 (Chroma Narrow Band Filter) and subtracted from the analysis. The staining was conducted manually because of several shortcomings, such as a staining gradient or poor signal-to-noise ratio of autostainers capable of mfIHC. The time frame from tissue preparation (1 hour), tissue dehydration (2 hours), tissue deparaffination (2.5 hours), five rounds of 3 + 1 mfIHC (38 hours), five scanning rounds (28 hours), and deep-learning–based image analysis (20 hours) comprises less than one week for about 3,000 patients in a TMA format (for details of the BLEACH&STAIN approach, see Fig. 1; Supplementary Fig. S1A–S1B; and Supplementary Table S2 in the Supplementary Appendix).


### Deep-learning–based framework for automated 15-plex BLEACH&STAIN mfIHC image analysis
  Image analysis was performed using the previously trained (22) deep-learning–based (U-Net) framework for cell detection, cell segmentation, intensity measurement of the used fluorophores (range 0–255, i.e., a continuous numerical value indicating the fluorescence signal strength), processing the intensity values, cell-to-cell distance, and cell-to-cell interaction analysis using Python Programming Language version 3.8 (RRID:SCR_008394; ref. 21), R version 3.6.1 (The R foundation, R Project for Statistical Computing; RRID:SCR_001905; ref. 23) and the Visiopharm software package (VIS Image Analysis Software; RRID:SCR_021711). The intensity of each fluorochrome was recorded as raw intensity for the individual cells and normalized by reference cell lines for immune checkpoints (i.e., PD-L1, PD-1, and CTLA-4; ref. 24). Marker positivity (i.e., PD-L1, PD-1, CTLA-4, panCK, CD68, CD163, CD11c, iNOS, CD3, CD8, CD4, FOXP3, CD20, Ki67, and CD31) was evaluated by a deep-learning (U-Net) system for every marker individually that classified the marker positivity based on multiple parameters which have been identified by the convolutional neural network within the training set (e.g., the intensity level, distribution of the marker intensity across the cell, and cell shape). Thresholding was used to annotate the first training set. This set was manually corrected upon necessity and then used to train “provisional” deep-learning systems to continuously extend the training set along with an improved accuracy of the deep-learning system (22). The final deep-learning systems (U-Net) for cell type identification has been trained on 800 to 2,500 tissue samples from more than 30 different carcinoma entities (Supplementary Figs. S2 and S3) using the deep-learning frameworks Keras and Tensorflow (RRID:SCR_016345, in Python version 3.87) and the Visiopharm software package. The image analysis workflow of 17 different deep-learning systems and the spatial analysis algorithms have been described in detail earlier (22, 25).


### Immune cell phenotyping and cell type specific PD-L1 quantification
  The per cell data, derived from the deep-learning–based framework for threshold-free cell type identification, has been used for unsupervised X-shift clustering (26) and 2D/3D t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) using the “Rtsne” (27) package (Rtsne; RRID:SCR_016342) to identify 132 cell subtypes with coexpression of several markers (Supplementary Table S3). The major immune cell subtypes analyzed in this study were M1 macrophages (CD68+ CD163− iNOS+), M2 macrophages (CD68+ CD163+), DCs (CD68− CD11c+), tumor cells (panCK+), cytotoxic T cells (CD3+ CD4− CD8+), T helper cells (CD3+ CD4+ CD8− FOXP3−), regulatory T cells (CD3+ CD4+ CD8− FOXP3+), B cells (CD20+), and vessels (CD31+) that showed PD-L1, PD-1, CTLA-4, or Ki67 expression at different levels (relative expression or labelling index). The combined positive score (CPS) was calculated as the sum of PD-L1+ tumor and immune cells divided by the number of tumor cells multiplied by 100. The Ki67 labeling index (Ki67-LI) was calculated as the quotient of the number of Ki67+ tumor cells and all tumor cells multiplied by 100.


### Statistical analysis
  Statistical calculations were performed with R version 3.6.1 (The R foundation; ref. 23) and JMP Pro 15 software package (Statistical Analysis System, SAS Institute Inc. RRID:SCR_008567; ref. 28). The cutoff for the fraction of tumor PD-L1 positivity was set at ≥1% PD-L1+ tumor cells, following a well-established threshold of the FDA-approved PD-L1 companion assays (29, 30), and the immune cell PD-L1 positivity was defined as ≥5% PD-L1+ immune cells (31) to characterize the three major PD-L1 cluster (PD-L1+ tumor and immune cells; Cluster 1 = C1, n = 817), PD-L1+ immune cells and PD-L1− tumor (Cluster 2 = C2, n = 1196), and PD-L1− patients (Cluster 3 = C3, n = 1085; Fig. 2; Supplementary Fig. S5; Supplementary Table S4). Unsupervised X-shift (26) and Markov Cluster algorithm (MCL) were applied to differentiate inflamed and noninflamed subcluster within the three major PD-L1 clusters (inflamed PD-L1+ tumor and immune cells; Cluster 1.1 = C1.1, n = 444), noninflamed PD-L1+ tumor and immune cells (Cluster 1.2 = C1.2, n = 373), inflamed PD-L1+ immune cells and PD-L1− tumor (Cluster 2.1 = C2.1, n = 634), noninflamed PD-L1+ immune cells and PD-L1− tumor (Cluster 2.2 = C2.2, n = 562), inflamed PD-L1− patients (Cluster 3.1 = C3.1, n = 491), and noninflamed PD-L1− patients (Cluster 3.2 = C3.2, n = 594; Fig. 2; Supplementary Fig. S5; Supplementary Table S4). To identify inflamed and noninflamed clusters, all immune cells were divided according to the distribution in the X-shift cluster that resulted in a cutoff of 122 (CD8), 266 (CD4), 38 (FOXP3), and 159 (DCs) cells/mm2 for Cluster 1.1–1.2, 66 (CD8), 224 (CD4), 27 (FOXP3), and 113 (DCs) cells/mm2 for Cluster 2.1–2.2, and 40 (CD8), 175 (CD4), 17 (FOXP3), and 103 (DCs) cells/mm2 for Cluster 3.1–3.2. The clinico–histopathologic parameters of the patients were reported as counts and percentages for categorical data and compared using likelihood ratio χ2 tests. To estimate the prognostic value of the spatial parameters, Kaplan–Meier Estimates (R “survival” package; ref. 32) were used for OS after surgery as the study endpoint. The log-rank test was applied to assess differences between cluster in Kaplan–Meier estimates. Time-dependent areas under receiver operating characteristic curves were used to estimate the predictive performance of these parameters (R “riskRegression” package; ref. 33). All P values were two-sided, and P values < 0.05 were considered as significant.

## Results

### BLEACH&STAIN validation
  We developed and validated a novel BLEACH&STAIN mfIHC approach that enables high-throughput 15 marker staining. Bleeding, cross-talk, or leftover staining were not detected across the five staining cycles (Fig. 1A–C; Supplementary Fig. S1A and Supplementary Fig. S1B). Linear regression confirmed low signal deterioration across the different cycles (mean 2.8%; Supplementary Fig. S1B) and markers known for a biologically absence of coexpression (e.g., cycle 1: CD68+ macrophages, cycle 5: CD20+ B cells) were lacking detectable coexpression (< 0.1%: Supplementary Fig. S1C). A key component for reliable BLEACH&STAIN mfIHC was to achieve a time-efficient reduction of the fluorescence signal along with preserved tissue integrity for restaining. Several validation experiments revealed that the combination of initial pretreatment using an autoclave, highly validated primary antibodies, bleaching in a cooling chamber (4°C), incubation with hydrogen peroxide under a 1,600 W metal-halide lamp, reduced microwave–based antibody retrieval, and using positively charged glass slides played a pivotal role for improved staining quality (Fig. 1A; Supplementary Fig. S1D–S1E).


### Deep-learning–based framework for automated PD-L1 quantification
  To facilitate automated immune checkpoint quantification (i.e., PD-L1, PD-1, and CTLA-4) on tumor and a wide range of immune cells, we have trained and validated convolutional neural networks (U-Net, one for each marker) for fully automated cell type identification (Fig. 1B; Supplementary Fig. S2). The performance of the U-Net–based cell type identification showed a significantly higher accuracy compared with any intensity-based threshold (each ≤0.001), because of its ability to adapt the threshold for the staining properties of every individual patient that comes close to a manual cell type identification by a trained pathologist (Supplementary Fig. S3). The BLEACH&STAIN framework for automated PD-L1 assessment on tumor cells and immune cells (fraction; % of PD-L1+ tumor cells, fraction; % of PD-L1+ immune cells, sum of proportion score, combined positive score, PD-L1 RE in immune cells) was highly concordant with manual PD-L1 quantification using conventional brightfield IHC (ref. 20; each P < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. S4).


### Tumor grouping based on PD-L1 status and quantity of inflammatory cells
  A total of 3,098 (88%) of 3,536 carcinoma tissue samples were analyzed in this study. The remaining 438 tumor samples were excluded due to the lack of tissue and the lack of representative cancer cells. The framework for fully automated PD-L1 assessment identified the three expected cohorts – irrespective of the used threshold for PD-L1 positivity – of PD-L1+ tumor/immune cells (C1, n = 817), PD-L1+ immune cells (C2, n = 1196), and PD-L1− patients (C3, n = 1085; Fig. 2; Supplementary Fig. S5; Supplementary Table S4). Interestingly, unsupervised X-shift (26) and MCL clustering identified a major proportion of PD-L1+ cases as CD11c+ DCs, CD4+ T-helper cell, CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, and FOXP3+ regulatory T-cell noninflamed (C1.2, C2.2; Fig. 2A and B; Supplementary Figs. S5–S7; Supplementary Table S5). Curiously, a high M1 macrophage density was found – in contrast to all other immune cell subtypes – linked to the noninflamed PD-L1+ immune phenotype (C2.2; Fig. 2A; Supplementary Table S5). Although a high relative PD-1 expression on T cells was associated with PD-L1 positivity (P < 0.001 each), elevated relative CTLA-4 expression on Tcells was linked to the PD-L1− immune phenotype (P < 0.001 each; Fig. 2; Supplementary Tables S4 and S5). In addition, PD-L1 positivity on both tumor and/or immune cells (C1 and C2) was linked to unfavorable tumor parameters such as high tumor grade, advanced tumor stage, and nodal metastasis (each P < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. S8A).


### PD-L1 spatial analysis
  Spatial analysis revealed that inflamed PD-L1+ patients (C1.1) showed the most dramatic intratumoral (intraepithelial) macrophages and DCs infiltration (P < 0.001) accompanied by the highest degree of intratumoral T-cell exclusion (P < 0.001) and the highest relative PD-1 expression level (P < 0.001; Fig. 3A, B, and D; Supplementary Figs. S9 and S10). The pivotal role of macrophages and DCs for immune evasion via direct interaction with T cells was supported by a significantly lower cell-to-cell distance and higher rate of normalized interactions between PD-1+ T cells and PD-L1+ immune cells compared with PD-1+ T cells and PD-L1+ tumor cells (P < 0.001 each; Fig. 3C; Supplementary Figs. S9 and S10).


### Findings in different tumor entities
  PD-L1 positivity was detected in 43 (98%) of 44 different carcinoma entities and the majority (72%) of analyzed tumor entities harbored at least five of the six immune phenotypes (Supplementary Fig. S8B). Squamous cell carcinomas (SCC) of various origins showed as expected the highest proportion of patients with PD-L1+ tumor and immune cells (C1 and C2), while low grade prostate cancer and different breast cancer entities were characterized by a particularly high rate of noninflamed PD-L1− patients (C3.2; Supplementary Fig. S8B). The immune cell composition was found dominated by macrophage and DC subtypes in the PD-L1+ immune phenotype (C1 and C2) compared with a T-cell subtype–driven iTME in the PD-L1− immune phenotype (C3) across all carcinoma entities (Supplementary Fig. S11).


### Breast cancer prognosis
  The availability of 587 analyzable breast cancers with follow-up data enabled us to study the prognostic impact of more than 250 different spatial parameters. 23 of 287 parameters showed a significant prognostic impact (P ≤ 0.04; Supplementary Fig. S12). Furthermore, a particularly poor prognosis was seen for noninflamed PD-L1+ patients (P < 0.001, C1.2, C2.2; Fig. 4A). Interestingly, the area under time-dependent receiver operating characteristic curves (AUC) revealed a higher predictive performance for OS for the relative PD-L1 expression (fluorescence intensity) on tumor cells (AUC, 73.6; CI, 67.6–79.7) and immune cells (AUC, 66.5; CI, 60.3–72.8) compared with the commonly used fraction of PD-L1+ tumor (AUC, 54.2; CI, 50.8–57.6) and immune cells (AUC, 55.0; CI, 49.2–60.8; Fig. 4B).


## Discussion
  Here we have developed a novel BLEACH&STAIN method for translational research studies that enables us to simultaneously stain 15 antibodies on one slide. The method is based on up to five sequential cycles of staining and imaging of three biomarkers at a time and a bleaching step between every cycle to inactivate the fluorophores on the tissue. The technical key requirements include a complete, tissue preserving, and fast removal of bound antibodies along with a reduction of the fluorescence signal. The most important steps for tissue preservation was cooling the slides to 4°C during incubation in hydrogen peroxide and a 1,600 W metal-halide lamp as well as a reduction of the microwave-based antibody removal after every staining round. To remove bound antibodies, a much less tissue damaging 5 + 5 minutes microwave pretreatment (1,000 W + 270 W) was sufficient for more than 100 antibodies tested in our laboratory. For heat-induced epitope retrieval a high-temperature autoclave pretreatment was performed (100°C –120°C) before the first staining round to make the epitopes accessible for all subsequent staining rounds. The almost identical signal-to-noise ratio of antibodies irrespective of whether they were used in the first, second, or fifth round of staining can be seen as validation of our approach. Hence, this method is ideally suited for the analysis of multiple targets that are localized to the same subcellular compartments because interdependencies between antibodies or dyes cannot affect more than three markers. Of note, not every antibody was equally suited for application in later rounds. It appears that the applicability of antibodies depends on the quantity of epitopes and the quality of antibodies. For instance, an anti-panCK antibody was ideally suited for later rounds while CTLA-4 staining was more depended on interindividual antibody properties. Tyramide signal amplification (TSA) is driven by an enzymatic amplification, as known from chromogenic staining (34). Different to chromogen deposition, TSA IHC is suited for serial staining as we proved in this study with five sequential staining cycles. We considered independence of costly equipment and the ability to analyze large tissue sections including TMA as a major advantage of our approach as compared with previously described bleaching based mfIHC approaches (17–19, 35). The “nonbleaching” CODEX mfIHC technology is currently restricted to small 15 mm × 15-mm tissue section that disables the use of medium and large-size TMAs (12).

  The successful manual staining of 15 different biomarkers across 3,098 tumor samples from 44 different tumor entities was performed in less than one week and enabled the analysis of PD-L1 expression on tumor and immune cell subsets. To analyze coexpression patterns and interrogate the spatial relationship of cell types, we have developed a deep-learning–based framework for automated cell type identification. The trained deep-learning system–based on the interplay of marker intensity, cell shape, and the cell's marker expression pattern showed a higher accuracy than a commonly used intensity-based threshold for every individual biomarker. Hence, the deep-learning–based framework for automated PD-L1 assessment on tumor and immune cells described here facilitates reproducible and fully automated PD-L1 quantification.

  Indirect validation of our technical approach was provided by finding numerous well-established biologic findings in this study. Given that these biological relevant parameters and clusters represents a strong validation of our BLEACH&STAIN method, we have used this method so study the immune TME. For example, our approach identified three well-known patient clusters – with respect to the PD-L1 staining – that include tumors with PD-L1 expression in both tumor and immune cells (C1), PD-L1 expression solely on immune cells (C2), and with a lack of PD-L1 expression (C3; refs. 36, 37). We also confirmed that PD-L1 expression is particularly frequent in SCC of various origins (38, 39) and infrequent in prostate cancer (40). In combination with the multitude of further immune components analyzed in this study, various additional biologically relevant observations could be made including a major proportion of PD-L1+ cases containing a paucity of immune cells (subcluster C1.2 and C2.2). These findings are consistent with a pan-cancer study on 9,677 samples from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) that reported a significant fraction of PD-L1+ samples showing a low abundance of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells (41). In addition, anecdotal findings in earlier reviews emphasized that there are PD-L1+ cases with a low degree of lymphocytic infiltration, but the abundance and clinical relevance of this phenomenon are still unknown (42, 43). To further study the composition of tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) subsets within the three major PD-L1 phenotypes (C1–3), unsupervised MCL and X-shift clustering were applied. The clustering revealed that a paucity of all T-cell subsets and DCs – mostly accompanied by a low number of M2 macrophage – was a hallmark of a noninflamed PD-L1+ tumor microenvironment (C1.2 and C2.2). These findings are supported by the fact that a low CD8+ cytotoxic T-cell infiltration was described to be accompanied by low CD4+ T cells (44), FOXP3+ regulatory T cells (44), DCs (45), and M2 macrophage infiltration (46). Of note, our observation of a particularly poor prognosis for the patients with noninflamed PD-L1+ breast cancer (C1.2, and C2.2) provides evidence for a clinical relevance of a more differentiated immune subclassification of PD-L1+ patients.

  Finding a high density of intratumoral M2 macrophages and DCs in combination with a low intratumoral T-cell infiltration and a high PD-1 expression on T cells in inflamed PD-L1+cases (C1.1) suggests that PD-L1 expression on both tumor and immune cells drives epithelial M2 macrophages as well as DC recruitment to hamper intratumoral T-cell infiltration. This corresponds to a study using tumor slice imaging in SCC of the lung to demonstrate that tumor-associated macrophages were an important determinant of excluding T cells from the intratumoral compartment (47). These findings are also in line with another study that used conventional brightfield IHC to report a significantly lower intratumoral TILs infiltration in PD-L1+ non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) compared with PD-L1− cases (48). In addition, finding interactions between PD-1+ T cells and PD-L1+ macrophages were significantly more frequent than between PD-1+ T cells and PD-L1+ tumor cells, which also underlines the pivotal role of intraepithelial PD-L1+ macrophages and DCs for orchestrating immune evasion.

  The successful deep-learning–based analysis of 587 breast cancer samples with 15 antibodies in combination with cell type classification and a quantification of cell–cell interactions enabled us to study the prognostic impact of more than 300 cellular and spatial parameters. Interestingly, PD-L1 staining of tumor cells exerted the strongest prognostic impact of all these parameters, thereby further emphasizing the critical role of PD-L1 expression in breast cancer. The relative expression of PD-L1 staining showed superior predictive performance for OS in comparison to the commonly analyzed percentage of PD-L1+ cells, that corroborates and further extends the findings of a recent meta-analysis by Lu and colleagues describing an improved performance for predicting response to anti–PD-L1/PD-1 therapy by PD-L1 quantification using mfIHC compared with PD-L1 assays using other methods (11).

  It is of note that the BLEACH&STAIN approach lasting less than one week for more than 600 patients long is not considered for routine application. The ability to analyze 15 marker at a time to identify small panels is however, suitable for the application in routine clinal practice. We considered TMAs optimal for the search of prognostic markers in cancer because the tissue size analyzed per tumor comes close to the amount that is found in many small biopsies.

  In conclusion, we provide a deep learning-based framework for automated PD-L1 assessment on tumor and immune cells along with 14 other biomarkers using BLEACH&STAIN mfIHC to characterize the spatial orchestration of immune cells located in the TME of human carcinomas. This method enabled us to identify six major PD-L1 phenotypes ranging from a PD-L1+ tumor cell inflamed phenotype (C1.1) with a spatial T-cell exclusion to a noninflamed PD-L1+ immune cell phenotype showing a particular poor prognosis (C2.2) to a noninflamed PD-L1− phenotype (C3.2).
