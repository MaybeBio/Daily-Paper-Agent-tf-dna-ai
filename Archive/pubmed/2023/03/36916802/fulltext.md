# Structure-Activity Relationships of Ligand Binding to Oxysterol-Binding Protein (OSBP) and OSBP-Related Protein 4 (ORP4)


## Abstract
  Oxysterol-binding protein (OSBP) and OSBP-related protein 4 (ORP4) have emerged as potentially druggable targets in antiviral and precision cancer drug development. Multiple structurally-diverse small molecules function through targeting the OSBP/ORP family of proteins, including antiviral steroidal compounds, OSW-1 and T-00127-HEV2. Here, the structure-activity relationships (SARs) of oxysterols and related compounds binding to human OSBP and ORP4 are characterized. Oxysterols with hydroxylation at various side chain positions (i.e., C-20, C-24, C-25 and C-27) —but not C-22—confer high affinity interactions with OSBP and ORP4. A library of 20(S)-hydroxycholesterol analogs with varying sterol side chains reveal that side chain length modifications are not well tolerated for OSBP and ORP4 interactions. This side chain requirement is contradicted by the high affinity binding of T-00127-HEV2, a steroidal compound lacking the side chain. The binding results, in combination with docking studies using homology models of OSBP and ORP4, suggest multiple modes of steroidal ligand binding to OSBP and ORP4.


## Introduction
  Recently, oxysterol-binding protein (OSBP) and OSBP-related protein 4 (ORP4) have been identified as potentially druggable targets for multiple human diseases, including broad-spectrum RNA viral infections, leukemia, and ovarian cancer.1-4,5,6,7,8-10,11 The OSBP/ORPs constitute a conserved family of lipid-binding proteins in eukaryotes.12,13 The founding member of this family, OSBP, was discovered by its ability to bind 25-hydroxycholesterol (25-OHC) (1) (Fig. 1A) and other oxysterols in vitro.14,15 OSBP was successfully cloned in 1989 16, with the sequencing of the human genome later revealing 11 OSBP-Related Proteins in humans (ORPs 1-11), ordered into six subfamilies. 13,16-19 The OSBP/ORPs have a wide range of cellular functions that are still being elucidated; however, a central element of OSBP/ORP function is the ability to bind and transport lipids, including sterols, oxysterols, and phospholipids, using a conserved OSBP-related ligand binding domain (ORD).12 OSBP is a ubiquitously-expressed cytoplasmic protein that mediates lipid trafficking between organelles at membrane contact sites (MCSs), including at the ER-Golgi 20 and the ER-lysosome 21 interfaces. Recently, OSBP has been recognized as a broad-spectrum antiviral target required for the proliferation of a wide array of human RNA viral pathogens, including the Enterovirus genus, hepatitis C (HCV), dengue fever virus, Zika virus and EMCV 1-4,22,23,24; there is also evidence suggesting that OSBP is required for feline Coronavirus infection.25 The broad, evolutionarily-conserved necessity of OSBP for RNA virus replication makes OSBP an attractive target for the treatment of a broad array of RNA viral pathogens that currently lack treatment, including RNA viruses that have not yet emerged. OSBP is postulated to be required for the formation of the viral replication organelles(VRO). 1-4, The VRO’s are required for virion assembly and evasion of the cellular innate immune system. 1-4, Reduction of OSBP levels in cells has no effect on cellular viability or proliferation, suggesting a wide therapeutic window for OSBP-targeting antiviral compounds. 5,6,7 ORP4, the ORP family member most closely related to OSBP (i.e., ~64% amino acid sequence ID 26), is expressed only in select human tissues.9 ORP4 is reported to be selectively expressed in ovarian cancer cell lines and patient isolated T-ALL cells.8-10,11 ORP4 is reported to act as a scaffolding protein at the plasma membrane driving cell proliferation, survival, and bioenergetics in leukemia cells.8-10 Small molecule compounds targeting ORP4 have shown efficacy in mouse leukemia models 8,10 and in ovarian cancer 3D spheroids.11 Collectively, these results indicate that ORP4 is an attractive potential precision cancer target. 7,8-10,11

  Several structurally-diverse anticancer small molecules have been shown to interact with both OSBP and ORP4, including the natural product compounds OSW-1 (3), cephalostatin 1 (4), schweinfurthin A (5), and ritterazine B (6) (Fig. 1B).7 Additionally, multiple antiviral small molecules have been shown to target OSBP, including OSW-1 (3) (Fig. 1B), itraconazole (7), T-00127-HEV2 (THEV) (8), and TTP-8307 (TTP) (9) (Fig. 1C).1-4, 6 We have previously shown that the OSBP-targeting antiviral small molecules (Fig. 1C) interact with OSBP through multiple modes of binding that produce distinct effects on OSBP cellular activity.6 The evident roles of OSBP in viral replication and ORP4 in cancer cell survival and proliferation, in combination with the ability of biologically-active small molecules to interact with OSBP and ORP4 through multiple modes of binding, highlights the potential druggability of this disease relevant protein family. However, the lack of defined structure-activity relationships (SARs) of ligand binding to OSBP and ORP4 limit potential drug targeting of these proteins. OSBP and ORP4 ligand binding SAR is particularly important for the development of small molecules that selectively bind OSBP for antiviral drug targeting or ORP4 for precision anticancer drug targeting. No protein structure of OSBP or ORP4 has been reported, but the ORDs from multiple yeast ORP homologs (i.e., Osh proteins) have been crystallized with various ligands bound.28-31 More recently, crystal structures were determined for ORP subfamily 2 members, ORP1-ORD and ORP2-ORD, complexed with cholesterol and PI(4,5)P2 respectively 32,33, and ORP subfamily 3 member, ORP3-ORD with PI4P 34. The structures for both the yeast and mammalian ORPs revealed that sterols and oxysterols bind in a largely hydrophobic tunnel within the ORD, with the C-3-hydroxyl of the sterol A-ring at the bottom of the pocket, and the sterol side chain extended at the top of the hydrophobic tunnel to interact with a putative lid region of the ORD. 12,13,33

  Although the OSBP/ORP proteins are believed to interact with cholesterol in vivo, the OSBP/ORPs are reported to bind 25-OHC (1) with a greater binding affinity than cholesterol (2) (Fig. 1A).5,7,27 The biological significance of endogenous oxysterol binding to OSBP and ORP4 remains unclear, but the high binding affinity of 25-OHC for OSBP and ORP4 clearly indicates that hydroxylation of the sterol side chain is important in the binding avidity of OSBP and ORP4. Many of the biologically-active small molecules targeting OSBP and ORP4, such as OSW-1 (3), cephalostatin 1 (4), ritterazine B (6) (Fig. 1B), and THEV (8) (Fig. 1C) are structurally modified oxysterol compounds that inhibit the binding of [3H]-25-hydroxycholesterol ([3H]-25-OHC) to OSBP and ORP4.6,7 Therefore, understanding the SAR of oxysterol binding to OSBP and ORP4, particularly of the oxidized side chain component, will be important in developing improved lead compounds for drug development. Importantly, the binding of oxysterols to OSBP and ORP4 has not been extensively characterized, particularly using human OSBP and ORP4. The limited reports examining oxysterol binding to OSBP primarily used enriched lysate produced from animal tissue (i.e., hamster liver extract) or recombinant rabbit OSBP.7,27,35-39 Oxysterols binding to human ORP4, other than 25-OHC, has not been performed.7

  This paper provides the first comprehensive report of the SAR of oxysterol ligand binding to human OSBP and ORP4. Our results indicate that hydroxylation at multiple side chain positions (i.e., C-20, C-24, C-25, C-27)— with the exception of C-22— confers high affinity binding to both OSBP and ORP4. For the 25, 27-OHC and 24-OHC oxysterols, there is modest selectivity for OSBP binding of the (R) stereoisomers over the (S) stereoisomers, and the 25, 27(R)-OHC and 24(R)-OHC oxysterols show noticeably stronger competitive binding for OSBP and not ORP4. Additionally, we found that the sterol isohexyl side chain is required for high affinity interactions of oxysterols. Any modification in the length of the side chain impairs binding to OSBP and ORP4. This apparent side chain binding requirement is contradicted by the high affinity binding of the THEV compound (8) (Fig. 1C), which completely lacks a side chain. These results suggest OSBP and ORP4 can interact with a variety of different oxysterols, and perhaps other classes of small molecules, through multiple modes of binding that could be exploited for therapeutic development.


## Results and Discussion
  Ligand binding to OSBP and ORP4 was determined using a well-established [3H]-25-OHC competitive binding assay 9,5- 7,27,36,37,39,40. Compounds were assayed for the inhibition of [3H]-25-OHC binding to cloned and overexpressed human OSBP or ORP4 in S100 HEK293T lysate. HEK293T lysate overexpressing the negative control protein, LacZ, does not show detectable specific [3H]-25-OHC binding (Appendix).7 The background HEK293T S100 lysate is capable of solubilizing the oxysterols during the binding assays, which generates reliable and reproducible sigmoidal dose-response curves.40 The equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) for human OSBP binding 25-OHC is 22 ± 5 nM in this assay (Supp. Fig 1A), which is similar to previously reported values generated using rabbit OSBP.7 The [3H]-25-OHC KD for human ORP4 is reported to be 54 ± 23.7 In our assay, 25-OHC competing against itself in the competitive binding assay produces a Ki (i.e., inhibition binding constant) of 26 ± 7 for human OSBP and 55 ± 8 nM for human ORP4 (Supp. Fig 1B).7 Importantly, the competitive 25-OHC ligand binding assay is only capable of detecting ligand binding to the sterol binding pocket of the OSBP or ORP4 ORD. This assay does not exclude possible ligand binding at other positions on the protein outside the sterol binding pocket of the ORD.


### OSBP and ORP4 do not bind non-cholestane oxysterols
  A series of non-cholestane skeleton oxysterol compounds were tested for binding interaction to OSBP and ORP4, including bile acids, plant sterols, azacholesterol, cardiac glycoside drugs, and glucocorticoid drugs (Fig. 2, Table 1). These compounds did not show any inhibition of [3H]-25-OHC binding to OSBP or ORP4 at concentrations <30 μM. These results indicate that OSBP and ORP4 sterol binding pocket interactions could be limited to cholestane-derived oxysterol compounds and not general steroid structures.


### OSBP and ORP4 bind a variety of cholestane oxysterols with varying side chain oxidation position and stereochemistry
  A series of cholestane oxysterols were tested for binding interactions to OSBP and ORP4 using the [3H]-25-OHC competitive binding assay (Fig. 3, Table 2). 5α-hydroxy-6-ketocholesterol (5α-6KC) (21) (Fig. 3), which is produced through oxidation of the cholesterol C5-C6-alkene upon reaction with ozone in lung epithelial cells 41, displayed weak to no detectable inhibition in the [3H]-25-OHC competitive binding assay (Table 2, Appendix). 25-hydroxycholestanol (19), made through hydrogenation of the C5-C6 alkene of the 25-OHC sterol skeleton, resulting in trans stereochemistry, showed equivalent binding activity to 25-OHC (OSBP Ki = 24 ± 16 nM; ORP4 Ki = 51 ± 26 nM (Table 2, Appendix)). The competitive binding of 7α-25-dihydroxy-cholesterol (7α-25-diOHC (20)), which differs from 25-OHC through the introduction of an additional hydroxylation at the C7 position, is slightly attenuated compared to 25-OHC (OSBP Ki = 78 ± 42 nM; ORP4 Ki = 92 ± 55 nM (Table 2, Appendix)). These results suggest that the high affinity oxysterol interaction with OSBP and ORP4 in the sterol binding pocket are dependent on side chain hydroxylation and not hydroxylation on the sterol core.

  A library of cholesterol oxysterols with varying side chain hydroxylation positions and stereochemistry were tested for [3H]-25-OHC competitive binding to human OSBP and ORP4 (Fig. 3, Table 2). 20(S)-OHC (29), 24(R)-OHC (24), and 24(S)-OHC (25) displayed strong competitive inhibition against OSBP, although with Ki values higher than 25-OHC (Fig. 3, Table 2) (i.e., 25-OHC Ki = 26 ± 7 nM; 20(S)-OHC Ki = 130 ± 35 nM; 24(S)-OHC Ki = 330 ± 80 nM; and 24(R)-OHC Ki = 120 ± 60 nM). Interestingly, 24(R)-OHC (24) displayed a substantially lower Ki against OSBP than the 24(S)-OHC epimer (i.e., 120 ± 60 nM versus 330 ± 80 nM) (Table 2, Appendix). Additionally, C-27 hydroxylation modestly effects binding to OSBP, compared to 25-OHC. The OSBP Ki for 25(R), 27-OHC (22), is 68 ± 16 nM, and the OSBP Ki for the 25(S), 27-OHC epimer (23) is 120 ± 35 nM (Fig. 3, Table 2). The lower Ki values for the (R) epimers of both 24-OHC and 25, 27-OHC epimers compared to the (S) epimers, suggests a modest preference for (R) hydroxylation at the C-24 and C-25 positions in interacting with OSBP. Importantly, C-22 hydroxylation significantly abrogated OSBP interactions in the [3H]-25-OHC competitive binding assay. 22(R)-OHC and 22(S)-OHC displayed weak interactions with OSBP with apparent Ki values of >10 μM for 22(S)-OHC and >20 μM for 22(R)-OHC (Fig. 3, Table 2).

  Compared to OSBP, ORP4 is more limited in its scope and strength of oxysterol interactions. 20(S)-OHC (29) interacts with ORP4 with a Ki of 320 ± 90 nM (Fig. 3, Table 2). Against ORP4, 24(S)-OHC (25) increased the Ki approximately 7-fold (400 ± 130 nM) compared to 25-OHC (Table 2). For ORP4, 24(R)-OHC (24) failed to produce a sigmoidal curve indicative of a single binding site for the oxysterol ligand (Table 2, Appendix). Since 24(R)-OHC showed dose-dependent binding to OSBP in an identical assay (Table 2), the failure of 24(R)-OHC to produce clear inhibition binding curves against ORP4 is likely not due to solubility or other technical assay issue. The capability of 24(R)-OHC to inhibit [3H]-25-OHC binding for OSBP, but not produce a typical dose-dependent sigmoidal curve for ORP4 suggests that the ORP4 interaction with these ligands may be complex and involve a different mode of binding. Against ORP4, both 25(R), 27-OHC (22) and 25(S), 27-OHC (23) displayed much weaker competitive inhibition than for OSBP. Similar to OSBP, 22(R)-OHC only showed weak interactions with ORP4 at high micromolar concentrations; in contrast 22(S)-OHC displayed a Ki value of >3 μM (Table 2). Together, these results indicate 22 hydroxylation significantly abrogates oxysterol interaction with OSBP and ORP4 compared to the other side chain hydroxyls (i.e., C-20, C-24, C-25, and C-27) (Fig. 3, Table 2).

  To better define the role of the C-22 hydroxylation, 20(R), 22(R)-diOHC (Oxy-16 (28) (Fig. 3)) was tested. 20(R), 22(R)-diOHC (28), which is identical to 20(S)-OHC expect for hydroxylation at C-22, is a reported antagonist in the Hedgehog pathway.42 Since 20(S)-OHC (29) interacts with moderately high affinity to OSBP and ORP4 (Table 2), the dihydroxylated 20(R), 22(R)-diOHC (28) analog could define if the poor binding of 22-OHC oxysterols is due to a lack of C-22 hydroxyl positive interactions with the OSBP and ORP4 binding sites, or the C-22 hydroxyl position having deleterious interactions with the OSBP and ORP4 binding sites. 20(R), 22(R)-diOHC (28) displayed weak (i.e., high micromolar) binding to OSBP (Ki >30 μM) and ORP4 (Ki >15 μM), suggesting that the detrimental effect of C-22 hydroxylation overrides the positive binding effects of C-20 hydroxylation, potentially through C-22 hydroxylation clashing with the sterol binding pocket.


### Oxysterol side chains are optimized at six carbons for OSBP and ORP4 binding
  To better understand the SAR of oxysterol binding to OSBP and ORP4, a library of cholesterol oxysterol side chain analogs were synthesized and tested (Fig. 4, 30-40, Table 3). The analog compounds are derivatives of 20(S)-OHC with structurally modified alkyl side chains (Fig. 4, 30-40). 20(S)-OHC was selected for derivatization based on its moderate to high competitive binding with both OSBP (Ki 130 ± 35 nM) and ORP4 (320 ± 90 nM) (Fig. 3 (29), Table 2) and ease of synthetic analog generation. The produced 20(S)-OHC analogs varied the side chain lengths and branching positions (i.e., isopentyl, pentyl, butyl etc.) (Fig. 4). The 20(S)-OHC analogs, designated as SA compounds, were made starting from pregnenolone (Supp. Fig. 2). The 20(S) stereochemistry was preferentially produced over the 20(R) analog epimers presumably due to the steric directing effects of the D-ring and the C19-methyl group. For some of the analogs, the 20(R) analog epimer was produced in sufficient quantities for biological testing (i.e., SA-3 (32), SA-6 (35), and SA-8 (37) (Fig. 4). SA-1 (30) with a hydroxymethyl moiety, only showed weak competitive binding to OSBP or ORP4 (apparent Ki >50 μM). SA-2 ((S)-Pregenolol (31)) and SA-3 ((R)-Pregenolol (32)), which possess no carbon side chain off the C-20 position, also displayed no competitive binding for either OSBP or ORP4 at any concentration below 100 μM (Table 3, Appendix). The ethyl side chain ((S)-ethyl-pregenenolol, SA-4, (33)) showed only partial competitive binding at high micromolar concentrations (apparent Ki >30 μM) (Table 3, Appendix). The butyl side chain present on (S)-butyl-pregenenolol (SA-10, 39) only weakly inhibited [3H]-25-OHC binding with micromolar Ki values of >10 μM for OSBP and >30 μM for ORP4 (Table 3, Appendix). The pentyl side chain present on (S)-pentyl-pregenenolol (SA-11, 40) had the highest affinity of any 20-OHC analog compounds tested for both OSBP and ORP4 with Ki values of 600 ± 10 nM for OSBP and 780 ± 180 nM for ORP4 (Table 3, Appendix). 20-OHC oxysterols side chain analogs with octyl side chains were also prepared (Fig. 4 (36-37)), which extend beyond the typical cholesterol isohexyl side chain. (S)-n-octyl- pregnenolol (SA-7, (36)) displayed a greater ability to competitively inhibit [3H]-25-OHC binding for both OSBP and ORP4, relative to the (R)-n-octyl-pregnenolol epimer, with a Ki of >10 μM and >20 μM, respectively. (R)-n-octyl-pregnenolol (SA-8, (37)) displayed only partial competitive inhibition at high micromolar concentrations (apparent Ki >100 μM (Table 3, Appendix). The SAR results of the SA analog compounds indicate that oxysterol interactions with OSBP and ORP4 is likely optimized with an isohexyl side chain five carbons in length. The results show that shorter sider chains exhibit a significant loss of binding interaction with OSBP and ORP4, and, also, the longer octyl side chain had weak to no binding interactions. Within the tested analog series, the greater the deviation in length from the isohexyl side chain, the more significant the loss of interaction to OSBP and ORP4.

  A series of four, five and six carbon branched side chain analogs were also prepared and tested (Fig. 4). The isobutyl side chain present in (R)-isobutyl-pregnenolol (SA-6, (35)) showed weak high micromolar binding for both proteins (apparent Ki >50 μM), while (S)-isobutyl-pregnenolol (SA-5, (34)) inhibited 25-OHC binding at lower concentrations (apparent OSBP Ki > 20 μM and ORP4 Ki > 30 μM) (Table 3, Appendix).

  Interestingly, the branched 5-carbon long isopentyl side chain, (S)-isopentyl-pregnenolol (SA-9, (38)), showed moderate OSBP and ORP4 competitive binding (i.e., OSBP Ki =1300 ± 600 nM, ORP4 Ki >2 μM), which is weaker competitive binding than the straight chain pentyl analog, (S)-pentyl-pregnenolol, (SA-11 (40)), with an OSBP Ki of 600 ± 10 nM and ORP4, 780 ± 180 nM (Table 3). The binding results indicate that high affinity oxysterol binding to both OSBP and ORP4 is limited to the isohexyl side chain. The increased ability of the (S) epimers of n-octyl-pregnenolol (SA-7 (36)) and isobutyl- pregnenolol (SA-5 (34)) relative to the (R) epimer to competitively inhibit [3H]-25-OHC binding to both OSBP and ORP4 suggest a preference for (S) hydroxylation at the C-20 position; however this observation is limited due to the restricted generation of C-20 (R) epimers in this analysis (Fig. 4, Table 3).


### THEV SAR studies against OSBP and ORP4
  Despite the apparent necessity of the oxysterol side chain for high affinity interaction with OSBP and ORP4, our group recently reported that the oxysterol antiviral compound THEV (i.e., T-00127-HEV2) (Fig. 1C, (8)), competes with [3H]-25-OHC in the competitive binding assay with a Ki of 22 ± 15 nM for OSBP and a Ki of 98 ± 14 nM for ORP4. 6 The low Ki values for THEV clearly contradict the apparent requirement for the isohexyl side chain demonstrated by the 20-OHC side chain analogs (Fig. 4, Table 3). Importantly, the THEV compound does possess a C-3 tetrahydropyran (THP) moiety. We hypothesized that the THP group could approximate the cholesterol oxysterol side chain, but only if the THEV compound could interact with OSBP and ORP4 in an inverted matter, with the C-17 hydroxyl entering in the bottom of the binding pocket acting as the functional equivalent of the sterol C-3 hydroxyl. AlphaFold AI methodology was used to build a OSBP-ORD domain based on the human ORP2-ORD.32,33,43 Ligand docking studies in the modeled OSBP-ORD domain (Fig. 5) indicate that the THEV compound could bind in the inverted orientation with the THP group acting as an isostere for the cholesterol side chain. To test this hypothesis, we synthesized a truncated THEV analog with the THP moiety removed from the C-3 hydroxyl (SA-1, (41)). SA-12 did not show any interaction with either OSBP or ORP4 (Table 3, Appendix). The lack of interaction with SA-12 is supported by the OSBP docking studies, which only produced one weak docked interaction with SA-12 (see Supp. Fig. 8). This indicates that the THP moiety of THEV is essential for binding to OSBP and ORP4, and that OSBP and ORP4 could accommodate THEV binding in an inverted orientation, with the THP-group on the C3-hydroxyl serving in place of the cholesterol side chain.


## Conclusions:
  In summary, we report the first comprehensive SAR study of oxysterol binding to human OSBP and ORP4. Our results indicate an unexpectedly complex range of ligand binding accommodations for OSBP and ORP4. OSBP and ORP4 do not appear to interact with non-cholestane oxysterols in the sterol binding pocket (i.e., bile acids, plant sterols, azacholesterol, cardiac glycoside drugs, and glucocorticoid drugs) (Fig. 2, Table 1). In contrast, OSBP and ORP4 have the ability to interact with a variety of cholestane oxysterols with various side chain hydroxylation positions (i.e., C-20, C-24, C-25 and C-27) and stereochemistry. ORP4 is more limited than OSBP in both strength and scope of oxysterol interaction. Side chain hydroxylation at C-22 significantly impairs binding to both OSBP and ORP4. 22(R)-OHC is an important steroid hormone intermediate and agonist of liver X receptors (LXRs), and 22(R)-OHC is reported to bind to ORP1 and ORP2, with low nM affinity.44-46 ORP1 and ORP2 are also reported to weakly bind 25-OHC (KD 3.9 μM). 44,47,48 Additionally, ORP1 and ORP2 have a known role in LXR regulation, and ORP2 also plays a role in steroidogenic gene expression and steroid hormone biosynthesis. 44,47,48 The selective binding of 22-OHC to ORP1 and ORP2, and the lack of binding to OSBP and ORP4, is a probable basis for different OSBP/ORP family members controlling different biological activities through interacting with different endogenous oxysterols.

  Our results suggest that both OSBP and ORP4 better tolerate (S) side chain hydroxylation closer to the sterol core, at the C-20 and C-22 side chain positions; while OSBP better tolerates the addition of functional groups with (R) stereochemistry as the side chain extends from the sterol core at the C-24, C-25, and C-27 positions. In contrast, ORP4 interactions with ligands possessing (R) stereochemistry at the C-24 generated non-sigmoidal competitive binding curves, which suggests a more complex binding interaction with these ligands. These results indicate that the (R) stereo-configuration at C24 could produce OSBP selective binding steroidal compounds.

  This is the first report that both the (R) and (S) epimers of C25, 27-OHC (22, 23) are high affinity ligands for OSBP, but not for ORP4 (Fig. 3, Table 2), indicating this position may be exploited to develop OSBP or ORP4 selective therapeutic compounds. Conversely, a recent paper reported that an analog of OSW-1 (3) with a C-27 hydroxyl, among other structural changes, selectively targets ORP4 over OSBP.10 These differing results could indicate that OSW-1 does not bind to OSBP and ORP4 through exactly the same interactions as the oxysterols. Importantly, recent reports have shown that C25, 27-OHC plays a key role in cancer progression, particularly in ovarian cancer 49,50.

  Sterol core hydroxylation does not appear to increase ligand interaction with OSBP or ORP4 (Fig. 3, Table 2). Further probing of side chain binding requirements using a library of side chain analogs (i.e., SA compound) revealed that any change in the length of the sterol isohexyl side chain impairs binding to both OSBP and ORP4 (Fig. 4, Table 3). We hypothesize the strict requirement of the sterol isohexyl side chain for binding may be a mechanism to limit binding to the OSBP/ORPs to only cholestane oxysterols and not to other classes of steroids, such as bile acids and steroid hormones. Curiously, we have previously shown that the OSBP-targeting antiviral compound, THEV (8), which completely lacks a sterol side chain, is a high affinity ligand for OSBP and ORP4 (Fig. 1C) 6. Our competitive binding results using a THEV analog (41) and molecular docking homology modeling suggests that THEV could interact with these proteins in an inverted manner with the THP moiety acting as a proxy for the sterol side chain (Fig. 5). The postulated inverted oxysterol binding capability of OSBP and ORP4 could be relevant to the binding of natural products cephalostatin 1 (Fig. 1, (4)) and ritterazine B (Fig. 1, (6)) which are bissteroidal compound fused through their A-ring.7 Further, our results with the THEV compound would also be consistent with the existence of a second OSBP and ORP4 ligand binding modality that overlaps with the 25-OHC binding site or allosterically controls 25-OHC binding. A second oxysterol binding site on OSBP and ORP4 could also explain the higher-order, non-sigmoidal binding interactions observed with 24(R)-OHC against ORP4 in the [3H]-25-OHC competitive binding assay.

  Altogether, our results suggest that OSBP and ORP4 can be targeted by a variety of oxysterols with differing core and side chain hydroxylation, as well as structurally modified oxysterols, with varying affinities. OSBP and ORP4 may also be selectively targeted by manipulating side chain hydroxylation and stereochemistry. However, high affinity oxysterol interaction with OSBP and ORP4 in the sterol binding pocket will require the sterol isohexyl side chain or an isohexyl isostere. The THP moiety on THEV is a probable example of an isohexyl isostere. The information gained from these SAR studies will be critical for informing the development of novel antiviral and precision anticancer compounds specifically targeting either OSBP or ORP4.


## EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

### Biological Experiments:
  Ligand binding to OSBP and ORP4 was performed using our established 96-well [3H]-25-hydroxycholesterol competitive binding assay, as done previously.3 Briefly, OSBP or ORP4 cDNA in the pcDNA 3.1 plasmid is overexpressed in 293T cells. S-100 lysate of the OSBP or ORP4 expression cells are used for the ligand binding assay.


### Protein/Ligand Docking Experimental:
  A homology model of OSBP was built using the online tool SWISS-MODEL, which selected the crystal structure of human ORP2 as the most similar template (PDB code: 5ZM8, 40% sequence similarity and 48% sequence coverage) 4,5. The starting ORP4 homology model was obtained from Zhong et al. 7. The initial search box was determined by superimposing Saccharomyces cerevisiae OSH4 bound to 25-hydroxycholesterol (PDB code: 1ZHX) 8 onto the ORP4 and OSBP homology models. The homology models and ligand files were prepared for docking using Auto-DockTools-1.5.6 9. All docking was performed using Autodock Vina 10 against a rigid receptor, and docking results were visualized using UCSF Chimera 11. Additional experimental details provided in the Supporting Information.


### Organic Synthesis Experimental:
  A diverse library of 20-hydroxycholesterol side chain analogs (i.e., SA compounds 30-41) were made through reaction of pregnenolone with various alkyl Grignard reagents (Figure S-3B). The THEV and truncated THEV compound were made as done previously. 6 All compounds were purified via HPLC to >95% purity prior to biological testing. Compound purity was shown through HPLC analysis.
