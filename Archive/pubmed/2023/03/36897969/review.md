## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text (main text, methods summary, significance statement)
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific claims, evidence quality, methodological soundness, and interpretational validity as presented in the provided material
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The manuscript proposes that transcribing RNA polymerases act as moving barriers to loop-extruding cohesin complexes, thereby shaping 3D chromatin organization around active genes. The authors combine Hi-C, ChIP-seq, and polymer simulations to support a model in which RNAP translocation relocalizes cohesin, generating characteristic contact patterns including insulation at gene boundaries, stripes, and dots between gene ends. They further argue that cohesin loading is not preferentially targeted to promoters, challenging prior assumptions about NIPBL enrichment at TSSs.
- **Visible evidence base** Hi-C contact maps in WT, CTCF KO, Wapl KO, CTCF-Wapl DKO, and Smc3 KO cells; Scc1 ChIP-seq; Pol II ChIP-seq; HA-tagged NIPBL and MAU2 ChIP-seq with degradation controls; DRB inhibition experiments; polymer simulations with active and passive loop extrusion under uniform and TSS-targeted loading conditions
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Supplementary figures and methods referenced but not provided; full simulation parameter sweeps not shown; quantitative goodness-of-fit metrics between simulations and experiments not presented; raw ChIP-seq peak calling statistics and reproducibility metrics not shown; DRB treatment efficacy data not fully described

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript presents a conceptually important and timely hypothesis: that transcription itself, through moving RNAP barriers, can shape 3D genome organization by interacting with loop-extruding cohesin. The combination of experimental genomics and biophysical simulation is well suited to the question, and the authors are appropriately careful in distinguishing between competing models. The central claim, that RNAPs act as moving barriers that relocalize cohesin and generate specific contact patterns, is plausible and partially supported by the data shown. However, several key inferences rest on indirect evidence, and the manuscript would benefit from additional quantitative comparisons between simulations and experiments, as well as clearer presentation of control experiments. The NIPBL ChIP-seq reanalysis is a notable contribution but requires careful scrutiny of antibody specificity and peak calling methodology. Overall, the work is of high interest to the chromatin and gene regulation communities, but the case is not fully established from the provided material alone.

- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Chromatin biologists studying 3D genome organization, loop extrusion, and cohesin function; transcription biologists interested in how active transcription feeds back on genome architecture; computational biologists developing polymer models of chromosome folding; researchers studying enhancer-promoter communication and gene regulation. The work bridges biophysical mechanism and genomic observation, making it relevant across these fields.

- **Major strengths** The manuscript addresses a fundamental question with an elegant conceptual model. The combination of Hi-C, ChIP-seq, and simulations is powerful and appropriate. The authors test multiple competing models and clearly identify which class of model best matches experiments. The NIPBL ChIP-seq reanalysis with tagged cell lines and degradation controls is a rigorous approach to resolving a contentious issue. The predictions generated by the model are explicit and testable, and the authors follow through on testing them experimentally.

- **Major Concerns**

- **Concern ID** R1-M1
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** Yes
- **Axis** Evidence strength for causal mechanism
- **Claim pointer** The manuscript claims that transcribing RNAPs act as moving barriers that push or impede loop-extruding cohesin, and that this interaction generates the observed contact patterns around active genes.
- **Evidence pointer** Figures 1B, 1C, 2A, 2D; SI Appendix Figures S2, S3, S5, S8, S9
- **Concern** The causal role of RNAP translocation in generating the observed contact patterns is inferred primarily from correlations between transcriptional activity, cohesin accumulation, and contact features, plus simulations. The DRB experiments show partial suppression of features, but the manuscript does not provide a quantitative comparison between DRB-treated data and simulation predictions with stalled RNAPs. The distinction between RNAP acting as a physical barrier versus RNAP-associated factors or chromatin state changes mediating the effect is not directly tested.
- **Why it matters** The central claim of the manuscript is that the physical interaction between translocating RNAP and cohesin is the mechanistic driver. Without direct evidence that RNAP translocation per se, rather than transcription-associated chromatin remodeling or RNAP occupancy, is responsible, the causal model remains one of several possible interpretations.
- **Resolution test** Provide quantitative fits of simulation outputs to experimental contact maps under DRB conditions. Include a control where RNAP is stalled but chromatin state is otherwise unperturbed. Alternatively, use an experimental system where RNAP translocation speed is altered without changing occupancy.

- **Concern ID** R1-M2
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** Yes
- **Axis** Quantitative model-experiment comparison
- **Claim pointer** The manuscript claims that the moving barrier model with active extrusion "reproduced the major features of active gene organization remarkably well" and that uniform loading reproduces experimental Hi-C maps better than TSS-targeted loading.
- **Evidence pointer** Figure 2D; SI Appendix Figures S4, S5
- **Concern** The comparison between simulation and experiment is qualitative. No quantitative metrics (e.g., correlation coefficients, distance-scaled contact map similarities, insulation score comparisons) are provided. The claim that uniform loading is superior to TSS-targeted loading is based on visual inspection of representative maps. The manuscript does not report how simulation parameters were chosen, how robust the conclusions are to parameter variation, or whether the differences between models are statistically significant.
- **Why it matters** The manuscript draws strong conclusions about the mode of cohesin loading and the validity of the moving barrier model. Without quantitative model selection criteria, the reader cannot assess whether the conclusions are robust or whether alternative parameter regimes could produce similar or better fits.
- **Resolution test** Provide quantitative similarity metrics between simulated and experimental contact maps for all models tested. Perform parameter sweeps and show that the conclusions hold across a reasonable parameter range. Report statistical significance of model comparisons.

- **Concern ID** R1-M3
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** No
- **Axis** Interpretation of NIPBL ChIP-seq data
- **Claim pointer** The manuscript claims that NIPBL is not predominantly enriched at promoters and that previous ChIP-seq results may have been artifacts of antibody specificity and hyper-ChIP-able regions at active TSSs.
- **Evidence pointer** Figure 4A-E; SI Appendix Figures S12-S14
- **Concern** The reanalysis of NIPBL localization is an important contribution, but the manuscript does not fully address the possibility that the HA-tagged NIPBL fusion protein may behave differently from endogenous NIPBL. The degradation controls are commendable, but the manuscript does not report the efficiency of degradation in the ChIP-seq experiments, nor does it show that the remaining peaks after degradation are truly background. The comparison with previous antibody-based results is somewhat indirect, as the antibodies were tested in different cell lines and conditions.
- **Why it matters** The claim that NIPBL is not enriched at promoters challenges a widely held assumption. If this claim is to overturn prior conclusions, the evidence must be airtight, including demonstration that the tagged protein faithfully reports endogenous localization and that the degradation controls are complete.
- **Resolution test** Show degradation efficiency by Western blot alongside ChIP-seq. Perform spike-in normalization to quantify absolute changes. Test the HA-tagged NIPBL in the same cell line used for previous antibody studies, if feasible.

- **Concern ID** R1-M4
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** No
- **Axis** Generalizability of findings
- **Claim pointer** The manuscript implies that the moving barrier mechanism is a general principle for how transcription shapes 3D chromatin organization in mammalian cells.
- **Evidence pointer** Figures 1-3; SI Appendix Figures S1-S3, S10, S11
- **Concern** The experimental data are derived primarily from mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) and HeLa cells. The manuscript does not address whether the observed patterns are cell-type specific or generalizable. The simulations are performed on a generic 1D chromatin model with parameters that may not capture cell-type-specific features such as gene density, replication timing, or nuclear architecture.
- **Why it matters** The title and significance statement make a general claim about transcription shaping 3D chromatin organization. If the mechanism is only relevant in certain cell types or genomic contexts, the claim should be appropriately scoped.
- **Resolution test** Analyze additional cell types or publicly available Hi-C data from diverse cell lines. Discuss the parameter regimes in which the model predictions would differ and whether such regimes are biologically plausible.

- **Minor Comments**

- **Concern ID** R1-m1
- **Severity** Minor
- **Axis** Clarity of model description
- **Affected element** Methods section, "Computation of loop extrusion dynamics"
- **Evidence pointer** Methods, "Cohesin dynamics" and "RNA polymerase dynamics"
- **Issue** The description of simulation parameters is terse. The manuscript states that cohesin loading is either uniform or TSS-targeted but does not specify the exact loading rate distributions, the fraction of TSS-targeted loading in the mixed model, or how the 100-fold enrichment was chosen.
- **Required correction** Provide a table of all simulation parameters with values and units, and describe how each was chosen or calibrated.

- **Concern ID** R1-m2
- **Severity** Minor
- **Axis** Statistical reporting
- **Affected element** Hi-C and ChIP-seq analyses
- **Evidence pointer** Figures 1-4; Methods
- **Issue** The manuscript does not report the number of biological replicates for Hi-C and ChIP-seq experiments, nor does it provide error bars or confidence intervals for key quantitative claims (e.g., insulation strength, peak enrichment).
- **Required correction** Add replicate information and error estimates to figures and text, or explicitly state that data are from single experiments.

- **Concern ID** R1-m3
- **Severity** Minor
- **Axis** Interpretation of DRB experiments
- **Evidence pointer** SI Appendix Figures S1F, S1G, S3G
- **Issue** The DRB treatment is described as partially suppressing dots and insulation, but the manuscript does not discuss the possibility that DRB has off-target effects on cohesin dynamics or chromatin structure independent of transcription.
- **Required correction** Add a brief discussion of DRB specificity and any control experiments performed to rule out off-target effects.

- **Concern ID** R1-m4
- **Severity** Minor
- **Axis** Terminology consistency
- **Affected element** Throughout
- **Evidence pointer** Figures 1-3; Results
- **Issue** The terms "islands," "dots," and "stripes" are used to describe contact map features, but these are not formally defined or quantified. The relationship between "island-island dots" and previously described "dots" at CTCF sites is not explicitly clarified.
- **Required correction** Define these terms in the Results or Methods, and provide a schematic or annotation in figures to aid the reader.

- **Concern ID** R1-m5
- **Severity** Minor
- **Axis** Discussion of alternative models
- **Affected element** Discussion
- **Evidence pointer** Discussion section
- **Issue** The manuscript does not discuss alternative explanations for cohesin accumulation at TSSs, such as cohesin loading by the Scc2/4 complex at active promoters or recruitment by transcription-associated factors, beyond the moving barrier model.
- **Required correction** Add a paragraph acknowledging alternative models and explaining why the data favor the moving barrier interpretation.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that RNAP "actively relocalizes" cohesin is supported by simulation but not directly demonstrated experimentally; the experimental data show correlation, not causation.
- The claim that NIPBL is not predominantly enriched at promoters is supported by the new ChIP-seq data, but the generalizability to other cell types and the completeness of the degradation controls are not fully established from the provided material.
- The claim that uniform cohesin loading reproduces experimental data better than TSS-targeted loading is based on qualitative visual comparison; quantitative metrics are not provided.
- The prediction that TSSs of divergently oriented genes form contacts is stated as consistent with the model, but the statistical significance of this enrichment in experimental data is not reported.
- The manuscript states that "island-island dots are insulating (comparably to CTCF)," but the quantitative comparison of insulation strength between island-island dots and CTCF-associated boundaries is not shown.
