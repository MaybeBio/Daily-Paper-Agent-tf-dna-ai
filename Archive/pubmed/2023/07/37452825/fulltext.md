# Two-stage response adaptive randomization designs for multi-arm trials with binary outcome


## Abstract
  In recent years, adaptive randomization methods have gained significant popularity in clinical research and trial design due to their ability to provide both efficiency and flexibility in adjusting the statistical procedures of ongoing clinical trials. For a study to study multiple treatments, a multi-arm two-stage design could be utilized to select the best treatment from the first stage and further compare that treatment with control in the second stage. The traditional design used equal randomization in both stages. To better utilize the interim results from the first stage, we propose to develop response adaptive randomization two-stage designs for a multi-arm clinical trial with binary outcome. Two allocation methods are considered: (1) an optimal allocation based on a sequential design; (2) the play-the-winner rule. Optimal multi-arm two-stage designs are obtained under three criteria: minimizing the expected number of failures, minimizing the average expected sample size, and minimizing the expected sample size under the null hypothesis. Simulation studies show that the proposed adaptive design based on the play-the-winner rule has good performance. A phase II trial for patients with pancreas adenocarcinoma and a germline BRCA/PALB2 mutation was used to illustrate the application of the proposed response adaptive randomization designs.


## Introduction
  Phase II clinical trials play an essential role in the process of developing new cancer treatments as they evaluate the effectiveness and safety of a new treatment, which can help researchers determine if the treatment(s) should proceed to the next phase for further development and testing. For a single-arm phase II clinical trial with binary endpoint, Simon’s two-stage optimal design is frequently employed in practice because it optimizes the expected sample size [1, 2, 3]. When historical controls are not reliable [4, 5, 6], randomized trials are recommended to provide valid results [7, 8, 9].

  For a trial with multiple treatments being evaluated, the optimal multi-arm two-stage design by Thall et al.[10] may be utilized to assess the efficacy of these new treatments as compared to the control. In their design, the best treatment among multiple treatments was selected in the first stage with the gold treatment as the control group, and it was then further compared with the control arm in the second stage. Within each stage, equal randomization was employed to assign patients to the treatment groups and the control group. A test statistic was calculated at the end of the first stage and compared with the test cut-off to determine if the trial can be moved to the next stage.

  If futility was observed after the first stage (e.g., no treatments are better than the control), the trial was stopped to prevent participants from receiving ineffective treatments. Otherwise, the trial would proceed to the second stage with additional participants. Data from both stages were used to make a final decision by comparing the best treatment with the control. Their optimal design was derived by minimizing the average of the expected sample size under the null hypothesis (H0) and the expected sample size under the alternative hypothesis (Ha). Comparing the optimal two-stage design with the Dunnett balanced one-stage design, Thall et al. [10] concluded that the reduction in expected sample size was quite substantial, as in each case, the largest possible sample sizes of the two-stage designs were smaller than the fixed sample sizes of Dunnett’s balanced designs.

  In recent years, adaptive design approaches in clinical research and trial designs have become increasingly popular, as they offer both effectiveness and flexibility in adjusting statistical procedures of clinical trials in progress [11]. Researchers have utilized these methods to modify and enhance the existing designs [12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. For two-stage adaptive designs, one of the most widely studied response adaptive randomization schemes is the play-the-winner rule (PWR). Zelen [18] was among the first to introduce and implement the PWR in controlled clinical trials. Suppose in a two-arm clinical trial, a patient is randomly assigned to one of the two treatments. If the patient experiences success on the received treatment, an additional trial on the same treatment with a new patient is generated. If the patient fails on the treatment, then an additional trial on the opposite treatment with a new patient is generated. Besides the famous PWR, Rosenberger et al. [19] also derived an optimal allocation based on the criterion of minimizing the expected number of treatment failures, and this allocation rule was established through a theorem proposed by Melfi et al. [20].

  In this paper, we propose to incorporate response adaptive randomization (RAR) approaches into Thall’s optimal two-stage design. In the first stage, a certain number of patients are equally randomized to all the treatment groups and the control group. After picking the best treatment with the highest observed response rate, the trial proceeds to the second stage. In the second stage, instead of equal randomization or a fixed sample size ratio, RAR methods are applied to obtain a sample size ratio depending on the outcomes from the first stage. We use two optimal allocation formulas mentioned above: (1) the optimal allocation rule based on a sequential design [19], and (2) the well-known play-the-winner rule (PWR) [18] to calculate the stage 2 sample size ratio between the best treatment group and the control group. The use of RAR allows more patients to be assigned to the better treatment group in the second stage. Then the number of treatment failures is minimized [21, 22, 23, 24].

  The following of article is organized as follows. In section 2, we illustrate the two optimal RAR designs for multiple treatments including type I error rate and statistical power calculation, and details on optimal design search. Then, in section 3, we first use simulation studies to compare the proposed RAR designs with the Thall design under three criteria: the average of expected sample size under H0 andHa, the expected sample size underHa, and the expected number of failures. After that, we use an example from a cancer trial to demonstrate how the proposed RAR designs can be applied to clinical trial research. In the last section, we provide some comments.


## Methods
  In this paper, we use RAR approaches to develop optimal two-stage designs aimed at resolving the challenge of determining optimal treatment among K treatments and assessing its superiority over the control. The designs also have the capacity to halt ineffective treatments after the first stage. The procedures and steps for the designs are illustrated in Section 2.1. The calculation of statistical power and Type I error rate is given in Section 2.2. The methods employed to search for the optimal designs are described in Section 2.3.

  Consider a randomized clinical trial with K treatments E1,E2,…,EK and the gold standard as the control armE0. Let p1,p2,…,pK be the response rates for all K treatments and let p0 be the response rate for the control group. For simplification, we assume thatp1≤p2≤…≤pK. The statistical hypothesis of interest is presented as:  H0:p0=p1=p2=…=pK vs. Ha:p1=p2=…=pK−1=p0+δ1,pK=p0+δ2

  We let the response rate p0+δ1 be a limited improvement over the control and the response rate p0+δ2 be a significantly substantial improvement over the control, where δ1 and δ2 are constants with0<δ1<δ2<1−p0.


### Test statistics for two stages
  Let xij be the number of responders for group Ei at stage j (i=0,…,K;j=1,2). In the first stage, a total of N1=(K+1)n1 participants are randomly assigned to one of the groups with a sample size of n1 per group. Then the response rate at this stage is estimated as p^i1=xi1/n1 for eachEi. Define Zi1=(4n1)12a(p^i1) where a(x)=arcsin(x) is the inverse sine function ofx. The test statistic T1 for stage 1 is therefore calculated by  T1=12max1≤i≤K(Zi1−Z01).

  Suppose y1 is the critical value forT1. A high value of T1 is preferable. Then, ifT1>y1, we select the treatment Eb which has the highest response rate and move on to stage 2. Otherwise, we stop the study and fail to rejectH0.

  Suppose N2 is the total number of participants in the second stage and r is the possibility of allocating participants to the treatment Eb in the second stage. The sample size for the treatment group is then n2E=rN2 and the sample size for the control group isn2C=(1−r)N2. Then, the total sample size for the best treatment group and the control group from two stages isn=2n1+n2C+n2E, and the ratio of sample sizes from the first stage isπ=2n1/n.

  The response rates for the treatment and control are p^b2=xb2/n2E andp^02=x02/n2C, respectively. Define Z02=(4n2C)12a(p^02) andZb2=(4n2E)12a(p^b2). The test statistic T2 using data from both stage 1 and stage 2 is therefore  T2=12{π12(Zb1−Z01)+(1−π)12(Zb2−Z02)}.

  Suppose y2 is the test cut-off at stage 2. IfT2>y2, then we reject H0 and conclude thatpb>p0. IfT2≤y2, we fail to rejectH0.

  Optimal RAR designs are established based on the following two RAR approaches:  RAROA:r=p^b1p^b1+p^01,   RARPWR:r=1−p^01(1−p^01)+(1−p^b1).

  The RAROA approach is the optimal allocation (OA) that can minimize the expected number of failures, and the RARPWR approach is established from the play-the-winner rule. We propose to adjust the second stage sample size ratio depending on the results from stage 1, which can allow more participants to be assigned to the best treatmentEb. Thall’s two-stage design utilizes equal randomization with r=0.5 in the second stage.

### Search for optimal designs
  Three optimization criteria are employed in this article to search for optimal designs: expected sample size under H0(ESS0), averaged expected sample size (ESSavg), and expected number of non-responders (ENR). They are calculated as  ESS0=(K+1)n1+2n2 pr(T1>y1|H0),   ESSavg=12(ESS0+ESS1),  and  ENR=(q0+qK)n1+∑x0=0n1∑xK=0n1pr(x01=x0,xK1=xK|Ha)I(T1>y1)(q0n2C+qKn2E),  whereESS1=(K+1)n1+2n2 pr(T1>y1|Ha), q0=1−p0 andqK=1−pK.

  Two constraints, TIE≤αandPower≥1−β, are engaged across all the designs with pre-specified α and β values. We want to identifyn1, n2, y1, and y2 that minimize the objective functions while meeting the TIE and power constraints. The search algorithm starts with initial values n10 and n20 obtained from the Thall design. Let n1l=n10−12 and n1u=max(n10+12,(n10+n20)/2+12) be the low and upper bound for the first stage sample size per arm. Then, for each n1∈[n1l,n1u] andn2∈[10,n20+21], we use y1 ranging from 0.3 to 0.9 with an increment of 0.005 and y2 ranging from 1.6 to 4 with an increment of 0.005 to search for the optimal design that minimize the objective function. The range of y1 and y2 values were determined from the Thall design.

  For each optimization criterion, we search for optimal designs with equal randomization, RAROA, and RARPWR. The Thall design is the one under the criterion of ESSavg with equal randomization. The designs with equation randomization under the other two criteria are simple extension of the Thall design. The remaining 6 optimal designs ae RAR designs: RAROA-ESSavg design, the RAROA-ESS0 design, the RAROA-ENR design, the RARPWR-ESSavg design, the RARPWR-ESS0 design, and the RARPWR-ENR design, respectively.


## Results

### Simulation
  In this study, we conduct extensive studies to compare the balanced and unbalanced two-stage designs with RAR designs given power β=80% and significance levelα=0.05. In the balanced two-stage design, the sample size ratio between the best treatment and the control arm is 1 in the second stage. For the unbalanced two-stage design, we included the design with 40% participants being assigned to the control group in the second stage, and the remaining 60% to the best treatment identified from the first stage. This drop-the-loser design with fixed ratio of 40% to the control group is referred to be as the DF40 design [25, 23].

  The studied response rate of the control group, p0, is from 0.1 to 0.6 with an increment of 0.1. The response rate difference between the control group and the first K−1 treatment groups is set asδ1=0.05, while the rate difference between the Kth treatment group and the control group is set asδ2=0.2. Since the Thall design was developed only under the criterion of minimizing ESSavg, to provide a fair and comprehensive comparison between the equal randomization and RAR designs, we have also acquired optimal equal randomization designs under the ENR and ESS0 criteria.

  Table 1 presents the parameters for obtained optimal designs under the ENR criterion as well as comparisons between the two proposed RAR designs, the equal randomization design, and the DF40 design when there are K= 3 treatment groups. Under the criterion of minimizing ENR, the results in Table 1 allow us to observe several key trends. When comparing the proposed RAR designs with the equal randomization design, it becomes evident that the RARPWR design has a profound advantage over both the equal randomization design and the RAROA design in terms of the expected number of failures in all cases. On average, the RARPWR design can reduce the expected number of failures by more than 6% as compared to the equal randomization design. Moreover, it can be seen that the savings in ENR from the RARPWR design increases as p0 increases from 0.1 to 0.6, and whenp0=0.6, the savings in ENR from the RARPWR design can exceed10%. When examining the sample sizes under the minimized ENR criterion, the results suggest that the ESSavg, ESS0, and maximum possible sample sizes (Nmax) of the RARPWR-ENR design are all smaller in the RARPWR design than the equal randomization design and the RAROA-ENR design. This suggests that the RARPWR design not only results in fewer expected failures, but also requires fewer resources to achieve these results as compared to these two designs.

  As compared the DF40 design, the RARPWR-ENR design may have one more expected number of non-responders when p0 is small (e.g.,p0≤30%), but that trend is reversed when p0 is larger than 30% givenδ2=20%. We also investigated the effect of δ2 on ENR between these designs in Table 1 with δ2 from 20% to 40% given p0=20% andK=3. As δ2 increases, the average proportion of participants being assigned to the best treatment group in the second stage goes up, from 71% to 75% for the RARPWR-ENR design. As the difference between the best treatment and the control group becomes larger, the proposed RARPWR-ENR design has better performance than the DF40 design.

  When analyzing and comparing the RAR designs and the equal randomization design under the criterion of minimizing ESSavg, Table 3 shows that the RARPWR design stands out as being superior to the equal randomization design and the RAROA design. The RARPWR design is capable of reducing the average expected sample size by an average of 3.2% as compared to the equal randomization design. However, as p0 becomes larger and K increases, the savings in the average expected sample size from the RARPWR design becomes smaller. For the expected number of failures and the expected sample size under the null, the RARPWR-ENR design consistently results in smaller sample sizes and fewer failures than the equal randomization design and the RAROA-ENR design. The threshold value y1 for T1 in the DF40 design is much smaller than that for other designs. For that reason, the DF40 design is more likely to have a higher error rate in the first stage to identify the best treatment.

  Similar results are observed in Table 4 where the RAR designs and the equal randomization design are compared under the ESS0 criterion, with K= 3 treatment groups taken into consideration. The RARPWR design outperforms the equal randomization design and the RAROA design with regard to the expected sample size underH0. As compared to the equal randomization design, the RARPWR-ESS0 design lowers the ESS0 with an average of 1.9% in general. As p0 increases, the savings in ESS0 for the RARPWR design compared to the equal randomization design become smaller and eventually become trivial as p0 approaches 0.6.

From the figure, it can be observed that ENR is a decreasing function of p0 whenp0≥20%. ENR goes up as p0 is increased from 10% to 20% in a two-stage design setting. This follows the same trend for a traditional one-stage randomized trial comparing two proportions. Additionally, the figure highlights the behavior of the ESSavg as p0 increases, revealing that the ESSavg is an increasing function of p0 when p0≤0.4 and a decreasing function of p0 whenp0>0.4. Similarly, the relationship between the ESS0 and p0 also follows a bell-like curve, with the maximum value of ESS0 occurs when p0 is approximately 0.4. As depicted in the figure, it is also evident that the RARPWR design demonstrates superiority over the equal randomization design and the RAROA design whenK=3. The DF40 design may a fixed sample size ratio in the second stage, while the proposed RAR designs allow that ratio to be changed according the outcomes from the first stage. We observed similar results when K=2 andK=4.

  In Table 5, we presented the simulated type I error rate and statistical power for the optimal RAR designs under the ENR, ESSavg, and ESS0 criteria when there are K=2 treatment groups and 1 control group. The results show that the empirical type I error rate and statistical power are quite close to the nominal values α=0.05 andβ=0.2. Similar results are obtained when there are 3 treatments and 4 treatments. This indicates that the proposed RAR designs successfully maintain the desired level of statistical significance and power.


### Example
  A randomized two-arm phase II trial with an open-label multi-center design was conducted by O’Reilly et al. [26] to examine the effects of combining cisplatin and gemcitabine with or without the addition of veliparib in patients diagnosed with pancreas adenocarcinoma and a BRCA/PALB2 mutation in their germline. Patients who met the eligibility criteria with gBRCA/PALB2 positive and untreated pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) were randomly assigned to arm A or arm B, where patients in arm A received a combination of cisplatin, gemcitabine, and veliparib, and patients in arm B only received cisplatin and gemcitabine. The main objective of the trial was to compare the response rates of the two arms, and Simon’s two-stage minimax design was employed independently in arm A and arm B. The trial was originally designed to detect a promising rate of 30% as compared to the unacceptable response rate of10%.

  In order to apply the multi-arm equal randomization design and our proposed RAR designs to the trial, we assume another treatment arm C, and estimate the response rate for the control group (arm A) to be10%, for the treatment group 1 (arm B) to be20%, and for the treatment group 2 (arm C) to be30%. We expect to achieve the statistical power of 80% given α=10%. Under the smallest ESSavg criterion, the Thall design requires a total of N1=3×43=129 patients in the first stage and N2=21×2=42 patients in the second stage, and the RARPWR-ESSavg design can reduce the average expected sample size by3.6%. If the goal is to minimize ESS0, more patients are expected in the second stage and the RARPWR design can save ESS0 by4.5%. For minimizing ENR, the designs generally have more patients in the first stage and fewer patients in the second stage. Both the equal randomization design and the RAROA-ENR design would have 147 patients in the first stage and 20 patients in the second stage, while the RARPWR-ENR design would have 144 patients in the first stage and 20 patients in the second stage. With regards to the expected number of failures, the RARPWR-ENR design results in a smaller ENR of 91.7, compared to 93.0 for the RAROA-ENR design and 93.6 for the equal randomization design. The savings in ENR from the RARPWR design is 2.1% as compared to the equal randomization design. The DF40 design has a slightly smaller ENR than the RARPWR design (91.3 VS 91.7) that is consistent with the findings from simulation studies when δ2 is small (20% in this trial).


## Discussion
  RAR designs allow a study to modify the sample size ratio between the best treatment and the control group in the second stage. We compared the performance the RAR designs using two commonly used reasponse adaptive randomization schemes. Simulation results have suggested that the proposed RARPWR design consistently outperforms the equal randomization design and the RAROA design under all three criteria: minimizing the expected number of failures, minimizing the average of expected sample size under the null hypothesis and the alternative hypothesis, and minimizing the expected sample size under the null hypothesis only.

  It is important to note that in the rare cases where r is close to the boundary values of 0 or 1, it is possible that all patients may be assigned to one group and no patients in the other group. To avoid this issue, we have implemented a contingency plan where we would assign one patient to the other group in these cases to ensure that each group has at least one patient in the second stage, and therefore allows us to calculate the response rates for the treatment and control correctly and obtain meaningful test statistics for stage 2 [27, 28]. This contingency plan can help mitigate the risk of potential biases or inaccuracies in the results so that the design remains reliable and robust.

  Other than the type I error and type II error mentioned before, a third type of error may arise in the selection of treatments. This error occurs when one of the suboptimal treatments is chosen among the K−1 treatments instead of the optimal treatment Ek given the condition of Ha [10]. It is not appropriate to classify this selection as a type II error since the null hypothesis is not accepted; however, this error is considered to be highly unfavorable as it indicates the trial has failed to accurately identify a crucial treatment improvement. The probability of this third type error, denoted asγ=pr(choose sub optimal treatment|Ha), is calculated similarly to the calculation of power, and can also be estimated through simulations. In our study, we obtained γ values through calculations for all the optimal designs and compared to those estimated from performing 10,000 simulation runs [29, 30]. Our results show that the values generated from both approaches are found to be very close. In general, the probability of this third type of error is relatively small, with a range of 0.008 to 0.025. This suggests that the proposed RAR designs are highly effective in avoiding suboptimal treatment selections and accurately identifying essential treatment advance.
