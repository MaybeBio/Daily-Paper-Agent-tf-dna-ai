## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text (preprint, bioRxiv)
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific claims, methodology, experimental design, and evidence as presented in the provided text. No external data or code was accessed.
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors present EpiGePT, a transformer-based pretrained model that integrates DNA sequence, transcription factor (TF) expression profiles, and 3D chromatin interaction data to predict context-specific epigenomic signals and chromatin contacts. The model is claimed to outperform existing methods in epigenomic signal prediction, long-range interaction prediction, and variant effect prediction, and to enable predictions in novel cellular contexts not seen during training.
- **Visible evidence base** Abstract, Introduction, Results (including descriptions of Figures 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and Supplementary Figures S2-S14), Discussion, Methods (data processing, model architecture, evaluation, training strategy, baseline methods, interaction prediction, gradient importance scores, variant prioritization), Data availability.
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Figures and tables referenced in the text (Figures 1-6, Supplementary Figures S1-S14, Supplementary Tables S1-S9) were not provided. Detailed hyperparameter settings, model size, training compute, and convergence criteria are not fully specified. Code and trained model weights are not provided. Statistical test details (e.g., exact p-value calculations, multiple testing corrections) are partially described but not fully reproducible from the text.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript describes a timely and potentially impactful approach to epigenomic prediction, addressing a real limitation of existing sequence-only models by incorporating TF expression as a context signal and 3D genome data during training. The core idea of using a transformer with a context-dependent input is conceptually sound and the reported performance improvements are promising. However, the current manuscript has several significant gaps in methodological detail and experimental validation that prevent a full assessment of the claims. The lack of figures and tables in the provided material makes it impossible to verify the magnitude and statistical robustness of the reported improvements. The novelty relative to existing models, particularly Enformer, needs to be more clearly delineated. The evaluation of variant effect prediction and the COVID-19 application, while interesting, require more rigorous benchmarking and clearer biological validation. The manuscript would benefit from a more thorough comparison with a wider range of baselines and a clearer discussion of the model's limitations and failure cases.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Computational biologists and bioinformaticians working on regulatory genomics, epigenomics, and deep learning applications in biology. Researchers interested in predicting the functional impact of non-coding variants, understanding gene regulation, and developing cross-cell-type predictive models would find this work relevant. The web server may also be of interest to experimental biologists seeking to prioritize variants or predict regulatory elements in specific contexts.
- **Major Strengths**
    1.  The conceptual advance of making the model context-dependent via TF expression profiles is a clear and important step beyond sequence-only models like Enformer, enabling cross-cell-type prediction.
    2.  The incorporation of 3D chromatin interaction data into the training process via a novel loss term is a creative and potentially powerful idea.
    3.  The manuscript covers a broad range of applications, from basic epigenomic signal prediction to variant effect prediction and disease association, demonstrating the potential utility of the model.
    4.  The provision of a web server is a valuable contribution to the community.
- **Major Concerns**
    - **R1-M1** (Severity: Major, Blocking: Yes) The central claim of superior performance is not verifiable from the provided text alone. The manuscript relies heavily on figures (Figures 2, 3, 4, 5) and supplementary figures to demonstrate performance improvements, but these are not included in the material provided for review. Without seeing the actual data, effect sizes, and statistical significance, it is impossible to assess whether the claimed improvements are real, substantial, and consistent across all settings. This is a fundamental issue that blocks a full evaluation of the manuscript's core claims.
    - **R1-M2** (Severity: Major, Blocking: Yes) The novelty and technical contribution relative to existing models, particularly Enformer, are not sufficiently delineated. The manuscript states that EpiGePT is superior to Enformer, but the architectural differences are not clearly explained. Enformer also uses a transformer architecture and is pretrained on a large corpus of epigenomic data. The specific architectural innovations of EpiGePT, beyond the input features (TF expression) and the auxiliary loss (3D interactions), are not clearly articulated. A detailed comparison of the model architectures, training data, and computational cost is needed to establish the technical novelty.
    - **R1-M3** (Severity: Major, Blocking: No) The evaluation of variant effect prediction is not rigorous enough. The manuscript uses eQTL data and ClinVar data, but the comparison to existing methods is limited. For eQTLs, the comparison is only against Enformer. For ClinVar, the comparison is against CADD, but it is unclear if the comparison is fair (e.g., are the same features used? Is the same training/validation split used?). The manuscript should include a more comprehensive benchmark against state-of-the-art variant effect predictors (e.g., Eigen, FATHMM, etc.) and should clearly describe the evaluation protocol.
    - **R1-M4** (Severity: Major, Blocking: No) The biological validation of the COVID-19 application is weak. The manuscript identifies top-ranked SNPs and performs GO enrichment on nearby genes. While this is a reasonable exploratory analysis, it does not provide strong evidence that EpiGePT is specifically useful for COVID-19 research. The analysis should be compared to a null model (e.g., using randomly selected SNPs) to show that the enrichment is specific. Furthermore, the link between the identified genes and COVID-19 pathology is largely speculative.
    - **R1-M5** (Severity: Major, Blocking: No) The Methods section lacks sufficient detail for reproducibility. Key hyperparameters (e.g., number of layers, heads, embedding dimensions, learning rate schedule, dropout) are not fully specified. The exact architecture of the convolutional blocks in the sequence module is not described. The procedure for generating negative samples for the interaction prediction tasks is described, but the details of the fine-tuning process (e.g., number of epochs, early stopping criteria) are missing. The manuscript should provide a complete and detailed description of the model and training procedures.
- **Minor Comments**
    - **R1-m1** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Clarity) The abstract is dense and could be more accessible to a broader audience. The term "epigenomics" is used without a brief definition.
    - **R1-m2** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Reproducibility) The manuscript mentions that the model was trained on 1,175,374 genomic regions for the cross-region setting, but the exact number of training, validation, and test regions is not specified.
    - **R1-m3** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Statistical Rigor) The manuscript reports p-values for some comparisons (e.g., Wilcoxon signed rank test) but does not always specify the number of samples used in the test or whether multiple testing corrections were applied.
    - **R1-m4** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Clarity) The description of the "cross-both" setting is clear, but the description of the "cross-region" and "cross-cell type" settings could be more explicit about how the data are split.
    - **R1-m5** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Completeness) The manuscript mentions a comparison with a "k-mer frequency based method" for interaction prediction, but the details of this method are not provided.
    - **R1-m6** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Presentation) The text in the Results section is dense and could benefit from a more structured presentation, perhaps with subheadings for each application.
    - **R1-m7** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Reproducibility) The manuscript states that the model was trained with a batch size of 10 and a learning rate of 5e-5, but it does not specify the number of training steps or epochs.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established**
    - R1-M1: Lack of access to figures and tables prevents verification of the primary performance claims.
    - R1-M2: Insufficient detail on architectural novelty and comparison to Enformer.
    - R1-M3: Incomplete benchmarking of variant effect prediction.
    - R1-M5: Insufficient methodological detail for reproducibility.
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria**
    - **Originality:** The idea of using TF expression as a context signal and incorporating 3D genome data into a transformer model is a novel combination. However, the individual components (transformers for genomics, TF expression for context) are not entirely new. The originality lies in their integration, which is a meaningful step forward.
    - **Scientific importance:** The ability to predict epigenomic signals and regulatory interactions in novel cell types is of high importance for understanding gene regulation and interpreting non-coding variants. The potential to improve variant effect prediction has direct relevance to human genetics.
    - **Interdisciplinary readership:** The work is primarily of interest to computational biologists and bioinformaticians. The applications to disease (COVID-19) may attract a broader readership, but the technical nature of the manuscript may limit its accessibility to non-specialists.
    - **Technical soundness:** The technical approach is sound in principle, but the lack of methodological detail and the inability to verify the results from the provided text are significant concerns. The evaluation protocols for some applications (e.g., variant effect prediction) are not fully rigorous.
    - **Readability for nonspecialists:** The manuscript is written for a specialist audience. The abstract and introduction are relatively accessible, but the results and methods sections are dense and assume a strong background in deep learning and genomics.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The core idea is promising and the potential impact is high. However, the manuscript in its current form does not provide sufficient evidence to fully support its claims. The authors must provide the missing figures and tables, clarify the architectural novelty, strengthen the benchmarking, and provide complete methodological details. Once these issues are addressed, the manuscript could be a strong candidate for publication.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that EpiGePT "consistently outperformed" baseline methods is unsupported without the actual performance data in the figures.
- The claim that EpiGePT "enables prediction in novel contexts without sacrificing performance" is a strong claim that needs to be carefully evaluated against the data in Figure 2d, which is not available.
- The claim that the model "offers insight on the functional impact of genetic variants under different cellular contexts" is supported by the eQTL and ClinVar analyses, but the magnitude of the improvement and its practical significance are not fully established.
- The biological relevance of the COVID-19 analysis is speculative and not rigorously validated.
- The claim that the model "captures regulatory interactions" via self-attention is supported by the interaction prediction results, but the mechanistic interpretation of attention weights as regulatory interactions requires further validation.
