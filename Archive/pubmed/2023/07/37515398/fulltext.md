# Single‐cell RNA sequencing identifies macrophage signatures correlated with clinical features and tumour microenvironment in meningiomas


## Background
  Meningiomas are common primary brain tumours, with macrophages playing a crucial role in their development and progression. This study aims to identify module genes correlated with meningioma‐associated macrophages and analyse their correlation with clinical features and immune infiltration.


## Methods
  We analysed single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) data from two paired meningioma and normal meninges to identify meningioma‐associated macrophages. High‐dimensional weighted gene co‐expression network analysis (hdWGCNA) was employed to identify module genes linked to these macrophages, followed by functional enrichment and pseudotime trajectory analyses. A machine learning‐based model using the module genes was developed to predict tumour grades. Finally, meningiomas were classified into two molecular subtypes based on the module genes, followed by a comparison of clinical characteristics and immune cell infiltration.


## Results
  Meningiomas exhibited a significantly higher proportion of macrophages than normal meninges, including novel macrophage clusters referred to as meningioma‐associated macrophages. The hdWGCNA analysis of macrophages within meningiomas unveiled 12 distinct modules, with the blue, black, and turquoise modules closely correlated with the meningioma‐associated macrophages. Hub genes within these modules were enriched in immune regulation, cellular communication, and metabolism pathways. Machine learning analysis identified 13 module genes (RSBN1, TIPRL, ATIC, SPP1, MALSU1, CDK1, MGP, DDIT3, SUPT16H, NFKBIA, SRSF5, ATXN2L, and UBB) strongly correlated with meningioma grade and constructed a predictive model with high accuracy and robustness. Based on the module genes, meningiomas were classified into two subtypes with distinct clinical and tumour microenvironment characteristics.


## Conclusions
  Our findings provide insights into the molecular characteristics underlying macrophage infiltration in meningiomas. The molecular signatures of macrophages demonstrate correlations with clinical features and immune cell infiltration in meningiomas.


## INTRODUCTION
  Meningioma is the most common primary brain tumour, constituting around 30% of all brain tumours. [1, 2]. It originates from the meninges, the protective tissue between the skull and brain [3]. The incidence rates vary by ethnicity and region, with non‐Hispanic White individuals having the highest rates and Asian or Pacific Islander individuals having the lowest rates [4]. Most meningiomas are classified as benign (grade I) and can be effectively treated through surgical intervention. These low‐grade tumours exhibit slow growth and a low likelihood of recurrence. However, 10%–20% of meningiomas are classified as high‐grade tumours, including atypical or anaplastic subtypes, which present a greater risk of recurrence and poor prognosis [5]. Specifically, anaplastic meningiomas display a median survival of only 1.5 years, underscoring the urgent need for alternative therapeutic strategies [6]. Moreover, in situations where surgical intervention is unfeasible due to the tumour's proximity to vital central nervous system structures or its deep intracerebral location, alternative therapeutic approaches become necessary.

  Meningiomas are characterised by an immunosuppressive tumour microenvironment (TME) that hampers anti‐tumour immune responses. Within the TME, macrophages play a pivotal role in mediating immunosuppression. They produce factors that promote tumour growth and invasion, hinder the function of effector T cells, and recruit regulatory T cells (Tregs) [7, 8, 9]. Targeting macrophages may enhance the effectiveness of immunotherapies in treating meningiomas. For example, blocking the CSF1/CSF1R axis, which regulates macrophage survival and differentiation, has shown promising results in reducing tumour burden and potentiating anti‐PD1 therapy in preclinical meningioma models [8]. The development of novel immunotherapeutic strategies for meningiomas may improve patient outcomes and address the clinical challenges of higher‐grade tumours [10, 11].

  In this study, we utilised single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) data from two paired meningiomas and normal meninges to identify novel macrophage clusters specifically associated with meningiomas, referred to as meningioma‐associated macrophages. Through the high‐dimensional weighted gene co‐expression network analysis (hdWGCNA), we identified module genes that exhibited strong correlations with these meningioma‐related macrophages. Functional enrichment and pseudotime trajectory analyses were subsequently conducted on these module genes to gain insights into their functional roles and temporal dynamics. To predict meningioma grades, we developed a novel machine learning‐based model based on the expression patterns of the module genes. Furthermore, we explored the correlation between the module genes and the tumour microenvironment (TME) of meningiomas. Finally, we classified meningiomas into two distinct molecular subtypes based on the module genes. By comparing the clinical characteristics and immune cell infiltration between these subtypes, we gained insights into the heterogeneity and tumour microenvironment characteristics within meningiomas.


## METHODS

### Data sources used for analysis
  We obtained the scRNA‐seq data for our study from the GSE183655 dataset available in the GEO database. [12] Specifically, we selected paired samples of normal meninges and meningiomas from two patients (GSM5567096, GSM5567097, GSM5567098, GSM5567100). Bulk RNA‐seq data were downloaded from the GEO database, including GSE136661 [13] with 160 meningioma samples from the Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform and GSE16581 [14]with 68 meningioma samples from the Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 ArrayAffymetrix. To establish a predictive model for meningioma grades, we utilised the GSE136661 cohort as our training set. Additionally, the GSE16581 cohort was employed as a validation set for the model. We retained multiple probes corresponding to a single gene by taking the median expression level to eliminate redundancy and ensure consistency in gene expression levels. Conversely, probes that corresponded to multiple genes were removed from the analysis. We also extracted clinical information from the raw data available in the GEO database to complement our analyses.


### Comprehensive analysis of the scRNA‐seq dataset and cellchat analysis
  ScRNA‐seq dataset analysis was performed using the Seurat (v4.1.1) in R [15]. Firstly, disqualified cells were excluded from the integrated dataset based on the following quality control criteria: (1) 200< nFeature_RNA <2500; (2) percentage.mt < 10%. The scRNA‐seq data from two patients were then integrated and batch corrected using the “Harmony” function. Gene expression of cells was normalised using a linear regression model, followed by screening the top 2000 genes with highly variable characteristics using ANOVA. Subsequently, dimensionality reduction was performed by selecting the top principal components (PCs) with the cumulative variance explained (retaining over 90%) and the point of smooth change in the elbow plot. Cell clustering was performed using the 'FindClusters' function in Seurat. Marker genes for each cell cluster were identified using Seurat's 'FindAllMarkers' function with the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test and adjusted p‐value <0.05 as criteria. SingleR (v1.8.1) [16] was used for cell type annotation. To gain insights into the cell‐cell communication landscape and highlight specific interactions relevant to meningioma biology, we further conducted CellChat analysis using the Cellchat package [17].


### Annotating cell types in bulk RNA‐seq datasets
  MCP‐counter is a method that quantifies the absolute abundance of 10 distinct cell populations within heterogeneous tissues, including eight immune cell populations and two stromal cell populations. This method utilises gene expression data and cell type‐specific transcriptomic markers to estimate the abundance of these cell populations [18]. MCP‐counter has been successfully applied to various human tissues and tumours to investigate the composition of the tumour microenvironment. In our research, we utilised the MCP‐counter method to evaluate the tumour microenvironment of meningiomas and investigate its correlation with the module genes identified in our study.


### hdWGCNA analysis of macrophages in meningiomas
  This study utilised the hdWGCNA R package to perform weighted gene co‐expression network analysis (WGCNA) on scRNA‐seq data [19, 20]. This package offers a modular design that allows the construction of co‐expression networks across diverse cellular and spatial hierarchies. By incorporating biological knowledge and leveraging interconnectivity between genes, hdWGCNA enables the identification of robust gene modules and their associated functional contexts. Therefore, we conducted our analysis following the official pipeline of hdWGCNA to investigate gene modules and their functional roles in macrophages derived from meningioma tissue. The specific website is https://smorabit.github.io/hdWGCNA/articles/basic_tutorial.html. Initially, we selected macrophage cells from the eight clusters identified by Seurat and filtered out genes with low expression (requiring expression in more than 5% of cells). The gene expression data were then normalised using the sctransform function. We calculated the Pearson correlation coefficient to capture co‐expression relationships between each pair of genes. A scale‐free network was constructed by determining the optimal soft threshold using the pickSoftThreshold function. Based on this threshold, we generated the adjacency matrix and topological overlap matrix (TOM). Co‐expression modules were identified from the TOM using the dynamicTreeCut algorithm, with each module assigned a unique colour. Hub genes within their respective modules were assessed for connectivity using eigengene‐based connectivity (kME) values calculated by the ModuleConnectivity function. Genes with higher connectivity or kME values are considered more central or influential within their respective modules. Additionally, module eigengenes (MEs) were calculated using the GetMEs function to summarise gene expression profiles within each co‐expression module. Pearson correlation analysis was then employed to assess the correlations among these MEs. Finally, we extracted the top 50 hub genes from each module based on their kME values using the GetHubGenes function. These hub genes were subsequently utilised for further analyses, including functional enrichment and clinical correlation analysis.


### Pseudotime trajectory and functional enrichment analysis of module hub genes
  We performed functional enrichment analysis on the top 50 hub genes from each module using the clusterProfiler package v4.0 based on Gene Ontology (GO) terms with adjusted p‐value <0.05 [21]. To investigate the relationship between cellular pseudotime trajectories of macrophages and module genes, we employed the “Monocle” R package (Version 2.0) [22]. Highly variable genes were filtered according to mean expression ≥0.1 and empirical_dispersion ≥1*fit_dispersion. Dimensionality reduction was performed using the DDRTree method. Subsequently, the ‘plot_pseudotime_heatmap’ function was employed to visualise a heatmap that illustrates the dynamic expression of module genes along the pseudotime trajectories of macrophages in meningiomas.


### Identification and validation of a predictive for meningioma grade based on module genes using machine learning
  We conducted a series of analyses to evaluate the efficacy of our selected module genes in predicting tumour grade. Initially, we employed the DEseq2 package to identify genes that exhibited differential expression between high‐grade meningiomas (grade II or III) and low‐grade meningiomas (grade I), using a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of less than 0.05 in GSE136661 dataset. Next, we intersected these differentially expressed genes with the hub genes identified earlier and performed univariate logistic regression analysis. Genes with a significance level of p < 0.05 were selected. To streamline the gene set, we applied LASSO regression [23] with 10 folds cross validation, utilising the reduced gene set as predictors for meningioma grade. To improve the clinical applicability, we employed the mlr3 package, a valuable toolkit that facilitated a comprehensive analysis of the models' performance. This package facilitated model selection, refinement, and assessment of their robustness. Additionally, we incorporated ensemble learning methods to enhance the overall prediction accuracy. Specifically, we utilised seven machine learning algorithms from the mlr3 package in R, including kknn (k‐nearest neighbours), Ida (iterative dichotomiser 3), log_reg (logistic regression), naïve_bayes (naïve Bayes classifier), ranger (random forest), rpart (recursive partitioning and regression trees), and SVM (support vector machines). We compared the performance of these algorithms by their Sensitivity, Specificity, False negative rate (FNR), and False positive rate (FPR). Furthermore, the predictive accuracy of the best‐performing model was validated in an independent cohort comprising 68 meningioma samples from the GSE16581 dataset.


### Non‐negative matrix factorization of meningioma by module genes
  To gain further insights into meningioma subtypes, we utilised the non‐negative matrix factorization (NMF) algorithm from the NMF R package (Version 0.20.6) [24]. Initially, we selected 57 hub genes that exhibited differential expression between low‐grade and high‐grade meningiomas (p < 0.05) in the GSE136661 dataset. Subsequently, we utilised the expression matrix for these selected genes in the NMF analysis to identify distinct meningioma subtypes.


### Statistical analysis
  All plots and statistical analyses were done in R software (version 4.2.1). We compared continuous and categorical variables between the two groups by independent t‐test and χ2 test, respectively. We also conducted univariate analyses by the logistic regression model. A two‐sided p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.


## RESULTS

### Identification of macrophages in meningiomas
  The workflow of this article is depicted in Figure 1. After quality control, 7921 cells were included in our analysis. These cells were clustered into 22 clusters using Seurat's FindClusters function with resolution = 0.8 based on the top 20 PCs (Figure 2A). Then, they were subsequently annotated as nine cell types by singleR, including chondrocytes, monocytes, NK cells, tissue stem cells, T cells, B cells, endothelial cells, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), and macrophages (Figure 2B). The proportion of macrophages and tissue stem cells in meningiomas significantly increased, while monocytes, NK cells, and B cells decreased significantly compared to normal meninges (Figure 2C–E). Subsequently, we subclustered macrophages into 8 clusters using Seurat's FindClusters function with resolution = 0.6 based on the top 15 PCs derived from macrophage‐specific highly variable genes (Figure 2F). The macrophages in meningioma showed significant differences in type and amount compared to normal meningeal macrophages (Figure 2G), with new subclusters of macrophages appearing in meningiomas (clusters 1, 4, and 6), which were designated as meningioma‐related macrophages. To investigate the cellular communication network within the diverse cell types present in meningioma, we conducted the CellChat analysis. This analysis allowed us to identify significant ligand‐receptor pairs involved in communication between meningioma‐associated macrophages, other macrophages, and surrounding cell populations. Notably, our findings revealed enhanced communication between meningioma‐related macrophages and monocytes as well as tissue stem cells compared to other macrophages (Figure 2H–I).


### hdWGCNA analysis of macrophages in meningiomas
  To explore gene modules and elucidate their functional roles within macrophages derived from meningioma tissue, we conducted high dimensional weighted gene co‐expression network analysis (hdWGCNA). This analysis identified 12 modules, with an optimal soft threshold of 7 (Figure 3A). The WGCNA dendrogram visualised the hierarchical clustering of genes based on their expression patterns. Each leaf in the dendrogram represents an individual gene, and the assigned colour at the bottom indicates its membership in a specific co‐expression module. The “grey” module comprises genes not assigned to any co‐expression module (Figure 3B). Figure 3C–D displays the top 10 hub genes of these 12 modules and illustrates the inter‐module relationships. Furthermore, we examined the expression distribution patterns of the hub genes among the eight macrophage clusters in meningioma (Figure 3E). Notably, the hub genes from these 12 modules exhibited distinct expression patterns among the macrophage clusters. Figure 3F demonstrates that modules blue (cluster 1), black (cluster 4), and turquoise (cluster 6) were significantly enriched in meningioma‐associated macrophages.


### Functional enrichment analysis and pseudotime trajectory analysis of the hub genes of modules blue, black, and turquoise
  Next, we conducted pathway enrichment analysis on the top 50 hub genes ranked by kME from each module. The results revealed distinct functional enrichments for each module. The blue module showed enrichment in functions like kinase activator activity, regulation of viral genome replication, and mitochondrial inner membrane, suggesting its association with cellular signalling and metabolism (Figure 4A). The black module exhibited enrichment in genes related to the cellular response to biotic stimulus and chemokine receptor binding, indicating its involvement in cell communication (Figure 4B). The turquoise module was enriched in terms such as peptide binding, MHC protein complex, and antigen processing and presentation, indicating its involvement in immune‐related processes (Figure 4C). These findings collectively suggest that meningioma‐associated macrophages play crucial roles in immune regulation, cell communication, and cellular metabolism within the context of meningiomas.

  We further explored the changes in expression patterns of these hub genes during macrophage development using pseudotime trajectory analysis. Figure 4E–F illustrates the developmental trajectories and specific characteristics of macrophages in meningioma. Our focus was on three clusters of meningioma‐associated macrophages: Cluster 1 represents macrophages in the late developmental stage, Cluster 4 represents macrophages in the middle developmental stage, and Cluster 6 represents macrophages in the early developmental stage. The heatmap visualises the pronounced expression changes of hub genes during macrophage development in meningioma (Figure 4G). Importantly, these hub genes' expression patterns align with their corresponding clusters' developmental states. These findings indicate that the hub genes exhibit dynamic expression changes that coincide with the developmental stages of macrophages in meningioma.


### Identification and validation of a predictive model for meningioma grade based on hub genes
  We conducted further investigations to explore the relationships between hub genes and clinical features, which can provide insights into the underlying mechanisms driving the meningioma phenotypes. We focused on predicting the pathological grade of meningioma patients and constructed a predictive model using hub genes from the three modules. Firstly, transcriptome data from 160 meningioma patients (GSE136661) were analysed, and 57 differentially expressed hub genes between high‐grade meningiomas (grade II or III) and low‐grade meningiomas (grade I) were identified with FDR<0.05. Among these genes, 23 genes (P < 0.05) were selected using univariate logistic regression and further reduced to 13 genes using LASSO regression (Figure 5A, B). These 13 genes included RSBN1, TIPRL, ATIC, SPP1, MALSU1, CDK1, MGP, DDIT3, SUPT16H, NFKBIA, SRSF5, ATXN2L, and UBB. To assess whether the expression of these 13 genes could predict meningioma grade, we utilised seven machine learning algorithms from the mlr3 package (Figure 5C). The performance of these algorithms was evaluated, and the naïve Bayes algorithm demonstrated the highest accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity in the training set (Figure 5D, Table 1). Subsequently, we validated the performance of the model using an independent cohort of 68 meningioma samples. The model effectively distinguished high‐grade and low‐grade meningioma patients, achieving an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.884 (Figure 5E). Overall, our findings suggest that the expression of the identified 13 genes may serve as a potential predictor for the pathological grade of meningioma patients. These results provide new insights into the application of machine learning in meningioma research and may have implications for personalised treatment strategies.


### The hub genes correlated with the clinical features and tumour microenvironment of meningioma
  Subsequently, we explored the relationship between the hub gene and the tumour microenvironment, and the clinical features of meningioma. To achieve this, we first evaluated the gene set enrichment score of the hub gene in different grades in the GSE136661 dataset. As shown in the Figure 6A, GSVA scores were significantly higher in high‐grade meningiomas (p = 0.018), indicating a potential association between the hub genes and tumour grade. Furthermore, the 13 genes selected by LASSO regression exhibited significant heterogeneity and were closely related to immune cell infiltration in meningioma (Figure 6B). We then utilised NMF clustering to classify meningioma patients into two subtypes, type 1 and type 2, based on the clustering criteria derived from the differentially expressed hub genes (Figure 6C–D). Comparing the two subtypes, we observed that type 1 patients had a higher proportion of high‐grade tumours, a higher proportion of male patients, a higher incidence of recurrence, and a higher MIB1 index (Figure 6E–H). Additionally, the analysis of immune infiltration in the two subtypes revealed that type 1 patients had lower proportions of NK cells, neutrophils, and endothelial cells but higher proportions of cytotoxic lymphocytes compared to type 2 patients (Figure 6I–J). These results suggest that hub genes are closely related to the tumour microenvironment of meningioma. Based on these hub genes, meningioma patients can be divided into two subtypes with unique microenvironmental and clinical characteristics.


## DISCUSSION
  Through single‐cell analysis, we identified distinct cell types with varying proportions in meningiomas and normal meninges, particularly in macrophages. We found a significant increase in the proportion of macrophages in meningiomas, including the emergence of new macrophage subpopulations, which may be related to the occurrence and development of meningiomas. In addition, we utilised CellChat to analyse the communication pathways between different macrophage subpopulations and with other cell types. We discovered that macrophage subpopulations associated with meningiomas exhibit greater communication ability with monocytes and tissue stem cells, which may affect their behaviour and function within the tumour microenvironment. Moreover, our study unveiled the existence of distinct macrophage subpopulations within meningiomas, characterised by specific gene expression profiles and functional characteristics. These findings are consistent with previous research in the field. By mapping the distribution and communication pathways of these subpopulations within meningiomas, our study provides new perspectives and leads to understanding the intricate role of macrophages in the occurrence and development of meningiomas.

  Furthermore, we conducted high‐dimensional weighted gene co‐expression network analysis on macrophages in meningiomas, revealing 12 modules. Among these modules, three showed significant associations with meningioma‐associated macrophages. The hub genes within these modules were found to be enriched in pathways related to immune regulation, cell communication, and cell metabolism. It suggests that these pathways may be crucial in regulating macrophage functions within the meningioma microenvironment. Moreover, we observed distinct expression dynamics of the hub genes during macrophage differentiation, indicating developmental relationships and functional differences among the macrophage subpopulations. These findings provide novel insights into the genesis process and biological importance of meningioma‐associated macrophages.

  In addition, our study employed machine learning techniques to identify 13 genes that have the potential to predict the pathological grades of meningioma patients. The predictive model's robustness was validated using an independent cohort of meningioma patients, demonstrating its potential clinical applicability. These findings have significant implications for personalised treatment strategies in meningiomas. Previous studies have investigated the role of various genes in the development and progression of meningiomas, such as NF2, AKT1, and SMO [25, 26]. However, our study has identified a novel set of genes that may have predictive value for meningioma grade, suggesting their functional relevance to the pathogenesis of meningioma. Several identified genes have been previously associated with cancer progression and tumour growth. For instance, SPP1 is overexpressed in meningioma and linked to an aggressive phenotype [27]. Similarly, CDK1 is a gene that codes for a protein called cyclin‐dependent kinase 1, which is involved in cell cycle regulation and transition from the G2 phase to mitosis. CDK1 has been associated with various types of cancer, such as colorectal cancer [28] and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [29]. Overall, the identification of these predictive genes and the use of machine learning algorithms for model development represent a breakthrough in the field of meningioma research.

  In the final part of our study, we found that the gene set enrichment scores of the module genes significantly increased with tumour grade, indicating potential variations in macrophage infiltration in meningiomas of different grades. Similarly, Dustin et al. found that macrophage infiltration was higher in high‐grade meningiomas and mainly of the M2 subtype [30]. Chen et al. divided meningiomas into two subtypes based on m6a gene expression and found that the two subtypes had different immune microenvironment characteristics [31]. Similarly, we used NMF clustering to divide meningiomas into subtypes with unique immune microenvironments and clinical features based on macrophage‐related module genes. We found a subtype with a higher tumour grade, a higher possibility of recurrence, and a higher mitotic activity. These findings can help us better understand the biological mechanisms and prognostic factors of meningioma and provide new clues for future therapeutic strategies.

  This study has some limitations that need to be addressed in future research. First, the sample involved in scRNA‐seq is limited, which may affect the accuracy and reliability of the results. Second, our developed model needs more datasets to validate its generalisability and robustness. Third, the role of elected hub genes in meningioma should be further experimentally explored to elucidate their molecular mechanisms and therapeutic potential.


## CONCLUSION
  In summary, our study makes several significant contributions to meningioma research. Firstly, we observed a higher proportion of macrophages in meningiomas than adjacent normal meninges, along with identifying new macrophage clusters specific to meningioma. It highlights the potential importance of macrophages in the tumour microenvironment of meningiomas. Secondly, using hdWGCNA analysis, we identified module genes closely correlated with meningioma‐associated macrophages and elucidated their trajectory process. Functional enrichment analysis provided insights into the underlying mechanisms of macrophage infiltration in meningiomas. Thirdly, we developed a novel predictive model utilising machine learning techniques, which incorporated module genes to predict the grade of macrophages. This model holds promise as a valuable tool for clinical decision‐making in meningioma management. Finally, we identified module genes that correlate with the TME of meningioma and discovered two clusters with distinct clinical features. In summary, our study provides valuable insights into the role of macrophages in meningiomas and offers potential avenues for future research and therapeutic interventions.
