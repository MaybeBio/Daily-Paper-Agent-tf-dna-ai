# Conformational exchange at a C2H2 zinc-binding site facilitates redox sensing by the PML protein


## SUMMARY
  The promyelocytic leukemia protein, PML, plays a vital role in the cellular response to oxidative stress; however, the molecular mechanism of its action remains poorly understood. Here, we identify redox-sensitive sites of PML. A molecule of PML is cysteine-rich and contains three zinc–binding domains including RING, B-box1, and B-box2. Using in vitro assays, we have compared the sensitivity of the isolated RING and B-box1 domains and shown that B-box1 is more sensitive to oxidation. NMR studies of PML dynamics showed that one of the Zn-coordination sites within the B-box1 undergoes significant conformational exchange, revealing a hotspot for exposure of reactive cysteines. In agreement with the in vitro data, enhancement of the B-box1 Zn-coordination dynamics led to more efficient recruitment of PML into PML nuclear bodies in cells. Overall, our results suggest that the increased sensitivity of B-box1 to oxidative stress makes this domain an important redox-sensing component of PML.


## INTRODUCTION
  The promyelocytic leukemia protein (PML) plays a role in diverse cellular pathways, including DNA repair, antiviral responses, apoptosis, senescence, and stem cell maintenance 1-3. Most attributed functions of PML are indirect and are due to its critical role as an organizer of sub-nuclear compartments known as PML nuclear bodies (PML-NBs). These PML-NBs are spherical bodies consisting of an outer shell of PML protein and an interior that contains various partner proteins 4,5. The PML shell first forms in an oxidation-dependent manner through the covalent multimerization of PML molecules, which occurs via cross-linking of cysteine side chains through disulfide bonds 6. Recruitment of partner proteins follows the formation of this PML shell 4,7,8. The specific set of proteins in a PML-NB varies based on factors such as DNA damage, environmental stress, and phase of the cell cycle 1,9-11. Thus, PML-NBs appear to be the sites at which target proteins accumulate to modulate, enhance, or suppress their activity 1,3,8. More recent studies have also pointed to a role for PML-NBs in the detection of, and response to, oxidative stress 6,8,12,13. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) have been shown to promote both PML multimerization and NB enlargement in living cells 6,8.

  As a member of the tripartite motif (TRIM) family of proteins, PML is enriched in cysteine residues that could participate in redox sensing 14-16. The TRIM family is named for a common set of three domains: a RING domain, a B-box2 domain, and a coiled-coil domain 17. The PML protein, also known as TRIM19, contains an additional B-box1 domain located between the RING and B-box2 domains (Figure 1A). Each of the two B-box domains coordinates two Zn2+ ions through a combination of cysteine and histidine residues 14,15. Each zinc-binding site consists of four coordinating residues, at least two of which are cysteines. Consequently, most structural zinc-binding sites have a configuration of C4, C3H1, or C2H2 as the coordinating residues 18-20. In addition to the zinc-coordinating residues, the RING and B-box2 domains contain one surface-exposed cysteine each, and additional cysteines are found downstream of the TRIM motif. The integrity of the full TRIM is required for proper PML-NB formation, as point mutations in zinc-coordinating cysteine residues either preclude the recruitment of partner proteins to PML-NBs or eliminate PML-NBs altogether 14,15,21. Reduced PML forms non-covalent dimers through its coiled-coil domain 22. Covalent PML multimerization and NB formation occur spontaneously in cultured cells but are enhanced by treatment with oxidants such as arsenic and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) 4,6. In the absence of added oxidants, PML multimerization for NB formation likely utilizes surface-exposed cysteine thiol groups, as zinc-coordinating cysteine residues are required for the structural integrity of the protein. However, sufficiently high oxidant concentrations can remove zinc from proteins, thus exposing additional cysteine side chains to the oxidizing environment 23-25. While this often leads to protein unfolding and degradation, some proteins use zinc release as a means of ROS detection in the response to oxidative stress 24,26. This raises the question of whether ROS-induced NBs form through canonical cross-linking of surface-exposed cysteines or whether ROS buildup in cells induces PML unfolding and multimerization through cysteine residues that ordinarily bind zinc.

  Here we characterize the impact of oxidizing conditions on the stability and multimerization state of the RING and B-box1 domains of PML in vitro. We show that both domains multimerize and aggregate in the presence of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). Of the two domains, we observe that B-box1 is more prone to multimerization. We have characterized the conformational dynamics of RING and B-box1 using NMR spin-relaxation and NMR relaxation dispersion measurements and show that B-box1 undergoes significant conformational exchange that occurs around one of its two zinc-coordinating sites, thus potentially exposing reactive cysteine residues and triggering the PML multimerization process. We have further validated the connection between the conformational exchange and oxidation susceptibility with a novel mutant of B-box1 domain that exhibits increased conformational exchange. We show the enhanced conformational dynamics of the Zn-coordination site leads to elevated zinc release and greater susceptibility of PML to oxidizing agents in vitro, as well as more efficient formation of PML-NBs in cells. Collectively, our data point to a role for PML unfolding and exposure of zinc-coordinating side chains in the response to harsh oxidative stress and suggest that B-box1 may be especially sensitive to the oxidation state within cells.


## RESULTS

### PML RING and B-box1 domains multimerize in response to hydrogen peroxide treatment
  Our first objective was to assess the multimerization state of individual PML domains in response to various concentrations of the oxidizing agent hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). Out of the three Zn-binding domains of PML, including RING, B-box1, and B-box2, the former two could be readily expressed as individual domains 27-30. Both domains were treated with increasing concentrations of H2O2 from 0 to 10mM, and their multimerization was detected using reducing and non-reducing SDS-PAGE (Figure 1B). In the absence of H2O2, primarily monomeric species of both RING and B-box1 were observed. A minor fraction of RING dimerizes in the absence of H2O2, presumably due to disulfide formation through the single surface-exposed cysteine residue, C65. The addition of H2O2 induces multimerization of both domains in a concentration-dependent fashion. This multimerization is oxidation-dependent and occurs through the formation of intermolecular disulfide bonds, as multimeric species are seen only in the non-reduced gels. Furthermore, the formation of multimeric species larger than dimers by RING, and any multimeric species by B-box1, indicates that multimerization in the presence of H2O2 involves at least some cysteine residues that ordinarily coordinate zinc. This can only be possible if structural zinc is released from the domains as part of the response to H2O2. Thus, both RING and B-box1 are capable of multimerizing in response to oxidation, and cysteine residues that coordinate zinc in their reduced state contribute to this oxidation-driven multimerization.


### RING and B-box1 release coordinated zinc in response to oxidation
  To confirm the loss of zinc by RING and B-box1, we treated both domains with a range of concentrations of H2O2 for 2h, then used the zinc chelator 4-(2-pyridylazo)resorcinol (PAR) to detect Zn2+ that had been released into the buffer (Figure 1C). The reaction of free zinc with PAR shifts the peak absorbance of PAR from 411 to 493nm 31. Both domains were found to release zinc in the presence of high concentrations of H2O2, with the release of Zn2+ reaching saturation in the presence of 5 mM H2O2. These findings match very well with the concentrations of H2O2 that elicit multimerization of both proteins. The extent of zinc release by RING and B-box1 was similar at all concentrations measured. This result confirms that oxidation-dependent multimerization of zinc-binding PML domains is coupled to the release of structural zinc.


### B-box1 precipitates during H2O2 treatment, but RING remains soluble
  During the in vitro treatments of both PML domains with H2O2, we observed extensive precipitation of B-box1 at high H2O2 concentrations, whereas no visible precipitation was observed with RING. This suggested that B-box1 is more prone to aggregation than RING in an oxidizing environment. To quantify the solubility difference between RING and B-box1 following oxidation, we treated both purified domains with H2O2 for 2 hours, removed precipitates by centrifugation, and quantified the remaining soluble protein by absorbance measurements at 280nm. Interestingly, although RING and B-box1 domains both release zinc, unfold, and multimerize in response to hydrogen peroxide treatment, the aggregates of PML RING remain soluble, as seen in Figure 1D. In sharp contrast, the soluble B-box1 concentration dropped to about 50% in the 2mM H2O2 treatment and approached 0% at higher H2O2 concentrations. This observation confirms that B-box1 is more sensitive to an oxidizing environment than the RING domain.


### PML B-box1 exhibits conformational exchange around its C2H2-type zinc-binding site
  To characterize protein dynamics of the B-box1 and RING domains of PML we have used solution NMR spectroscopy.


#### Pico-nanosecond dynamics:
  To characterize the Brownian rotational diffusion of B-box1 and its conformational flexibility on the pico-nanosecond timescale, backbone 15N NMR relaxation measurements were used, including 15N R1 and R2 relaxation rates and heteronuclear 15N NOE values 32,33. Our data suggest a high degree of rigidity throughout most of the domain, with several flexible residues at the N terminus and the immediate C terminus. This is especially evident in a decrease in 15N{1H} NOE values and lower R2 rates at the termini (Figure S1A). Model-free analysis 34 provided a similar trend in residue-specific order parameters (S2), which serve as a more accurate indicator of flexibility. Lower S2 values were obtained for the B-box1 N and C termini, and the rest of the domain displayed higher S2, indicative of a rigid structure (Figure 2A-B). This analysis indicates that the region from residues 128-164, which encompasses both zinc-binding sites, is highly rigid. Flexibility increases near both termini, including a highly flexible region at residues 120-124. Importantly, our analysis does not indicate any fast-timescale motion in or near the zinc-coordinating sites of B-box1. These results suggest that the isolated B-box1 is a rigid molecule with increased sub-nanosecond-timescale flexibility of the termini, as evidenced by a decrease in 15N{1H} NOE values, lower R2 rates, and elevated R1 rates for amino acid residues located in loops I and II (Figure 2B). A similar analysis of the isolated RING domain indicated that this domain also consists of a highly rigid core with flexible N and C termini (Figure 2C-D and Figure S1B). Collectively, these results indicate that both B-box 1 and RING are rigid domains on the fast NMR timescale.


#### Micro-millisecond dynamics:
  To determine whether RING and B-box1 of PML undergo conformational exchange on the slower timescale, we have collected and analyzed Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill (CPMG) relaxation dispersion data for these domains. CPMG experiments are commonly used to detect the conformational exchange of a protein between its major NMR-visible state and its sparsely-populated, invisible state (as low as 1%), characterized by different chemical shifts and exchanging on a μs-ms timescale 35. CPMG analysis provides chemical shift differences (Δω) between the major and minor conformations, populations of the exchanging conformations, and the exchange rate constant (kex) for the process. Figure 3A shows representative 15N CPMG relaxation dispersion profiles for select residues in B-box1. Residues H155, Q156, F158, and K160, displayed the largest relaxation dispersions that indicate significant conformational exchange localized in this region. CPMG analysis yielded the global parameters of exchange, including a kex of 573.7 ± 29.1 s−1 and a population of the minor conformation of 2.3 ± 0.1%, as well as per-residue Δω that reflect structural differences between the minor and major conformations (Figure 3B). A similar analysis of the isolated RING domain did not reveal strong relaxation dispersions for any residues (Figure S2), which indicates an absence of conformational exchange on the μs-ms timescale.

  Figure 3C shows the Δω between the major and minor conformations mapped onto the structure of B-box1. Remarkably, residues undergoing the largest conformational exchange localize in and around the coordination site of Zn 2. H155 participates in the coordination of this zinc ion, while Q156, F158, and K160 occur in a segment that connects the two histidine residues of this C2H2-type site. This segment begins with the C-terminal end of the alpha helix and transitions into an unstructured loop. These data suggest that the C2H2 zinc-coordination site of B-box 1 undergoes significant exchange that may transiently interrupt zinc coordination at this site, leaving the two cysteine residues more vulnerable to oxidation. In contrast, none of the residues in or near the C4 zinc-coordination site (Zn 1) shows any particularly strong dispersions, suggesting that this site does not undergo significant conformational rearrangement between the major and minor conformations of B-box1. Thus, we propose that the decreased stability of the B-box1 domain in an oxidative environment is a consequence of conformational exchange that occurs at its C2H2-type zinc-coordinating site.


### The conformational exchange at the B-box1 C2H2 site is inconsistent with the monomer-dimer exchange
  PML B-box1 has been shown to dimerize 29. It is possible that the conformational exchange we observe reflects monomer-dimer exchange rather than the dynamics of Zn-coordination. To rule out this possibility, we estimated the population of the B-box1 monomer based on its dimerization affinity and compared it to the CPMG results. The dissociation constant (KD) of 167 ± 32 μM was experimentally determined using concentration-dependent NMR experiments (Figure 4A-D). Based on the KD, the 0.75mM CPMG sample of B-box1 would contain 28% of the monomer and 72% of the dimer. This is in stark contrast to the populations of 2.3% and 97.4% of the exchanging species detected by CPMG. The population of monomeric B-box1 and its dimerization KD were independently confirmed by analytical ultracentrifugation (AUC) (Figure 4E and Figure S3), resulting in the KD of 115 ± 5 μM.

  The gradual movement of peaks in the NMR spectrum as the dimer fraction increases suggests that B-box1 dimerization occurs on the fast timescale (Figure 4A) which is different from the intermediate timescale conformational exchange detected by CPMG experiments. To estimate the timescale of the B-box1 dimerization, we determined the lower limit for the kex of the process (see Experimental Procedures section for details). The average kex of the monomer-dimer exchange was determined to be ~29,000 s−1 (8,000 to 90,000 s−1 for individual residues), which is at least two orders of magnitude faster than the CPMG-detected conformational exchange localized to the Zn 2 binding site (kex of 574 s−1). Therefore, the monomer-dimer exchange is not expected to interfere with the CPMG data analysis.

  Although some amino acid residues that undergo conformational exchange are also involved in dimerization (Figure 4B-C), the estimated populations of the exchanging species and the timescales of the exchange are drastically different, suggesting that the CPMG experiments detect conformational exchange localized to C2H2 zinc-binding site rather than the dimerization process.


### A C2H2-to-C4 mutant of B-box1 exhibits increased conformational exchange around its Zn 2 binding site
  The C2H2 site of B-box1 is the only zinc-binding site of this type in the PML protein (Figure 1A). This led us to the initial hypothesis that the composition of this site is responsible for the conformational exchange that we observe. To test this hypothesis, we generated a novel mutant B-box1 domain, H155C/H161C (hereafter C4C4), in which the binding site of Zn 2 has been mutated from a C2H2 composition to a C4 arrangement. We characterized the molecular dynamics of this mutant by NMR spectroscopy to determine whether the replacement of the histidine residues in this site with cysteine would impact the conformational exchange observed in the WT domain.


#### Pico-nanosecond dynamics:
  We used backbone 15N R1 and R2 relaxation rates and heteronuclear 15N{1H} NOE values to characterize the conformational flexibility of C4C4 on the pico-nanosecond time scale. As was the case with WT B-box1, C4C4 was found to be highly rigid throughout the domain except at the N- and C-termini. Residues 120-127 and 166-168 exhibit reduced R2 rates and 15N{1H} NOE values relative to the more rigid interior of the protein (Figure S4A). Model-free analysis confirmed lower S2 values at the termini (Figure S4B-C), indicating greater flexibility. Interestingly, our data suggest that residues 121-124 are more flexible in the WT domain than in C4C4, while the opposite seems to be true for A125 and A127. Residues 128-163, including both zinc-coordinating sites, were determined to be rigid, indicating a lack of fast-timescale motion in these residues.


#### Micro-millisecond dynamics:
  We analyzed 15N CPMG relaxation dispersion data for the C4C4 mutant. Figure 5A shows representative relaxation dispersion profiles for select residues in C4C4. This mutant showed decreased dispersions for many residues relative to the WT domain. However, very strong dispersions were observed for C140, C155, and C161, three of the cysteine residues that coordinate Zn 2 (Figure 5B). These residues were also found to have the greatest Δω between the major and minor conformations (Figure 5C). Data were not available for C143, the fourth cysteine that coordinates Zn 2. CPMG analysis revealed a kex of 572.0 ± 57.9 s−1, which was similar to the value for the WT domain, but a greater population of the minor conformation of 3.5 ± 0.3%. Thus, it appears C4C4 undergoes exchange at its Zn 2 site, and the minor conformation population is larger than that of the WT domain. Interestingly, the exchange event in C4C4 is limited to the zinc-coordinating residues and does not seem to involve non-coordinating residues in the loop that undergo exchange in WT B-box1 (Figure 5B-C). Nevertheless, our results point to enhanced conformational exchange in the C4C4 mutant of B-box1 relative to its WT counterpart.


### The C4C4 mutant of B-box1 releases zinc more readily than its counterpart
  The increased conformational exchange observed in the C4C4 mutant of B-box1, along with the striking localization of exchange to cysteine residues within the Zn 2 site, led us to suspect that this mutant is more prone to zinc release than WT B-box1. To determine if this is the case, we treated C4C4 with a range of H2O2 concentrations and quantified zinc release using PAR. As controls, we also examined zinc release from the B-box1 mutants W157E and F158E. These mutants retain the C2H2 site but do not dimerize 29, allowing us to confirm that differences in the response of C4C4 to oxidation are not due to disruption of dimerization. Whereas zinc release by W157E and F158E was indistinguishable from that of WT B-box1 (Figure S5A), C4C4 released zinc much more readily than the WT domain, particularly at low H2O2 concentrations (Figure 6A). Even in the absence of H2O2, we detected an average of 7.8 ± 1.5μM Zn2+ released by C4C4, which accounts for nearly 20% of the total zinc bound to the protein. Zinc release by C4C4 was consistently higher than that of B-box1 except at H2O2 concentrations at or above 5mM, at which points we observed saturation of zinc release. Thus, C4C4 exhibits an elevated rate of zinc exchange relative to WT B-box1. These results suggest that H155C mutation destabilizes the Zn 2 coordination site and leads to poorer retention of zinc by C4C4 mutant.


### Destabilization of the C2H2 Zn-binding site further sensitizes B-box1 to oxidation-dependent multimerization
  Since C4C4 readily releases zinc in the presence and absence of H2O2, we reasoned that this mutant would also multimerize more extensively than WT B-box1 when exposed to oxidizing conditions. We began by determining the multimeric state of C4C4, W157E, and F158E by AUC in the absence of H2O2. Interestingly, we observed that C4C4 is primarily monomeric at both 1mM and 100μM (Figure 6B). In agreement with the previous findings, both W157E and F158E were shown to be primarily monomeric at 1mM and 100μM (Figure S5B). Thus, a comparison of C4C4 to W157E and F158E will enable us to examine the roles that both the mode of zinc coordination and the multimeric state play in the sensitivity of B-box1 to oxidative stress.

  We next subjected C4C4, W157E, and F158E to the multimerization and precipitation assays that were performed on RING and WT B-box1. Remarkably, the C4C4 mutant of B-box1 was even more sensitive to oxidation than its WT counterpart (Figure 6C). Dimers and trimers of C4C4 formed even in the absence of H2O2, most likely due to oxidation by atmospheric oxygen. Treatment with H2O2 resulted in dose-dependent multimerization into high molecular weight species, with near-total depletion of the monomeric species at the highest concentrations of H2O2. No multimers were observed on the reduced gels, which confirmed that the observed multimerization was through disulfide bonds. Unexpectedly, this increased multimerization did not coincide with aggregation propensity, as C4C4 remained highly soluble over the course of H2O2 treatment (Figure 6D). W157E and F158E exhibited similar H2O2-driven multimerization profiles to WT B-box1 (Figure S5C) and some loss of solubility, although both mutants were more soluble than the WT domain (Figure S5D). These results suggest that the composition of the zinc-binding site is the major determinant of B-box1 covalent multimer formation and stability under oxidizing conditions. In contrast, the ability or failure to dimerize in solution does not impact oxidation-driven multimerization, although it may play a minor role in stability during H2O2 treatment.


### The hypersensitive mutant of PML B-box1 results in a more effective formation of PML-NB in cells
  To test whether C4C4 mutation would make the full-length PML more sensitive to oxidation we have compared WT PML-NBs to those formed by C4C4 PML mutant in cells. Isoform I of either WT PML or its C4C4 mutant was used to transfect the PML-depleted U2OS cells (PML−/−), and the number and the size of PML-NBs were compared in live cells. As shown in Figure 7, both YFP-tagged WT and C4C4 PML mutant readily formed PML-NBs; however, the C4C4 mutant formed significantly larger and fewer nuclear bodies, suggesting that enhanced conformational dynamics around the Zn binding site leads to more effective formation of PML-NBs. These data are consistent with the notion that the conformationally dynamic Zn 2 site of the B-box1 domain sensitizes PML to oxidation in cells.


## DISCUSSION
  PML NBs are membrane-less organelles formed in the nucleus in response to cellular stress. Oxidation-dependent PML polymerization is the key to the formation of PML NBs; however, the molecular mechanism of PML multimerization remains poorly understood. Here we have demonstrated that two zinc-binding domains of the PML protein, RING and B-box1, are susceptible to H2O2-induced multimerization and aggregation, with B-box1 being more sensitive. We further observed that B-box1 undergoes conformational exchange localized to one of its two zinc-binding sites, making it more vulnerable to oxidation. C2H2 to C4C4 mutation of this site results in sub-optimal Zn-coordination geometry, which leads to further enhancement of conformational exchange around this site, more extensive multimerization, and larger PML-NBs compared to the WT B-box1. These data strongly support a functional connection between conformational exchange involving the Zn-coordination site and oxidation susceptibility of the B-box1 domain.

  Based on these results, we propose the following model for PML oxidation, which is depicted in Figure 8. In the absence of oxidative stress, B-box1 exchanges between a more stable major conformation and a minor conformation in which the Zn 2 coordination site is altered. The minor conformation leaves the side chains of the two cysteine residues at this site vulnerable to oxidation. When modest oxidative stress occurs, these cysteine side chains form intermolecular disulfide bonds that contribute to PML multimerization. More intense oxidative stress leads to the oxidation of cysteine residues within the Zn 1 site of B-box1 and within both zinc-binding sites of RING. Importantly, we have not tested the B-box2 domain, which has previously been implicated as a mediator of oxidation-dependent PML multimerization 6. The role of B-box2 in redox sensing thus requires further investigation.

  Oxidative damage leads to misfolding or unfolding of various proteins, which can result in their loss of function, aggregation, and degradation 24. This is certainly true of zinc-binding proteins, whose proper folding and stability are dependent on their ability to bind and retain zinc 19,20,36. In the case of PML, part of its normal response to an oxidative environment is to multimerize and localize to PML-NBs, a function that we propose is augmented by ROS-induced aggregation of RING and B-box1. Under strongly oxidative conditions, both RING and B-box1 release zinc, which enables extensive multimer formation (Figure 1). This activity may constitute a sophisticated method for redox sensing, in which PML both detects and responds to harsh oxidative stress through the localized unfolding of one or more redox-sensitive sites. Activation of PML function through oxidation of zinc-binding cysteine residues is reminiscent of the mode of activation of several other redox-sensing proteins. For instance, the bacterial chaperone protein Hsp33 responds to oxidative heat stress and is activated by a combination of oxidation and elevated temperature. These combined stresses promote zinc release, formation of intramolecular disulfide bonds, and partial protein unfolding, which collectively activate Hsp33 26,37-39. Another example is the anti-sigma factor RsrA, which binds and represses σR, and thus negatively regulates the oxidative stress response in Streptomyces coelicolor. Oxidation of RsrA induces zinc release and intramolecular disulfide bond formation, which leads to major conformational changes in RsrA that promote the release of σR 40-42. From these examples and others, zinc displacement and changes in secondary or tertiary structure seem to have evolved as one method of redox sensing shared by diverse stress-response proteins.

  Our CPMG data suggest that the C2H2 zinc-binding site and adjacent loop make up the only site in B-box1 that undergoes significant conformational exchange (Figure 3). Furthermore, this exchange impacts the zinc-coordinating histidine residue that occurs in the α-helix but has little to no effect on the cysteines of this site. Combined with the fact that this zinc-binding site is the only C2H2-type site in PML, it is tempting to speculate that the composition of zinc-coordinating residues in this site—specifically, the increased use of histidine—is the reason for the reduced stability of this site. This idea would contradict a recent claim, based on a review of known affinities of zinc finger peptides, that cysteine residues reduce zinc finger stability. According to this claim, cysteine, but not histidine, must be deprotonated before zinc can bind it at neutral pH, leading to an enthalpic cost for each cysteine in the zinc finger 18,20. However, zinc fingers vary by several orders of magnitude in their KD for zinc 18,20, and these differences do not reflect any clear correlation between stability and amino acid composition of the zinc-binding residues. Several additional factors contribute to the stability of a specific zinc-binding site, including the structural class of the zinc finger, the orientation of coordinating residues, and the presence of charged residues around the zinc-binding site 18,20,43. Furthermore, affinities of C2H2, C3H1, and C4 sites for zinc are all remarkably strong, with KD values often in the low micromolar to femtomolar range 18-20. It is thus unlikely that the presence of more histidine residues in the C2H2 site of B-box1 is solely responsible for the conformational exchange that we see at this site, particularly since the exchange is more pronounced within the C4C4 mutant.

  A recent study by Deshmukh et al. 44 suggests that conformational exchange is an inherent property of some, but not all, zinc-binding sites, independent of their type. Deshmukh and colleagues used CPMG relaxation dispersion measurements and chemical exchange saturation transfer (CEST) to study the μs-ms timescale dynamics of two tandem zinc-coordinating sites in the HIV-1 nucleocapsid protein, NCp7. Both sites are zinc knuckles of the C3H1 variety, with the same arrangement and relative spacing of the four zinc-coordinating residues. Nevertheless, conformational exchange was observed in residues of the C-terminal knuckle, but not the N-terminal knuckle. Interestingly, the authors identified two minor populations of NCp7 in which one of the four zinc-coordination bonds in the C-terminal knuckle—that of either C413 or H421—was transiently disrupted. They further connected the disruption of the C413-Sγ-Zn coordination bond with the antiviral mechanism of mercaptobenzamide thioesters, which selectively acylate C413 of NCp7 45,46. Deshmukh and colleagues propose that these NCp7 inhibitors target the transient state in which C413 has an exposed thiol group. Acylation of this residue prevents restoration of the C413-Sγ-Zn coordination bond, ultimately leading to zinc release from the C-terminal knuckle and subsequent aggregation of NCp7 44-46. We envision a similar model of sensitization to oxidation at the C2H2 site of B-box1. Because we observe significant relaxation dispersion for H155 but not for C140 or C143, the two cysteine residues that coordinate Zn 2, we propose that conformational exchange at this site consists of partial unfolding at the C-terminal end of the α-helix, impacting residues 155-160. This change either pulls Zn 2 slightly away from C140 and C143 or alters the orientation of Zn 2 relative to the two cysteines, leaving their side chains more exposed to the environment of the nucleoplasm. Transient disruption of either Sγ-Zn coordination bond may also occur in the minor conformation of B-box1. In the presence of ROS, these exposed cysteine side chains more readily form intermolecular disulfide bonds, leading to PML multimerization, PML-NB enlargement, and ultimately an antioxidant response.

  PML and many proteins that reside in PML-NBs are subject to post-translational modification with small, ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) proteins 7,47-51. SUMOylation of specific lysine residues in target proteins can have diverse effects, such as regulating their enzymatic activity or interaction with partner proteins, modulating their solubility or sub-cellular localization, and promoting their ubiquitin-dependent degradation 52-54. In PML, there are three preferred SUMOylation sites: K65 in RING, K160 in B-box1, and K490 in the nuclear localization signal 51. The K160 site is especially noteworthy, as mutation of K160 to arginine eliminates SUMOylation at K65, interferes with PML-NB formation, and precludes arsenic-dependent degradation of PML 4,51. Our CPMG data indicate that the unstructured loop containing K160 is the site of conformational exchange on a μs-to-ms timescale, with K160 itself exhibiting the greatest Δω between the major and minor conformations (Figure 3B-C). Our observation is reminiscent of a previous report by Huang et al. of slow-timescale motion in the residues flanking K160 based on spectral density analysis 27. Importantly, the zinc-coordinating residue H161 falls within the SUMOylation consensus sequence for K160; therefore, K160 SUMOylation may have a significant effect on the structure and dynamics of B-box1 and PML function.

  In summary, we have identified B-box1 as a highly redox-sensitive domain of PML and proposed a molecular mechanism by which this domain may act as a redox sensor. NMR relaxation dispersion analysis highlighted a region of significant conformational exchange in and adjacent to the C2H2 zinc-coordinating site of B-box1. Our biochemical and cellular data strongly support the functional connection between conformational exchange at this Zn-coordination site and oxidation susceptibility of the B-box1 domain both in vitro and in cells. Furthermore, our results indicate a potential role for both surface-exposed and zinc-coordinating cysteine residues in oxidation-dependent PML multimerization. These findings not only add to the growing understanding of PML-NBs formation but also reveal a possible new role for zinc-coordinating cysteines that can serve as sensors of oxidation. Such molecular mechanism might be shared by other redox-sensitive proteins.

### METHOD DETAILS

#### Plasmids
  Codon-optimized genes of RING (residues 49-104), WT B-box1(residues 120-168), and its C4C4 mutant were chemically synthesized (GenScript) and inserted into the pGEX-6p-3 vector for bacterial expression (GE Life Sciences) using the BamHI and XhoI restriction sites. The encoded proteins thus included an N-terminal GST tag followed by a Turbo 3C protease cleavage site. Site-directed mutagenesis was used to introduce W157E and F158E point mutations of B-box1. The mutations were confirmed by sequencing. All expression constructs are available through AddGene (addgene.org).

  Plasmid pLNGY-PML.I for mammalian expression of EYFP-tagged PMLI 21 was a gift from Dr. Roger Everett (University of Glasgow). The C4C4 mutation was introduced into the plasmid using site-directed mutagenesis and confirmed by sequencing.


#### Protein expression and purification
  PML expression constructs were transformed into the BL21 strain of E. coli by heat shock and plated on LB medium with ampicillin. For each experiment, an isolated colony of the appropriate clone was transferred to 50 mL of LB broth for overnight growth at 37°C. Cells from the overnight culture were pelleted by centrifugation the following day, and the pellet was transferred to either 1 L of LB (for unlabeled protein expression) or minimal M9 medium (for isotopically labeled protein expression). Cells expressing unlabeled protein were grown in 1 L of LB at 37°C to an OD600 reading between 1.0 and 1.2 o.u., at which point protein expression was induced by 1 mM isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) (Research Products International) overnight at 16-18°C. Cells were then pelleted by centrifugation, and the pellet was frozen at −20°C until protein purification. Cells expressing labeled proteins were grown in 1L of M9 minimal medium with 1 g of 15N-ammonium chloride (Sigma-Aldrich) as the sole nitrogen source and supplemented with 200 μM zinc chloride (Acros). Medium for expressing 15N, 13C double-labeled C4C4 mutant for backbone NMR assignment also contained 13C-glucose (Sigma-Aldrich) as the sole carbon source. These cells were grown at 37°C to an OD600 reading of 0.8-1.0 o.u., at which point 1 mM IPTG was added, and the temperature was reduced to 20°C. Protein expression proceeded overnight. Cells were pelleted the following morning, and the pellet was frozen at −20°C until protein purification.

  Cell pellets were lysed by sonication in 50 mM Tris, 300 mM NaCl, 0.1 mM ZnCl2, 10 mM β-mercaptoethanol (BME) at pH 7.5. Insoluble components were removed from the lysate by centrifugation at 15,000 rpm in a Sorvall RC 6+ centrifuge. PML protein constructs were purified from the lysate by affinity chromatography on a Pierce® glutathione agarose column (Thermo Scientific). Purified proteins were eluted from the column with 32.5 mM (10 g/L) of reduced glutathione into 50 mM Tris, 250 mM NaCl, pH 7.2. The GST tag was cleaved by incubation with 10 μL Turbo3C™ protease (Accelagen) for up to 7 days or until the protein was about 70-100% cleaved, as assessed visually by SDS-PAGE. Cleaved proteins were further purified by size-exclusion chromatography using HiLoad 16/600 Superdex 75 pg column (GE) into the buffers indicated in the following sections.


#### Gel multimerization assays
  To assess the effect of oxidation on PML multimerization protein samples dissolved in 20 mM MES, 100 mM NaCl, pH 7.0 were incubated with increasing concentrations of H2O2. Titration points of 0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, and 10 mM H2O2 were set up as individual reactions. Each reaction was 150μL in total volume and contained 0.2 mM protein. Reactions proceeded for 2h before gel samples were prepared. Multimerization through disulfide bond formation was assessed by a combination of reducing and non-reducing SDS-PAGE using pre-cast 4-20% gels (GenScript). The loading buffer for reducing SDS-PAGE was prepared with 1M BME as a reducing agent. Loading buffers for both reducing and non-reducing SDS-PAGE also contained 10mM N-ethylmaleimide (NEM) (Thermo Scientific) to prevent de novo disulfide bond formation in the loading buffer. Protein bands were visualized by staining with Coomassie reagent after electrophoresis. A minimum of three non-reduced samples and two reduced samples per construct were prepared to ensure the reproducibility of the data.


#### Quantification of zinc release
  Release of Zn2+ ions upon PML oxidation was quantified using 4-(2-pyridylazo)resorcinol (PAR)-based colorimetric assay. All proteins were dissolved in the same buffer used for the gel multimerization experiments. Titration points of 0, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.5, 1, 5, and 10 mM H2O2 were set up as individual reactions. Each reaction was 150 μL in total volume and contained 20 μM protein. Reactions proceeded for 2 hours, then two 50 μL samples were mixed with 50 μL of 100 μM PAR. Absorbance readings at 493nm were taken on a SpectraMax® M3 Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (Molecular Devices). Data are presented as the mean ± SD quantification of free zinc in the undiluted reaction from a minimum of three replicate treatments per protein. Pairwise statistical comparisons of each protein to WT B-box1 at each H2O2 concentration were performed in GraphPad Prism 8 using one-way ANOVA with the Šídák multiple comparison test 59.


#### Quantification of protein precipitation
  All proteins were purified and treated with H2O2 using the same buffer and protocol as for the gel multimerization experiments. Reactions proceeded for 2 h, after which aggregates were pelleted by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 30 min followed by recovery of the supernatant. The protein concentration in the supernatant was estimated from the absorbance reading at 280 nm (A280). All calculated concentrations were normalized to the concentration determined for the 0 mM H2O2 sample to generate the fraction of soluble protein. Data are presented as mean ± S.D. of at least three replicate treatments. Pairwise statistical comparisons between the 0 mM H2O2 condition and each H2O2 concentration were performed in GraphPad Prism 8 using one-way ANOVA with the Dunnett test for multiple comparisons to a control 60.


#### Analytical ultracentrifugation
  Unlabeled proteins were purified by FPLC into 20 mM MES, 100 mM NaCl, 0.1 mM ZnCl2, and 10 mM BME. Partial specific volumes and solvent densities and viscosities were determined using SEDNTERP 61. SV analysis was performed using two-channel aluminum-Epon double-sector centerpieces and sapphire windows. Absorbance data were collected in a Beckman-Coulter Optima analytical ultracentrifuge or in a Beckman-Coulter XL-I analytical ultracentrifuge at two concentrations operating at 50,000 RPM and 20° C. For 0.1 mM samples, absorbance scans at 280 nm were collected every 90 seconds for 12 hours. For 1mM samples, interference scans were collected every 60 seconds for 7 hours. c(S) distributions were calculated using SedFit 62 and plotted using GUSSI 63.

  For analysis of the self-association dissociation constant of WT B-box1, the unlabeled protein was purified into the above buffer. Samples at 0.2, 0.1, and 0.05 mM were examined by ultracentrifugation at 50,000 rpm and 20°C in a Beckman-Coulter Optima AUC analytical ultracentrifuge. Absorbance scans at 280 nm were collected every 20 seconds for 12 hours. The difference SV data obtained at 50, 100 and 200 μM were globally fit to a monomer-dimer model using SEDANAL 64 to give a best-fit KD μM. The S-value for the monomer was fixed at 1.04S and the dimer at 1.41S.


#### Characterization of B-box1 dimerization by NMR
  All NMR experiments were collected at 25°C on Varian VNMRS 500 MHz spectrometer equipped with RT HCN probe. All spectra were processed in NMRPipe 65 and analyzed in SPARKY 66.


##### Dimerization affinity and dimer interface:
  Concentration-dependent changes in 15N-HSQC spectra were used to characterize B-box1 dimerization affinity. The NMR samples contained 15N-labeled B-box1, 20 mM Tris, 100 mM NaCl, 0.1 mM ZnCl2, and 10 mM BME at pH 7.0 and 10% D2O. The protein concentration was ranging from 1.35 mM to 0.10 mM. Per-residue chemical shift perturbations were calculated for 57 residues using combined chemical shift change of the amide nitrogen (ΔωN) and proton (ΔωH) as follows: Δω=(ΔωN2+ΔωH2)1∕2. The sum of all Δω (Δωtotal) was plotted as a function of B-box1 concentration (Figure 4D). The KD was determined by fitting the plot into the following equation:  y=Δωx(KD+x)+a,  where y is the observed Δωtotal, x is the concentration of B-box1, KD is the dimer dissociation constant, Δω is the maximum chemical shift difference between the lowest measured B-box1 concentration and that of a dimer, and a is the chemical shift difference between the lowest measured B-box1 concentration and that of a monomer. Data analysis was done in GraphPad Prism.


##### Dimer population:
  Concentrations of monomeric and dimeric species for any given protein concentration and KD of dimerization were calculated using the following relationship:  KD=[M][M][D]=([M0]−2[D])2[D],  where [M] is the monomer concentration, [D] is the dimer concentration, and [M0]=[M]+2[D] is the total protein concentration.


##### Dimerization timescale:
  The lower limit for the exchange rate constant kex between the B-box1 monomer and dimer was estimated from the exchange contributions to transverse relaxation Rex obtained in the model-free analysis of 15N relaxation data assuming that Rex are solely caused by the monomer-dimer equilibrium (the 15N R2 were measured at CPMG frequency νCPMG of 500 Hz). Specifically, kex was calculated for residues exhibiting the monomer-dimer 15N chemical shift differences >0.5 ppm (as determined from NMR dilution data fits) using the following equation:  Rex=pApBΔω2kex  , where pA and pB are populations of the monomer and dimer states, and Δω are 15N frequency differences between the states. These calculations resulted in an average kex of ~29,000 s−1 for the monomer-dimer exchange (8,000 to 90,000 s−1 for individual residues). The resulting fast monomer-dimer exchange with kex of ~29,000 s−1 would result in nearly flat 15N CPMG relaxation dispersion profiles in the νCPMG range of 50-1000 Hz (to within 0.6 s−1 for a maximum 2.5 ppm 15N monomer-dimer chemical shift difference obtained from NMR dilution data fits). Therefore, the monomer-dimer equilibrium is not expected to interfere with the analysis of conformational exchange near the Zn 2 binding site occurring in the intermediate regime with kex of 574 s−1 and the minor state population of 2.3%, and cannot account for the observed pronounced 15N CPMG dispersion profiles shown in Figure 3.


#### Chemical shift assignment of 15N-HSQC spectra
  NMR chemical shift assignment of the PML RING and B-box1 domains have been previously reported (BMRB accession numbers 25376 and 25288, respectively) 27,28. The backbone assignment of C4C4 mutant was based on the WT assignment and confirmed with HNCA 67 and 15N-NOESY 68 experiments. The NMR sample contained 0.5 mM of 15N,13C-labeled C4C4 in 20 mM MES, 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM BME, 10 mM ZnCl2 and 10% D2O. 15N-HSQC, 15N-NOESY and HNCA spectra were collected at 5°C on an Agilent 800 MHz spectrometer and analyzed using NMRPipe and SPARKY. Residues 141-146, 156, and 164-165 could not be confidently assigned in the spectra of the non-reduced protein.


#### NMR spin-relaxation and relaxation dispersion experiments

##### NMR samples:
  15N-labeled samples of WT B-box1 and its C4C4 mutant contained 20 mM MES, 100 mM NaCl at pH 7.0 and 10% D2O. The B-box1 concentration was 0.75mM for all relaxation experiments. A 0.5 mM C4C4 sample was used for the collection of 15N-CPMG spectra, and 1.00 mM sample was used for the collection of 15N-T1, 15N-T2, and 15N{1H}-NOE spectra. The NMR sample of RING contained 1.3mM 15N-labeled domain in 25 mM Tris, 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM BME at pH 7.0 and 10% D2O.


##### Micro- to millisecond conformational exchange:
  15N-CPMG experiments 69,70 were performed at 500 MHz Agilent VNMRS spectrometer at 25 °C using a constant relaxation period of Trelax = 40 ms and with CPMG frequency (νCPMG) ranging from 25 to 1000 Hz. Per-residue peak intensities were measured for each vCPMG and converted to effective R2 relaxation rates (R2,eff) using the equation R2,eff=-(1∕Trelax)×ln[I1(νCPMG)∕I0], where I1(νCPMG) is the peak intensity at the νCPMG of interest and I0 is the peak intensity at vCPMG = 0. Uncertainties in R2,eff were estimated from repeat experiments at νCPMG=50 Hz. Minimal errors of 4% were used. The populations of the major and minor conformations pA and pB, the exchange rate constant kex, and per-residue chemical shift difference (Δω) values were calculated using the in-house program cpmg_fit 58,71 assuming a two-state exchange model 72,73.


##### Pico- to nanosecond conformational dynamics:
  The backbone 15N R1, R2 and 15N{1H}-NOE relaxation measurements for the isolated PML domains including RING, B-box1, and its C4C4 mutant were performed at 500 MHz Agilent VNMRS spectrometer at 25 °C using the pulse-sequences of Farrow et al. 32,33. The delay between 180° refocusing pulses in 15N R2 experiment was set to 1 ms, corresponding to a CPMG frequency of 500 Hz. 15N R1 and R2 relaxation rates and uncertainties were calculated from exponential fits of peak intensities in a series of 8 2D 1H-15N correlation spectra recorded at different relaxation delays (0.01, 0.08, 0.16, 0.24, 0.32, 0.4, 0.48 and 0.65 seconds for the 15N R1 experiments; and 0.016, 0.032, 0.047, 0.063, 0.079, 0.094, 0.126, 0.157 seconds for the 15N R2 experiments). 15N{1H}-NOE values were calculated as a ratio of peak intensities in two 2D 1H-15N correlation spectra recorded with or without 1H saturation using 2.9 s and 7.9 s delay between scans, respectively, to ensure complete restoration of longitudinal 15N magnetization. Errors in 15N{1H}-NOEs were calculated from signal-to-noise ratios in the corresponding 2D 1H-15N spectra. Minimal errors of 5% for R1 and R2 and 0.05 for 15N{1H}-NOE were used to account for systematic uncertainties in the NMR data.

  Overall rotation correlation time τR and the parameters of rotation diffusion tensor were calculated from 15N R1 and 15N R2 data using the software DASHA 74. Specifically, 15N R1 and R2 rates were globally fit to the models assuming either isotropic, axially symmetric, or fully anisotropic rotational diffusion tensor 75, and the best model was selected according to the F-test at 10% significance level. Excluded from this analysis were (i) residues that exhibited relaxation dispersions in CPMG experiments, (ii) residues with overlapped peaks in 1H-15N correlation spectra, and (iii) residues with 15N{1H}-NOE < 0.6. For all PML domains, including WT B-box1 domain, C4C4 B-box1 mutant and RING domain anisotropic models of rotational diffusion were selected resulting in τR = 8.12 ± 0.08 ns, 5.83 ± 0.05 ns and 5.13 ± 0.08 ns, respectively.

  Pico-nanosecond conformational dynamics of PML domains were assessed from the analysis of 15N relaxation data using the Lipari-Szabo model-free approach 76 in software DASHA 15N R1, R2, and 15N{1H}-NOE data were fit on per-residue basis (with fixed parameters of overall rotation) using three models of the spectral density function with adjustable {S2}, {S2,τe}, and {S2,Rex}, where S2 is generalized order parameter that describes the degree of restriction of angular motion of the N-H vector, τe is the correlation time for fs-ps timescale motions, and Rex is contribution to R2 due to conformational exchange. Models selection was performed based on the values of χ2 penalty function, describing goodness of relaxation data fit, according to the protocol of Mandel et al. 34. Significance levels of 5% and 20%, respectively, were assumed for χ2 and F-test. In addition, for a subset of residues with low 15N{1H}-NOE values, we fit the data using the extended model-free spectral density function 77 with {Sf2,Ss2,τs} adjustable (the number of adjustable parameters is equal to the number of experimental data per residue), where Sf2 and Ss2 are order parameters of fast (fs-ps) and slow (sub-ns) motions, τs is the correlation times for slow (sub-ns) motions.

  Unlike RING and C4C4 mutant, the WT B-box1 exists in a monomer/dimer equilibrium, which may affect accurate determination of its overall rotation diffusion tensors and rotation correlation time using “mode-free” approach. Nonetheless, the qualitative conclusion that B-box1 is a rigid domain can still be made supported by the 15N{1H}-NOE data.


#### PML-NB assays

##### Cell growth:
  PML−/− U2OS cells78 were grown in McCoy’s 5A modified medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and seeded onto a 96-well glass bottom plate at a density of approximately 104 cells per well. On the next day, cells were washed 1 time with PBS, and the media was replaced with Opti-MEM reduced serum media. Cells were transfected with plasmids containing pUC119 carrier DNA and YFP-tagged either WT or the C4C4 mutant of PMLI. Transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 2000 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Transfections were carried out for 24 hours upon which the transfection mixture was removed and the cells were washed 3 times with PBS. The expression level of the WT and mutant PML was assessed using Western Blot analysis of the cell lysates. Custom-made anti-GFP antibodies were used to detect PML. Anti-actin antibodies (AC-15, Sigma) were used to detect actin, which was used as a loading control.


##### Live cell imaging:
  Opti-MEM reduced serum media containing the manufacturer’s recommended amount of NucBlue™ Live Cell Stain was added to cells 15 minutes prior to imaging. Microscopy was performed using BioTek Cytation™ 5 Cell Imaging and Multimode Reader. Sample images were obtained using a 60x Olympus Plan Fluorite objective, DAPI filter cube (Excitation 377/50 Emission 477/60, mirror 409), and YFP filter cube (Excitation 500/24 Emission 542/27, mirror 520). Cell images were automatically background subtracted and deconvolved using Gen5™ analysis software.


##### Live cell data analysis:
  Analysis of live cell images was performed using the image processing software Fiji 79. Quantification of the number and size of PML-NBs was obtained by adjusting the intensity threshold in the YFP channel of cell images to select NBs as regions of interest. Regions of interest were then inspected manually to allow for the exclusion of overlapping NBs and cells overexpressing PML from subsequent statistical analyses. The size of PML-NBs was determined using the area of corresponding ROIs. NB size comparisons were based on area measurements from 623 PML-NBs in PML−/− U2OS cells expressing WT and C4C4 mutant PMLI. The number of PML-NBs was counted in 48 cells for both WT and mutant PMLI. Processing and graphing cell/NB data were performed using MATLAB R2021A software. Unpaired t-tests were used where applicable and a p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.
