# A Multi-tiered Hierarchical Bayesian Approach to Derive Toxic Equivalency Factors for Dioxin-Like Compounds


## Abstract
  In 2005, the World Health Organization (WHO) re-evaluated Toxic Equivalency factors (TEFs) developed for dioxin-like compounds believed to act through the Ah receptor based on an updated database of relative estimated potency (REP)(REP2004 database). This re-evalution identified a need to develop a consistent approach for dose-response modeling. Further, the WHO Panel discussed the significant heterogeneity of experimental datasets and dataset quality underlying the REPs in the database. There is a critical need to develop a quantitative, and quality weighted approach to characterize the TEF for each congener. To address this, a multi-tiered approach that combines Bayesian dose-response fitting and meta-regression with a machine learning model to predict REPS’ quality categorizations was developed to predict the most likely relationship between each congener and its reference and derive model-predicted TEF uncertainty distributions. As a proof of concept, this ‘Best-Estimate TEF workflow’ was applied to the REP2004 database to derive TEF point-estimates and characterizations of uncertainty for all congeners. Model-TEFs were similar to the WHO (2006) TEFs, with the data-poor congeners having larger levels of uncertainty. This transparent and reproducible computational workflow incorporates WHO expert panel recommendations and represents a substantial improvement in the TEF methodology.


## Introduction
  Chemicals are often categorized into groups based on similar chemical structure, physicochemical properties, the biological effects induced, or the similarity of their mechanisms of action. Such grouping allows for extrapolation from data-rich substances to data-poor ones and facilitates regulatory actions. A prototypical chemical group is the dioxin-like compounds (DLCs) based on their planar structure, hydrophobicity, persistence, and common mode of action, i.e., activation of the aryl-hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) (Safe, 1990; Safe, 1994). Due to the similar mechanism of action across the DLCs, the Toxic Equivalency Factor (TEF) approach was developed by the World Health Organization (WHO) to characterize the toxicity of each DLC in relation to a reference congener, primarily TCDD (EPA, 2010; Haws et al., 2006; Van den Berg et al., 1998; Van den Berg et al., 2006).

  These TEFs (last reviewed by a WHO Expert Panel in 2005) represent point estimate values for DLCs based on a database of 634 relative potency (REP) estimates (Haws et al., 2006). A REP value for a congener is calculated by performing a dose-response or concentration-response (D/C-R) study of the congener and of a reference compound, usually TCDD, for some biological endpoint of interest. One estimates a relative potency by comparing the doses or concentrations (D/Cs) of the congener and reference compound at which a specified response level is achieved for a given endpoint. For example, a REP can be defined as REP = (reference ED50)/(congener ED50), where ED50 = dose at which 50% of maximum response occurs. When the congener is less potent than the reference compound, (congener ED50) > (reference ED50), the resulting REP is < 1. For example, a REP of 0.1 indicates that the congener is 10 times less potent; a REP of 0.01 indicates the congener is 100 times less potent; etc.

  Relative potency has been measured using many different in vivo and in vitro endpoints, reflecting a wide variety of biological effects or bioactivity: everything from CYP1A1 mRNA levels in vitro to tumor incidence or body weight changes in vivo and more, making published REP studies heterogeneous. Additionally, heterogeneity is seen because study models vary widely: each study uses various species in vivo and various biological systems in vitro (different cell types, cell lines, or cell-free assays). Study protocols also vary widely: dosing protocols, dose/concentration ranges, and study durations vary depending on the endpoint, study model, and congener(s) being studied.

  In addition to study variability, heterogeneity is introduced by REPs being derived in various ways. Often, the response level at which reference and congener doses/concentrations are compared varies. It may be specified as a percentage of the reference compound’s maximal response, or as a percentage of each compound’s own maximal response (where congener and reference compounds may not have the same maximal response). Ideally, datasets demonstrate that the dose-response curves of the reference and the congener are parallel, a concept referred to as parallelism. If the curves are parallel, then the REP will be the same regardless of the response level where it is calculated. Parallelism means that the D/C-R curve of the congener should be the same as that of the reference, only shifted horizontally. For parallelism to occur, the congener should have the same maximal response and the same slope as the reference; only the ED50/EC50 should differ. However, in practice, D/C-R curves are not always parallel — and studies reporting REPs do not always report an assessment of parallelism. Some REP studies report the underlying D/C-R data, allowing post-hoc assessment of parallelism; others report only the final author-derived REP, making assessment of parallelism effectively impossible.

  As a result of this heterogeneity in REP studies and REP derivation methods, REPs measured for a single congener can vary widely across studies, up to several orders of magnitude. Moreover, the amount of REP data available varies by congener. Some congeners have been widely studied, with dozens or even hundreds of measured REP values. Other congeners may have only a few measured REP values.

  To assign a TEF for each dioxin-like congener, the WHO expert panel examined the distribution of REP values for each congener (Van den Berg et al., 2006). The panel acknowledged that the database of REP studies was heterogeneous with respect to reliability, relevance, and data availability for each congener (Haws et al., 2006; Van den Berg et al., 2006), and that more-reliable, more-relevant studies should be weighted more heavily in selecting a TEF. For this reason, the panel did not estimate TEFs as a fixed percentile of the measured REP values for each congener (e.g., median, 75th percentile, etc.). Rather, TEFs were selected using expert judgment applied to the distribution of measured REP values for each congener, qualitatively considering the reliability and relevance of the studies available. The WHO expert panel recognized that quantitative weighting could be applied to REP distributions in the future, and TEFs could then be derived as some fixed percentile. At that time, the panel recognized that developing such consensus REP weighting factors would require additional expertise (Haws et al., 2006).

  The WHO expert panel noted the limitations of the existing TEF derivation scheme and that the subjective, qualitative, and expert-driven approach needs to be replaced with a more objective and quantitative one. Any alternative method must account for the highly heterogeneous REP data and derivation methods that may be available for each congener. As such, the WHO Expert panel had established two recommendations: 1) REPs and their respective datasets can and will vary in quality (reliability and relevance), and REPs measured by higher-quality studies should be more heavily weighted in choosing the TEF; and 2) REPs derived using different methods, from underlying dose-response curves with varying or unknown amounts of parallelism, should be made comparable by recalculating them according to some common methodology.

  The first of these recommendations is addressed in a recent publication (Wikoff et al., Submitted 2023), with a quantitative approach in two phases. In the first phase, a machine-learning (ML) model is trained to reproduce the expert judgment of the Expert Panel in rating categorical study quality from study attributes, and to estimate the uncertainty in the Expert Panel’s study quality category ratings. The second phase of the approach incorporates a meta-analysis. Each REP for a given congener is treated as an estimate of an unknown TEF for that congener, with some unknown amount of error. The error is related to study quality category: lower-quality REPs are expected to have larger errors in estimating the TEF, and higher-quality REPs to have smaller errors. The expected amounts of error for each study quality category constitute weighting factors reflecting study reliability and relevance, such that higher-quality REPs are more influential in estimating the TEF. Given a set of measured REP values for a congener, and the ML-modeled probabilities of study quality category for each REP, Bayesian inference is used to probabilistically estimate (a) the most-likely TEF for the congener and its uncertainty, and (b) the most-likely expected amounts of error for each study quality category and their uncertainties.

  The second recommendation of the Expert Panel remains to be addressed: how does one integrate REPs derived using heterogeneous derivation methods in the potential absence of parallelism? The objective of this work is to address both recommendations of the 2005 WHO Expert Panel simultaneously, and we present a framework for this approach. The framework provides quantitative evidence integration of highly heterogeneous relative potency data that not only allows weighting of REP data based on study reliability and relevance, but also allows integration of REPs derived using different methods from D/C-R curves with varying or unknown amounts of parallelism. We have developed and implemented a multi-phase Bayesian workflow that adapts and extends the earlier work in Wikoff et al. (Submitted 2023) to accomplish this. For a given congener, this workflow synthesizes heterogeneous REP data and study quality information in a weighted Bayesian meta-analysis to estimate the most-likely full D/C-R relationship underlying the TEF, based on the weight of evidence.

  This workflow addresses the issue of parallelism and varying REP derivation methods by making direct use of underlying D/C-R data whenever available, combined with reported author-derived REPs when D/C-R data are not available. To quantitatively combine data across studies with highly heterogeneous biological endpoints, study models, and study protocols, the workflow models standardized D/C-R curves (sDR curves), in which D/C and response variables in each study are normalized to the reference compound. D/C-R data are direct measurements of sDR curves; author-derived REPs are indirect measurements of sDR curves. The workflow applies the previously developed REP quantitative weighting scheme (Wikoff et al., Submitted 2023) to weight the individual REPs according to their reliability and relevance, combining sDR curves in a weighted Bayesian meta-analysis. This Bayesian approach ultimately estimates the most-likely “average” sDR curve for each congener and for TCDD and quantifies uncertainty in the “average” sDR curves. From these congener-specific sDR curves, parallelism can be assessed directly, and a corresponding TEF (along with TEF uncertainty) can be derived. The Bayesian approach quantitatively estimates TEF uncertainty caused not by data heterogeneity, but also by database insufficiency.

  This method represents a vast improvement over the currently used qualitative approach, which derives a point-estimate TEF and does not quantify uncertainty. The ultimate result of this method development is an objective, automated, and quantitative workflow that can derive TEFs from all available evidence without requiring expert judgement. Moreover, this method is a flexible framework that can be adapted for quantitative evidence integration of dose/concentration-response data in other contexts. We have applied it to the database of REP studies considered by the 2005 WHO Expert Panel (hereafter referred to as the “REP2004 database” or “REP2004”) to evaluate this workflow. We compare and contrast the TEFs derived via the new quantitative workflow to the TEFs derived via qualitative expert judgement by the 2005 Expert Panel.


## Workflow Development Considerations

### The REP2004 Database
  The creation of the REP database in 1997 and its subsequent updating to the version used in this study, the REP2004 database, have been discussed in previous publications (Haws et al., 2006; Van den Berg et al., 2006). The REP2004 database is a highly heterogeneous dataset containing both author-derived REPs and dose/concentration-response (DR) data reflecting a wide variety of different endpoints and study designs. Following curation, the elimination of QSAR studies, and the exclusion of some REPs with endpoints not clearly linked to Ah receptor activation, there were a total of 604 REP datasets in REP2004 available for analysis.


#### Considerations to Address Heterogeneity in REP Study Quality
  After selecting TEFs based on expert judgment, the 2005 WHO Expert Panel suggested that in the future, TEFs could be derived quantitatively from distributions of REP values weighted to reflect reliability and relevance (“study quality”). However, the Expert Panel recognized that establishing consensus values for these quality-related weighting factors would require additional expertise. As a first step in this process, the Expert Panel applied judgment to identify study attributes related to quality (Haws et al. 2006), and rated study quality in the REP2004 database on a scale of 1 to 5.5 by half-steps, where 1 is best and 5.5 is worst (Supplemental Figure S1) (Wikoff et al., Submitted 2023). However, in 2005, it was not clear how to translate these categorical study quality ratings into quantitative quality weighting factors: Should a category-1 study be weighted twice as heavily as category 2? Ten times as heavily? 10% more heavily? To remove the impact of this subjective judgement, the current workflow adapts and extends the approach of Wikoff et al. (Submitted 2023), which was briefly described in the Introduction, as described in Section 3.


#### Considerations of Heterogeneity in REP Derivation Methods and Alternate Approaches Considered
  REPs reported by study authors are derived in different ways, some of which are illustrated in Figure 1. They are typically derived as the ratio of the doses/concentrations (D/Cs) of a congener and reference compound needed to achieve a specified response level. This response level may be specified as a percentage of the reference compound’s maximal response, or as a percentage of each compound’s maximal response (where congener and reference compounds may not have the same maximal response). To distinguish between these two situations, we use the prefix “BMD” to refer to the case when the response level is a percentage of reference maximum: for example, “REP BMD50” refers to REP calculated as the ratio of congener and reference D/Cs at 50% of reference maximum response. (Here, “BMD” is adopted from the term “benchmark dose” (EPA, 2012).) We use the prefix “ED” to refer to the case when the response level is a percentage of each compound’s own maximum response: for example, “REP ED50” refers to REP calculated as the ratio of the congener D/C at 50% of congener maximum response, and the reference D/C at 50% of reference maximum response. Unless there is parallelism between the reference and congener DR curves, different REP derivation methods can yield different REPs from the same underlying data. The impact of non-parallelism and different maximums is illustrated in panels B and D of Figure 1, where REP differs at the different response levels. Different REP derivation methods add an additional layer of heterogeneity to the REP2004 database. This is of particular concern when considering multiple REPs for a given congener as a distribution, as bias may be introduced if some REP derivation methods result in systematically lower or higher REPs than others. The 2005 Expert Panel considered differences in REP derivation method as part of the study quality category rating for each REP in the REP2004 database, but there was no clear method for quantitatively assessing potential bias introduced by heterogeneity in REP derivation methods.

  To address this concern, it was originally hypothesized that all REPs in the database could be recalculated using a consistent derivation method by performing DR modeling on the DR data originally used to calculate each REP, then re-deriving all REPs using a consistent method (e.g., ratio of BMD50s). This approach proved not to be a feasible solution. When we revisited the original publications from which the REP2004 database was curated, the majority did not include reported DR data. We considered analyzing only REPs with reported DR data (DR REPs), but this solution was also not feasible as some congeners had no DR REPs at all. There was also a concern about introducing potential bias by dropping measured REPs solely because they lacked DR data.

  Next, we considered whether it was possible to estimate a simple conversion factor between REP derivation methods for non-parallel curves: for example, is there a way to multiply each REP BMD20 by a conversion factor to estimate what it would have been if derived as REP BMD50? However, in the absence of parallelism, there is no conversion factor between REP types. Converting one type of REP to another requires knowledge of the shape and position of the congener DR curve relative to the reference DR curve, as illustrated in Figure 2. For DR REPs, this knowledge can be gained from DR modeling. However, for REPs where DR data were not reported (author-derived REPs), there is no way to convert a REP derived by one method to a REP derived by another method, because the relative shapes and positions of the underlying DR curves are unknown.

  While considering methods to convert all REPs to a consistent derivation method, another challenge was identified: if the congener maximum response is less than the reference maximum response, then the REP is not quantifiable (NQ) when derived as a ratio of D/Cs at a response level greater than the congener maximum response? An hypothetical example is shown in Figure 3. Although the example in Figure 3 is hypothetical, the situation is not: we found multiple publications that tested relative potency for a congener and could not report a REP because the congener’s maximum response was less than the desired fraction of the reference’s maximum response, at which the REP was to be calculated. These NQ REPs were not considered in the Expert Panel selection of WHO (2005) TEFs or in our recently published Bayesian meta-analysis of the REP2004 database (Wikoff et al. 2023, submitted). With no reported REP value, there was no way to incorporate such data.

  As such data have historically been omitted, there is potential for bias in ignoring these NQ REPs. Bias occurs as they are not missing at random. NQ REPs occur in studies where a congener was unable to achieve a specified fraction of the reference maximal response, which has implications for relative potency. However, it is unclear how to incorporate NQ REPs in any TEF analysis. Simply substituting these NQ REPs with zero may bias TEF estimates downward; the congener does produce some response and therefore has some potency. Yet, simply substituting these NQ REPs with a REP computed at some lower response level may bias TEF estimates upward. Standard statistical methods of estimation for use with censored data (ITRC, 2013) are not applicable because the NQ REPs are not censored per se. Rather, the REP is mathematically undefined at the specified response level.


### Conceptual Framework to Address REP2004 Heterogeneity Considerations
  To address all of these challenges, we developed a conceptual framework that allows for differences in slopes and maximum response between congeners and reference chemicals.

  Relative potency is derived from a pair of dose/concentration-response (DR) relationships: one for the reference compound and one for the congener. These DR relationships are modeled using a Hill model (a four-parameter logistic curve). This model can be parameterized to describe a wide variety of DR shapes, including responses that both increase and decrease with D/C. Further, the Hill model can also describe both continuous and dichotomous responses: for dichotomous responses, the Hill model can be used to describe the underlying probability of occurrence where as for continuous data it describes the average response outcome at a dose/concentration.

  Using these DR curves, we can write equations for REPs derived under the varying conditions described above: the ratio of D/Cs at a response level equal to a specified fraction F of either the reference maximal response (REP BMDF), or each compound’s own maximal response (REP EDF); see Equation 3.

  Equation 3 indicates that in order to compute a REP, or to convert REPs from one derivation method to another, it is not necessary to know all of the parameters of both the reference and congener DR curves, but only certain combinations of those parameters. The reference and congener EX50s only appear in the difference log10EX50r-log10EX50c, so it is only necessary to know that difference. The reference and congener Tops (Tr and Tc) and the bottom B appear only in the ratio Tc-B/Tr-B, so it is only necessary to know that ratio. In other words, it is only necessary to know the congener EX50 relative to reference EX50, and it is only necessary to know the congener response range relative to the reference response range. However, it is still necessary to know both the reference and congener Hillslopes.

  To reduce the number of parameters that need to be estimated, the Hill-model DR curves can be re-expressed in terms of these combinations of parameters. In effect, the congener and reference DR curves are standardized relative to the reference compound DR curve for that REP (see Figure 4 for an example). The standardized response is scaled to be a fraction or multiple of reference maximal response. The standardized dose/concentration is scaled to be a fraction or multiple of the reference EX50.

This means that there is still no method to convert all author-derived REPs to our approach to perform a meta-analysis to estimate a congener-specific TEF.

  However, there are typically multiple REPs per congener, and typically, some of those REPs do have DR data. This suggests an extension of the meta-analysis approach previously published in Wikoff et al. 2023: Rather than performing meta-analysis to estimate a congener-specific TEF from multiple REP values, perform meta-analysis to estimate a congener-specific standardized DR (sDR) curve from multiple REP-specific sDR curves.

  Each REP provides information about the sDR curve from which it was calculated. DR REPs provide the most information, since the sDR curve can be estimated directly by fitting a Hill model to the available DR data. Author-derived REPs with no DR data still provide indirect measurements of their sDR curves: they measure the combinations of parameters given in Equation 15, even if the parameters themselves are not identifiable separately. Even NQ author-derived REPs provide indirect information about their sDR curves: if a REP is NQ at a specified fraction F of reference maximum response, it means that Tc,std≤F for that sDR curve (see Equation 15, REP BMDF case).

  The information from all REPs for a congener — direct and indirect measurements of REP-specific sDR curves — can be combined in a Bayesian meta-analysis, to infer a set of congener-specific sDR parameters that is reasonably compatible with all the available REP data for that congener taken together. This inferred set of parameters essentially defines the “average” sDR curve for that congener. With a Bayesian meta-analysis, it is also possible to estimate the uncertainty around this congener-specific set of sDR parameters. The congener-specific set of sDR parameters, and the uncertainty in them, can be used to estimate a TEF for the congener, calculated by any derivation method desired, and estimate the uncertainty in that TEF.

### Synthesis of Data using Bayesian Meta-Analysis
  The next phase of the workflow consists of a Bayesian model that estimates the most-likely congener-specific sDR curve for each compound using hierarchical meta-analytic methods. This approach integrates author-derived REPs and DR models fitted to available DR REP data.

  We apply a hierarchical model to the REP data, illustrated in Figure 5. The most general level is the congener-specific level (top row in Figure 5). Within a congener, there are multiple REP datasets (REP-specific level, middle row in Figure 5). Within a REP dataset, there may be multiple samples/measurements (sample-specific/measurement-specific level, bottom row in Figure 5).

  At the congener-specific level, the model assumes there is a single, unknown congener-specific sDR curve for each compound, fully defined by a set of congener-specific standardized Hill model parameters for that compound. Our goal is to estimate the congener-specific parameter values for each compound. (For computational convenience, Top and Hillslope are natural log (ln)-transformed.)

  For compound j other than TCDD, the congener-specific standardized parameters are

  Congener-specific sDR curves are assumed to be standardized with respect to TCDD. (Some REPs used PCB126 as the reference compound; we adjust for these cases at the REP-specific level of the model hierarchy.) As the reference compound, TCDD’s standardized log10EX50 is always 0, and TCDD’s standardized Top is always 1, by definition (see Equation 8 and Equation 9). Therefore, the only congener-specific standardized parameter to be estimated for TCDD isμH,TCDD, the ln-transformed congener-specific Hillslope for TCDD

  At the REP-specific level, the model assumes that each REP study for congener j has its own individual REP-specific sDR curve, also unknown. The model assumes that all the REP-specific sDR curves for a given congener are related. For congener j, the REP-specific sDR curves are distributed around the congener-specific sDR curve. This is equivalent to each REP study having its own REP-specific set of standardized Hill model parameters, each of which is distributed around the corresponding congener-specific standardized parameter.

  If a REP used PCB126 as its reference compound, then its REP-specific standardized model parameters were standardized to PCB126 rather than to TCDD. In that case, its REP-specific standardized model parameters are assumed to be distributed around versions of the congener-specific model parameters that have been re-standardized to PCB126 rather than TCDD. These re-standardized congener-specific parameters are easily computed using the congener-specific standardized model parameters for PCB126. (The congener-specific parameters are re-standardized to PCB126, rather than re-standardizing the REP-specific parameters to TCDD, because re-standardizing the REP-specific parameters to TCDD would require knowing the REP-specific non-standardized parameters for TCDD. For REPs where PCB126 was used as the reference compound, there is usually no data for TCDD from which to estimate the REP-specific non-standardized parameters. However, re-standardizing the congener-specific parameters to PCB126 only requires knowing the congener-specific standardized parameters for PCB126, which can be estimated from all available data for PCB126.)

  Whether standardized to TCDD or PCB126, the REP-specific standardized parameters are assumed to be distributed around the congener-specific standardized parameters with a variance that depends on study quality category (lower-quality studies are distributed with larger variance). These variances quantify study-to-study variability in the standardized dose-response curves. They also constitute quantitative weighting factors for study quality. Because sDR curves from lower-quality studies are expected to be scattered more widely around the congener-specific sDR curve, they provide weaker information about the location of the congener-specific sDR curve — i.e., they are weighted less heavily. Because sDR curves from higher-quality studies are expected to be clustered more tightly around the congener-specific sDR curve, they provide stronger information about the location of the “true” standardized DR curve — i.e., they are weighted more heavily.

  At the sample-specific/measurement-specific level (third row in Figure 5), we have the observed data: individual measurements of the REP-specific sDR curves. Each individual measurement estimates the REP-specific sDR curve with an unknown, REP-specific amount of error. The measurements take different forms depending on whether the REP is a DR REP (leftmost box in second row of Figure 5) or an author-derived REP (second box in second row of Figure 5). If it is a DR REP (leftmost box in the second row of Figure 5), then the previous step in the workflow (Bayesian dose-response modeling) produced N = 12,000 MCMC samples from the posterior distribution of the fitted sDR curve parameters (leftmost group in the third row of Figure 5). For each parameter, each of these samples represents one estimate of the REP-specific sDR parameter, with an unknown (but REP-specific) amount of error. If the REP is author-derived (second box in the second row of Figure 5), then the reported author-derived REP constitutes one individual measurement of a combination of the REP-specific sDR parameters, again with an unknown (but REP-specific) amount of error. Specifically, it is a measurement of the combination of parameters in Equation 15 that calculates the REP according to the reported REP derivation method. If the author-derived REP was NQ by the reported derivation method, then the NQ REP constitutes one individual censored measurement of the REP-specific standardized Top parameter, again with an unknown (but REP-specific) amount of error. Specifically, an NQ author-derived REP means that the REP-specific standardized Top parameter was less than the specified fraction of reference maximal response. Note that each author-derived REP has only one sample; each DR REP has N = 12,000 samples. In-depth technical information pertaining to the Bayesian Meta-Analysis is provided in the supplemental materials (Supp Technical Info B)

  This Bayesian meta-analysis model was implemented using the open-source software package JAGS v4.3.0 (Plummer, 2003), accessed via R packages rjags v4-13 (Plummer, 2022) and jagsUI v1.5.2 (Kellner, 2021) MCMC was performed using 4 parallel Markov chains, with 1,000 warm-up iterations, 5,000 adaptive iterations, and 5,000 sampling iterations, for a total of 20,000 samples from the joint posterior distribution of the compound-specific standardized parameters, μEX50 j,μTj,μHj, and μHTCDD (as well as all the other parameters of the model, including the study-quality-specific variances that constitute the quantitative weighting factors). Convergence diagnostics indicated successful convergence with R-hat = 1.00 (Gelman, 2006).


### Derivation of Model TEF Estimates
  The 20,000 MCMC samples from the posterior distribution of the congener-specific sDR parameters, μEX50 j,μTj,μHj, and μHTCDD, characterize the uncertainty distribution for the congener-specific and TCDD sDR curves. To be clear, these MCMC samples do not correspond to individual REP studies. Rather, they correspond to possible values of the parameters for the congener-specific “average” sDR curve. Using Figure 6 as an illustration, the MCMC samples do not correspond to the thin curves (individual REP studies); rather, they correspond to the shaded area around the thick blue-green congener-specific curve, which encompasses 90% of the possible shapes (i.e., the possible sets of Hill model parameters) for that congene-specific curve.

  Each MCMC-sampled possible congener-specific sDR curve (characterized by μEX50 j,μTj,μHj) can be compared to its corresponding possible TCDD-specific sDR curve (characterized by μHTCDD) to derive the corresponding possible TEF value, specifically by taking the ratio of congener and reference BMD50s. The formula for the TEF is given in Equation 30 for MCMC sample n and congener j. (Equation 30 is based on Equation 15 with F=0.5. Recall that μTj,μHj, and μHTCDD are the congener means for the natural-log-transformed standardized congener Top, congener Hillslope, and TCDD Hillslope, respectively (Equation 22 in Supp Technical Info B); they need to be transformed back to the natural scale to be used in Equation 15, which is why they are exponentiated in Equation 30.) The TEF in Equation 30 is non-quantifiable for any samples where exp⁡μTj,n≤0.5. For these samples, log10TEFj,n is recorded as NQ.

  For each congener, the resulting derived sample of 20,000 TEF values (one for each MCMC-sampled possible congener-specific and TCDD-specific sDR curve) characterizes an uncertainty distribution for the TEF. The best-estimate TEF (BE-TEF) is the mean of these sampled log10 TEF values with NQs removed, converted back to natural scale. The Bayesian 90% credible interval (90% CI) on the BE-TEF is the lower and upper bounds of the 90% highest probability density interval (HDPI) of the sampled TEF values with NQs removed (where the 90% HDPI is the shortest interval containing 90% of the sampled TEF values). (The Bayesian credible interval is analogous to, but distinct from, the standard frequentist confidence interval. The TEF has a 90% probability of falling within the bounds of the 90% credible interval.) The probability that the TEF was non-quantifiable (NQ) – i.e., that the congener maximal response is less than 50% of TCDD maximal response – can be calculated simply as the fraction of TEF samples that are NQ. Importantly, the BE-TEF and TEF 90% CI are calculated only from the fraction of TEF samples that were not NQ — i.e., the TEF samples corresponding to possible congener-specific sDR curves where the standardized maximal response was greater than 0.5. In other words, the BE-TEF is meaningful only under the assumption that the congener achieves more than 50% of TCDD maximal response For some congeners, this assumption has a low probability of being true. Therefore, the probability of NQ TEF is important context for interpreting BE-TEFs and TEF 90% CIs.

  Another important piece of context for interpreting BE-TEFs and TEF 90% CIs is the TEF prior distribution. If the TEF posterior is not substantially different from the TEF prior, then BE-TEF and TEF 90% CI simply reflect prior assumptions, not the REP data. In the modeling framework, the TEF prior is not explicitly formulated, but it is a function of parameters whose prior distributions are defined in the meta-analysis (Equation 21 – 29) (Supp Technical Info B), combined with the TEF definition in Equation 30. The implicit TEF prior was numerically estimated by performing MCMC using the model, but without inputting any REP data — in this way, MCMC will draw samples directly from the parameter prior distributions and the implicit prior can be computed. Four independent Markov chains were used, with an adaptation period of 10,000 iterations, the first 10,000 iterations discarded as burn-in, and then retaining 50,000 samples per chain, for a total of 200,000 samples. Based on the results of this analysis, the TEF prior distribution for any congener is approximately uniform between 1×10−20 – 1×106 (and small, but nonzero, outside those bounds). If BE-TEF were computed from the TEF prior, it would be approximately 1×10−7; TEF 90% CI would be approximately 1×10−19 – 1×104; and there would be approximately an 18% probability that the TEF is NQ (see Supplemental Material).


### Quality Control and Reproducibility
  Quality control (QC) and reproducibility were confirmed for the data, workflow steps, and resulting model-derived TEFs. All data in the REP2004 database, including the newly extracted D/C-R data, were subjected to rigorous internal QC and curation to ensure that the data was extracted and entered correctly into the database. Numerical convergence of the MCMC sampling in the combined workflow was confirmed by running the workflow repeatedly using different initial states for the random number generator and confirming that the results were not sensitive to that initial state. Once numerical convergence was confirmed, reproducibility of results was ensured by fixing the initial state of the random number generator. (See the Discussion for more details on numerical convergence and reproducibility.)


## Results: Application of Workflow to the REP2004 Database
  To demonstrate and evaluate the combined workflow, it was applied to the REP2004 database to derive TEF values for each congener (Figure 8). Across 28 congeners, 604 REP datasets were available, of which about half had underlying DR data available for modeling; the rest were author-derived REPs (see Table 2). For this analysis and future applications to DLC data, datasets in quality category 5.5 were excluded.


### Predictions of Study Quality Category from Machine Learning Model
  For each REP, its study quality category probabilities were estimated using the trained RF model described above. For each REP, the RF model predicted eight quality category probabilities: the probability that it was in quality category 1, quality category 2, quality category 2.5, etc. up to quality category 5. Results are shown in Figure 7.

  Following DR data formatting, filtering, and wrangling, a total of 193 congener DR datasets (which can and do contain REP DR data for multiple congeners) and 96 reference DR datasets were identified. Groups of congener datasets that shared the same reference dataset were identified, yielding 96 groups. For each group, Bayesian dose-response curve fitting was performed. As a result, for each group, 9,000 MCMC samples were drawn from the joint posterior distribution of Hill model parameters: B,Tc,j,Hc,j, Log10EX50c,j,Tr,Log10EX50r,Hr where j indexes non-reference congener within a group, j=1,2,…J. Each sampled set of parameters was standardized according to Equation 8, Equation 9, Equation 13, and Equation 14, yielding standardized parameters Tc,std,jk, Log10EX50c,std,jk,Hc,jk, and Hrk for study k of congener j.

  For each congener, MCMC samples of estimated Hill model parameters were integrated with author-derived REP values in the Bayesian meta-analysis framework described above. The RF-model-predicted probabilities of study quality category for each REP were also integrated into the Bayesian meta-analysis as described above, such that the highest quality data has the most influence on the determination of the most probable sDR curves for each congener. A visualization of the combination of DR data, author-derived REPs, and quality weighting information as part of the meta-analysis is given in Figure 8.

  From the weighted Bayesian meta-analysis, best-estimate sDR curves were derived for each congener, along with 90% CIs on these curves (Figure 9). From these sDR curve distributions, the corresponding best-estimate TEFs and TEF 90% CIs were derived (Table 3).

  Uncertainty is quantified and propagated at every step of the workflow (Figure 6). The degree of uncertainty in the congener-specific sDR curve varies from congener to congener, and manifests mostly as uncertainty in maximal effect and horizontal position of the congener sDR curve relative to the reference. Uncertainty in the shape and location of the congener-specific sDR curve propagates directly to the uncertainty surrounding the TEF (Supplemental Figure S4). Uncertainty in the TEF is quantified by the width of the TEF 90% CI. TEF 90% CI width was 2 orders of magnitude or less for the majority of congeners, with the widest TEF 90% CIs corresponding to congeners with the fewest REPs (<10 REPs) (Supplemental Figure S5).


## Discussion
  In this study, we present a fit-for-purpose multistage workflow that combines dose-response modeling, machine learning, and meta-analysis in a Bayesian framework. We applied this workflow to the REP2004 database, demonstrating its ability to quantitatively integrate all available and usable evidence for each congener to estimate TEF values and quantify uncertainty in them. The Bayesian techniques used in this workflow, the parameterization of the D/C-R model, and the quantification of uncertainty represent reproducible, transparent, quantitative, and scalable “state-of-the-science methods as recommended by the NAS, EHC, and EFSA (EHC, 2020; Hardy et al., 2017; NRC, 2009). This combined workflow is a dramatic leap forward in sophistication compared to the qualitative, expert-judgement driven TEF assignments conducted by the expert panel (Wikoff et al., Submitted 2023). The combined workflow addresses the two key problems identified by the WHO Expert Panel: (1) the need for a quantitative weighting scheme to reflect differences in REP study reliability and relevance, and (2) the variety of methods used to derive the REPs in the database, and the fact that different REP derivation methods produce different results when parallelism is not present. Furthermore, the quantitative evidence integration of the combined workflow allows quantitative derivation of best-estimate TEF values and uncertainty in them, allowing more precise characterizations of hazard and risk for each congener. The workflow presented here is transparent, reproducible, and has been subject to peer review by third party experts, whose guidance, and recommendations to improve model performance and accuracy were implemented.

  The best-estimate TEFs for most congeners are within one order of magnitude of the WHO (2005) TEFs, suggesting that the combined workflow’s quantitative evidence integration is reasonably similar to the Expert Panel’s qualitative evidence integration. However, differences from the WHO (2005) TEFs do not imply that the best-estimate TEFs are wrong. Rather, they simply mean that the 2005 Expert Panel and the combined workflow integrated evidence differently for that congener. There are three major conceptual differences between the Expert Panel decision process and the combined workflow; in all three cases, the combined workflow is more objective, quantitative, transparent, and reproducible. The first major difference is in the consideration of study reliability and relevance. The Expert Panel used qualitative expert judgement to weight studies by their reliability and relevance. By contrast, the combined workflow applies a quantitative weighting scheme for study reliability and relevance, extending the quantitative weighting scheme first described in Wikoff et al. (Submitted 2023) to weighted Bayesian meta-analysis of the full dose/concentration-response relationships underlying the REPs. The second major difference is in the consideration of parallelism. The Expert Panel considered dose-response parallelism only as one component of its qualitative assessment of study reliability and relevance and did not consider non-quantifiable REPs. By contrast, the combined workflow quantitatively assesses dose-response parallelism, making direct use of dose-response data wherever possible, and quantitatively integrates non-quantifiable REPs. The third major difference is in the prior information assumed about TEFs. The Expert Panel anchored its decisions a priori to the WHO (1998) TEFs for many congeners, treating them (in Bayesian terms) as highly-informative priors that required substantial evidence to modify. By contrast, the Bayesian meta-analysis in the combined workflow used the same minimally-informative prior on TEF for all congeners, in which the only a priori assumption was that TEF could be anywhere between 1×10−20 – 1×106. The minimal prior assumptions allow the data from individual REP studies to be the primary driver of the TEF estimates. The Bayesian meta-analysis formulates its prior assumptions explicitly and transparently (required as part of the computational process), allowing them to be reproduced and examined by others. If desired, the prior assumptions could even be changed and the meta-analysis repeated, which would allow analysis of the sensitivity of BE-TEFs to the prior assumptions. Such an analysis could not be easily performed for the qualitative judgment of an expert panel.

  Uncertainty is captured at every stage of the combined workflow. Uncertainty in the study quality categories assigned to each REP is captured by the fact that the machine-learning model predicts the probability that each REP falls into each study quality category. Uncertainty in the dose-response curve fits for individual REP dose-response datasets is captured using Bayesian inference, which directly samples from the full uncertainty distribution of the fitted Hill model parameters for each REP dataset. Uncertainty in the study-quality categories and uncertainty in the fitted Hill model parameters for each REP dataset are then combined with any available author-derived REPs and propagated forward into the meta-analysis that estimates the congener-specific sDR curves. Uncertainty in the congener-specific sDR curves is captured using Bayesian inference and MCMC, which directly samples from the full uncertainty distribution (posterior distribution) of the congener-specific standardized Hill model parameters as informed by all of the available information. Finally, uncertainty in the resulting TEF estimates is captured directly, by calculating the TEF corresponding to each sample from the uncertainty distribution of the congener-specific standardized Hill model parameters, producing the corresponding samples from the uncertainty distribution of the TEFs.

  The ability to quantify uncertainty in a TEF estimation represents an additional major improvement to the previous TEF derivation method, whose subjective nature did not allow for a meaningful characterization of uncertainty. The characterization and incorporation of uncertainty has become increasingly important for risk assessments (EPA, 2014; Maertens et al., 2022; NRC, 2009). Indeed, Probabilistic Risk Assessments (PRAs) have emerged as a method of choice for risk assessments of varying scale; from large scale Superfund sites to the derivation of water quality criteria, and is gaining acceptance for use in the regulatory arena (Barnhart et al., 2022; EPA, 2014; Ruffle et al., 2018) due to the advantages it has over deterministic methods that use single-point estimates (Moe et al., 2022; Ruffle et al., 2018). Quantifying uncertainty in estimated TEFs is in line with the state-of-the-science and provides new and valuable context for the estimated TEF values, while also facilitating their use in future PRAs.

  TEF uncertainty is primarily driven by database insufficiency. Congeners with the largest TEF uncertainties also have the fewest REPs available. Out of the 13 congeners with the largest TEF uncertainties (TEF 90% CI spanning more than 2 orders of magnitude), 12 of these congeners have fewer than 10 REPs available. (The thirteenth is 1,2,3,4,7,8-HxCDF, which had only 12 REP datasets available.) This result highlights the utility of the Bayesian approach: Uncertainty from database insufficiency is quantitatively captured by the Bayesian priors used in the meta-analysis. If a congener had zero REPs available, the model would simply predict the prior assumption: that its TEF is equally likely to be anywhere between 1×10−20 and 1×106. This wide prior is slightly narrowed down by each REP that is available for a congener. If only a few REPs are available, then the prior cannot be narrowed down very much, and the resulting posterior distribution – the uncertainty distribution for the TEF – will remain very wide. If a large number of REPs are available, then the prior can be narrowed down much further, and the resulting posterior distribution – the uncertainty distribution for the TEF – can be much narrower.

  Database variability – i.e., variability of REP values reported for a congener — also contributes to TEF uncertainty. However, variability and uncertainty are captured separately in the Bayesian meta-analysis. Variability is captured by the study-quality-dependent residual errors (Equation 23 in Supp Technical Info B), which reflect the expected amount of study-to-study variability in sDR curves for studies in each quality category. In effect, for each congener, sDR curves from individual studies are assumed to be scattered around a central congener-specific sDR curve. sDR curves from lower-quality studies will be scattered more widely around the congener-specific curve, and sDR curves from higher-quality studies will be clustered more tightly around the congener-specific curve. Variability — the amount of scatter in REP-specific sDR curves — is captured separately from uncertainty — how precisely we can estimate the position of the central congener-specific curve.

  With a large-enough number of studies, even if variability is high, we may still be able to estimate the central curve with high certainty. An example of this is the data-rich congener in Figure 8. This data-rich congener exhibits a large amount of variability in sDR curves for individual studies. However, precisely because there are so many REP studies (and especially because there are many high-quality studies), the central tendency of the sDR curves is clear. For this reason, the congener-specific sDR curve can be estimated with a high degree of certainty, and therefore the TEF can also be estimated with a high degree of certainty.

  However, if there are relatively few studies and variability is high, then we may not be able to estimate the central sDR curve with much certainty. An example of this is the difference between 1,2,3,6,7,8-HxCDD and OCDD. These two congeners have similar numbers of REPs (5 and 6, respectively), However, the REPs for 1,2,3,6,7,8-HxCDD come from lower-quality studies and therefore have higher variability than the REPs for OCDD. Therefore, the five REPs for 1,2,3,6,7,8-HxCDD provide substantially less information about the location of the central “true” standardized DR curve than do the six REPs for OCDD. For this reason, uncertainty in the TEF for 1,2,3,6,7,8-HxCDD (90% CI bounds 0.003–5) is substantially greater than uncertainty in the TEF for OCDD (90% CI bounds 0.0001–0.007).

  TEF uncertainty must be considered in context with the best-estimate TEF value to be effectively and accurately be used in risk assessment applications. Uncertainty was not visible in the TEFs estimated by the 2005 Expert Panel, because it was not quantified. A major strength of this workflow is that it transparently quantifies uncertainty in TEF estimates. This workflow makes TEF uncertainty visible. It is fallacious to conclude that TEFs estimated using this method are more uncertain than the TEFs estimated by the 2005 Expert Panel. Instead, this workflow merely quantifies the uncertainty that was always present.

  However, uncertainty quantification needs to be interpretable in order to be useful in practical decision-making. Bayesian approaches can seem like a purely academic exercise to risk assessors and risk managers when results are presented only as a probability distribution, without clear guidance on where to draw a “bright line” to make a practical decision. Future work should develop clear criteria and guidance regarding the use of TEF uncertainty in risk-related decision-making. For example, a criterion could be established for the maximum width of the TEF credible interval, beyond which the best-estimate TEF would be considered too uncertain to be used. In the absence of a usable best-estimate TEF, guidance should be provided for how to calculate toxicity equivalents.

  Moreover, parallelism or lack thereof should also be integrated into guidance regarding the use of TEF uncertainty in risk-related decision-making. A major benefit of the Best-Estimate TEF workflow is that it quantifies lack of parallelism explicitly, summarized by the probability of NQ TEF; however, guidance should be developed on how to apply this information. For example, for OCDF, the workflow estimated a 94.32% chance of NQ TEF, i.e., a 94.32% chance that OCDF’s maximal response was less than 50% of TCDD’s maximal response. This high probability of lack of parallelism is visualized in Figure 9 (see panel labeled “OCDF”). The BE-TEF 0.002 (90% CI 0.0003–0.02) applies only for the 5.68% chance that OCDF’s maximal response reaches at least 50% of TCDD’s maximal response. Otherwise, the TEF is effectively zero for a response level equal to 50% of TCDD max. This example illustrates a consideration for use of the BE-TEFs: If parallelism fails such that the congener maximal response is less than TCDD maximal response, then TEF will be NQ — effectively zero — for any response level higher than the congener max, even though TEF will be greater than zero for any response level below the congener max. (TEF will be effectively zero for any response level higher than congener max because even an infinitely high dose of the congener would not produce the desired response level.) More generally, in the absence of parallelism, TEF is not constant, but depends on the response level of interest. Consideration of the response level of interest may be useful when applying a toxicity equivalence framework.

  Despite the use of fit-for-purpose Bayesian and Machine Learning methods, no model is perfect, and this workflow is no different. As such, the workflow has some caveats that should be considered in the application and adoption of this method to derive TEF values.

  A minor caveat regards the computational requirements of the workflow. From start to finish, the workflow has a runtime of approximately 6 hours on a 2019 Macbook Pro with a 2.4GHz 8-core Intel i9 processor, 32GB amount of RAM, and a 2TB solid state hard drive. A significant amount of storage (8.5GB) is also needed to contain the numerous intermediate input/output files that must be generated in the correct order of the workflow. However, as computational modeling techniques become more commonplace in risk assessment, and computational resources continually improve (including processor speed and storage capacity), these computational requirements are not expected to present major obstacles to the evaluation, acceptance, and use of this workflow.

  Another caveat regards reproducibility of the random sampling inherent to MCMC algorithms. Because the samples are randomly-generated, every run will produce results that are numerically different within a small error based upon the number of samples. It is necessary to verify that the results are not sensitive to the specific random sample that is drawn for a particular run — in other words, that the model has numerically converged, such that the only differences in the results across multiple runs are negligible numerical differences. When convergence has been verified, the results of a model run can be made reproducible by controlling the the pseudo-random number generator (RNG) seed, which is easily done in R and other computational environments by “setting a seed” for the RNG. It is essential that reproducibility of this inherently probabilistic approach is understood and controlled, to facilitate its evaluation, acceptance, and use by regulatory agencies and others.

  A major caveat should be considered when interpreting the uncertainty in the Best-Estimate TEFs (BE-TEFs), quantified by the Bayesian credible intervals (CI). The BE-TEF CI bounds cannot be used for quantitative risk assessment or risk management decision-making. That is, they cannot be interpreted as “best-case” and “worst-case” TEF values. As with all Bayesian analyses, BE-TEF CI bounds are impacted by prior assumptions; thus, the CI represents mathematical uncertainty and all assumptions therein, which is not the same as toxicological uncertainty. The prior bounds on the standardized Hill model parameters were not selected considering the toxicological plausibility of the resulting bounds on BE-TEF CI. If there is little information on the true TEF, the Bayesian posterior CIs would likely represent the prior assumptions: approximately uniform between 1×10−20 – 1×106 (see Section 3.4, “Derivation of Model TEF Estimates”). If relative potency were truly as low as 1×10−20, the compound would hardly be considered dioxin-like at all, and if relative potency were truly as high as 1×106, the compound would be a million times more potent than TCDD. We stress that, for data-poor congeners, the BE-TEF CIs primarily reflect prior assumptions because very little data is available to refine the uncertainty estimate. The central estimates of BE-TEF for these data-poor congeners are still valid, because they are in the range of the available data and their concordance with 2005 estimates; however, the BE-TEF CI bounds represent prior assumptions that are not necessarily toxicologically plausible. Further refinements of the model should include toxicological assumptions.

  Additionally, the statistical model for the meta-analysis did not account for potential correlations among REPs for different endpoints or different congeners measured by the same laboratory. In other words, potential laboratory effects are not included in the model. Though the laboratory may cause bias within a single study, the bias impact between different studies tends to cancel each other out and does not generally impact the central BE-TEF estimates; however, the correlation’s impact on uncertainty potentially complicates any uncertainty estimate. The intra-laboratory correlation will modify the estimate of BE-TEF uncertainty, which implies such correlation must be considered to quantify uncertainty correctly.

  Uncertainty could be underestimated if REPs measured in the same experiment are positively correlated, i.e., all shift in the same direction. In this case, multiple REPs from the same experiment provide less total information than if the REPs were from different experiments. However, uncertainty could also be overestimated if REPs measured in the same experiment are negatively correlated. Some types of intra-laboratory correlation might have no net effect on BE-TEF uncertainty estimates if non-standardized dose-response curves for the reference compound and all congeners were shifted by the same amount in the same direction; in that case, the standardized dose-response curves would be unchanged.

  For these reasons, the BE-TEF CI bounds should be interpreted only as qualitative estimates of uncertainty. They are useful in the context of scientific inquiry regarding database uncertainty and study quality differences, but they are not appropriate for use in justifying risk management decisions.

  Despite these limitations, the Bayesian meta-analysis framework represents a step forward in evidence integration. Before the Bayesian meta-analysis framework, TEF estimates were qualitative (i.e., based upon expert judgment from the literature). The Bayesian framework allows transparent analysis and interpretation of uncertainty estimates, including identifying limitations of those estimates. Future work may include refinements to the Bayesian hierarchical models to better model toxicological plausibility and potential intra-laboratory correlations.

  Numerous other future directions can be pursued to further increase the accuracy of this model and the use of its output. The first, and perhaps the most obvious, is the application of the workflow to an updated REP database. Since 2004, many new relative-potency studies have been published in the peer-reviewed literature. The REP2004 database has been substantially updated with curation and addition of new REP datasets from the literature. This workflow can be applied to this updated REP database to produce updated TEF estimates that integrate the most recent available REP data. The addition of new data, especially for those congeners that were more data poor in REP2004, may result in substantial improvement in TEF uncertainty. A substantial strength of this workflow is that it can be re-run each time the REP database is expanded to include newly published studies, allowing TEF estimates to be rapidly updated to reflect the current state of the science.

  A second future direction to further develop this workflow and its relevancy to humans is the incorporation and distinction between the primarily oral intake in vivo REPs and “systemic” REPs.

  In contrast to the primarily oral intake REPs, which are derived using administered doses, systemic REPs are derived using internal concentrations, such as serum levels or levels at a target tissue (CITATION. Administered dose is not necessarily directly proportional to internal concentration, because toxicokinetics of DLCs is not necessarily linear. Therefore, oral intake REPs and systemic REPs may not be directly comparable, even after the standardization applied in the Best-Estimate TEF Workflow. To address the translation between systemic and intake REP data and to assess their differences, the combination of a mass balance model such as the one developed by Armitage et al. (2021) and high throughput toxicokinetic models (both of which are available through the httk R package (Pearce et al., 2017) could be utilized to convert oral intake doses to systemic doses for subsequent analysis in the workflow. While systemic REPs are not strictly present in REP2004 (although in vitro human data could be seen as equivalent), they could not only serve to bolster the database for each congener, but they may also suggest different outcomes than those that are produced when capturing only oral intake data. It must be cautioned, however, that considerations will need to be made in the calculation of oral equivalent doses using HTTK for DLCs due to their ability to bioaccumulate, which complicates the conversion using steady-state models.

  Lastly, although this workflow was developed with the intention to apply it to the REP database to produce TEFs, it is sufficiently flexible to allow for applications beyond TEFs and DLCs. Of particular interest is the possibility of the workflows application to the risk characterization and assessment of Per- and Polyfluoro (PFAS) substances. Like DLCs, PFAS, PFOA, and other related compounds are structurally similar and work along the same pathways with overall similar mechanisms of action. In recent years, an effort has been made to assess and establish relative potency between PFAS and PFAS-like compounds, including mixtures, with a goal of ultimately using this relative potency framework to derive TEFs for PFAS (Bil et al., 2021; Fenton et al., 2021; Fletcher, 2019). Like REPs, the Relative Potency Factor (RPF) for PFAS assumes that chemicals do not interact biologically but do contribute to a common effect (Schmidt, 2022) such that combined exposures result in an additive effect. Another key similarity is that PFAS risk assessments in practice will often involve mixtures of different compounds (Bil et al., 2023a; Bil et al., 2023b; Bil et al., 2021) as opposed to single exposures. These factors combined support the use of a relative potency approach for assessing mixture toxicity with toxic equivalency estimates for PFAS, whose RFP data could be highly amenable to the Best-Estimate TEF workflow, which seeks to evaluate DLCs the same way. Several studies have been published in which internal relative potency factors are derived from human biomonitoring data (Bil et al., 2023a; Bil et al., 2023b; Bil et al., 2021; Bil et al., 2022) and platforms have been established for high throughput in vitro testing of relative potency (Rowan-Carroll et al., 2021), demonstrating that there may be a sufficiently large database available and otherwise demonstrated that a sufficiently large database has the capability to be developed. While it would be a considerable effort to assemble a PFAS database to allow for the derivation of meaningful PFAS TEF values, the Best-Estimate TEF workflow would be highly amenable to an application to a PFAS database.

  The broader framework of the workflow — understood as a hierarchical Bayesian quality-weighted meta-analysis of dose-response data — could be applicable to quantitative integration of dose-response data of other kinds, including toxicokinetic data, such as the Concentration vs. Time Database of toxicokinetic data for hundreds of chemicals (Sayre et al., 2020) or the database of PFAS toxicokinetic data curated by Dawson and colleagues (2023). To apply this framework to toxicokinetic data, the Hill dose-response model would need to be replaced with an appropriate dose-time-concentration toxicokinetic model or models. Because toxicokinetic parameters are typically not defined relative to a reference compound, the standardization of the dose-response model would need to be re-formulated (for example, responses might be standardized to the peak concentration, and time might be standardized to elimination half-life). Moreover, a new machine-learning model for study quality categorization would need to be formulated, because different study attributes would be expected to affect reliability and relevance for toxicokinetic studies vs. REP studies. Nonetheless, the framework could be valuable for quantitative integration of toxicokinetic data and quantitative estimation of uncertainty in toxicokinetic parameters.

  In this work, we have presented the Best-Estimate TEF workflow, combining machine learning, Bayesian dose-response model fitting, and Bayesian meta-analysis to derive best-estimate TEFs for 28 congeners, based on modeling the dose-response relationship from which relative potency estimates are derived, synthesizing the highly heterogeneous relative-potency data available in the REP2004 database. The use of this workflow represents a dramatic leap forward in comparison to the expert judgement-driven derivation of TEFs done in the past. We have demonstrated its success in deriving TEFs using both dose-response REP data and author-derived REPs in the REP2004 database, accounting for differences in REP study quality through a built-for-purpose quantitative weighing scheme. The Best-Estimate TEF workflow’s ability to integrate highly heterogeneous REP data in an objective, quantitative, reproducible, and highly automated fashion allows rapid estimation of data-driven TEF values to ensure that risk assessments are supplied with the most accurate values to ultimately be as health protective as possible.
