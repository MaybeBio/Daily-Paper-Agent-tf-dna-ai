# A Markov State Model of Solvent Features Reveals Water Dynamics in Protein-Peptide Binding


## Abstract
  In this work, we investigate the role of solvent in the binding reaction of p53 transactivation domain (TAD) peptide to its receptor MDM2. Previously, our group generated 831 μs of explicit-solvent aggregate molecular simulation trajectory data for the MDM2-p53 peptide binding reaction using large-scale distributed computing, and subsequently built a Markov State Model (MSM) of the binding reaction (Zhou et al. 2017). Here, we perform tICA analysis and construct an MSM with similar hyperparameters, while using only solvent-based structural features. We find a remarkably similar landscape but accelerated implied timescales for the slowest motions. The solvent shells contributing most to the first tICA eigenvector are those centered on Lys24 and Thr18 of p53 TAD peptide in the range of 3–6 Å. Important solvent shells were visualized to reveal solvation and desolvation transitions along peptide-protein binding trajectories. Our results provide a solvent-centric view of the hydrophobic effect in action for a realistic peptide-protein binding scenario.


## Introduction
  There has been great interest in using molecular dynamics (MD) simulation to probe the mechanisms of protein-peptide association generally, and the MDM2-p53 binding interaction in particular.1,2 MDM2, an E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase, binds the transactivation domain (TAD) of p53 and recruits it for degradation. Whereas p53 TAD is intrinsically disordered in solution, it forms a helix upon binding to MDM2.3–5

  One focus of past molecular simulation work has been to understand the physical mechanisms by which intrinsically disordered peptides are able to recognize and bind to their receptor.2,6–9 For the MDM2-p53 system, experimental and simulation studies suggest an ’induced-fit’, or ’fly-casting’ mechanism, in which binding of p53 to MDM precedes folding to a well-structured helix. A related goal of molecular simulation studies has been to predictively understand the binding mechanism of inhibitors of MDM2 (and its related homolog MDMX) designed to mimic p53 TAD.10–12

  Despite the central importance of water to the structure and dynamics and proteins13 and our mechanistic understanding of ligand binding,14,15 previous simulation studies of MDM2-p53 binding have provided only modest insight into the role water plays in the dynamics of the binding reaction. Spectroscopic studies suggest that protein motions can be strongly coupled to protein hydration layers.16,17 Indeed, the main driving force of protein folding and binding is the hydrophobic effect, which is mediated by water.

  Guo et al. used MD simulations of p53 TAD and MDM2 at several fixed encounter distances, along with a Variational Implicit-Solvent Method (VISM), to examine density fluctations of water near the binding surfaces of MDM2 and p53,18 and found that a dewetting transition occurs at a separation of 4–6 Å. Ricci et al. found a similar dewetting transition that occurs at separations of 5.6–7.6 Å for the hydrophobic MDM2-p53 interface, but no such distinctive dewetting transition for the hydrophilic binding interface of barnase-barstar.19 In each of these studies, the peptide and protein receptor were restrained to have a fixed conformation, to aid in the grid-based analysis of solvent density around a solute. Thus, these studies do not address the dynamics of desolvation for the more plausible binding-then-folding mechanism of an intrinsically disordered p53 associating with MDM2.

  To better address this question, we turn to recent molecular simulations of p53 TAD binding to the MDM2 receptor performed in explicit solvent by Zhou et al.2 In that work, a Markov State Model constructed from ~831 μs of aggregate trajectory data suggested that p53 binding pathways involve a misregistration of Trp23 outside the hydrophobic cleft, later reorganizing to a fully helical conformation with Trp23 inserted into the hydrophobic cleft. The mechanistic picture provided by this work is decidedly a protein-centric one, as only protein conformational features were analyzed in building the model, and the results interpreted in terms of how hydrophobic interactions dictate the slowest motions.

  Here, we instead ask: to what extent it is possible to examine protein binding dynamics using only solvent degrees of freedom? If so, can we gain particular insight into the role of water dynamics in MDM2-p53 protein-peptide association?

  Below, we describe our efforts to construct an MSM of p53-MDM2 binding solely from solvent shell density features, and discuss the interpretation of the results. Remarkably, we find that information about binding pathways are largely preserved, although kinetic timescales are accelerated. These results suggest that cooperative protein motions and solvent motions can provide complementary information to describe the peptide association reaction.


## Methods

### Molecular Dynamics Simulation
  Molecular simulations of ab initio p53 TAD peptide binding to MDM2 were performed on the Folding@home distributed computing platform20–22 using the Gromacs 4.5.423 software package, as described by Zhou et al.2 The AMBER ff99sb-ildn-nmr force field24 was used with the TIP3P explicit solvent model.25 Starting receptor configurations were taken from the p53-bound MDM2 receptor structure3 (PDB:1ycr), with unfolded and folded configurations of p53 TAD (residues 17–29) initiated at a range of receptor-ligand distances (2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 Å). Stochastic (Langevin) integration at 300 K using 2-fs time steps produced an ensemble of 2776 trajectories with an aggregate simulation time of 831 μs. Full details are described in Zhou et al.2


### Markov State Models
  Markov State Models (MSMs) have emerged as a vital tool in biophysical modeling, with applications in protein folding,26–31 ligand binding,2,32–34 and as a framework for adaptive sampling35,36 and inference of conformational ensembles.37 An MSM described conformational dynamics as a network of metastable conformational states and transition rates connecting them.38,39 In practice, an MSM is constructed by first assigning trajectory snapshots to discrete metastable states, and then estimating a transition probability matrix from the observed number of transitions that occur between states i and j within some chosen lag time τ.

  A crucial step in constructing an MSM is the unsupervised identification of relevant metastable states. For large trajectory datasets, this requires some kind of dimensionality reduction method to map samples to a space where distances describe the kinetic separation of molecular configurations, followed by conformational clustering to define a set of discrete states for MSM construction. As done in many other works,10,40–42 Zhou et al. used time-independent component analysis (tICA) to perform dimensionality reduction. tICA is analogous to Principal Component Analysis (PCA), which finds a small number of degrees of freedom that best capture the variance in the data, by diagonalizing the feature covariance matrix. In contrast, tICA finds the degrees of freedom that capture most of the time-lagged covariance, i.e. the slowest, most time-correlated changes in the features.

  Speicifcally, the tICA method works by finding a low-rank set of M components, α(m), m = 1, …, M, where each α(m) is an eigenvector of N linear coefficients for each feature fi, i = 1, …, N, such that ⟨α|C(Δt)|α⟩ is maximized while constrained to have unit variance ⟨α|Σ|α⟩. Here, C(Δt) is a time-lagged covariance matrix of elements Cij=fi(t)-f‾ifj(t+Δt)-f‾jt and Σ is the covariance matrix. The quantity Δt is called the tICA lag time.

  In Zhou et al, tICA was performed using a set of 1953 interatomic distance features as input: all pairwise distances between Cα and Cβ atoms from p53 (residues 17–29) and 19 residues in the binding site of MDM2. In the present work, we also use tICA, but choose a set of input features that describes the structure of hydration around the p53 TAD peptide and MDM2 receptor.


### Solvent Shell Featurization
  To describe the solvent dynamics, we used a featurization technique pioneered by Harrigan et al. that quantifies the number of water molecules observed in a “shell” around each solute atom.43 This method is implemented in the SolventShellFeaturizer tool of the MSMBuilder v3.8.044 software package, which utilizes MDTraj v1.9.2.45

  We designated selected protein and peptide Cα and Cβ atoms as solute atoms, and water oxygens as the solvent atoms. The computed solvent features are thus numbers of water oxygens binned by respective shell occupancies around the solute atoms (Figure 1). We followed the the solvent shell featurization scheme of Harrigan et al.,43 which uses of four solvent shells around each solute atom, with a shell width equal to 3.0 Å each (0–3 Å, 3–6 Å, 6–9 Å, 9–12 Å). There are 63 solute atoms (Cα’s and Cβ’s of selected residues). Each have 4 solvent shells features, for a total of 252 solvent features. Selected residues are the same as those used in Zhou et al.:2 all of the p53 TAD peptide residues (Glu17, Thr18, Phe19, Ser20, Asp21, Leu22, Trp23, Lys24, Leu25, Leu26, Pro27, Glu28, Asn-29), and a subset of MDM2 residues in the binding cleft (Glu25, Thr26, Met50, Lys51, Leu54, Leu57, Gly58, Ile61, Met62, Tyr67, Gln71, Gln72, His73, Val75, Phe91, Val93, Lys94, His96, Ile99).


### Constructing MSMs from solvent features
  To construct MSMs from solvent features that can be compared with the results of Zhou et al.,2 we used similar hyperparameters for tICA and MSM construction. As in Zhou et al. , tICA analysis was performed using a tICA lag time of Δt = 5 ns. Solvent features were projected to the ten tICA eigenvectors with the largest eigenvalues, to obtain the tICA components tICm=α(m)∣f, and k-means clustering was performed in this space to define 600 microstates. As in Zhou et al., MSMs were constructed using an MSM lag time of 5 ns. One important aspect of this model is that the solvent trajectory data is sampled at a lower resolution (1 ns snapshots) that the trajectory data used by Zhou et al. to construct MSMs form protein distance features (100 ps snapshots). Chapman-Kolmogorov tests46 for MSMs estimated using lag times of τ = 5 ns and τ = 30 ns are shown in Figure S1.


### MolMap
  Chimera’s molmap function47 allows for visualization of water density, as well as the absence of water density, observed in trajectories through molecular surfaces. We found this approach was an effective way to view the dynamics of solvent in regions that would otherwise by difficult to visualize, such as the binding cleft of MDM2. We selected all water oxygens for each snapshot when computing the density surface. We found the following parameters resulted in a surface that encapsulates volume with the absence of water: The initial contour level to enclose a fraction of the total mass was set to 0.9895 and the grid spacing to 1.3. We eliminated surface noise by Gaussian filtering with a volume contour level set to 0.015.


## Results

### MSMs constructed from solvent features are similar to those constructed from protein distance features
  How do MSMs constructed from solvent features compare ones constructed from protein distance features? A side-by-side comparison of 2D TICA landscapes computed from these two sets of features reveals they are visually similar (Figure 2). This suggests that the two slowest motions reflect similar mechanistic processes.

  The calculated implied timescales of the solvent-feature MSMs (Figure 3), however, are found to be accelerated compared to the protein-feature MSMs of Zhou et al. (2017), which predict slowest relaxation timescales around 1 μs at the MSM lag time (5 ns). Instead, our solvent-feature MSM predicts implied timescales around 200 ns at a lag time of 5 ns. This suggests that solvent features poorly capture the key degrees of freedom that determine the binding rate. This is reasonable, as Zhou et al. found the slowest motions correspond to rearrangement of the peptide into a helical conformation once the peptide is already bound, with Trp23 correctly inserted in the hydrophobic binding cleft; these motions are apparently poorly captured by solvent features alone.

  Consequently, the association rate kon predicted by the solvent-feature MSM is slightly accelerated compared to the protein distance-feature model. Zhou et al. estimated an association rate of kon = 2.5 × 107 M−1 s−1 using Transition Path Theory (TPT) and a structural definition of bound and unbound states.2 Because structural definitions of these states are hard to obtain with solvent features alone, we turn to the fact that for a two-state binding reaction, the slowest implied timescale τ2 is related to the observed binding relaxation rate kobs, as  (1) τ2=1kobs=1konc+koff≈1konc

  where c = 7.1 mM is the simulated peptide concentration, and kobs≈kon since koff≪konc. Using Zhou et al.’s TPT estimate of kon in the above equation, we should expect a slowest relaxation implied timescale of τ2 = 5.6 μs. According to the implied timescale plot published in Zhou et al., an implied timescale of ≈ 5.6 μs is predicted when the lag time of the MSM is set to 30 ns. For our solvent-feature MSM, the slowest implied timescale τ2 is 1.0 ± 0.3 μs using an MSM lag time of 30 ns (Figure 3). This corresponds to a predicted kon = (τ2c)−1 = 1.4 ± 0.5 × 108 M−1 s−1, nearly an order of magnitude larger. The uncertainties in these values come from a bootstrap procedure in which MSMs are built using random selections of 80% of the input trajectories.

  For the MSM constructed from protein distance features, a high density of trajectory samples in the lower right corner (+tIC1, −tIC2) represents the p53 TAD bound in a fully helical state to the hydrophobic binding cleft of MDM2, while the upper right corner (+tIC1, +tIC2) and lower left corner (−tIC1, −tIC2) correspond to long-lived intermediates in which p53 is bound but misfolded. Zhou et al. characterized these two states as ones where residue Trp23 is incorrectly inserted into the binding cleft, and is instead associated with with MDM2 helix α2 or α4, respectively.

  For the MSM constructed from solvent features, these intermediate states are largely preserved. Inspection of trajectories that start at (+tIC1, +tIC2), and finish at the native state (+tIC1, −tIC2) predominantly display Trp23 facing out of the page when looking at MDM2-p53 from the orientation shown in the figures we present in this work. Likewise, trajectories starting at (−tIC1, −tIC2) show Trp23 oriented into the page. One observed trend–impossible to detect in the MSM constructed from protein distance features—is that burial of Trp23 in the binding cleft is preceded by fluctuations in the number of surrounding waters, a desolvation transition that is further discussed below.


### Solvent features most correlated with slow binding motions provide a unique view of hydrophobic driving forces
  The largest TICA component, tIC1, is the linear combination of features that are most correlated with the slowest motions present in the trajectory data. For the MSM constructed from solvent features, tIC1 directly corresponds to desolvation. When moving from −tIC1 to +tIC1, we find a direct correlation in the number of water molecules inside the pocket, corresponding to the proximity of p53 to MDM2’s binding site (see further discussion below). As bulky nonpolar residues such as W23 and F19 enter the hydrophobic cleft of MDM2, the reduction of water from their surrounding solvent shells creates a positive shift along tIC1, with the stability of the bound α-helix maximized when W23 is in the correct orientation (into the page, with reference to Figure 4).

  While one might expect the desolvation of hydrophobic residues to dominate the set of solvent features contributing to tIC1, a TICA analysis shows that it is water shells around polar residues that contribute most to tIC1. Figure 5a shows the values of αi2 for each solvent feature fi, normalized by the maximum value. The five features that contribute most to tIC1 are solvent shells centered on Cα and Cα atoms in the p53 TAD peptide: the second solvent shell (3–6 Å) of Lys24-Cβ, Thr18-Cβ, Lys24-Cα, Thr18-Cα, and Ser20-Cβ. All of these residues are adjacent to either Phe19 or Trp23, hydrophobic residues that insert into the binding cleft upon binding. The next most important group of features correspond to the first solvent shell (0–3 Å) of MDM2 atoms in the loop region that interact with the N-terminus of the p53 TAD peptide in the bound state: His73-Cα, Gln72-Cα, and Gln72-Cβ.

  The chosen sign structure of the tIC1 eigenvector (Figure 5b) describes how the solvent features change upon binding. Negative eigenvector components (pink) show an increase in the number of shell waters as the binding reaction proceeds, while positive eigenvector components (yellow) show a decrease in the number of shell waters. For example, The second solvent shell around Lys24-Cβ in the p53 TAD peptide atoms shows a decrease upon binding as many fewer waters can occupy the shells when bound to MDM2.

  A similar analysis of important features and eigenvector structure for tIC2 (Figure S2) show that the largest contributions come from first solvent shell (0–3 Å) features of Gln72-Cα, Gln71-Cβ, and Met62-Cα, Phe91-Cβ and Tyr67-Cα.


### Visualization of desolvation pathways in protein binding
  Since our TICA analysis found that second solvent shell (3–6 Å) features around p53 TAD peptide atoms most significantly contributed to tIC1, waters within this region were highlighted and traced along specific trajectories. In addition to this approach, instantaneous (1 ns) solvent density was visualized with the help of Chimera’s molmap tool. At each instant along a trajectory an occupancy surface represents the absence of water oxygens. As one would expect, an inverse relationship was found for the proximity of p53 to MDM2 and the number of water oxygens in the vicinity of the pocket.

  Several trajectories were projected to the solvent-feature 2D (tIC1, tIC2) TICA landscape to examine and visualize the intermediates in desolvation pathways. In Figure 6 we highlight a particular example: a 531-ns trajectory that traverses the most of the span of tIC1. This particular trajectory undergoes transitions between five intermediates (Figures 6a–e), represented by snapshots manually selected from the corresponding MSM state. Trajectories projected the TICA landscape in this way are shown for additional trajectories in Figures S3–S7, along with Supporting Movies. These trajectories span all four quadrants of the (tIC1, tIC2) TICA landscape.

  In the highlighted example, four main transitions can be seen in the trajectory, which starts in a configuration where p53 TAD peptide is bound to MDM2 but in the incorrect, unfolded pose. Since p53 TAD is disordered in its unbound state, it is expected that most binding pathways (at physiological concentrations) follow a pattern of binding-then-folding, consistent with the so-called “fly-casting” mechanism.48,49

  In this trajectory, Thr18 (cyan) and Phe19 (light green) of p53 TAD enters the binding pocket first, displacing some transient water molecules, although water still remains, as shown by large molmap surface cavities (Figure 6a,b). The next transition occurs at 201 ns (00:07 in Supplemental Movie M1) where Trp23 (yellow) enters the pocket, displacing a large water cluster from the pocket, resulting in reorganization to increase polar interactions (Figures 6c). Also at this point, local water density around Thr18 quickly increases (see Figure 7 at 200 ns). At 308 ns (00:10 in Supplemental Movie M2), Trp23 inserts deeper into the binding cleft, and the p53 TAD peptide is pulled down, simultaneously moving Thr18 upwards to maintain a large local water density, enabling F19 to move further down into the cleft at 314 ns (00:11) (Figures 6d). In the final transition, the last α-helix turn of p53 TAD peptide near Lys24 forms (Figures 6e).

  A video animation corresponding to Figures 6 is shown in Supporting Movie M1. Supporting movies M2 through M6 showcase similar content to provide additional trajectories, and their corresponding movies are M8 and M9, respectively.

  To better understand solvent dynamics in this example trajectory, we used UCSF Chimera to calculate the number of waters in various solvent shells around all atoms in Trp23 and Thr18 over time. Plots of these traces, color-coded according to the states shown in Figure 6, reveal key mechanistic steps of desolvation (Figure 7). Generally, traces show that Trp23, a bulky hydrophobic residue, has fewer waters in its solvent shells over time, while Thr18, which starts more buried in the binding cleft, gains an increased number of waters over time.

  More specifically, we can see that there are coupled changes in Trp23 and Thr18 solvent shells at 200 ns as the trajectory transitions from state (b) to (c), demonstrating that cooperative solvent interactions occur in binding. After entering state (c), Trp23 has strikingly large fluctuations in its solvation environment, until it transitions to state (d), where it is able to insert deeper into the binding cleft, in the correct helical conformation.

  Animations simultaneously showing the number of hydrogen bonds and numbers of waters in solvent shells over time, a visualization of water structure, and the trajectory on the TICA landscape for this example trajectory are shown for p53 TAD peptide residues Thr18, Lys24, and MDM2 residue Gln72, in Supporting Movies M7, M8, and M9, respectively.


## Discussion
  Solvent density fluctuations have been much discussed as a characteristic of dewetting transitions.50–53 Ricci et al. observe similar fluctuations around Trp23 in simulations of MDM2 and p53 TAD peptide binding where the peptide was constrained in a helical conformation.19 It is therefore instructive to observe such fluctuations also occuring around Trp23 in an unconstrained simulation of binding, in which the peptide is non-specifically associated and must rearrange to find the correct binding pose.

  Our tICA analysis identified solvent features around Thr18 as a significant contributor to the tIC1 coordinate, gaining more waters in its second solvent shells throughout binding, with many trajectories showing partial insertion of Thr18 into the binding cleft at early times. Interestingly, Yadahalli et al.54 found a ten- to twenty-fold decrease in binding affinity when Thr18 is phosphorylated in comparison to the wild-type p53 TAD peptide. While this can be partially explained by the decrease in helical propensity upon phosphorylation at T18, it is also possible that the phosphate group disrupts solvent hydrogen bonding networks around Thr18, especially when in proximity to Gln72 of MDM2 (purple residue in Figure 6).


### A solvent-centric view of the hydrophobic effect
  Molecular simulations of protein conformational dynamics often are analyzed from a protein-centric view, where the quantities of interest are distances and positions of protein residues. Indeed, it is common practice to remove solvent coordinates from trajectory data before analysis, even though driving forces such as hydrophobicity and hydrogen bonding are intimately water-mediated.

  By analyzing simulation trajectory data through solvent shell features, we explicitly take a solvent-centric view of the hydrophobic effect. Remarkably, we find that solvent features completely recapitulate the shape of p53 TAD-MDM2 binding landscape, and quantitatively predict the slowest timescales involved in binding. Mechanistically, we see the hydrophobic effect through pathways of collective solvation and desolvation transitions around both MDM2 and p53 TAD peptide residues. Instead of focusing on hydrophobic residues, the solvent-feature tICA analysis we perform identifies the importance of polar residues Thr18, Lys24, Ser20, as they seek to achieve favorable solvent environments. This is the “flip side” of the hydrophobic effect, as seen from a solvent-centric view.

  If Trp23 is such an important hydrophobic residue, then why don’t we see it as being a more significant contributor to tIC1 in the solvent shell analysis? One reason might be the observed density fluctuations for solvent around Trp23 for the desolvation process. The tICA method identifies important features to be those that contribute most significantly to slow time-correlated motions; residues with fluctuations in the number of solvent shell waters that happen on timescales faster than the binding time scale (see Figure 7) may obscure the importance of Trp23.

  As mentioned in Results, the accelerated timescales of our solvent-feature MSM suggest that solvent features poorly capture the slowest motions in the binding reaction, which involve the slow rearrangement of MDM2-bound peptide into a helical conformation. In future work, therefore, it would be interesting to analyze MSMs constructed solvent features vs. protein features in a system where solvent dynamics and protein conformational changes occur on comparable timescales. For example, in the dewetting-induced collapse of the Mellittin tetramer,55 the large-scale water drying transition and the hydrophobic collapse of the protein occur almost simultaneously. This would be a challenging yet valuable system to investigate using solvent shell featurization, as the comparable dynamical timescales of solvent and protein motions may reveal more coupled transitions.


### The utility of solvent shell featurization
  The ‘Solvent Shells Featurization’ method of Harrigan et al. used here has the potential to be extremely useful for analyzing other biomolecular dynamics as well: water and ions inside a membrane system, sugars involved in hydrogen bonding, or the dynamics of water clusters in various cavities, which is pertinent to drug design. It is likely possible to uncover substantial detail when turning to this solvent-centric point of view.

  Gu et al.56 employed a strategy akin to the solvent shell featurization, leveraging a Gaussian kernel function to articulate water densities and formulating a solvent signature vector that encapsulates the solvent distribution surrounding the solute. The approach simplifies solvent configurations into a succinct vector representation by considering the Euclidean distance between solute and solvent atom positions, facilitating a potentially more streamlined and efficient featurization. Furthermore, they propose that distance functions can be amalgamated to formulate a model incorporating both solute and solvent information.

  The main difference between this approach and the solvent-shell featurization method lies in the refinement of the sum of weighted solute-solvent distances for each solute atom to define a vector description of the solvent configuration, wherein spatial resolution may be compromised. Solvent shell featurization, conversely, preserves spatial resolution by employing spherical shells to yield instantaneous solvent density.

  A potential disadvantage of our solvent featurization approach is the somewhat arbitrary definition of solvent shells into 3 Å bins. While this was shown to work reasonably in previous studies,43 the optimization of solvent shells would likely improve the spatial and dynamical resolution of the MSM. Solvent shells binned according to the radial distribution function (RDF) of solvent around each amino acid type might improve the featurization. Adding a larger number of finer-width shells may also improve the featurization, at the risk of increasing finite sampling error and computational expense.

  Although we did not attempt to build MSMs using both solvent-shell and protein-distance features, we suspect that, given the accelerated implied timescales when using solvent features alone, that MSMs built from multiple features may be dominated by the protein distance features. Although outside the scope of this work, nonlinear featurizations enabled by neural net approaches such as DeepTime57 would also be a promising avenue to find optimal models.

  We did not attempt a more detailed analysis of solvation thermodynamics along binding pathways, in part, due to the fact that explicit-solvent trajectory data has a time resolution of 1 ns. We have, however, publicly released the full trajectory dataset (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/8SC9G), and invite researchers to perform such analysis as they may see fit. Code used for movie making and analysis is available on GitHub (https://github.com/robraddi/WaterMSM).


## Conclusion
  Using approximately 831 μs of trajectory data simulated in explicit solvent, a MSM of p53 TAD peptide binding to MDM2 was built from solvent shell features, offering a complementary, solvent-based view of hydrophobicity in protein-peptide binding. We find that the MSM built from only solvent features exhibits a landscape very similar to the MSM built by Zhou et al. from protein distance features.2 A tICA analysis of the solvent feature trajectory data, and calculations of instantaneous water density over time, revealed that regions within approximately 3–6 Å of Thr18 and Lys24 in the p53 TAD peptide are particularly important. In this work, the hydrophobic effect is reaffirmed from a solvent-centric viewpoint, and visualized with calculated water density surfaces to illuminate how solvent mediates protein-peptide binding.
