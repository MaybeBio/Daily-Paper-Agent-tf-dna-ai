# Machine learning and single‐cell transcriptome profiling reveal regulation of fibroblast activation through THBS2/TGFβ1/P‐Smad2/3 signalling pathway in hypertrophic scar


## Abstract
  Hypertrophic scar (HS) is a chronic inflammatory skin disorder characterized by excessive deposition of extracellular matrix, and the mechanisms underlying their formation remain poorly understood. We analysed scRNA‐seq data from samples of normal skin and HS. Using the hdWGCNA method, key gene modules of fibroblasts in HS were identified. Non‐negative matrix factorization was employed to perform subtype analysis of HS patients using these gene modules. Multiple machine learning algorithms were applied to screen and validate accurate gene signatures for identifying and predicting HS, and a convolutional neural network (CNN) based on deep learning was established and validated. Quantitative reverse transcription‐polymerase chain reaction and western blotting were performed to measure mRNA and protein expression. Immunofluorescence was used for gene localization analysis, and biological features were assessed through CCK8 and wound healing assay. Single‐cell sequencing revealed distinct subpopulations of fibroblasts in HS. HdWGCNA identified key gene characteristics of this population, and pseudotime analysis was conducted to investigate gene variation during fibroblast differentiation. By employing various machine learning algorithms, the gene range was narrowed down to three key genes. A CNN was trained using the expression of these key genes and immune cell infiltration, enabling diagnosis and prediction of HS. Functional experiments demonstrated that THBS2 is associated with fibroblast proliferation and migration in HS and affects the formation and development of HS through the TGFβ1/P‐Smad2/3 pathway. Our study identifies unique fibroblast subpopulations closely associated with HS and provides biomarkers for the diagnosis and treatment of HS.


## INTRODUCTION
  Hypertrophic scar (HS) is a type of abnormal scar that results from improper wound healing characterized by chronic inflammation in the reticular dermis and excessive production of collagen protein in response to skin injuries such as burns or surgical incisions. 1 ,  2  HS can severely impact patients' quality of life, causing physical and psychological discomfort, including itching, pain, sleep disorders and anxiety, and reducing their overall well‐being. 3 ,  4  Further research into the molecular mechanisms of HS formation and development is crucial as there is currently no single method that can effectively cure or prevent HS recurrence, despite the availability of various treatments.

  The differentiation of fibroblasts plays a significant role in the development of HS by stimulating collagen synthesis, which can result in the formation of fibrotic lesions. 5 ,  6  Considerable evidence suggests that the transforming growth factor (TGF) β1/Smad signalling pathway plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of HS. 7  Elevated TGFβ1/Smad activity stimulates the proliferation of fibroblasts, leading to an overproduction and excessive deposition of collagen that contributes to the formation of HS. 8  In contrast, research suggests that decreased TGFβ1/Smad signalling activity can prevent or reduce the development of HS. 9

  Single‐cell sequencing analysis has proven to be a groundbreaking research technique that enables scientists to group cells and investigate the variations in gene expression between these groups and the differences in cell development. 10  Given that HS is made up of various types of cells, such as fibroblasts, keratinocytes and endothelial cells, 11 ,  12  single‐cell sequencing analysis provides an effective approach to precisely study the genetic attributes of HS at the individual cell level.

  This study aims to investigate the key characteristics and cellular signalling pathways involved in the development and formation of HS, identify genes that can serve as potential biomarkers for HS and determine molecules that can be used for treating patients with HS. With the aid of bioinformatics methods, this research provides valuable insights into the future development of HS treatments.


## METHODS AND MATERIALS

### Data acquisition
  The gene expression omnibus (GEO) is a comprehensive database developed by the National Center for Biotechnology Information that contains a vast amount of gene expression data. Our research involved obtaining multiple single‐cell datasets, including GSE156326, which consists of three normal skin (NS) samples and three HS samples, as well as GSE188952 from the GEO database, which includes five HS samples and three NS samples. Moreover, specific samples were chosen from various datasets to augment our analysis. These include six NS samples from GSE158395, as well as three HS samples and three NS samples from GSE210434. Additionally, we incorporated three NS samples from GSE90626. Furthermore, an additional dataset labelled as OEP002674 was obtained from The National Omics Data Encyclopedia (NODE, https://www.biosino.org/), comprising five HS samples and five NS samples. To ensure comparability across these diverse datasets, multiple annotations, normalization techniques and log2‐transformations were applied. Notably, the ‘sva’ R package was deployed for the purpose of removing any potential batch effects prior to merging the datasets.


### Single‐cell RNA statistical processing
  Single‐cell sequencing analysis has been widely used in a variety of diseases and fields. 13 ,  14 ,  15 ,  16 ,  17  In this study, the ‘Seurat’ R package was utilized to create Seurat objects from scRNA‐seq data. 18  Normalization and scaling were performed on cells, resulting in 45 095 cells retained after excluding those with less than 200 expressed genes, more than 2500 expressed genes or mitochondrial gene content higher than 10%. We conducted principal component analysis on the top 3000 highly variable genes, with a setting of 15 principal components for dimensionality reduction and clustering. Samples were integrated, and batch effects were removed using the ‘harmony’ function. Unsupervised cell clusters were obtained through graph‐based clustering (with a resolution of 0.5) and visualized using t‐SNE plots. Cell types were determined for each cluster based on previously reported differentially expressed genes and typical cell markers, with visualization of typical differentially expressed genes within each cell type via heatmap. To further identify fibroblast clusters, we selected clusters of fibroblast cell types for additional t‐SNE analysis, graph‐based clustering and marker gene analysis.


### High dimensional WGCNA analysis
  In this study, a high dimensional WGCNA (hdWGCNA) approach was used to construct a scale‐free network at the single‐cell level. 19  The R package ‘hdWGCNA’ was employed to ensure optimal network connectivity, setting a threshold for the scale‐free topology model fit to >0.85 and selecting a soft threshold of 7. We employed the Pearson correlation method to generate a topological overlap matrix that reflects gene expression similarity and then used the ‘PlotDendrogram’ function to create a cluster tree. Genes were subsequently clustered into modules and ModuleEigengenes were utilized to perform correlation analyses between modules and cell clusters, ultimately determining the most relevant modules. To identify hub genes within these modules, three modules were selected and we chose the top 50 genes from each module as our hub genes. Functional enrichment analysis of these hub genes was conducted using gene ontology (GO) and kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) through the use of the ‘clusterProfiler’ and ‘circlize’ R packages.


### Pseudotime analysis
  Pseudotime analysis is a powerful method for determining cell fate, and in our study, we utilized the Monocle2 algorithm (http://cole-trapnell-lab.github.io/monocle-release) to conduct single‐cell trajectory analysis. To ensure an optimal sample size, we randomly selected 4000 cells using the ‘sample’ function for subsequent pseudotime analysis. The ‘DDRTree’ method was employed to reduce the dimensionality of the cells, followed by the utilization of the ‘reduceDimension’ function to calculate the cell differentiation state. The visualization of the cell differentiation trajectory was achieved through the application of the ‘plot cell trajectory’ function. Moreover, changes in gene expression during the cell differentiation trajectory were observed by employing the ‘plot pseudotime heatmap’ function, with module genes exhibiting an adjusted p‐value threshold of <0.05.


### Identification of molecular subtypes using NMF algorithm
  The non‐negative matrix factorization (NMF) clustering algorithm was employed to identify distinct subtypes associated with the expression of 150 hub genes in HS samples. A k‐means consensus clustering analysis was performed using the ‘NMF’ R package. The NMF method utilized the standard ‘brunet’ option and underwent 100 iterations. The number of clusters (k) was set from 2 to 10, and the common member matrix's average silhouette width was determined using the ‘NMF’ R package, with each subclass's minimum member set to 10. The optimal number of clusters was determined using the cophenetic, dispersion and silhouette metrics, with 2 being identified as the optimal number of clusters.


### Identification of hub genes through screening process
  Machine learning algorithms are effective in identifying key genes, with the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) algorithm commonly used for multiple linear regression, and it has been used in a large number of studies. 20 ,  21 ,  22 ,  23 ,  24  To screen for key genes and identify the appropriate algorithm, we utilized the LASSO regression algorithm and learned from seven different machine learning algorithms in our study.


### Convolutional neural network model
  Convolutional neural network (CNN) is a widely used deep learning model in fields such as image processing. 25  It employs basic components such as convolutional layers, pooling layers and fully connected layers to learn the feature representations of input data for classification, regression and other tasks. We merged OEP002674, GSE188952 and GSE158395 datasets together as the training set (10 HS samples, 11 NS samples), and the remaining datasets were merged as the validation set (6 HS samples, 6 NS samples). Using the ‘keras’ R package, we constructed a CNN model based on a two‐dimensional array constructed from three key genes and immune cells.


### Immune infiltration and inflammation factor analysis
  To estimate the extent of immune cell infiltration in the samples, we employed the ‘IOBR’ R package to implement the MCP algorithm. 26 ,  27  Additionally, we explored the relationship between the three key genes and immune cell infiltration, as well as the expression levels of inflammatory factors.


### Patients and ethics
  HS and NS tissue were collected from the Department of Plastic Surgery at Xijing Hospital. Some tissues were frozen at −80°C for protein and RNA extraction, while others were temporarily stored at −4°C to isolate and culture primary fibroblasts. Informed consent was obtained from all patients.


### Isolation and culture of HSF and NSF
  We utilized the monolayer culture method to isolate primary fibroblasts. The tissue was soaked in PBS containing a 2% penicillin mixture for 10 min to remove any blood stains, and the subcutaneous fat and epidermis were then removed using scissors. The remaining dermis was cut into small pellets and placed flat in a T25 bottle, with a spacing of 0.5 cm between each pellet to maintain tissue moisture. Subsequently, 5 mL of medium containing 10% FBS was added to the T25 bottle, which was then inverted and placed in a 37°C, 5% CO2 cell culture incubator for 3 h. Afterward, the T25 bottle was gently placed upright. The medium, containing 10% FBS, was changed every 3 days, and the primary fibroblasts were observed under a microscope for isolation. Typically, primary fibroblasts could be observed after 14 days. Once the fibroblasts reached a certain density, the cells were digested and cultured with trypsin. Cells from the 3rd to 6th generation were used for subsequent experiments.


### siRNA
  Fibroblasts were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum. We used Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) to transfect siRNAs targeting the THBS2 gene (Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) to fibroblasts according to the instruction. SiRNA sequences were designed for three pairs: Forward 5′‐GCAAGAUCACCAAGAUCAU‐3′ and reverse: 5′‐AUGAUCUUGGUGAUCUUGC‐3′ for si‐THBS2‐1, Forward 5′‐CGUCAGAUGUGCAACAAGA‐3′ and reverse 5′‐UCUUGUUGCACAUCUGACG‐3′ for si‐ THBS2‐2, Forward 5′‐GCACCACGUGUACCUGCAA‐3′ and reverse 5′‐UUGCAGGUACACGUGGUGC‐3′ for si‐THBS2‐3.


### RNA extraction and qRT‐PCR
  We performed RNA extraction using TRIzol and quantified the RNA concentration with the Nanodrop 2000C instrument. Briefly, tissue RNA was extracted by adding TRIzol to the tissue for homogenization, followed by collection of the cellular debris containing TRIzol to extract cellular RNA. Subsequently, chloroform was added and the resulting supernatant was centrifuged. After 10 min at room temperature, an equal volume of isopropanol was added and the supernatant was discarded by centrifugation. The precipitate was then treated with 75% ethanol, followed by centrifugation to remove the supernatant and air drying. The RNA was lysed in enzyme‐free water. For cDNA synthesis, we used a reverse transcription kit (11141ES10, Yeasen), followed by qRT‐PCR using SYBR Green (11201ES03, Yeasen) and specific primers. The primer sequences for the genes were: Human CTSK forward sequence: 5′‐GCAGAAGAACCGGGGTATTGA‐3′, reverse sequence: 5′‐GAAGGAGGTCAGGCTTGCAT‐3′. CTHRC1 forward sequence: 5′‐GTGGCTCACTTCGGCTAAAAT‐3′, reverse sequence: 5′‐CACTAATCCAGCACCAATTCCTT‐3′. THBS2 forward sequence: 5′‐ATAGACAGCTTCGCTCTGGAC‐3′, reverse sequence: 5′‐CAAACCCCTGAAGTGACTCTC‐3′. Coll forward sequence: 5′‐CTCAGGGTGTCAAGGGTGAAAGTG‐3′, reverse sequence 5′‐GCCAGCTGCACATCAAGGAC‐3′. Col3 forward sequence: 5′‐TTGAAGGAGGATGTTCCCATCT‐3′, reverse sequence: 5′‐ACAGACACATATTTGGCATGGTT‐3′. αSMA forward sequence: 5′‐TGCCAACAACGTCATGTCG‐3′, reverse sequence: 5′‐CAGCGCGGTGATCTCTTTCT‐3′. TGFβ1 forward sequence: 5′‐CAATTCCTGGCGATACCTCAG‐3′, reverse sequence: 5′‐GCACAACTCCGGTGACATCAA‐3′. GAPDH forward sequence: 5′‐CTGGGCTACACTGAGCACC‐3′, reverse sequence: 5′‐AAGTGGTCGTTGAGGGCAATG‐3′.


### Western blot
  Skin tissue, approximately the size of a pea grain, was retrieved from −80°C storage. It was then cut up and placed in a tissue grinding tube along with 3–5 magnetic beads. A mixture of RIPA (CW2333, Cwbio, Beijing, China) containing 1% protease inhibitor (CW2200, Cwbio, Beijing, China) and phosphatase inhibitor (CW2383, Cwbio, Beijing, China) was added to break down the tissue into a homogeneous pulp using a tissue disruptor. The tissue sample was subsequently paired and centrifuged at 14000 g for 30 min using a centrifuge set at 4°C. The resulting cell pellet was washed with PBS, and a mixture of RIPA containing 1% protease inhibitor and phosphatase inhibitor was added. The cell debris mixture was then scraped with a cytoscraper and collected in EP tubes. These EP tubes were placed on ice and subjected to lysis in a shaker for 30 min. Afterwards, the EP tubes were paired and centrifuged at 14000 g for 30 min using a centrifuge set at 4°C. Equal amounts of protein were loaded onto SDS‐PAGE gels, selecting different concentrations of SDS‐PAGE gels based on the molecular weight of the target protein (P2011, NCM Biotech, Shanghai, China). The proteins were electrophoresed and transferred onto PVDF membranes, which were incubated with a TBST solution containing 5% skimmed milk powder for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the PVDF membranes were washed with TBST before being incubated overnight at 4°C with the following rabbit anti‐human primary antibodies: GAPDH (10494‐1‐AP, Sanying, Wuhan, China, 1:1000), THBS2 (ab84469, Abcam, 1:1000), Col1 (14695‐1‐AP, Sanying, Wuhan, China, 1:1000), Col3 (22734‐1‐AP, Sanying, Wuhan, China, 1:1000), αSMA (14395‐1‐AP, Sanying, Wuhan, China, 1:1000), TGFβ1 (ab215715, Abcam), P‐Smad2/3 (8828 s, Cell Signalling Technology, 1:1000), Smad2/3 (8685s, Cell Signalling Technology, 1:1000). The next day, the PVDF membrane was washed with TBST and incubated with Goat anti‐rabbit IgG‐HRP antibody (CW0103, Sanying, Wuhan, China, 1:5000) for 2 h at room temperature. The protein bands were visualized using an ECL developer (310212, Zeta Life) under a Tanon 4600 instrument.


### CCK‐8 assay
  The cells were seeded overnight in 96‐well plates at a density of 3000 cells per well. The following day, once the cells were observed to be in good condition, the medium in each well was replaced with 90 μL of the respective medium. After 0, 1, 2, 3 and 4 day, 10 μL of CCK‐8 reagent (C0038, Beyotime) was added to each well, and the cells were incubated at 37°C for 1 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using an enzyme marker with excitation light to determine cell proliferation.


### Wound healing assay
  Fibroblasts were seeded in a six‐well plate. Once the cells reached confluence and appeared healthy under the microscope, a straight vertical line was drawn evenly across the centre of the plate using a 20 μL pipette tip. The scratches were gently washed with PBS and replaced with serum‐free medium. Images of the scratches were captured at 0, 24 and 48 h respectively.


### Immunofluorescence
  The cells were plated on confocal plates and fixed with a solution containing 4% paraformaldehyde. To enhance membrane permeability, 0.2% Triton X‐100 was added, followed by the addition of 2% BSA to seal the cells. The cells were then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against THBS2 (ab84469, Abcam) and αSMA (67735‐1‐AP, Sanying). On the following day, a secondary antibody (A11011/A11029, Invitrogen) was applied and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. The cell nucleus was stained with DAPI, and immunofluorescence images of the cells were captured using a laser scanning microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ti).


## RESULTS

### Unveiling cellular heterogeneity in human HS tissue using single‐cell RNA‐sequencing profiling
  Figure 1A illustrates the flow of this study. A study was conducted to analyse cellular heterogeneity in human skin tissue through scRNA‐seq profiling. scRNA‐seq datasets of three NS and three HS samples were obtained from the GEO database. After rigorous quality control measures, a total of 45 095 cells were selected for analysis. Using dimensionality reduction clustering, eight cell types with high heterogeneity between different cell populations were identified (Figure 2A). Figure 1C presents a colour‐coded heatmap illustrating the top five marker genes among the different annotated cell types. Further analysis of fibroblasts revealed 11 clusters, with four clusters (0, 4, 7, 9) showing higher proportions in HS compared to NS, making them potential candidates for further analysis in the context of HS (Figure 1D,E).


### Selection of key modules using hdWGCNA algorithm
  The hdWGCNA algorithm was utilized to identify key modules with molecular characteristics of fibroblasts. A soft threshold of 7 was employed to construct a scale‐free network of fibroblasts based on the best connectivity, leading to the identification of a total of nine gene modules (Figure 2A–D). Correlation analysis was conducted among these nine gene modules as well as between the gene modules and fibroblast clusters, facilitating the discovery of gene modules with strong correlations to clusters 0, 4, 7 and 9 (Figure 2E,F). Notably, the magenta and black modules exhibited high correlation with cluster 4, while the yellow module demonstrated high correlation with cluster 7. Therefore, the top 50 genes from each of the magenta, black and yellow modules were selected as hub genes for subsequent analysis.


### Functional and pathway enrichment analysis
  We conducted GO and KEGG enrichment analysis to investigate the functional and pathway enrichment of hub genes. In the biological process category, the genes were mainly enriched in functions related to extracellular matrix (ECM) organization, extracellular structure organization and collagen fibril organization. In the cellular component category, the genes were mainly enriched in functions such as collagen‐containing ECM and endoplasmic reticulum lumen. In the molecular function category, the genes were mainly enriched in functions such as ECM structural constituent, glycosaminoglycan binding and collagen binding (Figure 3A,C). The KEGG analysis revealed that the genes were mostly enriched in pathways such as diabetic cardiomyopathy, oxidative phosphorylation and ECM‐receptor interaction (Figure 3B,D).


### Pseudotime analysis reveals fibroblast differentiation and hub gene expression changes
  To obtain a more comprehensive understanding of the intricate cell trajectory of fibroblasts, pseudotime analysis was conducted. As illustrated in Figure 3E, six distinct states were identified during the process of fibroblast differentiation, each distinguished by its own distinctive coloration. Moreover, Figure 3F demonstrates that the degree of blue colour intensity corresponded to the cells' differentiation time, with darker blue indicating earlier differentiation and lighter blue representing more recently differentiated cells moving from right to left over time. In addition, Figure 3G illustrates how cell subpopulations evolve and differentiate from each other. Furthermore, we investigated the expression changes of hub genes during the differentiation of these fibroblasts. Figure 3H visualizes a heatmap of hub genes that changed concomitantly with fibroblast differentiation. These genes were categorized into three types based on their differential expression patterns. Specifically, some genes exhibited an increase in expression as cells differentiated, while others displayed a decrease in expression. These observations suggest that hub genes may exert distinct functions in the pathogenesis of fibroblast differentiation induction.


### NMF consensus clustering analysis of HS
  To classify the 150 hub genes into molecular clusters, NMF consensus clustering was initially employed. The optimal number of clusters (K) was determined by conducting an NMF rank survey, and K = 2 was selected (Figure 4A). Subsequently, the HS dataset was divided into two groups using K = 2, and the gene expression profiles of these groups were analysed, resulting in the generation of heatmaps (Figure 4B). By assessing the expression levels of the 150 HS fibroblast‐related hub genes between the two subtypes, it was observed that cluster 1 exhibited higher scores compared to cluster 2 (Figure 4D). Further analysis was conducted to investigate the disparities in the expression of inflammatory factors and immune cells between the two clusters. Notably, it was found that TGF‐β3 and TGF‐β1 were significantly upregulated in cluster 1 as opposed to cluster 2, and cluster 1 displayed a greater degree of fibroblast infiltration (Figure 4C,E). The results of GSVA showed that cluster 1 was mainly enriched with IL6 JAK STAT3, TGF‐β and other pathways (Figure 4F). All these findings suggest that fibroblasts in cluster 1 may be more active and exhibit more HS characteristics.


### Screening of key genes and comparison of multiple machine learning models
  To investigate whether the 150 hub genes related to HS fibroblasts at the single‐cell transcriptome level were also closely associated with HS at the bulk transcriptome level, we merged previously collected datasets and scored them in HS and NS. As shown in Figure 5A, the results indicated that HS had significantly higher scores than NS (p < 0.01). Subsequently, LASSO analysis was performed to select the final key genes. The minimum parameters were used to determine the penalty parameter (λ), as indicated in Figure 4A,B. Ultimately, three genes (THBS2, CTSK and CTHRC1) were selected from hub genes. Machine learning models were established based on these three genes to verify their predictive ability. The machine learning methods included K‐nearest neighbour algorithm, linear discriminant analysis, logistic regression, naive bayes, random forest algorithm, recursive partitioning and support vector machine model. After five‐fold cross‐validation, the boxplot of each model is displayed in Figure 5D.


### Establishment and validation of a deep learning CNN model for HS diagnosis
  A two‐dimensional array was constructed for each patient in the training and validation sets, utilizing the ratio of key genes to various types of cells including immune cells, fibroblasts and epithelial cells. This approach was employed to demonstrate the association between these key genes and specific cell types, and the findings were visually presented through a heatmap display (Figure 4E). Remarkably, a strong association between three key genes and fibroblasts was observed, which aligns with our initial predictions. The training was set to 200 iterations, resulting in a decrease in bias and an increase in accuracy (Figure 5F). The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for the training and testing groups were 0.853 and 1.000, respectively, demonstrating the sensitivity and accuracy of the deep learning CNN model for HS diagnosis (Figure 5G,H).


### Immune infiltration analysis
  Additionally, immune cell infiltration analysis was performed on both HS and NS samples using the MCP immune infiltration algorithms. The degree of immune cell infiltration in the samples was calculated, and correlations were established between the three key genes and the predicted degree of immune cell infiltration, as well as the known distribution of inflammatory factor expression in the samples. As shown in Figure 5I,G, it is evident that fibroblasts are strongly positively correlated with THBS2, CTSK and CTHRC1, B lineage cells, monocytic lineage and endothelial cells are positively correlated with CTSK.


### THBS2 facilitates collagen expression, proliferation and migration in fibroblasts
  The expression of CTSK, CTHRC1 and THBS2 was investigated based on biogenic analysis, revealing elevated levels of these three genes in HS (Figure 6A). Given the lack of research on THBS2 in keloid or HS, further investigation was therefore focused on THBS2. To assess the impact of THBS2 on scar fibroblasts, the THBS2 gene was knocked down using siRNA, with si‐THBS2‐2 selected due to its pronounced knockout effect for subsequent experiments. qRT‐PCR demonstrated the knockout efficiency of the three sequenced siRNAs targeting THBS2 (Figure 6B). Subsequently, we validated whether fibroblasts expressing fibronectin exhibited decreased expression following THBS2 knockout. qRT‐PCR and WB analyses confirmed reduced expression of Col1, Col3 and αSMA in fibroblasts upon THBS2 knockout (Figure 1C,D). Immunofluorescence staining of cells further supported decreased THBS2 expression in fibroblasts subjected to THBS2 knockout, accompanied by a significant decrease in αSMA fluorescence intensity (Figure 1E). Next, to explore the impact of THBS2 on fibroblast migration ability, scratch experiments demonstrated that THBS2‐knockout fibroblasts exhibited decelerated migration rates compared to the control group. Finally, to verify any changes in fibroblast proliferation after THBS2 knockout, a CCK8 cell proliferation assay was conducted, affirming reduced proliferation capacity in scar fibroblasts with diminished THBS2 expression compared to the control group at day 1, 2 and 4 (Figure 1G). These findings indicate that THBS2 promotes the expression of Col1, Col3 and αSMA in scar fibroblasts, as well as cell migration and proliferation.


### THBS2 activates scar fibroblasts through the TGFβ1/P‐Smad2/3 pathway
  To investigate the activation mechanism of fibroblasts by THBS2 in HS, a single gene GSEA approach was employed. The potential functional role of THBS2 in HS was explored, and a significant enrichment of the TGF‐β pathway was observed (Figure 7A). To validate this hypothesis, fibroblasts were divided into four groups: the blank group, fibroblasts cultured with 200 ng/mL THBS2, fibroblasts cultured with 200 ng/mL THBS2 and 10 ng/mL SB‐431542 (a TGFβ1 receptor inhibitor), and fibroblasts cultured with 200 ng/mL THBS2, 10 ng/mL SB‐431542 and 10 ng/mL TGFβ1. qRT‐PCR analysis revealed that THBS2 upregulated the expression of TGFβ1 in fibroblasts, specifically Col1, Col3 and αSMA (LANE2). Conversely, the addition of SB‐431542 resulted in decreased TGFβ1 expression in fibroblasts of Col1, Col3 and αSMA (LANE3). Notably, the restoration of TGFβ1 expression in fibroblasts of Col1, Col3 and αSMA was not significant following the addition of TGFβ1 (LANE4) (Figure 7B). Western blot results aligned with the qRT‐PCR findings, demonstrating that THBS2 enhanced the expression of P‐Smad2/3 in fibroblasts. Furthermore, the presence of SB‐431542 inhibited the promotion of THBS2 on P‐Smad2/3 expression (Figure 7C). CCK8 and wound healing assays exhibited that THBS2 stimulated fibroblast proliferation and migration rates. However, the inhibitory effect of SB‐431542 on cell proliferation and migration significantly counteracted the promotional impact of THBS2 (Figure 7D,E). Collectively, these findings suggest that THBS2 induces the expression, proliferation and migration of fibroblast marker proteins through TGFβ1/P‐Smad2/3 signalling pathway.


## DISCUSSION
  HS is a chronic skin condition characterized by the formation of fibrous lesions due to the excessive accumulation of fibroblasts and collagen. 28  This disease is caused by a combination of genetic, biomechanical, metabolic, endocrine and immunological factors. 29  Given its impact on physical, mental and social well‐being, HS has been recognized as a major challenge to global health in terms of unmet functional and psychosocial needs. 1  Unfortunately, available treatments such as cryotherapy, corticosteroids, laser therapy, pressure therapy, radiation and surgery often have limited success in managing HS. 30  As a result, early diagnosis and timely intervention are critical for effective management of this condition. Therefore, the discovery of new biomarkers that can help control the proliferation of HS and reduce its development is essential.

  Previous studies have identified fibroblast proliferation and ECM deposition as key characteristics of HS formation. 31  Therefore, this study focused on a subpopulation of fibroblasts enriched in HS, referred to as scar fibroblasts, identified through analysis of HS single‐cell datasets. By utilizing the hdWGCNA approach, gene modules closely correlated with the scar fibroblast subpopulation were elucidated, aiding in the identification of key genes contributing to HS development. Gene enrichment analysis highlighted their involvement in critical processes, including ECM organization, collagen‐containing ECM and oxidative phosphorylation. To refine the gene selection, LASSO analysis and seven other machine learning methods narrowed down the list to three genes: CTSK, CTHRC1 and THBS2.

  CTSK, implicated in the turnover of the cutaneous ECM, displayed up‐regulation in a silica‐induced lung fibrosis model and demonstrated strong expression in keloids and dermatofibromas. 32 ,  33  CTHRC1, with roles in vascular remodelling, bone formation and developmental morphogenesis, was shown to activate hepatic stellate cells, promoting liver fibrosis through TGF‐β signalling activation. 34  It also emerged as a potential therapeutic target for keloids. 35  Thrombospondins (THBSs) are a group of ECM proteins that belong to the THBS family, which consists of five members. 36  Among them, THBS1 and THBS2 have been extensively studied in fibrosis. THBS1 is highly expressed in fibroblasts specific to systemic sclerosis skin and plays a significant role in cardiac fibrosis. 37 ,  38  THBS2, a significant anti‐angiogenic matricellular protein, assumes a crucial role as a regulator of ECM homeostasis during the progression of fibrosis. 39 ,  40  In the context of cardiac fibrosis, miR‐29a‐3p exerts its anti‐fibrotic effect by targeting and inhibiting THBS2 expression. 41  Moreover, transcriptomic analyses have identified THBS2 as a potential biomarker for non‐alcoholic steatohepatitis and advanced liver fibrosis. 42

  Deep learning CNN was employed for model construction and validation, demonstrating stability and accuracy with the ROCs of the training set (AUC = 0.853) and validation set (AUC = 1.000). Notably, HS has been well‐known to be influenced by immune factors and proinflammatory cytokines. 43 ,  44  Our investigation unveiled three key genes significantly associated with high fibroblast activity, corroborating findings from previous analyses. Furthermore, we observed positive correlations between CTHRC1 and THBS2 gene expression levels with the presence of IL‐13, TGFB1 and TGFB3. Remarkably, IL‐13 emerged as a pivotal driver of progressive fibrosis and has been found to be elevated in patients with keloid scars. 45 ,  46 ,  47 ,  48  Existing reports indicate that IL‐13 induces the production of anti‐apoptotic proteins and directly enhances myofibroblast function, further implicating its role in fibrotic processes. 48  Additionally, the TGF‐β superfamily, well‐established as an important mediator of tissue repair. 47  The sustained activation of the TGF‐β signalling pathway leads to prolonged and excessive activation of fibroblasts and myofibroblasts, which is necessary for the excessive collagen formation observed in abnormal scars. 49  Overexpression of TGF‐β has been linked to the initiation of HS formation. 50

  We conducted qRT‐PCR to assess the expression levels of CTSK, CTHRC1 and THBS2, confirming that all three genes were significantly upregulated in HS tissues compared to NS tissues, consistent with our initial predictions. Notably, THBS2 had not been previously studied in the context of HS and keloids, prompting us to select THBS2 for subsequent experiments. Fibroblast polarization into myofibroblasts constitutes a pivotal process in fibrosis, where αSMA, a marker of myofibroblast polarization, correlates with increased ECM synthesis capacity and scar contracture. 51 ,  52  Silencing of the THBS2 gene in HS fibroblasts notably hindered their proliferation and migration capabilities. Moreover, THBS2 silencing in HS fibroblasts attenuated the expression of fibrosis‐associated molecules, including Col1, Col3 and αSMA. Furthermore, single gene GSEA analysis indicated significant enrichment of THBS2 in the TGF‐β signalling pathway. To delve deeper into the impact of THBS2 on the TGF‐β signalling pathway, protein immunoblotting experiments were conducted, revealing the contribution of THBS2 to the functional outcome of this signalling cascade. Specifically, THBS2 appeared to modulate the TGF‐β1/Smad2/3 signalling pathway, leading to Smad2/3 phosphorylation. Collectively, these compelling findings suggest that THBS2 may play a crucial role in the formation and development of HS by activating the downstream TGF‐β1/P‐Smad2/3 signalling pathway, thereby promoting the progression of HS. These discoveries advance our understanding of the intricate molecular mechanisms underpinning HS pathogenesis and underscore THBS2 as a potential therapeutic target in the management of HS formation.

  Our study has certain limitations that should be acknowledged. First, to gain a comprehensive understanding of the integrative role of key genes in HS, further in vivo and in vitro experiments are necessary. These additional investigations will provide deeper insights into the complex mechanisms underpinning HS pathogenesis, enhancing the robustness of our findings. Second, although we successfully developed a diagnostic model for HS, its validity and reliability in real clinical settings require further rigorous validation. Extensive testing on a larger and diverse cohort of HS patients will be essential to ascertain the model's accuracy and generalizability, thus ensuring its practical applicability.


## CONCLUSION
  In this study, we employed a multi‐faceted approach, including single‐cell analysis, hdWGCNA and multiple machine learning techniques, to identify novel biomarkers of HS. Through the integration of these methodologies, we successfully developed and validated a diagnostic model using deep learning CNN. Among the biomarkers identified, THBS2, in particular, emerged as a significant factor influencing the formation and progression of HS by activating the downstream TGFβ1/P‐Smad2/3 pathway. These findings highlight the potential of THBS2 and other biomarkers as promising therapeutic targets for the management of HS.
