## Review setup
- **Input scope** Abstract only
- **Assessment boundary** Claims and evidence as presented in the abstract; no methods, figures, tables, or supplementary materials were provided
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors propose SPREd, a simulation-supervised neural network that directly predicts gene regulatory network edges from expression relationships between target genes and transcription factors, and between transcription factor pairs. The model is trained on synthetic data generated by a biophysics-inspired simulation incorporating linear and nonlinear regulatory relationships. The authors claim superior performance over GENIE3, ENNET, PORTIA, and TIGRESS on synthetic datasets with high transcription factor co-expression, robustness to small numbers of conditions, and performance significantly better than or comparable to existing methods on yeast gold standard benchmarks.
- **Visible evidence base** Abstract text only; no quantitative results, methodological details, or benchmark descriptions are provided
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Full manuscript, methods section, all figures and tables, synthetic data generation parameters, benchmark dataset specifications, statistical significance tests, and code availability

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** The abstract presents a conceptually interesting shift in GRN inference paradigm, moving from feature importance on expression prediction to direct edge prediction. The simulation-supervised training approach is a reasonable strategy given the scarcity of gold standard regulatory networks. However, the abstract lacks quantitative evidence for the central performance claims, and several methodological ambiguities prevent assessment of the approach's validity and generalizability. The claim of incorporating biophysics principles is not substantiated by any described mechanism. The work may be of interest to computational biologists, but the current evidence base is insufficient to establish the case.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Computational biologists and bioinformaticians working on gene regulatory network inference, particularly those developing or applying machine learning methods for transcriptomics data. The proposed paradigm shift from feature importance to direct edge prediction could interest method developers. Researchers studying transcriptional regulation in yeast may also find the benchmark comparisons relevant.
- **Major strengths** The conceptual novelty of directly predicting edges rather than inferring them from feature importance is a genuine departure from common practice. The use of simulation-supervised training with biophysics-inspired generative models is a principled approach to address the label scarcity problem in GRN inference. The stated robustness to small numbers of conditions addresses a practical limitation of existing methods.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **Concern ID** R1-M1
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Evidence sufficiency
  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim SPREd outperforms GENIE3, ENNET, PORTIA, and TIGRESS on synthetic datasets with high TF co-expression, and performs significantly better than or comparably to existing methods on yeast benchmarks.
  - **Evidence pointer** Abstract only; no figures, tables, or quantitative results provided
  - **Concern** The abstract makes strong comparative performance claims without reporting any numerical results, effect sizes, or statistical significance measures. No information is given on the number of datasets, the specific metrics used, or the variability of results across replicates.
  - **Why it matters** Comparative performance claims are the central contribution of the work. Without quantitative evidence, the reader cannot assess whether the observed improvements are meaningful or within noise. The claim of "significantly better" requires statistical testing that is not described.
  - **Resolution test** Provide performance metrics (e.g., AUROC, AUPRC) with confidence intervals or standard deviations across multiple synthetic and real datasets, along with statistical significance tests comparing SPREd to each baseline method.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M2
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Methodological clarity
  - **Claim pointer** The abstract states that inputs comprise expression relationships between the target gene and each TF and between pairs of TFs, and that the output includes binary labels for each TF.
  - **Evidence pointer** Abstract only; no methods section provided
  - **Concern** The input representation is described only at a high level. It is unclear how correlation and mutual information are combined, how many input features are used per TF, how the network architecture handles variable numbers of TFs across genes, and how the binary output labels are generated during training from the simulation.
  - **Why it matters** The reproducibility and generalizability of the approach depend critically on these details. Without understanding the input encoding and network architecture, it is impossible to evaluate whether the method can scale to genomes with thousands of TFs or whether the approach is applicable to non-model organisms.
  - **Resolution test** Provide a complete description of the input feature construction, neural network architecture, training procedure, and output decoding in the methods section, with a diagram of the overall pipeline.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M3
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Simulation realism and transferability
  - **Claim pointer** The authors state the model is trained using synthetic expression data generated by a biophysics-inspired simulation model that incorporates linear and non-linear TF-gene relationships and diverse GRN configurations.
  - **Evidence pointer** Abstract only; no simulation details provided
  - **Concern** The abstract does not describe the simulation parameters, the range of network sizes and topologies, the noise models, or the degree to which the simulation captures known properties of real transcriptional regulation. The transferability of a simulation-trained model to real data depends critically on the fidelity of the simulation to real biological processes.
  - **Why it matters** If the simulation does not adequately capture the complexity of real regulatory networks, including indirect effects, post-transcriptional regulation, and measurement noise, the model may learn simulation-specific artifacts that do not generalize. The claim of incorporating biophysics principles is not supported by any described mechanism.
  - **Resolution test** Describe the simulation model in detail, including the mathematical formulation of TF-gene relationships, the parameter ranges explored, and a comparison of simulated data statistics to real expression data. Provide evidence that the simulation captures relevant biological properties.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M4
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Benchmark adequacy
  - **Claim pointer** The authors evaluate SPREd on real datasets in yeast that represent gold standard benchmarks of GRN reconstruction.
  - **Evidence pointer** Abstract only; no dataset names or details provided
  - **Concern** The abstract does not specify which yeast datasets were used, how the gold standard networks were defined, or whether the evaluation protocol accounts for the known limitations of these benchmarks, such as incomplete coverage and condition-specific regulation.
  - **Why it matters** The choice of benchmark and evaluation protocol can substantially influence comparative results. Without this information, the reader cannot assess whether the reported performance reflects genuine improvement or benchmark-specific artifacts.
  - **Resolution test** Specify the yeast datasets, the gold standard network sources, the evaluation metrics, and the protocol for handling TFs not represented in the gold standard. Discuss known limitations of the benchmarks and how they affect interpretation.
- **Minor Comments**
  - **Concern ID** R1-m1
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Clarity of terminology
  - **Affected element** "Simulation-supervised" terminology
  - **Evidence pointer** Abstract, first paragraph
  - **Issue** The term "simulation-supervised" is not standard in the machine learning literature and could be confused with self-supervised or semi-supervised learning. The abstract does not clarify how this differs from standard supervised learning on synthetic data.
  - **Required correction** Define the term explicitly and clarify the distinction from other learning paradigms, or use more standard terminology such as "supervised learning on simulation-derived data."
  - **Concern ID** R1-m2
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Computational efficiency
  - **Affected element** Speed claim
  - **Evidence pointer** Abstract, final paragraph
  - **Issue** The abstract mentions "high accuracy and speed" but provides no runtime comparisons or complexity analysis relative to existing methods.
  - **Required correction** Provide runtime measurements on comparable hardware and discuss computational complexity, particularly for genome-scale applications.
  - **Concern ID** R1-m3
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Scope of evaluation
  - **Affected element** Generalizability claim
  - **Evidence pointer** Abstract, evaluation section
  - **Issue** The evaluation is limited to yeast, and the abstract does not discuss whether the approach is expected to generalize to higher eukaryotes with larger genomes, alternative splicing, or chromatin structure effects.
  - **Required correction** Add a discussion of expected generalizability and potential limitations for application to other organisms.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** R1-M1 (no quantitative evidence for performance claims), R1-M2 (insufficient methodological detail), R1-M3 (simulation fidelity not established)
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria**  
  Originality: The direct edge prediction paradigm is a genuine departure from common feature importance approaches and represents a novel contribution.  
  Scientific importance: GRN inference is a long-standing problem, and a method that robustly outperforms existing tools would be valuable. However, the importance is not yet established without quantitative evidence.  
  Interdisciplinary readership: The work bridges machine learning and systems biology, which could attract readers from both communities, but the abstract is written primarily for a computational audience.  
  Technical soundness: Cannot be assessed from the abstract alone. The simulation-based training approach is plausible, but the lack of methodological detail and quantitative results prevents evaluation.  
  Readability for nonspecialists: The abstract is reasonably clear for a computational biology audience but uses field-specific terminology without sufficient context for a broader readership.
- **Recommendation posture** Currently not established from the provided evidence. The conceptual approach is promising and warrants full review, but the abstract alone does not provide sufficient quantitative or methodological evidence to support the central claims. Supportive if technical concerns are resolved in the full manuscript.
