# Simulated pressure changes in LacI suggest a link between hydration and functional conformational changes


## Abstract
  The functions of many proteins are associated with interconversions among conformational substates. However, these substates can be difficult to measure experimentally, and determining contributions from hydration changes can be especially difficult. Here, we assessed the use of pressure perturbations to sample the substates accessible to the Escherichia coli lactose repressor protein (LacI) in various liganded forms. In the presence of DNA, the regulatory domain of LacI adopts an Open conformation that, in the absence of DNA, changes to a Closed conformation. Increasing the simulation pressure prevented the transition from an Open to a Closed conformation, in a similar manner to the binding of DNA and anti-inducer, ONPF. The results suggest the hydration of specific residues play a significant role in determining the population of different LacI substates and that simulating pressure perturbation could be useful for assessing the role of hydration changes that accompany functionally-relevant amino acid substitutions.


## Introduction
  Protein function often involves multiple ensembles of distinct protein conformations. These conformational ensembles can be perturbed by ligand binding or amino acid substitutions, and individual conformations can be isolated via experimentally determined structures. However, available experimental structures seldom (if ever) fully describe a protein’s conformational landscape and often fail to detect many of the subtle changes that can occur in the dynamics of the protein. Furthermore, many structure/function studies focus on the roles of the protein backbone and side chains, ignoring the fact that a protein’s conformational ensemble strongly depends on its environment and hydration shell. However, water plays an important role in macromolecular structure and stability,1,2 and hydration changes are likely to occur among the ensemble of available protein conformations. Indeed, water plays such a crucial role in protein function that Levy and coworkers defined it as the ‘twenty first amino acid’.3

  Many studies have shown that water plays an active role in determining the structure and dynamics of many biological systems. For example, an experimental study by Salvay and coworkers on hemoglobin suggested that excess water molecules on the protein surface stabilize the high-affinity transition state that is key to cooperative O2 binding.4 Other experimental measurements have used co-solvent to perturb the osmotic pressure and thereby detect hydration changes that occur in response to various changes in ligand binding or conformational equilibria.5,6 The promiscuous peptide binding protein OppA incorporates various numbers of water molecules in a spacious binding pocket to accommodate different peptide ligands.7,8 Finally, the observed functional changes among a series of caspase variants were found to correlate well with changes in the number of bound crystallographic waters.2 The picture emerging from such studies is that, in addition to the hydration changes expected for large changes such as protein folding and ligand binding, hydration changes may also be important for smaller interconversions between conformational substates of the same folded protein.

  In addition to cosolvent perturbation studies, pressure perturbation can be used to affect equilibria of conformational ensembles. Increasing the hydrostatic pressure shifts any equilibrium towards the state with the lowest overall volume.9,10 As we illustrate below, the volume of a particular protein conformation is intricately linked to the local distribution of water molecules. As such, pressure perturbation is a powerful technique to study changes in the hydration of macromolecules.11,12 One protein for which pressure-induced transitions have been experimentally measured is the E. coli lactose repressor protein, LacI.13–15

  LacI has been extensively studied by a variety of biophysical and biochemical techniques. Structurally, the LacI protein is a homotetramer, of which the homodimer lacking the tetramerization domain is its minimal biologically active unit.16–18 We will refer to the latter as the full-length dimer. Functionally, LacI represses transcription by binding to specific sequences of DNA, known as operator sites, thereby blocking the action of RNA polymerase.19 When transcription of downstream genes is desirable, sugar molecules such as allolactose20 and the gratuitous inducer isopropyl-D-thiogalactoside (IPTG)21 bind to an allosteric site on LacI, thereby weakening DNA binding affinity.19 Other small molecules, such as orthonitrophenyl-β-D-fucoside (ONPF), also bind to this allosteric site and enhance DNA binding.21 As such, the minimum number of states required to describe the natural function of LacI include: (i) apo protein; (ii) operator DNA bound LacI; (iii) inducer bound LacI; (iv) operator DNA + inducer bound LacI; and (v) LacI bound to nonspecific DNA, in addition to other known states of (vi) LacI + anti-inducer and (vii) LacI + anti-inducer + operator DNA.

  For several of these LacI states, crystal structures have been determined for the full-length protein or the isolated regulatory domains (see Table 1). Several NMR structures of the isolated DNA binding domains have also been obtained.22–24 The crystal structure of the full-length dimeric LacI bound to operator DNA and anti-inducer ONPF is displayed in Figure 1 (PDB ID: 1EFA).17,25 In each chain of the dimer, residues 1–49 compose the DNA binding domain that contains a helix-turn-helix motif and binds to the major groove of the DNA, as well as a short linker to the hinge helix. Residues 50–58 comprise the hinge helix, which interacts with the minor groove of the operator DNA.17 Following the hinge helix, another short linker connects the DNA binding domain to the remaining residues (62–333) which comprise the regulatory domain. The regulatory domain consists of two subdomains: the N-subdomain (residues 62–161 and 293–320) and the C-subdomain (residues 162–289 and 321–329).17 Additional C-terminal residues, which would constitute the tetramerization domain, were not present in the experiments used to generate 1EFA.

  Ligand binding to LacI triggers several known conformational changes. In the absence of DNA, the hinge helix is unfolded and the DNA binding domain can move freely relative to the regulatory domain.23,29 The anti-inducer (or inducer) molecules bind to a pocket in between the N and C subdomains. The crystal structures indicate that the relative orientations of the regulatory subdomains change depending on whether DNA, inducer, and/or anti-inducer molecules are bound.28 These subdomain orientations can be characterized as being in an “Open” conformation, as observed in 1EFA where DNA and the anti-inducer ONPF are bound, or a “Closed” conformation, as observed in apo LacI (1LBI) and in 1LBH where LacI is bound to the inducer IPTG. We prefer this nomenclature over the alternative “repressed” and “induced” terminology,25,28 as the true induced form involves both DNA and IPTG bound to LacI, for which there is no known structure.

  Water is also known to play a key role for several of the LacI complexes. Formation of the LacI-DNA complex is accompanied by changes in hydration,31,32 and changes in the water distribution in the ligand binding site appear to contribute to the differing roles of ONPF and IPTG.33 High pressure (2.2 kbar) is known to dissociate the LacI:operator DNA complex, which suggests that pressure perturbs the waters located at the interface.14 Given the highly charged nature of this interface, such interactions are perhaps not surprising. In addition, other LacI interactions appear to involve specific hydration sites. Tryptophan fluorescence studies under high (3 kbar) hydrostatic pressure suggest the dissociation of the LacI tetramer to monomer occurs with a volume change of −170 cm3/mol.13 A subsequent study with fluorescent tags suggests a tetramer to dimer transition with a volume change of −82 cm3/mol,14 consistent with that of the former transition. High pressure NMR studies of a truncated version of LacI, that only includes the regulatory domains, suggest partial unfolding and/or an alternative folded conformation at high pressures in the presence of IPTG.15

  One way to explore the role of water in LacI conformational substates in atomic detail is via molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. Previous simulation studies of the LacI protein focused on (i) the structure and dynamics of the DNA binding domain and hinge helix regions,34,35 (ii) the transition between Open and Closed states of the regulatory domains using targeted molecular dynamics,36 (iii) the effects of DNA looping,37 (iv) the mechanism of sliding of LacI along DNA,38 (v) the detailed nature of the protein-DNA interaction,39–41 and (vi) the stability of the hinge helix region.42 All were performed under ambient conditions; we know of no simulations studying the effect of increased pressure on the structure or dynamics of the LacI protein. The above experimental studies prompted our investigations into whether combining the effects of pressure with classical MD simulations of various liganded LacI conformations could be used to describe the LacI conformational ensemble with enhanced breadth (more conformations) and depth (atomic-level resolution, including water). To that end, we used the recently-developed Kirkwood-Buff theory based method for calculating residue based protein volumes,12,43 as further described below. The results indicated striking effects of pressure on the conformational equilibrium in a ligand dependent fashion that are consistent with a large body of published structural and functional literature. Thus, pressure perturbation MD simulations could be a useful tool for revealing the ensemble of conformations important to a protein’s function.


## Theory
  A system in equilibrium can be perturbed by changing temperature, pressure, pH or by adding a cosolvent.44 Here, we focus on the effects of hydrostatic pressure (p) on the conformational equilibrium between the DNA bound (Open) and DNA unbound (Closed) conformational states of the lac repressor protein at a fixed absolute temperature T. The effect of pressure on a conformational equilibrium is given by,45  1 ∂ΔGo∂pT=-RT∂lnK∂pT=ΔV  where ΔG∘,K and ΔV are the standard Gibbs free energy, equilibrium constant and corresponding volume difference between the two conformational states, respectively, and R is the gas constant. Hence, an increase in pressure favors the conformation with the smallest volume. We note that volume changes in biomolecular systems are often small. For example, reductions in volume of 25–200 cm3/mol are typically observed for protein unfolding.9 This is equivalent to the volume of 1–11 water molecules (~18 cm3/mol each). Therefore, one requires a method with high precision to determine these volume changes in a meaningful manner.

  The determination of protein volumes from a crystal structure, NMR structure, or MD generated coordinates has generally involved subjective definitions of the overall volume (see the discussion in Refs. 12 and 46). These are satisfactory for many applications, but often provide errors that are larger than the volume changes observed for different conformational transitions. Fortunately, a rigorous approach for the determination of the volume of any molecule in any solvent is provided by the Kirkwood-Buff theory of solutions,47 and is particularly appropriate for simulations performed in explicit solvent.12 In Kirkwood-Buff theory, the (pseudo) volume of an infinitely dilute protein solute (2) in a pure solvent (1) is given by,  2 V2*=-4π∫0∞g21(r)-1r2dr  and involves an integral over the solute-solvent radial distribution function, g21(r), where r is the intermolecular separation. The protein pseudo volume is related to the protein partial molar volume through V2*=V‾2-RTκT where κT is the isothermal compressibility of the solution (pure water for infinitely dilute solutes), and the overbar indicates a partial molar quantity. The exact meaning of the pseudo volume is discussed in detail elsewhere.48 The value of RTκT is ~1 cm3/mol for pure water under ambient conditions, which is negligible compared to the volume changes of interest here. Furthermore, this term will cancel when comparing differences in volumes for two protein conformations. Consequently, the distinction between pseudo volume and partial molar volume can be ignored in the present work.

  An alternative equivalent expression for the protein pseudo volume can also be obtained that avoids the integration step such that,12,43  3 V2*=<N1>0-<N1>2V1∘  where, <N1>2 is the average number of solvent molecules within a fixed local volume centered on the protein, <N1>0 is the average number of solvent molecules within the same fixed volume in pure water, and V1∘ is the molar volume of pure solvent at the corresponding temperature and pressure. The fixed local volume can be of any size or shape, but it must extend far enough away from the protein surface so that one encounters bulk waters unaffected by the presence of the protein. Hence, the more solvent molecules found within the fixed local volume, the smaller the pseudo volume of the protein appears. Clearly, in aqueous solutions, the protein pseudo volume is “measured” by the accommodation of water molecules and is therefore directly related to the degree of protein hydration. An added advantage of this approach is that one can easily determine contributions to the pseudo volume from each residue of the protein by simply assigning each water molecule to a specific amino acid based on their proximity to the nearest heavy atom of the protein.12 That is, the analogous equation for the volume of residue i is written by simply changing the index 2 in Equation 3 to index i. Full details of the method are described in our past publication.12 The approach provides an exact value for the total protein pseudo volume. The subsequent assignment of water molecules to residues is subjective but, as the results of this study illustrate, appears to be informative.

  In this study we are interested in volume differences, ΔV≡VA*-VB*, where the pseudo volumes correspond to two different conformations, A and B, of the LacI protein. Due to the subtraction, the pseudo volume difference is the same as the partial molar volume difference, because the RTκT terms cancel. Thus, for simplicity we will drop the use of “pseudo” and “partial molar” and their associated notations for the rest of the paper. It is often more convenient to think of the above volume differences in terms of the volume occupied by a corresponding number of water molecules, obtained by rearranging Equation 3 to give the dimensionless quantity V2*/V1o, where the volume of pure water is approximately 18 cm3/mol per water under ambient conditions. In this case, and assuming the difference in compressibility between each conformation is negligible, Equations 1 and 3 indicate that one can shift the standard free energy change by approximately −1.8 kJ/mol per equivalent water molecule volume decrease per 1 kbar increase in pressure. In addition to total protein changes, residue volume changes can also be obtained, ΔVi≡Vi,A*-Vi,B*.


## Methods

### Systems and Models
  The simulations performed here use initial coordinates provided by the crystal structure of the LacI dimer bound to operator DNA and the anti-inducer ONPF after removing any crystallographic waters (PDB ID: 1EFA),17,25 or the crystal structure of the LacI tetramer bound to the inducer IPTG where the DNA binding domain has been truncated (PDB ID: 1LBH).25 Several residue sidechains are missing in the 1EFA structure. Fortunately, these residues are located on the surface of the protein away from the dimer interface, and therefore the missing coordinates were generated using simple model building using the LEaP module in AMBER (and were provided to us by Paul Campitelli and S. Banu Ozkan at Arizona State University).49,50 The 1EFA structure includes a homodimer formed between chains A and B, together with an additional homodimer formed from two C chains after applying crystallographic transformations. The structures of the two homodimers are only slightly different. As the DNA and DNA binding domain associated with chain C were unresolved in the final structure, we used the homodimer formed between chains A and B when generating the initial solution configurations.

  Five different systems were generated for study: Unit cell of LacI in the presence of operator DNA and anti-inducer ONPF starting from the Open conformation. Coordinates for the simulation of the full unit cell of the 1EFA crystal structure were obtained by generating the symmetry related molecules (18 total copies) for each of the equivalent protein chains A (residues 2–329), B (residues 2–331), and C (residues 46–331) together with the DNA chains D (nucleotides 5–21) and E (nucleotides 2–18), and the corresponding 2 ONPF molecules bound to each LacI dimer. This unit cell was then solvated with water and enough sodium ions were added to neutralize the system. The final system contained 18 copies of chains A-E, 54 ONPF molecules, 738 Na+ ions and 281124 water molecules in a hexagonal unit cell of volume 11212.2 nm3. Due to the size of this system, MD trajectories were limited to 100 ns. For the unit cell simulation analysis, only the dimers formed from chains A and B were analyzed, since the simulated chain C dimers were lacking DNA, DNA binding domains, and hinge helices.Unliganded LacI dimer starting from the Open conformation. The crystal structure 1EFA was also used to provide initial coordinates for the simulation of the LacI dimer, after removing both the DNA and ONPF molecules. As described below, the DNA binding domains and hinge helices were either restrained (to mimic DNA binding) or left free to move in subsequent simulations. The final system contained 1 copy of chains A and B, 6 Na+ ions and 74255 water molecules in a rhombic dodecahedron of volume 2377.8 nm3.LacI dimer with ONPF bound starting from the Open conformation. Initial coordinates for simulations of the LacI dimer in the presence of ONPF were taken from the 1EFA structure. After removal of the DNA, the coordinates of the two equivalent protein chains A and B (residues 2–329 in each) and the corresponding 2 ONPF molecules were solvated with water and enough sodium ions were added to neutralize the system. The final system contained 1 copy of chains A and B, 2 ONPF molecules, 6 Na+ ions and 74242 water molecules in a rhombic dodecahedron of volume 2378.0 nm3.Truncated LacI dimer starting from the Closed conformation. Initial coordinates for simulating the LacI dimer with Closed conformations of the regulatory domain were taken from the 1LBH tetrameric structure after removing the inducer, IPTG. Although the full-length protein was used to generate 1LBH crystals, the structures of the DNA binding domains and hinge helix could not be resolved in the absence of DNA.25 For the current simulations, the coordinates of the two truncated protein chains A and B (residues 62–329 in each), which corresponds to residues in the regulatory domain only, were solvated with water and enough sodium ions were added to neutralize the system. The initial (helical) coordinates for these simulations were taken from residues 50–58 in the 1EFA crystal structure. The final system contained the hinge helix peptide with 1 chloride anion and 5074 waters in a volume = 157.8 nm3.

  The AMBER14SB51 force field for the protein, DNA, sodium and chloride ions, together with TIP3P water model were chosen for the simulations. The AMBER14SB force field is an improved version of AMBER99SB,52 to which adjustments have been made to the amino acid side chain and backbone parameters. Therefore, this version is recommended for protein and peptide simulations among the other AMBER force field versions.51 The partial atomic charges used for ONPF were obtained from the R.E.D. server,53 and combined with standard Lennard-Jones and bonded parameters assigned based on the appropriate atom types. The force field parameters used for the ONPF molecule are provided in the SI.53,54


### Molecular Dynamics Simulations
  The simulations were performed using GROMACS program, versions 2016 or 2016.4.55 All simulations were performed in the isothermal isobaric ensemble (NpT) at 300 K temperature, and either 1 bar, 3 or 6 kbar pressure. All protein and ONPF bonds were constrained with the LINCS algorithm,56 and water bonds and angles with the Settle algorithm.57 A time step of 2 fs was used to integrate the equations of motion with the Leap Frog algorithm.58 The particle mesh Ewald (PME)59 technique with 0.12 nm Fourier grid spacing was used to calculate the coulombic interactions. The Verlet cut-off scheme was used with a 0.9 nm cut-off distance for van der Waals and real-space electrostatic interactions. Periodic boundary conditions and the minimum image convention were applied. All the systems were first minimized for 1000 steps using the steepest descent algorithm. Then the systems were equilibrated up to 100 ps by gradually increasing the temperature (100, 200 and 300 K) with protein (and DNA) heavy atom position restraints (1000 kJ mol−1 nm−2) and Berendsen T-coupling (τT = 0.1 ps) and Berendsen isotropic p-coupling (τP = 0.5 ps). Parrinello-Rahman pressure coupling (τP = 2.5 ps),60,61 and the Nose-Hoover temperature coupling62,63 (τT = 2.0 ps) were used to maintain the constant pressure and temperature during the production simulations. Cα atom position restraints (1000 kJ mol−1 nm−2) were applied to the DNA binding domain and hinge helix residues during the production runs for some simulations (see text below). A summary of the LacI protein systems simulated here is provided in Table 2.


### Analysis of the Molecular Dynamics Trajectories
  The conformation of the LacI regulatory domains changes from an Open conformation in the presence of DNA and anti-inducer to a Closed conformation in the absence of DNA and the presence of an inducer. Two metrics were used to determine the conformation of the regulatory domains and to monitor any changes during simulations. First, an angle (θ) was defined to measure the overall movement of chain A relative to chain B. The angle between two vectors is given by cosθ=r1→⋅r2→/|r1→||r2→|. The two vectors were defined using the center of mass of each subdomain and are shown in Figure 2. The initial measured angle for the 1EFA (Open) crystal structure was 14° (using the dimer formed from chains A & B or two C chains), while for the 1LBH (Closed) structure it was 23°. Second, to further differentiate the conformational states of the LacI dimer, we took advantage of the fact that two β-strands in the N subdomains near the monomer interface moved towards each other over time. The distance, d, between the central Cα atoms (residue V96 in chain A and B) was used to follow the movement of these two strands. The corresponding measured distance for the 1EFA (Open) crystal structure was 0.84 nm in the chain A/chain B dimer (or 0.85 nm for the chain C-based dimer), whereas for the 1LBH (Closed) structure it was 0.49 nm. The same distance metric has been used in previous simulation studies.36 A summary of these two measures for a series of relevant crystal structures is provided in Table 1. Although a more formal approach, such as principal component analysis,64 could have been used, use of these two simple measures added clarity to the analysis. Furthermore, as we demonstrate in the Results, the two measures were not significantly correlated. Indeed, in many cases we observed changes in the angle resulting in no change to the distance between the two domains, and vice versa.

  Next, the water probability distributions (gi1) and ΔVi values for individual residues (i) (described in the Theory section) were calculated using the number of water molecules assigned to each residue based on their proximities.12 To determine proximity, the center of geometry of each water molecule and the heavy atoms of each residue were used. Each water molecule is assigned to only one residue, and therefore the resulting distributions are different from many traditional approaches. Normalization of the distributions was performed by dividing the actual number of water molecules observed a given distance from an individual residue by the number of water molecules observed at the same distance in bulk water. For the bulk water analysis, the coordinates of the protein at a given time were superimposed on a bulk water configuration that was synthesized by using a random number generator to generate water center of geometry coordinates according to the density of pure water.12 The number of water molecules was then counted in the same manner as for the protein solution.12 The number of local waters within 1.0 nm of each residue were used to determine the volumes according to Equation 3.


## Results

### Performance of the force field
  Biomolecular force fields used in MD simulations are not traditionally tested for accuracy under high pressure conditions. Hence, we wanted to first establish that the force field provided reasonable behavior for LacI under ambient and high-pressure situations. This is particularly important for the current study as we are focusing on the water distribution around the protein to determine the volume changes, and there are known issues with hydration in many traditional biomolecular force fields.65 Furthermore, simulation studies of small molecules at high pressures also suggest potential differences in the solvent distributions between force fields.66,67

  The performance of the force field was investigated by examining simulations of the Free LacI dimer, without DNA or ONPF, starting from the Open structure simulated at 1 bar and 3 kbar pressures. The corresponding Cα root mean square deviation (RMSD) time histories are provided in Figures S1 and S2. Both the N and C subdomains of the regulatory domain and the DNA binding domains remained stable over the course of the simulations, displaying very low RMSD values under ambient and 3 kbar pressures. The N and C subdomains were particularly stable, with RMSDs of 0.1 nm or less, in agreement with previous simulation data.37 Of the two subdomains, the C-subdomains displayed smaller RMSD values, suggesting that they are slightly more stable. This is consistent with the observations from the starting crystal structures: the C-subdomains were very similar among the available structures, whereas more changes were observed in the N-subdomain.17,25–27 The RMSDs for the DNA binding domains were slightly higher (0.2 nm) than the regulatory subdomains but remained stable under both low and high pressures. However, the hinge helices connecting the regulatory and DNA binding domains were not stable, leading to changes in the relative orientation of the domains (as discussed extensively below). These results indicate that the structures of the N and C subdomains remain stable and very similar to the crystal structure during the simulations at ambient and higher pressures using the current Amber force field. Therefore, any differences in the conformational ensembles described below must arise from changes in the juxtapositions and orientations of the various domains.


### Simulation of the Unit Cell
  The 1EFA unit cell simulation included 18 copies of the LacI dimer in the presence of DNA and the anti-inducer ONPF, plus 9 copies of a truncated LacI dimer, also bound to ONPF, for which the DNA and DNA binding domain were unresolved (and therefore not included in this simulation). The results for the angle and distance distributions describing the relative orientation of the N and C regulatory domains (defined in Figure 2), are presented in Figure 3. The simulation data suggests a range of states are available to the set of LacI dimers in the crystal structure environment. The ensemble of conformations adopted by the dimers included the original 1EFA conformation (Figure 3, state O), although the center of the ensemble was closer to that of the 1LBG crystal structure (Figure 3, state O′), which was also bound to DNA but not to ONPF. The slightly larger angle and smaller distance corresponding to the O’ state are provided in Table 1.

  The shift away from state O could have arisen from several possible sources. One possibility is that the deviations are due to small issues with the force field or system preparation (see Discussion below). Another possibility is that the free energy surface at 100 K, where the crystal structure was solved, is different from the free energy landscape at 300 K, where the simulations were performed. Thirdly, the mixed solvent system used in the crystallization conditions (2.0 M ammonium sulfate, 10% PEG 400, 0.1 M HEPES, 14% glycol, 1.5 molar excess of DNA, and 10-fold excess of ONPF)17 was not used here, but replaced with just water and counterions. Lastly, the homodimer formed from two copies of chain C did not show density for DNA or most of the residues in the DNA binding domains, and the present study did not include them. Visual inspection of the unit cell suggests the unoccupied region was sufficiently large to accommodate the missing DNA binding domains and bound DNA. In the current simulation, that volume was occupied by water, which would not restrict the biomolecule’s motions in the same way as the DNA and DNA binding domains. Thus, there may be additional experimental intermolecular interactions required for the stabilization of the Open conformation.

  In summary, due to the low RMSDs for each domain, the multiple possible sources for the drift in the unit cell simulation from state O to state O′, and the results provided below that suggest a large and shallow conformational free energy surface for LacI, we concluded that the current force field did not exhibit obvious deficiencies that would caution against a deeper interpretation of the results.


### Simulations of the LacI dimer under ambient pressure
  Next, we aimed to establish the conformational behavior of the LacI dimer in solution under ambient conditions in various liganded states. Figure 4 displays the distance and angle distributions obtained from simulations of the Free LacI dimer, in the absence of DNA and ONPF, starting from the Open conformation. Simulations of this state were performed in the presence and absence of Cα position restraints on the DNA binding domains. The time histories of these and other metrics are shown in Figure S3. Figure 4 also shows results from a corresponding simulation of the truncated LacI dimer starting from the Closed conformation.

  The presence of position restraints on the DNA binding domain residues in LacI (Figure 4, red) is sufficient to maintain the orientation of the N and C subdomains in the Open form (Figure 4, yellow ellipse). Hence, this simple approach mimics, to a reasonable degree, the presence of DNA on the conformational substates. In the absence of these restraints, the Free LacI dimer changed conformation after less than 100 ns to favor states more like the Closed conformation, in which the distance between domains is smaller and the angle of rotation between the domains is larger. This shift to Closed is in agreement with the experimental (d, θ) metrics of the apo LacI structure 1LBI (Table 1). Notably, considerable conformational heterogeneity was nonetheless observed in the simulation. Truncated LacI (Figure 4, green), which began in the Closed state (Figure 4, state C), displayed a distribution very similar to the transitioned Free LacI dimer.


### ONPF binding affects the conformational substate of LacI
  The conformation of the regulatory domain is affected by binding small molecules in the allosteric pocket between the N and C subdomains; these changes correspond to changes in DNA binding affinity. The presence of inducer IPTG in this pocket destabilizes DNA binding and the available crystal structures suggest IPTG binding also stabilizes the Closed conformation (Table 1). Therefore, we did not simulate it in this work. However, the presence of anti-inducer ONPF in this pocket stabilizes DNA binding.17,68 To examine the effects of ONPF on the ensemble of conformational substates available to the LacI dimer, we simulated full-length LacI dimer in the presence of ONPF, with and without position restraints on the DNA binding domain, starting from the Open conformation under ambient conditions. Results are shown in Figure 5.

  In the presence of position restraints (Figure 5, brown), the addition of ONPF shows little effect on the distance and angle distributions, with only a small shift towards the Open conformation (Figure 5, state O). This is consistent with ONPF favoring, and thereby stabilizing, the Open conformation of DNA-bound LacI. In the absence of position restraints, the effect of binding ONPF is larger (Figure 5, blue) with a narrower distance distribution similar to that of the Open conformation (Figure 5, yellow ellipse). Again, this suggests that ONPF-bound ensembles are shifted towards the Open conformation compared to their ONPF-unbound equivalent ensemble, thereby facilitating the experimentally known increase in DNA binding.


### Total protein and residue volume changes accompanying LacI dimer conformational changes
  In the absence of DNA and ONPF, the regulatory domains of the full-length LacI dimer change from the starting Open conformation (Figure 4, state O) to the vicinity of the Closed conformation (Figure 4, state C). Before simulating the LacI dimer under pressure, we next established the volume change associated with this transition, ΔV=VC*-VO*. The volume difference for the full-length LacI dimer (656 residues) was positive, indicating that the volume of the transitioned Closed state is larger than the volume of the starting Open conformation. The total protein volume change is small (Table 3), less than the volume of two water molecules (one TIP3P water molecule is 0.03 nm3 at 300 K and 1 bar), which is consistent with experimentally-determined volume changes associated with protein conformational transitions.9,46,69 According to Le Chatelier’s principle, these results suggest that pressure could affect the ensemble of LacI conformational substates.

  We next decomposed the total protein volume into contributions from individual residues.12 To achieve this, one must assign water molecules to specific residues. We note that the residue-level decomposition is not unique and that “residue volumes” are not thermodynamically defined; other decompositions could be considered that also sum to the same unique, rigorous, thermodynamically defined protein pseudo volume.12 However, the results of this study and our previous work suggest that the present decomposition is useful.

  The small overall protein volume change arises from extensive cancellation when summing numerous positive and negative residue volume changes. Although the volume changes for individual residues can be very small, often equivalent to a fraction of a water molecule, our method is precise enough to detect these changes (see Figure S4).25,36,70–73 The volume differences between the conformational states are provided in Table 3 (for sums over various subsets of residues) and Figure 6/Figure S4 (for select individual residues). The Figure 6 and Figure S4 data are the same and express the volume change as an equivalent number of waters, with the former figure mapping these changing onto the tertiary structure and the latter providing the quantitative values and the primary and secondary structure perspectives. These figures only show the results for those residues with large, statistically significant, volume changes (see figure legends for a description of the threshold). Several residues (Figure 6, blue) have a smaller volume in the transitioned structure, suggesting that they may have more assigned water molecules within 1 nm in the transitioned Closed structure compared to the starting Open structure. Other residues (Figure 6, red) have a larger volume in the transitioned structure. Many of the differences affect residues located in the interfaces between domains, including the hinge helix. These findings suggest that increased pressure would predominantly perturb the hydration of interfacial residues or the relative orientations of the monomers within the dimer rather than making major changes to the structure of a monomer.


### The effect of increased hydrostatic pressure on the conformation of the LacI dimer
  Finally, we investigated whether pressure changes led to the predicted changes in the distribution of LacI substates. MD simulations of the LacI dimer starting from an Open conformation were performed at 3 and 6 kbar for comparison with that at 1 bar. Simulations of the truncated LacI dimer starting from a Closed conformation were also performed. Results are presented in Figure 7.

  The effect of increasing the pressure on the LacI dimer (Figure 7, magenta and orange) was to resist the Open to Closed transition that occurred under ambient conditions (Figure 7, black). Indeed, at 6 kbar, the ensemble of protein states closely resembles that of the Open conformation (Figure 7, orange). Since pressure favors the smaller volume conformation, and the Open conformation has a smaller overall volume, these pressure effects are consistent with the simulated volume differences between the Open and Closed states.

  Intriguingly, truncated LacI simulated at 3 kbar maintained a narrow distance distribution centered around its initial configuration of state C (Figure 7, brown) with a similar angle distribution as was observed for both ambient Trunc. and transitioned Free LacI. Thus, we conclude that a major effect of pressure on the full-length LacI is likely due to changes in hydration of the hinge helix region and/or the contacts between the DNA binding domain and the regulatory domain. Of these, the hinge helix region displayed the largest predicted change in hydration (see Figure 6).

  We also studied the effects of pressure on the conformation of the full-length LacI dimer in the presence of the anti-inducer, ONPF. Results are shown in Figure 8 and are similar to the effects of pressure in the absence of ONPF (Figure 7). Indeed, the combination of 3 kbar pressure and ONPF was very effective at stabilizing the LacI dimer in the Open conformation. In the absence of ONPF, this ensemble was only populated at 6 kbar pressure (Figure 7).

  In summary, the full-length LacI dimer appears to adopt a variety of conformational substates. The population of the substates is affected by the presence of ONPF and by increases in pressure. A summary of the conformational changes is provided in Figure 9, and selected distances between residues in different domains are provided in Figure S3. In addition to changes in the juxtapositions of the N and C subdomains, the juxtapositions of the DNA binding domains to the regulatory domain also change. Under ambient conditions (Figure 4), the DNA binding domains move closer together and away from the regulatory domain (Figure 9, 2nd row, red). In contrast, at higher pressure the DNA binding domains tend to move closer to the regulatory domain (Figure 9, 3rd and bottom rows, red) at 3kbar, while the shift is to move further away at 6 kbar.


### The effect of pressure on the hinge helix conformation is context dependent
  The results above implicate the hinge helix region as playing a significant role in the pressure-induced effects on the structure of the full-length LacI dimer. Hence, we examined the behavior of the hinge helices in more detail, comparing their structures from the Free LacI dimer simulations to simulations of an isolated hinge helix in aqueous solution. Results are shown in Figure 10. At ambient pressure (Figure 10, top), the hinge helix in chain A was stable for about 500 ns and then unfolded; the hinge helix in chain B partially unfolded more quickly (~100 ns) and refolded around 400 ns, remaining in a helical conformation for the rest of the simulation. In contrast, the isolated hinge helix transitioned from an unfolded to a helical structure several times at ambient pressure. The dynamic nature of the hinge helix is in agreement with previous experimental and simulation results.23,42 Strikingly, at 3 kbar, both the hinge helices in the LacI dimer remained folded, indicating that pressure stabilized them. In contrast, the isolated hinge helix in aqueous solution was unfolded, as indicated by large RMSD values for most of the simulation. These results suggest that the most favorable conformation of the hinge helix depends on its environment, with pressure only stabilizing the hinge helix within the LacI dimer environment.


### Changes in hydration of the hinge helix residues
  As stated above, pressure stabilizes protein conformations with smaller volumes. For the subset of residues in the hinge helix region, the overall volume changes were positive (Table 3), which indicates that the volume of the Closed conformation is larger than the volume of the Open conformation. This is consistent with pressure driving the LacI conformation to the Open conformation. To examine this in more detail, we plotted the gi1 distributions (Figure S5) for all residues initially located within 0.5 nm of hinge helix residue V52, for which amino acid substitutions cause dramatic functional effects.74,75 The gi1 distributions clearly indicate that several residues (especially N50, V52 and A53) experience hydration differences between the starting Open and transitioned Closed states. The gi1 curves for the transitioned Closed conformation indicate a decrease in hydration for N50 and A53 as the DNA binding domains and hinge helices move closer together in the transitioned state. Other residues within 0.5 nm from the residue V52 do not show significant changes in hydration (e.g., R51, Q54, Q55, see Figure S5). The gi1 distributions for all residues are shown in Figure S6. Many of the residues display the same water distributions for both chains and both conformations. This suggests that these distributions are statistically reliable, and any differences observed for other residues are then related to real changes in their degree of hydration due to conformational transitions.

  Having identified N50, V52 and A53 as key residues, we then compared the time histories of the number of assigned water molecules found within a given distance from each residue, (N1)i, during the simulations at 1 bar and 3 kbar. Figure 11 was calculated by analyzing waters out to 0.5 nm to characterize how the first layer of water changes around the residues during the first 160 ns of the trajectories. This window encompasses the times used to analyze the Open (0–60ns) and Closed (60–100ns) ambient Free conformations (Figures 6 and S4). Under ambient conditions, residues N50 and A53 displayed a gradual decrease in the number of hydrating water molecules, whereas the solvation of V52 remained consistently low. This decrease coincides with a transition of the LacI dimer from the Open to Closed form. This is consistent with the general conclusions of the previous sections where we infer a less-hydrated, larger-volume, Closed form. In contrast, at higher pressures the hydration of N50 and A53 remained constant or slightly increased, corresponding to the stabilization of the Open form under pressure, with no significant changes for V52.

  In contrast to Figure 11, Figures 6 and S4 were calculated using Equation 3 and a distance of 1 nm from the surface of the protein so that the ΔVi values were converged with respect to distance. A comparison of (N1)i using 0.5 nm and 1.0 nm is shown in Figure S7. The results are qualitatively the same regardless of which distance is chosen, and nearly quantitatively the same in many cases. The near quantitative agreement is because these three residues are in an interfacial region of the protein. In contrast, a surface residue that is not near an interface (e.g., Q209) shows a large increase in the number of assigned waters 1.0 nm from the surface compared to 0.5 nm (Figure S7).


## Discussion
  The current simulations suggest that, when starting from the Open conformation of LacI, the Free protein quickly transitions to a Closed conformation under ambient conditions. The transition agrees with the crystallographic results provided in Table 1. This transition is not limited to the regulatory domains – it affects the juxtapositions of all domains and subdomains. Nonetheless, all LacI conformations at ambient pressure closely resemble one of three known crystal structures determined for differently liganded states (states O, O’, and C on Figures 3–5 and 7–8). The different conformational distributions also overlap significantly, which suggests the free energy landscape consists of several valleys separated by relatively small barriers. Notably, in the absence of DNA binding domains, this conformational landscape is not fully accessible to the regulatory domains themselves, which indicates that the domains exhibit reciprocal effects on each other. More importantly, it appears the equilibrium can be shifted back towards the Open conformation upon binding the anti-inducer ONPF and/or increasing pressure. In contrast, according to the known crystal structures (see Table 1), binding of IPTG has no effect on the conformational change that was characterized by the metrics used here. In future studies, it would be very interesting to build a full-length model of LacI bound to IPTG and determine whether this state resists the pressure-induced conformational change toward the Open conformation.


### Alternative conformational ensembles are accessed at higher pressures
  One key observation from the simulations is that increasing pressure impedes the transition of Free LacI towards the Closed state. Instead, at 3 kbar, the ensemble of conformations “overshoots” the crystal structure of the Open conformation and displays larger distances between the N-terminal domains. It would be interesting to know whether this conformation would exhibit the strong DNA binding affinities of the ONPF-bound state, or whether the conformation is too different to tightly bind operator DNA. Alternatively, this conformation could be adopted when LacI binds different DNA operator sequences. In addition to Osym of 1EFA and O1 of 1JWL (noted in Table 1), LacI also binds two additional natural operators,76–79 multiple engineered operators,75,80 and non-specific DNA.81,82 When structures were determined for non-specific DNA bound to the isolated DNA binding domains, the hinge helices displayed a large conformational change.82 Given the effects of the DNA binding domains/hinge helices on the juxtapositions of the regulatory subdomains in the current simulations (e.g., Figure 7), it would not be surprising if the hinge helix changes associated with non-specific DNA binding also alter the juxtapositions of the regulatory subdomains.

  When the pressure is raised to 6 kbar, the conformational ensemble is again centered near the Open (1EFA) structure. The pressure sensitivity indicates that the Open conformation is smaller in volume and therefore more strongly hydrated than other conformations. The residue volume changes determined here, and the behavior of the truncated form of LacI, suggest this is due to differences in the hydration of residues near the hinge helix region. This illustrates the power of the approach used here that allows one to determine residue-based volumes to probe the subtle changes in hydration that can occur during conformational transitions.


### Ambient simulations are in agreement with experimental data
  In the absence of DNA, no crystal structures of full-length LacI protein have been solved with all the domains resolved (presumably because the DNA binding domains and hinge helix regions are too dynamic). Fortunately, some information on this system is available from low resolution small angle scattering (SAXS) experiments. In the SAXS experiments, the full-length unliganded LacI (no DNA) was best modelled with unfolded hinge helices, which extended the length of the dimer by 2.0 nm relative to 1LBG (O′).29 Although the current LacI simulations do not show the extensive hinge helix unfolding that was observed for the isolated peptide (see Figure 10), other changes in the DNA binding domains and hinge helices extended the length of the major principal axis of the simulated Free LacI by ~2 nm from the beginning to the end of the simulation (see Figure S8). This extension does not correlate with the Open to Closed transition, as the major change in the extension only occurred at the very end of the simulation. A direct comparison of the simulated radius of gyration with that reported in the SAXS study was not possible because the construct used in the SAXS study has extra residues (a GCN4 dimerization domain) relative to the simulated structure.29 Intriguingly, the model built from the SAXS data also show the two DNA binding domains of a dimer move closer together, similar to what was observed here (Figure 9 and Figure S3). Unfortunately, the types of regulatory domain changes observed in the simulations are likely too small to be detected by SAXS. Thus, the simulations and the SAXS provide complementary information about the conformational transitions.

  Limited experimental evidence regarding the distribution of water around LacI is also available for comparison with our simulations. Two crystal structures, 1EFA17,25 and 2P9H,28 provide waters of solvation corresponding to the Open and Closed conformations, respectively. In the Open conformation (1EFA), no crystal waters are located in the vicinity of the hinge helices, which suggests the current analysis could be probing more subtle changes in the general hydration of LacI rather than a few very specific water binding sites. A single water is located between the two V96 residues in the Open conformation (1EFA) and is absent in the Closed conformation (2P9H). In the simulations performed under ambient conditions, the distance between the two V96 residues decreases by 0.3 nm as the regulatory domain transitions from the Open to the Closed unbound form, which is consistent with the diameter of a single water molecule. Furthermore, analysis of the final simulation configurations indicated water was indeed present between these two residues during the 6 kbar simulation, which retained the Open conformation, whilst it was absent at the end of the ambient simulation, which transitioned to the Closed conformation.

  The presence of a putative water between the side chains of V96 might provide yet another explanation why the simulation results of the full crystal unit cell of LacI with ONPF and DNA bound slightly shifted away (O to O’) from the experimental distance and angle measures (Figure 3). A deeper analysis of this simulation indicated that not all the crystal waters between the V96 pairs within the 27 dimers were retained (or exchanged). This could explain the shift to smaller V96 distances during that simulation. Subtle hydration changes are often difficult to capture and traditional biomolecular force fields are known to have problems with under-solvation,65 so this is not surprising. Furthermore, the crystal structures of LacI are relatively low resolution (Table 1) causing the nature of the electron density interpreted as a water molecule to be replaced with a chloride ion in a previous simulation study due to the proximity of two K84 residues.36 Replacing the water for a chloride in the initial configuration could certainly affect the resulting distance between the V96 residues.

  Many other possible reasons for the shift from state O were already discussed above (see Results). Further computational work would be necessary to distinguish among all these possibilities. Taken together, it is most likely an indication that the conformational landscape is quite flat and therefore small changes might be expected depending on the exact conditions simulated (see Results).


### Comparison of high pressure simulations and experimental data
  We also considered whether these simulations can give insights into previous experimental pressure-induced changes in fluorescence for tetrameric LacI.13,14 These experimental results were originally interpreted as pressure-induced subunit dissociation of the LacI tetramer. However, the tetramer to monomer (or dimer) dissociation was difficult to reconcile once the “V-shaped” dimer of dimers crystal structure was published a few years later.25,27 One possibility is that the pressure-induced changes in LacI tetramer were related to the angle of the “V” between the pair of dimers forming the tetramer, which in SAXS was shown to be highly dynamic.29 Another possibility is that the C-terminal tetramerization domains (not present in the current simulation) did indeed dissociate under pressure and adopt new juxtapositions relative to the regulatory domains, leading to the observed volume change. Either of these outcomes could also include the changes observed for the isolated dimer in the current simulations.

  The fluorescence experiments also measured the effect of IPTG binding on the volume change for the tetramer to monomer dissociation and showed that the effect of binding the inducer IPTG was much larger than expected for simple ligand binding of IPTG on tetrameric LacI.13,14 However, because we did not perform simulations with IPTG, and both apo and IPTG-bound LacI are in the Closed state (Table 1), we cannot compare to these studies. Likewise, high pressure NMR has also been used to study the effect of pressure on the dissociation of a truncated LacI dimer bound to IPTG.15 At 3 kbar, changes were observed and interpreted as partial denaturation of the regulatory domains without dissociation. The same study also indicated an alternative high pressure folded state with a small volume change of −24 cm3/mol. The current simulations also show a small pressure-related effect on the conformation of truncated LacI (Figure 7), which could match this latter observation. Since our simulations do not include IPTG, we cannot make a direct comparison with these results. Nevertheless, both experiments and simulations indicate that pressure affects LacI in multiple ways, which suggests an extensive conformational free energy surface with many minima connected by relatively small barriers.


### Volume and hydration changes
  In the previous sections, we have used the terms volume and hydration interchangeably. This seems natural given the fact that the number of waters is used to determine the volume according to Equation 3, which is exact. Alternative views of the degree of hydration, and thereby changes in hydration, are provided by the water probability distributions gi1 as shown in Figure S5 and the simple counting approach as shown in Figure 11. While only the volumes are thermodynamically relevant for understanding the effects of pressure, all three approaches provide slightly different insights into changes in hydration.

  For instance, at first glance the changes in Figures S4 and 11 may appear to contradict each other for residues V52 (no significant change in N1i but a significant change in volume) and A53 (a significant change in N1i but no significant change in volume). However, this arises from the fact that the volume calculation shown in Figure S4 (see Equation 3) includes a <N1>0 term in addition to the <N1>i term, whereas the Figure 11 data directly plots N1i. Thus, the N1i time history data for the transition provides slightly different information than the volume change for the transition. Which approach is more relevant would depend on the problem to be answered.

  Furthermore, in an effort to understand volume changes in biological systems, it is common to decompose the changes into contributions from excluded volume, electrostriction, cavities, etc.83,84 The approach used here provides quantitative information on the total volume or volume changes, but we cannot shed any light on exactly why a particular volume change occurred using the current analysis. What is clear, however, is that the volumes are determined by the number of waters displaced by the protein, irrespective of exactly why they may have been displaced, and any change in the volume must correspond to a change in the total number of displaced waters around the protein.


## Conclusions
  Simulations of the full-length LacI dimer suggest the protein can adopt different conformations of the regulatory domains that vary around the experimentally observed conformations. Under ambient conditions, the unliganded LacI dimer prefers the Closed conformation. However, the most stable conformation changes when either DNA and/or ONPF is bound and is also sensitive to the hydrostatic pressure. The latter implicates changes in solvation as a major factor in the relative free energies of these states. These observations also suggest that the protein’s conformational ensemble occupies an extensive and relatively flat free energy surface. Using a recently developed technique to determine residue volume changes between different conformations, pressure-induced changes were located to the hinge helix region. Indeed, stabilization of the regulatory domain’s Open conformation appears to be directly related to the stability of the folded hinge helices. We are encouraged by the potential for using pressure perturbation simulations to explore the conformational landscapes associated with different liganded protein states. In future studies, it will be interesting to determine whether amino acid substitution induced changes in the ensemble can also be detected with this approach.
