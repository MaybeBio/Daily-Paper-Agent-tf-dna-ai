# Lipid metabolism-associated genes serve as potential predictive biomarkers in neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy combined with immunotherapy in rectal cancer


## Highlights

## Introduction
  Colorectal cancer (CRC) is considered as one of the most common malignancies with extremely high morbidity and mortality rates with over 30%‐50% of cases being rectal cancer [1]. For locally advanced rectal cancer, neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT) followed by total mesorectal excision represents the standard of care [2]. However, patients’ responses to nCRT may differ among individuals with a ladder from no tumor regression to pathologic complete response [3]. The resistance of tumor cells to ionizing radiation often leads to treatment failure and becomes a serious and urgent challenge for rectal cancer. Despite extensive effort for exploring radiation sensitization strategies and predictive biomarker to improve therapeutic efficacy, there is still no effective way to solve this problem [4]. Therefore, the identification of new biomarkers for response prediction gains more and more concerns in therapeutic management.

  In recent years, lipid metabolism has attracted a lot of attention. Lipids are important biomolecules that are not only a vital part of the cell membrane but also provide precursors for necessary substances in the growth and differentiation pathways [5]. Fatty acids are substrates of the catabolic pathway of tumor cells, and the oxidation of fatty acids produces 2.5 times more ATP per mole than the oxidative phosphorylation of glucose per mole [6]. Therefore, tumor cells will choose fatty acid oxidation as the primary mode of energy source for cell growth, even in the presence of adequate nutrition [7]. In rapidly proliferating tumor cells, lipid metabolism, previously only used as a source of energy, is abnormally active and abnormal lipid metabolism becomes an important feature [8]. Previous studies on the metabolism of tumors and lipids have shown that in tumor cells, in order to generate membranes and various signaling molecules, there is a dependence on a greater supply of fatty acids [9]. Numerous studies have shown that tumor cells contain more lipid droplets and lipid droplet-related proteins, such as ATGL, perilipin 1 and ADRP, than surrounding normal cells [10]. Lipid droplets in these tumors and lipid droplet-associated proteins may be new tumor biomarkers; thus, blocking the formation and utilization of lipid droplets is expected to be a new strategy for tumor suppression [11]. Moreover, preliminary studies have revealed the potential use of lipid metabolism in improving radiotherapy efficacy [12]. However, whether abnormal lipid deposition in tumor cells and abnormal lipid metabolism can affect tumor radiosensitivity is still unknown and is an important scientific question to be explored.

  The tumor microenvironment (TME), which comprises a series of infiltrating cells, such as neoplastic cells, immune cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, plays a crucial role in the response to immunotherapy [13]. Accumulating evidence indicates the active role of lipid metabolism in shaping the TME [14]. Lipids in the TME are activated and utilized as a major source of energy and key regulator of tumor cells and associated immune cells [15]. Reprogramming lipid metabolism can prevent effector T cell senescence and improve tumor immunotherapy [16]. Targeting lipid-metabolic pathways may provide a potential therapeutic target for tumor immunotherapy [17]. Nevertheless, the specific role of lipid metabolic reprogramming in the tumor immune response remains to be clarified. Lipid metabolism-associated genes (LMAGs) are important in rectal cancer and related pathologies [18]. These genes are involved in the regulation and modulation of lipid metabolism, which has an important impact on the development and treatment of rectal cancer. It has been found that there are differences in the expression of lipid metabolism-related genes in rectal cancer tissues, and these differences are closely related to the pathological features and clinical prognosis of rectal cancer. By studying the expression and function of these genes, we can better understand the development and prognosis of rectal cancer and provide new directions for individualized treatment and immunotherapy.

  To sum up, the specific tumorigenic effects and underlying mechanisms of lipid metabolism involved in influencing the response to nCRT of rectal cancer are still unknown and the correlations between lipid metabolism and immunotherapy response as well as the potential clinical prognosis of rectal cancer has not been investigated. Thus, elucidating the underlying lipid metabolism-related mechanisms involved in nCRT and immunotherapy is critical for clinical decision making and improve patient survival.

  To more fully understand the role of lipid metabolism in rectal cancer, we performed an integrated bioinformatics analysis to identify key lipid metabolism-related genes for predicting the response to nCRT. Firstly, we performed differential analysis on the nCRT microarray data of rectal cancer in the GEO database to screen differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from the tumor samples of responders and non-responders to nCRT. Next, we collected LMAGs and intersected them with the above DEGs to obtain differential LMAGs. We further performed functional enrichment analysis of the differential LMAGs to obtain their related functions. Moreover, we applied multiple machine learning methods to obtain candidate LMAGs. We then used receiver operating characteristics (ROC) curves analysis to assess the accuracy of candidate LMAGs in predicting the efficacy of nCRT for rectal cancer. Afterwards, we performed further analyses of the top-ranked LMAGs, including the expression patterns, prognostic value, gene alterations, and immune cell infiltrations. In addition, we constructed an effective nomogram to predict the prognosis based on the top-ranked LMAGs and other clinically relevant factors. ## Materials and methods

### Data collection and data processing
  The LMAGs were collected from Molecular Signature Database (MSigDB) with the following search keywords including “lipid”, “lipids”, “phospholipid”, “fatty acid”, and “glycerophospholipid” in the word banks including hallmark, KEGG and Reactome [19]. The gene expression dataset GSE123390 was retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, which contained 28 patients with rectal cancer who had approved to receive nCRT including 11 responders and 17 non-responders [20]. Global gene expression was detected through the Affymetrix Human Transcriptome Array 2.0. RNA-seq expression data of rectal cancer and corresponding clinical follow-up data were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database, containing 162 patients with rectal cancer [21]. The expression data of top-ranked LMAGs in normal tissues were obtained from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) database [22]. The expression data of top-ranked LMAGs involved each tumor cell line were collected from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) database [23].


### Differential expression analysis of LMAGs
  We applied R package “LIMMA” to identify the DEGs between the responders and non-responders to nCRT in rectal cancer. P-value<0.05 and |logFC| > 0.58 was used as the threshold. The volcano plot was plotted to indicate the differential expression of DEGs. Then, we intersected the LMAGs with DEGs to obtain differential LMAGs.


### Functional correlation analysis of LMAGs
  The gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were conducted with the differential LMAGs using the R package “ClusterProfiler” and visualized in bubble plots with the “ggplot2” R package [24,25]. For GO analysis, biological process (BP), molecular function (MF) and cell component (CC) ontologies were explored.


### Screening candidate LMAGs based on multiple machine learning algorithms
  We then identified candidate LMAGs based on multiple machine learning algorithms including support vector machine-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE), random forest (RF) and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression [26]. During the screening process of machine learning algorithms for LMAGs, the R packages “e1071”, “caret”, “randomForest”, “gmlnet”, and “MASS” were employed. The data visualization was later used R packages such as “ggplot2” and “VennDiagram”. The LMAGs identified by all the three prediction algorithms were identified as candidate LMAGs. ROC analysis was further performed to examine the biomarker value of candidate LMAGs in predicting the response to nCRT.


### Expression analysis of candidate LMAGs
  The GTEx database and CCLE database were applied to analyze the expressions of candidate LMAGs in normal tissues and tumor cell lines, respectively. In addition, the expression of candidate LMAGs in tumor tissues was investigated by using the TCGA database. Furthermore, the expression difference of candidate LMAGs between tumor and normal tissues was evaluated using the Kruskal-Wallis test by combined analysis of TCGA and GTEx databases.


### Survival analysis of candidate LMAGs
  To indicate the prognostic value of candidate LMAGs in patient's prognosis in rectal cancer, survival analysis of overall survival (OS) was performed by log-rank test based on TCGA-rectal cancer dataset. The time-dependent ROC analysis was applied to evaluate the predictive accuracy of candidate LMAGs. Afterwards, univariate and multivariate cox regression analysis were performed to identify independent prognostic factors. In addition, a predictive nomogram for predicting the 1-year, 2-year and 3-year OS of rectal cancer was established by integrating the expression of candidate LMAGs and other prognostic factors [27]. The concordance index (C-index) was employed to assess the accuracy of the constructive nomogram.


### Co-expression genes analysis of candidate LMAGs
  We applied the LinkedOmics database to identify the co-expression genes of candidate LMAGs by using the TCGA-rectal cancer cohort [28]. The Pearson correlation coefficient was employed to test the results of co-expression while volcano plots and heatmaps were used to visualize these results. Afterwards, survival maps were generated to assess the associations between top 50 genes most positively and negatively related to candidate LMAGs and OS/disease-free survival (DFS) in rectal cancer.


### PPI analysis and functional enrichment analysis of candidate LMAGs
  The protein–protein interaction (PPI) of candidate LMAGs binding proteins was conducted via the STRING website [29]. The top 50 binding proteins were retrieved for further analysis. Then, we performed functional enrichment analysis with the interacting genes of the candidate lipid LMAGs including GO and KEGG based on the R package “ClusterProfiler”. The most significant network module of the PPI network was identified for further analysis. Survival maps was plotted to indicate the association between module genes expression and OS/DFS in rectal cancer. Additionally, the correlation analyses between the candidate LMAGs and the module genes in rectal cancer were tested based on Spearman's correlation analysis and visualized by scatter plot.


### Genetic alterations analysis of candidate LMAGs
  The somatic mutation landscape of candidate LMAGs in TCGA-rectal cancer samples was accomplished by using the R package “maftools”. The differences between the high- and low-risk groups regarding somatic mutation data were evaluated and displayed as waterfall charts.


### Correlation analysis of candidate LMAGs and immune cell infiltration
  The Tumor and Immune System Interaction Data Base (TISIDB) is a powerful online platform that integrates multiple types of data resources in oncoimmunology between tumor and the immune system [30]. To further elucidate the immune correlates of candidate LMAGs in multiple cancer types including rectal cancer, TISIDB tool was used to reveal the landscape of correlation between the candidate LMAGs expression and 28 tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs). The correlations between candidate LMAGs and TILs were measured by Spearman's test.


### Correlation analysis of candidate LMAGs and immune related genes
  We further explored the associations of candidate LMAGs and immune related genes including immune regulators, chemokines, chemokine receptors and MHC genes by Spearman's correlation analysis. The relevant immune data was collected from the TISIDB database based on “immunomodulator” and “chemokine” module. The appropriate candidates with P-value <0.05 of Spearman's correlation was presented as scatter plots.


### Immunotherapeutic response prediction of candidate LMAGs
  The relationship between candidate LMAGs and immunotherapy response was investigated using the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) database. TIDE database is a comprehensive platform for exploring and analyzing the potential of tumor immune escape from the gene expression profiles of cancer samples, providing valuable insights for cancer research and precision medicine [31].


## Results

### Identification of differential LMAGs
  We first collected LMAGs from the MSigDB database. In total, 759 LMAGs were selected. Next, we performed a differential expression analysis of GSE123390 from GEO database to identify DEGs between nCRT responders and non‑responders of rectal cancer. Based on the aforementioned cut-off criteria, a total of 1293 upregulated DEGs and 1176 downregulated DEGs were identified in tumor samples of nCRT responders and non-responders (Fig. 2a). Then, intersection analysis of LMAGs and the DEGs in GSE123390 dataset screened a total of 45 differential LMAGs, of which 27 were down-regulated and 18 were up-regulated (Fig. 2b). ### Functional analyses of differential LMAGs
  We then conducted functional enrichment including GO and KEGG analysis to investigate the underlying mechanisms involved in nCRT response. GO enrichment analysis was performed in BP, CC, and MF ontologies. With respect to BP ontologies, the differential LMAGs were all primarily enriched in metabolic processes such as fatty acid metabolic process, steroid metabolic process, lipid catabolic process, cholesterol metabolic process and cellular lipid catabolic process (Fig. 2d). Regarding the enriched CC ontologies, the differential LMAGs were all mainly clustered in some key cell structures including nuclear outer membrane, lipid droplet, and intrinsic component of organelle membrane (Fig. 2e). In addition, with regard to MF ontologies, the differential LMAGs were all highly involved in enzymes activity containing phospholipase activity, phospholipase A1 activity, lipase activity, and intramolecular oxidoreductase activity (Fig. 2f).

  KEGG enrichment indicated that the LMAGs may play a crucial role in metabolic pathways and several important tumor-associated pathways including PPAR signaling pathway, arachidonic acid metabolism, steroid biosynthesis, VEGF signaling pathway, glycerophospholipid metabolism, sphingolipid metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, regulation of lipolysis in adipocytes, fatty acid biosynthesis, and inositol phosphate metabolism (Fig. 2g).

### Prognostic value of candidate LMAGs in rectal cancer
  The predictive efficacy of candidate LMAGs in rectal cancer was evaluated using a risk model. The survival score distribution, and the expression heat-map were also plotted (Fig. 5a). Kaplan–Meier analysis confirmed that rectal cancer patients in the high-risk group had significantly longer OS than those in the low-risk group (HR:0.336, 95 % CI: 0.134–0.843, log-rank P-value = 0.02, Fig. 5b).   To better forecast the prognosis of rectal cancer patients, a nomogram was constructed by integrating SREBF2 expression and independent clinical factors from multivariate Cox regression including age and N stage (Fig. 6c). ### The regulatory roles of candidate LMAGs in immune regulation
  Since a close association between candidate LMAGs and TILs was found in the immune infiltration analysis, we further investigated the regulatory roles of SREBF2 in immune regulation including immune regulators, chemokines, chemokine receptors and MHC genes. The results indicated that SREBF2 was significantly associated with a series of immune-inhibitors and immune-stimulators (Fig. 12a–b). ## Discussion
  It is of great clinical significance to search specific biomarkers for predicting response to nCRT in rectal cancer. Lipid metabolism is one of the most important metabolic pathways in human body and is essential for the maintenance of the tumor microenvironment. A growing number of studies have shown a direct relationship between lipid metabolism and development of tumors. Recently, there is growing evidence that metabolic reprogramming plays a role in the promotion of radiation resistance in several cancer types. The presence of lipid deposits and abnormal lipid metabolism in the tumor tissue of rectal cancer may be a key factor in the radio-resistance of the tumor and thus affect the response to nCRT. However, how abnormal lipid deposition in tumor cells and abnormal lipid metabolism affect tumor radiosensitivity is unclear and is an important scientific question that needs to be explored. In this study, we applied an integrated bioinformatics analysis involving multiple machine learning methods to identify potential LMAGs. We have also verified the function and underlying mechanisms of the candidate LMAGs in a number of bioinformatic ways.

  To our knowledge, this was the first study to identify lipid metabolism-related biomarkers for nCRT response in rectal cancer. We identified 45 differential LMAGs that were significantly differentially expressed between nCRT responders and non‑responders. GO analysis revealed that the differential LMAGs were highly related to metabolic processes at the BP level, strongly associated with key cell structures at the CC level, and significantly enriched in metabolism-related enzymes at the MF level. Consistent with the GO analysis, the KEGG analysis revealed that the differential LMAGs were enriched in metabolic pathways and tumor-associated pathways. For example, the PPAR signaling pathway plays an important role in lipid synthesis and metabolism, and is responsible for regulating fatty acid oxidation levels and energy balance in the body [32]. Arachidonic acid metabolism is a major component that participate in the initial inflammatory responses and is highly correlated with the radiosensitivity of cancer cells [33]. Targeting the enzymes involved in arachidonic acid metabolism to improve radiotherapy might be a potential radiosensitizing strategy for success of cancer therapy [34]. Fatty acid biosynthesis and metabolism has emerged as a major regulator of tumorigenesis and disease progression as well as treatment resistance through enhanced lipid synthesis, storage and catabolism [35]. Other enriched metabolic pathways also play a central role involved in tumor microenvironment and may influence the radiation response and innate immune responses [36]. These results suggested that the differential LMAGs between nCRT responders and non‑responders in rectal cancer were highly involved in important lipid metabolism processes.

  Machine learning algorithms have become powerful tools for exploring the relationships inherent in high-dimensional data and setting optimal parameters for gene selection in biologically significant pivotal genes [37]. SVM-RFE is a classical machine learning approach based on a recursive feature elimination strategy that selects significant genes through training a support vector machine model [38]. LASSO regression is a reliable method that determines the variable by finding λ when the classification error is the smallest [39]. RF is an algorithm that uses an ensemble decision tree to draw random subsets from the data and perform substitutions [40]. These three algorithms are principally applied to screen the most specific LMAGs of the differential LMAGs in order to improve the accurate identification of nCRT response-related predictive biomarkers in rectal cancer. Overlapping the results from three machine learning algorithms, ten LMAGs were identified as candidate LMAGs for predicting the response to nCRT in rectal cancer. ROC analysis further demonstrated the high predictive power of each LMAG in discriminating nCRT responders from non-responders in rectal cancer.

  Among the ten candidate LMAGs, SREBF2 was identified as the central LMAGs with the highest diagnostic efficiency when used in predicting the response to nCRT in rectal cancer. SREBF2 (sterol regulatory element-binding factor 2), also known as SREBP2, is a nuclear transcription factor that participated in the modulation of the lipid homeostasis by regulating its target genes, which are crucial for the cholesterol and fatty acid metabolism [41]. Moreover, recent new evidence has revealed the potential participation of SREBP2 in immunometabolism, fibrometabolism and mitochondrial function [42]. However, the specific function of SREBF2 has not been determined. In order to better understand the regulatory mechanism in rectal cancer, we carried out a systematic analysis of SREBF2, including the expression patterns, prognostic value, genetic alterations, and immune cell infiltrations.

  The expression patterns and prognostic value of SREBF2 were first revealed. In our study, SREBF2 showed significantly high expression in rectal cancer based on TCGA data. Survival analysis showed that up-regulation of SREBF2 was associated with favorable prognosis in rectal cancer patients. The time-dependent ROC curve analysis revealed moderate predictive power of SREBF2 in forecasting the OS of rectal cancer patients. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses confirmed that SREBF2 can be used as an independent predictor of prognosis in patients with rectal cancer. Furthermore, we developed an integrative nomogram by enrolling SREBF2 expression and other independent clinical factors. The nomogram was validated as an effective forecasting tool for the survival of rectal cancer patients. These results suggested that SREBF2 may serve as a promising prognostic biomarker for rectal cancer patients.

  It is well established that a specific gene does not act in isolation in the tumor microenvironment, but in conjunction with its interacting genes through specific signaling pathways [43,44]. To understand the underlying biological mechanisms of SREBF2, we identified the co-expressed genes and interacted genes of SREBF2. The results revealed that SREBF2 had synergistic effect with its co-expressed genes in the prognosis of rectal cancer. Moreover, the GO analysis of SREBF2-interacted genes indicated that they were significantly enriched in metabolism-related biological processes, cell components, and molecular functions. Consistent with the above results, we obtained similar results in the KEGG pathway analysis, further probing that SREBF2 was highly involved in metabolism-related pathways such as PPAR signaling pathway, steroid biosynthesis, valine, leucine and isoleucine degradation, cholesterol metabolism, butanoate metabolism, cortisol synthesis and secretion, fatty acid degradation, fatty acid metabolism, fat digestion and absorption, lipid and atherosclerosis, fatty acid elongation, glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, tryptophan metabolism, and pyruvate metabolism .

  As a new approach to the treatment of tumors, immunotherapy has developed rapidly in recent years and has been applied to the clinical treatment of a variety of tumors with promising results [45]. Preclinical studies have shown that radiotherapy can induce the release of tumor neoantigens, promote the activation of anti-tumor T cells and the accumulation of TILs, and cause immunogenic death of tumor cells [46]. The combination of radiotherapy with immunotherapy can modulate the tumor microenvironment and relieve the immunosuppressive effect of PD-L1 antibody, while enhancing the secretion of T cell-derived anti-tumor cytokines, increasing the killing effect on tumor cells and enhancing the efficacy of radiotherapy [47]. In recent years, nCRT combined with immunotherapy for rectal cancer has opened a new era for anal preservation of rectal cancer [48]. In this study, we found that SREBF2 was highly associated with the immune infiltration level of multiple TILs in rectal cancer. To further explore the underlying immune-related mechanisms of SREBF2 involved in immunity, we analyzed the relationships between SREBF2 and immune-related proteins. The results indicated that SREBF2 was strongly linked with multiple immune regulators, chemokines, chemokine receptors and MHC genes. Interestingly, SREBF2 was negatively associated with CD274 (PD-L1) and PDCD1L2 (PD-L2), which are vital immune-checkpoint inhibitors during anti-tumor immunotherapy [49,50]. Chemokines and the receptors, as regulators of immune cell trafficking, play a vital part in the composition of the tumor microenvironment and exert pro- and anti-tumor functions [51]. MHC proteins are one of the important molecules that present antigens and activate T lymphocytes for anti-tumor immunity [52]. The above findings lead us to believe that SREBF2 may be a promising nCRT-related and immune-related biomarker, providing a new avenue for the potential application of SREBF2 in the nCRT combined with immunotherapy.

  To the best of our knowledge, there is currently no corresponding suitable dataset available for validation of the predicting power of SREBF2 in patients treated by immunotherapy. We retreated to use the TIDE framework, which is a computational framework developed to evaluate potential of tumor immune escape based on the gene expression profiles of cancer samples. As a result, SREBF2 served as potential predictors of immunotherapy in some studies (Uppaluri2020, Zhao2019 and Ruppin2021). It is worth noting that these studies were not cohorts of rectal cancers, and there were significant differences with rectal cancers, which could have seriously affected the results. Therefore, large-scale, prospective, well-designed studies focusing on the combination of NCRT and immunotherapy in rectal cancer are necessary to substantiate the predictive efficacy of the SREBF2. Notably, with the more widespread use of neoadjuvant radiotherapy combined with immunotherapy in the neoadjuvant treatment of rectal cancer, I believe we will soon have our own cohort. In the meantime, we will also see relevant cohorts from other treatment centers.

  Despite the promising findings, there were still some limitations in this study. To begin with, the relatively small sample size of GSE123390 were included in exploring candidate LMAGs, which lacked validation on a large cohort. In addition, the potential value of LMAGs in predicting survival and response to rectal cancer immunotherapy has not been verified in clinical samples of rectal cancer. Therefore, the clinical value of LMAGs involved in the reprogramming of lipid metabolism should be validated by clinical sample. Moreover, most of the findings from of the present study was explored based on bioinformatics analysis. The detailed mechanism underlying the involvement of SREBF2 in the nCRT of rectal cancer still need to be validated experimentally.


## Conclusions
  Taken together, we identified candidate LMAGs for predicting the nCRT response in rectal cancer by combining machine learning methods and other different bioinformatic mining analysis methods and then a key LMAG (SREBF2) was identified. These LMAGs were demonstrated to had high predictive power for discriminating nCRT responders from non-responders in rectal cancer. The findings also indicated that LMAGs were significantly associated with the survival outcome in rectal cancer patients and highly involved in multiple metabolism-related biological processes and pathways. In particular, the identified LMAGs were closely relevant to multiple infiltrating immune cells and various important immune regulators, chemokines, chemokine receptors and MHC genes, supporting that LMAGs may also be a potential biomarker for prediction of immunotherapy response for rectal cancer patients. However, further validation through large-scale clinical trials and mechanistic investigations is warranted to fully understand the clinical significance and underlying mechanisms of these LMAGs.
