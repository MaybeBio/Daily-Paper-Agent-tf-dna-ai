# Molecular Docking, Dynamics, and Preclinical Studies Reveal Morin Hydrate as a Potent PPARγ and Nrf2 Agonist That Mitigates Colon Inflammation


## ABSTRACT
  Peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptors (PPARs), functioning as nuclear receptors, regulate the expression of genes associated with inflammation, lipid metabolism, and glucose metabolism. The primary isotypes of PPARs are PPARα, PPARβ, and PPARγ. PPARγ is mostly expressed in adipose tissue and the colon. The activation of PPARγ modulates signaling pathways associated with metabolism and inflammation. Inflammatory bowel diseases (IBDs) include Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis (UC). Ulcerative colitis is predominantly localized to the colon. Considering PPARγ's expression profile and its role in alleviating inflammation, there exists an opportunity to explore pharmaceutical targeting in the colon to diminish inflammation. We conducted molecular docking and dynamics investigations utilizing Morin hydrate (MH), a flavonoid derived from the Moraceae family, with the cocrystal structure of PPARγ and Nrf2. They demonstrated a consistent interaction. Consequently, we conducted comprehensive preclinical studies of these interactions utilizing both in vivo and in vitro models of colon inflammation. Our findings showed that MH reduced the disease activity index, colon length shortening, and myeloperoxidase enzyme activity in mice treated with dextran sodium sulfate (DSS). MH also safeguarded colon histology by reducing proinflammatory cytokines. MH induced Nrf2 nuclear translocation, enhanced antioxidant response, and elevated Nrf2 promoter activity. MH selectively enhanced PPARγ protein expression while leaving other PPAR isotypes unaffected. HT‐29 cells, treated with tumor necrosis factor‐alpha (TNFα) as an in vitro model of colon inflammation, exhibited a reduction in proinflammatory chemokines with exposure to MH. MH also enhanced PPARγ promoter activity. These findings demonstrate that MH is a potent agonist of Nrf2 and PPARγ, resulting in reduced colon inflammation.


## Introduction
  Peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptors (PPARs), or peroxisome proliferator‐receptors, are members of the nuclear receptors responsible for regulating the expression of genes associated with inflammation, lipid, and glucose metabolism. PPARα, PPARβ, or PPARγ are the major members or isotypes of PPARs [1]. Adipose tissue, intestine, liver, kidney, brain, immune system, and muscles have elevated PPARγ expression, the widely studied subtype [2, 3, 4]. It modulates target gene transcription to control metabolic and inflammatory signaling pathways. After ligand binding and activation, PPAR directly heterodimerizes with RXRs to regulate this process. Then, both heterodimerize and bind to the target gene promoter PPAR response elements (PPREs) [5, 6]. Different ligands have been found to activate PPARγ. Studies indicate that PPARγ agonists reduce intestinal inflammation in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) by reducing proinflammatory cytokines such as TNFɑ, IL‐6, and IL‐1β, and other chemokines [7, 8]. IBD, which exists in two forms, Crohn's Disease (CD) and Ulcerative Colitis (UC), is a chronic and destructive disease affecting the gastrointestinal tract, particularly the intestinal mucosa [9]. It is characterized by a persistent inflammation and a dysregulated immune response with the infiltration of immune cells, which leads to tissue injury and damage [10]. PPARγ is expressed the most in the colon tissue, including macrophages, T‐cells, and epithelial cells [4, 11]. Activation of PPARγ diminishes the severity of IBD and dampens the inflammatory and immune responses [12, 13, 14]. Research suggests that IBD leads to reduced PPARγ receptor function. The decrease in its intestinal mucosa expression is connected to colitis progression and poor prognosis [15, 16]. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that the genetic mutation in the PPARγ is correlated with the development of IBD, suggesting that any PPARγ abnormalities could be the reason behind the chronic inflammation in IBD patients [17].

  In recent years, the epidemiology of Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD) has undergone significant changes, with a notable increase in global prevalence and disease burden. The annual incidence of IBD varies widely across regions, ranging from 10.5 to 46.14 per 100 000 in Europe, 1.37 to 1.5 per 100 000 in Asia and the Middle East, 0.21 to 3.67 per 100 000 in South America, and 7.3 to 30.2 per 100 000 in North America [18]. The main goal of IBD therapies, including biologics such as anti‐TNFα, steroids, 5‐aminosalicylic acid, and thiopurines, is to block the proinflammatory and immune signaling responses [19]. Approximately 30% of IBD patients do not respond to anti‐TNFα drugs, the gold standard IBD therapy, and others develop resistance over time. The current IBD therapeutic limitations highlight the unmet need for long‐term, safe, and effective treatments to act on different and novel targets implicated in disease development and progression [20]. Thus, the PPARγ activity derives from its anti‐inflammatory potential, making it a promising target in developing novel therapies.

  Flavonoids are natural compounds that have exhibited considerable efficacy in treating inflammatory diseases, notably IBD, indicating their potential promise for managing IBD [21, 22]. Some clinical trials have shown promising findings in their potential to address IBD by targeting inflammation, oxidative stress, and microbiome dysbiosis [21, 22, 23]. They exert a dual action in suppressing the inflammatory pathways and their downstream mediators and cytokines, while simultaneously boosting the expression of the anti‐inflammatory ones [24, 25]. Moreover, they have antioxidant properties by activating the antioxidant pathways, quenching the IBD‐associated oxidative stress in the gut [22, 25]. Interestingly, flavonoids display a favorable impact on restoring the gut microbiota balance, which is often dysregulated in IBD [22].

  Morin hydrate (MH), with a molecular weight of 302.24 g/mol and a formula of C15H10O7·H2O, is a flavonoid that has been extracted from plants in the Moraceae family, including  Maclura pomifera  and Maclura tinctoria, native to Mexico to Tropical America. It has been demonstrated to display anti‐inflammatory, antioxidant, anticancer, and antiproliferative characteristics [26, 27]. It has also exhibited protective properties against the toxicity of the anticancer drugs without altering their efficacy, so it is considered a chemoprotective agent [28]. MH's molecular mechanism mediating its action has not yet been fully elucidated. However, it has been reported that MH exhibits potent anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant properties by modulating the Nrf2/HO‐1 signaling pathway. Also, it significantly diminishes the generation of ROS and NO, induces the activity of antioxidant enzymes, and suppresses the expression of inflammatory factors [29]. Furthermore, MH also suppresses the cascade of inflammatory genes by downregulating NFκB [30].

  Although the existing literature has explored the role of MH in various experimental disease models, research on its involvement in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and the underlying molecular regulations remains limited. Recently, it has been demonstrated that MH modulates the gut microbiome to mediate beneficial effects in the DSS‐induced colitis model [31]. Docking and dynamics studies have shown stable interactions between MH, PPARγ, and Nrf2. Therefore, the current study evaluates the role of MH as an agonist of PPARγ and Nrf2 in colon inflammation.

  Growing evidence indicates that phytochemicals and medicinal plants hold substantial potential for treating gastrointestinal disorders, especially as many therapeutic drugs are derived from natural sources [32]. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the possibility of MH as a PPARγ and Nrf2 agonist, highlighting its prospects as a potential therapeutic agent for IBD.


## Materials and Methods

### Molecular Docking Methodology
  A molecular docking study was performed on the Human Kelch‐like ECH‐associated protein‐nuclear factor‐erythroid factor 2‐related factor 2 (KEAP1‐NRF2) ligand‐binding and PPAR‐γ ligand‐binding domains. The KEAP1‐NRF2 crystal structure (PDB: 4L7D) resolved at 2.25 Å in complex with activator 1VX was retrieved from the RCSB protein data bank (www.rcsb.com [accessed on 8 August 2024]) and used in docking studies. The crystal structure of the PPAR‐γ ligand‐binding domain (PDB ID: 4A4W) resolved at 2.0 Å was used in the docking studies. The bound ligands and the water molecules were removed during the docking studies. The input protein structures were first stripped off for water and ligand molecules. The hydrogen atoms were added using a Discovery Studio Visualizer Program. The resultant protein structure was optimized using the Tinker ([Verison 8.0], Jay Ponder Lab, Department of Chemistry, Washington University, Saint Louis, Missouri 63 130 USA, https://dasher.wustl.edu/tinker/ [accessed on 1 October 2021]). Program using the gradient norm of 0.05 kcal/mol. The structures of the ligands were drawn in ChemDraw Ultra (Version 8.0). Later, these structures were imported into the Avogadro Program (version 1.2.0) and optimized using the MMFF94 force field. Specifically, we used the geometry optimization criteria with 50 000 steps with the steepest descent algorithm with the default convergence criteria of 10–7 kcal/mol, and 50 000 steps with the conjugate gradient algorithm with the default convergence criteria of 10–7 kcal/mol. Here, the Avogadro program, by default, calculates Gasteiger–Marsili sigma charges. The input neutral states of ligands with zero net charge were considered during the optimization.

  In the case of KEAP1‐NRF2, the grid box was set around the center of the bound ligand having dimensions 18 × 18 × 18 Å (center_x = −23.572, center_y = 39.402, and center_z = −35.915). While in the case of PPAR‐γ, the grid box was set around the bound ligand having dimensions 18 × 18 × 18 Å (center_x = −15.164, center_y = 21.299, and center_z = −10.09). In both cases, the exhaustiveness was set to 100 to access the entire search space of the binding site. The binding free energy estimates, the docked poses, and key interactions at the binding site were analyzed from the docking results.


### Molecular Dynamics Studies
  The docked complexes of NRF2 as well as PPAR‐γ with corresponding cocrystal ligands and morin were subjected to 200 ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulations using Gromacs (version 2020) program [33] on an HPC cluster at the Bioinformatics Resources and Applications Facility (BRAF), C‐DAC, Pune, Maharastra, India. The parameters for the proteins were prepared using the CHARMM‐36 force field [34]. At the same time, the parameters for the respective ligands were obtained from the CGenFF server using the CHARMM General Force Field [35]. The complexes were solvated with TIP3P water models [36] in a dodecahedron unit cell and neutralized with appropriate sodium and chloride counter ions. All the systems were further energy minimized using the steepest descent and conjugate gradient algorithms until the energy threshold reached 100 kJ mol−1 nm−1. All the systems were later equilibrated at constant volume and temperature conditions (NVT) and then at constant volume and pressure (NPT) conditions. The NVT equilibration at the temperature conditions of 300 K was achieved with the modified Berendsen thermostat [37]. The NPT equilibration at the constant pressure conditions of 1 atm was achieved with the Berendsen barostat [38]. The final production phase 200 ns MD simulations were performed using the Parrinello‐Rahman barostat [39]. A time step of 2 fs was employed with the Leap‐Frog integrator, totaling 100 000,000 steps. Trajectory outputs were saved every 10 ps, and energy/log data were captured at the same interval. The covalent bonds were retrained with the LINCS algorithm [40], and the long‐range electrostatic energies were measured with a cut‐off of 1.2 nm with the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method [41]. The equilibrated position of the respective protein and ligand obtained from the NPT state was used for the RMSD and RMSF calculations. The trajectories were analyzed for the root mean square deviations (RMSD) in backbone atoms, ligand atoms, root mean square fluctuations (RMSF), hydrogen bonds, radius of gyration (Rg), and solvent accessible surface area analysis (SASA). The hydrogen bond analysis used the default distance cut‐off of 0.35 nm and donor‐hydrogen‐acceptor (D‐H‐A) angle less than 30°. During the visualization of hydrogen bonds in the selected trajectories in the PyMOL program, the same distance and angle cut‐off were employed. For SASA calculations, a probe radius of 1.4 Å was used, and buried SASA was calculated using the equation below. B−SASAnm2=0.5SASAprotein+SASALigand−SASAprotein−ligand complex

  Where B‐SASA is buried in a solvent‐accessible surface area shared between protein and ligand, SASAprotein, SASAligand, and SASAprotein‐ligand complex are the total solvent‐accessible surface areas of respective proteins, ligands, and protein‐ligand complexes. We used the known inhibitors as positive controls to compare binding free energies in the docking studies. Specifically, we used the cocrystal ligand, 1VX, in the case of Keap1‐NRF2 protein and the cocrystal ligand, amorfrutin B, in the case of PPAR‐γ. The docking protocol was validated, where the RMSD between the cocrystal pose and the docked pose was below 2 Å. The RMSD between the cocrystal pose and the docked pose was calculated in the PyMOL program.

Kits of IL‐6, IL‐1β, TNF‐α and IL‐17A ELISA were obtained from R&D systems (Minneapolis, MN, USA). The reverse transcription kit was purchased from Applied Biosystems (Foster City, CA, USA). Quantitative RT‐PCR primers and Evagreen 5X master mix were supplied by Macrogen Inc. (Seoul, South Korea) and Solis Bio‐Dyne (Tartu, Estonia), respectively. Hexadecyltrimethylammonium bromide (HTAB), and ortho‐dianisidine dihydrochloride (ODD) were procured from Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Thermo‐Scientific provided the phosphatase and protease inhibitor cocktail (Rockford, IL, USA). Antibodies were supplied by both Thermo‐Scientific (Rockford, IL, USA) and Santacruz Biotechnology (Dallas, TX, USA). Catalog numbers were provided in this publication [42]. The commercially available pNL1.3 and pNL1.3 CMV vectors were obtained from Promega (Cat# N1021 & N1101, Madison, WI, USA), while the PPRE‐pNL1.3 plasmid was supplied by Addgene (Cat#84394, Watertown, MA, USA). The HT‐29 colorectal adenocarcinoma cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA). The suppliers indicated in this paper [42] provided the remaining reagents.


### Animals
  Twelve‐week‐old C57BL/6J mice weighing 25–30 g was provided by the Central Animal Facility, CMHS, UAE University. A week before the start of each experiment, three (3) animals were retained per cage for acclimatization. These animals were housed in polycarbonate cages (Cat # 114B, dimension 331 × 159 × 132 mm, Techniplast, Italy) Animals were housed in a 12‐h light and dark cycle, a temperature of 23°C ± 1°C, and a humidity of 50%–60%. Food (Standard rodent feed supplied by National Feed Flour Production and Marketing LLC, Abu Dhabi, UAE) and water were provided ad libitum. The animal experiment followed Institutional Guidelines for Animal Research and Ethical Regulations (IACUC). This study was approved by The UAEU Institutional Animal Ethical Committee (ERA_2023_3012).


### Experimental Design
  A total of 40 mice were randomly assigned to five groups, eight mice each. I: Untreated control group. Group II: Only DSS to induce colitis. Fresh 2% DSS solutions were made every morning in autoclaved drinking water. Group III: DSS + 25 mg/kg/day of MH. Group IV: DSS + 50 mg/kg body/day of MH. The MH doses were selected based on prior studies [43, 44, 45]. Group V: DSS + 50 mg/kg body weight/day of Sulfasalazine. 2% DSS was administered in the drinking water throughout the study period. On the 8th day of the treatment regimen, a pentobarbital overdose (100 mg/kg) was used to euthanize the animals. The length of the colon, including the caecum, was measured to calculate the Disease Activity Index (DAI) after it was surgically removed. Later, feces were cleared from the colons using ice‐cold saline. Then, the colon was scraped to collect the mucosa, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and maintained at −80°C until required. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed using 5 mm sections of colons sliced and fixed with 10% formalin.


### Evaluation of Disease Activity Index (DAI) Score
  Daily weight measurements were recorded. Also, the appearance and frequency of diarrhea, loose stools, and bleeding were noted. The DAI scores were obtained using three parameters: body weight, stool consistency, and rectal bleeding [46].


### Proinflammatory Cytokines Measurement by Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)
  The Precellys 24 tissue homogenizer (Bertin Instruments, Montigny‐le‐Bretonneux, France) was used to homogenize the colonic mucosa in homogenizing tubes containing 2 mm zirconium beads (Cat#11079124zx, Biospec, Bartlesville, OK, USA) and phosphatase and protease inhibitor cocktail tablets (Cat#A32959, Thermo‐Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA) prepared in RIPA buffer (Cat#20–188, Millipore, St. Louis, MO, USA). The resulting homogenate was centrifuged at 4°C at 1000 × g. After centrifugation, the supernatant was separated into new microcentrifuge tubes and mixed gently on a tube rotator overnight at 4°C. The homogenate was centrifuged for 30 min at 15000 × g at 4°C. The supernatant was then diluted (1:3) with RIPA buffer, and the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Cat#23225, Thermo‐Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA) was used to determine the protein concentration. Aliquots of undiluted homogenate were stored at −80°C for any further use. ELISA was performed under the manufacturer's instructions to measure TNF‐α, IL‐1β, IL‐6, and IL‐17A cytokines in the homogenates of the colonic mucosa.


### Myeloperoxidase (MPO) Assay
  Tissue MPO activity was assessed as previously reported [47]. In brief, 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 6) with 0.5% hexadecyltrimethylammonium bromide (HTAB) was used to homogenize 25 mg of freshly scraped intestinal mucosa using zirconium beads. Homogenates were exposed to three freeze–thaw cycles using liquid nitrogen and a water bath at 25°C followed by sonication for 30 s. After that, the suspensions were centrifuged for 20 min at 20 000 g at 4°C. The supernatant (0.1 mL) was combined with 2.9 mL of phosphate buffer (50 mM) (pH 6) that contained both 0.15 mM hydrogen peroxide as well as 0.53 mM o‐dianisidine hydrochloride. Then, absorbance was recorded every 15 s for 5 min at 460 nm. Findings were presented as units (U) of MPO/mg of protein. Using a Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit [Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA], the protein content of the supernatants was measured. The mean absorbance at 460 nm/incubation time/protein concentration was used to calculate the MPO activity.


### Histopathological Evaluation
  Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining was used to stain the specimens. Colon samples were collected immediately after euthanizing the mice, and tissue fragments were fixed in 10% formalin for 72 h. The samples were then dehydrated using increasing concentrations of ethanol (70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, 100%), cleared with xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Thin sections (4–5 μm) were cut and stained with hematoxylin to highlight cell nuclei, followed by eosin to stain the cytoplasm and extracellular matrix. A clinical pathologist assigned a score to each sample to assess the histopathological changes. During the histopathological analysis, samples were blinded for evaluation. The score of Inflammation = Inflammation grade X Percentage of involvement. According to a previously published study [48], the degree of colonic inflammation was measured.


### RNA Extraction and Real‐Time RT‐PCR
  RNA was extracted using the Trizol method, involving homogenization in Trizol, phase separation with chloroform, and RNA precipitation with isopropanol. The pellet was washed with 75% ethanol, dissolved in RNase‐free water, and assessed for purity and concentration using a NanoDrop before cDNA conversion and RT‐PCR analysis. The High‐Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems Catalog number 4368814) was used for cDNA synthesis. RNA samples were reverse transcribed following the manufacturer's protocol, using random primers, reverse transcriptase, and reaction buffers to ensure efficient and high‐quality cDNA conversion for qPCR. The following real time PCR run protocol settings are used: 25°C for 10 min, 37°C for 120 min, and 85°C for 5 s. cDNA was stored at −20°C for qPCR analysis. The 18S rRNA gene was chosen as the internal control for its high abundance, stability, and low variability. Its expression was validated as stable under DSS colitis by analyzing Ct values, ensuring minimal variation and reliable qPCR normalization. The delta CT approach, CT (2−ΔΔCT), was utilized to calculate the change in CT readings [49]. All the primer sequences used in this study have been previously published [50].


### Western Blot
  Precellys beads homogenizer was used to homogenize frozen colonic mucosa samples in RIPA buffer containing a cocktail of protease and phosphatase inhibitors, as reported in this publication [50]. Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Cat#23225, Thermo‐Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA) was used to determine the concentrations of undiluted homogenate proteins. 20 μg of the proteins of colon tissue and HT‐29 cells were separated using sodium dodecyl sulfate‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) through 8%–12% gels. Then the resolved proteins were transferred onto the PVDF membrane. Consequently, immunoblotting was performed where proteins transferred on the PVDF membranes were detected using certain antibodies, including PPAR‐γ, PPAR‐α, PPAR‐β/δ, NFκB p65, P‐ NFκB p65 (pSer536), HO‐1, NQO1, and nuclear and cytosolic fractions of Nrf2 and Keap‐1. We initially used GAPDH as the endogenous control for Western blotting; however, upon depletion of its stock, we switched to β‐Actin, which was readily available. Both are well‐established internal controls commonly used in Western blot analysis. Using the ImageJ program, Western blot bands were densitometrically analyzed. To calculate the optical density (OD), the pictures taken by the Azure Sapphire biomolecular imager were converted to an eight‐bit format. The peak area was quantified after selecting each band independently and using the rectangle ROI selection to circumscribe it. The graph shows the pixel intensities of the bands as measured by ImageJ.


### Measurement of Superoxide Dismutase, Catalase Enzyme Activity, and Tissue Nitrite Concentration
  The Kakkar et al. assay method [51] was used to measure the Superoxide dismutase (SOD), which is based on the 50% suppression of the synthesis of NADH–phenazine methosulphate–nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT) formazan at 520 nm. One unit of the enzyme reflects the enzyme amount required for the 50% inhibition of NBT reduction/min/mg protein. The Sinha method [52] was applied to estimate the catalase activity (CAT). The CAT activity measurements were reported as moles of H2O2 used/min/mg protein. Using the Griess reagent and the Lu et al. method [53], the concentrations of nitric oxide (NO) in colon lysate were measured. Nitrite concentration, a measure of NO formation, was determined from a NaNO2 standard curve and represented as μmoles/mg protein. The next day following sample collection, snap‐frozen colonic samples were processed for the biochemical analysis.


### HT‐29 Cells
  HT‐29 cell line was a generous gift from Prof. Samir Attoub (Department of Pharmacology and Therapeutics, CMHS, UAE University) purchased from ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA, RRID: CVCL_0320). The cell line was routinely screened and verified to be free of microbial and mycoplasma contamination. In a humidified incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2, the HT‐29 colon cancer cells were cultured in high‐glucose DMEM, supplemented with 10% (v/v) heat‐inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. The optimal MH dosages were determined by cell viability assay using a previously established method [42]. Six‐well plates were seeded with 1.5 × 105 cultured cells per well 24 h before treatment. For the induction of inflammation, HT‐29 were subjected to 1 ng/mL of TNF‐α, either with or without MH (5 and 10 μM) for 24 h. Following treatment, media were collected to analyze the chemokine's expression (IL‐8 and CXCL‐1). In addition, cells were collected using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors to quantify the PPAR‐γ protein and mRNA expression. Also, HT‐29 cells were utilized for the promotor/Nanoluciferase assay and PPARγ‐siRNA knockdown experiments.


### PPARγ and NRF2 Promoter and Nanoluciferase Assay
  Commercially available pNL 1.3 [secNluc], pNLF1‐NRF2 [CMV/neo] Vector and pKEAP1 [CMV/hyg] Vector purchased from Promega (Cat# N1021, N1391 Madison, WI, USA) and PPRE‐pNL1.3 plasmid purchased from Add gene (Cat#84394, Watertown, MA, USA). Using the ligation method, vectors and plasmids expressing luminescent NanoLuc luciferase were transformed into competent bacterial cells (JM109 and E. coli). The cells were cultured in media (125 mL) at 37°C overnight. According to the manufacturer's instructions, the plasmid was isolated using the Plasmid Purification Maxiprep kit (Cat#12162, Qiagen, Germany). As previously illustrated [42], the plasmid DNA and Lipofectamine LTX mixture was prepared in serum and antibiotic‐free DMEM media. pNL1.3 basic secreted luciferase reporter used as control. Based on the manufacturer's instructions, transfection of the HT‐29 cells was performed in 24‐well plates (Cat#3516, Corning Costar, Kennebunk, ME, USA) using Lipofectamine LTX Plus (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). 24 h later, the media containing Lipofectamine LTX Plus was discarded and substituted with 2% FBS‐media which included MH 5 and 10 μM, 10 μM GW1929 (PPARγ agonist), 10 μM GW9662 (PPARγ antagonist), 5 μM sulforaphane (NRF2 agonist), and 5 μM ML385 (NRF2 antagonist) treatment groups. 24‐ and 48‐h posttreatment, 20 μL of each well supernatant was loaded into a 96‐well black plate (Cat#237105, Thermo‐Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA). According to the manufacturer protocol, the released Nano Luc luciferase activity was assessed using the Nano‐Glo Luciferase assay buffer (Cat#N1120, Promega, Madison, WI, USA). A Tecan Multimode plate reader was used to quantify the luminescence in each well.


### siRNA‐Mediated PPARγ Knock Down
  HT‐29 cells were seeded into 24‐well plates (40 000 cells/well) and transfected with 30 nM PPAR Silencer Select siRNA (ID s10888) or 30 nM Silencer Select Negative Control siRNA (Ambion, Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) for 2 days, using the Lipofectamine LTX Plus Transfection Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Following a 48‐h transfection, the media was discarded, and the cells were subsequently treated for a further 24 h in growth media with MH (10 μM) or vehicle control followed by TNFα treatment before RNA/protein collection. After the extraction of protein and RNA, the expression knockdown was verified using western blot analysis.


### Statistical Analysis
  Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0, San Diego, CA, USA). Group data were compared using analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's post hoc test for multiple comparisons. The mRNA expression data from PPARγ siRNA‐treated samples were analyzed using a student's t test between the control and siRNA‐treated groups. Data are presented as mean ± S.E.M., with a p‐value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.


## Results

### Effect of MH Treatment on DAI, Colon Length, MPO Activity, and Spleen Weight
  Morin hydrate treatment significantly attenuated DAI and MPO activity (Figure 1a,d) in DSS‐induced colitis. It is well known that DSS administration significantly decreases colon length due to fibrotic changes. MH treatment effectively mitigated colon length shortening in DSS‐administered mice (Figure 1c). One of the hallmarks of colitis progression is the increase in spleen weight due to infiltration of immune cells. Therefore, we measured spleen weight at the end of the experiment [46]. As expected, DSS administration increased spleen weight, and MH treatment significantly decreased it, indicating the inhibition of the immune response stimulated by colitis progression (Figure 1e). However, MH treatment alone to observe whether it has any adverse consequences did not change any of the following parameters, and it was comparable to the control groups (data not shown).


### Effect of MH Treatment on Colon Histology
  Dextran sodium sulfate administration significantly alters colon microarchitecture regarding the denudation and degradation of the surface epithelium, crypt aberration, focal necrosis, and immune cell infiltration in the lamina propria. We assessed MH treatment on all these aspects of colon microarchitecture. We observed that MH treatment significantly protected surface epithelium, with less crypt aberration, focal necrosis, and immune cell infiltration with decreased inflammation scores (Figure 2a,b).


### Effect of MH Treatment on Proinflammatory Cytokines and Mediators (COX‐2 and iNOS) Protein and mRNA Expression
  Dextran sodium sulfate administration dramatically increases the proinflammatory cytokines in the colon [46]. To investigate the effect of MH on the proinflammatory cytokines in DSS‐induced colitis, a small piece of the colon from each group was homogenized, and changes in the proinflammatory cytokines were estimated at protein and mRNA levels. MH treatment significantly decreased proinflammatory cytokines (IL1β, IL6, TNFα, and IL17A) expression in DSS‐administered mice (Figure 3i–m). These results indicate MH's potent ability to mitigate inflammation induced by these cytokines at protein and mRNA levels. Previous studies have shown that the colon mucosa of IBD patients exhibited a robust expression of COX‐2 and iNOS, proinflammatory mediators [54]. Therefore, we investigated the effect of MH treatment on COX‐2, iNOS expression of protein and mRNA, and tissue nitrite levels. MH treatment significantly decreased COX‐2 and iNOS expression at both protein and mRNA levels. A decrease in iNOS expression was also associated with a significant reduction in tissue nitrite levels, indicating MH's potent ability to decrease inflammatory mediators.


### Effect of MH on Keap‐1/Nrf2 Signaling Mechanism: Molecular Docking and Dynamics Simulation Studies
  Elevated oxidative stress is associated with inflammatory disorders in the gastrointestinal tract. The nuclear factor erythroid 2‐related factor 2 (Nrf2) and its inhibitor, Kelch‐like ECH‐associated protein‐1 (Keap1), are crucial in alleviating oxidative stress. Under typical circumstances, Nrf2 and Keap1 reside in the cytoplasm; however, during stressful conditions, Nrf2 dissociates from the Keap1 regulatory protein and translocates to the nucleus. Many phytochemicals mediate its action by stimulating the Keap/Nrf2 signaling mechanism [55]. Therefore, we docked MH to the Nrf2 crystal structure of the protein and carried out dynamic studies to understand its interaction potency. MH, when docked at the binding site of Keap1‐NRF2, showed hydrogen bond interaction with Ser363 and hydrophobic interactions with Tyr572, Tyr334, Ala556, Ser602, Gly509, Gly462, and Arg415 (Table 1, Figure 4a–h). Most of these interactions were similar to those of the cocrystal ligand 1VX. To further confirm the stability of the protein‐ligand complex, the buried solvent accessible surface area (B‐SASA) was calculated. The SASA of protein‐ligand complex is shown in (Figure 4g). Higher B‐SASA indicates that more surface area of the ligand is involved in the interaction, suggesting better affinity. The results of the B‐SASA calculation are given in (Table 2). In the Keap1‐NRF2 complex with cocrystal ligand, the B‐SASA was higher than the complex with MH, where B‐SASA values were 3.52 and 2.11 nm2, respectively. The cocrystal ligand has more shared SASA, indicating more contact surface for the key interactions at the binding site. Meanwhile, MH has a slightly lower contact surface for key interactions. However, the positive values of B‐SASA suggest that both ligands can favorably interact at the binding site.

  To further characterize the effect of MH on the Keap‐1/Nrf2 signaling mechanism, colon homogenates from the experimental animals were subjected to Western blot analysis. MH treatment facilitated Nrf2 nuclear translocation but did not affect Keap‐1 (Figure 5a–c). Subsequently, we also investigated the effect of MH on the Nrf2 promoter by transfecting the Nrf2 promoter using HT‐29 cells. MH significantly stimulated the Nrf2 promoter, which was similar to the level of sulforaphane, and was used as a positive control for the comparison (Figure 5d). The molecular docking, dynamics, and experimental evidence suggest that MH is a potent stimulator of Nrf2. The Nrf2, when translocated into the nucleus, subsequently increases antioxidant response by binding to the antioxidant response element and transcribes genes responsible for hemeoxygenase‐1 (HO‐1) and NQO‐1 proteins. Therefore, we also carried out protein expression studies of HO‐1 and NQO‐1. MH treatment significantly increased HO‐1 and NQO‐1 protein expression, which was reduced upon DSS administration. We further estimated colon superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) activity to understand MH's effect on these antioxidant enzymes. MH treatment effectively increased SOD and CAT, indicating its potent antioxidant activity (Figure 5e–h).


### Effect of MH on PPAR‐γ Protein Interaction
  PPAR‐γ agonists exhibit anti‐inflammatory properties by inhibiting the production of proinflammatory cytokines and promoting the synthesis of anti‐inflammatory cytokines. Prior research has demonstrated that MH acts as a PPAR‐γ activator [56]. Nonetheless, the integration of molecular docking and dynamics research with preclinical investigations on colon inflammation has not been conducted. Therefore, we carried out MH docking and dynamic interactions with the PPAR‐γ protein crystal structure. The molecular docking simulations on PPAR‐γ complexes with the cocrystal ligand, amorfrutin B, and MH, the results of RMSD in backbone atoms showed that the RMSD was relatively stable for the complex with the cocrystal ligand with an average of around 0.15 nm (Figure 6a–i) and interacted with a few amino acids as indicated in Table 3. The B‐SASA calculations showed that the cocrystal ligand had almost twice the B‐SASA compared to the MH, where B‐SASA for cocrystal ligand and MH were 5.74 and 2.16 nm2, respectively (Figure 6g). The molecular shape of the cocrystal ligand, which is more of the component of the hydrophobic region, imparted more B‐SASA than the comparably smaller shape of MH. However, the positive B‐SASA for MH suggests favorable interactions at the binding site of PPAR‐γ (Table 4). The hydrogen bonds formed in the trajectories isolated at different time intervals showed that the cocrystal ligand formed a consistent hydrogen bond with Ser140 (Figure 6h).

  Western blot analysis was carried out to confirm MH's effect on PPAR‐γ protein. MH significantly increased the PPAR‐γ protein expression (Figure 7a). However, other PPARs, such as PPAR‐α and δ isotypes, showed no statistically significant increases (Figure 7b,c). These results confirm that MH preferentially stimulates PPAR‐γ expression. To further characterize MH's role in enhancing anti‐inflammatory activity, NFκB transcription factor protein expression was investigated. MH treatment significantly decreased phosphorylated NFκB protein expression compared to the DSS‐administered group (Figure 7d). These results indicate that the observed anti‐inflammatory effects of MH are mediated by increasing PPAR‐γ protein expression, subsequently inhibiting the phosphorylated NFκB signaling.


### Effect of MH on PPAR‐γ Gene Silencing on Proinflammatory Chemokines in TNF‐α Stimulated HT‐29 Cells and on PPAR‐γ Promoter
  To further elucidate the role of the MH‐induced anti‐inflammatory activity potentially mediated by PPAR‐γ, HT‐29 cells were employed. The HT‐29 adenocarcinoma cell line, upon differentiation, closely mimics several features of human colonic epithelial cells. It expresses brush border‐associated hydrolases and villin levels comparable to normal colonocytes, making it a suitable in vitro model for intestinal epithelium. While it lacks certain enzymes like lactase and maltase‐glucoamylase, gene expression profiling strongly resembles differentiated HT‐29 cells and native human colonic tissue [57]. The initial experiments involved investigating different doses of MH (in micromolar concentration) to ascertain the cytotoxic dose. The cell viability assay demonstrated that elevated doses of MH over extended periods (up to 48 h) exhibited cytotoxic effects (Figure 8a). Therefore, we selected 5 μM and 10 μM concentrations for further investigations. HT‐29 cells were stimulated with TNF‐α, and the mRNA expression of proinflammatory chemokines IL‐8 and CXCL‐1 was measured. TNF‐α significantly increased mRNA expression, effectively reduced by MH treatment (Figure 8b,c). Additionally, we examined the impact of MH on HT‐29 PPAR‐γ protein expression, finding that MH significantly enhanced it (Figure 8d). Our subsequent objective was to investigate the impact of MH on IL‐8 and CXCl‐1 chemokines following the inhibition of PPAR‐γ. We silenced PPAR‐γ protein expression by using the siRNA system and observed that after siRNA treatment, PPAR‐γ expression was downregulated at protein and mRNA levels (Figure 8e,f). Silencing PPAR‐γ in HT‐29 cells activated with TNFα and subsequently exposed to MH resulted in an elevation of CXCL‐1 mRNA, drastically decreasing when PPAR‐γ was not silenced. Nevertheless, we did not see a statistically significant reduction in IL‐8 chemokine mRNA expression. To further elucidate the impact of MH on PPAR‐γ, the complete PPAR‐γ promoter was transfected and subsequently treated with 10 μM MH in conjunction with a known agonist (GW1929) and a known antagonist (GW9662). MH administration markedly enhanced PPAR‐γ promoter activity in a time‐dependent manner (Figure 8i,j). These data suggest that MH facilitates anti‐inflammatory activity through PPAR‐γ, which may be regulated at the transcriptional level.


## Discussion
  The rising incidence of ulcerative colitis (UC) in the Middle East is a growing public health concern, contributing significantly to the region's economic and healthcare burdens [58]. This increase in cases of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) may be attributed to shifts in traditional dietary patterns, which have been linked to alterations in the gut microbiome—a key factor in maintaining gut health and preventing chronic inflammation [59]. Conventional treatments for UC, including corticosteroids, aminosalicylates, and immunomodulators, are often associated with significant side effects, high costs, drug resistance, and variable response rates. As a result, there is an urgent need for safe and effective alternatives. Plant‐based therapies are gaining popularity as adjuncts or alternatives to conventional treatments, with over 30% of IBD patients turning to complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) [60]. In this context, our study explores the pharmacological potential of MH, a plant‐based compound, by elucidating its molecular mechanisms of action through molecular docking and dynamic simulations using cocrystal structures of Nrf2 and PPARγ proteins. These nuclear receptors play crucial roles in regulating oxidative stress, inflammation, and cellular homeostasis—pathways that are dysregulated in UC. Our in silico findings showed that MH exhibits strong binding affinity and stable interactions with both Nrf2 and PPARγ, suggesting that it could modulate key pathways involved in antioxidant defense and anti‐inflammatory responses. This study assessed the anti‐inflammatory effects that mitigated colitis using Nrf2 and PPARγ signaling mechanism. The oral treatment of MH markedly diminished colitis severity and enhanced clinical symptoms, as seen by improved DAI score, histological, and morphometric results in a DSS‐induced colitis model. As colitis advanced, heightened immunological activity and elevated cell counts resulted in splenic enlargement in the DSS group, signifying a robust immune response. Colon shortening, an indicator of chemically induced colitis, is utilized to evaluate IBD therapies. The therapy with MH diminished spleen weight and inhibited colon shortening, underscoring its effectiveness in alleviating colon inflammation. Natural flavonoids are recognized for their potent antioxidant and medicinal characteristics. MH, a powerful flavonoid, may diminish reactive oxygen species and inflammation generated by oxidative stress in the colon, consistent with findings from earlier research [28, 61, 62].

  NRF2 modulates anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant mechanisms. Augmenting NRF2 may facilitate the treatment of numerous disorders. Typically regulated by KEAP1, NRF2 evades degradation when electrophiles impede its ubiquitination, resulting in its accumulation, enhanced transcription of antioxidant genes, and less inflammation [63]. Nrf2 activation triggers phase II detoxifying enzymes like CAT, HO‐1, and NQO1, protecting against oxidative stress [64]. We assessed MH's ability to interact with Nrf2 protein by molecular docking and dynamics simulation experiments before we undertook preclinical investigations. The cocrystal ligand, 1VX, has hydrogen bond interactions with the Keap1‐NRF2 binding site residues Arg415, Asn414, and Ser602 and hydrophobic interactions with residues Ala556 and Tyr572. The docked conformation of 1VX showed hydrogen bond interactions with the binding site residues Ser602, Arg415, and Asn414 and hydrophobic interactions such as π‐π stacking with Tyr572, π‐alkyl with Tyr334, and π‐sigma with Ala556. The results of interactions at the binding site and RMSD between the cocrystal pose and docked pose validate the docking protocol. MH, when docked at the binding site of Keap1‐NRF2, showed hydrogen bond interaction with Ser363 and hydrophobic interactions with Tyr572, Tyr334, Ala556, Ser602, Gly509, Gly462, and Arg415. The molecular dynamics study with Nrf2 showed a propensity of MH in stabilizing the structure through 200 ns simulations. The RMSD in backbone atoms of Keap1‐Nrf2 in complex with cocrystal ligand showed a stable and reasonably converged RMSD with an average of 0.08 nm. The RMSD in complex with MH also showed stable RMSD with few deviations during the 100 to 125 ns simulation period with an average of around 0.09 nm. The RMSD in ligand atoms was below 0.2 nm, where the deviations were observed between 75 to 100 ns in cocrystal ligands and between 50 to 125 ns in MH. The RMSD in backbone and ligand atoms below 0.2 nm indicate reasonably stable systems. The radius of gyration (Rg), rotation around the center of mass, and analysis provide valuable insights into the system's compactness [65]. The Keap1‐NRF2 showed stable Rg for the complex with cocrystal ligand, averaging 1.785 nm. The Rg for the complex with MH was stable until 100 ns and deviated until 175 with an overall average of around 1.795 nm. The hydrogen bond interaction, one of the nonbonded interactions, is a key interaction where the higher the number of hydrogen bonds between protein and ligand, the better the binding affinity. A prior study established the protective effects of MH against DSS‐induced colitis through its ability to diminish ROS levels and activate Nrf2, along with its downstream antioxidant enzymes, such as SOD, catalase, HO‐1, and NQO1 in carbon tetrachloride (CCl4) produced liver fibrosis (28). Our study confirms these findings along with the increase in Nrf2 promoter activity.

  Peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor, a nuclear receptor superfamily member, is among the most extensively studied ligand‐inducible transcription factors. While its role in adipocyte function is well‐established, recent evidence highlights its critical involvement in anti‐inflammatory responses, immune cell regulation, and cell proliferation [66]. In this study, we performed molecular docking and dynamic simulation analyses to evaluate whether MH exhibits stable interactions with PPARγ cocrystal structures. Our results indicated that MH stably binds to PPARγ cocrystal structure proteins. The molecular docking simulations of PPAR‐γ complexes with the cocrystal ligands amorfrutin B and MH suggested that the RMSD of backbone atoms remained relatively stable for the complex with the cocrystal ligand, averaging approximately 0.15 nm. The RMSD analysis indicates that MH may have engaged with slightly varied binding sites. The RMSF analysis suggested that the PPAR‐γ residues exhibited reduced fluctuations in the cocrystal ligand complex compared to the MH complex. The complex with MH exhibited more significant fluctuations in the residues within 50 to 75 and 220 to 240. The Rg was lower and more stable for the complex with the cocrystal ligand, averaging approximately 1.96 nm, in contrast to the slightly higher Rg observed for the complex with MH. The Rg stabilized after approximately 100 ns when competing with MH. The findings from RMSD, RMSF, and Rg analyses indicate that MH can stabilize PPAR‐γ; however, its stabilizing efficacy is considerably inferior to that of the cocrystal ligand. The analysis of hydrogen bonds indicated that the cocrystal ligand established a single consistent hydrogen bond during the simulation period, in contrast to the two hydrogen bonds formed with MH.

  Peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptors modulate gene transcription by recruiting coactivators for transactivation and corepressors for transrepression. Transrepression involves the negative regulation of genes through the retention or release of corepressors, interaction with MAPK pathways, and physical binding to transcription factors such as NF‐kB, Smad‐3, AP‐1, and STAT, thereby competing with target genes for coregulator binding [67]. A recent study by Qiu et al. published last month demonstrated that MH can reduce MAPK signaling pathways [31]. Our findings can be connected to MH's capacity to stimulate PPARγ, which may have modulated MAPK pathways.

  Dysregulated chemokine synthesis, notably the increased levels of CXCL1, is observed in the inflamed colonic mucosa of patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) [68]. Therefore, we evaluated the ability of MH to mitigate increased levels of chemokines by employing HT‐29 human adenocarcinoma cells challenged with TNFα. MH significantly prevented a further rise in the chemokines such as CXCL‐1 and IL‐8, which are upregulated due to TNFα challenge. HT‐29 cells express PPARγ [69]; we utilized this as an in vitro model to evaluate the effect of MH and its influence to mitigate increased chemokine response using the PPARγ pathway. When PPARγ was silenced, chemokine CXCL‐1 remained elevated in the presence of MH, indicating PPARγ stimulation's importance in mitigating chemokine response. MH also increased PPARγ promoter activity to suggest this may be mediated transcriptionally. These results indicate that MH is a potent Nrf2 and PPARγ activator that mitigates colon inflammation.


## Conclusion
  Molecular docking and dynamics studies confirmed strong interactions between Morin Hydrate (MH) and Nrf2 and PPARγ proteins. In vivo model using DSS‐induced colitis further validated these findings, showing that MH enhances Nrf2 activation, induces an antioxidant response, and increases Nrf2 promoter activity. Additionally, MH selectively activated PPARγ, reducing proinflammatory chemokine responses. In vitro model using TNF‐α stimulated HT‐29 cells treated with MH showed reduced proinflammatory chemokines with effects linked to PPARγ transcriptional regulation. These results suggest that MH is a promising therapeutic agent, leveraging its dual role in enhancing antioxidant defenses and modulating inflammation through Nrf2 and PPARγ pathways.
