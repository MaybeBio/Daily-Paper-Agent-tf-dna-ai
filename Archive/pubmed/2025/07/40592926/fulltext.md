# Binary curcuminoid complex CRE-Bin inhibits osteoclast differentiation by suppressing the canonical NF-κB signaling pathway and modulating microRNA-223 expression


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-04314-8.


## Introduction
  Bone homeostasis maintains the stability of the mammalian skeletal system. The balance between bone formation and resorption is essential for bone development and repair. Disruption of this balance can interfere with osteogenic signaling, leading to bone disorders and diseases1. Osteoclasts, which are bone-resorbing cells differentiated from monocyte/macrophage precursors, play key roles in maintaining this balance1. Many studies employed receptor activators of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL)-stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages as the in vitro model of osteoclasts, owing to ease of culture, broad accessibility of the cell line, uniform population of osteoclast precursors free from other cell types, and strong similarities in biomolecular characteristics with osteoclasts formed in vivo2. RANKL stimulates osteoclast differentiation by activating the canonical NF-κB signaling pathway and increasing the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS)3–5. Mature osteoclasts express specific markers, including tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) and cathepsin K (CTSK)1.

  MicroRNA (miRNA) is a short, endogenous noncoding single-stranded RNA that regulates gene expression. In its canonical biogenesis, RNA polymerase II transcribes miRNA genes into primary miRNA (pri-miRNA), which is processed by a microprocessor complex into precursor miRNA (pre-miRNA)—a single hairpin miRNA. Exportin-5 transports pre-miRNA to the cytoplasm, where Dicer cleaves it into a double-stranded miRNA, which contains miRNA-5p and -3p strands. One strand is incorporated into the Argonaute RISC component (AGO) protein, forming the microRNA-induced silencing complex (miRISC), which suppresses translation or degrades target messenger RNA (mRNA). Beyond targeting the 3′ untranslated region (UTR), miRNA can also bind exonic and 5′ UTR regions, and some mature miRNAs translocate to the nucleus to modulate transcription, including interactions with transcription factors to guide them into target sites6.

  miR-223 is a key regulator of osteoclast differentiation in RAW 264.7 macrophages (osteoclast precursors), exhibiting both promotive and inhibitory roles depending on the cellular context7. Its expression is modulated by myeloid transcription factors such as PU.1, CAAT/enhancer-binding proteins (C/EBP)-α and -β, and nuclear factor I-A (NFI-A). PU.1 and C/EBP-α enhance miR-223 transcription, promoting osteoclast and granulocyte differentiation, while NFI-A antagonizes this effect. NFI-A and C/EBP-β are direct targets of miR-223, forming negative feedback loops that further regulate its activity. Bioinformatics analysis suggests that the 3′-UTR of NFI-A is a potential miR-223-binding site, providing an additional layer of regulation in the interaction between miR-223 and NFI-A8. Experimental studies have demonstrated that miR-223 regulates NFI-A through cytoplasmic and nuclear mechanisms. In the cytoplasm, miR-223 suppresses NFI-A translation, thereby reducing the expression of transcription factors that promote cell proliferation in myeloid progenitors. This activity contributes to the differentiation of osteoclasts from their precursors. Nuclear miR-223 targets the NFI-A promoter, where it facilitates the transcriptional repression of NFI-A expression by recruiting epigenetic modifiers, such as polycomb repressive complex, which increases DNA methylation levels. Together, these dual regulatory roles enable miR-223 to finely modulate NFI-A expression, ultimately influencing osteoclast differentiation9,10.

  Modulating osteoclast maturation has been suggested as a strategy to prevent imbalances in bone remodeling. Certain chemicals and herbal compounds have shown potential in modulating osteoclastogenic pathways, thereby reducing osteoclast differentiation11,12. Curcuma longa L., commonly known as turmeric, is a rhizomatous perennial plant belonging to the ginger family, Zingiberaceae, and is cultivated in tropical and subtropical areas of the world13. Its primary bioactive components, known as curcuminoids, including curcumin (Cu), demethoxycurcumin (De), and bisdemethoxycurcumin (Bis), are phenolic compounds commonly isolated for medicinal applications13,14. Many studies have demonstrated that curcuminoids are safe14 and can suppress osteoclast differentiation11,15–19. Due to variations in the number of methoxy groups on their aromatic rings, De and Bis help stabilize Cu when all are present in a mixture. Research suggests that curcuminoid mixtures are more stable than pure curcumin, making it advantageous to prepare extracts without isolating individual components, because this approach can improve the overall stability of turmeric extract20. However, Cu—the main component of curcuminoids—has poor water solubility, high lipophilicity, low polarity, and low chemical stability. These properties limit absorption, dissolution, and oral bioavailability and contribute to rapid metabolism21. To overcome these challenges, various strategies for curcuminoid formulations have been developed. Lateh et al. used C. longa rhizomes to prepare a curcuminoid-rich extract (CRE), containing a mixture of Cu, De, and Bis. CRE was mixed with hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin to obtain a binary complex of CRE called CRE-Bin (Fig. 1), which has a higher solubility than its curcuminoid constituents22.

  Although curcuminoids show promise in inhibiting osteoclast differentiation, their molecular modulation with miR-223 and NFI-A is unexplored. To clarify this mechanism, this study investigates the effects of CRE-Bin on osteoclast differentiation, focusing on miR-223-regulated pathways. Specifically, we examined the effects of CRE-Bin on TRAP activity, CTSK expression, NFI-A, and miR-223 expression levels, bone resorption activity, total p65 expression, and ROS production in murine RANKL-stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages. In addition, we analyzed the interactions between the IκBα/p50/p65 complex, which has a major contribution to the canonical NF-κB signaling pathway, and miR-223 using molecular docking simulation and predicted the binding affinities between curcuminoids and miR-223 using an RNA–small molecule binding affinity predictor (RSAPred). By integrating these in vitro and in silico approaches, this study reveals the potential of CRE-Bin to modulate miR-223-mediated pathways, offering prospects of its therapeutic applications for bone-related disorders and diseases.


## Results

### Effects of CRE-Bin on osteoclast differentiation in vitro
  To determine the optimal RANKL incubation period for osteoclast differentiation, we first measured TRAP activity and relative CTSK expression. Both increased with RANKL incubation period. TRAP activity significantly increased from days 1 to 5 of incubation, with a large effect size (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Tables S1–S4; p values compared with day 0: day 1 = 0.0144, day 2 = 0.0028, day 3 = 0.0027, day 4 = 0.0025, and day 5 = 4.6425 × 10−4), whereas CTSK expression showed a significant increase from days 2 to 5, with a large effect size from days 1 to 5 (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Tables S5–S8; p values compared with day 0: day 1 = 0.1189, day 2 = 0.0244, day 3 = 0.0112, day 4 = 0.0038, and day 5 0.0014). These findings suggest that a minimum incubation period of 2 d with RANKL is sufficient to initiate osteoclast differentiation. However, according to Cheng et al. and Yeh et al., a 5-day RANKL incubation period is necessary to achieve full differentiation of murine RAW 264.7 macrophages into mature osteoclasts18,23. Therefore, based on our findings and those of other studies, we selected a 5-day RANKL incubation period as the optimal condition for subsequent experiments.

  We investigated the effects of CRE-Bin, along with its constituents, on osteoclast differentiation by treating RANKL-stimulated murine RAW 264.7 macrophages with 0–30 μg/mL of Cu, De, Bis, CRE, CRE-Bin. Cell viability was unchanged across all CRE-Bin treatment conditions, despite significant decreases in the cells treated with 20 μg/mL of De and 30 μg/mL of Cu, De, Bis, CRE, and CRE-Bin (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Tables S9–S23, and Supplementary Fig. S1). Consequently, 10 μg/mL of De and 20 μg/mL of Cu, Bis, CRE, and CRE-Bin were selected for cell treatment. Brightfield microscopy images of Wright–Giemsa-stained RAW 264.7 macrophages revealed no multinucleated cells following treatment with RANKL and 20 μg/mL CRE-Bin, although binucleated cells were observed (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. S2). TRAP activity and CTSK expression in RANKL-stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages significantly and dose-dependently decreased with CRE-Bin treatment at concentrations up to 20 μg/mL with a large effect size. The cells treated with 20 μg/mL of CRE-Bin had the lowest TRAP activity, compared with those treated with its constituents (Fig. 2e,f, Supplementary Tables S24–S47, and Supplementary Fig. S3; p values of TRAP activity compared with OC: Cu5 = 0.0306, Cu10 = 0.0024, Cu20 = 9.2330 × 10−4, De5 = 0.0214, De10 = 0.0040, Bis5 = 0.0779, Bis10 = 0.0051, Bis20 = 0.0079, CRE5 = 0.0298, CRE10 = 0.0020, CRE20 = 0.0030, Bin5 = 0.0239, Bin10 = 0.0072, and Bin20 = 0.0012; and p values of CTSK expression compared with OC: Bin5 = 0.0377, Bin10 = 6.9715 × 10−4, and Bin20 = 3.4895 × 10−4). CRE-Bin significantly and dose-dependently increased relative NFI-A expression with a large effect size (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Tables S48–S51; p values compared with OC: Bin5 = 0.0230, Bin10 = 0.0091, and Bin20 = 0.0045). Microscopy images from the in vitro bone pit assay showed that treatment of RANKL-stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages with 20 μg/mL CRE-Bin, its constituents, and 2 nM alendronate sodium trihydrate reduced bone pit area. CRE-Bin had the lowest percentage of the pit area, compared with its constituents (Fig. 2h,i, Supplementary Tables S52–S55, and Supplementary Figs. S4–S5; p values compared with OC: OC + PC = 0.0499, OC + Cu20 = 0.0015, OC + De10 = 0.2770, OC + Bis20 = 0.3160, OC + CRE20 = 0.0416, and OC + Bin20 = 0.1372). There was no significant difference in fluorescence intensities between the two treatment groups (Fig. 2j and Supplementary Tables S56–S59; p value = 0.3478 and Cohen’s d = − 0.3062). However, fluorescence intensities decreased significantly and dose-dependently with 5, 10, and 20 μg/mL CRE-Bin (p-values compared with OC: Bin5 = 0.0038, Bin10 = 0.0028, and Bin20 = 8.6161 × 10−4).

  To investigate the effects of CRE-Bin on these molecular pathways in osteoclasts, we analyzed total p65 expression and ROS production. Western blot analysis indicated that 20 μg/mL CRE-Bin reduced total p65 intensity (Fig. 2k and Supplementary Figs. S6–S11). ROS production decreased significantly and dose-dependently with CRE-Bin treatment up to 20 μg/mL and the effect size was large (Fig. 2l and Supplementary Tables S60–S63; p values compared with OC: Bin5 = 0.0237, Bin10 = 0.0048, and Bin20 = 0.0018).

  We measured the expression levels of pri-, pre-, and mature-miR-223 in RANKL-stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages treated with 0–20 μg/mL CRE-Bin. All three forms of miR-223 showed dose-dependent downregulation. Pri- and mature-miR-223 levels decreased significantly at all CRE-Bin concentrations, whereas pre-miR-223 expression showed a significant reduction with 10 and 20 μg/mL CRE-Bin treatments (Fig. 3a–c and Supplementary Tables S64–S75; p values of pri-miR-223 compared with OC: Bin5 = 0.0264, Bin10 = 0.0047, and Bin20 = 0.0028; p values of pre-miR-223 compared with OC: Bin5 = 0.0548, Bin10 = 0.0012, and Bin20 = 0.0027; and p values of mature-miR-223 compared with OC: Bin5 = 0.0277, Bin10 = 0.0054, and Bin20 = 0.0013). The effect sizes of these forms of miR-223 were large across all CRE-Bin treatment conditions. Next, we measured TRAP activity and relative CTSK expression in cells treated with CRE-Bin and transfected with a miR-223 inhibitor. Both TRAP activity and CTSK expression decreased significantly and dose-dependently across all CRE-Bin treatment conditions, along with large effect sizes (Fig. 3d,e and Supplementary Tables S76–S83; p values of TRAP activity compared with OC: Bin5 = 0.0503, Bin10 = 0.0086, and Bin20 = 0.0032; and p values of relative CTSK expression compared with OC: Bin5 = 0.0469, Bin10 = 0.0048, and Bin20 = 0.0036).

  The stem-loop mmu-mir-223 exhibited the most negative docking score at − 370.47, paired with a high confidence score of 0.9880, indicating strong interactions. mmu-miR-223-5p demonstrated a docking score of − 349.08 and a confidence score of 0.9817, whereas mmu-miR-223-3p had a docking score of − 311.38 and a confidence score of 0.9619.   To ensure the translational relevance of our findings and explore potential therapeutic implications in human systems, we performed molecular docking simulations on human miR-223 in addition to murine miR-223, to compare their interactions with the IκBα/p50/p65 complex. Human miR-223 displayed strong interactions. Stem-loop hsa-mir-223 had a docking score of − 318.74 and a confidence score of 0.9669. hsa-miR-223-5p achieved a docking score of − 364.40 and a confidence score of 0.9865, representing the strongest binding among all mature forms analyzed. In comparison, hsa-miR-223-3p had a docking score of − 311.38 and a confidence score of 0.9619.

  In the p65–p50 dimer of the IκBα/p50/p65 complex, the N-terminal Rel homology domain spans positions 19–315 in p65 and 39–368 in p50 and contains key functional sequences. These include a DNA-binding subdomain (positions 19–185 in p65 and 39–238 in p50), a dimerization subdomain (positions 191–291 in p65 and 245–350 in p50), and a nuclear localization sequence at positions 301–304 in p65 and 361–363 and 365 in p5024. Our results demonstrated that the stem-loop mmu- and hsa-mir-223 interacted with some contact residues of the DNA-binding and dimerization subdomains of the p65 subunit. mmu-miR-223-3p, mmu-miR-223-5p, and hsa-miR-223-3p interacted with some contact residues of the dimerization subdomain of the p50D subunit. hsa-miR-223-5p interacted with some contact residues of the dimerization subdomain of the p65 and p50D subunits. Additionally, all the stem-loop and mature forms of miR-223 in both species interacted with some contact residues of the IκBα subunit.


### Prediction of the binding affinity of curcuminoids with the stem-loop and mature forms of miR-223
  The binding affinities and effective concentrations of Cu, De, and Bis with stem-loop mir-223 and mature mmu- and hsa-miR-223 were predicted using RSAPred (Table 2). The predicted pKd of all three curcuminoids with the stem-loop mmu- and hsa-mir-223 ranged from 3.99 to 4.01. Based on the reference scale from RSAPred, these values correspond to effective concentrations in the millimolar range, suggesting low binding affinity and a high likelihood of inactivity of curcuminoids against the stem-loop mir-223. By contrast, mature-miR-223 demonstrated higher predicted binding affinities with all three curcuminoids. For mature-miR-223-3p and miR-223-5p of both species, pKd values ranged from 7.57 to 7.81. These values fall within the micromolar range, indicating moderate binding affinity. Among the curcuminoids, Cu exhibited the highest binding affinity with mature mmu- and hsa-miR-223-3p and hsa-miR-223-5p (pKd = 7.81), followed closely by De (pKd = 7.80) and Bis (pKd = 7.79). Mature mmu-miR-223-5p showed slightly lower but comparable binding affinities, with pKd values ranging from 7.57 to 7.59 for all three curcuminoids. These findings suggest that curcuminoids have a higher potential to interact with mature-miR-223 compared with the stem-loop precursor. This increased binding affinity with the mature form could lead to functional effects, such as the inhibition of miR-223 activity.

## Discussion
  We demonstrate that CRE-Bin inhibits RANKL-stimulated osteoclast differentiation in RAW 264.7 macrophages by modulating canonical NF-κB signaling pathway and miR-223 expression. CRE-Bin treatment significantly and dose-dependently reduced TRAP activity and CTSK expression. Additionally, CRE-Bin samples had the lowest percentage of TRAP activity compared with Cu, De, Bis, and CRE-Bin. These findings implied that CRE-Bin is likely to suppress osteoclast differentiation effectively, in comparison with its constituents. However, no multinucleation (number of nuclei ≥ 3) was observed after treatment of RAW 264.7 macrophages with RANKL and 20 µg/mL CRE-Bin. These results align with others, indicating that osteoclasts, even when failing to undergo multinucleation, continue to express TRAP and CTSK1. To better characterize the extent of osteoclast differentiation, TRAP staining for visualizing TRAP-positive multinucleated cells25,26 and F4/80 detection for identifying macrophage lineage cells27 are important complementary approaches. These methods can provide morphological and lineage-specific confirmation of osteoclast formation. However, due to current limitations in resources and access to immunostaining reagents and imaging equipment, we were unable to perform these additional assays. Future studies incorporating these techniques will provide more comprehensive insights into the anti-osteoclastogenic activity of CRE-Bin.

  Considering the in vitro bone pit assay, the lowest bone pit area compared with curcuminoid components, as well as the significant and dose-dependent decrease in fluorescence intensity, indicated that CRE-Bin may have a superior inhibitory effect on bone resorption activity. The observed reduction in total p65 expression and ROS production suggests the involvement of CRE-Bin in suppressing the canonical NF-κB signaling pathway. Studies have reported similar findings with other curcuminoid formulations, such as liposomal CRE-SD25 and CRE-Ter28, which inhibited osteoclast differentiation by reducing p65 and IκBα phosphorylation and ROS production. Molecular docking results supported strong interactions between curcuminoids and some contact residues of functional sequences of the IκBα/p50/p65 complex25, highlighting a potential mechanism for their effects. This study is the first to represent the inhibition of CRE-Bin in osteoclast differentiation in RAW 264.7 macrophages through the canonical NF-κB signaling pathway. However, the band of phosphorylated p65 in CRE-Bin-treated cells was not observed on the blot image (not shown).

  In addition to its involvement in the canonical NF-κB signaling pathway, CRE-Bin modulated miR-223 expression, decreasing pri-, pre-, and mature-miR-223 levels. Microarray analysis by Huang et al.29 showed the lower expression of mmu-miR-223-3p after the RANKL incubation in RAW264.7 macrophages for 5 days, supporting our finding. The downregulation of miR-223 was accompanied by a significant and dose-dependent increase in NFI-A expression, suggesting that the CRE-Bin-mediated suppression of miR-223 relieves its inhibitory effects on NFI-A. Transfection experiments further supported the role of miR-223 in osteoclast differentiation; inhibition of miR-223 led to a significant and dose-dependent decrease in TRAP activity and CTSK expression. While these results support the regulatory role of miR-223 in the anti-osteoclastogenic action of CRE-Bin, the lack of a rescue experiment using miR-223 overexpression limits the full mechanistic elucidation. The inclusion of such a group—co-treatment with CRE-Bin and miR-223 mimic—would provide direct evidence for the miR-223-dependent mechanism by assessing whether miR-223 overexpression reverses the inhibitory effects of CRE-Bin. However, due to limited resources, further investigation in overexpressed miR-223 models could not be conducted at this stage. Despite this limitation, the current findings strongly support a dual mechanism of CRE-Bin through suppression of the canonical NF-κB pathway and modulation of miR-223 activity (Fig. 5).

  In this study, RANKL was treated in RAW264.7 macrophages to stimulate osteoclast differentiation in vitro. Apart from this approach, the treatment of recombinant macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) and RANKL in primary osteoclast precursor cells, including bone marrow macrophage (BMMs), can stimulate osteoclast differentiation2. Ng et al. demonstrated that the expression changes of some proteins in osteoclasts derived from RAW264.7 macrophages and BMMs were slightly different. CTSK and TRAP were downregulated in BMMs at 3 and 5 days of differentiation, along with TRAP in RAW264.7 macrophages. The expression of p65 in BMMs decreased at 3 and 5 days of differentiation but insignificantly changed throughout the five-day culture period30. Although further examination of the regulation of CRE-Bin in BMMs is warranted, the limited availability of this cell line, the high variability among primary cells, and the difficulty in genetic manipulation2 may pose challenges. Nevertheless, the current findings in RAW264.7 macrophages provide a valuable model to elucidate the mechanisms by which CRE-Bin modulates osteoclastogenesis and highlight its potential therapeutic application in diseases characterized by excessive bone resorption.

  Our molecular docking analysis revealed strong interactions between murine and human miR-223 and the IκBα/p50/p65 complex, particularly involving the 5p strands. This is evidenced by the highly negative docking scores and high confidence scores. The observed interactions involve the major subdomains of the p65 and p50D subunits as well as the IκBα subunit. These findings suggest crosstalk between miR-223 and canonical NF-κB signaling pathway, further supporting the dual modulatory role of CRE-Bin. However, the dynamic nature of these interactions remains unclear, necessitating molecular dynamics simulations to understand the interplay between miR-223 and the IκBα/p50/p65 complex.

  Analysis of curcuminoid–miR-223 interactions is one major challenge in investigating the modulation of CRE-Bin through miR-223-mediated pathways. Although RNA–ligand docking methods have the potential to elucidate this mechanism, the limitations of the algorithms in use were evident. Gunasinghe et al. evaluated the performance of RNA–ligand docking simulation using four docking methods as follows: AutoDock Vina, HADDOCK, HDOCK, and RLDOCK. Their study revealed significant limitations in these methods, including an overall success rate of less than 50% for all methods even after refinement using molecular dynamics simulations. All four docking methods demonstrated poor performance in scoring potential evaluations, indicating suboptimal reliability31. These findings may be attributed to the inherent challenges of RNA–ligand docking, including the conformational flexibility of RNAs and small molecules, significant structural changes upon ligand interaction, the highly charged nature of RNA requiring stabilization by water molecules, and the lack of well-defined binding pocket information. To improve the accuracy of binding site identification and pose prediction, further optimization of docking algorithms, potentially through the application of machine learning-based methods, is required31.

  In this study, we used RSAPred to predict the binding affinities of curcuminoids with miR-223, leveraging its RNA-specific machine learning models optimized for accuracy and interpretability. RSAPred provides a robust platform for evaluating miRNA–ligand binding affinities, with a high Pearson correlation (r = 0.882) and low mean absolute error (0.434) obtained from jack-knife tests32. Our binding affinity prediction showed that curcuminoids exhibited moderate binding affinities with mature-miR-223-3p and miR-223-5p, with pKd values in the micromolar range, suggesting functional inhibition of miR-223 activity. By contrast, the binding affinities of curcuminoids with the stem-loop precursor of miR-223 were lower, with pKd values in the millimolar range, indicating reduced likelihood of interaction. These findings support the hypothesis that mature-miR-223 is more accessible to small molecules due to its exposed and simplified secondary structure than the precursor form. The higher binding affinity of curcuminoids to mature-miR-223 may underlie the observed dose-dependent downregulation of pri-, pre-, and mature-miR-223 expression in murine RANKL-stimulated CRE-Bin-treated RAW 264.7 macrophages. This downregulation suggests that CRE-Bin interferes with the miR-223 biogenesis pathway, which could subsequently impact miR-223-regulated targets, such as NFI-A. However, due to the limited availability of resources, we could not perform experimental validations, such as binding assays, to confirm these predicted interactions and their functional relevance.

  Studies have predicted and validated the interactions between NFI-A and miR-223. The miRWalk database identified the 3′UTR region of both human and murine NFI-A as the putative binding targets of hsa-miR-223-3p (predicted probability scores = 1 in eight prediction tools) and mmu-miR-223-5p (predicted probability scores = 0.85–0.92 in three prediction tools)33, respectively. The TargetScan 7.1 database identified the 3′-UTR region of human NFI-A as the putative binding target of hsa-miR-223-3p (total context++ score = − 0.48) and -5p (total context++ score = − 0.02), whereas the 3′-UTR region of mouse NFI-A was identified as the putative binding target of mmu-miR-223-3p (total context++ score = − 0.30)34. Experimental evidence from other studies has validated the interaction between miR-223 and NFI-A. The miRTarBase database corroborates these findings, reporting the validation of hsa-miR-223-3p targeting the 3′UTR of human NFI-A using methods such as reporter assays, qRT-PCR, Western blotting, and microarray analyses. Despite this robust evidence in human systems, similar experimental validations are lacking for mmu-miR-223 binding to murine NFI-A. This gap underscores the need for the experimental validation of the predicted interaction in murine models.

  In our study, the increased relative expression of NFI-A in RANKL-stimulated CRE-Bin-treated RAW 264.7 macrophages highlights the potential downstream regulatory effects of miR-223 modulation. These findings suggest that CRE-Bin-mediated downregulation of miR-223 could relieve its suppressive effects on NFI-A, which is consistent with reports on the role of NFI-A in osteoclast differentiation8. Further experimental validation, including luciferase reporter assays and immunoprecipitation experiments, is necessary to confirm this mechanism in murine systems. Although our in vitro and in silico results are promising, we acknowledge that in vivo validation—such as using ovariectomized or diet-induced obesity animal models—is essential to fully assess the therapeutic potential of CRE-Bin. Due to current limitations in resources of animal models, we were unable to perform in vivo experiments in this study. In addition, the dynamic interactions between NFI-A, miR-223, and curcuminoids warrant investigation using advanced computational methods, such as molecular dynamics simulations and RNA-immunoprecipitation sequencing. Computational prediction is required to elucidate the interaction between NFI-A protein and miR-223. However, experimentally validated structures of mouse and human NFI-A proteins are not available in the PDB35. The average predicted local distance difference test of the predicted structures of mouse and human NFI-A proteins in the AlphaFold database are 62.8 and 62.4636,37, respectively, indicating low confidence in the predicted structures. This may lead to inaccurate and unreliable results of docking simulation between these NFI-A proteins and miR-223. Hence, the acquisition of experimentally validated structures is essential for accurate docking simulations. Additionally, integrating experimental validation approaches, such as cross-linking mass spectrometry or RNA–protein cocrystallization, could provide insights into the binding interfaces between NFI-A and miR-223. These efforts would enhance our understanding of the regulatory mechanisms underlying the miR-223–NFI-A interaction and its implications in osteoclast differentiation.


## Conclusions
  This study demonstrates that CRE-Bin inhibits RANKL-stimulated osteoclast differentiation in RAW 264.7 macrophages through two mechanisms as follows: suppressing canonical NF-κB signaling pathway and modulating miR-223 activity. CRE-Bin significantly reduced TRAP activity, CTSK expression, ROS production, and bone resorption activity, while increasing NFI-A expression via miR-223 downregulation. Molecular docking analyses revealed strong interactions between miR-223 and the IκBα/p50/p65 complex, suggesting crosstalk between miR-223 and canonical NF-κB signaling pathway. These findings underscore the therapeutic potential of CRE-Bin as a multitarget agent for treating bone-related disorders and diseases and highlight the need for further experimental and computational studies to elucidate the mechanisms involved.


## Methods

### Materials
  Murine (Mus musculus) RAW 264.7 macrophages were purchased from the ATCC Cell Bank (Biomedia, Bangkok, Thailand). RPMI 1640 culture medium and supplements (10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, 2 mmol/L glutamine, and 100× antibiotic–antimycotic) were purchased from Gibco Co. (Bangkok, Thailand). The TRAP assay kit was purchased from Sigma (St. Louis, MO, USA). RNeasy Kit for RNA Purification was purchased from Qiagen (Valencia, CA, USA). RANKL was purchased from R&D Systems (Minneapolis, USA). The other reagents were purchased from Sigma Chemical Co. (Bangkok, Thailand).


### Preparation of CRE-Bin and its constituents
  Cu, De, Bis, and CRE containing 89.54% w/w total curcuminoids (72.81% ± 0.83% w/w Cu, 12.49% ± 0.57% w/w De, and 4.24% ± 0.16% w/w Bis) were prepared from C. longa rhizomes, according to Lateh et al.38. The dried powder of C. longa rhizomes was suspended in ethanol and subjected to microwave-assisted extraction at 900 W, 70–75 °C, and three irradiation cycles (each cycle consisting of 3 min of power-on followed by a 30 s power-off period). The resulting extract was filtered and fractionated using a Diaion HP-20 column, which was eluted with ethanol concentrations of 55% and 60% v/v to obtain CRE. CRE was mixed with hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin at a 1:1 molar ratio, and the solvent was evaporated under low pressure to produce CRE-Bin, containing ≈ 17% w/w curcuminoids. The curcuminoid content in CRE and CRE-Bin was measured using high-performance liquid chromatography39.


### Cell culture
  Murine RAW 264.7 macrophages were cultured in RPMI 1640 culture medium with supplements. The cells were incubated in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C. At the semiconfluent stage, the cells were treated with 20 ng/mL RANKL as stated25,40 and 0, 5, 10, and 20 μg/mL CRE-Bin for 5 days unless stated otherwise.


### Confirmation of osteoclast differentiation
  Murine RAW 264.7 macrophages were treated with 20 ng/mL RANKL for 5 days. For TRAP detection, TRAP assays were performed using a TRAP assay kit (TaKaRa Bio, Inc., Tokyo, Japan), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For CTSK detection, total RNA was extracted using an RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, USA). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using a ReverTra Ace qPCR Kit (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan). Fast-Start SYBR Green Master Mix and BIOER LifeECO PCR Thermal Cycler (Bangkok, Thailand) were utilized for quantitative reverse transcription (qRT)-PCR. Percentages of TRAP activity and relative expression of CTSK were measured on days 0–5 of incubation, according to Jantarawong et al.25.


### Cell viability analysis
  Murine RAW 264.7 macrophages were seeded in 96-well plates (5 × 103 cells/mL) and treated with 20 ng/mL RANKL and 0, 5, 10, 20, and 30 μg/mL of Cu, De, Bis, CRE, and CRE-Bin for 5 days. Then, an MTT assay was performed. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific Multiskan FC, Pittsburgh PA, USA).


### Cell morphology analysis
  Murine RAW 264.7 macrophages were treated with 20 ng/mL RANKL and CRE-Bin (0 or 20 μg/mL) in 6-well plates for 5 days. The cells were sedimented onto glass slides. The glass slides were air-dried, stained with Wright–Giemsa stain, and left to stand for 4 min. Then, phosphate buffer (pH 6.4) was dropped onto the slides, which were left to stand for 5 min, rinsed with distilled water, and dried. Images were captured using a brightfield microscope (Olympus CX23, Germany).


### RNA isolation and qRT-PCR
  To measure CTSK and NFI-A expression, total RNA was isolated using an RNeasy Mini Kit from Qiagen (USA). cDNA was synthesized using a ReverTra Ace qPCR Kit from Toyobo (Osaka, Japan). qRT-PCR analysis of CTSK and NFI-A was performed using Fast-Start SYBR Green Master Mix from Roche Diagnostics (Mannheim, Germany). The relative expression of target genes compared with GAPDH expression was calculated using a Molecular Imager equipped with a chemiluminescent image analyzer (Imager ChemiDoc with Image Lab 6), as described25,41.

  The primers used were as follows:CTSK:Forward: 5′-ATGTGGGGGCTCAAGGTTCTG-3′Reverse: 5′-CATATGGGAAAGCATCTTCAGAGTC-3′NFI-A:Forward: 5′-TTACATGTTGGTTCCCTCAGC-3′Reverse: 5′-TGCTTACCTGGGTGAGACAG-3′GAPDH:Forward: 5′-AAATGGTGAAGGTCGGTGTG-3′Reverse: 5′-GAATTTGCCGTGAGTGGAGT-3′

  miR-223 expression was measured according to Madhyastha et al.26. miRNA-enriched total RNA was extracted using an miRNeasy kit (Qiagen). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg RNA using miScript II RT Kit (Qiagen). qRT-PCR analysis of primary (pri-), precursor (pre-), and mature-miR-223 was performed using miScript primer assays (Qiagen), a SYBR Green polymerase chain reaction kit (Qiagen), and ABI Prism 7500 HT sequence detection system (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA).


### In vitro bone pit assay
  To evaluate bone resorption activity, an in vitro bone pit assay using 24-well bone resorption assay plates coated with fluorescein-labeled calcium phosphate (PG Research Inc., Tokyo, Japan) was performed, according to Pengjam et al.42. Semiconfluent RANKL-stimulated murine RAW 264.7 macrophages (1 × 103 cells/mL) treated with 10 µg/mL of De and 20 µg/mL of Cu, Bis, CRE, and CRE-Bin in 3 mL RPMI 1640 medium with supplement were seeded in 24-well plates. Then, 2 nM alendronate sodium trihydrate (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was used as a positive control. After 21 days of incubation (at 37 °C and 5% CO2 humidified chamber), 24-well plates were washed with sterile phosphate-buffered saline, treated with 5% sodium hypochlorite for 5 min, washed with tap water, and dried. Images in each well were captured using a microscope integrated with a digital camera (ZEISS, Axiocam 705 color, Thailand). The percentage of pit area was measured using ImageJ program version 1.54g. Then, 100 μL culture supernatant was transferred to a 96-well plate and mixed with 50 μL of 0.1 N NaOH. Fluorescence intensity was measured at excitation and emission wavelengths of 485 and 535 nm, respectively, using a fluorescence plate reader (Beckman Coulter Multimode Detector DTX 800).


### Western blot analysis
  Cells were cultured under various conditions in 6-well plates (4 × 105 cells/well). Protein extraction was performed using RIPA buffer, followed by centrifugation of the cell lysates at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to collect the supernatant. Protein separation was conducted using SDS-PAGE on a 12% acrylamide gel at 100 V for 90 min. The separated proteins were transferred onto polyvinyl difluoride membranes at 100 V for 60 min. The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk in TBS-Tween for 4 h at room temperature; incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-total p65 and anti-β-actin, each diluted 1:1000 in blocking buffer; washed three times for 10 min each with TBS-Tween; incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (diluted 1:10,000 in the blocking buffer) at room temperature for 2 h; and washed three times for 10 min each with TBS-Tween. Protein bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence substrate, and images were captured using the Chemiluminescence Vilber (Fusion Solo S) imaging system.


### ROS assay
  OxiSelect Intracellular ROS Assay Kit (CellBio Lab, Inc.) was used to investigate the effect of CRE-Bin on intracellular ROS production. RANKL-stimulated murine RAW 264.7 macrophages were first incubated with 0, 5, 10, and 20 μg/mL CRE-Bin for 12 h and then with 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (10 μM). Fluorescence intensity, which is proportional to the percentage of intracellular ROS production, was measured using a spectrofluorometer (Beckman Coulter, Inc.) at excitation and emission wavelengths of 485 and 530 nm, respectively.


### MiRNA transfection
  An miR223 inhibitor was transfected into RANKL-stimulated murine RAW 264.7 macrophages in OptiMEM medium (Invitrogen, NY, USA) using HiPerfect transfection reagent (Qiagen), as described26,43. After 48 h, CTSK expression and TRAP activity were analyzed in the transfected cells.


### Statistical analysis of in vitro experiments
  In vitro experiments were conducted in triplicate under independent conditions, except for the MTT assay (n = 8). Descriptive statistics and Cohen’s d were calculated using Microsoft Excel (Version 2410). Numeric data are expressed as mean ± SD. Cohen’s d was calculated by dividing the mean difference between treatment and control groups by the pooled SD. Effect sizes were interpreted as small (|Cohen’s d|= 0.2), moderate (|Cohen’s d|= 0.5), and large (|Cohen’s d|= 0.8). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test (https://www.statskingdom.com/shapiro-wilk-test-calculator.html). Statistical analyses of the significance of differences among symmetrically distributed groups were calculated using a one-way analysis of variance in Microsoft Excel (Version 2410), followed by a post hoc one-tailed Student’s t-test (Microsoft Excel, Version 2410) and Tukey’s HSD test (https://www.statskingdom.com/180Anova1way.html). Statistical analyses of the significance of differences among asymmetrically distributed groups were calculated using the Kruskal–Wallis test (https://www.statskingdom.com/kruskal-wallis-calculator.html), followed by a Mann-Whitney U test (https://www.statskingdom.com/170median_mann_whitney.html). Statistical significance was defined as a p value of < 0.05. The p values calculated in the one-tailed Student’s t-test and Kruskal–Wallis test are reported in the “Results” section.


### Molecular docking simulation
  The three-dimensional structure of the IκBα/p50/p65 complex (Protein Data Bank [PDB] ID: 1IKN), containing chain A (p65), chain C (p50D), and chain D (IκBα) subunits, was downloaded from RCSB PDB (https://www.rcsb.org/, accessed on December 21, 2022)44 in PDB format. The stem-loop (precursor) and mature forms of M. musculus (mmu-) and Homo sapiens (hsa-) miR-223 were retrieved from the miRbase database (https://www.mirbase.org/, accessed on December 3, 2024)45 (Table 3). The sequences were submitted to RNAComposer (https://rnacomposer.cs.put.poznan.pl/, accessed on December 6, 2024)46 to predict three-dimensional RNA structures. RNA structure files were retrieved in the PDB format. To perform molecular docking simulation, the structure files of the IκBα/p50/p65 complex and the predicted three-dimensional RNA were uploaded to the HDOCK server (http://hdock.phys.hust.edu.cn/, accessed on December 6, 2024)47. Molecular interactions were visualized using the BIOVIA discovery studio visualizer (version 21.1.0.20298; https://discover.3ds.com/discovery-studio-visualizer-download/, accessed on December 7, 2024).

### Binding affinity prediction
  The three-dimensional chemical structures of Cu, De, and Bis (PubChem CIDs: 969516, 5469424, and 5315472, respectively) in SDF format were retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/, accessed on December 20, 2022)48.
