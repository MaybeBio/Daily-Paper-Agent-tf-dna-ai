# Ginsenoside Rh2 alleviates inflammatory response and enhances motor function recovery in spinal cord injury mice via the ROS/MAPK14 signaling pathway


## Background
  Inhibiting inflammation in nervous system is a vital part of treating for spinal cord injury (SCI). Compounds originating from plants can decrease the damage in the spinal cord for recovering the function of nerve and interrupting the inflammatory reaction. 20(S)-Ginsenoside Rh2 (G-Rh2) from Panax ginseng is an anti-inflammatory property inhibiting the expression of MAPK14 protein by enabling the MAPK pathway. Therefore, this research explored the anti-inflammatory effects of G-Rh2 on SCI using cellular and animal models.


## Methods
  To explore G-Rh2's anti-inflammatory potential in SCI, we employed bioinformatics analysis to anticipate target proteins and signaling pathways associated with G-Rh2 and SCI. We utilized BV2 microglia cells to model inflammation induced by lipopolysaccharide (LPS), and a modified Allen's technique in a mouse SCI model. Our investigation utilized a range of methodologies including quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA), Immunofluorescence (IF) and Western Blot (WB).


## Results
  Bioinformatics analysis and molecular docking identified MAPK14 as a key target of G-Rh2. In BV2 cells experiments, G-Rh2 reduced the expression and generation of inflammatory factors, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and transcription factors involved in the MAPK signaling pathway. G-Rh2 showed a decrease in inflammatory reaction and improvements in motor function in the mouse model using the modified Allen's approach for SCI.


## Conclusion
  G-Rh2 mitigates inflammatory responses and enhances motor function recovery in mice with SCI via the ROS/MAPK14 signaling pathway.


## Introduction
  Significant harm is done to the central nervous system by spinal cord injury (SCI), which has a high incidence, a high rate of disability, and a protracted recovery period [1]. Globally, the absolute count of SCI cases has notably risen from 1990 to 2019 [2]. However, due to the complex pathogenic causes of SCI and the challenges associated with nerve regeneration in the central nervous system [3], there are currently no widely accepted and effective treatment approaches to enhance nerve healing in SCI patients [4].

  A key element in the pathogenesis of secondary SCI is neuroinflammation [5]. Macrophages in the spinal cord, known as microglia, are essential in neuroinflammatory diseases [6]. After acute secondary SCI, microglia become excessively activated [7], leading to the accumulation and infiltration of numerous inflammatory cells at the injury site [8]. Reactive oxygen species (ROS), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) are among the inflammatory mediators released as a result of this process, which aggravates the progression of SCI and causes severe secondary immunological reactions [9].

  The MAPK14 signaling pathway is implicated in various environmental stresses and inflammatory responses [10]. Among its subtypes, MAPK14 (also known as p38α) is the most abundant and distinctive, particularly in coordinating pro-inflammatory responses induced by microglia [11]. Cellular responses to stress and inflammation are mostly regulated by downstream substrates of the MAPK14 signaling cascade, which include a variety of transcription factors and protein kinases [12]. In the preliminary research of the team, MAPK pathway is one of the vital inflammatory pathways in SCI [13], which drew our attention to the subunit protein p38MAPKα encoded gene MAPK14 of the p38MAPK family. Furthermore, studies have shown that the p38α MAPK play a vital role in inflammatory responses during the acute and subacute post-injury time points in the traumatic brain injury model via microglia [14]. Additionally, ROS regulates the MAPK14 signaling cascade to induce inflammatory responses [15]. Therefore, targeting the ROS/MAPK14 axis holds promise for promoting SCI recovery.

  The Araliaceae family's perennial ginseng (Panax ginseng Meyer) has long been prized in traditional Chinese medicine for its many therapeutic benefits, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and cognitive-boosting effects [16]. Among its active compounds, 20(S)-Ginsenoside Rh2 (G-Rh2) stands out for its analgesic, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidative characteristics [17,18]. Previous investigations have shown that G-Rh2 has neuroprotective effects in mitigating memory impairment in mice [19], and can inhibit mast cell reactive inflammation by way of the p38MAPK-Nrf2/NF-κB signaling pathway [20].

  However, it is yet unknown, exactly what function and mechanism G-Rh2 serves in SCI. In this study, we utilized in vitro microglial cell culture and an in vivo mouse SCI model to explore the potential effects and mechanism of G-Rh2 in SCI. Therefore, the study aims to investigate the effect of G-Rh2 on the neuroinflammation of ROS and MAPK14 pathway and to illustrate the potential mechanisms.


## Materials and methods

### SCI mouse model: Establishment and treatment
  48 female C57BL/6 J mice between the ages of 8 and 10 weeks were used in this research. All mice were kept at 22–24 °C with a 50 % relative humidity. They were surrounded by a 12-h cycle of light and shade and had unrestricted access to food and water. Four groups of twelve mice each were created from the animals: Sham, SCI, SCI + G-Rh2 25 mg/kg, and SCI + G-Rh2 50 mg/kg. After weighing, mice were disinfected with 2 % iodine tincture, and then administered with intraperitoneal anesthesia using a 20 mg/mL concentration of tribromoethanol. The exposed spinal cord section at each mouse's 10th thoracic vertebra was subjected to a 15 g rod with a 3.0 mm diameter, dropped from a height of 15 mm using Allen's weight-drop method [21]. Spasms in the hind limbs and involuntary twisting of the tail indicated successful modeling [22]. Regarding administration, in the first period starting from Day 1 to Day 7, all the mice in the treatment group were intraperitoneally injected with G-Rh2 once a day for 7 days. The dosage was either 25 mg/kg or 50 mg/kg per injection. Sham and SCI mice were injected with an equal volume of physiological saline solution daily. After 7 days, the spinal cords of half of the mice were collected for related factor detection and pathological staining analysis. In the second period starting from Day 8 to Day 14, the rest of mice were injected with G-Rh2 once a day for 7 days, using the same dosage and injection route as the former. After 14 days, the recovery of motor function in rest of mice was assessed (Fig. S1). After surgery, all of the SCI mice sustained bladder management twice daily until their micturition reflex recovered. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of The Animal Care & Welfare Committee of Guangxi Medical University (NO. 202309005), and all procedures adhered to relevant guidelines and regulations.


### Motor function score
  Footprint analysis and the Basso Mouse Scale (BMS) score were utilized to assess the motor performance of mice. The hind-limb motor function was assessed using the BMS [23]. The BMS consists of anterior-posterior coordination, trunk stability, limb mobility, and hind-limb stability. On days 1, 3, 5, 7, and 14 after treatment, the mice were placed on a platform where they were free to wander. Two observers who were not involved in the therapy recorded the scores. Based on previous studies, footprint analysis was employed to estimate the motor function measure, which accurately evaluates the coordination ability of each limb [24]. Initially, the hindlimbs of the mice were dipped in blue ink, while their forelimbs were dipped in red ink. Then, the stained mice were placed on white paper and given permission to go from their original location to their final destination. Afterward, the trail marks left by each mouse on the paper were observed and recorded.


### Network pharmacology screening of target proteins and pathways
  Using the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology platform (TCMSP; https://www.tcmsp-e.com/tcmsp.php) [25] and the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD; http://ctdbase.org/search/) [26], potential G-Rh2-related target proteins were identified, and SCI treatment-related target genes were identified from the GeneCards, Human Gene Database (https://www.genecards.org/) [27]. Venny (version 2.1; https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/) [28] was employed to analyze common genes related to G-Rh2 and SCI. Constructed a interaction network diagram using STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) [29] and Cytoscape 3.9.1 software (Cytoscape Consortium, USA) [30], and used the CytoHubba plugin to identify the top 10 differentially expressed genes based on their degree values. The biological functions and gene expression distribution of the core genes were assessed using the Metascape database (https://metascape.org/) for KEGG pathway enrichment works [31]. The findings were visualized using online Bioinformatics plots (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/) [32], an online platform for data analysis and visualization.


### Dataset GSE151371 verification
  Gene expression can be used to predict the association between genes and diseases. The dataset expression data for GSE151371 were provided from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) [33], which included 10 healthy individuals as the control group and 38 patients with SCI as the experimental group. Differential analysis was carried out using R software (RStudio, Version info: R 4.2.2, Forrester, USA). Utilized online bioinformatics plots (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/) [32] for visualizing results through heatmap visualization.


### Validation of molecular docking
  The RCSB protein database (https://www.rcsb.org/) [34] and PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) were utilized to download the PDB format of significant target proteins and the MOL2 format of G-Rh2. PyMOL 2.2.0-0 software (Schrodinger, LLC, USA) was used to display docking results. AutoDock Vina 1.2.2 software (The Scripps Research Institute, USA) was utilized to evaluate the association between G-Rh2 and target genes [35]. Binding activity was considered good when the binding energy between the receptor and the ligand was ≤ −5 kcal/mol; the more stable the complex formed by docking, the lower the binding energy [36].


### Treatment and cultivation of cells
  The BV2 (CL-0493 A) microglia cells were kindly provided by Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd and grown in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM; containing 10 % fetal bovine serum and 5 % penicillin-streptomycin; Gibco, USA, Solarbio, China). The cells were cultured at 37 °C in a sterile cell culture incubator with 5 % CO2. Until the adherent cells achieved 80–90 % confluence, the medium was changed every one to two days.


### Assay for cell viability
  The impact of G-Rh2 (purchased from Chengdu Must Bio-Technology Co., Ltd.; http://www.cdmust.com/) on microglial cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (UElandy, China). During the logarithmic phase, 5000 BV2 cells were placed per well in a 96-well plate. After that, they received 24 h of treatment with various G-Rh2 concentrations (0, 2.5, 5, 10, or 20 μM). Subsequently, adding 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent to each well, the wells were placed in an incubator and left for 2–4 h. With the use of a microplate reader, the samples' absorbance at 450 nm was determined.


### Establishment of an inflammatory model in vitro
  Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) was able to induce polarization of microglia into the M1 pro-inflammatory phenotype, thereby effectively triggering an inflammatory response [37]. In this study, LPS was chosen as the inducer for our inflammation model in vitro due to its ability to continuously activate neuroinflammation [38], which is highly relevant for investigating the mechanisms underlying SCI-induced inflammation. LPS was dissolved in sterile PBS to a final concentration of 1 μg/mL. The cells were seeded in a 96-well plate or 6-well plate and stimulated with LPS once they had adhered and reached 80 % confluence. The cells were collected 24 h post-stimulation for subsequent analysis.


### Monitoring of cellular ROS
  ROS assay kit (Beyotime, China) was used to measure the intracellular ROS levels. BV2 cells with inflammatory model (with or without G-Rh2 treatment) were incubated in 10 μM DCFH-DA for 30 min in the absence of light. For oxidation reaction, intracellular 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein (DCF) origined from DCFH-DA showed the green fluorescence under a fluorescence microscope at 488/525 nm, respectively. The images were analyzed by ImageJ software (NIH, US) on the fluorescence area. Results were standardized using control values.


### Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)
  An RNA extraction kit (Corning, China) was used to extract RNA from spinal cord tissue and treated cells. After that, the extracted RNA was transformed into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (Servicebio, China). Complementary DNA was amplified by a 2 × Universal Blue SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Servicebio, China). Each experiment was performed in triplicate for reliable results. To evaluate the expression levels, an internal standardized reference (GAPDH) was used. The relative expression levels were determined using the 2−ΔΔCT approach. ### Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
  To eliminate contaminants and cell debris, the processed supernatant from each cell culture group was spun up at 1000×g for 20 min at 2–8 °C. The cell supernatant was stored in −80°. As instructed by the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Elabscience, China), the amounts of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in the cell supernatant were measured. An enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay reader (BioTek, USA) was utilized to assess absorbance at 450 nm.


### Western blot (WB) analysis
  Protease and phosphatase inhibitors, together with 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) (Solarbio, China, Cat: P0100), were added to ice-cold RIPA buffer (high) (Solarbio, China) to homogenize the BV2 cell protein. Proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, MA, USA) by electroblotting after being separated by 10 % SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (Servicebio, China). The membranes were then blocked for 1 hour in 5 % skim milk in Tris-buffered saline tween (TBST). Antibodies against MAPK14 (1:1000, Cat: #8690, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) and p-MAPK14 (1:1000, Cat: 28796-1-AP, Proteintech, China) were then incubated with the membranes overnight at 4 °C. After being incubated with secondary antibodies (1:10000, Cat: #5151, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) for 1 hour at room temperature (RT), the membranes were met with three TBST washes. The DLX dual-color infrared laser imaging system with Odyssey® was used to visualize the results, and ImageJ ( NIH,US) was utilized for quantitative analysis.


### Immunofluorescence (IF) staining
  In a 96-well plate, BV2 cells were seed at a density of 5000 cells/well for cultivation. The cells were induced with LPS and treated with G-Rh2 when they had reached a density of 80–90 %. After fixation for 30 min at RT with 4 % paraformaldehyde (4 % PFA) (Servicebio, China), they underwent three washes with PBS. Subsequently, the cells were permeabilized for 5 min at RT using 0.1 % Triton X-100-PBS, followed by blocking for 30 min with 3 % BSA-PBS. After two washes with 0.2 % BSA-PBS, they were incubated with p-MAPK14 antibody (1:200, Cat: 28796-1-AP, Proteintech, China) overnight at 4 °C. Following three rounds of washing with 0.1 % BSA-PBS and PBS, the cells were then treated with fluorescent secondary antibodies (1:500, Cat: ab150077, Abcam, UK) for 1 h at RT. Afterward, the cells were stained with DAPI (Servicebio, China) for 5 min, followed by three washes with PBS. A fluorescent microscope was used to view the outcomes, and the resulting images were subsequently analyzed with Image J software (NIH, US).


### Spinal cord histomorphometry analysis
  Mice subjected to different treatments were perfused with 0.9 % sodium chloride and 4 % PFA (Servicebio, China) after being anesthetized. Approximately 1 cm spinal cord segments were cut on each side of the injured area. After fixing the tissue in 4 % PFA for 48 h at RT, it was placed in ethanol with gradually increasing concentrations to remove moisture. The dehydrated tissue specimens were then treated with xylene for permeation, followed by embedding in paraffin with a suitable melting point and subsequent slicing. Spinal cord sections were collected for Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Nissl staining (Beyotime, China). Morphometric changes and Nissl positive bodies in the spinal cord tissue from the mice 7 days after SCI were observed under an optical microscope. The histomorphometry analysis was conducted with ImageJ software (NIH, US).


### Data analysis
  Version 8.0 of GraphPad Prism was utilized for statistical analysis. Data with a normal distribution were compared applying one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). For post-hoc comparisons, the Games-Howell test was utilized if the variance was uneven and the least significant difference (LSD) test was employed if the variance was homogeneous. When dealing with non-normally distributed data, a non-parametric rank-sum test was applied. A statistically significant P-value was defined as one less than 0.05. For every set of data, the mean ± SEM was used.

Following G-Rh2 treatment, the reduction in DCF fluorescence area was evident, with the high dose showing a more pronounced effect compared to the low dose (Fig. 4A–B). Under normal physiological conditions, ROS clearance primarily relies on multiple antioxidant enzymes regulated by Kelch like ECH associated protein 1 (KEAP1)/(NFE2 like bZIP transcription factor 2) NRF2 [39,40]. Hence, after treating LPS-induced BV2 cells with G-Rh2, we determined the mRNA expression levels of KEAP1, NRF2, and antioxidant enzymes Catalase (CAT), Glutathione Peroxidase 4 (GPX4), and Heme oxygenase 1 (HMOX1). Results indicated significant inhibition in KEAP1 transcription level upon G-Rh2 addition, while NRF2, CAT, GPX4, and HMOX1 transcription levels were increased (Fig. 4C–G). Furthermore, we analyzed G-Rh2 and MAPK11, MAPK12, MAPK13 and MAPK14 protein docking models using AutoDock Vina software (Fig. S3 A-C; Fig. 4H) to explore how G-Rh2 reduces ROS. The theoretical binding energy was −6.8, −7.7, −6.6 and −7.1 kcal/mol respectively (Table 3). Among them, G-Rh2 interacts with ILE-346, ARG-23, and VAL-89 in MAPK14 protein. Meanwhile, we performed LPS-induced neuroinflammation in BV2 cells at varying concentrations (10, 20 μM) of G-Rh2 and measured the levels of MAPK11, MAPK12 and MAPK13 using qRT-PCR. The qRT-PCR results (Fig. S3 D-F) showed that G-Rh2 had no effect on their mRNA expression compared to the LPS-induced group. Subsequently, we assessed p-MAPK14 and MAPK14 protein expression. ### G-Rh2 promotes the restoration of motor function and tissue histology in SCI mice
  To test the role of G-Rh2 in vivo, the assessment of motor function recovery was done utilizing the footprint analysis and BMS score. Initially, all SCI mice showed full hind limb paralysis (BMS score = 0) on the first day post-injury, followed by a time-dependent recovery. Notably, mice treated with G-Rh2 displayed substantially more fantastic BMS scores while exhibited statistically indispensable on day 14 compared to the SCI group (Fig. 5A). For behavioral analysis, footprint analysis intuitively reveals differences in hind limb trajectories. Footprints from the forelimb are shown in red, while those from the hindlimb are shown in blue. Following 14 days post SCI surgery in mice, evident dragging marks were observed on the hind limbs of the SCI group mice. Meanwhile, there was a significant reduction in these dragging marks under G-Rh2 treatment compared with SCI group (Fig. 5B–C). For histology analysis, 7 days post-SCI induction, the spinal cord tissue cells in the Sham group displayed a uniform shape, orderly distribution, and robust white matter. Conversely, the SCI group displayed hemorrhagic plaques, numerous cavities, structural irregularities, abnormal cell shapes, and extensive inflammatory cell infiltration. However, the G-Rh2 treatment group prevented significantly the lesion area of spinal cord compared to the SCI group. Notably, white matter and gray matter structures remained largely intact, with a marked decrease in the number of cavities, bleeding points, and inflammatory cell infiltration. (Fig. 5D–E). On the 7th day post-SCI induction, the spinal cord morphology and structure of the Sham group mice appeared normal, characterized by patchy Nissl bodies, regular neuronal morphology and size, and clear nuclei. Conversely, mice in the SCI group exhibited considerable neuronal damage, loss of cell nuclei, and a decreased number of bodies. ## Discussion
  SCI is a severe disabling condition often leading to functional and sensory impairments [41]. The pathophysiology and progression of SCI are significantly influenced by neuroinflammation and oxidative stress, which are mediated by hyperactivated microglia [42]. Consequently, targeting excessive microglial activation emerges as a crucial therapeutic approach. Despite a few clinical interventions showing benefits in select patients, they fall short of significantly enhancing SCI recovery [43]. G-Rh2, a tetracyclic triterpenoid saponin, exhibits promising potential driven by its anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-apoptotic properties [44]. Our findings shed light on the mechanism that allows G-Rh2 to exerts its effect in suppressing the production of inflammatory factors and ROS through the ROS/MAPK14 pathway, ultimately promoting neurological function recovery in mice with SCI.

  We observed significantly elevated levels of MAPK14 in the GSE151371 dataset, which showed notable differences between healthy and SCI groups. As a member of the p38MAPK subfamily [45], MAPK14 initiates an inflammatory cascade reaction by activating the p38MAPK pathway in response to a variety of chemical and physical stimuli, including LPS and UV radiation [46]. This process contributes to the development of secondary SCI [47]. Our findings demonstrate that G-Rh2 can inhibit MAPK14 expression.

  Consistent with previous research [48], our research also indicates that G-Rh2 may inhibit microglial production of inflammatory mediators (TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β) when LPS is present, which is in line with other findings. These findings suggest that G-Rh2 regulates the local immune environment of the spinal cord.

  Consistently, reduction of the inflammatory response in rats after SCI was found to promote motor function recovery [13,49].

  Therefore, we employed a network pharmacology strategy to acquire G-Rh2 targets in SCI, and conducted KEGG enrichment analyses to determine its likely biological activities. According to a KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, MAPK signaling pathways are crucial ROS-regulating mechanisms in the immune inflammatory system associated with G-Rh2-treated SCI. Our research also indicates that G-Rh2 suppresses the activity of downstream transcription factors (ATF2, MYC, STAT1 and ELK1) in the MAPK pathway, underscoring its impact on this pathway. ROS plays an essential role in secondary SCI injury because they are produced at the lesion site by activated macrophages and neutrophils [50], triggering cellular inflammation and immunological responses, and promoting secondary SCI injury [51]. NRF2 is a key regulatory factor exerting antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, typically attached to its repressor, KEAP1, in a functionally inhibited state within the cytoplasm [52]. When stimulated by inflammation or oxidative stress, NRF2 dissociates from KEAP1 and triggers the production of antioxidant enzymes such as HMOX1 and CAT [53]. According to earlier research, controlling the KEAP1/NRF2 pathway is necessary for the development and course of SCI. [54]. Our data indicates that G-Rh2 can degrade KEAP1 and activate NRF2, thereby upregulating antioxidant enzymes and inhibiting intracellular ROS. Stimulation of the MAPK signaling pathway can lead to cellular oxidative stress and inflammatory responses due to increased intracellular ROS levels [55]. Conversely, suppressing ROS expression can lower MAPK protein phosphorylation, ameliorate oxidative stress, and enhance neurological function recovery in SCI models [56]. Our investigation revealed that G-Rh2 targets the MAPK14 protein, which is in line with earlier research findings. Using molecular docking research, it was shown that G-Rh2 can bind to the active pocket of MAPK14. Studies have shown that the p38α MAPK has a positive effect on inflammatory responses in the traumatic brain injury model via microglia [14]. While less researches about the relationship between MAPK12 and other MAPK family members and inflammation in nervous system. Although MAPK12 shows stronger binding ability with G-Rh2, the cell experiment could not verify similar tendency except for MAPK14. The relatively high binding energy of MAPK14 still indicates a significant interaction and prevent the phosphorylation of the p-MAPK14 protein in activated microglia. In this study, LPS-induced BV2 cells significantly increased the expression of p-MAPK14 protein. As a potent immune stimulant, LPS can specifically bind to the TLR receptor family, thereby triggering the activation of the downstream MAPK signaling pathway [57,58]. Previous studies have shown that Diplovicim can activate TLR-like receptors, thus activating the MAPK pathway [59,60]. The results as expected, the p-MAPK14 protein increased in the Diplovicim group, aligning with the mechanism of action stimulated by LPS. In contrast, G-Rh2 decreased p-MAPK14 protein expression in Diplovicim and LPS-induced group. Which revealed the inhibition effect of G-Rh2 on the phosphorylation of MAPK14. In addition, IF confirmed elevated p-MAPK14 fluorescence in LPS- induced BV2 cells, aligning with protein upregulation. G-Rh2 treatment reduced this fluorescence, demonstrating inhibition of MAPK14 phosphorylation. These results strongly demonstrate that G-Rh2 restrains the LPS-induced ROS/MAPK14 pathway to suppress the inflammation in nervous system via microglial cell.

  In order to assess the possible neuroprotective benefits of G-Rh2, more research was conducted using an in vivo model of SCI based on the findings of in vitro tests. In mice treated with G-Rh2, spinal inflammatory factor levels decreased, accompanied by improved motor ability and enhanced spinal cord tissue structure. However, our study has limitations. While we proved that G-Rh2 could promote SCI recovery through the ROS/MAPK14 signaling pathway, further research is crucial to confirm G-Rh2 and MAPK14's relationship. Additionally, our investigation solely focused on G-Rh2's impact on the inflammatory response post-SCI, overlooking the multifaceted development process of SCI. Hence, future studies should explore G-Rh2's role in various pathological and physiological aspects of SCI.


## Conclusion
  This work shows that G-Rh2 can reduce microglial-mediated inflammatory responses by modulating ROS/MAPK14 signaling pathways, enhancing tissue regeneration and motor function recovery following SCI. Our findings provide additional directions for creating of medications that use G-Rh2 to treat SCI. In the future, we will continue investigating the various pathophysiological processes influenced by G-Rh2 in the onset and progression of SCI.
