# Direct pharmacological targeting of Piezo1 by Paeoniflorin: a novel therapeutic approach for renal fibrosis


## Graphical abstract
  Mechanism model of PF alleviating renal fibrosis via endothelial cell Piezo1/HIF-1α axis. In chronic kidney disease (CKD), activation of endothelial Piezo1 is driven by dual pathological stimuli: secondary hypoxia due to microvascular injury and increased tissue stiffness caused by extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition and elevated intraluminal pressure. Piezo1-mediated Ca2+ influx activates HIF-1α, promoting endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT), while exacerbating microcirculation dysfunction. The newly transformed myofibroblasts further enhance ECM deposition, increasing tissue stiffness and establishing a positive feedback loop that perpetuates Piezo1 activation. Paeoniflorin (PF) specifically inhibits Piezo1 channel activity in endothelial cells, effectively suppressing pathological Ca2+ influx and subsequent HIF-1α stabilization. This intervention maintains endothelial integrity, blocks the progression of EndMT, reduces ECM accumulation, and restores microcirculation perfusion. Ultimately, PF breaks the “hypoxia-stiffness-EndMT” self-reinforcing cycle in renal fibrosis, mitigating kidney damage and fibrosis progression. This model emphasizes how PF disrupts the vicious cycle of endothelial dysfunction, ECM deposition, and fibrosis through its specific action on the Piezo1/HIF-1α axis. By Figdraw.

  


## Introduction
  Chronic kidney disease (CKD) represents a major global public health burden, affecting 10 % of the general population and contributing to 1.2 million annual deaths worldwide [1]. Progression to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), driven by renal fibrosis, serves as the final common pathological pathway across nearly all etiologies of CKD, culminating in renal failure and mortality [2]. The kidney, one of the most microvascular-rich visceral organs, relies on dense glomerular and peritubular capillary networks to maintain its filtration and metabolic functions [3]. However, CKD progression is frequently accompanied by microvascular rarefaction, characterized by diminished blood flow and capillary density. This ischemia-hypoxia cascade triggers cellular stress, metabolic dysregulation, mesangial hyperplasia, endothelial injury, and fibrotic remodeling, establishing a self-perpetuating cycle that exacerbates renal deterioration [4]. Endothelial-mesenchymal transition (EndMT), a process wherein endothelial cells acquire mesenchymal features and secrete excessive extracellular matrix (ECM), has emerged as a pivotal mechanism in renal fibrosis [5]. The interplay between microvascular dysfunction and EndMT remains a critical yet underexplored axis in fibrotic pathogenesis, warranting deeper mechanistic insights to inform therapeutic strategies.

  Paeoniflorin (PF), the principal bioactive monoterpene glycoside derived from Paeonia lactiflora, exhibits multifaceted pharmacological properties, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anti-apoptotic, and anti-fibrotic effects [6]. Recent studies highlight its renoprotective potential: PF mitigates diabetic nephropathy by restoring autophagy via VEGFR2-PI3K/AKT signalling [7], attenuates inflammatory pain through TRPV1/SUCNR1-HIF-1α/NLRP3 axis modulation [8], and polarizes macrophages via KLF4-mediated mitochondrial autophagy [9]. Despite these advances, the precise mechanisms underlying PF’s anti-fibrotic actions, particularly its interplay with mechanosensitive pathways in renal microcirculation, remain elusive.

  Piezo1, a mechanosensitive ion channel expressed in endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, and immune cells, transduces hemodynamic forces (e.g., shear stress, stretch) into Ca2+-dependent signaling cascades [10]. Beyond its established roles in vascular development and blood pressure regulation [[11], [12], [13]], Piezo1 critically governs microvascular permeability, perfusion, and hypoxia adaptation [[14], [15], [16]]. Emerging evidence implicates Piezo1 in renal fibrosis: macrophage-specific Piezo1 activation exacerbates fibrosis via CCL2/CCR2-driven inflammation and Notch signalling [17], while endothelial Piezo1-HIF-1α crosstalk amplifies hypoxic stress and fibrogenic gene expression [18]. These findings position Piezo1 as a key mechano-metabolic regulator in fibrotic microenvironments and a potential therapeutic target.

  This study investigates the hypothesis that PF ameliorates renal fibrosis by targeting Piezo1-mediated HIF-1α signaling to restore microcirculatory homeostasis and suppress EndMT. Through integrative approaches—including in vivo models of nephrectomy and diabetic kidney disease (DKD), molecular docking, surface plasmon resonance (SPR), and mechanobiological assays—we delineate PF’s capacity to inhibit Piezo1-dependent Ca2+ influx, mitigate hypoxia-driven HIF-1α activation, and reverse EndMT in stiff matrix-stressed endothelia. Our findings unveil a novel mechano-pharmacological axis for renal fibrosis therapy and underscore PF’s potential as a multi-target agent against microvascular dysfunction and fibrotic progression.


## Materials and methods
  The detailed methods for drugs and reagents, biochemical analyses, histological analysis, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA), systems pharmacology analysis, molecular docking and surface plasmon resonance, cytotoxicity assays, and abdominal ultrasound for renal blood flow measurement are provided in the supplementary materials.


### Animal experiments
  Eight-week-old male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (SPF, Beijing Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) were housed under controlled environmental conditions. One week later, the rats underwent surgery. Briefly, the procedure was as follows: two-thirds of the left kidney (both poles) was removed. Hemostasis was achieved by applying gelatin sponge to the remaining kidney for approximately 30 s. One week later, the right renal blood vessels were ligated, and the right kidney was excised. The sham operation group only exposed the kidneys without excising them. One week post-operation (Week 0), the rats were randomly assigned into four groups, with six rats in each group as follows: (i) Sham CTL group (treated with distilled water for sham-operated rats); (ii) 5/6nx CRF group (treated with distilled water for 5/6nx model rats); (iii) 5/6nx PFL group (treated with 25 mg/kg PF for 5/6nx model rats); (iv) 5/6nx PFH group (treated with 50 mg/kg PF for 5/6nx model rats). PF was administered daily by oral gavage for 16 weeks. Animals were euthanized at Experimental Week 18, corresponding to Week 16 of the treatment phase. Type 2 diabetes (T2DM) mouse models are detailed in the appendix. All animal studies were conducted following the “Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals” and were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the PLA General Hospital (Approval ID: 2022-X18-30).


### Cell culture and treatment
  Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and rat kidney fibroblast cell line (NRK-49F) were purchased from ATCC (American Type Culture Collection, https://www.atcc.org). They were cultured in ECM medium (ScienCell) and DMEM medium (Gibco), respectively, with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS, Corning) and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin (P/S, Gibco) in a 37 °C incubator with 5 % CO2.

  For hypoxia induction, cells were cultured for 24 to 48 h in a humidified chamber at 37 °C under 1 % O2, 5 % CO2, and 94 % N2 atmosphere. Piezo1-targeting (5′-GGCAGCGCATGAACTTTCT-3′) and scrambled control siRNA were synthesized by GenePharma and transfected into HUVECs using GP-transfect-Mate (GenePharma) according to the manufacturer's instructions for the specified time.


### Preparation of soft and stiff matrix
  The two-dimensional cultures on matrix gels with varying stiffness (high: 40.40 ± 2.39 kPa, low: 1.00 ± 0.31 kPa) were prepared according to manufacturer’s guidelines (advanced BioMatrix, GS1005) [19]. To prepare the hydrogels, soft and stiff matrix solutions were created by combining 5 mg·mL−1 Extralink with (10 mg·mL−1 Glycosil + 10 mg·mL−1 Gelin-S) for the soft matrix, or 25 mg·mL−1 Extralink with (20 mg·mL−1 Glycosil + 20 mg·mL−1 Gelin-S) for the stiff matrix, in a 1:5 ratio. The prepared solution was then applied to glass coverslips or well plates for subsequent cell culture.


### Western blot analysis
  Kidney tissues and cells were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer containing phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF). After centrifugation, the supernatant was used for protein quantification with a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein were separated using SDS-PAGE, then transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. The membrane was blocked at room temperature with 1X casein solution for 2 h, followed by incubation with primary and secondary antibodies at 4 °C. The list of primary and secondary antibodies is provided in Table S2.


### Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)
  RNA was extracted from lysed cells or kidney tissues using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen). The RNA was then reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Kit (6215A, TAKARA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. SYBR Select Premix (4368706, ThermoFisher) and an RT-qPCR detection system (ABI) were used for quantitative real-time PCR. Gene expression was normalized to the 18S gene, and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. A list of primers is shown in Table S3.


### Measurement of intracellular calcium levels
  To measure intracellular calcium ion changes, HUVECs were washed three times with PBS and incubated with Fluo-3 AM solution (1 μM, diluted in normal or calcium-free medium) at 37 °C for 30 min. Afterward, cells were incubated with PBS for an additional 30 min to remove the ester groups. After loading with Fluo-3 AM, cells were treated with Yoda1 (diluted in normal or calcium-free medium). Intracellular calcium levels were determined by measuring fluorescence intensity using an FV3000 confocal microscope (Olympus), with excitation at 488 nm and emission at 530 nm.

  Changes in intracellular calcium were quantified by the fluorescence intensity of Fluo-3 AM, and data were analyzed using image processing software (FV31S-SW).


### HUVEC tube formation assay
  HUVECs were plated on Matrigel-coated 24-well plates (Corning, Cat# 356230) and incubated under normoxic or hypoxic conditions with or without PF for 48 h. Tube formation was evaluated under an inverted microscope.


### Statistical analysis
  Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analysis and graph construction were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5.0 software (GraphPad, CA, USA). For normally distributed data, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's test was applied. For non-normally distributed data, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used, followed by the non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.


## Results

### PF exhibits renoprotective effects in models of renal dysfunction

The chemical structure of PF is illustrated (Fig. 1B). Key indicators of renal function revealed that PF significantly and dose-dependently reduced serum creatinine (SCr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (uACR) levels in CRF rats (Fig. 1C–E). After 16 weeks of treatment, serum albumin (ALB) levels in low- and high-dose PF groups were notably elevated compared to the CRF group, suggesting improved nutritional status and protein metabolism. Meanwhile, total cholesterol (TCh) and triglyceride (TG) levels in treated groups were significantly lower than those in CRF rats, indicating PF’s lipid-modulating effects, while total protein (TP) levels showed no significant differences across groups (SFig. 1A–E). The results showed that compared to the CTL animal, CFR rats exhibited a significant decrease in renal blood flow, while in the PF treated animal, it was remarkedly restored.   Additionally, the morphology of the renal small arteries was analyzed by PAS staining. In CFR rats, the renal small arteries showed characteristics of thickened vessel walls, narrowed lumens, and morphological loss. However, in the PF treatment group, these damages were significantly improved, indicating that PF can alleviate structural damage to the renal small arteries (Fig. 4C and D).   To quantify arterial density, vascular structures were identified based on co-localization of α-SMA and Desmin signals, enabling distinction from interstitial myofibroblasts. The results showed that the arterial density in CFR rats was significantly reduced compared to the CTL group. In contrast, PF treatment markedly increased arterial density (Fig. 4E and F). These findings suggest that PF may improve renal perfusion by promoting angiogenesis or preserving vascular integrity.

  Finally, microcirculatory status was assessed by labeling microvessels with PECAM-1. Compared to the CTL group, both CRF rats and db/db mice exhibited a significant decrease in PECAM-1 expression. As expected, in the PF intervention group, PECAM-1 expression was markedly increased. Consistently, similar improvements in PECAM-1 expression were also observed in the peritubular capillary regions (SFig. S4A-D), further confirming PF’s protective effect on renal microcirculation.

  Collectively, these findings indicate that PF effectively ameliorates microcirculatory dysfunction in CRF rats and db/db mice by enhancing renal blood flow, alleviating vascular injury, and promoting microvessel repair and regeneration.


### PF effectively improves endothelial function
  Since the functions of blood vessels are mainly mediated by endothelial cells—including angiogenesis, vasoconstriction, and vasodilation [21]—we next investigated the effects of PF on these endothelial cell-mediated processes. To explore the impact of PF on endothelial cell-mediated angiogenesis, we investigated its effects on HUVEC proliferation and survival under hypoxic conditions. CCK8 assays showed that high concentrations of PF (800 and 1000 μM) significantly reduced HUVECs viability, while the 50–400 μM concentration range did not show significant toxicity (SFig. 5A). During renal fibrosis, excessive collagen production and ECM accumulation lead to restricted peritubular blood flow and hypoxia in nephron. Under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions, the OD values showed no significant difference after 24 h of culture. However, after 48 h of hypoxia, the OD values in the PF-treated group were significantly higher than in the hypoxia group (Fig. 5A), suggesting that PF improves HUVECs survival and function under hypoxic conditions. Induction of angiogenesis to maintain adequate capillary density surrounding renal tubules has been demonstrated to effectively prevent ischemic damage in affected nephrons [22]. Matrigel assays showed that under hypoxic conditions, PF significantly enhanced HUVECs tube formation ability, with an increased number of nodes and total tube length (Fig. 5B-D). Through cytoHubba network analysis, it was found that VEGFA is the gene related to the regulation of PF and angiogenesis (SFig. 2D). VEGFA is a key angiogenic factor that directly promotes the formation of new blood vessels [23]. We observed that the mRNA and protein levels of VEGFA were significantly reduced in kidney of CRF rats, while PF treatment effectively reversed it (SFig. 5B-D).   To further assess PF's influence on endothelial function, we examined its effects on the production of key vasoconstrictive and vasodilative molecules, integral to the regulation of vascular homeostasis.Endothelin-1 (ET-1) is a potent vasoconstrictor, while nitric oxide (NO) is a vasodilator, primarily synthesized by endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) [24]. We found that eNOS mRNA and protein levels were significantly decreased in CRF rats, but PF treatment prevented this decline. Additionally, serum NO levels were restored. On the other hand, it was showed that there is a significant increase in ET-1 and Ang II expression of kidney tissue in CRF rats, which was suppressed by PF treatment, indicating that PF may improve endothelial function by regulating these factors (Fig. 5E–M).

  In conclusion, PF demonstrates comprehensive protective effects on microvascular endothelial function by promoting angiogenesis, enhancing endothelial cell proliferation, and modulating vasomotor regulation. Notably, its ability to improve HUVEC survival and vascular remodeling under hypoxic conditions suggests potential involvement of mechanosensitive signaling pathways, particularly highlighting Piezo1 as a plausible mediator given its established role in vascular mechanotransduction and hypoxia-responsive angiogenesis [18].

  We explored whether PF affects endothelial cell biology by modulating the expression or function of Piezo1. We first analyzed the effect of PF on Piezo1 protein levels. Co-staining of PECAM-1 and Piezo1 in glomeruli revealed that Piezo1 expression was significantly increased in the CRF group compared to the control, but no differences in Piezo1 expression was observed between the PF-treated animals and CRF animals (Fig. 6A).   To investigate the effect of PF on the functional activity of Piezo1 channels, we assessed its ability to inhibit the Ca2+ response induced by Yoda1, a known Piezo1 agonist that facilitates Ca2+ influx through Piezo1 channels. Fluo-3 calcium fluorescence probe experiments demonstrated that PF significantly inhibited the Yoda1-induced Ca2+ influx (Fig. 6E and F), with a notable reduction in the fluorescence intensity of Ca2+ (Fig. 6G and H). These findings further confirm that PF can suppress the Ca2+ influx triggered by Yoda1 activation of Piezo1 channels.

  To investigate the potential interaction between PF and Piezo1, we first employed molecular docking to calculate the binding energy between PF and wild-type Piezo1. The results showed a binding energy of −7.9 kcal/mol, indicating a strong affinity between PF and Piezo1. Hydrogen bonds were primarily formed at key residues, including CYS-977, PHE-984, LYS-988, ARG-1295, and LYS-1154 (Fig. 6I and J). To verify the importance of these residues in binding, we simultaneously mutated them to alanine (Ala). Subsequent docking analysis revealed a marked decrease in binding affinity, with the binding energy reduced to −4.6 kcal/mol (SFig. 6A), supporting the critical role of these residues in mediating the interaction between PF and Piezo1. To further validate the specificity of PF's binding to Piezo1, we used surface plasmon resonance (SPR) technology, which measures the kinetics and amplitude of protein/compound binding. The SPR data revealed that PF could directly bind to Piezo1 with a strong binding affinity, as shown by a dissociation constant (KD) of 2.44 μM (Fig. 6K and L), confirming strong affinity between PF and Piezo1.

  To establish the pathophysiological relevance of PF-mediated Piezo1 inhibition, we evaluated its impact on Yoda1-induced EndMT—a hallmark of microvascular dysfunction. Yoda1 stimulation significantly upregulated mesenchymal markers (Vimentin, TGF-β1) while downregulating endothelial-specific proteins (VE-cadherin, eNOS), consistent with progression toward a profibrotic phenotype. Strikingly, PF treatment reversed these changes, restoring endothelial identity (Fig. 6M–Q). This functional rescue directly links PF’s inhibition of Piezo1-dependent Ca2+ signaling to the maintenance of endothelial homeostasis, providing a mechanistic bridge to its therapeutic effects on vascular remodeling.

  In summary, PF specifically binds to the mechanosensitive domain of Piezo1, inhibits its channel-mediated Ca2+ influx, and thereby modulates downstream mechanotransduction signaling pathways. This functional-level regulation (rather than gene expression regulation) likely constitutes the central mechanism by which PF improves endothelial function and alleviates microcirculatory dysfunction.

Using tunable PA hydrogels (0.5–50 kPa) mimicking fibrotic ECM stiffness [26], we demonstrated PF significantly inhibits stiffness-induced Piezo1 activation and subsequent EndMT progression. Stiffness also downregulated VE-Cadherin and eNOS and upregulated Vimentin and TGF-β1, indicative of EndMT. PF treatment restored endothelial markers and reduced mesenchymal markers without affecting Piezo1 expression (Fig. 7A–F).   In conclusion, PF mitigates stiffness-induced ECM secretion and fibroblast activation, likely via inhibition of Piezo1-mediated endothelial mechanotransduction and the resultant paracrine fibrotic signaling.


### PF inhibits EndMT through the Piezo1-mediated HIF-1α signaling pathway
  Through KEGG pathway analysis, we identified the HIF-1 signaling pathway as a potential key player in PF’s effect on microcirculation. Previous studies have shown that Piezo1 activation by Yoda1 induces calcium influx and upregulates hypoxia-inducible factor-1 alpha (HIF-1α), which regulates EMT in pulmonary fibrosis[27]. These led us to explore whether PF inhibits EndMT via the Piezo1-mediated HIF-1α signaling pathway and further assess its role in endothelial-fibroblast interactions. In vivo experiments showed that, compared to the sham group, the expression of HIF-1α was significantly elevated in CRF rats, but PF treatment significantly reduced HIF-1α expression (Fig. 8A–C). In vitro experiments first examined the effects of PF and an HIF-1 inhibitor on Piezo1 and HIF-1α expression following Yoda1 stimulation. Results showed that neither PF nor the HIF-1 inhibitor significantly affected Piezo1 protein expression, indicating that these treatments do not directly regulate Piezo1 expression (Fig. 8D–E). However, both PF and the HIF-1 inhibitor significantly suppressed HIF-1α expression under the same stimulation conditions. Notably, combining PF and the HIF-1 inhibitor resulted in a more pronounced decrease in HIF-1α expression (Fig. 8D–F), suggesting a synergistic inhibitory effect on HIF-1α. Next, we analyzed the expression of endothelial markers VE-Cadherin and eNOS, as well as EndMT markers Vimentin and TGF-β1. After Yoda1 stimulation, the protein levels of VE-Cadherin and eNOS significantly decreased. However, following treatment with PF or the HIF-1 inhibitor, the expression of these markers significantly increased. The most pronounced restoration was observed with the combination of PF and the HIF-1 inhibitor (Fig. 8D–H). In contrast, Yoda1 stimulation led to an increase in Vimentin and TGF-β1 expression, while PF or HIF-1 inhibitor treatment significantly reduced their levels, with the combination of both showing the most significant reduction (Fig. 8I–K). ## Discussion
  While renal fibrosis remains the central pathological driver in CKD progression, our findings position PF as a multi-mechanistic therapeutic candidate by concurrently addressing microcirculatory impairment and EndMT dysregulation. This study elucidates PF's novel capacity to interrupt fibrosis-perpetuating biomechanical circuits through Piezo1/calcium signaling modulation. This study is the first to reveal that PF alleviates renal fibrosis by targeting the mechanosensitive ion channel Piezo1, regulating the HIF-1α signaling pathway, inhibiting EndMT then improving renal microcirculation. This finding not only provides a novel theoretical framework for the renoprotective effects of PF but also opens potential directions for targeting mechanosignaling pathways in the treatment of fibrotic diseases.


### PF's effects on renal function and fibrosis
  PF significantly improved renal function and attenuated renal histopathological damage, particularly glomerulosclerosis and interstitial fibrosis. Notably, the regulatory effect of PF on lipid metabolism suggests that it may indirectly inhibit the fibrosis process through metabolic regulation. This result is consistent with previous studies that PF can effectively prevent renal interstitial fibrosis, with the potential mechanism partially mediated by the restoration of BMP-7 and inhibition of the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway to block EMT [28]. However, this study further expands its action dimension by achieving systemic protection through improving microcirculation and endothelial function. One limitation of this study is the absence of a positive control drug group. Although our aim was to mechanistically investigate PF’s effects on the Piezo1/Ca2+/HIF-1α axis in renal fibrosis, inclusion of a clinically validated agent such as losartan or pirfenidone would have provided a comparative reference for PF’s therapeutic efficacy. Future studies will incorporate such comparators to better assess the translational value of PF.


### Improvement of microcirculation: from hemodynamics to angiogenesis
  Microcirculatory dysfunction is a core driver of renal fibrosis progression. We established CRF rat models and db/db mice models, which simulate microvascular lesions caused by renal unit loss and metabolic disorders, respectively [29,30]. These models revealed the universal mechanism by which PF inhibits fibrosis through improving microcirculation. Systems pharmacology revealed PF's multi-target engagement in shear stress and hypoxia signaling pathways critical for microcirculatory homeostasis. Functional validation confirmed its capacity to restore renal hemodynamics through vascular remodeling, evidenced by augmented α-SMA+/PECAM-1+ vascular density and improved perfusion parameters. Notably, PF promotes angiogenesis directly by upregulating VEGFA expression and enhancing tube formation ability of HUVECs under hypoxic conditions, which is consistent with results observed in diabetic models [31,32]. Although HIF-1α is a canonical activator of VEGFA, chronic renal injury may impair this pathway through epigenetic and inflammatory mechanisms [4,33,34]. Our study primarily focuses on HIF-1α–driven EMT-like transition, which may not involve the classical angiogenic route. While HUVECs are not kidney-specific, they are widely used for modeling renal endothelial injury [35,36] and have well-characterized Piezo1-mediated mechanosensitive responses [37,38]. Piezo1 functions, such as shear stress sensing and calcium signaling, are generally conserved across endothelial cell types [39,40], though tissue-specific differences may exist. Future studies will validate the PF–Piezo1 pathway in kidney-derived endothelial cells.


### Piezo1 channel: the core target of PF’s effection
  Piezo1, a mechanosensor, plays a dual role in renal fibrosis: it responds to pathological matrix stiffening signals and contributes to the formation of a hypoxic microenvironment [41,42]. Mechanistically, myeloid-specific Piezo1 knockout attenuated renal fibrosis by suppressing macrophage infiltration and activation via calpain-dependent modulation of pro-inflammatory cytokine signalling [17]. In this study, we demonstrated that PF directly binds to Piezo1, without altering its expression levels. This contrasts with pro-fibrotic Piezo1 hyperactivation seen in Yoda1-treated models [41], suggesting PF specifically attenuates pathological mechanosignaling while preserving physiological channel function. The influx of Ca2+ is a critical component in Piezo1 signaling, playing a central role in cellular responses to mechanical stress and the regulation of physiological processes such as vascular tone and cellular adaptation [11]. As a second messenger, Ca2+ not only mediates immediate mechanotransduction but also influences downstream transcriptional programs, particularly under conditions of hypoxia or hemodynamic stress. The interplay between Ca2+ and HIF-1α exhibits bidirectional, context-dependent regulation across physiological systems. In hepatocellular carcinoma, Piezo1-induced Ca2+ influx stabilizes HIF-1α by inhibiting ubiquitination, driving angiogenesis [18], whereas bone unloading triggers HIF-1α-mediated glycolysis that disrupts Ca2+ oscillations, impairing mechanosensitivity [43]. Conversely, in osteosarcoma, TRPV1-mediated Ca2+ elevation suppresses HIF-1α via hypoxia alleviation and ferroptosis induction, enhancing therapy efficacy [44]. These findings highlight that Ca2+-HIF-1α crosstalk is shaped by tissue-specific pathways and channel dynamics, with implications for targeting mechanical adaptation.


### HIF-1α signaling axis: a bridge connecting mechanical stimulation and EndMT
  During fibrosis progression, kidney stiffness increases as compliant cells are replaced by rigid matrix, and the crosslinking of these matrix fibers further exacerbates the stiffness [45]. This stiffness likely provides a favorable mechanical microenvironment to activate Piezo1. To simulate glomerulosclerosis, we used hydrogel to model the condition. In a hard matrix-induced EndMT model, Piezo1 activation drives endothelial cells to undergo mesenchymal transition via the HIF-1α signaling pathway. TGF-β1, a key mediator of renal fibrosis, promotes ECM accumulation by enhancing the synthesis of ECM proteins and inhibiting their degradation [46]. After PF intervention, the loss of endothelial markers was reversed, and the downregulation of Vimentin and TGF-β1 suggests its effective inhibition of the EndMT process. Notably, knockdown of Piezo1 in HUVECs, PF lost its regulatory effect on EndMT markers, confirming that its effects are dependent on the Piezo1 channel. In vivo experiments further demonstrated that PF reduced HIF-1α expression in CRF rat kidney tissue and synergistically inhibited downstream fibrosis genes with HIF-1 inhibitors [47]. Studies have shown that HIF-1α promotes the expression of fibrosis-related factors such as TGF-β1, driving tubulointerstitial transition and ECM accumulation, thereby exacerbating renal fibrosis. This mechanism is similar to the role of the Piezo1-HIF-1α axis in pulmonary fibrosis, but this study is the first to clarify its function in the context of renal microcirculation [27].


### Endothelial-mesenchymal-fibroblast interactions: breaking the fibrotic vicious cycle
  In the progression of renal fibrosis, the interaction between endothelial cells and mesenchymal cells plays a crucial role [48]. Traditional anti-fibrotic strategies primarily focus on directly inhibiting the proliferation of fibroblasts and ECM accumulation [49]. However, the interaction between endothelial cells and fibroblasts acts as a key “amplifier” in the fibrosis process. Co-culture experiments revealed that endothelial cells, in a stiff matrix environment, sense mechanical changes through Piezo1, activate the downstream HIF-1α signaling pathway, and further promote the activation of fibroblasts and ECM secretion. However, under PF intervention, this process was effectively inhibited, with significant reductions in the expression of fibrotic markers such as Fibronectin and Collagen I, indicating that PF effectively blocks the paracrine activation of mesenchymal cells by endothelial cells through regulating Piezo1, breaking the fibrotic vicious cycle.


## Conclusion
  This study identifies PF as a mechanobiology-informed therapeutic that targets Piezo1 to suppress HIF-1α-driven EndMT, thereby disrupting the endothelial-fibroblast crosstalk critical for renal fibrosis progression. By integrating mechanical sensing (Piezo1), hypoxic signaling (HIF-1α), and phenotypic remodeling (EndMT) into a unified pathway, PF redefines antifibrotic strategies through dual microvascular protection and matrix deposition inhibition. Its ability to concurrently improve microcirculation and block fibrotic amplification highlights the translational potential for microvasculopathy-associated nephropathies, while underscoring the value of traditional medicine-derived polypharmacology in treating multifactorial diseases.
