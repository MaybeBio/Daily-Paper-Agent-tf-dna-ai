# Identification of STAT3 signaling as a shared pathogenic signature in systemic lupus erythematosus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and asthma


## Abstract
  While patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) have a higher risk of developing chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and asthma, the underlying mechanisms have yet to be elucidated. To this end, by integrating transcriptomic data obtained from blood specimens for each disorder, this study made an effort to investigate shared pathogenic signatures in these three disorders. After identification of shared differentially expressed genes (DEGs), their protein interactions (PPIs) were mapped using a high confidence threshold (0.7). Construction of the networks led to the identification of STAT3 as the central hub gene. Functional enrichment analysis of the shared PPI network of SLE, COPD, and asthma revealed involvement of the shared DEGs in the activation of innate immunity in conjunction with the dysregulation of adaptive immunity, specifically the differentiation of Th1, Th2, and Th17 cells. Moreover, Gene Ontology (GO) analysis highlighted a substantial imbalance between kinase and phosphatase. These observations suggest the STAT3 signaling pathway not only as a pathogenic link between SLE, COPD, and asthma but also as a potential therapeutic target for patients diagnosed with a combination of these disorders.


## Introduction
  Patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) are at increased risk of developing chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and asthma1,2. The emergence of these pulmonary disorders was reported to markedly lower the quality of life in patients with SLE3. Although the exact molecular mechanisms underlying the development of these pulmonary disorders have yet to be elucidated, inflammation and autoimmune responses are considered as potential links between autoimmune disorders and lung dysfunction4. On the other hand, this co-occurrence may stem from exposure to the risk factors independently associated with each condition, such as cigarette smoke or viral infections5.

  SLE is an autoimmune disorder with a wide range of manifestations due to immune-mediated damage to various organs, resulting in skin lesions, arthritis, central nervous system (CNS) complications, cardiovascular diseases, and lupus nephritis6. Furthermore, 50 to 70 percent of patients with SLE develop pulmonary issues7. In particular, the incidence rate ratio of COPD in patients with SLE has been evaluated as 1.73 (95% CI 1.62–1.84) times higher than in healthy individuals in Taiwan’s population1. Similarly, in a nationwide Taiwanese cohort, the incidence rate of asthma in SLE patients was 2.61 times higher (95% CI 2.47–2.75) than in non-SLE individuals2.

  A wide range of immune cells are involved in SLE pathogenesis, including B and T lymphocytes, macrophages, neutrophils, dendritic cells, basophils8, and natural killer (NK) cells9. B cells are implicated in the production of anti-double-strand DNA (dsDNA) autoantibodies that lead to the expression of type I interferon and trigger an inflammatory response upon binding to dsDNA10. They also play a pathogenic role by acting as antigen-presenting cells and activating T cells11. During the last few years, studies have underscored the role of B cells in the pathogenesis of COPD12 and asthma13. T cells are involved in the immunopathology of SLE by producing inflammatory cytokines that sustain inflammation14. Moreover, they amplify the production of autoantibodies by interacting with B cells15. Various subsets of T cells are abundant in the COPD lungs and contribute to the disease immunopathology by producing cytokines such as IL17, IL21, and IFN-γ5. Besides, the study of circulating T cells demonstrated that their anomalies are correlated with the severity of COPD16. In the context of asthma, Th2 has long been recognized as the central player in the pathogenesis, while recent advancements revealed Th1 and Th17 contributions17.

  Given that both adaptive and innate immunity contribute to the pathogenesis of these disorders, circulating immune cells may serve as a link between SLE, COPD, and asthma. Based on this holistic perspective, blood specimens are a viable source for investigating shared pathogenic mechanisms that may facilitate the onset of COPD or asthma in individuals with SLE. To this end, an integrated transcriptomic analysis was employed to discover hub genes, regulatory miRNAs, and shared pathogenic pathways of SLE, COPD, and asthma.

### Shared networks between SLE and COPD
  A PPI network consisting of 407 nodes was created using 851 DEGs shared between SLE and COPD. The filtration of nodes with less than four interactions reduced the number of nodes from 407 to 183. Signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) was the most central hub gene with 31 interactions. Exploring the regulatory miRNAs for the 41 highly connected genes, which ranked in the top 10% based on degree metrics and had interaction numbers ranging from 13 to 31, revealed that a notable proportion of these miRNAs belong to the mir15/107 family, which simultaneously targets IL7R and CD3E (Supplementary Fig. S1).


### Shared networks between SLE and asthma
  The PPI network constructed using the 366 DEGs shared between SLE and asthma incorporated 155 nodes. Excluding nodes with fewer than three interactions reduced the total number of nodes from 155 to 96. RPS27A, with 28 interactions, emerged as the most prominent gene, followed by RPS6, which had 23 interactions. The remaining hub genes included STAT3 and several other genes encoding various ribosomal subunits, each with 21 interactions. The findings from the investigation of regulatory miRNAs for the upper decile of nodes based on degree metrics, with a confidence threshold of 80, were confined to miRNAs that specifically target STAT3 and RPL31 (Supplementary Fig. S2).

### Functional enrichment of the shared PPI network of SLE, COPD, and asthma
  The gene ontology analysis was carried out to specify the biological process (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF) of the shared DEGs and to attain insight into the biological functions of this subset of genes.

  In the BP category, upregulated genes were mainly enriched in defense response to bacterium (GO:0042742), cellular response to chemical stress (GO:0062197), response to lipopolysaccharide (GO:0032496), and response to molecule of bacterial origin (GO:0002237), while downregulated genes contributed to the immune response-activating and response-regulating of cell surface receptor signaling pathway (GO:0002429 and GO:0002768, respectively) and regulation of innate immune response (GO:0045088).

  In the category of CC, the genes exhibiting upregulation were predominantly involved in the vesicle lumen (GO:0031983), cytoplasmic vesicle lumen (GO:0060205), and secretory granule lumen (GO:0034774). Conversely, the genes that displayed downregulation were enriched in the external side of plasma membrane (GO:0009897), membrane microdomain (GO:0098857), membrane raft (GO:0045121), and plasma membrane signaling receptor complex (GO:0098802).

  In the classification of MF, the genes demonstrating upregulation were primarily associated with the protein serine kinase activity and protein serine/threonine kinase activity (GO:0106310 and GO:0004674, respectively). Meanwhile, the genes exhibiting downregulation were involved in phosphatase binding (GO:0019902). These findings suggest dysregulation of kinase and phosphatase activities as a shared pathogenic signature between SLE, COPD, and asthma.

  KEGG pathway analysis revealed the contribution of upregulated genes to the innate immunity-related pathways, such as NOD-like and C-type lectin receptor signaling pathways, neutrophil extracellular trap formation, and Fc gamma R-mediated phagocytosis. On the contrary, the genes demonstrating downregulation were significantly enriched in pathways reflecting dysregulation of adaptive immunity, including natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity and Th1, Th2, and Th17 differentiation (Fig. 3). ## Discussion
  Individuals diagnosed with SLE encounter an increased risk for the emergence of respiratory conditions such as COPD and asthma, which considerably impair their overall quality of life. However, the precise molecular pathways that contribute to the onset of these lung disorders remain unclear. Herein, by integrating gene expression datasets obtained from blood specimens, a sample pool was provided for each of the disorders. After the determination of shared DEGs between SLE, COPD, and asthma, PPI networks were constructed to overlay SLE with COPD or asthma, as well as for the genes exhibiting a consistent direction of alteration across all of the disorders. Interestingly, STAT3 was identified as the central hub gene in the shared PPI network of SLE and COPD, as well as in the network shared among the three disorders. Furthermore, it ranked third according to degree metrics in the shared PPI network between SLE and asthma. This suggests that STAT3 plays a key role in the shared pathogenic mechanisms that may facilitate the development of these pulmonary issues in patients with SLE.

  By affecting activation and differentiation of T18 and B lymphocytes19, STAT3 significantly contributes to the pathogenesis of SLE. STAT3 is not only important for the differentiation of Th17 cells from naïve CD4 + T lymphocytes, but it is also crucial for sustaining the functional capacity of Th17 cells to secrete IL-17, a cytokine that facilitates the recruitment of neutrophils, the initiation of innate immune mechanisms, and the activation of B cells20,21. The subsequent activation of B cells leads to an amplified production of autoantibodies, thereby exacerbating autoimmune manifestations22. Consequently, STAT3 has long been regarded as a viable therapeutic target in SLE, and its inhibition has yielded promising indications of therapeutic efficacy in both human subjects19 and murine models23. Furthermore, emerging research has underscored the curative impacts of the STAT3 inhibitors on other autoimmune disorders, such as multiple sclerosis (MS)24, rheumatoid arthritis (RA)25, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)26, and psoriasis27. Moreover, an association study on STAT3 (rs8078731) revealed its significant positive correlation with SLE, underscoring its role as a risk factor in SLE susceptibility28.

  STAT3 serves as a transcriptional regulator stimulated through tyrosine 705 or serine 727 residue phosphorylation29, leading to its dimerization and subsequent translocation to the nucleus, where it promotes the expression of its target genes30. By conducting GO analysis on the genes with consistent changes in expression in SLE, COPD, and asthma, it was observed that the upregulated genes were enriched in protein serine, threonine, and tyrosine kinase activity, while the downregulated genes were enriched in phosphatase binding. This imbalance between kinase and phosphatase, coupled with the upregulation of STAT3 in these disorders, suggests that the dysregulation of the STAT3 signaling pathway may serve as a potential pathogenic mechanism that facilitates the development of either COPD or asthma in patients with SLE.

  STAT3 plays a critical role in the transduction of IL6/JAK signaling to the nucleus, wherein it modulates inflammatory responses, the activation of proteolytic enzymes, and apoptotic processes, which are pathogenic mechanisms in COPD. In line with the results of the present study, the elevated expression of STAT3 in the circulating lymphocytes of patients diagnosed with COPD, compared to healthy individuals, was reported through qPCR analysis31. Therefore, emerging studies have evaluated the effectiveness of therapeutic approaches in COPD by examining the STAT3 signaling pathway, highlighting its promising potential as a therapeutic target32,33. For instance, the inhibition of cyclooxygenase 2-mediated translocation of phosphorylated STAT3 to the nucleus by the administration of vitamin E resulted in the alleviation of COPD in rats34. Likewise, in a lung-on-a-chip model, it has been observed that cigarette smoke induces STAT3 activation, promoting epithelial changes and intercellular disruption, which accelerates chronic inflammation and potentially lung cancer. The study also demonstrated that these adverse consequences can be inhibited using HJC0152 as an inhibitor of STAT335. Moreover, in a study on the murine model of COPD induced by cigarette smoke, the inhibition of STAT3 resulted in a reduction of structural alterations, as evidenced by a decrease in alveolar enlargement36. Furthermore, an association study found that variants in STAT3 were correlated with both the onset and worsening of COPD37.

  By playing a central role in the activation of various structural and immune cells, STAT3 is involved in the pathogenesis of asthma38. In asthma, the IL-6/STAT3 pathway is crucial for Th17 cell differentiation, leading to severe, corticosteroid-resistant inflammation. A study revealed that Hedgehog pathway activation increased STAT3 phosphorylation, enhancing Th17 differentiation and inflammation in a Th17-dominant asthma model39. A study on group 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s) revealed that STAT3 activation promotes allergic lung inflammation via mitochondrial methionine metabolism, resulting in epigenetic reprogramming that intensifies allergic responses40. The inhibition of STAT3 in the mouse model of asthma was reported to be beneficial, as it not only attenuated respiratory inflammation and structural remodeling but also reduced the proportion of Th2 and Th17 cells, which play a key role in the development of asthma41. Furthermore, the interruption of STAT3 phosphorylation through the administration of kinase inhibitors in a house dust mite-induced asthma model attenuated pulmonary inflammation42. Recent studies using therapeutics on mouse models of asthma showed that the drug’s efficacy was associated with the inhibition of the STAT3 signaling pathway43,44 Together, these findings indicate that STAT3 is a critical inflammatory mediator in asthma.

  In addition to SLE, pulmonary involvements are widely reported in monogenic autoinflammatory disorders, such as COPA syndrome and STING-associated vasculopathy with onset in infancy (SAVI)45. Mutations in the coatomer-associated protein alpha (COPA) gene result in early-onset autoimmune disorder, frequently associated with pulmonary complications such as follicular bronchiolitis, diffuse alveolar haemorrhage (DAH), and cysts46,47. Similarly, SAVI syndrome, stemming from mutations in the STING1 gene, leads to systemic inflammation, ILD, and skin vasculopathy48. Pulmonary involvement has been well-documented as a prominent clinical symptom associated with this auto-inflammatory disorder49. Collectively, these reports linking lupus-like pathogenic conditions with respiratory issues further underscore the imperative to study mechanisms that potentially favor co-occurrence of these conditions.

  Previous studies have revealed that germline gain-of-function (GOF) mutations in STAT3 significantly result in the autoimmune response affecting various organs. Furthermore, GOF mutation of STAT3 is also associated with interstitial pneumonitis50. Although STAT3 GOF mutations typically cause interstitial lung disease (ILD) in childhood or later, often after the onset of other autoimmune features, a case report showed fatal ILD in infancy due to a novel p.P330R variant. Functional assays demonstrated that P330R both constitutively and IL-6-inducibly hyperactivates STAT3, leading to a strong Th17 cell bias and severe autoimmune-mediated lung damage51. On the other hand, it has been reported that patients with loss-of-function (LOF) mutations of STAT3 exhibit lupus-mimicking autoimmune responses52. Collectively, these findings link monogenic STAT3 mutations to SLE-like pathogenic signatures and ILD, underscoring STAT3 signaling as a potential shared pathogenic signature of SLE and respiratory complications.

  RPS27A, the ribosomal protein S27a (RPS27a) encoding gene, was identified as the central hub gene in the shared PPI network of SLE and asthma. Given that previous studies have suggested that Chlamydia trachomatis (CT) and the Chlamydophila species (CS) promote the production of autoantibodies that are implicated in SLE, a study on prenatal brain protein arrays revealed RPS27a as the most frequent interaction partner of antisera targeting these two intracellular pathogens. This highlights RPS27a as a potential bridge between bacterial infections, such as CT and CS, and SLE53. Using qRT-PCR, it has been previously demonstrated that RPS27A expression is significantly reduced in patients with active SLE compared to both patients in the inactive phase and healthy controls54. In the context of asthma, a previous study found marked dysregulations of RPS27A in children with asthma. However, there was an inconsistency in its alterations based on Tandem Mass Tag (TMT) and Parallel Reaction Monitoring (PRM) methods, and thus it was not considered for further analysis55.

  RPS6 was identified as the second most connected node in the shared PPI network of SLE and asthma. This gene encodes ribosomal protein S6, which appears in the SLE gene list in the GeneCards56 database and has been previously found to be involved in the SLE-associated protein interaction networks57. In asthma, RPS6 contributes to the pathogenesis of the disease via its involvement in IL-3-induced translational upregulation of pro-fibrotic proteins, such as semaphorin-7A in airway eosinophils. This mechanism is involved in airway remodeling and sustained eosinophil activation, which are prominent features of asthma progression58.

  Prior investigations have elucidated that blood miRNAs are implicated in the immunopathology network of SLE59, COPD60, and asthma61. To shed light on the shared miRNAs that may serve as an epigenetic link among these disorders, hub genes raked at the top 10% according to the number of interactions in PPI networks, were used to discover key regulators that potentially modulate shared pathogenic pathways. By targeting both STAT3 and FASLG, miR-21-5p and miR-590-5p were the only miRNAs that targeted more than one hub gene of the network.

  miR-21-5p was observed to be upregulated in the PBMCs of patients with SLE compared to healthy individuals62. In line with this finding, conducting qPCR on the plasma of patients with SLE and lupus nephritis (LN) revealed a significant elevation in the expression of miR-21-5p compared to healthy subjects63. The upregulation of miR-21-5p has been documented in COPD lungs, and by suppressing PTEN expression and promoting the Akt/NF-κB signaling pathway, miR-21-5p contributes to COPD pathogenesis, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target64. In a study on the alveolar macrophages belonging to the rat model of asthma, it was reported that increased levels of miR-21-5p are capable of enhancing epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) via the TGF-β1/Smad signaling pathway by suppressing Smad7, which could be prevented by inhibition of miR-21-5p65. In the light of aforementioned reports, it can be hypothesized that the heightened expression of miR-21-5p in SLE patients may act as a pathogenic contributor to the development of COPD or asthma.

  While both miR-590-5p and miR-590-3p exhibit a downward expression pattern in Th17 cells compared to naïve CD4 + T lymphocyte differentiation66, to date, only miR-590-3p has been specifically studied in the context of SLE. It was reported that PBMCs obtained from SLE patients had reduced expression levels of miR-590-3p in comparison with healthy controls. Furthermore, by targeting autophagy-related 7 (ATG7), miR-590-3p interrupts autophagy and subsequently induces apoptosis in Th17 cells, which could be avoided by the suppression of miR-590-3p67. In a bioinformatic study, miR-590-3p was reported to serve as a prominent regulator of dysregulated genes in COPD68. Emerging research has indicated that miR-590-5p is involved in multiple pathways modulating smooth muscle cell proliferation and migration, including TUG1/miR-590-5p/FGF169 and PVT1/miR-590-5p/FSTL170. Given that this miRNA targets Smad771, it may contribute to the pathogenesis of asthma by affecting EMT65.

  Examination of biopsies obtained from the kidneys of patients with SLE revealed that the expression levels of let-7 family members were markedly increased72. In alignment with the decreased expression of FASLG, it can be concluded that members of this family play a pathogenic role in SLE. Previous studies have shown that multiple members of the let-7 family demonstrate downregulation in COPD lungs. Given that the let-7 family targets RORγt73, a transcription factor located downstream of IL6 and phosphorylated STAT3 signaling, and induces the expression of IL-17 and IL-22 in T cells74, they are implicated in COPD pathogenesis by amplification of the inflammatory response. By regulating the expression of IL-13, particularly in Th2 cells, the let-7 family contributes to the inflammatory response in asthma75.

  Evaluation of the plasma level of miR-92a-3p in patients with SLE revealed its upregulation, suggesting it as a potential biomarker for SLE76. In this study, it was evident that, by targeting downregulated FASLG, miR-92a-3p and miR-92b-3p are involved in the PPI network shared between SLE, COPD, and asthma. In human bronchial epithelial cells of COPD patients, miR-92a-3p is overexpressed and promotes inflammation, which can be attenuated by its inhibition. Moreover, miR-92a has been shown to play a pathogenic role by exacerbating inflammation through amplifying the PTEN/AKT/NF-κB signaling cascade, leading to the production of IL-6 and TNF-α77. In a study on the extracellular vesicles in the plasma of patients with COPD, it was reported that the expression of miR-92b-3p is increased in patients with an exacerbating disease status compared to those with a stable condition, and this miRNA showed acceptable diagnostic efficacy regarding the disease status78. By affecting airway remodeling and inflammation, miR-92b-3p is involved in asthma progression. Furthermore, its expression level is decreased in the blood of asthmatics and it has demonstrated acceptable diagnostic efficacy in differentiating asthma from healthy individuals79.

  This integrative transcriptomic analysis indeed has limitations and deficiencies. Although the ComBat method is a robust, widely employed approach for batch-effect removal, the distribution of samples in the SLE integrated data after batch-effect correction shows detectable clustering of samples according to PC3, which accounts for 4.47% of the variation. Furthermore, clinicodemographic information, such as smoking history, race, and age, might have implicitly affected the identification of DEGs. In this study, this valuable data was not retrievable using the GEOquery package in most cases. Meanwhile, the initial preprocessing stage excluded samples with pretreatment conditions, such as omalizumab-treated samples in GSE134544. Given that leukopenia is frequently observed in patients with SLE, it is also important to employ deconvolution methods to provide insights into the immune-cell proportions in cases and controls.


## Conclusion
  In this investigation, shared gene expression patterns and their functionalities between SLE, COPD, and asthma were studied utilizing integrated transcriptomic analysis to determine the potential mechanisms that facilitate the development of COPD or asthma in SLE patients. Examination of the shared pathological mechanisms among these disorders underscored the STAT3 pathway. The findings of the present study, in alignment with the existing body of literature, suggest that targeting the STAT3 signaling cascade at multiple points offers a promising therapeutic strategy not only for SLE but also for addressing COPD and asthma as its associated comorbidities. While microarray expression datasets derived from blood specimens provide holistic insight, they may not capture the full spectrum of shared pathogenic mechanisms. Therefore, future studies using single-cell transcriptomic analysis will be essential to broaden the current understanding of the interconnectedness of these disorders. Such advancements could pave the way for more targeted, personalized therapies.


## Methods

### Data acquisition and preprocessing
  Blood gene expression datasets were acquired from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) database by searching for related keywords such as “SLE,” “COPD,” and “asthma,” simultaneously with “blood” or “PBMC. Seven datasets, including GSE211700, GSE121239, GSE99967, GSE81622, GSE65391, GSE50772, and GSE24706, were retrieved to examine gene expression alterations in SLE. Meanwhile, GSE76705 and GSE54837 were chosen for COPD, and GSE134544 was selected for asthma (Table 1). The analysis of gene expression datasets in this study was carried out using the R programming language. Hierarchical clustering of samples (using average linkage) and principal component analysis (PCA) were utilized to detect outliers. The outliers in the SLE, COPD, and asthma datasets are presented in Supplementary Figs. S4–S6, respectively. Before outlier detection, a filtration was applied to GSE134544 to exclude omalizumab-treated samples, using the sample information retrieved with the GEOquery80 package. Subsequently, gene expression data for each disorder were integrated and underwent batch-effect removal using the SVA81 package. The scatterplot3d82 package was employed to illustrate the distribution of samples in the PCA graph following batch-effect removal. Lastly, the integration of datasets for SLE resulted in a total of 1563 samples, including 1381 patients with SLE and 182 healthy controls. ### Identification of shared DEGs between SLE, COPD, and asthma
  The differential expression analysis was conducted using the limma96 package. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in each disorder were selected according to the criteria of having an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change > 0.1. Then, the converging results of gene expression alterations in SLE with COPD and asthma were determined separately. Moreover, genes that exhibited consistent upregulation or downregulation across SLE, COPD, and asthma were identified, and Venn diagrams were drawn using the ggVennDiagram97 package. Even slight alterations in the expression of transcription factor-encoding genes, such as STAT3, can substantially affect biological pathways98. With this in mind, a lenient logFC threshold was initially applied for the determination of DEGs. However, to compensate for this leniency, stringent criteria were subsequently utilized for network construction and hub gene identification.


### Construction of shared PPI and miRNA-mRNA networks between SLE and the pulmonary disorders
  To construct a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network shared between SLE and COPD, the shared DEGs among these disorders were supplied to the STRING plugin99 of Cytoscape v3.10.3100, and the confidence threshold was set at 0.7. Subsequently, the network was analyzed to determine the highly connected nodes, defined as hub genes. Furthermore, to achieve a more effective representation of key genes, nodes with fewer than four interactions were not displayed in the final network. Then, the highly connected nodes, ranking in the top 10% of the network in terms of degree, were used as input entries in miRDB101 to identify regulatory miRNAs. miRNAs that targeted more than one hub gene were displayed in the final regulatory network.

  To establish a PPI network shared by SLE and asthma, a confidence threshold of 0.7 was applied to reveal interaction of shared DEGs between these conditions. Nodes with fewer than three interactions were not exhibited in the final network. Subsequently, regulatory miRNAs were sought for the top 10% of nodes according to the degree metrics. The criterion of a minimum target score of 80 was utilized to retrieve miRNAs from miRDB.

  Lastly, to design a PPI network that highlights shared gene expression alterations in SLE, COPD, and asthma, genes showing consistent directional changes across these disorders were used to construct the network with a confidence threshold set at 0.4. Regulatory miRNAs were then identified for the top 10% of highly connected nodes. Moreover, PPIs were also mapped using 0.7 as a high confidence threshold to highlight the most prominent genes.


### KEGG pathway and gene ontology analyses of shared PPI network of SLE, COPD, and asthma
  Upregulated and downregulated genes of the shared PPI network of SLE, COPD, and asthma, which was constructed using the 0.4 confidence threshold, were separately subjected to Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway and Gene Ontology (GO) analyses using the ClusterProfiler102 and org.Hs.eg.db103 packages. Terms with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 were considered significant and were sorted according to their enrichment.
