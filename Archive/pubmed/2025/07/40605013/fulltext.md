# A review of the use of tumour DNA methylation for breast cancer subtyping and prediction of outcomes


## Abstract
  DNA methylation in breast tumours has been extensively studied and has provided valuable insights into the clinical heterogeneity of breast cancer. In this review, we summarise the current literature that has used DNA methylation markers to subtype breast cancer and predict progression and survival. Widespread methylation differences have been observed across breast cancer subtypes at both the candidate genes and in genome-wide analyses, most notably between oestrogen receptor (ER) positive and ER-negative subtypes and for triple-negative tumours. Studies that attempted to create breast cancer subtypes using methylation data showed limited agreement in their capacity to group breast tumours, possibly due to methodological differences. Although many studies have reported associations of tumour DNA methylation with breast cancer outcomes and used machine learning methods to derive prediction models for survival, the extent to which these would replicate in independent datasets is currently unclear. We conclude that despite the potential of genome-wide methylation markers to unravel the heterogeneity of breast cancer, they currently appear to have limited clinical utility. Larger studies and replication of findings across studies are required to address the limitations of the existing literature.


## Background
  The considerable advances in breast cancer molecular profiling that have been achieved in the past two decades highlights the heterogeneity of breast cancer [1]. The traditional histopathology-driven classification is increasingly being integrated with molecular markers to better reflect the intrinsic features of each breast cancer [2]. Currently, recognised subtypes of breast cancer show different responses to treatment and associations with survival, but heterogeneity within these subtypes is still observed [3–6]. Intrinsic subtypes of breast cancer, defined by gene expression profiling, include luminal A-like, luminal B-like, HER2-like, and basal-like tumours. These subtypes were initially identified by Perou et al. [7] through hierarchical clustering of gene expression data and were later formalised in the PAM50 classifier developed by Parker et al. [8], which assigns tumours to these subtypes using a 50-gene expression signature. Gene expression-based assays such as Prosigna (PAM50-based), MammaPrint, and Oncotype DX are now used in many clinical settings to inform prognosis and guide treatment [9–13]. Although previously limited by factors such as high cost, dependence on high-quality RNA, and variability across assays [7, 9, 14, 15], these tests are increasingly considered for further clinical implementation. Analyses of the breast cancer genome have also provided valuable insights into the heterogeneity of breast tumours [16–20]. For instance, somatic mutations in TP53, PIK3CA, and ERBB2 are well-established markers of breast cancer subtypes [21–23]. The identification of somatic driver mutations has also enabled advancement of targeted therapies [24, 25]. For example, BRCA1 mutation is thought to be a driving mutation in triple-negative breast cancers (TNBC) associated with therapy response [16].

  Epigenomic research has considerable potential to contribute to breast cancer subtyping and survival prediction. Epigenetic alterations are well-established hallmarks of cancer [26]. The most widely studied epigenetic mechanism is DNA methylation at cytosine-guanine sites (CpG), and current molecular technologies allow measurement of genome-wide DNA methylation at affordable costs in large-scale studies [26]. Many studies have reported tumour-based DNA methylation alterations in breast cancers associated with clinicopathological characteristics [27–31], but there is currently no consensus as to whether DNA methylation may be used as an additional means to subtype breast cancer or better predict prognosis. In this review, we summarised the studies that have measured DNA methylation in breast tumours, with a particular focus on those that have used methylation markers to i) subtype breast cancer and ii) predict progression and/or survival. ## Methylation-based classification of breast tumours
  The capacity of tumour DNA methylation to improve subtyping of breast cancer has been explored in a number of contexts including; i) methylation differences at candidate genes or across genome between established subtypes; ii) the ability of DNA methylation to predict these subtypes; and iii) delineating novel breast cancer subtypes using clustering algorithms. Clinically relevant existing subtypes are routinely determined by an immunohistochemical (IHC) panel that has been informed and expanded by research applying expression profiling [7]. The most fundamental subtyping is based on hormone receptor status [32–34], and studies investigating DNA methylation as a means to improve subtyping have done this cognisant of the tumours’ oestrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone (PR) status [35–41].


### Methylation differences between existing subtypes
  A selection of studies that have investigated methylation differences at candidate genes across IHC-based subtypes is presented in Table 1. Analyses of methylation differences by hormone receptor status were carried out in the large Western New York Exposures and Breast Cancer (WEB) study. A first study (803 tumours) reported higher promoter methylation of E-cadherin, p16, and RARB2 in ER negative compared with ER-positive tumours [41]. In a second study (720 tumours) of nine candidate genes, promoter methylation of SCGB3A1 was higher in ER-positive tumours [40]. In a study of 238 breast tumours and 12 candidate gene promoter regions, ER-negative tumours had lower methylation than ER-positive tumours at FOXC1, GSTP1, PPP2R2B, and RASSF1A, whereas HER2-positive tumours showed higher methylation at PTEN and CDKN2A [38]. A study of 337 breast tumours for six breast cancer-related genes reported that ER/PR negative tumours had lower promoter methylation levels of EGFR and RASSF1A and higher methylation of GSTM2 compared with ER/PR positive tumours [36]. Promoter methylation of nine breast cancer-related genes was investigated in 114 breast tumours of Korean women, showing that basal-like tumours were hypomethylated in HIN-1, RASSF1A, and TWIST compared to luminal/HER2 tumours and hypermethylated in BRCA1 compared with luminal tumours [42]. In a study of 180 breast tumours, 48 candidate gene promoter regions were assessed; lower methylation in ER-negative tumours was found for ACADL, ADAMTSL1, CAV1, NPY, PTGS2, and RUNX3, and similar findings were observed for basal-like versus other tumours [43]. Several other studies have reported methylation differences across subtypes, with generally smaller sample sizes [35, 37, 39, 44–47].   Genome-wide methylation differences have also been investigated [48]. Using the Illumina HumanMethylation27 BeadChip (HM27K) array, a study of 24 breast tumours from Korean women found methylation differences between ER/PR positive (N = 12) and ER/PR negative (N = 12) tumours at four genes (FAM124B, ST6GALNAC1, NAV1, and PER1), after correction for multiple testing [49]. Another study [50] using the HM27K array and 103 tumours from American women showed that methylation was higher at 22 genes in ER-positive cases and higher at 6 genes ER-negative tumours. A larger study of 256 breast tumours (American women) analysed 276,108 CpG sites using the HM450K array [51] found that genome-wide, ER-positive tumours generally had higher methylation than ER-negative tumours. A recent study using the EPIC Illumina array and 24 breast tumours from a Brazilian cohort reported higher methylation levels at BCAT1 and TBX15 and lower methylation levels of CXCL12 in TNBC compared to non-TNBC samples [52]. A recent study of genome-wide expression-methylation QTLs (emQTL) in three breast cancer cohorts (N = 584) identified two main regulatory clusters: one related to tumour-infiltrating immune cell signatures and the other to oestrogen receptor signalling. The latter showed strong enrichment of enhancer methylation and transcription factor binding activity, particularly involving ERα, FOXA1, and GATA3, suggesting that large-scale DNA methylation differences between ER-positive and ER-negative tumours are driven by lineage-specific regulatory networks [53]. In another recent emQTL study using the OSL2 breast cancer cohort (N = 277) as the discovery set and validation in TCGA (N = 558), a total of 10,264,807 CpG-gene associations were identified and validated. These association patterns were grouped into five clusters, one of which was related to cell proliferation that was mainly observed in ER-negative tumours [54].

  While genome-wide studies provide more comprehensive coverage, several were based on relatively small datasets, and independent validation remains limited, which constrains the interpretation and generalisability of the results. Recent studies also suggest that methylation differences between subtypes may reflect broader gene regulation patterns, not just changes at individual genes, which was seldom investigated.

  While non-exhaustive, the above summary highlights that many studies have investigated methylation differences across hormone receptor-based subtypes, with little overlap in candidate genes. The most frequently assessed genes include RASSF1A, RARB2, CDH13, BRCA1, ESR1, ABCB1, CDKN2A, GSTP1, FOXC1, IGF2, MGMT, PTEN, HIN-1, and p16. Genome-wide studies have been conducted, but large studies are necessary to comprehensively assess and replicate methylation landscapes across IHC/gene expression-based subtypes.


### Prediction of existing subtypes using DNA methylation data
  Several studies have instead assessed the ability of DNA methylation data to predict existing subtypes. This may have wide-ranging applications, such as distinguishing closely related subtypes with greater accuracy, improving their molecular classification by consideration of epigenetic markers, or providing an alternative method for subtyping that currently relies on IHC data and a pathologist [55–57]. Comparison of methylation with other molecular markers, such as gene expression and copy number alterations, has demonstrated the potential of methylation to provide comparable and complementary insights into subtype prediction [56].

  Publicly available datasets such as The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) [55, 56] or available at Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) have been used to perform analyses. In a study including 254 breast tumours (OSL2 cohort, Norwegian hospital patients), the authors applied a series of machine learning methods to discriminate IHC-based subtypes. A Monte Carlo feature selection method was first used to select the most relevant CpGs after which incremental feature selection was applied together with either support vector machine (SVM) or random forest (RF). Both strategies were reported to have very good performance in discriminating IHC-based subtypes with high sensitivity and specificity reported for all subtypes, and overall accuracy, as assessed by area under the receiver operating characteristic curves (AUCs) of 0.95 and 0.91, for SVM and RF, respectively. The ability to discriminate TNBC and HER2-positive cases from other tumours appeared greater than discrimination between luminal A and B tumours [57]. These findings were based on internal tenfold cross-validation, therefore lacking external validation.

  Other studies used multi-omic datasets including DNA methylation to discriminate breast cancer subtypes. List et al. used 521 breast tumours from TCGA with gene expression and DNA methylation data. The RF method was applied to these data types both separately and combined. The adjusted area under the roc curve (AAUC) and classification error rate (proportion of inaccurately classified tumours) were calculated [55]. Gene expression data provided accurate discrimination of gene expression-based subtypes (AAUC > 99%; classification error < 10%), and DNA methylation data alone showed good overall performance (AAUC = 88%; classification error: 20%). Basal-like tumours were accurately discriminated using both gene expression (classification error: 1%) and DNA methylation (classification error: 7%), and good performance was reported for luminal A tumours (classification error of 8% and 13%, respectively). The discrimination accuracy was poor for luminal B tumours using methylation data (classification error: 50%) [55]. These reported accuracies were based on an internal validation analysis [55]. Similarly, a study compared the performance of subtype prediction using gene expression, copy-number alterations, and DNA methylation, based on 606 breast tumours from TCGA and multiple kernel learning (MKL) [56]. DNA methylation slightly outperformed other omics data types in discriminating TNBC from luminal B tumours (AUC = 0.88). These results were similar when SVM was used to optimize the MKL model [56]. While DNA methylation data distinguished luminal A from TNBC tumours with high accuracy, the accuracy was inferior to discriminate luminal B from luminal A tumours and HER2-positive from TNBC tumours [56]. No external validation analysis was carried out. These data suggest potential for DNA methylation to predict existing breast cancer subtypes, but external validation remains limited, and classification performance is often suboptimal for closely related subtypes such as luminal A and B, which may have hindered adoption in other research or clinical contexts.

These methylation-based subtypes were then typically compared with existing subtypes (e.g. IHC- or gene expression-based) and other clinical characteristics.   For example, hierarchical clustering was applied to create three subtypes from 108 breast tumours for which genome-wide methylation was first restricted to the 600 most variable CpGs (compared to 11 paired normal samples) [58]. Subtype 1 mainly consisted of luminal tumours and subtype 2 mainly basal-like and HER2-positive tumours, whereas subtype 3 was more heterogeneous and included all normal samples. The authors applied the same strategy to an independent set of 25 breast tumours which yielded similar subtype composition. Hierarchical clustering was also applied to create three methylation-based subtypes using a dataset including 1,016 CpGs and 80 breast tumours from the OSLO2 cohort [59]. Two subtypes included mainly luminal tumours, whereas the third one mainly included HER2-like and basal-like tumours. Another study derived three subtypes through hierarchical clustering of 40 breast tumours from the Population-based Icelandic Cancer Registry, after pre-selection of the 5,000 most variable CpGs [30]. Similarly, one subtype contained a high proportion of HER2-like and basal-like tumours [30]. Hierarchical clustering was also applied to create three subtypes (pre-selected 5,492 most variable CpGs) within 109 breast tumours from French women [28]. Subtype 1 was mainly basal-like tumours, whereas the two other subtypes included mainly luminal tumours. HER2-positive tumours and normal samples were more evenly distributed across the three subtypes. Seven subtypes were created via hierarchical clustering [29] applied to the most variable 2,108 CpGs (using a bootstrap procedure to improve cluster stability) from a cohort of 188 tumours of Swedish and Icelandic women. Four of these included a high proportion of luminal tumours, one mainly HER2-positive tumours, one mainly basal-like tumours and one was more heterogeneous. The same approach was applied to 669 breast tumours (TCGA), which appeared to produce similar subtype representation. Six subtypes were derived by applying hierarchical clustering to 119 breast tumours and 4 normal samples (Belgian women), after pre-selection of 2,985 differentially methylated CpGs [60]. Two subtypes predominantly grouped HER2-positive and basal-like tumours, while the remaining subtypes included mainly luminal-like tumours. The authors validated their findings on 117 breast tumours and 8 normal samples and found a comparable composition of gene expression-based subtypes across clusters [60].

  Non-hierarchical clustering methods such as K-means clustering have also been used [27, 61, 62]. This was applied to 679 breast tumours from TCGA, using the 2,000 most variable CpGs to create four methylation-based subtypes, one of which contained all basal-like tumours [61]. Another study grouped 517 breast tumours diagnosed in The Carolina Breast Cancer Study into four subtypes; the analysis used consensus clustering with the K-means algorithm to enhance the stability of clusters and was restricted to the 167 most variable CpGs (standard deviation ≥ 0.2) [27]. Similarly, one of the subtypes included mainly basal-like tumours. Nine subtypes were derived using consensus clustering with the K-means algorithm, applied to a TCGA data subset (335 breast tumours and 3,869 CpGs showing nominally significant association with survival) [31]. Basal-like tumours were predominantly grouped in two subtypes, HER2 tumours mainly in one subtype, and luminal tumours were spread out across the remaining subtypes. One subtype included all types of tumours. Limited validation of these subtype representations was observed in another TCGA subset (334 breast tumours and 1252 CpGs associated with survival).

  Another commonly used method is the recursively partitioned mixture model (RPMM) [63, 64], which was primarily designed to accommodate the typical distribution of DNA methylation values. RPMM was used in the original TCGA investigation [65] for subtyping of 446 breast tumours after selection of 574 CpGs of the HM27K array. This approach identified five subtypes which did not appear to strongly overlap with gene expression-based subtypes, although one subtype contained mostly basal-like tumours and very few HER2-positive tumours. Another example is a study that used RPMM to subtype 162 tumours from the Kaiser Division of Research Pathways Study into eight methylation-based subtypes [66].

  Some studies have attempted to further subdivide existing breast cancer subtypes [28, 58, 61, 62]. For example, one study pre-selected 4,987 CpGs from the TCGA TNBCs (N = 73) and applied consensus clustering using K-means to create three subtypes [62]. Another study reported potential new methylation-based subtypes within the luminal A and B subtypes using a similar methodology [67].

  We recently evaluated the potential impact of clustering algorithms, cluster numbers, and CpG selection on methylation-based breast cancer subtypes, using data from two independent cohorts (Melbourne Collaborative Cohort Study (MCCS): N = 409 and TCGA: N = 781) [68]. Agreement between clustering approaches, assessed by the adjusted Rand index (ARI), a standardized measure ranging from -1 to 1, where -1 indicates complete disagreement, 0 represents agreement by chance, and 1 shows perfect agreement. Agreement was generally poor to moderate (ARI < 0.7), though it appeared somewhat higher when specifying 2 clusters and a moderate number of CpGs. There was no noticeable impact of restricting the analysis to CpGs differentially methylated between tumour and normal tissues. These findings emphasise the need for careful implementation and evaluation of clustering strategies to ensure robust and biologically meaningful methylation-based subtypes.

  In summary, hierarchical and K-means clustering were the most frequently applied algorithms, e.g. multiple times to TCGA data. Several analytical aspects varied across studies such as the subset of tumours, the approach used to filter CpGs, the array used to measure genome-wide methylation data, and enhancement of cluster stability via, e.g. consensus clustering. The number of subtypes created was wide-ranging (from 2 to 9), even if higher numbers (e.g. 5–9) of subtypes are unlikely to reflect biologically meaningful and homogeneous groups, especially in the absence of external validation. There was some consistency in the findings that basal-like (and possibly HER2-positive) versus luminal tumours usually clustered in distinct methylation-based subtypes. Overall, the relevance of these clusters remains unclear. Additionally, unsupervised clustering may partly capture other sources of heterogeneity, such as variations in tumour purity or immune cell infiltration rather than true molecular subtypes. The number of CpGs used for subtyping may also play a critical role, e.g. using a small number of the most variable CpGs might primarily reflect cell composition differences or key factors such as ER status or age, whereas including all CpGs may introduce substantial unwanted technical artefacts such as batch effects, which may reduce clustering accuracy. Whether reliable, biologically grounded methylation-based subtypes of breast cancer exist remains an open question, and a major challenge for the field.


## Tumour DNA methylation as a predictor of breast cancer survival
  Tumour DNA methylation as a predictor of breast cancer survival has been investigated in various contexts including: (i) methylation-based subtypes [27, 28, 30, 31, 58, 61, 62, 69]; (ii) candidate genes [40, 58, 70–74]; and (iii) genome-wide screens [75–79]. While most studies focused on overall or breast cancer-specific survival, some examined other outcomes indicative of progression, such as recurrence, metastasis and relapse-free survival [58, 70, 71, 80–82].


### Survival differences between DNA methylation-based subtypes
  Some of the studies that identified methylation-based subtypes (Part 1) further investigated their association with survival [27, 61, 62]. These generally reported shorter survival for subtypes with an overrepresentation of basal-like/TNBCs [27, 29–31, 61]. Studies that created methylation-based subtypes within existing subtypes also reported survival differences. For example, an hypermethylated cluster within luminal A tumours had shorter five-year overall survival after adjustment for age and stage [61]. Within the three methylation-based subtypes identified within TNBCs (TCGA: 73 TNBCs, 12 deaths), the two hypermethylated clusters had shorter survival [62].

  While most studies that derived methylation-based subtypes observed crude survival differences [29, 31, 69], it is unclear whether these merely reflected overrepresentation of specific subtypes (e.g. TNBCs) [27, 30, 61]. In addition, most analyses were based on a small number of deaths, limiting the reliability/precision of these findings [31, 62, 69].


### DNA methylation in candidate genes associated with breast cancer survival
  The potential association between DNA methylation at candidate genes and breast cancer survival or progression has been investigated in many studies.

  Promoter hypermethylation at BRCA1 has been studied extensively [83–85]. A 2013 meta-analysis [83] reported a strong association of BRCA1 promoter hypermethylation with overall survival (hazard ratio (HR) = 2.0, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.3–3.0, based on N = 3,227 cases from eight studies) and disease-free survival (pooled HR = 2.9, 95%CI 1.7–4.8, based on N = 2,217 cases from six studies). A more recent meta-analysis (2020) [84] confirmed these findings; overall survival: HR = 1.7, 95%CI 1.2–2.5 based on N = 4,262 cases from 13 studies, and disease-free survival: HR = 2.7, 95%CI 1.8–4.2 based on N = 2,718 cases from nine studies.

  Other tumour suppressor genes have been investigated for their prognostic role, with varying levels of evidence for RASSF1A, CDH13, GSTP1, p16, ESR1, and PITX2 [86–88]. For RASSF1A promoter hypermethylation, a meta-analysis of eight studies including 1,439 cases for overall survival and 1,795 for disease-free survival found a nearly twofold increased mortality risk [87]. For CDH13 promoter methylation, a meta-analysis [88] based on 726 cases from 13 studies found no association with either overall (HR = 0.8, 95%CI 0.3–2.2) or disease-free survival (HR = 0.4, 95%CI 0.1–1.7), which was further confirmed in an independent set of 669 cases from TCGA [88]. Another meta-analysis based on > 1,000 cases investigated the prognostic significance of four tumour suppressor genes and reported shorter overall survival associated with promoter methylation of PITX2, ESR1, and potentially GSTP1, albeit with relatively small HRs (HR ~ 1.5 for overall survival) [86]. Other candidate genes, including APC, HLA-A, TWIST2, SCGB3A1, CCND2, and SPFRP1, have been examined [40, 85, 86, 89]. A systematic review [85] found 74 eligible studies that assessed survival in ER- and/or PR-positive breast cancer, including 18 candidate genes analysed in multiple studies. The authors reported that hypermethylation at seven genes RASSF1, BRCA1, PITX2, CDH1, RARB, PCDH10 and PGR associated with poorer survival in at least two studies. This review provided further support for previously reported associations at BRCA1, RASSF1A and PITX2, but the authors concluded that while associations were detected for several genes, the overall level of evidence was generally low due to small sample sizes, study heterogeneity, and inconsistent findings across studies, particularly for GSTP1 and ESR1 [85]. Most individual studies of candidate genes had a small sample size, which is associated with a greater risk of false-positives and subsequent publication bias in meta-analyses. Only a few large-scale studies (N > 100 cases) were conducted, limiting the robustness of findings from individual studies [40, 89, 90].

  Multi-gene panels have also been considered [71, 91, 92]. One study examined combined methylation values at MINT17, MINT31, and RARB2 in 242 breast cancer patients and reported that ER-negative cases with consistent hyper- versus hypomethylation of these genes had a threefold to fourfold increased risk of distant recurrence, overall recurrence, and disease-free survival; these findings were again limited by the small sample size (e.g. N = 34 in total for distant recurrence) [71]. In a study of 241 breast tumours from a Dutch cohort, 61 genes were investigated. Hypermethylation of PITX2 was associated with an increased risk of distant recurrence and poorer disease-free and overall survival and further analyses accounting for clinical characteristics suggested that a four-gene panel: PITX2, BMP4, FGF4, and C20orf55 enhanced the prediction of time to metastasis compared to PITX2 only [93]. A recent study evaluated the clinical performance of a newly developed methylation-specific PCR assay, the therascreen® PITX2 RGQ PCR Kit, designed to quantify PITX2 promoter methylation in FFPE breast tumour tissue. Using data from a cohort of 204 breast cancer patients (N deaths = 46), the association between PITX2 methylation and overall survival was similar when using this assay (HR = 2.5, P < 0.001) compared with the microarray platform (HR = 2.3, P < 0.0001) [94].

  To conclude, methylation at several candidate genes has been found to be associated with survival outcomes, with stronger and more consistent evidence for BRCA1 [40, 73, 83, 84, 95–98], RASSF1A [87–92] and PITX2 [85, 93]. For other genes, e.g. GSTP1 and ESR1 [85], the evidence appears less consistent, limited by small sample sizes and methodological variations. The overall strength of evidence remains low as even meta-analyses that included several thousand cases (e.g. for BRCA1) yielded imprecise estimates, as few breast cancer deaths occurred in patients with promoter hypermethylation [83–85]. Other methodological factors, such as multiple testing correction threshold and adjustment for clinical prognostic factors, have been inconsistently considered. This lack of standardisation limits the robustness and as a consequence the clinical applicability of these findings.

  A recent epigenome-wide study used a discovery set including 692 breast tumours from TCGA (all-cause death N = 87) for which the analysis was carried out separately for overall survival and progression-free survival and by gene expression-based subtypes (luminal A, luminal B, basal-like). In another study of TCGA data (N = 780), analyses adjusted for ER status found that 3 out of pre-selected 368 CpGs (located in TDRD10, PRAC2, and TMEM132C) were associated with survival (P < 0.05), with no replication set [77].

  Genome-wide DNA methylation data have been used to develop epigenetic signatures predictive of survival in several studies. For example, a study using TCGA [76] first identified 123 CpGs associated with survival (P < 0.05) and used penalised (elastic net) Cox regression to develop a 16-CpG signature of survival, which remained associated with outcome after adjustment for age, ER status, stage and treatment. This signature was reported to have excellent prediction accuracy in the training set (AUC = 0.97), as well as in an internal validation set of 62 breast cancers (AUC = 0.94) [76]. Another study also applied penalised regression (elastic net) to DNA methylation data using 765 breast tumours from TCGA. A 28-CpG signature of survival was developed using 60% of the data as the training set (N = 460) and validated in the remaining samples (N = 305). The signature was further tested in five independent sets available from GEO (Baltimore hospitals: N = 51; Jules Bordet Institute I: N = 117; Jules Bordet Institute II: N = 113; Swedish cohorts: N = 181; The Australian Breast Cancer Tissue Bank: N = 66). Their findings showed moderate but consistent performance in the external test sets with AUCs ranging approximately from 0.62 to 0.65 across various follow-up time points [100]. Another study developed a methylation-based signature using two groups of breast cancer patients: before and after neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) (training set N = 83, cause-specific death within 5 years after diagnosis, N = 24). Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression with leave-one-out cross-validation was applied to 29,484 differentially methylated CpGs identified before and after NACT in the training set, resulting in a four-CpG methylation signature. This signature was then tested in 85 breast cancer patients treated with doxorubicin or 5-FU and mitomycin NACT. Patients grouped as high-risk, based on the median epigenetic score, showed poorer 5-year breast cancer-specific survival [101]. Another study used 790 TCGA breast tumours and developed based on their training set (N = 520, pre-selected differentially methylated CpGs) two epigenetic signatures of survival, using LASSO Cox regression and boosting, respectively, with tenfold cross-validation. The LASSO model resulted in a 7-CpG signature, while boosting resulted in an 8-CpG signature, with three overlapping CpGs. Validation in an independent cohort of 65 breast tumours from a Chinese study showed a concordance index (C-index) of 0.63 for the LASSO-based signature and 0.61 for the boosting-based signature [102]. Finally, a study of 239 breast tumour samples from Norwegian and Swedish cohorts developed an 18-CpG signature using the LASSO Cox model applied to 182,653 CpGs at which DNA methylation correlated with expression. This signature was validated in a cohort of 583 TCGA breast tumours, demonstrating a similar association with overall survival in a clinically adjusted model as observed in the discovery cohort [103].

  While the aforementioned studies used genome-wide DNA methylation data from all tumour subtypes to develop methylation-based signatures, other studies focused on TNBC outcomes [70, 79, 104]. A study identified 86 CpGs associated with overall survival in 158 TNBC cases from TCGA. LASSO Cox regression applied to their training set (N = 120) resulted in a 15-CpG signature. This signature was validated in two internal validation sets (TCGA; N = 38 cases) and an external test set (Swedish cohort, N = 37 cases), demonstrating strong predictive power for 5-year overall survival in the training set and internal validation set (C-index of 0.92 and 0.97, respectively) but limited performance in the external test set (c-index = 0.64). Methodologically, the high C-index in the internal validation set is likely explained by the fact that it was used to identify the CpGs entered into model training [104]. Another methylation-based signature of survival was developed using the TNBC cases from TCGA as training set (N = 114, N cause-specific deaths = 14) and tested in two external GEO test sets including 130 breast tumours from IBCSG trials VIII and IX and 63 from The Australian Breast Cancer Tissue Bank [79]. LASSO Cox regression was applied to 103 CpGs nominally associated with survival, resulting in a 5-CpG signature. This signature, dichotomised by the median, showed a strong association with survival in the training set (HR = 7.5, 95%CI 1.4–40.9) but its predictive power largely attenuated in the two test sets (HR = 1.8, 95%CI 1.0–3.0 for overall survival; HR = 2.7, 95%CI 1.4–5.1 for disease-free survival) after adjusting for clinical characteristics [79].

  In summary, while some studies have conducted epigenome-wide studies of survival, there is an overall lack of independent replication of findings. These studies used different methodological approaches, e.g. in terms of CpG filtering, statistical significance thresholds and clinical characteristics adjusted for [77, 99]. Several studies developed methylation-based signatures of survival using machine learning methods involving all breast cancer subtypes [70, 75, 76, 100, 101]; others restricted their analysis to aggressive subtypes such as TNBC [70, 79, 104]. We recently conducted a replication study using 425 tumours from the MCCS dataset, focusing on individual CpG sites [77, 99] and multi-CpG signatures described by others [75, 76, 100], which showed partial evidence of replication; this emphasises the value of replication studies to reliably identify epigenetic markers that may enhance the prediction of breast cancer outcomes [105]. In addition to the lack of testing in independent datasets, few studies have evaluated the clinical applicability of their methylation-based signatures and only considered discrimination metrics such as AUC or C-index, which may overstate model performance without assessing how well predicted risks align with observed outcomes. Furthermore, few models were compared with existing clinical predictors to determine whether they meaningfully improved risk stratification. As a result, the clinical utility of many proposed methylation signatures remains uncertain. Larger harmonised studies may also enhance the generalizability of findings and their clinical applicability.


## Conclusions
  Extensive research on DNA methylation changes in breast tumours has provided valuable insights into the disease's clinical heterogeneity [2]. These changes, particularly in the aggressive triple-negative subtype, are observed not only at recognised tumour suppressor genes (e.g. BRCA1) but also throughout the genome, which supports the potential role of DNA methylation for breast cancer subtyping and prediction of outcomes. However, attempts to establish new breast cancer subtypes based on DNA methylation data have resulted in inconsistent subtypes possibly due to methodological variations across studies and biological heterogeneity of datasets—with unclear clinical relevance. Epigenome-wide studies and machine learning methods used to develop methylation-based signatures of survival have also received limited validation in independent datasets and overall necessitate further investigation in sufficiently robust and well-powered analyses.

  Although not covered in this review, DNA methylation data are also used to investigate tissue heterogeneity, due to its highly cell-type-specific profiles that reflect the underlying cellular composition of tumour samples [106–109]. In bulk tumour tissue data, low tumour purity and infiltration of immune cells may bias methylation measurements and confound associations with clinical outcomes if not properly accounted for [110]. For example, low tumour purity may mask tumour-specific methylation signals by introducing noise from surrounding normal or stromal cells. Various methods such as InfiniumPurify allow for the estimation and adjustment of tumour purity using DNA methylation data [111]. Cell-type deconvolution algorithms have been developed [112–114] to estimate the proportions of immune (e.g. tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes), stromal and other cell types, which may be associated with prognosis and treatment response [115–117] and can be inferred using DNA methylation data (e.g. EpiDISH and RefFreeEWAS) [118, 119]. Incorporating these methods into methylation analyses is increasingly recognised as an important step to ensure the accuracy and clinical relevance of biomarkers in breast cancer [67].

  Several in vitro diagnostic tests have also been proposed to guide treatment decisions. Although our review focused on covering research studies that aimed to assess the role of DNA methylation in breast cancer survival, we highlight the therascreen® PITX2 RGQ PCR Kit (Qiagen) as an example of commercial test that has been adopted in clinical practice. This test quantifies PITX2 promoter methylation and helps identify ER-positive, HER2-negative, lymph node-positive patients that are more likely to benefit from anthracycline-based chemotherapy [94, 120–122]. Clinical studies have suggested its value in predicting treatment response and outcomes, supporting the potential of methylation markers in personalised breast cancer care [123–126]. Future studies should further explore how clinically validated assays and targeting which genes may better inform breast cancer prognosis.

  While this review focused on investigating and evaluating tumour-based DNA methylation at individual CpGs, other aspects, such as global methylation and methylation changes due to ageing, also play important roles in reflecting breast cancer classification and prognosis. These broader epigenetic changes warrant further exploration to fully understand their contributions to breast cancer heterogeneity and outcomes [127]. Additional molecular features, such as gene expression, copy number alterations, and transcriptomic profiles, are closely interconnected with DNA methylation and collectively influence tumour behaviour and prognosis. Further research should focus on exploring these complementary molecular alterations in large cohorts to advance personalised breast cancer management and improve patient outcomes [127–129].
