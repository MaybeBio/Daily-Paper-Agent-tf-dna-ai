# Paucity of optineurin gene variants in Indian juvenile open-angle glaucoma patients


## Purpose:
  In the current study, we have screened the OPTN gene in a cohort of unrelated juvenile open-angle glaucoma (JOAG) patients negative for possible pathogenic variants in CYP1B1 and MYOC genes.


## Methods:
  Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification and sequencing were employed to identify nucleotide variants within the coding sequence and intron-exon boundaries of the OPTN gene in 85 JOAG patients and 100 controls. A pathogenicity prediction of identified variants was performed by six distinct online algorithms. Possible structural alterations caused by pathogenic variants were investigated using GOR IV, PyMol, ChimeraX, and molecular dynamics simulations using Gromacs software.


## Results and Discussion:
  PCR amplification and sequencing revealed a total of 27 variations, encompassing eight missense, six synonymous, and 13 intronic changes. Out of the 85 patients, three JOAG individuals exhibited possible pathogenic variants. A novel missense variant p.(Q518L) was also observed and registered at NCBI with accession number PP898303. Computational algorithms identified three potential pathogenic variants. These variants induce disruptions and structural alterations which in turn compromise their functionality. This ultimately leads to retinal ganglion cells death and the eventual manifestation of glaucomatous damage resulting in vision loss.


## Conclusion:
  This is the first report showing the involvement of pathogenic OPTN gene variants in JOAG cases from North Indian population which was unknown till now. This study provides population-specific data on genetic contribution of OPTN genes in JOAG pathogenesis. Genetic investigations like this may help in the understanding of disease pathogenesis and development of therapy for glaucoma management/treatment in the near future.


## Methods

### Patient recruitment
  We enrolled a cohort of 111 unrelated JOAG patients at the Regional Institute of Ophthalmology, Pt. Bhagwat Dayal Sharma Post Graduate Institute of Medical Sciences, Rohtak, India. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal representatives, accompanied by assent forms for minors. The study conformed to the ethical principles delineated in the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee (BREC).

  The inclusion criteria for the patients with JOAG encompass disease-onset age ranging from 3 to 40 years. The patients exhibit elevated IOP (>21 mmHg), in the absence of structural or functional abnormalities in the anterior chamber angle as determined by gonioscopic examination. In JOAG, gonioscopy typically reveals an open anterior chamber angle with no evidence of angle closure, developmental anomalies (e.g., prominent iris processes or anterior insertion of the iris), or secondary causes such as neovascularization or peripheral anterior synechiae. The exclusion criteria involved patients with primary congenital glaucoma (presence of features like Haab’s striae), patients having onset age above 40 years, traumatic glaucoma, aniridia, steroid-induced glaucoma, neovascular glaucoma, angle-recession glaucoma, uveitic glaucoma, or any secondary glaucoma due to ocular surgery or other systemic diseases. We also excluded patients with conditions that could mimic JOAG based on thorough ophthalmic evaluation by professional ophthalmologists. This included ocular inflammation, which can elevate IOP and distort the clinical picture; normal tension glaucoma (NTG), characterized by IOP with in normal range (10–21 mmHg); ocular hypertension (OHT), which lacks optic nerve damage; pigment dispersion syndrome (PDS), identified by iris transillumination defects and pigment deposition; Axenfeld-Rieger anomaly, associated with anterior segment abnormalities; physiological cupping, which is symmetric and lacks glaucomatous changes; and megalopapillae, where the optic disc is enlarged without glaucomatous damage.

  The control group consists of 100 individuals aged over 50 years who underwent a comprehensive ophthalmic evaluation and are free from any ocular or systemic diseases. The control group inclusion criteria consisted of individuals with normal IOP ranging from 10 to 21 mmHg and no family history of ocular disorders. The person having an elevated IOP or having any family history of ocular diseases was excluded after evaluation.

  Initially, all participants were screened for pathogenic variants in the CYP1B1 and MYOC genes using PCR amplification and sequencing. The entire exonic regions and intron-exon boundaries of both genes were analyzed. Among the 111 patients, 85 individuals who lacked pathogenic variants in the CYP1B1 and MYOC genes were selected for further screening of the OPTN gene. The data of the patients having confirmed mutations in the CYP1B1 and MYOC genes have already been published.[18] The patients included in this study presented with sporadic juvenile open-angle glaucoma.

  The compilation of medical records, including IOP, visual acuity, and vertical cup-to-disc ratio (VCDR), was conducted to investigate the relationship between genotype and phenotype in the patient cohort.


### Mutational screening
  Genomic DNA was manually isolated from peripheral blood utilizing the phenol-chloroform-isoamyl alcohol extraction method.[19] DNA quantification was performed using a Nanodrop Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

  The OPTN gene comprises 16 exons, of which the first three are non-coding, while the remaining 13 exons encode a multifunctional protein consisting of 577 amino acids. All the coding exons and the intron-exon boundaries of the OPTN gene were amplified using previously published primers.[20] Polymerase chain reactions (PCRs) were executed in a 40 µL reaction volume containing 100–110 ng of genomic DNA. The resultant PCR products were analyzed on 1.8% agarose gel. The amplified PCR products were subsequently purified using the PCR Product Purification Kit (Favorgen, Biotech Corp.) and outsourced to Barcode Biosciences Pvt Ltd. for Sanger sequencing.


### Bioinformatics analysis

#### Pathogenicity prediction
  All the results of Sanger sequencing were analyzed by using ChromasPro and ClustalW. The results were analyzed against the Human OPTN gene sequence ENSG00000123240 from Ensembl database.[21]

  The pathogenicity of the missense variants identified in the study was performed using various online available algorithms, including Polyphen2 (http://genetics.bwh.harvard.edu/pph2/), Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion –(CADD) (https://cadd.gs.washington.edu/snv), Rare Exome Variant Ensemble Learner (REVEL) (https://sites.google.com/site/revelgenomics/), Mutation Taster (https://www.mutationtaster.org/), MutPred2 (http://mutpred.mutdb.org/), and IMutant2.0 (https://folding.biofold.org/i-mutant/i-mutant2.0.html). Only the variations identified as potentially pathogenic by at least five out of these six algorithms were subjected to further analysis.

  We have performed a multiple sequence alignment of the OPTN protein using the ClustalW (https://www.genome.jp/tools-bin/clustalw) to investigate its conservation among different species, including Human, Monkey (Macaca mulatta), Dolphin (Tursiops truncates), Gorilla (Gorilla gorilla), Dog (Canis lupus familiaris), Cat (Felis catus), Zebrafish (Danio rerio), and Salmon (Oncorhynchus tshawytscha). The amino acid sequences of the OPTN protein from the aforementioned species were retrieved from the Ensembl database (https://www.ensembl.org/index.html). The examination of OPTN protein sequences across various species provides invaluable insights into the evolutionary relationships and the conservation of important biological functions.


#### Secondary and tertiary-structure analysis
  The secondary structure of the mutants was compared to the wild-type proteins using Garnier–Osguthorpe–Robson version IV (GOR IV).[22] The tertiary-structure of wild-type OPTN protein (Q96CV9) was obtained from the Alphafold database (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/) and further visualized using ChimeraX[23] and PyMOL Molecular Graphics System (The PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Version 3.0 Schrödinger, LLC.).


### Molecular dynamic simulation
  Comprehensive all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, extending over 50 nanoseconds (ns), were conducted for both the wild-type protein and its mutants [p.(A93P), p.(Q518L), and p.(G538E)] within an explicit aqueous solvent environment. These simulations were performed at a temperature of 300 K using the GROMOS96 43a1 force field within the molecular mechanics framework. SPC16 water model was employed for solvation purposes. To ensure system neutrality and maintain physiological ionic strength (0.15 M), sodium and chloride ions were incorporated. The energy minimization phase consisted of 5000 steps utilizing the steepest descent algorithm. Following this, MD simulations were carried out for 50 ns for each system, employing a leap-frog integrator to trace the temporal evolution of the molecular trajectories. To elucidate the functional implications of the pathogenic variants on the protein structure, root mean square deviation (RMSD) analysis was performed.[24]


## Results
  Sanger DNA sequencing revealed a total of 27 nucleotide variants which include eight missense, six synonymous, and 13 intronic variations in the OPTN gene. All exonic variations are depicted within the tertiary conformation of the OPTN protein, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1. All eight missense variations were evaluated using six computational algorithms to predict their pathogenic potential [Table 1]. Out of these eight missense variants, three variants such as p.(A93P), p.(Q518L), and p.(G538E) [chromatograms shown in Supplementary Fig. 2] were classified as potential pathogenic variants. These variants are anticipated to influence the functionality of proteins and were absent in a cohort of 100 control subjects devoid of glaucoma.

  One of the three variants identified was a novel missense variant, designated p.(Q518L), and it is predicted to be likely deleterious. The novel genetic variant has been registered at NCBI under accession number PP898303.


### Protein homology analysis
  The mutated OPTN protein variants were analyzed for homology across eight different organisms using ClustalW as shown in Supplementary Fig. 3. The pathogenic missense variants exhibit a high degree of conservation at amino acid positions 93, 518, and 538. This suggests that the variants occur in regions that have remained highly conserved throughout the evolution, indicating their critical role in the proper functioning of the OPTN protein. These results are further strengthened by the structural comparison of the wild-type and mutant proteins.


### Structure analysis
  We utilized the GOR IV software to assess alterations in secondary protein structure resulting from pathogenic variants. The software generated graphical representations depicting variations in secondary structure elements such as helices, sheets, and coils. Our analysis reveals a distinct shift in secondary conformation across all mutated proteins compared to the wild-type protein, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

  The impact of pathogenic variants on the tertiary-structure was examined utilizing PyMol and ChimeraX, aiding in elucidating the alterations in intramolecular interactions among amino acids. In the wild-type protein, the amino acid alanine at position 93 forms two hydrogen bonds with the neighboring amino acids at positions 89 and 97. However, the p.(A93P) variant results in the loss of one hydrogen bond between the amino acids at positions 93 and 89. Additionally, the substitution of alanine with proline introduces steric clashes with the amino acids at positions 89 and 90, as illustrated in Fig. 2a and b, respectively. Both alanine and proline are non-polar and aliphatic amino acids. Alanine’s simple side chain confers greater flexibility to protein structures in contrast to proline’s rigid cyclic structure, which restricts its flexibility and induces kinks or bends in the protein chain.

  In the wild-type protein, the glutamine residue at position 518 establishes two hydrogen bonds with the leucine at position 514 and the histidine at position 521. The p.(Q518L) variant does not alter the hydrogen bonding network; however, it induces steric clashes with the leucine at position 514, the methionine at position 515, and the serine at position 519, as depicted in Fig. 2c and d. Glutamine is a polar, uncharged, and hydrophilic amino acid, whereas leucine is a non-polar, hydrophobic amino acid. Substituting a hydrophilic residue like glutamine with a hydrophobic one like leucine can significantly affect the protein’s conformation and its interactions with the aqueous environment. This alteration may result in protein misfolding or aggregation, particularly if the variant occurs within a hydrophilic region of the protein.

  At amino acid position 538, the glycine residue does not form hydrogen bonds with adjacent amino acids due to its location within the coil region of the protein. The G538E variant results in the substitution of glycine with glutamic acid at this position, leading to steric clashes with the glutamic acid residue at position 540, as illustrated in Fig. 2e and f. Glutamic acid possesses a bulkier side chain compared to glycine, leading to potential modifications in the protein’s conformation. Such alterations can influence the protein’s affinity toward various molecules and its functional behavior. At physiological pH, glutamic acid carries a negative charge, whereas glycine remains electrically neutral. This charge discrepancy can significantly impact the protein’s molecular interactions, particularly in regions crucial for binding or catalytic activity. Furthermore, the modified side chain can influence the protein’s hydrophobic properties, potentially impacting its solubility and structural stability.


### Molecular dynamics simulation analysis
  MD simulations help to evaluate the atomic movements within highly complex macromolecules. The RMSD is the most frequently utilized criterion to quantify structural fluctuations in macromolecules, such as proteins. Supplementary Fig. 4 illustrates the RMSD data for both the wild-type and mutant proteins, clearly depicting measurable differences in RMSD values between wild-type and mutated proteins. The average RMSD values are 2.5 nm for the wild-type, 2.2 nm for the p.(A93P) mutant, 1.5 nm for the Q518L mutant, and 2 nm for the p.(G538E) mutant. For the wild-type protein, the RMSD stabilizes at 3.7 nm after 35 ns, following initial fluctuation spikes at 20 ns and 30 ns, a pattern not observed in any of the mutants. The p.(A93P) mutant exhibits a gradual spike to 2.5 nm during the first 5 ns, fluctuating between 2–2.5 nm until 40 ns, after which it shifts to 3 nm. The RMSD values for the p.(Q518L) and p.(G538E) mutants show less fluctuation but maintain lower average values than the wild-type protein. These variations in fluctuations suggest alterations in protein stability, which may impact the protein’s normal function, as indicated by the analysis of its secondary and tertiary structures.


## Discussion
  In glaucoma, vision impairment arises from the degeneration of retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) and their axons within the optic nerve head. RGCs function is to receive signals from photoreceptor cells through bipolar and amacrine cells, transmitting these visual signals as action potentials to the brain via the optic nerve. The multifunctional protein OPTN plays pivotal roles in various cellular processes, such as inflammatory responses, Golgi apparatus maintenance, vesicle trafficking, and signal transduction. Optineurin protein serves as a crucial receptor for selective mitochondrial autophagy, known as mitophagy, which is a homeostatic mechanism specifically targeting and removing the damaged mitochondria.

  Optineurin’s role in the development of glaucoma is linked to its interactions with key proteins involved in autophagy, such as Rab8, Htt, myosin VI, and TBK1 [Fig. 3a].[25] The organizational framework of the optineurin protein and its associated molecular counterparts has been illuminated within the STRING network visualization depicted in Supplementary Fig. 1 sourced from the STRING database (https://string-db.org/). The variants in various domains of OPTN [shown in Fig. 3a] can result in impaired autophagic processes, leading to the accumulation of cellular debris and increased oxidative stress in RGCs, both of which are contributory factors in glaucoma pathogenesis.[13]

  Mutations in OPTN were found in 16.7% of the hereditary forms of NTG, a form of POAG without elevated IOP.[15] Among these mutations, the p.(E50K) variant stands out, displaying a strong association with NTG in <2% of NTG cases and <1% of POAG cases.[26] The relationship between other OPTN mutations and glaucoma is more intricate, showing population-specific associations. For instance, the p.(M98K) variant appears more frequently in NTG patients compared to control groups in certain Caucasian and Asian populations,[27] though not universally.[28] Overall, OPTN mutations are generally not linked to POAG cases involving elevated IOP in most populations,[262829] although some exceptions also exist in the literature.[29] Furthermore, the role of OPTN in JOAG remains ambiguous, with conflicting evidence.[3] Rare OPTN variants, including p.(L41), p.(H486R), p.(R329G), and p.(L494W), have been reported in some JOAG cases within Canadian,[10] Chinese,[30] and Korean cohorts[31]; however, their pathogenicity is uncertain, and functional studies have yet to validate these findings.

  The functional characterization of pathogenic OPTN variants remains limited, with current research on OPTN’s involvement in glaucoma primarily focused on the p.(E50K) and p.(M98K) variants. Studies in mice have shown that the p.(E50K) mutation disrupts OPTN’s interaction with several proteins, including TBK1, Rab8, TBC1D17, and transferrin receptor.[32] These altered interactions likely impact the functions of these proteins, potentially contributing to RGC death and the development of glaucoma. Additionally, the p.(E50K) mutation modifies OPTN’s oligomeric state, suggesting further implications for its cellular functions.[33] The p.(M98K) variant, on the other hand, is associated with increased phosphorylation at S177 in RGC-5 cells, a process regulated by TBK1 activated by M98K-OPTN and implicated in autophagy. Notably, this phosphorylation event is observed in RGC-5 cells but not in HeLa or IMR32 cells.[13] For other less common variants, such as p.(H486R), there is a reduction in OPTN’s interaction with CYLD, leading to compromised NF-κB regulation in response to TNFα, IL-1β, and lipopolysaccharide.[34] However, transgenic mice expressing H486R-OPTN do not exhibit RGC loss or retinal changes.[35] Furthermore, mutations like p.(H486R), p.(H26D), p.(T202R), and p.(E322K) do not induce cell death in RGC-5 or 661W cells, implying that these variants may contribute to glaucoma pathogenesis via indirect mechanisms, possibly involving glial cell activation.[36]

  In our study, we have identified three rare potentially pathogenic variants: p.(A93P), p.(Q518L), and p.(G538E), in our JOAG cohort. The p.(A93P) variant was identified in patient P90; a 17-year-old female JOAG patient in our study. This patient has undergone trabeculectomy in both eyes and has current visual acuity of hand motion (HM) in the right eye and no light perception (PL-ve) in the left eye. Further clinical information for this patient is provided in Table 2. Optineurin functions as an adaptor protein that bridges ubiquitinated cargo with autophagic vesicles, thereby promoting the removal of damaged mitochondria and protein aggregates. The p.(A93P) variant has already been reported in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) in the Japanese population[37] but has not been observed in glaucoma before the current study. Upon thorough systemic and clinical examination, no symptoms of ALS have been detected so far in this patient. Typically, the ALS onset occurs between the ages of 51 to 66 years, as reported in various studies.[38] Therefore, the patient is advised to undergo regular clinical follow-ups.

  The interaction between optineurin and Rab8 is essential for the effective delivery of autophagosomes to lysosomes, a process critical for the degradation and recycling of cellular debris.[35] Rab8, a small GTPase, is vital for vesicle trafficking and membrane dynamics, ensuring the correct formation, movement, and fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes. The variant p.(A93P) in the optineurin protein may disrupt its interaction with Rab8, as suggested by the software Mutation Taster. This disruption could impair the autophagic flux, leading to the accumulation of damaged cellular components and increased oxidative stress [Fig. 3b] in RGCs. The elevated oxidative stress can lead to an increase in the susceptibility of RGCs to further injury.[39] In the context of glaucoma, such disruptions result in the degeneration of RGCs, a characteristic feature of the disease. The most common variant in this domain is p.(E50K), observed in familial cases of NTG. Studies using E50K transgenic mice and E50K OPTN-overexpressing RGC-5 cells have shown that impaired autophagy is linked to the death of retinal ganglion cells.[40]

  In our investigation, we identified the p.(Q518L) variant in patient P16, who exhibits significantly elevated IOP of 49 mm Hg in the right eye and 38 mm Hg in the left eye [Table 2]. The patient’s current visual acuity is perception of light negative (PL-ve) in the right eye and 6/36 in the left eye. According to predictions from the Mutation Taster software, this variant is likely to disrupt the interaction between optineurin and myosin VI. Myosin VI is an unconventional motor protein that moves toward the minus end of actin filaments. It is involved in endocytic and exocytic pathways and plays a role in maintaining cellular organization and function. Myosin VI has been shown to interact with optineurin protein, suggesting a collaborative role in cellular processes relevant to glaucoma.[41] Optineurin mediates the recruitment of myosin VI to autophagosomes, facilitating their movement along the actin cytoskeleton. This interaction is crucial for the efficient delivery of autophagosomes to lysosomes, where cellular components undergo degradation. This variant can interfere with the interaction of optineurin protein with myosin VI, impairing autophagic flux and resulting in cellular dysfunction and death [Fig. 3b]. Disruption of autophagy leads to the accumulation of damaged proteins and organelles, thereby exacerbating neurodegenerative processes.

  The variants such as p.(Q518L) and p.(G538E) were observed in patients P16 and P85, respectively. Patient P85 presents with significantly elevated IOP, measuring 28.4 mmHg/30.4 mmHg in the right and left eye, respectively, alongside markedly impaired vision, recorded at 3/60 in both eyes [Table 2]. These specific alterations in the OPTN gene compromise its capacity to interact with huntingtin, as predicted by the Mutation Taster software. The interaction between optineurin and htt protein is vital for preserving cellular operations, particularly the structural integrity of the Golgi apparatus and proper vesicular transport of proteins. Disruption of this interaction results in Golgi complex fragmentation, which subsequently impedes the vesicular protein transport throughout the cell [Fig. 3b]. Efficient protein transport is critical for cellular homeostasis and function. Consequently, impaired vesicular transport leads to cellular dysfunction, stress, and potential damage.[42]

  In summary, defects in Golgi apparatus-mediated trafficking due to the pathogenic variants in optineurin, comprising its binding with the vesicle trafficking proteins like Rab8, myosin VI, and huntingtin, contribute significantly to the pathogenesis of glaucoma by disrupting protein homeostasis, leading to cellular stress and apoptosis in RGCs. Research into these mechanisms has the potential to develop targeted treatments to prevent or slow the progression of glaucoma.


## Conclusion
  This study is the first to document the involvement of OPTN gene variants in JOAG patients from northern India, an area previously unexplored for such associations. We identified a total of 27 OPTN variants, with three classified as potentially pathogenic by various pathogenicity prediction tools. While OPTN variants appear infrequent in this North Indian cohort, our findings highlight the importance of further large-scale studies using whole-exome sequencing across diverse Indian and global populations. The identification of the novel OPTN gene variant, p.(Q518L), not only advances our understanding of the genetic factors underlying JOAG but also holds promise for improving early diagnosis and facilitating individualized management. Genetic screening may be implemented as a tool for clinicians to identify at-risk individuals, thereby enabling earlier and more precise interventions that could reduce the burden of glaucomatous damage in this patient population.
