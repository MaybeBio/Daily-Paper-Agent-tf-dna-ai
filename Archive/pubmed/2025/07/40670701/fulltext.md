# Identifying the crucial oncogenic mechanisms of DDX56 based on a machine learning-based integration model of RNA-binding proteins


## Abstract
  RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) play a fundamental role in cellular metabolism, with their disturbance leading to large-scale transcriptomic dysregulation. RBP dysregulation is highly prevalent in human cancers; however, its role in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) has not been systematically investigated. To establish a more effective and robust risk model, a machine learning integration program was used to screen hub prognostic RBPs. Our risk model C-index performed extremely well among 103 published signatures. The high-risk group had a lower immune score and worse immunotherapy effects. As one of the members of the RNA helicase family, DDX56 can interact with certain transcription factors, thereby regulating the expression of its downstream targets. DDX56 exerts an anti-apoptotic effect and reduces the sensitivity to carboplatin treatment by promoting Bcl-2 transcription in LUAD cells. Additionally, DDX56 activates NF-kB signaling pathways, which may be related to DDX56-mediated promotion of Bcl-2 transcription, proliferation, migration, and invasion in LUAD patients.


## Introduction
  Post-transcriptional gene regulation (PTGR) includes RNA maturation (splicing, editing, polyadenylation, etc.), localization, stability, translation, and degradation, which is the core of gene expression1–3. Increasing evidence has demonstrated that PTGR disorders, such as aberrant alternative splicing and changes in RNA stability, are key to cancer development4,5. Meanwhile, these disorders often result in a reduced therapeutic effect and poor prognosis in cancer patients. RNA-binding proteins (RBPs), as special functional proteins, have a crucial regulatory role in various RNA metabolism processes6,7. Because RBP dysregulation is the key condition for PTGR disorder occurrence8, conducting systematic research on RBP expression in cancer patients is extremely necessary.

  Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths globally9. Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most common pathological type of lung cancer, with a 5-year survival rate of only approximately 15%10,11. Considerable progress has been made in chemoradiation, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy, but effective treatments for LUAD patients remain lacking12,13. In recent years, RBP dysregulation in LUAD patients has gradually attracted the attention of researchers, but it has been explored to a limited extent. Therefore, developing RBP-related prognostic biomarkers for LUAD treatment is exceptionally promising and necessary.

  The big data screening ability and accuracy of machine learning can be used to discover more promising therapeutic targets for LUAD14. However, LUAD-related research is lacking. Therefore, we conducted a comprehensive study on RBP-related genes involved in LUAD and the potential mechanisms through which they affect tumor progression, to discover more efficacious therapeutic targets and better guide clinical treatment. In this study, machine learning was employed for the systematic screening of the data from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) based on RBP-related genes. This technique calculated the C-index of 72 prediction model combinations of five public datasets and screened the genes with greater prognostic prediction values for the follow-up analysis. Subsequently, a promising prognostic risk model was established. Four LUAD GEO cohorts were used to validate the prediction performance of the model for survival. The risk model was significantly related to the TNM stage, tumor microenvironment (TME) differences, and response to immunotherapy. Finally, the levels of the hub gene DEAD-box helicase 56 (DDX56), an RNA helicase family member, were significantly upregulated in LUAD tissues compared with normal tissues, which predicted poor prognosis. The high expression of this gene was associated with the disorder of the immune microenvironment and tumor immune escape in LUAD. DDX56 promotes LUAD progression and reduces patients’ sensitivity to platinum-based drugs by regulating Bcl-2 and NF-kB pathways. Therefore, DDX56 can serve as a LUAD oncogene and is a promising therapeutic target.


## Results

### Identification and analysis of prognosis-associated differentially expressed RBPs
  We determined differentially expressed RBPs in LUAD tissues by analyzing differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (|logFC| > 5, adjusted p-value < 0.001) between LUAD and normal lung tissues in the TCGA database and GSE7670 (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B). Subsequently, these DEGs intersected with 2334 RBPs obtained from the previous studies6,7, which resulted in 115 RBPs with extremely significant differences in expression (Fig. 1A). Supplementary data lists these RBPs. By performing the univariate COX regression analysis of these 115 RBPs, 33 prognosis-associated RBPs (p < 0.05) were identified (Fig. 1B). Additionally, gene ontology and Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes enrichment analyses were performed on these 33 RBPs. The significantly enriched GO-terms included “cadherin binding,” “translation regulatory activity,” and “RNA catabolic process” (Supplementary Fig. 1C). The KEGG terms were primarily enriched in “glycolysis/gluconeogenesis,” “amino acid biosynthesis,” and “HIF-1 signaling pathway” (Supplementary Fig. 1D). The results of differential expression analysis of these 33 RBPs are illustrated in Fig. 1C. By combining the results of the expression correlation and univariate COX regression analyses, the comprehensive crosstalk network of these 33 RBPs was displayed (Fig. 1D). ### Construction and validation of a prognostic risk model associated with RBPs in LUAD
  In total, 33 prognosis-related differentially expressed RBPs were subjected to the machine learning ensemble algorithm to develop key RBPs and establish more stable and accurate prognostic models. Overall, 10 machine learning algorithms and 72 algorithm combinations were executed in the TCGA-LUAD training cohort and the GEO-LUAD validation cohort. We then calculated the C-index of each model across all datasets (Fig. 2A). Notably, the optimal model was a Lasso and SuperPC combination with the highest average C-index (0.679). In the Lasso regression, the optimal λ (λ = 13) was achieved when the partial likelihood of deviance was the minimum (Fig. 2B, C). Finally, 13 hub RBPs were identified through LASSO and SuperPC screening, namely SNRPE, SMG7, EIF6, GAPDH, PNRC2, DDX56, PABPC1, EIF4G1, AKAP1, PKM, CKAP4, LDHA, and PEBP1. The multivariate COX regression analysis was performed using these 13 key RBPs in the TCGA and GEO cohorts. A risk score was calculated for each patient by using their regression coefficients and the expression of these 13 RBPs (Refer to Eq.(1) in the “Methods” section). In the training and validation cohorts, the patients were divided into high- and low-risk groups based on the median risk score. As illustrated in Fig. 2D–H, the overall survival rate was significantly lower in the high-risk patients than in the low-risk patients (all p < 0.05). ### Evaluation of the RBP-based prognostic risk model
  According to the ROC analysis, the performance of the model for predicting overall survival in the LUAD patients from the training and validation cohorts was excellent (Fig. 3A; Supplementary Fig. 2A–F). The C-index [95% confidence interval] values were 0.728 [0.657–0.8], 0.772 [0.635–0.909], 0.893 [0.793–0.993], 0.855 [0.752–0.959], 0.751 [0.635–0.866], and 0.675 [0.622–0.728] for the six cohorts, respectively (Fig. 3B). Meanwhile, the risk score correlated with survival time and status (Supplementary Fig. 2G) and could serve as an independent prognostic factor, according to the univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses (Supplementary Fig. 2I, J). Additionally, based on the heatmap, the risk score significantly correlated with several clinical characteristics (gender, stage, and T and N stages) (Supplementary Fig. 2H). Furthermore, the performance of our risk model in predicting prognosis was compared with that of other clinical and molecular variables (Fig. 3C). Our risk model exhibited better predictive performance than the other variables, including age, gender, TNM, stage, smoking, gene alteration (EGFR, KRAS, or ALK), and MYC expression. Finally, our RBP-based model was compared with 103 published gene signatures (Fig. 4). Of note, our model had a higher C-index than most signatures in different datasets. Although the model performed relatively poorly in one dataset (GSE30219), its C-index continued to rank at the top. To verify the effect of the model on other LUAD molecular subtypes, TP53, KRAS, LKB1, and EGFR mutant LUAD patients were isolated. The KM survival analysis and ROC results indicated that the RBP risk model can well predict the OS of all four LUAD molecular subtypes (Supplementary Fig. 3). ### Correlation analysis of the prognostic model with gene mutation and drug sensitivity
  To investigate the correlation between the risk score and somatic mutations, the mutations of the top 20 most frequently mutated genes were analyzed in the LUAD patients in the high- and low-risk groups. The frequency of somatic mutations was higher in the high-risk group than in the low-risk group (Fig. 5A, B). The GSEA results revealed that the high-risk group was significantly enriched in terms such as “CELL_CYCLE” and “REGULATION_OF_RESPONSE_TO_DNA_DAMAGE_STIMULUS”(Fig. 5C, D). These enrichment score changes possibly further suggested the reason for the higher mutation rate in the high-risk group. The “oncoPredict” package was used to analyze the sensitivity of the LUAD patients to chemotherapeutic agents to determine the possible application value of the risk score in drug prediction. The sensitivity of 17 drugs, such as JQ1, PRIMA-1MET, and Niraparib, was markedly higher in the high-risk group than in the low-risk group, where the low-risk group was more sensitive to Erlotinib than the high-risk group (Fig. 5E; Supplementary Fig. 4A–R). The GSVA was performed to determine the enrichment scores of tumor hallmarks. ### The ability of the prognostic model to evaluate TME and predict immunotherapy efficacy
  To further explore the correlation between risk scores and the TME, the constituent cellular components of the TME were analyzed. A significant negative correlation was observed between the risk score and the TME immune score. This suggested that the abundance of tumor-infiltrating immune cells decreased in the high-risk group (Fig. 6A). The number of some immune cells including infiltrating dendritic cells, B cells, mast cells, and some CD4+ T cells significantly reduced in the high-risk group (Fig. 6B), which indicated that the TME of these high-risk patients had a “cold” immunophenotype. These results were supported by the inverse correlation between risk scores and immune-activating genes (Fig. 6D). The high-risk group had lower levels of T cell dysfunction, higher levels of T cell exclusion and MDSCs, and higher TIDE scores based on the TIDE algorithm (Fig. 6C). Subsequently, the potential association between risk subtypes and the efficacy of immunotherapy was investigated. The IPS scores were lower in the high-risk group than in the low-risk group (Fig. 6E). ### DDX56 could be a prognostic biomarker of LUAD
  We selected DDX56 with the largest hazard ratio in the risk model for further study. DDX56 is significantly highly expressed in various cancers, including LUAD (Fig. 7A, B). Patients with DDX56 high expression had lower overall survival rates than those with low DDX56 expression (Fig. 7C). According to the ROC analysis, DDX56 had excellent performance in predicting 1-, 3 -, and 5-year survival rates (Fig. 7D). The DDX56 expression level was also significantly correlated with the cancer and N stages (Fig. 7E). Notably, the univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses confirmed that DDX56 can be used as an independent prognostic factor in LUAD (Fig. 7F, G). In our validation cohort, qRT-PCR was used to analyze the relative DDX56 expression in 30 LUAD tissue samples from Shandong Provincial Hospital. Because survival information of one of the samples was lacking, DDX56 expression information from the remaining 29 samples was used for the survival analysis. Consistent with the aforementioned findings, the DDX56 high-expression group had worse survival outcomes (Fig. 7H). The ROC curve unveiled the excellent performance of DDX56 in predicting the prognosis of LUAD patients (Fig. 7I). ### Effect of DDX56 on the immune regulation of the TME
  To identify whether high-DDX56 expression changes the immune microenvironment and immunotherapy effect of LUAD, the effect of DDX56 on the TME of LUAD was further studied. DDX56 expression was negatively correlated with the tumor stromal score and immune score, according to the ESTIMATE analysis (Supplementary Fig. 5C). The DDX56 high-expression group had higher TIDE and T cell exclusion scores and lower T cell dysfunction scores (Fig. 8A–C). According to the ssGSEA analysis results, DDX56 expression was markedly negatively associated with immune-related factors such as “APC_co_stimulation,” “Type_II_IFN_Reponse,” “T_helper_cells,” and “dendritic cells” (Fig. 8D). Moreover, DDX56 expression negatively correlated with immune checkpoint-related genes (Supplementary Fig. 5D). These results indicate that high-DDX56 expression inhibits antigen presentation in the TME and mediates the immune escape of tumor cells, which suggests that the LUAD patients with low DDX56 expression benefit from immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy. To identify the difference in the DDX56 expression distribution between normal and tumor tissues, single-cell data of GSE229253 and GSE13955 were analyzed. The DDX56 expression pattern at the single-cell resolution revealed that DDX56 expression was significantly higher in the epithelial cells and T cells of LUAD than in the normal lung tissue (Fig. 9A–C). Through further analysis and cell annotation, we found that DDX56 expression was significantly higher in the CD4+ effector memory T cells in the LUAD patients than in the normal tissues (Fig. 9D–F). ### DDX56 boosts the malignant processes of LUAD cells in vitro

The results exhibited that DDX56 knockdown remarkably inhibited LUAD cell proliferation compared with the control group. ### DDX56 transcriptionally upregulates Bcl-2 and activates the NF-kB pathway
  Based on our findings, we reviewed some published literature. Our review revealed that the resistance of human tumors to platinum drugs is closely linked to the expression of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-215–18. Western blotting demonstrated that the Bcl-2 protein level decreased after DDX56 knockdown in the LUAD cell lines (Fig. 13A, B). DDX56 is an RNA helicase family member, which is characterized by the regulation of various processes from gene transcription to transcriptional regulation and degradation19,20. Thus, RT-PCR was performed, and DDX56 knockdown was found to reduce Bcl-2 mRNA levels (Fig. 13C, D). Based on previous studies, nucleic acid-binding proteins can interact with certain transcription factors and thereby regulate the transcription of their downstream targets21. Therefore, DDX56 was speculated to upregulate Bcl-2 transcription. Consistent results were obtained when DDX56 knockdown was followed by the addition of the proteasome inhibitor (MG132) and autophagolysosome inhibitor (CQ) to block the protein degradation pathway (Fig. 13E, F). This suggests that DDX56 promotes Bcl-2 upregulation without affecting protein stability. Bcl-2 transcription is regulated by the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-kB) in human cancers22–24, so we then investigated whether DDX56 exerts a regulatory effect on P65 activation. According to Western blotting results, the phosphorylated P65 (P-P65) protein content decreased after DDX56 knockdown (Fig. 13G, I). Additionally, studies have exhibited that TNFαand vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)A can serve as downstream target genes of the NF-kB pathway25,26. Therefore, through RT-PCR, we demonstrated that TNFα and VEGFA mRNA expression decreased in the LUAD cells with DDX56 knockdown (Fig. 13H, J). ## Discussion
  RBPs are central to cell metabolism. They are somewhat conserved during evolution and exhibit highly dynamic spatiotemporal regulation. Their dysregulation can cause a large-scale regulatory imbalance of the transcriptome, which results in abnormal protein phenotypes and promotes cancer occurrence. Growing evidence has confirmed that RBPs are highly expressed in various human malignant tumors and are key players in the progression of malignant tumors, including hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)27, gastric cancer28, colorectal cancer29, and breast cancer30. Therefore, to identify novel and effective treatment strategies for LUAD and improve the overall prognosis of affected patients, the potential carcinogenic mechanism of RBPs in LUAD must be comprehensively explored to discover new therapeutic targets.

  To identify RBPs associated with LUAD development and progression, the gene expression profiles of LUAD were analyzed in public databases. In total, 33 RBPs exhibiting extremely significant differences in expression were identified compared with those in normal lung tissues. These RBPs were remarkably correlated with the prognosis of LUAD patients. Their overall mutation frequency was not high, which is also related to their conservation.

  To establish a more stable and greater application potential of the LUAD prognostic model, an ensemble algorithm of machine learning was used on the aforementioned 33 RBPs. The advantage of machine learning is that it can fit a model with consistent performance for LUAD prognosis, reduce the dimension of the variables, and simplify the model. We then executed 10 machine learning algorithms and their 72 algorithm combinations on the training cohorts and four validation cohorts. Finally, Lasso + SuperPC was determined to exhibit the highest average C-index. Thirteen key RBPs, used to develop a prognostic model, were then identified. In this model, the high-risk group had lower overall survival than the low-risk group. Our risk model could be distinguished from other clinical traits as an independent prognostic factor and maintained the highest C-index compared with other clinical variables. We also investigated the relationship between the risk model and the TME. The immune score was significantly lower in the high-risk group than in the low-risk group, suggesting that the TME of the high-risk group had a “cold” immunophenotype. Consistent with the aforementioned results, the risk score negatively correlated with the infiltrating demeanor of B cells, mast cells, and CD4+T cells. Meanwhile, the low-risk group was more likely to benefit from IPS- and TIDE score-based immunotherapy. The MDSC infiltration was markedly higher in the high-risk group than in the low-risk group, which may be a reason for the “cold” immunophenotypes in the high-risk group. The sensitivity to 17 chemotherapeutic drugs was significantly higher in the high-risk patients than in the low-risk patients. These results can offer guidance for the clinical treatment of LUAD; that is, patients in the low-risk group can benefit from immunotherapy, whereas those in the high-risk group can select highly sensitive drugs such as JQ1, PRIMA-1MET, and Niraparib.

  DDX56, an RNA helicase, is highly expressed in various human cancers and is closely related to tumorigenesis and progression. The change in the DDX56 copy number and low DNA methylation may be related to the abnormally high expression of DDX56. This can promote tumor cell proliferation, inhibit cell apoptosis, induce drug resistance, and affect immune cell infiltration31. In HCC, DDX56 promotes HCC proliferation through mono-methylation of H3K9 (H3K9me1) on the MIST1 promoter, which leads to improved MIST1 transcription and subsequently the regulation of the PTEN/AKT signaling pathway32. Furthermore, DDX56 promotes alternative splicing of the G2-M DNA damage checkpoint WEE1, which is associated with lymphatic invasion and distant metastasis of colorectal cancer33. Despite the demonstrated critical role of DDX56 in various human malignancies, the role of DDX56 in LUAD progression needs to be elucidated. Using 29 LUAD tissue samples from Shandong Provincial Hospital, the relationship between DDX56 expression and patient prognosis was verified. High-DDX56 expression was significantly associated with poor prognosis in the LUAD patients. The single-cell data analysis exhibited that DDX56 expression in T cells, especially CD4+ effector memory T cells, was significantly higher in the tumor tissues than in the normal tissues. Consistent with these results, the T cell exclusion score was significantly higher in patients with high-DDX56 expression. Therefore, we hypothesized that high-DDX56 expression in the T cells of tumor tissues was among the reasons for the disorder of the immune microenvironment of tumor tissues and the immune escape of tumor cells. This resulted in the worst effect of immunotherapy in LUAD patients with high-DDX56 expression. However, the link between DDX56 expression in T cells and immune escape remains largely correlative based on computational analyses. There is a lack of direct functional experiments to mechanistically prove the causal role of DDX56 in T cell dysfunction or exclusion within the LUAD TME, which will be further verified in subsequent studies.

  Our study confirmed that high-DDX56 expression reduces the sensitivity of LUAD cells to platinum drugs by suppressing cancer cell apoptosis. DDX56 expression, therefore, may result in platinum drug resistance in LUAD patients, but this needs to be supported by more rigorous proof. This study found that DDX56 promotes Bcl-2 transcription, which may be among the reasons for DDX56-mediated inhibition of LUAD cell apoptosis. P65 phosphorylation at Ser536 promotes nuclear translocation of P65 in esophageal cancer, thereby serving as a transcription factor that binds to the promoters of VEGF-c and Bcl-2, thereby promoting angiogenesis and inhibiting apoptosis23. Finally, DDX56 was found to promote P65 phosphorylation at Ser536 in the LUAD cells, which was possibly related to DDX56 promoting Bcl-2 transcription and cell proliferation and inhibiting cell apoptosis. These encouraging findings will help researchers have a deeper understanding of the effect of DDX56 on cancer promotion.

  In summary, RBPs are a massive family. An extensive systematic study of these proteins in various human cancers is a crucial and long step toward their future exploration. While this study identifies a prognostic RBP signature and highlights DDX56 as a potential therapeutic target in LUAD, several limitations warrant consideration. These data are retrospective and rely on computational inferences based on TME and drug responses. Further validation of the impact of RBP dysregulation on the treatment and prognosis of LUAD patients is needed through a larger clinical validation cohort. Moreover, this study only examined a subset of prognostic RBPs in LUAD. The effect of DDX56 on the immune microenvironment and cell interaction in LUAD patients, as well as the deeper mechanism of cell proliferation and apoptosis, needs to be explored in the future.

  In brief, we developed an RBP risk model in LUAD patients, which accurately predicted their prognosis and evaluated the efficacy of chemotherapy and immunotherapy in these patients. Additionally, DDX56 was found to be highly expressed in LUAD tissues, which was associated with poor prognosis and the immune escape of tumor cells. DDX56, an oncogene of LUAD, promotes LUAD cell proliferation and inhibits their apoptosis by transcriptionally upregulating Bcl-2 and activating the NF-kB pathway. DDX56 may serve as a promising molecular target for LUAD treatment.


## Methods

### Data source and preprocessing
  The gene expression information of 1147 LUAD patients from six public data cohorts were included in this study as training and validation cohorts (TCGA-LUAD cohort, n = 594; GSE7670 cohort, n = 57; GSE3141 cohort, n = 58; GSE31210 cohort, n = 226; GSE30219 cohort, n = 85; GSE50081 cohort, n = 127). The mRNA expression data, clinical information, and genomic mutation data of the TCGA-LUAD patients were downloaded from https://cancergenome.nih.gov/. Genomic information and clinical data of the GEO-LUAD patients, including GSE7670, GSE3141, GSE31210, GSE30219, and GSE50081, were collected from https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/. Based on the gene mutation status obtained from the cBioPortal database (https://www.cbioportal.org), we isolated the TP53, KRAS, LKB1, and EGFR mutant types from the TCGA-LUAD tumor samples.

  Furthermore, 29 LUAD tissue samples from Shandong Provincial Hospital were used to verify the relationship between DDX56 expression and patient prognosis. This study was reviewed and approved by the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee of Shandong Provincial Hospital (Ethical Approval Number: SWYX2024-464). The ethics committee has approved the exemption of informed consent for the following reasons. This project only involves the study of past medical records, and there is no intervention or handling of the subjects during the process. During the follow-up process, the subjects have been informed that the prognosis information can be used in clinical research. Furthermore, this project is a retrospective study and does not make any intervention in the rights or health of the participants. This study does not disclose any personal privacy or identity information of the participants.


### Identification and prognosis analysis of differentially expressed RBPs in LUAD
  In total, 2334 RBPs were obtained from previous studies6,7. Supplementary data presents the list of these RBPs. The “limma” package in R was used to perform differential expression analysis of RBP mRNA levels in the TCGA-LUAD and GSE7670-LUAD patients with |logFC| > 5 and an adjusted p-value of <0.001. Following the intersection with the RBPs, differentially expressed RBPs were obtained. The prognosis-associated RBPs were identified through univariate Cox regression analysis with p < 0.05.


### Construction of a prognostic risk model based on the machine learning ensemble algorithm
  To develop more critical RBPs and establish a more accurate and stable prognostic risk model, 10 machine learning algorithms34 and 72 algorithm combinations were implemented. These algorithms included Lasso, Supervised Principal Component (SuperPC), survival support vector machine, random survival forest, stepwise Cox, partial least squares regression for Cox, elastic network, Ridge, CoxBoost, and generalized boosted regression modeling. Based on the leave-one-out cross-validation framework, the 72 algorithm combinations were performed on prognosis-related RBPs to obtain hub RBPs. The TCGA-LUAD cohort was used as the training cohort. All models were detected in the four GEO validation cohorts (GSE3141, GSE31210, GSE30219, and GSE50081). For each model, Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) was calculated using the training and validation data cohorts. The model with the highest average C-index was considered optimal. Subsequently, executing the “survival” package, multivariable Cox regression analysis was performed with these hub RBPs to construct a prognostic risk model. ### Validation of a hub RBP-based prognostic risk model
  Based on the median risk score, patients in the training and validation cohorts were divided into high- and low-risk groups. The “survival” and “survminer” software packages were executed to generate Kaplan–Meier (K–M) curves. The “timeROC” software package was used to generate the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) and calculate the 1-, 3-, and 5-year area under the curve. Moreover, the “survcomp” package was implemented to calculate the C-index of risk models, age, sex, TNM stage, smoking status, and genetic alterations in the two datasets.


### Genomic mutation analysis
  The mutation data of the LUAD patients were downloaded from the TCGA database and processed using the “maftools” package. The mutation status of prognosis-related RBPs and the top 20 genes with mutation probability in risk subtypes were analyzed.


### Analysis of therapeutic response
  The “oncoPredict” package was executed to calculate the IC50 values of 198 drugs in the TCGA-LUAD patients and analyze differences in drug sensitivity between the high- and low-risk patient groups35. The immunophenoscore (IPS) of the TCGA-LUAD patients obtained from The Cancer Immunome Atlas (https://tcia.at/) was used to predict their immunotherapy sensitivity. Data about TIDE scores, T cell dysfunction, T cell exclusion, and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) were obtained from the TIDE website (https://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/).


### TME analysis
  The TME of the LUAD patients and their stromal, immune, and ESTIMATE scores were evaluated using the ESTIMATE algorithm. Based on risk scores, the xCell36, TIMER37, quanTIseq38, MCPcounter39, EPIC40, CIBERSORT−ABS, and CIBERSORT41 algorithms were employed for further analyzing tumor-infiltrating immune cells and stromal cells.


### Cell culture
  Human PC9 and A549 cell lines were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd., and cultured in RPMI 1640 and F12K medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, respectively. Both cell lines were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2.


### Small-interfering RNA transfection
  DDX56 short-interfering RNAs (siRNAs) and control-siRNA were purchased from Keyybio (Shandong, China). The jetPRIME® Transfection Reagent (PolyPlus Transfection, Illkirch, France) was employed for siRNA transfection. Supplementary Table 1 lists DDX56 siRNA sequences.


### RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR
  Total RNA was extracted from the LUAD cells by using the AG RNAex Pro Reagent (Accurate Biotechnology (Hunan) Co., Ltd., China). The reverse transcription kit (Accurate Biotechnology (Hunan) Co., Ltd.) was used for cDNA synthesis. The SYBR Premix Ex Taq Kit (Accurate Biotechnology (Hunan) Co., Ltd.) was used to perform quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR). RNA quality was assessed and normalized based on human 18S rRNA. Supplementary Table 2 presents the list of primer sequences used.


### Western blotting
  RIPA buffer was used to extract the total protein from the cells. The BCA reagent (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was used to determine the protein concentration. Next, equal amounts of the total protein extract were loaded into a 10% SDS-PAGE gel and transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane. After the membranes were blocked with 5% BSA solution for 1 h at room temperature, they were incubated with the primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The next day, the secondary antibody (Beyotime; 1:1000) was incubated with the membranes at room temperature for 1 h. The proteins were visualized through enhanced chemiluminescence. The primary antibodies were as follows: P65 and P-P65 (Ser536) (Cell Signaling Technology, USA: 1:1000); DDX56 and GAPDH (Santa Cruz, USA: 1:1000); Bcl-2 (Proteintech Group, USA: 1:1000).


### Cell proliferation assay
  After the cells were plated, 10% cold trichloroacetic acid was used to fix them for at least 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were stained with sulfonamide B sodium salt (Sigma, USA) for 20 min and then washed with 1% (vol/vol) acetic acid. After the cells were dried, 150 μL of 10 mmol/L Tris was added to the cells. A microplate reader (Thermo Fisher, USA) was used to measure the cell absorbance at 562 nm. To analyze cell proliferation, 96-well plates were seeded at a density of 3000 cells/well and fixed with trichloroacetic acid every 12 h. All data were normalized to those of day 1 and expressed as the mean ± SD. All experiments were performed in 10repeats.


### Transwell assay
  The Transwell experiment was conducted on the Transwell plate. First, 200 μL of FBS-free cell suspension (4 × 104 cells) was filled in the upper chamber, and 600 μL of 20% FBS culture medium was filled in the lower chamber. After the cells were incubated for 24 h, those in the lower chamber were fixed and stained with crystal violet. After the cells were photographed, ImageJ software was employed for cell counting.


### EdU incorporation assay
  The cells were seeded into 12-well plates. When the cells reached 50% confluence, the BeyoClick™ EdU Cell Proliferation Kit containing Alexa Fluor 594 (Beyotime) was used to determine EdU incorporation rates.


### Wound healing assay
  The cells were seeded into 12-well plates. When the cells attained 95% confluence, a pipette tip was used to scratch the cell monolayer. The cells were subsequently photographed every 24 h. Changes in the scratched area were calculated using ImageJ software.


### Cell apoptosis analysis
  The adherent cells were digested with trypsin, centrifuged (300 × g, 4 °C for 5 min), collected, and washed twice with pre-cooled PBS at 4 °C (centrifuged under the aforementioned conditions each time). The Annexin V-FITC/PI (annexin v-fluorescein isothiocyanate/propidium iodide (FITC/PI)) Apoptosis Kit was used for cell staining. A binding buffer (1×) was used to resuspend the cells before Annexin V-FITC/PI was added to stain and incubate the cells according to the reagent-specific protocol. Flow cytometry (FCM) was performed to determine apoptosis, with the results analyzed using FlowJo v10.


### Bioinformatic analysis of DDX56
  The single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was conducted to calculate the enrichment scores of the immune function and compare them between the DDX56 high and low expression groups. Data from the TIMER database (https://timer.comp-genomics.org/timer/) were used to conduct the pan-cancer differential expression analysis. The TIDE score difference analysis, K–M survival curve analysis, ROC curve analysis, independent prognosis determination, and clinical correlation analysis were performed in the R package. Data from single-cell RNA sequencing of LUAD patient tumors and their paired normal lung tissues were obtained from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The “Seurat” package in the R language was employed to determine the expression patterns of single cells, and the “SingleR” package was used for cell annotation.


### Statistical analysis
  R and GraphPad Prism 9.0 software were used to perform statistical analysis. The t-test was conducted to analyze the statistical significance of quantitative data. The Wilcoxon rank sum test was applied for non-normal distribution variables. Statistical significance was compared between more than two groups by performing the Kruskal–Wallis test and one-way analysis. The R package “survminer” was implemented to identify the difference in the KM survival analysis. p < 0.05 was considered the threshold for statistical significance.
