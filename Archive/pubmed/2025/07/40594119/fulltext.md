# Hydroxytyrosol induced ferroptosis through Nrf2 signaling pathway in colorectal cancer cells


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-04415-4.


## Introduction
  The incidence of colorectal cancer (CRC) is increasing and affects younger people. Globally, there were approximately 900,000 CRC-related deaths and 1.92 million additional cases in 20221. The main treatments are surgery, chemoradiotherapy and immunotherapy, but the prognosis is poor and drug resistance and toxic side effects reduce the effectiveness of these treatments2. Therefore, the discovery of novel drugs and the proposal of new therapeutic strategies for CRC are particularly important.

  Ferroptosis refers to a programmed cell death process that requires iron, which differs from apoptosis, pyroptosis, and programmed necrosis. The essential process is that an iron surplus activates the Fenton reaction, resulting in excessive ROS, and causing damage to GPX4 and SLC7A11, resulting in deepening lipid peroxidation damage to the cell membrane until the cell membrane ruptures and cell death occurs3. Recent research has indicated that ferroptosis in many types of tumor cells, including CRC cells, can effectively kill tumor cells, and this strategy is increasingly being used in the clinical treatment of cancer4.

  HT is a polyphenolic compound derived mainly from olives5. It has attracted the attention of many scientists because of its powerful antioxidant6, anti-tumor7, anti-inflammatory8, cardiovascular protective9 and other pharmacological effects. Ferroptosis is intimately connected to the to the imbalance of oxidative stress in the body, and Nrf2 plays a key role in regulating ferroptosis10. HT has potent antioxidant activity and can promote ROS generation by inhibiting the nuclear translocation of Nrf2, thereby inhibiting CRC cell proliferation and inducing apoptosis. In addition, the silencing of mammalian TrxR1, a downstream target gene of Nrf2, significantly inhibited ROS generation11. Moreover, natural plant extracts such as HT have been shown to exert significant anti-tumour activity via the ferroptosis pathway. Studies have shown that ginsenosides induce ferroptosis in HT-29 and HCT116 CRC cells through activation of the ROS/P53 pathway12. Curcumin has also been shown to induce ferroptosis in HCT-8 CRC cells exhibiting characteristics of ROS overload13. Another study showed that copper ions play important roles in the early stages and development of cancer14, and HT interacts with copper to reduce the invasiveness of MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-468 and SUM159 cells by decreasing copper ion levels15.

  Herein, we found that HT can induce ferroptosis in CRC cells. Based on the early results from molecular docking and network pharmacology, we explored the mechanism and action mode of HT-induced ferroptosis in CRC cells through in vitro studies. Our experimental results revealed that HT-treated HCT116 and SW480 cells exhibited obvious ferroptosis features, including significant production of ROS and lipid peroxides, decreased mitochondrial membrane potential and typical mitochondrial morphological damage. Mechanistically, HT induced ferroptosis in HCT116 and SW480 cells via the Nrf2 pathway, and our current investigation proposes a possible therapeutic strategy for CRC.


## Materials and methods

### Cell culture
  The cultivation of HCT116 cells was performed via McCoy’s 5A medium (Pricella, Wuhan, China), and SW480 cells were maintained in high-glucose DMEM medium (Solarbio, Beijing, China), supplemented with 10% serum (HyClone, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Solarbio, Beijing, China). The cells were cultured in a cell culture incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

  The culture conditions used in this study refer to ATCC standardised recommendations for colorectal cancer cells (HCT116: ATCC® CCL-247™; SW480 ATCC®CCL-228™), and the treatment time (48 h) was consistent with the literature to ensure comparable results16.


### Drugs and reagents
  HT (CAS: 10597-60-1; SN: S382602; purity: 99.92%), NAC(N-Acetyl-L-cysteine)(CAS: 616-91-1; SN: S162305; purity: 99.95%), Fer-1 (CAS: 347174-05-4; SN: S724303; purity: 99.96%), SFN (CAS 4478-93-7; SN: S577103; purity: 99.21%), and CDDO (CAS: 218600-44-3; SN: S664701; purity: 98.45%) were all purchased from Selleck Company (Selleck, USA).


### Cyber pharmacology

#### Screening for HT, ferroptosis and CRC-related targets
  The “TCMSP(Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology) database” was searched with the keyword “hydroxytyrosol” to obtain 30 potential targets of HT; a search for “hydroxytyrosol” in the “PubChem database” yielded the sdf file of the 3D structure of HT, which was then entered into the “PharmMapper database” ,identifying 228 potential targets for HT. After removing the duplicates, 255 potential targets of HT were obtained, and these 255 targets were entered into the “UniProt database” to be converted into gene symbols. Using the keyword “colorectal cancer” as the search term, disease-related targets were retrieved from the disease databases Genecards (score > 1), OMIM(Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man), TTD(Therapeutic-Target-Database) and DisGeNET(Disease Gene Network) (score > 0.1), yielding 2,296 CRC-related targets after removing duplicates. A collection of 259 genes linked to ferroptosis was sourced from the “FerrDb database”. A Venn diagram was created using “Venny 2.1.0 online software” by inputting the relevant targets for HT, ferroptosis, and CRC, resulting in 14 overlapping genes.


#### Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks
  Fourteen overlapping genes were analysed via the “STRING database” to construct a PPI network. The species selected was ‘Homo sapiens’ and the minimum required interaction score > 0.4 was used as the selection criterion. We downloaded the .tsv file of the PPI network obtained above, imported it into Cytoscape 3.7.0 software and analysed the data using the CytoNCA plug-in, filtering the core targets according to the ‘degree’ greater than the median.


### Molecular docking technology
  The core targets were screened according to the PPI network and molecular docking was performed. We searched the PubChem database using the keyword “hydroxytyrosol” to obtain the sdf file of the 3D structure of HT, and then converted the sdf file of the 3D structure of HT to mol2 format using Open Babel software, and then converted the sdf file to PDBQT format using AutoDock 1.5.7 software. The sdf file of the 3D structure of HT was converted to mol2 format using Open Babel software and then converted to PDBQT format using AutoDock 1.5.7 software. First, the PDB database was used to filter the target proteins with the criteria “Reviewed” and “Human”. In the “Structure” column, ligand molecules with similar structure to HT, X-ray source and lower value of protein conformation were screened. The PDB file of the crystal structure of the ligand molecule obtained in the previous step was downloaded and saved. The PDB file was then opened with Pymol 2.5.2 software to remove water molecules and small ligands from the protein conformation, and then the resulting input was pre-processed with AutoDockTools 1.5.7 software for hydrogenation and other pre-processing, and the final file type was converted to PDBQT format. VINA 1.1.2 software was then used to dock the protein receptor and the small molecule of the compound, i.e. HT, and the binding energy was used to analyse the degree of binding of the drug HT to each protein receptor. Finally, the results were again visualised using Pymol 2.5.2 software.


### Cell growth curve assay
  The cells were placed in 96-well plates and cultured for 2 h until they attached to the walls. After adding CCK8 (Solarbio, Beijing, China), the mixture was incubated for 1 h to assess the optical density(OD) value using a microplate reader at 450 nm. Readings were taken every 12 h until reaching 108 h.


### Cell viability assay
  After being cultured overnight, the cells were exposed to varying levels of HT for 48 h (0, 50, 100, 150, 200 µM for HCT116 cells; 0, 40, 80, 160, 320 µM for SW480 cells). CCK8 was added to each well, and the absorbance at 450 nm was measured with a microplate reader.


### HT and NAC combination assay
  After being inoculated, the cells were cultured for 24 h and then treated with NAC at various concentrations (0, 1, 2, 4, and 8 mM) for 48 h, after which the change in the OD value at 450 nm was determined. Moreover, the samples were pretreated for 1 h with 3 mM NAC. The cells were exposed to different concentrations of HT, and the OD at 450 nm was measured after 48 h.


### HT and Fer-1 combination assay
  Logarithmic growth phase cells were grown in plates overnight, and then treated with Fer-1 (1 µM) for 2 h. Afterward, they were exposed to HT for 48 h. Each well received 10 µL of CCK8 solution and was incubated for 2 h. The OD value was measured at 450 nm.


### Clonogenic assay
  The cultivation of cells in 6-well plates lasted for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were exposed to HT treatment for 48 h, after which the complete medium was replaced, and the cells continued to incubate for 5 days. The cells were washed with Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBS) 2 to 3 times and subsequently fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 30 min. Following the removal of the fixative, the cells were treated with a 1% crystal violet ammonium oxalate solution (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 30 min, and the staining solution was slowly rinsed with tap water and observed under UV/white light conditions.


## Scratch assay
  The cells at a density of 80–90% were homogeneously inoculated into 6-well plates, and after 24 h, three straight lines were vertically scratched with the tip of a 10 µL gun. The detached cells were then removed by gentle rinsing with PBS and treated with HT culture medium containing 1% serum (0,40,80, and 160 µM for HCT116 cells; 0, 45, 90, and 180 µM for SW480 cells). The healing of the cells was observed and photographed under an inverted fluorescence microscope after 24 and 48 h.


### Phalloidin-FITC/DAPI staining assay
  Prepare cell crawls. Following a 48 h HT treatment, the cells were rinsed twice with PBS and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min. Twice washed with PBS, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 solution (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 5 min. Repeat the rinsing with PBS three times and then add 300 µL of FITC-phalloidin (Solarbio, Beijing, China) solution, incubate for 30 min in the dark, then wash three times with PBS, Add DAPI (4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole) staining solution (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 10 min and seal with glycerol. After incubation for 30 min and protection from light, the cells were washed three times with PBS, blocked with glycerol and observed under an inverted the fluorescence microscope.


### Transmission electron microscope observation
  The cells in the logarithmic phase were cultured on a wall for one day and then exposed to HT, followed by rinsing the cells with PBS, adding electron microscope fixative (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) and fixing them for 6 min at 37 °C. Next, the cells were scraped in one direction, and transferd to a centrifuge tube using a pasteur pipette and centrifuged at 200 g for 2 min, dispose of the fixative and add new electron microscope fixative to fix the cells for 30 min. Cells were fixed in 1% osmotic acid (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 2 h. The cells were subsequently washed with ultrapure water and dehydrated through an anhydrous ethanol gradient. Finally, the cells were embedded in 812, sectioned (5 μm) and stained with saturated uranyl acetate and lead citrate. They were then examined by means of transmission electron microscopy and photographed for archiving purposes.


### Measurement of cellular iron
  Cell iron content assay kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China) was used to measure cellular iron contents based on assay contains hydroxylamine hydrochloride, which reduces Fe3+ to Fe2+ in cells, and under weak acid conditions, Fe2+ and phenoline form a reddish-orange mixture, and the iron content can be calculated by measuring the OD value at 510 nm. Briefly, cells were incubated overnight in six-well plates and then treated with HT. After 48 h the cells were collected and the extracts were added, the cells were broken by sonication at 4 °C, and centrifuged at 8000 g for 10 min. Collect the supernatant and measure the absorbance at 510 nm.


### Measurement of reductive GSH
  Reduced GSH assay (Solarbio, Beijing, China) has been used to determine the iron content in cells based on the yellow 2-nitro-5-mercaptobenzoic acid produced by glutathione and 5,5’-dithiobis-2-nitrobenzoic acid (DTNB), which was detected at a wavelength of 412 nm. Cells were cultured overnight and treated with HT, and the absorbance value at 510 nm was determined with a microplate reader after 48 h. The cells were collected and broken by ultrasonication in an ice bath at 4 °C, and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min. Measure the absorbance of the supernatant at 412 nm.


### Measurement of LPO
  LPO was measured using a LPO content assay kit (Solebo, Beijing, China). LPO was heated under acidic conditions to produce malondialdehyde (MDA), which condensed with thiobarbituric acid (TBA) to produce a brownish-red substance called trimethoprim (3,5,5-trimethyloxazolidine-2,4-dione), and the OD value at 532 nm could be used to estimate the amount of LPO in the samples. In brief, cells were treated with HT for 48 h after overnight incubation in six-well plates. Cells were collected and added to the extraction solution, followed by sonication in an ice bath. Following centrifugation at 8000 g for 10 min, the OD at 532 nm was measured in the supernatant.


### Measurement of ROS
  The measurement of cellular ROS levels was achieved through the utilisation of a fluorescent probe, 2’,7’-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) (Solebo, Beijing, China). Following a 48 h treatment period with HT, the cells were thoroughly washed with PBS on two separate occasions. Thereafter, the cells were subjected to an incubation with 10 µM DCFH-DA staining solution for a duration of 30 min at ambient temperature. The removal of the staining working solution and subsequent rinsing of the cells with PBS was imperative, pre-warmed PBS was added and the cells were incubated for 5 min and photographed and documented under an inverted fluorescence microscope.


### Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔψM) assay
  Cellular mitochondrial membrane potential was measured using a Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay Kit (JC-10) (Solebo, Beijing, China). In brief, in cases of low mitochondrial membrane potential, the JC-10 monomer is unable to accumulate in the mitochondrial matrix and, as a consequence, green fluorescence is produced. After HT treatment for 48 h, the culture medium was discarded, and the cells were washed twice with PBS. Add 300 µL complete medium and 300 µL JC-10 staining working solution, mix well and incubate in a incubator for 20 min, then discard the working solution and wash twice with 1x JC-10 staining buffer, followed by 600 µL cell culture medium. Observe under an inverted fluorescence microscope and take photographs to record the results.


### Western blot analysis
  The extraction of total cellular proteins was conducted, followed by the measurement of protein concentration using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Solebio, Beijing, China). Followed by SDS-PAGE electrophoresis, the transfer of proteins was conducted onto PVDF (Millipore) membranes (Solebio, Beijing, China) and immunocontainment experiments were performed after the membrane transfer was completed, the primary antibody was added and incubated at 4 °C overnight with slow shaking. The secondary antibody was washed 3 times with TBST and incubated for 2 h. The secondary antibody was subjected to three washes with TBST, after which ECL luminescence solution was added. The experimental images were taken in ECL chemiluminescence instrument.


### Statistical analysis
  The experimental data were analysed, processed, and graphically plotted with GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. One-way ANOVA was used to comparing data between different dose groups in pairs. Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and the significance of differences between group was reflected by the P-value. A P-value of < 0.05 indicated a significant difference between the groups, which was considered statistically significant.

  The targets of HT pharmacological actions were obtained from the PharmMapper and TCMSP databases, and 255 unique values were obtained after deletion of duplicates. The obtained HT potential targets were visualised using Cytoscape 3.9.1 software (Fig. 1A). Disease-related targets were derived from the Genecards, OMIM, TTD and DisGeNET disease databases. Deduplication yielded 2296 CRC-related targets. The obtained key targets of HT acting on CRC were visualised and analysed using Cytoscape 3.9.1 software (Fig. 1B). Ferroptosis-related targets were obtained from the FerDb database, and a total of 259 targets were obtained after removing duplicates. The intersection of CRC-acting targets, HT-acting targets and ferroptosis-related genes yielded 14 key targets (Fig. 1C).


### PPI mesh topology analysis
  A PPI network diagram for the three overlapping HT genes was created using the STRING database, the ferroptosis-related genes and CRC targets (the threshold for interaction score was set at 0.4.), as shown in Fig. 1D.


### Core target screening
  Among the core targets screened by Cytoscape 3.7.0, NFE2L2 (Nrf2) was ranked first (Fig. 1E). Consequently, five targets related to oxidative stress and interconnected with each other were selected: Nrf2, NQO1, TXNRD1, PTGS2 and AKR1C3. Nrf2 and NQO1 were identified as potential targets for the regulatory effects of HT on of CRC ferroptosis.


### Molecular docking results
  The molecular docking method was employed to dock HT with the five selected core targets. Based on the docking findings, further analysis identified a possible pathway through which HT could affect CRC by inducing ferroptosis. The molecular docking results were indicated that HT demonstrated robust binding affinity for the docked molecules (Fig. 1F, G). HT interacts with the downstream target genes of Nrf2, NQO1, and TXNRD1, showing binding energies of − 7.8 kcal/mol and − 8.9 kcal/mol; it also binds to AKR1C3 and PTGS2 with energies of − 8.2 kcal/mol and − 7.9 kcal/mol, respectively. These data provide theoretical guidance for the selection of targets for the subsequent study of the molecular mechanism of HT against CRC.

## Results of in vitro experiments

### Cell growth curve assay
  HCT116 maintained good condition until 96 h of growth, with a growth bottleneck at 96 h and then entered a decline phase. Conversely, SW480 cells initially grew slowly for 24 h, after which their growth rate increased significantly between 24 and 72 h (Supplementary Fig. 1).


### HT inhibits cell viability
  To examine the effects of HT on CRC cell viability, CRC cells were treated with HT for 24 and 48 h (Fig. 2A, B). The results indicated that the median effective concentration (IC50) of HT on the HCT116 cells was 101 µM and 75 µM at 24 and 48 h post-treatment; and the following IC50 values were obtained for HT on SW480 cells were 137.4 µM and 90.65 µM at 24 and 48 h. Therefore, low, medium, and high concentrations of HT (40, 80, 160 µM for HCT116 cells; 45, 90, 180 µM for SW480 cells, respectively) were selected for the following experiments. The results demonstrated that HT exerted a dose- and time-dependent inhibitory effect on the growth of HCT116 and SW480 cells.


### NAC reduces the toxic effects of HT on cells
  The objective of this study was to ascertain whether NAC exerts an inhibitory effect on HT-induced cellular toxicity. To begin, we first examined the extent of cytotoxicity of NAC in CRC (Supplementary Fig. 2). These findings indicated that NAC (1, 2, 4, 8 mM) did not significantly inhibit the proliferation of HCT116 and SW480 cells. We investigated the cytotoxicity of 3 mM NAC in combination with HT. Compared with HT alone, NAC significantly reduced the cytotoxicity of HT in HCT116 and SW480 cells, and the difference was statistically significant, as shown in Fig. 2C, D.


### HT inhibits the cloning ability, cell migration abilities
  Subsequently, the present study investigated the influence of HT on the colony formation of HCT116 and SW480 cells, employing a plate colony formation assay. (Fig. 2E, F). These findings suggested that HT inhibited the clonogenic capability of both HCT116 and SW480 cells. Simultaneously, to ascertain the impact of HT on the migration capacity of HCT116 and SW480 cells, a migration assay was employed (Fig. 2G, H). The results demonstrated that HT decreased the migration ability of these cells depending on the dose and time.


### HT destroys the cytoskeleton
  The effects of HT on the cytoskeleton of HCT116 and SW480 cells were investigated using FITC-phalloidin (Fig. 2I, J). FITC-Phalloidin ghost pen cyclic peptide binds to F-actin, showing the microfilament distribution in cells. The results demonstrated that following 48 h of HT treatment, the cytoskeleton in HCT116 and SW480 cells gradually blurred and contracted. Additionally, chromatin in the nucleus coalesced to form a compact sphere, which was subsequently followed by nuclear fragmentation.

## HT induces ferroptosis in HCT116 and SW480 cells
  In this study, we aimed to determine whether HT induces ferroptosis in colorectal cancer cells. Transmission electron microscopy revealed characteristic morphological alterations associated with ferroptosis, including reduced cristae, diminished size, and membrane rupture following HT treatment17 (Fig. 3A, B). Fer-118 has been utilized as an inhibitor of ferroptosis to facilitate the identification of the specific type of cell death induced by HT. It is well established that intracellular iron ions can be reduced to ferrous ions, and the aggregation of ferrous ions can induce the generation of excessive ROS, which in turn induces cell death19. Subsequently, alterations in iron ion and ROS levels throughout the HT-induced cell death process were assessed. As illustrated in Fig. 3C and D, elevated iron levels were observed in cells as a result of HT treatment. Furthermore, GSH content exhibited a range of degrees of downregulation (Fig. 3E, F), whereas the LPO content decreased (Fig. 3G, H) after HT treatment for 48 h. Furthermore, HT treatment enhanced ROS production in cells (Fig. 3I, J). Ferroptosis often begins with a reduction in mitochondrial membrane potential20. Interestingly, we observed a decrease in the mitochondrial membrane potential after HT treatment in cells (Fig. 3K, L). These findings indicate that HT-induced cell death caused by HT in HCT116 and SW480 cells is linked to increased iron and LPO levels, along with reduced GSH levels and mitochondrial membrane potential, which are typical features of ferroptosis.

### HT induces ferroptosis by inhibiting the Nrf2 signaling pathway
  To investigate the intrinsic mechanism of HT-induced ferroptosis in HCT116 and SW480 cells, western blot analysis was conducted, which revealed that HT induced cellular ferroptosis and was associated with inhibition of the Nrf2 pathway. When redox homeostasis is disrupted, SystemXc-, which is located on the cell membrane, facilitates the exchange of extracellular cystine with intracellular glutamate. This generates cysteine intracellularly, which provides raw material for the synthesis of GSH, an important antioxidant in the body. Consequently, it resists the onset of ferroptosis. SLC7A11 (also known as xCT) is the major light chain subunit of SystemXc-, which plays a role in cystine/glutamate exchange and promotion of GSH production within the body21. Furthermore, GSH production is directly regulated by GPX422. The experimental findings showed that HT inhibited the expression of Tfr1, GPX4, and SLC7A11 in HCT116 and SW480 cells, indicating that HT may induce ferroptosis in these cells. Tfr1 plays a pivotal role in regulating cellular iron overload and its elevated expression level substantiates this conclusion. It is widely acknowledged that Nrf2 is a fundamental transcription factor that oversees oxidative stress regulation and is critical in ferroptosis development10. It translocates into the nucleus to regulate downstream targets, such as SLC7A11, GPX4, and NQO1. Our findings revealed that HT markedly suppresses this process. The expression of the Tfr1 protein was elevated by HT treatment, which also decreased the levels of Nrf2, SLC7A11, GPX4, and NQO1 proteins in HCT116 cells (Fig. 4A). Similarly, comparable outcomes were observed in the SW480 cells (Fig. 4B). The results of the present study indicate that HT exerts its antineoplastic activity through the induction of ferroptosis in colorectal cancer cells via the Nrf2 signalling pathway.

Similarly, Nrf2 and NQO1 protein expression were suppressed in these cells. Statistical analyses were performed for the HT-treated and control groups: * p ≤ 0.05, ** p ≤ 0.01.


### Fer-1 inhibits HT-induced ferroptosis
  To gain further insight into the mechanism of action of HT-induced ferroptosis in colorectal cancer cells, we employed a widely reported inhibitor of ferroptosis, Fer-1. Our findings revealed that Fer-1 significantly inhibited HT-induced cell death. The prevailing view is that Fer-1 resistance to ferroptosis is achieved by scavenging alkoxyl radicals and other rearranged products produced by ferrous ions from lipid hydroperoxides18. The toxic effects of HT on HCT116 and SW480 cells were diminished by the co-administration of Fer-1 and HT (Fig. 5A, B). Following this, Western Blot analysis was used.In HCT116 cells, Fer-1 pretreatment (1µM) was observed to reverse the HT-induced up-regulation of Tfr1 expression and down-regulation of SLC7A11 and GPX4 protein expression levels (Fig. 5C). The same procedure was employed to analyze the effect of Fer-1 pretreatment on the protein expression levels of SLC7A11, Tfr1, and GPX4 in SW480 cells. The study demonstrated that Fer-1 effectively reversed the expression levels of the proteins SLC7A11, GPX4, and Tfr1 in SW480 cells (Fig. 5D). These results indicated that HT induced CRC cell death, which could be inhibited by Fer-1, a ferroptosis inhibitor.

### Nrf2 activators SFN and CDDO inhibit ferroptosis by activating the Nrf2 pathway
  To further elucidate how HT-induced ferroptosis works in colorectal cancer cells, two Nrf2 activators, SFN and CDDO, were employed. HT induces cellular ferroptosis by inhibiting the Nrf2 signalling pathway. SFN is a naturally occurring is othiocyanate widely found in cruciferous vegetables, including broccoli, cabbage, and collard greens. It has been demonstrated that SFN enhances the body’s antioxidant capacity, thereby preventing ferroptosis by activating Nrf2 and its downstream target, heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1)23. CDDO is an oleanolic acid-derived semisynthetic synthetic triterpenoid that exerts resistance to ferroptosis by promoting nuclear translocation of Nrf224. Cells were initially treated with 20µM SFN for 2 h, and then with HT for 48 h. The results demonstrated that SFN reversed the protein expression levels of Nrf2 and its downstream target NQO1 in HCT116 cells (Fig. 6A). Similar, results were obtained for SW480 cells (Fig. 6B). Subsequently, the cells were pretreated with (1µM) CDDO, which reversed HT-induced downregulation of Nrf2 and NQO1 protein expression in HCT116 cells (Fig. 6C). Similarly, pretreatment with 1µM CDDO resulted in the reversal of Nrf2 and NQO1 protein expression in SW480 cells (Fig. 6D). These findings suggest that HT leads to cellular ferroptosis by inhibiting the Nrf2 signalling pathway.

## Discussion
  Ferroptosis is a recently identified form of programmed cell death that is iron-dependent and characterized by the accumulation of lipid peroxides. Recently, it has been suggested that this process may be important for inhibiting tumor proliferation and resisting conventional therapies4. The patient was critically affected by iron overload and lipid peroxide accumulation. Excessive ROS accumulation accelerates lipid peroxide accumulation. Meanwhile, it is evident that ROS generation is regulated by the Nrf2 signalling pathway. Numerous findings highlight the involvement of ferroptosis is inextricably linked to the homeostatic imbalance of oxidative stress, with the Nrf2 signalling pathway pivotal in its regulation10,25,26. In recent years, research on novel drugs targeting ferroptosis for antitumor effects has intensified, and traditional Chinese medicine and researchers, both domestically and internationally, have shown great interest in their monomer components because of their excellent therapeutic effects and reduced toxic side effects27.

  HT is a principal phenolic compound in the Chinese herb green fruit (also known as olive) and has endowed with potent antioxidant properties. Research increasingly suggested that HT has considerable potential as a cancer therapy. HT has been demonstrated to exert an inhibitory effect on the proliferation and migration of colorectal cancer cells (LS180, HT-29, and WiDr) while concomitantly inducing apoptosis and cell cycle arrest28–30. However, the role of HT in inducing ferroptosis, another mode of programmed cell death in CRC cells, and its mechanism of action have been relatively understudied.

  This study utilized a series of in vitro techniques to validate scientific hypotheses and explore mechanisms involving ferroptosis inhibitors and Nrf2 activators. The results demonstrated that HT inhibited the proliferation, clonogenic and migratory abilities of HCT116 and SW480 cells, which is consistent with the findings reported in the literature that HT has anti-CRC cell proliferation properties. Phalloidin/DAPI staining showed that HT disrupted the cytoskeletons of HCT116 and SW480 cells. Additionally, transmission electron microscopy revealed that the mitochondrial morphology of the two cell lines exhibited typical morphological changes associated with ferroptosis following HT treatment.

  Existing literature on the anti-CRC effects of HT suggests that HT promotes intracellular ROS generation in HCT116 and SW620 cells through the inhibition of TrxR1, a downstream target of Nrf2, leading to the inhibition of cell growth11. Other studies have shown that HT generates ROS and activates the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/Akt pathway, leading to FOXO3a(Forkhead box O3) phosphorylation and subsequent down-regulation, which induces apoptosis in DLD1 cells31. In HCT116 and SW480 cells, our study revealed that HT increased intracellular ROS production and inhibited cellular value-addition, which is consistent with previously reported results. Our findings indicated that HT-induced ferroptosis in CRC cells was linked to increased cellular iron, reduced GSH, elevated LPO, decreased mitochondrial membrane potential, and crucial ferroptosis proteins. Fer-1 is a well-known inhibitor of iron metastasis. It was shown that Fer-1 inhibited Shen-Qi-Ling-Bi Decoction (SQLB)-induced ferroptosis in HCT116 and HT29 cells and reversed the protein levels of Nrf2, GPX4, and SLC7A1132. High carbonyl graphene oxide (HC-GO) induced ferroptosisi in HCT116 and HCT15 cells and reduced the levels of ferroptosis-inhibiting proteins SLC7A11 and GPX4, an effect that was counteracted by Fer-133. Similarly, we found no statistically significant difference between the blank group and the coadministered Fer-1 group. These findings suggest that Fer-1 significantly restored the expression levels of key ferroptosis-associated proteins (i.e., Tfr1, SLC7A11, and GPX4). Our experiments confirmed that Fer-1 blocked HT-induced ferroptosis in HCT116 and SW480 cells. SFN and CDDO are the most widely used Nrf2 activators. Existing studies have shown that SFN can activate the Nrf2 pathway and its downstream target genes to promote cellular autophagy, which in turn counteracts ferroptosis to attenuate liver injury34. In addition, in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cells, erastin and acetaminophen (APAP) also trigger ferroptosis by affecting the nuclear translocation of Nrf2. However, ferroptosis triggered by erastin and APAP was reversed by CDDO35. We found by WB that there was no statistically significant difference between the blank and co-administration groups. These findings similarly indicated that SFN and CDDO effectively reversed the expression levels of Nrf2 and NQO1 proteins, suggesting that HT-induced ferroptosis in colorectal cancer is linked to the suppression of the Nrf2 signaling pathway.

  This study primarily utilized in vitro models to validate the ferroptosis-inducing effects of HT, without evaluation in animal models or clinical samples. To enhance the reliability of the results, inhibitors and activators were incorporated; however, the metabolic complexities within the tumor microenvironment may influence the efficacy of HT. Future research will focus on validating its in vivo anti-tumor effects and potential toxicities using murine models. Additionally, it is imperative to investigate whether the HT-induced ferroptosis via the Nrf2 pathway is modulated by other pathways. Specifically, in Nrf2 knockout SW480/HCT116 cells, we will analyze differentially expressed genes associated with iron metabolism (e.g., TFR1, FTH1) and LPO (e.g., ACSL4, LOXs). Subsequently, we will employ a GPX4 inhibitor (RSL3) and an iron death inhibitor (ferrostatin-1) to elucidate whether the effect of HT is contingent upon the Nrf2-GPX4/ACSL4 axis.

  As a phenolic hydroxyl compound, HT is susceptible to autoxidation and produces hydrogen peroxide in the culture medium. Studies have shown that HT not only induces ROS production in tumour cells but also that its antitumor effect may be influenced by its own oxidative properties11,36. To investigate the effect of HT autoxidation in vitro in the present study, a combination of the reducing agent NAC and HT was used. The results showed that NAC treatment partially reversed the HT-induced decrease in cell viability, suggesting that HT’s ability to inhibit the proliferation of HCT116 and SW480 cells may be related to the generation of its autoxidative effect. This phenomenon may also be influenced by its regulation of GSH metabolism. However, the role of ROS in this process remains unclear. These findings highlight the need for a comprehensive study of the mechanism underlying the antitumor activity of HT.
