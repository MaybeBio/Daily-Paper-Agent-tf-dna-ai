# Decoding yeast transcriptional regulation via a data-and mechanism-driven distributed large-scale network model


## Abstract
  The complex transcriptional regulatory relationships among genes influence gene expression levels and play a crucial role in determining cellular phenotypes. In this study, we propose a novel, distributed, large-scale transcriptional regulatory neural network model (DLTRNM), which integrates prior knowledge into the reconstruction of pre-trained machine learning models, followed by fine-tuning. Using Saccharomyces cerevisiae as a case study, the curated transcriptional regulatory relationships are used to define the interactions between transcription factors (TFs) and their target genes (TGs). Subsequently, DLTRNM is pre-trained on pan-transcriptomic data and fine-tuned with time-series data, enabling it to accurately predict regulatory correlations. Additionally, DLTRNM can help identify potential key TFs, thereby simplifying the complex and interrelated transcriptional regulatory networks (TRNs). It can also complement previously reported transcriptional regulatory subnetworks. DLTRNM provides a powerful tool for studying transcriptional regulation with reduced computational demands and enhanced interpretability. Thus, this study marks a significant advancement in systems biology for understanding the complex transcriptional regulation within cells.


## Main
  The transcriptional regulatory networks (TRNs) aim to elucidate the regulatory interactions among genes. These interactions modulate transcription rates and, ultimately, affect gene expression levels, playing critical roles in essential biological functions. The inference and modeling of TRNs have been extensively studied to better understand the complexity and dynamics of these networks under both genetic and environmental perturbations.

  The structure of a TRN is typically characterized using Boolean models, differential equation models, and machine learning approaches. The Boolean model is intuitive, utilizing binary values (0 and 1) to represent the presence or absence of gene regulation. The Boolean thresholds are applied to infer likely regulatory interactions based on gene expression data [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. While binary networks use fewer parameters, they may overlook regulatory signals at low expression [[6], [7], [8]]. TRNs based on differential equations are also widely used as a modeling approach [9]. These models can describe the dynamics of gene expression [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. The construction and parameterization of differential equations require extensive prior knowledge and careful parameter tuning, which is hard to achieve [14].

  The continuous accumulation of omics data, such as transcriptomics, has further facilitated the inference of TRNs through the application of machine learning methods [[15], [16], [17]]. Several deep learning methods have also been employed in the construction of TRNs [18], such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs) [19], meta-learning techniques [20], and graph neural networks (GNNs) [21]. Deep learning-based TRNs, driven by the large-scale single-cell omics data, have also become widespread. One notable example is STGRNS, an interpretable transformer-based method, specifically designed for inferring gene regulatory networks from single-cell transcriptomic data [22]. Additionally, scGREAT utilizes a transformer-based and biomedical language models, leveraging spatial transcriptomics data for external validation to uncover novel regulatory relationships between genes [23]. As the complexity of TRN models increases, pre-trained models based on transfer learning have gained increasing attention [[24], [25], [26], [27]] for probing gene regulatory mechanisms.

  TRNs based on machine learning, particularly deep learning, are perceived as black boxes, making interpretation challenging [28]. Moreover, these machine learning-based methods fail to incorporate prior knowledge of transcriptional regulatory relationships. These models primarily offer a global analysis of the TRN, but they are not well-suited for targeted investigations of individual genes to identify their key TFs. Moreover, constructing the TRN as a single large-scale model demands substantial computational resources and extensive data, which may not always be available.

  To address the above issue, we propose a distributed large-scale transcriptional regulatory neural network model (DLTRNM) that integrates mechanistic knowledge with data-driven methods. Using Saccharomyces cerevisiae as a model organism, DLTRNM constructs an individual neural network for each gene, incorporating prior transcriptional regulatory knowledge. It features a distributed structure where each subnetwork operates independently. Transfer learning, including pre-training and fine-tuning, is applied to obtain improved subnetworks. Subsequently, DLTRNM can simulate how TGs respond to perturbations of TFs. Additionally, it facilitates the identification of key TFs within complex TRNs. Finally, DLTRNM can complement previously reported transcriptional regulatory subnetworks, advancing our understanding of transcriptional regulation.


## Result

### The framework of DLTRNM
  The framework of DLTRNM consists of several modules (Fig. 1). Firstly, prior knowledge of transcriptional interactions between TFs and TGs for S. cerevisiae was retrieved from YEASTRACT database [29]. These prior regulatory relationships are unlabeled, meaning they only reflect the presence of regulatory interactions without including any further details, such as regulatory direction. Next, a data-driven model was constructed using artificial neural networks, with TGs as the outputs and TFs as the inputs. Each TG is modeled independently, resulting in its unique subnetwork. Here, TGs refer to genes that are regulated by other TFs within the TRN. It is important to note that each gene, including TFs themselves, can be regulated by other TFs. Therefore, in DLTRNM, every gene can be regarded as a TG and used as a modeling object. The subnetworks are designed to operate in a parallel and independent manner, allowing each to be trained separately without interference from others. This parallel structure is motivated by decoupling the complex TRN using prior knowledge, thereby enabling independent analysis of each gene. Such a structure allows researchers to perform targeted investigations on specific genes without retraining or evaluating the entire TRN. In addition, this design improves model's flexibility, allowing individual subnetworks to be quickly updated when prior regulatory knowledge is revised, without retraining the entire model. Moreover, DLTRNM incorporates transfer learning during the model training process. Initially, to capture the features of the relationships, the model is pre-trained using pan-transcriptomic data [30]. This is followed by fine-tuning the model with time-series transcriptomics data [31] to learn how TFs dynamically regulate TGs.Fig. 1The overall framework of DLTRNM. DLTRNM integrates mechanism-driven modeling approaches with data-driven techniques, using prior transcriptional regulatory relationships (without direction) sourced from databases. It then employs machine learning methods, such as artificial neural networks. Additionally, DLTRNM leverages transfer learning to extract high-dimensional features from pan-transcriptomic data, which are subsequently applied to time-series prediction tasks, enabling the prediction of transcriptional regulatory directions.Fig. 1


### Pre-training and fine-tuning of DLTRNM
  During the pre-training phase of DLTRNM, each subnetwork was processed independently, allowing it to capture features from the pan-transcriptomic data (Methods). Based on the cumulative probability distribution of the loss function for 6085 subnetworks after pre-training, 73.9 % of the subnetworks had an L2 loss below 100 (Fig. 2A).Fig. 2The pre-training and fine-tuning strategies and results. (A) Cumulative probability distribution plot of the errors for 6085 subnetworks during the pre-training phase. (B) Transfer learning strategy in DLTRNM. In cases where new TFs or TGs arise, the parameter fine-tuning methods differ. The green line indicates parameters that require retraining, while the black line denotes fixed model parameters. (C) Cumulative probability distribution plots of training and testing errors for the fine-tuned 5859 subnetworks.Fig. 2

  After the pre-training phase, the pan-transcriptomic data were compared with the time-series data to enable transfer learning. It was found that 5855 genes were present in both datasets, and 4 additional TFs appeared in the time-series dataset. Considering that TFs are key variables in the TRN and can also be modeled as TGs, these 5859 genes were selected as the modeling targets for transfer learning (Methods). This resulted in two scenarios: the introduction of new TFs and new TGs. For a new TG, since this gene does not exist in the original dataset, the entire network must be retrained. When a new TF appears for a specific TG, the new TF is added as an input. The hidden layer weights of the original neural network are fixed, and the other layers are retrained to complete the transfer learning process (Fig. 2B).

  During the fine-tuning phase, the DLTRNM benefited from aggressive parameter settings, which enabled it to effectively fit the time-series data. For each subnetwork, interpolation was applied to align the time intervals in the dataset (Methods). The interpolated dataset was then subjected to quality filtering, removing data where gene expression remained unchanged or exhibited minimal variation. All 5812 subnetworks were fine-tuned and subsequently evaluated on the dataset. The fine-tuning results showed that 98.02 % (5743) of the subnetworks in DLTRNM achieved a training loss value of less than 0.2, with an average of 0.03. When tested on the reserved testing dataset, 86.87 % (5090) of the subnetworks achieved a loss of less than 0.2, with an average of 0.144 (Fig. 2C).

  Due to the dynamic nature of transcriptional regulatory relationships, numerical predictions of gene expression are quite challenging. As a result, a margin of error is typically allowed. In this study, since the time-series data are defined as the ratio of gene expression relative to their original state, most data points are concentrated around 1, indicating minimal changes in gene expression. Therefore, an average loss of 0.144 is regarded as an acceptable error, which also demonstrates that the fine-tuned DLTRNM performs well in predicting changes in gene expression. It is important to note that the varying fine-tuning losses of the subnetworks may be due to differences in the quantity and quality of the datasets. The fine-tuning loss for each subnetwork is likely to be decreased with improvements in the quantity and quality of the input datasets.


### DLTRNM can independently simulate TG responses
  After fine-tuning, DLTRNM, thanks to its distributed structure, can independently simulate how each TG is influenced by variations in TFs (Methods), accurately capturing the relationships between TFs and TGs without interference from other subnetworks.

  In this study, DLTRNM employed the labeled regulatory relationships (i.e. positive or negative regulation) from SGD [32], which were not considered during model training, to validate whether the models have successfully learned regulatory direction from the data after being trained on the unlabeled regulatory interactions. “Positive” regulation indicates that changes in TG expression align with the direction of TF changes, while “negative” regulation means that TG expression changes in the opposite direction of TF changes. Each subnetwork in DLTRNM can be analyzed by up-regulating or down-regulating the corresponding TFs. Due to the large number of relationships, the complete results cannot be fully displayed here. Therefore, a partial result is provided in Fig. 3A. Notably, each subnetwork in DLTRNM is based on an artificial neural network structure, which can lead to nonlinear predictions. This reflects the complex and dynamic nature of transcriptional regulation. As such, the simulated changes in TG expression may not strictly adhere to positive or negative trends. However, the labels from the SGD database are static, meaning that the model's nonlinear outputs cannot be validated with these static labels. Therefore, these cases are labeled as “other” predictions.   Next, the subnetwork of HSP12 is selected for a case study to observe its prediction. HSP12 plays a role in maintaining tissue integrity under stress conditions [33] and is an essential gene for lifespan extension induced by dietary restriction [34]. In DLTRNM, eight TFs have labeled regulatory relationships with HSP12, with directionality indicated (Fig. 3B). As shown, DLTRNM accurately predicts changes in the gene expression of HSP12 in response to six TFs (HOT1, HSF1, IXR1, MSN2, MSN4, and YAP5). Additionally, the prediction for SKO1 is labeled as “other”, indicating that the model predicts the change in HSP12 induced by SKO1 to be nonlinear. This outcome from our model may reflect the dynamic nature of transcriptional regulation, though the validation is not possible with static labels recorded in SGD database.


### DLTRNM can systematically simulate TF knockouts
  In addition to analyzing individual subnetworks, DLTRNM allows the simulation of transcriptional changes resulting from the knockout of TFs (Methods). The validation dataset for simulating TF knockout was obtained from the published studies [35]. If the predicted TG expression changing trend after TF knockout matches the experimental data, it is considered a correct prediction. Otherwise, it is considered an incorrect prediction. Knockout simulations were performed for all 225 transcription factors associated with the 5859 genes modeled in DLTRNM. Due to the large volume of complete results leads to poor readability, only a partial result is provided in Fig. 3C. Since the changes in TFs are set to zero (rather than being up-regulated or down-regulated), the resulting predictions will not exhibit nonlinear behavior, meaning that no “other” predictions will occur. Due to the dynamic nature of regulatory relationships [36] and the varying accuracy of each subnetwork, the overall prediction accuracy also remains at a moderate level (0.52).

  Next, the predicted results of knocking out ROX1 were analyzed in detail. ROX1 regulates the function of repressors by reducing promoter occupancy in response to oxidative stress [37] and mediates the aerobic transcriptional repression of hypoxia-inducible genes [38]. In DLTRNM, a total of 166 TGs are regulated by ROX1. After simulating the knockout of ROX1, the predicted changes in the expression directions for these 166 TGs were compared with the labels (Fig. 3D). “Positive” indicates that the expression of TGs increases after the knockout, while “negative” indicates that the expression of TGs decreases after the knockout. The results show that 106 labels were correctly predicted, yielding an accuracy rate of 63.86 %.


### DLTRNM can predict key TFs for each TG
  Identifying key TFs for each TG is essential for understanding the mechanisms of gene expression. The distributed architecture of DLTRNM facilitates the identification of the most significant TFs influencing TG expression. To identify key TFs, SHAP analysis was performed on each subnetwork (Methods). A TF with a higher SHAP value is more likely to play a critical role in regulating TG expression. Therefore, the TFs with the highest SHAP values, as inferred by the model, were classified as key regulators for their corresponding TGs.

  Based on the identification of key TFs, the entire TRN was simplified by retaining the top 5, top 3, and top 1 key TFs for each TG (Fig. 4A, drawn by Cosmograph [39]). This simplification reduced the original complex and highly interconnected network into a clearer structure, facilitating the identification of core TRNs. The simplified network exhibited a narrower node degree distribution, which was more suitable for revealing the core structure (Fig. 4B).Fig. 4The results and analysis of key TF identification. (A) Simplification of the entire TRN by utilizing the key TFs for each TG. (B) Cumulative probability distribution plots of node degrees for the original and simplified relationship network, showing a significant reduction in complexity. (C) CV analysis of variables in all subnetworks. More than 87.27 % of the subnetworks have large CV values, indicating significant differences in the importance of most variables across the networks. The SHAP, PCCs, and p-values for the subnetworks of the target genes HAP4 (D) and PYK2 (E). The orange lines represent the SHAP values and the blue ones represent the PCCs.Fig. 4

  We further calculated the coefficient of variation (CV) of SHAP values for each subnetwork to assess whether the calculated SHAP values effectively capture the importance of key TFs (Fig. 4C) (Methods). The CV analysis of SHAP values within each subnetwork aimed to prevent overly concentrated distributions of SHAP values, ensuring that the identified key TFs exhibit distinct and significant contributions to the model. In DLTRNM, 87.27 % of the subnetworks (5113 out of 5859) had CV values greater than 1. In these subnetworks, this allowed for clear identification of each TF's contribution.

  Furthermore, we selected two TGs, HAP4 (associated with ethanol metabolism [40]) and PYK2 (associated with pyruvate metabolism [41]), for more detailed analysis. In addition to SHAP values, we calculated the Pearson correlation coefficients (PCCs) between each TF and TG based on the fine-tuned dataset to further screen the key TFs (Fig. 4D and E). Among these TFs, those with both high SHAP values and high PCCs were selected as potential key TFs.

  For HAP4, the most influential TF was CAT8 (Fig. 4D). CAT8 is essential for repressing multiple genes under non-fermentative growth conditions [42,43], and may play a significant regulatory role in HAP4 expression. For PYK2, the most influential TF was CBF1 (Fig. 4E). CBF1 is directly involved in centromere function, and its deletion may result in chromosome instability [44,45]. Although the regulatory mechanism by which CBF1 influences PYK2 expression remains unclear, our analysis may offer valuable insights for future experimental investigations.


### DLTRNM can complement the existing transcriptional regulatory subnetworks
  Finally, we found that DLTRNM can complement previously proposed transcriptional regulatory subnetworks and identify potential key transcriptional regulatory interactions.

  The transcriptional subnetwork of the 11 most highly overexpressed S. cerevisiae genes in response to temperature changes was analyzed [46] (Fig. 5A). Interestingly, after reviewing the databases, MED8 was found not to be listed as a TF. Based on its functional description, MED8 is a subunit of the RNA polymerase II mediator complex and forms the RNA polymerase II holoenzyme by binding with the core polymerase subunits [47,48]. While it may play an important role in transcriptional regulation, it is not a TF. Therefore, the two transcriptional regulatory relationships, MED8-HXK1 and MED8-GLK1, could be complemented through this subnetwork, in order to avoid misleading interpretations in regulatory studies caused by redundant or unsupported regulatory relationships.Fig. 5DLTRNM could complement the existing transcriptional regulatory subnetworks. (B) The regulatory network in S. cerevisiae under oxidative stress. The definitions of nodes and connections in the figure are the same as in (A). The green connections are those added by our model to complement the regulatory network.Fig. 5

  As the second example, DLTRNM can complement the regulatory network of S. cerevisiae under oxidative stress [49] (Fig. 5B). In this subnetwork, we found that the extra relationships among YAP1, CAD1, and STB5 revealed by DLTRNM are also supported by researches [[50], [51], [52], [53]], whereas the original network did not indicate any transcriptional regulatory relationships among these three genes. Completing these regulatory relationships facilitates the study of this regulatory subnetwork by helping to avoid the omission of potential key regulatory interactions.

  DLTRNM can proposed potential key regulatory relationships. For instance, DLTRNM presents four potentially important regulatory interactions, three of which involve MSN4 (Fig. 5A). According to the SGD database, MSN4 regulates the expression of approximately 200 genes under various stress conditions, including heat shock [54]. This suggests that these regulatory relationships involving MSN4 are likely to be key components of this regulatory subnetwork under the temperature change condition. Similarly, potential key regulatory relationships under oxidative stress proposed by DLTRNM can also be identified (Fig. 5B). However, the actual significance of these regulatory relationships has not been previously studied or supported by existing literature. Nevertheless, these potentially important transcriptional regulatory interactions may offer valuable insights for identifying the core regulators within regulatory subnetworks.


## Discussion
  This study introduces a novel, distributed, large-scale transcriptional regulatory neural network model (DLTRNM) for S. cerevisiae, which integrates mechanistic knowledge with data-driven approaches to construct independent subnetworks for TGs. The model demonstrates high accuracy in predicting TG responses, TF knockouts, and identifying key TFs within specific subnetworks. DLTRNM can simulate how TFs interact with TGs both from the entire network and individual subnetworks. DLTRNM also complements existing transcriptional regulatory subnetworks and highlights key regulatory interactions. By combining mechanistic insights with machine learning techniques, DLTRNM offers a powerful tool for understanding and predicting transcriptional regulation.

  However, the results show that DLTRNM's simulation accuracy is still limited, potentially due to multiple underlying factors. The primary factor, which we consider to be the most critical, is that transcriptional regulatory relationships in S. cerevisiae are not stable [36]. These regulatory relationships vary across different specific conditions, such as growth stages, environmental conditions, and external stimuli. However, both the labels obtained from the literature and SGD represent static regulatory directions defined under different specific conditions. As a result, only those regulatory relationships that remain consistent across all conditions can be effectively verified. Secondly, the current datasets are insufficient to fully capture transcriptional regulation in S. cerevisiae under all possible conditions. Moreover, transcriptional regulation is a dynamic process in which regulatory relationships at a given time point can be influenced by preceding states. The absence of such temporal information in the available data results in its exclusion from the model, which further limits the model's accuracy. As large-scale time-series data in S. cerevisiae continue to accumulate, it is expected not only to improve the model's predictive accuracy but also to enable the adoption of more advanced models, such as Transformer, to better capture more complex regulatory relationships. Finally, since gene-level regulatory mechanisms have not yet been fully elucidated, current modeling still relies on black-box approaches like machine learning. With a deeper understanding of these mechanisms, DLTRNM can be improved by adopting more suitable subnetwork structures, thereby enhancing both the interpretability and accuracy of the model.

  In conclusion, DLTRNM's distributed architecture and integration of mechanistic knowledge with data-driven learning provide unique insights into transcriptional regulation. DLTRNM not only accelerates our understanding of yeast transcriptional regulation but also paves the way for more efficient and comprehensive analyses of gene regulatory networks across diverse species.


## Methods

### Acquisition of transcriptional regulatory relationships
  The quality of these relationships is critical to DLTRNM. A large number of experimentally validated regulatory relationships have already been established for S. cerevisiae. Each regulatory association in the YEASTRACT database has been meticulously annotated by reviewing relevant references [29]. In YEASTRACT, each TF is associated with two sets of TGs: the documented targets and the potential targets. The documented target set consists of TGs supported by multiple publications, while the potential target set includes TGs that lack direct experimental evidence. The documented transcriptional regulatory relationships are considered to be of higher quality and reliability. Therefore, DLTRNM uses only the documented TGs as the transcriptional regulation relationships. A statistical analysis of these relationships revealed that most genes in S. cerevisiae are regulated by between 10 and 50 TFs (Fig. S1), illustrating the highly coupled and complex nature of the TRN in S. cerevisiae.


### Preprocessing of pan-transcriptome data preprocessing
  In the pre-training phase of DLTRNM, the model was designed to acquire the generalized transcriptional regulatory relationships in S. cerevisiae. To achieve this, a coarse-precision version of DLTRNM was pre-trained using pan-transcriptomic data This dataset includes 969 high-quality transcripts derived from RNA sequencing of 1032 natural isolates of S. cerevisiae, in TPM format [30]. To stabilize the pre-training process, the data were logarithmically transformed, as described in equation (1).(1)pre−trainingdata=log2(TPM+1)

### Alignment of the two datasets
  Since DLTRNM requires transfer learning, it is necessary to compare the differences between the pan-transcriptomic and the time-series data, including the number of TGs and TFs (Fig. S3). The original dataset refers to the pan-transcriptomic dataset, while the target dataset refers to the time-series dataset. The comparison revealed that the two datasets shared 5855 common genes. Additionally, the target dataset contained four unique TFs. Since TFs are key variables in the regulatory network, they should be fully incorporated into the model. Therefore, a total of 5859 genes were selected as modeling targets for transfer learning.


### Distributed subnetworks and pre-training
  Each subnetwork in DLTRNM uses a two-hidden-layer artificial neural network. The number of nodes in the hidden layers is set to three times the number of input variables in order to capture three potential regulatory relationships: enhancement, repression, and no significant effect.

  Since this was the pre-training phase, the entire pan-transcriptome dataset was used for training. Additionally, an all-ones dataset, representing no change in gene expression, was manually added to ensure the model could learn the baseline state of gene expression. The hyperparameters were set conservatively during pre-training. Specifically, the maximum number of epochs was set to 2,500, and the optimizer's learning rate was 0.005. The standard L2 loss function was used, as presented in Equation (3).(3)Loss=(yexp−yout)2

  yexp represents the training data, and yout represents the model's output. The standard L2 loss function was chosen due to its computational efficiency and appropriateness for large-scale optimization tasks.


### Transfer learning for fine tuning
  In DLTRNM, the weights between the two hidden layers of each subnetwork are fixed, while the weights of the input and output layers are fine-tuned based on the time-series dataset. For each subnetwork, the TF data from the previous sampling point serve as input, while the TG data from the subsequent sampling point serve as output, simulating the response of the TG.

  A large number of ones were found at sampling time points in the time-series data, indicating that these data do not capture changes in TGs. Therefore, data with minimal changes in TFs (0.8 < x < 1.2) or no changes in TGs (y = 1) were excluded from the fine-tuning datasets of each subnetwork. Additionally, the excluded data still reflect the inherent robustness of the regulatory process. To avoid impacting the modeling of TG responses, 15 % of the training dataset was randomly selected from these excluded data points.

  After splitting the dataset into 90 % training data and 10 % testing data, it was observed that some subnetworks had few training data. However, for subnetworks with relatively large but still insufficient training data, standard ten-fold cross-validation can lead to substantial computational burden and significantly slow down the training process. To address this issue and reduce computational costs, a hierarchical strategy was adopted, where the number of repetitions was adjusted flexibly based on the size of each subnetwork's dataset size. Specifically, the procedure follows these rules: for datasets with fewer than 20 samples, training was repeated 5 times; for datasets with 20–30 samples, 4 repetitions were used; for 31 to 40 samples, training was repeated 3 times; for datasets with 41–50 samples, 2 repetitions were used; and for datasets with more than 50 samples, training was performed only once. The model that yielded the lowest test error was selected and saved.

  In the fine-tuning phase, the hyperparameters were set more aggressively. The maximum number of training epochs for each subnetwork is determined based on its dataset size. On one hand, smaller datasets do not require a large number of epochs to avoid overfitting. On the other hand, since smaller datasets may undergo repeated training under the hierarchical strategy, large epoch numbers would demand significant computational resources. Therefore, the maximum number of training epoch for each subnetwork is defined in Equation (4), with a limit of 20,000 epochs set as the upper bound for all subnetworks.(4)epoch=200×dataset_size≤20000

  The optimizer's learning rate was set to 0.001 with a 0.01 wt decay. The mean squared error loss function was used, as presented in Equation (5).(5)Loss=(yexp−yout)2n

  n represents the number of data points. yexp represents the training data and yout represents the model's output.


### Response and knockout simulation
  In DLTRNM, each subnetwork can be analyzed by modifying its input (i.e., TFs) by factors ranging from 2 to 5 times and 0.2 to 0.5 times, in order to observe the resulting changes in its output (i.e., the TG). If the changing trends in TFs align with the trends in TGs (i.e., both increase or decrease simultaneously), it is considered a positive correlation. Conversely, if the trends are opposite (i.e., when one increases while the other decreases), it is considered a negative correlation.

  A similar method is used in TF knockout simulations. In these simulations, the TF is “knocked out” by setting its value to zero. The changes in TG expression are then observed to assess the impact of TF removal. If the knockout leads to an increase in TG expression, it is considered a positive impact. Conversely, if the knockout results in a decrease in TG expression, it is considered a negative impact.


### SHAP value
  SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values were used to identify the input variables in the fine-tuned subnetworks that had the greatest impact on the output. SHAP is a method for interpreting machine learning model predictions, grounded in the concept of Shapley values from cooperative game theory. This approach offers a consistent, interpretable way to understand complex black-box models and to identify the importance of individual variables. In this study, the SHAP package [56] in Python was employed to compute and analyze SHAP values.
