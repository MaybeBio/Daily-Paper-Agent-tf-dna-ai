# Mechanisms and Strategies
for Engineering Oxidative
Stress Resistance in Saccharomyces cerevisiae


## Abstract
  Oxidative stress, driven by the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), poses a significant challenge to the productivity and robustness of Saccharomyces cerevisiae in industrial applications. This review provides an overview of oxidative stress mechanisms, focusing on transcription factors (Yap1p, Skn7p, Msn2/4p) and their regulation through different stress signaling pathways such as HOG, CWI, TOR, and cAMP/PKA. Advanced strategies for enhancing oxidative stress resistance are discussed, including antioxidant enzyme overexpression, redox cofactor optimization, transcription factor modulation, and promoter engineering. Emerging tools like omics-guided gene discovery, biosensor-based feedback regulation, and machine learning-driven optimization are highlighted as promising approaches for constructing robust yeast cell factories. These insights pave the way for intelligent strain design to improve industrial performance under oxidative stress conditions.


## Introduction
  Saccharomyces cerevisiae, a microorganism with Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) status, is widely employed as a chassis organism in industrial biotechnology due to its genetic tractability, rapid growth, and metabolic versatility. It has been extensively used to construct engineered strains for the production of diverse metabolites, including biofuels, pharmaceuticals, and fine chemicals. ,  However, as metabolic engineering efforts advance, the inherent stress tolerance limitations of engineered strains have emerged as an additional bottleneckalongside pathway optimizationin enhancing product yields. Among various stresses encountered during industrial processes, oxidative stress has been recognized as a critical challenge. ,

  Oxidative stress arises from an imbalance in the intracellular redox state, favoring oxidation over reduction. This imbalance results in the excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), including superoxide anions (O2 –), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and hydroxyl radicals (OH·). These highly reactive molecules can cause severe damage to essential cellular components, including nucleic acids, proteins, and lipids (Figure  C), −    which can result in the fragmentation of mitochondria and activation of cell death processes such as apoptosis, and, at high concentrations, even necrosis. While S. cerevisiae possesses a sophisticated oxidative stress defense system that senses ROS signals and activates transcription factors to regulate antioxidant gene expression, certain endogenous and exogenous factors can overwhelm these intrinsic mechanisms. For instance, the accumulation of specific metabolic products like farnesol (FOH) or toxic intermediates such as farnesyl diphosphate (FPP) ,  are associated with increased ROS generation. Additionally, in industrial bioprocesses like second-generation (lignocellulosic hydrolysates) and third-generation (C1 substrate utilization) platforms, specific inhibitors or feedstocks such as furfural, formate, and acetate induce ROS accumulation. Metabolic burdens, including high levels of protein synthesis, NADPH depletion and redox imbalance, exacerbate oxidative damage. To address these challenges, significant research efforts have been devoted to understanding the molecular mechanisms by which S. cerevisiae detects and responds to oxidative stress. Additionally, advances in metabolic engineering have enabled the development of strategies to enhance oxidative stress resistance in yeast cell factories.

  Despite extensive research on oxidative stress mechanisms in S. cerevisiae, their integration into systematic engineering strategies for industrial applications remains limited. This review bridges this gap by summarizing recent advances in molecular mechanisms and engineering approaches to enhance oxidative stress resistance, providing a framework for improving strain robustness in industrial settings.


## ROS

### Endogenous ROS Formation
  In S. cerevisiae, mitochondria serve as the primary site of ROS production. During aerobic respiration, electrons occasionally leak from the electron transport chain (ETC) and reduce molecular oxygen to form superoxide anions (O2 –) (Figure  A). These anions can be further converted into hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) by superoxide dismutase (SOD) and subsequently into hydroxyl radicals (OH·) via Fenton or Haber-Weiss reactions catalyzed by transition metals like Fe2+ (Figure  B). Beyond mitochondria, other cellular processes also contribute to ROS generation. For example, protein folding in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) generates H2O2 as a byproduct of disulfide bond formation, and fatty acid β-oxidation in peroxisomes produces H2O2 during the breakdown of long-chain fatty acids. Enzymatic reactions catalyzed by oxidases are also significant contributors to intracellular hydrogen peroxide production (Figure  A). ,

  An increasing body of research indicates that ROS acts as secondary messengers in pathways regulating growth and stress responses and modulates translation machinery to prioritize stress-responsive proteins. While mild ROS levels are integral to cellular signaling and adaptation, excessive accumulation triggers oxidative stress that compromises cell viability and productivity. Specific metabolites can exacerbate endogenous ROS production (Figure  C). For instance, the accumulation of certain intermediates in lipid and isoprenoid biosynthesis pathways has been linked to increased ROS levels. Knockout mutants of ERG9 (encoding squalene synthase) and COQ1 (involved in coenzyme Q biosynthesis) exhibit elevated intracellular levels of farnesyl diphosphate (FPP) and related pyrophosphate compounds, which are associated with increased ROS generation. Similarly, overexpression of GGPP synthase led to enhanced ROS generation. Farnesol (FOH), a dephosphorylation product of FPP, has also been shown to inhibit the ETC by interfering with phosphatidylinositol signaling, further amplifying ROS accumulation. Additionally, metabolic burden can also aggravate ROS production. For instance, high levels of protein biosynthesis accelerate the protein folding rate in the ER, thereby facilitating ROS formation. Meanwhile, the depletion of NADPH limits the regeneration of various antioxidants, such as GSH and TRX, consequently impairing their capacity to scavenge ROS and further resulting redox imbalance. These findings underscore the impact of metabolic imbalance on oxidative stress.


### Exogenous ROS Induction
  Exogenous factors also play a critical role in ROS generation in S. cerevisiae. Environmental stressors, such as high temperatures, ultraviolet (UV) radiation, and oxidative agents, induce ROS through diverse mechanisms (Figure  C). ,  In industrial fermentation processes, specific inhibitors, byproducts and/or metal ions can exacerbate oxidative stress. For example, the inhibitors generated during lignocellulose pretreatment, such as furfural and acetic acid, have been shown to disrupt cellular redox balance and induce ROS accumulation. ,  Ethanol, a common byproduct in yeast fermentation, impairs membrane integrity and mitochondrial function at high concentrations, leading to elevated ROS levels. Active metals derived from the fermentation media or equipment, such as iron and cadmium, promote the formation of hydroxyl radicals via the Fenton and Haber-Weiss reactions, or disrupt the redox homeostasis by consuming reduced glutathione.

  Both endogenous metabolic activities and exogenous environmental factors contribute to ROS generation in S. cerevisiae. Understanding the sources of ROS and the natural mechanisms to fight against them provides a foundation for developing strategies to mitigate oxidative stress in yeast cell factories.


## Transcription Factors Involved in Oxidative
Stress Responses
  Transcription factors are central to the oxidative stress defense system in S. cerevisiae. Upon activation by oxidative stress signals, these proteins bind to specific DNA sequences in the promoter regions of target genes, modulating their transcription. This regulation ensures the timely expression of oxidative stress reaction genes, which restore the cellular redox homeostasis. The primary transcription factors involved in oxidative stress responses in S. cerevisiae include Yap1p, Skn7p, and Msn2/4p. Each operates through specific activation mechanisms and plays distinct and complementary roles in the response to oxidative stress.


### Yap1p
  Yap1p, a member of the AP-1 family of basic leucine zipper (bZIP) DNA-binding proteins, is a key regulator of oxidative stress responses in S. cerevisiae. Yap1p binds to specific promoter regions of target genes containing the consensus sequence 5′-TT/GAC/GTAA-3′, first identified in the promoter of GSH1, which encodes γ-glutamylcysteine synthetase 1. Under nonstress conditions, Yap1p predominantly resides in the cytoplasm due to its rapid export from the nucleus by Crm1p, a nuclear exportin. ,  This export is mediated by interactions with cysteine residues in the C-terminal cysteine-rich domain (C-CRD) of Yap1p.

  Upon oxidative stress, Yap1p accumulates in the nucleus due to inhibition of its nuclear export. This process occurs through two distinct mechanisms depending on the type of ROS. For hydroperoxide or superoxide-induced stress, the glutathione-dependent peroxidase Gpx3p acts as a sensor and facilitates the formation of intramolecular disulfide in Yap1p ,  This conformational change prevents Crm1p-mediated export and promotes nuclear accumulation. Alternatively, diamide-induced stress triggers disulfide bond formation within the C-CRD cysteines of Yap1p independent of Gpx3p, thereby preventing its export from nucleus (Figure  A).

  In the nucleus, Yap1p activates the expression of genes such as TRX2 (thioredoxin), TRR1 (thioredoxin reductase), and CTT1 (catalase), which are critical for ROS detoxification. Once the cellular antioxidants reach sufficient levels to counter oxidative stress, thioredoxin reduces Yap1p back to its inactive state, restoring its cytoplasmic localization and terminating its activation. ,  This dynamic regulation ensures a tightly controlled response to fluctuating ROS levels.


### Skn7p
  Skn7p is a nuclear-localized transcription factor with dual roles in response to oxidative and osmotic stresses. It contains an N-terminal heat shock factor-like DNA binding motif and a C-terminal response regulator domain. Unlike Yap1p, Skn7p responds to oxidative stress via phosphorylation-dependent mechanisms.

  Under oxidative stress conditions, Skn7p cooperates with Yap1p to regulate oxidative stress reaction (OSR) genes via a phosphorylation mechanism independent of Asp427, ,  a site that is critical for Skn7p’s response to osmotic stress. Promoters of many OSR genes contain binding sites for both Skn7p and Yap1p. Skn7p recruits Yap1p to these promoters to form a protein complex that enhances transcriptional activation. Additionally, Skn7p undergoes serine/threonine phosphorylation, which stabilizes its interaction with target promoters, increasing its transcriptional activity. Beyond its cooperation with Yap1p, Skn7p interacts with the heat shock factor Hsf1p to regulate the expression of heat shock proteins during oxidative stress. These heat shock proteins, acting as molecular chaperones, enhance the activity of antioxidant enzymes, thereby contributing to the cellular defense against oxidative stress. The similar coiled-coil structure enables strong interaction between Skn7p and Hsf1p, forming a stable protein complex, which subsequently recognizes and binds to the heat shock elements (HSEs) in target promoters. (Figure  B).


### Msn2/4p
  Msn2p and Msn4p are homologous zinc-finger transcription factors that regulate general stress responses by binding to stress response elements (STREs) in target gene promoters. These factors are redundantly regulated but exhibit distinct dynamics under oxidative stress.

  The nuclear import and export of Msn2p/4p is mediated by the conserved nuclear localization signal (NLS) and nuclear export signal (NES), respectively. ,  Studies on the nuclear translocation mechanism of Msn2p have revealed its predominant localization in the cytoplasm under nonstress conditions. This localization is driven by the combined effects of protein kinase A (PKA)-dependent phosphorylation targeting the NLS and the Target of Rapamycin Complex 1 (TORC1)-regulated association of Msn2p with Bmh2p. ,  Under stress conditions, both PKA and TORC1 become inactivated, leading to dephosphorylation of Msn2p and its release from Bmh2p, allowing Msn2p to enter the nucleus. Meanwhile, the NES-mediated nuclear export of Msn2p is inhibited under stress, which contributes to its accumulation in the nucleus (Figure  C). Interestingly, Msn2p/Msn4p exhibit pulsatile dynamics during stress responses characterized by transient bursts of nuclear localization followed by rapid export.

  The Msn2p/Msn4p activity is also regulated by CK2 kinase. Under stress conditions, CK2-mediated phosphorylation at Ser194 and Ser638 enhances Msn2p’s transcriptional activity. However, these phosphorylation sites are absent in Msn4p, suggesting distinct regulatory mechanisms for these two proteins. Additionally, the nuclear retention of Msn2p during stress responses is promoted by CK2 through a yet unknown mechanism independent of the above-mentioned phosphorylation sites.

  Yap1p, Skn7p, and Msn2/4 are pivotal regulators of oxidative stress resistance in S. cerevisiae. Their distinct yet interconnected mechanisms enable precise control over antioxidant gene expression in response to fluctuating ROS levels. Understanding the regulatory networks involving these regulators will provide valuable insights into the development of engineering strategies aimed at enhancing yeast robustness while maintaining cellular productivity and viability.


## Signal Transduction Pathways Involved in Oxidative
Stress Responses
  Oxidative stress signals in S. cerevisiae are transduced through a network of signaling pathways that detect intracellular and extracellular stress cues, ultimately activating transcription factors to coordinate cellular defense responses. Among these, the High Osmolarity Glycerol (HOG) and Cell Wall Integrity (CWI) pathways, both belonging to the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways, play pivotal roles. Additionally, other pathways such as the TOR and cyclic adenosine monophosphate/protein kinase A (cAMP/PKA) pathways contribute significantly to oxidative stress responses. The interplay between these pathways creates a robust signaling network that enables S. cerevisiae to adapt to oxidative stress (Figure  ). ,


### CWI Pathway
  The CWI pathway primarily maintains the structural integrity of the cell wall but is also activated under oxidative stress conditions. This pathway detects external and internal signals through transmembrane sensors such as Wsc1/2/3p, Mid2p, and Mtl1p. These sensors activate Rho1, a small GTPase, via GDP/GTP exchange factors such as Rom1/2p and Tus1p. Activated Rho1 interacts with protein kinase C (Pkc1p), which initiates a phosphorylation cascade involving Bck1p (MAPKKK), Mkk1/2p (MAPKK), and Slt2p (MAPK).

  Lipid hydroperoxides constitute the first class of oxidants identified as activators of the CWI pathway. When the yeast cells were exposed to linoleic acid hydroperoxide, a rapid and marked elevation in the phosphorylation level of Slt2 was observed. Further studies indicate that oxidative stress activates the CWI pathway through distinct mechanisms depending on the oxidant type.

  Activated Slt2p phosphorylates transcription factors such as Rlm1p and the Swi4p-Swi6p complex (SBF), which regulate genes involved in cell wall biosynthesis, remodeling, and oxidative stress resistance. Additionally, Slt2p mediates the degradation of cyclin C, a repressor of RNA polymerase II transcription. ,  Cyclin C degradation relieves transcriptional repression on stress-responsive genes, enhancing cellular resistance to oxidative stress.


### HOG Pathway
  The HOG pathway responds to osmotic and oxidative stresses via two upstream branches: SHO1 and SLN1. Both branches converge at Pbs2p (MAPKK), which activates Hog1p (MAPK) through phosphorylation. The SHO1 branch involves mucin-like proteins Hkr1p and Msb2p that sense osmotic changes and relay signals via Ste11p (MAPKKK), ,  while the SLN1 branch operates through a phosphorelay system involving Sln1p, Ypd1p, and Ssk1p. Under oxidative stress, Hog1p is phosphorylated by Pbs2p and translocates to the nucleus, where it regulates gene expression by interacting with transcription factors such as Msn2p/Msn4p. Hog1p also targets downstream kinases like Rck1p and Rck2p, which modulate cellular processes critical for oxidative stress defense. Studies suggest that hydrogen peroxide preferentially activates the HOG pathway via the SLN1 branch, ,  highlighting its critical role in managing oxidative damage.


### TOR Pathway
  The TOR pathway is a central regulator of cell growth and metabolism that also responds to stress. ,  It consists of two complexes, TORC1 and TORC2. TORC1 integrates signals from nutrients, energy levels, and stress conditions to regulate processes such as protein synthesis and autophagy. The deficiency of TORC1 sensitizes cells to oxidative stress, and proteomics analysis further reveals significant modulation of TOR signaling in response to various oxidant treatments. A major target of TOR signaling is the transcription factors Msn2/4p, which play a pivotal role in cellular resistance to oxidative stress. Under stress conditions, TORC1 activity decreases, leading to the activation of downstream effectors such as Sit4p, which subsequently promotes nuclear localization of Msn2/4p. TORC1 also regulates Sch9p kinase activity. Under oxidative stress, TORC1 inhibition relieves Sch9p-mediated repression of Rim15p, which further activates downstream gene expression through activation of Msn2/4p and Gis1p. ,  These findings underscore the critical role of TOR signaling in coordinating nutrient sensing with oxidative stress resistance.


### cAMP/PKA Pathway
  The cAMP/PKA pathway is regulated by Ras proteins (Ras1p and Ras2p), which activate adenylate cyclase (Cyr1) to produce cAMP. cAMP binds to the regulatory subunit Bcy1p of protein kinase A (PKA), releasing its catalytic subunits Tpk1/2/3 for activation of target genes. Under oxidative stress, reduced PKA activity facilitates the nuclear translocation of Msn2p/Msn4p by reducing their phosphorylation levels. ,  Yap1p and Skn7p are also negatively regulated by PKA through a mechanism similar to that of Msn2p/4p. However, overactive Ras proteins can impair mitochondrial respiration, increasing ROS production due to elevated mitochondrial membrane potential. This suggests that the cAMP/PKA pathway not only regulates antioxidant defense but also influences ROS generation through mitochondrial dysfunction.


### Interplay between Pathways
  Significant interactions exist among these pathways to ensure an integrated response to oxidative stress. For example, under oxidative stress conditions, Mtl1p inhibits Ras2p/Tor1p signaling through Rho1p, thereby enhancing Msn2/4-mediated gene expression, which highlights the interactions between CWI pathway and other signaling networks such as cAMP/PKA and TOR pathways. Rim15p regulation serves as a convergence point for the cAMP/PKA and TOR pathway during nutrient stress responses, ,  while its role in oxidative stress requires further investigation. Additionally, Rim15p has been suggested to be directly activated by Hog1p and work together to mediate the cellular defense against acetic acid-induced stress, suggesting the crosstalk of TOR and PKA pathways with HOG pathway. Moreover, Sch9p, as a downstream target of TORC1, inhibits PKA activity by regulating the localization and phosphorylation of the PKA regulatory subunit Bcy1p. In Sch9p-deficient cells, Bcy1p is predominantly localized in the nucleus, thereby alleviating the inhibition of the catalytic subunits of PKA, which primarily translocate to the cytoplasm, resulting in elevated PKA activity. ,  These interactions create a robust signaling network that allows S. cerevisiae to sense and respond effectively to oxidative stress.


## Strategies for Enhancing Oxidative Stress Resistance
  Under normal conditions, the redox homeostasis in S. cerevisiae is maintained by the above-mentioned oxidative stress defense system composed of various enzymes, antioxidants, and regulatory proteins. However, under industrial conditions or metabolic engineering scenarios, the basal expression levels of these defense elements are often insufficient to counteract excessive ROS accumulation. This necessitates the development of engineering strategies to enhance the oxidative stress resistance. Below, we outline key strategies categorized by their molecular targets and mechanisms, accompanied by a summary table for clarity (Table  ).


### Overexpression of Antioxidant Enzymes
  Superoxide dismutases (SODs) and catalases are the primary enzymes involved in ROS detoxification. SODs convert superoxide anions into hydrogen peroxide, which is subsequently reduced to water by catalases or peroxiredoxins.

  In mitochondria, the reduction is catalyzed by mitochondrial peroxiredoxins, such as Prx1p, while in the cytoplasm, the process is carried out by cytosolic catalase Ctt1p, the glutathione system, and the thioredoxin system. In peroxisomes, hydrogen peroxide generated from fatty acid oxidation is reduced by peroxisomal catalases, such as Cta1p. Sod1p has also been identified as a transcription factor regulating genes involved in oxidative stress resistance and DNA damage repair. Therefore, overexpression of SODs and catalases is one of the common strategies to enhance cellular resistance to oxidative stress. ,  Simultaneous overexpression of cytoplasmic Cu/Zn-SOD (Sod1p) and its Cu chaperone (Ccs1p) has been shown to significantly enhance oxidative stress resistance by reducing superoxide levels, and increased the survival rate of stationary-phase cells by up to 2-fold. Similarly, overexpression of cytosolic catalase (Ctt1p) and peroxisomal catalase (Cta1p) improves cellular survival under oxidative stress, and contributed to patchoulol production in the yeast cell factory.

  In addition to overexpression, enhancing the catalytic activity of these enzymes is another effective approach. For example, activation of cytochrome c peroxidase (Ccp1p) enhances the catalytic activity of Cta1p under oxidative stress, enabling cells to effectively mitigate oxidative stress induced by excessive hydrogen peroxide. The activation mediated by Ccp1p resulted in a 3-fold increase in the activity of Cta1p, while the introduction of the W191F mutation further enhanced the catalytic activity by approximately 30%.


### Optimization of Antioxidant Systems
  The glutathione (GSH) and thioredoxin (TRX) systems are central to maintaining redox balance. Two strategies can be adopted to ensure their sufficient supply: enhancing the biosynthesis of GSH and TRX to expand their total pool, which comprises both oxidized and reduced forms, and optimizing the reduction and regeneration pathways to increase the proportion of their reduced forms.


#### Glutathione Biosynthesis
  GSH, a tripeptide composed of glycine, cysteine, and glutamate, acts as a major antioxidant by directly scavenging ROS and serving as a cofactor for glutathione peroxidases (Gpx). Increasing the expression of Gpx can directly strengthen the cellular capacity to reduce hydroperoxides. Moreover, ensuring a sufficient supply of GSH is equally essential, as its elevated levels have been shown to significantly enhance the resistance to furfural-induced oxidative stress. Overexpression of Gsh1p (γ-glutamylcysteine synthetase) and GSH2 (glutathione synthetase) increases intracellular GSH levels, but the amplitude is limited by feedback inhibition on GSH1. This limitation can be mitigated by introduction of bacterial bifunctional enzymes with both Gsh1p and Gsh2p activities, which is resistant to feedback inhibition, such as GshF. By introducing GshF from Actinobacillus pleuropneumoniae, along with an adapted chaperone protein system derived from E. coli, into S. cerevisiae, the intracellular GSH accumulation was improved by 6.6-fold. Alternatively, molecular modification of Gsh1p to alleviate or eliminate feedback inhibition may represent another feasible strategy. Augmenting the supply of GSH precursors by exogenous addition to the medium or modulating the cysteine synthesis pathway can also enhance GSH synthesis.


#### Thioredoxin System
  Different from GSH, thioredoxin (Trx) participates in ROS detoxification by reducing protein disulfides. Trx also participates in other intracellular responses to oxidative stress, such as serving as a cofactor in the reduction of hydrogen peroxide by thioredoxin peroxidases (Tpx). Similar to Gpx, increasing the expression of Tpx can also promote the reduction of hydroperoxides. Besides, enhancing sufficient supply of Trx by increasing the copy number of genes encoding thioredoxin (TRX1, TRX2) ,  or thioredoxin reductase (TRR1) enhances the ROS detoxification capacity. For instance, the simultaneous overexpression of TRX2 and TRXR from Kluyveromyces marxianus endowed S. cerevisiae with enhanced resistance against the oxidative stress induced by formic acid, acetic acid, and furfural. Additionally, a significant reduction in the lag phase during ethanol fermentation was observed, accompanied by a more than 30% increase in glucose consumption rate. Since regeneration of Trx relies on the catalysis of thioredoxin reductase, with NADPH providing the necessary reducing power for this process, concurrently boosting NADPH synthesis further facilitates thioredoxin regeneration.


### Optimization of Redox Cofactor Systems
  The regeneration of GSH and TRX is mediated by glutathione reductases (Glr1p) and thioredoxin reductase (Trr1p), both are NADPH-dependent. ,  Furthermore, NADPH has been shown to directly scavenge ROS. Consequently, efficient regeneration of redox cofactors such as NADPH is essential for sustaining antioxidant defense.

  In S. cerevisiae, the cytosolic pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) serves as the primary source of NADPH. Overexpression of key PPP enzymes such as Zwf1p (glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase) and Gnd1p (6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase) enhances NADPH production, supporting antioxidant systems like glutathione reductase (Glr1p) and thioredoxin reductase (Trr1p). Downregulation of glycolytic flux via phosphoglucose isomerase (Pgi1p) repression further redirects metabolic flux toward the PPP. Upregulation of the zinc cluster protein Stb5p which regulates the PPP provides an alternative route for boosting NADPH levels. ,  Aside from the PPP, NADPH-regenerating reactions also occur in other subcellular compartments of S. cerevisiae, such as the glycolytic pathway and the TCA cycle. ,  Strategies that enhance the expression of genes responsible for these reactions or modulate their upstream pathways have shown promising results. In industrial processes, a synergistic application of the aforementioned regulatory strategies is commonly utilized to optimize outcomes. For example, in ethanol fermentation, enhancing the PPP pathway and TCA cycle effectively mitigated ROS accumulation, leading to an 11% increase in fermentation yield. Additionally, the conversion of NADH to NADPH, catalyzed by the ATP-dependent enzyme NADH kinase, represents another strategy for NADPH production. In S. cerevisiae, the cytoplasmic Utr1p and Yef1p, along with the mitochondrial Pos5p, mediate this process, and modulating the expression and location of Pos5p has been demonstrated as an effective strategy.


### Modulation of Transcription Factors
  Transcription factors such as Yap1p, Skn7p, and Msn2/4p play a crucial role in the oxidative stress defense system by regulating the expression of antioxidant genes in response to oxidative stress. The primary strategies for modulating these transcription factors involve enhancing their expression by increasing their copy number in the genome, ,  or modulating their activation state via regulation of their nuclear localization.

  Mutations in Yap1p’s cysteine-rich domain (e.g., C620F) result in its persistent nuclear localization, enhancing S. cerevisiae’s resistance to oxidative stress caused by furfural and 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF). As compared to the control strain overexpressing the wild-type Yap1p, the strain overexpressing Yap1pC620F exhibited a significantly shorter lag phase in cellulosic ethanol fermentation, and accumulated approximately 35% more biomass under stressed condition with high HMF concentration (40 mM). Overexpression of Ybp1p, which facilitates Yap1p activation, is another effective strategy. In the protopanaxadiol (PPD)-producing strain, YBP1 overexpression decreased 75.2% of ROS production and significantly enhanced cell viability during fermentation (from 71.3% to 88.3% at 84 h). The homologous transcription factors Msn2p and Msn4p exhibit dynamic cytoplasmic shuttling during the stress response. Therefore, temporal redundancy modulationaltering the pulse dynamics between Msn2p and Msn4phas been proposed as an engineering strategy to enhance target gene expression without imposing excessive metabolic burden. By modulating the differential affinity of Msn2/4p for PKA, the temporal redundancy between Msn2p and Msn4p can be reduced, which contributes to enhancing the activation of redundant target genes and ultimately enhances cell survival under stress conditions.

  Unlike Yap1p and Msn2p/4p, the transcriptional activity of nuclear localized Skn7p is primarily related to its phosphorylation status and nuclear abundance. Enhancing Skn7p phosphorylation via CDK8 kinase or Trx2p-mediated hyperphosphorylation improves its transcriptional activity under oxidative stress.

  Recent studies have identified a number of other transcription factors with potential roles in cellular oxidative stress resistance, including Hap1p. Overexpression of Hap1 increases oxidative stress resistance and contributes to the production of α-amylase. Another study demonstrated that Hap1p regulates ethanol-induced acquired resistance to oxidative stress in S. cerevisiae by modulating expression of Ctt1p specifically under ethanol stress. Since ethanol accumulation precedes aerobic respiration and ROS formation, strains capable of preemptively inducing oxidative stress defenses via Hap1p and Ctt1p gain a survival advantage.

  Furthermore, the introduction of heterologous regulatory factors, such as the global regulator IrrE from Deinococcus radiodurans, can also enhance the yeast’s resistance to oxidative stress. In the S. cerevisiae strain expressing IrrE, the ROS level decreased by approximately 61.4% compared to the control when exposed to oxidative stress induced by acetate, furfural, and phenol. Further studies revealed that IrrE mitigates intracellular ROS accumulation through multiple mechanisms, including enhancing antioxidant enzyme activity and promoting NADPH biosynthesis.


### Modulation of Signal Transduction Pathways
  Signal transduction pathways sense and transmit oxidative stress signals, ultimately leading to the activation of transcription factors. However, due to the incomplete understanding of these mechanisms under oxidative stress, strategies to engineer such pathways for enhanced stress resistance remain limited.

  Downregulation of TOR signaling has been shown to reduce ROS formation during the stationary phase, accompanied by the upregulation of Sod2p, thereby contributing to an extended chronological lifespan (CLS). In contrast, during the growth phase, reduced TOR signaling enhances ETC activity, which remains efficiently coupled to ATP production. This elevated ETC activity increases the mitochondrial membrane potential, subsequently leading to higher ROS generation, which is considered to act as an adaptive signal that promotes CLS extension.

  Furthermore, recent research demonstrates that mild overexpression of peroxiredoxin (Tsa1p) in S. cerevisiae enhanced H2O2 resistance and extended the lifespan. Tsa1p does not only function as a scavenger of H2O2, but also negatively regulates the cAMP/PKA signaling pathway by inhibiting PKA activation, thereby attenuating pathway signaling and further activating the downstream transcription factors such as Msn2p/4p.

  Moreover, the overexpression of Rim15p, a key component of the PKA and TOR signaling pathways, enhances cellular resistance to oxidative stress induced by hydrogen peroxide and acetic acid. Rim15p directly modulates catalase activity and the GSH/GSSG ratio to facilitate ROS detoxification, and also indirectly contributes to oxidative stress resistance by promoting the expression of stress-responsive genes through Yap1p.


### Dynamic Regulation of OSR Genes Using Stress-Driven
Promoters
  Upregulating the expression of OSR genes is a common strategy to enhance cellular resistance to oxidative stress. However, constitutive overexpression of these genes, particularly transcription factors, may disrupt cellular metabolism. For instance, excessive overexpression of Stb5p would induce an imbalance in the cellular redox state, thereby impairing cell growth. Dynamic regulation using stress-responsive promoters ensures that antioxidant genes are expressed only under oxidative stress conditions, minimizing metabolic burden during normal growth.

  Promoters regulated by Yap1p (P TRX2 , P TRR1 , P MET16 ) have been widely used for this purpose. The application of Yap1p-responsive promoters has been shown to enhance ethanol production under oxidative stress conditions by dynamically regulating antioxidant gene expression. Similarly, transcriptomic analyses have identified numerous target genes regulated by Msn2/4p and Skn7p, whose promoters also show potential for further application, although practical examples remain limited. ,  Some more stress-responsive promoters have been identified in comparative transcriptomics such as P HSP104 . When P HSP104  was used to control the overexpression of SOD1 in S. cerevisiae, a 6.4% increase in ethanol fermentation yield was observed. Engineering synthetic promoters by modifying upstream activation sequences (UAS) or combining elements from natural promoters may further improve their performance under stress conditions. For instance, in the modification of the Yap1p-dependent promoter P TRX2 , the optimization of the spacer length between transcription factor binding sites, along with the fine-tuning of the number and sequence of these sites, resulted in a synthetic promoter with an enhanced dynamic range and increased sensitivity.


### Addition of Exogenous Antioxidants
  Addition of exogenous antioxidants to the culture medium of the yeast can also mitigate oxidative stress. ,  For example, the addition of 20 mM ascorbic acid resulted in a 2-fold increase in cell survival at 168 h. However, it should be noted that some exogenous antioxidants may exhibit cytotoxic effects at high concentrations, and the addition amount should be carefully controlled. For instance, caffeine reduces ROS levels but requires precise concentration control due to its cytotoxicity mediated by Flr1p efflux transporters, whose expression is regulated by Yap1p and is dependent on the activation of oxidative stress signaling pathways.


## Conclusions and Perspectives
  The construction of S. cerevisiae as a robust cell factory often involves extensive genetic modifications, including the introduction of heterologous biosynthetic pathways, optimization of metabolic modules, and suppression or deletion of competing pathways. While these modifications are essential for improving the production of high-value compounds, they frequently disrupt the cellular redox balance, leading to oxidative stress. Additionally, adverse environmental factors encountered during industrial-scale fermentationsuch as high temperatures, ethanol accumulation, or inhibitory byproductsfurther exacerbate oxidative stress, ultimately compromising cell viability and productivity. Enhancing the oxidative stress resistance of S. cerevisiae is therefore critical for unlocking its full potential in industrial applications.

  This review has summarized current knowledge on the molecular mechanisms underlying oxidative stress responses in S. cerevisiae, including the roles of key transcription factors (e.g., Yap1p, Skn7p, Msn2/4p), signal transduction pathways (e.g., HOG, CWI, TOR), and antioxidant systems (e.g., glutathione and thioredoxin). Furthermore, various engineering strategies have been discussed to enhance oxidative stress resistance, such as overexpression of antioxidant enzymes, optimization of redox cofactors, transcription factor modulation, and promoter engineering. While core oxidative stress response components-including transcription factors, signaling pathways, and antioxidant systems-are evolutionarily conserved across fungi, their expression levels may exhibit significant strain-specific variation. Given the genomic diversity among natural yeast isolates and extensive genetic modifications in industrial strains, caution remains critical when extrapolating engineering strategies for stress tolerance from laboratory to industrial strains.

  While oxidative stress can be caused by various conditions, and each condition has its specific impact on gene expression and regulation, the development of intelligent yeast strains with precise and adaptive stress resistance mechanisms provides an exciting future direction to construct robust cell factories (Figure  ). Key perspectives for advancing this field are outlined below.


### Precision in Target Selection
  Accurate identification of target genes is fundamental for improving oxidative stress resistance in S. cerevisiae. This requires a comprehensive understanding of cellular responses to oxidative stress at multiple levels, including transcriptional, translational, and post-translational regulation. Advanced omics technologies, such as transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, can provide a system-level view of the regulatory networks involved in oxidative stress responses. For example, transcriptomic analyses under oxidative conditions can identify differentially expressed genes encoding antioxidant enzymes and redox regulators, while metabolomic studies can reveal bottlenecks in redox cofactor regeneration. Integrating omics data with computational modeling will uncover novel regulatory hubs and interactions within oxidative stress networks, such as the interplay between the transcription factors and metabolic pathways associated with redox homeostasis. These insights inform the selection of genes for targeted modifications to enhance oxidative stress tolerance while minimizing metabolic burden.


### Dynamic Regulation via Promoter Engineering
  Promoter engineering offers a promising approach to achieving precise control over gene expression under oxidative stress conditions. Modulating promoter property and strength ensures that antioxidant genes are expressed at the right time and at optimal levels to balance metabolic burden and stress resistance, thereby improving the performance of yeast cell factories. Native stress-responsive promoters regulated by transcription factors such as Msn2/4p and Yap1p can be engineered to dynamically activate OSR genes in response to intracellular ROS accumulation. Advances in synthetic biology enable the design of artificial promoters with tunable strength and specificity. For instance, combining upstream activation sequences (UAS) from natural oxidative stress-responsive promoters with strong core promoter elements can enhance both inducibility and basal activity. Such engineered promoters can ensure optimal timing and intensity of expression of key antioxidant enzymes, thereby mitigating ROS damage while conserving cellular resources during nonstress conditions.


### Real-Time Feedback Regulation Using Biosensors
  Biosensors provide a precise and efficient means of maintaining redox homeostasis while minimizing unnecessary metabolic burdens. Based on the mechanism that specific endogenous proteins, including peroxiredoxins (Prxs), undergo conformational rearrangements under oxidative stress, a series of biosensors that respond efficiently to oxidative stress have been developed. ,  Recent advances have developed a genetically encoded redox biosensor system based on redox-sensitive fluorescent proteins to detect redox metabolites, allowing real-time monitoring of the distribution and spatiotemporal dynamics of ROS in yeast under various stress conditions. Developing real-time feedback regulation systems based on these biosensors can ensure timely activation of oxidative stress defense systems only when needed, preventing the metabolic cost of constitutive antioxidant expression. For example, the NADH/NAD+ ratio-responsive transcription factor Rex has been used to dynamically regulate NADH oxidase (NoxE) expression to maintain cofactor balance. By replacing Rex with an ROS-responsive biosensor, it may be possible to link antioxidant gene expression or redox cofactor regeneration directly to intracellular ROS levels, enabling dynamic allocation of cellular resources and improved oxidative stress resilience.


### Machine Learning for Strain Optimization
  Machine learning (ML) tools hold great promise for advancing strain engineering by enabling data-driven predictions and optimizations. ML algorithms can analyze large-scale gene expression and multiomics data sets to identify novel genetic targets and regulatory motifs associated with oxidative stress resistance. By integrating these data sets with metabolic network models, ML can predict optimal pathway modifications to enhance redox homeostasis, such as upregulating NADPH-generating enzymes or modulating antioxidant enzyme expression. ML-based models trained on protein sequence-function relationships can predict beneficial mutations in antioxidant enzymes or transcription factors, enabling rational design beyond traditional trial-and-error approaches. This approach accelerates the development of yeast strains with improved oxidative stress tolerance and industrial robustness.

  Enhancing oxidative stress resistance in S. cerevisiae is a multifaceted challenge that requires integrating fundamental biological insights with advanced engineering tools. Leveraging omics technologies to dissect oxidative stress regulatory networks, synthetic biology to construct dynamic and responsive promoters, biosensors for real-time feedback control, and machine learning for predictive strain optimization will enable the development of intelligent yeast strains. These strains will dynamically adapt to fluctuating oxidative stress conditions, improving robustness and productivity under industrially relevant environments. Ultimately, these innovations will facilitate more sustainable and efficient biotechnological processes for producing biofuels, pharmaceuticals, and other valuable compounds.
