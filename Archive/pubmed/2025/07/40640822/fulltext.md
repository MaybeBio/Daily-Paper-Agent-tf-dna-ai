# Non-invasive identification of TKI-resistant NSCLC: a multi-model AI approach for predicting EGFR/TP53 co-mutations


## Objectives
  To investigate the value of multi-model based on preoperative CT scans in predicting EGFR/TP53 co-mutation status.


## Methods
  We retrospectively included 2171 patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) with pre-treatment computed tomography (CT) scans and predicting epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) gene sequencing from West China Hospital between January 2013 and April 2024. The deep-learning model was built for predicting EGFR / tumor protein 53 (TP53) co-occurrence status. The model performance was evaluated by area under the curve (AUC) and Kaplan-Meier analysis. We further compared multi-dimension model with three one-dimension models separately, and we explored the value of combining clinical factors with machine-learning factors. Additionally, we investigated 546 patients with 56-panel next-generation sequencing and low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) to explore the biological mechanisms of radiomics.


## Results
  In our cohort of 2171 patients (1,153 males, 1,018 females; median age 60 years), single-dimensional models were developed using data from 1,055 eligible patients. The multi-dimensional model utilizing a Random Forest classifier achieved superior performance, yielding the highest AUC of 0.843 for predicting EGFR/TP53 co-mutations in the test set.


## Conclusion
  The multi-dimensional model demonstrates promising potential for non-invasive prediction of EGFR and TP53 co-mutations, facilitating early and informed clinical decision-making in NSCLC patients at risk of treatment resistance.

## Introduction
  Non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients with EGFR mutations typically respond well to tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) [1–4]. However, concurrent TP53 mutations, which could be found in 30–72% of all EGFR-mutated NSCLCs, have been associated with TKI resistance and suboptimal responses [5–8]. This presents a critical challenge in determining optimal treatment strategies for patients with these co-mutations.

  he identification of EGFR/TP53 co-mutations currently depends on invasive tissue sampling and time-consuming genetic testing. Although these methods are effective, they may delay the initiation of treatment and increase the healthcare burden. Recent studies have indicated that radiomic features derived from computed tomography (CT) scans may correlate with underlying genetic alterations, offering a promising non-invasive alternative for mutation prediction [9–11]. Artificial intelligence (AI) has also shown potential in enhancing such predictive models [12, 13]. While previous research has successfully applied AI to predict EGFR mutations in lung cancer, with reported the Area Under the ROC curve (AUC) values ranging from 0.7 to 0.8 [14–17], there remains a significant knowledge gap in predicting EGFR/TP53 co-mutations—particularly in Asian populations, where EGFR mutations are more prevalent. Understanding these co-mutations is critical for optimizing treatment strategies, as they may necessitate alternative therapies beyond standard EGFR-TKI treatment. This study aims to address this gap by developing an AI-driven model that integrates CT imaging and clinical data to predict EGFR/TP53 co-mutations in Asian patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). By characterizing the clinical and imaging features associated with co-mutated cases and constructing a non-invasive prediction framework, we seek to enable faster, more accurate therapeutic decision-making and enhance precision oncology in lung cancer care. Ultimately, this approach may reduce the need for invasive diagnostics while preserving diagnostic accuracy.


## Method
  This retrospective study was approved by our Institutional Review Board, which waived the need for informed consent from the patients. This study did not constitute a clinical trial and as such did not require formal registration.


### Study population
  We retrospectively collected patients with pathologically confirmed primary NSCLC at the West China Hospital from January 2013 to April 2024. The clinical information of the patients was collected and verified using the electronic pathology system of the West China Hospital. To mitigate selection bias in this single-center study, we included all patients who met the pre-defined inclusion and exclusion criteria. This approach approximates a consecutive case series from our institution during the specified period.

  Including criteria: (a) patients were pathologically confirmed with non-small cell lung cancer. (b) patients had undergone CT scan within 30 days before the surgery. (c) patients had undergone 56-panel gene detection. Exclusion criteria: (a) patients whose lesions are indivisible due to massive pleural fluid, pulmonary atelectasis, etc. (b) patients who lost follow-up. (c) patients whose CT scans did not meet the technical requirements for radiomic feature extraction.

  The CT images of the patients were read using picture archiving and communication system (PACS), and the maximum diameter of the lesion (mm) was measured to evaluate the macroscopic imaging characteristics. The CT scans included a 5 mm plain scan, an enhanced scan, 0.625 mm and 1 mm thin slice CT scan. Immunohistochemical staining of pathological tissues was performed according to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines. The lung cancer tissues were classified according to their pathology.

  The previous visit records of patients were checked and verified by the electronic medical record system, and the deadline for telephone follow-up was April 28 2024. For patients who received EGFR-TKI as first-line therapy, without immunotherapy, chemo-radiotherapy and surgery at the same time, and who had regular follow-up with whole-body imaging CT were especially focused. Patients were evaluated for disease progression according to Response Evaluation Criteria 1.1 (RECIST) for response to solid tumors. Progression-free survival (PFS), the first endpoint, was calculated as the time from the start of TKI treatment to disease progression or death or to the end of follow-up. Overall Survival (OS), which was defined as the time between the diagnosis of primary lung cancer and death from any cause, was the secondary endpoint of this study. Commonly used clinical tumor markers (within 30 days before pathological diagnosis) were drawn from the blood samples.


### Image acquisition
  CT images with a thickness of 1mm within 30 days before pathological diagnosis were selected. All non-enhanced, 1 mm thin-slice CT images were processed in the DICOM format using IQQA software (IQQA-CHEST, EDDA Technology, Princeton Junction, NJ, USA) from the PACS station. The chest CT testing devices used for all the patients were Siemens, Philips Brilliance Big Bore, and GE Discovery CT750 HD. The parameters set as following, tube voltage as 120 V, tube current as 200–500 mA, rotation time as 0.4–0.7 s, and pixel matrix as 512 × 512. The Siemens scanner used a soft convolution kernel (B31f, B30f), and the GE scanner used the STANDARD kernel.


### Image preprocessing
  Image preprocessing steps, including voxel resampling and intensity normalization, were implemented in accordance with principles outlined by the Image Biomarker Standardization Initiative (IBSI) to enhance data consistency and feature robustness [18]. To ensure data consistency and prepare images for model training, a multi-step preprocessing pipeline was implemented. First, all patient CT images and their corresponding segmentation masks were resampled to the dataset’s median voxel spacing using nearest-neighbor interpolation, standardizing spatial resolution. Second, extensive data augmentation techniques were applied, encompassing common geometric transformations and intensity alterations to enhance model robustness and generalization. Third, for intensity standardization, values within the segmented the region of interest (ROI) were initially collected and then truncated to their 0.01 and 0.99 percentile range. The mean and standard deviation derived from these truncated foreground intensities were subsequently used to perform Z-score normalization on the relevant image patches. Following Z-score normalization, these intensities were linearly scaled to a fixed range of [−1, 1]. Finally, as a further preprocessing step, images underwent a probabilistic Bézier curve-based nonlinear transformation.


### Image segmentation
  To ensure the generalizability of the model and its adaptability to various CT imaging devices, a self-supervised learning mechanism was employed to optimize a customized VB-Net framework [19], which modified the original image under nonlinear changes. Based on displaying VB-Net using ITK-Snap software [20], the Bezier Curve [21] was used to preprocess the ROI segmentation. Detailed procedures are provided in the Supplementary Materials (Figures S1−2, S1-3).


### Feature extraction
  Following segmentation with the optimized VB-Net model, lesion ROIs were obtained. To address the limited diversity of feature types, this study applied a range of filters to enhance feature extraction. A total of 14 classes comprising 24 filters, along with 8 preprocessing techniques, were used for image processing. Key settings included a fixed bin width of 25 Hounsfield Units (HU) for intensity discretization. All images were resampled, and the default interpolator was set to sitkBSpline. Additional details are available in the Supplementary Materials.


### Feature selection
  We divided all features into three categories: first-order, shape, and texture. To reduce dimensionality, we introduced three steps: (1) features with variance greater than 0.8 were included in the next step; (2) the remaining features were assessed using the F-statistic test in one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) [22] for their association with the presence of genetic mutations. Features with a p-value greater than 0.05 were removed. (3) for features deemed statistically significant, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient between the label and each feature was calculated [23]. This study employed randomized shuffling of the data series prior to allocation.


### Model establishment
  We constructed four predictive models based on the filtered features: a clinical model, a cancer-indicator model, a radiomics model, and a combined model. A total of 1,055 patients eligible for imaging analysis were randomly divided into training and testing sets in an 8:2 ratio. All models employed the same preprocessing techniques and classifiers across both datasets.

  The combined model was formulated as follows: Mcombined = wRadiomicsMRadiomics + wclinicalMclinical + wcancer−indicatorsMcancerindicators.

  The weights wRadiomics, wclinical and wcancer−indicators represent the learned contribution of each sub-model. The contribution weights in the combined model are not pre-defined or static. They are learned dynamically b the model during the end-to-end training process of the combined model. Specifically, these weights are derived through a component of our fusion architecture where learnable parameters, corresponding to the influence of each sub-model’s output, are passed through a softmax function. This mathematical operation ensures that the resulting weights are positive and sum to unity, thereby representing a normalized and dynamically adjusted contribution of each sub-model to the final integrated prediction. These learnable parameters are optimized concurrently with the rest of the combined model’s parameters using backpropagation.

  We evaluated multiple machine learning algorithms, including AdaBoost [24], Decision Tree [25], bootstrap aggregating decision tree [26], Gaussian process [27], gradient boosting decision tree (GBDT) [28], K-nearest neighbor (KNN), logistic regression [29], Random Forest(RF) [30],support vector machines (SVM) [31], eXtreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) [32], and quadratic discriminant analysis (QDA) [33] for machine learning. To mitigate the class imbalance in our dataset, where patients with co-mutations constituted a substantially smaller proportion relative to other cohorts, we applied the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) [34], a widely used method for addressing imbalanced classification tasks, to improve the predictive performance of our model. To further improve predictive accuracy, hyperparameter tuning was conducted for each classifier using a grid search strategy with 5-fold cross-validation on the training dataset. The AUC on the validation folds served as the primary metric for selecting the optimal hyperparameter set for each algorithm. The detailed hyperparameters are presented in supplementary materials Table S2.


### Biologic mechanisms of the radiomic features
  To investigate the biological basis of high-dimensional radiomic features, we analyzed a subset of 546 patients who underwent both 56-gene panel next-generation sequencing and LDCT. Hierarchical clustering was performed using the Ward linkage method, and the optimal number of clusters was determined based on the silhouette score.


### Statistical method
  The model performance was evaluated by AUC and Kaplan-Meier analysis. To select best performance model, we employed 5-fold cross-validation method. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 23.0, using mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables, rank sum test for between-group comparisons of continuous variables, and frequency for subtyped variables, and chi-square test or Fisher’s exact probability method for between-group comparisons of subtyped variables. Two-sided tests with P < 0.05 were statistically different. For pairwise comparisons between model AUCs, we performed the DeLong test, which provides a statistical assessment of whether the difference between two AUCs is significant [35].


### Software and implementation details
  All computational analyses were performed using Python version 3.7.1. The VB-Net was implemented using the PyTorch framework 1.8.2, with NVIDIA CUDAToolkit 10.2.89 employed for GPU acceleration where applicable. Radiomic feature extraction was conducted using the PyRadiomics 3.1.0. Standard machine learning models, feature selection techniques, and statistical analyses were implemented primarily with the scikit-learn version 1.0.2 and SciPy version 1.7.3. ITK-SNAP 5.2.0 was utilized for image visualization and to support segmentation processes. The SMOTE was applied using imblearn version 0.12.4.

### Image segmentation
  After using the Bezier Curve to preprocess the VB-Net, the segmentation ROIs were more sensitive to edges. Specifically, when the randomly assigned probability was less than 0.6, conventional preprocessing methods were applied directly. Conversely, when the probability exceeded 0.6, the images first underwent a nonlinear transformation based on Bézier curves to introduce geometric variability. Subsequently, the transformed images were further normalized using both quantile normalization and standard z-score normalization (mean/standard deviation) to standardize the pixel intensity distribution. At the same time, the recognition accuracy of the optimized model for the images outside the lung was increased, and the false positive rate of the false-positive extrapulmonary lesions segmented by the original model was also reduced after preprocessing and optimization. Figure 3 showed CT images of four patients in different co-mutation groups acquired at three different time points, before surgery, at the time of recurrence, and after treatment with TKI for outcome.


### Feature selection
  After preprocessing, 1084 radiomic features were used for model construction. Initially, features were categorized into three distinct groups: first-order statistics, shape attributes, and texture characteristics. The ‘texture’ category encompasses all the high-dimensional features. For each feature, we determined the p-value to assess statistical significance. Boxplots were subsequently used to visually represent the distribution of p-values across the different feature categories. Notably, all features within the ‘shape’ category demonstrated p-values below the 0.05 threshold, indicating statistical significance, as depicted in Figure S3-1. In addition, we computed the Spearman rank correlation coefficient for each feature. Boxplot visualizations revealed that the ‘first-order’ and ‘texture’ categories exhibited a more extensive range of correlations, encompassing both positive and negative associations, in contrast to the ‘shape’ category, as shown in Figure S3-2. All groups had a median correlation of zero or close to zero, indicating that features in all groups had no strong monotonic relationship for the majority of features on average. The outliers suggested that there were some features with particularly strong positive or negative correlations compared with the rest of the features in their respective groups. Furthermore, we calculated the distance correlation between the three groups and gene mutation status. The central line in each box in figure S3-3 represents the median of the distance correlation coefficients for each category, suggesting a relatively strong association between features and labels. The ‘shape’ category appears to have the highest median correlation, followed by ‘texture’ and then ‘first-order’. The height of each box represents the interquartile range (IQR). A larger IQR indicates greater variability in the distance correlation coefficients. ‘texture’ showed smaller IQR compared with other two groups, indicating lower variability. ‘texture’ showing several points that lie significantly outside the rest of the data. Also, ‘texture’ appears to have the widest range, suggesting that the distance correlation varies more across different features within this category. When comparing across categories, ‘shape’ features tend to have a higher median distance correlation and a slightly tighter spread, suggesting that these features may consistently have a strong and more uniform association with the labels.


### Performance of models
  Figure 4 presents a comprehensive receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis comparing four predictive models for genotype classification, with particular focus on EGFR-TP53 co-mutations. Label 0, 1, and 2 stood for EGFR-mutation, EGFR-TP53 co-mutations and other gene mutations, respectively. Each model demonstrates distinct performance characteristics across training and test sets. The clinical model, employing L2-Normalization preprocessing and Random Forest classification, achieved notable performance in detecting co-mutations (label 1) with AUC values of 0.711 and 0.771 in training and test sets, respectively (Fig. 4A). The radiomics model, developed through extensive algorithmic optimization across 90 different combinations of preprocessing and classification methods, demonstrated enhanced predictive capability with an AUC of 0.755 for co-mutations using Quantile transformer and Random Forest (Fig. 4B). A comprehensive summary of the AUC values obtained on both the training and test sets for the various feature preprocessing techniques and classifier algorithms explored during this extensive optimization process is provided in the Supplementary Materials Table S3. The cancer-indicator model, constructed from four blood-based biomarkers, showed moderate performance in co-mutation detection with a test set AUC of 0.683 (Fig. 4C). Notably, the combined model, which integrates features from all three previous approaches, exhibited superior performance in identifying co-mutations (AUC = 0.843) (Fig. 4D), demonstrating the synergistic benefit of multi-modal data integration in genomic prediction tasks. This progressive improvement in predictive performance across models, particularly for the challenging task of co-mutation detection, underscores the value of comprehensive data integration in enhancing diagnostic accuracy. ## Discussion
  Our study demonstrated that the combined AI model, which integrates radiomic features and clinical data, achieved superior performance (AUC = 0.843) in predicting EGFR/TP53 co-mutations compared to single-modality approaches. In comparison, the imaging-only model and the clinical-only model yielded AUCs of 0.711 and 0.683, respectively. Notably, shape and texture-related radiomic features exhibited strong associations with mutation status, highlighting their potential as non-invasive biomarkers for genetic profiling. Incorporating a broader set of features—particularly clinical information—significantly enhanced the model’s predictive performance, consistent with findings from prior studies such as the EGFR prediction model proposed by Tu et al. [38]. Our best-performing model, which combined imaging and clinical data, achieved an AUC of 0.843 in cross-validation, underscoring its robust predictive capability and potential for clinical implementation. Several models have been constructed to predict the mutation status in patients with lung cancer. However, these studies predicted the gene mutation status separately [39–41], which could not identify patients with EGFR and TP53 co-mutations. Zhang et al. used a Logistic Regression model to simultaneously determine the presence of four types of gene mutations: EGFR, KRAS, Erb-B2 receptor tyrosine kinase 2 (ERBB2) and TP53 [42]. The AUCs for discriminating between these four mutations reached good results, ranging from 0.78 to 0.88. However, their sample size was relatively small compared with that in our study. Furthermore, we offer deeper insight into EGFR+/− and TP53+/− patients. Some studies have also explored the use of machine learning on pathological slides. For example, one study constructed a model for histopathology slides to automatically classify lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), or normal lung tissue, and further predict the ten most commonly mutated genes in LUAD, which could reveal the mutation status of TP53 with an AUC of 0.76 [43]. In contrast, our study utilized CT imaging, which offers a more efficient and non-invasive alternative to histopathological analysis.

  Our investigation is supported by a relatively large dataset, significantly exceeding the sample sizes used in previous studies. The development of our model incorporated a comprehensive set of features and employed 11 distinct machine learning classifiers, a strategy that enhances the robustness and reproducibility of the integrated model. To explore the underlying biological mechanisms, we further examined the correlations between genetic mutations and radiomic features. Both the heatmap and silhouette score revealed strong associations between EGFR/TP53 status and radiomic characteristics. Additionally, we conducted decision curve analysis to evaluate the clinical utility of the combined model. The results demonstrated the model’s potential to accurately and reliably stratify patients by genotype, thereby assisting clinicians in making early and informed treatment decisions, particularly for individuals at risk of developing drug resistance.

  Several limitations in the present study warrant consideration and highlight important avenues for future research. First, the retrospective nature, while allowing for a large initial cohort, may introduce selection bias. Future prospective multi-center studies with standardized data collection protocols would be optimal to minimize such biases and confirm our findings. Second, our single-center design limits the immediate generalizability of the findings, and the performance discrepancy between training and testing datasets suggests potential overfitting. To address this, future research should prioritize external validation using diverse, multicenter datasets to robustly assess and improve model generalizability. Furthermore, exploring advanced regularization technique during model development could mitigate overfitting and enhance model stability. Third, our focus on EGFR and TP53 co-mutations, though clinically significant, does not encompass the full spectrum of genetic alterations that may influence radiomic phenotypes and TKI resistance. Future studies should aim to develop models capable of predicting a broader array of actionable mutations and co-mutation patterns, which may involve integrating more comprehensive genomic data and leveraging machine learning architectures designed for multi-label classification. Furthermore, while the combined model showed improvement, the modest gains in specificity and sensitivity suggest that the feature integration methodology could be further optimized. Future work could explore more sophisticated data fusion techniques, advanced ensemble methods, or end-to-end deep learning models that can learn optimal representations directly from multi-modal data. Adopting state-of-the-art AI development frameworks and MLOps (Machine Learning Operations) principles could also streamline the experimental workflow, enhance model optimization, and improve reproducibility.


## Conclusion
  This study presents a promising non-invasive approach for identifying EGFR/TP53 co-mutations in NSCLC patients. The high predictive accuracy of our combined model could facilitate earlier identification of patients likely to exhibit TKI resistance, enabling more personalized treatment strategies.
