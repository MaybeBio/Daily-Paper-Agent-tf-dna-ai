# Negative pressure mechanical signal increases the phosphorylation of eNOS Ser1177 by upregulating HSP90 expression to promote wound angiogenesis


## Graphical abstract
  Graphical abstract


## Introduction
  The process of wound repair involves a sophisticated mechanism that is dependent on the synchronized collaboration of various regulatory components [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. However, under certain conditions such as postsurgical infection, chemoradiotherapy, and underlying disease, wounds often display issues such as impaired angiogenesis, leading to chronicity. One common characteristic of chronic wounds is poor local blood supply, which can result from a variety of causes [[5], [6], [7]]. Consequently, promoting angiogenesis to improve the local blood supply has become a key focus in the treatment of chronic wounds. Angiogenesis is a multistep, complex process that occurs throughout the healing phase, during which vascular endothelial cells proliferate and form a new vascular network through dynamic changes in cell shape and behavior, thus facilitating nutrient acquisition for wound cells [[8], [9], [10]]. Therefore, regulating the balance of angiogenesis-related growth factors is crucial in chronic wound repair, as this equilibrium is beneficial for wound healing [11,12].

  Among various wound healing therapies, negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT) offers distinct clinical advantages. It is applicable to nearly all chronic wounds and has recently shown promising results in improving surgical outcomes, preventing wound nonhealing, etc. [[13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20]]. NPWT promotes healing by inducing macro- or micro deformations in wounds, removing extracellular fluid, and offering additional benefits such as reducing the bacterial load and stimulating the production of growth factors. These effects help create a microenvironment conducive to wound healing [[21], [22], [23]]. NPWT has proven to be especially irreplaceable for wounds that are difficult to heal, including those with infections after surgery and those that cannot be treated surgically immediately [[24], [25], [26], [27], [28], [29], [30], [31]]. During NPWT, endothelial cells in wounds experience mechanical forces such as stretching and shear stress. Various mechanosensitive molecules on the cell membrane, such as G protein-coupled receptors, stretch-activated ion channels, growth factor receptors, or integrins, detect mechanical stimuli and modulate cellular behavior by activating distinct mechanotransduction pathways [32]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that appropriate mechanical forces can promote wound angiogenesis and microvascular remodeling [[33], [34], [35], [36]], with nitric oxide-mediated vasodilation playing a key role in this process [37,38]. Upon mechanical stimulation, intracellular calcium ions (Ca2+) are released and bind to calmodulin (CaM) to form a Ca-CaM complex, which then activates endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS). Activated eNOS helps maintain vascular tone, and homeostasis, and promotes angiogenesis [39,40]. Heat shock protein 90 (HSP90), an important stress protein and chaperone, participates in the folding, transport, and assembly of cytoplasmic proteins [41]. Some studies suggest that HSP90 may regulate eNOS activation [42,43]. Caveolin-1 (Cav-1), which is widely distributed in the vascular endothelium, plays a crucial role in maintaining the balance between the proliferation and apoptosis of endothelial cells and promoting angiogenesis. It has been reported that Cav-1 forms a complex with eNOS, inhibiting its activation [44]. However, the role and mechanism of HSP90 and Cav-1 in regulating eNOS under NPWT conditions remain poorly understood.

  This study aimed to explore the effect of NPWT on wound angiogenesis from a mechanical force perspective. Using wound samples from patients undergoing NPWT, we applied single-cell transcriptome analysis and tandem mass tags (TMT) proteomics to elucidate the mechanisms by which NPWT promotes angiogenesis. Specifically, we focused on the regulation of HSP90, Cav-1, and eNOS expression, activation, and their interactions. These findings may provide novel therapeutic targets for improving wound healing.


## Materials and methods

### GEO datasets and clinical samples collected and analyzed
  The data of a transcriptomic analysis by NPWT from GEO datasets (Series Accession: GSE272918, ID: 200272918) were collected and analyzed. The study involving human samples was approved by the ethics committee of Tangdu Hospital, Fourth Military Medical University (No. K202203-07). Wound tissues from six patients (three treated with NPWT and three untreated) were obtained from individuals undergoing flap repair surgery at Tangdu Hospital, with informed consent.


### Animal experiments
  All animal and cell experiments were approved by the Experimental Animal Center of the Fourth Military Medical University (No. 20240251) and conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health guidelines. To evaluate wound healing under negative pressure, the rats were anesthetized with isoflurane (5 % induction, 3 % maintenance), and full-thickness skin wounds (2 cm diameter) were established on their backs. The wounds were covered with a porous sponge, connected to a suction tube, and sealed with surgical film. The animals were firstly divided into two groups: the NPWT group, which was treated with intermittent negative pressure via an aspirator, and the control group, which received sham surgery and no negative pressure. The treatments included 17-AAG (200 mg/kg) and lentivirus (100 µ l/wound, per wound injection). Each group contains 3 rats for experiment. The NPWT group underwent 7 days of negative pressure treatment, whereas the control group received sham surgery. Wound blood flow was assessed on day 21 via laser Doppler imaging. The rats were euthanized via carbon dioxide overdose, and wound tissues were collected for further analysis.


### Single-cell detection of rat wound tissues
  Single-cell RNA sequencing was conducted by Singleron Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). The raw reads were processed via FastQC and Fastp for quality control, followed by adaptor and poly-A trimming with Cutadapt. Clean reads were aligned to the GRCh38 genome (Ensemble v92) via STAR, and gene/UMI counts were quantified with FeatureCounts. Data analysis was performed via Seurat v3.1.2, which applies quality filters to exclude cells with <200 or top 2 % gene counts, top 2 % UMI counts, >50 % mitochondrial content, or genes expressed in <5 cells, resulting in 21,525 high-quality cells (average: 1,559 genes, 5,877 UMIs/cell). Normalized data were subjected to PCA on the top 2000 variable genes, with batch effects corrected via Harmony v1.0. The cells were clustered into 33 groups (resolution = 1.2) on the basis of the top 20 PCs and visualized via t-SNE/UMAP. Cluster annotation utilized canonical markers from the literature and the SynEcoSys™ database. Subclustering of endothelial cells, fibroblasts, myeloid cells, myocytes, pericytes, and T cells was performed postbatch correction. Differential gene expression analysis (Wilcoxon test, logFC > 0.25, p < 0.05 with Bonferroni correction) was used to identify DEGs, followed by functional enrichment analysis using clusterProfiler (v3.16.1). Developmental trajectories were inferred via Monocle2 (v2.10.0) with DDRTree reduction, and cell–cell interactions were predicted via CellPhoneDB (v2.1.0) on the basis of ligand-receptor pairs (1000 permutations, p < 0.05, average log expression > 0.1); and visualized via circlize (v0.4.10).


### Development of an in vitro cell negative pressure gradient simulator
  The device comprises a gradient negative pressure regulation system and an intelligent control system. The gradient system included a pressure regulating valve, a culture device with six stimulation chambers (each covered by an elastic membrane), a vacuum pump, a laser ranging device, and a control unit. The culture device featured a negative pressure suction cup that moved vertically along guide rails to seal the chambers. The vacuum pump applied negative pressure, whereas the laser ranging device measured real-time membrane deformation. The pressure of the control unit was adjusted on the basis of deformation data. The intelligent control system, consisting of a control box, panel, and touchscreen, enables automated parameter selection (e.g., pressure mode, pressure levels, and cycle duration) and real-time chamber monitoring. Developed by Beijing Taibaite Technology Co., Ltd., the device was operated by seeding cells onto the elastic membrane, setting parameters via the control panel, and monitoring pressure levels via the pressure sensor and laser ranging device. For this experiment, intermittent mode was used (20 s of negative pressure followed by a 10-second interval as a intact period).


### Finite element analysis of flexible culture plates and wound tissues
  Finite element modeling was conducted via Abaqus 2020. The flexible culture plate was modeled symmetrically with a radius of 17.5 mm and a thickness of 0.5 mm, and was clamped 15–17.5 mm from the center via 1.5 mm-thick clamps (Young’s modulus: 300 MPa, Poisson’s ratio: 0.4). The elastic membrane was modeled via the Mooney-Rivlin hyperelastic model. A global mesh size of 0.1 mm was applied, with four-node bilinear axisymmetric quadrilateral elements (CAX4R) and reduced integration. A fixed constraint was applied to the clamp’s right boundary, and a downward uniform load was used to simulated the air pressure during cell culture, with the maximum strain recorded at the central axis. For experimental validation, rat wounds were prepared as described in the main text and coated with matte white aerosol paint and black speckles. A digital speckle system (camera resolution: 2448 × 2048 pixels, 25 mm lens focal length) was calibrated at a standard measurement distance of 656 mm and camera spacing of 290 mm. The displacement and strain fields were measured at six negative pressure levels: −20, −40, −60, −80, −100, and −120 mmHg.


### Cell culture and treatment
  Primary human dermal microvascular endothelial cells (HDMECs) were isolated from cryopreserved human dermal tissue via established methods [45] and cultured in ECM (Sciencell) at 37 ℃ with 5 % CO2 for 24 h. The cells in the corresponding groups were treated with 0.5 mM 17-AAG (MCE) or 0.5 mM 666–15 (MCE). The design and protocol of the in vitro cell negative pressure gradient simulator are detailed in the Supplementary Materials. The NPWT group was cultured in flexible culture plates (Flexcell) and exposed to 40 mmHg negative pressure in intermittent mode (8 h of treatment, 4 h of rest totally), whereas the control group was incubated for 12 h.


### Cell proliferation, apoptosis, necrosis, wound healing and lumen formation assays
  The processed cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 5 × 103 cells/well and cultured in ECM. Cell proliferation was detected via the CCK-8 assay (7seabiotech), with the absorbance measured at 450 nm. For the apoptosis and necrosis assays, processed cells were washed 2–3 times with fresh ECM (3 min per wash) and stained with Hoechst 33,342 nuclear dye (Enzo Life Sciences Inc.) for 10 min. Apoptosis and necrosis were assessed via TUNEL (Solarbio) and AO/EB (Solarbio) assay kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions. For the wound healing assays, processed cells were plated in 6-well plates at 1 × 10⁶ cells/well in serum-free ECM. Wounds were created via cell scratch inserts (ibidi), and the scratch areas were quantified at 0 h and 12 h postremoval. For lumen formation assays, flexible culture plates were precoated with Matrigel (Corning) and incubated at 37 ℃ for solidification. The processed cells were seeded at 1 × 10⁶ cells/well. Lumen formation was subsequently evaluated at 12 h.


### Mass spectrometry analysis
  NanoLC–MS/MS analysis and TMT-based proteomic profiling were conducted by Baiqu Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd. and Jikai Gene Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), respectively. The cells were lysed in buffer containing phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, followed by low-temperature grinding, incubation (4 ℃, 800 rpm, 10 min), and centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 10 min, 4 ℃). The protein concentration was quantified via a BCA assay (absorbance at 562 nm after 37 ℃ incubation for 30 min). For each sample, 100 µg of protein was acetone-precipitated overnight at −20 ℃, washed with 80 % acetone, and reconstituted. Proteins were reduced with 5 mM dithiothreitol (55 ℃, 10 min), alkylated with 10 mM iodoacetamide (room temperature, 15 min in the dark), and digested with trypsin (1:50 ratio, 37 ℃ overnight). The TMT-labeled peptides were processed by SDC removal via TFA precipitation, desalted via a C18 column, and separated via high-pH reverse-phase chromatography (C8 column, 150 mm × 2.1 mm) with a 60-min gradient before fractionation.


### General methodology

#### NO and ROS detection
  NO and ROS levels in human tissue samples were quantified via ELISA kits (Solarbio). For the cell samples, HDMECs were incubated with DAF-FM-DA (50 µmol/L, Beyotime) and DCFH-DA (10 µmol/L, Beyotime) at 37 ℃ for 20 min to measure the NO and ROS contents, respectively.


#### Lentiviral construction, RNA interference, and cell transfection
  Lentiviral vectors encoding shRNA-GNAS and Ad-Scramble were constructed by HANBIO (Wuhan, China). HSP90-targeting and CREB1-targeting siRNAs were customized from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Cell transfection was performed via siRNA and Lipomaster 3000 Transfection Reagent (Vazyme). Briefly, HDMECs were incubated with 10 nmol of siHSP90 or siCREB1, which were mixed with Lipo3000 in Opti-MEM (Gibco). The control siRNA (siNC), at an equal dosage, served as the control of this transfection system. The transfected cells were incubated in a humidified incubator with 5 % CO2 at 37 ℃ for 5 h, after which the medium was replenished with ECM for an additional 72 h before subsequent confirmation and functional investigations.


#### RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR analysis
  Total RNA was extracted from HDMECs or tissue samples via TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen). Reverse transcription of mRNA was performed with the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara), followed by quantitative real-time PCR via the SYBR Green PCR Kit (Takara) on a Biorad-CFX96 thermal cycler (Bio-Rad). Gene expression levels were calculated via the 2-ΔΔCt method.


#### Western blotting, coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and molecular docking assays
  Tissues and cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche). Protein concentrations were determined via a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Proteins were resolved by 10 % SDS–PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore). The membranes were blocked with NcmBlot blocking buffer (NCM Biotech), incubated with primary antibodies, and then probed with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. For co-IP experiments, samples were prepared via an immunoprecipitation kit (Proteintech), and protein interactions were visualized via an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (Bio-Rad). The interaction between Cav-1 and eNOS interaction was predicted via molecular docking, with additional details provided in the supplementary materials.


#### Prediction of Cav-1 and eNOS interactions through molecular docking
  To explore potential interaction sites in the Cav1-eNOS complex, we performed computational molecular docking using HADDOCK 2.4. The docking procedure consisted of three phases: (1) rigid-body docking for energy minimization through random molecular rotation and translation; (2) annealing for interface optimization, including side chain and backbone residue refinement; and (3) solvent refinement within an explicit solvent shell. Owing to the absence of resolved structures for Cav-1 (UniProt Q03135) and eNOS (UniProt P29474), we utilized AlphaFold2 to predict their full-length structures (complete sequences in Supplementary Table 3). Key interaction residues were identified: eNOS (P-1191, ALA-1192, ARG-1183, SER-1177, GLN-176, ARG-1172, GLU-1168, ASP-966) and Cav-1 (U-76, HIS-79, GLU-74, GLU-69, ASP-61, HIS-58, GL-11, HIS-12, LEU-13, LYS-5, THR-15). HADDOCK analysis yielded one distinct cluster, from which the highest-scoring model was selected, providing mechanistic insights into the protein–protein interactions and elucidating the structural basis of the Cav1–eNOS complex.


#### Prediction of transcription factors and dual luciferase reporter activity assay
  Transcription factors regulating HSP90 were predicted through integrated database analysis. Binding scores for promoter sequences were obtained from the JASPAR database (85 % threshold), whereas the 2000 bp upstream HSP90 promoter region was retrieved from NCBI. Human CREB1 binding sites on the HSP90 promoter were identified, and sequences with positive orientation and prediction scores > 10 were selected for visualization. Dual luciferase assays were performed via standard protocols (Promega): the luciferase gene was cloned downstream of either the wild-type or mutant HSP90 promoter region and co-transfected with pcDNA3.1-CREB1 or the control vector into HDMECs. After 48-hour incubation, firefly luciferase activity was measured via the Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System (Promega), with Renilla luciferase as an internal normalization control.


#### Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay
  Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was performed via the Magna ChIP-seq™ Kit (Millipore). The cells were crosslinked with formaldehyde, sonicated, and incubated with target-specific antibodies. The DNA–protein complexes were immunoprecipitated, and the cross-links were reversed to release DNA fragments, which were then purified and amplified via PCR. The antibody and primer sequences are detailed in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.


#### Histological staining and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observation
  Histological analyses were performed via H&E, Masson, and PAS staining kits (Solarbio). For immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF), wound tissues were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at a thickness of 4 µm. IF staining involved deparaffinization, blocking with bovine serum albumin, and incubation overnight with primary antibodies at 4 ℃. After being washed with PBS washing, the sections were incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature, followed by DAPI counterstaining (15 min) and mounting for fluorescence microscopy (Olympus). ImageJ software was used for fluorescence intensity quantification and colocalization analysis. For transmission electron microscopy (TEM), 1 mm3 wound tissue samples were fixed in glutaraldehyde phosphate buffer, processed through standard dehydration and embedding protocols, and sectioned for ultrastructural examination of capillary endothelial cells.


### Statistical analysis
  Statistical analyses were conducted via SPSS 24.0. The quantitative data are expressed as the means ± standard deviations (SDs) from a minimum of three independent experiments. Comparisons between two groups were performed via Student’s t-test, whereas one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc analysis was used for multiple group comparisons. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

### HSP90 modulates eNOS phosphorylation at Ser1177
  RT–PCR revealed that 17-AAG inhibited HSP90 activity without affecting its expression, and that NPWT did not affect the knockout effect of siHSP90. (Fig. 3A), which was consistent with previous findings [47,48]. Additionally, neither 17-AAG nor siHSP90 affected the expression of Cav-1 or eNOS (Fig. 3B and C). Immunofluorescence staining revealed that NPWT significantly increased the expression of the HSP90 protein and promoted the translocation of HSP90 from the nucleus to the cytoplasm, where it colocalized with Cav1. In contrast, 17-AAG or siHSP90 inhibited this translocation. In the normal state, Cav-1 and eNOS were colocalized in the cytoplasm; under NPWT intervention, their colocalization was significantly diminished, accompanied by inconsistent alterations in fluorescence intensity. Notably, these effects were effectively suppressed by either the HSP90 inhibitor 17-AAG or by HSP90 knockdown (siHSP90) (Fig. 4D and E). Western blotting confirmed that NPWT increased HSP90 expression and phosphorylated eNOS at Ser1177, which was blocked by 17-AAG or HSP90 knockdown (siHSP90). NPWT, the HSP90 inhibitor 17-AAG or siHSP90 had no significant effect on the total protein levels of eNOS or Cav-1 (Fig. 4F). The liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry results obtained by immunoprecipitation with Cav-1 antibodies indicated that, compared with that of the negative control IgG the abundance of HSP90 in the liquid was significantly greater. Similarly, the abundance of Cav1 was significantly increased as detected by the eNOS antibody (Fig. 4G). Coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP) results suggested that under the intervention of NPWT, the interaction of HSP90 with Cav-1 was significantly enhanced, and this interaction competitively inhibited the interaction between Cav-1 and eNOS; supplementation with the HSP90 inhibitor 17-AAG inhibited the interaction between HSP90 and Cav-1. Accordingly, the effect of NPWT on inhibiting the interaction between Cav-1 and eNOS also disappeared. In simple terms, NPWT reduced the interaction between Cav1 and eNOS, whereas 17-AAG or HSP90 knockdown (siHSP90) reversed this interaction (Fig. 4H and I). ### NPWT enhances wound healing and alters the cellular composition
  Finite element analysis of the elastic culture plate and laser speckle imaging of the rat wounds revealed that when the negative pressure was between 75 mmHg and 90 mmHg, the degree of tissue displacement was basically consistent with the optimal elastic membrane deformation (40 mmHg) (Supplemental Fig. 2A–C). Therefore, the optimal conditions for animal studies were 80 mmHg in intermittent mode for 7 consecutive days. The process of animal experimentation and the scene of a rat with a full-skin-layer wound model receiving NPWT are depicted. Wound healing was observed at days 0, 7, 14, and 21 (Fig. 5A and B). By observing the changes in wound healing among the control, NPWT and NPWT + 17-AAG groups, we found that NPWT improved wound healing since the existing wound area of the negative pressure group was significantly lower than that of the control group on the 7th day after wounding was created, and it remained significant until complete healing was achieved (Fig. 5C and D). However, the wound area in the NPWT + 17-AAG group was not significantly different from that in the control group. Through comparisons between the NPWT and control groups, we showed that negative pressure treatment promoted wound healing. To further explore the cellular composition of the wound healing tissue under negative pressure conditions and the transcription level during the wound healing process, rat wound tissues from the NPWT group and control group were collected for single-cell transcriptome sequencing (Fig. 5E). Single-cell RNA sequencing of wound tissues revealed 31,508 cells, categorized into six populations: myeloid cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, mural cells, erythrocytes and T cells. We also found that these cell types significantly differed in marker gene expression, these marker genes were reported to be specifically expressed in cells, and we distinctly aligned the marker gene expression levels with those of the other cell subclusters, which further confirmed our cell subclustering results (Fig. 5F–H). Moreover, there was a noticeable difference in cell composition between the NPWT and the control group, which might indicate that the same type of cell demonstrated different transcriptional patterns under various treatments. We systematically analyzed the difference in the cell subtype composition of each cell under the two conditions and found that the proportion of cell subtypes between the NPWT and the control group changed, with endothelial cells showing the most obvious differences between the groups, suggesting that the difference in endothelial composition between the two conditions might be related to the promotion of wound healing under negative pressure (Fig. 5I and J). ### NPWT increases ECs and alters gene expression
  To further investigate changes in single-cell transcription levels and the cell composition of endothelial cells under NPWT conditions, we performed further reclassification of endothelial cells. Reclassification of endothelial cells has identified four subtypes: lymphatic endothelial cells (LECs), arterial endothelial cells (AECs), vein endothelial cells (VeECs), and capillary endothelial cells (CapECs). NPWT significantly increased the proportion of CapECs (Fig. 6A). Gene expression analysis revealed significant upregulation of MKI67 in CapECs under NPWT, indicating their activation (Fig. 6B). Moreover, the activation of CapECs was also supported by pseudotime analysis (Fig. 6C). Histological analysis revealed improved inflammatory cell infiltration, collagen arrangement, and capillary integrity and increased VEGFA and CD31 expression in NPWT-treated wounds, whereas the HSP90 inhibitor 17-AGG reversed these positive effects (Fig. 6D and 6). ## Discussion

### Main interpretation
  Promoting the growth and proliferation of blood vessels contributes to angiogenesis during normal wound healing [49]. Angiogenesis relies on a balance between angiogenesis-promoting and antiangiogenesis molecules that regulate endothelial cell proliferation and migration [50]. Well-regulated angiogenesis is crucial for many physiological processes, such as reproduction and embryonic development. In contrast, abnormal angiogenesis underlies various pathophysiological conditions, including tumor metastasis and atherosclerotic plaque formation. In the clinic, insufficient blood supply to wounds is a significant factor contributing to delayed wound healing. In severe cases, chronic wounds may form, which presents a major challenge in the field of wound repair. Restoring blood flow to wounds and promoting angiogenesis are critical steps in the treatment of chronic wounds. Vascular endothelial cells, which form the inner lining of blood vessels, play an essential role in the repair of chronic wounds [51]. NPWT is a technique that applies negative pressure to wounds of various causes to promote healing. It has been widely used to stimulate wound angiogenesis and granulation, making it one of the most successful mechanical aids for wound healing [52]. The results of this study demonstrate that NPWT improves wound healing, likely reflecting underlying changes at the cellular or molecular level. Further research will provide new insights into these changes and potentially identify novel drug targets for wound treatment.

  Given the unique clinical advantages of NPWT, further exploration of its mechanisms is crucial. However, the role and underlying mechanisms of the negative pressure microenvironment in promoting the proliferation of vascular endothelial cells on the wound surface remain unclear. The field of biomechanics is rapidly advancing, but technological limitations prevent the replication of many in vivo models in vitro. The NPWT is one such example. In the present study, a negative pressure environment was applied to the cellular mechanical microenvironment [[53], [54], [55]]. A comparison of mechanical strain in vitro and real tissue deformation was achieved via finite element analysis [56,57]. Through finite element analysis and deformation monitoring, we confirmed a suitable negative pressure, which caused the deformation of the elastic culture plates to mimic in vivo tissue deformation. Specially, by influencing cell deformation through negative pressure, we can affect cellular biological behavior and intracellular pathways. Furthermore, we used a laser rangefinder to measure displacement (the maximum distance between the normal and negative pressure states and correlated it with negative pressure. This relationship can be compared with the displacement and negative pressure data obtained from animals via digital speckle technology. Owing to differences in the Young's modulus and Poisson's ratio between flexible culture plates and the outer layer of skin, and because cells in vivo are in a three-dimensional state while cells in vitro are two-dimensional, it is not possible to achieve a one-to-one correspondence between in vivo and in vitro negative pressure values. However, a correlation can be established to some extent on the basis of displacement corresponding to negative pressure. Therefore, we determined the negative pressure intervention conditions for animal experiments on the basis of the optimal negative pressure value from cell experiments.

  This study revealed that negative pressure intervention significantly increased the expression of HSP90 in HDMECs, indirectly affecting the phosphorylation level of eNOS in the cytoplasm of these cells. HSP90 is a molecular chaperone involved in various biological processes. Notably, 17-AAG, an inhibitor of HSP90, can bind to HSP90 and inhibit signals, leading to the inactivation of HSP90, but it does not reduce the expression of HSP90. Previous studies have shown that inhibiting HSP90 activity with substances such as 17-AAG can exert anticancer and antifibrotic effects [58,59]. However, our research revealed its positive role in wound angiogenesis, and the application of siHSP90 further enhanced the persuasiveness of our findings. Cav-1, an essential cell membrane protein, forms a lipid raft structure in vascular endothelial cells through the invagination of the lipid membrane. This structure can also detach into the cytoplasm, where it regulates vascular endothelial cell neogenesis by interacting with other proteins through its 82–101 amino acid scaffolding domain (CSD) [60]. HSP90 can regulate and transmit the biological effects of Cav-1 and eNOS through interactions with each other. In this study, we further revealed that Cav-1 not only interacts with HSP90, but also interacts with eNOS, which results in a competitive relationship between Cav-1 and HSP90, and thus participates in the regulation of vascular endothelial neogenesis. NOS proteins can be classified into three subtypes, namely, inducible NOS (iNOS), endothelial NOS (eNOS), and neuronal NOS (nNOS), on the basis of their synthesis sites, expression patterns, and Ca2+ dependence. eNOS, a Ca2+-dependent NOS, plays a crucial role in the cardiovascular system, particularly in maintaining vascular endothelial function. Since eNOS phosphorylation protects the vascular endothelium, its regulation has become a focal point of research. The known phosphorylation and dephosphorylation sites of eNOS include Ser1177/1179 (human), Ser633/635, Ser615/617, Ser114/116, and Thr495/4977. Previous studies have shown that protein phosphatase-2A (PP2A) dephosphorylates the Ser1177 site of eNOS by hydrolyzing phosphate ester bonds [61].

  In this study, we found that negative pressure intervention increased the proliferation and migration of vascular endothelial cells, suggesting that negative pressure can promote vascular endothelial cell regeneration. At the molecular level, we observed no significant difference in the expression of total eNOS protein or total Cav-1 in the cytoplasm of HDMECs. Therefore, we proceeded to examine the level of eNOS phosphorylation at Ser1177 and the interaction between Cav-1 and HSP90, as well as Cav-1 and eNOS. The results revealed that negative pressure intervention significantly increased the phosphorylation of eNOS at Ser1177 in the cytoplasm of HDMECs. More importantly, Cav-1 in the cytoplasm of HDMECs interacts with both HSP90 and eNOS. NPWT increased the expression of HSP90, which increased Cav-1 binding to HSP90, thereby competitively inhibiting the interaction between Cav-1 and eNOS. This led to a decrease in Cav-1 binding to eNOS and an increase in eNOS Ser1177 phosphorylation. Additionally, readministration of the HSP90 inhibitor 17-AAG or HSP90 knockdown (siHSP90) enhanced the interaction between Cav-1 and eNOS. We further analyzed the spatial structure of Cav-1 and eNOS and discovered that the domain adjacent to the eNOS Ser1177 site has strong hydrophobic interactions. The binding of Cav-1 to eNOS causes the Ser1177 site to become embedded within Cav-1 amino acid residues, preventing its hydrophobic surface from being exposed. This inhibits phosphorylation by phosphate groups, explaining the findings that molecular structure-level. NPWT can promote the proliferation and migration of vascular endothelial cells and enhance their regeneration. Mechanistically, NPWT increases HSP90 expression, leading to enhanced Cav-1 binding to HSP90. This competitively reduces Cav-1 binding to eNOS, alleviating Cav-1′s inhibitory effect on eNOS, which activates Ser1177 phosphorylation. This process plays a key role in promoting angiogenesis. Next, we explored how NPWT enhances the expression of HSP90, to further verify this mechanism.

  Importantly, our single-cell data revealed that NPWT significantly altered the cellular composition of the wound bed and, crucially, enhanced ligand–receptor-mediated communication networks, particularly between endothelial cells and both fibroblasts and myeloid cells, as shown in Fig. 5L. Fibroblasts are pivotal for extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition and remodeling during wound healing, and they secrete key angiogenic factors (e.g., VEGF and FGF) and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) that facilitate endothelial cell migration and vascularization [[62], [63], [64]]. Myeloid cells, particularly macrophages, dynamically regulate inflammation and the transition toward a pro-healing (M2) phenotype that actively promotes angiogenesis through the secretion of factors (e.g., VEGF and TGF-β) [65,66]. The observed reduction in inflammatory cell infiltration scores and tissue ROS levels under NPWT suggests that the immune microenvironment is modulated toward a less inflammatory, more pro-regenerative state. Therefore, while the GNAS/CREB1/HSP90/eNOS axis represents a direct endothelial mechanotransduction pathway activated by NPWT, the concurrent enhancement of intercellular communication between ECs, fibroblasts, and immune cells likely creates a synergistic proangiogenic and prohealing niche. The improved collagen organization observed histologically further supports the involvement of activated fibroblasts. Future studies specifically targeting fibroblast activation or macrophage polarization in the context of NPWT will be essential to fully reveal the relative contributions and potential interplay between direct endothelial signaling and this supportive paracrine microenvironment.

  In additon, we found that the proportion of endothelial cells was significantly increased under NPWT. These findings indicate that, at the single-cell level, endothelial cells play a crucial role in wound healing. Moreover, angiogenesis is a central step in wound repair, and endothelial cells play an extremely important role in this process. Therefore, further investigations of the cellular and molecular characteristics of ECs during wound healing are necessary. Single datasets provide us with a unique opportunity to analyze cellular communications mediated by ligand–receptor interactions. To determine the role of mechanical signals and biological signal conversion in the microenvironment during negative pressure treatment, we detected protein expression in the wound tissue before and after negative pressure. In vitro NPWT intervention transmits mechanical stimulus signals through the cell membrane to further regulate the expression of downstream signals. Therefore, we studied whether NPWT regulates or activates cell membrane proteins to further transmit signals into the cell. We found that NPWT significantly increased the expression of the membrane protein GNAS. As an important signal transduction protein, the main function of GNAS is to activate adenylate cyclase in the G protein-coupled receptor signaling pathway, leading to an increase in cAMP levels, which transmit and amplify extracellular signals, regulate downstream signaling pathways, and control target genes [67,68]. Our research revealed that NPWT significantly increases the expression of the membrane protein GNAS, leading us to hypothesize that NPWT may be transmitted through GNAS, potentially regulating the expression of HSP90. Furthermore, prediction of the transcription factor for HSP90 revealed that CREB1 strongly activates HSP90 transcription. A previous study showed that GNAS activates cAMP, which in turn activates CREB1 [69]. Therefore, we sought to clarify the relationship between NPWT and the transcription factor CREB1. Further experiments using immunofluorescence colocalization revealed that NPWT increases the expression of GNAS leading to the phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of CREB1. Our Ch-IP and dual-luciferase reporter gene experiments revealed that after phosphorylated CREB1 enters the nucleus it can bind to the promoter region of HSP90, enhancing its transcription. The application of siCREB1 or the CREB inhibitor 666–15 blocked CREB1-mediated expression of HSP90. Therefore, we elucidated the mechanism by which NPWT regulates the expression of HSP90.


### Limitations
  This study has the following limitations: Our in vitro NPWT model was built via deformation of elastic culture plates. A comparison of mechanical strain in vitro and real tissue deformation was achieved via finite element analysis. The method we used focused on physical analysis, which lacks a comparison of biological features. In our further experiments, the single-cell sequencing data from the in vitro model and in vivo model will be compared and analyzed to perform a general evaluation of our in vitro NPWT model. Moreover, the current study utilized a modeling method involving lentivirus injection during wound creation, but using transgenic animal models with Cre-LoXP technology, wound tissue-specific knockout/overexpression of HSP90, eNOS, Cav-1, and GNAS might be a better choice, and this could be incorporated into our future research plans. This study partially elucidates the mechanisms by which NPWT promotes wound angiogenesis, but the effects of NPWT on epidermal cells, fibroblasts, immune cells and their interactions need further investigation. In the end, the focus of our study was centered on demonstrating the downstream mechanism of how endothelial cells behave after NPWT, which ignores the upstream mechanism of how mechanical stimulation activates the whole molecular pathway. Our further exploration will focus on how cells sense changes in the mechanical microenvironment and convert these mechanical signals into the activation of key proteins in the inner signaling pathway (such as GNAS).


### Conclusion
  In summary, NPWT is able to transmit negative pressure mechanical stimuli to the cytoplasm through the membrane protein GNAS, which activates cAMP to phosphorylate CREB1, allowing it to enter the nucleus and bind to the promoter region of the HSP90 gene, enhancing the transcription of HSP90 and ultimately leading to an increase in HSP90 expression. This results in increased binding of HSP90 with eNOS, competitively inhibiting the binding of Cav-1 with eNOS, thereby derepressing the inhibition of eNOS by Cav-1. This activates the Ser1177 site of eNOS, promoting the release of NO by vascular endothelial cells, reducing ROS in vascular endothelial cells, inhibiting apoptotic necrosis of vascular endothelial cells, promoting the proliferation of vascular endothelial cells, and promoting angiogenesis in wound beds. This study provides new insights into the mechanisms that protect the vascular endothelium by regulating eNOS and offers potential therapeutic strategies for wound treatment.
