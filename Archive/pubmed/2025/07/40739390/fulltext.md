# Targeting PTK2 by vaccarin alleviates osteoporosis through inhibiting ferroptosis via modulating P53 acetylation/succinylation

## Introduction
  Osteoporosis (OP) is a systemic skeletal disorder marked by deterioration of bone microarchitecture, primarily affecting postmenopausal women and older adults (Chan etal 2024). This disease leads to reduced bone mineral density, disruption of trabecular structure, and overall decline in bone quality, all of which contribute to increased bone fragility and a heightened risk of fractures—even from minor trauma (Kenkre and Bassett 2018). Under normal physiological circumstances, bone homeostasis is maintained by a careful balance between the activities of osteoblasts (which form bone) and osteoclasts (which resorb bone) Hu et al. 2021. Among these, osteoblasts play a crucial role in bone formation, skeletal development, and the regulation of bone mass (Esen and Long 2014). The main pharmacological treatments for OP in clinical settings are anti-resorptive agents, especially bisphosphonates, which work by inhibiting osteoclast-mediated bone breakdown. However, prolonged use of these drugs is associated with significant risks, including rare but serious complications like atypical femoral fractures (Reid and Billington 2022; Liu et al. 2023). To advance the field, it is essential to better understand the molecular mechanisms that regulate osteoblast differentiation. Gaining such insights could pave the way for new therapeutic strategies, offering more effective management of OP progression.

  Ferroptosis has a substantial impact on the osteogenic differentiation and mineralization processes of osteoblasts (Xu et al. 2022). Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), which play a key role in maintaining bone metabolic balance, are capable of differentiating into osteoblasts and regulating osteoclast function (Liu et al. 2019). Emerging evidence suggests that ferroptosis signaling is strongly activated in BMSCs during tobacco-induced osteoporosis, causing significant disruption to osteogenic differentiation (Jing et al. 2023). Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms by which ferroptosis affects BMSC-driven bone formation remain incompletely understood.

  PTK2 is a crucial non-receptor tyrosine kinase that plays a central role in regulating cellular motility and survival (Lee, et al. 2020). Upon activation, this versatile kinase orchestrates several essential physiological processes, including cell adhesion, proliferation, migration, and resistance to apoptosis (Fan et al. 2019). PTK2 also acts as a key regulator of skeletal homeostasis and is thought to participate in signaling pathways that either drive osteoblast maturation or influence commitment to the osteogenic lineage (Salasznyk et al. 2007). Notably, PTK2 has been shown to inhibit ferroptosis in the context of chronic liver injury (Chen et al. 2024). However, the specific molecular mechanisms by which PTK2 modulates ferroptosis to support osteogenic differentiation remain to be fully elucidated.

  Previous research has demonstrated that PTK2 deficiency in murine embryos (Lim et al. 2008) leads to activation of P53, a central regulator of ferroptosis. Conversely, depletion of P53 has been shown to boost mesenchymal stem cell proliferation and increase bone mass (Liu and Li 2010). Despite these findings, the precise role of P53-associated ferroptosis in the osteogenic transformation of BMSCs remains poorly understood. P53 activity is dynamically regulated through various post-translational modifications (PTMs). For example, reduced acetylation at the K120 site diminishes the transcriptional activity of P53, thereby reducing both apoptosis (Tang et al. 2006) and ferroptosis (Sen et al. 2013) susceptibility in cancer cells. Additionally, SIRT5-mediated desuccinylation at K120 has been found to suppress P53 function in DNA-damaged HCT116 cells (Liu et al. 2022). However, it is still unclear whether PTK2 influences P53 acetylation or succinylation to mitigate ferroptosis and support osteogenesis—an important question that merits further investigation.

  Vaccarin, a dodecahydroxyl flavonoid monomer extracted from the seeds of Vaccaria segetalis, exhibits a range of pharmacological effects. Notable among these are its ability to protect endothelial cells, promote wound healing (Hou et al. 2020), safeguard the liver and kidneys, and inhibit both osteoclast formation and osteoporosis (Li et al. 2022). Pharmacological screening of 3,060 bioactive compounds has identified this Vaccaria segetalis-derived flavonoid as the most potent osteoinductive agent, substantially boosting the differentiation of BMSCs into osteoblasts and enhancing subsequent mineralization. Nevertheless, the relationship between Vaccarin and ferroptosis-related osteoporosis has yet to be clarified.

  In this study, we explored the roles of PTK2, P53, and ferroptosis in regulating osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs and the progression of osteoporosis. Our findings demonstrate that overexpression of PTK2 protects against erastin-induced ferroptosis, thereby enhancing BMSCs’ osteogenic differentiation and mineralization. In models of calvarial bone defects, BMSCs transduced with adenovirus-expressing PTK2 (ADV-PTK2) significantly improved bone regeneration, while also reducing ferroptosis and bone loss induced by ovariectomy (OVX). Mechanistically, we found that PTK2 interacts with MOF, leading to decreased P53 acetylation and succinylation at the K120 site, which in turn restores the expression of FN1. Furthermore, molecular docking analysis identified Vaccarin as a compound that targets PTK2, inhibits ferroptosis, and alleviates osteoporosis. These results highlight PTK2 as a promising therapeutic target for osteoporosis and suggest Vaccarin as a potential candidate drug for future treatment strategies.


## Materials and methods

### Chemicals, antibodies and cell culture
  The utilized chemicals and antibodies are detailed in Table 1.

  Mouse bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) were obtained from Chuanqiu Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China; website:http://www.chuanqiubio.com/). Cells were maintained at 37 °C under 5% CO₂ in DMEM containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin.


### RNA sequence
  Femoral bone specimens were harvested from three OVX and three SHAM mice, followed by total RNA extraction with TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #15,596,018) in accordance with the manufacturer's guidelines. The integrity and concentration of isolated RNA were verified using a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer to meet quality control criteria. Inclusion criteria comprised: > 50 ng/μL concentration, RIN ≥ 7.0, and ≥ 1 μg total yield. PCR-amplified target fragments (300 ± 50 bp) were used for strand-specific library preparation. PE150 sequencing was conducted on the illumina NovaseqTM 6000 platform (LC Biotechnology) per standard protocols.


### Bioinformatics analysis
  The gene regulatory networks were generated with Cytoscape 3.8.2, whereas STRING was employed to examine protein–protein interactions (PPI), elucidating PTK2's possible involvement in ferroptosis regulation (Luo et al. 2022).

  To delineate P53 downstream effector genes, we extracted pertinent expression profiles from the GEO repository by employing the search terms Osteoporosis, P53 knockout, and ferroptosis. Through intersection analysis of datasets GSE202265, GSE171572, and GSE204847, we identified 75 common genes. Subsequent analysis using HIplot revealed the most differentially expressed gene, which was selected as our target gene (Dixon et al. 2012).

  FerrDB represents the inaugural globally accessible database designed for the systematic classification of ferroptosis regulators (Chen et al. 2020). This platform enables efficient retrieval of comprehensive ferroptosis-associated gene datasets.


### Cell viability assay
  Cell proliferation was detected by the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Topscience) following the supplier’s instructions. Briefly, 6,000 cells/well were plated in 96-well plates, cultured for 24 h to ensure adhesion, and exposed to experimental compounds. Subsequently, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was introduced into each well, and the plates were incubated (37 °C, 5% CO₂) for 2 h. Optical density at 450 nm was quantified using a BioTek Epoch microplate spectrophotometer.


### Collection of human femoral tissues
  Femoral tissues were collected from female patients aged > 50 years undergoing hip arthroplasty. All subjects furnished written consent after being fully informed, and their demographic as well as clinical information was meticulously documented. Exclusion criteria included: (a) non-osteoporotic bone metabolic disorders; (b) recent (≤ 12 months) use of bone-modulating agents (e.g., bisphosphonates, SERMs, or calcitonin); and (c) history of tobacco/alcohol use. Inclusion criteria: (a) Osteoporosis is definitively diagnosed when femoral neck bone mineral density (BMD), measured by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), meets WHO diagnostic criteria with a T-score ≤ −2.5 standard deviations. (b) The samples should contain complete cortical and trabecular bone structures and be of sufficient size for experimental analysis; (c) no recent fracture was happened (within 6 months). Tissues were stratified by BMD for RNA comprehensive analysis to investigate molecular pathways in osteoporosis pathogenesis.


### RNA purification and real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)
  Total RNA extraction from BMSCs, murine bone tissue, and clinical samples was conducted using TransZol Up reagent (TransGen Biotech, China), following the manufacturer's instructions. cDNA synthesis was achieved through reverse transcription with EasyScript® One-Step gDNA Removal and cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (TransGen Biotech). Quantitative PCR analysis was performed using TransStart® Green qPCR SuperMix (TransGen Biotech) on a Thermo Fisher Scientific platform (Singapore).


### Cell transfection
  To achieve ectopic gene expression, the open reading frames of PTK2, TP53, and MOF were subcloned into the pcDNA3.1(+) mammalian expression plasmid. Plasmid transfection was performed with GP-transfect-Mate transfection reagent (Shanghai Genepharma) following the manufacturer's protocol. Following a 72-h incubation period to allow adequate protein expression, subsequent analyses were performed.

  To knockdown P53 and FN1, unmineralized BMSCs were transfected with gene-specific siRNAs using GP-transfect-Mate reagent. Three independent siRNA oligonucleotides per target gene, synthesized by Tsingke Biotech Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), were employed. Transfected BMSCs were harvested 48 h post-transfection for further analysis.


### Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining and activity assay
  Following a 14-day period of osteogenic induction, the BMSCs were disrupted via sonication. Subsequently, the ALP activity was measured using a commercial assay kit according to the manufacturer's protocol. Cellular ALP was further assessed by histochemical staining and subsequent microscopic examination.


### Alizarin red S (ARS) staining and quantification
  Osteogenic mineralization was evaluated following 14 days of induction using ARS staining. Subsequently, the stained cells were lysed with 5% cetylpyridinium chloride solution (Solarbio, Cat. No. C9890, China), and the absorbance at 562 nm was measured with a microplate reader to quantify the mineralization.


### Transmission electron microscope (TEM)
  The mitochondrial morphology was examined utilizing transmission electron microscopy (Hitachi H-7650 model). Briefly, BMSCs were fixed overnight at 4 °C with 2.5% electron microscopy-grade glutaraldehyde, followed by standard dehydration, embedding, thin-sectioning, and staining procedures.


### Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨ) and mtROS determination
  Cells were stained with JC-1 kit (Beyotime, China) to measure mitochondrial membrane potential. To assess mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) levels, cells were incubated with MitoSOX Red (1 μl/ml; Beyotime, China) for 30 min at 37 °C, followed by observation under a fluorescence microscope (Caikon, Shanghai, China).


### Detection of intracellular ROS
  The intracellular ROS content was quantified employing a commercially available detection kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Following a 24-h incubation with graded concentrations of the experimental compounds in 12-well culture plates, cellular ROS production was evaluated using fluorescence microscopic analysis (Caikon Instruments, Shanghai).


### Mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) openings
  The mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) opening status of BMSCs across experimental groups was assessed employing a commercial fluorescent detection kit (Abbkine Scientific Co., Ltd, China) following the standardized protocol. Briefly, PBS-washed cells were incubated with calcein AM staining solution (37 °C, 30 min, dark). After replacing with 10% FBS-supplemented medium, the samples underwent further incubation under identical conditions. Nuclear counterstaining was performed using Hoechst (15 min), followed by PBS washes prior to fluorescence microscopic observation (Caikon, Shanghai, China).


### Detection of GSH, MDA and Fe content
  Commercial assay kits were employed to measure glutathione (GSH) (Nanjing Jiancheng Biotechnology Co., China), malondialdehyde (MDA) (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Co., Ltd., China), and iron levels (Fe colorimetric detection kit, Elabscience, China), following the manufacturers'protocols. Briefly, cells were plated in 12-well plates, treated with various compounds, and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, samples were processed by adding the respective reagents as specified in each kit’s instructions before analysis.


### Luciferase reporter assay
  We generated FN1-wt-luc and FN1-2000 bp-Luc reporter constructs in the pGL3-Basic vector. The BMSCs were transfected with these constructs or empty vector in 24-well plates using PGP-Transfection-Mate. Luciferase activity was assessed 24 h post-transfection, followed by treatment and luminescence measurement after 48 h using TransGen Biotech's dual luciferase kit according to the manufacturer's protocol.


### Molecular docking and virtual screening
  Molecular docking between PTK2 (PDB: 4NY0) and MOF (PDB: 7CMR) was performed using the HDock web server (Jiang et al. 2015a) Following 100 independent simulation runs, the generated protein complexes were systematically evaluated and prioritized according to their binding affinity. Structural visualization was performed using PyMOL.

  To identify PTK2-activating natural compounds, we performed structure-based virtual screening against the L1400 natural product library (3,060 compounds), using AutoDock Vina (Li et al. 2009). Potential binding sites on PTK2 (PDB: 4NY0) were initially predicted using ProteinPlus. Molecular docking results were subsequently analyzed and visualized in PyMOL.


### Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)
  The supernatant fractions were incubated with either DMSO or Vaccarin at 37 °C for 120 min. Before electrophoretic separation, aliquots were subjected to thermal stress (0–60 °C, 5 min) followed by prompt centrifugation at 4 °C (10,000 × g, 10 min). Protein extracts were then immunoblotted for analysis.


### Animal experiments

#### Establishment of the osteoporosis model in the mice
  We employed forty 8-week-old female C57BL/6 J mice (Dalian Medical University Laboratory Animal Center origin) in this ethically approved study. The experimental protocol received institutional approval (Approval No. AEE21084) from Dalian Medical University's Animal Ethics Committee, adhering strictly to NIH guidelines for laboratory animal care. Animals were randomly assigned to four treatment cohorts (n = 10/group) employing a blocked randomization protocol: (I) SHAM group, (II) OVX + ADV-NC group, (III) OVX + ADV-PTK2 group, and (IV) OVX + Vaccarin group. Animals were maintained under pathogen-free conditions with standardized illumination (12 h light/dark) and thermoregulation at 23 ± 2 °C. Female mice were housed under standard conditions with free access to purified water and regular chow. After sodium pentobarbital-induced anesthesia, the animals were randomly allocated to receive either bilateral OVX or sham surgery. Postoperative animals were housed under standard conditions, with the experimental cohort (Group IV) initiating daily intraperitoneal administration of Vaccarin (5 mg/kg body weight) commencing 7 days after surgical intervention. At the 4-week postoperative time point, adenovirus was administered via tail vein injection in II/III groups. After completing a 9-week controlled dietary regimen, subjects were euthanized humanely via sodium pentobarbital overdose, followed by femoral tissue collection for further analysis.


### Establishment of the calvarial defects
  Female C57BL/6 J mice (8 weeks old) were administered anesthesia to achieve surgical immobilization and alleviate procedural distress. Following standard procedures, the cranial fur was removed, and a 15-mm sagittal incision was made along the skull midline. Bilateral 2-mm craniotomies were then performed using an electric drill, symmetrically positioned relative to the midline. The prepared cells were mixed with basal-membrane-matrix (phenol red). (I) The left side was filled with blank basal-membrane-matrix (phenol red), and the right side was filled with a basal-membrane-matrix (phenol red) and osteoblast mixture. (II) The left side was filled with a basal-membrane-matrix (phenol red) and osteoblast mixture, and the right side was filled with vaccarin-treated osteoblasts and basal-membrane-matrix (phenol red) mixture. (III) The left side was filled with a mixture of basal-membrane-matrix (phenol red) and ADV-NC transfected cells, and the right side was filled with a mixture of ADV-PTK2 transfected cells. The wound was sutured, and the skull was harvested 4 weeks later for subsequent experiments.


### Micro-CT and analysis
  The left femurs were taken from each of the three mice for micro-CT analysis. After anesthesia with 1% pentobarbital, the femurs of the mice were removed and fixed with paraformaldehyde. The femur was imaged by a Venus Micro CT (China). After image reconstruction, a 1 mm section was taken 1 mm below the growth plate for analysis. Data acquisition and parameter quantification were performed utilizing the Cruiser analytical platform. The assessed morphometric indices included trabecular separation (Tb.Sp, mean value in mm), connectivity density (Conn.D, mm⁻3), bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular number (Tb.N, mm⁻1), and three-dimensional trabecular network integrity (trabecular connectivity).


### The contents of Ca, ALP, ACP, GSH and MDA in serum were detected
  Calcium (Ca) levels were determined using microplate method (No: C004-2–1) following standard protocols.

  Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activities were measured using colorimetric assays with specific substrates (p-nitrophenyl phosphate for ALP (No.: A059-1–1)) and quantified at 520 nm. Acid phosphatase (ACP) activities were measured using colorimetric assays with specific substrates (acid phosphatase substrate for ACP (No.: A058-1–1)) and quantified at 530 nm.

  Glutathione (GSH) and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels were assessed using commercially available kits (Manufacturer) following the prescribed protocols. For GSH quantification (Catalog No.: A003-4–1), an enzymatic recycling assay utilizing 5, 5'-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid (DTNB) as chromogen was performed with absorbance read at 405 nm. Concurrently, MDA levels (Catalog No.: A006-2–1) were evaluated through thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) methodology with spectrophotometric detection at 532 nm.


### Histological and immunofluorescence staining
  Femorods were cross-linked in 4% paraformaldehyde (48 h, RT), PBS-washed (3 × 10 min), and ethanol-dehydrated before methyl methacrylate embedding. Tissue sections were subsequently stained using hematoxylin/eosin (H&E) and Masson trichrome protocols.

  For immunofluorescence staining, bone sections were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 (5 min) prior to blocking with 1% BSA (60 min, RT). Subsequently, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting transferrin receptor (TFR, 1:500) and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4, 1:500). After extensive washing with TBST (3 × 10 min), species-matched secondary antibodies were applied (60 min, RT), followed by nuclear counterstaining with DAPI (10 min).


### Immunoblotting and immunoprecipitation analysis
  Protein expression levels were determined through immunoblotting analysis following standardized experimental procedures (Nagasaka et al. 2022). Co-immunoprecipitation assays were performed using cellular extracts prepared with RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai), followed by pre-clearing with 10 µL protein A-agarose beads. Following centrifugation at 12,000 × g, the resultant supernatant was immunoreacted with 1 μg of primary antibody at 4 °C for 2 h, then mixed with 20 μl protein A-agarose suspension and maintained at 4 °C for 12 h. The immunocaptured protein complexes were subsequently subjected to immunoblotting analysis with corresponding antibodies. Antigen–antibody interactions were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence kit (Advansta, Beijing, China), with signal acquisition performed on a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Tanon, China). Densitometric quantification of protein bands was carried out using ImageJ analysis software (Version 1.8.0).


### Statistical analysis
  Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Results are expressed as mean ± SD (n = 3–6 independent replicates). Between-group comparisons were assessed by Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA, as specified in respective figure legends, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.


## Results

### PTK2 expression is reduced in OVX-induced mice and is negatively associated with the P53
  To uncover the molecular mechanisms underlying OP, we conducted RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and performed KEGG/GO pathway enrichment analyses on femoral bone samples from SHAM and ovariectomized (OVX) mice. The experimental workflow is summarized in Fig. 1a. Our integrative data analysis revealed significant enrichment of dysregulated genes within key metabolic networks (Fig. 1b), particularly those involved in protein–ligand interactions and the regulation of redox homeostasis (Fig. 1c). Given that ferroptosis is a form of programmed cell death driven by iron accumulation and reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation (Luo et al. 2022), we next explored its potential role in OP pathogenesis.Fig. 1PTK2 expression is decreased in OVX mice and osteoporotic patients, and is negatively associated with ferroptosis. (a) Schematic of the sequencing process. (b, c) RNA-seq analysis of differentially expressed genes in OVX murine bone tissues, including KEGG and GO enrichment analyses. (d) Volcano plot displaying distinct gene expression patterns in OVX murine bone tissue. (e) Cytoscape analysis of central targets among the top 100 ferroptosis driver genes were analyzed by Cytoscape. (f) PPI analysis by String. (g) Expression of OCN mRNA in the bone tissue of the clinical samples by qRT-PCR (n = 12). (h) Expression of PTK2 mRNA in the bone tissue of the clinical samples by qRT-PCR (n = 12). (i) Expression of P53 mRNA in osteoporotic tissue samples in the bone tissue of the clinical samples by qRT-PCR (n = 9). (*P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, versus Control group)

  Using the top 20 most significant differential genes identified from our RNA-seq data (Fig. 1d), along with the top 100 ferroptosis driver genes from the FerrDB database, we constructed a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network based on central target genes identified through Cytoscape analysis (Fig. 1e). Network analysis highlighted PTK2—a gene among the top 20 differentially expressed—as closely associated with core ferroptosis driver targets (Fig. 1f, S1a-d).

  RNA-seq-based genetic profiling revealed a substantial decrease in PTK2 expression in the OVX group compared to the SHAM group (Fig. S1e), a result confirmed by qRT-PCR, which further demonstrated reduced PTK2 mRNA levels in OVX bone samples (Fig. S1f). Additionally, PPI scoring indicated that PTK2 exhibited the strongest interaction with P53, a principal ferroptosis driver gene (Fig. S1g). We then quantified mRNA expression levels of osteocalcin (OCN), PTK2, and P53 in clinical bone tissue specimens. As shown in Fig. 1g, OCN mRNA expression was significantly reduced in osteoporotic patients. A similar trend was observed for PTK2 (Fig. 1h), while p53 expression was elevated and inversely correlated with PTK2 levels (Fig. 1i). These findings suggest that reduced PTK2 expression is associated with increased ferroptosis-linked p53 levels and the progression of OP, whereas p53 expression itself is positively correlated with OP development.


### Overexpression of PTK2 promotes osteogenic differentiation by alleviating ferroptosis and maintaining mitochondrial function in the BMSCs
  To investigate the role of PTK2 in OP-related ferroptosis, we treated BMSCs with erastin, a well-established ferroptosis inducer (Dixon et al. 2012). Initially, the cytotoxic effects of erastin on BMSCs were quantitatively evaluated using the CCK-8 assay, which determined the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) of erastin to be 25 μM (Fig. 2a). Based on this result, all subsequent experiments were performed using 25 μM erastin as the treatment concentration. Immunoblotting analysis revealed that erastin treatment significantly decreased both total and phosphorylated PTK2 expression in BMSCs (Fig. 2b, c). Importantly, overexpression of PTK2 effectively counteracted the suppression of BMSC viability induced by erastin (Fig. S1k). To further explore the oxidative stress response associated with ferroptosis, we measured malondialdehyde (MDA) and glutathione (GSH) levels in BMSCs. Erastin administration triggered characteristic oxidative changes, marked by a reduction in GSH synthesis and an increase in MDA levels. However, these effects were substantially mitigated by PTK2 overexpression (Fig. 2i, k). (b), (c) PTK2 overexpression plasmid was transfected into erastin-induced BMSCs. Detection of PTK2 total protein and its phosphorylation level was accomplished via immunoblotting (n = 6). (d) Representative images: mtSOX staining for mitochondrial superoxide, JC-1 staining for membrane potential and mitochondrial permeability transition pore (n = 3; scale bar = 200 μm).   Fluorescence staining with JC-1 and Mitochondrial Permeability Transition Pore Assay, as well as transmission electron microscopy (TEM), demonstrated that erastin-induced ROS accumulation disrupted mitochondrial membrane potential (△Ψm), promoted the opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP), and triggered the release of matrix contents. These changes led to a reduction in mitochondrial volume and membrane shrinkage. In contrast, overexpression of PTK2 ameliorated these ultrastructural mitochondrial abnormalities, reversed mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) production, and restored △Ψm balance (Fig. 2d, e).

  We next assessed the levels of ferroptosis-related biomarkers in erastin-treated BMSCs. The results showed that erastin increased the protein expression of the transferrin receptor (TFR) and intracellular iron content, while decreasing glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) protein expression. PTK2 overexpression effectively reversed these changes (Fig. 2f, h, j). Additionally, erastin treatment led to a significant reduction in the osteogenic capacity and mineralization of BMSCs, both of which were substantially rescued by PTK2 overexpression (Fig. 2g, l, m).

  Collectively, these findings demonstrate that PTK2 overexpression protects BMSCs from ferroptosis, preserves mitochondrial integrity, and enhances osteogenic differentiation.


### PTK2 inhibits ferroptosis by reducing the acetylation and succinylation level of P53 in the BMSCs
  We next explored the mechanism by which PTK2 regulates P53. Our investigations revealed that erastin treatment significantly increased both P53 protein and mRNA expression levels. However, PTK2 overexpression effectively counteracted this upregulation (Fig. 3a–c). These results indicate that PTK2 may promote osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs by suppressing ferroptosis, at least in part through downregulation of P53 expression. Considering the well-established roles of acetylation and succinylation (Liu et al. 2022) in modulating P53 activity during stress responses (Chen et al. 2020) and ferroptosis (Jiang et al. 2015a), we quantitatively measured these post-translational modifications on P53. Immunoprecipitation experiments using pan-acetylated and pan-succinylated antibodies demonstrated that erastin treatment increased both acetylated and succinylated P53 levels. Notably, PTK2 overexpression reduced these modifications (Fig. 3d–g). ### PTK2 inhibits P53 acetylation and succinylation by competitively binding to MOF
  We further investigated the mechanism by which PTK2 inhibits P53 acetylation and succinylation. P53 acetylation is essential for its interaction with response elements (REs) that govern target gene transcription, and MOF is a key acetyltransferase responsible for this modification (Li et al. 2009). Using molecular docking, we identified a direct interaction between PTK2 and MOF (Fig. 4c), which was subsequently confirmed by co-immunoprecipitation assays (Fig. 4b). Immunoblotting analysis showed that erastin treatment increased MOF protein expression in BMSCs, but this effect was reversed by PTK2 overexpression (Fig. 4a). Further immunoprecipitation experiments demonstrated that PTK2 overexpression reduced the interaction between P53 and MOF, thereby suppressing both P53 acetylation and succinylation (Fig. 4d, e). These findings suggest that PTK2 and MOF compete for binding, with PTK2 inhibiting MOF’s catalytic activity.Fig. 4PTK2 inhibits P53 acetylation and succinylation by competitively binding MOF. (a) BMSCs were treated with erastin, followed by transfection with PTK2-overexpressing plasmid; MOF protein levels were analyzed by immunoblotting (n = 6). (b) Endogenous interaction between PTK2 and MOF was confirmed by immunoprecipitation (n = 3). (c) PTK2–MOF binding was further validated via molecular docking analysis. (d, e) BMSCs were co-transfected with pcDNA3.1 vectors expressing P53, MOF, and/or PTK2, and protein interactions were examined by immunoprecipitation followed by immunoblotting (n = 3). (f) Acetylation of P53 at lysine 120 (K120) by MOF was evaluated via immunoprecipitation and immunoblotting (n = 3). **P < 0.01 vs. NC group; #P < 0.05 vs. P53-overexpression group; &P < 0.05 vs. P53 + MOF co-transfection group

  Although MOF has been shown to acetylate P53 at the K120 (Nagasaka et al. 2022) site, its ability to induce succinylation at this residue remains uncertain. To explore the role of MOF in P53 K120 modification, we overexpressed MOF in BMSCs and transfected them with either wild-type P53 (WT) or a K120R mutant (in which lysine at position 120 is replaced by arginine). Our results indicated that MOF overexpression had little effect on P53 acetylation or succinylation at the K120R site (Fig. 4f). Collectively, these results demonstrate that PTK2 competitively binds MOF, thereby inhibiting MOF-mediated acetylation and succinylation of P53 at K120.


### PTK2 inhibits ferroptosis through modulating P53/FN1 axis to promote osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs
  As a central transcriptional regulator, P53 governs a broad array of cellular processes, including cell cycle regulation, senescence, programmed cell death, and iron-dependent cell death (Gnanapradeepan et al. 2022). However, the specific P53 target genes implicated in ferroptosis during OP remain poorly defined. This study further explores the involvement of P53-regulated genes in ferroptosis within the context of OP.

  We analyzed RNA sequencing data from three publicly available datasets: “Exploring the dataset of P53 target genes in ferroptosis” (GSE202265), “P53-related dataset” (GSE171572), and “Osteoporosis-related dataset” (GSE204847). Venn analysis was used to identify overlapping genes among these datasets, resulting in the identification of 75 common genes (|log₂(FC)|> 1, P < 0.05) (Fig. S2b). Volcano plot analysis further visualized these overlapping genes, revealing FN1 as the most significantly differentially expressed gene (Fig. S2d). This finding was further corroborated by differential gene expression ranking (Fig. S2e). FN1 has previously been shown to promote osteogenic differentiation (Yang et al. 2020).

We next investigated whether PTK2 inhibits ferroptosis in erastin-treated BMSCs via the P53/FN1 axis. Dual luciferase reporter assays confirmed that PTK2 overexpression counteracted the suppressive effect of P53 overexpression on FN1 promoter activity in erastin-treated BMSCs (Fig. 5m). Additionally, simultaneous overexpression of MOF and P53 decreased FN1 promoter activity, but this effect was reversed by PTK2 overexpression (Fig. 5n).

  Our findings collectively demonstrate that PTK2 competitively interacts with MOF, thereby attenuating P53-mediated suppression of FN1 expression. In summary, PTK2 confers resistance to ferroptosis and improves compromised osteogenesis by modulating the P53/FN1 axis.


### Screening of natural products targeting PTK2 for OP therapy
  Building on our previous findings, we screened a library of 3,060 natural compounds (Selleck.cn Natural Product Library) to identify potential therapeutic agents for OP that specifically target and activate PTK2. The overall drug screening workflow is illustrated in Fig. S3a. Using binding free energy analysis, we initially selected the top 10 candidate compounds (Fig. S3b, c).

  We then evaluated these compounds for their effects on cellular viability, osteogenic differentiation, and mineralization in BMSCs. CCK-8 assays revealed that Dioscin and Geraniin exhibited significant cytotoxicity in BMSCs (Fig. S3d), and were therefore excluded from further analysis. The remaining eight compounds were then tested for their osteogenic potential.

  ALP and ARS staining indicated that Vaccarin was the most effective among the screened compounds in promoting BMSC differentiation and mineralization, particularly following erastin treatment (Fig. S3e, g, h). To further confirm the interaction between Vaccarin and PTK2, we performed a cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA). The results demonstrated a notable increase in PTK2 thermal stability after Vaccarin treatment compared to the DMSO control group (Fig. S3f).

  In summary, these findings suggest that Vaccarin has strong potential as an agent to promote osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs by activating PTK2.

## Discussion
  Ferroptosis is a recently identified form of programmed cell death, predominantly triggered by the iron-dependent accumulation of ROS (Minami et al. 2023). Accumulating evidence indicates that ferroptosis plays a significant role in the pathogenesis and progression of OP (Zhang et al. 2022). For instance, Balogh and colleagues demonstrated that excessive iron accumulation impairs the osteogenic capacity of mesenchymal stem cells, a process mediated by increased ferritin expression (Balogh, et al. 2026). Similarly, Jaime Tsay et al. reported that iron overload in C57/BL6 mice results in a thinner bone cortex and reduced femoral trabeculae compared to controls (Tsay et al. 2010). Although ferroptosis is emerging as a promising therapeutic target for OP, the exact molecular mechanisms by which it contributes to disease progression remain incompletely understood. In this study, we employed a combination of in vitro experiments and animal models to systematically investigate the role of PTK2 in ferroptosis-associated osteoporotic bone loss. Our findings establish PTK2 as a critical suppressor of ferroptosis, exerting its bone-protective effects by finely regulating the p53/FN1 signaling axis. Furthermore, computational virtual screening identified Vaccarin as a compound with high binding affinity for PTK2. Subsequent experimental validation using both cellular and animal models confirmed that Vaccarin exerts anti-ferroptotic effects and demonstrates significant therapeutic potential for osteoporosis by specifically targeting PTK2.

  PTK2 is a vital cytoplasmic tyrosine kinase that integrates and transduces signals from a variety of bioactive molecules—including growth factors—to regulate multiple signaling pathways. Its critical role in osteogenic differentiation (Feng et al. 2023) is well established, as evidenced by a reduction in osteoblast numbers and decreased bone mineral density observed in PTK2 knockout murine models (Sun, et al. 2016). However, whether PTK2 is involved in ferroptosis-related mechanisms underlying osteoporosis has remained unclear. In this study, we demonstrate that both total and phosphorylated PTK2 protein levels are significantly reduced in OVX mice and in BMSCs treated with the ferroptosis inducer erastin. Importantly, overexpression of PTK2 in these models not only attenuated ferroptotic phenotypes but also promoted bone formation. Skull defect experiments further corroborated the essential role of PTK2 in bone remodeling. Taken together, our findings establish PTK2 as a key suppressor of ferroptosis in bone homeostasis. Upregulation of PTK2 enhances osteogenic differentiation while mitigating osteoporotic progression, underscoring its therapeutic potential in the management of OP.

  The tumor suppressor p53 is a central regulator of genomic integrity, orchestrating cellular responses such as survival, division, and adaptation to stress (Ou et al. 2016). As a transcription factor, p53 modulates critical processes including apoptosis, autophagy, cell cycle progression (Wang et al. 2016), and ferroptosis (Jiang et al. 2015b). Prior research has shown that PTK2 exerts a negative regulatory effect on p53 expression in certain cancer models (Zhou et al. 2019). Our study expands on this observation by demonstrating that PTK2 suppresses p53 expression, inhibits ferroptosis, and enhances osteogenic differentiation and mineralization in BMSCs, ultimately ameliorating OP. Despite these advances, the detailed mechanisms linking the PTK2-p53 axis to ferroptosis in OP remain incompletely understood. In this work, we identified FN1 as a downstream effector of p53 in the context of ferroptosis. FN1 is a versatile glycoprotein that interacts with extracellular matrix components—such as collagen and fibrin—as well as cellular structures (Maurer et al. 2016). Although osteoblast-derived FN1 is known to promote bone formation (Bentmann et al. 2010), its relationship with p53 in ferroptosis-associated OP had not been previously defined. Our findings show that knockdown of p53 increases FN1 expression and confers resistance to ferroptosis in BMSCs. Moreover, PTK2 overexpression counteracts the transcriptional repression of FN1 promoter activity mediated by p53, further elucidating the protective role of the PTK2-p53-FN1 axis in ferroptosis and bone homeostasis.

  PTMs represent a crucial regulatory layer in the functional control of P53. Among these, acetylation and succinylation at K120 (Cao, et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2022) are particularly significant for modulating P53 activity. The DNA-binding domain of P53 contains multiple acetylation sites that influence its transcriptional function (Wang et al. 2016). In mammalian cells, the MYST family acetyltransferase MOF plays a central role in transcriptional activation by acetylating both histone H4 at K16 and P53 at K120 (Li et al. 2009). Our results reveal that PTK2 downregulates MOF expression and enhances FN1 promoter activity in erastin-treated BMSCs. Mechanistically, PTK2 competes with MOF for binding, thereby disrupting the interaction between P53 and MOF and reducing both acetylation and succinylation of P53 at K120. Importantly, these findings indicate that MOF not only acetylates but also succinylates P53 at K120—a novel insight that broadens our understanding of P53 regulation in cellular stress responses.

  Recent research highlights that bioactive compounds derived from a variety of dietary sources and botanical medicines can regulate ferroptosis through multiple pathways. This suggests their potential as therapeutic agents for OP, supporting the development of complementary pharmacotherapies that are safer, more cost-effective, and suitable for long-term use with favorable tolerability profiles (Li et al. 2023). Although several compounds have been investigated for their anti-ferroptotic properties, few have shown both efficacy and specificity in the context of OP. Guided by these molecular insights, we utilized computational drug discovery methods to identify natural compounds that target PTK2 and may have anti-osteoporotic effects. Among the candidates, Vaccarin emerged as the lead compound. Vaccarin is a bioactive flavonoid glycoside characterized by twelve phenolic hydroxyl groups, isolated from the seeds of Vaccaria segetalis (Caryophyllaceae). It is known to offer a range of biological benefits, including protection against endothelial cell damage, promotion of angiogenesis, support for wound healing, and hepatoprotective effects (Li et al. 2022). Our findings indicate that Vaccarin alleviates ferroptosis and helps prevent OP by targeting PTK2 to modulate the P53/FN1 signaling axis. While further preclinical evaluation in large-animal models is necessary to fully characterize the pharmacodynamic and toxicological profiles of Vaccarin, this flavonoid glycoside demonstrates significant promise as a novel ferroptosis inhibitor for OP intervention.

  In summary, our study demonstrates that PTK2 competitively binds with MOF, thereby reducing the acetylation and succinylation of P53. This interaction alleviates the inhibitory effect of P53 on FN1 expression and ultimately suppresses ferroptosis, contributing to the mitigation of OP. We further establish that Vaccarin, by targeting PTK2, effectively inhibits ferroptosis and helps alleviate OP. These findings introduce a novel pharmacological strategy with significant potential for the management of OP.
