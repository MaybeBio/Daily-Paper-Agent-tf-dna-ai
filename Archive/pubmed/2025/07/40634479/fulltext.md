# TRIM29 alleviates intervertebral disc degeneration through the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-10272-y.


## Introduction
  Low back pain (LBP) is a prevalent musculoskeletal disorder affecting both developed and developing nations, contributing to a growing global disease burden1,2. Intervertebral disc degeneration (IDD) is a major underlying cause of LBP3,4 and can lead to various degenerative spinal conditions, including lumbar disc herniation, spinal stenosis, and spondylolisthesis4,5. Early interventions with medications and functional exercises typically focus on alleviating pain and inflammation, prioritizing symptom control over addressing the underlying pathological changes6. As the condition progresses, surgery to remove the protruding nucleus pulposus (NP) is often required, but not all patients achieve favorable outcomes, and some continue to experience pain afterward7,8. Though the exact etiology remains unclear, it is widely accepted that IDD results from the combined influence of genetic and environmental factors9,10. Therefore, investigating the underlying molecular mechanisms of IDD is crucial for developing more effective strategies to prevent and manage its progression.

  IDD is characterized by distinct radiological and pathological features, including disc dehydration, reduced disc height, disc herniation, endplate cartilage calcification, and facet joint degeneration in the spine11,12. Molecularly, IDD involves various alterations, including extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation, inflammation, abnormal fibrotic ECM synthesis, accelerated senescence of nucleus pulposus cells (NPCs), reduced NPCs proliferation, and increased NPCs apoptosis13–15. Recently, advances in single-cell analytical technologies have enabled a more in-depth exploration of the cellular mechanisms involved in IDD. This innovative method overcomes the limitations of conventional sequencing techniques, which typically analyze bulk cell populations, by revealing subtle inter-cellular variations that are often overlooked when examining cells collectively16. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) provides unique opportunities for both basic and clinical research, including the identification of new cell types, exploration of cell heterogeneity, and deep insights into the genetic, transcriptional, and epigenetic landscapes of individual cells17,18.

  Machine learning (ML), an interdisciplinary field combining computer science and statistics, has emerged as a highly promising approach for disease diagnosis and clinical outcome prediction in medicine19,20. This study aimed to identify and characterize NPC subpopulations contributing to IDD progression. ML were applied to selectively discover key genes within the NPC subpopulations. Both clinical specimens and IDD animal models consistently demonstrated that tripartite motif containing 29 (TRIM29) expression levels correlate with disease progression. Therefore, we hypothesized that TRIM29 plays an essential role in the progression of IDD.


## Results

### Proportional shifts of NPCs subpopulations in IDD
  To investigate the changes in cellular composition of IDD NP tissues, we conducted an analysis of the GSE244889 dataset to elucidate their cellular architecture. Demographic and clinical characteristics of the specimens in the GSE244889 dataset are provided in Supplementary Table S1. The samples were divided into mild disc degeneration (MDD,grades I-II) and severe disc degeneration (SDD, grades III–V) IDD groups based on the Pfirrmann grading21. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) analysis revealed seven distinct cell populations: B cells, endothelial cells, macrophages, monocytes, NPCs (ACAN+ and SOX9+), smooth muscle cells, and T cells (Fig. 1a–c). We isolated NPCs and categorized them into subgroups based canonical markers identified in primary research22. These subgroups were classified as follows: fibrotic NPCs (Fibro-NPCs), characterized by ACAN, SOX9, and FBLN1; integral regulatory NPCs (IR-NPCs), marked by ACAN, SOX9, and CH3L2; metabolic NPCs (Met-NPCs), identified by ACAN, SOX9, and DKK1; adhesive NPCs (Adh-NPCs), defined by ACAN, SOX9, and MSMO1; and stress-responsive NPCs (SR-NPCs), distinguished by ACAN, SOX9, and CP (Fig. 1d–e). Different NPC subgroups displayed distinctive marker gene expressions: Fibro-NPCs (FBLN1), IR-NPCs (CH3L2), Met-NPCs (DKK1), Adh-NPCs (MSMO1), and SR-NPCs (CP) (Fig. 1f), with differential expression highlighting their unique functional roles (Fig. 1g).

### Selection of the co-expressed gene modules associated with NPCs using hdWGCNA algorithm
  To elucidate the gene co-expression patterns within different subgroups associated with IDD-associated NPCs, we applied hdWGCNA, an advanced framework for scRNA-seq analysis. This approach enabled the identification of functional gene modules, offering insights into IDD molecular mechanisms. A soft threshold of 9 was utilized to create a scale-free network of NPCs, optimizing connectivity and resulting in the identification of 13 distinct gene modules (Fig. 2a–b). Correlation analysis was performed on the 13 gene modules and their associations with NPC clusters (Fig. 2c–e). The gene expression analysis of various NPC subgroups revealed the average expression values and proportions of genes within different modules (Fig. 2f). It was the purple and yellow modules that showed a significant correlation with the Adh-NPC cluster (Fig. 2f). Consequently, the 486 genes from purple and yellow modules were selected as hub genes for further analysis.

### Functional enrichment analysis of hub genes
  To investigate the functional roles and pathway enrichment of hub genes, we conducted GESA, Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Disease Ontology (DO) enrichment analyses. GSEA analysis demonstrated significant gene set enrichment in Adh-NPCs, emphasizing their crucial role in the progression of IDD (Fig. 3a). GO analysis further refined the enriched biological processes and molecular functions, including protein processing, glycoprotein biosynthesis, and cytoskeleton remodeling, revealing the specific roles of core genes in cellular activities (Fig. 3b). The KEGG analysis indicated that the genes were predominantly enriched in pathways, including the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, Hippo signaling pathway, cellular senescence, and focal adhesion (Fig. 3c). DO enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of multiple skeletal-related disorders within the studied gene set or features (Fig. 3d). In brief, these analyses offer key molecular insights into the functions and signaling pathways of core gene modules in IDD.

### Screening of key genes and comparative analysis of multiple machine learning models
  By applying the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression algorithm, 6 key genes intensely associated with IDD were identified (Fig. 4a). To assess the diagnostic performance of these genes, we incorporated 6 machine learning algorithms, including logistic regression (log reg), iterative dichotomizer 3 (lda), random forest (ranger), support vector machines (SVM), naive Bayes classifier (naïve bayes), and k-nearest neighbors (kknn). Demographic and clinical characteristics of the specimens in the GSE23130 and GSE15227 datasets are provided in Supplementary Table S2. According to Thompson grading23, specimens were categorized into two groups: MDD (grades I–III) and SDD (grades IV-V). Using the training set GSE23130, we conducted 10 repetitions of fivefold cross-validation to evaluate the stability of each model. Most models showed strong stability, with the exception of log reg, which performed poorly. In contrast, the ranger algorithm demonstrated the best performance (Fig. 4b). For more detailed information, please refer to Supplementary Table S1. Internal validation AUCs showed that ranger had the best diagnostic performance (Fig. 4c). As a result, ranger was selected as the final model and tested on the external validation set GSE15227, where it also performed excellently (AUC = 0.889, sensitivity = 1.000, specificity = 0.833) (Fig. 4d).

(b) Stability comparison of 6 machine learning models: logistic regression (log_reg), iterative dichotomizer 3 (lda), random forest (ranger), support vector machines (SVM), naive Bayes (naive_bayes), and k-nearest neighbors (kknn). SVM exhibited the highest mean AUC. (c) Internal validation ROC curves highlight the superior diagnostic performance of the SVM algorithm. (d) External validation ROC curve for the SVM model on dataset GSE15227, with an AUC of 0.972, sensitivity of 0.986, and specificity of 0.909.


### Identification and diagnostic significance of key genes
  To explore the identification and diagnostic value of key genes, the GSE17077, GSE34095, and GSE70362 datasets were integrated. Demographic and clinical characteristics of the specimens in the GSE17077, GSE34095, and GSE70362 datasets are provided in Supplementary Table S4. The samples were divided into MDD (grades I-III) and SDD (grades IV–V) groups based on the Thompson grading23. The mild group included 43 samples (9 from GSE17077, 6 from GSE34095, 28 from GSE70362), while the severe group had 36 samples (10 from GSE17077, 6 from GSE34095, 20 from GSE70362). A total of 79 samples were included in the study. These samples were further analyzed after removing low-quality genes, reducing dimensionality, and addressing batch effects. The normalization results indicated that the datasets exhibited similar expression distributions after normalization (Fig. 5a). The principal component analysis (PCA) plots clearly showed differences in sample distributions before and after correction. The initial PCA plot (Fig. 5b) revealed notable batch effects among samples from GSE17077, GSE34095, and GSE70362. In contrast, the adjusted PCA plot (Fig. 5c) demonstrated much tighter clustering of samples, indicating successful batch effect mitigation. Differential expression analysis revealed that TRIM29 expression was significantly lower in the severe group relative to the mild group (Fig. 5d). No significant differential expression was observed between the two groups for the genes 3’-Phosphoadenosine 5’-phosphosulfate synthase 1 (PAPSS1), 3-Hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR), Reticulon 4 (RTN4), and Latent transforming growth factor beta binding protein 1 (LTBP1). Solute carrier family 38 member 5 (SLC38A5) was not matched in the merged transcriptome matrix data.

### Validation of key genes in clinical samples and IDD animal models
  To further explore the role of TRIM29, which showed the most significant differential expression in IDD, we analyzed its levels in NP tissues from clinical samples and IDD animal models. Patient demographics and clinical parameters corresponding to the surgically obtained human NP specimens are detailed in Supplementary Table S5. The samples were divided into MDD (grades I–II) and SDD (grades III–V) groups based on the Pfirrmann grading21. The mRNA levels of TRIM29 were significantly lower in SDD group compared to MDD group (Fig. 6a). Similarly, western blot analysis revealed significantly reduced TRIM29 protein levels in SDD group (Fig. 6b–c). An injury-induced IDD rat model was established. Gross morphological examination showed significant degenerative changes in IDD tissues compared to healthy tissues (Fig. 6d). Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining highlighted notable cellular structure and morphological damage in IDD tissues (Fig. 6e). Safranin O and Fast Green (S&O) staining indicated a substantial reduction in proteoglycan levels in IDD tissues (Fig. 6f). The Thompson grading scores were significantly higher in the IDD group than in the healthy group, confirming successful establishment of the animal model (Fig. 6g). Immunohistochemistry indicated significantly lower TRIM29 protein expression in rat NP tissues with IDD (Fig. 6h–i). Additionally, Western blot analysis revealed a significant downregulation of TRIM29 protein expression in rat NP tissues with IDD (Fig. 6j–k), consistent with the observed pattern in human NP specimens. These findings suggest that TRIM29 may be a critical factor in the pathogenesis of IDD.

### TRIM29 ameliorates IDD through the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT)/mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway
  To further investigate the impact of TRIM29 on IDD, human NPCs overexpressing TRIM29 and control NPCs were treated with or without Interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) for 48 h. To simulate IDD, NPCs were exposed to IL-1β at a concentration of 10 ng/ml. Western blot analysis confirmed the overexpression of TRIM29 in IL-1β-induced NPCs (Fig. 7a–b). Cell viability, assessed using the Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay, showed a significant decrease in the IL-1β-induced inflammation group. This reduction was notably mitigated by TRIM29 overexpression (Fig. 7c). Consistently, EdU assays indicated that TRIM29 overexpression significantly countered the proliferation inhibition caused by IL-1β in NPCs (Fig. 7d–e). Quantitative analysis of gene expression showed that upregulating TRIM29 led to a notable decrease in Matrix Metalloproteinase 13 (MMP13) and Interleukin-6 (IL-6) levels, coupled with a substantial rise in Type II Collagen (COL2) expression within NPCs exposed to IL-1β (Fig. 7f–h). Follow-up analyses using immunofluorescent labeling offered concordant evidence for these results (Fig. 7i–l). These findings indicate that TRIM29 plays a crucial role in regulating intervertebral disc homeostasis and attenuating inflammatory responses in NPCs, suggesting its potential as a pivotal factor in the pathogenesis of IDD. Additionally, single-cell data was stratified into high and low groups based on mean TRIM29 expression (Supplementary Fig. S1). Differential analysis via Findmarker and subsequent KEGG pathway enrichment revealed significant PI3K/AKT involvement (Supplementary Fig. S2). Moreover, western blot analysis confirmed the involvement of TRIM29 in the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway (Fig. 7m–p). After treating with IL-1β, there was a significant decline in p-PI3K, p-AKT, and p-mTOR levels, which were effectively restored through the overexpression of TRIM29. These findings support the results from the KEGG analysis, demonstrating that TRIM29 alleviates IDD via the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway.

## Discussion
  IDD is a polygenic condition affected by both environmental and genetic influences24. Numerous studies on twins and families prone to early-onset sciatica and lumbar disc herniation have confirmed that genetic factors significantly contribute to IDD9,25. The intervertebral disc comprises a gel-like NP, encased by the annulus fibrosus and endplates5. Degeneration of any intervertebral disc component, particularly NP tissue, leads to IDD26,27. NPCs are the predominant cell type, and their heterogeneity is central to IDD progression22. Accumulated evidence indicates a significant link between the adhesive characteristics of NPCs and both the initiation and progression of IDD28–30. In this study, we categorized NPC populations and identified Adh subpopulation-associated co-expression gene modules in IDD using hdWGCNA. Machine learning pinpointed key gene sets, offering insights into disease prediction and intervention. Experimental validation confirmed a potential IDD biomarker and revealed therapeutic targets. Moreover, it was found that TRIM29 modulates intervertebral disc homeostasis and attenuates inflammatory responses in NPCs via the PI3K/AKT pathway.

  The tripartite motif (TRIM) family of proteins is predominantly recognized as E3 ubiquitin ligases, distinguished by conserved domains, including the RING domain, B box domain, and coiled-coil domain31. TRIM proteins are key regulators in various biological processes, including cell signaling, development, cell death, inflammation, and tumor progression32. TRIM29, an atypical member of the E3 ligase TRIM family, distinguishes itself by possessing dual B-box domains rather than the characteristic RING domain33,34. TRIM29 exhibits context-dependent roles in cancer. Its overexpression correlates with progression and poor outcomes in colorectal, pancreatic, lung, and cervical cancers35–38. Conversely, TRIM29 demonstrates tumor-suppressive effects in breast cancer and hepatocellular carcinoma39,40. The paradoxical pattern of TRIM29 expression highlights that its function may be influenced by the specific cellular or tissue context. In this study, we identified a substantial link between TRIM29 and the pathological advancement of IDD. Further investigations using clinical samples and IDD animal models revealed a substantial downregulation of TRIM29 expression in IDD tissues. Moreover, in an IL-1β-induced IDD model, overexpression of TRIM29 promoted the proliferation of NPCs, reduced MMP13 and IL-6 expression, while increased COL2 expression. Cumulative studies have reported that IL-6 and MMP13 are key in activating matrix-degrading enzymes, fostering IDD. Conversely, COL2 serves as a vital extracellular component, critical for NP regeneration41,42. These findings suggest that TRIM29 may be integral to the regulation of intervertebral disc homeostasis and the attenuation of inflammatory responses in NPCs.

  Previous studies have illustrated that overexpression of TRIM29 promotes cell growth and transformation, acting as a negative regulator of p53 by inhibiting Tip60-mediated acetylation43. Another study indicates that TRIM29 functions as a scaffold protein, recruiting DNA repair proteins to chromatin and enabling efficient activation of the DNA damage response44. Furthermore, it is reported that TRIM29 deficiency promotes neuronal death, whereas its upregulation protects against ischemic injury by facilitating NLRC4 degradation45. In the current study, GO analysis elucidated enriched processes including protein processing, glycoprotein biosynthesis, and cytoskeleton remodeling. KEGG analysis revealed gene enrichment in pathways such as PI3K/AKT signaling, cellular senescence, and focal adhesion. Moreover, functional experiments demonstrated that TRIM29 alleviates IDD through the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. The PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway critically regulates core cellular functions including energy metabolism, proliferation, and protein synthesis46. Growing evidence highlights its essential role in intervertebral intervertebral disc matrix homeostasis, inflammation suppression, and repair47,48. As a pleiotropic regulator, TRIM29 modulation may impact signaling networks beyond the PI3K/AKT pathway, potentially causing off-target effects. Future studies should use scRNA-seq and global transcriptomic profiling to map TRIM29’s regulatory network comprehensively. Additionally, tissue-specific delivery via nucleus pulposus-targeted nanocarriers and epigenetic editing tools could enable spatiotemporal control of TRIM29 expression for more precise modulation.

  This study has certain limitations. First, most included studies employed cross-sectional designs and observational data, thereby limiting direct evaluation of how molecular mechanisms temporally influence IDD. Second, access to completely normal human intervertebral disc specimens remains clinically difficult. Control NP tissue, while carefully selected, may not fully represent healthy molecular signatures. Third, although the needle-puncture IDD rat model is well accepted, the degenerative triggers in rodents may not fully replicate human pathophysiology. Fourth, isolation of pure human nucleus pulposus cell populations remain challenging due to unavoidable contamination from other disc cell types, which may confound experimental results. Fifth, in vivo investigations are needed to further characterize the functional impact of TRIM29 on IDD pathogenesis. Sixth, multicenter randomized controlled trials with larger samples are essential to confirm these results.


## Conclusions
  In summary, our findings suggest that TRIM29 could play a modulatory role in IDD, potentially influencing disease progression through the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. While further validation is needed, these observations may contribute to a deeper understanding of IDD pathogenesis.


## Materials and methods

### Data acquisition
  Using the keywords ‘intervertebral disc degeneration (IDD)’, ‘degenerative disease’, and ‘nucleus pulposus tissue’, a search was conducted on the NCBI Gene database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The scRNA-seq transcriptome dataset (GSE244889) and bulk RNA-seq datasets (GSE23130, GSE15227, GSE17077, GSE34095, and GSE70362) were obtained.


### scRNA-seq analysis and hdWCNA
  Analysis of the scRNA-seq data was executed through the R package ‘Seurat’49, which facilitated the identification of highly variable genes, data normalization, PCA for dimensionality reduction, cell clustering, and the application of UMAP for nonlinear dimensionality reduction. Subsequently, differential genes were identified, and the cells were annotated. To refine the identification of specific NPCs subpopulations, the NPCs underwent re-dimensioning and reclustering. The hdWGCNA package was used for WGCNA analysis of the scRNA-seq data, following the standard hdWGCNA pipeline50.


### Enrichment analysis
  The analysis of the identified hub genes was performed using GSEA software (version 3.0) to elucidate the key signaling pathways associated with their regulation. To conduct the KEGG and GO enrichment analysis, the following methods were employed: The latest annotations for KEGG pathways were obtained from the KEGG rest API51. To retrieve GO annotations for the genes, the R package ‘org.Hs.eg.db’ (version 3.1.0) was utilized52. Based on the obtained hub genes, we used the R package ‘clusterProfiler’ (version 3.14.3) to perform GO and KEGG enrichment analysis53. Furthermore, Hub genes were analyzed for disease associations using DO enrichment analysis. Utilizing the ‘DO.db’ R package, which provides terms from the DO database54, in conjunction with ‘clusterProfiler’, we identified and prioritized significant disease categories.


### Machine learning
  To evaluate the predictive value of IDD-related genes identified by hdWGCNA in IDD progression, transcriptome datasets GSE23130 and GSE15227 were obtained. The acquired datasets were strategically allocated: one was designated as the training cohort for model development, while the other served as the testing cohort for performance evaluation. The identified IDD-related gene signature was employed to construct a prediction model using the training set. To ensure high accuracy and robust performance, we applied six machine learning algorithms using the ‘mlr3’ package. Our analysis incorporated the following machine learning algorithms: log reg, lda, ranger, SVM, naïve bayes, and kknn. In the training cohort, IDD-related prognostic genes were determined through LASSO and univariate cox regression analyses. ROC curves were generated and the predictive performance of each model was evaluated with the ‘timeROC’ R package (version 0.4). The model demonstrating the highest accuracy was adopted as the final predictor and its performance was additionally validated using independent testing datasets.


### Bulk RNA-seq analysis
  To further investigate the identification and diagnostic value of key genes, we integrated the GSE17077, GSE34095, and GSE70362 datasets and conducted mRNA differential expression analysis. Data normalization was performed using the normalize.Quantiles function from the ‘preprocessCore’ package in R. Batch effects were removed using the removeBatchEffect function provided by the ‘limma’ package in R. The effectiveness of the data preprocessing was assessed through boxplots, while the presence of batch effects was evaluated by comparing PCA plots before and after batch effect removal.


### Human NP samples
  Approval for this study was granted by the Research Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University (2025-E0012). All procedures followed the relevant guidelines and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants were recruited from the Spine Surgery Department of The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University. Informed written consent was obtained from all adult participants. NP tissues from 30 patients with IDD (mean age 41.6 ± 4.8 years), diagnosed with degenerative spinal conditions such as disc herniation, spinal canal stenosis, and degenerative scoliosis, were collected. Normal NP tissues were obtained from 30 patients (mean age 42.8 ± 4.6 years) who had experienced trauma. MRI scans and physical assessments confirmed the diagnoses. MDD group exhibited severe intervertebral disc degeneration (Pfirrmann grade IV–V), while those in MDD group showed MRI results consistent with Pfirrmann grade I-II. Detailed donor information is provided in Supplementary Table S5.


### RNA Extraction and qRT-PCR
  RNA extraction from the NP tissue and cells was accomplished using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA). Quantification of the isolated RNA was subsequently performed utilizing a Nanodrop 2000. Using random primers and the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (TaKaRa, Dalian, China), RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA. qRT-PCR was subsequently conducted with the SYBR Premix Ex Taq Kit (TaKaRa, Dalian, China) on the LightCycler®96 system (LIGHTCYCLER, Mannheim, Germany). β-Actin served as the reference control. The sequences of primers for TRIM29, COL2, MMP13, IL-6 and β-actin are indicated in Supplementary Table S6.


### Western blot analysis
  Total protein extraction from tissues and cells was performed with RIPA buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), and their concentrations were measured employing a BCA assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Separated via 10% SDS-PAGE, the total protein was subsequently transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Sigma, Darmstadt, Germany). The membranes were incubated with Protein Free Rapid Blocking Buffer (Epizyme, Shanghai, China) for 10 min to block nonspecific binding, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-TRIM29 (17542-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), anti-TRIM29 (DF8050, Affinity, Taizhou, China), anti-AKT (10176-2-AP, proteintech, Wuhan, China), anti-p-AKT (80455-1-RR, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), anti-PI3K (67071-1-Ig, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), anti- p-PI3K (AF7421, Affinity, Taizhou, China), anti-mTOR (1G11A3, proteintech, Wuhan, China), anti-p-mTOR (2A12G3, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), and anti-β-Actin (20536-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China). Following washing, the membranes were incubated with a secondary HRP-conjugated antibody (SA00001-2, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) and scanned using an Odyssey Infrared Imaging System (LI-COR Biosciences, NE, USA).


### Animal experiments
  All experimental procedures and protocols were reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University (Approval ID: 2025-E0012). The research was conducted and reported in accordance with ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines. SD rats (SPF, 12 weeks old) from the Guangxi Medical University Animal Center were randomly assigned to two groups: healthy (n = 10) and IDD (n = 10). As previously described, the IDD rat model was established (details in the Supplemental file) through the needle puncture method to induce injury55. Rats were euthanized at 8 weeks post-surgery via CO₂ inhalation (30–50%) followed by cervical dislocation, with disc samples collected for histological and molecular analysis. The samples were initially fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, decalcified, and photographed for macroscopic examination. Following these steps, dehydration, paraffin embedding, and sectioning were carried out. The obtained sections were stained with H&E and S&O dyes and subsequently analyzed by Immunohistochemistry.


### Immunohistochemistry
  The sections were first deparaffinized and rehydrated, then subjected to antigen retrieval using Citrate-EDTA buffer (pH 6.2) through autoclaving. Endogenous peroxidase activity was inhibited using 3% H2O2 for 10 min, and non-specific binding was blocked by incubating the sections with 5% BSA for 10 min at room temperature. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-TRIM29 (KHC2446, Proteintech, Wuhan, China). Next, a secondary antibody (ab6721, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) was applied for 30 min at 37 °C. The sections were then developed with DAB reagent for 5 min at room temperature, followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin for 1 min. After dehydration, the sections were examined using a Visitron Systems microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).


### Cell culture and transfection
  Primary NPCs were isolated and characterized (details in the Supplemental file) according to previously described methods56–58. The NPCs were then cultured in complete medium (Gibco, NY, USA) containing 10% FBS (Invitrogen, CA, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Biosharp, Hefei, China) at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere. After 48 h, the medium was replaced with fresh medium. pcDNA3.1-TRIM29 and the empty pcDNA3.1 vector were purchased from GENERAL BIOL (Chuzhou, China). NPCs were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, CA, USA) for 48 h, and transfection efficiency was confirmed by western blot analysis. After transfection, cells were treated with 10 ng/ml IL-1β to induce IDD59. Cultures were maintained for an additional 48 h before further analysis.


### CCK-8 and EdU assay
  Cell proliferation was measured using the CCK-8 assay (Biosharp, Hefei, China). Cells were cultured for 24, 48, and 72 h post-transfection, then incubated with CCK-8 reagent for 3 h. Absorbance was recorded at 450 nm. Proliferation was further assessed by the EdU assay. Transfected NPCs were incubated with EdU solution (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), collected, and stained with Hoechst 33,258.


### Immunofluorescence staining
  Immunofluorescence staining was performed to assess the expression of COL2 and MMP13 in NPCs. After transfection, cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. Permeabilization was carried out for 10 min using Triton® X-100 (Solarbio, Beijing, China). To block non-specific binding, cells were incubated with goat serum (Boster, Wuhan, China) at 37 °C for 1 h. Following this, the cells were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by 45 min of incubation with the respective secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 or Alexa Fluor 594. The primary antibodies used included anti-COL2 (ab307675, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-MMP13 (ab39012, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), and anti-IL-6 (ab214429, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). Nuclear visualization was achieved through the application of DAPI fluorescent stain. An Olympus confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) was utilized for image capture and analysis.


### Statistical analysis
  The Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests were employed to assess the normality of the data. Results are expressed as means ± SD or as medians (with interquartile ranges from the 25th to 75th percentiles), as specified in the figure legends. R or Prism 8.0 was used for data analysis and visualization. To evaluate statistical differences between two independent groups with parametric data, a student’s t-test was performed. For nonparametric data, the Mann–Whitney U test was employed to compare the two independent samples.
