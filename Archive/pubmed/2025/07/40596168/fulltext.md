# Integrating multi-omic data to identify key genes and pathways involved in rice bacterial leaf streak disease


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-07334-6.


## Introduction
  Rice bacterial leaf streak (BLS), which is caused by the Gram-negative bacterium Xanthomonas oryzae pv. Oryzicola (Xoc), represents a significant disease of rice, resulting in substantial yield reductions. Indeed, over the past decade, the average loss has been 30%. This disease represents a significant threat to rice cultivation, as it has the potential to negatively impact both the quality and quantity of harvested crops. The pathogen gains entry to plant tissues by invading stomata or wounds and subsequently colonizes rice cells. The pathogen resides in the intercellular spaces of the mesophyll, which results in the formation of water-soaked lesions at the cellular level. These lesions evolve into clear streaks, which represent the later stage of the infection and are referred to as BLS. In contrast to other bacterial pathogens that infect rice, Xoc does not invade xylem tissue. This bacterium focuses on intercellular spaces. This strategy distinguishes Xoc from other bacteria1. The infection process gives rise to a complex cascade of molecular events that ultimately result in regulatory changes in gene expression in both the bacterial and plant cell compartments. This interaction is a coevolutionary process, whereby changes in the genetic profiles of both the pathogen and host are observed in response to each other. Despite the existence of numerous models designed to analyze the molecular basis of adaptation in a variety of bacterial plant pathogens and/or their hosts, the precise relationship between Xoc and rice (the host) during the pathogenic process remains poorly understood. This lack of knowledge highlights the necessity for further research in this area. Several functional genomics studies have demonstrated that the Type Three Secretion System (T3SS) plays a crucial role in the infection of rice by Xoc2. The T3SS can be described as an intricate transmembrane system for the secretion of T3Es (T3SS effectors), which can alter the physiology of host cells3.

  The analysis of genes that are up-regulated in rice plants during a BLS infection reveals the involvement of some biological pathways that are responsive to biotic stress4. These pathways encompass hormonal signaling, cell wall modifications, proteolysis, and secondary metabolite production. Genes associated with cell wall synthesis and phytohormones, including salicylic acid, jasmonic acid, and ethylene, are integral to the plant’s defense against pathogens such as Xoc. RNA-Seq technology provides a comprehensive means of profiling gene expression in both the host and the pathogen, thereby offering valuable insights into the mechanisms of defense2. However, challenges remain due to the lack of optimal sampling models in plant disease studies5. Improved sampling techniques could facilitate a more profound comprehension of plant-pathogen interactions. In conclusion, while current research elucidates the sophisticated responses of rice plants to biotic stress, further investigation into practical applications and improved methodologies is essential for advancing agricultural practices and enhancing crop resilience against pathogens.

  The present research project is designed to identify target genes in rice through the use of RNA-Seq data2, to study the expression and interactions of genes involved in the pathogenesis of BLS infection. The primary objective is to evaluate changes in gene expression within a systems biology framework. This will elucidate the impact of infection on gene expression, uncover bacterial-host interactions, and identify pivotal genes that regulate microRNAs essential for resistance regulation. The study will further examine the functions of hub genes involved in rice BLS infection through analyses of the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks. These approaches are designed to elucidate the interaction networks among genes and identify potential functional modules involved in the disease process. In conclusion, the findings of this research may provide genetic or chemical targets to enhance rice resistance against BLS, which could contribute to the development of improved agricultural practices and greater crop resilience.


## Material and methods

### Selection of genes related to Xoc infection
  In the present study, transcriptomic data from rice infected by Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzicola (Xoc) were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under the accession number GSE129716, which was originally published by2, This dataset includes dual RNA-seq data from both wild-type and T3SS mutant strains of Xoc infecting rice leaves, providing a comprehensive view of the host–pathogen interaction at the transcriptome level.


### PPI networks and hub analysis
  To evaluate the interrelationships among the current set of genes, an analysis was conducted using the STRING tool version 12 (http://string-db.org) with a minimum confidence score of 0.150 (considered low confidence), and then a PPI list was prepared6. The resulting PPI list was then imported into Cytoscape (version 3.10.1), an open-source platform for visualizing and analyzing complex molecular interaction networks and data integration7. The CytoHubba software (version 0.1), an integrated tool within Cytoscape, was utilized to identify hub genes from the Cytoscape network and to pinpoint hub proteins among all nodes. Four computational algorithms from CytoHubba, namely MNC (Maximum Neighborhood Component), DMNC (Density of Maximum Neighborhood Component), MCC (Maximal Clique Centrality), and Degree, were employed to identify and rank hub genes, with four proteins selected as hub proteins for each algorithm8. These hub genes are hypothesized to play critical roles in the rice defense response to Xoc infection. The interactions among the hub genes were then represented in a subnetwork9.


### Gene ontology and pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes
  In this study, the STRING web-based tool was utilized to conduct a gene ontology analysis, as described by1, which encompassed Biological Processes (BP), Cellular Components10, and Molecular Functions11. These three approaches are complementary and comprehensively examine the biological function, cellular site of activity, and molecular role of genes, which allows for a more accurate and systematic assessment of gene function and their relationship to biological pathways12. Moreover, the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway was employed to enhance the hub genes derived from the subnetwork.


### Cluster analysis of the network
  The Cytocluster (version 2.1.0) was utilized to cluster the network nodes. Moreover, the Protein Complex Identification Algorithm (IPCA) was utilized for the analysis of the clusters within the subnetwork13. The IPCA calculates the weight of an edge by determining the common neighbors of the connected nodes and calculates the weight of each node with a threshold set at 10. The threshold of 10 is a tuning parameter that filters nodes based on their connectivity strength, chosen to optimize the biological relevance and accuracy of protein complex detection by IPCA14. Subsequently, the genes within each cluster were analyzed using STRING to identify the KEGG pathways associated with these genes8.


### Promoter motif analysis of hub genes
  The Upstream Flanking Regions (UFRs) located 1 kbp upstream of the gene hub were extracted from the Ensemble Plants web service (http://plants.ensembl.org). The process is comprised of three discrete steps. The MEME suite (version 5.5.5) was employed to identify conserved motifs within the sequences, utilizing default parameters except for the threshold P and E values, which were set at < 0.01 (meme.nbcr.net/meme/intro.html)15,16. The elimination of redundant motifs and identification of known motifs based on the JASPAR CORE PLANT 2023 database was conducted using the Tomtom tool with the threshold P and E values of < 0.01 and < 0.0001, respectively (version 5.5.5)(http://meme-suite.org/tools/tomtom). The GoMo tool (http://meme-suite.org/tools/gomo) was employed to identify potential motif-related pathways17.


### Identification of potential miRNAs for hub genes
  To detect miRNAs targeting hub genes, their sequences were entered into the psRNA Target server (http://plantgrn.noble.org/psRNATarget) with default settings, except for setting the maximum expectation to 418. These sequences were then compared with a set of 150 previously published miRNAs from Oryza sativa.


### Codon usage analysis of hub genes
  In this research, to investigate the codon preference for these genes, the ORF regions for each gene were determined using the Ensemble Plant online tool. In this way, ORF regions with a length of less than 100bp and regions with a termination codon were removed. The removal of these regions is done to increase the accuracy, validity, and precision of codon preference analysis. This ensures that only the true, complete protein-coding sequences are used to calculate codon preference indices and their correlation with gene expression19. The codon preference pattern was calculated based on indices such as CAI, ENC, GC, and GC3S for expressed genes. The use of three algorithms, CAI, ENC, and GC/GC3S, in the study of codon preference is due to the complementary information that each provides. The combination of these indices allows for the examination of the correlation between codon preference and gene expression, as studies have shown that CAI and ENC have a significant relationship with gene expression, and GC3S shows the role of mutations20. In addition, statistical analysis was performed using R 3.4.2 software (https://www.r-project.org) to assess the relationship and potential correlation between the considered indices and the codon preference pattern and gene expression21.


## Results and discussion

### Infection of rice plants by bacteria
  In the present study, dual RNA sequencing (dual RNA-seq) was used to analyze host–pathogen interactions, with experiments involving both a wild-type Xoc strain and a T3SS mutant strain (T3SD). It was found that the wild-type Xoc strain modulates the rice defense response by downregulating certain pathways, such as those related to phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, but activates the expression of genes responsible for loosening. In contrast, the T3SD mutant showed signs of oxidative stress and energy depletion. These results provide insights into the molecular mechanisms of host–pathogen interactions. Further details are described in a previous study2.


### PPI networks and hub analysis
  In this study, a total of 440 genes were selected for systems biology analysis, encompassing both up-regulated and down-regulated genes in rice plants infected with BLS. The full list of the 440 genes used in the network analysis is provided in Supplementary Table S1. PPI networks were constructed using STRING and Cytoscape tools (Fig. 1). STRING (http://string-db.org) serves as a repository of both predicted and known protein interactions, while Cytoscape is an open-source platform that facilitates the visualization of molecular network interactions22,23. The PPI network showed genes that were up-regulated and down-regulated during BLS infection (Fig. 1). In gene network analysis, lowering the confidence score threshold allows the inclusion of more edges in the network, capturing even those with weaker evidence from the data. This approach can help uncover possible gene interactions that are less studied or involve genes with low expression levels24. In the case of rice, which is known to have fewer gene interactions than other organisms, the inclusion of edges with lower confidence scores facilitates the identification of novel regulatory relationships. These newly identified interactions may merit further investigation to elucidate their biological significance25.   It’s common practice in network analysis to employ multiple algorithms for identifying hub proteins in network analysis to reduce bias and enhance confidence in the findings. Consequently, four algorithms (MCC, MNC, DMNC, and Degree) are utilized within CytoHubba for the identification of hub proteins. The four algorithms we chose have different approaches to identifying hub proteins, increasing the results’ diversity. MNC detects hubs involved in large, connected local subnetworks, helping to identify key regulators within functional defense modules. DMNC finds functionally important hubs embedded in dense subnetworks, even with few direct connections, which is crucial for uncovering genes involved in specialized stress and infection responses. The degree focuses on identifying proteins with the highest number of direct connections, highlighting structurally central hubs critical for maintaining network stability during rice’s response to BLS infection. MCC relies on recognizing hubs participating in maximal fully connected cliques, representing proteins central to multi-protein complexes and key signaling pathways in the rice immune response11. The use of algorithms with distinct methodologies allows for the capture of various aspects of the network, thereby facilitating a more comprehensive comprehension of hub proteins26. To ensure robustness, hub proteins were identified by integrating results from four complementary algorithms within CytoHubba.   Ubiquitination is a post-translational modification that involves the attachment of ubiquitin units to a target protein. This enzymatic process can result in the formation of chains of multiple ubiquitin units on the target protein, which may lead to ribosomal activation or determine the protein’s cellular location, Specifically, ubiquitination can regulate protein function by targeting proteins for degradation, altering their activity, or directing them to specific cellular compartments, thereby influencing processes such as ribosome function or protein trafficking31. The capacity of bacterial proteins to interact with host-specific factors is contingent upon the function of ubiquitin-binding as a molecular switch between disparate functions, molecular switch refers to the ability of ubiquitin-binding to modulate protein activity, switching proteins between different functional states or signaling pathways32. To illustrate, the Xanthomonas campestris effector AvrAC/XopAC-mediated uridylylation inhibits phosphorylation at the same residues, thereby influencing immune responses and reducing defense reactions against X. campestris. Uridylylation, the addition of a uridine nucleotide, competes with phosphorylation by modifying the same amino acid residues, preventing phosphorylation-dependent signaling and thus modulating the plant’s immune response33. Incorporating detailed molecular explanations, such as the interplay between uridylylation and phosphorylation, provides critical insight into the regulatory mechanisms underlying rice’s immune response to BLS infection, thereby strengthening the biological relevance and interpretability of the identified hub genes within the protein interaction network11. This consequently mediates avrAC/XopAC virulence functions. The B9G9A5-ORYSJ gene, classified as a Protein Coding gene, is linked to pathways such as rRNA processing in the nucleus and cytosol, as well as the MAPK-Erk Pathway. The MAPK-Erk pathway is a critical signaling cascade involved in cellular responses to stress and regulation of growth, highlighting the gene’s potential role in stress adaptation34. The gene in question is associated with several different GO annotations, including those relating to snoRNA binding and rRNA primary transcript binding35.

  YAO-ORYSJ, also designated as U3 small nuclear ribonucleoprotein-associated protein-like YAOH, represents a component of a nucleolar small nuclear ribonucleoprotein particle (snoRNP) involved in the processing and modification of pre-ribosomal RNA. It plays an essential role in embryogenesis and is categorized within the WD repeat RRP9 family (511aa), WD repeats are protein structural motifs involved in mediating protein–protein interactions, and ‘511aa’ refers to the length of the protein in amino acids36. Furthermore, Q2QY33-ORYSJ, which has been identified as small nuclear ribonucleoprotein Sm D3, serves as the core component of spliceosomal U1, U2, U4, and U5 small nuclear ribonucleoproteins (snRNPs), which are essential constituents of the spliceosome (134 aa), The spliceosome is a complex responsible for removing introns from pre-mRNA, and snRNPs are critical components of this machinery, ensuring proper RNA splicing36. Both the P0650C03.25 and OsJ-36091 gene identifiers are designated as DEAD-box ATP-dependent RNA helicases, belonging to the DEAD box helicase family, specifically the DDX10/DBP4 subfamily. DEAD-box helicases are enzymes that utilize ATP hydrolysis to unwind RNA structures, playing vital roles in RNA processing and ribosome assembly37. Genes such as P0650C03.25 and OsJ-36091 are indispensable for the regulation of vital cellular processes, including membrane signaling, RNA processing, and ribosome biogenesis, particularly in stress response. These processes are essential for maintaining cellular homeostasis and enabling plants to adapt and respond effectively to environmental stresses, making these genes potential targets for improving plant resilience and growth. The multifaceted roles of these proteins in maintaining cellular equilibrium and responding to environmental stressors highlight their potential as targets for enhancing plant resilience and growth.


### Analysis of GO and pathway enrichment for subnetwork genes in rice infected with BLS
  The genes within the subnetwork that interact with the hub genes play a pivotal role in elucidating the underlying pathways and essential processes within rice cells in response to Xoc infection. This research identified these biological pathways and processes through GO and KEGG pathway analyses. In the preceding study, the expression of rice genes was found to be significantly altered, as evidenced by the dual RNA-seq approach. This was particularly evident in genes associated with defense response pathways, including phenylalanine metabolism and flavonoid biosynthesis, which exhibited suppression. Suppression of these pathways may reflect pathogen-mediated interference with the plant’s defense mechanisms, as phenylalanine metabolism and flavonoid biosynthesis are critical for synthesizing antimicrobial compounds38. A total of 440 differentially expressed genes were identified, including 180 that were up-regulated and 260 that were down-regulated. These genes influence cell wall modification and auxin-induced elongation, thereby promoting infection. Cell wall modification can facilitate pathogen entry, while auxin-induced elongation may alter plant growth patterns to the pathogen’s advantage39. Additionally, alterations were observed in bacterial genes associated with energy utilization, stress response, and nutrient uptake. The present study concentrated on the subnetwork nodes (218 nodes) engaged in systems biology that interact with hub genes within the rice gene network during Xoc infection. These 218 nodes were selected based on their connectivity and functional relevance, allowing focused analysis of key regulatory modules. This approach allows for a more comprehensive analysis, with an emphasis on gene network modeling and pathway analysis utilizing a variety of bioinformatics tools.

  An ontology analysis of the subnetwork genes was conducted using web-based tools, including STRING and KEGG. GO provides a framework for characterizing gene products in organisms, including analyses of BP, CC, and MF. Biological Process (BP) terms describe the biological objectives to which a gene contributes, Cellular Component (CC) terms specify locations within the cell, and Molecular Function terms define biochemical activities of gene products40. In this instance, genes linked to cellular metabolism and cellular protein metabolism, as well as genes associated with macromolecular metabolism, were identified. Such metabolic alterations contribute to the rice’s defense against BLS by enhancing the synthesis of immune molecules and antioxidants. Additionally, nitrogen metabolism and nitrogen hybridization compounds contribute to the plant’s defense mechanisms.   To gain insight into the underlying biological functions, a subnetwork gene enrichment analysis was conducted using the KEGG pathways. In this study, KEGG, a database that assigns specific pathways to groups of differentially expressed genes (DEGs), was utilized. A two-fold change threshold was selected as it is widely accepted in transcriptomic studies to represent biologically meaningful alterations while minimizing the influence of experimental noise or minor fluctuations27. The results of our study indicate that the processing of secondary metabolites represents a significant pathway during the infection process. Secondary metabolites, including phenolics, carotenoids, terpenes, terpenoids, and alkaloids (e.g., nicotine, morphine, caffeine, atropine, and quinine), serve a crucial role in providing plants with a sophisticated defense system against various invading pathogens. This contributes to enhanced plant survival and establishes a metabolic foundation for disease resistance42. Within plant systems, flavonoids, phenols, and phenylpropanoids are among the most prevalent secondary metabolites identified. These compounds primarily affect cell signaling pathways or exert effects on pathogen components, including enzyme inhibition, DNA alkylation, and interference with the reproductive system43. The inhibition of ribosome function by drugs occurs either through interference with messenger RNA translation or by preventing the formation of peptide bonds at the peptidyl transferase center44. This highlights the ribosome as a critical target during bacterial infection and explains why genes involved in ribosome biogenesis and function are central in the host–pathogen interaction. This study demonstrates the pivotal role of the ribosome in the bacterial infection cycle, establishing it as a primary target for antibiotics45.


### Cluster analysis of the network
  To comprehend host–pathogen interactions and ascertain pathways that adapt to disparate stresses, it is imperative to elucidate the molecular-level interactions between host cell components and pathogen genes. Understanding these molecular interactions is fundamental in identifying how pathogens manipulate host cellular machinery and how hosts mount defense responses, which is critical for developing effective disease management strategies. A network gene analysis of the interaction between Arabidopsis thaliana and three pathogens, namely Pseudomonas syringae, Hyaloperonospora arabidopsidis, and Golovinomyces orontii, has revealed essential elements of Arabidopsis and plant hubs that are susceptible to pathogen targeting46. This highlights the value of network-based approaches in pinpointing key regulatory hubs that pathogens exploit, providing a framework applicable to other plant-pathogen systems including rice and Xoc. Cluster analysis of biological networks represents a crucial strategy for comprehending the biological structure of networks. This approach facilitates the identification of functional modules, the prediction of protein complexes, and the discovery of network biomarkers. The study employed five clustering algorithms, including the IPCA algorithm, a density-based clustering algorithm that identifies dense subgraphs in protein interaction networks. The use of multiple clustering algorithms, especially density-based methods like IPCA, increases the robustness of cluster identification by capturing different network topologies and interaction patterns, thereby improving confidence in the detected functional modules47. The calculation of edge weights is based on the common neighbors of the two connected nodes, with the total weight of the incoming edges determining the weight of each node. In the initial stage, the seed node is designated as a cluster, and other nodes are incorporated into the cluster based on the priority of their neighbors, considering the probability of node interaction and the shortest path between nodes within the cluster, This method ensures that clusters reflect biologically meaningful groupings by integrating both local connectivity and interaction likelihood, which is essential for accurate modeling of protein interaction networks14.

  In this investigation, the subnetwork cluster analysis yielded 29 clusters with clusters ranked 1 to 5 selected for further investigation (Table 2). Among the identified clusters, those of ribosomes and ribosome biogenesis in eukaryotes were found to be common pathways. The prominence of ribosome-related clusters underscores the central role of protein synthesis and ribosome assembly in the host’s response to Xoc infection, reflecting cellular efforts to regulate translation under stress conditions. However, the endocytosis pathway was identified as a specific pathway within cluster 1. The confirmation of the overlap between the genes in the KEGG pathways and the cluster analysis results highlighted the importance of the bacterial infection pathway as the primary focus. This overlap validates the biological relevance of the clusters and emphasizes bacterial infection pathways as key targets for understanding disease mechanisms and potential intervention points. Network analysis using RNA-Seq and clustering data facilitated the identification of crucial responses to Xoc infection. The results of the cluster analysis corroborated the assertion that five pathways represent the primary focus of our research2. ### Promoter motif analysis of hub genes
  The upstream flanking regions (URFs) encompassing 1 kbp of the hub genes were subjected to an examination to identify conserved motifs and consensus cis-regulatory elements (CREs). Analyzing these 1 kbp upstream regions is critical because they typically contain promoter elements and regulatory sequences that control gene expression, thus providing insights into transcriptional regulation mechanisms of the hub genes48. The aforementioned regions were obtained from the Ensemble Plants database and analyzed using the MEME software. Six significant promoter motifs (21–29 bp) were identified using MEME analysis, as detailed in Table 349. MEME is a widely used tool for motif discovery that identifies statistically significant conserved sequences, which may represent binding sites for transcription factors and other regulatory proteins. The GoMo analysis for transcription factor motifs (TF) revealed a range of biological functions associated with MF, CC, and BP. GoMo analysis links discovered motifs to known transcription factor binding sites and their associated gene ontology terms, thereby connecting sequence motifs to potential biological functions. The full list of identified promoter motifs, along with their sequences and positions relative to the transcription start site (TSS), is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Furthermore, the predominant GO terms for BP, which is located in the membrane, nucleus, and plasma membrane, indicated its involvement in transcriptional regulation, receptor protein signaling pathways between membrane tyrosine kinases, and responses to water deprivation (Table 3). These biological processes are essential for stress responses and signal transduction, highlighting the functional relevance of the identified CREs in mediating the plant’s adaptation to biotic and abiotic stresses.

  Our findings indicate that the primary processes during infection involve transcriptional regulation and the involvement of transcription factors. Transcriptional regulation is a critical control point in gene expression, allowing organisms to rapidly respond to environmental and cellular signals, which is essential for adapting to pathogen attacks. Transcriptional regulation represents a fundamental mechanism inherent to all living organisms. It is orchestrated by transcription factors and collaborating proteins that regulate RNA production through various mechanisms. In bacteria, well-characterized transcriptional factors integrate cellular and environmental signals, thereby controlling cell biology. This integration enables pathogens to modulate virulence gene expression in response to host conditions, optimizing infection efficiency. This is achieved through interactions with RNA polymerase (RNAP) and DNA promoters, which result in the switching on or off gene expression. These factors, frequently designated as "global regulators," possess the capacity to modulate the expression of numerous genes and integrate multiple signals to precisely regulate gene expression, thereby enabling bacteria to adapt and survive in diverse and dynamic environmental conditions. These findings contribute to the elucidation of the role of transcriptional factors in bacteria, illuminate their response to cellular changes, and enhance our comprehension of the mechanisms of pathogenicity in diverse bacterial species50.

  Water is vital for all living organisms and significantly influences plant–microbe interactions, particularly in pathogenesis51. Pathogenic bacteria use specialized virulence proteins to create an aqueous environment in the leaf apoplast under high humidity, facilitating disease progression. Water-soaking spots on leaves often mark early disease stages, highlighting the role of water in microbial success. These water-soaking symptoms indicate the pathogen’s manipulation of host tissue to enhance nutrient availability and colonization efficiency. Alterations in the apoplastic environment further affect plant-pathogen dynamics51. Therefore, studying these interactions provides insight into how environmental factors and pathogen strategies converge to influence disease outcomes, which is directly relevant to understanding and managing BLS in rice.


### Identification of miRNAs that target hub genes in rice infected by BLS
  MicroRNAs are small, highly conserved non-coding RNA molecules that play a pivotal role in regulating gene expression52. miRNAs are typically 20–24 nucleotides in length and are evolutionarily conserved across species, indicating their essential regulatory functions. They regulate gene expression primarily by binding to complementary sequences in the 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR) of target mRNAs, leading to translational repression or mRNA degradation53. In rice, miRNAs have been extensively studied and are known to regulate numerous biological processes, influencing important agronomic traits such as tiller development, flowering time, panicle establishment, grain formation, and yield production. Additionally, they play a role in mediating responses to biotic and abiotic stresses54. miRNAs exert their function post-transcriptionally by binding to the 3′UTR of target mRNAs, thereby repressing protein synthesis. Although prokaryotes lack miRNAs, they possess small regulatory RNA (sRNA), which performs a comparable function. In prokaryotes, sRNAs regulate gene expression by base pairing with target mRNAs, affecting their stability or translation, serving an analogous regulatory role to eukaryotic miRNAs despite mechanistic differences55. The findings underscore the significance of identifying microRNAs that target hub genes. Hub genes are highly connected nodes within gene regulatory networks, often controlling key biological pathways; thus, miRNAs targeting these hubs can have broad regulatory impacts. To identify miRNAs that target the identified hub genes, the web-based psRNATarget program was employed to predict potential miRNAs that may target these hub genes. The psRNATarget uses complementarity scoring and target accessibility models to computationally predict miRNA-mRNA interactions, providing a reliable in silico approach to identify candidate miRNAs18. For example, miR5075 exerted a significant influence on grain strength, as demonstrated by the observation that knockout and overexpression resulted in decreased and increased grain strength, respectively. This functional evidence indicates that miR5075 modulates the expression of genes involved in cell wall biosynthesis or structural integrity of the grain, directly affecting mechanical strength. Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzicola (Xoc) is a bacterial pathogen causing bacterial leaf streak in rice. The differential expression of hub genes upon Xoc infection highlights their involvement in the plant’s immune response and resistance mechanisms, with miRNAs acting as fine-tuners of this regulatory network.


### Codon usage analysis of hub genes targeting rice
  The study used various algorithms to analyze the sequencing data of hub genes extracted from rice plants, focusing on examining the pattern of codon preference within these genes. Codon preference, or codon usage bias, reflects the non-random usage of synonymous codons in coding sequences and is influenced by factors such as gene expression level, GC content, and evolutionary selection. Analyzing codon bias in hub genes is essential because it can affect translation efficiency and accuracy, ultimately impacting protein synthesis and plant phenotype56. To identify the main factors influencing codon formation, the correlation between key indicators of codon preference was calculated (Table 4). These indicators typically include metrics such as the Codon Adaptation Index (CAI), Effective Number of Codons (ENC), and GC content, which together provide insights into the selective pressures and mutational biases shaping codon usage.   ENC (Effective Number of Codons): This metric indicates the ratio of efficient codons utilized in gene expression. The index ranges from 21 to 61, where a single synonymous codon within a gene corresponds to 21, and all synonymous codons within a gene correspond to 6121. This measure is important because a lower ENC reflects stronger codon bias, often associated with higher gene expression and translational efficiency.

  GC3S (GC content at third codon base): This measure evaluates the total GC content at the third codon position. Since the third codon position is often degenerate, GC3S is a key indicator of mutational pressure and selection affecting codon usage, influencing mRNA stability and translation efficiency. The GC values for the analyzed genes ranged from 0.40 to 0.65, while the GC3S values were calculated to be within the range of 0.30 to 0.96 (Table 4) Examining the correlation between the total GC values and these parameters provides insight into the impact of mutations on codon formation within the genes under investigation57.

  RSCU (Relative Synonymous Codon Usage): This parameter examines the diversity of common codons used across genes. Typically, the observed codon frequency is compared to the expected codon frequency. Identifying preferred codons through RSCU helps reveal translational selection optimizing protein synthesis. The index varies from 0 to 1, with values above 0 indicating a preference or tendency for a particular codon58.

  CAI (Codon Adaptation Index): This index represents the ratio of preferred codons, which are synonymous with high frequency, to all synonymous codons within a gene. It is in ranges from 0 to 1. In this study, CAI values ranged from 0.92 to 0.79 (Table 4)10. High CAI values suggest strong adaptation to the host’s translational machinery, indicating efficient translation and high expression levels of these hub genes, which is critical for their functional roles in rice.

## Conclusion
  The present study identified 16 hub genes that were identified in rice plants during the Xoc infection. Many of these genes are involved in signaling pathways related to ribosomes and ribosome biogenesis. An analysis of functional pathways via gene ontology revealed that metabolic processes exert significant control over diverse functions. The identified genes have the potential to serve as promising diagnostic biomarkers for Xoc infection. Additionally, promoter analysis revealed the existence of distinct metabolic pathways associated with either susceptibility or resistance to Xoc. Moreover, crucial microRNAs targeting these hub genes were identified as playing a role in disease response. In general, the expression levels of the genes under examination were increased, indicating their potential involvement in the response and resistance mechanisms of rice plants against Xoc-induced damage. The findings of this study, when considered alongside those of related research, play an essential role in identifying genes that are implicated in rice plant resistance to diseases caused by Xoc, or in enhancing their expression for optimal transgenic plant development. Furthermore, these genes may serve as diagnostic indicators for Xoc infection.
