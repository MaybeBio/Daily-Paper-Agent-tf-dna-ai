# RNA Binding Mechanism
of the FUS Zinc Finger in Concert
with Its Flanking Intrinsically Disordered Region


## Abstract
  The zinc finger (ZnF) motif is one of the most common nucleic acid binding motifs in proteins and comprises approximately 5% of the human genome. Proteins often carry multiple copies of ZnF motifs, enhancing DNA- and RNA-binding affinity and specificity. Fused in Sarcoma (FUS) is an RNA-binding protein that contains one ZnF motif and one RNA recognition motif. We identified the molecular basis for the strong and specific binding of FUS ZnF to RNA at atomic resolution by performing molecular dynamics and dissociation parallel cascade selection molecular dynamics simulations of a single-stranded RNA complexed with FUS ZnF and the flanking RGG2 domain. The RGG2-ZnF construct was classified into two regions based on atomic fluctuation: an intrinsically disordered region (IDR) consisting mainly of RGG2 and a structured region consisting mainly of the ZnF motif. Our results on intermolecular interactions, dissociation process, binding affinity, and free energy landscape indicate that the structured region specifically recognizes the GGU sequence of RNA, while the IDR interacts nonspecifically with the RNA backbone and distorts it. Although the binding of the structured region alone with the short RNA sequence is relatively weak, the binding of the IDR exhibits a 2-fold lower dissociation constant due to the addition of nonspecific charge interactions with RNA backbone phosphate groups. Further comprehensive analysis of nucleic acid binding motifs in the DisProt database suggests that the stabilization of protein-RNA binding by only one or a few nucleic acid binding motifs with flanking disordered regions, as in the case of FUS, ZnF is a common mechanism for ensuring strong and specific nucleic acid binding in nucleic acid binding proteins, as is the involvement of more nucleic acid binding motifs. Our findings allow us to expand the repertoire of disordered region-assisted nucleic acid binding by ZnF from double-stranded DNAs to single-stranded RNAs.


## Introduction
  The zinc finger (ZnF) motif is the largest class of DNA-binding protein motifs in the human genome and is among the most common nucleic acid binding motif in eukaryotes. −    To date, this motif has been found encoded in 1723 human proteins, accounting for approximately 5% of the human genome. ,−   ZnF-containing proteins participate in diverse biological pathways, including transcription, post-translational modification, DNA damage repair, apoptosis and cell proliferation, and are found in the nucleus, cytoplasm, and cell membrane, interacting with single- and double-stranded DNAs and RNAs, proteins, and lipids. −

  ZnF motifs are classified into 30 types based on their structure and sequence. ,,  Representative examples include the C2H2, RING, PHD, and LIM types. −  ,,  Each type of ZnF binds specific partners. For example, C2H2-type ZnFs bind DNAs, RNAs, proteins, and lipids; RING-type ZnFs bind proteins such as ubiquitin; PHD-type ZnFs bind histones; and LIM-type ZnFs bind proteins. ,,

  The wide range of biological roles of ZnFs arises from their structural stability and flexibility. ZnF is a small motif coordinating one or more zinc ions but lacks a hydrophobic core, which typically contributes to the structural stabilities of globular proteins. Classical ZnFs are stabilized by the coordination of a zinc ion by four residues consisting of cysteine and histidine residues. ,,−   Proteins with ZnF motifs often contain multiple copies of these motifs, enhancing their nucleic acid binding affinity and specificity. ,,,−   Additionally, the flexible linker region between tandem C2H2-type ZnFs further enhances binding affinity with double-stranded DNAs. ,,

  Fused in Sarcoma (FUS) is an RNA-binding protein that contains a single-stranded RNA-binding RanBP2-type ZnF surrounded by a long disordered region. −    FUS is a heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein that regulates transcription, splicing, and miRNA biogenesis by forming complexes with RNA and is involved in DNA damage repair by interacting with DNA. ,−   The FUS-RNA complexes assemble into stress granules comprising translation initiation factors and other RNA-binding proteins in the cytoplasm in response to various cellular stresses such as oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and viral infection. −      FUS-RNA interactions have yet to be characterized in detail because dysregulation of FUS-RNA interactions is linked to neurodegenerative diseases such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and frontotemporal dementia (FTD). ,,,,,

  FUS specifically recognizes target RNAs, allowing FUS to exert precise physiological functions. FUS contains two RNA-binding regions: the RNA recognition motif (RRM) and the ZnF motif. RRM binds stem-loop RNAs with low sequence-motif specificity and might bind single-stranded RNA. ,  The ZnF motif of FUS mainly recognizes single-stranded RNA motifs, GGU, GGUA, and GGUG in the 3′ and 5′ untranslated regions (UTRs) of pre-mRNA. ,−       FUS ZnF is flanked by intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) that fluctuate greatly and do not adopt specific structures. The IDRs are arginine-glycine-glycine (RGG) rich regions, called RGG2 and RGG3 domains. ,,,  Despite possessing only one ZnF motif, FUS exhibits strong and specific binding to single-stranded RNA sequences containing GGU motifs.

  Here, we investigate the mechanisms of binding of FUS ZnF to a single-stranded RNA with the GGU sequence. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of a single-stranded RNA (UGGUG) complexed with the ZnF and the flanking disordered RGG2 domain of FUS, followed by dissociation simulation of the FUS-RNA complex by parallel cascade selection molecular dynamics (PaCS-MD), identified how FUS ZnF binds strongly and specifically to RNA by analyzing intermolecular interactions, the dissociation process, binding affinity, and the free energy landscape. Our results indicate that ZnF specifically recognizes the GGU sequence of RNA, while the RGG2 domain nonspecifically interacts with the RNA backbone and distorts it. Although the binding of ZnF alone with the short RNA sequence is relatively weak, the binding of RGG2 exhibits a 2-fold lower dissociation constant due to the addition of nonspecific charge interactions with RNA backbone phosphate groups. These results, along with additional comprehensive sequence analysis of nucleic acid binding motifs in the database of disordered proteins, allowed us to expand the repertoire of IDR-assisted nucleic acid binding of ZnFs from double-stranded DNA to single-stranded RNA. Furthermore, our findings suggest two possible mechanisms for strong and specific binding between protein and nucleic acid. One mechanism is that proteins encode one or few nucleic acid binding motifs with flanking IDRs that assist nucleic acid binding through nonspecific interactions, as in the case of FUS ZnF proteins, and the second is that proteins encode multiple nucleic acid motifs.


## Materials and Methods

### Modeling of the FUS-RNA
Complex Structure
  Our starting structure for the MD simulation was the model structure of a protein-RNA complex composed of a segment of FUS (residues 414–454) and an RNA (UGGUG), determined by solution NMR (PDB ID: 6G99). To date, this is the only structure reported for the ZnF domain of FUS. The FUS segment contains the C-terminal segment of RGG2 and ZnF. The sequence of residues 414–419 of the PDB structure was restored to the wild-type sequence using PyMOL 3.0.0. Acetyl and N-methyl groups were added to the N- and C-termini of the protein segment, respectively, using PyMOL. The system was solvated in a cubic water box and neutralized with 0.15 M KCl ions. The ff19SB, ZAFF, and DES Amber force fields were used to model the protein, zinc, and RNA atoms, respectively, while the TIP4P-D model was used for water and ion parametrization. The RNA force field of DES Amber combined with TIP4P-D was shown to reproduce experimentally observed structures of various RNA single-stranded tetranucleotides more accurately than OL3, which is the most commonly used RNA force field. ,  DES Amber RNA force field is designed for use with TIP4P-D. We used TIP4P-D, which is designed to be compatible with ff14SB. The combination of ff14SB, an earlier version of ff19SB, and TIP4P-D reproduced the experimental value of the radius of gyration of FUS ZnF bound to RNA. The combination of ff19SB and TIP4P-D was tested on other disordered proteins, and the experimental radius of gyration was reproduced. MD simulation of the FUS RRM and stem-loop RNA complex with the OL3 force field reported progressive distortions of the RNA–protein interface inconsistent with experimental data. DES AMBER was shown to reproduce the NMR structure of single-stranded tetranucleotide RNA. RMSD of the single-stranded RNA complex in this work was in the range of 0.3–0.5 nm, which is significantly less than that in the case of the FUS RRM/RNA complex. All modeling was performed using the tLEaP tool from the AmberTools23 package. Molecular visualization was conducted using UCSF ChimeraX.


### Energy
Minimization, Equilibration, and CMD Simulation
  Energy minimization and conventional molecular dynamics simulation (CMD) for equilibration and production runs were performed using GROMACS 2024.2, employing the conversion of topology and coordinate files from AMBER to the GROMACS format using ParmEd 4.2.2.

  Energy minimization was carried out using the steepest descent algorithm for up to 5000 steps, with positional restraints of 1000 kJ/mol·nm2 applied to all the atoms except those modeled by PyMOL. During equilibration, the system experienced NVT conditions with the Nosé-Hoover thermostat, ,  where it was heated from 100 to 303.15 K over 1 ns, followed by an additional 1 ns NVT simulation with positional restraints. This was followed by NPT equilibration at 1 bar using the Parrinello–Rahman barostat for 1 ns, during which positional restraints (1000 kJ/mol·nm2) were progressively reduced by 100 kJ/mol·nm2 every 1 ns, for a total of 9 ns. The final equilibration step involved an NPT ensemble simulation for 400 ns without positional restraints. Subsequently, a production run was performed for 800 ns.


### PaCS-MD Simulation
  Protein-RNA dissociation simulations were conducted with the parallel cascade selection MD (PaCS-MD) framework using PaCS-Toolkit 1.0.0. PaCS-MD is an enhanced sampling method in which several short MD simulations, called replicas, are performed in parallel and independently. The snapshots of structures from the replicas whose ‘selection feature’ of interest is closest to the target value are selected as the initial structures for the next cycle. The center-of-mass distance of two molecules is, for example, often used to observe ligand dissociation from proteins in dissociation PaCS-MD (dPaCS-MD). The selected snapshot structures are then used as the initial structures for the next short simulations. This cycle of parallel short simulations and selection feature calculations is repeated until the selection feature reaches the target value, as reported in the example of dPaCS-MD, in which ligands, proteins, and DNA dissociate from distinct proteins. −

  The initial structures for dPaCS-MD were selected from snapshots taken every 25 ns during the CMD simulation. Each cycle consisted of 64 replica runs for 100 ps, and 32 independent trials were conducted. The center-of-mass distance between the FUS and RNA was used to rank the snapshots. The hyperparameters, such as the timespan of each replica (=100 ps), the number of replicas (=64), and the number of trials (=32), were determined based on previous studies in which similar values were used and the simulation results were validated. −     We also constructed the FUS model without IDR (residues 423–454) and conducted equivalent dPaCS-MD simulations and MSM analyses.


### Trajectory Analysis, MSM Construction, and Free Energy Calculation
  Trajectory analysis was conducted using MDTraj 1.10.0. Hydrogen bonds were identified based on a distance of <0.3 nm between the hydrogen and acceptor and an angle of >120° between the donor, hydrogen, and acceptor. ,  Electrostatic interactions were determined for positively and negatively charged groups separated by a distance of <0.4 nm. π-Electron interactions were categorized as cofacial, displaced parallel, or T-type. The former two were identified by an interplanar angle of <15° and a distance of <0.45 nm between the geometric centers of the aromatic rings, while T-type interactions required an interplanar angle of between 75° and 105° and a distance of <0.6 nm between the geometric centers of the aromatic rings. ,,  van der Waals interactions were defined as a distance of <0.3 nm between all the protein atoms and RNA heavy atoms.

  Markov state model (MSM) analyses and free energy calculations were performed using Deeptime 0.4.4. dPaCS-MD generates dissociation pathways as a series of multiple MD trajectories that mutually overlap in the conformational space. In the MSM-based free energy analysis, MD simulation trajectories are discretized into microstates by using a clustering method. The free energy of each microstate is calculated from its stationary probability, which can also be converged to the free energy of the microstate. Our MSM analysis was constructed along the dissociation pathway by using the trajectories of each dPaCS-MD trial and the center-of-mass distances of the protein and the RNA as the MSM feature. The center-of-mass distances were discretized into microstates using regular space clustering. The number of clusters (=15) and lag time (=20 ps) were chosen based on the convergence of the implied time scales. The bound state and the unbound state were defined as a center-of-mass distance of <1.5 nm and a center-of-mass distance of >4 nm, based on the potential of mean force (PMF) landscape. The standard binding free energy was calculated via a volume correction (eq S1). ,  The free energy landscape of the bound state was calculated from the dPaCS-MD trajectories that satisfied the center-of-mass distance of ≤1.5 nm. Following principal component analysis of the Cα atoms of FUS and of the C3′ atoms of RNA, the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2, respectively) were used as the MSM features. The contributions of PC1 and PC2 to the total mean-square fluctuation were 53 and 17%, respectively. The MSM was constructed using 40 clusters and a lag time of 30 ps to provide the 2D free energy landscape versus PC1 and PC2.


### Sequence Analysis
  The nucleic acid binding sites on the sequences were annotated in DisProt (the Database of Protein Disorder, 2024 December release), a manually curated database of disordered proteins. HMMER v 3.4 was used for a hmmsearch against the PANTHER database ,  to obtain comprehensive information on gene families, including gene function and class. DNA- and RNA-binding sites were identified based on the gene ontology annotations GO:0003677 (DNA binding) and GO:0003723 (RNA binding), respectively. If hit regions were separated by more than 10 residues in the amino acid sequence, then they were considered distinct binding sites. Annotations with a conditional E-value of less than 1e–5 were considered.


## Results

### Identification
of Disordered and Structured Regions of ZnF of
FUS
  We investigated the interaction between FUS and RNA at atomic-level resolution using MD simulations of ZnF and a part of the RGG2 domain (residues 414–454) bound to a single-stranded RNA (sequence: UGGUG) (Figure  a,b). The dPaCS-MD framework was used to efficiently sample the RNA dissociation dynamics of the complex (Figure  b). The root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) of the protein during RNA dissociation indicated that residues 414–422 are disordered, whereas residues 423–454 are structured (Figure  c). For simplicity, we hereafter refer to residues 414–422 as the IDR and residues 423–454 as the structured region. To examine the consistency between the structures sampled by CMD and the NMR structures, we compared the distances of the atom pairs employed as the NOE distances to the average distances during the CMD simulation (Figure S1). This comparison shows that the average distances are in good agreement with the restraint distances in most cases. However, major inconsistencies were found in the U1–W440 pairs. This may be due to larger fluctuations at the end of the RNA chain or conditional differences between the NMR experiment and the simulation, or it may be caused by an artifact resulting from insufficient sampling and/or force field incompatibility between the DES AMBER RNA and AMBER ff19SB force fields.


### Classification of the Trials into High and Low Free Energy Dissociations
  We investigated the binding affinity of the complex by calculating the standard binding free energy based on the dissociation trajectory of each trial (Figures  , S2, and S3; Tables S1 and S2). The average standard binding free energy was −6.6 ± 1.7 kcal/mol, consistent with the experimental result (−6.3 ± 0.8 kcal/mol) obtained by isothermal calorimetry for the same construct. We classified the dPaCS-MD trials into two groups based on the calculated free energy values: 13 high binding free energy trials (Movie S1) with free energies >−6.0 kcal/mol, and 19 low free energy trials (Movie S2) with free energies <−6.0 kcal/mol. The average binding free energy of the high free energy pathways was −5.0 ± 0.7 kcal/mol, while that of the low free energy trials was −7.6 ± 1.2 kcal/mol. The binding free energy difference between the high and low free energy trials (2.6 kcal/mol) may be due to the difference in the bound state because interactions between two molecules should be negligible in the plateau PMF range, and sufficiently dissociated states are free-energetically equivalent even though they differ in spatial position. In addition, we modeled the FUS segment as comprising only the structured region, without the IDR, and conducted dissociation simulations and subsequent binding free energy analyses (Figures  a,c, S4, and S5, and Table S1). The obtained average binding free energy was −5.2 ± 0.8 kcal/mol, which is close to that of the high free energy trials, suggesting that the IDR plays a small role in binding in the high free energy trials. All but one trial started from the snapshot of 400–800 ns of CMD were low free energy trials, while all but two trials started from the snapshot of 825–1175 ns of CMD were high free energy trials, suggesting that the bound state switched once in 800–825 ns (Table S2).

  We further investigated the free energy landscape of the bound state (up to 1.5 nm in the center-of-mass distance) by MSM analysis (Figures  b and S6). The structures in the bound state were projected onto two principal components: PC1 contributed 53%, and PC2 contributed 17%. Motion along PC1 captures the IDR fluctuation and RNA backbone distortion (Figure S7). We analyzed the correlation between the free energies of the dissociation starting point on the free energy landscape and the binding free energies. The correlation coefficient was 0.63, indicating a positive correlation despite possible errors in both free energy calculations. The variations in the binding free energy of the FUS-RNA complex are primarily determined by the free energy variations in the bound state. We classified the microstates in the bound state into two groups (macro states) by perron-cluster cluster analysis (PCCA+). One microstate that covers the higher free energy region (the right side of Figure S8) contains 12 out of 13 high free energy trials, and another microstate that covers the lower free energy region (the left side of Figure S8) contains 15 out of 19 low free energy trials, indicating that two metastable states correspond to high and low binding states. The mean first-passage time (MFPT) from the low to high free energy macrostates was 28 ns and that from high to low free energy macrostates was 8.6 ns. The MFPTs calculated from the bound state are much shorter than the timespan of conformational changes observed in CMD. This discrepancy may be due in part to the insufficient sampling of the bound states during CMD, since the switching from the high to low free energy bound states was not observed many times with CMD. It may also be partially due to the difficulty of calculating the MFPTs from PaCS-MD trajectories. The probabilities of the high and low free energy macrostates were 0.23 and 0.77, respectively, which are slightly different from the ratio between the high and low free energy trials, 0.41:0.59. Since the standard binding free energy (−6.6 ± 1.7 kcal/mol) might be affected by the selection ratio of the initial structures for the dPaCS-MD trials, we also calculated the standard binding free energy reweighted by the microstate probabilities (eq S2), which was slightly lower (−7.2 ± 1.2 kcal/mol), but these values are within the range of the error (Figure  c). The greater deviation of the binding free energy from the experimental value roughly implies a possible range of errors in the free energy calculation.

  Figure S9 indicates the positions of the three lowest free energy minima in the free energy landscape, M1–M3. Compared to the lowest free energy minimum, M1, the second (M2) and third (M3) lowest free energy minima are higher only by 0.25 and 0.45 kcal/mol, respectively. Considering typical force field error in free energy (∼1 kcal/mol), M1–M3 can be regarded as comparable in free energy. Figure  b also shows the positions of the 20 NMR structures, which are distributed around M2. The result of this comparison indicates that the position of one of the free minima is consistent with those of the NMR structures.


### Interactions between RNA
and IDR Determine Differences in Binding
Affinity
  Since protein-RNA interactions are well characterized by nonbonding interactions (hydrogen bonds, charge interactions, π-electron interactions, and van der Waals interactions), ,  we investigated the RNA recognition mechanism of FUS ZnF and its relationship with binding free energy by analyzing representative nonbonding interactions between FUS and each RNA nucleotide during the dissociation of the complex (Figure  a).

  Hydrogen bonds were primarily formed between the FUS side chains and the G2, G3, and U4 bases, but the main chain carbonyl groups of Q420, M436, and F438 also formed hydrogen bonds with RNA (Figure  b). The specific RNA sequence (GGU) is therefore primarily recognized through these hydrogen bonds. The number of hydrogen bonds between the structured region and RNA in the low free energy trials was similar to those in the high free energy trials, suggesting that RNA binding to the structured region contributes little to the binding free energy difference. The hydrogen bonds between the IDR and RNA in the low free energy trials persisted up to 4 nm in the center-of-mass distance, while almost no hydrogen bonds were observed in the high free energy trials. This difference indicates that the IDR interacts with RNA in the bound state and during dissociation in the low free energy trials, but not in the high free energy trials.

  Oxygen atoms of the RNA backbone phosphate groups provided the negative charges for charge interactions, while the positive charge sources in FUS were the amino groups of two arginine residues (one in the IDR and one in the structured region) and two lysine residues in the structured region. Charge interactions were observed between R422 in the IDR and G2/G3 of the RNA in the low free energy trials up to 3 nm in the center-of-mass distance, while no charge interactions were observed in the high free energy trials. Additionally, there were no charge interactions between the structured region and RNA, further supporting that the IDR interacts with the RNA backbone in the bound state and during dissociation in the low free energy trials but not in the high free energy trials. We also investigated the possible effects on potassium ion binding to the RNA phosphate groups (Figure S10). The radial distribution function (RDF) of potassium ions around the oxygen atoms of the RNA phosphate groups showed a relatively low first peak at around 0.27 nm, and the running coordination number of the ion at the first RDF minimum (0.36 nm) is 0.0095, indicating that on average approximately 0.01 potassium ions are coordinated per oxygen atom of the RNA phosphate. This result shows weak preferential localization of potassium ions near the RNA backbone. The maximum binding duration between potassium ions and phosphate oxygen was only 0.4 ns in the CMD, suggesting that these interactions are transient rather than site-specific. These results are consistent with previous studies on the effects of monovalent cations on the RNA backbone structures. ,

  π-electron interactions were analyzed using the aromatic ring atoms of each RNA base and of one phenylalanine and two tryptophan residues, all in the structured region, and nonring π-electrons from two arginine residues (one in the IDR and one in the structured region), two aspartic acid residues in the structured region, and two glutamic acid residues in the structured region. π-electron interactions were observed with G2/G3 of the RNA up to 3 nm in the center-of-mass distance. R422 in the IDR and D425, F438, W440, and R441 in the structured region participated in π-electron interactions. There were no differences in π-electron interactions in the structured region between the high and low free energy trials, while there were more π-electron interactions in the IDR in the low free energy trials than in the high free energy trials. This result also supports that the IDR interacts with RNA in the bound state and during dissociation in the low free energy trials but not in the high free energy trials.

  van der Waals interactions between the structured region and RNA were observed up to 3 nm in the center-of-mass distance, while those between the IDR and RNA were observed up to 5 nm in the center-of-mass distance. No substantial differences were found in the few van der Waals interactions of the structured region between the high- and low-free-energy trials. On the other hand, up to 10 van der Waals interactions between the IDR and RNA were identified in the low free energy trials. These results also support that the IDR interacts with RNA in the bound state and during dissociation in the low free energy trials but not in the high free energy trials.

  To examine the flexibility of the IDR during dissociation, we calculated the end-to-end distance of the IDR during dissociation (Figure S11). There were considerable cases of both decreased and increased distance as well as cases with less fluctuation. This tendency is common in both low and high free energy trials. These results suggest that the fluctuation of the end-to-end distance primarily originates from the intrinsically disordered nature of the IDR. Additionally, we conclude that the choice of selection has little impact on the conformational change of the IDR.

  In summary, a greater number of hydrogen bonds, charge interactions, π-electron interactions, and van der Waals interactions with the IDR were observed mainly up to a center-of-mass distance of 4 nm and slightly up to 5 nm in the low free energy trials. In contrast, fewer interactions were observed up to 3 nm in the high free energy trials (Figure  a). The difference in the persistence range of these interactions influences the PMF profiles: The PMFs in the low free energy trials converged around 4 nm, while those in the high free energy trials converged around 3 nm (Figure S3). The only exception is the PMF of trial 1, which converged at 5 nm. In this case, the numbers of hydrogen bonds, charge interactions, and van der Waals interactions were particularly greater than those in the other low free energy trials. The low ΔG° of this trial originates from the enthalpic contributions of these interactions. The charge interactions between the RNA backbone and the IDR in the low free energy trials stabilize nonspecific binding to the RNA sequence. In contrast, the reach and the number of each interaction between RNA and the structured region of the FUS segment were similar to those observed in the high free energy trials (Figures  a and S12). Although RNA binding with ZnF alone is relatively weak (submillimolar order in K d), the binding of IDR lowers the K d to the μM order (Figure  c). These results suggest that the interactions of the IDR with RNA strengthen the binding affinity by adding nonspecific interactions. Few interactions between FUS and G5 were observed, supporting the previous observations that the FUS ZnF specifically binds GGU rather than GGUA/G. ,−


### Hydrogen Bonds of the Structured Region Specify the Sequence-Dependent
Binding of RNA
  We identified the dominant hydrogen bonds responsible for ZnF binding to a specific RNA sequence by analyzing the hydrogen bonds between RNA and FUS in the range of <1.5 nm from the center-of-mass (Figures  c and S13). G2 of RNA forms strong hydrogen bonds with the main chain of Q420 and with the side chains of D425 and W440 of the FUS structured region. G3 primarily maintained hydrogen bonds with the main chains of M436 and F438 and the side chain of R441. The dominant hydrogen bonds of U4 were with the side chains of N435 and N445. The FUS segment mainly formed hydrogen bonds only with the structured region, corresponding to the structured region of the high free energy trials (Figure S13). These results also indicate that the amino acid residues in the structured region primarily enable sequence-specific RNA binding.


### Sugar-Puckering Is Linked to the Binding Free Energy
  We investigated the effect of RNA backbone structure on binding free energy by calculating the phase angle of sugar puckering, which numerically indicates structural distortion of the ribose ring. In the C3′-endo conformation, for example, the C3′ carbon atom of ribose is below the plane formed by C1′, C4′, and O (Figure  a). The ribose units of RNA typically adopt either the C3′-endo conformation with a phase angle of between 0° and 36° or the C2′-endo conformation with a phase angle of between 144° and 180°. ,  Free RNAs favor the C3′-endo conformation. We calculated the phase angles of the free RNA and FUS-RNA complex during CMD and of RNA at the beginning of its dissociation from FUS (up to 1.5 nm in the center-of-mass distance) during dPaCS-MD (Figure  b). We found differences in the phase angles of G3 and U4 in free RNA, the RNA-protein complex, and RNA at the beginning of dissociation.

  For G3, the phase angle distribution of the free RNA peaked at 10° (C3′-endo). In contrast, the FUS-RNA complex exhibited two peaks, around 18° (C3′-endo) and 176° (C2’-endo), suggesting two equilibrium states of the FUS-RNA complex: one is the same conformation as that of free RNA, and the other is a conformation that is distorted from that of free RNA. The phase angle of RNA at the beginning of dissociation in the low free energy trials exhibited a high peak around 148° (C2′-endo) and a low peak around 25° (C3′-endo), while the peak of the phase angles of the high free energy trials exhibited a high peak around 25° (C3′-endo) and a low peak around 162° (C2′-endo), indicating that the binding free energy depends on the initial conformation of the dissociation complex.

  Similar to G3, the distribution of the phase angles of U4 in the FUS-RNA complex exhibited two peaks, indicating the coexistence of two equilibrium states of U4: the C3′-endo conformation, which is the same as that of the free state, and the C2′-endo conformation. The phase angles of RNA at the beginning of dissociation in the low free energy trials exhibited a high peak around 148° (C2′-endo), while those in the high free energy trials exhibited a high peak around 32° (C3′-endo). These results also show that if dissociation starts with U4 in the C3′-endo conformation, the free energy of dissociation is high, and if dissociation starts from U4 in the C2′-endo conformation, the free energy of dissociation is low.

  The sugar-puckering states of the FUS segment, comprising only the structured region, corresponded to those of the structured region of the high free energy trials (Figure S14). Overall, the binding free energy of dissociation is high if the dissociation starts from the C3′-endo conformations of G3/U4, while it is relatively low if the dissociation starts from the C2′-endo.


### Possible General Mechanisms of Nucleic Acid Binding Assisted
by Flanking Disordered Regions
  The above results demonstrate the sequence-specific recognition of RNA bases by the ZnF side chains and the stabilization of binding by nonspecific charge interactions between the RNA backbone phosphate groups and the positively charged side chains of arginine residues in the IDR flanking ZnF. We investigated the generality of this mechanism by analyzing the positions of nucleic acid binding motifs and the disordered regions of proteins registered in DisProt (2024 December release version), a manually curated database of 3113 disordered proteins. The nucleic acid binding motifs were identified and annotated using the PANTHER 19.0 database. ,

  We analyzed the nucleic acid binding sites. Sequence nonspecific binding sites like IDRs are not detected by this analysis. A total of 2454 nucleic acid binding sites, including ZnF, were identified from 1201 proteins. The proportion of nucleic acid binding sites flanked by disordered regions in proteins with several nucleic acid binding sites was larger than the proportion of nucleic acid binding sites flanked by disordered regions in proteins with many nucleic acid binding sites: e.g., 43% of RNA binding sites in proteins with one RNA binding site were flanked by disordered regions, while 19% of RNA binding sites in proteins with ≥5 RNA binding sites were flanked by disordered regions (Figure  ). The difference in the ratio of disordered-regio-flanked nucleic acid binding sites suggests two strategies for stabilizing the binding of nucleic acids in proteins. One is stabilization with many nucleic acid binding sites, and the other is stabilization with fewer binding sites but assisted by flanking disordered regions as in the case of FUS ZnF. The latter scenario is consistent with previous studies of flexible linkers flanking nucleic acid binding sites. −


## Discussion
  We performed PaCS-MD simulations of RNA dissociation from the C-terminal side of the RGG2 domain and the ZnF of FUS (Figure  a,b). We found that eight amino acid residues in the N-terminal side of ZnF (a part of RGG2) were largely disordered (a region referred to as the IDR), while the other residues were structured (referred to as the structured region) (Figure  c).

  The dissociation trials of dPaCS-MD were classified into high and low free energy trials based on the standard binding free energy between FUS and RNA (Figures   and S2–S5, Tables S1 and S2). A larger number of interactions were identified between RNA and the IDR in the low free energy trials than in the high free energy trials (Figure  a). Additionally, the FUS segment, comprising only the structured region, exhibited interactions similar to those of the high free energy trials (Figures  a,c, , S12, and S13). These results suggest that interactions between RNA and the IDR significantly contribute to binding stability in a nonsequence-specific manner, suggesting that FUS may capture an RNA in a nonsequence-specific manner through the IDR, slide the RNA along the IDR, and finally bind to the specific RNA sequences analogous to p53, a tumor suppressor protein that scans DNA with its C-terminal IDR to facilitate recognition of specific DNA sequences by the structured DNA binding domain. ,  This IDR-assisted binding mechanism could also apply to the FUS RRM, whose flanking IDR fluctuates significantly. This study focused only on the interactions between the RGG2-ZnF fragment. The RGG2 fragment is located near the ZnF RNA binding site and interacts directly with RNA as observed in the NMR study. Previous experimental studies showed that RGG3 also interacts with RNA. ,  One possible next challenge to study the interactions between FUS and RNA would be to perform MD simulations of a longer construct, including RGG3.

  Our results confirmed that G2 is recognized by FUS ZnF through hydrogen bonds from the main chain of Q420 and the side chains of D425 and W440 in an RNA sequence-specific manner, as indicated in the previous study (Figure  b,c). In addition, RNA sequence-independent interactions were observed: π-electron interactions with F438 and R422, and charge interactions with R422, consistent with a previous study. The previous study reported interactions of G2 with Q421 and S439, but we did not observe these strong interactions. Rather, we found that G3 formed hydrogen bonds with the side chain of R441 and the main chains of M436 and F438 in an RNA sequence-specific manner and that G3 stacked on F438. Hydrogen bonds were observed between U4 and the side chains of N435 and N445 sequence-specifically. U1 weakly interacted with W440 through π-electrons. These interactions were reported in a previous study, but we did not observe the previously reported interactions between U4 and Q446. Nonetheless, our analysis supports the previous study that π-electron interactions dominate RNA recognition.

  Our data support the GGU motif of single-stranded RNA as the binding target of FUS ZnF, consistent with previous studies. ,,− ,,  Several studies reported that FUS ZnF binds to GGUA/GGUG ,  while our simulations for GGUG show that the last nucleotide (G5) does not form a strong direct interaction with FUS. Experimentally, the binding affinities of GGUA and GGUG with FUS ZnF are nearly identical. These results suggest that GGU is more essential for binding to FUS ZnF and that the fourth nucleotide (G or A) contributes less. The difference in binding free energy between the high and low free energy trials reflects the absence/presence of the IDR interaction with RNA. The binding free energies of the two groups allowed us to calculate the contribution of the IDR to the binding free energy, which was found to be −2.6 kcal/mol. This difference shifts the equilibrium dissociation constant (K d) substantially from 249 to 3.3 μM (Figure  c). A comparison between the full construct (17 μM) and the ZnF-only construct (178 μM) also shows one order shift in K d. These results indicate that the binding of the ZnF motif alone to specific short nucleotides is relatively weak, but the binding of RGG2 significantly enhances the binding affinity by adding nonspecific charge interactions.

  The sugar-puckering state of ribose reflects the conformation of the RNA backbone and differs between the high and low free energy trials, particularly in G3 and U4 (Figure  b). The sugar-puckering state in the high free energy trials was similar to that of free RNA, suggesting that RNA is sequence-specifically locked by the structured region and is stabilized by nonspecific charge interaction between the RNA phosphate groups and the flanked IDR in the low free energy trials. During RNA locking, the RNA backbone bends around the IDR, which has a high curvature. This alters the RNA structure from that of the free state. In contrast, in the high free energy trials, RNA binds to the structured region without being locked by the flanking IDR. In this case, the RNA backbone conformation is not bent and its curvature remains low. There are two known forms of double-helical nucleotides: A-form and B-form. A-form nucleotides form helices with a small radius (high curvature), and each ribose adopts a C3′-endo conformation. In contrast, B-form nucleotides form helices with a large radius (low curvature), and each ribose adopts a C2′-endo conformation. ,  Our results on structural interactions and sugar puckering are consistent with this established knowledge.

  In cells, methylation of arginine residues in FUS RGGs neutralizes their charge, helping FUS localize correctly in the nucleus and avoiding liquid–liquid phase separation. ,−     Disruption of arginine methylation promotes droplet formation through liquid–liquid phase separation, mislocalization to the cytosol, and the onset of ALS. Furthermore, mutations of arginine residues to noncharged residues in FUS RGGs have been linked to neurodegenerative diseases such as ALS and ET (Essential Tremor). ,,  Our results showed that the charges of nonmethylated arginines play a role in stabilizing the RNA binding. They also imply that a loss of arginine charges could destabilize binding and be related to FUS dysfunction. Cooperative RNA binding of FUS with the RanBP2-type ZnF and the flanking flexible region is analogous to that observed in the DNA binding of C2H2-type ZnF. Our results suggest that the binding mechanism of C2H2-type ZnFs extends to the RNA binding of RanBP2-type ZnFs.

  Our findings further expand the repertoire of IDR-assisted binding of ZnFs, from double-stranded DNAs to single-stranded RNAs, although the structural nature of double-stranded DNA differs greatly from that of single-stranded RNA. This is because charge interactions and van der Waals interactions, which play important roles in the FUS ZnF–RNA interaction, do not require a highly specific structure. Previous studies reported that tandem C2H2-type ZnFs bind to double-stranded DNAs with the help of the intervening flexible linker sequence. ,,  Our current results indicate that single-stranded RNA binding to the RanBP2-type ZnF of FUS is assisted by the flanking IDR (Figure  ). However, this binding mechanism is not universal for all of the ZnFs. For example, the disordered sequence comprising 25 residues between two RanBP2-type ZnFs in ZRANBP2 does not contribute to the recognition of single-stranded RNA. Nonetheless, our findings can be generalized to the mechanisms underpinning other nucleic acid binding proteins. We revealed that nucleic acid binding sites in proteins with one or a few nucleic acid binding sites are more likely to be flanked by disordered regions than nucleic acid binding sites in proteins with numerous nucleic acid binding sites. Examples include Arid5a, Sex-lethal, HuD, nucleolin, PABP, and Hrp1. −       Our results support flanking disordered region-assisted nucleic acid binding.


## Conclusions
  This study suggests that ZnF specifically recognizes the GGU sequence of RNA while the flanking RGG2 domain interacts nonspecifically with the RNA backbone. Although the binding of ZnF alone with the short RNA sequence is relatively weak, the binding of RGG2 exhibits a 2-fold lower dissociation constant due to the addition of nonspecific charge interactions with the RNA backbone. Our analysis of nucleic acid binding sites in the database of disordered proteins suggests that the binding of the nucleic acid binding motif in concert with the flanking disordered region is a common mechanism for increasing the binding affinity of nucleic acid binding proteins to specific short RNA sequences.
