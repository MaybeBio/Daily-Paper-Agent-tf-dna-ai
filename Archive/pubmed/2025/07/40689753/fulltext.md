# The intrinsically disordered protein NUPR1 binds to phospholipids


## Abstract
  The nuclear protein 1 (NUPR1) is an intrinsically disordered protein (IDP) involved in stress‐mediated cellular conditions, with an interactome including many other partner proteins, as well as nucleic acids. We wondered whether its great conformational flexibility and biological versatility could include interactions with lipids. Binding between NUPR1 and phosphatidylserine (PS) and phosphatidylinositol biphosphate (PIP2) was verified in cellulo by using proximity ligation assay (PLA) techniques in MiaPaCa‐2 cells. Binding in vitro was assayed against PS, and against PS in a mixture with phosphatidylcholine (PC), and it was confirmed by using nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and biolayer interferometry (BLI). Furthermore, results in silico also showed the association between NUPR1 and the lipids, occurring in a mostly aspecific way on the membrane surface, but with a slight preference for the binding through the protein hot‐spot regions: the most common interaction sites with other molecular species: around Ala33 and Thr68. All together, these techniques unambiguously indicate that NUPR1 was bound to lipids. We discuss the potential biological consequences of our findings, including the possible relevance of NUPR1 in participating in the stability of membrane organelles, as well as in modulating cellular signaling.


## INTRODUCTION
  NUPR1 is an 82‐residue‐long (8 kDa), highly basic, monomeric intrinsically disordered protein (IDP). It is overexpressed during the acute phase of pancreatitis, and it is also involved in the development and regeneration of the pancreas and in cancer development, as well as at several stages of the lifespan of healthy cells (Mallo et al., 1997). NUPR1 does not have a stable structure (Uversky, 2013; Wright & Dyson, 2015); in fact, there is no evidence of secondary or tertiary structure along any patch in its sequence when it is isolated in solution—not even of a transient one. Furthermore, when NUPR1 binds to other macromolecules, it remains fuzzy (disordered) in the corresponding complexes (Aguado‐Llera et al., 2013; Encinar et al., 2001; Lan et al., 2020; Neira et al., 2017; Neira et al., 2020; Santofimia‐Castaño et al., 2021). NUPR1 binds to DNA (Encinar et al., 2001), and it is involved in its repairing (Aguado‐Llera et al., 2013), after being translocated into the nucleus by means of importin α3 (Lan et al., 2020; Neira et al., 2020). It does interact with different proteins during transcription, where it is considered a crucial protein, as well as being an essential element in the stress‐cell response and cell‐cycle regulation (Aguado‐Llera et al., 2013; Cano et al., 2011; Goruppi & Iovanna, 2010; Lan et al., 2020; Santofimia‐Castaño et al., 2017; Santofimia‐Castaño et al., 2022). In all these protein–protein interactions (PPIs), or in the binding to DNA (Aguado‐Llera et al., 2013), NUPR1 uses two hydrophobic regions, the so‐called “hot‐spots”, centered around residues Ala33 and Thr68 (Araujo‐Abad et al., 2023; Lan et al., 2020; Santofimia‐Castaño et al., 2017; Santofimia‐Castaño et al., 2021). Recently, we have shown that NUPR1 can form membraneless organelles in the presence of high amounts of RNA (Santofimia‐Castaño et al., 2024). Therefore, we also wondered whether NUPR1 could also be involved in the interaction with lipids, which are the main component of cell membranes, and then, it could be also implicated in the interaction with membranes.

  To test this hypothesis, in this study we explored NUPR1 lipid‐binding properties towards different types of lipids, namely phosphatidylcholine (PC) and brain phosphatidylserine (PS), by using nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), biolayer interferometry (BLI), and complemented with bioinformatic analyses and docking simulations. Phosphatidylserine (PS) and phosphatidylcholine (PC) are some of the lipids forming cell membranes and having key functions in lipid metabolism. Cho‐containing phospholipids are the most abundant component in the cell membranes of eukaryotes, and zwitterionic phosphatidylcholine (PC) is the predominant phospholipid in most mammalian membranes, constituting 30–50% of all the nuclear lipids (Molendijk et al., 2020). Phosphatidylserine (PS) is a negatively charged phospholipid, important for the function and integrity of the eukaryotic cell membrane, which is normally asymmetrically distributed in the plasma membrane (Kay & Grinstein, 2011). Furthermore, recent research has revealed that PS is uniquely enriched in the inner nuclear membrane and the nuclear reticulum, suggesting specialized functions beyond its well‐known roles in the plasma membrane (Niu et al., 2024). Moreover, we also showed NUPR1 lipid‐binding properties towards phosphatidylserine (PS) and phosphatidylinositol biphosphate (PIP2) in cellulo; the use of PIP2, a key player in many cellular processes in spite of its relatively low abundance (Czech, 2000), was motivated by the availability for this membrane phospholipid PIP2 (as well as for PS, and at variance with PC) of antibodies to perform proximity ligation assay (PLA), a technique that allows one a detection of protein/lipid interactions in situ with high sensitivity. Since NUPR1 is a paradigm of a fully disordered protein, which remains disordered even when bound to other biomolecules (Aguado‐Llera et al., 2013; Araujo‐Abad et al., 2023; Santofimia‐Castaño et al., 2017; Santofimia‐Castaño et al., 2021), our findings in this study are an example of how a textbook IDP could interact with lipids, since so far only the binding of intrinsically disordered regions of proteins to lipids has been described under different environmental conditions (see, for instance Chan et al., 2011; Gérard et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2014). Our findings indicate that NUPR1 interacted with several kinds of lipids in cellulo and in vitro. These experimental results were also confirmed by in silico results, where binding was shown to occur by a rather unspecific mechanism, but through the preferential participation of the two hot spots of NUPR1. This study raises new questions about the role of this protein in the interactions with other macromolecules, and how it is involved in the development of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma.


## RESULTS

### NUPR1 interacted in cellulo with phosphatidylserine (PS) and phosphatidylinositol biphosphate (PIP2)
  To test whether interaction between NUPR1 and the two phospholipids, PS and PIP2, occurred in cellulo, we used MiaPaCa‐2 cells. In this way, the presence of such interactions could be verified by targeting the endogenous lipids and the protein. Duolink in situ assay was used, which resolves the proximity of two molecules that occurs at distances less than about 16 Å. Our results showed that both PS and PIP2 efficiently interact with NUPR1 (Figure 1), as green fluorescent spots were observed, corresponding to the PLA signals.

  In addition, we used ZZW‐115, a NUPR1 inhibitor which binds to the hot‐spots of NUPR1 (Aguado‐Llera et al., 2013), to test whether this drug might hamper the interaction between NUPR1 and both phospholipids. In fact, we observed a decrease in the number of dots upon ZZW‐115 treatment, indicating that this compound hampered the binding of this protein to both phospholipids.


### Measuring in vitro the affinity of the lipids and NUPR1
  After demonstrating in cellulo the binding between NUPR1 and both lipid species, next we wanted to measure quantitatively such interaction in vitro. The charge and degree of chain saturation affect the structure and surface properties of bilayers and, hence, membrane binding of the protein and the other macromolecule species. Therefore, two phospholipids of different size and composition were used. The lipids employed were: (i) PC, which is a zwitterionic lipid; and (ii) PSm, consisting of a mixture of 7.5 mM of PC and 2.5 mM of brain PS (for a standard final concentration of 10 mM of the lipid). It is important to indicate that PS is an anionic lipid, and by using that lipid mixture, we are trying to: (i) mimic the cell membrane composition of some mammalian cells; and (ii) find out whether the additional presence of an anionic lipid could alter the binding to the protein.

  In our initial attempt, we tried to use fluorescence to monitor the binding between NUPR1 and lipids, but our efforts were unsuccessful because NUPR1 has only two tyrosine residues, resulting in a relatively weak fluorescence signal. Therefore, we decided to follow a two‐part experimental approach. First, since we had the complete NMR assignment of NUPR1 (Aguado‐Llera et al., 2013), we decided to use this technique to monitor changes due to the possible binding of the protein to both lipids. And second, we monitored the possible binding to lipids by using BLI.


#### Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)
  As we had already done with our unsuccessful attempts with fluorescence, we added the solution containing the LUVs (large unilamellar vesicles) of each of the two lipids to a fixed amount of NUPR1. The addition of LUVs led to different effects on NUPR1, depending on the type of lipid used. For PC, we observed a general broadening in the intensity of all signals, and this was especially evident for residues around Ser23 and Ser31 in the protein sequence (Figure 2). For those residues, we also observed small changes in chemical shifts of some cross‐peaks. A similar behavior (i.e., general broadening of cross‐peaks) has been observed when NUPR1 binds to some of its protein partners (Araujo‐Abad et al., 2023; Santofimia‐Castaño et al., 2017; Santofimia‐Castaño et al., 2021), to DNA (Aguado‐Llera et al., 2013), and drugs (Lan et al., 2020; Neira et al., 2017). In all those binding reactions, NUPR1 remained disordered when it is bound. We suggest, based on the absence of drastic changes in the chemical shifts observed, that NUPR1 also remained disordered when it was bound to PC.

  On the other hand, binding of NUPR1 to PSm, consisting of a mixture of 7.5 mM of PC and 2.5 mM of brain PS (Figure S1), resulted in a stronger general broadening of all signals. Among others, the cross‐peaks of Thr68 and Ser31, as well as many of the Gly residues (appearing very upfield shifted in the 15N dimension), disappeared in the spectrum acquired in the presence of the lipid. The fact that there were some cross‐peaks broadened beyond the possibility of detection, whereas others were still observed—although with a lower intensity when compared to their intensity in the spectrum of the isolated NUPR1—indicated that there were certain regions of NUPR1 that were preferentially bound to PSm. Furthermore, the specific behavior of some residues, when compared with the general tendency observed for PC, indicates that the mechanism of interaction of NUPR1 with the LUVs was lipid‐dependent. Finally, based also on the absence of drastic changes in the chemical shifts of the cross‐peaks, we can conclude that NUPR1 also remained disordered when it was bound to PSm.


#### Biolayer interferometry (BLI)
  For both lipids, we had to use a double exponential to fit the sensorgram of the association event of the LUV to NUPR1; this suggests that the binding process was not two‐state. The difference between the two k obs values for the two exponentials for each of the lipids was an order of magnitude. We worked with the largest k obs value because the smallest one did not show a linear lipid‐concentration dependence with a positive slope.

  For PC, the range of concentrations explored was from 0.6 to 2.5 mM, and the pseudo‐first order plot (the k obs vs. LUV concentration) yielded values for the apparent dissociation constant of 2.8 ± 0.9 mM (Figure 3a,b), with k on = 0.2 ± 0.2 mM−1 s−1 and k off = 0.5 ± 0.3 s−1. For PSm, the range of explored lipid concentrations was from 0.7 to 6.3 mM, and the pseudo‐first order plot yielded values for the dissociation constant of 3.3 ± 0.5 mM (Figure 3c,d), with k on = 0.010 ± 0.002 mM−1 s−1 and k off = 0.033 ± 0.008 s−1.

  It is important to pinpoint that the above estimated apparent equilibrium constants for each lipid were obtained on the assumption that the kinetic process is a two‐state one, that is K D = k off/k on. This assumption was not right, as the binding process to the biosensor‐attached NUPR1 could be modeled by using several exponentials in the fit (as indicated above, see also Section 4.6.2); therefore, the estimated equilibrium constant values must be taken with caution and they must be considered rough estimates, although the occurrence of binding between NUPR1 and each of the lipids is unambiguously supported by the findings of this technique.

  Thus, although the apparent equilibrium dissociation constant was similar for both lipids, the kinetic rate constants, k on and k off, were much slower for PSm than for PC, and the mechanisms of binding (in agreement with the NMR results, Figures 1 and S1) seemed to be different for the two lipids.


### Prediction of NUPR1 sequence patches capable of interacting with membranes
  Recently, the DisoLipPred software has been developed to predict the propensity of IDPs to interact with lipids (Katuwawala et al., 2021). We tested DisoLipPred against NUPR1 by using the binary (yes/no) predictions based on the calculated propensity score and comprising more than two consecutive protein residues. As shown in Figure 4, the portions of the NUPR1 sequence predicted to interact with lipids were: Ser22‐Leu24, Ser27‐Gly41 (in this region there is also a short patch formed by four consecutive glycines, Gly38‐Gly41), Glu49‐Thr54, and Pro57‐Ser73. Then, the two longest predicted regions by DisoLipPred comprise the hot spots of NUPR1 (Neira et al., 2017; Santofimia‐Castaño et al., 2017; Santofimia‐Castaño et al., 2022): the region around the two tyrosines Tyr30 and Tyr36, and the nuclear localization signal (NLS) that is located around Thr68 (Neira et al., 2020). Residues belonging to the two longest lipid‐binding polypeptide patches predicted by DisoLipPred were those showing changes in NMR chemical shifts in the presence of PC (Figure 2), or a broadening of the signal beyond detection in the presence of PS (Figure S1), namely the Gly‐rich region.

  On the other hand, when software to predict transmembrane regions was used (the MPex approach by White and Wimbley (1999)), no specific polypeptide patch in the NUPR1 sequence was obtained (data not shown). Then, those polypeptide patches predicted by DisoLipPred are not transmembrane regions, but NUPR1 residues that are directly involved in binding to lipids and involving superficial lipid regions. These results seem reasonable because NUPR1 is a totally disordered IDP with a high affinity for the aqueous solvent, which was not expected to show a high affinity towards the hydrophobic regions of lipid membranes.


### Simulated docking of NUPR1 on the lipid membranes
  Molecular docking was used to simulate the binding of NUPR1 to the lipid membranes. Because of the highly flexible and heterogeneous nature of the conformation of NUPR1, the simulations were performed through a ‘divide & conquer’ approach that we had already used in the past to simulate the binding of this protein to other molecular partners (Araujo‐Abad et al., 2023; Neira et al., 2020; Santofimia‐Castaño et al., 2018; Santofimia‐Castaño et al., 2021). Each of these fragments was separately docked onto two lipid membranes also explored in vitro: one mimicking the same composition of PSm for the in vitro studies (75% DPPC and 25% DPPS), and the other one made of PC only (100% DPPC). The regions of DPPC and DPPS (75% DPPC and 25% DPPS) expecting to interact with NUPR1 were the surface‐facing moieties PC and PS, respectively. In addition to the membrane composition, the two sets of simulations differed in other parameters, including the type of bilayer equilibration, the area per lipid, and the search volume in the docking simulations performed by AutoDock Vina (Eberhardt et al., 2021).

  The Figure 5 reports the binding energies obtained for the first 20 docking poses of each fragment along the NUPR1 sequence. All the fragments were bound to the membrane surface (examples of the docking poses obtained are reported in Figure S2). Despite the differences between the two sets of simulations, the curves were in reasonable agreement with each other, with the two most prominent minima visible around residue 30 and 60. However, both curves showed a relatively small variability of affinity among different fragments because the average binding energies among different residues (black lines in Figure 5) were −6.0 ± 0.6 and −5.5 ± 0.5 kcal/mol for PSm and PC, respectively. In fact, the standard deviations here reported (0.6 and 0.5 kcal/mol) were roughly three times smaller than the variability observed for each docking pose (corresponding to the ‘thickness’ of the gray regions in the two figure panels “a” and “b”). This observation suggests that NUPR1 did not rely on a few well‐defined anchoring points to interact with the membranes; in contrast, the binding interface exhibited a high degree of frustration in the energy landscape, which parallels the versatility observed in the interactions of IDPs with other proteins (Freiberger et al., 2021; Jemth et al., 2014).

  In summary, the binding energies observed in the simulations are enough to drive the binding of NUPR1 to the bilayer surface. They also suggest a rather aspecific binding mechanism that maintains a disordered structure of the protein; nevertheless, this fuzzy structure shows a preference for some residues (e.g., Ser31) to anchor to the membrane surface, in qualitative agreement with our NMR findings.


## DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION
  Lipids are involved in several cellular functions, including cell trafficking, energy storage, compartmentalization, regulation, and signaling. These functions are perfectly modulated through interaction with other macromolecules, including protein partners. In fact, there are studies on the interaction between lipids and proteins, but the interaction between lipids and IDPs has rarely been documented in the literature. In a pathological context, the development of some diseases implicates the binding of IDPs with fatty acids (Deryusheva et al., 2019 and references therein); for instance, the association of either α‐synuclein or tau protein to fatty acids leads to an increase in fibril formation for both of these disordered proteins (Gamblin et al., 2000; Ruipérez et al., 2010). NUPR1, a prototypical IDP, has been extensively studied over the past two decades due to its role in tumor development. Numerous protein partners have been well characterized, enabling its involvement in processes such as nuclear transport, PARylation, and SUMOylation. Additionally, interactions with RNA have been identified, which probably, together with binding to RNA‐binding proteins and other macromolecules, allow NUPR1 to participate in membraneless structures like stress granules (Santofimia‐Castaño et al., 2024); then, we wondered whether the molecular properties of NUPR1 could also allow binding to proper membranes, which could represent an alternative to RNA and the other macromolecular complexes involved in stress granule formation, as a scaffolding molecule to participate in the construction of other types of organelles. The use of orthogonal techniques has now made it possible to investigate and describe NUPR1 interaction with lipids, paving the way for further exploration of new functions. In our study, to test this hypothesis, we have probed the interaction of NUPR1 with a variety of lipids that included PIP2, PC, and PS (being this, in the case of in vitro studies, a mixture of PC and brain PS). The combination of in cellulo, in vitro, and in silico analyses pointed out that NUPR1 interacted with all these lipid species. We used different types of lipids for the in cellulo and in vitro studies because, for the studies in cellulo, there are only available a selected range of antibodies.

  At first stage, by using PLA, we were able to detect the interaction of NUPR1 with two phospholipids in celullo, namely PS and PIP2. The pharmacological NUPR1 inhibition (by using ZZW‐115) reduced the interaction between these two macromolecules. The description of these lipid‐protein interactions in cellulo is challenging, as there is a very limited number of antibodies available against lipids. In addition, permeabilization protocols must be optimized to avoid disrupting these interactions.

  At second, we observed by using NMR that the NUPR1 regions involved in binding were the most hydrophobic regions of NUPR1 (Santofimia‐Castaño et al., 2024), namely the region around the two tyrosines (in the 30s of the sequence), and the region comprising the NLS (Neira et al., 2020; Aguado‐Llera et al., 2013). Thus, we could conclude that the same region of NUPR1 involved in the binding to other proteins (Araujo‐Abad et al., 2023; Santofimia‐Castaño et al., 2017; Santofimia‐Castaño et al., 2022), drugs (Lan et al., 2020; Neira et al., 2017) and DNA (Aguado‐Llera et al., 2013) is the same involved also in the binding to lipids. Furthermore, it is important to pinpoint that NUPR1 remained disordered in the presence of the lipids (Figures 2 and S1), as it happens with other large biomolecules, such as proteins and DNA (Aguado‐Llera et al., 2013; Araujo‐Abad et al., 2023; Encinar et al., 2001; Malicet et al., 2006; Santofimia‐Castaño et al., 2017; Santofimia‐Castaño et al., 2021) or even large synthetic ones, such as dendrimers and crowders (Bonucci et al., 2021; Neira et al., 2019). It could be thought that the binding of the two lipids to NUPR1 was unspecific due to: (i) the absence of changes in chemical shifts of the cross‐peaks in the 2D 1H‐15N HSQC spectra (Figures 2 and S1); and (ii) the high values of the apparent equilibrium constants as determined by BLI (Section 2.2.2). However, we have previously indicated that the values of the apparent equilibrium constants must be taken with caution, as they are obtained based on assumptions which are not right (Section 2.2.2). Furthermore, we have previously shown that when NUPR1 binds to: (i) any biomolecule, such as proteins or DNA (Aguado‐Llera et al., 2013; Araujo‐Abad et al., 2023; Encinar et al., 2001; Malicet et al., 2006; Santofimia‐Castaño et al., 2017; Santofimia‐Castaño et al., 2021); (ii) large synthetic molecules, such as dendrimers (Neira et al., 2019); or even (iii) drugs or small peptides (Liu et al., 2024; Neira et al., 2017; Rizzuti et al., 2021; Santofimia‐Castaño et al., 2018; Santofimia‐Castaño, Xia, et al., 2019) the chemical shifts of cross‐peaks of NUPR1 do not change, in most of the cases, but rather a general broadening of selected signals is observed. Only in the binding with two other proteins (Araujo‐Abad et al., 2023; Santofimia‐Castaño et al., 2021), were there concomitant changes of chemical shifts of some cross‐peaks, and the broadening of others in the 2D 1H‐15N HSQC spectra of NUPR1. Therefore, the absence of changes in the chemical shifts of cross‐peaks upon addition of potential partners does not invalidate the presence of specific binding, as it can be also observed in other examples of IDPs in DisProt (Aspromonte et al., 2024); it just indicates that the protein remains disordered upon complex formation (fuzzy complex). Finally, we have two other additional pieces of evidence to support the specificity of the NUPR1/lipid interaction. First, the results of the docking simulations also pinpoint to binding occurring at the hot spots of NUPR1. And second, the fact that binding is abolished in cellulo in the presence of ZZW‐115 (Figure 1), which also binds to the hot spots of the protein (Neira et al., 2017; Santofimia‐Castaño, Rizzuti, et al., 2019; Santofimia‐Castaño, Xia, et al., 2019), indicates that lipids and ZZW‐115 are competing for the same site under these conditions. Furthermore, this latter piece of evidence provides an upper limit for the value of the association constant of the lipids since the IC50 of ZZW‐115 is 1.5 μM (Santofimia‐Castaño, Xia, et al., 2019).

  Our bioinformatic analysis and docking simulations fully support these observations. The binding of NUPR1 appeared to be on the surface of lipid membranes, and with a good theoretical binding affinity (from the calculated energies shown in Figure 5). The interaction sites were distributed along the whole protein sequence, as it could be expected due to the large local frustration generally observed in IDPs, especially at their binding interfaces. In fact, the presence of suboptimal interactions is a requisite for the possibility of multiple bound sub‐states, as it has been already demonstrated for complexes of disordered proteins (Freiberger et al., 2021). The unusual combination of aspecific interactions and yet specific binding propensities for each partner explains how NUPR1 could preserve its totally unfolded structure while also maintaining control of the binding to multiple molecular partners.

  At this stage, we do not know the biological consequences of the NUPR1 binding to lipids. In a recent study, we demonstrated that NUPR1 can undergo liquid–liquid phase separation (LPPS), a property that could enable its participation in a wide variety of macromolecular complexes within the cell (Santofimia‐Castaño et al., 2024). In this study, we have shown that it can also bind to lipids. The formation of these complexes with lipids does not invalidate the LPPS observed previously, since the latter can be observed within the membrane (Nesterov et al., 2021) providing adaptive responses to stress situations. It is interesting to note that the formation of complexes with lipids and membraneless organelles requires the same residues (namely Ala33 and Thr68). Then, the prevalence of one of those mechanisms (LPPS vs. interaction with lipids) will depend on the cell situation, its environment, or even will rely on the presence of other macromolecules, which also can interact with the same NUPR1 sites (Aguado‐Llera et al., 2013; Araujo‐Abad et al., 2023; Santofimia‐Castaño et al., 2017; Santofimia‐Castaño et al., 2021). Moreover, as the formation of LPPS (Santofimia‐Castaño et al., 2024) is enhanced by the presence of RNA or PAR molecules, which probably help in the scaffolding of the organelle, we do not know now whether the presence of the lipids will also enhance the formation of membraneless organelles within the membrane (Nesterov et al., 2021). Thus, NUPR1 appears to play a key role in organizing macromolecular complexes of different origins. In this context, numerous fundamental biological processes take place in close proximity to cellular membranes, such as intracellular signaling mediated by transmembrane receptors, exo‐ and endocytosis processes, cytoskeleton organization, and the metabolism of various phospholipids (some of them involved in the development and progression of several types of cancer; Fowle‐Grider et al., 2024; Senft, 2025). Some of these processes are essential for tumor development and progression, a context in which an increase in NUPR1 expression has been reported (Gill & von Hippel, 1989). Further studies will be needed to unravel the role of this interaction, and at what extent it is lipid‐dependent, as suggested by the results in this study.

  To conclude, our study shows that NUPR1 is an IDP capable of binding to natural lipids specifically, and this interaction is worth being further investigated under both physiological conditions and pathological disease states.


## MATERIALS AND METHODS

### Materials
  Imidazole, Trizma base and acid, DNase, SIGMAFAST protease tablets, NaCl, Ni(II)‐resin, deuterated Tris buffer, and DAPI (4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole) were from Sigma (Madrid, Spain). Isopropyl‐β‐D‐1‐thiogalactopyranoside and ampicillin were obtained from Apollo Scientific (Stockport, UK). Dialysis tubing with a molecular weight cut‐off of 3500 Da, Triton X‐100, and the SDS protein marker (PAGEmark Tricolor) were from VWR (Barcelona, Spain). Amicon centrifugal devices with a molecular weight cut‐off of 3 kDa were from Millipore (Barcelona, Spain). The rest of the materials used were of analytical grade. Water was deionized and purified on a Millipore system.

  The lipids L‐α‐phosphatidylcholine from egg chicken (PC) and L‐α‐phosphatidylserine from brain porcine (PS) were from Avanti Polar Lipids (Alabaster, AL). The zwitterionic PC and anionic PS are glycerophospholipids, purified from the egg and the brain, respectively. They are formed by a scrambled mixture of different fatty acid chains with the same headgroup: PC or PS, respectively.


### Protein expression and purification
  NUPR1 was purified as previously described (Aguado‐Llera et al., 2013; Encinar et al., 2001). Protein concentrations were determined by UV absorbance, employing an extinction coefficient at 280 nm estimated from the number of tyrosines (NUPR1 has only two tyrosine residues) in the sequence (Gill & von Hippel, 1989).


### Proximity ligation assay (PLA)
  Fifty thousand MiaPaCa‐2 cells were seeded on coverslips in 24‐well plates. After 24 h, cells were treated with the NUPR1 inhibitor ZZW‐115 for 6 h. Then, cells were washed in phosphate buffer solution, fixed with paraformaldehyde at 4% concentration, and permeabilized with Triton X‐100 0.2%. Immunostaining with PLA Duolink (Merk), following the manufacturer's protocol, was performed. Anti‐NUPR1 (1:200, rabbit, homemade) and anti‐PS (1:1000, mouse, ZMS1038, Sigma‐Aldrich) or anti‐PIP2 (1:500, mouse, MABS2283, Sigma‐Aldrich) antibodies were used. Image acquisition was carried out in a Zeiss Axio Imager Z2 microscope apotome (×40 magnification lens). ImageJ (NIH, Bethesda, USA) was used to count the number of green foci resulting from the staining, and no <400 cells were counted for each experimental sample. Statistical analyses were conducted by using the unpaired two‐tailed Student's t‐test.

  It is important to stress that the lipids monitored in cellulo are those for which there are commercially available antibodies for the PLAs. The PIP2 lipid could not be used in the preparation of LUV (Section 4.4) due to its low availability.


### Lipid vesicle and sample preparation
  Lipid vesicles, made from PC or PS, were prepared by dissolving the required amount of lipid in CHCl3/CH3OH (2:1 v/v) and drying, first under a gaseous nitrogen stream and then under vacuum for 3 h to remove all traces of organic solvents. The thin lipid layer formed was resuspended in 20 mM Tris–HCl buffer, pH 7.2, while vortexing. The resulting multilamellar liposome suspensions were extruded through 100 nm polycarbonate filters (Nucleopore, Cambridge, MA) to obtain large unilamellar vesicles (LUVs).

  PC and PS were used in the preparation of LUVs in vitro because: (i) their importance and percentage in the composition of the lipid membranes of mammalian cells (van Meer et al., 2008); and (ii) the existence of antibodies for PS to carry out the PLA (Section 4.3).


### Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)
  The NMR experiments were performed on a Bruker Avance DRX‐500 spectrometer (Karlsruhe, Germany) equipped with a triple resonance probe and z‐pulse field gradients. Spectra were acquired at 25 °C and pH 7.2 (deuterated Tris buffer, 50 mM); the probe temperature was calibrated with a methanol NMR standard (Cavanagh et al., 2007).

  The cross‐peaks in the 2D 1H‐15N HSQC (Bodenhausen & Ruben, 1980) spectra of NUPR1 were identified by using previously determined assignments at pH 4.5 (Aguado‐Llera et al., 2013). The sample containing the mixture of NUPR1 and the corresponding lipid was prepared by using Amicon centrifugal devices with a 3 kDa cut‐off, where NUPR1 was concentrated, and the resulting stock was added to a solution containing 10 mM of LUV of the corresponding lipid, mixed in buffer 50 mM deuterated Tris (pH 7.2), and then adding aqueous solution to a final volume of 500 μL. The final concentration of the corresponding lipid was 2.5 mM, and that of NUPR1 was 80 μM.

  Spectra were acquired in the time proportional phase increment mode. The spectra were typically acquired with 2,048 complex points in the 1H dimension, 60 complex points in the 15N dimension, and with 32 or 64 scans. Typical spectral widths were 6,000 (1H) and 1,500 (15N) Hz. The resulting matrix of data derived from each experiment was zero‐filled to double the number of original points in all dimensions, and shifted squared sine‐bell apodization functions were applied before Fourier transformation. NMR data were processed and analyzed using TopSpin 1.3 (Bruker, Karlsruhe, Germany). Signal intensities in the two NMR spectra (of NUPR1 in isolation, and in the presence of either PC or PS) were measured and, in each experiment, intensities were corrected by the corresponding value of the receiver gain during spectral acquisition. Spectra were calibrated with external TSP for 1H and for the indirect dimensions, as previously described (Cavanagh et al., 2007).


### Biolayer interferometry (BLI)

#### Experimental design of BLI experiments
  The association (k on) and dissociation (k off) rate constants on the binding of NUPR1 to PC or PSm were determined by using a BLItz system (ForteBio, Pall, Barcelona, Spain) (Frenzel & Willbold, 2014; Pantoja‐Uceda et al., 2016). The buffer used in the experiments was that recommended by the manufacturer. As NUPR1 had a His‐tag, it was immobilized on His‐tag biosensors (Forte Bio) at 0.45 μM, while the corresponding lipid was present in the solution. Experiments were carried out at 25 °C. The general schemes of the protein/lipid association/dissociation reactions in the BLItz system were: 30 s of acquisition of the initial baseline with the 10 × kinetics buffer; 120 s of loading NUPR1 into the biosensor; 30 s of baseline with the 10 × kinetics buffer; 120 s of association of the lipid to the biosensor (loaded with NUPR1); and 120 s of dissociation of the lipid from the biosensor.


#### Fitting of the sensorgrams
  Fittings of the sensorgrams was carried out by using KaleidaGraph (Synergy software, Reading, PA, USA) (Skibinsky et al., 2005). The interferometry response during the association step, R(t) (measured in response units, RU), and the binding rate, dR(t)/dt, can be used to evaluate the kinetics of the formation of the NUPR1/lipid complex, according to: (1) dRtdt=konlipidRmax−Rt−koffRt where R max is proportional to the total concentration of biosensor‐bound NUPR1, and [lipid] represents the corresponding concentration of lipid.

  In Equation (1), R(t) is given by: (2) Rt=Req−Reqe−kobst−t0 where R eq is the steady‐state (or equilibrium) response obtained at virtually infinite time when dR(t)/dt = 0, and t 0 = 180 s is the time at which the association step between biosensor‐immobilized NUPR1 and lipid in the solution started. We fitted the experimentally obtained R(t) under any condition as two exponential contributions: (3) Rt=Req−Reqe−kobst−t0−R´eqe−kobst−t0−R´´eqt−t0 since we observed a slope of the signal at the largest sensorgram acquisition times. We tried to fit the sensorgrams to a single exponential equation, but F‐test analyses indicated that the fitting was better with a double‐exponential. The fact that two exponentials were observed in data fitting precluded further speculation on the two‐state nature of the kinetic process of the NUPR1/lipid binding. In addition to the presence of several binding processes, the use of two exponentials in the fitting procedure could also suggest: (i) avidity effects; (ii) possible unspecific binding of the lipid in the solution to the biosensor (as it is indicated in the manufacturer instructions); or, even (iii) mass‐transfer effects due to the large size of the LUVs.

  The determined k obs was used for the pseudo‐first order plots, where its value is given by: (4) kobs=konlipid+koff

  It must be stressed that the apparent equilibrium constant, K D, obtained from the quotient of k off and k on for each of the lipids, is an apparent one, and its estimated value (Section 2.2.2) must be taken with caution.

  The dissociation process of the lipid from the biosensor was always fitted to a single exponential, with R(t) given by: (5) Rt=R1e−kofft−t0 where t 0 = 300 s is the time at which the dissociation of lipid from the biosensor‐bound NUPR1 started in our experimental set‐up, and R 1 is the response level when dissociation starts.


### Bioinformatic analysis of the lipid affinity of NUPR1 sequence
  A bioinformatic analysis of the sequence of NUPR1 was performed to predict its propensity to interact with lipids. The DisoLipPred software was used (Katuwawala et al., 2021), which is a webserver designed for the prediction of disordered lipid‐binding regions. This algorithm uses a deep neural network to generate a score for each protein residue in the input sequence, which quantifies the putative propensity of each amino acid to be part of a lipid‐binding region. A binary annotation is also implemented, which identifies a threshold to predict whether residues belong or not to a region prone to interact with lipids.

  In addition, to predict a possible tendency of NUPR1 to be included into a transmembrane region of a lipid bilayer, the MPex approach by White and Wimbley was used (White & Wimley, 1999). This software consists of a hydropathy‐plot tool to analyze potential membrane protein sequences, using a sliding‐window analysis of the sequence hydrophobicity.


### Protein‐lipid molecular docking
  Molecular docking was used to determine the structure of the complex between NUPR1 and the same lipid membranes investigated in vitro. The PSm LUV used for the in vitro experiments was mimicked by using the webserver MemGen (Knight & Hub, 2015), with a composition of 75% dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine (DPPC) and 25% dipalmitoylphosphatidylserine (DPPS). A total of 100 lipids per monolayer (75 DPPC and 25 DPPS) were organized in a square shape, at the default value of area per lipid (65 Å2). In contrast, pure PC was modeled by using a bilayer of 80 DPPC molecules pre‐equilibrated in molecular dynamics simulation (Skibinsky et al., 2005) at a slightly larger value of area per lipid (80 Å2).

  For NUPR1, because of the necessity to account explicitly for every degree of freedom consisting in the rotation around its dihedral angles, due to the very flexible (100% disordered) conformation of this protein, we used a ‘divide & conquer’ approach previously described (Araujo‐Abad et al., 2023; Neira et al., 2020; Santofimia‐Castaño et al., 2018; Santofimia‐Castaño et al., 2021). In brief, 16 fragments containing each seven protein residues (except for the first fragment, M1ATFPP6, and of the last one, R75KKRGARR82) were build, with a shift of five residues from each other (P5PATSAP11, A10PQQPPG16, …), up to encompass the entire sequence of 82 residues. Each fragment was capped at the N‐ and C‐terminal ending by using an acetyl and amide moiety, respectively, to avoid introducing fictitious charges—again with the exceptions of the first and last fragment, were the corresponding –NH3 + and –COO− charged groups were preserved.

  Docking simulations were performed by using AutoDock Vina, version 1.2.5 (Eberhardt et al., 2021), with full flexibility of each protein fragment. To avoid docking of the fragments to the lateral side of the bilayers, search areas of 50 Å × 50 Å and 40 Å × 40 Å centered on the bilayer were used for the DPPC/DPPS and DPPC membranes, respectively. In addition, along the lipid z‐axis, a thickness of 30 Å was considered for the DPPC/DPPS membrane to probe only its upper layer surface, and of 64 Å for the DPPC membrane to search both layer surfaces, as well as the aliphatic tail region in between them.
