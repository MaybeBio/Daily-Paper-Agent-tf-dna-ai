# In vivo CRISPR screening identifies POU3F3 as a novel regulator of ferroptosis resistance in hepatocellular carcinoma via retinoic acid signaling


## Background
  Sorafenib, a ferroptosis agonist, is a first-line treatment for advanced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). However, its clinical efficacy is limited due to drug resistance, resulting in modest improvements in patient survival. Hence, the present study has been designed to identify critical molecular targets associated with sorafenib resistance and investigate the potential inhibitors in overcoming this therapeutic challenge.


## Methods
  In vivo whole-genome CRISPR/Cas9 library screens were conducted to identify resistance factors to ferroptosis agonists, such as RSL3 and sorafenib, in HCC. The effects and underlying molecular mechanisms of these resistance factors were investigated in HCC cells using ferroptosis detection assays, xenograft tumor models, chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP), and dual-luciferase reporter assays. Potential inhibitors targeting these factors were evaluated through computer-aided virtual screening, molecular dynamics simulations, surface plasmon resonance analysis, and functional evaluations.


## Results
  A retinoic acid metabolism gene cluster, including ADH4, ALDH1A1, ALDH1A3, FABP5, RBP1, and RDH10, was found demonstrating upregulation in HCC cells treated with ferroptosis agonist, sorafenib. This gene cluster contributes to the ferroptosis resistance by producing the strong reducing agent retinoic acid. The transcription factor POU3F3 was identified as a key regulator for the retinoic acid metabolism gene cluster, which simultaneously binds to their promoters, increasing their transcription and promoting retinoic acid production. Knockdown of POU3F3 significantly enhanced the pro-ferroptotic and inhibitory effects of sorafenib on HCC cells by suppressing retinoic acid metabolism. Furthermore, rosarin was identified as a POU3F3 inhibitor, with an equilibrium dissociation constant of 7.57 µM, and demonstrated a synergistic effect with sorafenib against HCC cells both in vitro and in vivo.


## Conclusions
  According to the results, POU3F3 acts as a protective regulator against sorafenib-induced ferroptosis in HCC cells by enhancing the transcription of multiple retinoic acid metabolism genes and promoting retinoic acid production. The POU3F3 inhibitor, rosarin, shows potential as an ideal candidate for overcoming sorafenib resistance in HCC.

## Background
  Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) comprises 90% of primary liver cancer cases and has a poor 5-year survival rate of ~ 15% due to late detection and impaired liver function [1]. HCC cells demonstrate resistance to chemotherapy [2], and thus, sorafenib has been considered a current first-line treatment for HCC [3]. However, one of the main problems associated with sorafenib during chemotherapy is the capacity of cancer cells in the development of resistance [4, 5]. Hence, identifying effective combination therapies to enhance HCC sensitivity to sorafenib and improve treatment outcomes is vital. Ferroptosis is a regulated cell death mechanism driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxide accumulation and demonstrates involvement in various physiological and pathological events [6, 7]. The System Xc−/GSH/GPX4 axis prevents ferroptosis by converting toxic lipid hydroperoxides into non-toxic alcohols, accelerating tumor growth [8, 9]. Targeting this pathway for ferroptosis induction provides a promising approach for cancer therapy. Sorafenib is a key drug approved for HCC treatment and stimulates ferroptosis by targeting the function of SLC7A11 [10]. However, in addition to the GSH-dependent ferroptosis defense mechanism, tumor cells also possess various non-classical ferroptosis defense mechanisms. Tumor cells can produce multiple reducing substances, such as ubiquinone/coenzyme Q10, to prevent excessive ROS generation and thereby suppress lipid peroxidation [11, 12]. Therefore, enhancing the susceptibility of HCC cells to ferroptosis can significantly improve sorafenib sensitivity. Retinoic acid (RA), a metabolite of vitamin A, plays a critical role in regulating growth, differentiation, and apoptosis [13]. The metabolism of RA involves several key enzymes, such as alcohol dehydrogenases (ADHs) and aldehyde dehydrogenases (ALDHs), which sequentially convert retinol to retinaldehyde and then to RA [14, 15]. RA plays a dual role in cancer, acting as a tumor suppressor through its differentiation and anti-proliferative effects, while its metabolism by CYP26 enzymes can promote malignancy. Deregulation of RA signaling is implicated in various cancers, making RA pathways both a target and tool in cancer therapy [16, 17]. However, in cancer therapies, depending on the ROS overload, RA has been found to trigger drug resistance.. Therefore, further investigation into RA metabolism may provide valuable insights for HCC therapy.

  In this study, based on in vivo CRISPR screens, it was found that multiple genes involved in the retinoic acid metabolism were upregulated in HCC cells treated with sorafenib, participating in the drug resistance. POU3F3 was identified as a novel transcription factor that directly binds to the promoter of retinoic acid metabolism genes, enhances their transcription, and promotes RA production, thereby elevating sorafenib resistance. Targeting POU3F3 could serve as a potential strategy to overcome sorafenib resistance in HCC.


## Methods

### Cell culture
  HCC cell lines, including PLC/PRF/5, Huh7, JHH7, Hep3B, and Li-7, were obtained from Procell (https://www.procell.com.cn/; Wuhan, China), while the SNU387 cell line was purchased from iCell Bioscience Inc. (https://www.icellbioscience.com/product; Shanghai, China). All cell lines were authenticated using short tandem repeat (STR) profiling. The HCC cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.


### In vivo CRISPR/Cas9 screen
  Stable Cas9-expressing HCC cell lines (Hep3B and JHH7) were generated via lentiviral transduction of a Cas9 coding sequence linked to a blasticidin resistance gene. Hep3B-Cas9 and JHH7-Cas9 cells were subsequently transduced with the TKO v3 library, which contains 70,948 gRNAs targeting 18,053 protein-coding genes (4 gRNAs per gene) and 142 control gRNAs (targeting EGFP, LacZ, and luciferase) [18]. Transduction was performed at a low multiplicity of infection (MOI ~ 0.3) to ensure effective barcoding. Transduced cells were then selected with 1.0 µg/mL puromycin for 7 days to generate a mutant cell pool.

  After expanding the sgRNA library-transduced cells, one-fourth of the population was harvested for sgRNA sequencing to establish baseline expression levels, and set as day 0. The remaining cells (2 × 10⁶ per mouse) were implanted into the left axilla of nude mice. The mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 20 per group): the treatment group received intraperitoneal injections of 40 mg/kg sorafenib (dissolved in DMSO) or 40 mg/kg RSL3 (dissolved in DMSO) every three days for a total of seven doses, while the control group received an equivalent dose of DMSO. Tumor tissues were collected from these groups, lysed, and subjected to sgRNA sequencing. Resisters were identified using the DrugZ algorithm, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05 and Z score < 0.


### Lentiviral and plasmid transfection
  The PCR-amplified full-length human RBP1, ALDH1A1, ALDH1A3, FABP5, RBP1, and RDH10 cDNAs were subcloned into the pCMV5 empty vector to construct plasmids for overexpression of the corresponding target genes. Plasmids were transfected into Hep3B and JHH7 cells using the Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Lentiviral particles overexpressing POU3F3 were generated by subcloning the PCR-amplified full-length human POU3F3 cDNA into the pMSCV retroviral plasmid, while short hairpin RNA (shRNA) sequences targeting POU3F3 were cloned into the pSuper-retro-puro vector to generate pSuper-retro-POU3F3-RNAi. The POU3F3 shRNA sequence used was TAACTCTTAGTGTCCGGCC. Stable Hep3B and JHH7 cell lines overexpressing POU3F3 or with POU3F3 knockdown were selected with 1.0 µg/mL puromycin for 14 days, starting 48 h after infection.


### Clinical sample collection and human ethics
  A total of 60 HCC tissues and their corresponding adjacent non-tumor tissues were collected at the Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University (Guizhou, China) with approval from the institution’s Human Ethics Committee. Following surgical excision, the samples were promptly preserved at − 80 °C to maintain integrity until further analysis. None of the patients had undergone radiotherapy or chemotherapy prior to sample collection. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before the specimens were collected.


### Immunohistochemistry (IHC)
  Tissue samples were sliced into 4-µm sections and underwent deparaffinization and rehydration. Antigen retrieval was performed by boiling the sections in 0.01 M citric acid buffer (pH 6.0; ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China) for 15 min under high pressure. To eliminate endogenous peroxidase activity, the sections were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 15 min. Non-specific binding was blocked using 10% goat serum (ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China). Finally, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies including POU3F3 (1:400; cat no. ab247159, Abcam, USA), ADH4 (1:200; cat no. 16474-1-AP, Proteintech Inc., Wuhan, China), ALDH1A1 (1:100; cat no. 15910-1-AP, Proteintech Inc.), ALDH1A3 (1:100; cat no. 25167-1-AP, Proteintech Inc.), FABP5 (1:100; cat no. A6373, Abconal Inc., Wuhan, China), RBP1 (1:2000; cat no. 22683-1-AP, Proteintech Inc.), RDH10 (1:200; cat no. 14644-1-AP, Proteintech Inc.), KI67 (1:800; cat no. A20018, Abconal Inc.) and PCNA (1:5000; cat no. A12427, Abconal Inc.) at 4 °C for 12 h. Then, secondary antibody solutions were used to incubate the HCC slides. Following a phosphate-buffered saline wash, DAB was introduced to the HCC sections, and counterstaining was completed using hematoxylin.


### Western blotting
  Total protein was extracted from Hep3B and JHH7 cells using RIPA reagent (Solarbio Inc., Beijing, China) containing 2% protease inhibitor (Solarbio Inc., Beijing, China) according to the instructions. A total of 30 µg proteins of each sample were separated using 10% SDS-PAGE (Solarbio Inc., Beijing, China) and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). After blocking with 5% skim milk for 2 h at room temperature, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, followed by a 1-hour incubation with secondary antibodies at room temperature. Protein signals were detected using the Bio-Rad Imaging System (Bio-Rad, USA). The antibodies used included: POU3F3 (1:1000; cat no. 18999-1-AP, Proteintech Inc.), ADH4 (1:1000; cat no. 16474-1-AP, Proteintech Inc.), ALDH1A1 (1:10000; cat no. 15910-1-AP, Proteintech Inc.), ALDH1A3 (1:1000; cat no. 25167-1-AP, Proteintech Inc.), FABP5 (1:500; cat no. A6373, Abconal Inc.), RBP1 (1:2000; cat no. 22683-1-AP, Proteintech Inc.), RDH10 (1:2000; cat no. 14644-1-AP, Proteintech Inc.), CREB1 (1:2000; cat no. 12208-1-AP, Proteintech Inc.), FOXA1 (1:2000; cat no. 20411-1-AP, Proteintech Inc.) and β-actin (1:5000; cat no. 20536-1-AP, Proteintech Inc.).


### qRT-PCR
  Total RNA was extracted from Hep3B and JHH7 cells using TRIzol reagent (Solarbio Inc., Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Invitrogen, USA). cDNA synthesis was performed using PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit RR047A (TOYOBO, Japan). qRT-PCR was conducted on a LightCycler 480 system (Roche, Switzerland) with the SYBR green reagent (Yeasen Inc., Shanghai, China). mRNA expression of target genes was quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with ACTB as the internal control. Primers were designed and synthesized by TIANYI HUIYUAN Inc. (Beijing, China), and their sequences are provided in Supplemental Table 1.


### Immunofluorescence
  Hep3B and JHH7 cells were cultured on sterilized coverslips, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Solarbio Inc., Beijing, China), and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (Boster Inc., Wuhan, China). They were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies including CREB1 (1:100; cat no. 12208-1-AP, Proteintech Inc.), FOXA1 (1:50; cat no. 20411-1-AP, Proteintech Inc.), and POU3F3 (1:100; cat no. PA5-70366, Invitrogen, USA). The next day, the coverslips were washed three times with TBST, incubated for 1 h at room temperature in the dark with secondary antibodies, washed again three times, and imaged.


### CHIP-qPCR
  A total of 2 × 10⁷ Hep3B cells overexpressing His labeled POU3F3 were fixed with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature, and the reaction was quenched with 125 mM glycine for 5 min. After treatment with ChIP lysis buffer, the nuclear pellet was centrifuged at 2000×g for 5 min at 4 °C. Chromatin was sonicated to 200–500 bp fragments and incubated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-His antibody (1:50; cat no. 66005-1-Ig, Proteintech Inc.). DNA was extracted using the phenol-chloroform method, and the purified products were subjected to PCR.


### Dual-luciferase reporter assay
  The POU3F3 promoter region was amplified and inserted into the GL002 luciferase reporter vector (Promega, USA). Mutant constructs were generated through site-directed mutagenesis. Both wild-type and mutant constructs were transfected into vector-only and POU3F3-overexpressing cells. After 48 h, firefly luciferase activity was measured and normalized to Renilla activity.


### ROS detection
  Hep3B and JHH7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with indication. To measure ROS levels, these HCC cells were incubated in fresh DMEM for 6 h, followed by staining with 10 µM 2′,7′- DCFH-DA probe (Solarbio Inc., Beijing, China). ROS levels were analyzed by flow cytometry, and the data were processed using FlowJo software (version 7.4.1).


### Metabolite detection
  The MDA concentration in Hep3B and JHH7 cells was determined using a cell MDA assay kit (Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) based on the thiobarbituric acid method. Iron levels were measured with an Iron Assay Kit (Abcam, USA), and RA levels were assessed using a Human Retinoic Acid ELISA Kit (CUSABIO Inc., Shanghai, China). Relative metabolite levels were normalized to protein concentration.


### CCK-8 assay
  Hep3B and JHH7 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 10³ cells per well and treated with indication. At 48 h, 10 µL of Cell Counting Kit-8 (Yeasen Inc., Shanghai, China) solution was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The optical density at 450 nm was then measured using a microplate reader (Bio-rad, USA).


### EDU assay
  The EdU incorporation assay was conducted using an EdU assay kit (Beyotime Inc., Nanjing, China) following standard protocols. Hep3B and JHH7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 1 × 10⁵ cells/well overnight to adhere, then incubated with 10 µM EdU buff and treated with indication for 2 h at 37 °C. EdU-positive cells were visualized in a fluorescence microscope (Olympus) after treatment with Click buff, and their percentage was calculated.


### Colony formation assay
  Hep3B and JHH7 cells were transfected with lentivirus for 48 h, then digested and resuspended. The cells were seeded into 6-well plates at 600 cells per well and cultured for 2 weeks with the indicated treatment. Cell colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.5% crystal violet, and photographed.


### Xenograft tumor model
  For the subcutaneous tumor xenograft model, 2 × 10⁶ Hep3B cells in PBS were subcutaneously injected into the left axilla of 6- to 8-week-old male BALB/c nude mice. Tumor volumes were measured with vernier calipers and calculated using the formula: tumor volume (mm³) = length × width2/2. When tumors reached 40–50 mm³ (day 9), mice were randomized into treatment groups (n = 5 per group). On day 24, mice were euthanized, and tumor tissues were collected for IHC analysis.


### Computer-aided virtual screening
  The 3D protein structure of POU3F3 was predicted on the AlphaFold3 website (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/). The POU3F3 structure was imported into Maestro software (version 2021.02) for preprocessing and minimization using the ‘Protein Preparation Wizard’ module. Meanwhile, 6688 small-molecule natural products were obtained from the COCONUT database (https://coconut.naturalproducts.net/), and 3560 FDA-approved small-molecule compounds were sourced from the MCE website (https://www.medchemexpress.cn/). Before virtual screening, the molecular structures were optimized using Maestro’s ‘LigPrep’ module. The POU3F3 protein binding pocket was identified via the ‘Receptor Grid Generation’ module, with a pocket size of 20 Å set for docking. Docking was performed in extra precision mode using the ‘Ligand Docking’ module, and the results were visualized with PyMOL (version 3.0).


### Molecular dynamics simulation
  In PyMOL 2.6, the docked POU3F3 protein and Rosarin small molecule were separated and subjected to hydrogenation. GROMACS 2020.3 was chosen as the molecular dynamics simulation software, with the CHARMM force field used to describe the interactions between the protein and the solvent. The TIP3P water model was used for the solvation system, a solution box was created, and appropriate amounts of Na+ or Cl− ions were added to neutralize the system’s charge. The entire system was then subjected to energy minimization to eliminate unfavorable geometric configurations introduced during the solvation process. The volume was fixed, and the temperature was gradually increased to reach thermal equilibrium. In addition, the pressure and temperature were fixed to achieve pressure equilibrium and adjust the density of the solution box. Production simulations were then run, starting long-term production MD simulations to generate trajectory files and record the conformational changes of the protein throughout the simulation process.


### Surface plasmon resonance (SPR)
  Purified POU3F3 proteins were obtained from Sino Biological Inc. and desalted using the AKTA protein purification system (Cytiva). The proteins were dissolved in a buffer containing 200 mM HEPES, 2 mM NaCl, and 0.5% DMSO. Conjugation with sodium acetate solution (pH 4.0) was performed on a Biacore T200 system (Cytiva). After conjugation with a human metal-organic framework, uncoupled proteins were blocked with 1.0 M ethanolamine solution (Shanghai Ruji Biological Technology Co., Ltd.) at 28 °C for 6 h. The chip was then immersed in buffer for 10 h. Rosarin was dissolved in a buffer containing 10 mmol/L HEPES, 150 mmol/L NaCl, and 3 mmol/L EDTA, which served as the mobile phase. Binding constants between proteins and erianin were measured using a multi-cycle model. The mobile phase flow rate, binding time, and dissociation time were set at 30 µL/sec, 240 s, and 300 s, respectively.


### Statistical analysis
  Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). All experiments were performed at least three times, and data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical significance between groups was analyzed using the two-tailed Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA combined with Bonferroni post-test, as appropriate. Survival curves were generated using Kaplan–Meier analysis and compared with the log-rank test. P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.


## Results

### In vivo CRISPR screens indicated that RA metabolism gene cluster contributed to the resistance to ferroptosis agonist
  To identify genes associated with sensitivity to ferroptosis agonists such as RSL3 and sorafenib, an in vivo CRISPR screen strategy was employed (Fig. 1A). After sgRNA sequencing of tumor tissues and DrugZ analysis, a total of 1255 and 1505 genes were identified as inducing resistance to sorafenib and RSL3 in JHH7 cells, respectively (Fig. 1B). Similarly, a total of 1201 and 1053 genes were found to confer resistance to sorafenib and RSL3 in Hep3B cells, respectively (Fig. 1B). Notably, 15 resisters overlapped across the four conditions (Fig. 1C). Pathway enrichment analysis using the KEGG database (Fig. 1D) and Reactome database (Fig. 1E) revealed that these 15 shared resisters were significantly enriched in the RA metabolism signaling pathway. Enriched genes in this pathway included ADH4, ALDH1A1, ALDH1A3, RBP1, FABP5, and RDH10, and their resistance effects across the four conditions were ranked by Z scores (Fig. 1F). Further analysis indicated that the primary function of the RA metabolism gene cluster was the induction of RA production (Fig. 1G). Additionally, sgRNA count analysis in Hep3B and JHH7 samples showed minimal changes in the DMSO-treated group compared to the day 0 group (Fig. 1H). Collectively, these findings suggest that the RA metabolism gene cluster has a limited effect on HCC cell proliferation but significantly contributes to resistance against ferroptosis agonists, including sorafenib and RSL3.

  To confirm the findings from the in vivo CRISPR screens, functional assays were conducted. IC50 analysis in HCC cells identified PLC/PRF/5 and Huh7 as sensitive to RSL3 (Fig. 2A) and sorafenib (Fig. 2B), while JHH7, SNU387, Hep3B, and Li-7 were classified as resistant (Fig. 2A-B). Baseline RA measurements showed significantly higher levels of RA in resistant cells compared to sensitive ones (Fig. 2C). Exogenous RA addition markedly reduced the inhibitory effects of RSL3 and sorafenib in Hep3B and JHH7 cells (Fig. 2D). Additionally, sorafenib treatment led to elevated mRNA (Fig. 2E) and protein levels (Fig. 2F) of RA metabolism-related genes in Hep3B and JHH7 cells. CCK-8 assays further demonstrated that overexpression of these RA metabolism genes (ADH4, ALDH1A1, ALDH1A3, RBP1, FABP5, and RDH10) significantly mitigated the inhibitory effects of sorafenib in HCC cells (Fig. 2G). Given that RA is a potent reducing agent that decreases intracellular ROS levels, we propose that the RA metabolism gene cluster enhances RA production, thereby lowering ROS and inhibiting ferroptosis induced by ferroptosis agonists.

### POU3F3 simultaneously regulated the transcript of RA metabolism-resisters
  Since multiple metabolic enzymes involved in RA metabolism contribute to sorafenib resistance in HCC cells, targeting or knocking out a single enzyme is not an effective strategy. Based on the evidence that mRNA levels of RA metabolism-related genes (ADH4, ALDH1A1, ALDH1A3, RBP1, FABP5, and RDH10) were consistently elevated in HCC cells treated with sorafenib, we hypothesized the presence of transcription factors (TFs) capable of simultaneously regulating the transcript of these RA metabolism-related resisters and acting as an even stronger mediator of resistance to ferroptosis agonists.

  To test our hypothesis, we conducted transcription factor enrichment analysis using the JASPAR-SVM algorithm. This analysis identified three TFs—CREB1, FOXA1, and POU3F3—that were predicted to simultaneously and directly regulate the transcription of all RA metabolism-related resisters (Fig. 3A). Notably, among these, only POU3F3 was found to be upregulated in HCC cells treated with sorafenib, while CREB1 and FOXA1 showed no significant changes (Fig. 3B). Upregulated POU3F3 protein was predominantly localized in the cell nucleus (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, we found that POU3F3 was a stronger resister for RSL3 and sorafenib, while the Z-score plots showed it ranked higher than any single RA metabolism-resisters (Fig. 3D-E). However, the CREB1 and FOXA1 did not act as resisters for RSL3 and sorafenib (Fig. 3D-E). Therefore, we focused on POU3F3 for further investigation.

  After obtaining the motif of POU3F3 from JASPAR (http://jaspar.genereg.net/), the top three binding sites of POU3F3 in the promoters of ADH4, ALDH1A1, ALDH1A3, FABP5, RBP1, and RDH10 were predicted (Fig. 3F). Subsequently, ChIP-PCR was performed to analyze the interaction between POU3F3 and these gene promoters. The results revealed that POU3F3 significantly binds to the following regions: -1575 to -1563 on the ADH4 promoter, -1455 to -1443 on the ALDH1A1 promoter, -861 to -849 and − 854 to -842 on the ALDH1A3 promoter, -1639 to -1627 and − 1624 to -1612 on the FABP5 promoter, -788 to -776 on the RBP1 promoter, and − 1796 to -1784 on the RDH10 promoter (Fig. 3G). Consistent with these findings, luciferase reporter assays demonstrated that mutations in these binding site sequences significantly reduced luciferase activity in Hep3B cells induced by POU3F3 overexpression (Fig. 3H). Furthermore, knockdown of POU3F3 consistently decreased the expression of ADH4, ALDH1A1, ALDH1A3, FABP5, RBP1, and RDH10 protein in Hep3B and JHH7 cells, while POU3F3 overexpression resulted in increased expression of these proteins (Fig. 3I). Collectively, these findings identified POU3F3 as a novel TF for RA metabolism, directly regulating the transcription of multiple RA metabolism-related genes.


### Knockdown of POU3F3 increased the pro-ferroptosis and proliferation inhibitory effects of sorafenib in vitro
  Since POU3F3 significantly regulates the transcription of RA metabolism-related resisters, we next investigated its impact on the effects of sorafenib. Our results showed that sorafenib treatment in NC cells led to a compensatory increase in RA levels, whereas knockdown of POU3F3 alleviated this compensatory effect (Fig. 4A). Similarly, knockdown of POU3F3 significantly elevated ROS levels in HepG2 and JHH7 cells upon sorafenib treatment (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, compared to NC cells, POU3F3 knockdown cells treated with sorafenib exhibited higher levels of MDA (Fig. 4C) and iron (Fig. 4D). However, the exogenous addition of RA in POU3F3 knockdown cells significantly reversed these sorafenib-induced effects (Fig. 4D). Collectively, these findings indicated that POU3F3 knockdown reduces the compensatory increase in RA and enhances the pro-ferroptosis effects of sorafenib in HCC cells.

  Using CCK-8 assays, we found that knockdown of POU3F3 significantly reduced the IC50 of sorafenib in Hep3B and JHH7 cells (Fig. 4E). Similarly, EDU assays revealed that, compared to NC cells, Hep3B and JHH7 cells with POU3F3 knockdown exhibited a lower EDU-positive rate following sorafenib treatment (Fig. 4F). However, the exogenous addition of RA in these cells significantly reversed the effects of sorafenib (Fig. 4F). Furthermore, colony formation assays demonstrated that sorafenib exerted stronger inhibitory effects on Hep3B and JHH7 cells with POU3F3 knockdown, while RA treatment reversed these enhanced effects (Fig. 4G-H). In conclusion, our findings indicated that knockdown of POU3F3 enhanced the pro-ferroptosis and proliferation-inhibitory effects of sorafenib in vitro.


### Knockdown of POU3F3 increased the proliferation inhibitory effects of sorafenib in vivo by suppressing RA metabolism
  We then evaluated the effects of POU3F3 in vivo. NC cells and Hep3B cells with POU3F3 knockdown were subcutaneously injected into nude mice, which were subsequently treated with 40 mg/kg sorafenib (Fig. 5A). The results showed that Hep3B cells with POU3F3 knockdown exhibited a lower proliferation rate under sorafenib treatment compared to NC cells under same condition (Fig. 5B-C). Similarly, tumor tissues derived from Hep3B cells with POU3F3 knockdown showed lower weights after sorafenib treatment compared to those derived from NC cells (Fig. 5D). Additionally, IHC analysis was performed on the tumor tissues to detect the expression of RA metabolism-related genes, KI67, and PCNA (Fig. 5E). Tumor tissues derived from Hep3B cells with POU3F3 knockdown exhibited lower expression levels of RA metabolism-related genes, KI67, and PCNA following sorafenib treatment compared to those derived from NC cells (Fig. 5F). Furthermore, tumor tissues from POU3F3 knockdown cells showed lower RA levels (Fig. 5G), higher MDA levels (Fig. 5H), and higher iron levels (Fig. 5I) after sorafenib treatment compared to those from NC cells.

  Additionally, we examined the expression of POU3F3 in adjacent non-tumor tissues and HCC tissues. The results showed that POU3F3 was negatively expressed in non-tumor tissues, while 68.75% of HCC tissues exhibited positive POU3F3 expression (Fig. 5J). HCC patients with positive POU3F3 expression had shorter overall survival (Fig. 5K). Furthermore, higher RA levels were observed in HCC tissues with positive POU3F3 expression, compared to those with negative POU3F3 expression (Fig. 5L).


### Rosarin was identified as a POU3F3 inhibitor
  Given that POU3F3 acts as a protective regulator against sorafenib-induced ferroptosis in HCC, we next analyzed whether targeting POU3F3 could enhance sorafenib sensitivity. To this end, we predicted the 3D structure of the POU3F3 protein using the AlphaFold3 platform (Fig. 6A) and conducted computer-aided virtual screening with 3,560 FDA-approved drugs and 6,688 natural products to identify potential inhibitors of POU3F3 (Fig. 6B). Among the FDA-approved drugs, the top five candidates with the highest binding scores to POU3F3 were cangrelor (docking score = -7.890), lactulose (docking score = -7.489), ibandronate (docking score = -7.468), eovist (docking score = -6.811), and risedronate (docking score = -6.634) (Fig. 6C). Similarly, the top five natural products with the highest binding scores were manninotriose (docking score = -10.540), maltotriose (docking score = -10.483), S-(4-Hydroxybenzyl)glutathione (docking score = -10.448), voglibose (docking score = -10.350), and rosarin (docking score = -10.133) (Fig. 6D). We then conducted high-throughput screening using the CCK-8 method to analyze the synergistic effects of these drugs in combination with sorafenib (Fig. 6E). Among them, we observed that rosarin alone exhibited minimal inhibitory effects on HCC cell proliferation but significantly enhanced the inhibitory effects of sorafenib on HCC cells (Fig. 6F). Based on these findings, we focused on rosarin to further investigate its interaction with the POU3F3 protein and its role in RA metabolism.

  Visualization of the interaction between rosarin and the POU3F3 protein revealed that multiple hydroxyl groups of rosarin form hydrogen bonds with the amino acid residues 390SER, 396SER, and 403GLN of the POU3F3 protein, as shown in the 3D and 2D binding models (Fig. 6G). Similarly, molecular dynamics simulations conducted over 100 ns demonstrated that the binding between rosarin and the POU3F3 protein remained in a stable equilibrium state throughout the simulation period (Fig. 6H). Furthermore, using the SPR method, we determined that the equilibrium dissociation constant (Kd) of rosarin for the POU3F3 protein was 7.57 µM (Fig. 6I). These findings suggested that rosarin may serve as a potential inhibitor of POU3F3.

  We then treated Hep3B and JHH7 cells with rosarin (10 µM). Western blotting results showed that rosarin treatment led to decreased POU3F3 protein expression in both Hep3B and JHH7 cells (Fig. 6J). Similarly, rosarin-treated Hep3B and JHH7 cells exhibited reduced RA expression levels (Fig. 6K). Furthermore, rosarin demonstrated the potential to enhance the pro-ferroptosis effects of sorafenib in Hep3B and JHH7 cells by synergistically increasing MDA levels (Fig. 6L) and iron levels (Fig. 6M). Collectively, these findings identify rosarin as a POU3F3 inhibitor that can synergize with sorafenib to induce ferroptosis in HCC cells.


### Rosarin exhibited synergistic effects with sorafenib for HCC cells in vitro and in vivo
  We then treated Hep3B and JHH7 cells with varying concentrations of rosarin and sorafenib (Fig. 7A). Using the CCK-8 assay (Fig. 7B), we observed that 2.0/4.0 µM rosarin demonstrated moderate-to-high synergistic effects with 2.0/4.0 µM sorafenib (Fig. 7C). Notably, the combination of 2 µM rosarin and 2 µM sorafenib showed the strongest synergistic effect (Fig. 7C). Based on this, the 2 µM concentration pair was selected for further analysis using EDU and colony formation assays. The results showed that the combination of 2 µM rosarin and 2 µM sorafenib synergistically reduced the EDU-positive rate of HCC cells (Fig. 7D) and significantly suppressed colony formation in Hep3B and JHH7 cells (Fig. 7E). These findings indicate that rosarin exhibits synergistic effects with sorafenib in inhibiting HCC cells in vitro.

  We treated nude mice harboring Hep3B cell-derived tumors with sorafenib, rosarin, and their combination (Fig. 7F). The results showed that, compared to the groups treated with sorafenib or rosarin alone, the combination treatment significantly slowed tumor growth (Fig. 7G-H). Notably, tumors in the combination treatment group exhibited the lowest weight (Fig. 7I). Additionally, rosarin treatment was found to attenuate the sorafenib-induced increase in the expression of POU3F3 target genes, including ALDH1A1 and ALDH1A3 (Fig. 7J). The protein expression levels of KI67 and PCNA were significantly reduced in tumor tissues from both the rosarin and sorafenib single-treatment groups, with an even greater reduction observed in the combination group (Fig. 7K). Moreover, rosarin effectively mitigated the compensatory increase in RA levels induced by sorafenib in tumor tissues (Fig. 7L). Tumor tissues in the combination treatment group also exhibited higher iron levels compared to the single-drug treatment groups (Fig. 7M). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that rosarin exerts synergistic effects with sorafenib in inhibiting HCC tumors in vivo.


## Discussion
  The emergence of therapy resistance poses a significant clinical challenge across multiple therapeutic strategies, including modern molecularly targeted treatments. Sorafenib, a widely used treatment for advanced HCC, functions by disrupting intracellular protein kinase signaling pathways and other factors in cancer cells [19, 20]. Additionally, it has been also demonstrated that sorafenib inhibits the cystine-glutamate antiporter SLC7A11, a key regulator that opposes ferroptosis [21]. However, HCC cells employ multiple ferroptosis defense mechanisms that significantly diminish the efficacy of sorafenib. Therefore, identifying these defense mechanisms in HCC cells is crucial for improving therapeutic strategies against HCC.

  High-throughput screening is a widely used method in cancer research, providing the efficient identification of the genes associated with phenotypes such as drug resistance [22]. In the past, researchers often utilized shRNA libraries to downregulate specific target genes, thereby uncovering functionally relevant targets. For instance, a shRNA library screening approach has been deployed and identified MAPK14 as a key gene involved in the sorafenib resistance and it is also beneficial in screening of the regulators of sorafenib resistance [23, 24]. However, RNAi-based library screening, represented by shRNA libraries, has several limitations. RNAi only partially suppresses gene expression and cannot completely eliminate it, with unstable silencing efficiency. As a result, it may obscure the observation of loss-of-function phenotypes, leading to false-negative results. Furthermore, RNAi often demonstrates off-target effects, inadvertently interfering with the expression of other genes, which can result in false-positive findings. More information about RNAi can be found in these two studies [25, 26].

  The CRISPR/Cas9 technology can specifically cut, replace, and add target DNA, blocking the expression of target genes at the DNA level. Compared to the traditional RNAi technology, CRISPR/Cas9 provides advantages such as high efficiency, stable silencing efficiency, and reduced off-target effects [27, 28]. Herein, we performed reliable in vivo CRISPR screens for ferroptosis agonists, including RSL3 and sorafenib, in HCC cells. We provided the first evidence that RA metabolism contributed to the resistance of ferroptosis agonist, and various RA metabolism-associated proteins can increase the production of RA, thus inducing the resistance of ferroptosis agonist. Our evidence provided new insights for overcoming sorafenib resistance.

  Since multiple RA-metabolizing enzymes are associated with sorafenib resistance, targeting any single enzyme may not be effective. To address this, we explored a cluster regulation approach. We found that POU3F3, a member of DNA-binding POU domain-containing proteins, was up-regulated in sorafenib-treated HCC cells, and simultaneously transcribed these RA-metabolizing enzymes.

  DNA-binding POU domain-containing proteins represent a class of TFs related to key embryonic stem cell master regulators, such as OCT4 [29]. Dysregulation of POU domain-containing proteins has been observed in various cancer tissues and is closely associated with cell proliferation, migration, and drug resistance. For instance, elevated levels of POU2F1 have been detected in colon cancer tissues, contributing to chemoresistance through the transcriptional activation of glycolytic metabolism [30]. Similarly, POU5F1B expression is upregulated in cervical cancer, and its knockdown significantly reduces cell proliferation and migration [31]. BRN2 (encoded by the gene POU3F2) has been shown to enhance anoikis resistance and confer resistance to mutated BRAF inhibitors [32]. POU3F3, another member of the DNA-binding POU domain-containing protein family, is primarily expressed in brain tissues, where it plays a critical role in neuronal development [33]. However, the role of POU3F3 in HCC remains largely unexplored. In the current study, we provided the first evidence that knockdown of POU3F3 significantly increased the sensitivity of HCC cells to sorafenib via reducing RA production in vitro and in vivo. The evidence we provided indicated that POU3F3 was a novel therapeutic target for HCC, and targeting POU3F3 can significantly enhance the sensitivity of HCC cells to sorafenib.

  In the past, most transcription factors were considered undruggable proteins due to the lack of structurally stable small-molecule binding pockets, allosteric regulation sites, and unclear regulatory mechanisms [34, 35]. However, with advancements in modern drug development technologies, inhibitors targeting various transcription factors have been identified and approved by the FDA for the treatment of multiple cancers, including HCC. For instance, the STAT6 inhibitor TTI-101 has been approved by the FDA for the treatment of relapsed/refractory locally advanced, unresectable, or metastatic HCC [36]. Similarly, a phase II clinical study demonstrated that the β-catenin inhibitor tetrandrine exhibited sufficient antitumor efficacy and safety in patients with relapsed acute myelogenous leukemia [37]. Given that POU3F3 is a critical target for enhancing sorafenib sensitivity in HCC, we investigated its potential inhibitors. Our findings provided evidence that POU3F3 possesses sufficiently large active pockets suitable for molecular docking, and the natural compound rosarin shows a favorable equilibrium dissociation constant with POU3F3. Rosarin, a cinnamyl alcohol glycoside isolated from Rhodiola rosea, has been shown in previous studies to exert neuroprotective effects by inhibiting the production of TNF-α and other related factors [38]. Herein, we provided evidence that rosarin can act as an anti-HCC drugs, while it can target POU3F3 and synergize with sorafenib to enhance its ferroptosis-promoting and tumor-suppressing effects.


## Conclusion
  Sorafenib-treated HCC cells upregulated multiple RA-metabolizing proteins, driving the synthesis of RA to counteract sorafenib-induced ferroptosis. POU3F3 protected HCC cells from ferroptosis induced by sorafenib by enhancing the transcription of RA metabolism genes and promoting RA production. The POU3F3 inhibitor rosarin shows strong potential as a candidate to overcome sorafenib resistance in HCC.
