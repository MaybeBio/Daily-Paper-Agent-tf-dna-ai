# FRMD8 inhibits tumor metastasis in BRCA1-associated TNBC by negatively regulating tmTNF-α


## Abstract
  Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), particularly in patients with metastasis, is associated with limited treatment options and shorter survival times. In this study, through library screening and animal experiments, we identified that the low expression of FERM domain-containing protein 8 (FRMD8) in breast cancer type 1 susceptibility protein (BRCA1)-mutant breast cancer cells (TNBC subtype), significantly enhances the metastatic potential of tumor cells to various organs. Further functional experiments revealed that FRMD8low inhibited the cleavage of tmTNF-α (transmembrane TNF-α) and promoted the expression of surface tmTNF-α. We also identified that the mechanism by which FRMD8 regulates tmTNF-α is related to the inhibition of inactive rhomboid protein 2 (iRHOM2) degradation, which acts mainly through the endocytic pathway. Furthermore, FRMD8low/iRHOM2low greatly facilitated the in vivo metastasis of TNBC. Finally, we found that combined treatment with paclitaxel and etanercept reversed the expression of FRMD8 and iRHOM2, concomitantly inhibiting the metastatic potential in vivo. This study explores how FRMD8 influences TNF-α processing and the metastatic behavior of breast cancer, providing insights into molecular dynamics that could guide future therapeutic strategies to improve outcomes in patients with breast cancer.

## Introduction
  TNBC (triple-negative breast cancer) remains one of the most aggressive subtypes of breast cancer, accounting for approximately 15–20% of all breast cancer cases globally. It is characterized by the absence of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) expression, which makes it particularly difficult to treat [1]. TNBC is highly metastatic, with a propensity for early spread to distant organs, especially the lungs, liver, and brain. The prognosis for patients with TNBC is often poor due to the high metastatic potential, and survival rates significantly drop as metastasis progresses [2]. While patients with early-stage TNBC may have relatively favorable outcomes, the survival rate decreases drastically for those diagnosed with metastatic disease, with a 5-year survival rate dropping to less than 20% in patients with stage IV disease [3]. Therefore, understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying TNBC metastasis is crucial for developing targeted therapies to improve patient survival and outcomes.

  Tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), a member of the TNF superfamily, primarily exists in two forms: transmembrane TNF-α (tmTNF-α) and soluble TNF-α (sTNF-α) [4]. TmTNF-α, a transmembrane protein, can initiate the classical NF-κB pathway via tumor necrosis factor receptor 1 (TNFR1) and the non-classical pathway via TNFR2 [5]. Through these pathways, tmTNF-α influences the expression of genes related to cell adhesion and migration, such as intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1) and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), while also modulating immune evasion within the tumor microenvironment and impacting extracellular matrix remodeling [6]. In contrast, sTNF-α, resulting from the cleavage of tmTNF-α, generally binds solely to TNFR1, activating the classical NF-κB (nuclear factor kappa B) pathway. While extensive studies have examined sTNF-α’s relationship with cancer, tmTNF-α remains less explored [7, 8]. In breast cancer, it is crucial to determine which genes can modulate TNF-α cleavage and potentially enhance metastatic processes.

  FERM domain-containing protein 8 (FRMD8 ), a protein belonging to the FERM domain family, has shown a pivotal role in regulating tmTNF-α activity [9]. By anchoring to the intracellular N-terminus of inactive rhomboid protein 2 (iRHOM2 ), FRMD8 forms a ternary complex with ADAM metallopeptidase domain 17 (ADAM17), enhancing the latter’s stability and sheddase activity, thereby facilitating the efficient release of inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α [10]. The absence of FRMD8 leads to the degradation of both iRhom2 and mature ADAM17 via the lysosomal pathway, significantly reducing ADAM17’s activity [11]. Also known as TNF-α converting enzyme (TACE), ADAM17 enzymatically releases mature cytokines into the extracellular space by cleaving their membrane-bound precursors [12]. However, the specific mechanisms through which FRMD8 may foster breast cancer metastasis via TNF-α release still warrant further investigation.

  In this study, using clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats–CRISPR-associated protein 9 (CRISPR-Cas9) library screening and animal model analyses, we identified that knockdown of FRMD8 significantly promotes the in vivo metastatic capacity of TNBC cell lines. Following FRMD8 knockdown, tumor cells of various breast cancer subtypes exhibited a marked increase in cell surface tmTNF-α expression. Mechanistically, FRMD8 protects iRHOM2 from recognition by intracellular endocytic signals, thereby regulating the cleavage of the ADAM17 enzyme. Consequently, FRMD8low/iRHOM2low expression not only suppresses ADAM17 cleavage but also substantially enhances the in vivo metastatic potential of TNBC cell lines. Notably, this FRMD8low/iRHOM2low-associated signaling can be blocked by paclitaxel and etanercept. These findings may facilitate the development of a targeted therapeutic strategy to prevent metastasis in patients with breast cancer.

The study procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of University of Science and Technology of China (USTC) (Hefei, China), approval number: 2024-103(YS), approval date: 1 August 2024. All the procedures were performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

  Tumor samples from seven patients with metastatic breast cancer and seven patients with non-metastatic breast cancer were collected from Ningbo Beilun People’s Hospital.


### Cell lines
  The cell lines used in this study include 759, 642, MCF-7, BT-549, MDA-MB-231, and MDA-MB-436. The 759 and 642 cell lines were extracted from the primary tumor of Brca1co/co; Tp53+/co; MMTV-Cre mice, which has minimal invasive metastatic ability. The 759 cell line lacks metastatic capacity in vivo, while the 642 cell line displays pronounced metastatic potential. The MCF-7, BT-549, MDA-MB-231, and MDA-MB-436 cell lines were preserved in our laboratory, and purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). All cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere.


### RNA-seq sample preparation
  The RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) samples in this study consist of two parts: 14 TNBC samples, 7 cases with metastasis and 7 cases without metastasis, provided by Ningbo Beilun People’s Hospital. Three breast cancer samples with low FRMD8 expression and three breast cancer samples with high FRMD8 expression, were obtained from the First Affiliated Hospital of the University of Science and Technology of China (USTC). The preliminary screening of metastasis-related genes was conducted using a combination of our hospital’s dataset sequencing and public dataset analysis.


### Hospital dataset analysis procedure
  The RNA sequencing sample analysis workflow in our institution is as follows: Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples, consisting of three with high FRMD8 expression and three with low FRMD8 expression (among a total of ten samples), were evaluated by senior pathologists. Tumor cells were scraped for RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) to measure mRNA expression levels.After obtaining the FASTQ files from RNA-seq, the analysis process followed the tutorial at https://github.com/griffithlab/rnaseq_tutorial. The FASTQ files were first analyzed for sequencing quality using FastQC software, and adapters were removed using flexbar software. The FASTQ files were then aligned to the reference genome hg38 using hierarchical indexing for spliced alignment of transcripts (HISAT2). The sequence alignment map (SAM) files generated by HISAT2 were converted to binary alignment map (BAM) files using the samtools sort command.Gene coverage in the BAM files was observed using Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) software, and the alignment efficiency was checked using samtools-flagstat and FastQC. The stringtie software was used to generate transcripts.gtf and gene_abundances.tsv files, and the htseq-count command was used to generate the gene.tsv file (gene expression file).After obtaining the gene expression files, edgeR software was used to normalize the gene expression data and compare the differentially expressed genes between five patients with TNBC and metastasis and five without metastasis. Genes with an absolute log2 fold change (logFC) value greater than 6 and a p-value less than 0.05 were considered significant, resulting in 895 downregulated genes. The sequencing process for three breast cancer samples with low FRMD8 expression and three with high FRMD8 expression followed the same procedure as the FFPE samples.


### Public dataset analysis procedure
  (1) The public dataset selected for metastasis in this study including GSE5327, GSE1456, GSE4922, GSE5301, GSE69031, GSE86166, GSE103091, GSE25055, GSE25065, GSE6532, GSE9195, GSE26304, GSE48390, GSE71258, and TCGA, which were downloaded using the GEOquery package. Datasets and the corresponding platform information GPL96 were downloaded using the getGEO function. (2) The expression matrix was obtained using the exprs function on the Genomic Spatial Event (GSE) database, and gene names were annotated using the GPL96 platform. Duplicate gene names were removed from the GSE data and the maximum expression value for each gene was retained. (3) The GSE expression data was normalized using the normalize.quantiles function from the preprocessCore package. Patients’ survival time and outcomes were extracted using the pData function. The average expression of all genes in the GSE data was calculated, and samples with expression higher than the average were set as the high-expression group, while those below the average were set as the low-expression group. (4) The survfit function was used to construct a prognosis model, and the surv_pvalue function was used to calculate the p-value of the model, obtaining genes with p-values less than 0.05. In this study, 718 survival-related genes were identified. (5) After obtaining the genes from our hospital dataset analysis and the public dataset analysis, the two gene lists were intersected, resulting in 39 genes that were lowly expressed in primary tumors with metastasis and associated with lung metastasis survival. These 39 genes were designated as the library genes. (6) For the meta-analysis, we used the coxph function to calculate the hazard ratio (HR) of FRMD8 in different datasets, and used ggplot2 package to draw the forest plot.


### Single-cell dataset and spatial transcriptomics analysis
  Data download and preprocessing: The GSE161529, GSE180286, and GSE176078 (single-cell sequencing dataset) and GSE210616 (spatial transcriptomics dataset) were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) website (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) and imported using Seurat.Quality control and normalization: Low-quality cells (e.g., those with few detected genes or high mitochondrial gene expression) were removed. The data were then normalized using the SCTransform method, batch effects were corrected using Harmony, and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was employed for data visualization and dimensionality reduction. Cell clustering was performed using the FindClusters function in Seurat.Cell type annotation: The SingleR package was used to annotate cell types for each cluster.Cell–cell communication analysis: After annotating the cell types, ligand–receptor interactions were analyzed using the CellChat package to identify potential intercellular communication.Spatial transcriptomics analysis: For the spatial transcriptomics data, SpatialDE was used to identify differentially expressed genes across spatial regions, and clusterProfiler was employed for pathway enrichment analysis.


### Single-guide RNA (sgRNA) library screening process
  After identifying 41 library genes, a knockout library was designed for these genes and library screening was conducted. The library screening process is as follows: The 41 library genes were cross-referenced with mouse genes, resulting in 39 genes in mice. Six sgRNAs were designed for each gene, with the sgRNA sequences provided in the appendix.The sgRNA sequences were synthesized by Nanjing Cobioer Biotechnology. After synthesis, the sgRNAs were packaged into lentiviruses using second-generation lentivirus packaging technology. Briefly, 12 µg of target plasmid was mixed with 9 µg of pSPAX2 and 3 µg of pMD2.G and transfected into 293 T cell lines using Lipofectamine 2000. After 48 h of transfection, the viral supernatant was collected to obtain the library virus.The titer of the library virus was measured using a single-clone method, and 759 cell lines were infected with the virus at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1. After 48 h of infection, the cells were selected with 2 µg/mL puromycin for 7 d to obtain the library cells.The library cells were injected into the mammary glands of ten nude mice, with 1 × 106 cells injected per mouse. After 8 weeks, the mice were sacrificed, and the primary tumors, lungs, spleens, livers, kidneys, and brains were collected.DNA was extracted from these organs and amplified using universal sgRNA primers. In addition, the sgRNAs corresponding to the 39 library genes were used as forward primers (sgRNA-F1) and library-R1 as reverse primers for PCR to determine which sgRNAs were present in the lungs, spleens, livers, kidneys, and brains.


### Mouse experiments
  The mice used in this study were mainly nude mice, all purchased from Zhejiang Ziyuan Company. The mice were female, 6–8 weeks old, and weighed around 20 g. The injection method was mammary injection, with 1 × 106 cells injected per mouse. Metastasis in different organs was quantified on the basis of fluorescence intensity, which was measured using the IVIS Spectrum system. Fluorescence intensity was analyzed with Living Image software by defining regions of interest (ROIs) and calculating radiance (photons/s/cm2/sr) corresponding to green fluorescence protein (GFP) expression levels.

  The drug treatment experimental procedures are as follows:Paclitaxel was purchased from Selleckchem, USA (Cat. no. S1150), and etanercept was purchased from Pfizer Pharmaceuticals Limited (New York, NY, USA).759 Frmd8 sgRNA1 + iRhom2 shRNA cell lines were injected into the nude mice at a dose of 1 × 106 cells per mouse. Two weeks after cell injection, the mice were divided into four groups: Control (CON) group, Paclitaxel group, Etanercept group, and Paclitaxel + Etanercept group. Each group received intraperitoneal injections of 100 µL dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), 5 mg/kg paclitaxel, 2 mg/kg etanercept, or 5 mg/kg paclitaxel + 2 mg/kg etanercept, respectively, with an injection frequency of once every 2 weeks.Eight weeks after cell injection, the mice were sacrificed to examine the volume of primary tumors and metastasis in various organs in each group.

### Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and H&E (hematoxylin and eosin) staining
  The IHC and H&E experiments were performed by Xinle Biotechnology in Hefei. The general steps for IHC are as follows:Mouse or human tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight and washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The tissues were then dehydrated using a graded ethanol series (70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100%).Tissues were cleared with xylene and embedded in paraffin. Once the paraffin solidified, the tissue blocks were cut into 3–5 μm thick sections and mounted on slides.Paraffin sections were deparaffinized with xylene, rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and rinsed with distilled water. Antigen retrieval was performed by high-temperature and high-pressure treatment.The tissue sections were blocked to reduce nonspecific antibody binding. After incubation with specific primary antibodies, the sections were washed with Tris-buffered saline + Tween 20 (TBST) buffer, followed by the addition of secondary antibodies corresponding to the primary antibody’s host species. 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining was used to observe the protein expression intensity of the target genes.Quantification of IHC staining intensity: DAB staining intensity was quantified by extracting the DAB channel via color deconvolution (HED model), and calculating the total integrated optical density (IOD) across the entire image without background subtraction. Higher absolute IOD values indicated stronger target protein expression. Image analysis was performed using Python (scikit-image).

  The general steps for H&E staining are as follows:Tissue fixation and sectioning steps were the same as those for IHC. After obtaining slides with tissue sections, the slides were immersed in hematoxylin stain and rinsed under running water.Slides were immersed in acidic ethanol solution, rinsed with running water, then immersed in bluing solution, and rinsed again with running water.

  The slides were immersed in eosin stain, rinsed under running water, and passed through an increasing concentration gradient of ethanol solutions.(3)The tissue sections were cleared with xylene to remove ethanol, and finally, coverslips were applied to the slides for observation under a microscope.(4)After completing the IHC and H&E staining, the slides were scanned using the Pannoramic MIDI machine from 3DHISTECH and stored using CaseViewer scanning software.


### Immunofluorescence
  Immunofluorescence staining includes both tissue and cell immunofluorescence staining. The preliminary staining steps were performed by Xinle Biotechnology in Hefei. Tissue immunofluorescence pretreatment and primary antibody labeling steps followed the IHC protocol. After primary antibody labeling, tissue sections were labeled with fluorescently tagged secondary antibodies and scanned using the same scanning software as in the IHC experiments.Place cell coverslips in a 6-well plate and add 1 × 106 cells per well. After the cells adhere, fix them with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min.

  Wash the cells with PBS, permeabilize them with 0.1% Triton X-100, and wash again with PBS.(3)Block the cells with BSA blocking solution and label them with primary antibodies and corresponding secondary antibodies. Finally, add DAPI to stain the nuclei. Mount the cell coverslips onto slides and observe the fluorescence intensity using a Zeiss LSM780 microscope.

  Image analyses for parts (4) and (5) were performed using Python 3.8, with packages including NumPy, scikit-image, and Matplotlib.(4)Calculation of red and green fluorescence intensities: Immunofluorescence images were separated into red (red fluorescent protein), green (green fluorescent protein), and blue (DAPI, nuclear marker) channels. Total fluorescence intensities were obtained by summing pixel grayscale values for each channel. To account for variations in cell number, exposure, and imaging area: red and green intensities were normalized to the blue signal using the following formulae: normalized red intensity = sum of blue/sum of red; normalized green intensity = sum of blue/sum of green.(5)Detection of spatial overlap between red and green fluorescence signals (overlap coefficient, K1): To quantify the spatial colocalization of red and green fluorescence signals, the overlap coefficient (K1) was calculated. Otsu’s automatic thresholding was applied to the red and green channels to generate binary masks, followed by noise removal and morphological closing to obtain smooth masks. The number of overlapping pixels (double-positive area) was determined by pixel-wise Boolean operations. K1 was calculated as: overlap coefficient (K1) = NRed∩Green/min (NRed, NGreen). K1 values range from 0 (no overlap) to 1 (complete colocalization).


### Quantitative PCR (qPCR)
  mRNA expression levels were detected using qPCR. The steps are as follows:Place tumor tissue in liquid nitrogen and add an appropriate volume of TRIzol solution. Grind the tissue and collect 500 µL of the supernatant.Add 200 µL of chloroform, shake vigorously, centrifuge and collect 100 µL of the upper aqueous phase.Add 100 µL of isopropanol, mix by pipetting, centrifuge and wash the precipitate twice with 70% ethanol to obtain total mRNA.Reverse transcribe total RNA into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (Takara Bio) and detect the relative expression of genes using a SYBR Green dye kit (Takara Bio). The primer sequences used in this study are: Gapdh mouse F’-5′-CATCACTGCCACCCAGAAGACTG-3′, R’-5′-ATGCCAGTGAGCTTCCCGTTCAG-3′; Frmd8 mouse F’-5′- CTTCACCAACGCCTCCGATGAT-3′, R-5′-CTTGGCTTCCTCATAGAGCAGC-3′; Adam17 mouse F’-5′-TGTGAGCGGTGACCACGAGAAT-3′, R-5′-TTCATCCACCCTGGAGTTGCCA-3′; Rhbdf2 (iRhom2) mouse F’-5′-TCACCTTGCTGGTGATCTGCAC-3′, R-5′-GCCAATCCAGAAGTTCTCCTGC-3′.After obtaining the relative expression levels of the target gene and GAPDH, use GAPDH as the internal control and quantify the relative expression using the 2–ΔΔCT method.


### Western blot
  Western blotting was performed as follows:Cells were lysed on ice for 30 min with radio-immunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer, and an appropriate volume of protein loading buffer was added. The mixture was boiled for 10–15 min to prepare the samples.Samples (30 µg each) were loaded onto pre-cast SDS-PAGE gels. Electrophoresis was performed at 50 V until the dye front reached the separating gel, then at 120 V until the dye front reached the bottom of the gel.

  Proteins were transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane at a constant current of 400 mA.(3)The membrane was incubated with the primary antibodies listed in the appendix at 37 °C for 4 h, followed by incubation with the corresponding secondary antibodies (1:5000 dilution).(4)Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) solution and quantified using ImageJ software. The relative expression level of the target protein was calculated as the ratio of the target protein to GAPDH. Each experiment was repeated three times, and the average value was taken.


### Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
  The CCL3 ELISA kit was purchased from Merck (USA) (catalog no. RAB0074), and the TNF-α ELISA kits were purchased from R&D Systems (USA) (Catalog nos. DTA00D for human and MTA00B for mouse). The experimental procedure was as follows: (1) Collect cell supernatant and centrifuge at 1500 rpm, 4 °C for 10 min. (2) Add 0.2 mL capture antibody per well, Incubate at 37 °C for 1 h, wash three times with PBST. (3) Add 0.2 mL blocking buffer (5% BSA-PBS) per well, incubate at 37 °C for 30 min. (4) Add 0.2 mL of sample or standard per well, incubate at room temperature for 1–2 h. (5) Add 0.2 mL diluted primary antibody per well, incubate at room temperature for 2 h. (6) Add 0.2 mL TMB substrate solution per well, incubate in the dark for 30 min. (7) Add stop solution to each well, measure the OD value using a microplate reader. Finally, the result is expressed as the ratio of OD values between the experimental and control groups.


### Cytometry by time-of-flight (CyTOF) and flow cytometry experiments
  The CyTOF experiments were performed by Zhejiang Puluoting Health Technology Company. The general steps are as follows:(1) Chop the tissue into small pieces (1–2 mm3) and incubate with a suitable enzymatic cocktail to dissociate the extracellular matrix and release single cells.

  Pass the digested tissue suspension through a cell strainer (40–70 µm mesh size) to remove undigested tissue fragments and obtain a single-cell suspension.(2) Centrifuge the single-cell suspension at low speed, wash, and assess cell viability using viability dyes. Perform a final wash with PBS to remove any remaining debris. Label the cells with appropriate antibodies and use a mass cytometry machine from Fluidigm to detect the content of immune cells.


### T-cell suppression assay
  Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) were isolated from primary tumor tissue 4 weeks post-implantation of 759-GFP cells expressing Frmd8 sgRNA1/iRhom2 shRNA. Tumor tissues were digested using a digestion buffer for 1 h at 37 °C. Following digestion, cells were treated with red blood cell (RBC) lysis buffer to lyse red blood cells. Single-cell populations were labeled with CD11b and Gr1 antibodies and incubated on ice for 45 min. MDSC populations were sorted using a fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) Aria II flow cytometer.T cells, isolated from wild-type spleen and bone marrow, were labeled with carboxyfluorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester (CFSE) and combined with anti-CD3 and anti-CD28 antibodies. These T cells were then mixed with MDSCs at ratios of 1:0 or 5:1, with a total of 5.0 × 105 cells per well in a 24-well plate. T-cell proliferation was assessed by FACS analysis after 96 h of culture.


### Drug library screening and public dataset analysis
  A drug library containing 146 Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved drugs was synthesized by Genscript and distributed in a 384-well plate. The distribution of the drugs in the wells is provided in the appendix. Both 759 CON and 759 Frmd8 sgRNA1 cells were plated onto the drug library plate. After 48 h, 10 µL of 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) solution (5 mg/mL) was added to each well. The plate was incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 4 h, after which 100 µL of DMSO was added. The absorbance (OD value) of each well was measured using a microplate reader at a wavelength of 570 nm. Cell sensitivity to the drugs was calculated on the basis of the absorbance ratios.


### Promoter assay
  The promoter sequence of TNF-α, spanning from −1499 to +100, was downloaded from the EPD website (https://epd.expasy.org/epd/) and cloned into a pGL-basic vector. This pGL-basic vector or the pGL-TNF vector, along with mBRCA1 cDNA, control DNA, and Renilla DNA, were co-transfected into wild-type and BRCA1-mutant mammary epithelial cells in 24-well plates for 72 h. After incubation, cells were lysed, and luciferase activity was measured using a luciferase assay system kit (Promega). Quantification of signal intensity was performed by calculating the ratio of firefly to Renilla luciferase activity.


### Cell co-culture experiments
  After digesting the primary tumor into a single-cell suspension, MDSCs (Gr1+) were obtained using flow cytometry. These cells were mixed with CON and Frmd8 sgRNA1 cells at a ratio of 3:1. The mixed cell culture was placed in a Precise Live Cell Imaging incubator for 48 h, and cell dynamics were monitored using an Olympus IX71 microscope. The recorded effects were analyzed using the OpenCV-Python package.


### Statistical analysis
  Quantitative data were analyzed using t-tests, while qualitative data were analyzed using Chi-squared tests. Correlation analysis was performed using the Pearson correlation coefficient. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

  For these 41 genes, we designed a small sgRNA knockout library (Supplementary Table S4) and used transcriptional regulatory network analysis in the GSE5327 dataset to identify significantly regulated genes. Master regulator analysis (MRA) showed that FRMD8 had the most significant regulatory capability (Fig. 1B), and FRMD8 was also downregulated in our hospital’s dataset (Fig. 1C). In The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset, FRMD8 was confirmed to be downregulated in the TNBC subtype (Supplementary Fig. S1A), which is highly invasive. This, combined with its low expression in metastatic tumors, suggests FRMD8 as a candidate metastasis-suppressor gene.

  We further selected the 759 cell line, derived from tumors in Brca1-deficient mice, which lacks metastatic capability in vivo. We divided the 759 cell line into CON and library groups, injecting five nude mice with 759 CON cells and ten mice with 759 library cells. After 8 weeks, we harvested DNA from primary tumors and used primers for the 39 library genes for amplification. All ten library mice tumors contained Frmd8 sgRNA, indicating that low Frmd8 expression may support breast cancer proliferation (Fig. 1D).

  PCR analysis of lungs from these mice showed Frmd8 sgRNA in the lungs of five library mice but not other library genes, breast tumor DNA was amplified with universal sgRNA primers, and were positive for the lenti v2 backbone  (Fig. 1E).   To investigate the association between FRMD8 expression and clinical outcomes in patients with TNBC, we selected datasets from TCGA and GEO databases and analyzed the relationship between FRMD8 and disease-free survival (DFS), metastasis-free survival (MFS), and overall survival (OS).

  To further confirm these findings, we isolated membrane proteins from both CON and Frmd8 sgRNA1-transduced cells and performed western blot analysis to assess the protein levels of iRHOM2 and ADAM17. Both proteins were markedly reduced following FRMD8 knockdown (Fig. 4B), suggesting that FRMD8 is required for maintaining the expression of iRHOM2, as well as the mature, membrane-associated forms of ADAM17. Flow cytometry analysis of membrane-bound iRHOM2 and ADAM17 revealed that iRHOM2 expression on the cell surface was substantially decreased in FRMD8-knockdown cells (Fig. 4C; Supplementary Fig. S4B). Also, the reduction in ADAM17 expression was decreased (Fig. 4D; Supplementary Fig. S4C). Consistent results were observed in MCF-7, BT-549, and MDA-MB-231 cell lines (Supplementary Fig. S4D–I), where Frmd8 knockdown similarly led to a marked decrease in membrane iRHOM2 expression.

  We further confirmed, through immunofluorescence assays both in cell lines and orthotopic tumors, that Frmd8 knockdown markedly reduced the colocalization of iRHOM2 and ADAM17 with the membrane marker E-cadherin (Fig. 4E). Through quantitative analysis of the images, we further confirmed that in both 759 Frmd8 sgRNA1 cells and the tumors derived from these cells, the expression levels of iRHOM2 (Supplementary Fig. S4J), ADAM17 (Supplementary Fig. S4K), and E-cadherin (Supplementary Fig. S4K) were decreased. In addition, colocalization among iRHOM2, ADAM17, and E-cadherin was also significantly reduced (Supplementary Fig. S4L). All these differences were statistically significant.

  Additional co-immunoprecipitation experiments revealed that FRMD8 can bind to iRHOM2 (Fig. 4F) and ADAM17 (Supplementary Fig. S4M) at the protein level. However, no changes were detected in the promoter activities of iRhom2 and Adam17 following Frmd8 knockdown (Fig. 4G), and the mRNA levels of iRHOM2 (Supplementary Fig. S4N) and ADAM17 (Supplementary Fig. S4O) remained unchanged.

  Using a CHX chase assay, we further demonstrated that FRMD8 primarily regulates the stability of iRHOM2 at the protein level in MDA-MB-231 cells (Fig. 4H), and grayscale intensity analysis indicated that the differences were statistically significant (Supplementary Fig. S4P). According to existing literature, FRMD8 is known to mediate iRHOM2 degradation via the endocytic–lysosomal pathway [13]. Consistent with this, we observed a significant increase in the colocalization of iRHOM2 with lysosomes upon Frmd8 knockdown, indicating that FRMD8-regulated iRHOM2 expression is closely associated with lysosomal function (Fig. 4I). Furthermore, analysis of fluorescence intensity and colocalization confirmed that Frmd8 knockdown led to a reduction in iRHOM2 expression (Supplementary Fig. S4Q), and a significant increase in its colocalization with Lysotracker (Supplementary Fig. S4R) further supported our conclusions.

  Moreover, treatment of Frmd8 sgRNA1-transduced 759 cells with the endocytosis inhibitor bafilomycin suppressed iRHOM2 degradation and restored its expression (Fig. 4J). In contrast, proteasome inhibition via MG132 did not mitigate iRHOM2 degradation (Supplementary Fig. S4S), providing additional evidence that FRMD8-mediated iRHOM2 degradation is specifically linked to the endocytic–lysosomal pathway.

  On the basis of the above experiments, we hypothesized that FRMD8 may bind to iRHOM2 and protect it from recognition by endocytic signals. To test this, we employed AlphaFold3 software to predict potential binding interactions between FRMD8 and iRHOM2 (Supplementary Fig. S4T). We additionally calculated the minimal distances between each amino acid of iRHOM2 and FRMD8 to pinpoint possible binding regions on iRHOM2. By comparing these regions to the YXXΦ endocytic recognition motifs on iRHOM2, we identified two sequences—YSHL starting at residue 229 and YITF starting at residue 790—that lie in close proximity to FRMD8 and possess endocytic recognition capabilities (Fig. 4K).

  We then generated a Flag-iRHOM2-Δ construct lacking these two sequences and co-transfected it with FRMD8 sgRNA1 into MDA-MB-231 cells. CHX chase assays showed that iRHOM2 degradation was alleviated when these sequences were deleted (Fig. 4L), and grayscale intensity analysis showed no statistically significant differences between groups, suggesting that the degradation of iRHOM2 was alleviated (Supplementary Fig. S4U, V) and that the removal of these motifs inhibits the iRHOM2 degradation pathway. Furthermore, FRMD8 failed to bind the Flag-iRHOM2-Δ mutant (Fig. 4M), indicating that the YSHL motif at residue 229 and the YITF motif at residue 790 are both required for FRMD8–iRHOM2 binding and for regulating iRHOM2 degradation.

  In our previous results, we observed that in the Brca1-deficient and Frmd8-low 759 cell line, there was enhanced in vivo tumor metastasis accompanied by a marked increase in tmTNF-α. Loss of BRCA1 is known to result in sustained activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway, and NF-κB is a well-established transcription factor that directly promotes TNF-α transcription [14]. Therefore, the increased secretion of TNF-α following BRCA1 loss is an anticipated outcome. However, whether BRCA1 deficiency can directly regulate the transcription of TNF-α in TNBC, and whether BRCA1 also influences the cleavage of TNF-α, remains unreported. To test this theory, we first introduced BRCA1 siRNA into the BRCA1 wild-type TNBC cell line BT-549. The results showed that BRCA1 did not affect FRMD8 protein levels, whereas total TNF-α and iRHOM2 protein levels were significantly increased (Fig. 4N). Consistent with these observations, qPCR revealed that TNF and iRHOM2 mRNA levels were markedly elevated following BRCA1 knockdown (Fig. 4O). Flow cytometry further confirmed that tmTNF-α expression decreased after BRCA1 siRNA transfection, whereas FRMD8 knockdown led to a marked increase in tmTNF-α expression (Supplementary Fig. S4W, X).

  Lastly, we found that BRCA1 directly binds to the Tnf promoter in 759 cells (Fig. 4P). Moreover, BRCA1 inhibition significantly increased TNF-α secretion, while FRMD8 knockdown substantially reduced TNF-α secretion, in line with our earlier findings. By contrast, iRHOM2 inhibition further increased TNF-α secretion in the BT-549 cell line (Fig. 4Q). Collectively, these data demonstrate that in cell lines with reduced BRCA1 expression, although TNF-α transcription and expression are elevated, activation of the TNF-α cleavage pathway leads to higher TNF-α secretion and lower tmTNF-α. However, if FRMD8 or iRhom2 expression is diminished, tmTNF-α is markedly increased, potentially promoting tumor metastasis.

### Paclitaxel inhibits FRMD8-related metastasis by reversing FRMD8 expression
  We sought drugs that could block FRMD8-related metastasis. Analysis of the TCGA dataset revealed that patients responsive to paclitaxel treatment showed significantly increased FRMD8 expression (Fig. 7A). In addition, iRHOM2 (Supplementary Fig. S7A) and ADAM17 (Supplementary Fig. S7B) levels were elevated, which was confirmed in the GSE22513 dataset (Supplementary Fig. S7C–E). Although both paclitaxel and doxorubicin were found to increase the protein levels of FRMD8 and iRHOM2 in our western blot analysis, only paclitaxel treatment consistently elevated FRMD8 mRNA expression across concentrations of 0, 50, 100, and 200 nM, exhibiting a dose-dependent pattern. Therefore, among the three candidate drugs—paclitaxel, anastrozole and doxorubicin—we inferred that only paclitaxel regulates FRMD8 at the transcriptional level, thereby achieving sustained upregulation of FRMD8 expression. Given that mRNA-level regulation impacts the entire transcriptional process, its effects are generally more durable following drug withdrawal, whereas regulation limited to the protein level is more susceptible to loss of effect due to protein turnover. On the basis of these considerations, we selected paclitaxel for subsequent experiments (Fig. 7B, C). ## Discussion
  Library screening is an efficient method for identifying target genes involved in disease processes [15]. In previous studies, we identified ATP11B as a metastasis-related gene using a whole-genome knockout library. In the current study, we combined sequencing data from our hospital with public datasets to identify 41 candidate genes associated with breast cancer metastasis. Of these, 39 genes were found to be present in mice. Screening these genes in the 759 cell line led to the identification of FRMD8 as a novel breast cancer metastasis-related gene.

  Our analysis revealed that low expression of FRMD8 significantly increased the levels of tmTNF-α, with iRHOM2 identified as a direct target. The reduced expression of FRMD8 and iRHOM2 further promoted tmTNF-α expression and tumor metastasis, enhancing immune suppression pathways within the tumor microenvironment. These findings align with previous studies highlighting the role of tmTNF-α in promoting tumor progression and metastasis through activation of NF-κB pathways and modulation of the tumor microenvironment [16–19].

  Existing literature highlights various functions of FRMD8, including the stabilization of the iRHOM2/ADAM17 complex, regulation of Wnt signaling, maintenance of cellular stability, and tumor suppression [20–22]. Our study confirmed FRMD8’s role in stabilizing membrane proteins in breast cancer. Despite the potential for cell cycle regulation pathways, no direct enrichment was observed in our analysis. Knockdown of FRMD8 resulted in decreased TNF-α secretion while increasing tmTNF-α expression. This suggests that inhibition of FRMD8 blocks the cleavage of tmTNF-α into sTNF-α, thereby promoting tmTNF-α expression and its associated pro-metastatic effects.

  FRMD8 regulates the lysosomal degradation of iRhom2, which influences the cleavage and activity of ADAM17. This, in turn, affects downstream molecules such as TNF-α and EGFR [23–25]. Our study demonstrated that FRMD8 interacts with both iRHOM2 and ADAM17, regulating iRHOM2 expression on the cell membrane without altering ADAM17 membrane levels. Reduced expression of FRMD8 and iRHOM2 led to increased tmTNF-α expression and tumor metastasis.

  BRCA1 is primarily known for its role in DNA repair, but recent studies have demonstrated that BRCA1 also regulates inflammatory and immune responses, including the transcription and function of TNF-α [26]. BRCA1 can bind to the TNF promoter, enhancing TNF transcription levels [27–29]. In our research, we identified that BRCA1 deficiency promotes the membrane expression of TNF-α. The interaction among BRCA1, FRMD8, iRHOM2, and ADAM17 facilitates TNF production and the activation of the TNF signaling pathway. These findings suggest that in BRCA1-deficient tissues, the TNF-α pathway is further activated.

  Unlike sTNF-α, which primarily activates the classical NF-κB pathway via TNFR1, tmTNF-α can bind both TNFR1 and TNFR2, activating both classical and non-classical NF-κB pathways. This leads to the expression of metastasis-related molecules. tmTNF-α also plays a crucial role in activating MDSCs and regulatory T cells in the tumor microenvironment, thereby enhancing immune suppression pathways [30]. Our study demonstrated increased TNFR2 signaling in FRMD8 low-expression tumors, confirming that FRMD8’s function is heavily dependent on tmTNF-α.

  In vitro experiments demonstrated that tmTNF-α promotes the activation of the EMT pathway, a crucial process in cancer metastasis. In vivo, tmTNF-α enhances tumor progression by modulating immune responses. Frmd8 knockdown resulted in increased expression of CCL3, a known inducer of MDSCs. CyTOF screening confirmed elevated levels of MDSCs in tissues with Frmd8 knockdown. Further studies showed that both classical and non-classical NF-κB pathways were activated in MDSCs, reinforcing the role of FRMD8 in MDSC activation.

  Combining drug library screening and public datasets, we identified paclitaxel as a compound capable of reversing the expression of FRMD8, iRhom2, and ADAM17. FRMD8 knockdown cells showed increased sensitivity to paclitaxel, suggesting that paclitaxel effectively blocks Frmd8-related metastasis by altering Frmd8 expression. In vivo experiments demonstrated that paclitaxel partially inhibited Frmd8-related metastasis. While etanercept, a TNF inhibitor [31], partially reduced metastasis, it was not fully effective on its own. However, the combination of paclitaxel and etanercept completely blocked Frmd8-related metastasis, indicating that this combination therapy could effectively prevent FRMD8-related metastasis and may serve as a promising therapeutic strategy for patients with breast cancer.

  Our study suggests that FRMD8-related metastasis involves multiple molecular mechanisms, including the regulation of tmTNF-α, activation of NF-κB pathways, and modulation of immune cell functions. The interaction between FRMD8 and iRHOM2 plays a crucial role in maintaining ADAM17 stability and function, which is essential for tmTNF-α cleavage and subsequent activation of metastasis-related pathways. In addition, the increased expression of CCL3 in Frmd8 knockdown cells and tissues indicates a significant role in recruiting and activating MDSCs, which further supports tumor growth and immune evasion.

  The identification of FRMD8 as a key regulator of breast cancer metastasis has significant clinical implications. Targeting FRMD8 and its associated pathways could provide new therapeutic strategies for preventing and treating metastatic breast cancer. The combination of paclitaxel and etanercept shows promise in effectively inhibiting FRMD8-related metastasis, suggesting potential for clinical application.

  In conclusion, our study identified FRMD8 as a key player in breast cancer metastasis. Low expression of FRMD8 promotes tmTNF-α expression and enhances tumor metastasis through multiple mechanisms, including the activation of NF-κB pathways and immune suppression. The combination of paclitaxel and etanercept shows potential as an effective therapy to inhibit FRMD8-related metastasis. Further research is warranted to explore the full therapeutic potential of targeting FRMD8 and its associated pathways in breast cancer treatment.


## Conclusions
  We integrated public datasets with CRISPR library screening and preliminarily identified Frmd8 as a potential factor associated with metastasis in Brca1-deficient TNBC tumor cells. Through further functional validation, we confirmed that FRMD8 is not only lowly expressed in metastatic TNBC tumor tissues but also directly promotes multi-organ metastasis of TNBC and facilitates the entry of CTCs into the bloodstream, indicating that FRMD8 plays a role in the initiation of metastasis.

  Further investigation into the function of FRMD8 revealed that its low expression significantly increases the expression of membrane-bound TNF-α (tmTNF-α) on tumor cells. In addition, FRMD8 protects iRHOM2 from degradation, while its low expression leads to increased iRHOM2 degradation, thereby suppressing the maturation of ADAM17 and reducing ADAM17-mediated cleavage of tmTNF-α. As a result, in TNBC cell lines with simultaneous low expression of FRMD8 and iRHOM2, tmTNF-α expression on the cell membrane reaches its peak, which is accompanied by a substantial increase in in vivo metastasis.

  Moreover, tumor cells with high tmTNF-α expression recruit MDSCs in primary tumors, leading to early enrichment of MDSCs during the initial stages of metastasis. Finally, through drug screening, we identified paclitaxel as a potential agent capable of reversing the expression of FRMD8 and iRHOM2. When combined with the TNF-α inhibitor etanercept, the metastatic phenotype driven by FRMD8low/iRHOM2low was effectively suppressed, providing insights for future therapeutic strategies to block metastasis.
