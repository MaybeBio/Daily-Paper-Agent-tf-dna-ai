# Ectopic expression of GDF15 in cancer-associated fibroblasts enhances melanoma immunosuppression via the GFRAL/RET cascade


## Background
  A key aspect of tumor biology is the involvement of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) in shaping the immunosuppressive microenvironment. However, the dynamic and complex key roles of CAFs in the melanoma immune microenvironment have not been elucidated.


## Methods
  The CAFs landscape in melanoma was characterized using single-cell RNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics. Molecular dynamics simulations were employed to validate the interactions between CAFs and melanoma cells. Bulk RNA-seq was used to establish a prognostic model. To validate the expression of key targets, western blotting, quantitative real-time PCR, and ELISA were performed. The molecular interactions were confirmed via co-immunoprecipitation, chromatin immunoprecipitation, and luciferase gene reporter assays. In-depth molecular mechanisms were explored using lentiviral transfection, cell co-culture experiments, recombinant protein rescue experiments, flow cytometry, knockout mice, and Cre-loxP system mice.


## Results
  This study identified a unique group of CAFs expressing high levels of growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15). The paracrine secretion of GDF15 was regulated by the transcription factor FOXP1, which subsequently binds to the TGFBR2 receptor on melanoma cells, driving their proliferation and metastatic capacity. In addition, CAFs-derived GDF15 interacts with the GFRAL receptor on melanoma cells, thereby promoting RET phosphorylation and triggering downstream signaling axes, inducing increased tumor cell stemness and secretion of inflammatory factors CCL18 and TGF-β. This cascade reaction ultimately induces macrophage polarization to the immunosuppressive M2 phenotype, assists in the establishment of an immunosuppressive microenvironment, and leads to accelerated melanoma lung metastasis.


## Conclusion
  By integrating single-cell RNA-seq, spatial transcriptomics, bulk RNA-seq, molecular dynamics simulation and complete experimental design, this study comprehensively characterized that ectopic expression of CAFs-derived GDF15 can act as an accomplice in melanoma progression by inducing increased tumor cell stemness and macrophage M2 polarization, reshaping the immune landscape of melanoma, and providing new ideas and new targets for precision immunotherapy of melanoma.


## Introduction
  Melanoma is an aggressive malignant tumor caused by melanocytes, which is generally divided into cutaneous melanoma (CM), acral melanoma (AM), and mucosal melanoma according to the location of the lesion.1 The incidence of melanoma is increasing annually.2 Despite constituting only 4% of skin cancers, melanoma is responsible for over 80% of skin cancer-related deaths due to its metastatic potential.3 4 Surgical resection is the first-line treatment for primary melanoma with a cure rate of 90%.5 However, for the treatment of melanoma that has metastasized to the distance, it is not possible to remove the lesion by surgery. Therefore, chemotherapy is the standard treatment, but chemotherapy is prone to drug resistance, and sometimes even leads to poor response to subsequent immunotherapy.68 In addition, although some progress has been made in understanding the etiology of melanoma, its pathogenesis is not well understood.9 Therefore, exploring the pathogenesis of melanoma and finding new molecular targets is imperative.

  Targeted cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) may be central to overcoming resistance to traditional chemotherapy, molecularly targeted drugs, and immunotherapies.10 CAFs are a stromal cell population,11 constituting one of the most prominent and abundant cell populations within the tumor microenvironment (TME)12 and play crucial roles in tumor invasion, metastasis, and the establishment of tumor immune microenvironments.13 14 Recently, CAFs in the TME have been demonstrated to play significant roles in the pathogenesis of various cancers.1519 However, there is not enough research on CAFs in melanoma. The extensive interaction between CAFs and melanoma cells indicates that CAFs may establish a protective network around melanoma cells, creating a barrier that diminishes the efficacy of immunotherapy and contributes to drug resistance.20 21 Regarding treatment, the sensitivity of melanoma cells to targeted therapeutic compounds depends on the TME, and fibroblasts have been shown to affect the sensitivity of metastatic melanoma cells to BRAF and MEK dual inhibition.22 Therefore, CAFs are not only essential for the initiation and progression of melanoma but also play a pivotal role in establishing an immunosuppressive TME.

  Despite the development of diverse advanced techniques—including single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq), spatial transcriptomics (ST), molecular dynamics simulations, and molecular biology assays—the comprehensive integration of these approaches to elucidate the role of melanoma-associated CAFs remains unexplored. Furthermore, there are clear gaps in the study of the interactions between CAFs, tumor cells, and immune cells, highlighting the urgent need for further research in this area. Therefore, this study aims to elucidate the complex mechanisms by which CAFs contribute to melanoma immune resistance, metastasis, and progression. By doing so, it aims to provide valuable theoretical perspectives and constructive insights for future studies that can inform the development of future immunotherapy strategies.


## Results

### Identification and heterogeneity of CAFs
  We obtained 80,025 high-quality cells through batch correction, and based on specific marker genes, these 32 cell clusters were identified as different cell types: melanoma cells, natural killer cells, CAFs, CD4 T cells, CD8 T cells, B cells and plasma cells, monocytes and macrophages, and endothelial cells (online supplemental figure 1A and figure 1A). Analysis of AM and CM patient samples highlighted a higher proportion of CAFs derived from AM (figure 1B). Furthermore, figure 1C depicted melanoma cells accounted for the largest proportion of AM and CM, followed by CD8 T cells and CAFs. Moreover, compared with T cells and melanoma cells, CAFs are more likely to be in the G1 phase (online supplemental figure 1A). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) revealed positively enriched biological pathways in CAFs (online supplemental figure 1B).

  Subsequently, we focused on CAFs. To verify the accuracy of the previously annotated CAFs, we revisualized the expression patterns of fibroblast-specific marker genes in all cells (online supplemental file 3). We found that the vast majority of these markers were highly expressed in the annotated CAFs population. Notably, LUM, DCN, COL1A2, and COL1A1 exhibited both the highest expression levels and the most prominent expression densities (online supplemental figure 2A-D). Following quality control and batch effect removal, 2,624 high-quality CAFs were identified and categorized into C0 SFRP2+ CAFs, C1 NDUFA4L2+ CAFs, C2 growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15)+ CAFs, and C3 PCLAF+ CAFs based on specific marker genes (figure 1E and online supplemental figure 1D). Among these, C2 GDF15+ CAFs exhibited a higher proportion of cells in G1 phase, followed by those in G2/M phase (figure 1D and online supplemental figure 1C). Notably, C2 GDF15+ CAFs predominantly originated from AM (online supplemental figure 1C). Visualization through Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) confirmed the expression of named genes SFRP2, NDUFA4L2, GDF15, and PCLAF in respective subgroups (figure 1F–G). Moreover, C2 GDF15+ CAFs displayed significantly higher cell stemness and copy-number variant (CNV) scores (figure 1H–I). Analysis of stemness gene expression differences among subgroups revealed higher CD44, CTNNB1, and KDM5B expression in C2 GDF15+ CAFs (online supplemental figure 1E-F). There was no significant difference in the expression of the above genes among different groups (online supplemental figure 1G).

  To highlight heterogeneity among subtypes, volcano plots showcased the most upregulated genes and the most downregulated genes in C2 GDF15+ CAFs (figure 1J). Enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes across C2 GDF15+ CAFs revealed that these cells were mainly involved in cytoplasmic translation, protein stabilization, cell growth, chaperone-mediated protein complex assembly, plasminogen activation, regulation of plasminogen activation, response to topologically incorrect protein, developmental cell growth, and positive regulation of telomerase activity (figure 1K). Additionally, GSEA indicated enrichment of cellular pigmentation and pigment cell differentiation in C2 GDF15+ CAFs (online supplemental figure 4A).

  Considering the advantages of C2 GDF15+ CAFs in stemness, we further investigated the differentiation potential of CAF subtypes using a series of CytoTRACE2 analyses. C2 GDF15+ CAFs exhibited the highest CytoTRACE2 score, indicating the greatest degree of undifferentiation and the highest differentiation potential. Moreover, C2 GDF15+ CAFs showed a higher proportion of unipotent and oligopotent states (online supplemental figures 1H-J and figure 2A–C).


### Molecular biological characteristics of C2 GDF15+ CAFs
  It is commonly known that distinct functions within the same CAF subtypes exhibit notable variability, regulate distinct biological processes, and result in distinct patient outcomes. In order to shed light on the heterogeneity of CAFs in this study, we built on this foundation by performing AUCell scoring of relevant gene sets for CAFs based on their biological characteristics, including matrix CAFs (mCAFs), vascular CAFs (vCAFs), antigen-presenting CAFs (apCAFs), inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs), lipoCAFs, and EMT-like CAFs (EMT-CAFs). Interestingly, C2 GDF15+ CAFs not only showed higher mCAFs scores but also relatively higher apCAFs scores compared with other subtypes (figure 2D). Both AM and CM-derived C2 GDF15+ CAFs showed considerable expression of the apCAFs-related genes HLA-DRB1, CD74, and HLA-DRA (online supplemental figure 4B). This suggests that C2 GDF15+ CAFs may be associated with the immunosuppressive microenvironment of melanoma.

  In addition, to identify whether isolated C2 GDF15+ CAFs expressed apCAFs markers, we performed immunofluorescence staining using myCAFs as a control. The results demonstrated that C2 GDF15+ CAFs exhibited features similar to those of apCAFs, characterized by high expression levels of CD74, HLA-DRA, and HLA-DRB, along with elevated expression of GDF15 and PCLAF (online supplemental figure 3A). These findings suggest that C2 GDF15+ CAFs share functional similarities with apCAFs and display immune-related molecular features.

  To study ST characteristics of melanoma and elucidate vital molecular events in malignancy transformation, we conducted ST analysis on melanoma tissues. However, we visualized the ST landscapes of cellular pluripotency, GDF15, PCLAF, and mCAFs, along with the ST landscapes of G2/M score, S score, nCount, and nFeature. These analyses revealed their high expression in regions corresponding to C2 GDF15+ CAFs (figure 2E and online supplemental figure 4C).

  The proliferation, growth, and migration capabilities of CAFs were crucially tied to their cellular metabolism.23 Exploration of the top five metabolic pathways across all CAFs subgroups identified elevated scores for glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and oxidative phosphorylation in C2 GDF15+ CAFs (online supplemental figure 4D).

  To further validate the expression of GDF15 and PCLAF in melanoma, we selected eight pairs of melanoma and corresponding normal tissues for verification. In tumor tissues, a significant upregulation of the expression of GDF15 and PCLAF was observed (online supplemental figures 5A-D and online supplemental figures 6A,B). Immunofluorescence analysis showed that the fibrin marker proteins α-SMA and FAP were more highly expressed in CAFs (online supplemental figure 5E). Consistently, ELISA assays showed elevated levels of GDF15 in the serum of patients with melanoma (online supplemental figures 6C, D). Combined with the results of scRNA-seq analysis, melanoma-CAFs highly expressed PCLAF and GDF15. Therefore, we used four melanoma cell lines and four fibroblasts to investigate the differences in the expression of PCLAF and GDF15. The results showed that CAFs had significantly increased GDF15 and PCLAF expression compared with melanoma cell lines and normal fibroblasts (online supplemental figures 5F-H and online supplemental figures 6E,F). In summary, we confirmed that GDF15 and PCLAF were highly expressed in melanoma tissues, and the elevated expression of GDF15 was mainly found in CAFs rather than melanoma cells.

  Further knockdown of GDF15 in CAFs showed that the expression of GDF15 and PCLAF was significantly decreased in the knockdown group (online supplemental figures 7A, B). When A375 and WM-115 cells were co-cultured with conditioned medium from CAFs, the proliferation of melanoma cells increased significantly, and their migration and invasion abilities were significantly enhanced. In contrast, when co-cultured with conditioned medium from GDF15-silenced CAFs, the proliferation of melanoma cells slowed down, and their migration and invasion abilities were inhibited (online supplemental figures 7C-H). These experiments collectively demonstrate that GDF15 secreted by CAFs significantly promotes the malignant biological behavior of melanoma.


### GDF15 mediates the interaction between CAFs and melanoma cells
  Building on the above findings, and considering the evolution of fibroblasts into CAFs in the TME, with tumor cells shaping the TME, we inferred the interaction between C2 GDF15+ CAFs and melanoma cells. First, we mapped the interaction count and weight across all melanoma cell subtypes (online supplemental figure 4E) and compared interaction metrics between AM and CM, with CM showing higher interaction count and weight (online supplemental figure 4F). Further analysis of CAF interactions with other cells revealed increased interaction levels between C2 GDF15+ CAFs and melanoma cells (figure 2F), supporting our results. Heatmap and bubble chart analysis (figure 2G) showed that C2 GDF15+ CAFs and melanoma cells interact through the GDF15/TGFBR2, with CAFs as signal senders and melanoma cells as receivers. Importantly, we validated that this interaction occurs through paracrine signaling within the GDF signaling network (figure 2H).

  Molecular dynamics simulations confirmed the binding of 5VT2 (GDF15) to 1PLO (TGFBR2). Analysis of root mean square deviation (online supplemental figure 8A), gyration (online supplemental figure 8B), and solvent accessible surface area (online supplemental figure 8E) indicated stable binding after 200ns of simulation. The protein secondary structure (online supplemental figure 8F) showed minimal changes during binding. Root mean square fluctuation analysis revealed that the complex was flexible, with regions outside the C-termini and N-termini remaining stable (online supplemental figure 8C). The simulations also revealed stable hydrogen bonds between the proteins over time (online supplemental figure 8D). Dynamic cross-correlation matrix and residue distance matrix plots indicated that binding occurred mainly around the N-terminal amino acids of GDF15 (online supplemental figures 8G, H). Free energy landscape analysis, based on Principal Component Analysis, identified the two major motions in the system and confirmed stable binding between TGFBR2 and GDF15 (online supplemental figures 8I, J).

  The binding energy of TGFBR2 and GDF15 was determined using Molecular Mechanics/Poisson-Boltzmann Surface Area was consistently around −53 kcal/mol (online supplemental figure 8K). The energetic breakdown analysis revealed that the amino acids ARG34 and PHE35 of TGFBR2 and ARG13 and ARG16 of GDF15 significantly contributed to the binding process. On the other hand, the amino acids GLU70, GLU75, and ASP87 of TGFBR2 were found to have an adverse effect on the binding (online supplemental figure 8L). The three-dimensional plots of the GDF15–TGFBR2 complex showed minimal changes in the binding geometry of the protein complex during the simulation, indicating stable and long-lasting binding (online supplemental figure 8M). The results of the PLIP tool revealed that TGFBR2 and GDF15 engaged in hydrogen bonding, salt bridging, and hydrophobic interactions at the binding interface (online supplemental figure 8N). Online supplemental figure 8O depicted the most effective network for information transmission within the protein complex. The study found that the amino acids F11 of TGFBR2 and D128 of GDF15 were responsible for transferring information. The influence of residue energy on protein complex binding was illustrated in online supplemental figure 8P. Essentially, this result provided profound molecular mechanistic insights into the complex interactions between CAFs and melanoma cells in the field of melanoma.

  To confirm the results of bioinformatics and molecular dynamics simulations, we performed co-immunoprecipitation experiments and found that GDF15 binds to TGFBR2, activating the TGF-β signaling pathway (online supplemental figures 9A-C). Furthermore, we investigated whether GDF15 promotes malignant behavior in melanoma by activating the TGF-β pathway. In vivo lung metastasis experiment, we observed that recombinant human GDF15 protein significantly enhanced melanoma lung metastasis, while the addition of the TGF-β pathway inhibitor LY2109761 markedly suppressed the lung metastatic ability induced by recombinant human GDF15 protein (online supplemental figures 9D, E).

  To investigate the upregulation mechanism of GDF15 in melanoma further, we identified FOXP1 as a potential transcription factor for GDF15 through the JASPAR database. FOXP1 is a core transcription factor that regulates the transcriptional activity of target genes by binding to specific DNA sequences. The abnormal expression of FOXP1 in tumors is associated with tumorigenesis, development, and prognosis. It has the potential to act as an oncogene or tumor suppressor gene to regulate the proliferation, invasion, and metastasis of tumor cells. The chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) (online supplemental figures 9F, G) revealed FOXP1 as the transcription factor for GDF15. Knockdown validation (online supplemental figures 9H, I) further confirmed that FOXP1 promotes the expression of GDF15. Visual validation confirmed the co-expression of FOXP1 and GDF15 in CAF subgroups (online supplemental figure 9J).

  We further used the Human Transcription Factor Database (TFDB) to predict FOXP1 binding sites on the human GDF15 promoter (online supplemental figure 10A). Subsequently, three high-scoring GDF15 promoter transcription factor binding sites (TFBSs) along with corresponding mutations were incorporated into the pGL3-basic vector (online supplemental figure 10B). FOXP1 expression was detected in HEK-293T cells using western blotting analysis (online supplemental figures 10C, D). Finally, luciferase activity assays revealed that the luciferase activity was decreased in the h-GDF15 (Mut1)+h-FOXP1 and h-GDF15 (Mut2)+h-FOXP1 groups compared with the h-GDF15 (Full)+h-FOXP1 group, while the h-GDF15 (Mut3)+h-FOXP1 group showed no change (online supplemental figure 10E). These findings indicate that FOXP1 may bind to TFBS1 and TFBS2 of the GDF15 promoter, thereby modulating GDF15 transcription and mediating the malignant behavior of melanoma (online supplemental figure 10F).

  Finally, considering the critical significance of CAFs in patients with melanoma, we constructed a prognostic model for melanoma based on the C2 GDF15+ CAFs subtype. The results revealed that 16 genes were identified as significantly related to patient prognosis (online supplemental figures 11A-D). Survival differences and gene expression differences between high GDF15+ CAFs risk score (GFRS) group and low GFRS group were then visualized (online supplemental figure 11E-G). Meanwhile, the results showed that the modeled genes had good predictive ability, and the overall survival rate was lower in the high GFRS group compared with the low GFRS group (p<0.0001) (online supplemental figures 11H, I). In addition, C2 GDF15+ CAFs can interact with macrophages via the APP-CD74 pathway (online supplemental figures 12A-D). In order to further study the heterogeneity between the high-risk and low-risk groups, as well as the immunological signatures, we performed a comprehensive analysis of their tumor immune microenvironment to illustrate the immune infiltration landscape (online supplemental figures 12E-J). Biological function enrichment analysis suggested that the poor prognosis of patients in the high GFRS group might be related to the imbalance of epidermal differentiation and immune effects (online supplemental figures 13A-E).


### CAF-secreted GDF15 promotes the self-renewal ability of melanoma cells via interaction with its receptor GFRAL
  In recent years, GDF15 has been found to be associated with cell stemness,24 but the ability of GDF15 to regulate melanoma cell self-renewal in melanoma is still unclear. To test this hypothesis, GDF15 non-expressing melanoma cell lines A375 and WM-115 were treated with varying concentrations of recombinant human GDF15 protein in vitro. The results demonstrated that GDF15 enhanced the stem-like properties and multiplication capacity of melanoma cells in a concentration-dependent manner, with the maximum effect observed at 250 ng/mL (figure 3A–E), but this effect was reversed at 500 ng/mL. Consistent with these findings, melanoma cells treated with CAFsGDF15+ supernatants or co-cultured with CAFsGDF15+ showed increased stem-like properties, invasiveness, and migration capability. This enhancement was significantly attenuated following the knockdown of GDF15 in CAFsGDF15+ (figure 3F–H).

  We also validated the correlation between GDF15 and cell stemness in the transcriptome of patients with melanoma. First, cell stemness was highly expressed in all five melanoma cell subtypes identified (online supplemental figure 14A). We further divided the melanoma cells into high GDF15 melanoma group and low GDF15 melanoma cell group according to the difference in GDF15 expression, among which C0 BCAN+ melanoma cells and C1 ECM1+ melanoma cells showed higher GDF15 expression (online supplemental figure 14B). Consistent with our experimental results, the expression of MYC and SOX2 in high GDF15 melanoma cells was significantly higher than that in low GDF15 melanoma cells, but the expression of NANOG was not significant in both (online supplemental figure 14C). This result was also confirmed in online supplemental figure 14D At the same time, we also looked at the differential genes and biological functions of different melanoma cell subtypes (online supplemental figures 14E–H).

  To investigate the role of the GDF15 receptor GFRAL in this mechanism, we generated and validated GFRAL knockdown melanoma cell lines (figure 3I). Results revealed that the stemness of melanoma cells was significantly enhanced after treatment with CAFsGDF15+ supernatants or co-culture, and this enhancement was suppressed on GFRAL knockdown (figure 3J). Sphere formation assays confirmed these findings (figure 3K). Clonogenic assays demonstrated a significant increase in the proliferative capacity of tumor cells following treatment with recombinant human GDF15 protein, which was also inhibited by GFRAL knockdown (figure 3L).

  To validate this hypothesis in vivo, we used a subcutaneous tumor model in NOD/SCID mice. Co-injection with CAFsGDF15+ and shCtrl-A375 cells resulted in a greater tumor-initiating capacity compared with shCtrl-A375 cells alone, shGDF15-CAFsGDF15+ and shCtrl-A375 cells, or CAFsGDF15+ and shGFRAL-A375 cells, as determined by limiting dilution xenograft analysis (figures 3M, P). Mechanistic studies indicated that the stemness of melanoma cells-enhancing effect of GDF15 was mitigated by knockdown GFRAL (figure 3N). Subsequent sphere formation assays (figure 3O) and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) experiments (figure 3Q and R) further validated this hypothesis.


### GDF15 upregulates the self-renewal ability of melanoma cells via RET/AKT/ERK1/2 signaling in melanoma cells
  To investigate how GDF15 regulates the stemness and proliferative capacity of melanoma cells through signaling pathways, we designed a series of experiments as depicted in figure 4A. #15CAFsGDF15+, #22CAFsGDF15+, and #25CAFsGDF15+ were infected with adenovirus to down-express GDF15, as confirmed by transfection efficiency shown in figure 4B, which significantly reduced GDF15 expression in CAFsGDF15+. Cloning experiments demonstrated that co-cultivation with CAFsGDF15+ or their supernatant notably enhanced melanoma cell proliferation. However, this effect was attenuated on knocking down GDF15 in CAFs GDF15+(figure 4C), corroborated by qRT-PCR results (figures 4E, F). Concurrently, we observed that supplementation of recombinant human GDF15 protein significantly increased levels of CCL18 and TGF-β in the supernatant, whereas other cytokines like IL-8 remained unchanged (figure 4D). Consistent with this, CCL18 and TGF-β protein expressions were significantly increased, but IL-8 levels did not change significantly (figure 4G). We conducted experiments using GFRAL-knockdown melanoma cells and CAFsGDF15+ to ascertain which tumor receptors mediate these effects. Interestingly, co-cultured melanoma cells showed enhanced proliferation, which was reversed by GFRAL-knockdown (figure 4H). qRT-PCR results supported this hypothesis that the regulation of tumor stemness was impaired in GFRAL-knockdown melanoma cells, while melanoma cells with intact GFRAL exhibited enhanced stemness (figures 4J, K). Western blotting assays further validated these findings (figure 4M). Further investigation focused on whether GDF15 and GFRAL regulate tumor-secreted CCL18 and TGF-β. We found that adding recombinant human GDF15 protein to melanoma cell cultures significantly increased secretion of CCL18 and TGF-β, which was reversed on GFRAL knockdown in melanoma cells (figure 4I). The qRT-PCR and ELISA confirmed that the upregulation of CCL18 and TGF-β expression was mediated by GDF15 (figures 4O–R). To evaluate the functional role of CAFsGDF15+ in vivo, we isolated the CAFsGDF-15+ subtype via flow cytometry and co-injected them with melanoma cells into mice. Bioluminescence imaging revealed that co-injection with CAFsGDF15+ significantly enhanced tumor burden. In contrast, knockdown GDF-15 in CAFsGDF15+ or knocking down its receptor GFRAL in tumor cells markedly attenuated tumor growth (figure 4L).

  Consistent findings were observed in C57BL/6 mice. Co-implantation of melanoma cells and CAFsGDF15+ led to enhanced tumor progression, which was significantly reduced on CRISPR-Cas9-mediated knockdown of GDF-15 in CAFs or GFRAL in tumor cells (figure 4L and online supplemental figures 15A-F). Together, these results highlight the critical role of GDF-15/GFRAL signaling in mediating CAFs-tumor cell communication to promote tumor growth in vivo. To elucidate the signaling pathways involved in these effects, we conducted western blotting analysis after adding recombinant human GDF15 protein to melanoma cell cultures or knocking down GFRAL in melanoma cells. The results demonstrated significant activation of RET/AKT/ERK1/2 signaling pathway on recombinant human GDF15 protein addition while knocking down GFRAL on melanoma cells inhibited the activation of this signaling pathway (figure 4N).


## GDF15 upregulates CCL18 and TGF-β via RET/Smad signaling in melanoma cells
  As GDF15, a member of the TGF-β superfamily, is a novel cytokine intricately linked with cytokine-mediated signaling pathways, we evaluated its impact on the expression of cytokines, chemokines, and their receptors in melanoma cells. Among these, CCL18 and TGF-β were most significantly upregulated. The GDF15-induced expression of CCL18 and TGF-β was substantially suppressed by RET knockdown, which also considerably inhibited the phosphorylation of the Smad pathway, suggesting that RET/Smad may be responsible for GDF15-mediated upregulation of CCL18 and TGF-β (figure 5A). Notably, knockdown of RET significantly reduced GDF15-mediated enhancement of melanoma cell stemness, while the administration of CCL18 and TGF-β partially restored the stem-like property (figure 5B).

  RET-mediated cell proliferation is partially dependent on CCL18 and TGF-β. Specifically, CCL18 and TGF-β directly promoted the proliferation of melanoma cells, and GDF15-induced multiplication capacity could be partially reversed by CCL18 and TGF-β neutralizing antibodies (figure 5C). To explore the transcriptional mechanisms affecting cell stemness, we used TFDB screening to predict relevant transcription factors and validated them using ChIP and dual-luciferase gene reporter assays. ChIP assays revealed that transcription factor EP300 directly binds to putative sites in the promoter regions of SOX2, NANOG, and c-MYC. These genes were upregulated in cells treated with CCL18 but downregulated on CCL18 blockade in GDF15-treated cells, indicating that EP300 directly regulated the expression of SOX2, NANOG, and c-MYC (figures 5D–I). EP300 knockdown effectively inhibited the stem-like properties of melanoma cells (figure 5J), indicating that CCL18 potentially facilitated melanoma stemness via EP300 upregulation. Nuclear accumulation is essential for EP300 activation and subsequent gene regulation. Significant nuclear translocation of EP300 was observed on CCL18 and GDF15 treatment in melanoma cells, while blockade of CCL18 markedly reduced EP300 nuclear translocation induced by GDF15 (figures 6A–B).


### Crosstalk among CAFGDF15+, tumor cells, and macrophages mediated by GDF15, CCL18, and TGF-β
  We focused on the effect of CCL18 and TGF-β on macrophage biology. Macrophages isolated from the peripheral blood of healthy donors were identified as CD163+ and CD206+, with more than half remaining viable for 24 hours in vitro (figures 6C, E). As anticipated, rhCCL18 significantly enhanced macrophage recruitment (figure 6F). RhTGF-β induced an increase in M2 macrophages but a decrease in M1 macrophages, whereas rhCCL18 did not induce an increase in M1 or a reduction in M2 macrophages (figure 6D). Supernatants harvested from tumor cells treated with GDF15 or CAFsGDF15+ supernatant or co-cultured with CAFsGDF15+, displayed a greater potential for recruiting macrophages, which was efficiently attenuated through CCL18 blockade, the experimental design was shown in figure 6G.

  In addition, macrophages treated with specific conditions showed a high level of M2 polarization trend, and TGF-β blocking could inhibit this trend. At the same time, the secretion of TNF-α by melanoma cells treated with rhGDF15 and CAFsGDF15+ supernatant or co-culture was downregulated, and IDO was upregulated. TGF-β antibody could also reverse this secretion trend (figures 6H-J). In particular, the secretion of the IL-10 secreted by melanoma cells showed a decreasing trend under the influence of rhGDF15 or CAFsGDF15+, while the secretion of IL-10 by melanoma cells increased after the addition of TGF-β antibody. Assessing the profile expression in the presence of small-molecule inhibitors, we identified ERK1/2 signaling as involved in TGF-β-driven macrophage M2-polarization. Inhibition of ERK1/2 reversed this effect (figure 6K, L).

  Compared with mice injected with shCtrl-A375 cells and CAFsGDF15+, the fluorescence intensity in mice injected with shCtrl-A375, rhGDF15, and CAFsGDF15+ was significantly enhanced. This result was confirmed in both knockout mice and NC mice (figure 6M,N). Similar results were obtained in C57BL/6 mice with skin-specific deletion of GDF15 using the Cre-loxP system. Co-injection of melanoma cells with CAFGDF15+ cells markedly promoted tumor progression; an effect further enhanced by administration of rhGDF15. Notably, tumor burden was significantly reduced following CRISPR-Cas9–mediated knockdown of GDF15 in CAFs or GFRAL in tumor cells, with even more pronounced suppression observed in skin-specific GDF15 knockout mice (figure 6O).

  Similar trends were also observed in GDF15/GFRAL conditional knockout animals, indicating that GDF-15 facilitates macrophage recruitment within the TME (figure 6P). In macrophage infusion experiments, intratumoral macrophage infiltration 48 hours postinfusion was significantly elevated in mice co-injected with shCtrl-A375, rhGDF15, and CAFsGDF15+ compared to those receiving only shCtrl-A375 and CAFsGDF15+. In summary, these findings highlight the critical role of GDF15/GFRAL signaling-mediated CAFs-tumor cells communication in promoting tumor progression and macrophage infiltration in vivo.


## Discussion
  Fibroblasts receive continuous stimulation from various carcinogenic signals in the TME and eventually evolve into CAFs, assisting the rapid progression of tumors.25 Currently, there is extensive research on the role of CAFs in various cancers.26 27 It has been found that specific CAF subtypes preferentially locate in and around the tumor and exhibit higher cell stemness.28 The C2 GDF15+ CAFs in this study was significantly more stem and was primarily recruited into the peritumoral stroma. We elucidated the heterogeneity of CAFs in melanoma and identified four CAFs subtypes named after their highly expressed genes. C0 SFRP2+ CAFs exhibited a relatively low CytoTRACE2 score, indicating a higher degree of differentiation, reduced differentiation potential. This may be related to the fact that SFRP2 mainly functions as a tumor suppressor.29 30 NDUFA4L2 is associated with tumor formation, high invasiveness and poor survival.31 32 PCLAF has been shown to be associated with tumor cell proliferation in many cancers. Consistent with these results, our experimental results also confirmed that PCLAF was highly expressed in melanoma-derived CAFs.

  GDF15 are secreted proteins that are members of the TGF-β superfamily. Our study demonstrated significantly higher GDF15 protein levels in melanoma tissues than in paired normal tissues or dermatitis patient sera. Subsequent analysis revealed that CAFs had a significantly higher level of GDF15 expression than both normal fibroblasts and melanoma cells. Interestingly, this result has been verified in multiple cell lines, that is, melanoma cells themselves have low expression of GDF15, while CAFs have significantly high expression of GDF15.

  Based on the CNV score and cell stemness analysis, we speculated that GDF15+ CAFs might exhibit higher cellular plasticity. This can explain the higher differentiation potential of GDF15+ CAFs.33 34 In fact, our team has shown that when CAFs overexpressing GDF15 are co-cultured with cancer cells, melanoma cell proliferation, invasion, and migration are increased, but melanoma development is inhibited when GDF15 is silenced. According to enrichment analysis, this subgroup of CAFs was highly enriched in biological processes such as protein folding, cell differentiation, and development.

  We scored our CAF subgroups using a functional gene set unique to CAFs. Interestingly, GDF15+ CAFs had a higher apCAFs score; this rating includes expressions of HLA-DRB1, CD74, and HLA-DRA. Multiplex fluorescence staining also confirmed that apCAFs may be associated with the tumor immune microenvironment. Previous research has linked apCAFs to tumor immunity, particularly in increasing the activation and infiltration of CD4+ T cells.35 At the same time, studies have shown that CAFs also promote immune evasion in tumor cells by expressing major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II molecules.36 Furthermore, apCAFs have been observed to present MHC-II-restricted antigens, activating CD4 T cells and promoting regulatory T cell expansion, thereby enhancing tumor growth in vivo.37 Therefore, GDF15+ CAFs are a type of CAFs that may have the functional tendency of apCAFs. Targeting this type of CAFs may be a new immunotherapy for the treatment of melanoma in the future.

  TGF-β is a crucial cytokine that regulates cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and extracellular matrix synthesis. Aberrant expression or mutation of its receptor TGFBR2 may lead to abnormal activation or inhibition of the TGF-β signaling pathway, thereby promoting tumorigenesis, progression, and metastasis. Consistent with previous studies,3840 our study also confirmed the combination of GDF15–TGFBR2. Based on previous studies, we confirmed that FOXP1 regulates the transcription of GDF15 by binding to the TFBS1 and TFBS2 sites of the GDF15 promoter, further inducing the phosphorylation of the receptor TGFBR2 on melanoma cells, activating TGF-β signaling, and ultimately accelerating the progression of melanoma.

  The research confirmed that the dysregulated expression of GDF15 accelerated melanoma cell proliferation and tumor progression in mice by upregulating the expression of stemness-associated genes SOX2, NANOG, and c-MYC in melanoma cells. knockdown its receptor, GFRAL, inhibited this effect. In-depth research found that this is related to the binding of GDF15 to GFRAL to activate the tyrosine kinase RET signal, which further induces the activation of AKT/ERK1/2 signal.

  We also looked at the relationship between CAFs, macrophages, and melanoma cells in light of the synergistic role of CAFs in tumor immunosuppression. Activation of the TGF-β/Smad pathway has been shown to promote M2 macrophage polarization.41 Through its binding to GFRAL, GDF15, which is overexpressed on CAFs, controls the release of TGF-β and CCL18 by tumor cells. An immunosuppressive environment is fostered by this well-established process, which causes M2 macrophage polarization and sustains chronic inflammatory activation within the TME. TGF-β and CCL18 are also markers of M2 macrophage activation.42 43 Studies have shown that this process is linked to RET kinase activation and the nuclear accumulation of the transcription factor EP300. Notably, the receptor for GDF15 is a GFRAL-RET heterodimer,41 44 meaning GDF15 not only exerts biological effects through GFRAL but also activates the RET co-receptor. This activation triggers a cascade of signaling events that further amplify the pathway by which GDF15 accelerates melanoma progression.

  The relationship between GDF15 and M2 macrophages varies across different tumor models. GDF15 promotes the M2 phenotype transformation of macrophages in esophageal and prostate cancers.45 46 However, in lung cancer, the expression of GDF15 in tumor is negatively correlated with M2 macrophages.47 These differences can be attributed to the varying molecular mechanisms and intervention pathways in different cancers. In our study, CAFsGDF15+, rather than melanoma cells, exhibited high expression of GDF15. Therefore, GDF15-mediated M2 polarization is a result of the interaction between CAFs, melanoma cells, and macrophages. These findings offer new insights and potential directions for targeted macrophage therapy, complementing previous research in the field.48 49 Moreover, to prove it, we first demonstrated the key subtype C2 GDF15+ CAFs that can act on macrophages through the APP-CD74 signaling pathway through CellChat. Subsequently, we explored the risk grouping we constructed and the immune infiltration landscape under GDF15 signaling, and visualized the expression differences of specific immune cells, providing another possible immunological perspective for melanoma treatment.

  It has yet to be confirmed whether GDF15 inhibitors work well in conjunction with the current melanoma therapies. Second, the study only considered AM and CM as melanoma subtypes, which reduced the findings’ generalizability.

  In summary, GDF15 overexpression on CAFs markedly accelerates melanoma progression and metastasis by activating tumor cell and macrophage responses. This highlights GDF15 as a promising target for precision therapy and immunotherapy, providing a robust theoretical foundation for the development of future antitumor drugs.
