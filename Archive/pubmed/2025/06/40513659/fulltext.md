# SAE1 emerges as a pan-cancer driver and key regulator of HCC metastasis


## Graphical abstract
  Graphical abstract


## Introduction
  SUMOylation, a conserved posttranslational modification, plays critical yet complex roles in cancer pathology [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. The process operates through three sequential enzymatic steps, initiated by the activating enzyme E1 (SUMO E1, also known as SAE1/2) [1,6]. SUMOylation demonstrates dual roles in cancer development: it can promote tumorigenesis by maintaining cancer stem cell populations in breast and carcinomas [7]. Noteworthy is the propensity of SUMOylated oncoproteins to experience enhanced stability and increased transformative capacity [8,9]. A typical example is the SUMOylation of MYC, disrupting its phosphorylation, thus amplifying MYC stability and eventually facilitating MYC-driven carcinogenesis in B-cell lymphoma [8,9]. Conversely, SUMOylation can also counteract tumorigenesis by modifying tumor suppressors or attenuating oncoprotein function. For instance, SUMOylation stabilizes and activates p53 which has been demonstrated to inhibit tumor growth [10]. It also deactivates PIP3, suppressing anchorage-independent cell proliferation in prostate cancer cells [11].

  SUMOylation and ubiquitination exhibit intricate crosstalk in regulating protein fate and function, where SUMOylation can either compete with or facilitate ubiquitination at the same lysine residues, orchestrating diverse cellular processes, including protein stability, localization, and activity [12,13]. The ubiquitination process functions through a sophisticated enzymatic network, where E3 ubiquitin ligases like UBR7 mediate protein ubiquitination [14], while deubiquitinating enzymes such as USP12 reverse this process [15]. Recent studies reveal that this dynamic balance between modifications plays critical roles in tumor development and progression [16,17], suggesting potential therapeutic implications.

  Within the SUMOylation cascade, SUMO E1 functions as a heterodimer comprising SAE1 and UBA2 (ubiquitin-like modifier-activating enzyme 2). SAE1 has been found to be upregulated in glioma, gastric cancer, and papillary thyroid cancer [[18], [19], [20]]. In glioma, the increased SAE1 expression leads to elevated SUMOylation level of protein kinase B(AKT), which increases AKT phosphorylation and activates downstream cell cycle-related processes, ultimately promoting glioma cell growth both in vitro and in vivo [19]. In gastric cancer, SAE1 becomes a target of miR-129-3p, resulting in inhibitory effects on cell proliferation, migration, and invasion [20].

  While SAE1 has been implicated in several cancer types, its specific role and mechanisms in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) progression remain poorly understood. HCC, accounting for 80 % to 90 % of primary liver cancer, has a dismal 5-year survival rate below 20 % in the advanced stage. Current treatments, including sorafenib and immunotherapy, show limited efficacy and are often associated with drug resistance, underscoring the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies. Given SAE1′s critical function in initiating SUMOylation and its potential as a therapeutic target, elucidating its role in HCC could provide crucial insight for comprehending liver cancer biology and helping develop new treatment strategies [21].

  The objective of this study is to elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which SAE1 affects the progression of HCC. We employ a unique multi-omics approach, integrating genomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics data, along with advanced structural prediction tools like AlphaFold3. This comprehensive strategy allows us to uncover novel interactions and mechanisms underlying SAE1′s role in HCC progression. Our study aims to establish SAE1 as a prognostic biomarker and elucidate the molecular mechanisms through which SAE1 promotes HCC progression. We found that upregulated SAE1 expression is correlated with poorer prognosis and higher driver gene mutation frequencies in six pan-cancers. Notably, heightened SAE1 expression showed a significant association with increased morbidity among HCC patients. We also unveil a novel interaction between SAE1 and a transcription factor (TF), Yin-yang 1 (YY1), within the cellular nucleus and characterized this interaction employing AlphaFold3. Consequently, the upregulation of SAE1 elevated Wnt3a transcript levels, thereby activating the Wnt pathway and consequentially promoting cancer progression. Our findings reveal a previously unknown mechanism by which SAE1 promotes HCC progression, offering new insights into the molecular pathways driving this disease.


## Material and methods

### Cell lines
  The human HCC cell line PLC cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection biobank. The Huh7 cell line was obtained from the Japanese Collection of Research Biosources. HEK293T cells, MHCC97H and HCCLM3 were bought from Shanghai Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology Co. MHCC97L and HCCLM3 were cultured following previous research [22,23]. All cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS; Invitrogen) and 1 % penicillin–streptomycin (Invitrogen) incubated in a humidified atmosphere of 5 % CO2 at 37 °C. The specific experimental details of the cell experiment are shown in the Supplementary Method.


### Mouse models
  BALB/c-nude male mice aged 4 weeks were purchased from Aoyide Laboratory Animal Technology (Beijing, China). For the in situ xenograft, 2 × 106 cells, suspended in 20  μl DMEM and 20 μl Matrigel, was injected into the mouse's left hepatic lobe. Mice were examined twice a week and were euthanized 6 weeks later, and mouse liver tissues were collected for analysis. For the vein tail injection, 2 × 106 cells were suspended in 200  μl PBS and injected into the mouse's lateral tail vein. The mice were euthanized 4 weeks after injection. The harvested mouse tissues were fixed in paraformaldehyde for 24 h, embedded in paraffin (Leica Biosystems, Richmond, USA), and cut into 5–7 μm sections for following hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. For H&E staining, the tissue sections were de-waxed with xylene, rehydrated, and stained using an H&E staining kit (Beyotime Institute of Biology, Suzhou, China). All animal assay protocols were approved by the animal experimentation committee of Zhengzhou University (license no. ZZU-LAC20220729[02]).

  For detailed materials and methods, please refer to the Supplementary Meterial.


## Results

### Validation of SAE1 as a cancer-enriched prognostic biomarker
  To understand the potential roles of SUMO proteins in HCC metastasis, we conducted an analysis of expression changes in 23 genes within the SUMOylation pathway across the transcriptomes of three HCC cell lines exhibiting varying degrees of metastatic ability-Huh7, MHCC97L, and HCCLM3 (Fig. 1A, left panel). Our investigation revealed that among these genes, SAE1 exhibited the highest correlation coefficient with metastatic potential (Fig. 1A, right panel), suggesting SAE1 is a promising candidate indicator for HCC. To ascertain whether SAE1 could function as a universal cancer biomarker across multiple cancer types, we explored the expression level-dependent survival probabilities of SAE1 in 28 pan-cancer subtypes (C1-C28). Pan-cancer subtypes are classified into several groups: 12 homogeneous subgroups (C6:OV, C8:UCEC, C11:LGG [IDH1 mut], C12:THCA, C14:LUAD, C15:SKCM/UVM, C16:PRAD, C19:BRCA [luminal], C21:DLBC, C22: TGCT, C24:LAML, C26:LIHC), copy-number alteration-associated subtypes (C2, C7, C13, C17), kidney malignancies (C28), gastrointestinal malignancies (C1, C4, C18), squamous cell malignancies (C10, C25, C27), CNS/endocrine-related cancers (C5), GBM/LGG [IDH1 wild type] (C23), subtypes defined by immune/stromal features (C3, C20), and KICH grouped with ACC (C9). These subtypes represent distinct molecular signatures associated with different cell-of-origin patterns in cancers [24]. For mitigating biases arising from variations in tissue-specific expression, we normalized SAE1 expression levels in different tumors by those of corresponding normal tissues. The dichotomy of SAE1 expression levels into two groups for each subtype showed variable survival probabilities, with six subtypes exhibiting significant differences in survival rates (Fig. 1B–G). Specifically, subtypes C26, C6, C8, and C9 predominantly encompassed cancers originating from liver, ovarian, uterine, and kidney tissues, respectively. Alternatively, C15 and C27 primarily included various types of melanoma and squamous cell carcinoma. Notably, C26, associated with HCC, showed the most significant difference in survival among the 6 pan-cancer subtypes (Fig. 1B). In 7 of the remaining 22 subtypes (C5, C7, C11, C12, C17, C22, and C25), patients expressing higher levels of SAE1 exhibited a trend towards shorter lifespans compared to those with lower SAE1 expression levels, though statistical significance was not achieved (Supplementary Fig. S1).   A typical tumor routinely harbors 2 to 8 driver gene mutations that serve as catalysts to promote tumorigenesis [25]. To explore the potential association between SAE1 expression and driver genes, we initially identified 142 driver genes across the six pan-cancer subtypes previously delineated (see Fig. 1B–G). Subsequently, we focused on 6 driver genes (TP53, PTEN, RB1, NFE2L2, CTNNB1, and CDKN2A), as they were represented in at least 3 pan-cancer subtypes, indicating potential involvement in the carcinogenesis of multiple pan-cancer subtypes (Fig. 1H). Notably, TP53 driver mutations were consistently observed across all 6 subtypes (Fig. 1H). To discern the likelihood that increased SAE1 mRNA expressions in patients from the 6 subtypes could be attributed to these 6 driver genes (Fig. 1I–N), our analysis unveiled a significant enrichment of high SAE1 expression levels in patients with TP53 mutation in C26, C15, C8, C9, and C27. This implies that TP53 mutations may play a contributory role in promoting SAE1 expressions across multiple cancer subtypes. Additionally, mutations in RB1, CTNNB1, and CDKN2A were also identified as potential impactors of increased SAE1 expression levels. In summation, our findings posit SAE1, functioning as an independent cancer-enriched prognostic biomarker, with potential associations with driver genes implicated in the landscape of carcinogenesis.


### SAE1 upregulation is associated with adverse prognosis in HCC patients and facilitates cell migration and invasion in HCC cells
  After identifying SAE1 as a potential prognostic marker, we next sought to understand its functional role in HCC progression. The examination of SAE1 expression unfolded through an assessment of 50 pairs of HCC tissue and para-tumor tissue samples (Supplementary Table S1). Significantly elevated SAE1 expression levels were corroborated in HCC tissues, validating by both qPCR (Fig. 2A) and Western blotting assays (Supplementary Fig. S2A). Moreover, TCGA data pertaining to liver cancer affirmed the upregulation of SAE1 in tumors relative to normal tissues (Fig. 2B). Delving into potential clinicopathological factors, our investigation revealed a significant correlation between elevated SAE1 expression levels and increased HCC vascular invasiveness, coupled with larger tumor sizes in HCC. This trend was observed in additional HCC clinical data from the ONCOMINE database (Fig. 2C; Supplementary Fig. S2B). To further validate these findings in an independent cohort, we conducted immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis using 53 pairs of HCC tissue and para-tumor tissue samples. The results consistently exhibited stronger SAE1 immunostainings in tumor tissues compared to para-tumor tissues (Fig. 2D, Supplementary Fig. S2C).Fig. 2Association of upregulated SAE1 expression with poor prognosis in HCC patients (A) Evaluation of SAE1 expression levels in HCC and corresponding peri-tumor tissue samples from 50 patients using qRT-PCR analysis.   Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed a compelling association between higher SAE1 expressions in tumors and significantly shorter overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) in HCC patients (Fig. 2E). Conducting univariate Cox regression analysis encompassing 10 clinicopathological factors, we identified several significant correlations with OS, including TNM stage (II-IV), BCLC stage (B-C), T stage (T3/T4), satellite metastasis, macrovascular invasion, larger tumor size, intraoperative hemorrhage, intraoperative blood transfusion, higher AFP level, and upregulated SAE1 expression (Fig. 2F, left panel). Notably, among these factors, upregulated SAE1 expression, along with satellite metastasis, macrovascular invasion, and higher AFP level, emerged as significant independent prognostic factors for poorer OS in HCC patients, as revealed by multivariate Cox regression analysis (Fig. 2F, right panel). Similarly, univariate Cox regression analyses identified upregulated SAE1 expression as a significantly correlated factor to DFS, while on multivariate analysis, higher AFP level and upregulated SAE1 expression were confirmed as independent prognostic indicators (Fig. 2G). Again, the TCGA HCC dataset demonstrated poor OS and DFS outcomes for patients with elevated SAE1 expression levels (Fig. 2H). Collectively, our findings indicate that increased SAE1 expression stands as an independent predictor of poorer prognosis in HCC patients, implicating SAE1 upregulation in the promotion of HCC progression and metastasis.

  To substantiate the impact of SAE1 expression changes on HCC metastasis, a series of in vitro experiments were conducted to elucidate its role in cell migration and invasiveness. SAE1 was stably overexpressed in PLC and Huh7 cells and downregulated in HCCLM3 and MHCC97H cells (Supplementary Fig. S2D). Notably, this overexpression significantly increased cell migration in PLC and Huh7 cells, as evidenced by enhanced migratory ability observed in both wound-healing (Supplementary Fig. S3A–B)and Transwell chamber assays (Supplementary Fig. S3E–F). Conversely, the downregulation of SAE1 in HCCLM3 and MHCC97H cells demonstrated an impairment in migratory capability (Supplementary Figs. S3C–D & G–H). In Matrigel invasion assays, SAE1 overexpression enhanced the invasiveness of PLC and Huh7 cells, while SAE1 knockdown conversely reduced the invasiveness of HCCLM3 and MHCC97H cells (Supplementary Figs. S3E–H). These collective findings confirm that SAE1 overexpression plays a promotive role in both HCC cell migration and invasiveness.

  We also observed the enhancement in cell migration of SAE1′s role in three distinct cancer cell lines selected explicitly from cancer subtypes shown in Fig. 1B: endometrial cancer (from subtype C27), lung squamous cell carcinoma (from subtype C27), and ovarian cancer (from subtype C6). These results consistently demonstrate that SAE1 promotes cell migration across different cancer types (Supplementary Fig. S4).


### SAE1 interacts with YY1 in HCC cells
  Our observations of SAE1′s effects on HCC progression led us to explore the mechanistic underpinnings of SAE1 functions in HCC metastasis. We conducted a large-scale screening of potential interacting proteins of SAE1. This involved an integrated analysis of microarray data (GEO accession ID: GSE97626) and the SAE1 protein interactome (Fig. 3A). Employing SAE1 co-expression analysis on the microarray data, we identified 2840 genes with a potentially similar expression pattern to SAE1 in HCC cells. Subsequently, immunoprecipitation (IP) with SAE1 antibodies coupled with mass spectrometry was performed (Supplementary Fig. S5A, Supplementary Table S5). These data led to the enrichment of 364 proteins by SAE1 antibodies, with 72 proteins overlapping with SAE1 co-expressed genes (Fig. 3B, left panel). These SAE1-interacting proteins encompassed four major biological functions: binding, catalytic activity, transcription regulator activity, and translation regulator activity (Fig. 3B, right panel), thus providing a more comprehensive overview of the biological functions of SAE1 in HCC metastasis and suggesting its potential roles in diverse cellular processes.Fig. 3Interaction between SAE1 and YY1 in HCC cells (A) Overview of the strategy used to identify interacting proteins of SAE1 in HCC cells.   Within these SAE1-interacting proteins, we focused on one of hub genes in the SAE1 co-expression network, YY1 (Fig. 3B). We confirmed the co-expression of YY1 with SAE1 in the TCGA HCC patients (Supplementary Fig. S5B). Moreover, in pan-cancer subtypes C26, C8, C27, and C15 patients expressing both high levels of SAE1 and YY1 exhibited a shorter lifespan (Fig. 3C, Supplementary Fig. S6). Then, the endogenous interaction between SAE1 and YY1 was investigated through Co-IP. As shown in Fig. 3D, YY1 was immunoprecipitated with SAE1 antibodies in HCCLM3 and MHCC97H cells. Reciprocally, IP using YY1 antibodies successfully pulled down SAE1 (Fig. 3D). To ascertain the specificity of the SAE1 and YY1 association in HCC cells, we co-expressed HA-tagged SAE1 and His-tagged YY1 plasmids in 293 T cells. As anticipated, an HA antibody facilitated the pull-down of His-YY1, and in the reciprocal IP, a His antibody effectively pulled down HA-SAE1, confirming the in vivo interaction between SAE1 and YY1 (Fig. 3E). Next, we observed that HA-SAE1 and His-YY1 fusion proteins exhibited colocalization within the nucleus when co-transfected in HCC cells (Fig. 3F). This colocalization was corroborated by a well-overlapped image of endogenous SAE1 and YY1 in the nucleus of HCCLM3 and MHCC97H cells (Fig. 3G). In conclusion, these findings indicate that SAE1 can interact with YY1 in the nucleus of HCC cells.


### SAE1 enhances protein stability and transcriptional activity of YY1 in HCC cells
  Prior research has reported that YY1 can undergo SUMOylation through interaction with a SUMO ligase, PIASy [26]. In light of this, we investigated whether the abundance of YY1 could be affected by SAE1 overexpression. As shown in Fig. 4A, SAE1-overexpressing cells exhibited a significant increase in YY1 protein levels compared to their corresponding control cells, while the knockdown of SAE1 led to a marked reduction in YY1 protein levels (Supplementary Fig. S7A). However, the mRNA level of YY1 remained unaltered by either SAE1 overexpression or knockdown (Supplementary Fig. S7B).   Next, to substantiate SAE1 function on YY1, we used cycloheximide (CHX) treatment to inhibit protein translation, and observed that the steady-state level of YY1 increased with SAE1 overexpression (Fig. 4B), resulting in significantly slower degradation kinetics. One possible explanation is that the heightened stability of the YY1 protein is a result of SAE1-mediated SUMOylation. To explore this potential hypothesis, we introduced an exogenous SUMO1 into PLC cells as a marker to ascertain if SAE1 can function in SUMOylating YY1. As anticipated, YY1 SUMOylation increased in cells overexpressing SAE1, as indicated by an upward shift in the YY1 band, potentially signifying SUMO-modified YY1 triggered by SAE1 activation (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, to provide additional evidence, we co-transfected both an E2 conjugation enzyme (Ubc9) and SUMO1, which resulted in a more pronounced SUMO-modified YY1 band compared to cells transfected only with SUMO1 (Fig. 4D). These findings suggest that SAE1 overexpression promotes the process of YY1 SUMOylation. Given that SUMOylation can compete with ubiquitination for lysine residues modification of substrates [27,28]. We investigated whether SAE1 overexpression could also affect YY1 ubiquitination. Moreover, ubiquitin conjugation of YY1 exhibited a significant reduction in SAE1-overexpressing PLC cells compared to control cells (Fig. 4E), suggesting that competion of ubiquitination-SUMOylation serves as a regulator of YY1 stability in SAE1-modified HCC cells.

  YY1, also known as an essential and ubiquitously expressed regulator of gene expression, functions by facilitating enhancer-promoter contacts [29]. Given that acetylation of histone H3 (acetyl-H3) serves as a marker for active gene promoters [30,31], we explored whether the binding of YY1 to acetyl-H3 could be affected by SAE1. Indeed, Fig. 4F displayed that the acetyl-H3 antibody enriched more YY1 in SAE1-overexpressing cells compared to control cells. Meanwhile, the YY1 antibody pulled down more acetyl-H3 with SAE1 overexpression (Fig. 4G), suggesting an augmented binding of YY1 to enhance acetylation of histone H3. Taken together, the interaction between SAE1 and YY1 impedes the proteolytic degradation of YY1 by SUMOylation and simultaneously enhances its prospective transcriptional activity by acetyl-H3.

Analyzing RNA-sequencing data from siYY1-treated M010817 melanoma cells and siSAE1-treated HCCLM3 cells (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Fig. S8A), we identified differentially expressed genes upon SAE1 and YY1 knockdown and subsequently enriched them into 43 and 80 pathways using gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), respectively (Fig. 5B, upper panel).Fig. 5SAE1-mediated upregulation of Wnt3a expression (A) Overview of the strategy used to identify genes co-regulated by SAE1 and YY1.   We recognized 13 overlapped pathways, including focal adhesion, Wnt signaling pathway, JAK-STAT signaling pathway, ECM-receptor interaction, etc. Notably, the Wnt signaling displayed similar enrichment scores in both siSAE1-HCC cells and siYY1-melanoma cells (Fig. 5B, below panel), showed by the significant rank metric of the gene set from the Wnt signaling pathway under GSEA analysis(Fig. 5C). These results indicate that the interaction between SAE1 and YY1 may regulate the transcription of genes within the Wnt pathway. Among significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the Wnt pathway in SAE1-knockdown HCCLM3 cells (Supplementary Fig. S8B), only Wnt3a was validated by qPCR, displaying a consistent expression change in both SAE1-knockdown and SAE1-overexpressing cells (Fig. 5D). Concurrently, with SAE1 upregulation, the protein levels of Wnt3a exhibited a significant increase (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, Wnt3a expression levels were upregulated in HCC tissues compared to peri-tumor samples (Fig. 5F) and showed a positive correlation with SAE1 expression in HCC tissues (Fig. 5G). Additionally, analysis of the pan-cancer TCGA data demonstrated that patients with simultaneous upregulation of SAE1 and Wnt3a experienced shortened OS and DFS in C15 and C26, including HCC and melanoma patients (Fig. 5H).


### SAE1 elevates Wnt3a expression by YY1 binding
  Given the observed effects on Wnt signaling, we next examined the structural basis of the SAE1-YY1 interaction and its binding to the Wnt3a promoter. To determine whether YY1 acts as a TF for Wnt3a, we first used JASPAR databases [32] to predict potential YY1 binding sites in the Wnt3a gene promoter (Supplementary Fig. S9A). We then confirmed the putative YY1 binding site on the Wnt3a promoter, which is located approximately −748 to −635 relative to the transcription start site, using ChIP assays (Fig. 6A, Supplementary Fig. S9B). Additionally, we employed Alphafold3 to model the YY1:Wnt3a promoter complex (Supplementary Fig. S10A). The PAE matrix for the model indicated that the C-terminal domain of YY1 (YY1-CTD, residues 298 to 414 of YY1) has a higher confidence in binding to the Wnt3a promoter (Supplementary Fig. S10B). According to the predicted model for YY1-CTD: Wnt3a promoter (-748 ∼ -635), the sequence spanning from −730 to −720 on the Wnt3a promoter interacts with the YY1-CTD. This interaction involved nine hydrogen bonds between residues of YY1-CTD and the Wnt3a promoter (Fig. 6B, Supplementary Fig. S10C). Furthermore, the binding of YY1 to the Wnt3a promoter was enhanced under SAE1 overexpression (Fig. 6C), which aligned with the increased Wnt3a expression in SAE1-overexpressing cells (Fig. 5D&E, Supplementary Fig. S10D). Conversely, the decreased expression of YY1 significantly hindered the enhanced binding of YY1 to the Wnt3a promoters in SAE1-overexpressing cells (Fig. 6C). Additionally, the capacity of YY1 to bind to Wnt3a promoters was notably reduced in SAE1-knockdown cells, and these effects could not be reversed even with overexpression of YY1 (Fig. 6D). These findings suggest that YY1 executes its transcriptional function in Wnt3a expression under the upregulated expression of SAE1 in HCC cells. Based on YY1-CTD is usually acetylated [33], the YY1-CTD: Wnt3a promoter model was revised to include SAE1 and acetylation (lysine 332) of YY1-CTD, which displayed higher confidence between YY1-CTD and Wnt3a promoter than that without SAE1 (Fig. 6B). In addition, YY1 binds to SAE1 and Wnt3a promoter at different positions along its peptide chain (Fig. 6E). Furthermore, we studied whether YY1 contributes to the impact of SAE1 on Wnt3a expression. We found that at both mRNA and protein levels, the increased Wnt3a expression facilitated by SAE1 overexpression can be rescued by YY1 downregulation (Fig. 6F). However, reducing SAE1 did not solely restore Wnt3a expression by upregulating YY1 (Fig. 6G). ### SAE1 promotes cell migration and invasive through activating the Wnt3a pathway
  Given our results on the upregulation of Wnt3a expression by SAE1, it is plausible that the Wnt pathway might be activated upon SAE1 overexpression. Typically, increased Wnt3a expression activates the canonical Wnt pathway, leading to upregulated expression of downstream genes [34]. We herein initially examined three established Wnt-target genes in HCC cells, namely TBX3, GLUL, and LRG5. The qPCR assays showed increased mRNA expression levels of TBX3, GLUL, and LRG5 in SAE1-overexpressing HCC cells, while knockdown of SAE1 resulted in decreased levels (Fig. 7A). Moreover, the upregulated expressions of these genes induced by SAE1 were counteracted by YY1 downregulation (Fig. 7B). Transwell assays were conducted involving the knockdown of YY1 in Huh7 cells that exhibit SAE1 overexpression. The results indicated that the downregulation of YY1 substantially reduced the enhancement of the migratory and invasive capacities of cells associated with SAE1 overexpression.   Next, we examined whether SAE1 affects cell migration and invasiveness via the activation of the Wnt3a pathway using the specific Wnt3a inhibitor, LGK-974. Wound-healing and Transwell chamber assays showed that SAE1-overexpressing cells treated with LGK-974 exhibited similar migratory and invasive abilities as the control cells (Fig. 7C–D). Additionally, considering the common involvement of β-catenin activation in the canonical Wnt pathway [35], we explored whether SAE1 affects the β-catenin pathway. Interestingly, SAE1 overexpression induced minimal changes in β-catenin expression levels, phosphorylation levels (Supplementary Fig. S12A–C), or cellular localization compared to its control (Supplementary Fig. S12D). This suggests that SAE1 might activate the non-canonical Wnt pathway.

  Having established the pro-metastatic effects of SAE1 in vitro, we proceeded to validate these findings in vivo using in situ and tail vein injection xenograft mouse models. Mice injected with SAE1-overexpressing cells showed a significant increase in in situ metastasis compared to control cells (Fig. 7E). In a tail vein injection mouse model, SAE1 overexpression notably induced lung metastasis of PLC cells (Fig. 7F). Consistently, in HCC tissue samples, we further observed the positive correlation between SAE1 and Wnt3a expressions (Fig. 7G). Additionally, considering the patient-derived organoids as a powerful instrument for disease modeling, we also overexpressed SAE1 in HCC organoids culturing. Truly, the expression of Wnt3a level was induced upon SAE1 overexpression (Supplementary Fig. S13A&B). In summary, our findings suggest that increased SAE1 expression promotes HCC metastasis, likely through the activation of the Wnt3a pathway.


## Discussion
  In cancer research, the concept of pan-cancer, focusing on cell-of-origin patterns, has emerged as a crucial direction [24]. In this study, we embraced pan-cancer subtypes, leveraging our microarray data derived from three HCC cell lines with different metastatic abilities [36,37]. Interestingly and firstly, our results not only position SAE1 as a potential prognostic biomarker in HCC but extend its significance as an independent cancer-enriching prognostic biomarker across various tissues. These tumors encompass tissues originating from the liver, ovaries, uterus, and kidneys, as well as subsets of melanoma and squamous cell carcinoma, suggesting broad applicability beyond specific cancer types. Previous studies have proven the oncogenic role of SAE1 in glioma and gastric cancer [6,19]. However, our analysis suggests that these cancers do not constitute major constituents of the six pan-cancer molecular subtypes exhibiting significant differences in survival rate. Secondly, our exploration of driver genes in these subtypes reveals a close association between mutations in TP53, RB1, CTNNB1, and CDKN2A and high SAE1 expression. Specifically, we observed that SAE1-associated HCC could be driven by TP53, but found no evidence implicating other known HCC drivers, such as CTNNB1 [38]. Thirdly, our selective focus on YY1 among potential interacting proteins stems from the significantly poorer survival observed in patients with elevated expression of both SAE1 and YY1 across 4 out of 6 pan-cancer subtypes. This suggests a broader implication of our study in understanding various tumors, for instance, melanoma (primarily observed in subtype C15). Lastly, our identification of the downstream effects of the SAE1-YY1 interaction, particularly involving the Wnt signaling pathway, was achieved by a comparative analysis of RNA-sequencing data from siSAE1-HCC cells in this study and siYY1-melanoma cells from a previous investigation [39]. Consistently, this SAE1-YY1-Wnt3a axis has a critical role in the survival ability of patients in related pan-cancer subtypes, C15 and C26.

  In the past, the SUMOylation pathway, recognized for its essential role in intracellular regulation [1], has garnered significant attention imbalanced components of the SUMOylation pathway have been identified in various tumors [[7], [8], [9], [10],40,41], impacting transcription by modifying transcription factors [9]. For example, the enzymatic activity of SUMO E1, particularly the subunit of UBA2, has been implicated in Myc-driven breast cancer [8]. Interestingly, our study reveals a distinct role for SAE1, the other subunit of SUMO E1, demonstrating its potency in increasing the transcriptional activity of YY1 in HCC cells. Further significant finding in our study is the identification of 72 novel potential interacting proteins of SAE1, encompassing a diverse array of molecular functions. A substantial portion of these interacting proteins are implicated in the regulation of transcription or translation, indicating the potential involvement of SAE1 in various cellular processes. In the narrative at hand, we focus on one of the transcription factors, YY1, among these interacting proteins.

  SUMOylation and ubiquitination are two major post-translational modifications that intricately regulate protein stability, with SUMOylation often competing with ubiquitination for the same lysine residues [[42], [43], [44]]. SUMO proteomics has revealed that a significant proportion (∼25 %) of known SUMOylation sites overlap with ubiquitination [45], highlighting the widespread nature of this regulatory crosstalk. This interaction is particularly significant in cancer development, as seen in breast cancer cells, where the E3 SUMO ligase BCA2 promotes IκBα SUMOylation to prevent its ubiquitin-mediated degradation, thereby enhancing cancer cell proliferation [44]. In HCC cells, we found that this SUMO-ubiquitin interplay regulates YY1, a transcription factor that fosters tumor malignancy through angiogenesis [46] and lipid accumulation [47]. Our study demonstrates that SAE1 binds to YY1 and enhances its stability by inducing SUMOylation and reducing ubiquitination. This finding extends previous observations where PIASy-mediated YY1 SUMOylation was noted in cos-7 cells [26], and a decrease in ubiquitin-dependent degradation of YY1 had been observed in HCC cells [48]. The complex regulation of YY1 stability is further evidenced by Smurf2-mediated ubiquitination promoting its degradation during the development of lymphoma [49]. Conversely, the deubiquitinase OTU deubiquitinase 3 protects YY1 from degradation and enhances colorectal cancer cell growth [50]. Drawing on these findings and established SUMO-ubiquitin crosstalk mechanisms [44,51], we propose that SAE1-mediated YY1 SUMOylation may enhance protein stability through three mechanisms: competing for specific lysine residues with ubiquitination, inducing conformational changes that mask ubiquitination sites, or recruiting deubiquitinating enzymes. The precise molecular mechanism, including whether this modification is direct or indirect, awaits further investigation.

  The regulatory mechanisms of YY1 on gene transcription have modular characteristics and depend on the cellular context, whether it activates or represses, and are still relatively obscure [42,45,52]. Based on previous reports, the DNA-binding zinc finger located at the YY1 C-terminal region is acetylated in the natural context and cannot be deacetylated by binding to histone deacetylases (HDACs), resulting in the stable activity of HDACs, which creates the transcriptional represser domain of YY1 [53]. In our study, we revealed that YY1 interacts with SAE1 using its C-terminal region. This interaction enhances the expression of Wnt3a and increases the proliferation, migration, and invasion of HCC cells. This could be due to the binding of YY1 to SAE1, which reduces the interaction of its C-terminal region with HDAC1, leading to a decrease in its inhibitory effect on gene expression. However, further exploration is needed to understand the possible involvement of epigenetic modification or other cofactors during the impact of SAE1 on YY1 stability and activity.

  Wnt3a is considered a canonical Wnt ligand in HCC [54] and a pivotal therapeutic target across diverse cancers. Here, the elevation of three well-established downstream genes (TBX3, GLUL, LGR5) [35] was noted in SAE1-overexpression HCC cells, underscoring the potential influence of SAE1 on the Wnt pathway. In addition, we showed that SAE1 decreases the sensitivity of HCC cells to the Wnt3a inhibitor LGK-974. Moreover, the elevated Wnt3a expression induced by SAE1 overexpression was contingent on the presence of YY1 thereby contributing to the progression of HCC. Surprisingly, our examination of the canonical Wnt signaling pathway’s key component, β-catenin [35], revealed no significant alterations in either cellular levels or phosphorylation levels with SAE1 overexpression. Moreover, the immunofluorescence assay indicated that SAE1 upregulation had no discernible impact on the intracellular distribution of β-catenin. These results collectively suggest that SAE1 might activate the non-canonical Wnt3a pathway in HCC cells. This aligns with the documented role of Wnt3a triggering in the non-canonical Wnt pathway during chondrogenesis in chondrocytes and mesenchymal stem cells [55].

  Targeting the Wnt pathway has emerged as a promising strategy to address the current deficit of systemic therapies for HCC; however, the efficiency of targeting this pathway needs further exploration [35]. Concurrently, the SUMOylation pathway has gained recognition as a developing anti-tumoral target. For example, the SUMO E1 inhibitor TAK-981 exhibits potent activity by inducting a type-1 interferon-dependent immune response and directly inducing cell death in lymphoma, acute myeloid leukemias, and pancreatic tumor cells [42,45,52]. In recent years, there has been a growing focus on synergistic strategies involving multi-drug combinations. Considering our novel findings connecting the SUMO E1 enzyme SAE1, epigenetic regulation, and cancer progression, we anticipate that the SAE1/YY1/Wnt3a axis could serve as a mechanistically relevant target in cancer.

  Our study provides valuable insights into SAE’s role in HCC progression, yet several limitations should be considered in future research. First, our patient cohort was relatively small and geographically limited. Future studies should include larger, more diverse patient populations to enhance results' generalizability. Additionally, since the molecular classification of HCC helps understand its complexity and informs personalized therapies [56], further exploration of the relationship between SAE1 and various molecular subtypes is essential. Our pan-cancer analysis revealed a significant correlation between TP53 mutations and increased SAE1 expression, suggesting potential regulation by p53 pathways. This implies that SAE1 expression may involve complex regulatory mechanisms with various signals, making it crucial to unravel its regulatory network, including transcription factors and microRNAs. Such studies could clarify the mechanisms behind SAE1′s dysregulation in HCC. Moreover, our study shows that SAE1 knockdown can inhibit HCC progression, but we haven't investigated specific SAE1 inhibitor development. Strategies may include designing small molecule inhibitors for the SAE1-YY1 interaction or its catalytic activity, peptide-based inhibitors for key binding areas, and techniques like PROTACs or antisense oligonucleotides for selective SAE1 degradation. These require extensive structural studies and drug screenings to ensure specificity and effectiveness. Off-target analyses are also essential to minimize side effects due to SAE1′s role in global SUMOylation pathways, such as observation of the differential effects between cancer cells and normal hepatocytes. Finally, to translate these findings into clinical practice, further testing of SAE1 inhibition in patient-derived samples and on the background of a fibrotic liver is needed to translate our findings into clinical applications. Addressing these limitations is crucial for fully understanding the role of SAE1 in HCC and providing a foundation for furture translational research toward therapeutic development.
