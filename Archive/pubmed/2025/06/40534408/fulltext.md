# Panaxatriol saponins exert anti-renal fibrosis by suppressing TNF-α mediated inflammation and TGF-β1/Smad3 signaling pathway


## Abstract
  Renal fibrosis is a key pathological process in the progression of chronic kidney disease (CKD). Panaxatriol saponins (PTS), the main bioactive compounds extracted from Panax notoginseng (Burk.) F.H. Chen, have demonstrated antioxidative and anti-inflammatory activities. This study aimed to investigate the potential protective effects of PTS against renal fibrosis and explore the underlying pharmacological mechanisms. A unilateral ureteral obstruction (UUO) model was established in Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats to induce renal fibrosis. Histopathological changes were assessed using hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining, Masson’s trichrome staining, and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Network pharmacology and molecular docking approaches were employed to identify potential signaling molecules through which PTS may mitigate renal fibrosis. Western blotting, quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR), and immunohistochemistry were utilized to validate the involvement of specific signaling pathways in PTS-mediated anti-fibrotic effects. Our data demonstrated that PTS alleviated renal dysfunction and provided protective effects against renal fibrosis, primarily through the TNF-α and TGF-β1 signaling pathways. Moreover, PTS treatment significantly downregulated pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and Smad3 activity. Additionally, PTS inhibited the expression of key fibrosis markers, including α-SMA, collagen I, and fibronectin. Our study suggests that PTS exert a prevention effect in renal fibrosis by blocking the TGF-β1/Smad3 signaling pathway.


## Introduction
  Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is one of the most common chronic diseases worldwide, with increasing morbidity and mortality rates in recent years [1,2]. Renal fibrosis is a progressive pathological morphology change, defined as the excessive accumulation of extracellular matrix (ECM) in the renal tissue, with the progression of CKD, especially in end-stage renal disease [3]. Renal fibrosis is primarily characterized by glomerulosclerosis, tubulointerstitial fibrosis, arteriosclerosis, and perivascular fibrosis. The severity of these pathological manifestations typically correlates with the prognosis of CKD [4]. Many studies have shown that this process involves dysregulation of several chemokines and inflammatory signaling pathways [5]. Besides, several immunity cells, such as lymphocytes, polymorphonuclear leukocytes, eosinophils, basophils, mast cells, and macrophages, are engaged in the chronic inflammation and fibrosis stimulation with CKD process, activating transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), epithelial growth factor receptor (EGFR), and interleukins to induces transformation of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts resulting in ECM deposition [6–8]. Macrophages have been studied more comprehensively, which regulate inflammation via phagocytosis, antigen presentation, and cytokine secretion. Activated macrophages release many proinflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin 6 (IL-6), TNF-α, and MMP, to facilitate renal fibrosis processes [9]. Studies indicated that TGF-β1 is the most important profibrotic cytokine for renal fibrosis, and downstream components activity status of the TGF-β1/Smad signaling pathway, such as Smad2 and Smad3, have been proven as keynote biomarkers [10–12]. In addition, Smad3 plays a role in activated fibroblasts expressing α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), fibronectin (FN), and collagen I (Col-I) via the recruitment of macrophages, accelerating renal fibrosis progression [13].

  Understanding these mechanisms involving renal fibrosis could provide feasible orients for potential drug discovery or improving existing therapeutic measures for CKD management. However, limited clinical treatment or anti-fibrotic drug options for renal fibrosis have confused us all so far. In recent years, studies have testified that various traditional Chinese herbal compounds, extracts, and monomers possess curative effects on renal fibrosis for CKD treatment [14–17]. Panaxatriol saponins (PTS) are active components extracted from Panax notoginseng (Burk.) F.H. Chen, a famous traditional Chinese medicinal herb. Researchers have manifested that PTS exhibit neurological protection for Parkinson’s disease and anti-platelet aggregation for ischemic stroke treatment [18–20]. While, its role in affecting kidney disease progression was reported rarely. Our previous studies have revealed that PTS could alleviate chronic kidney disease by regulating intestinal microbes in vivo and inhibiting the activation of NLRP3 inflammasome in HK2 cells in vitro [21,22]. However, the role of PTS in kidney function regulation and further mechanisms are ambiguous. Herein, we investigated PTS’s specific effect on renal fibrosis and the potential mechanism of anti-inflammatory signaling through network pharmacology and in vivo experiments. This study may offer valuable information for expanding the adaptation of PTS for CKD treatment.


## Materials and methods

### Isolation and identification of PTS
  PTS, extracted from Panax notoginseng (Burk.) F.H. Chen, were obtained from Huasun Group Co., Ltd. (Sichuan, China). PTS were composed of ginsenoside Rg1 (46.1%), notoginsenoside R1 (12.3%), and ginsenoside Re (5.7%), as determined by High Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC). The isolation and purification procedures are described elsewhere [21]. The chromatographic conditions were as follows: Chromatographic column: Length: 0.25 m, internal diameter: 4.6 mm. Stationary phase: Octadecylsilane chemically bonded to silica (5 μm). Column temperature: 30 °C. Mobile phase A: Acetonitrile (R). Mobile phase B: Water (R). Flow rate: 1.0 mL/min. Detector: Ultraviolet (UV) detector at 210 nm. Reference Solution: The reference solution was prepared by accurately weighing 25 mg of ginsenoside Rg1 CRS, 4 mg of ginsenoside Re CRS, and 8 mg of notoginsenoside R1 CRS. These were dissolved in a 10 mL volumetric flask containing acetonitrile (R) and water (R) (19.5:80.5, V/V), then diluted to 10 mL. Test Solution: To prepare the test solution, 0.12 g of PTS were added to a 25 mL volumetric flask. Then, 20 mL of acetonitrile (R) and water (R) (19.5:80.5, V/V) were added. The mixture was sonicated (power 160 W, frequency 40 kHz) for 30 min, allowed to cool, and subsequently diluted to 25 mL with the same solvent.


### Animals
  Eight-week-old male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (240–280 grams; certification: SCXK-chuan-2020-030) were provided by Da Shuo Biological Technology Co. Ltd. (Chengdu, China). All animals were bred and maintained in the SPF Laboratory Animal Center of the Chengdu Medical College. All experiments conformed to the Principles of Laboratory Animal Care and Guide for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the ethics committee of Chengdu Medical College (Ethical approval number: 2022-013). All rats had free access to food and water throughout the experiment.


### Animal experiments and sample preparation
  Twenty-four SD rats were randomly divided into four groups: sham, unilateral ureteral obstruction (UUO) model, low-dose PTS (PTS-L) (54 mg/kg/day), and high-dose PTS (PTS-H) (81 mg/kg/day) (n = 6). An equivalent dose table of the body surface areas of rats and humans was used to determine the clinical treatment dose, which served as the basis for determining the PTS dose. The UUO model was established as previously reported [23]. Animals were anesthetized using 5% chloral hydrate (7 mL/kg), and administered via intraperitoneal injection. After disinfection, the skin and subcutaneous tissue were separated layer-by-layer to expose and isolate the left ureter, which was cut after ligation using 4-0 silk sutures. The peritoneum, muscle layer, and skin were sutured. Sham rats underwent the same surgical procedure except for ureter ligation and disconnection. On the second successful modeling day, rats in the sham group and the model group were given physiological saline (equal volume for drugs, 0.5 mL/100 g/d) via gavage. The PTS-L and PTS-H groups were pretreated with the PTS by oral gavage. And the sham group did not receive PTS drug intervention. Interventions were administered daily for 21 consecutive days. Following 21 days of drug administration, rats were fasted (water allowed) for 18 h and transferred to metabolic cages equipped with mesh screens. Urine samples were collected over a 24-h period post-filtration. After anesthesia, blood samples from the same rats were collected from the femoral arteries. Kidney tissue specimens were collected following euthanization at the same time points.


### Measurement of blood and urine specimens
  An automated hematology analyzer [24] (Model: BC-2800Vet) detected red blood cells (RBCs), hemoglobin (Hb), white blood cells (WBCs), and platelets (PLTs). An automated biochemical analyzer [25] (Model: BS-240Vet) detected serum electrolytes, glucose, serum creatinine (Scr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and albumin. ELISA kits measured cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α, and TGF-β1) and microalbuminuria (MAU). Utilized quantitation kits to measure experimental rats’ urine creatinine, and 24-h urinary protein levels. The urine albumin to creatinine ratio (ACR) was calculated as the urine albumin divided by the urine creatinine. Each group contained six rats, and each experiment was performed independently three times. The MAU, IL-6, TNF-α, and TGF-β1 ELISA kits were purchased from Shanghai ZCIBIO Technology Co., Ltd. The urine protein quantitative test kit and urine creatinine assay kit were purchased from the Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China).


### Staining of kidney tissue
  We fixed isolated kidney tissues with a 4% formaldehyde solution, embedded them in paraffin, and made kidney paraffin sections with a thickness of about 5um. We performed Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) and Masson’s trichrome staining following standard staining kits procedure. Tissue slides were observed and photographed under a microscope (Leica DM2000). Collagen deposition in the renal interstitium served as a proxy for the severity of renal fibrosis. To observe changes in kidney appearance and morphology, such as the glomerular and mesangial basement membranes, PASM staining was performed for positive expression area analysis. HE-stained images were acquired at magnifications of 10× and 40×, while Masson-stained and PASM-stained images were captured at 40× magnification. The Image-Pro Plus (version 6.0; IPP) image analysis system was utilized for image quantification. Grayscale values corresponding to fibrotic areas were counted at 40× magnification in six random field views, and the percentage of area positive for the staining was estimated.


### Observe transmission electron microscope structure of renal tissue
  Some specimen slides were first fixed with 3% glutaraldehyde and then double-fixed with 2% osmium tetroxide. Concentration gradient acetone was applied for tissue dehydration, epoxy resin was utilized for infiltration and embedding, and an ultramicrotome was used to obtain the ultrathin sections of about 50 nm tissue. Uranyl acetate and lead citrate were used as the staining agents. The cell ultrastructures were observed and filmed under TEM (10000×), including the glomerular endothelial cells, mesangial cells, epithelial cells, tubular epithelial cells, and the mitochondria and lysosomes.


### Immunohistochemistry measurement of renal tissue
  We performed immunohistochemistry on all samples from each group, with three sections for each sample. The following primary antibodies were applied: anti-α-SMA (Proteintech, 55135-1-AP, 1:200 dilution), anti-TGF-β1 (ABclonal, A15103, 1:200 dilution), anti-VEGF (Proteintech, 19003-1-AP, 1:500 dilution), anti-CD68 (Servicebio, GB113109, 1:200 dilution), anti-TNF-α (Affinity Biosciences Cat, AF7014, 1:200 dilution), anti-p-Smad2 (Abcam, ab280888, 1:2000 dilution), anti-p-Smad3 (Affinity Biosciences Cat, AF8315, 1:200 dilution), anti-collagen I (Servicebio, GB11022-3, 1:800 dilution), and anti-fibronectin (Proteintech, 15613-1-AP, 1:500 dilution). For each group, 10 microscope photographs were captured randomly from these sections (40×). IPP was utilized to measure the signal area and integrated optical density (IOD) values to calculate the mean optical density (MOD) for each image.


### Network pharmacology and molecular docking
  The TCM System Pharmacology (TCMSP), Encyclopedia of TCM (ETCM) database, HERB, and Swiss databases, and the PharmMapper Server were used to predict the potential targets of the PTS components of ginsenoside Rg1, notoginsenoside R1, and ginsenoside Re. The Therapeutic Target Database (TTD), Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man database (OMIM), DisGeNET database, Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) dataset, and DrugBank database were searched using the keywords ‘Renal Fibrosis OR Kidney Fibrosis’. The intersections of PTS and disease targets were visually presented using the STRING database and Cytoscope v 3.6.0. Gene Ontology (GO) functional annotation and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis of ‘PTS-disease targets’ was performed using the DAVID Bioinformatics database and OmicShare platform. The AutoDock 1.5.6 software was used for molecular docking. The related online websites and software programs included the PyMOL molecular graphics system, Protein-Ligand Interaction Profiler (PLIP) server, and PDB website.


### Western blotting
  The total protein was extracted using the RIPA lysis buffer and a 0.1% protease inhibitor cocktail (Abmole) and determined with a BCA kit (Biyuntian, China). Proteins were separated using SDS-PAGE electrophoresis and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. After blocking with 5% skimmed milk in TBST buffer, the PVDF membranes were incubated at 4 °C overnight with several primary antibodies: anti-α-SMA (CST, #19245, 1:1000 dilution), anti-fibronectin (Proteintech, 15613-1-AP, 1:1000 dilution), anti-β-actin (Proteintech, 20536-1-AP, 1:5000 dilution), anti-TGF-β1 (Proteintech, 21898-1-AP, 1:2000 dilution), anti-Smad3 (Proteintech, 66516-1-AP, 1:2000 dilution), anti-p-Smad3 (Affinity Biosciences Cat, AF8315, 1:2000 dilution). The following day, the membranes were washed and exposed to appropriate secondary antibodies and developed in an ECL solution for exposure to photographic film. All experiments were repeated three times independently.


### Quantitative real-time PCR
  Total RNA was extracted from the kidney tissue using the TRIzol kit following its procedure (Invitrogen). Adjusted isolated RNA concentrations with 1ug/ul and reversely transcripted them to cDNA using the iScript cDNA synthesis kit (Bio-Rad). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using the SYBR Green kit (SsoAdvanced™ Universal SYBR Green Supermix, BIO-RAD). The experiment was independently repeated three times.


### Statistical analysis
  Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0. All data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way ANOVA, Dunnet’s, Bonferroni, and Kruskal-Wallis tests were used to determine statistical significance. p < 0.05 was defined as significant difference.


## Results

### PTS mitigated impairment of kidney function and renal morphological destruction in UUO model rats
  For the renal fibrosis study, UUO was considered a classical model of kidney fibrosis and inflammation induced by surgical obstruction of the ureter. To assess the renal damage induced by UUO and the retrieval of renal dysfunction after treating different dosages of PTS. Renal function index data from urine and serum samples revealed that BUN and Scr levels of the UUO model rats significantly increased compared to sham group rats. Reversely, BUN and Scr levels decreased after PTS-H intervention (Figure 1a, b). Meaningfully, PTS-H demonstrated a better effect on renal function than the PTS-L group and improved blood albumin levels considerably (Figure 1a-c). However, there was no discernible change in blood glucose levels among experimental groups (Figure 1d). Data from urine specimens showed that the alteration in 24-h urinary protein and creatinine levels with PTS were not statistically significant (Figure 1e, f). But PTS-H significantly reduced MAU and ACR (p < 0.05) (Figure 1g, h).

  HE staining revealed that inflammatory cell infiltration, tubular epithelial cell vacuolization, and mesangial proliferation co-existed in renal tissues of UUO model rats. In contrast, these destruction indicators of tissue were reduced in the PTS treatment group, especially in the PTS-H group (Figure 1i). Transmission electron microscope structure of renal tissue could reflect its micro-structure, and its change can be considered a good monitor of renal viability. It displayed that PTS could alleviate mitochondria destruction and autophagosome formation triggered by UUO (Figure 1j). These results indicated that it had a protective effect on kidney dysfunction induced by detrimental unilateral ureteral obstruction.


### PTS ameliorated renal fibrosis triggered by unilateral ureteral obstruction
  To investigate whether PTS ameliorate renal fibrosis, we performed Masson’s trichrome staining and PASM staining. Kidney tissue from UUO model rats exhibited significant interstitial collagen fibril deposition, which was notably different from the sham group. PTS-H significantly attenuated collagen fiber deposition, whereas PTS-L treatment demonstrated only a comparable directional trend (Figure 2a, b). PASM staining of renal tubules and glomeruli yielded similar findings, with an increase in PASM-positive areas in the UUO model rats (Figure 2a, c). These results revealed a significant reduction in fibrosis-positive areas following PTS treatment (Figure 2b, c).

  α-SMA, Col-I, and FN are well-established biomarkers of fibrosis. To further assess the anti-fibrotic effect of PTS, we examined protein expression levels of these markers using immunohistochemical analysis. As expected, all these three markers were significantly elevated in the renal tissue of UUO model rats, confirming the successful establishment of the fibrosis model. PTS substantially reduced the expression of α-SMA, FN, and Col-I in kidney tissues (Figure 2d-g). Additionally, mRNA expression levels of α-SMA, Col-I, and FN in renal tissues were measured by qPCR. Following PTS treatment, the levels of all three markers were markedly decreased (Figure 3a-c). Western blot analysis further corroborated these findings, showing that both PTS-L and PTS-H significantly downregulated the expression of FN (Figure 3e). In α-SMA, only PTS-H significantly reduced its expression (Figure 3f). Together, these data illustrated that PTS effectively inhibit renal fibrosis.


### Network pharmacology analysis suggests that PTS attenuate renal fibrosis by inhibiting inflammatory signaling pathways
  Panaxatriol saponins (PTS) are a bioactive compound extracted from Panax notoginseng (Burk.) F.H, and several studies have demonstrated that it contains three primary components including ginsenoside Rg1, notoginsenoside R1, and ginsenoside Re [26]. In this study, we identified these three components occupying distinct peaks and determined their quantitative composition ratios in PTS through liquid chromatography (Figure 4b-c). To explore the mechanisms underlying PTS’s role in mitigating renal fibrosis, we applied a network pharmacology approach to uncover the potential molecular pathways involved. The ginsenoside Rg1, notoginsenoside R1, and ginsenoside Re chemical structures were displayed according to the PubChem database (Figure 4a). The data indicated they could target 413 potential genes associated with various diseases. By intersecting these with 1,402 genes related to renal fibrosis, we identified 23 key targets (Figure 4d). These keynote targets, including TNF, TGF-β1, ALB, VEGFA, SRC, INS, and IL-6, have displayed a high degree of interaction among these targets in the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network being screened from the STRING database (Figure 4e).

  Additionally, GO enrichment analysis uncovered that these key targets were predominantly involved in positive regulation of protein phosphorylation and the negative regulation of the acute inflammatory response, suggesting that PTS may modulate inflammation to alleviate renal fibrosis (Figure 5a). All genes involved in the GO analysis results are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Moreover, the molecular function terms identified, such as cytokine activity, growth factor activity, and protein phosphatase binding, indicate that the anti-fibrotic effects of PTS may be linked to the regulation of cytokines and protein phosphorylation. KEGG pathway analysis identified the top 15 enriched signaling pathways, many of which are associated with inflammation, including IL-17, MAPK, TNF, and PI3K-Akt pathways (Figure 5b). Among these, the TGF-β1 signaling pathway is particularly well-established and critical in the progression of fibrosis. Herein, network pharmacology analysis suggests that PTS could exert anti-fibrotic effects primarily by inhibiting the inflammatory pathways, with a particular emphasis on the TGF-β1 signaling pathway.


### Molecular docking suggests PTS may modulate inflammation through gene expression rather than direct protein binding
  Molecular docking is a computational technique used to assess ligand-receptor interactions and predict the binding affinity between interacting molecules. In this study, we utilized molecular docking to investigate the potential binding of TNF-α and TGF-β1 to three components of PTS individually. The results demonstrated that ginsenoside Rg1, notoginsenoside R1, and ginsenoside Re could interact with TNF-α and TGF-β1 via hydrogen bonds or van der Waals forces respectively (Figure 6a, b). Previous studies have suggested that a docking score below −7 kcal/mol typically indicates a favorable binding interaction. Our findings suggest that the binding affinity of the two key inflammatory targets, TNF-α and TGF-β1 with the three PTS components was relatively weak, although TGF-β1 exhibited a more stable interaction with ginsenoside Rg1 (Figure 6c). These results suggest that PTS may exert its effects by modulating the expression of TNF-α and TGF-β1.


### PTS inhibit macrophage infiltration into the kidney and reduces inflammatory response
  Inflammation is a critical initiator and driver of renal fibrosis. Macrophages, through the secretion of inflammatory mediators and profibrotic factors, directly activate fibroblasts, thereby contributing to the progression of renal fibrosis [19,20]. CD68 is widely recognized as a marker of inflammatory macrophages. Our immunohistochemical staining revealed markedly increased CD68 expression in the kidney tissue of UUO model rats, while PTS-H treatment significantly reduced CD68 expression, indicating that PTS inhibits macrophage infiltration into the kidney (Figure 7a, b).

  Furthermore, PTS treatment resulted in a substantial decrease in the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and VEGF, further supporting its anti-inflammatory effects (Figure 7a, c, d). Serum levels of the inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α were also significantly reduced in the PTS-H-treated groups, compared to the UUO model rats (Figure 7e, f). To further confirm the impact of PTS on the inflammatory response, we assessed the mRNA expression of key inflammatory mediators. Consistent with the changes observed in serum cytokine levels, results demonstrated a significant reduction in the mRNA levels of TNF-α and IL-6 in the PTS-treated rats (Figure 7g, h). These findings collectively suggest that PTS effectively mitigate inflammation, particularly by downregulating TNF-α, thereby contributing to the attenuation of renal fibrosis.


### PTS downregulate the TGF-β1/Smad3 signaling pathway
  TGF-β1 is a multifunctional cytokine that regulates a wide range of cellular processes, including cell growth, differentiation, and extracellular matrix production. Upon binding to its receptor, TGF-β1 activates intracellular Smad proteins, which transduce the signal to the nucleus, where they regulate the expression of target genes such as α-SMA, collagen I, and fibronectin. Notably, Smad3, a member of the Smad family, plays a critical role in the fibrotic response in various organs, including the kidney [15]. As aforementioned, our network pharmacology analysis suggests that PTS may modulate the TGF-β1 signaling pathway. We further explored the effects of PTS on the TGF-β1/Smad3 signaling pathway in vivo. Immunohistochemical analysis revealed significantly elevated levels of TGF-β1 and phosphorylated Smad2/3 in kidney tissues of the UUO model rats compared to the sham group. However, these elevated levels were significantly reduced by PTS-H treatment (Figure 8a-d). Similarly, serum levels of TGF-β1 followed a comparable trend, showing a significant decrease in the PTS-H-treated group (Figure 8e). Numerous studies have demonstrated that TGF-β1 signaling plays a pivotal role in the progression of fibrosis. To further investigate the molecular effects of PTS, we examined the mRNA expression levels of TGF-β1, Smad2, and Smad3, results indicated that PTS-H treatment significantly inhibited the transcription of these molecules (Figure 8f-h). Additionally, Western blot analysis showed a dramatic increase in TGF-β1 expression and Smad3 phosphorylation in the UUO model rats, which were significantly suppressed by PTS-H treatment (Figure 8i-l). Furthermore, analysis of the p-Smad3/Smad3 ratio revealed that PTS-H moderately reduced Smad3 phosphorylation and attenuated the corresponding signaling pathway, although statistical significance was not achieved (p > 0.05) (Figure 8m).

  Collectively, these data suggest that PTS leads to a downregulation of the TGF-β1/Smad3 signaling pathway which could limit renal fibrosis.


## Discussion
  Renal fibrosis is a key pathological feature of CKD and is a major contributor to the progression of kidney dysfunction. It is characterized by the excessive accumulation of ECM components, leading to tubulointerstitial fibrosis, glomerulosclerosis, and eventually renal failure. Despite the increasing prevalence of CKD, therapeutic strategies to effectively combat renal fibrosis remain limited [6,27]. In the present study, we investigated the potential anti-fibrotic effects of PTS, a group of active compounds derived from Panax notoginseng (Burk.) F.H. Chen, in a well-established UUO model of renal fibrosis in rats. Our findings provide novel insights into the mechanistic basis of PTS’s therapeutic effects, particularly focusing on its ability to modulate inflammatory responses and key signaling pathways such as TNF-α and TGF-β1/Smad3 (Figure 9).

  UUO is a well-established model for studying the pathophysiology of renal fibrosis, characterized by immune cell infiltration, tubular cell apoptosis, tubular atrophy, and excessive ECM protein deposition In this model, obstruction of the ureter induces renal injury, inflammation, and fibrosis [28]. Chronic inflammation is a hallmark of renal fibrosis, and pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α play pivotal roles in the initiation and progression of fibrosis. The overproduction of TNF-α leads to the activation of downstream inflammatory mediators, which subsequently promote the fibrotic process by stimulating ECM deposition and fibroblast activation. Notably, TNF-α synergizes with TGF-β1 to collaboratively enhance Smad2/3 phosphorylation and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), further accelerating ECM accumulation [12,29,30]. PTS are a class of bioactive compounds known for anti-platelet aggregation, neurotrophic, and brain microcirculation-enhancing effects, providing protective effects against ischemic stroke and Parkinson’s disease [19–22,31]. It has been widely utilized in the clinical management of ischemic stroke for several years in China. In this study, PTS significantly reduced serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, albumin, and microalbuminuria levels, as well as the ACR. Notably, PTS also provided partial protection against albuminuria. Although some parameters, such as glucose levels, 24-h urinary protein, and total urine protein, did not exhibit significant improvement, trends in the PTS-treated groups were notably better than those observed in the UUO group. Examination of kidney tissue sections using HE staining revealed significant structural damage in the UUO model rats, including tubular dilation, interstitial inflammation, and increased collagen deposition. However, PTS treatment significantly alleviated these pathological changes, demonstrating its potential to reduce renal injury and fibrosis. Furthermore, Masson’s trichrome staining, which highlights collagen deposition, and TEM analysis confirmed the protective effects of PTS on renal tissues. These findings are consistent with previous studies that have reported the anti-fibrotic potential of various bioactive compounds derived from medicinal plants, underscoring the therapeutic promise of natural products in kidney fibrosis [18,32,33].

  Previous studies have reported that ginsenoside Rg1 alleviates renal fibrosis associated with aging by modulating inflammatory immune responses, specifically through downregulation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and inhibition of the TRPC6/NFAT2 signaling pathway. Notoginsenoside R1 has been shown to activate the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway to reduce reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels via TGF-β1 signaling, while also inhibiting NF-κB and MAPK pathways, thus mitigating fibrosis [34,35]. Ginsenoside Re has been suggested to reduce oxidative stress [36]. Despite some research into the role of these active components in fibrosis regulation, their exact mechanisms remain poorly understood.

  In our mechanistic investigation, we observed that PTS treatment significantly downregulated the expression of TNF-α and other pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1 and IL-6, both at the mRNA and protein levels. These results suggest that PTS exert its anti-fibrotic effects, at least in part, by modulating the inflammatory response. However, our current experimental data lack mechanistic insights into how PTS inhibits TNF-α-associated inflammation and modulates the interplay between TNF-α and TGF-β1. To provide deeper mechanistic elucidation of PTS’s anti-fibrotic actions, future studies will prioritize investigating these molecular pathways.

  The TGF-β1/Smad3 signaling pathway is considered the central mediator of fibrosis in various organs, including the kidney. TGF-β1 is a potent fibrogenic cytokine that promotes the transformation of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts, the primary effector cells responsible for ECM deposition. Upon binding to its receptor, TGF-β1 activates Smad proteins, especially Smad3, which translocate to the nucleus to regulate the expression of pro-fibrotic genes, including α-SMA, collagen I, and fibronectin. Several studies have demonstrated that inhibition of TGF-β1 or Smad3 can effectively attenuate renal fibrosis in animal models and clinical settings [10,15,29]. We observed that PTS significantly inhibited the expression of TGF-β1 and its downstream target Smad3, both at the mRNA and protein levels. Furthermore, PTS treatment reduced the phosphorylation of Smad3, a crucial step in the activation of the TGF-β1 signaling pathway. Our findings are consistent with a growing body of literature that highlights the importance of TGF-β1/Smad3 signaling in the pathogenesis of renal fibrosis [15]. The ability of PTS to downregulate the TGF-β1/Smad3 pathway suggests that PTS may act as a potential therapeutic agent to counteract renal fibrosis by targeting this critical signaling axis, or have broader implications in other fibrotic diseases beyond the kidney. Moreover, PTS has been shown to modulate other signaling pathways involved in fibrosis, such as the PI3K/Akt pathway. The PI3K/Akt signaling pathway is involved in cell survival, inflammation, and fibrosis, and its dysregulation has been linked to kidney injury and fibrosis [3,37]. In our study, we did not explore this pathway in detail, but future research should investigate whether PTS also exerts its effects through PI3K/Akt or other signaling cascades.

  Our study provides compelling evidence that PTS may serve as a potential therapeutic agent for the prevention of renal fibrosis and CKD. Given the multifaceted nature of renal fibrosis, which involves inflammation, fibrosis, and ECM accumulation, targeting multiple signaling pathways with a single agent is an attractive strategy. PTS’s ability to modulate both inflammatory and fibrotic pathways makes it an ideal candidate for further clinical development. However, there are several limitations in our study that should be addressed in future research. First, while our findings suggest that PTS attenuates renal fibrosis in the UUO model, the effects of PTS in other models of kidney disease, such as diabetic nephropathy or ischemia-reperfusion injury of kidney, remain to be investigated. Additionally, the pharmacokinetics and bioavailability of PTS should be explored to determine its clinical applicability. It would also be valuable to conduct dose-response studies to identify the optimal dosage for further therapeutic use. Finally, although our study focuses on the TGF-β1/Smad3 and TNF-α pathways, other signaling pathways may be involved in fibrosis, such as Wnt/β-catenin and Notch, should be considered for a more comprehensive understanding of PTS’s molecular mechanisms.


## Conclusion
  In conclusion, our study demonstrates that Panaxatriol Saponins (PTS) exert significant protective effects against renal fibrosis in vivo, primarily through the inhibition of inflammatory responses and the downregulation of the TGF-β1/Smad3 signaling pathway. These findings provide a strong rationale for the further exploration of PTS as a potential therapeutic agent for the treatment of renal fibrosis and other fibrotic diseases. By targeting multiple key molecular pathways, PTS hold promise as a novel, natural-based therapeutic strategy for combating CKD and its associated complications.
