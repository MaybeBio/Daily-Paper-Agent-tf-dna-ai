# Recognition of the CREB-Binding Protein/Mixed Lineage Leukemia Interface by Sulfonyl-γ-AApeptides: Beyond Mimicry of Mixed Lineage Leukemia


## Abstract
  The kinase-inducible (KIX) domain, a structured region within the transcriptional coactivators CREB-binding protein (CBP) and p300, serves as a docking site for multiple transcription factors, including the mixed lineage leukemia (MLL) protein. The MLL–KIX protein–protein interaction (PPI) has been implicated in various diseases, such as leukemia, cancer, and neurodegenerative disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease. In this study, we developed a series of MLL-mimicking peptidomimetic foldamers based on a sulfonyl-γ-AApeptide backbone. These helical foldamers successfully mimic the folded transactivation domain (TAD) of MLL and bind the KIX domain with a high affinity. Consistent with the notion that MLL–KIX interaction can allosterically enhance CREB signaling, we found that the sulfonyl-γ-AApeptides strongly stimulate CREB-dependent gene expression. Moreover, they exhibit no significant cytotoxicity under the tested conditions, demonstrating both binding specificity and therapeutic potential for targeting CREB-related pathways in neurodegenerative disorders.


## INTRODUCTION
  Protein–protein interactions (PPIs) are viable drug targets due to their crucial roles in numerous biological processes ranging from transcription and translation and signal transduction to cell growth and differentiation. Although considerable success in modulating structurally defined PPIs has been achieved in recent years, disrupting PPIs involving intrinsically disordered proteins bearing transient domains is more challenging. One of the main prototypical types of these PPIs is the interactions occurring between transcriptional coactivators and transcription factors. Specifically, as one of the most crucial structured domains in the transcriptional coactivators CREB-binding protein (CBP) and p300, the KIX (kinase-inducible domain interacting) domain functions as a docking site for a range of transcription factors, including cAMP-responsive element-binding protein (CREB), mixed lineage leukemia (MLL) protein, proto-oncogene protein c-Myb, c-Jun, etc (Figure 1a).1,2 Unlike c-Myb and CREB, which bind to a similar hydrophobic groove formed by the first and third helices of the KIX three-helix bundle,3,4 MLL binds in a distinct site on the surface of KIX, opposite to the binding groove occupied by c-Myb and pKID (Figure 1b).5 Whereas inhibition of KIX/MLL PPI by MLL-mimicking ligands including small molecules or peptides/peptidomimetics could have potential therapeutic application for acute lymphoid leukemia involving MLL rearrangements, such ligands could instead stimulate c-Myb and CREB signaling pathways, as it is known now that the interaction between MLL and the CBP KIX domain could enhance the affinity of KIX for either pKID or c-Myb with approximately 2-fold higher compared to binding with KIX alone.2,6,7 CREB is identified as the nuclear transcription factor to selectively bind with the cAMP-responsive element (CRE) region of the somatostatin gene as dimers.8 Its transaction activity is initiated upon phosphorylation at Ser133 by various extracellular signals including hormones, growth factors, and neuronal activity,9 and then it binds with coactivator CREB-binding protein (CBP) and its paralogue p300 to start the transcription of multiple genes such as somatostatin and c-fos.10 CREB is widely expressed in the brain and particularly in regions fundamental for learning and memory.11 A large body of evidence has proved the critical role of CREB-dependent transcription in synaptic plasticity and long-term memory.11-22 Decreased CREB levels impair spatial memory, whereas overexpression of CREB in the dorsal hippocampus enhances memory.23,24 These findings generate high enthusiasm in developing drugs targeting the CREB signaling pathway for the treatment of neurodegenerative disorders.25 For example, thioperamide and 7,8-dihydroxycoumarin have been reported to enhance CREB-mediated transcription via activation of the PI3K-Akt-CREB-BDNF signaling, while HDAC2 inhibitors such as JRM-28 promote neuronal plasticity through upregulation of CREB expression.21,22 However, these compounds function primarily through indirect upstream mechanisms, and to date, no small molecules or peptidomimetics have been shown to activate CREB signaling through direct allosteric modulation of the CBP KIX domain.

  In this study, we report the development of peptidomimetic foldamers based on a sulfonyl-γ-AApeptide backbone that mimic the core region of the MLL minimal transactivation domain (residues 2844–2854), which is essential for binding to the KIX domain. While originally designed as MLL mimetics to modulate the MLL/KIX protein–protein interaction, these helical sulfonyl-γ-AApeptides unexpectedly function as cell-permeable stimulators of CREB activity. Specifically, peptides L5, L6, and L7 effectively enhance CREB-mediated gene transcription and promote the growth of neuronal cells, highlighting their potential applications for the treatment of neurodegenerative disorders.


## RESULTS

### Rational Design of MLL Mimics Based on the Sulfonyl-γ-AApeptide Backbone.
  Analysis of existing NMR structural data reveals that only 15 residues (2844–2858) of the MLL sequence adopt an ordered structure, forming an amphipathic helix that binds to the hydrophobic groove on the surface of the KIX domain (Figure 1b, PDB: 2LXT).7,26 Specifically, residues I2844, L2845, and P2846 adopt an extended structure that projects deeply into a shallow hydrophobic groove formed by the side chains of residues Y631, V635, M639, K656, I660, and L664 in KIX. The side chains of residues I2849, M2850, F2852, V2853, L2854, and T2857 in MLL also make extensive hydrophobic contacts with KIX residues I611, F612, A619, R624, L628, Y631, I660, and L664 in the binding groove. Additionally, NMR structures indicate that there are some electrostatic interactions between the side chain of N2856 of MLL and two KIX side chains, T614 and D616. Overall, the residues in MLL involved in the contacts with KIX are located on one face of the helix, roughly aligned in two parallel lines along the helix axis (Figure 1c). We, therefore, speculated that molecules bearing similar residues would be able to target the MLL-binding site on the KIX, leading to the discovery of new CREB modulators (Figure 1d).

  To address this issue, we harnessed the strong α-helix-like forming potential of the sulfonyl-γ-AApeptide. In the crystal structure of homogeneous L-sulfonyl-γ-AApeptides, they adopt 414 left-handed helices with a helical pitch of 5.1 Å, which are highly analogous to that of α-helix (5.4 Å),27 making them suitable for applications in modulating several protein–protein interactions (PPIs).28-33 However, the opposite helical handedness of L-sulfonyl-γ-AApeptides, in contrast to the α-helix, may potentially complicate the design of the PPI inhibitor. Therefore, we replaced L-sulfonyl-γ-AA building blocks with D-sulfonyl-γ-AA building blocks, leading to the formation of right-handed helices from their mirror-imaged left-handed helices (Figure 2a). In the structure of the sulfonyl-γ-AApeptide, the chiral side chains 1a, 3a, 5a, and 7a are on the same face of the helical scaffold, and sulfonyl side chains 1b, 3b, 5b, and 7b are on the adjacent face (Figure 2b). They were thus chosen to mimic the residues on I2844, L2845, P2846, I2849, M2850, F2852, V2853, L2854, N2856, and T2857. For side chains of sulfonyl-γ-AApeptides not directly involved in contact with the KIX, we employed some positively or negatively charged hydrophilic side chains in the hope of enhancing the solubility and cell permeability (Figure 2c). Thus, we designed seven γ-AApeptide sequences, L1 to L7, as shown in Table. 1. Using L1 as a sample, an overlay of L1 with N1 (MLL helix: 2844–2858) suggested that the side chains of 1a, 3a, 5a, and 7a would occupy positions similar to I2844, I2849, V2853, and T2857 in the MLL helix. The isobutyl and phenyl groups placed at positions 1b, 3b, 4b, 5b, and 6b could effectively mimic the residues of L2845, M2850, F2852, L2854, and N2856, respectively (Figure 2d-f).


### Binding Affinity Screening and Structure–Activity Relationship (SAR) Analysis.
  A fluorescence polarization (FP) assay was conducted to measure the binding affinity of peptides with the CBP KIX domain prior to activity screening. The natural MLL helix N1 was included as a control. In addition, peptide L8 was designed as a comparison, where all of the critical positions that may be involved in the interaction with the KIX domain were changed to methyl groups. As expected, L8 did not show any binding ability toward the KIX domain, while the other peptides exhibited modest to good Kd values, ranging from 0.11 to 1.1 μM, as shown in Figure 3. This result indicates that mimicking residues at the crucial positions in the MLL sequence is essential for activity.

  To further understand the importance of each residue in the interaction with the KIX domain, we conducted a SAR analysis based on their binding affinities. Specifically, the replacement of the methyl group in the side chain 7b of L1 with a benzyl group resulted in L5, which showed a more than 6-fold increase in binding affinity toward the KIX domain with a Kd value of 0.18 μM (Figure 3b,f), indicating that a bulky hydrophobic group is preferable for their interaction. This was also confirmed by the comparison of peptides L4 and L6 (Figure 3e,g). Additionally, replacing the amino group at position 6b of L1 with a methyl group (L4) effectively enhanced its binding with KIX by about 2-fold (Figure 3b,e). In the structures of L2 and L3, substituting the methyl group at 2b and the phenyl group at 4b with benzoyloxy and 4-methoxyphenyl groups, respectively, led to a slight increase in the Kd values from 1.1 μM to 0.73 and 0.79 μM, respectively, compared to L1 (Figure 3c,d).


### 2D NMR Study of L5 with KIX.
  To gain insight into the interaction of MLL peptidomimetics with KIX, we compared 1H─15N HSQC spectra for 15N-labeled KIX in the presence and absence of L5 (Figure 4). Similar to a longer recombinantly produced MLL fragment,5 a 4-fold excess of synthetically produced MLL WT fragment, corresponding to human residues 2845–2859, results in significant chemical shift changes throughout KIX. The most pronounced shifts occur in the MLL binding pocket, created by the C-terminus of the α1 helix, the G2 helix, and the N-terminus of the α2 helix of KIX (Figure 4a). L5 also induces chemical shift changes throughout KIX, with a notable enrichment of shifts greater than twice the experimental resolution limit in the MLL binding pocket. Significant chemical shift changes are observed in MLL-direct-contact residues V630 and L664, as well as in contact-adjacent residues L608, I611, E626, V629, A631, and A655 (Figure 4b). Additionally, G2 helix residues L620 and K621, which are not direct contact residues, also demonstrate large shifts in the presence of both the MLL WT and L5. These findings suggest that L5 specifically targets the MLL-binding sites on KIX.


### Cell Permeability Study.
  Based on their superior binding affinity toward the KIX domain, peptides L5, L6, and L7 were selected for further evaluation of their cell-penetrating properties. FITC-conjugated versions of these peptides were synthesized, and native MLL peptide N1 labeled with FITC was included as a control. HeLa cells were incubated with the FITC-labeled peptides (10 μM), and cellular uptake was assessed by confocal microscopy. As shown in Figure 5, peptides L5, L6, and L7 exhibited substantial intracellular fluorescence, indicating an efficient membrane permeability. In contrast, no obvious uptake was observed for FITC-N1, further highlighting the enhanced cell permeability of the designed sulfonyl-γ-AApeptides (Figure S1).


### Effect of Peptides on CREB-Mediated Gene Transcription.
  We then assessed the potential effect of peptide L5, L6, and L7 on CREB-mediated gene transcription using a CREB Renilla luciferase (RLuc) reporter assay in HEK 293T cells.34 Additionally, we include a human MLL peptide N1 (residues 2844–2858) as a control. Excitingly, all the designed sulfonyl-γ-AApeptides exerted a strong stimulatory effect on CREB-mediated gene transcription, while native MLL peptide N1 showed a slight inhibitory effect. Specifically, peptide L5, L6, and L7 enhanced the CREB -mediated gene transcription with an EC50 value of 4.93, 1.24, and 0.62 μM, respectively (Figure 4d and Table 1). This difference may result from the fact that N1 binds the KIX domain without inducing the allosteric changes necessary to enhance the CREB-KIX interaction, thereby acting as a weak competitor. In contrast, our foldamers not only mimic the helical interface of MLL but also appear to favor a conformational state of KIX that promotes CREB recruitment.


### Effect of Peptidomimetics on Cell Survival.
  To develop drugs for the treatment of neurodegenerative disorders, we first evaluated the cytotoxicity of the peptides on primary neuron cells using the MTT assay. The human natural MLL peptide N1 was also included as a control. As shown in Figure 6a-d, both N1 and sulfonyl-γ-AApeptide L5 significantly promoted neuronal viability at higher concentrations. Importantly, all tested peptides exhibited no observable cytotoxicity, indicating a favorable safety profile in neuronal cells. In addition, we used a potential-dependent Mitoview 633 to dye functional mitochondria to image the living cells. The cellular activity level can be indirectly assessed through mitochondrial activity, which directly reflects the overall metabolic state and physiological function of the cells. As shown in Figure 6d, compared to the negative control, both N1, L5, L6, and L7 could promote cellular activity. Although native MLL peptide N1 exhibited a slight inhibitory effect on CREB-mediated transcription in the luciferase assay, it still promoted neuronal viability and mitochondrial activity. This apparent discrepancy suggests that N1 may influence mitochondrial function through a CREB-independent mechanism—possibly by engaging the KIX domain without inducing the conformational changes necessary for CREB activation or through interactions with alternative cellular pathways. In contrast, the sulfonyl-γ-AApeptides not only enhanced neuronal viability and mitochondrial function but also robustly activated CREB-dependent gene transcription, indicating that their cellular effects are more directly linked to CREB pathway modulation.


### Circular Dichroism and Serum Stability Analysis.
  To assess the conformational properties of the sulfonyl-γ-AApeptide-based foldamers, circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy was performed to evaluate the helical propensity of lead compound L6. For comparison, native MLL peptide N1 was included as a control. As shown in Figure S2, N1 exhibited a characteristic minimum around 196 nm, consistent with a random coil conformation, reflecting its intrinsically disordered nature that only adopts a helical structure upon binding to KIX. In contrast, L6 exhibited a single minimum at approximately 220 nm, indicative of a right-handed helical structure. It is important to note that the CD signatures of α-helical peptides and D-sulfonyl-γ-AApeptides differ significantly due to the distinct nature of their molecular scaffolds.

  In addition to their defined secondary structure, a notable feature of sulfonyl-γ-AApeptide foldamers is their exceptional resistance to enzymatic degradation. To evaluate serum stability, compound N1 and L6 were incubated in human serum for 24 h, and the remaining intact peptide was analyzed by HPLC. As shown in Figure S3, N1 underwent ~25% degradation over the incubation period, while L6 remained virtually intact, demonstrating its remarkable proteolytic stability. These results highlight the enhanced serum stability conferred by the sulfonyl-γ-AApeptide scaffold and support its potential as a robust platform for therapeutic development.


## DISCUSSION
  The development of peptidomimetic foldamers based on sulfonyl-γ-AApeptide backbones as cell-permeable CREB stimulators represents a significant advancement in the field of neurodegenerative disease treatment. Our study aimed to enhance CREB-mediated gene transcription by designing molecules that mimic the core region of the MLL minimal transactivation domain. By replicating the residues of MLL helix with D-sulfonyl-γ-AA building blocks, we achieved the desired helical conformation and successfully mimicked the essential residues involved in binding with KIX. The binding affinity screening using fluorescence polarization assays confirmed that most of the designed peptides had strong binding interactions with the KIX domain, with Kd values ranging from 0.11 to 1.1 μM. This highlights the importance of precise residue mimicry in achieving effective binding and modulation of protein–protein interactions. A detailed SAR analysis elucidated the critical positions in the peptides for optimal interaction with the KIX domain. The CREB Renilla luciferase reporter assay demonstrated that peptides L5, L6, and L7 significantly stimulated CREB-mediated gene transcription, exhibiting superior EC50 values compared to the natural MLL peptide. Specifically, L6 and L7 showed exceptionally low EC50 values of 1.24 and 0.62 μM, respectively, indicating their potent stimulatory effects on CREB activity. This strong transcriptional activation suggests that these peptidomimetics can effectively enhance CREB-dependent gene expression, which is crucial for synaptic plasticity and long-term memory. Importantly, the cytotoxicity evaluation on primary neuron cells revealed that the sulfonyl-γ-AApeptides were nontoxic at concentrations up to 100 μM. Instead, they promoted cell growth at high concentrations, demonstrating their potential as safe and effective therapeutic agents for neurodegenerative disorders. The lack of toxicity in primary neuron cells is particularly promising, as it indicates that these peptides can be used in physiological conditions without detrimental effects on neuron health.


## CONCLUSIONS
  In conclusion, our study successfully developed peptidomimetic foldamers based on sulfonyl-γ-AApeptide backbones that act as potent stimulators of CREB-mediated gene transcription. By mimicking the core region of the MLL minimal transactivation domain, we achieved a high binding affinity to the KIX domain and significant enhancement of CREB activity. The peptides L5, L6, and L7 demonstrated strong stimulatory effects on CREB-dependent gene expression and promoted growth of neuronal cells, highlighting their potential as therapeutic agents for neurodegenerative disorders. These findings provide a promising foundation for the further development and optimization of CREB stimulators, paving the way for new treatments targeting CREB signaling pathways in the brain.


## EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

### Reagents.
  Fmoc-protected amino acids were purchased from Chem-Impex (Wood Dale, IL). Rink-Amide-MBHA resin (loading 0.623 mmol/g) was purchased from GL Biochem and was used for the solid-phase synthesis of sulfonyl-γ-AApeptides. All solvents and other chemical reagents used for building block synthesis were obtained from commercial suppliers and used without purification, unless otherwise indicated. The sulfonyl-γ-AA building blocks were synthesized based on previous reports. All the peptides (Table S1) were prepared based on standard SPPS and purified on a Waters Breeze 2 HPLC system installed with both an analytic module (1 mL/ min) and a preparative module (16 mL/min) by employing a method using 5–100% linear gradient of solvent B (0.1% TFA in ACN) in solvent A (0.1% TFA in H2O) over 45 min, followed by 100% solvent B over 5 min. All compounds are >95% pure by analytical HPLC. The molecular weight of each peptide was confirmed by Bruker UltraFleXtreme MALDI- TOF/TOF (Table S2).


### Preparation of KIX Protein.
  The KIX domain of the CREB binding protein (mouse residues 586–672, Uniprot ID P45481) was ordered from Eurofins (Lancaster, PA) and subcloned into a pET28a vector. KIX was grown in 2 L increments in M9 minimal media in BL21 (DE3) E. coli to an O.D. of 0.6 and induced with 1 mM IPTG at 15 °C for 22 h. Bacteria were pelleted at 8000 g and stored at −80 °C. Pellets were resuspended in 25 mL of lysis buffer composed of 50 mM NaH2PO4, pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, and 0.02% NaN3 with a Pierce Protease Inhibitor mini tablet, EDTA-free (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA) and lysed in three rounds using a French Press at greater than 1000 psi, then centrifuged at 38,000 g. The supernatant was filtered and passed over the Ni-NTA resin, which was then washed with lysis buffer and eluted with 250 mM imidazole. KIX was dialyzed into buffer composed of 50 mM NaH2PO4, pH 7.0, 300 mM NaCl, and 0.02% NaN3 and the six-histidine tag was cleaved for two hours at room temperature with the Thrombin RECOMT kit (Millipore Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, MA). Cleaved protein was concentrated to 4–6 mL and passed over a GE HiLoad 16/60 Superdex 75 column.


### Primary Neuron Culture.
  Mouse cortical neurons were isolated from newborn B6SJLF1 mice (within 24 h of birth). The procedures involving animals were approved by the animal component by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the University of South Florida under IACUC number IS00005895. All efforts were made to minimize animal suffering, to reduce the number of animals used, and to utilize alternative in vivo techniques. Briefly, after the newborn mouse was euthanized, the brain was removed from the skull, and the cortical lobes were dissected. After the removal of the meninges from the cortical lobes, the cortex was diced and digested at 37 °C for 20 min in 0.05% trypsin. Then, the supernatants containing trypsin were carefully removed after the tissue had sedimented. The tissue was then washed three times with ice-cold HBSS and once with culture medium (neurobasal medium with B27 supplement, antibiotics, and GlutaMAX). The cortical tissues were gently triturated with a pipet to dissociate larger aggregates. The neurons were collected by centrifugation at 300×g for 10 min. Cortical neurons were then resuspended with culture medium and seeded onto poly l-lysine-coated 96-well cell culture plates with 30,000 cells/well. The primary neurons were cultured for seven days at +37 °C and 5% CO2 for a cell viability test. AraC was added to the cultures on day 2 and was removed on day 4 in culture.

### Cell Permeability Assay.
  HeLa cells were cultured in six-well plates and allowed to attach to the bottom of the plate overnight. Then, appropriate amounts of FITC-labeled peptides L5, L6, and L7 dissolved in DMEM (0.1% DMSO) were added to the cells to final concentrations of 10 μM. The cells were incubated with the peptides for 4 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2. After incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS and stained with DAPI (10 μg/mL) for 10 min, and then it was washed with PBS twice and fixed with methanol for 5 min. They are then washed twice with PBS and imaged using a microscope.


### Effect of the Peptides on CREB-Mediated Gene Transcription.
  HEK 293T cells in a well of a six-well plate were transfected with a plasmid (2 μg) encoding Renilla luciferase under the control of three copies of CRE (pCRE-RLuc) using Lipofectamine2000 for 3 h. Then the cells were replaced with a 96-well plate, and the cells were allowed to attach to the bottom of the plate overnight. The cells were treated with different concentrations of peptides for 30 min, followed by the addition of Fsk (10 μM). The cells were further incubated for 5 h at 37 °C. The media were removed, and the cells were lysed in 30 μL of 1× Renilla luciferase lysis buffer (Promega); the renilla luciferase activity was measured using the Renilla luciferase assay system (Promega). The protein concentration in each well was determined using Protein Assay Dye Reagent Concentrate (Biorad). The luciferase activity in each well was normalized to the protein concentration and expressed as a relative luciferase unit (RLU)/μg protein. The IC50 was calculated with Prism 9.0 using the nonlinear regression analysis.


### NMR Data Collection and Analysis.
  NMR experiments utilized uniformly 15N-labeled KIX samples at 200–425 μM in apo form or bound to a 4-fold excess of unlabeled MLL WT or MLL mimics. Initial spectra were collected at 425 μM in the apo state to ensure an optimal peak visibility and resolution. Subsequent spectra at 200 μM showed identical peak positions, indicating no significant concentration-dependent shift. For peptide-binding experiments, 200 μM KIX was used with 800 μM peptide as the mimics exhibited better solubility at this concentration than at higher levels (e.g., 1700 μM), allowing for reliable spectral acquisition under binding conditions. Samples were prepared in a buffer composed of 90% H2O, 10% D2O, 10 mM NaH2PO4, 1.8 mM KH2PO4, 2.7 mM KCl, 137 mM NaCl, 3% DMSO, and 0.02% NaN3, pH 7.4. 1H–15N HSQC spectra were collected at 25 °C on a Varian VNMRS 800-MHz spectrometer with a triple resonance pulse field Z-axis gradient cold probe. Sweep widths were 4844.96 and 2592.26 Hz for the 1H and 15N dimensions with 1024 and 256 complex data points, respectively. Data were processed with NMRFx and analyzed with NMRViewJ software.35,36 Average chemical shift values were found with the equation [((Δ1H)2 + (Δ15N/5)2)/2]1/2.


### Cytotoxicity Evaluation.
  The cell viability was determined by MTT (3-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-Diphenyltetrazolium Bromide) assay. The peptides were added to the tested cell wells with a series of concentrations and incubated with cells for 24 h. The medium has then been discarded to remove the drug residue and then replaced with 100 μL of medium containing 0.5 mg/mL MTT to each well. Incubate the microplate for 4 h in a humidified atmosphere (+37 °C, 5% CO2). 100 μL of the solubilization solution (10% SDS in 0.01 M HCl) was then added into each well. The plate was standing overnight in the incubator in a humidified atmosphere. The cell viability was captured using a Bio-Rad Synergy HT plate reader at 590 and 660 nm. Cell viability was calculated as Cell viability = (OD590–OD660)/(OD590 control average - OD660 control average) × 100%. The result has been analyzed by one-way ANOVA(α<0.05(*) to α<0.0001(****)).


### Cell Mitochondrial Imaging.
  The mitochondrial potential-dependent fluorescent dye MitoView 633 (Biotium, lot#70055) has been employed to assess functional mitochondrial activity in live cells. Cells were incubated with peptides for 24 h. The medium with the peptide was then replaced with a medium containing 100 nM mitochondrial staining dye MitoView633. After staining for 20 min, Hoechst 33342 was added to a final concentration of 1 μg/mL and incubated for another 10 min. The cell was then washed and captured by a Keyence BZ-X810 fluorescence microscope with an appropriate filter (DAPI filter for Hoechst and Cy5 filter for MitoView 633).


### Circular Dichroism.
  Circular dichroism (CD) spectra of peptides were recorded in PBS buffer at a concentration of 100 μM using JASCO Model J-1500 Circular Dichroism Optical Rotatory Dispersion (CD/ORD) spectrometer equipped with a 1 mm path length quartz cuvette. Each spectrum was obtained by averaging 10 scans, and three independent experiments were performed to ensure reproducibility. The final spectra were baseline-corrected by subtracting the average blank (PBS) signal. Molar ellipticity [θ] (deg·cm2·dmol−1) was calculated using the equation: [θ] = θobs/(n × l × c × 10), where θobs is the observed ellipticity in millidegrees, n is the number of amino acid residues, I is the path length in centimeters (0.1 cm), and c is the molar concentration of the peptide.


### Serum Stability Assay.
  The serum stability of peptides was evaluated in 50% (v/v) human serum prepared from pooled male AB plasma (Sigma-Aldrich, Milan, Italy). Each peptide (1 mg) was first dissolved in 10 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide and then diluted into the 990 μL of serum solution. Samples were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, 100 μL of the reaction mixture was mixed with 100 μL of ice-cold acetonitrile (CH3CN) to precipitate serum proteins. The mixture was kept on ice for 15 min and then centrifuged at 4 °C for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was analyzed by using a Waters analytical HPLC system with a flow rate of 1 mL/min. Separation was performed using a 5–100% linear gradient of solvent B (0.1% trifluoroacetic acid in acetonitrile) in solvent A (0.1% trifluoroacetic acid in water) over 50 min. UV detection was carried out at 215 nm.
