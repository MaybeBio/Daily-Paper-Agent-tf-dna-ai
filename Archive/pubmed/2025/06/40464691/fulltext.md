# Structural and functional characterization of human SLFN14


## Abstract
  The Schlafen (SLFN) family of proteins are a group of DNA/RNA processing enzymes with emerging importance in human health and disease, where their functions are implicated in a variety of immunological and anti-tumor processes. Here, we present the cryo-electron microscopy structure of full-length human SLFN14, a member with antiviral activity and linked to an inherited bleeding disorder. SLFN14 is composed of an RNase domain, a SWADL domain, and a two-lobe helicase domain. SLFN14 exhibited strong RNase activity over different substrates, and the positively charged patches at the valley of the RNase domain, which contains the thrombocytopenia-related missense mutation sites, are crucial for binding oligonucleotides. SLFN14 lacks helicase activity, which can be attributed to the inability to bind ATP and the absence of positive charges at the canonical DNA-binding site of its RecA-like folds. SLFN14 is structurally similar to SLFN11, but differs from SLFN5 in the orientation of the helicase domain. Live-cell fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) assays and AlphaFold2 analysis hinted that SLFN14 may adopt multiple conformations in cells. These results provide detailed structural and biochemical features of SLFN14, and greatly expand our knowledge of the functional diversity of the SLFN family.


## Introduction
  The Schlafen (SLFN) family members are a group of mammalian interferon-inducible proteins with multiple cellular functions, including cell differentiation, DNA repair/replication, immune response regulation, restriction of viruses, and tumor suppression [1–3]. Based on the length and domain composition, the SLFNs are classified into three subgroups. All SLFNs possess an N-terminal RNase domain, which is the sole domain of the smallest subgroup, subgroup I. Subgroup II and III SLFNs contain another SLFN-specific SWADL domain, and subgroup III SLFNs have the third C-terminal helicase domain homologous to the superfamily I (SF1) DNA/RNA helicases that translocate along and unwind substrate nucleic acids in an ATP hydrolysis-dependent manner [4, 5]. Some subgroup III members contain a potential C-terminal nuclear localization signal. Though 10 SLFN members from all three subgroups are found in mice, humans have only five SLFNs, namely SLFN12 from subgroup II, and SLFN5, SLFN11, SLFN13, and SLFN14 from subgroup III [6, 7].

  The first functional glimpse of SLFNs was reported for subgroup I members in mouse thymocyte development [8]. Human SLFNs are widely involved in tumor suppression [7, 9–16] and the restriction of viruses via various mechanisms [2, 17–20]. SLFN11 is the most extensively studied member in humans and is found to strongly inhibit human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and other viruses by manipulating tRNAs, and to sensitize cancer cells by blocking stressed DNA replication forks [17, 21–28]. SLFN14 has been reported to repress the replication of influenza A virus (IAV) and varicella zoster virus (VZV) [19]. In human tissues, steady-state expression of SLFN14 is only found to be active in the bone marrow [29]. Pathogenic missense mutations in SLFN14 are linked to an inherited thrombocytopenia and bleeding disorder [30, 31]. Apart from SLFN5, human SLFNs all exhibit RNase activity [32–36].

  Our group has previously reported structural analysis of the N-terminal RNase domain of SLFN13, and revealed its RNA cleavage feature. Subsequently, the structures of SLFN12 in complex with phosphodiesterase 3A (PDE3A) and small molecules were resolved, which uncovered a new cell apoptosis pathway [12, 35–39]. The cryo-electron microscopy (EM) structure of human SLFN5 offered the first glimpse of the helicase domain of subgroup III SLFNs, though a large portion at the C-terminal region is missing [40]. Recent structural studies on SLFN11 have shown how it manipulates different types of nucleic acids [41, 42]. This progress provided valuable clues for understanding how individual SLFN members may work, but in the meantime also raised new questions as to whether and how the unexpected structural variation of the SLFNs defines their functional diversity. For example, SLFN5 and SLFN11 have distinct features in stoichiometry and domain arrangement [40, 41]. In addition, for subgroup III SLFNs, there is less structural and functional knowledge on the helicase domain than on the N-terminal RNase domain.

  In this study, we report the cryo-EM structure of full-length human SLFN14. Through systematic structural, biochemical, and biophysical analysis, we show that SLFN14 is a potent RNase but lacks helicase activity, and the RNase activity is essential for its antiviral function. These results contribute to a better understanding of the SLFN family and various related biological processes.


## Materials and methods

### Molecular cloning
  The SLFN cDNAs purchased from Source BioScience were individually cloned into the pFastBac1 vector (restriction sites, EcoRI/XhoI) with an N-terminal His6-tag followed by a PreScission protease (PSP) cleavage site for overexpression in insect cells. cDNAs of the SLFN14 RNase domain (SLFN141–392, SLFN14-R) and helicase domain (SLFN14-H566–912, SLFN14-H) constructs were individually cloned into a modified pET28 vector (pSKB, BamHI/XhoI) with an N-terminal His6-tag followed by a PSP cleavage site for overexpression in Escherichia coli. cDNAs of SLFN14 constructs were individually cloned into the pcDNA3.1 vector (BamHI/EcoRI) for HIV infection assay. cDNAs of cyan fluorescent protein (CFP)/yellow fluorescent protein (YFP)-fused SLFN14 generated by homologous recombination were individually cloned into the pCAGGS (KpnI/XhoI) vector for live-cell fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) assay. All SLFN mutants were generated by site-directed mutagenesis and confirmed by sequencing. Primers used in these experiments are listed in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2.


### Protein expression and purification
  Recombinant pFastBac1-SLFNs plasmids were expressed in Sf9 or High Five insect cells using the Bac-to-Bac Baculovirus Expression System (Invitrogen). Recombinant bacmids were generated in DH10Bac E. coli cells, and the resulting baculovirus was produced and amplified in Sf9 cells. For protein expression, Sf9 cells or High Five cells were grown in Sf-900 II Serum Free Medium (Gibco) and infected with recombinant baculovirus at a ratio of 1:100 (virus versus cell, v/v). SLFN5 protein was expressed in Sf9 cells at 27°C for 72 h, and SLFN11, SLFN13, and SLFN14 proteins were expressed in High Five cells at 27°C for 60 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4000 × g for 10 min at 18°C. Cells overexpressing SLFN14 were lysed in ice-cold buffer containing 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.0), 300 mM NaCl, 30 mM imidazole, 1 μM DNase I, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), and 2 mM β-mercaptoethanol (β-ME) using a cell disruptor (JNBIO), and subjected to centrifugation at 40 000 × g for 2 h at 4°C. The supernatant was filtered and applied to an Ni-NTA (first Ni-NTA) column (Cytiva) equilibrated with binding buffer A containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.0), 300 mM NaCl, 30 mM imidazole, and 2 mM β-ME. The column was washed with binding buffer A and proteins eluted with elution buffer containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.0), 300 mM NaCl, 300 mM imidazole, and 2 mM β-ME. Eluted proteins were incubated with glutathione S-transferase (GST)-fused PSP to remove the His6-tag and dialyzed overnight at 4°C against buffer B containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.0), 300 mM NaCl, and 2 mM β-ME. After dialysis, PSP was removed using a GST column (Cytiva). Proteins were reapplied to a second Ni-NTA column equilibrated with buffer B and eluted with binding buffer A. The eluted proteins were loaded onto a Superose 6 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) equilibrated in 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.0), 600 mM NaCl, and 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) for size exclusion chromatography (SEC). The peak fractions were pooled for cryo-EM sample preparation or flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. The purification procedures for SLFN5, SLFN11, and SLFN13 constructs were the same as for SLFN14, except that the HEPES (pH 7.0) was replaced with Tris–HCl (pH 8.5) in the buffer for SLFN11 and SLFN13. SLFN14(E206A/E211A), SLFN11(E209A/E214A), and SLFN13(E208A/E213A) variants and wild-type SLFN5 were used in ATP hydrolysis, DNA unwinding, and analytical gel filtration assays.

  Recombinant pSKB-SLFN14-H were expressed in E. coli Rosetta (DE3) cells. Transformed bacteria were cultured at 37°C in Terrific Broth (TB) medium until the OD600 reached 0.6, and then induced by the addition of 100 μM isopropyl-1-thio-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG). Induced bacteria were grown overnight at 18°C and collected by centrifugation. Collected cells were lysed in ice-cold buffer containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.0), 500 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 30 mM imidazole, 1 μM DNase I, 1 mM PMSF, and 2 mM β-ME using a cell disruptor (JNBIO), and subjected to centrifugation at 40 000 × g for 1 h at 4°C. The supernatant was filtered and applied to an Ni-NTA column (Cytiva) equilibrated with 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.0), 500 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 30 mM imidazole, and 2 mM β-ME. After washing with the binding buffer, proteins were eluted with an elution buffer containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.0), 500 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 300 mM imidazole, and 2 mM β-ME. Eluted proteins were concentrated and loaded onto a HiLoad 16/60 Superdex 75 column pg (Cytiva) equilibrated with gel filtration buffer 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.0), 150 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT. The peak fractions were collected. SLFN14-R constructs were purified by the same procedure as SLFN14-H, except that HEPES (pH 7.5) was used instead of HEPES (pH 7.0) and no glycerol was added in the buffer.

  The severe acute respiratory coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) non-structural protein 13 (Nsp13) recombinant expression plasmid was kindly provided by Professors Liming Yan and Zihe Rao from Tsinghua University. The expression and purification of Nsp13 were carried out as described previously [43]. The Zika virus (ZIKV) helicase (residues 172–617) recombinant expression plasmid was a generous gift from Professor Haitao Yang, ShanghaiTech University. The protocols for expression and purification of ZIKV helicase were performed as described previously [44].


### Cryo-EM grid preparation and data acquisition
  Purified SLFN14(E206A/E211A) proteins were loaded onto Superose 6 Increase 10/300 GL (Cytiva) equilibrated with buffer containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.0), 300 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT. The SLFN14 variant collected from the peak fraction (0.5 mg/ml) was used for cryo-EM grid preparation. A 3 μl aliquot of freshly purified SLFN14 variant was applied to glow-discharged R1.2/1.3 holey copper grids (Quantifoil Micro Tools GmbH). Grids were blotted and flash-frozen in pre-cooled liquid ethane using a Vitrobot Mark IV machine (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 100% humidity.

  Cryo-EM data for SLFN14 variants were collected under a Titan Krios 300 kV electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a K3 camera (Gatan) working in super-resolution mode. Automatic data collection was performed with EPU software (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with defocus ranging from 1.0 to 3.0 μm. Movies were recorded in 40 frames under a dose rate of 27 e−/Å2/s, giving a total dose of 60 e−/Å2. The nominal magnification was 105 000×, giving a calibrated pixel size of 0.4275 Å. Statistics for data collection are summarized in Table 1.


### Cryo-EM data processing
  Beam-induced motion correction and dose weighting were performed using MotionCor2 [45]. Contrast transfer function parameters were estimated using Gctf [46]. Protein particles were automatically picked by the BoxNet convolutional neural network in the program Warp [47]. Rounds of reference-free 2D classification were performed using Relion3 [48] to select the clear and homogeneous particles. Higher order aberrations, anisotropic magnification, and per-particle defocus values were refined and particles were polished using Relion3. Shiny particles were 3D refined using Relion3 [49], generating the final reconstruction with C2 symmetry applied. The resolution was estimated using the gold standard FSC = 0.143 criterion [50, 51]. The maps were sharpened using DeepEMhancer [52] and displayed and segmented using UCSF ChimeraX (version 1.16) [53].


### Cryo-EM model building
  The atomic models of SLFN14 were built from the corresponding AlphaFold2 models [54] and then manually tuned in COOT [55] based on the density map. Models were refined and validated by Phenix [56]. Refinement and validation statistics are summarized in Table 1. Structural illustrations were prepared using UCSF ChimeraX (version 1.16) [57] and the PyMOL Molecular Graphics System (version 0.99, Schrödinger LLC; http://www.pymol.org/).


### Preparation for nucleic acid substrates
  DNA and RNA oligonucleotides (HPLC grade) were purchased from RuiBiotech. The double-stranded (ds) nucleic acid substrates were generated from single-stranded (ss) substrates by annealing. tRNA was generated by in vitro transcription as described previously [32]. Briefly, DNA templates (with a T7 promotor) for the tRNAs were synthesized from RuiBiotech and amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR). PCR products were extracted using phenol and precipitated with 70% ethanol at –80°C for 2 h. The DNA pellet was re-dissolved in diethyl pyrocarbonate (DEPC)-treated water. In vitro transcription of tRNAs was performed following the instructions for the Transcript Aid T7 High Yield Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The tRNAs were purified by 12% denaturing urea–PAGE, extracted, and precipitated by ethanol. The tRNA pellet was re-dissolved in DEPC-treated water to a concentration of 500 ng/μl. The tRNA was heated at 65°C for 10 min and slowly cooled to room temperature. The annealed RNA was aliquoted and stored at –80°C. For cleavage assays of native tRNAs, small RNAs (mainly composed of tRNA) were extracted from cultured 293T cells following the mirVana miRNA Isolation kit (Ambion) manual. For cleavage assays of rRNAs, total RNA was extracted from cultured 293T cells using TRI Reagent (Sigma-Aldrich). The nucleic acid substrates used in this study are listed in Supplementary Tables S4−S6.


### SEC-RALS
  A coupled right angle light scattering (RALS)-refractive index detector (Malvern) was connected in line to SEC using a Superose 6 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) to determine the apparent molecular masses of the applied protein samples. Purified SLFN14 at 2 mg/ml was used for SEC in buffer 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.0), 150 mM NaCl. The RALS data were analyzed by OMNISEC software. For SEC assay without RALS, purified SLFN14, SLFN11, SLFN13, and SLFN5 constructs were loaded onto a Superose 6 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) equilibrated with buffer containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.0) or Tris–HCl (pH 8.5), 300 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT. Bovine serum albumin (BSA) was used as a molecular mass marker.


### Mass photometry
  Mass photometry was conducted using Refeyn OneMP (Refeyn Ltd), with data acquisition facilitated by AcquireMP software (Refeyn Ltd v.2.3). SLFN14 (25 nM) was applied in a filtered buffer containing 20 mM Tris (pH 7.0), 1 mM DTT with variable concentrations of NaCl. Mass photometry signals were recorded for 60 s. Data analysis was conducted using DiscoverMP software.


### Protein thermal shift assay
  The 20 μl reaction mixture was prepared by adding 2 μl of 500-fold diluted 5000× SYPRO Orange dye (Thermo Fisher Scientific, S6650) to an assay buffer containing 40 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 20 mM KCl, 4 mM MgCl2, 2 mM DTT, along with either SLFN14-H and ATP/ADP (1:10 molar ratio) or ssDNA (1:1 molar ratio), achieving a final SLFN14-H concentration of 8 μM. The reaction mixtures were subsequently dispensed into a 96-well plate and analyzed using a real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad CFX96). The thermal program was set as follows: temperature ramping from 25°C to 95°C at a rate of 0.05°C/s, with continuous monitoring of SYPRO Orange fluorescence signals. Melting temperatures (Tms) were determined through derivative analysis of the fluorescence intensity versus temperature curves.


### Nucleic acid cleavage assay
  SLFN proteins were individually incubated (2 μM) with or without small RNA/ssRNA/dsRNA (2 μM) or total RNA/tRNA (50 ng/μl) in cleavage buffer containing 40 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 20 mM KCl, 4 mM MgCl2, 2 mM DTT, and 5% glycerol for 30 min at 37°C. The total RNA cleavage products were analyzed on an agarose gel. Other cleavage products were resolved on a 12% denaturing urea–polyacrylamide gel. After electrophoresis at 250 V for 40 min in 1× TBE buffer, the samples were stained with ethidium bromide (EB) and visualized using the Molecular Imager Gel Doc XR + System (Bio-Rad). All experiments were repeated twice, and the data showed satisfactory consistency.


### Electophoretic mobility shift assay
  The SLFN14-R wild type (WT) and mutations of increasing concentrations were individually incubated with 1 μM tRNASer in a buffer containing 40 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.5), 20 mM KCl, 2 mM MgCl2, and 2 mM DTT for 30 min at 4°C. SLFN14-H of increasing concentrations was incubated with 1 μM ssDNA in a similar buffer but with pH 8.0 for 30 min at 4°C. After incubation, each sample was mixed with a loading buffer containing 3% glycerol and 0.05% bromophenol blue before being loaded onto a 2% native agarose gel. Electrophoresis was carried out at 14.0 V cm−1, 4°C for 30 min in a running buffer containing 40 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.5/8.0), and visualized by UV light.


### Assay for HIV infection
  Lentivirus packaging was performed in 293T cells seeded in 6-well plates (4 × 105 cells/ml, 2 ml/well) and cultured overnight. The cells were then transfected using a polyethylenimine (PEI)-based protocol. Briefly, two mixtures were prepared: Mix 1 containing 125 μl of Opti-MEM, 0.5 μg of pCMV-VSV-G, 1.5 μg of pNL4-3-EGFP-ΔENV, and 1.5 μg of pcDNA3.1; and Mix 2 containing 125 μl of Opti-MEM and 10.5 μl of PEI. The mixtures were combined, incubated for 20 min, and added to cells for 48 h. Target cells were seeded in 96-well plates (4 × 105 cells/ml, 200 μl/well). After 48 h of transfection, the virus-containing supernatant was harvested (centrifuged at 4000 × g for 15 min), and 40 μl of clarified supernatant was used to infect target cells (triplicate wells) for 2 h. The inoculum was replaced with Dulbecco's modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with fresh 2% fetal bovine serum (ExCell Bio) and cultured for 48 h. Infection efficiency was analyzed via flow cytometry.

  For RNA quantification, 100 μl of viral supernatant was processed using the Beyotime Viral RNA Kit (Vazyme, R0035S). cDNA synthesis was performed with HiScript III RT SuperMix (Vazyme, R323-01), followed by quantitative real-time (q)PCR (ChamQ SYBR Master Mix, Q711-02). Primers used in qPCR are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

  For protein analysis, cells were lysed with RIPA buffer (Beyotime, P0013B) containing 50 mM Tris (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 1% sodium deoxycholate, and 0.1% SDS, supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Selleck, B14001). Immunoblotting was performed to assess glyceraldehyde phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), HIV p24, and SLFN14 expression. All steps were conducted under standard conditions (37°C, 5% CO2).


### ATP hydrolysis assay
  The ATP hydrolysis assay was performed using the Malachite Green Phosphate Assay Kit (Sigma-Aldrich). A total of 100 nM protein was pre-incubated at room temperature in 150 μl of reaction buffer containing 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, and 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.0) for Nsp13, SLFN14, and SLFN5 constructs, or Tris–HCl (pH 8.5) for ZIKV helicase, SLFN11, and SLFN13 constructs. We added 2 mM ATP to initiate the ATP hydrolysis reaction, and 20 μl of sample was collected at each time point (1, 5, 10, 20, and 30 min). The reaction was terminated by adding 180 μl of stop buffer containing 150 mM NaCl, 20 mM EDTA, and 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.0) or Tris–HCl (pH 8.5). An 80 μl aliquot of the reaction mixture was incubated with 20 μl of Working Reagent (generated by mixing Reagent A and Reagent B at a ratio of 100:1 v/v) in a 96-well plate at room temperature for 30 min. The absorbance was measured at 620 nm by a Bio-Tek Epoch2 spectrophotometer (Agilent).


### DNA unwinding assay
  The helicase activity was monitored by measuring the fluorescence intensity of CY3-labeled dsDNA with a 5′ or 3′ overhang. The excitation and emission wavelengths were set to 547 nm and 562 nm, respectively. Proteins (50 nM) were mixed with 100 nM dsDNA and 1 μM trap ssDNA in reaction buffer containing 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, 0.1 mg/ml BSA, and 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.0) or Tris–HCl (pH 8.5) on a 96-well plate for 5 min. We added 1 mM ATP to initiate the DNA unwinding reaction, and the fluorescence intensity was instantly recorded by a Spark™ 10M microplate reader (Tecan) at 1, 2, 5, 10, 15, 20, and 30 min. The nucleic acid substrates used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S6.

### Structure prediction
  SLFN14 structure predictions were executed using ColabFold-AlphaFold2 [60, 61] both web-based and locally. For both prediction methods, the MSA depth was reduced by adjusting the max_msa parameter to 32:64. To increase the chance of sampling alternative conformations, multiple seeds were used to predict more models. For each seed, five models were generated, resulting in a total of 90 structures.


### Structural analysis
  TM scores were calculated using TM align [62]. Principal component analysis (PCA) was carried out in CPPTRAJ [63], with residues unresolved in our SLFN14 structure omitted.


### Molecular dynamics simulation
  The molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of SLFN14 were conducted using GROMACS 2023. The initial structure, derived from AlphaFold predictions, was parameterized with the AMBER99SB-ILDN force field and solvated in a TIP3P water model within a cubic box. The system was neutralized with 0.15 M K+/Cl– ions and subjected to energy minimization. Subsequently, equilibration was performed in two stages: (i) NVT ensemble at 310 K for 100 ps with positional restraints on the protein backbone; and (ii) NPT ensemble at 1 bar for 100 ps to stabilize pressure. MD simulations were carried out for 20 ns under periodic boundary conditions, utilizing GPU acceleration and the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method for long-range electrostatics. Trajectories were recorded at 4 ps intervals for subsequent analysis.


## Results

### Overall structure of full-length SLFN14
  To gain molecular insights into SLFN14, we solved a cryo-EM structure of the full-length human SLFN14 variant to an average resolution of 3.36 Å (Fig. 1A; Supplementary Fig. S1A, B). The SLFN14 variant contains two point mutations in the RNase catalytic center (E206A and E211A) because the recombinant WT form exhibited a strong cytotoxic effect for host cells. SLFN14 forms a hollow medal-shaped dimer (Fig. 1B; Supplementary Fig. S1C). In the structural model, the N-terminal RNase domain (residues 9−350), the SWADL domain (residues 379−570), and the C-terminal helicase domain (571−907) are clearly observed, and the three domains tandemly aligned in a crescent shape. The electron density of residues 1−8, 161−178, 351−378, 518−524, and 908−912 is missing due to potential flexibility of the corresponding regions. The RNase domain folds into a U-pillow-shaped pseudodimer consisting of an N-lobe, a C-lobe, and a bipartite bridging region (BR) with intrinsic 2-fold topological symmetry (Supplementary Fig. S2A), which is in accordance with the RNase domains of other SLFNs [12, 32, 35, 40]. The resolved SLFN14 SWADL domain is composed of a central five-stranded β-sheet and seven surrounding α-helices (Supplementary Fig. S2B). Notably, the last α-helix (αS7) points away from the globular main body of the SWADL domain and is directly succeeded by the first α-helix (αH1) of the helicase domain (Supplementary Fig. S2C). The complete SLFN14 C-terminal helicase domain, composed of two lobes, is oriented antiparallel with αS7–αH1 (Supplementary Fig. S2D). As such, the second lobe (lobe-2) sits on top of the globular core of the SWADL domain, whereas the first lobe (lobe-1) is at the end of the crescent architecture. Several pathogenic missense mutations have been reported in SLFN14 which are associated with the occurrence of inherited thrombocytopenia and clinical bleeding [30, 31, 64]. Corresponding SLFN14 protein mutations include K218E, K219N, V220D, and R223W. All four residues are located on the C-lobe of the RNase domain, covering one side of the central valley entrance (Fig. 1B, C). Another genetic study found that a nonsense mutation in SLFN14 causing premature termination of translation at Thr853 is associated with an atrial septal defect [65]. In the absence of the C-terminal residues from Thr853 onwards, the central β-sheet of lobe-2 would be seriously perturbed (Fig. 1D). Intimate inter-domain contacts are found for the three domains of SLFN14, especially in the area surrounding αS7–αH1 (Supplementary Fig. S2E−J).


### The dimeric interface of SLFN14
  Based on structural comparison with other subgroup III members, the dimeric medal-shaped SLFN14 resembles SLFN11 [41] (Supplementary Fig. S3A). Consistent with the structural information, SEC analysis indicated that SLFN11 and SLFN14 are dimeric in solution, whereas SLFN5 is monomeric (Fig. 2A). Further RALS assay confirmed that purified SLFN14 proteins had a molecular mass of ∼225 kDa, corresponding to a dimeric composition (Supplementary Fig. S3B). We also performed mass photometry assay to analyze the oligomeric state of SLFN14 in different NaCl concentrations. Consistent with the RALS result, the SLFN14 dimer was predominant, but here the SLFN14 monomer fraction could be observed. With 50, 150, or 200 mM NaCl, the SLFN14 monomer took up 5−6% of the total protein sample. With 300 mM NaCl, the monomer fraction increased to 11% (Fig. 2B), suggesting that SLFN14 had a relatively weak NaCl concentration-dependent dimer–monomer equilibrium in solution as compared with SLFN11 [41].

  Two dimeric interfaces mediated by the two RNase domains (interface II) and two helicase domains (interface I) were found for the SLFN14 dimer (Fig. 1B). Interface II is characterized by a face-to-face contact of neighboring RNase domains and perfect alignment of their central valleys (Supplementary Fig. S3C), which are likely to accommodate pairwise nucleic acid substrates in a way similar to SLFN11 [41]. The N-lobe, C-lobe, and BR all contribute to formation of the ∼2130 Å2 interface II (Fig. 1B). The major interactions include hydrogen bonds between Asp134 and Gln85/Gln93, and between Lys252 and Gln72 (Fig. 2C). In addition, Asp182 in the RNase domain may form a salt bridge with Arg531 in the SWADL domain of the neighboring molecule (Fig. 2D), but this interaction seems not to be conserved among SLFNs (Supplementary Fig. S3D). Face-to-face dimerization of the RNase domain was also reported for subgroup II SLFN12 [12, 35]. On the opposite side of interface II in the SLFN14 dimer, the two helicase domains constitute the ∼420 Å2 interface I in a head-to-head manner (Fig. 1B). Lobe-1 accounts for the intermolecular contact via a main polypeptide chain hydrogen bond between Lys585O and Arg725N (Fig. 2E). Though SLFN14 and SLFN11 share similar overall architectures, the dimeric interfaces of the two SLFNs are not strictly conserved [41].


### The RNase activity of SLFN14
  The U-pillow-shaped RNase domain is the signature of the entire SLFN family. SLFN14 possesses the catalytic carboxylate triad and zinc finger motif in the C-lobe (Fig. 1B). The widths of the central valleys of SLFN RNase domains range from 18 Å for SLFN12 to 23 Å for SLFN13 (Fig. 3A). Surface electrostatic potential analysis revealed that SLFN14 has rich positively charged patches around the central valley as other SLFNs do [12, 32, 35, 42], and corresponding residues include Lys38/Arg39 of the N-lobe (patch 1), Lys213/Arg214 (patch 2) and Lys218/Lys219/Arg223/Lys261 (patch 3) of the C-lobe, as well as Arg127/Arg338 of the BD (patch 4) (Fig. 3B).

  Previous studies indicated that the positively charged patches are critical to the RNase activity of SLFN13, SLFN12, and SLFN11 via binding to the substrate RNAs [12, 32, 35, 42]. To investigate the endonuclease feature of SLFN14 and the role of the positively charge patches, we performed systematic biochemical analysis. First, we incubated the purified RNase domain of SLFN14 (referred to as SLFN14-R hereafter) with various types of nucleic acids, and found that SLFN14-R efficiently digested all types of RNA but showed only a limited or undetectable effect on DNA:RNA duplexes and DNAs (Fig. 3C). Further in vitro cleavage assays using type I and type II tRNA substrates confirmed the RNase activity of SLFN14-R in a temporal and protein concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 3D). Unlike SLFN11-R and SLFN13-R which yielded specific cleavage products of tRNA indicating a primary cleavage site between the acceptor stem and T-loop [32, 41, 42], SLFN14-R seemed to digest tRNA at random sites and left no clear featured band as the specific product. SLFN14-R also exhibited strong RNase activity on 28S and 18S rRNAs (Fig. 3E). We managed to obtain WT full-length SLFN14 with moderate purity, which cleaved rRNAs and tRNAs with a similar pattern to SLFN14-R (Supplementary Fig. S4A−C). Interestingly, SLFN14-R favored Mg2+ and Ca2+ over Mn2+ as cofactors in cleaving tRNA (Fig. 3F). The preference for divalent ions of SLFN14-R differs from that of SLFN11-R and SLFN13-R which both showed the highest RNase activity in the presence of Mn2+ [32, 41, 42]. Mutation(s) on the catalytic residue Glu206 or the positively charged patch 2 (pa2-mut, K213A/R214A) eliminated RNase activity of SLFN14-R on tRNASer, 28S and 18S rRNA, and total small RNAs from 293T cells, whereas the other three patch mutants (pa1-mut, K38A/R39A; pa3-mut, K218A/K219A/R223A/K261A; and pa4-mut, R127A/R338A) exhibited decreased cleavage efficiency compared with WT SLFN14-N (Fig. 3G−I). Consistent with this, the patch 2 mutant did not bind tRNASer in the electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA), but the other three patch mutants were still able to retard tRNASer (Supplementary Fig. S4D). In addition, the thrombocytopenia-related mutation K219N at patch 3 reduced the RNase activity of SLFN14 (Supplementary Fig. S4E, F). These data suggest that SLFN14 is a potent RNase with some unique properties in substrate processing with regard to other subgroup III SLFNs, and the positively charged patch 2 at the central valley of the RNase domain is particularly crucial in binding substrate RNA.


### RNA binding at the central valley is crucial for SLFN14’s antiviral function
  Next, we sought to study the role of positively charged patches in the antiviral function of SLFN14. We first performed an RNA cleavage assay on synthesized RNA substrates with predicted stem–loop structures derived from viral genomes. As expected, intact SLFN14-R efficiently digested different stem–loops from HIV and IAV, and the catalytic residue mutant E206A exhibited much weakened cleavage (Fig. 4A). We then transfected 293T cells with pNL4-3-EGFP-ΔENV and pCMV-VSV-G as previously described [32], and measured the titer of VSV-G pseudotyped HIV-1 (HIVVSV-G) produced from these cells in the presence of different SLFN14 constructs (Fig. 4B). The infection rate of HIVVSV-G showed a dramatic reduction in cells pre-transfected with WT SLFN14, indicating the restriction of HIVVSV-G (Fig. 4C). The nuclease catalytic residue mutant E206A/E211A and the nucleic acid-binding patch 2 mutant of SLFN14 exhibited significantly reduced anti-HIV activity compared with WT SLFN14, while patch 1/3/4 mutants retained robust antiviral efficacies (Fig. 4C). This trend in anti-HIV activity is congruent with their behaviors in RNA cleavage assays and EMSA (Fig. 3G–I; Supplementary Fig. S4D). To determine whether SLFN14 inhibited HIVVSV-G by suppressing virion release, we analyzed viral RNA (vRNA) and viral protein levels in supernatants from HIVVSV-G-producing cells transfected with SLFN14 or corresponding mutants. For these samples, the phenotypes in extracellular concentrations of total vRNA, partially spliced vRNA, unspliced vRNA, and p24 capsid protein level were all consistent with that in the viral titer measurement (Fig. 4D–G). These results suggest that SLFN14 suppresses HIV primarily by reducing the number of virions, and its RNase activity and substrate binding ability are indispensable for this process.


### Diversity of the SWADL domain among the SLFN family
  The SWADL domain is a unique fold of the subgroup II and III SLFN family members and has no apparent structural homolog according to the Dali Server (Supplementary Fig. S5A) [66]. We made a structural comparison of the SLFN14 SWADL domain with its counterparts of SLFN5, SLFN11, and SLFN12. SWADL domains of SLFN14 and SLFN11 showed high consistency in structural overlay, with a root mean square standard deviation (RMSD) value of 1.58 Å for aligned Cα atoms (Supplementary Fig. S5B). However, major structural differences were found between the SWADL domains of SLFN14 and SLFN5, though the sequences of corresponding regions are conserved within subgroup III SLFNs (Fig. 5A, B; Supplementary Fig. S5C). In SLFN5, the SLFN14 αS7–αH1-equivalent region at the C-terminus of the SWADL domain folds into two antiparallel β-strands, which form a β-sheet together with another two antiparallel β-strands extended from the RNase domain–SWADL connector loop. The corresponding region of this connector loop was not resolved in SLFN14, and its trace in SLFN5 would cause a major clash with αS1 and αS7 of SLFN14 (Fig. 5C). As a subgroup II family member, SLFN12 lacks the helicase domain, and the C-terminal region of its SWADL domain is less conserved than that of subgroup III SLFNs (Fig. 5B). The C-terminal region was not resolved in the SLFN12 solo structure, but only in the SLFN12–PDE3A complex structures [12, 35], where it stretches in the same direction as SLFN14 αS7 to accommodate the PDE3A molecule. In addition, the N-terminal region of the SLFN12 SWADL domain presented significant differences from SLFN14 involving αS1 (Fig. 5D). These data reveal the structural diversity of the SLFN SWADL domains.


### Characterization of the SLFN14 helicase domain
  The helicase domain of SLFN14 is composed of two topologically similar lobes which resemble the tandem RecA-like folds (domain A1 and A2) in SF1 DNA/RNA helicases [67, 68] and contain characteristic sequence motifs (Fig. 6A). To gain more insight into the SLFN14 helicase domain, we performed a Dali search using the structural model of the complete SLFN14 helicase domain. Beyond subgroup III SLFNs, the SF1 helicase UvrD from E. coli [69] is the closest kin to the SLFN14 helicase domain, followed by several other SF1 helicases from various bacterial species (Fig. 6A; Supplementary Fig. S6A, B). Interestingly, the SARS-CoV-2 helicase Nsp13 [70] was also among the most similar structural homologs of the SLFN14 helicase domain. Despite the RecA-like folds, the SLFN helicase domains lack other accessory domains or inserts required by SF1 helicases to coordinate nucleic acid substrates [5]. Regarding the characteristic helicase motifs, SLFN14 does not possess motifs Ic, Id, IVb, and IVc, which are in the subfamily-typical 1B and 2B inserts of UvrD, and motif VIa due to a shorter C-terminus (Fig. 6B; Supplementary Fig. S6C). In addition, SLFN14 exhibited different folding with UvrD at the Va motif (Supplementary Fig. S6D). Compared with UvrD and Nsp13, the SLFN14 helicase domain has two notable protruding areas, one between motifs II and III in lobe-1, and the other between motifs IVa and V in lobe-2 (Fig. 6C, D). Interestingly, the first protruding loop of SLFN14 is also unique in the SLFN family, as it contains a seven-residue insert compared with other subgroup III SLFNs (Supplementary Fig. S6E).

  Next, we investigated the residues of SLFNs potentially involved in substrate binding. In addition to the known conservation on Walker A and B motifs in helicase motifs I and II [4], most of the other ATP-coordinating residues are also identical for SLFNs and UvrD, including those in motifs Q, III, V, and VI (Fig. 6B). In contrast to other SLFNs, SLFN14 does not have an intact GxxxxGKT sequence for the Walker A motif, and the corresponding region fails to form the characteristic groove to accommodate the phosphate groups of ATP (Fig. 6E). Like UvrD, SLFN11 accommodates ssDNA with the canonical groove between the two RecA-like lobes [41]. However, surface electrostatic potential analysis revealed that SLFN14 lacks a positive charge at the equivalent region (Fig. 6F), implying different nucleic acid binding properties between SLFN14 and SLFN11. To confirm this, we tested the ssDNA binding capacity of a purified SLFN14 helicase domain construct (SLFN14-H). ssDNAs was not retarded by SLFN14-H in EMSA (Fig. 6G), nor did it alter the Tm of SLFN14-H in the protein thermoshift (PTS) assay (Fig. 6H), which confirmed the lack of ssDNA binding capacity of the SLFN14 helicase domain.


### SLFN14 is an inactive helicase
  Despite harboring intact Walker A and B motifs, human SLFN5 and SLFN11 both were shown to lack ATP hydrolysis activity [40, 41]. We subjected purified human SLFN14, SLFN11, SLFN13, and SLFN5 to the ATP hydrolysis assay. Compared with the ZIKV helicase and SARS-CoV-2 Nsp13, all SLFNs exhibited no ATPase activity (Fig. 7A). Supplementation with various nucleic acids did not increase the ATP turnover rate of SLFN14 (Fig. 7B). Next, we tested different divalent cations for viral helicases and selected SLFNs. The ATPase activities of ZIKV helicase and SARS-CoV-2 Nsp13 could be stimulated by Mg2+ but inhibited by Zn2+, which is consistent with previous studies [71, 72]. However, both SLFN11 and SLFN14 remained inactive in the presence of these cations (Supplementary Fig. S7A). We then subjected two dsDNA substrates, each with a 5′ or 3′ overhang, to the in vitro DNA unwinding assays. While Nsp13 efficiently unwound DNA with a 5′ overhang, human SLFNs exhibited negligible helicase activity on all tested DNA substrates (Fig. 7C; Supplementary Fig. S7B, C).

  When inspecting the domain connections, we noticed that the long helix αS7–αH1 at the SWADL–helicase domain interface crosses over the potential ATP-binding site of SLFN14. A structural comparison with AMPPNP-bound UvrD indicated that αS7–αH1 partially occupies the space for the adenine group of the nucleotide (Fig. 7D), rendering SLFN14 in an ATP-occluding state which is similar to SLFN11 [41], but distinct from SLFN5 (Fig. 7E). The lack of adenine nucleotide binding capacity of SLFN14 was confirmed by PTS experiments (Supplementary Fig. S7D). In SLFN5, due to the aforementioned structural diversity of the SWADL domain, especially at its C-terminal region, the starting α-helix of the helicase domain points in the opposite direction to SLFN14 αS7–αH1 [40] (Fig. 7F). As a result, the orientation of the helicase domain is inverted in SLFN5 compared with SLFN14, and lobe-1 of SLFN5 takes the space of SLFN14 lobe-2 next to the SWADL domain (Fig. 7G). Such a local conformation of SLFN5 would lead to an open ATP-binding pocket for the adenine group, though the unusual folding of the P-loop is unfavorable for holding the phosphate groups of ATP (Fig. 7E).


### Possible conformational change of SLFN14 in living cells
  Given the structural diversity of subgroup III SLFNs mentioned above, as well as a recent study reporting that a single-site phosphorylation changes SLFN11 from a SLFN14-like conformation to a SLFN5-like conformation [73], we were curious about whether SLFN14 confers a similar conformational change in living cells. We performed live-cell FRET. First, we fused CFP or YFP N-terminally to SLFN14, and co-overexpressed two types of labeled proteins in 293T cells. As indicated by the SEC result for CFP-fused SLFN14 (Supplementary Fig. S8A), fluorescent protein fusion was non-disruptive to the dimerization of SLFN14. The proximity of CFP- and YPF-labeled SLFN14 (e.g. via dimerization) would incur FRET signals with intensities negatively correlated to the distance of the donor and acceptor (Supplementary Fig. S8B). FRET signals were almost undetectable from tested cells, suggesting that either the two fluorophores in the CFP–SLFN14/YFP–SLFN14 dimer were too distant from each other to incur FRET signals, or such hetero-dimerization barely occurred in living cells (Supplementary Fig. S8C, D).

  Next, we generated an SLFN14 construct with CFP and YFP fused at the N- and C-termini, respectively, and applied it to live-cell FRET analysis for monitoring the in-cell conformation of SLFN14. For tested cells, we detected a FRET efficiency of 0.097, corresponding to a calculated average distance of 71.2 Å between the FRET pairs (Supplementary Fig. S8E). This value, however, did not match either our SLFN14 structure (51.2 Å) or the reported SLFN5 structure (78.4 Å) [40]. AlphaFold2 has proved to be a useful tool to study protein conformational change [74, 75]. To further investigate the possible domain-wide structural dynamics of SLFN14, we generated a total of 90 models using AlphaFold2 with reduced multiple sequence alignment (MSA) depth [60, 61]. Subsequent PCA [74] of these predicted models captured most of the conformational differences. Two clusters were formed at the extremes of PC1, each comprising structural models with a similar type of overall conformation (Supplementary Fig. S9A). Interestingly, these two clusters structurally corresponded to SLFN14 and SLFN5, respectively. We then calculated the similarity of each of the 90 predicted models to experimental structural models of SLFN14 and SLFN5 using TM-align [62]. The above SLFN14-like and SLFN5-like structures again were individually clustered (Supplementary Fig. S9B), and their N- and C-terminal distances also did not match the live-cell FRET results. By analyzing the models between the two clusters in PCA and TM-align plots, we found that they were likely to represent intermediate states between SLFN14-like and SLFN5-like structures, which comprised the conformation matching our live-cell FRET-derived estimate (Supplementary Fig. S9C−F). For a representative FRET-matching model, the folding of individual domains is highly consistent with those of SLFN14 and SLFN5, but the connecting region between the SWADL and helicase domains (αS7–αH1 of SLFN14) differs from that in the two cryo-EM structures. Unlike in SLFN14 (a tandem αS7–αH1 long α-helix) or SLFN5 (forming no α-helix), αS7 and αH1 in the FRET-matching model are two separate and perpendicularly aligned α-helices, which renders the helicase domain in an intermediate relative position as compared with SLFN14 and SLFN5 structures (Fig. 5C; Supplementary Fig. S9C). However, a technical limitation of the reduced MSA depth strategy was that though it facilitated generation of models with more structural diversity, the risk of model inaccuracy was also raised. Thus, the generated models require further validation. In subsequent full-atom molecular dynamics simulation analysis, however, SLFN14 displayed limited domain flexibility within 20 ns, and the relative movement between the helicase domain and SWADL domain was not observed (Supplementary Fig. 9G−I), suggesting that SLFN14 kept a stable conformation in the native form. Altogether, these results provide some hints, but not decisive evidence, for a potential conformational change of SLFN14 in cells. Whether SLFN14 performs protein modification-based domain rearrangement as SLFN11 does requires further study.


## Discussion
  In this study, we presented the first full-length structure of subgroup III SLFN family member SLFN14, and systematically characterized its biochemical properties. SLFN14 structurally resembles unphosphorylated SLFN11 in cryo-EM analysis, but may undergo conformational change in cells. SLFN14 is an efficient RNase and exhibits distinct tRNA digestion patterns compared with other SLFNs. The RNase activity of SLFN14 is essential for its antiviral activity, and can be affected by the SLFN14-related thrombocytopenia missense mutations. On the other hand, SLFN14 is an inactive helicase, characterized by the lack of the positive charge area at the canonical ssDNA-binding site of the helicase domain, and intrinsic occlusion of ATP in the SLFN11-like dimeric form. The inability to bind DNA with the helicase domain is a special feature of SLFN14 as compared with SLFN5 and SLFN11. These data contribute to a better understanding of the cellular functions of SLFN14 and expand our knowledge of the diverse SLFN family.

  The diverse domain arrangement of SLFN14, SLFN5, and SLFN11 is somewhat unexpected, because the connector region between the SWADL and helicase domains (αS7–αH1), which by its position should be a key determinant of helicase domain orientation, is quite conserved among group III SLFNs. Recently, this issue has been reconciled by a study showing that phosphorylation at Ser753 renders SLFN11 from an SLFN14-like conformation to an SLFN5-like one [73]. While cryo-EM analysis revealed that SLFN14 took a dominant unphosphorylated SLFN11-like conformation in solution, the live-cell FRET data implied an otherwise different conformation. Considering that this technique records the bulk FRET signals of given areas in cells [59], and the FRET peak value of the applied CFP–SLFN14–YFP construct was in between the theoretical values for cryo-EM-revealed SLFN14 and SLFN5 conformations, it is possible that SLFN14 experiences SLFN11-like conformational changes in cells and our live-cell FRET results represented an intermediate state averaged from experimental SLFN14 and SLFN5-like structures. This hypothesis is backed up to some extent by AlphaFold2 analysis, which generated the predicted structural models corresponding to the plausible intermediate state. However, according to the molecular dynamics simulation result, native SLFN14 displayed no major conformational change over time. We noticed that though the key regulatory residue Ser753 of SLFN11 is not strictly conserved among subgroup III SLFNs, SLFN14 possesses a serine residue (Ser755) neighboring the Ser753-equivalent residue, which may also be susceptible to phosphorylation and have a similar role to SLFN11 Ser753 in cells. On the other hand, live-cell FRET has limitations, as bulk FRET signals were unable to provide direct evidence for real-time conformational changes of target proteins. Moreover, the size of CFP/YFP and the flexibility of the linkers between the target protein and fluorescent tags may also affect the property (folding and oligomerization state, etc.) of the target protein and/or the accuracy of the FRET measurement. Further biophysical studies are needed to confirm whether SLFN14 undergoes conformational changes in physiological conditions.

  Our structural and biochemical analysis indicated that the face-to-face dimerization of the RNase domain of SLFN14 creates a longer valley for binding and cleavage of pairwise substrates. For SLFN11, dimerization is required for efficient tRNA cleavage [41]. However, as shown in our previous study, the monomeric SLFN13 RNase domain alone is able to efficiently digest various RNAs both in vitro and in cells [32]. In our current study, the RNase domain of SLFN14 also showed potent RNase activity on various substrates. Therefore, dimerization at the RNase domain seems not to be essential for the endonuclease activity of SLFN14, but may alter the substrate preference and cleavage style by doubling the length of the substrate-binding groove and numbers of catalytic sites compared with the monomeric form. Regarding the potential helicase activity, dimerization of subgroup III SLFNs seems to facilitate the autoinhibitory state because the helicase domain in the ATP-occluding conformation contributes to the dimeric interface, which was well demonstrated for SLFN11 [41, 73]. Interestingly, the dimerization interface of SLFN14 and SLFN11 does not seem to be consistent with each other, and SLFN14 exhibits a much stronger dimerization tendency than SLFN11 at least in solution. Due to the long distance between the N-termini of the two SLFN14 protomers, the FRET incidence might not be fulfilled from the tagged CFP/YFP pair even upon the formation of dimers. Thus, our current live-cell FRET data cannot explicitly reflect the oligomeric state of SLFN14 in cells. Further FRET-based studies with improved labeling strategies (e.g. using smaller fluorophores and choosing proper labeling sites on SLFN14) are needed to investigate this point.

  To hold double-stranded substrate nucleic acids, SF1 helicases usually possess specific dsDNA/dsRNA-binding domains other than the core tandem RecA-like folds. Our results and those of other groups indicate that subgroup III SLFNs exhibit no helicase activity in vitro [40], but there is evidence that SLFN11 acts as an effective helicase in cells [22]. Although the N-terminal RNase domain may potentially work as a dsDNA/dsRNA-binding element, it is spatially distant from the helicase domain, as shown by the structural models. To work alone as effective dsDNA/dsRNA unwinders, subgroup III SLFNs need to confer domain rearrangement or proper oligomerization in cells. Alternatively, subgroup III SLFNs may require other cofactor(s) directing them to the target dsDNA/dsRNA or may be involved in large complexes in a similar way to their viral homolog SARS-CoV-2 Nsp13 [70]. For the former possibility, whether subgroup III SLFNs share the same cofactor or have specific cofactors is an interesting question to pursue. For the latter, as the integration of SLFN11 into the replication fork was reported in previous studies [22], it is worthwhile studying whether other subgroup III SLFNs are also recruited to certain ribonucleoprotein complexes to exert their cellular functions. Finally, although the vast majority of SF1 members are bona fide helicases, there are exceptions that do not translocate along or unwind dsDNA/dsRNAs [76–78]. Considering the inability of the SLFN14 helicase domain to bind either DNA or ATP, SLFN14 probably lacks the helicase activity and acts only as an RNase in cells. Rabbit reticulocytes strongly express a C-terminally truncated form of SLFN14 with an incomplete helicase domain, which was shown to have different subcellular localization and endonuclease activity from the full-length version [34]. However, this truncated SLFN14 has not been reported for human cells. Whether the helicase domain of SLFN14 is a mere obsolete relic of evolution or has certain functions in humans requires further study.

  We noticed that overexpression of SLFN14 caused severe cell growth arrest, while the RNase-deficient mutants were normal. This observation of the cytotoxicity of SLFN14 is consistent with the fact that its protein level is undetectable or extremely low in almost all human tissues except bone marrow [29]. In addition, a retrospective analysis of previous transcriptomic data revealed that the mRNA level of SLFN14 was in general far less than those of other subgroup III SLFN genes in > 1600 tested tumor cell lines [3]. Yet, the RNase activity is crucial for the reported antiviral activity of SLFN14 [79]. As we showed that SLFN14 cleaved both viral genomic RNA fragments and tRNAs/rRNAs, the interferon-inducible SLFN14 is likely to act as a sweeper to eradicate viral RNA together with infected cells. Besides its antiviral function, the physiological function of SLFN14 is implicated in platelet maturation [30]. Several point mutations were found to be associated with the occurrence of thrombocytopenia accompanied by excessive bleeding and platelet secretion defects [30, 31, 80, 81]. A prior study indicated that these mutations did not strongly affect rRNA degradation, but reduced the protein expression levels of the corresponding SLFN14 mutants [80]. Our in vitro RNA cleavage assays showed that the disease-related K219N mutant and positively charged patch 3 mutant involving the disease-related residues Lys218, Lys219, and Arg223 preserved RNase activity. Given this phenotypic evidence, the role of RNase activity of SLFN14 and how it is regulated are still unclear and need to be resolved for the development of corresponding therapeutic approaches against thrombocytopenia in humans.
