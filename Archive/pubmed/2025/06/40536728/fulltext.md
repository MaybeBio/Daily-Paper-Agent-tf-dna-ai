# Integrative Proteomics and Genomics Identify Novel Biomarkers and Therapeutic Targets in Vitiligo via Mendelian Randomization


## Introduction
  Given that the proteome is a major source of therapeutic targets, we conducted a proteome-wide Mendelian randomization (MR) combined with transcriptome sequencing analysis to identify candidate protein markers and therapeutic targets for vitiligo.


## Methods
  Based on protein quantitative trait loci (pQTLs) and genetic associations with vitiligo obtained from the European Bioinformatics Institute (EBI) database (60 vitiligo cases and 402,672 controls), and the UK Biobank (95 vitiligo cases and 337,064 controls), bidirectional MR and colocalization analyses identified genetically predicted levels of nine proteins collectively linked to vitiligo risk. Based on the RNA-seq data and single-cell RNA-seq data of vitiligo, bioinformatics analysis and model prediction of genes associated with vitiligo progression evaluated the relationship between candidate core proteins and the development of vitiligo.


## Results
  Four proteins (KLF4, MYL4, TNFRSF13C, TNFSF13B) were associated with lower vitiligo risk, while five proteins (ALPI, CDH1, ITGB1, SERPINH1, TNFSF10) were linked to higher risk. Of these, three proteins (KLF4, TNFRSF13C, and TNFSF10) were high priority with the most convincing evidence. Bioinformatics analysis and model prediction of genes associated with vitiligo progression showed these three protein-coding genes were significantly associated with vitiligo occurrence, and their functions were related to cell cycle, apoptosis, oxidative stress, inflammatory response, and immune infiltration. Mechanistically, the expression of these key candidate molecules was regulated by various miRNAs and transcription factors. The druggability assessment and molecular docking identified some drugs targeting these proteins, such as APTO-2535 and butyric acid.


## Conclusion
  KLF4, TNFRSF13C, and TNFSF10 may be involved in regulating the occurrence and development of vitiligo, providing potential targets for improving the diagnosis and treatment of vitiligo.

## Introduction
  Vitiligo is a skin disease characterizing the loss of melanocytes or their function, leading to depigmentation. It is an acquired condition affecting 0.5–2% of the global population, causing a significant psychological burden for patients [1]. Currently, the precise cause of the onset of vitiligo is not fully understood. It is believed to involve a combination of genetic and environmental factors including oxidative stress, inflammation, autoimmune reactions, and melanocyte self-destruction [2]. Despite significant advancements in treating vitiligo, a deeper understanding of its pathogenesis and stable and satisfactory treatment efficacy are yet to be achieved [3]. Notwithstanding the limited development of new drugs, the complex pathogenic mechanism of vitiligo necessitates further exploration of new potential molecular targets.

  Cyclic proteins are crucial regulatory factors in molecular pathways, which have been considered targets for drug therapy. Cyclic proteins may play a role in the development of vitiligo and could present therapeutic benefits [4, 5]. Many studies have reported a strong correlation between vitiligo and various inflammatory proteins in serum, including interleukin (IL)-17, transforming growth factor-β (TGFβ), interferon-γ (IFNγ), chemokine (C-X-C) ligand (CXCL)9, CXCL10, IL-1β, and IL-6 [6]. Additionally, many circulating proteins in serum have been closely linked to the onset and progression of vitiligo. An example is fatty acid-binding protein 4 (FABP4), which may contribute positively to vitiligo development by mediating blood lipid abnormalities and high blood sugar levels [7]. Further, S100B has also been found to be closely associated with the deposition of pigments in active vitiligo cases [8]. A previous study showed the differential expression of 28 proteins using two‑dimensional gel electrophoresis coupled with mass spectrometry in progressive patients with vitiligo. Interestingly, 13 proteins were differentially expressed in patients with stable vitiligo compared to healthy groups [4]. However, these reported associations between circulating proteins and vitiligo were based on observational data, which may be influenced by confounding factors and reverse causation. Furthermore, conducting randomized control trials to investigate the causal relationships between thousands of proteins and vitiligo is not practical, especially in the absence of any definitive evidence.

  Mendelian randomization (MR) studies investigate the causal relationship between exposure (including circulating proteins) and outcomes using genetic variation—single nucleotide polymorphism (SNPs)—as instrumental variables (IVs) of exposure [9]. These instrumental genetic variants in MR are randomly assigned and, therefore, they are not associated with confounding factors, such as self-selected lifestyle and behavior [10]. Furthermore, as genetic IVs occur before the onset of the disease, the interference of reverse causality is overcome to some extent. Currently, MR studies around vitiligo focus mainly on the association between a single exposure factor and vitiligo, for instance, causal relationships between circulating vitamin D, proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) levels, and vitiligo risk [11, 12]. A recent MR-based study revealed causal relationships between vitiligo and 49 proteins related to inflammation and immunity, 39 immune cell features, and 59 metabolites [13].

  In this study, we performed a proteome-wide MR analysis based on protein quantitative trait loci (pQTLs). We also performed colocalization analyses to identify novel blood proteins that are causally associated with vitiligo. In addition, bioinformatics analysis was used to further screen and explore the mechanisms and cellular functions of key proteins, aiming to provide potential therapeutic targets for vitiligo; an overview of the study design is shown in the graphical abstract.


## Methods

### Data Sources
  The following data sources were used: (1) protein quantitative trait loci (pQTLs): pQTL data were from two UKB-PPP (UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project) databases that characterized the plasma proteomic profile of 54,219 UKB participants [14]. This study selected data from the baseline cohort, which described pQTLs in 34,557 participants of European ancestry. (2) Outcome data for vitiligo: participants in the outcome-related genome-wide association studies (GWAS) studies included were mainly people of European ancestry who were included in the EBI database (GCST90436590), including 60 cases and 402,672 controls, and the UK Biobank database (ukb-a-115), including 95 cases and 337,064 controls. (3) The two datasets (GSE75819 and GSE203262) from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) were used in this study. GSE75819 is a microarray containing transcriptomic data with annotation platform GPL6884, including skin samples of the control group (n = 15) and the vitiligo group (n = 15). GSE203262 is single-cell RNA sequencing data from patient-matched lesional and nonlesional skin from seven patients with vitiligo. No ethics committee approval was required and the dataset was taken from public databases so no permissions were required.


### Protein Quantitative Trait Loci Mendelian Randomization Analysis
  The outcome IDs filtered through the GWAS-related database were extracted from the risk (outcome) summary data to derive the relevant causal relationships in pQTL. The SNPs associated with each gene at the locus-wide significance threshold (p < 1e−5) were selected as potential IVs, and the LD (linkage disequilibrium) between SNPs was calculated. Only SNPs with R2 < 0.001 (clumping window size = 10,000 kb) were retained, and weak instrumental variables were filtered out by F value (> 10). Further analyses entailed inverse variance weighted (IVW, using meta-analysis method combined with the Wald estimate of each SNP), MR Egger (based on the assumption that the instrument strength is independent of direct effects or InSIDE), weighted median (allows one to correctly estimate the causality in up to 50% of cases where IVs are invalid), weighted model estimation (has a greater ability to detect causal effects, smaller bias, and lower type I error rate than MR-Egger regression). Relevant statistical analyses were done to evaluate the reliability of the causal relationship to obtain an overall estimate of the impact of all cis and some cross-region gene expression in whole blood on vitiligo. The Wald ratio was used when there was only one statistical method available for the SNP in the causal relationship. Finally, the screened causal relationships were verified and analyzed through the leave-one-out method. Sequentially, the exposure factors and outcome data were then read using the extract_instruments and extract_outcome_data functions.


### Colocalization Analysis
  Colocalization analysis was performed using the colocalization (coloc) method with eQTL summary data and GWAS for vitiligo. The 100-kb region around the index SNP was used to estimate the posterior probability. In the outcomes of coloc analysis, H3 represents the posterior probability that two traits (gene expression and vitiligo) were associated but had distinct causal variants; H4 represents the posterior probability that two traits were associated and shared a single causal variant. SNP.PP.H4 > 0.8 was used as the colocalization threshold.


### Single Sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA)
  ssGSEA was performed with the GSVA package on the expression profile data of the vitiligo samples in GSE75819 (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/, including 15 cases of vitiligo skin tissue and 15 cases of non-skin tissue) based on the core protein genes. The vitiligo samples of GSE75819 were divided into high- and low-score groups according to ssGSEA scores of the core gene (high: score > average score; low: score < average score). Subsequently, the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were analyzed using the R package limma, and outstanding DEGs were selected on the basis of p value (P < 0.05) and log2foldchange (> 1 or < − 1). Finally, the GSEABase package was utilized to examine the alterations in biological functions and signaling pathways between the high- and low-score groups. The data were analyzed and graphed using R script (version 4.0.4) on a Linux system (Ubuntu, version 20.04 LTS).


### Immune Infiltration Analysis
  Package CIBERSORT in R was used to calculate the infiltration levels (scores) of immune cells in vitiligo samples in the high- and low-score groups. The Pearson correlation coefficient was used to analyze the correlation between the ssGSEA scores of core genes and the immune cell scores.


### Regulatory Network Analysis of Core Genes
  This study utilized the R package RcisTarget to predict transcription factors. All calculations performed by RcisTarget are motif-based. The normalized enrichment score (NES) of motifs depends on the total number of motifs in the database. Besides the motifs provided by the source data, this analysis also generates additional annotation files by considering motif similarity and gene sequences. In the initial step of evaluating the overexpression of each motif within the gene set, the area under the curve (AUC) is calculated for each motif–gene set pair. The AUC is determined by analyzing the recovery curve of motif sorting within the gene set and the NES for each motif is then calculated using the AUC distribution of all motifs within the gene set.


### Potential Drug Prediction
  The DGIdb (Drug–Gene Interaction database, https://www.dgidb.org/) is a drug–gene interaction database that provides information on genes and their known or potential drugs [15]. The database contains more than 14,000 drug–gene interactions, including 2600 genes and 6300 drugs that target these genes, and 6700 other genes. We obtained potential drugs through key proteins and visualized gene and drug networks through Cytoscape software.


### Molecular Docking
  The protein 3D structures of core proteins were retrieved from the PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org/) and the Alphafold database (https://alphafold.com/). The information was used to predict key gene drugs in the DGIdb database (https://www.dgidb.org/) and obtain relevant molecules. Next, the drug component structure was obtained from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). AutoDock software was employed for molecular docking, the genetic algorithm was chosen as the docking algorithm, and docking was performed 50 times. The docking result with the lowest binding energy for visualization was then selected. These results were imported into PYMOL to visualize the binding sites of small molecules with the protein.


### miRNA Network Construction
  MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are small non-coding RNAs that have been shown to regulate gene expression by promoting the degradation of mRNAs or inhibiting their translation. Therefore, we further analyzed whether there are some miRNAs in key genes that regulate the transcription or degradation of some dangerous genes. We obtained key gene-related miRNAs from the miRcode database and visualized the miRNA network of genes using Cytoscape software.


### Construction of Nomogram Prediction Model
  A nomogram is based on regression analysis, which uses scaled line segments plotted on the same plane according to a certain proportion based on gene expression levels, to express the interrelationships between variables in the prediction model. By constructing a multifactor regression model and assigning scores to each value level of each influencing factor on the basis of their contribution to the outcome variable (the size of the regression coefficient), and then adding up the scores to obtain the total score, the predicted value can be calculated.


## Results

### Proteome‑Wide MR Analysis Based on Training and Validation Sets for Nine Circulating Proteins in Vitiligo
  The outcome ID of a summary of the statistical data of 402,732 samples related to vitiligo (with 402,672 controls and 60 cases) was identified as GCST90436590. Further screening using MR analysis revealed 140 proteins that were associated with pQTL positive outcomes of causal relationships (Fig. 1a). Notably, certain proteins, such as SPRR1B, DCDC2C, CCND2, and others may be linked to a decreased risk of vitiligo while proteins like TNFSF10, TIMD4, and LRRC37A2 may be associated with an increased vitiligo risk (Fig. S1).Fig. 1Mendelian randomization (MR) screening for proteins with causal effects on the risk of vitiligo. a Volcano plot showing results from MR proteome-wide in the training set. b Scatter plots showing four ways to reflect the causal relationship between proteins and vitiligo in the validation set

  We obtained the outcome ID ukb-a-115 from the summary statistics of 337,159 vitiligo-related samples (337,064 controls, 95 cases) to identify the key proteins affecting vitiligo among the 140 pairs of pQTL proteins mentioned above. We found nine pairs of proteins with positive pQTL outcomes in the MR, namely ALPI, CDH1, ITGB1, KLF4, MYL4, SERPINH1, TNFRSF13C, TNFSF10, and TNFSF13B (Fig. 1b). Notably among these proteins, KLF4, MYL4, TNFRSF13C, and TNFSF13B may be associated with a lower vitiligo risk. In contrast, ALPI, CDH1, ITGB1, SERPINH1, and TNFSF10 may be associated with a higher risk of vitiligo. Sensitivity analysis confirmed the reliability of these findings as the the overall error bars were not impacted by removing any SNP, indicating the robustness of the nine selected causal relationships (Fig. S2).


### Colocalization and Reverse MR Validation Support the Causal Relationship of Three Proteins with Vitiligo
  We conducted a colocalization analysis of nine proteins at the pQTL-vitiligo level to further identify the key proteins affecting vitiligo. Among them, there were three proteins with colocated SNP.PP.H4 > 0.8, namely KLF4, TNFRSF13C, and TNFSF10 (Table S1, Fig. 2a–c). Additionally, reverse Mendelian verification results to further verify the observed findings showed that only these three proteins had a unidirectional relationship with vitiligo (p > 0.05, Fig. 2d). ### KLF4, TNFRSF13C, and TNFSF10 Are Closely Related to Occurrence and Development of Vitiligo
  In order to further explore the potential relationship between candidate core proteins and vitiligo, we obtained vitiligo-related progression genes through the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/) to investigate whether the identified molecules are involved in vitiligo occurrence and development. We conducted a Pearson correlation analysis between the expression levels of the top 20 genes associated with vitiligo and the core molecules selected above based on the relevance score. We found that KLF4 had a negative relationship with several vitiligo progression-related genes, such as MITF, PTPN22, FOXD3, and CTLA4 to a certain extent, with the most significant negative relationship observed with FOXD3 (p = − 0.889, p < 0.001). Further, TNFRSF13C was positively correlated with various pigment genes, such as TYR, NLRP1, MLANA, and DCT. Additionally, TNFSF10 was positively correlated with FBXO11 and CAT, and the correlation with FBXO11 was significantly positive (p = 0.852, p < 0.001). However, the expression of other genes, such as TYR, POMC, MELANA, and DCT, showed a negative correlation (Fig. 3a).Fig. 3Relationship between core proteins and the progression of vitiligo. a Heatmap showing the Pearson correlation coefficients of each core gene with vitiligo-related genes. b–d Nomogram model, ROC, and decision curve analysis (DCA) curve are constructed using core protein-coding genes involved in the process

  Subsequently, the results of regression analysis were illustrated through a column chart depicting the expression levels of key genes in vitiligo. The analysis revealed that the distribution of key gene expressions in vitiligo contributed to varying degrees across all our samples throughout the scoring process (Fig. 3b). In addition, we further investigated the predictive performance of three protein-coding genes in vitiligo data by assessing ROC and DCA curves for diagnostic validation. A higher AUC value signifies better predictive effectiveness. Results from both ROC and DCA curves demonstrate that the three core protein-coding genes can accurately predict the onset and progression of the disease (Fig. 3c, d).


### Influence of Protein-Coding Genes on Cell Functions and Potential Mechanisms
  In order to further explore the potential mechanism of candidate core proteins in vitiligo, we further explored how these identified core protein-coding genes regulate the occurrence and development of vitiligo. The results of ssGSEA showed that the pathways enriched by KLF4 include the IL-17 signaling pathway, the oxytocin signaling pathway, and the ribosome pathway (Fig. 4a). The pathways enriched by TNFRSF13C were the estrogen signaling pathway, the thermogenesis, and the oxidative phosphorylation pathway (Fig. 4b). The pathways enriched by TNFSF10 include the oxytocin signaling pathway, the estrogen signaling pathway, and the IL-17 signaling pathway (Fig. 4c). There suggested that candidate core proteins may affect the occurrence and development of vitiligo by regulating the immune inflammatory response.Fig. 4Biological functional enrichment of protein-coding genes. a–c Network diagram shows the gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)-based enrichment analysis results of KLF4, TNFRSF13C, and TNFSF10, respectively. d, e Bar chart shows the enrichment analysis results of each gene based on gene set variation analysis (GSVA)

  Further analyses were carried out using GSVA enrichment on the genes for the core proteins to comprehend their effects on biological functions. These genes were sorted in descending order based on log2foldchange and showed that KLF4 could activate functions primarily related to cell cycle, apoptosis, oxidative stress, inflammatory response, glycolysis, fatty acid metabolism, etc. (Fig. 4d). Additionally, TNFRSF13C can stimulate signaling pathways and biological functions, including the cell cycle, apoptosis, inflammatory response, estrogen metabolism, and fatty acid metabolism, to name a few (Fig. 4e). The biological function of TNFSF10 is similar to the enrichment profile reported for the other two key molecules (Fig. 4f).


### Immune Infiltration Analysis Based on Core Protein-Coding Genes
  On the basis of the regulatory role of candidate core proteins in inflammation and immune response, we first evaluated the changes in vitiligo immune infiltration in the selected dataset (PCA and volcano maps for sample quality control shown in Fig. S3A-B), and the results showed that dendritic cells, M0 macrophages, M1 macrophages, M2 macrophages, resting mast cells, resting NK cells, activated memory CD4 T cells, gamma delta T cells, and regulatory T cells (Tregs) showed significant differences. Specifically, M1 macrophages, CD4 memory T cells, and gamma delta T cells were significantly upregulated in vitiligo (Fig. S3C). Subsequently, we further explored the relationship between core molecules and immune cells in the high-low expression group based on the ssGSEA algorithm. We found that KLF4 was significantly positively correlated with resting CD4 memory T cells, neutrophils, and follicular helper T cells (Fig. 5a). Further, TNFRSF13C was significantly positively correlated with regulatory T cells (Tregs) and has a significant negative correlation with activated memory CD4 T cells (Fig. 5b); TNFSF10 had a significant positive correlation with resting memory CD4 T cells, neutrophils, and follicular helper T cells (Fig. 5c). We hypothesize that these three core molecules may be involved in the regulation of vitiligo occurrence and development by influencing immune infiltration.Fig. 5Analysis of the correlation between core proteins and immune infiltration and inflammatory factors. a–c Correlation between core encoded protein genes and the distribution of immune cells. d Bubble chart showing the correlation between each core encoded gene and inflammatory factors

  Meanwhile, the analysis result based on TISIDB database showed that KLF4 was negatively correlated with various immune inflammatory factors, including chemokines, their receptors, and tumor necrosis family (TNF) family proteins. In contrast, TNFSF10 showed positive correlations with a range of the immune factors analyzed. Interestingly, the correlation between TNFRSF13C and various immune inflammatory factors was not obvious (Fig. 5d). These analyses suggest that core molecules, especially KLF4 and TNFSF10, are closely associated with the levels of immune cell infiltration, and play a key role in the immune microenvironment.


### Transcriptional Regulatory Network of Core Protein-Coding Genes and Drug Target Prediction
  Aiming to uncover the potential mechanisms that regulate the candidate core genes, this study initially utilized the miRcode database for reverse prediction on three pivotal protein-coding genes, identifying 53 miRNAs and a total of 71 mRNA–miRNA relationship pairs, which were then visualized through Cytoscape (Fig. 6a). Subsequently, we used the RcisTarget function to scan the regulatory transcription factors to further explore the potential mechanisms that regulate these protein-coding genes. The Motif-TF annotation and selection analysis of key genes showed that the motif with the highest standardized enrichment score (NES: 4.03) was cisbp_M2174. The motif included multiple transcription factors, such as BORCS8-MEF2B, MEF2A, MEF2B, MEF2C, MEF2D, other motifs, and corresponding transcription factors of the core molecules (KLF4, TNFRSF13C, and TNFSF10).   The DGIdb database was utilized in this study to analyze drugs that may interact with the core molecules. The results revealed that APTO-253 and hydroxyurea could potentially interact with KLF4, and TNFRSF13C might interact with ianalumab and BAFF. ### Expression Profiling of Core Protein-Coding Genes in Vitiligo Skin Using Single-Cell Transcriptomic Data
  To evaluate the expression patterns of three core protein molecules in vitiligo pathogenesis, we analyzed the single-cell RNA-seq dataset GSE203262 from vitiligo skin samples. ## Discussion
  This is the first study to investigate the causal association of more than 4800 circulating proteins with vitiligo using a two-sample MR study. The aims were to overcome the interference of reverse causality and confounding factors present in traditional epidemiological studies and provide a higher level of evidence for studying the causal relationship between circulating proteins and vitiligo. Firstly, the study identified nine protein markers with a potential causal association with vitiligo based on a vitiligo training and validation GWAS sets. Of these, three proteins (Krüppel-like factor 4 or KLF4, TNFRSF13C, and TNFSF10) had a strong support of colocalization and reverse MR analysis. Further, KLF4 and TNFSF10 were particularly likely to be involved in the occurrence and development of vitiligo. Function prediction showed that these protein-coding genes were enriched in many pathways, mainly involving the cell cycle, apoptosis, inflammatory response, estrogen metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, and response to oxidative stress. In addition, KLF4 and TNFSF10 may be involved in regulating vitiligo occurrence and development by influencing the distribution of immune cell infiltration and inflammatory factors. The druggability assessment and molecular docking further suggested that the three protein biomarkers may have the potential to be therapeutic targets for vitiligo. The results of model prediction suggest that KLF4, TNFRSF13C, and TNFSF10 can be used as important indicators of the occurrence and development of vitiligo. Finally, single-cell data analysis of vitiligo suggests that KLF4, TNFRSF13C, and TNFSF10 may serve as important potential therapeutic targets.

  We found that circulating proteins KLF4 and TNFRSF13C were potential low-risk factors for vitiligo using MR and colocalization analysis, while TNFSF10 was a potential high-risk factor. Previous studies had confirmed that KLF4 was highly expressed in the epidermis of the skin. Its function in the skin was first reported in mice with whole-body homozygous Klf4 deletion, which die shortly after birth and suffer from loss of skin barrier function [17]. Additional research found that KLF4 was involved in the fibroblast and melanocyte reprogramming to form induced pluripotent stem cells [18, 19]. In addition, KLF4 promotes wound healing by driving the generation of fibrocytes from myeloid-derived suppressor cells [20]. It was reported that the KLF4 gene level in dark sheep skin was significantly higher than that in light sheep skin using gene chip technology [21]. Recent studies have shown that KLF4 was involved in regulating the proliferation, apoptosis, and differentiation of sheep skin melanocytes, while promoting melanogenesis by upregulating the expression of TYR and MITF [22]. These results suggest that KLF4 is associated with pigment formation, but its specific function remains unknown. In particular, the link between melanin metabolism in human skin melanocytes and the development of vitiligo has not yet been elucidated. In this study, we found that KLF4 can negatively regulate the occurrence and development of vitiligo based on colocalization and reverse validation analysis. In addition, the correlation analysis of genes related to vitiligo showed that KLF4 had a negative relationship with several vitiligo-related progression genes such as MITF, PTPN22, FOXD3, and CTLA4 to a certain extent. The most significant negative relationship was observed with FOXD3, which had been reported to inhibit melanogenesis [23]. Hence, we hypothesize that KLF4 may enhance the rate of melanogenesis by suppressing FOXD3 expression, thereby potentially inhibiting the onset and progression of vitiligo. This research avenue will be the focal point of our upcoming study.

  In addition, TNFRSF13C was another key protein we screened, which is associated with a low risk of vitiligo. Analyses showed that TNFRSF13C has varying degrees of positive relationships with several vital core genes that promote melanogenesis, such as TYR, MLANA, and DCT. This suggests that TNFRSF13C promotes melanogenesis, and alleviates vitiligo occurrence and development by mediating or influencing the upregulation of these core genes involved in melanogenesis. Previous studies have reported that TNFRSF13C was a specific receptor for B cell activating factor belonging to the TNF family (BAFF), with a crucial role in regulating B lymphocyte function [24]. Other studies have confirmed that inhibition as well as the overactivation of the BAFF-TNFRSF13C signaling pathway can lead to an immune imbalance in the body, triggering various diseases such as tumors, autoimmune diseases, and transplant rejection reactions [25–27]. However, TNFRSF13C was not expressed in normal human skin, inflammatory skin tissues such as psoriasis, and keratinocytes [28]. Concurrently, our study demonstrates no significant association of TNFRSF13C with inflammatory immune molecules. These results suggested that TNFRSF13C may play a function in negatively regulating the onset of vitiligo through non-classical receptor pathways. Therefore, TNFRSF13C expression in melanocytes and its role in regulating melanogenesis and its specific mechanism remains to be explored further.

  Among the three core proteins that we screened in relation to vitiligo, TNFSF10 is the only protein positively correlated with a high risk of vitiligo [29]. TNFSF10 is a cytokine of the TNF ligand superfamily, produced primarily by macrophages in response to stimuli that induce apoptosis [30]. Research has shown that TNFSF10-induced cell apoptosis mainly occurs by binding with the TNF death domain receptors, thereby triggering and transmitting death signals, activating the caspase protease reaction, and causing apoptosis [31]. Another study found that TNFSF10 is a melanoma metastasis-related gene, which may lay the foundation for developing novel and molecular targeted drugs [32]. A recent study confirmed that TNFSF10, as an autophagy gene, can promote skin melenoma occurrence and development by regulating the distribution of immune infiltration and its immune-related genes, such as CXCL1 and CXCL9 [33]. These reports suggest a key role of TNFSF10 in regulating immune cell infiltration, inflammatory response, and apoptosis. In this study, we found a significant positive correlation of TNFSF10 with several core immune chemokines associated with vitiligo, such as CXCL9 and CXCL10 [34, 35]. Additionally, GSEA and ssGSEA analysis of TNFSF10 also demonstrated the enrichment of multiple inflammatory and immune signaling pathways. Hence, we hypothesize that TNFSF10 may promote vitiligo occurrence by regulating the expression of immune infiltration and related immune inflammatory factors. In addition, Pearson correlation analysis showed that TNFSF10 had a negative relationship with the expression of genes, such as TYR, POMC, MELANA, and DCT. On the basis of these results, we preliminarily confirmed that TNFSF10 may affect the expression of the core molecule involved in melanogenesis in melanocytes by regulating the immune inflammatory response to induce vitiligo.

  This study’s strength lies in systematically examining the correlations between plasma protein biomarkers and vitiligo risk through a two-stage proteome-wide MR approach involving a substantial number of plasma proteins and cases of vitiligo. Moreover, the incorporation of vitiligo RNA transcriptome data, enrichment analysis, and transcription factor prediction of core molecules provided insights into the potential pathogenic impact of the identified proteins on vitiligo. In addition, druggability assessment and molecular docking identified a few drugs targeting these molecules in this study. Particularly, APTO-253 exhibited a better binding ability with KLF4, which will be our focus in subsequent research. However, several limitations of this study should also be considered. Firstly, the GWAS dataset used in this study is mainly from Europeans, and there is a lack of data from other ethnic groups, which may lead to bias in the estimates and affect the generalizability of the study results. However, combined with the results of previous studies, the candidate proteins we screened still showed a close correlation with vitiligo. Secondly, the conclusions rely heavily on statistical analysis. Although the statistical power of the validation set was generally low (all at 2.5%), this reflects the objective limitations of the current sample size of vitiligo GWAS. While preliminary single-cell data in vitiligo suggest potential associations of KLF4, TNFRSF13C, and TNFSF10 with disease pathogenesis, further studies are required to comprehensively investigate the roles of circulating inflammatory mediators in disease initiation and progression. Subsequent validation using clinical cohorts will be essential to confirm these findings. In addition, conducting further clinical trials is essential to assess the therapeutic potential of these proteins as targets for treating vitiligo.


## Conclusion
  Our study based on proteome-wide MR and colocalization analysis identified several plasma proteins highly causally associated with vitiligo risk and provided new insights into the etiology of vitiligo. Further, promising targets for developing screening biomarkers and therapeutic drugs for vitiligo were also identified. Additional experimental and clinical investigations are essential to assess the effectiveness and utility of these potential candidates to corroborate the present discoveries.
