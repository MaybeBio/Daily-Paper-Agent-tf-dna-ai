# The impact of dietary restriction on transcriptional profiles of hematopoietic stem cells in aged female mice


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10522-025-10263-6.


## Introduction
  Aging is a complex biological process characterized by the progressive decline in cellular and tissue function, which leads to increased vulnerability to age-related diseases. Recent advances in aging research have highlighted key molecular hallmarks, including genomic instability, epigenetic alterations, mitochondrial dysfunction, and chronic low-grade inflammation, which collectively drive functional deterioration across tissues (Flori et al. 2024; Mundo Rivera et al. 2024). These mechanisms not only exacerbate age-related pathologies but also present actionable targets for therapeutic interventions aimed at mitigating aging-associated decline. Emerging strategies such as mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and MSC-derived extracellular vesicles have shown promise in promoting longevity and treating age-related diseases (Rudnitsky et al. 2025). One hallmark of aging is the progressive deterioration of the hematopoietic system, which compromises immune function and predisposes individuals to hematologic malignancies and other disorders. Hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) serve as the foundation of the hematopoietic system, responsible for the continuous generation of blood cells throughout life. However, aging induces alterations in HSC function and number, contributing to hematopoietic decline and associated pathologies (Sudo et al. 2000; Rossi et al. 2008). Unlike other experimental mouse strains that demonstrate a reduced size of the HSC pool with age, C57BL/6J mice exhibit hematopoietic aging phenotypes similar to those observed in humans (Ertl et al. 2008; Chen et al. 2003), characterized by diminished regenerative capacity and biased lineage differentiation (Tang et al. 2016; Tao et al. 2020). Specifically, the number of phenotypically defined HSCs in the bone marrow increases two- to tenfold with age in both human and mice. However, this increase does not compensate for the functional decline, leading to an overall reduction in the regenerative capacity of aged HSCs. Another central hallmark of aged HSCs is their skewing towards myeloid cell differentiation, providing more myeloid progenitor cells and fewer lymphoid progenitor cells compared to young HSCs (Geiger et al. 2013; Dykstra et al. 2011). Notably, the decline in the regenerative potential of aging HSCs is not limited to C57BL/6J mice; in BALB/cByJ (BALB), CBA/CaJ (CBA), and DBA/2J (D2) mouse strains, the regenerative capacity of HSCs also significantly declines with increasing age (Ertl et al. 2008; Chen et al. 2003). Dietary restriction (DR), defined as a 20–40% reduction in daily food intake without malnutrition (McCay et al. 1935), has emerged as a promising intervention for extending lifespan and improving healthspan across various species. Studies have shown that DR enhances the regeneration of muscle stem cells, augments the self-renewal capacity of intestinal stem cells (ISCs) and exerts an anti-proliferative effect on adipose-derived stem cells (Cerletti et al. 2012; Yilmaz et al. 2012; Schmuck et al. 2011). We also demonstrated that DR preserved the viability and the regeneration capacity of ISCs exposed to high-dose methotrexate injury (Tang et al. 2020). Previously, we demonstrated that a 4- and 9- month 30% DR regimen in 15–18-month-old C57BL/6J mice significantly enhanced the maintenance of the repopulating capacity of aging HSCs. However, DR has been shown to strongly inhibit B lymphopoiesis under undisturbed conditions. Transplantation of HSCs from late-onset DR-treated mice into young recipients improves the repopulating capacity of old HSCs in serial competitive transplantation experiments, indicating that intrinsic changes in HSCs induced by DR play a crucial role in HSC aging (Tao et al. 2020). The beneficial effects of DR are believed to be mediated through various mechanisms, including nutrient-sensing pathways, metabolic reprogramming in response to DR, and effector mechanisms that mediate the downstream effects of DR to improve homeostasis (Wilson et al. 2021). Despite these advantages, the impact of DR on the transcriptomic alterations of HSCs in aging organisms remains poorly understood.

  Recent advances in high-throughput sequencing technologies, particularly RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), have revolutionized our ability to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying complex biological processes. RNA-seq facilitates a comprehensive analysis of gene expression profiles, thereby enabling the identification of key regulatory pathways and signaling networks implicated in cellular function and aging.

  In this study, HSCs were isolated from aged mice under DR and ad libitum (AL) feeding conditions. Subsequently, RNA-seq analysis was conducted to generate comprehensive transcriptomic data. Differentially expressed genes and enrichment analysis were employed to identify relevant pathways and biological processes, thereby elucidating the transcriptional changes associated with DR and potential key regulatory factors involved in maintaining HSC function and delaying the aging process. This research enhances our understanding of the molecular mechanisms through which energy intake influences hematopoietic aging and identifies potential targets for therapeutic strategies aimed at protecting hematopoietic function and promoting healthy aging in elderly populations.


## Results

### DR modulates gene expression in aged HSCs
  Hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) were sorted from 3-month-old (YAL), 23-month-old (OAL-L), and 23-month-old mice subjected to DR starting at 17 months of age (ODR-L) for RNA-seq. Basal physiology was evaluated by measuring body weight, abdominal fat, and blood composition parameters. DR resulted in a gradual reduction in body weight within the first two months of dietary intervention, followed by a slow increase, stabilizing by in the fourth month until the end of the experiment (Fig. S1C). Additionally, a six-month DR period led to a significant decreased in abdominal fat (Fig. S1D). Compared to YAL, OAL-L showed a significant reduction in the absolute counts of white blood cells (WBCs), red blood cells (RBCs), hemoglobin levels (HGB), and lymphocytes, accompanied by a concomitant increase in platelet counts, which could not be rescued by DR (Fig. S1E). We performed weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) across the three groups, which systematically analyzed the overall gene expression patterns, reflected the interaction patterns among genes in the samples, and identified key genes and their functions (Fig. 1A). Principal component analysis (PCA) based on differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (Fig. 1B) and the heatmap of expression levels (Fig. 1D) revealed significant differences among the YAL, OAL-L, and ODR-L groups. Notably, during the aging process of HSCs, there is a predominance of upregulated gene expression, while DR induces a more suppressive gene expression profile. Specifically, we identified 144 upregulated DEGs and 1,315 downregulated DEGs in ODR-L compared to OAL-L; 166 upregulated DEGs and 692 downregulated DEGs in ODR-L compared to YAL; and 477 upregulated DEGs and 54 downregulated DEGs in OAL-L compared to YAL (Fig. 1C). To validate the RNA-Seq results, DEGs in ODR-L compared to OAL-L were randomly selected for quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis. As expected, the expression levels of these genes determined by qRT-PCR were consistent with the RNA-Seq results (Fig. S1A). Furthermore, the correlation coefficient of Pearson correlation analysis (R2 = 0.8069, p = 0.001) confirmed the reproducibility and reliability of the RNA-Seq data (Fig. S1B). We also performed gene function enrichment analysis on the three groups, with the top 10 pathways shown in Fig. 1E, highlighting that DEGs in ODR-L compared to OAL-L distinctly differentiated from those in the other two groups (Fig. 1E). The pathways enriched for DEGs, both upregulated and downregulated, among the three groups are shown in Fig. S2. Compared to YAL, the gene expression of several biological processes associated with ion transport, bone development, regulation of NF-κB transcription factor activity, calcium ion transport, cell migration, and the inflammatory response is significantly upregulated in OAL-L. In contrast, when compared to OAL-L, the gene expression of several biological processes related to interstrand cross-link repair, the apoptotic process, viral entry into host cells, protein transport, and immune system processes is significantly downregulated in ODR-L. However, in ODR-L, transcription regulation, DNA damage response, protein transport, cell cycle, and RNA polymerase II-mediated transcription regulation are significantly upregulated. When comparing ODR-L to YAL, the gene expression of several biological processes related to cell population proliferation, cell adhesion, immune response, transcription regulation, and the inflammatory response is significantly upregulated in ODR-L. Conversely, several key biological processes including tRNA processing, viral entry into host cells, response to ionizing radiation, DNA repair, intracellular protein transport, and apoptotic processes, are significantly downregulated (Fig. S2). Overall, these results indicate that DR induces a distinct transcriptional landscape in aged HSCs compared to the age-matched AL group, characterized by a predominance of downregulated genes. This finding suggests that DR may exert its protective effects by diminishing the expression of genes involved in detrimental processes such as inflammation, apoptosis, and stress response, which are typically upregulated with aging.Fig. 1Analysis of differentially expressed genes in HSCs between young AL (YAL), old AL (OAL-L) and old DR (ODR-L) mice. ### WGCNA analysis reveals key modules and pathways associated with HSC Aging and DR-induced protection
  We performed WGCNA to identify expression modules for all genes in the YAL, OAL-L, and ODR-L groups. Figure 2A shows the hierarchical clustering dendrogram of all genes, utilizing the cutreeDynamic function for dynamic tree cutting to segment the hierarchical clustering tree into distinct modules. Ultimately, genes were successfully divided into 14 modules, each represented by a distinct color, with the gray module indicating a collection of genes not assigned to any specific module. Subsequently, we performed hierarchical clustering analysis on all gene modules and generated a correlation heatmap (Fig. 2B). The ODR-L group clustered with the salmon, tan, and purple modules, while the OAL-L group was associated with the blue, green, green-yellow, red, brown, yellow, and black modules. In contrast, the YAL group clustered with the turquoise, magenta, and pink modules (Fig. 2B). The degree of association between each module and the three groups is shown in Fig. 2C. Notably, the black module (correlation coefficient = 0.72), brown module (correlation coefficient = 0.67), yellow module (correlation coefficient = 0.70), green-yellow module (correlation coefficient = 0.66), and red module (correlation coefficient = 0.57) are strongly positively correlated with OAL-L, while the YAL and ODR-L groups show similar patterns (Fig. 2C), indicating that these genes may play a role in the aging of HSCs. Consequently, the black, brown, yellow, green-yellow, and red modules have been selected for further functional analysis (Fig. 2D). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis shows that the DEGs in these five modules are significantly enriched in the Top 10 biological processes, including cilium assembly, cellular response to DNA damage stimulus, protein phosphopantetheinylation, protein O-linked glycosylation, activation of GTPase activity, double-strand break repair via homologous recombination, multicellular organism development, DNA recombination, intracellular signal transduction, and cell projection organization. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis shows that the DEGs in these five modules are significantly enriched in the top 10 pathways which include Herpes simplex virus 1 infection, Glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchor biosynthesis, Circadian rhythm, Axon guidance, Glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis-chondroitin sulfate/dermatan sulfate, Homologous recombination, Adherens junction, Fatty acid degradation, Primary immunodeficiency, and MAPK signaling pathway (Fig. 2D). Biological processes, including cellular response to DNA damage stimulus, activation of GTPase activity, and double-strand break repair via homologous recombination, are significantly implicated in the aging of HSC (Beerman et al. 2014; Kumar Pazhanisamy et al. 2009). Transcriptional enrichment analysis has revealed an age-associated upregulation of pathways related to DNA damage response, suggesting a potential increase in the DNA damage burden in aged HSCs. Defects in homologous recombination and dysregulation of the MAPK signaling pathway are closely associated with HSC aging. Research indicates that aged HSCs exhibit increased DNA damage and reduced expression of genes involved in DNA repair, particularly those related to homologous recombination (Zhang et al. 2020). In aged HSCs, there is often increased activation of the MAPK pathway, particularly the p38 MAPK, which is linked to the loss of HSC quiescence and increased apoptosis. This elevated activity contributes to the decline in HSC function and regenerative capacity observed with aging (Richardson et al. 2015).Furthermore, most of the DEGs in these five modules are highly expressed in the OAL-L group, while they are expressed at lower levels in the ODR-L and YAL groups (Fig. 2E). The gene expression levels in the GO and KEGG enrichment processes closely related to HSC aging within these five modules are shown in Fig. S3. ### DR attenuates aging-associated immune dysregulation and stress responses in HSCs by modulating key gene expression modules
  The analysis of the pink, turquoise, magenta, blue, salmon, purple, and tan modules in the WGCNA reveals significant findings. The pink module (correlation coefficient = −0.52), turquoise module (correlation coefficient = −0.55), magenta module (correlation coefficient = −0.47), and blue module (correlation coefficient = −0.57) exhibit strong negative correlations with ODR-L (Fig. 2C). GO enrichment analysis shows that the DEGs within these four modules are significantly enriched in the top 10 biological processes, which include immune system processes, viral entry into host cells, cell projection organization, innate immune responses, protein transport, response to unfolded proteins, cellular responses to lipopolysaccharide, chromosome segregation, apoptotic processes and negative regulation of transcription (DNA-templated) (Fig. 3A). DR influences various biological processes and pathways, particularly those related to the immune system, protein quality control, and cellular responses to stress. Aging in individuals leads to a concurrent enhancement of multiple pathways that positively regulate the inflammatory response, innate immune response and cell death, and this enhancement mitigated by DR (Ma et al. 2020). Concurrently, HSCs experience functional decline with aging, partially attributable to chronic inflammation and altered immune signaling. for instance, aged HSCs demonstrate increased NF-κB activity and JAK/STAT signaling (Li et al. 2020). Additionally, HSC quiescence is mediated by the mitochondrial unfolded protein response (UPRmt) which is heightened in response to elevated mitochondrial protein folding stress (PFSmt) during aging (Mohrin et al. 2015, 2018). The expression of a UPRmt regulator, Sirt7, is diminished in aged HSCs, and its inactivation compromise the regenerative capacity of HSCs. Conversely, upregulation of Sirt7 improves the regenerative capacity of aged HSCs (Mohrin et al. 2015). Our previous study has shown that DR can improve the repopulation abilities of aging HSCs, while impairing their lymphoid differentiation capacity, Additionally, DR significantly rejuvenates the expression of UPRmt genes and reduces aging-associated caspase 1-induced pyroptosis. In mice that started DR at a young age, DR inhibited lymphocyte production. Upon returning to AL feeding, these lymphocytes exhibited rapid proliferation driven by homeostasis and peripheral factors (Tang et al. 2016; Contreras et al. 2018). This reversible lymphocyte suppression may serve as a protective mechanism for maintaining the functionality of aging HSCs. Moreover, the majority of DEGs in the four modules in the ODR-L group are significantly lower than those in the OAL-L and YAL groups (Fig. 3B). The gene expression levels in GO and KEGG enrichment processes, closely associated with immune system processes within these four modules are shown in Fig. S3. This result illustrates the multifaceted impact of DR on HSCs during aging, particularly emphasizing its effects on immune function, protein quality control, and cellular stress responses. ### DR positively correlates with transcriptional regulation in aged HSCs
  The salmon module (correlation coefficient = 0.57), purple module (correlation coefficient = 0.77), and tan module (correlation coefficient = 0.61) demonstrate strong positive correlations with ODR-L (Fig. 2C). GO enrichment analysis indicates that the DEGs in these three modules are significantly enriched in the Top 10 biological processes, which include negative regulation of transcription RNA polymerase II, chromatin organization, regulation of transcription RNA polymerase II, inflammatory response, positive regulation of cytosolic calcium ion concentration, cellular response to DNA damage stimulus, protein transport, RNA splicing, cell cycle and mRNA processing (Fig. 3C). Moreover, these DEGs are predominantly highly expressed in the ODR-L group (Fig. 3D). The gene expression levels associated with the GO and KEGG enrichment processes that are closely involved in the regulation of transcription by RNA polymerase II within these five modules are shown in Fig. S4. Aged HSCs exhibit increased transcriptional activity of RNA polymerase II (Flohr Svendsen et al. 2021). However, DR was positively correlated with the gene expression related to the negative regulation of RNA polymerase II transcriptional. These findings emphasize the critical role of transcription regulation and signal transduction pathways in the ODR-L group, thereby highlighting potential targets for further research into the molecular mechanisms underlying these processes.


### Transcription factor-mediated gene regulation and its impact on HSC aging and function under DR
  The DEGs in the purple, tan, and salmon modules are primarily enriched in the regulation of RNA polymerase II transcription, with these DEGs being predominantly highly expressed in the ODR-L group. Transcription factors (TFs) play a crucial role in gene regulation by binding to specific DNA sequences and modulating the transcription of target genes. Consequently, it is hypothesized that DR leads to differential gene expression in HSCs through the regulation of transcription factor, thereby influencing the biological processes associated with aging HSCs. We identified the TFs within these three modules and predicted their target genes using relevant databases (Fig. 4A–D). The GO enrichment analysis of the DEGs targeted by these key TFs, including Gata2, Klf16, and Runx3 revealed significant biological processes, such as signal transduction, RNA polymerase II transcription regulation, phosphorylation, cellular response to DNA damage stimulus, protein transport, cell cycle, and chromatin organization (Fig. 4E). By examining the DEGs associated with these TFs, we investigated their roles in signal transduction, transcription regulation, and cellular response. Our findings highlight the critical pathways and processes modulated by Gata2 and Runx3, establishing a foundation for further exploration of the potential mechanisms through which these transcription factors may influence HSC aging and functional improvement under DR condition. ### Identification of hub genes via machine learning
  Elderly human hematopoietic progenitor cells exhibit an increased propensity for pyroptosis, an inflammatory form of programmed cell death (PCD). This susceptibility is linked to oxidative stress, telomerase activity deficiency, and telomere attrition, all of which limit their regenerative capacity and contribute to immune decline (Fali et al. 2018). Notably, our previous research reported that the aging-associated increase in caspase 1-induced pyroptosis was diminished under DR (Tao et al. 2020). In comparison to OAL, the functional enrichment of DEGs down-regulated by ODR-L included the apoptosis process (Fig. S2A). Consequently, we further investigated the expression changes of genes related to PCD in HSC during aging. PCD encompasses various forms, including apoptosis, necroptosis, ferroptosis, pyroptosis, netotic cell death, entotic cell death, lysosome-dependent cell death, parthanatos, autophagy-dependent cell death, cuproptosis, oxeiptosis, and alkaliptosis. We first cross-analyzed the DEGs from the black, brown, yellow, green-yellow, and red modules with 12 PCD-related genes, ultimately identifying 29 marker genes (Figs. 5A and S6). Subsequently, we performed functional enrichment analysis on these marker genes. The results of GO enrichment analysis indicated that these genes are primarily involved in the regulation of autophagy, RNA polymerase II transcription regulation, cellular responses to DNA damage stimulus, and apoptosis processes (Fig. 5B). In this study, we employed three distinct machine learning algorithms—LASSO, Random Forest (RF), and Support Vector Machine-Recursive Feature Elimination (SVM-RFE)—to identify key hub genes associated with PCD in HSC aging. The LASSO algorithm pinpointed 5 potential hub genes (Naglu, Prom2, Ap1s2, Plekhm1 and Gnptg) (Fig. 5C). The RF algorithm, which ranks genes based on their importance scores, yielded 6 top candidate genes (Gnptg, Plekhm1, Ubqln1, Zc3h12a, Il4 and Naglu) (Fig. 5D). Similarly, the SVM-RFE algorithm indicated optimum accuracy with a set of 3 genes (Gnptg, Ubqln1 and Il4), leading us to select these as candidates (Fig. 5E). The intersection of results from these algorithms identified Gnptg as the leading candidate gene associated with PCD in HSC aging (Fig. 5F). Compared to ODR-L and YAL mice, the expression of the Gnptg gene was significantly up-regulated in OAL-L mice (Fig. S6). In the ROC curve analysis, the sensitivity and specificity of this hub gene for assessing the significance of PCD in HSC aging were evaluated through the area under the curve (AUC) values, which exceeded 0.98 (Fig. 5G, H). This finding suggests that Gnptg demonstrates superior predictive capability for PCD-related markers in the aging process of HSCs, warranting further investigation as a potential biomarker. Additionally, we predicted that the TFs Klf16, Runx3, and Gata2 possess binding sites on the promoter of the Gnptg gene, as indicated by JASPAR (Fig. 5I). This finding suggests that Klf16, Runx3, and Gata2 may regulate the transcription of Gnptg. Furthermore, the TFs within the salmon, purple, and tan modules, which are positively correlated with ODR-L, along with Klf16, Runx3, Gata2 and 29 PCD-related genes in the black, brown, yellow, green-yellow, and red modules, exhibit a positive association with OAL-L. ### DR reduces lysosome number and protects lysosomal membrane integrity in aging HSCs
  Gnptg (N-acetylglucosamine-1-phosphotransferase, gamma subunit) is a crucial enzyme that ensures the proper intracellular localization of multiple lysosomal enzymes and is classified among the regulatory genes involved in lysosome-dependent cell death (Zou et al. 2022; Westermann et al. 2020). As the age, HSCs continuously accumulate DNA damage, including double-strand breaks (DSBs) (Fig. 2D). When the extent of DNA damage exceeds the repair capacity, cells may initiate a cell death program through lysosomal membrane permeabilization (LMP). LMP results in the leakage of lysosomal contents such as proteases, into the cytoplasm, thereby triggering cell death (Wang et al. 2018). Consequently, we further investigated how DR influences lysosomal quantity and membrane integrity in aging HSCs. Immunofluorescence images revealed a significant increase in the protein level of LAMP1, a lysosomal marker, in the OAL-L group, while the lysosomal hydrolase CTSB exhibited no significant difference (Fig. 6A). This indicates an increase in lysosome quantity in the OAL-L group without a corresponding change in degradation function, suggesting a potential for lysosomal dysfunction. To better examine the morphological changes in lysosomes, we employed transmission electron microscopy (TEM), which demonstrated that the ODR-L group had fewer lysosomes and maintained better membrane integrity compared to the OAL-L and YAL groups (Fig. 6B). Additionally, we also compared the FPKM of lysosomal-related genes, including Ctsc, Tmem175, Tpp1, Atp6v1a, Slc17a9, and CD68. The expression levels of these DEGs were significantly lower in the ODR-L group compared to the OAL-L and YAL groups (Fig. 6C). ## Discussion
  Since McCay’s research in 1935 demonstrated that DR could double the lifespan of experimental rats (McCay et al. 1935), DR has been recognized as a significant strategy for promoting healthy aging. Our previous studies have shown that DR improves and maintains the regenerative capacity of HSCs in both steady-state and post-transplant environments (Tang et al. 2016; Tao et al. 2020, 2024). Specifically, DR initiated in middle and old age has been shown to reverse the HSC aging, thereby improving their regenerative capacity and functional maintenance (Tao et al. 2020). Although numerous mechanisms have been proposed to explain the benefits of DR on the aging body, the effects of DR on the transcriptome of HSCs in aged organisms remain poorly understood. This knowledge gap could elucidate the observed improvement in the regenerative capacity and functional maintenance of aged HSCs observed with late-onset DR. Therefore, it is crucial to investigate the transcriptional landscape of aged HSCs under DR to explore the potential mechanisms mediating the improvement of HSC function by DR. The current study describes the effect of DR on the transcriptional profile of aging HSCs and provides insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying the DR-mediated improvement of HSC aging. Aging HSCs are characterized by upregulated gene expression, as observed in the OAL-L group. Specifically, a comparison between OAL-L and YAL revealed 477 upregulated genes and only 54 downregulated genes. The biological processes associated with these upregulated genes include inflammatory response, calcium ion transport, cell migration, regulation of NF-κB transcription factor activity, bone development and ion transport. These findings are consistent with previous studies, highlighting the roles of inflammation and cellular stress response in HSC aging (Geiger et al. 2013; Beerman et al. 2014; Li et al. 2020; Fujino et al. 2022). In contrast, DR induced a more suppressive gene expression profile in aging HSCs. When comparing the OAL-L group to the ODR-L group, 144 DEGs were upregulated, while a striking 1,315 DEGs were downregulated in the ODR-L group. DR appears to downregulate genes associated with immune system processes, protein transport, virus entry into host cells, apoptotic processes and interstrand cross-link repair. This suggests that DR may exert a protective effect by reducing the expression of genes implicated in detrimental processes such as inflammation, apoptosis, and stress responses, which are typically upregulated with aging.

  Of note, our experimental design effectively eliminates potential confounding effects of over-eating by employing appropriate dietary controls. The control cohort consisted of AL-fed mice maintained on a standard chow diet (24.02% protein, 12.95% fat, and 63.03% carbohydrate composition), not a high-calorie diet. The DR intervention was implemented through time-restricted feeding (11:00 a.m.–12:00 a.m. daily) using the identical standard diet formulation. Physiological monitoring revealed that body weight homeostasis was maintained in AL-fed mice throughout the experimental timeline (Fig. S1C), confirming the absence of excessive caloric consumption in control animals. Our experimental paradigm aligns with established models of metabolic research, where AL-fed rodents on standard diets serve as appropriate comparators for over-eating models, including both high-fat diet regimens (60% fat content) (Porras et al. 2017) and leptin-deficient ob/ob mice (Suriano et al. 2021). To further elucidate the distinct biological effects of DR versus over-eating, future investigations should incorporate comprehensive comparisons between DR interventions, AL feeding conditions, and specifically formulated high-calorie dietary models.

  The WGCNA identified several gene modules that are significantly correlated with various experimental groups. The black, brown, yellow, green-yellow, and red modules, which are strongly associated with the OAL-L group, are enriched in the DNA damage response and MAPK signaling pathways, consistent with previous studies. Research indicates that compared to young HSCs, aged HSCs accumulate more DNA damage, which regulates changes in HSC functions during aging, including self-renewal, differentiation, cell cycle arrest, and apoptosis (Geiger et al. 2013; Beerman et al. 2014; Walter et al. 2015; Niazi et al. 2025). Our WGCNA analysis identified transcriptional modules enriched for DNA damage response pathways in aged HSCs, corroborating our previous research that showed a significant accumulation of DNA damage in aged HSCs. However, in the HSCs of DR mice, DNA damage signals are significantly downregulated. This finding suggests that DR can partially alleviate DNA damage, reverse the functional decline of aged HSCs, and enhance their regenerative capacity (Tao et al. 2020). The MAPK pathway is a critical component of aging characteristics, particularly p38 MAPK, which is closely associated with HSC aging. Studies show that the activation of p38 MAPK is related to rapid HSC differentiation and the induction of cellular aging. Furthermore, the activation of p38 MAPK mediates radiation-induced hematopoietic suppression by promoting the aging of hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (Liu et al. 2007). The addition of p38 MAPK inhibitors to the culture can eliminate p38 MAPK activation, inhibit the onset of cellular aging, and promote HSC self-renewal (Wang et al. 2009). Our results further confirm the roles of DNA damage and the MAPK signaling pathway in HSC aging and functional decline. In contrast, the pink, turquoise, magenta, and blue modules, which are negatively correlated with ODR-L, highlight processes related to immune response, protein quality control, and cell stress responses. This negative correlation suggests that DR modulates these processes, potentially alleviating some detrimental effects of aging, such as chronic inflammation and impaired immune signaling (Ma et al. 2020). Our previous research found that under steady-state conditions, DR initiated in middle-aged mice significantly improves HSC regeneration while reversing the expression of genes associated with the mitochondrial unfolded protein response. However, it also exerts a marked inhibitory effect on B lymphocyte production in donors (Tao et al. 2020). The WGCNA analysis in this study demonstrated that the gene enrichment results in the pink, turquoise, magenta, and blue modules were consistent with the phenotypic results. Previous studies have shown that the number of pro-B, pre-B, and immature B cells in the bone marrow decreased following DR in mice, while the number of circulating mature B cells increased. This clearly suggests that dietary intervention halts the production of additional B cells while prompting the bone marrow to conserve energy to maintain metabolic balance, which may represent a protective mechanism induced by DR (Shushimita et al. 2014). Furthermore, DR has been proven to effectively reduce major inflammatory disorders associated with aging (Kökten et al. 2021; Zeng et al. 2019). In summary, DR initiated in middle age suppresses the immune response in aged mice. The functional consequences of these immune changes, as well as their involvement in the preservation of HSC function by DR, warrant further in-depth investigation. Additionally, our analysis identified key TFs such as Gata2, Klf16, and Runx3, which likely mediate the gene expression changes observed under DR. These TFs are implicated in crucial processes including signal transduction, transcription regulation, and cellular response to DNA damage. The differential expression of TF-targeted genes further supports the hypothesis that DR influences HSC aging through specific transcriptional regulatory networks. This provides a valuable foundation for future research to elucidate the precise mechanisms by which these TFs modulate HSC function under DR conditions. Furthermore, the integration of machine learning approaches such as LASSO, Random Forest, and SVM-RFE has identified Gnptg as a key hub gene associated with PCD in HSC aging. The upregulation of Gnptg in OAL-L mice compared to ODR-L and YAL mice, along with its high AUC value in ROC curve analysis, suggests its potential as a biomarker for PCD in HSC aging. Further validation of Gnptg and other identified candidate genes could facilitate the development of targeted interventions to mitigate HSC aging and enhance regenerative capacity. The TFs Klf16, Runx3, and Gata2 are predicted to regulate the expression of Gnptg, thereby linking the regulatory networks in the salmon, purple, and tan modules to those associated with HSC aging in the black, brown, yellow, green-yellow, and red modules. Gnptg (N-acetylglucosamine-1-phosphotransferase, gamma subunit), a key enzyme that ensures the proper intracellular localization of multiple lysosomal enzymes, is classified as one of the regulatory genes involved in lysosome-dependent cell death (Zou et al. 2022). Its gene products act synergistically in the M6P pathway to ensure effective targeting of lysosomal enzymes, thereby maintaining lysosomal homeostasis (Braulke et al. 2024). During normal aging, lysosomes undergo various alterations that reduce their degradative capacity and increase their susceptibility to instability (Gómez-Sintes et al. 2016). Research has demonstrated that lysosomal content and activity play a crucial role in maintenance of HSC quiescence (Arif et al. 2019). HSC quiescence is maintained by a significant number of large lysosomes, and inhibiting lysosomal activation in HSC results in further lysosomal expansion and a reduction in glucose uptake. This also induces an increase in the lysosomal segregation of mitochondria, enhancing the competitive repopulation ability of primed HSCs (Liang et al. 2020). Collectively, these findings suggest that inhibiting of lysosomal activity is essential for preserving HSC quiescence and potency.

  Lysosomes play a critical role in maintaining cellular homeostasis, particularly in the degradation of macromolecules and damaged cellular components. The accumulation of DNA damage, such as double-strand breaks (DSBs), is a hallmark of aging in HSCs. When the cellular repair capacity is overwhelmed, lysosomal membrane permeabilization (LMP) is often triggered, leading to the release of lysosomal enzymes into the cytoplasm and the initiation of cell death. This process has been implicated in various forms of cellular aging and dysfunction, especially in stem cells. Our findings suggest that DR may influence lysosomal quantity and membrane integrity in aging HSCs, potentially altering cellular responses to accumulated DNA damage. The improved membrane integrity observed in the ODR-L group suggests that DR may help maintain lysosomal stability, potentially mitigating the effects of aging and promoting the preservation of HSC function. Additionally, the expression levels of lysosomal-related genes were significantly lower in the ODR-L group, suggesting that DR may downregulate the expression of genes associated with lysosomal biogenesis and function. This downregulation could reflect a more efficient or selective process in lysosomal activity under dietary restriction, contributing to the observed improvement in lysosomal membrane integrity. Taken together, these findings highlight the intricate relationship between lysosomal function, DNA damage, and the aging process in HSCs. DR appears to exert a beneficial effect on lysosomal stability and membrane integrity, which may help mitigate the detrimental effects of DNA damage accumulation in aging stem cells. Further studies are needed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the regulation of lysosomal function in aging and how dietary interventions can modulate these pathways to promote stem cell health and longevity.

  While our study provides valuable insights into the transcriptional changes associated with aging and DR in HSCs, there are several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the study is based on RNA-seq data derived from sorted HSCs, which may not fully capture the complexity of the HSC niche or the influences exerted by other cell types. Additionally, the role of key transcription factors (TFs) such as Klf16, Runx3, and Gata2 in regulating genes like Gnptg were predicted based on bioinformatics analysis, however, experimental validation of TF binding sites and their regulatory effects on the promoter region of Gnptg is necessary. Further research employing chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) or similar techniques would strengthen our understanding of TF-mediated gene regulation in the context of aging and DR. Although we observed an increase in lysosomal number without a corresponding change in degradation function in the OAL-L group, leading to lysosomal dysfunction, the specific molecular mechanisms underlying this imbalance remain to be fully elucidated. Future studies could focus on identifying key proteins involved in the regulation of lysosomal biogenesis and degradation that may be altered by DR, and further explore how lysosomal dysfunction in aging HSCs contributes to stem cell senescence and functional decline. Investigating the role of autophagy in maintaining lysosomal function in response to DR would also be beneficial. Moreover, although we identified key genes and pathways associated with aging and DR, the causal relationships between gene expression changes and functional outcomes remain to be established. Further experiments, including gene knockdown or overexpression studies, are essential to validate the roles of these genes in HSC aging and DR.

  In summary, our results indicate that DR induces significant changes in gene expression in aged HSCs, contributing to a distinct regulatory landscape that may mitigate age-related dysfunctions. The identification of key gene modules, transcription factors, and hub genes provides valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying HSC aging and the beneficial effects of DR. Additionally, DR reduces the number of lysosome and protects lysosomal membrane integrity in aged HSCs. Our findings suggest that DR modulates gene expression and lysosomal integrity in aged HSCs, revealing critical molecular mechanisms for mitigating aging-associated dysfunctions.


## Materials and methods

### Mice and dietary restriction
  C57BL/6J female mice were obtained from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co. Ltd. (Hunan, China) and were maintained in the animal facilities of Nanchang Royo Biotech under pathogen-free conditions on a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle at 23–25 °C. DR was performed as previously described (Tang et al. 2016). Briefly, mice were housed individually and provided with a timed daily food supply from 11:00 a.m. to 12:00 a.m., amounting to 70% of the food consumed by age- and gender-matched ad libitum (AL) mice (mean baseline body weights for the three groups were 18 g for YAL, 28 g for OAL-L, and 28 g for ODR-L). For the AL group, mice were fed with an unlimited amount of food. Three-month-old mice were used as young control and DR was initiated from 17 months of age (the equivalent change in humans would roughly be 50–60 years (Dutta and Sengupta 2016).

  The study is reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. All mouse experiments were approved by the Animal Experimental Ethical Inspection of Nanchang Royo Biotech Co. Ltd (RYEI20170513-1), and all experimental protocols and methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

  Mice were anesthetized by inhaling isoflurane at the time of sacrifice, followed by cervical dislocation for euthanasia.


### HSC sorting
  The experiment commenced in the morning, when the DR mice had not been fed for the day. Bone marrow (BM) cells were obtained by crushing all hind limbs and pelvis with sterile PBS and filtered with 40 μm cell strainer. Afterwards, the BM cells were resuspended in red cell lysis buffer and incubated at room temperature for 5 min to lyse the red cells. Then BM cells were washed with PBS, counted by the cell counter and were incubated with APC-conjugated anti-c-Kit antibody, and c-Kit+ cells were enriched using anti-APC magnetic beads and LS columns (Miltenyi Biotec). The positively selected cells were then stained for HSC markers using a lineage cocktail (biotinylated anti-TER-119, -Gr-1, -B220, -CD11b, -CD3, -CD4, and -CD8 antibodies; BioLegend) and FITC-conjugated anti-CD34 (BD), PerCP-Cy5.5-conjugated anti-CD150 (BioLegend), APC-conjugated anti-c-Kit, and PE-conjugated anti-Sca-1 (BioLegend) antibodies and streptavidin-APC-Cy7. After staining, cells were sorted on a cell sorter (FACS Aria III; BD). Live cells are rigorously screened during cell sorting using a membrane-permeable viability dye (DAPI). FACS gating strategies were shown in Fig. S1F.


### RNA-seq
  HSCs (nearly 1000 cells) were collected into the sample collection tubes which contain cell lysis buffer and RNase Inhibitor. The samples were directly amplified by using the Smart-Seq2 method, resulting in amplification products of cDNA approximately 1–2 kb in length, then the cDNA was purified and recovered using Beckman Ampure XP beads. After the cDNA concentration of the amplified product was detected by Qubit® 3.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies, CA, USA), the distribution of the amplified product cDNA sample fragment was detected by Agilent 2100 High Sensitivity DNA Assay Kit (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA), waiting for library construction. Bioruptor® Sonication System (Diagenode Inc.) was used to ultrasonically break the c DNA of the sample into a small fragment of about 350 bp, which was subjected to end repair, A-tailing and adaptor ligation. Each step was purified with Beckman Ampure XP beads, and then the adaptor ligation product was subjected to PCR amplification. The obtained PCR amplification product was subjected to Pippin HT automatic high-throughput fragment recovery system. The library fragments containing 470 bp were recovered and the DNA molecules were purified by Beckman Ampure XP magnetic beads and dissolved in EB buffer, which was the final library. After the library construction is completed, preliminary quantification is performed using Qubit 3.0, followed by detection of the library’s insert size using Agilent 2100. Once the insert size meets expectations, the effective concentration of the library is accurately quantified using Q-PCR. Clusters are then generated on the cBot. Finally, paired-end sequencing is performed on the HiSeq sequencing platform (Illumina). Sample amplification, library construction and sequencing were performed at Linker Biotechnology Corporation, Kunming, China.


### Data processing
  The raw reads were trimmed of low-quality bases using a FASTX-Toolkit (v.0.0.13; http://hannonlab.cshl.edu/fastx_toolkit/) which was used to trim the data by removing low-quality bases (Remove the base with terminal mass less than 20; Remove 30% of the reads whose base mass is less than 20) in raw reads. Then the clean reads were evaluated using FastQC (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc).


### Reads alignment and differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis
  Clean reads were aligned to the mouse genome GRCm39 by HISAT2(v.2.2.1) (Kim et al. 2015). Uniquely mapped reads were ultimately used to calculate read number and reads per kilobase of exon per million fragments mapped (FPKM) for each gene. The expression levels of genes were evaluated using FPKM. When we do gene differential expression analysis, we choose the software DEseq2(v.1.30.1). DEseq2 will model the original reads and use the scale factor to explain the difference of Library depth. Then, DEseq2 estimates the gene dispersion, and reduces these estimates to generate more accurate dispersion estimates, so as to model the reads count. Finally, the model of negative binomial distribution is fitted by DEseq2, and the hypothesis is tested by Wald test or likelihood ratio test. DEseq2 can be used to analyze the differential expression between two or more samples, and the analysis results can be used to determine whether a gene is differentially expressed by fold change (FC) and false discovery rate (FDR). The criteria of significant difference expression were as follows: (1) FC ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5 (i.e., |log₂FC| ≥ 1) and (2) FDR < 0.05.


### Weighted gene co-expression network analysis
  To fully understand the gene expression pattern, we applied weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA,v.1.72.1) (Langfelder and Horvath 2008) to cluster genes that have similar expression pattern with default parameters. All differentially expressed genes were used as input data. Eigengenes for each clustering module was used as the representative expression pattern of genes in each module.


### Functional enrichment analysis
  To sort out functional categories of DEGs, Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways were identified using KOBAS 2.0 (Xie et al. 2011). Hypergeometric test and Benjamini–Hochberg FDR controlling procedure were used to define the enrichment of each term.


### Other statistical analysis
  Principal component analysis (PCA) analysis was performed by R package factoextra (https://cloud.r-project.org/package=factoextra) to show the clustering of samples with the first two components. After normalizing the reads by TPM (Tags Per Million) of each gene in samples, in house-script (sogen) was used for visualization of next-generation sequence data and genomic annotations. The pheatmap package (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/pheatmap/index.html) in R was used to perform the clustering based on Euclidean distance. Student’s t-test was used for comparisons between two groups.


### Machine learning
  We employed three machine learning algorithms, including the support vector machine (SVM), Random Forest (RF), and LASSO regression. The SVM analysis was carried out using the Scikit-Learn software library. We used the RF function in the “randomForest” package to identify important genes using ten-fold cross validation, the “randomForest” R package for RF. The LASSO regression model was performed with the package “glmnet” and the penalty parameter “lambda” was selected to choose the best model based on leave-one-out cross-validation, which is more suitable than tenfold cross-validation for a smaller number of samples.


### Transcription factor and target prediction
  A catalogue of 1623 transcription factors (TFs) of mouse was retrieved from AnimalTFDB 3.0 (Hu et al. 2019). Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was performed using GSVA package (version 1.46.0). We used the anova methods in the stat_compare_means function of the R package ggpubr (v0.6.0) to perform multiple pairwise tests.


### Quantitative real-time PCR analysis
  Total RNA was isolated from freshly sorted cells by using RNAsimple Total RNA Kit (TianGen Biotech). The first strand of the reversetranscribed cDNA was synthesized using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Scientific). qRT-PCR was performed with an ABI 7900 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) and TransStart Tip Green qPCR SuperMix (TransGen Biotech). The relative expression levels of the genes was normalized to β–actin in each sample and was calculated using 2-ΔΔCt. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1.


### Immunofluorescence
  Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature (RT), followed by permeabilization in 0.25% Triton/PBS for 10 min at RT and blocked with 3% BSA/PBS for 30 min. Then, slides were incubated with anti-LAMP1 and anti-CTSB primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C in a dark humidified chamber, After washing, fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies were added for 60 min. To visualize the nuclei the cells were counterstained by DAPI. Images were acquired on a fluorescent microscope (NIKON ECLIPSE C1).


### Transmission electron microscopy
  Cells were collected and fixed with electron microscope fixative for 4 h at 4 °C. The fixed cells were centrifuged. The 0.1 M PB (pH 7.4) was added into the tube after supernatant was discarded, and then the precipitation was re-suspended and washed in PB for 3 min. This washing step was repeated for 3 times. The 1% agarose solution was prepared by heating and dissolving in advance. After being cooled, the agarose solution was added into the EP tube. Before agarose solidification, the precipitation was suspended with forceps and wrapped in the agarose. Agarose blocks with samples avoid light post fixed with 1% OsO4 in 0.1 M PB (pH 7.4) for 2 h at room temperature. After remove OsO4, the tissues are rinsed in 0.1 M PB (pH 7.4) for 3 times, 15 min each. Tissues were sequentially dehydrated in gradient alcohol for 20 min each time, and 100% acetone twice for 15 min each time. after osmotic embedding and polymerization, the resin blocks were removed and sliced in ultrathin slicer at 60–80 nm, and slices were retrieved by 150 mesh square Chinese membrane copper mesh. 2% uranium acetate saturated alcohol solution avoid light staining for 8 min, rinsed in 70% ethanol for 3 times and then rinsed in ultrapure water for 3 times. 2.6% Lead citrate avoid CO2 staining for 8 min, and then rinsed with ultrapure water for 3 times. After dried by the filer paper, the cuprum grids were put into the grids board and dried overnight at room temperature. The cuprum grids are observed under transmission electron microscope (HITACHI, HT7700) and take images.
