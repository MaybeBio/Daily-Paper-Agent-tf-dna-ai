# Exploring Key Genes of Glutathione Metabolism in Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Based on Mendelian Randomisation, Single‐Cell RNA Sequencing and Multiple Machine Learning Approaches


## ABSTRACT
  Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a complex autoimmune disease characterised by immune dysregulation leading to inflammation and organ damage. Despite the rising global incidence of SLE, its aetiology remains unclear. We applied Mendelian randomisation (MR), multi‐omics integration, machine learning (ML), and SHAP to identify key metabolites and genes associated with SLE, revealing the crucial role of the glutathione pathway. MR analysis was performed on 1400 serum metabolites, revealing significant enrichment in the glutathione metabolic pathway. Single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) data classified monocytes into Metabolism_high and Metabolism_low groups based on glutathione metabolism scores. Differentially expressed genes were analysed using GSEA, metabolic pathway activity assessment, transcription factor prediction, cellular communication analysis, and Pseudotime analysis. LASSO regression identified hub genes and machine learning models (CatBoost, XGBoost, NGBoost) were developed. The SHAP method was used to interpret these models. Expression of key genes was validated across multiple datasets. MR analysis confirmed that metabolites were enriched in the glutathione pathway, identifying nine hub genes. Machine learning models achieved AUCs of 0.85, 0.80, and 0.83 in the validation set. SHAP analysis highlighted LAP3 as the top contributing gene across all models. scRNA‐seq data showed that LAP3 plays a significant role in the immune microenvironment of SLE. Validation across multiple datasets (training, validation, and GSE112087) revealed elevated LAP3 expression in PBMCs of SLE patients, with AUCs of 0.935, 0.795, and 0.817, respectively, suggesting strong diagnostic potential. Glutathione metabolism is closely associated with SLE development and LAP3 may play a key role in its progression. Both glutathione metabolism and LAP3 could serve as potential targets for SLE diagnosis and treatment.


## Introduction
  Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic autoimmune disease characterised by immune dysregulation, which leads to widespread inflammation and damage to various organs. The clinical manifestations of SLE are diverse, often affecting the skin, joints, kidneys, central nervous system, and cardiovascular system, making it a highly heterogeneous disease [1, 2]. Currently, SLE diagnosis primarily relies on serological tests combined with clinical manifestations. However, serological antibodies are also present in other autoimmune diseases and may be negative in the early stages of SLE, complicating early diagnosis and delaying treatment initiation [3, 4]. Thus, further development of novel SLE biomarkers for early detection is essential. The immunopathogenesis of SLE is well understood, but the underlying metabolic changes remain poorly understood. Recent studies emphasise the crucial role of metabolomics in elucidating disease mechanisms, identifying biomarkers, and providing insights into pathophysiological processes [5, 6]. Studies have shown that several metabolites and pathway‐related molecules are abnormally expressed in the serum of SLE patients, correlating with disease activity and clinical manifestations [7]. Zhang et al. analysed serum samples from 32 SLE patients and 25 gender‐matched healthy controls using liquid chromatography‐tandem mass spectrometry. They found differences in serum metabolic profiles, suggesting that elevated L‐pyroglutamate levels may serve as a promising diagnostic marker for SLE [8]. Another lipidomics study collected plasma samples from 18 SLE patients, 20 rheumatoid arthritis patients, and 20 healthy controls. It found that phospholipid, glycerol, and sphingolipid metabolism was disturbed in SLE patients, and that lipid metabolism biomarkers outperformed clinically used inflammation markers in diagnosing SLE [9]. Another study analysed the metabolic profiles of stool samples from SLE patients and healthy controls using ultra‐high‐performance liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry (UPLC‐MS). A diagnostic model was developed using logistic regression and ROC analysis, demonstrating strong diagnostic performance [10]. Despite the growing number of metabolomic profiling and biomarker studies in SLE, there remains a need to explore key genes involved in altered metabolic regulation and the causal relationship between metabolites and disease. This is essential for discovering reliable biomarkers and therapeutic targets for SLE.

  MR reduces confounding bias and the risk of reverse causation by using single nucleotide polymorphisms as instrumental variables for exposure factors, under the assumption that they affect outcomes solely through these factors, rather than through other confounding pathways [11, 12]. This method has been widely applied in various fields, including cardiovascular disease, psychiatric disorders, metabolic disorders, and cancer research [13, 14, 15]. In recent years, the extensive use of machine learning (ML) in the biomedical field has significantly advanced our understanding of the complex phenotypes of diseases. ML algorithms have become irreplaceable in various aspects of disease diagnosis, prognosis assessment, drug target prediction, and genomic data analysis [16, 17]. As data complexity in biomedical research grows, the application of machine learning in medicine is becoming increasingly critical. Single‐cell multi‐omics data can uncover intricate cellular states within disease microenvironments. The widespread adoption of machine learning and deep learning models has significantly enhanced the ability to analyse these complex single‐cell datasets, thereby offering valuable insights into their biological significance [18, 19]. However, the ‘black box’ nature of many models hinders their real‐world applicability, especially in clinical and metabolomics research. In contrast, the SHAP approach to interpreting machine learning predictions offers more insightful interpretations than traditional methods, excelling in metabolomics data categorisation and enhancing the understanding of biomedical problems [20]. Furthermore, SHAP has demonstrated significant benefits in feature selection across several machine learning models, particularly in diagnostic tasks involving datasets such as diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and thyroid disease [21]. The SHAP framework is utilised to interpret the prediction outcomes of various models and to quantify the contribution of individual features to these results [22]. It improves model interpretability and predictive accuracy, providing valuable insights for biomedical research.

  We first employed Mendelian randomisation analysis to identify blood metabolites causally linked to SLE. Our metabolite enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment in the glutathione pathway, suggesting that glutathione metabolism may play a critical role in the onset and progression of SLE. Next, we analysed peripheral blood monocyte data to correlate glutathione pathways with monocytes, classifying them into high and low metabolism groups based on glutathione metabolism scores. Differential genes were extracted from these monocytes, and key genes for SLE were identified using a combination of multi‐omics, machine learning (ML), and SHAP methods. In conclusion, we have identified key genes associated with SLE from a novel perspective.


## Materials and Methods

### Mendelian Randomisation

#### GWAS Data Sources
  The GWAS data utilised in this study were sourced from the GWAS Catalogue (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/) and IEU OpenGWAS (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/). Specifically, the GWAS data for SLE (ebi‐a‐GCST90018917) were obtained from recent research [23]. The sample predominantly consisted of participants from European populations, including 647 cases and 482,264 controls. A total of 24,198,877 SNPs were detected. The GWAS dataset for 1400 plasma metabolites was sourced from the GWAS Catalogue, with accession numbers GCST90199621 to GCST90201020 [24].


#### Instrumental Variable Selection Conditions
  The SNP screening criteria for the 1400 plasma metabolites included a p‐value threshold of p < 1 × 10−5, with a requirement that any two SNPs be located more than 10,000 kilobases apart. Additionally, the r 2 threshold for linkage disequilibrium between genes was set to less than 0.001. SNPs with inconsistent allele frequencies of the effect allele between the exposure and outcome datasets were removed. The inclusion criterion for SNPs was an F‐statistic greater than 10 [25]. Confounding SNPs were identified and excluded using the PhenoScanner tool.


#### Mendelian Randomisation Design
  MR analyses were performed using the ‘TwoSample MR’ R package in R version 4.3.1. The causal relationship between exposure and outcome was primarily estimated using the inverse variance weighting (IVW) method. The IVW method employs a fixed‐effects meta‐analytic model, assuming that all instrumental variables (SNPs) are valid and share a true effect size. It is considered the most powerful method for providing efficient causal estimation [26]. A p‐value < 0.05 for the IVW method was considered indicative of a significant relationship between exposure and outcome. Additionally, 4 MR analyses—MR‐Egger, weighted median, weighted mode, and simple mode—were employed to assess the robustness of the results.


#### Sensitivity Analysis
  Heterogeneity was evaluated using the p‐value from the Cochran Q‐test, with a p‐value < 0.05 indicating significant heterogeneity. The MR‐Egger intercept test was used to assess the presence of horizontal pleiotropy [27]. The MRPRESSO method was used to detect anomalous instrumental variables (IVs) [28]. The leave‐one‐out method identifies outliers and evaluates the stability of the results by sequentially removing each SNP.


#### Metabolite Annotation and Enrichment Analysis
  Metabolites with a p‐value < 0.05, filtered by the IVW method, were annotated using the ‘Compound ID Conversion’ function on MetaboAnalyst (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/). Next, metabolites with KEGG IDs were enriched and analysed in the KEGG database, using a background database consisting of 80 metabolite sets based on KEGG human pathways.


### Single‐Cell RNA Sequencing (scRNA‐Seq) Data Processing
  The SLE PBMC scRNA‐seq dataset was obtained from the GEO database (GSE135779). GSM4029940, GSM4029942, and GSM4029947‐GSM4029949 were designated as healthy controls, while GSM4029943‐GSM4029946 and GSM4029950‐GSM4029952 represented the adult SLE disease group. Seurat objects for total and individual cell types were generated using the Seurat package in R software, based on the scRNA‐seq gene expression matrix. The top 2000 genes were selected as the most significant variable features and the FindVariableFeatures function from the Seurat package was applied to normalise the scRNA‐seq data. The data were normalised using the NormalizeData function with a scale factor of 10,000. Dimensionality reduction was then performed through Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Harmony to correct for batch effects between samples. Harmony‐related run parameters are as follows: max_iter = 10, group. by.vars = “Sample”; FindNeighbors: dims = 1:10, reduction = “harmony”; FindClusters: resolution = 0.8; RunUMAP: dims = 1:10, reduction = “harmony”. The top 10 principal components were selected for cell clustering analysis, based on the ElbowPlot results. Cells were filtered to exclude those with less than 10% mitochondrial gene content and fewer than 200 or more than 4000 detected genes. After quality control, the remaining high‐quality samples were integrated. Further downscaling and visualisation using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) identified 21 distinct cell clusters. Cellular annotation was then performed based on marker gene expression. Monocytes were also scored using the AddModuleScore function, based on the glutathione pathway gene set, and categorised into Metabolism_high and Metabolism_low groups.


### Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA)
  GSEA was employed to assess the enrichment of predefined gene sets across different phenotypic conditions, to determine whether these sets exhibited significant positive or negative regulatory trends. The FindMarkers function was used to identify differential genes related to glutathione metabolism between the Metabolism_high and Metabolism_low groups in peripheral blood mononuclear cells from the SLE cohort. Additionally, differential genes between LAP3+Mono and LAP3‐Mono were identified using the same function. These differential genes were then subjected to Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). A significance threshold was set at an adjusted p‐value of less than 0.05.


### Prediction of Transcription Factor Activity
  The transcription factor activity differences between the Metabolism_high and Metabolism_low groups, as well as between the LAP3+Mono and LAP3‐Mono groups, were analysed using the Dorothea package [29]. The top 20 transcription factors with the largest changes were then selected for visualisation.


### Cellular Communication Analysis
  CellChat includes ligand‐receptor interaction databases for both human and mouse, which can be used to analyse intercellular communication networks in scRNA‐seq data annotated with different cell clusters. CellChat was used to assess differences in cellular communication between Metabolism_high and Metabolism_low monocytes in the SLE group, as well as other cellular subpopulations within the immune microenvironment. The same method was applied to analyse differences in cellular communication between LAP3+Mono and LAP3‐Mono.


### Screening Key Genes, Construction of Bulk Seq Training Set, Validation Set
  GSE72326 was selected as the training dataset, containing blood samples from 20 healthy individuals and 157 SLE patients. Probe names were converted to gene names based on the GPL10588 platform. GSE50772 was used as the test dataset, which includes blood samples from 20 healthy individuals and 61 SLE patients. Probe names were converted to gene names based on the GPL570 platform. Common genes were extracted and merged from the training and test datasets to form a unified dataset. Batch effects were then corrected using the SVA package. The corrected training and test datasets were used for further analysis and modelling. Differential genes between Metabolism_high and Metabolism_low were then screened based on the results of single‐cell differential analysis. The FDR threshold was set to < 0.05, and the log2FC threshold was set to > 0.25, identifying the differential genes related to glutathione metabolism in monocytes.


### Pseudotime Analysis
  To explore the relationship between cellular pseudotemporal trajectories and differential genes related to monocyte glutathione metabolism, scRNA‐seq data from all cell types in the SLE group were analysed using the Monocle2 R package. The screening criteria included mean_expression ≥ 0.1 and dispersion_empirical ≥ 1 × dispersion_fit. Dimensionality reduction was performed using the DDRTree method. The starting point of cell development was inferred using the VECTOR algorithm.


### Several Machine Learning Algorithms and SHAP Methods
  Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was applied to remove collinearity and further select hub genes. We selected CatBoost, XGBoost, and NGBoost as machine learning (ML) models for hub gene analysis. CatBoost, XGBoost, and NGBoost are widely used for classification and regression tasks in the biomedical field and across various research areas due to their advantages in flexibility, scalability, and usability [30, 31, 32]. The parameters of the CatBoost model are set as follows:learning_rate:0.1, l2_leaf_reg:5, subsample:0.6, best_model_min_trees:50, depth:4, min_data_in_leaf:500, one_hot_max_size:4, rsm:6, max_ctr_complexity:2, metric_period:1, early_stopping_rounds:20, border_count:254, bootstrap_type:Bernoulli. The parameters of the NGBoost model are set as follows: Dist: k_categorical, Score: LogScore, Base: default_tree_learner, n_estimators: 1000, learning_rate: 0.04, minibatch_frac: 1.0, col_sample: 1.0, tol: 0.0001, random_state:1. The XGBoost parameters are set as follows:eta: 0.3, num_boost_round:1000, gamma:0,max_depth: 6, min_child_weight:1,lambda: 1, alpha: 0, subsample: 1, colsample_bytree: 1, colsample_bylevel:1, scale_pos_weight:1, max_delta_step:0. In this study, the SHAP algorithm is used as a novel approach to interpret a variety of black‐box machine learning models. SHAP provides both local and global interpretability, as well as the ability to rank important features in the model. Compared to alternative methods, SHAP benefits from a strong theoretical foundation [33].


## Results

### MR Analysis
  The flow chart designed for this study is shown in Figure 1.

  The results of the MR analyses of 1400 metabolites are shown in Table 1. 14‐acetamidobutanoate, N‐acetylglutamate, gamma‐glutamylalanine, N‐acetylphenylalanine, and 1‐linoleoyl‐2‐arachidonoyl‐GPC (18:2/20:4n6) are identified as risk factors associated with an increased risk of SLE (Table 1). N‐acetylhistidine, threonine, androsterone glucuronide, 1‐methylxanthine, carnitine, 1‐methylhistidine, stachydrine, cysteinylglycine, xylose, 2‐aminobutyrate, and 3‐formylindole are identified as protective factors for reducing the risk of SLE (Table 1). The results above indicate that metabolites associated with an increased risk of SLE primarily include amino acids (e.g., 14‐acetylaminobutyric acid, N‐acetylglutamic acid, γ‐glutamylalanine) and lipid metabolites (e.g., 1‐linoleoyl‐2‐arachidonoyl‐GPC (18:2/20:4n6)). However, amino acid metabolites also constitute a significant portion of metabolites linked to a reduced risk of SLE. (e.g., N‐acetylhistidine, threonine, 1‐methylhistidine, cysteinylglycine, and 2‐aminobutyrate). Amino acids, as fundamental components of proteins, are extensively involved in regulating various biological processes, including cellular functions, signalling, and immune responses [34]. Several studies have demonstrated that amino acid metabolites are crucial in the pathogenesis of SLE, particularly in regulating immune cell activation, immune cell metabolism, and other biological processes [35, 36]. Additionally, lipids, as essential nutrients, play diverse physiological roles, and lipid metabolism is critical in regulating immune responses and disease progression in SLE [37, 38].

  Subsequently, scatter plot (Figure 2), forest plot (Figure S1) and leave‐one‐out plot (Figure S2) were generated for further revealing causal relationships between metabolites and SLE in MR analysis. The results of the other MR analysis methods are presented in Supporting Information. We then conducted sensitivity analyses on the aforementioned metabolites. The results (Table 2) indicated that both the MR Egger and IVW methods identified pleiotropy for 1‐methylxanthine and 3‐formylindole levels, while pleiotropy tests revealed heterogeneity in carnitine levels. The presence of pleiotropy and heterogeneity can violate the underlying assumptions of MR, potentially biasing the results. Therefore, metabolites exhibiting pleiotropy and heterogeneity were excluded from further analysis. We then conducted enrichment analyses on blood metabolites that passed the sensitivity test. The results (Figure S3) revealed significant enrichment of the glutathione metabolism pathway. Additionally, the glutathione metabolism pathway has been underexplored in SLE, and we will therefore focus on it in subsequent analyses.


### Analysis of Single‐Cell RNA Sequencing Data
  To further investigate the potential mechanisms through which the glutathione pathway mediates the development of SLE. We linked SLE with glutathione metabolism by performing single‐cell RNA sequencing of peripheral blood, aiming to investigate its role in the immune microenvironment of SLE. Using dimensionality reduction and clustering analysis, we classified the cells into 18 subpopulations (Figure 3A top left). UMAP plots showed the clustering of cells from different samples and groups, highlighting cellular heterogeneity between healthy controls (HCs) and SLE samples (Figure 3A top right and bottom left). These cells were then annotated into 9 subpopulations based on marker gene expression (Figure 3A bottom right). To validate and summarise the expression of key marker genes in the annotated cell types, bubble plots were generated (Figure 3B). The bubble size reflects the percentage of cells expressing the marker gene in each cell type, while the colour intensity indicates the average gene expression level. This analysis confirmed the specific expression patterns of marker genes in the corresponding cell clusters, further validating the accuracy of the cell type annotation and revealing the transcriptional profiles of each cell population. The heatmap shows the correlation between cell subpopulations from different groups (Figure 3C). The cell ratio graph (Figure 3D) showed a significant reduction in CD4+ T cells in the SLE group, while CD8+ T cells and monocytes were significantly elevated, suggesting that the reduced proportion of CD4+ T cells may impair immune regulation and the increased proportion of CD8+ T cells may indicate an enhanced cytotoxic immune response. Elevated monocyte levels indicate the activation of myeloid immune cells, which may differentiate into pro‐inflammatory macrophages that release large quantities of cytokines and chemokines, thereby exacerbating the inflammatory response [39]. The cell ratio graph (Figure 3E) further illustrates the differences in the proportions of cell subpopulations across different samples.

  Based on the metabolite enrichment analysis results (Figure S3), we focused our study on the glutathione pathway. We found that glutathione pathway levels were higher in the SLE group than in the healthy population (Figure 4A). Further analysis revealed significant heterogeneity in the expression of glutathione pathway scores across cellular subpopulations. Notably, monocytes exhibited significantly higher metabolism scores than other subpopulations, except for platelets (Figure 4B). This finding suggests that monocytes may play a key role in maintaining redox homoeostasis and antioxidant stress, reflecting their activation status and metabolic adaptation in the inflammatory microenvironment of the SLE group. Additionally, the high activity of the glutathione pathway in monocytes may be linked to their role in secreting pro‐inflammatory cytokines and mediating immune responses, further suggesting the critical regulatory role of glutathione metabolism in the inflammatory microenvironment of SLE. We divided the monocytes into two groups, Metabolism_high and Metabolism_low, based on the glutathione metabolism score, and analysed their differential genes using the FindMarkers function, followed by GSEA enrichment analysis. The results (Figure 4C) showed that in the Metabolism_high group, pathways such as glutathione metabolism, carbon metabolism, drug metabolism, metabolic pathways, and amino acid biosynthesis were upregulated. Additionally, antigen processing and presentation, xenobiotic metabolism by cytochrome P450, and phagosome pathways were also activated, indicating that the Metabolism_high group exhibits increased metabolic activity, oxidative stress response, energy supply, and exogenous antigen metabolism, as well as a stronger immune response. The downregulation of the T cell receptor signalling pathway suggests that Metabolism_high monocytes may influence the inflammatory microenvironment by promoting immune depletion or suppressing regulatory T cells (Tregs), resulting in an imbalance in T cell immune function. To further investigate the metabolic activity of Metabolism_high monocytes, we analysed several metabolic pathways using the scMetabolism package. The results (Figure 4D) revealed significant activation of several pathways in the Metabolism_high group, including energy production, lipid synthesis and catabolism, carbohydrate utilisation, amino acid and nucleotide metabolism, and synthesis of signalling molecules and steroids. The regulation of these metabolic activities is essential for cellular homoeostasis, environmental adaptation, and supporting cellular functions. This metabolic reprogramming in Metabolism_high monocytes may lead to an imbalance between metabolism and immunity, contributing to the onset of autoimmune diseases. Transcription factor prediction analysis (Figure 4E) indicated that Metabolism_high and Metabolism_low predicted opposite transcription factors, with Metabolism_high showing higher transcriptional activity. We observed that key transcription factors, such as NFKB1 and TP53, were significantly activated in monocytes from SLE patients with increased glutathione metabolism. NFKB1, a key component of the NF‐κB signalling pathway, plays a crucial role in its transcriptional activation [40]. Previous studies have demonstrated that circulating microparticles derived from SLE patients (SLE‐MPs) significantly induce monocyte activation, promote the release of pro‐inflammatory factors, and activate the NF‐κB signalling pathway [41]. Prolonged activation of the NF‐κB signalling pathway further enhances the expression of pro‐inflammatory factors, which subsequently increases oxidative stress levels in SLE patients [42]. Furthermore, transcriptomic and proteomic analyses have identified TP53 as a key regulator of DNA damage and cell cycle in SLE patients, contributing to immune activation in monocytes [43]. A separate study assessed the relationship between TP53 gene polymorphisms and SLE susceptibility, suggesting that TP53 plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of SLE, particularly in monocyte oxidative stress responses [44]. Glutathione, a major intracellular antioxidant, is crucial for regulating cell proliferation, apoptosis, and immune function [45, 46]. Thus, monocytes may preserve cellular homoeostasis by regulating glutathione synthesis and utilisation to mitigate oxidative stress. These findings highlight the potential role of metabolic reprogramming in monocytes, where activated transcription factors may protect against oxidative stress by driving changes in glutathione metabolism. To explore the differences in cellular communication between Metabolism_high and Metabolism_low, we conducted a cellular communication analysis using CellChat. Figure 4F illustrates the complex intercellular communication relationships and the strength of communication, both as senders and receivers of signals, with stronger interactions between Metabolism_high and other cell subpopulations (Figure 4G). Bubble plots (Figure 4H, I) revealed stronger interactions between Metabolism_high and CD4 T cells, CD8 T cells, macrophages, and NK cells via LGALS9, CD44, and CD45. These results suggest that Metabolism_high exhibits higher metabolic and transcriptional activities and plays a central regulatory role in the immune microenvironment, which may be crucial in the immunopathological process of SLE.

  Differential genes for Metabolism_high and Metabolism_low, obtained from scRNA‐seq analysis, were further screened based on the threshold of FDR < 0.05 and the absolute value of log2FC > 1. This screening initially identified 22 key genes involved in glutathione metabolism in monocytes. To further explore the expression patterns of key glutathione metabolism genes during monocyte development, we conducted a single‐cell mimetic time series analysis on the SLE group using Monocle2. The results indicated that these genes exhibit distinct expression patterns across the six temporal states of monocyte development (Figure 5A left). Additionally, the GO enrichment analysis revealed that monocytes at various time points display distinct biological functions (Figure 5A right). Monocytes in the C1 and C2 clusters were associated with pathways related to cellular oxidant detoxification, cellular detoxification, and other processes. Their primary biological functions included maintaining cellular homoeostasis, detoxification, and cytoprotection. Monocytes in the C3 cluster were enriched in pathways related to the negative regulation of the acute inflammatory response and tricarboxylic acid metabolism. These were primarily associated with energy metabolism, inflammation regulation, and other biological functions. Monocytes in the C4 cluster are enriched in pathways related to the Lipoxygenase pathway and the response to vitamin E, mainly involved in the prevention of lipid peroxidation and reduction of cellular damage caused by environmental stressors, such as oxidative stress and UV radiation. Monocytes in the C5 cluster were enriched in pathways related to the release of cytochrome c from mitochondria and mitochondrial changes, primarily associated with mitochondria‐dependent apoptosis signalling. Monocytes in the C6 cluster were enriched in pathways related to positive thymic T cell selection, lymph node development, and thymic T cell selection. These monocytes are likely involved in T cell development, immune organ formation, and cytokine regulation. Figure 5B illustrates the expression trends of key genes involved in glutathione metabolism across six monocyte clusters, organised by temporal development nodes. Pseudotime is represented on the horizontal axis, while expression levels are shown on the vertical axis. Using the VECTOR algorithm, we identified the starting point of monocyte development in the SLE group. By constructing a developmental trajectory map (Figure 5D), we localised the initial cell clusters. When combined with the previously calculated glutathione metabolism scores (Figure 5C), we observed that these scores peaked during the early and middle stages of monocyte development. These findings suggest that glutathione metabolism plays a crucial role in the early and middle stages of monocyte development, particularly in maintaining intracellular redox homoeostasis and regulating cellular maturation. Additionally, monocytes in the SLE condition may depend on elevated glutathione metabolism to manage oxidative stress.


### Machine Learning and SHAP
  We further applied Lasso regression to 22 key genes involved in glutathione metabolism to eliminate covariance, ultimately narrowing down the list to 9 key genes (Figure 6A, B). Protein interactions were analysed using the GeneMANIA database (Figure 6C). Immune infiltration analysis (Figure 6D) revealed that the majority of these genes were associated with endothelial cells, neutrophils, and monocytic lineage. These correlations suggest that hub genes related to glutathione metabolism may interact with endothelial cells, neutrophils, and monocytes in the immune microenvironment, playing a crucial role in the development of SLE. We selected CatBoost, XGBoost, and NGBoost to build machine learning models for the hub genes. These models exhibited strong performance on the training set, with Area Under the Curve (AUC) values near or equal to 1 (Figure 6E). In the validation set, the AUCs for CatBoost, NGBoost, and XGBoost were 0.85, 0.80, and 0.83, respectively (Figure 6F). The ROC values for all three models were ≥ 0.8. The CatBoost model slightly outperformed NGBoost and XGBoost. Its superior performance in handling complex datasets, particularly medical datasets with categorical features, can be attributed to its built‐in feature processing functions (e.g., automatic category coding and feature importance weighting), which enhance its adaptability to such data [30, 47]. Next, we applied the SHAP method to interpret the machine learning models and generate summary plots (Figure 7A,D,G), beeswarm plots (Figure 7B,E,H), and heatmap plots (Figure 7C,F,I) for visualisation. Interestingly, LAP3 emerged as the most important gene across all three machine learning models.


### LAP3 Plays a Crucial Role in the Immune Microenvironment of SLE
  We identified LAP3 as a key gene for glutathione metabolism in SLE through multiple machine learning algorithms. We conducted single‐cell co‐expression analysis of LAP3 and key glutathione metabolism enzyme genes. The results revealed that LAP3 expression, along with the genes related to glutathione transport (SLC7A11, ABCC1), synthesis (GCLC, GCLM, GSS), and catabolism (GGT1), was significantly higher in the SLE group compared to the HC group (Figure 8A). Densitograms further showed that LAP3 was predominantly expressed in monocytes (Figure 8B). These findings suggest that LAP3 may be involved in monocyte‐mediated glutathione metabolism. To further investigate the role of LAP3 in the SLE immune microenvironment, we conducted a comprehensive biological function analysis of LAP3‐positive (LAP3+Mono) and LAP3‐negative monocytes (LAP3‐Mono). Transcription factor activity prediction revealed that LAP3+Mono exhibited higher transcriptional activity (Figure 8C). We conducted differential analysis of LAP3+Mono versus LAP3‐Mono and GSEA enrichment of differential genes. The analysis revealed significant enrichment of these genes in Systemic Lupus Erythematosus, Inflammatory Bowel Disease, Autoimmune Thyroid Disease, Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus, and other autoimmune‐related diseases. Notably, pathways related to Th1 and Th2 cell differentiation, antigen processing and presentation, cell adhesion molecules, and other immune‐regulatory processes were also significantly enriched (Figure 8D). Metabolic pathway analysis showed extensive activation of multiple metabolic pathways in LAP3+Mono (Figure 8E). These results suggest that LAP3+Mono may play a critical role in the SLE immune microenvironment through metabolic reprogramming, particularly in regulating immune and inflammatory responses. Further cellular communication analysis revealed that LAP3+Mono exhibited stronger communication than LAP3‐Mono in the immune microenvironment, both as a signal receiver and sender (Figure 8F). Cellular interaction analysis revealed that LAP3+Mono had stronger interactions with various immune cells, including CD4 T cells, CD8 T cells, DC cells, macrophages, NK cells, and others, via LGALS9‐CD44 and LGALS9‐CD45 interactions (Figure 8G, H). Cell communication analysis indicated that LAP3+Mono plays a more significant role in immune cell signalling, suggesting its potential as a key regulator in the immune response.


### LAP3 Were Identified as Biomarkers for SLE
  Furthermore, we confirmed that LAP3 expression was significantly higher in the SLE group compared to the HCs across the training set, test set, and another dataset (Figure 9A–C). Its ROC curve exhibited an AUC greater than 0.7 in all three datasets (Figure 9D–F). These results suggest that LAP3 may be a potential driver of SLE and could serve as a new molecular marker for the disease.


## Discussion
  We employed a combination of Mendelian randomisation (MR), multi‐omics analysis, various machine learning methods, and the SHAP approach to investigate metabolites and hub genes associated with SLE pathogenesis. Through annotation and enrichment analysis of metabolites with causal relationships identified by MR, we found that glutathione metabolism was significantly enriched. To explore the potential mechanisms of glutathione metabolism in SLE development, we linked SLE to glutathione metabolism through single‐cell RNA sequencing of peripheral blood and examined its role in the immune microenvironment. Using various machine learning methods and the interpretable SHAP technique, we identified LAP3 as a hub gene. Together with glutathione metabolism, it offers new therapeutic targets and biomarkers for SLE.

  Glutathione is the primary intracellular antioxidant, existing mainly as reduced glutathione (GSH) and oxidised glutathione (GSSG) [45]. Glutathione, a key regulator of redox signalling, is essential for detoxifying exogenous substances, as well as regulating cell proliferation, apoptosis, immune function, and fibrosis [46]. Imbalances in glutathione are associated with various pathologies, including autoimmunity, making it a potential diagnostic and therapeutic target [48]. Research evidence suggests that changes in cellular glutathione levels are a key indicator linking immune‐inflammatory responses to disease progression [49, 50]. A recent study found that blocking a novel pathway for GSH synthesis significantly reduces GSH levels, increases reactive oxygen species (ROS), and markedly affects T‐cell differentiation, thus slowing the pathological progression of experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) [51]. Another study showed that ATP‐induced glutathione efflux leads to intracellular ROS accumulation, placing the cell in a state of redox imbalance and promoting the subsequent activation of NLRP3 inflammasomes [52]. GSH deficiency results in the dysregulation of serine metabolism, decreased FoxP3 expression, and loss of Treg inhibition, triggering a severe autoimmune response [53]. GPX4 is a key peroxide‐degrading enzyme in glutathione metabolism, and its regulation of neutrophil ferroptosis has been shown to induce systemic autoimmunity [54]. A clinical study found that high levels of GGT may be a risk factor for SLE exacerbations in females, as GGT is a key enzyme in glutathione degradation [55]. These studies suggest a strong link between glutathione metabolism and inflammatory responses, indicating that targeting key processes in glutathione metabolism may provide a novel strategy for treating autoimmune diseases.

  Monocytes and monocyte‐derived macrophages are crucial in the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases, such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) [56, 57]. SLE is characterised by redox imbalance, increased apoptosis, and impaired phagocytosis, which enhance autoantigen exposure, subsequently leading to the formation of autoantibodies and disease progression [58, 59, 60]. Our study revealed that SLE patients exhibit significantly higher levels of glutathione metabolism in the peripheral blood immune microenvironment compared to healthy controls. Furthermore, monocytes within cellular subpopulations show elevated glutathione metabolism levels. KEGG pathway genes, used to generate metabolic scores, include both anabolic and catabolic steps in glutathione metabolism. This indicates metabolic reprogramming of both glutathione catabolism and anabolic pathways in peripheral blood mononuclear cells of SLE patients. GSEA enrichment analysis, pathway enrichment analysis, and transcription factor prediction revealed that Metabolism_high monocytes exhibited heightened activity in metabolic processes, oxidative stress response, energy supply, exogenous antigen metabolism, and transcriptional activity. This indicates that monocytes from SLE patients adapt to the inflammatory microenvironment through metabolic reprogramming, which may also reflect monocyte overactivation or dysfunction. A study found that under conditions of beta‐(1,3)‐D‐glucan‐induced host infection by fungi and certain bacteria, monocytes respond to acute inflammation‐induced auto‐oxidative stress by synthesising and metabolising glutathione [61]. Another study demonstrated that exposure of human monocytes to Borrelia burgdorferi infection results in significant and specific alterations in GSH metabolism, leading to a marked increase in intracellular reduced glutathione levels [62]. These results support the adaptive changes in monocytes through metabolic reprogramming in response to acute inflammatory injury. The pseudotime analysis revealed that alterations in glutathione metabolism are associated with distinct biological functions of monocytes at various developmental stages. The pseudotime analysis also revealed that during early development, monocytes primarily maintain cellular homoeostasis, while in mid‐development they are mainly involved in energy metabolism, inflammation regulation, and other biological processes. In late development, their key role is associated with the mitochondria‐dependent apoptosis signalling pathway. This progression reflects stage‐specific functional differentiation in monocytes, from homoeostasis maintenance to inflammation regulation, and ultimately to apoptosis signalling, with each stage accompanied by distinct changes in glutathione metabolism. A recent study reported similar findings, showing that monocytes maintain homoeostasis in vivo by sequentially activating anabolic immune effector mechanisms, followed by switching to immune inactivation mechanisms, and ultimately restoring immune metabolic homoeostasis under acute inflammation conditions [63]. These changes reflect functional variations in monocytes at different time points. These results suggest that disease‐induced inflammation may drive monocytes to undergo glutathione metabolic reprogramming in response to changes in endogenous oxidative stress. Notably, this metabolic reprogramming is adaptive but can also exacerbate the pathological disease process. On one hand, monocytes adapt to environmental changes and maintain immune homoeostasis through metabolic reprogramming; on the other hand, this reprogramming disrupts inflammation regulation, further aggravating the disease. Cellular communication analysis revealed that Metabolism_high monocytes exhibit stronger interactions with other cell subpopulations. Specific signalling molecules (e.g., LGALS9, CD44, CD45) may mediate communication between glutathione hypermetabolising monocytes and other immune cells, playing a critical role in immune function, inflammatory responses, and pathological processes. These findings deepen our understanding of monocyte metabolic reprogramming in immune regulation and offer new insights into the mechanisms underlying monocyte function in SLE.

  By analysing the results from the SHAP method applied to three machine learning models, we found that LAP3 was the most significant among the 11 hub genes. Leucine aminopeptidase (LAP) is an enzyme that sustains cellular function by catalysing the hydrolysis of leucine residues from the amino terminus of proteins or peptides [64]. LAP performs several essential functions in mammals, microorganisms, and plants, including protein maturation, degradation, and amino acid recycling through peptide hydrolysis and peptide‐dependent DNA interaction [64, 65]. Recent studies have provided additional evidence of LAP3's crucial role in various diseases. For instance, Mendelian randomisation studies suggest a causal association between LAP3 and atrial fibrillation (AF) [66]. A proteomics‐based study found significantly elevated plasma levels of LAP3 in patients with nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [67]. In NAFLD, LAP3 expression is significantly elevated in both serum and hepatocytes, where it contributes to the onset and progression of the disease by inhibiting autophagy [68]. LAP3 expression was elevated in the livers of patients with Chronic Hepatitis B (CHB) and CHB combined with Nonalcoholic Steatohepatitis (NASH), and it showed a strong correlation with lipidomic profiles [69]. Additionally, during the excessive inflammation induced by COVID‐19, LAP3, an inflammatory gene linked to the neutrophil response, was significantly upregulated and is considered a potential target for anti‐inflammatory therapies [70]. Moreover, LAP3 plays a critical role in the progression of various cancers. For instance, inhibiting LAP3 expression significantly reduces fascin and MMP‐2/9 levels, thereby inhibiting the migration and invasion of ovarian cancer cells [71]. Studies indicate that LAP3 promotes glioma malignancy by enhancing cell proliferation, cell cycle progression, migration, and invasion [72]. Similarly, in oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), inhibiting LAP3 expression significantly reduces cancer cell proliferation, clone formation, and migratory invasiveness, resulting in G1 phase cell cycle arrest [73]. LAP3 is significantly upregulated in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), where it promotes cell proliferation, migration, and enhances G1/S cycle regulation [74]. In summary, LAP3, as a key hub gene, plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of various diseases, particularly inflammatory and tumour‐related conditions that have been extensively studied. However, research on LAP3's function and mechanisms in autoimmune diseases (e.g., SLE) is still limited, and its specific biological roles and potential pathological associations are yet to be fully understood. Therefore, further in‐depth research on the regulation of LAP3 expression, signalling pathways, and its potential clinical significance in autoimmune diseases will offer valuable insights for developing new therapeutic strategies.

  This study's strength lies in employing Mendelian randomisation (MR) analyses to estimate causal relationships between metabolites and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) using genetic variation, which reduces confounding bias and enhances causal inference. Additionally, we employed multi‐omics, machine learning, and SHAP methods to identify key genes involved in glutathione metabolism in SLE, thus enhancing the reliability of our findings. However, our analyses have several limitations. First, the GWAS data in our study predominantly represent European populations, which may limit the generalisability of our findings to other populations. Second, the GWAS data were not stratified by sex, age, or disease severity, preventing further analyses in these subgroups. Finally, our study did not functionally or mechanistically test the findings, which will be the focus of our future research.


## Conclusion
  In conclusion, MR analysis and metabolite enrichment analysis revealed a connection between glutathione metabolism and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). Through multi‐omics analysis, machine learning, and SHAP, we identified LAP3 as a key gene among several hub genes potentially involved in the development of SLE. Analysis of multiple datasets indicated that LAP3 expression was significantly higher in PBMCs from SLE patients compared to those from healthy individuals, and it demonstrated strong diagnostic potential. These findings suggest that glutathione metabolism and LAP3 could represent novel diagnostic and therapeutic targets for SLE. Future research should focus on validating these targets and exploring their mechanisms and functions, paving the way for innovative therapeutic approaches that target both the immune and metabolic aspects of SLE.
