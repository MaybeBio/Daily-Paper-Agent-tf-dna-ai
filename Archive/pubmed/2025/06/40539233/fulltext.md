# DeepTFtyper: an interpretable morphology-aware graph neural network for translating histopathology images into molecular subtypes in small cell lung cancer


## Abstract
  Small cell lung cancer (SCLC) is a highly aggressive high-grade neuroendocrine carcinoma with a poor prognosis. Molecular subtyping of transcription factors (SCLC-A, -N, -P, and -Y) shows great potential for guiding treatment decisions. However, its clinical application are limited by insufficient samples and the complexity of molecular testing. In this study, we developed DeepTFtyper, a graph neural network–based deep learning model for automatically classifying SCLC molecular subtypes from hematoxylin and eosin-stained whole-slide images. DeepTFtyper was trained and tested on the Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Science cohort (n = 389) with 4-fold cross-validation, and achieved high performance with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve above 0.70 for all four molecular subtypes identified by immunohistochemistry (IHC). Furthermore, the digital H-scores predicted by DeepTFtyper showed a significant correlation with IHC-based H-scores. Patch-level visualization and morphological analysis revealed that DeepTFtyper identifies interpretable and generalizable features corresponding to areas of relevant transcription factor expression as revealed by IHC staining and correlates well with morphological features. This study represents the first deep learning framework for predicting SCLC molecular subtypes from hematoxylin and eosin-stained histology slides, providing a scalable, accurate, and clinically relevant tool to improve patient management and guide personalized treatment decisions.


## Introduction
  Small cell lung cancer (SCLC) is a highly aggressive neuroendocrine carcinoma that accounts for approximately13%–15% of all lung cancers and is associated with a disproportionately high mortality rate due to its rapid growth, early metastasis, and limited therapeutic options [1]. In contrast to non-small cell lung carcinoma, clinical management strategies for SCLC have remained largely unchanged for decades until recent advances in molecular biology have unraveled the complex and heterogeneous nature of SCLC [2–4].

  A landmark classification system based on four transcription factors—ASCL1, NeuroD1, POU2F3, and YAP1—has provided significant insight into the molecular diversity of SCLC [5]. Preclinical studies, both in vitro and in vivo, and clinical trials have revealed subtype-specific therapeutic vulnerabilities of SCLC. For example, the SCLC-A subtype is particularly sensitive to therapies targeting DLL3, BCL2, and LSD1 [6–8], whereas SCLC-N is selectively responsive to Aurora kinase inhibitors and Seneca Valley virus-based therapies [7, 9]. In addition, SCLC-P is vulnerable to insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor inhibitors, Poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors, and anti-metabolite therapies [7]. The IMpower133 trial revealed that a specific subset of SCLC patients—those lacking expression of ASCL1, NeuroD1, and POU2F3—derive the greatest benefit from immune checkpoint blockade therapy [7, 10]. Despite the promise of transcription factor-based molecular classifications in guiding stratified therapeutic strategies for SCLC, their clinical application remains limited, primarily due to the difficulty and high cost associated with performing molecular analyses, such as RNA sequencing and immunohistochemistry (IHC) in routine clinical practice. While informative, these methods are labor-intensive, expensive, and not always practical in resource-limited settings [5, 11]. In contrast, digital pathology, powered by artificial intelligence (AI) and deep learning (DL) techniques, offers a cost-effective and efficient alternative for tumor classification and prediction of molecular features in various cancers [12–20].

  In this study, we aimed to develop and validate a DL framework, DeepTFtyper, for the prediction of transcription factor subtypes of SCLCs and tumor classification based on histopathological images. Our results demonstrate that DeepTFtyper can accurately predict the expression levels of key transcription factors and classify tumors into distinct molecular subtypes using digital whole-slide images (WSIs). This study represents the first attempt to predict molecular subtypes in SCLC and highlights the potential of DL to assist pathologists in improving diagnostic accuracy and clinical decision-making.


## Materials and methods

### Patient cohorts
  This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review boards of all participating centers, including the Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Science (approval no. 22/250-3452). Informed consent was waived by the ethics committee for the retrospective use of anonymized data. A total of 461 patients with SCLC who had formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor material and complete molecular and clinicopathological data were collected from the Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Science (CHCAMS). After excluding cases with combined SCLC (n = 46), poor quality slides (n = 1), and H-score ≤50 (n = 25), 389 cases were enrolled in the model construction and validation (Fig. 1A).


### IHC staining and transcription factor-based subtyping
  IHC was performed on 3–5 μm thick FFPE tissue sections using fully automated Roche IHC instruments (Bench-Mark ULTRA IHC/ISH System, Roche Diagnostics) according to standard protocols. The following primary antibodies were used: ASCL1 (ab74065, Abcam, 1:200 dilution), NeuroD1 (ab60704, Abcam, 1:150 dilution), POU2F3 (bs-21046R, Bioss, 1:300 dilution), and YAP1 (ab52771, Abcam, 1:80 dilution). Nuclei staining was considered positive for ASCL1 and POU2F3, and both nuclei and cytoplasm staining were positive for NeuroD1 and YAP1.

  Immunoreactivity was quantified using the H-score (Histo-Score), which was calculated by multiplying the percentage of positively stained cells (ranging from 1% to 100%) by the intensity of staining (1 = weak, 2 = moderate, 3 = strong), yielding a cumulative score between 0 and 300. To ensure the accuracy and reproducibility of the results, a double pathologist review was conducted, and image analysis was performed using QuPath (version 0.3.2), an open-source digital pathology software (https://qupath.github.io). Positive staining was defined as an H-score > 50 and the molecular subtype of each tumor was assigned based on the highest H-score marker and designated as SCLC-A, SCLC-N, SCLC-P, and SCLC-Y [11].


### WSI acquisition and pre-processing
  WSIs of hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained tissue sections were acquired using high-resolution digital slide scanners (KF-LPE-006, Jiangfeng Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China). Each WSI was then divided into non-overlapping image patches at a 10× magnification, with each patch sized at 224 × 224 pixels. To ensure that each patch contained sufficient tissue for analysis, the Otsu algorithm was used to determine the optimal threshold for separating the tissue areas from white and blurred background regions [21]. The Otsu algorithm evaluates the pixel intensity histogram of each patch and selects a threshold value that minimizes the intra-class variance between tissue and background. Patches with <70% tissue coverage of the total patch area were discarded to exclude non-informative regions. The remaining patches were then normalized by standardizing the pixel intensities based on the mean and standard deviation of the ImageNet dataset. For feature extraction, we used the pre-trained model CTransPath [22], a transformer-based architecture pre-trained on over 30,000 WSIs using a self-supervised learning approach, to extract 768-dimensional features from each patch (Fig. 2A).


### Development of the DL model
  In this study, we developed a DL model, DeepTFtyper, for predicting the transcription factor subtypes of SCLCs. The architecture of DeepTFtyper integrates graph convolutional networks (GCNs) with dual-channel processing, leveraging WSI data for feature extraction and classification. A detailed schematic of the model architecture is shown in Fig. 2.


#### Graph construction
  To capture both local and global spatial relationships between image patches, we construct two types of graphs: dense graphs and sparse graphs. The dense graph is constructed by establishing connections between each node with its eight potential neighboring nodes (including orthogonal and diagonal directions) in two-dimensional space. The sparse graph is built upon the dense graph by applying a sparsification strategy to the edges. Specifically, we calculated the cosine similarity between the node’s feature vector and its neighboring nodes. Only edges with similarity scores greater than or equal to a preset threshold (s = 0.9) are retained. Figure 2B illustrates the global and local visualization of the two types of graph structures.

The SM units update the features of a node by aggregating the features of its neighboring nodes. Notably, the weights of the second layer of SM units in each channel are shared, promoting information exchange and enhancing feature fusion between the two graph structures.


#### Feature aggregation module
  Following the graph convolution layers, the feature aggregation module integrates information from both graph channels. Each graph is subjected to DMoNPooling [25] to reduce the number of nodes, retaining the most significant 300 key nodes from each graph. The key nodes from both the dense and sparse graphs are then concatenated to form a 600-node feature vector, which is subsequently condensed using mean aggregation to generate a unified representation. The final aggregated feature vector is passed to the prediction head, which outputs prediction scores for the four SCLC transcription factor subtypes.

#### Training and implementation
  The framework is implemented using the open-source PyTorch library. The model is trained using the Nadam optimizer, with an initial learning rate of 1e − 3 and dynamically adjusted using an exponential learning rate scheduler. The batch size is set to 8, and the hyperparameter s, which determines the cosine similarity threshold for sparse graph construction, is set to 0.9.


### Visualization and interpretability
  To ensure the interpretability of the DeepTFtyper, we used the integrated gradients (IGs) algorithm, which provides insight into the contribution of each feature in the model’s predictions by integrating gradients with respect to the input feature dimensions. Specifically, for each n × 768 feature vector representing patches of WSI, the IG calculates the n × 4 × 768 gradient integrals corresponding to the four predicted subtypes. The mean gradient for each feature is calculated, yielding n × 4 contribution scores that represent the importance of each feature vector across the four predicted subtypes. A linear transformation is applied to scale the contribution scores in the range of [−1, 1]. Scores closer to 1 indicate a high positive contribution to the prediction. Scores closer to −1 indicate a negative contribution, meaning the feature reduces the likelihood of that subtype being predicted. Scores close to 0 indicate that the feature has a negligible effect on the model output. These contribution scores were visualized as heatmaps, overlaid onto the WSI, and highlighted regions that contributed positively or negatively to the prediction of each transcription factor subtype. To explore significant visual features associated with each subtype at the patch level, we applied the Leiden algorithm [27] for unsupervised clustering of high-contribution patches. Representative patches that exhibited key visual features for each transcription factor subtype were selected and displayed.


### Statistical analysis
  All statistical analyses were conducted using Python (version 3.9) and R (version 4.4.0). Specifically, the packages or software comprised PyTorch (version 2.0.1) for DL model implementation and training, CUDA (version 11.8), and cuDNN (version 8.1.0.77) for graphics processing unit (GPU) acceleration and QuPath for image annotation and pre-processing. Model performance was assessed using 4-fold cross-validation and external validation strategies with various performance metrics including the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), accuracy, precision, F1-score, recall, and confusion matrices. The relationship between the model’s predicted scores and the Smooth H-scores are measured using the Pearson correlation coefficient. The Wilcoxon rank sum test was used to determine differences in predicted scores between subtypes. A P-value <.05 was considered statistically significant.


## Results

### Study design and characteristics of patient cohorts
  A total of 389 patients from CHCAMS were included in this study. The mean age of the total cohort was 58.39 ± 9.4 years, with a median age of 58 years (range: 29–82 years). The majority of patients were male (291 cases, 74.81%) and 67.61% were smokers. The majority of patients were diagnosed at Stage III (37.02%), followed by Stage I (29.31%) and Stage II (29.31%). A small proportion of patients were at stage IV (3.08%). The distribution of molecular subtypes in the SCLC cohort was as follows: SCLC-A 132 cases (33.93%), SCLC-N 157 cases (40.36%), SCLC-P 47 cases (12.08%), and SCLC-Y 53 cases (13.63%).

  We used H&E-stained tumor slides from the CHCAMS cohort to train the DL model. To assess model performance, we first conducted a 4-fold cross-validation process on the CHCAMS cohort, where the model was iteratively trained on a subset of patients and tested on the remaining subset.


### Development and validation of the DeepTFtyper
  The DeepTFtyper model was trained and validated using H&E-stained tumor slides from 389 patients in the CHCAMS cohort. We used a 4-fold cross-validation approach, iteratively training the model on 75% of the dataset and assessing its performance on the remaining 25%. The performance metrics and ROC curves for the DeepTFtyper’s predictions of the four SCLC subtypes across each fold of cross-validation are shown in Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. S1. The model demonstrated strong performance, particularly in predicting the SCLC-A and SCLC-N subtypes, with minimal variation across the different folds. When predicting the SCLC-P subtype, the model performed well across key metrics. For the SCLC-Y subtype, the model outperformed the other subtypes, achieving a maximum AUC of 0.86 and an accuracy of 0.85. The AUC values for the trained model in predicting the four subtypes were 0.76 (SCLC-A), 0.73 (SCLC-N), 0.70 (SCLC-P), and 0.83 (SCLC-Y) (Fig. 3B). We also assessed the correlation between the predicted values of the model and the smoothed H-scores for each subtype. As shown in Fig. 3C, the predicted scores derived from DeepTFtyper showed a strong positive correlation with smoothed H-scores, with an average Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.439, with specific values of R = 0.455 (SCLC-A, P < .001), R = 0.470 (SCLC-N, P < .001), R = 0.203 (SCLC-P, P = .044), and R = 0.626 (SCLC-Y, P < .001). Furthermore, the model’s predicted scores for a patient being classified into a specific subtype were significantly higher than those for the other three subtypes (Fig. 3D), indicating the strong ability of the model to discriminate between the different molecular subtypes of SCLC. Finally, we conducted comprehensive benchmarking experiments to compare the performance of our proposed graph neural network (GNN) approach with that of models using only features from the convolutional neural network (CNN) transformer model. The performance results clearly show that our DeepTFtyper model significantly outperforms both the MeanMLP and AttentionMIL models (Supplementary Table S1).


### Interpretability and visualization of DeepTFtyper predictions
  To further elucidate the model’s decision-making process, we used the IG algorithm to quantify the contribution of each WSI patch to the final predictions. The resulting contribution scores were visualized as heatmaps overlaid onto the original WSIs, revealing the tissue areas that contributed most to the model’s subtype predictions. Figure 4 shows examples of WSIs for each subtype, along with their corresponding heatmaps and IHC images. WSI regions with high-contribution scores (close to 1) and low or negative contributions (close to 0 or −1) are shown in the heatmaps. Overall, the contribution scores correlate well with the H-score magnitudes observed in each WSI. For example, the IHC image of the SCLC-A subtype highlights strong ASCL1 expression in tumor regions, which matches the areas with high-contribution scores in the corresponding heatmap. Similarly, the IHC images of other subtypes show differential expression of key markers, which further validates the predictions of the model based on histopathological features. Additionally, the heatmaps reveal that the model focuses predominantly on tumor regions within the WSIs. For example, Fig. 4 shows that the red and blue areas correspond to known tumor regions, while the white areas, indicating low or neutral contribution scores, correspond to non-tumor regions. This pattern suggests that the model effectively identifies and analyzes tumor-specific regions to make subtype predictions, reinforcing its ability to focus on clinically relevant tissue areas.


### Patch-level visualization and morphological characteristics of high-contribution features
  To gain deeper insight into the specific visual characteristics that the model uses to differentiate between subtypes, we performed unsupervised clustering on high-contribution patches. Specifically, we randomly selected 40 WSIs (10 for each subtype) and extracted patches that ranked in the top 20% of contribution scores for each subtype prediction. In total, we analyzed 43 851 patches. Using the Leiden algorithm, we clustered the feature vectors corresponding to these high-contribution patches. The clustering results were visualized using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP), mapping the contribution scores of the patches onto the UMAP plot (Fig. 5). For each subtype, we identified a key cluster of patches with a high contribution to the model’s predictions and selected 40 representative patches from each significant cluster for further visualization. As shown in Fig. 5, the clustering results revealed distinct morphological features within the high-contribution patches for each subtype. To correlate the high-contribution digital patches with morphological characteristics, the pathologists reviewed the patches for visual features that could explain the model’s decisions. The analysis revealed that features of the tumor microenvironment, in particular cancer cell morphology and the proportion of fibrosis, played a key role in distinguishing between subtypes. Specifically, high-contribution patches for SCLC-A and SCLC-N resembled classic SCLC morphology, exhibiting a high-density sheet-like pattern with small, round tumor cells. However, patches from the SCLC-N subtype contained more spindle-shaped tumor cells compared to SCLC-A, indicating a morphological preference within the variant SCLC spectrum. In contrast, high-contribution patches from the SCLC-P and SCLC-Y subtypes predominantly exhibited spindle-shaped tumor cells arranged in a nest-like distribution, with a higher proportion of surrounding stromal fibers, which resembles the histopathological morphology of variant SCLC [28].

  Furthermore, we performed a comprehensive quantitative analysis of fibrotic tissue and tumor areas using HALO™ (v3.6.4134, Indica Labs), an advanced AI-driven quantitative image analysis platform to validate our histological observations at the patch level. We performed quantitative analysis on 11 representative H&E-stained whole-slide images from four different subtypes using HALO™ platform. The quantitative analysis results from HALO™ closely matched our initial qualitative observations, confirming that SCLC-P and SCLC-Y subtypes have a higher proportion of stromal fibrosis within tumors compared to other subtypes (Supplementary Fig. S2 and Supplementary Table S2).


## Discussion
  In this study, we introduced DeepTFtyper, a GCN-based DL model capable of predicting molecular features and subtypes of SCLC from histopathological H&E-stained slides. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to leverage a large dataset of H&E-stained WSIs of SCLC to develop a DL framework specifically designed to predict transcription factor-based classifications and guide molecular subtype-oriented treatment strategies. The primary strength of our model lies in its applicability to address the challenges associated with extensive-stage SCLC, which accounts for ~80% of SCLC cases. This subtype often presents challenges due to the limited availability of biopsy samples, which are insufficient for transcriptomic or IHC testing.

  Although transcription factor-based classification has shown substantial potential for guiding targeted and precision therapies in SCLC [7, 29], its clinical application is often hampered by the difficulty of obtaining sufficient molecular data. Our model, trained on WSIs using four major SCLC subtypes (SCLC-A, SCLC-N, SCLC-P, and SCLC-Y) derived from IHC results, represents a significant step forward in addressing these challenges. While most existing prediction models focus on binary classification (presence or absence) [30–32], our model is capable of performing multi-class classification, enabling the prediction of four distinct molecular subtypes. This added complexity was achieved through careful development of classification labels, model selection, and optimization of multiple hyperparameters. For class labeling, we used a combination of expert evaluation and QuPath software to obtain accurate H-score readings for the ASCL1, NeuroD1, POU2F3, and YAP1 proteins [33, 34]. We also took care to address inter-observer variability in IHC scoring, thus ensuring reliable and reproducible training labels. For model selection, we chose to use GCNs over traditional CNNs due to their ability to capture graph-structured data and account for the dense spatial relationships between features in tissue slides [35–37]. CNNs are limited to processing 2D images and lack contextual awareness [38, 39], whereas GCNs can aggregate features from neighboring nodes, enabling the model to better capture the complex morphology of tumor regions. We also observed an imbalance in sample distribution, with SCLC-P and SCLC-Y comprising significantly smaller proportions compared to SCLC-A and SCLC-N, which may increase the risk of misclassification for these minority subtypes. To address this, we used label smoothing, which optimized model generalization and improved predictive performance for the less-represented categories [40, 41].

  A common concern with DL models is their "black-box" nature, which can hinder their adoption in clinical settings. We visualized the contribution of individual patches to the model’s decision-making process by applying the IG algorithm. The resulting heatmaps, overlaid on the original WSIs, highlighted the tumor regions most relevant to the model’s predictions. These areas of high contribution were consistent with the regions typically assessed by pathologists, improving the interpretability of the model and increasing confidence in its potential clinical utility. Additionally, expert pathologists reviewed the high-contribution patches and observed that the predicted subtypes displayed morphological patterns consistent with established pathological knowledge. For example, SCLC-A was characterized by high-density, sheet-like clusters of small, round tumor cells, while SCLC-P and SCLC-Y subtypes predominantly displayed spindle-shaped tumor cells arranged in a nest-like distribution with surrounding stromal fibers. These findings are consistent with previous studies [42] and were further validated by using a commercial AI-driven quantitative image analysis platform (HALO), providing additional confidence in the interpretability and clinical relevance of the model.

  Despite the promising results, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the model was primarily trained on surgical resection specimens and has not yet been validated on extensive biopsy specimens, which limits its current clinical applicability to patients with extensive disease. Second, although our model performed well on the used dataset, its generalizability to other patient populations, particularly those from different ethnic backgrounds or institutions with different diagnostic practices, remains untested. Third, the ROC curve with an AUC of 0.7 does not represent an ideal state of perfection, which is important in clinical practice. Many clinical diagnostic methods face the challenge of achieving very high accuracy, and the ability of our model to predict molecular subtypes from H&E-stained slides provides a useful and cost-effective tool for preliminary assessment. However, the AUC value highlights the need for ongoing model refinement and further validation. The main advantages of our model are its ability to [1] rapidly analyze a large number of pathological images and provide preliminary subtype predictions that could guide further molecular testing, and [2] serve as a complementary diagnostic tool when molecular classification is not feasible due to issues with sample acquisition, limitations in molecular detection technologies, or other practical challenges. Although our focus was on transcription factor-based subtyping, future iterations of the model could benefit from incorporating additional molecular data, such as gene expression profiles or genetic mutations, to further refine the classification process.

  In conclusion, we have developed and validated a DL model capable of performing histopathological image-based molecular classification of SCLC using standard diagnostic histology sections in a digital pathology workflow. Given the increasing importance of molecular classification in SCLC prognosis and treatment, our model holds significant potential to support personalized treatment strategies and improve patient management, especially in settings where molecular testing is challenging or unavailable.
