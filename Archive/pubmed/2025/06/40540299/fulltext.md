# Deciphering the Regulatory Networks of the Migrasome‐Associated Cell Subpopulation in Heterotopic Ossification via Multi‐Omics Analysis


## ABSTRACT
  Heterotopic ossification (HO) is a pathological process where bone forms in extraskeletal tissues, often occurring as a complication of tissue repair following injury. This condition can lead to movement limitations, pain, and functional impairment. However, the underlying pathomechanisms remain poorly understood. This study aims to elucidate key biomolecular networks involved in HO through a comprehensive multi‐omics analysis. Single‐cell, bulk, and spatial transcriptome datasets were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Migrasome score analysis identified a critical cell subtype associated with HO. Key genes were identified through high‐dimensional weighted gene co‐expression network analysis (hdWGCNA), machine learning, and dataset validation from clinical samples. Then we analyzed immune infiltration, microRNA (miRNA) networks, co‐expression networks, transcription factor (TF) regulatory networks, and signaling pathways to investigate potential regulatory mechanisms of HO. Spatial transcriptomics revealed the spatial patterns of cell subpopulation distribution and key molecule expression. Experimental validation further confirmed the expression patterns of key molecules in HO. As a result, we identified mesenchymal lineage cells (MLin) as the key migrasome‐associated cell subtype and determined peptidylprolyl isomerase B (Ppib) and transgelin (Tagln) as the key molecules. We constructed a regulatory network of these biomolecules and clarified their spatial distribution. Notably, the expression of Ppib and Tagln is temporally correlated with HO progression. Collectively, the identification of Ppib and Tagln, along with the construction of key biomolecular networks, facilitates the discovery of novel biomarkers for HO, offering promising potential for the development of preventive and therapeutic strategies.


## Introduction
  Heterotopic ossification (HO) is a pathological process characterized by the formation of bone tissue in non‐skeletal soft tissues, commonly observed after trauma, burns, surgery, or neurological injury [1]. As a common complication of tissue repair following injury, HO can lead to restricted movement, pain, and functional impairment, significantly impacting the patient's quality of life [2]. Current treatments, such as nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), radiation therapy, and surgical resection, have limited efficacy and carry risks of recurrence or side effects [3]. Moreover, due to the lack of reliable early biomarkers, HO is often diagnosed only after mineralized bone has formed, making prevention and early intervention challenging [4].

  Migrasomes, a recently discovered type of organelle, play crucial roles in cell migration and intercellular communication [5]. These organelles form on retraction fibers at the trailing edge of migrating cells and participate in various physiological processes [6], such as mitochondrial quality control [7], intercellular material transport [8], and information transfer [9, 10]. Recent research has highlighted the role of migrasomes in the bone marrow microenvironment, where mesenchymal stromal cells generate migrasomes to conduct cell–cell communication and promote hematopoiesis [11]. Furthermore, it has been found that migrasomes mediate the spatiotemporal interaction between tumor cells and osteoclasts in tumor bone metastasis [12]. However, the role of migrasomes in ectopic bone formation remains unreported. As organelles mediating various pathological processes [13, 14, 15], migrasomes are expected to bring new ideas for the mechanism research of HO and the development of methods for predicting and preventing HO. Indeed, several studies have explored the application of migrasome‐related signatures in various contexts. For instance, a pan‐cancer analysis identified migrasome‐associated genes as potential immunotherapeutic targets. The migrasome score, constructed through gene set enrichment analysis, was found to correlate significantly with tumor immunity and patient prognosis [16]. Additionally, a machine‐learning‐driven migrasome‐associated signature was recently developed for diagnosing acute myocardial infarction, highlighting its significance in cardiovascular disease [17]. These findings raise the possibility that migrasome‐based evaluations may offer new avenues for studying HO pathogenesis.

  In parallel, bioinformatics tools allow for the integration of data from various omics, leading to a more comprehensive understanding of disease pathways [18]. The synergy between multi‐omics analysis and machine learning (ML) offers transformative potential for understanding complex biological systems and advancing precision medicine. For example, in colorectal cancer, high‐throughput omics and statistical learning integration have been utilized to discover and validate novel diagnostic signatures [19]. In the context of osteoarthritis, integrative multi‐omics and ML approaches have been employed to characterize mitochondrial features [20]. In recent years, multi‐omics technology has also been widely applied in the study of HO. Integrating single‐cell and spatial transcriptomic data can deepen our understanding of HO by linking cellular subpopulations and gene expression with their spatial distribution and interactions [21, 22]. A recent study using single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) and spatial RNA sequencing (spRNA‐seq) identified the molecular mechanisms and microenvironmental changes during tendinopathy, showing that the pathological process of tendinopathy is essentially the process of tendon HO [23]. Furthermore, a spatiotemporal analysis using scRNA‐seq and spRNA‐seq uncovered the dynamics of osteochondral differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) during HO formation and provided the molecular landscape of osteoimmunological interactions that could aid HO treatment [24]. Another investigation with single‐cell and spatial transcriptome analysis clarified that the HIF‐1α/PLOD2 axis in mesenchymal progenitor cells is a key driver of traumatic HO [25]. However, to date, no studies have integrated bioinformatics and ML approaches for multi‐omics profiling in HO.

  In this study, we aimed to address this gap by identifying the key migrasome‐associated cell subpopulation and regulatory molecules involved in HO progression. We first utilized single‐cell data to conduct a comparative analysis of cell subtypes between HO and control samples. The key cell subtype was identified based on migrasome score. Then we applied high‐dimensional weighted gene co‐expression network analysis (hdWGCNA) to the key subtype, intersected their differential genes with hdWGCNA module genes, and employed random forest (RF) ML to select feature genes from this intersection. The clinical sample dataset validated these genes, enabling us to identify key genes of HO. Subsequently, using the bulk transcriptome in combination, a comprehensive suite of analyses was performed to elucidate the regulatory network of key molecules in HO. Furthermore, by combining the spatial transcriptome, we analyzed the spatial distribution of key cell subtypes within tissues through deconvolution, and elucidated the spatial expression patterns of key molecules. Finally, the expression of key molecules in HO was validated through quantitative real‐time polymerase chain reaction (qRT–PCR). Overall, this study reveals the key migrasome‐associated cell subpopulation and biomolecular regulatory mechanisms in HO, providing novel insights into prevention and treatment strategies for this condition.


## Materials and Methods

### Data Acquisition
  The data used in this study were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/info/datasets.html). The single‐cell data file GSE126060 was downloaded from the GEO public database, including 15 samples with complete single‐cell expression profiles for single‐cell analysis [26, 27]. Among these, 4 samples were from the control group, and 11 samples were from the HO group (disease group). The Series Matrix File of GSE126118 was downloaded, with the annotation file GPL13112 [28]. A total of 5 samples were included for expression profile analysis, with 3 control group samples and 2 HO group samples. The Series Matrix File of GSE106253, with the annotation file GPL21827, was downloaded, including 8 human samples, with 4 from the control group and 4 from the HO group [29]. The GSE255942 dataset, comprising 2 HO model samples from wild‐type C57BL/6 mouse, was used for spatial transcriptomics analysis [25].


### Single‐Cell Data Analysis
  The expression profile was processed using the Seurat package [30]. Cells were filtered based on the total count of unique molecular identifiers (UMI), the number of expressed genes, the percentage of mitochondrial gene expression, and the percentage of ribosomal gene expression. Quality control was performed using the median absolute deviation (MAD), and values greater than 3 MAD from the median were considered outliers and excluded. Based on the quality of data from multiple samples, cells with fewer than 200 captured genes were filtered out, using the following filtering criteria: (nFeature_RNA > 200 & percent.mt ≤ median + 3 MAD & nFeature_RNA ≤ median + 3 MAD & nCount_RNA ≤ median + 3 MAD). DoubletFinder (V2.0.4) was then used to filter out doublet cells from each sample, completing the cell quality control process.

  The global scaling normalization method, LogNormalize, was used. The total expression of each cell was adjusted to 10,000 by multiplying by a coefficient (s0) and then log‐transformed. Cell cycle scores were calculated using CellCycleScoring, and highly variable genes were identified using FindVariableFeatures. We used the ScaleData function to remove gene expression fluctuations due to mitochondrial gene expression, ribosomal gene expression, and cell cycle. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was applied for linear dimensional reduction on the expression matrix, and the selected principal components (PC) were used for subsequent analysis. Batch effects were corrected using Harmony.

  Nonlinear dimensionality reduction was performed using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) to determine the spatial relationships between clusters [31]. Cell annotation was performed by searching CellMarker and PanglaoDB databases and literature, supplemented by automated annotation using SingleR software, to identity cell types and corresponding marker genes.


### Migrasome Score Evaluation
  The migrasome score is a gene set–based quantitative metric designed to reflect the transcriptional activity associated with migrasome biogenesis and function at the single‐cell level. To construct this score, we curated a set of 11 migrasome‐related genes (Itga5, Ndst1, Pigk, Cpq, Pkd1, Calr, Eogt, Pkd2, Tomm20, Itgb1, and Tspan4), based on Li et al.'s study [32] and the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/). The AUCell algorithm was employed to calculate the migrasome score for each cell in the scRNA‐seq dataset. AUCell identifies cells with active gene sets by computing the Area Under the Curve (AUC) based on the ranked expression values. This approach enables the evaluation of input gene set enrichment in each cell and the detection of cell populations with specific transcriptional signatures. Accordingly, the AUCell‐based migrasome score allows us to distinguish cells with high migrasome‐associated activity from those with low or absent activity, providing insights into cell‐type–specific roles of migrasomes during HO progression.


### Ligand–Receptor Interaction Analysis
  CellChat is a tool that can quantitatively infer and analyze intercellular communication networks from single‐cell data [33]. It uses network analysis and pattern recognition methods to predict the major signal inputs and outputs of cells and how these cells and signals coordinate their functions. The normalized single‐cell expression profiles were used as the input data, whereas the cell subtypes derived from single‐cell analysis served as the cell information. We quantified the intercellular interaction strength (weights) and frequency (counts) to evaluate the degree of interaction among cells, thus observing the activity and impact of each cell type in the disease.


### HdWGCNA
  HdWGCNA provides built‐in functions for network inference, gene module identification, functional gene enrichment analysis, network reproducibility statistical testing, and data visualization [34]. A co‐expression network of genes expressed in at least 5% of the cells in the Seurat object was constructed using SetupForWGCNA. The dendrogram from WGCNA was visualized with PlotDendrogram, showing different co‐expression modules identified through network analysis. Each leaf on the dendrogram represents a gene, with colors at the bottom indicating the assignment of co‐expression modules. Finally, the level of module eigengenes (MEs) for each module were visualized using GetMEs.


### Enrichment Analysis of Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG)
  The R package “ClusterProfiler” was used for functional annotation of important genes to comprehensively explore their functional relevance [35]. GO and KEGG were used to assess the relevant functional categories.


### RF Feature Selection
  RF is an ensemble learning algorithm based on decision trees [36]. It uses sampling with replacement to select multiple samples from the sample set as a training set, generating a decision tree from the sampled set obtained by sampling. At each node, features are randomly selected without repetition to partition the sample set, identifying the best partitioning feature and determining the prediction outcome. In this study, the RF algorithm was used to evaluate the importance of features based on %IncMSE (increase in mean squared error), and the top 5 features were selected for subsequent analysis.


### Immune Cell Infiltration Analysis
  Cell‐type Identification By Estimating Relative Subsets Of RNA Transcripts (CIBERSORT) is a computational method used to assess the abundance of immune cell types in the microenvironment [37]. It uses the principle of linear support vector regression to deconvolve expression matrices of immune cell subtypes. The method includes 511 biomarkers that distinguish 25 immune cell phenotypes. The CIBERSORT algorithm was applied in this study to infer the relative proportions of 25 immune‐infiltrating cells in the sample data.


### MicroRNA (miRNA) Networks Related to Key Genes
  MiRNAs are small non‐coding RNAs that regulate gene expression by promoting mRNA degradation or inhibiting translation [38]. We further analyzed whether some miRNAs regulate the transcription or degradation of certain risk genes in the key genes. Key gene‐related miRNAs were obtained from the miRDB database, and the miRNA network was visualized using Cytoscape software.


### Single‐Cell Regulatory Network Inference and Clustering (SCENIC) Analysis
  SCENIC is a technique based on co‐expression and motif analysis to infer transcription factors (TFs) and their target genes in single‐cell transcriptomic data and construct regulatory networks [39]. This allows for a clear view of gene expression regulatory relationships and the identification of cell states. First, GENIE3 was used to identify gene sets co‐expressed with TFs. Motif enrichment analysis was then performed on each co‐expression module, and significantly enriched motifs were retained for TF annotation and gene scoring within co‐expression modules. Genes with lower motif scores were removed, leaving a gene set termed a “regulon.” A regulon consists of a TF and its target genes. SCENIC then scores the activity of each regulon in different cells, with higher scores indicating greater activation of the gene set.


### Pseudotime Analysis
  Monocle introduces a strategy for ordering individual cells in pseudotime, taking advantage of the asynchronous processes of individual cells to place them on trajectories corresponding to biological processes like cell differentiation [40]. In this study, pseudotime analysis was conducted using the Monocle 2 package. The Seurat object was converted into a Monocle CellDataSet, and highly variable genes were selected for trajectory construction. Dimensionality reduction was performed using the DDRTree algorithm, and cells were ordered along pseudotime using the orderCells function. The resulting trajectory was visualized in reduced‐dimensional space to depict dynamic transcriptional changes during disease progression.


### Deconvolution Analysis
  Robust Cell‐Type Decomposition (RCTD) is a supervised learning method that decomposes RNA sequencing mixtures into individual cell types, enabling cell type assignment to spatial transcriptomic pixels [41]. Specifically, we used the spacexr package, combined with annotated scRNA‐seq data, to perform deconvolution analysis of spatial transcriptomics, determining the dominant cell types in each spot and defining the expected cell type‐specific profiles in the spatial transcriptomics data.


### Animal Model and Specimen Collection
  All animal experiments received approval from the Animal Ethic Committee of Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University (No. NFYY‐2022‐0390). The HO model was established by performing Achilles tenotomy on 6‐week‐old female C57BL/6J mice [42]. A complete transverse incision was first made at the midpoint of the right Achilles tendon in each mouse, followed by sewing the skin to aid tendon self‐repair. In the case of sham surgery, the skin was cut without harming the Achilles tendon. A total of 48 mice were randomly allocated into 8 groups, comprising 4 HO groups and 4 sham groups. Each group was euthanized at 1, 4, 7, and 10 weeks post‐surgery (n = 6 per group). Blinding was applied to group assignments. The right Achilles tendons were collected for analysis at each time point.


### qRT–PCR
  TRIzol was used to extract total RNA from samples, which was subsequently reverse‐transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using PrimeScriptTM RT Master Mix (both Takara). qRT–PCR was executed on an ABI QuantStudio5 (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) using SYBR Premix Ex TaqTM II (Takara). Relative gene expression levels were computed using the 2−∆∆Ct method after normalizing the data to glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (Gapdh). Table S1 lists the primer sequences for the relevant genes.


### Statistical Analysis
  Statistical analyses in this study were performed using the R language (version 4.3.0). All statistical tests were two‐tailed, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.


## Results

### Quality Control and Standardization for Single‐Cell Data
  The single‐cell data used in this study were obtained from the GSE126060 dataset. The data were filtered based on specific parameters, and doublets were removed, resulting in a total of 23,922 cells retained (Figure S1A,B). We identified 2000 highly variable genes (Figure S1C), followed by data normalization, uniformity adjustment, PCA, and Harmony analysis (Figure S1D–F).


### Cell Annotation
  After dimensionality reduction using UMAP, 17 subclusters were identified (Figure 1A). Each subcluster was then annotated, and all clusters were assigned to 11 cell types: macrophages, mesenchymal lineage cells (MLin), endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells (SMC)/Pericytes (Peri), erythrocytes, T cells, satellite cells, schwann cells, polymorphonuclear leukocytes (PMNs), plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDC), and B cells (Figure 1B). The expression levels of classical markers across these 11 cell types were visualized using a bubble plot (Figure 1C), and the proportions of each cell type were displayed in a bar plot (Figure 1D). Subsequently, AUCell was used to quantify the metabolic pathway gene scores in single cells. A heatmap was generated using the pheatmap package to visualize the activity matrix of metabolic pathways across cell types (Figure 1E). The results indicated that macrophages, MLin, endothelial cells, and SMC/Peri exhibited relatively higher activity in most pathways.


### Migrasome Score Quantification, Cell Communication Analysis, and Secondary Clustering
  Next, we quantified migrasome scores, and the results revealed that the MLin subgroup not only exhibited the highest migrasome scores among all cell types but also showed the largest difference in migrasome scores between the control and disease groups, with a marked up‐regulation in the disease condition (Figure 2A). These findings suggest a potential association between MLin and disease‐specific migrasome activity. Therefore, MLin was selected for further analyses. Using the criteria of |avg_log2FC| > 0 and adjusted p‐value (p_val_adj) < 0.05, a total of 1384 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified in this cell type, as illustrated in the volcano plot (Figure 2B). These DEGs were used as the gene set for subsequent analysis. We then divided MLin into High_MLin and Low_MLin groups based on the median migrasome score. Cell communication was analyzed using the CellChat package. The extensive network of ligand–receptor pairs was observed among multiple cell populations, characterized by both high connectivity and various interaction strengths, indicating complex intercellular interactions (Figure 2C). Notably, High_MLin displayed particularly active signaling behavior with other cell types (Figure 2D). Further subsetting of MLin identified 6 subtypes through PCA, Harmony, ElbowPlot, and FindClusters (Figure 2E–G). Subtype C1, a specific subgroup found only in disease samples, was identified as a disease‐specific subgroup (Figure 2H). In short, these findings suggested that the MLin subpopulation, identified through migrasome scoring, might play a pivotal role in the progression of HO.


### HdWGCNA and ML for Key Gene Identification in HO
  To identify gene co‐expression networks within the MLin subtype, we performed hdWGCNA. Here, group.by was set to clustering subtypes and custom subtypes to construct the co‐expression network, aiming to explore biomarkers involved in HO development. The soft threshold was determined using the function “TestSoftPowers” and was set to 6 (Figure 3A). In this analysis, 5 gene modules were detected: turquoise, blue, brown, yellow, and green modules (Figure 3B). Further analysis between modules and MEs (Figure 3C,D) revealed that the MEs level of the turquoise module was elevated in the disease subgroup (Figure 3E), so the top 100 genes with the highest eigengenes connectivity (kMEs) in the turquoise module were selected as feature genes for further analysis. We then performed functional prediction on the genes obtained from hdWGCNA using GO (Figure S2A) and KEGG (Figure S2B) enrichment analysis. Next, we intersected the DEGs in MLin with the genes from hdWGCNA, resulting in 100 intersecting genes (Figure 3F). Among these, only genes that were detectably expressed in the bulk RNA‐seq dataset GSE126118 were retained as input features for the RF algorithm. Based on feature importance rankings, the top 5 genes were identified as feature genes: Ppib, Tagln, Myl9, Dad1, and Fkbp11 (Figure 3G). We used the clinical sample dataset GSE106253 for external validation and performed differential analysis on these 5 genes. The results showed significant down‐regulation of Ppib (peptidylprolyl isomerase B) and Tagln (transgelin) in the disease group (Figure 3H), and these two molecules were selected as HO key genes for further analysis.


### Immune Infiltration Analysis
  The microenvironment is composed of immune cells, extracellular matrix (ECM), growth factors, and inflammatory factors, significantly influencing disease diagnosis, survival outcomes, and clinical treatment sensitivity. We performed immune infiltration analysis, displaying the immune cell infiltration levels and correlations between immune cell types (Figure S3A,B). The analysis revealed that M1 Macrophages, Mast Cells, CD8 Memory T Cells, and CD8 Naive T Cells showed significant differences between the disease and control groups (Figure S3C). We further investigated the relationship between key genes and immune cells and found that Ppib was significantly positively correlated with M1 Macrophage, M2 Macrophage, and CD8 Naive T Cells, but negatively correlated with Mast Cells and CD8 Memory T Cells (Figure S3D).


### MiRNA Networks of Key Genes and Correlation Analysis Between Key Genes and Disease Regulatory Genes in HO
  We performed reverse prediction of the key genes using the miRDB database and identified 56 miRNAs, resulting in 56 mRNA−miRNA pairs, which were visualized as a non‐coding RNA network using Cytoscape (Figure 4A). The disease regulatory genes related to HO were retrieved from GeneCards. We analyzed the expression levels of the top 20 genes ranked by Relevance Score that were also expressed in the GSE126118 bulk RNA‐seq data, revealing that Sox9, Spi1, Mmp9, Bmp2, Smad6, Adrb2, and Cdh11 exhibited significant expression differences between the two groups (Figure 4B). We also performed correlation analysis between the key genes and disease regulatory genes. The results, visualized in a bubble plot, showed a significant positive correlation between Tagln and Adrb2 (r = 0.968), and a significant negative correlation between Ppib and Smad6 (r = −0.977) (Figure 4C). We obtained HO disease regulatory genes with high Relevance Score from GeneCards and analyzed their correlation with key genes. Co‐expression networks revealed the expression of key genes and disease‐related genes in single cells, along with their correlations. Notably, the disease genes Spi1, Bmp2, Mmp9, Smad6, and Sox9 showed significant correlations with Tagln. Among these, Bmp2 and Smad6 were positively correlated with Tagln, whereas Spi1, Mmp9, and Sox9 were negatively correlated with Tagln (Figure 5A–E). Similarly, the disease genes Spi1, Bmp2, Mmp9, Smad6, and Sox9 were all negatively correlated with Ppib (Figure S4A–E).


### SCENIC Analysis
  Considering that MLin exhibited the largest difference in migrasome scores between the disease and control groups, we selected the MLin subgroup for SCENIC analysis and output all regulons within this subgroup (Step2_regulonTargetsInfo.tsv). MLin was divided into high‐score (Hsco) and low‐score (Lsco) groups based on the median migrasome score. A heatmap was generated to visualize the regulon activity scores for each cell (Figure 6A). We calculated the Regulon Specificity Score (RSS) for each regulon to assess its specificity between the Hsco and Lsco groups. The results were visualized using a bubble plot (Figure 6B). Subsequently, based on the expression levels of the 2 key genes Ppib and Tagln, we divided MLin into high‐expression (Hexp) and low‐expression (Lexp) groups. The top 5 most specific regulons in the Hexp groups were visualized using scatter plots. In MLin with high Ppib expression, Jund, Creb3, Atf4, Jun, and Junb exhibited the highest regulon specificity (Figure 6C). Similarly, in MLin with high Tagln expression, Creb3, Sox4, Jund, Atf4, and Jun showed the greatest specificity (Figure 6D).


### Cell Development Trajectory and Immunometabolic Pathway Analysis
  Pseudotime analysis was performed by first calculating the similarity between cells and constructing a cell differentiation trajectory. Through visualizing this trajectory, we could track the developmental process of cells and study gene expression patterns at different time points. Pseudotime is a probability calculated by Monocle based on cellular gene expression information, reflecting the temporal order. Pseudotime values and the cell type were used to color the cells (Figure 7A,B). Differential gene expression was observed at different branches, which was not represented in the overall heatmap. We identified genes with large expression differences before and after branch points and visualized them in a branch heatmap (Figure 7C). The expression of HO key genes, Ppib and Tagln, showed an overall increasing trend during cell differentiation (Figure 7D). We used AUCell to quantify immune and metabolic pathway gene scores in single cells, and a bubble plot was generated to show the activity differences of HO key genes in these pathways. Results revealed high activity of Ppib and Tagln in pathways, such as epithelial−mesenchymal transition, and angiogenesis (Figure 7E).


### Deconvolution Analysis and Expression of Key Genes in Spatial Transcriptomics
  The deconvolution results indicated the proportion of different cell types in each spot of the sample. In GSM8458769, the majority of spots were occupied by MLin and satellite cells. In GSM8458770, the majority were occupied by MLin and PMNs (Figure 8A,B). To validate the deconvolution accuracy, we used the FindAllMarkers function to identify DEGs for each cell type (Figure 8C). The filtering parameters used were: logfc.threshold = 0, min.pct = 0.1, only.pos = F. We then analyzed the expression of key genes in spatial transcriptomics, visualizing the expression of Ppib and Tagln in scatter plots (Figure 8D) and bubble plots (Figure 8E). The scatter plots showed the specific distribution of key genes in the spatial transcriptomics data.


### Experimental Validation of Key Gene Expression in HO
  To validate the expression levels of key genes, qRT–PCR was conducted. The results indicated a significant up‐regulation of Ppib and Tagln gene expression in the HO model at 1 (Figure 9A) and 4 (Figure 9B) weeks post‐injury, followed by a notable down‐regulation at 7 (Figure 9C) and 10 (Figure 9D) weeks.


## Discussion
  HO represents a tissue repair process that has deviated from its normal course. Current research has indicated that inflammatory responses, activation of osteogenic or osteochondrogenic programs, and reprogramming of stem/progenitor cells are pivotal in the pathogenesis of HO [43]. The formation of HO involves multiple cell types and their complex interactions, which collectively promote HO through various signaling pathways and molecular mechanisms. Given the complexity and diversity inherent in the pathogenesis of HO, the development of more advanced coping strategies is necessary.

  ScRNA‐seq enables the dissection of cellular heterogeneity and the identification of specific cell types and molecular mechanisms involved in the pathological ossification of soft tissues [21]. In a study of elbow HO, scRNA‐seq identified 13 cell populations and analyzed subpopulations of 4 major cell types, revealing osteoblast and chondrocyte differentiation pathways and multiple signaling pathways [44]. Another study highlighted the role of injury‐related senescent cells in HO formation through single‐cell analysis [45]. Furthermore, a single‐cell integration analysis revealed that ectopically ossified cells share transcriptional characteristics with cells in the tendon fibrocartilaginous zone, identifying Xbp1 as a key transcriptional regulator potentially up‐regulating Notch signaling pathway in HO [46]. Recent evidence suggests that applying migrasome‐based scoring system can enhance our comprehension of diseases [32]. In this study, we utilized the migrasome score to identify MLin as a pivotal cell subpopulation involved in HO. Notably, MSCs, part of the MLin subpopulation, are also considered the main cell population involved in HO [47]. Subsequent analysis revealed that MLin exhibited a high abundance of DEGs. Up‐regulated genes including Col12a1, Col11a1, Tnc, C1qtnf3, Tagln, Acta2, Thbs4, Thbs1, Spp1, and Tnmd were predominantly associated with ECM organization, cytoskeletal remodeling, and osteogenic signaling. In contrast, down‐regulated genes, such as Clec3b, Gsn, Tnxb, Rarres2, Igfbp6, Plpp3, Tppp3, C3, Pi16, and Apod were implicated in immune regulation, ECM maintenance, and metabolic homeostasis. Furthermore, MLin with high migrasome scores demonstrated the most significant intercellular interactions, underscoring the potential importance of migrasome within HO cells.

  Utilizing the hdWGCNA methodology, we identified module genes within MLin and conducted gene network and functional analyses to elucidate their biological functions and associated signaling pathways. GO enrichment analysis revealed genes predominantly participated in glucose catabolic process, collagen fibril organization, glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate biosynthetic process and so on. Their products were mainly localized in myelin sheath, collagen‐containing ECM, actin filament bundle, and other cellular components. They principally performed molecular functions, such as actin filament binding, isomerase activity, actin binding and so on. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant associations with Glycolysis/Gluconeogenesis, Pyruvate metabolism, Carbon metabolism, HIF‐1 signaling pathway, among other pathways. These pathways may be crucial in the metabolic reprogramming during HO progression. Reportedly, under hypoxic conditions, HIF is activated, which subsequently regulates glycolysis and enhances cell survival, inflammation, angiogenesis, and vascular permeability within tissues. The stability of HIF under these conditions facilitates the recruitment of HO precursor cells, promotes the differentiation of MSCs, and modulates intricate signaling networks, including bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs), vascular endothelial growth factor, and neuropilin‐1, finally inducing HO formation [48]. Although the particular role of hdWGCNA module genes in HO requires further investigation, their participation in biological processes and potential interactions with signaling pathways make it a promising area of research for understanding and treating HO.

  As a computer‐based approach, ML utilizes predefined algorithms to execute tasks and discover solutions [49]. RF is an extensively utilized ML method, particularly in biomedical fields. Here, we applied a RF approach to identify feature genes from the intersection of MLin DEGs and hdWGCNA module genes. Through validation with clinical sample datasets of HO, Ppib and Tagln were identified as key genes associated with HO. So far, the contribution of Ppib and Tagln to HO has not been described. The Ppib gene encodes cyclosporine B (Cyclophilin B/CypB), an important peptidylprolyl isomerase that plays a crucial role in protein folding. The study has shown that PPIB mutations cause a delay in type I procollagen chain association, leading to osteogenesis imperfecta phenotypes [50]. The function of the PPIB gene in ossification may be realized through its roles in protein folding and collagen biosynthesis [51]. A recent research presented evidence that mice lacking CypB show early development of aging‐related characteristics, such as kyphosis, fat loss, weight reduction, and abnormalities in teeth, skin, and muscle [52]. Interestingly, HO, a new marker of aging [53], also underscores the importance of Ppib in our study. Although HO shares certain similarities with normal osteogenesis, it also presents distinct differences as a form of pathological osteogenesis. Consequently, the specific regulatory relationship and underlying pathological mechanisms between Ppib and HO warrant further investigation. Transgelin (Tagln) is an actin‐associated protein, that is involved in the dynamics of actin filaments, which are crucial for cellular structure and movement. Its expression and regulation have been linked to several pathological and physiological conditions. First, Tagln is considered an inducible gene of transforming growth factor β (TGF‐β), which can influence the osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation of human bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells by regulating the organization of the cytoskeleton [54]. Additionally, the low expression of the Tagln gene in osteosarcoma is associated with poorer overall survival, suggesting that Tagln may have inhibitory effects on the progression of bone tumors [55]. Angiogenesis is essential for providing the necessary blood supply to growing tissues, including bones. A study has shown that Tagln is expressed in endothelial cells and is involved in the elongation of these cells during angiogenesis, suggesting that Tagln may play a role in the vascularization required for ossification [56]. Notably, a recent report has highlighted the involvement of TAGLN in aortic valve calcification, a condition characterized by a pathological osteogenic process [57]. Likewise, our investigation underscored the importance of Tagln in abnormal osteogenesis. Thus, Tagln is likely to emerge as a promising therapeutic target for HO.

  In the dataset from clinical samples, the expression levels of Ppib and Tagln were found to be down‐regulated in mature HO tissues compared to controls. Similarly, in our murine HO models, both genes showed significant down‐regulation at 7 and 10 weeks post‐tenotomy, corresponding to the ossification maturation phase [42, 58]. In contrast, pseudotime analysis of single‐cell transcriptomes revealed increasing expression of Ppib and Tagln during cell differentiation. Notably, the single‐cell data were derived from tissues harvested within 3 weeks following HO modeling, representing early‐stage lesions. To verify this trend, we examined gene expression at 1 and 4 weeks post‐injury and observed a marked up‐regulation of both Ppib and Tagln, likely in response to early tissue injury and matrix remodeling. These results suggest a correlation between Ppib and Tagln expression and the progression of HO. The temporal expression patterns of both genes can be characterized as follows: their expression levels were elevated during the early phase of tissue injury and subsequently declined during the later stages, which involved the maturation of ossified tissues. Although Ppib and Tagln are implicated in the development of HO, their precise functions and underlying molecular mechanisms remain unverified experimentally, which represents a current limitation of this study. Future investigation is needed to elucidate the mechanistic contributions of these genes through in vitro functional assays, thereby providing deeper insights into their roles in HO progression.

  Previous studies have shown that the infiltration of immune cells plays an important role in the initiation and progression of HO. In the early stage, the inflammatory response is a crucial initiating factor. After trauma or surgery, signaling molecules released by damaged tissues recruit immune cells to the injury site, where they promote osteoblast activation and bone formation via cytokine secretion [59]. Among these immune components, monocytes and their maturation into macrophages are the main culprits in the formation of HO through the secretion of cytokines such as TGFβ1 [26]. Additionally, macrophages also facilitate the progression of HO by secreting tumor necrosis factor α (TNFα) and subsequently activating the mTOR signaling [60]. Research has revealed that neutrophils promote the activation of bone formation signaling pathways by releasing inflammatory factors and neutrophil extracellular traps, thereby accelerating the process of HO. And the overactivation of neutrophils can disrupt immune homeostasis, further triggering HO [61]. In addition, mast cells contribute to local inflammatory responses by secreting various inflammatory mediators, thereby facilitating the formation of HO, and inhibiting mast cell activation can significantly reduce the occurrence of HO [62]. The role of the adaptive immune system involving lymphocytes in HO is also noteworthy. Studies have indicated that activated B cells may be implicated in BMP4‐mediated HO [63]. In the absence of mature B‐ and T‐lymphocytes, MSCs have reduced osteogenic potential and the progression of HO is lessened [64]. Furthermore, the dysregulation of immune checkpoints on T and B cells may play a role in HO development [65]. Our study identified significant differences in the levels of M1 macrophages, mast cells, CD8 memory T cells, and CD8 naive T cells between the HO and control groups. Further investigation revealed that Ppib exhibited a positive correlation with M1/M2 macrophages and CD8 naive T cells, whereas demonstrating a negative correlation with mast cells and CD8 memory T cells. Although these immune cells are linked to HO, their exact contributions to the onset and development of the disease are not well comprehended. Dysregulated immune responses and alterations in the immune microenvironment associated with Ppib may play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of HO. In‐depth studies of the specific mechanisms of immune cells in HO may provide novel immunomodulatory strategies for its prevention and treatment.

  The involvement of miRNAs in HO pathogenesis has been previously explored [66]. In this study, a miRNA network comprising 56 mRNA–miRNA interaction pairs involving Tagln and Ppib was established, suggesting that both genes are subject to complex post‐transcriptional regulation. Further analysis of the regulatory gene networks revealed significant correlations between HO‐related genes (e.g., Spi1, Bmp2, Mmp9, Smad6, and Sox9) and key genes, indicating that Tagln and Ppib may participate in HO progression through the regulation of immune responses, osteogenesis, chondrogenesis, angiogenesis, and ECM remodeling. Furthermore, we constructed a TFs regulatory network within MLin, revealing that Jund, Creb3, Atf4, Jun, and Junb exhibited high specificity in cells characterized by high Ppib expression. Similarly, Creb3, Sox4, Jund, Atf4, and Jun demonstrated high specificity in cells with high Tagln expression. Significant gaps persist in the study of these TFs within the domain of HO, and our findings may provide some valuable inspirations for future research directions. In addition, using AUCell quantification, we observed that Ppib and Tagln were highly active in epithelial–mesenchymal transition and angiogenesis pathways, both of which have been proven to contribute to HO formation [67, 68, 69, 70]. Complementing single‐cell analysis, spatial transcriptomics provided spatial information to gene expression, enabling us to map the location of key cell types and gene expression patterns within injured tissues. Overall, these discoveries lay the groundwork for further exploration of the mechanisms behind the migrasome‐associated key biomolecular networks in HO.


## Conclusions
  The present study identified MLin as a crucial cell subtype in the progression of HO through migrasome score analysis in single‐cell transcriptomics. Subsequently, using hdWGCNA, RF algorithm, and dataset validation from clinical samples, Ppib and Tagln were pinpointed as key molecules involved in HO. The bulk transcriptome was integrated to conduct analyses of immune infiltration, miRNA networks, co‐expression networks, TF regulatory networks, and signaling pathways, thereby elucidating potential regulatory mechanisms underlying HO development. Additionally, spatial transcriptomics further uncovered the spatial patterns of cell subpopulation distribution and key gene expression. Finally, experimental validation demonstrated that Ppib and Tagln exhibit dynamic expression patterns correlated with HO progression. These findings bring new enlightenment and suggest promising directions for future clinical diagnosis and therapeutic strategies for HO.
