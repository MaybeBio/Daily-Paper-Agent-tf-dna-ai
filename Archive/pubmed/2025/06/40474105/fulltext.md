# Plasma cfDNA multi-omic biomarkers profiling for detection and stratification of gastric carcinoma


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-14409-0.


## Introduction
  GC ranks the fifth for incidence and the third for mortality worldwide [1], with most cases in East and Southeast Asia, and was associated with with Helicobacter pylori infection [2]. The poor prognosis of GC, with 5-year survival rates less than 30% [3, 4], can be partially attributed to delayed diagnosis, usually at advanced stages (eg. stage III or IV). Currently, the standardized means for GC screening, endoscopic surveillance accompanied by pathological examination of tissue biopsies, is limited to high-risk populations due to its invasive sampling and high cost, hence no clinically proven biomarkers for timely detection of GC is available yet [5]. Therefore, the development of biomarkers and methods for sensitive detection of GC would greatly improve the clinical outcomes of GC patients, especially when it can be achieved in a cost-efficient manner.

  Plasma cfDNA test has been widely applied in diagnosis and monitoring of multiple types of cancers [6], by exploring molecular characteristics of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), the cfDNA molecules released by tumor cells, as cancer biomarkers. Among these biomarkers, point mutations in cfDNA sequence are the common type and have been used for drug development and treatment selection [7]. However, sequence mutations have not been proven in early detection of cancer due to their extremely low abundance in early stages of carcinogenesis. Numerous studies have reported other types of ctDNA biomarkers in recent years, the sensitivity of which is enough for early detection of cancers. For example, cancer patients tend to have more ctDNA fragments shorter than normal cfDNA molecules [8]. Therefore, a left-shifted cfDNA fragment size distribution may indicate the presence of ctDNA-shedding tumors. End motifs of cfDNA molecules have been reported to carry information about their tissue of origin [9]. Consequently, the profile of plasma cfDNA end motifs reflect certain pathophysiologic states [10]. Furthermore, the CNV profiles inferred from cfDNA data also have been shown in concordance with those from tissue samples [11], and numerous studies from tumor tissue samples have found that CNV plays a crucial role in the formation of cancer [12, 13]. Abnormal CNV profiles in cfDNA may be contributed by gene copy number losses and gains in cancer cells. Despite these evidences for multi-omic cfDNA features as promising biomarkers to identify cancers, few studies have conducted assessments of these cfDNA features for early detection of GC [14]. Therefore, we extracted these multi-omic features into machine learning classifier to discriminate GC patients from non-cancer individuals, achieving highly accurate detection and stratification of GC in a non-invasive and cost-efficient manner. Additionally, transcription factors (TFs) are key regulatory elements for gene expression, and abnormal expression of TFs has been found in multiple types of cancers [15, 16]. Thus the expression of TFs can be used as potential biomarker for cancer risk prediction [17] and subtyping [18], which was also investigated in this study.


## Materials and methods

### Pipeline and participants
  The enrollment procedure has followed the Declaration of Helsinki and all protocols have been approved by the Ethics Committees of the Shenzhen Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital (No. (2018)58) and Shenzhen Guangming District People’s Hospital (No. LL-KT-2022053) to enroll benign gastric and GC patients at the time of diagnosis, and Peking University Shenzhen Hospital (No. 2021–075) to enroll healthy individuals.

  We enrolled totally 733 participants into three cohorts, including 521 healthy individuals, 81 patients with benign gastric diseases, and 131 GC patients. And we performed whole genome sequencing (WGS) to plasma cfDNA of all these participants. Then we extracted cfDNA fragment size, motif features, CNV, and TF from the WGS data and we used these features to build prediction models based on machine learning algorithm following training and validation to obtain the optimal result to discriminate different cohorts from each other (Fig. 1).Fig. 1General workflow of this study


### Plasma DNA extraction and whole-genome sequencing (WGS)
  We collected 6–10 mL peripheral blood from each of the 733 participants using tubes dedicated to preserve cfDNA (cat. 20,092,421, Xinle Medical Instrument Technology Inc., Shijiazhuang, Hebei, China) and shipped the tubes to the Molecular Biology Laboratory (Rapha Biotechnology Inc., Shenzhen, Guangdong, China) for cfDNA extraction within 72 h. The blood samples were centrifuged at 1,600 × g for 10 min and then the supernatant was transferred to a new tube and further centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 15 min to remove cell debris to finally obtain pure plasma. Then we extracted cfDNA from these plasma samples for every participant using the HiPure Circulating DNA Midi Spin Kit S (Magen Biotech Inc., Guangzhou, Guangdong, China). And we checked the cfDNA fragment size distribution using Qsep100 (Bio-optic. Inc., New Taipei City, Taiwan) and concentration using Qubit 4.0 (Thermo Fisher Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). Finally, cfDNA samples of all 733 participants have been confirmed free from genomic DNA contamination and were therefore used for subsequent analysis.

  Furtherly, we took 10 ng from cfDNA sample of each of the 733 participants for library construction using the RainbowOne Universal DNA Library Prep Kit for MGI (Rapha Biotechnology Inc., Shenzhen, Guangdong, China), amplified the libraries using VAHTS HiFi Amplification Mix (cat. N616-01, Vazyme Biotech Inc., Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) and purified the amplified libraries using VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (cat. N411-02, Vazyme Biotech Inc., Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). Then we checked the library quality following the same procedure for cfDNA QC as previously described and sent QC-passed libraries to be sequenced in batches of 16, each on a separate lane of an MGI-2000 sequencer at the Genome Center of BGI Genomics Inc. (Wuhan, Hubei, China) using DNBSEQ™ technology and PE100 sequencing mode. Finally, we obtained an sequencing depth of around 1.1× for each of the cfDNA samples, a typical low-depth WGS coverage for cancer detection [19].


### Multi-omic biomarkers profiling
  Firstly, we removed adapters and low quality reads from raw sequencing data using fastp [20] and mapped the qualified reads to the reference genome(GRCh39/UCSC hg38) using the sequence aligner BWA [21] with PCR duplicates marked by SAMtools [22]. Then we extracted fragment size of the read pair with a mapping quality score greater than 30 for either read, and we defined short fragments and long fragments as suggested by previous studies [23, 24]. To generate the fragmentation profile, we divide the mappable regions of human genome in 472 bins, each 5 Mb in size, and calculated the ratios of the short/long fragments of each bin for every sample as suggested by a reported study [25]. And we extracted end motif features by counting the proportion of 44 motif codes of the first four bases of the R1 end of each cfDNA fragment.

  Furthermore, the ichorCNA tool was used to profile copy number variations (CNV) as described by Wan et al. [26]. ichorCNA is dedicated for CNV profiling in assays based on low-depth WGS by using HMMcopy for coverage statistics followed by data calibration and normalization, which guarantees the high efficiency, accuracy and user-friendliness of ichorCNA. The reference genome was tiled into non-overlapping 0.5-Mb bins, totaling 4915 bins. The depth of each bin was used to compare against the software baseline and compute the log2 ratio. As a result, totally 5643 dimensions of features were used subsequently to train prediction models.


### Prediction models for GC detection
  The participants in each cohort were randomly assigned into a training set and test set at 7:3 ratio (Fig. 1). We employed machine learning algorithm XGBoost [27] to achieve in silico GC detection with high precision. To elucidate the contribution of each biomarker to building up the model, we performed the model training using each of the three biomarkers individually to obtain the three AUC values. And based on these AUC values, we determined the ‌feature weighting for each of the three biomarkers and ‌normalized the weights, with parameters set as 150 estimators, max depth 2, and learning rate 0.1 to train the model using the three biomarkers in combination. And to avoid the ‌overfitting, we employed ‌feature weighting when training the model with five-fold cross-validation (nfold = 5) and early_stopping _rounds parameter as 50. After obtaining the optimal model, we performed prediction on the separate test set using the optimal to calculate the performance of the model. Key statistic parameters including sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, true positive rate (TPR) and false positive rate (FPR) were calculated and used further to draw receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve yielding the value of area under the ROC curve(AUC), as described in previous studies [28, 29].


### GC localization
  The locations of GC were divided into three groups: proximal, middle, and distal. The proximal group included the cardia and fundus zones. The middle group included the stomach body regions. The distal group included the incisura angularis and pyloric antrum zones. The GC connects to the esophagus through the cardia and to the duodenum through the pylorus.


### Transcription factor analyses
  We downloaded RNAseq data of 740 healthy individuals and 460 TCGA Stomach Cancer (STAD) patients from the TCGA database. And we used edgeR (R package) to concatenate the count values of all samples into one file for differential gene expression analysis, through which we obtained the significantly differentially expressed genes (SDEGs), as the genes with a fold change greater than 2 and a q-value less than 0.05. Then we annotated these SDEGs using Animal TFDB, a transcription factor database, and interpreted their biological meaning using Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis.

  And to obtain detailed transcription factor binding site (TFBS) information, we utilized the Gene Transcription Regulation Database (GTRD). Considering there might be huge numbers of TFBSs for each TF, only TFs with more than 1000 defined sites in the GTRD were used for subsequent analysis. The position of each TF was recalculated by investigating the peak ChIP-seq signals in the GTRD database, and the top 1000 sites supported by most samples were extracted, namely 1000-msTFBS. Then we computed the coverage for the ± 1000 bp region of each TFBS and and standardized the coverage using copy number variation and average coverage. And we also showed the mean coverage of each nucleotide surrounding the TFBS. Finally, we identified the GC-specific TFs by comparing the TF coverage of cfDNA samples from the three different cohorts of this study.


## Result

### cfDNA fragmentation profiles and end motif features
  We performed WGS to 733 participants, obtaining an average sequencing depth of 1.1× and mapping rate of 91.61% (Supplementary Table S1). And based on this data, cfDNA fragmentation profile was extracted and visualized in 472 bins, each 5 Mb in size (Fig. 2). The X-axis represents 5 Mb bins across the human genome by chromosomes, while Y-axis represents the difference to the average ratio of short to long cfDNA fragments for each bin(fragment feature of the bin minus the average fragment feature of all 472 bins)

  We also extracted all the 256 end motifs of cfDNA fragments from the WGS data for all 733 participants (Supplementary Figure S1) and we observed discrepancies of end motif features between different cohorts in terms of means of the abundance value of participants in each cohorts for each motif. The 10 motifs with the largest abundance values were shown in Fig. 3. The motif feature values of patients with benign gastric diseases were the generally highest, and the feature values of healthy and GC cohorts are also different for these 10 motifs. ### Plasma cfDNA CNV of three cohorts
  By comparing CNV features across three cohorts (Fig. 4), we found that CNV values in the healthy and benign groups exhibited minimal fluctuations genome wide. In contrast, CNV values in the GC group showed remarkable changes across chromosomes. In the chromosomal regions 1p-7p, CNV profile showed localized gain and loss. And in the chromosomal regions 7q-16p, CNV profile exhibited an overall gain trend. Furtherly, in the chromosomal regions 16q-22q, CNV variations showed a higher proportion of loss. In summary, CNV can serve as a biomarker to discriminate GC patient from other groups.Fig. 4CNV profiles of cfDNA samples from different cohort. The abscissa represents 0.5 Mb-bins across the human genome, while the ordinate represents the CNV log2 ratio values in each bin


### Prediction model for GC detection using multi-omic biomarkers
  Using the feature values of the multi-omic biomarkers to build prediction models, we found the the powers after normalization are 0.305, 0.329 and 0.366 for cfDNA fragmentation profile, end motif and CNV respectively, meaning the contribution of the three biomarkers to build the prediction models are similar to each other. And the contribution from CNV is relative more than the rest two biomarkers, this might be because there are more feature values extracted from the genome wide CNV profiles than the feature values extracted from other two biomarkers. And to illustrate the contribution of each biomarker to the prediction model, we performed a SHAP-based evaluation (Fig. 5A).Fig. 5Contribution of individual biomarkers to the prediction model and the performance of the prediction models. A. The top 20 biomarkers in terms of contribution to the prediction model; B. Green curve represents the prediction model to discriminate GC patients from healthy individuals. Blue curve represents the prediction model to discriminate GC patients from none cancer individuals. Yellow curve represents the prediction model to discriminate GC patients from benign gastric diseases

  We employed all 733 samples and combined healthy individuals and patients with benign GC to be none cancer group (Fig. 5B). The AUC of the prediction model for healthy vs. GC is 0.998, with a sensitivity of 94.87% and a specificity of 99.35%. The AUC of the prediction model for none cancer vs. GC is 0.960, with a sensitivity of 71.79% and a specificity of 97.79%. The AUC of the prediction model for Benign vs. GC is 0.891, with a sensitivity of 91.66% and a specificity of 83.33%.

  Overall, these results suggests that cfDNA multi-omics biomarkers profiling may potentially be a noninvasive assay for the detection and stratification of GC.


### Discrimination between benign and malignant gastric diseases
  To validate the accuracy and robustness of the model, we conducted clinically challenging discrimination between benign and malignant diseases, including gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GIST) and cardiac carcinoma for malignancies [30, 31], and colorectal polyps, gastrointestinal polyps, esophagitis, and gastroduodenal ulcers for benign diseases. Using the none cancer vs. GC model, all benign diseases (19/19) and malignancies (22/22) were correctly identified (Fig. 6). The results proved the high precision of our model, regardless of the location of GC.Fig. 6Prediction scores for benign diseases vs. GC groups using multi-omic biomarkers. The X-axis represents different groups, while Y-axis represents prediction scores of the model


### Prediction scores of different stages of gastric cancer patients
  As commonly observed in clinic, early-stage GC patients generally respond better to surgical treatment compared to those with GC at advanced stages. Therefore, early detection and treatment are crucial. According to pathology records, we managed to collect staging information for 52 out of the totally 131 GC patients recruited in this study. And these 52 GC patients were categorized into three groups, including 16 patients with stage I and stage II GC as the early-stage group, 16 patients with stage III GC as the middle-stage group, and 20 patients with stage IV GC as the late-stage group (Supplementary Table S1). The median prediction scores were 98.72, 99.01 and 99.34 for the early-stage group, middle-stage group, and late-stage group respectively (Fig. 7A), which roughly shows a scenario that prediction scores increases as the pathological stage advances especially after removing outliers (Fig. 7B). To some extent, such scenario suggests a possibility of our prediction scoring model to be a potential indicator for staging of GC.Fig. 7Prediction scores of GC patients by pathological staging. A The X-axis represents different stage, while Y-axis represents model prediction scores. B Prediction scores of GC staging groups after removing outliers


### GC-associated transcription factors
  We performed gene expression differential analysis on the two TCGA datasets (Fig. 8A). Totally, the expression of 2673 genes were found to be significantly different between healthy individuals and GC patients, with 899 gene up-regulated and 1774 down-regulated(GC patients vs. healthy individuals). Then through enrichment analysis via Gene Ontology (GO), TFs genes were identified from the aforementioned differentially expressed genes to be involved in carcinogenic signalling pathways, including pathways related to transcription regulation, cell fate decision and regulation of oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes (Fig. 8B).Fig. 8GC Candidate TFs Analysis. A Genes showing significant differences between the healthy individuals and STAD patients. B Enrichment analysis of gene ontology for differential TFs between healthy individuals and STAD patients. C The read depth of GC-associated TFBSs in the healthy cohort (curves in green), gastric benign disease cohort (curves in yellow) and GC cohort (curves in red)

  Furtherly, we analyzed the coverage of the TFBSs of these TFs and found that the depth of TFBSs in the gastric benign diseases group is significant greater than that of the healthy individual group and the depth further increased in those of the GC patients group (Fig. 8C). Lastly, we selected TFs with significant inter-group differences in coverage of TFBS to be GC-specific TFs (Supplementary Table S2). Previous studies have reported that the aberrant expression of some of these GC-specific TFs are associated with the occurrence and development of GC, including CTCFL, EGR1, EGR2,ETV4,FOXM1 and KLF15 [32–37]. However, our study identified previously unreported GC-specific TFs, including FEZF, TBX5, and WT1. This result proved the potential of TFs to be an indicator for gastric carcinogenesis.


## Discussion
  Numerous means for GC screening have been utilized, eg. fiberoptic gastroscopy, X-ray barium meal examination, abdominal ultrasound [38, 39], etc., yet the sensitivities of which are generally insufficient for early detection of GC. Besides, the high cost of these means has prevented them to be widely applied, especially in the developing world where the incidence of GC is high, eg. Asia. Plasma cfDNA has been proven by many studies to be biomarker for cancer diagnosis and prognosis [40], but its potential to be used in early detection of GC has not yet been appreciated.

  As a proof of concept, we recruited 733 participants, including healthy individuals, benign gastric patients and GC patients, for their plasma cfDNA to obtain WGS data, from which we were able to extract multi-omic features. And based on these features and machine learning algorithm, in silico classifiers, namely prediction models for GC detection and stratification, achieved a sensitivity of 94.87% and a specificity of 99.35% to discriminate healthy people from GC patients. Meanwhile, all cfDNA features in this study were analyzed from low depth WGS (approximately 1.1x), which is quite cost- and labor- saving compared to Fiberoptic gastroscopy, X-ray barium meal examination and methylation based test. Besides, compared to Fiberoptic gastroscopy, X-ray barium meal examination, abdominal ultrasound, the sampling means of our method is blood draw and is much easier to handle. Such unprecedentedly high accuracy, low cost and convenience of sampling suggest potential of our method to be implemented in GC screening in large populations.

  And to imitate the real world setting of cancer screening in asymptomatic population, we combined the healthy people and the patients with gastric benign patients together to form a none cancer group for building prediction model to discriminate of all these non-cancer participants from GC patients. The AUC of the prediction model for Non-cancer vs. GC was 0.960. And furtherly, regardless of cancer location, our prediction model can achieve discrimination between benign and malignant patients’ data at 100% precision.

  Furthermore, we compared prediction scoring on early-, middle-, and late-stage GC patients and found the mean prediction scores increased with the advancement of pathological staging, which potentially serves a preliminary proof-of-principle of our method for the staging of GC. However, this scenario has not yet been supported in terms of statistical significance due to the insufficient number of patients with precise staging information, merely 52 of the three groups in total, which is too few for a descent statistical analysis. This is a limitation of this study and thus further studies with larger number of GC patients assigned with staging information and rigid statistical analysis are necessary to investigate the feasibility of our method for the staging and stratification of GC.

  Another limitation of this study is too few early-stage GC patients were assigned, only one at stage I and 15 at stage II, making it unrealistic to test the performance of our method on early detection of GC with statistical analysis. And this problem should be solved in future studies with more early-stage GC patients recruited.

  Studies have revealed that the cfDNA fragment coverage is associated with epigenetic regulations in tissues [41], and the altered cfDNA fragment coverage patterns at transcription start sites, where TFBS usually locates, may reflect aberrant gene expression in cancers [42]. Additionally, the identification of tissue-specific TFs allows for cancer typing [18]. In this study, we investigated the coverage of TFBS using WGS data of cfDNA samples of participants recruited. And we also extracted GC-related RNA-seq data from the TCGA database. Through comparison between these two datasets, series of TFs have been identified to be associated with carcinogenesis of GC. However, more GC-specific TFs are needed for cancer type specific identification of GC, which could be discovered in further clinical studies with larger sampling size and validated in prospective cohort study.

  Furthermore, this is only a retrospective study, thus the performance and generaliz-ability of our method should be further validated through perspective and large population follow-up studies. And the combination of multiple biomarkers used can also be comprehensively studied to see if more molecular features could be included to improve the overall performance of the method. Lastly, there are intrinsic features for every software or modelling algorithm, thus employing different tools of analysis may return different result. For example, CNV profile is an important feature to build the prediction model in this study, but we might obtain different profiles of CNV if using another CNV detection tool, which is also a limitation of study. Therefore, different CNV detection tools should be employed and compared in terms of contribution to GC detection in future studies.

  Generally, we developed a minimum-invasive method for highly accurate detection of gastric carcinoma using multi-omic biomarkers of plasma cfDNA, including cfDNA fragmentation profile, molecular end motifs and genome-wide CNV pattern, which were all extracted from simply low depth WGS data and were aided by machine learning based in-silico classifier. This method demonstrates excellent and stable performance, validated across different cancer locations, benign vs. malignant discrimination, and pathological stages. Its ultra-high sensitivity as well as specificity show potential for GC screening in large-scale population. Therefore, we offer this retrospective study to prove a convenient, accurate, and affordable means for early detection and stratification of gastric carcinoma.
