# Ginsenoside Rg1 alleviates PCPA-induced insomnia by inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome activation and pyroptosis through the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway in mice


## Objectives
  This study aims to investigate the neuroprotective effects of Ginsenoside Rg1 in alleviating P-chlorophenylalanine (PCPA)-induced insomnia and explore its underlying mechanisms involving the inhibition of NOD-Like Receptor Family Pyrin Domain Containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation and pyroptosis through the Nuclear Factor Erythroid 2-Related Factor 2 (Nrf2)/Heme Oxygenase-1 (HO-1) pathway in mice.


## Methods
  Sprague-Dawley rats were randomly divided into five groups: control, sleep deprivation (SD, PCPA-induced insomnia), and three treatment groups receiving different doses of Ginsenoside Rg1 (low, medium, and high). Behavioral assessments included the Pentobarbital Sodium-Induced Sleep Test (PIST), Sucrose Preference Test (SPT), and Morris Water Maze (MWM). Histopathological and immunofluorescence evaluations of hippocampal tissues were performed. Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) was used to measure neurotransmitter levels (5-Hydroxytryptamine [5-HT], 5-Hydroxytryptophan [5-HTP], Gamma-aminobutyric acid [GABA], glutamate [GLU]) and pro-inflammatory cytokines (Tumor Necrosis Factor Alpha [TNF-α], Interleukin-6 [IL-6], Interleukin-1 beta [IL-1β], Interleukin-8 [IL-8]). In vitro, corticosterone-induced neurotoxicity in HT22 hippocampal cells was assessed, and the role of the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway was examined through molecular docking, gene silencing, and Western blot.


## Results
  Ginsenoside Rg1 treatment significantly improved PCPA-induced insomnia symptoms in a dose-dependent manner, as evidenced by reduced sleep latency, increased sleep duration, restored sucrose preference, and improved spatial memory. Histopathological analysis revealed that Ginsenoside Rg1 mitigated neuronal damage and astrocytic activation. Neurotransmitter imbalances were corrected, and inflammation was alleviated, as reflected by reductions in pro-inflammatory cytokines and increased interleukin-10 (IL-10) levels. Mechanistically, Ginsenoside Rg1 inhibited NLRP3 inflammasome activation, pyroptosis, and reduced IL-1β and IL-8 levels in both in vivo and in vitro models. The activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway was further confirmed by molecular docking, immunofluorescence, and Western blot, demonstrating that Nrf2 activation was critical for the anti-inflammatory effects of Ginsenoside Rg1.


## Conclusion
  Ginsenoside Rg1 effectively alleviates PCPA-induced insomnia by inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome activation and pyroptosis, with its neuroprotective effects mediated through the activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway. These findings suggest Ginsenoside Rg1 as a potential therapeutic agent for insomnia and related neuroinflammatory conditions.


## Background
  As one of the most fundamental physiological activities, sleep plays an indispensable role in various bodily functions, including immune regulation, cognitive processing, and memory consolidation (Maheshwari and Shaukat 2019; Salari et al. 2024; Abel et al. 2023). With the accelerating pace of modern life and the increasing pressure of social competition, a growing number of individuals sacrifice valuable sleep time in pursuit of academic or professional ambitions. These choices often lead to chronic sleep deprivation (SD), which triggers a cascade of detrimental health effects, most notably cognitive impairment (Kong et al. 2023). The P-chlorophenylalanine (PCPA)-induced insomnia model in rats is widely used to study the pathophysiology of insomnia and screen potential therapeutic agents. PCPA is a selective serotonin (5-HT) synthesis inhibitor that depletes serotonin levels, resulting in sleep disturbances that mimic some characteristics of human insomnia (Hu et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2024a, b). This model has been extensively used to evaluate the effects of various compounds on sleep regulation and neuroinflammation (Wang et al. 2020, 2024a, b; Si et al. 2020). PCPA-induced insomnia in rats is associated with alterations in neurotransmitter systems, such as reduced serotonin and Gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) levels, as well as increased pro-inflammatory cytokines, which are thought to play a role in sleep disruption (Murray et al. 2015; Feng et al. 2024).

  In addition to its impact on sleep, insomnia has been shown to significantly affect cognitive function and mental health. Chronic sleep disturbances can lead to cognitive impairments, such as difficulties with memory, attention, and executive function ((Palmer et al. 2018; Olaithe et al. 2018). These cognitive deficits are thought to result from alterations in neural plasticity and hippocampal function, areas critical for learning and memory ((Weerasinghe-Mudiyanselage and Moon 2024). Moreover, insomnia is strongly linked to psychiatric disorders, particularly depression and anxiety, with a bidirectional relationship between sleep disturbances and mental health conditions (Gebara et al. 2018; Alvaro et al. 2013). Insomnia exacerbates symptoms of depression and anxiety, while these psychiatric conditions, in turn, increase the risk of developing persistent sleep disturbances ((Riemann et al. 2022). Thus, addressing insomnia not only has the potential to improve sleep but also to alleviate associated cognitive and psychological issues.

  The hippocampus, a critical brain region involved in learning and memory, is essential for maintaining the circadian rhythm of sleep and wakefulness ((Eckel-Mahan 2012; Albrecht 2011). When exposed to inflammatory stimuli, hippocampal neurons may gradually undergo pyroptotic damage, negatively impacting cognitive function ((Han et al. 2023). Studies have demonstrated that chronic sleep deprivation disrupts hippocampal structural integrity and alters neurotransmitter levels, impairing neuroplasticity and subsequently hindering learning and memory (Gaine et al. 2021; Wu and Zhang 2023; Li et al. 2021). Furthermore, SD has been shown to activate the NOD-Like Receptor Family Pyrin Domain Containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, leading to pyroptosis in hippocampal neurons and exacerbating memory deficits (Fan et al. 2021; Amini et al. 2022). Currently, the primary therapeutic approach to address SD-related insomnia involves the use of hypnotic drugs ((Pagel et al. 2018). The most commonly prescribed sleep medications include benzodiazepines and non-benzodiazepines, which are synthesized through chemical processes. However, the use of these drugs is associated with several drawbacks, including compromised safety, addiction, dependence, and a limited therapeutic range ((Blanco et al. 2020).

  Currently, the primary treatments for insomnia include pharmacological interventions, such as benzodiazepines and non-benzodiazepine hypnotics (e.g., eszopiclone, zolpidem) (Madari et al. 2021; Edinoff et al. 2021a, b), and psychological therapies, such as cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) (Hertenstein et al. 2022). Although effective, pharmacological treatments are often associated with adverse effects, including tolerance, dependence, residual daytime sedation, and cognitive impairment (Madari et al. 2021; Edinoff et al. 2021a, b). Psychological therapies like CBT-I, while safer, require long treatment periods, are resource-intensive, and exhibit variable patient compliance (Wiklund et al. 2022). Consequently, there is an urgent need for safer, more accessible, and effective alternatives. Natural compounds with neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory properties are increasingly being explored as promising therapeutic candidates. Ginsenoside Rg1, one of the most pharmacologically active components of ginseng, has garnered attention for its low toxicity, minimal side effects, and neuroprotective properties (Sun et al. 2022). Rg1 exerts neuroprotective effects through multiple mechanisms, including enhancing nerve growth factor (NGF), anti-inflammatory and antioxidant actions, anti-apoptosis, inhibiting excitotoxicity, preventing excessive calcium influx into neurons, maintaining cellular ATP levels, and preserving neuronal structural integrity (Manju 2024; Huang et al. 2019). These properties make it a promising candidate for the treatment of various neurological disorders, including Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) (Wu et al. 2022), Parkinson’s Disease (PD) (He et al. 2021), Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS) (Huang et al. 2023), Multiple Sclerosis (MS) (Liu et al. 2022), as well as cognitive and memory impairments (Zhang et al. 2023a, b). Compared to conventional hypnotics, Ginsenoside Rg1 offers potential advantages such as low toxicity, reduced risk of dependence, and broader modulation of underlying pathological mechanisms, including oxidative stress and neuroinflammation. Therefore, exploring the efficacy of Ginsenoside Rg1 in the treatment of insomnia could provide a novel therapeutic strategy that not only improves sleep quality but also addresses cognitive decline and neuroinflammation associated with chronic sleep disturbances.

  Given this background, our study aims to investigate the neuroprotective effects of Ginsenoside Rg1 in alleviating PCPA-induced insomnia in rats. Specifically, we focus on the inhibition of NLRP3 inflammasome activation and pyroptosis through the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway, thereby mitigating hippocampal neuronal damage. PCPA, a classic 5-HT inhibitor, was used to induce insomnia in the animal model. Both in vivo and in vitro approaches were employed to elucidate the potential therapeutic effects of Rg1.


## Methods

### Animal modeling and grouping
  Sprague-Dawley rats (180–220 g, male) were obtained from the Animal Center of Fujian Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12-hour light/dark cycle, controlled temperature (22 ± 2 °C), and humidity (50 ± 10%). All procedures were approved by the Fujian Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (FJATCM-IAEC2017017). Ginsenoside Rg1 was purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (CAS No.: 22427-39-0). p-Chlorophenylalanine (PCPA) was obtained from HARVEYBIO (Catalog No.: AA1051). Eszopiclone was supplied by Shanghai Xiari Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd. (Catalog No.: TW00055). Rats were randomly assigned to five groups (n = 8 per group): (1) Control group, receiving saline intraperitoneally and orally; (2) SD group, receiving PCPA (500 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) once daily for three consecutive days; (3) SD + Low-dose Ginsenoside Rg1 treatment (SD + Low-Rg1) group, receiving Ginsenoside Rg1 (25 mg/kg, orally) once daily for seven days; (4) SD + Medium-dose Ginsenoside Rg1 treatment (SD + High-Rg1) group, receiving Ginsenoside Rg1 (20 mg/kg, orally) once daily for seven days; (5) SD + eszopiclone treatment group, receiving eszopiclone once daily for seven days as positive control. All rats were sacrificed 24 h after the final treatment, and hippocampal tissues were collected for further analysis. Detailed experimental design was depicted in Fig. 1B.

### Behavioral experiment
  Behavioral assessments were conducted to evaluate the effects of Ginsenoside Rg1 on PCPA-induced insomnia in Sprague-Dawley rats. All behavioral tests were conducted in a controlled environment 24 h after the final Ginsenoside Rg1 administration.


#### Pentobarbital sodium-induced sleep test (PIST)
  This test was used to evaluate sleep latency and total sleep duration. Rats were administered pentobarbital sodium (40 mg/kg, intraperitoneally), and the time to fall asleep (sleep latency) and total sleep duration were recorded. Sleep onset was defined as the loss of the righting reflex for at least 1 min. The test was performed 24 h after the final Ginsenoside Rg1 administration.


#### Sucrose preference test (SPT)
  The SPT was used to measure anhedonia, a core symptom of insomnia-related depressive behavior. Rats were given access to two bottles, one containing a 1% sucrose solution and the other water, for 48 h prior to the test. After 24 h of water deprivation, the rats were allowed free access to the two bottles for 12 h. Sucrose preference was calculated as the percentage of sucrose solution consumed relative to the total fluid intake.


#### Morris water maze (MWM)
  The MWM was conducted to assess spatial learning and memory. Rats were placed in a circular pool filled with opaque water and trained to find a hidden platform submerged just below the water surface. Each rat underwent four trials per day for five consecutive days. The time taken to find the platform (escape latency) was recorded. After the training period, a probe trial was conducted in which the platform was removed, and the time spent in the target quadrant was recorded to assess memory retention.


### Histopathological evaluation
  Histopathological analysis of hippocampal tissue was conducted to evaluate the neuroprotective effects of Ginsenoside Rg1 in PCPA-induced insomnia. After sacrifice, hippocampal tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm. H&E staining was performed to assess histological structure and neuronal pathology. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Neuronal morphology, integrity, and signs of inflammation or damage were examined under a light microscope. Nissl staining was used to evaluate neuronal viability and morphology by detecting Nissl bodies, markers of protein synthesis. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with 0.1% cresyl violet solution. Neuronal health, morphology, and the presence of Nissl substance were analyzed, and cell counts were performed in the hippocampal subregions.


### Cell culture and grouping
  HT22 hippocampal neuronal cells were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C and 5% CO₂. To examine the neuroprotective effects of Ginsenoside Rg1 on corticosterone-induced neurotoxicity, cells were divided into 4 groups: (1) Control group, receiving no treatment; (2) CORT group, treated with corticosterone (200 µM) for 24 h; (3) CORT + Rg1 treatment group, pretreated with Ginsenoside Rg1 (20 µM) for 24 h, then treated with corticosterone (200 µM); (4) Rg1 treatment group, treated with Ginsenoside Rg1 (20 µM) for 24 h without corticosterone.

  To further assess the role of the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway, cells were divided into 5 groups: (1) Control group; (2) CORT group, treated with corticosterone (200 µM); (3) CORT + Rg1 treatment group; (4) CORT + Rg1 treatment + si-Nrf2 group, transfected with si-Nrf2 to knock down Nrf2, then treated with Rg1 and corticosterone. After the respective treatments, cells were harvested for subsequent experiments.


### Immunofluorescence staining
  Immunofluorescence staining was conducted to assess the expression of GFAP, NLRP3, caspase-1, and Nrf2 in hippocampal tissue and HT22 hippocampal neuronal cells. After euthanasia, tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h and cells for 20 min. Tissues were embedded in paraffin and sectioned at 5 μm, while cells were cultured on coverslips. Both tissues and cells were rehydrated and underwent antigen retrieval in sodium citrate buffer (10 mM, pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 15 min. After cooling, samples were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 and blocked with 5% BSA for one hour. Primary antibodies (1:200) against GFAP (Abcam, ab7260), NLRP3 (Abcam, ab270449), caspase-1 (Abcam, ab62698), and Nrf2 ((Abcam, ab270449)) were applied overnight at 4°C. After PBS washes, Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (Abcam, ab150077, 1:500) were applied for one hour in the dark. Nuclei were stained with DAPI, and slides and coverslips were mounted with anti-fade medium. Fluorescence images were captured at 400x magnification, and ImageJ was used to quantify fluorescence intensity.


### ELISA
  Hippocampal tissues were immersed in PBS, homogenized, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min. The supernatants obtained from this process were subsequently used for further analysis. Concentrations of 5-HT (Paisenon Biotech, PR-5-HT-001), 5-HTP (Paisenon Biotech, PR-5-HTP-002), GABA (Paisenon Biotech, PR-GABA-003), GLU (Paisenon Biotech, PR-GLU-004), TNF-α (Paisenon Biotech, PR-TNFα−005), IL-6 (Paisenon Biotech, PR-IL6-006), IL-10 (Paisenon Biotech, PR-IL10-007), IL-1β (Paisenon Biotech, PR-IL1β−008), and IL-8 (Paisenon Biotech, PR-IL8-009) in hippocampal samples were quantified using specific ELISA kits, following the manufacturer’s instructions closely. For cell experiments, supernatants from treated HT22 cells were collected, centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, and analyzed concentrations using ELISA kits. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm, and concentrations were calculated based on standard curves provided in the kits.


### Western blot
  Western blot was used to assess proteins related to NLRP3 inflammasome and pyroptosis in hippocampal neurons. Tissues and HT22 cells from each group were homogenized in RIPA buffer (Beyotime) with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal protein amounts (20–40 µg) were separated on 10% SDS-PAGE gels, transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore), and blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against NLRP3 (Abcam, ab270449, 1:1000), Pro-caspase-1 (Abcam, ab179515), Caspase-1 (Abcam, ab62698, 1:1000), ASC (Abcam, ab309497, 1:1000), IL-1β (Abcam, ab283818, 1:1000), and GSDMD (Abcam, ab219800, 1:1000). After washing, membranes were treated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology, 18366, 1:5000) for 1 h. Proteins were detected using ECL (Bio-Rad) and visualized with a ChemiDoc™ MP Imaging System. Densitometry analysis was performed using ImageJ, and protein levels were normalized to β-actin.


### Flow cytometry
  Apoptosis in HT22 hippocampal neurons was assessed using Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) staining, following BD Biosciences’ protocol. HT22 cells (2 × 10⁵ per well) were seeded in 6-well plates and incubated for 48 h. After treatment, cells were collected, washed with cold PBS, and resuspended in 1× binding buffer at 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL. A 100 µL aliquot was stained with 5 µL Annexin V-FITC and 5 µL PI for 15 min in the dark. After adding 400 µL binding buffer, samples were analyzed by BD FACSCanto™ II flow cytometer to determine early and late apoptosis percentages.


### Cell transfection
  To suppress Nrf2 expression, a targeted approach was employed using specific small interfering RNA (siRNA) designed for Nrf2 (si-Nrf2), which was introduced into cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) following the protocol provided by the manufacturer. HT22 hippocampal neuronal cells underwent transfection with 100 nM si-Nrf2, and this process lasted for 48 h prior to any subsequent experimental procedures.


### EdU incorporation assay
  The 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) incorporation assay was utilized to assess cell proliferation in hippocampal neurons treated with corticosterone (CORT) to model neurotoxic stress, both with and without Ginsenoside Rg1 intervention. Control and Rg1-only groups were also included. Neurons were incubated with 10 µM EdU for 24 h, followed by fixation and fluorescent labeling of EdU-incorporated nuclei. DAPI staining was used to mark all nuclei. Fluorescence microscopy and image analysis quantified the percentage of EdU-positive cells.


### Molecular docking
  Molecular docking studies assessed the interaction between Ginsenoside Rg1 and Nrf2. Structural models for Rg1 and Nrf2 were sourced from PubChem and the Protein Data Bank (PDB), respectively. Rg1’s structure was optimized using Chem3D, and Nrf2’s crystal structure was prepared by adding hydrogen atoms and removing water molecules using AutoDock Tools. Docking simulations, conducted with AutoDock Vina, explored the binding modes and affinity of Rg1 to Nrf2’s active site, identified based on its interaction with Keap1. The docking grid centered around this site was set to match Rg1’s molecular dimensions. Multiple runs ensured the reproducibility of binding conformations, selecting poses based on lowest binding energy and optimal interactions, including hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions with key amino acids at the active site. Docking results were visualized and analyzed with PyMOL and AutoDock Tools, revealing interactions that could stabilize Nrf2 in a conformation conducive to nuclear translocation and increased transcriptional activity.


### Statistical analysis
  In this study, data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 23.0. Comparisons between multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). When a significant difference was detected, post-hoc comparisons were carried out using the Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference (HSD) test to evaluate pairwise differences between groups. The Tukey test was chosen for its ability to control the family-wise error rate when performing multiple comparisons. For data visualization, bar charts were generated using GraphPad Prism version 9.4.0 to ensure both accuracy and consistency. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.


## Results

### Ginsenoside Rg1 mitigates behavioral and physiological disturbances in PCPA-Induced insomnia mice
  The study first evaluated the dose-dependent effects of Ginsenoside Rg1 on alleviating PCPA-induced insomnia and its associated physiological and behavioral disturbances, using assessments of body weight, the PIST, SPT, and the MWM test. The molecular structure of Rg1 is presented in Fig. 1A. In terms of body weight, the control group maintained the highest weight, while the SD group showed significant weight loss (P < 0.05), indicating the detrimental impact of insomnia (Fig. 1C). Treatment with Ginsenoside Rg1 resulted in a gradual improvement in body weight, with the high-dose group showing the most substantial recovery (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1C). In the PIST, the control group exhibited the shortest sleep latency and the longest total sleep time, whereas the SD group had significantly prolonged sleep latency and reduced sleep duration (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1D). Ginsenoside Rg1 administration effectively shortened sleep latency and extended total sleep time in a dose-dependent manner, with the high-dose group approaching control levels (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1D). The SPT revealed that the SD group displayed markedly reduced sucrose consumption (P < 0.05), indicative of depressive-like behavior, which was progressively alleviated with increasing doses of Ginsenoside Rg1, with the high-dose group showing the greatest improvement (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1E). Finally, the MWM test showed that the SD group had the least frequency of crossing the platform, shortest detention time in target quadrant, lowest target quadrant distance/toal distance ratio and average speed traveled to locate the platform (P < 0.05), reflecting cognitive impairment (Fig. 1F). Ginsenoside Rg1 treatment improved spatial memory and learning ability in a dose-dependent manner, with the high-dose group showing a performance similar to the control group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1F and G). These findings suggest that Ginsenoside Rg1 significantly alleviates PCPA-induced insomnia and related comorbidities, with its efficacy increasing in a dose-dependent manner.


### Ginsenoside Rg1 mitigates hippocampal damage, neurotransmitter imbalance, and inflammation in PCPA-Induced insomnia mice
  Building on the behavioral observations from the previous section, the study further investigates the neuroprotective effects of Ginsenoside Rg1 on PCPA-induced hippocampal damage and inflammation. The histopathological evaluation of hippocampus revealed that PCPA-induced insomnia is associated with notable neuronal damage and astrocytic activation, as evidenced by H&E and Nissl staining, and increased GFAP expression, respectively (Fig. 2A and B). The SD group exhibited significantly compromised neuron integrity and enhanced astrocytic hypertrophy (Fig. 2A and B). In contrast, Ginsenoside Rg1 treatment ameliorated these pathological changes in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2A and B). Notably, the high-dose Ginsenoside Rg1 group approached the normal histological architecture observed in the control group, with substantially reduced GFAP expression, indicating alleviation of astrocytic activation (Fig. 2A-C). Neurotransmitter assessments via ELISA showed that the SD group had disturbed levels of 5-HT, 5-HTP, GABA, and GLU, pointing to a neurotransmitter imbalance favoring excitotoxic conditions (Fig. 2D). Treatment with Ginsenoside Rg1 progressively restored these neurotransmitters toward equilibrium, particularly at higher doses, where 5-HT and GABA levels were nearly equivalent to those in the control group (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, inflammatory profiling via ELISA demonstrated elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6) and reduced anti-inflammatory IL-10 in the SD group, reflecting a state of heightened inflammation (Fig. 2E). Ginsenoside Rg1 administration not only decreased TNF-α and IL-6 levels but also enhanced IL-10 levels, with the most pronounced improvements observed in the high-dose group (Fig. 2E). These results underscore Ginsenoside Rg1’s neuroprotective effects through its capacity to mitigate astrocytic activation, rebalance neurotransmitters, and modulate inflammatory responses in hippocampal tissues of rats with PCPA-induced insomnia.

### Ginsenoside Rg1 inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome activation, pyroptosis, and pro-inflammatory cytokine expression in hippocampal neurons of PCPA-induced insomnia mice
  Following the assessment of hippocampal damage and inflammatory markers, the study further explores the underlying mechanisms of inflammation and pyroptosis in PCPA-induced insomnia. In terms of immunofluorescence staining, the SD group showed significantly elevated NLRP3 and caspase-1 expression in the neurons of hippocampus, indicating heightened inflammasome activation and pyroptosis, while the control group had the lowest expression levels, reflecting minimal inflammatory activity (Fig. 3A and B). The low-dose Ginsenoside Rg1 group exhibited a partial reduction in NLRP3 and caspase-1 levels, whereas the medium- and high-dose groups displayed progressively greater inhibition, with the high-dose group approaching control levels (Fig. 3A and B). Similarly, ELISA results revealed significantly increased IL-1β and IL-8 expression in the hippocampal neurons of SD group, consistent with a strong inflammatory response (Fig. 3C). Ginsenoside Rg1 treatment produced a dose-dependent reduction in both cytokines, with the high-dose group showing the most pronounced decrease, approaching levels observed in the control group (Fig. 3C). These findings highlight the ability of Ginsenoside Rg1 to effectively suppress NLRP3 inflammasome activation, pyroptosis, and the associated pro-inflammatory cytokine response in a dose-dependent manner in PCPA-induced insomnia mice.

### Ginsenoside Rg1 inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome activation, pyroptosis, and pro-inflammatory cytokine expression in corticosterone-induced neurotoxicity of hippocampal neurons
  Building on the in vivo findings of Ginsenoside Rg1’s anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects, subsequent in vitro experiments examined its ability to inhibit pyroptosis and inflammasome activation in hippocampal neurons. The CCK8 assay was used to assess drug toxicity at concentrations of 0, 10, 20, 40, 80, and 100 µM over 24 and 48 h (Fig. 4A). The results showed no significant cytotoxicity up to 80 µM, supporting the safety of Ginsenoside Rg1 (Fig. 4A). Corticosterone (200 mM) induced neurotoxicity, decreasing cell proliferation and increasing pyroptosis in the CORT group, while Ginsenoside Rg1 treatment increased proliferation and reduced pyroptosis in the CORT + Rg1 group, approaching control levels (Fig. 4B and C). Immunofluorescence revealed increased NLRP3 and caspase-1 expression in the CORT group, which were significantly reduced by Ginsenoside Rg1 (Fig. 4D). ELISA showed elevated IL-1β and IL-8 levels in the CORT group, which decreased with Ginsenoside Rg1 treatment (Fig. 4E). Western blot analysis further corroborated these findings. In the CORT group, there was an upregulation of NLRP3, pro-caspase-1, active caspase-1, ASC, pro-IL-1β, mature IL-1β, and GSDMD, indicating inflammasome activation and pyroptosis (Fig. 4F). Ginsenoside Rg1 treatment significantly reduced the expression of these markers, particularly in the Ginsenoside CORT + Rg1 group, where protein levels were comparable to those in the control group (Fig. 4F). These findings demonstrate that Ginsenoside Rg1 effectively inhibits pyroptosis and inflammasome activation, offering neuroprotection against corticosterone-induced neurotoxicity.

### Ginsenoside Rg1 activates the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway to protect against corticosterone-induced neurotoxicity in hippocampal neurons
  The study further demonstrated the capability of Ginsenoside Rg1 to modulate the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway in hippocampal neurons. Molecular docking revealed favorable interactions between Rg1 and Nrf2, suggesting potential activating effects of Rg1 on Nrf/HO-1 pathway (Fig. 5A). As compared to control group, immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated enhanced nuclear translocation of Nrf2 in the Ginsenoside Rg1 groups, particularly in the combined CORT + Rg1 group, indicating active engagement of Nrf2 in response to Rg1 (Fig. 5B). To validate the functional role of Nrf2, HT22 cells were transfected with Nrf2 interference plasmid, and subsequent challenges with corticosterone modeled neurotoxic conditions. qPCR validated the successful knockdown of Nrf2 (Fig. 5C). Western blot analyses showed a marked decrease in NLRP3, pro-caspase1, and IL-1β expression in the Ginsenoside Rg1 treatment groups, supporting reduced inflammasome activation (Fig. 5D). Additionally, cell immunofluorescence confirmed a decrease in NLRP3 and caspase-1 expression after Ginsenoside Rg1 treatment, aligning with the reductions observed in pro-inflammatory cytokine levels as evidenced by ELISA (Fig. 5E-G). These findings collectively affirm that Ginsenoside Rg1 mitigates neuroinflammatory pathways and pyroptosis through the activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway, offering substantial neuroprotection against induced neurotoxicity. Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated the reduction in pyroptosis following treatment with Ginsenoside Rg1, especially in the CORT + Rg1 + si-Nrf2 group (Fig. 5H).

## Discussion
  Chronic sleep deprivation (SD) leads to cognitive decline and hippocampal damage through mechanisms such as NLRP3 inflammasome activation and pyroptosis (Fan et al. 2021; Amini et al. 2022; Niznikiewicz et al. 2017). Current insomnia treatments, like benzodiazepines, have significant drawbacks, including safety concerns and addiction risks, highlighting the need for alternative therapies (Edinoff et al. 2021a, b; Chapoutot et al. 2021). Ginsenoside Rg1, known for its neuroprotective, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant properties, presents a promising candidate (Yang et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2016). This study demonstrates the neuroprotective effects of Ginsenoside Rg1 in alleviating PCPA-induced insomnia in a dose-dependent manner. Our findings suggest that Ginsenoside Rg1 mitigates hippocampal neuronal damage, inflammation, and pyroptosis through the activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway and the inhibition of NLRP3 inflammasome activation.

  The results of our behavioral data from animal experiment showed that Ginsenoside Rg1 alleviates insomnia, as evidenced by reduced sleep latency and increased sleep duration in the PIST. Additionally, improvements in SPT and enhanced MWM highlight its cognitive and mood-related benefits. These findings are consistent with prior studies that have shown Ginsenoside Rg1 to enhance neuroplasticity and cognitive function in models of sleep deprivation and neurodegenerative diseases. Yang et al. have reported that long-term supplementation of Ginsenoside Rg1 in aged mice improves memory and cognitive function by upregulating synaptic plasticity-related protein expression and activating the mTOR signaling pathway ((Yang et al. 2013). Additionally, Ginsenoside Rg1 has also been found to protect hippocampal neurons and enhance neurogenesis by reducing oxidative stress and neuroinflammation, mechanisms which are often implicated in neurodegenerative diseases (Sun et al. 2022; Lu et al. 2023; Zhong et al. 2020; Zhu et al. 2014). In animal models, Rg1 can mitigate cognitive impairments caused by Alzheimer’s disease and chemotherapy-induced cognitive decline ((Shi et al. 2019).

  Histopathological analysis revealed that Ginsenoside Rg1 exerts significant neuroprotective effects, improving neuronal integrity and reducing astrocytic activation, particularly in the high-dose group, where hippocampal architecture was nearly restored to control levels. The reduction in GFAP expression indicates that Ginsenoside Rg1 alleviates astrocytic hypertrophy, a marker of neuroinflammation, reinforcing its role as a neuroprotective agent. Astrocytes are essential for regulating neurotransmitter balance, maintaining the blood-brain barrier, and providing metabolic support to neurons (Scimemi 2019; Jensen et al. 2013; Schiera et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2023a, b). Under normal conditions, they help protect neurons by clearing excess ions and neurotransmitters, promoting synapse formation, and maintaining brain homeostasis (Scimemi 2019; Jensen et al. 2013; Schiera et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2023a, b). However, astrocytes can become overactivated in response to injury or neurodegeneration, leading to neurotoxic molecule production, such as TNF-α, IL-1β, ROS, and NO, exacerbating neuronal damage (Vandenbark et al. 2021; Santiago-Balmaseda et al. 2024). Our study confirmed that Ginsenoside Rg1 modulates astrocyte activity, reducing astrocytic hypertrophy and neurotoxic production, thus supporting neuronal survival and mitigating neuroinflammation.

  Furthermore, the restoration of neurotransmitter levels (5-HT, 5-HTP, GABA, and GLU) in the hippocampus highlights Ginsenoside Rg1’s role in correcting neurotransmitter imbalances induced by PCPA. The increase in inhibitory neurotransmitters such as GABA, along with the reduction in excitotoxic conditions mediated by GLU, aligns with the anti-excitotoxic properties of Ginsenoside Rg1. It has been reported that Ginsenoside Rg1 protects neurons by modulating calcium influx and reducing excessive glutamate release, which is a key contributor to excitotoxicity ((Zhang et al. 2012). This helps to mitigate neuronal damage in conditions like Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease, where excitotoxicity is a major pathogenic factor​ ((Zhang et al. 2012). Studies also indicate that Rg1 can enhance GABAergic transmission, promoting neuronal survival and reducing excitotoxic stress by balancing the excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmitter systems in the brain (Sun et al. 2022; Yang et al. 2023). These effects further demonstrate the potential of Ginsenoside Rg1 as a therapeutic agent in preventing neurodegeneration (Sun et al. 2022; Yang et al. 2023). These findings suggest that Ginsenoside Rg1 not only restores sleep patterns but also prevents the neurotransmitter imbalances that contribute to cognitive dysfunction in insomnia.

  In addition, one of the key mechanisms by which Ginsenoside Rg1 exerts its neuroprotective effects is through the inhibition of NLRP3 inflammasome activation and pyroptosis. The results of our immunofluorescence and ELISA assays demonstrated significant reductions in NLRP3 and caspase-1 expression, as well as decreased levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β and IL-8) in the hippocampal neurons of the Ginsenoside Rg1-treated groups. These findings indicate that Ginsenoside Rg1 effectively suppresses the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, a key player in neuroinflammatory responses. The NLRP3 inflammasome has been implicated in various neurological disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease ((Liang et al. 2022), Parkinson’s disease ((Xu et al. 2023), and chronic sleep deprivation ((He et al. 2023). Chronic activation of NLRP3 leads to pyroptosis, a form of programmed cell death characterized by the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which exacerbates neuroinflammation and neuronal damage (Han et al. 2023; Faria et al. 2021; Song et al. 2017). Our findings demonstrate that Ginsenoside Rg1 can inhibit this pathological cascade, thereby preserving neuronal function. This inhibition of pyroptosis may contribute to the observed improvements in cognitive function and sleep quality, as pyroptotic cell death has been linked to cognitive decline in both animal models and human studies. It has been reported reducing pyroptosis via inhibition of the NLRP3 inflammasome in mouse models of cognitive impairment can reverse learning and memory deficits​ ((Yang and Tang 2023). Additionally, sleep deprivation-induced cognitive decline was mitigated when pyroptosis was blocked, suggesting that this mechanism plays a critical role in sleep-related cognitive impairments ((Xiong et al. 2022). Therefore, these findings suggest that the pyroptosis inhibition induced by ginsenoside Rg1 provides therapeutic approach for enhancing both cognitive function and sleep quality in neurodegenerative and neuroinflammatory disorders.

  The activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway appears to be a crucial mechanism underlying the neuroprotective effects of Ginsenoside Rg1. Our molecular docking analysis suggested that Ginsenoside Rg1 interacts directly with Nrf2, enhancing its nuclear translocation and subsequent activation of antioxidant defense genes, including HO-1. This finding was supported by our Western blot and immunofluorescence analyses, which showed increased Nrf2 activation and HO-1 expression in the Ginsenoside Rg1-treated groups. The Nrf2/HO-1 pathway is known to play a pivotal role in protecting cells against oxidative stress and inflammation ((Saha et al. 2020; Loboda et al. 2016; Luo et al. 2018). By upregulating antioxidant enzymes and suppressing pro-inflammatory signaling, Nrf2 activation has been shown to confer neuroprotection in a variety of neurological conditions. Researches have reported that activation of Nrf2 reduced oxidative damage, improved mitochondrial function, and decreased neuroinflammation, thereby protecting neurons from degeneration in animal models of neurodegenerative diseases (Saha et al. 2021; Mayer et al. 2024). This pathway also modulates inflammation by inhibiting pro-inflammatory cytokines and signaling molecules, such as NF-κB, which are known to contribute to neuroinflammation in conditions like cerebral ischemia and multiple sclerosis (Saha et al. 2021; Mayer et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2022). In this study, the knockdown of Nrf2 using siRNA in HT22 cells abrogated the protective effects of Ginsenoside Rg1, further confirming the central role of this pathway in mediating its anti-inflammatory and anti-pyroptotic actions. Previous studies have highlighted the potential of Nrf2 activators in treating neuroinflammatory disorders, and our results provide additional evidence for the therapeutic potential of Ginsenoside Rg1 as a Nrf2 activator ((Amoroso et al. 2023). Emerging evidence suggests that oxidative stress and neuroinflammation contribute to sleep disruption by impairing the function of critical brain regions involved in sleep-wake control, such as the hypothalamus and hippocampus (Neculicioiu et al. 2023; Bonilla-Jaime et al. 2021). By activating the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway, Ginsenoside Rg1 likely restores redox homeostasis, alleviates neuroinflammation, and promotes the recovery of normal sleep patterns. Moreover, the hippocampus, a key region for memory consolidation, is highly vulnerable to oxidative damage and inflammatory insults ((Forghani et al. 2024). Chronic insomnia exacerbates hippocampal oxidative stress and inflammation, leading to impairments in synaptic plasticity, neurogenesis, and ultimately cognitive function ((Weerasinghe-Mudiyanselage and Moon 2024). Activation of Nrf2/HO-1 has been shown to enhance synaptic plasticity, promote neuronal survival, and improve memory performance under various pathological conditions (Davies et al. 2021; Khan et al. 2021). In this study, the observed improvements in Morris Water Maze performance following Ginsenoside Rg1 treatment may be attributed, at least in part, to the activation of Nrf2-mediated antioxidative and anti-inflammatory pathways, which help preserve hippocampal integrity and cognitive abilities. Importantly, our in vitro findings further confirm that silencing Nrf2 abrogated the neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects of Ginsenoside Rg1 against corticosterone-induced neurotoxicity, underscoring the pivotal role of the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway in mediating these benefits. Thus, targeting the Nrf2/HO-1 axis represents a promising therapeutic strategy not only for ameliorating insomnia but also for preventing the cognitive decline often associated with chronic sleep disturbances. Future studies should further delineate the downstream targets of Nrf2 relevant to sleep regulation and cognitive preservation, and explore the potential synergistic effects of Nrf2 activation with other neuroprotective signaling pathways.

  This study has several limitations. First, the use of only Sprague-Dawley rats limits the generalizability of the findings to other species or human populations. Second, the short treatment duration (7 days) does not address the long-term effects of Ginsenoside Rg1. Third, while the PCPA-induced insomnia model provides valuable insights, it may not fully represent the multifactorial nature of insomnia, and further studies in other models are needed to confirm the results.

  In summary, our study demonstrates that Ginsenoside Rg1 effectively alleviates PCPA-induced insomnia by inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome activation and pyroptosis through the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway. These findings suggest that Ginsenoside Rg1 may be a promising therapeutic agent for the treatment of insomnia and other neuroinflammatory conditions. Future studies should further investigate the clinical applicability of Ginsenoside Rg1 in human models of sleep deprivation and explore its potential synergistic effects with other therapeutic agents targeting the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway.
