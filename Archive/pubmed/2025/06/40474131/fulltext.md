# A radiogenomics study on 18F-FDG PET/CT in endometrial cancer by a novel deep learning segmentation algorithm


## Objective
  To create an automated PET/CT segmentation method and radiomics model to forecast Mismatch repair (MMR) and TP53 gene expression in endometrial cancer patients, and to examine the effect of gene expression variability on image texture features.


## Materials and methods
  We generated two datasets in this retrospective and exploratory study. The first, with 123 histopathologically confirmed patient cases, was used to develop an endometrial cancer segmentation model. The second dataset, including 249 patients for MMR and 179 for TP53 mutation prediction, was derived from PET/CT exams and immunohistochemical analysis. A PET-based Attention-U Net network was used for segmentation, followed by region-growing with co-registered PET and CT images. Feature models were constructed using PET, CT, and combined data, with model selection based on performance comparison.


## Results
  Our segmentation model achieved 99.99% training accuracy and a dice coefficient of 97.35%, with validation accuracy at 99.93% and a dice coefficient of 84.81%. The combined PET + CT model demonstrated superior predictive power for both genes, with AUCs of 0.8146 and 0.8102 for MMR, and 0.8833 and 0.8150 for TP53 in training and test sets, respectively. MMR-related protein heterogeneity and TP53 expression differences were predominantly seen in PET images.


## Conclusion
  An efficient deep learning algorithm for endometrial cancer segmentation has been established, highlighting the enhanced predictive power of integrated PET and CT radiomics for MMR and TP53 expression. The study underscores the distinct influences of MMR and TP53 gene expression on tumor characteristics.


## Introduction
  Endometrial cancer, a prevalent female reproductive malignancy, exhibits a rising global incidence [1]. Despite overall declines in solid tumor mortality, endometrial cancer-related deaths are increasing. Understanding genetic mutations, particularly in Mismatch Repair (MMR) and TP53 genes, is essential for effective diagnosis and treatment [2–4].

  MMR and TP53 defects are typically detected via microsatellite instability (MSI) testing and immunohistochemistry (IHC). IHC, due to its cost-effectiveness and ease of use, has become the preferred method over MSI, with high consistency between the two (86–99%) [5, 6]. P53 IHC detection is cost-effective and serves as the primary method for assessing TP53 mutation status. Recent studies have confirmed its strong correlation with the functional effects of mutations [7]. However, IHC accuracy in fine-needle aspiration biopsies (FNAB) is limited and may not fully capture the tumor’s genetic mutation status [8].

  18F-FDG PET/CT, a key imaging technique for malignant tumors, reflects tumor biology through glucose metabolism, providing precise diagnostic information [9]. Research has demonstrated that PET/CT parameters correlate with P53 expression and MSI status in various cancers [10–15].

  Artificial intelligence enhances medical image feature quantification, improving tumor and gene expression analysis. Radiogenomics uses imaging to predict molecular characteristics [16, 17]. Deep learning offers automated tumor boundary detection and integrates multimodal PET/CT data for better tumor delineation and differentiation [17, 18].

  In summary, this study aims to: (1) develop an automated tumor segmentation algorithm for rapid and efficient tumor delineation, and (2) introduce PET/CT-based radiomics to predict MMR and TP53 gene expression in endometrial cancer patients. These objectives will explore the impact of differential MMR and TP53 expression on image texture features, complementing FNAB results to guide treatment strategies.


## Materials and methods

### Study subjects
  This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of China Medical University, and informed consent was waived (ethical number: 2023PS1227K). A retrospective collection of 400 patients who underwent PET/CT examinations at our hospital from January 2016 to September 2023 was conducted. Inclusion criteria included: ① Within two weeks of undergoing PET/CT imaging, the patient was pathologically diagnosed with endometrial cancer, without any underlying comorbidities; ② patients who underwent PET/CT examination after their first admission; and ③ patients who had not received treatment for endometrial cancer before the PET/CT examination. Exclusion criteria included: ① incomplete clinical data; ② poor image quality or small lesions; and ③ history of secondary tumors or other malignancies. In this study, we created two datasets:

  Dataset 1: The dataset was utilized to train the model for segmenting endometrial cancer. After employing the screening method mentioned earlier, 123 patients with distinct tumor core regions were chosen for the development of the model.

  Dataset 2 was employed to construct the MMR/P53 prediction model. Adhering to the established inclusion and exclusion criteria, an additional 249 endometrial cancer patients with immunohistochemical staining for MMR and P53 were assembled. These patients formed the basis for the MMR sub-dataset, while the P53abn endometrial cancer model was developed using patients with normal MMR gene expression [19]. Exclusion of patients with normal MMR expression resulted in a sub-dataset of 179 patients for the P53 model. Figure 1 depicts the patient selection workflow in this study.

### PET/CT
  Before the non-enhanced 18F-FDG PET/CT scan, all patients fasted for ≥ 6 h, and their blood glucose levels were ≤ 7 mmol/L. 18F-FDG was injected at rest at a dose of 3.70 to 5.55 MBq/kg. After 60 min, while the patients were lying on the scanning bed, the 18F-FDG PET/CT scan was performed using a Discovery PET/CT 690 scanner (GE Healthcare, Waukesha, WI, USA). The scanning range extended from the skull to the mid-thigh (120 s per bed position). The CT scan had a slice thickness of 3.75 mm, tube voltage of 120 to 140 kV, and tube current of 80 mA.


### ROI annotation
  All PET images were loaded into the 3D Slicer (https://www.slicer.org) software version 4.10.2. The regions of interest (ROIs) were manually obtained based on the patients’ PET images and should completely and exclusively encompass the main body of the tumor. The delineation was performed by two clinical radiologists with over 5 years of diagnostic imaging experience. The delineation results were reviewed by another senior radiologist. In case of disputes over the delineation results, the scope was determined through consultation between the two. The ROI annotation is shown in Fig. 2.

### Segmentation model
  The dataset, derived from PET axial images, was split into training and validation sets in a 6:4 ratio. We employed the Attention-UNet architecture for 2D segmentation, which was then compiled into 3D regions of interest (ROIs). These ROIs underwent 3D region growth, a technique that recursively merges pixels or small regions into larger ones based on predefined criteria, using coregistered CT and PET images. In our experiment, every pixel within the 3D-ROI, derived from the 2D segmentation outcomes, served as a potential seed point for the growth process. The growth conditions were defined as an SUV value greater than 4.5 and a CT value greater than 40. These thresholds were chosen to mitigate the interference from surrounding normal tissues (SUV value) and bladder urine (CT value). The growth direction was confined to the 26 neighboring points within the 3 × 3 × 3 voxel space. The algorithm was tested on abdominal images of patients and compared against manually delineated ROIs. A radiologist with over five years of experience in medical imaging diagnosis independently assessed whether the segmentation results included tumors. Subsequently, 18 texture features based on first-order statistics were extracted from the ROIs identified as containing tumors and were re-labeled accordingly. For ROIs containing the tumor and re-annotated ROIs, 18 first-order statistical texture features were extracted, and the consistency between the segmentation algorithm results and manual re-annotation was evaluated using ICC, with ICC > 75% indicating a strong correlation. Finally, accuracy and intersection ratio were used to evaluate the performance of the model. The flowchart of the segmentation algorithm is shown in Fig. 3.

  Fig. 3Flow chart of the segmentation algorithm. (A) Based on Attention-UNet architecture, segmentation is initially performed in 2D. The separate 2D segmentation results were then combined to form a 3D ROI for each patient. (B) Region growing based on 3D rois coregistered with CT and PET images. All pixels within the 3D ROI formed by the 2D segmentation results were considered as potential seed points for region growth. Growth conditions were set as SUV value > 4.5 and CT value>40 to exclude interference from peritumoral tissue and urine. The growth direction was set to include the remaining 26 voxels in the adjacent 3 × 3 × 3 space


### Immunohistochemistry
  We employed IHC to evaluate the status of MMR proteins and TP53 [20]. MMRd is defined as the absence of expression of one or more MMR proteins (MLH1, PMS2, MSH6, and PMS2) in the nuclei of tumor cells. Typically, P53 abnormal expression is defined as the absence of staining or overexpression in the tumor cell nuclei, which means at least 80% of the tumor cell nuclei show uniform and intense nuclear staining [21].


### Radiomics model
  We constructed a radiomics model using 249 patients from Dataset 2, with ROIs automatically segmented via the algorithm detailed in Sect. 2.4. Texture features were extracted from CT and PET images and standardized using MinMax and Mean algorithms. Feature selection was based on Pearson’s correlation coefficient to evaluate feature pair similarity to reduce multicollinearity and improve model performance. Features with a Pearson Correlation Coefficient (PCC) above 0.99 were randomly removed to ensure feature independence and minimize strong correlations, crucial for the radiomics model’s integrity and predictive power. Various screening methods were applied, and multiple machine learning classifiers were considered, tailored to the sample size and processing methods. The training dataset underwent five-fold cross-validation, with the Area under the curve (AUC) as the key evaluation metric across models.


### Statistics
  All analyses were performed using SPSS statistical software (version 24.0; IBM), R (version 3.63), Python (version 3.8.5), and MedCalc (version 15.2.2) statistical software was used for calculations. An independent sample t-test or Mann-Whitney U test was used to analyze the differences in age and body mass index (BMI) between the training group and the validation group, as well as the correlation between MMR protein and characteristics. Pearson or Spearman test was used for continuous categorical variables. Pearson c2 or Fisher exact probability method was used for categorical variables. The receiver operating characteristic curve was used to analyze the predictive efficacy of the two groups of models, and the Delong test was used for comparative analysis. In the above statistical analyses, a two-sided p value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

### Automatic segmentation results
  PET images of 123 patients in dataset 1 were segmented into 1409 axial images, which were divided into training set and validation set according to a 6:4 ratio. After 88 epochs of training, the model achieved the best effect, with an accuracy of 99.99% and an intersection ratio of 97.35% in the training set. The accuracy of the validation set was 99.93%, and the intersection ratio was 84.81%. The results of model training are shown in Fig. 4.

## Discussion
  Endometrial cancer treatment has shifted from traditional clinicopathological markers to molecular predictors, boosting the use of neoadjuvant and targeted therapies. The approval of pembrolizumab and lenvatinib has expanded options for advanced and recurrent cases, particularly for MMRd tumors. New treatments are emerging for P53abn tumors, necessitating further research into prognostic biomarkers and targeted therapies for P53abn/MMRd patients. Ongoing studies on adjuvant therapy by molecular subtypes will inform precision medicine approaches for endometrial cancer. Traditional FNAB, while a common diagnostic tool, has limitations due to small sample sizes and incomplete tumor heterogeneity assessment. Operator technique and puncture site variability can compromise sample quality, increasing the risk of misdiagnosis.

  This study introduces a PET/CT-based radiomics model for non-invasive MMR and TP53 gene status prediction in EC patients, offering clinical value for preoperative evaluation. The development of automated tumor segmentation algorithms enhanced clinician efficiency. This work fills a gap in PET/CT-based radiomics for endometrial cancer, providing a new diagnostic and treatment approach. By analyzing dataset 2, it was observed that FIGO staging of endometrial cancer generally favored early stages in specific subsets of MMR and P53. This observation is consistent with the early detection and diagnosis that can be obtained in women with endometrial cancer. In these patients, the most common histologic type was endometrioid carcinoma. However, there was no significant correlation between P53 and MMR mutation status, regardless of cancer stage or tissue type.

  Numerous tumor segmentation algorithms have been developed to date. In this study, we employed deep learning for the preliminary segmentation, which was then refined with 3D regional growth to circumvent hypermetabolic areas associated with the intestines and bladder. To guarantee complete tumor coverage in the segmented region, our approach typically results in a slightly larger area than that of the manually labeled image. However, by calculating the ICCs of the first-order texture features, 72.22% of the features had ICCs greater than 0.75, indicating that our images had both accuracy and the ability to include tumor edges. False segmentation primarily affected lymph node and metastasis identification, similar to other studies [22].

  This study developed three radiomics models (PET, CT, and PET + CT) for diagnosing MMR and TP53 mutations, with the PET + CT model demonstrating superior performance. The model did not exhibit significant overfitting, potentially due to the limited test set size. Artificial intelligence advancements have improved tumor microenvironment detection, Yan et al. [23]predicted P53ban status using multi-parametric MRI with AUCs of 0.870 and 0.845 for training and validation sets. Our model achieved AUCs of 0.8833 and 0.8150 for training and test sets, respectively, confirming its robustness in predicting TP53 expression in endometrial cancer.

  The correlations between radiomic features and MMR/TP53 protein expressions likely reveal distinct pathobiological mechanisms tied to these genomic alterations. The MMR radiomics model features correlated with MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, and PMS2 proteins, whose loss leads to MSI via defective DNA mismatch repair [24]. This instability manifests as metabolic heterogeneity (PET) and textural irregularity (CT), influencing both imaging modalities due to MLH1, MSH6, and PMS2 expression patterns. Heterogeneous MSH2 expression was mainly detected in PET images, possibly due to altered nucleotide metabolism in MSH2-deficient tumors affecting 18F-FDG uptake [25].

  TP53 mutations, which disrupt cell cycle regulation and metabolic programming, were effectively captured in PET images, aligning with the Warburg effect where TP53-mutated tumors increase glycolytic flux even in the presence of oxygen [26]. This metabolic change heightens intratumoral FDG uptake heterogeneity, making PET sensitive to TP53 status variations. This synergistic capture of metabolic (PET) and structural (CT) effects of oncogenic mutations underpins the PET + CT model’s superior predictive performance. Previous studies [27–30] have associated PET/CT metabolic parameters with endometrial cancer prognosis. S Ytre-Hauge et al. [31] found that CT texture features could reflect deep myometrial invasion and high-risk histological subtypes. Previous research [11, 12] has shown a positive correlation between SUVmax in PET/CT and P53 expression, which our study extends by demonstrating TP53-associated metabolic heterogeneity through advanced texture analysis.

  Our findings contribute to the field of radiogenomics while highlighting the obstacles that impede its clinical implementation. As Filippi et al. [32]emphasize, standardizing imaging protocols is crucial due to the potential for variability across centers to compromise radiomic feature consistency and result reproducibility. Uniform protocols and quality control are essential for generalizing radiogenomics findings. Regulatory hurdles also present significant challenges, with the validation and approval process for new radiomic biomarkers being complex and time-consuming. Overcoming these barriers necessitates collaboration among researchers, industry, and regulatory authorities. Moreover, ensuring the reproducibility of radiomic features across different scanner models and algorithms is critical. Advances in machine learning and artificial intelligence may help develop robust models that mitigate these variations, enhancing the reliability of radiomic predictions.

  This study has enhanced our understanding of imaging genomic features associated with MMR and P53 expression in endometrial cancer; however, several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the retrospective design and single-center data collection may limit the generalizability of the findings. To address this, future research should involve larger, multi-center studies to enhance the model’s accuracy and stability. Secondly, the study did not account for POLE mutations, which occur in 7% of endometrial cancer cases [29, 31], due to the high cost of detection, suggesting a potential area for improvement. Thirdly, the modest sample size is a limitation that could impact the statistical power of the results. Fourthly, the absence of external validation for both imaging and genomic findings is another significant limitation, as it is crucial for confirming the robustness of the results. Lastly, the segmentation accuracy was evaluated against a single expert radiologist’s re-annotation. Relying on one reader raises concerns about inter-observer variability and potential bias. To mitigate this, future work should include plans for multi-reader validation, which would involve obtaining agreement metrics from at least two independent readers to ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the segmentation results.


## Conclusion
  The PET + CT radiomics model shows promise in predicting MMR and TP53 expression, suggesting that MMR may influence tumor metabolism and structure, while TP53 predominantly affects metabolism. However, it is important to note that these findings are based on a relatively small cohort and exploratory analyses. Our fully automated endometrial cancer segmentation algorithm facilitates efficient and rapid tumor delineation, but the precision of this method should be further validated. Future studies with larger cohorts are necessary to confirm the strength of the association between deep learning-derived PET features and genomic signatures, and to validate the robustness of our segmentation approach.
