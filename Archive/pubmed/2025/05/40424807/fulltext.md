# Enhancing Ecological Risk Assessment of Dioxins in Aquatic Environments: AHR Diversity and Species Sensitivity Differences in Tiger Puffer (Takifugu rubripes)


## Abstract
  Dioxins and dioxin-like compounds (DLCs) exert toxicity through the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR), but species variations in AHR lead to differing sensitivities. Investigating the variation in AHR homolog diversity, expression levels, predominant forms, and AHR sensitivity across species—particularly in fish sensitive to dioxins—is essential for enhancing ecological risk assessment. This study focuses on the tiger puffer (Takifugu rubripes), identifying five AHRs and two ARNTs, with truAHR2a showing the highest expression and the truAHR1 subfamily displaying lower levels. All truAHRs are functional and can be activated by 2,3,7,8-tetrachlorodibenzo-p-dioxin (TCDD), with truARNT1 cooperating more efficiently with truAHRs than truARNT2. We determined EC50 values for truAHR1a (0.30±0.10 nM), truAHR1b (0.32±0.20 nM), truAHR2a (0.98±0.63 nM), truAHR2b (2.62±2.48 nM), and truAHR2c (0.43±0.22 nM), with truAHR1a showing the highest sensitivity. The truAHR1 subfamily displayed greater sensitivity than the truAHR2 subfamily, contrasting with medaka and zebrafish, where AHR2 is similar to or more sensitive than AHR1. Comparisons highlighted species- and subform-specific sensitivities in AHRs, differing by one to two orders of magnitude. Ligand-binding assays showed that all truAHRs bound [3H]TCDD specifically. Molecular docking indicated that although TCDD binds AHRs with similar affinities and conserved residues, other subform-specific factors likely contribute to their differential sensitivities. This study provides valuable data on AHR diversity and ligand-sensitivity, contributing to ecological toxicity assessment of dioxin-like compounds.


## Introduction
  Dioxins are generally considered as a class of highly toxic persistent organic pollutants (POPs) that exert significant toxic risks on humans and wildlife, including fish1, birds2, and mammals3. These risks include carcinogenicity4, reproductive and developmental toxicity5, etc. However, in recent years, there has been a growing discovery of novel organic pollutants with dioxin-like molecular structures, raising concerns about their potential biological toxicity. These compounds are referred to as dioxin-like compounds (DLCs), such as polyhalogenated carbazoles (PHCZs) and polychlorinated diphenyl sulfides (PCDPSs)6, 7. Although global regulations, such as the Stockholm Convention, have restricted the production and use of these substances, their persistence as POPs—characterized by their stability, long-range transport, and bioaccumulation potential—continues to raise concerns regarding their environmental presence and associated impacts8. Given this, there is an urgent need for toxicity assessments and studies on the toxicological mechanisms of these compounds. To achieve this goal, it’s crucial to better understand toxicity mechanisms and conduct a comprehensive assessment of these compounds.

  Dioxins and DLCs exert their toxic effects by activating the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR), a ligand-dependent transcription factor commonly known as the dioxin receptor9. AHR, a member of the basic helix-loop-helix/Per-ARNT-Sim (bHLH-PAS) superfamily, remains inactive in the cytoplasm without a ligand10, 11. Upon binding with dioxins or DLCs, AHR is activated and moves to the nucleus, where it forms a heterodimer with the partner aryl hydrocarbon receptor translocator (ARNT). This complex then binds to dioxin-responsive elements (DRE) in target gene promoters (e.g., cyp1a1 and cyp1b1)9, leading to toxic effects such as immunotoxicity (e.g., TCDD-induced thymic atrophy)12, carcinogenesis (e.g., p53-mediated apoptosis via cytochrome P450/AHR)13, and developmental defects (e.g., fetal cleft palate)14. The processes of transactivation, which influence target gene expression and associated toxic outcomes, are determined by factors such as the binding affinity and selectivity of specific AHR subforms for particular ligands, the efficiency of DRE binding, and the overall transactivation efficacy15, 16. Additionally, the AHR gene battery, comprising a diverse set of genes such as cyp1a1, cyp1a2, Aldh3a1, Nqo1, Ugt1a6, and Gsta1, plays a crucial role in mediating these effects. The scope and diversity of this battery, which encompass functions ranging from xenobiotic metabolism to oxidative stress modulation and apoptosis regulation17, collectively underscoring the mechanistic complexity and broad biological impact of AhR signaling. During animal evolution, the AHR gene has undergone extensive duplication and diversification, resulting in the presence of multiple AHR subforms (ranging from 1 to 5) within various species18. These AHR subforms, coupled with species-specific variations, contribute to the distinct toxicological responses to dioxins and DLCs observed across species. Due to these reasons, the sensitivity of different species to dioxins can differ markedly. For example, in certain bird species, the EC50 ranges from 0.22 nM to 25 nM19, while in fish, most species exhibit EC50 values below 10 nM, with some showing extreme sensitivity (< 0.1 nM)15, 20. This diversity highlights the need for specialized studies on the expression abundance and functional dominance of AHRs, as well as the sensitivity variance of AHRs in species9. This is essential for better ecological toxicological assessment of dioxins and DLCs and species conservation.

  However, conventional chemical toxicity testing is no longer sufficient for assessing the risks of large numbers of chemicals. A promising approach is integrating in vitro testing with biological pathway data. Adverse Outcome Pathways (AOPs), initially proposed by Ankley et al.,21 offer an innovative method for accurately evaluating the ecological and health risks of DLCs, particularly through AHR-based AOPs. A quantitative AOP (qAOP) has already been developed to enhance toxicity prediction of novel DLCs. Building on qualitative AHR-AOPs, the AHR-qAOP can link descriptive knowledge to predict adverse outcomes in chemical risk assessment22. This approach, combining experimental data with systems biology, helps explore toxicological mechanisms and supports ecological risk management. Currently, the application of AHR-qAOP faces challenges, such as the diversity of AHR receptors18 and significant sensitivity differences of dioxins and DLCs among species23, 24. Especially in native species, AHR genes often exist in two or more subforms with distinct ligand-binding and transcriptional activation properties, leading to orders-of-magnitude differences in sensitivity. Such variability complicates the establishment of generalized toxicity thresholds or predictive models. In a previous study, a qAOP was developed using nine fish species to quantify the dose-dependent relationship between AHR-mediated chemical sensitivity and early-stage mortality25. However, the broader application of this model is hindered by the limited characterization of AHR subforms in most fish species. For instance, only a few fish AHRs have been systematically characterized, and the AHR subforms situation in most fish species remains unclear26. This data gap prevents the accurate parameterization of species-specific responses within the qAOP framework. Therefore, more data on AHR identification and characterization are needed to effectively apply the AHR-qAOP21. This requires a thorough understanding of the AHR in fish. In addition to the native species prioritized in qAOP studies, model species are equally critical to support research on threatened or endangered native species.

  It has been demonstrated that pufferfish possess the smallest genome among vertebrates, at just 400 Mb with low repetitive content, containing key genes and regulatory elements for precise gene expression. This makes pufferfish suitable for research on gene regulation in cell lines and transgenic systems27. Among them, tiger puffer (Takifugu rubripes) is an excellent model. Recent study indicates that the wild population of this species is declining due to overfishing and environmental pollution28, 29. However, its AHR has not been systematically characterized. Therefore, the current study aims to systematically characterized the AHRs of tiger puffer, focusing on their phylogeny, expression abundance, functional properties, and sensitivity to dioxins, including the binding mode of ligands to AHR. Additionally, this research will conduct interspecies comparisons of AHR sensitivity with other species, such as zebrafish and medaka. This study will enhance understanding of AHR biology in tiger puffer and contribute aquatic toxicological data, aiding ecological toxicological assessments of dioxins and DLCs, and supporting the conservation of wild species.


## Material and Methods

### Tiger puffer RNA extraction
  Tiger puffer was purchased from a fishing farm and anesthetized with 0.03% MS-222. The fish were then homogenized on ice conditions for subsequent biochemical assays. Total RNA was extracted from the homogenate using a total RNA purification kit (TianGen, Beijing, China). The integrity was tested by agarose gel electrophoresis. Two μg of total RNA were reverse-transcribed in reactions containing an oligo-dT primers, using a first strand cDNA synthesis kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). This work has received approval for research ethics from the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Qufu Normal University (NO.2024137), and a proof/certificate of approval is available upon request.


### Phylogenetic analyses of AHR and ARNT
  The sequences of AHR and ARNT were retrieved from the NCBI GenBank database and aligned using Clustal W software. The chromosome numbers for the location of the five tiger puffer AHR genes were obtained by BLAST searching the latest reference genome assembly (GCF_901000725.2) from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCF_901000725.2/). For the construction of phylogenetic trees, the nucleotide sequences corresponding to the N-terminal amino acids of AHR (59–468 aa of truAHR1a, NCBI ID: NM_001037962.1, and the corresponding regions of other AHRs) and ARNT (39–448 aa of truARNT1, NCBI ID: XM_029838635.1, and the corresponding regions of other ARNTs) were utilized. The selected regions are more conserved and can be well-aligned; in contrast, the C-terminal halves cannot be aligned with confidence and would add uncertainty to the phylogenetic analysis30. Phylogenetic analysis employing the maximum-likelihood (ML) method based on the Jones–Taylor–Thornton model, was performed in MEGA X. The resulting phylogenetic trees were constructed with branch support was assessed using 1000 bootstrap replicates. The gene accessions are listed in the supplementary material.


### Expression of tiger puffer AHR and ARNT
  In general, the genes involved in the AHR pathway include AHR, ARNT, and AHRR. The quantification of AHR pathway expression was conducted using publicly available RNA-seq data from the NCBI SRA database, with accessions listed in the supplementary material and Table S1. The data were then uploaded to the Galaxy server and analyzed using the Kallisto tool, which employs specific mRNA sequences for gene quantification. The sequences used corresponded to the following accessions: truAHR1a (NM_001037962.1), truAHR1b (NM_001037959.1), truAHR2a (NM_001037960.1), truAHR2b (NM_001037963.1), truAHR2c (NM_001037958.1), truAHRR (NM_001037956.1), truARNT1 (XM_029838634.1) and truARNT2 (XM_029845653.1). Gene expression levels were quantified using transcripts per kilobase million (TPM), a standard metric in RNA-seq analysis for assessing gene abundance.


### Expression vector construction
  The full-length coding sequences of five AHRs and two ARNTs from tiger puffer cDNA were amplified using Phanta high-fidelity PCR (Vazyme) with primers detailed in Table 1. The PCR was conducted under the reported conditions by Karchner et al.,31: 94 °C, 1 min; 5 cycles of (94 °C, 5 s/72°C, 3 min); 5 cycles of (94°C, 5 s/70°C, 3 min); and 30 cycles of (94°C, 5s/68°C, 3min). PCR products were purified with a Gel DNA extraction kit (TianGen) and cloned into pcDNA3.1(+) vectors (Invitrogen, Burling, ON, USA) using the Seamless Cloning Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Subsequently, the product was transformed into DH5α competent cells. The positive clones were subjected to plasmid extraction using a plasmid purification kit (TianGen) and then sequenced at Sangon Biotech (Sangon, Beijing, China) to ensure the correct coding sequence.


### Functional testing of the tiger puffer AHRs and ARNTs
  COS-7 cells sourced from the National Infrastructure Cell Line Resource (Beijing, China) were maintained in DMEM medium (Meilunbio, Dalian, China) at 37°C with 5% CO2 and supplemented with 10% FBS (Sangon). The potent AHR agonist TCDD was prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) at a concentration of 100 μM using a high-purity standard (Wellington, Ontario, Canada; product number: CUS-DD-48-D). For the functional screening assay, cells were seeded at a density of 30,000 cells per well in 96-well plates and transfected twelve hours later. Transfection was carried out using Lipo8000 transfection reagent (Beyotime) and Opti-MEM medium (Invitrogen, Burlington, ON, USA), with expression constructs of AHR (30 ng) and ARNT (20 ng), along with AHR luciferase reporter constructs pGL3-dcp at 50 ng, and Renilla luciferase control vector pRL-SV40 at 2 ng per well15. Transfections included negative controls, AHR-only, ARNT-only, AHR and ARNT combinations. During negative control, AHR, or ARNT transfections, the total amount of transfection was supplemented with herring sperm DNA to reach 102 ng/well. Eight hours post-transfection, TCDD was added to the medium at a working concentration of 100 nM. After 28 hours of TCDD treatment, cells were lysed and assayed using the dual-luciferase reporter gene assay kit (Meilumbio) with Biotek Synergy H1 instrument. The final luminescence values were expressed as the ratio of firefly luciferase units to Renilla luciferase units.


### Tiger puffer AHR sensitivity determination
  The same cell culture method as described above was employed to co-transfect expression constructs of AHR (30 ng) and ARNT (20 ng), pGL3-dcp luciferase reporter construct (50 ng), and pRL-SV40 Renilla luciferase control reporter construct (2 ng) in each well using the Lipo8000 transfection reagent (Beyotime). Twelve hours post-transfection, a range of TCDD concentrations (from 6.35×10−13 to 1.25×10−7 M, this concentration typically covers the full range of TCDD responsiveness for AHRs of low and high affinity20) were added to the medium at a DMSO concentration of 0.125% v/v. After 24 hours of TCDD treatment, cells were lysed and assayed using the dual-luciferase reporter gene assay kit (Meilumbio) with Biotek Synergy H1 instrument. The final luminescence value is expressed as the ratio of firefly luciferase units to Renilla luciferase units. The fitting equation is Y = Bottom + (Top - Bottom)/(1 + 10^((logEC50 - X) × Hill slope)), where X is the logarithm of TCDD concentration and Y is the normalized luciferase activity. EC20/50/80 values were calculated from the fit parameters of each dose-response curve for AHR-ARNT pairs, including truAHR1a-truARNT1, truAHR1b-truARNT1, truAHR2a-truARNT1, truAHR2b-truARNT1, and truAHR2c-truARNT1. Our previous studies had shown that the dose-response curves obtained from these reporter gene assays are independent of the amount of AHR plasmid transfected (5–30 ng)32.


### Ligand-Binding assays
  Full-length tiger puffer AHR cDNAs (accession numbers DQ088137, DQ088138, DQ088139, DQ088140, and DQ088141) were inserted into the pcDNA 3.1/Zeo (+) vector (Invitrogen, Inc.). TnT-Quick Coupled Reticulocyte Lysate Systems (Promega, Madison, WI) were used to synthesize [35S]-methionine-labeled or unlabeled proteins following manufacturer’s directions. Five μl of the [35S]-labeled TnT reactions were subjected to SDS-PAGE, followed by fluorography. AHR ligand-binding was determined using unlabeled proteins by velocity sedimentation on sucrose gradients in a vertical tube rotor as described earlier30. The TnT reactions were incubated overnight at 4°C with [3H]TCDD (2 nM). Nonspecific binding was determined by reactions containing an empty vector (unprogrammed lysate (UPL)).


### Molecular modeling
  Homology models for the ligand binding domains (LBDs) of five AHR subforms (truAHR1a, truAHR1b, truAHR2a, truAHR2b, and truAHR2c) of the tiger puffer were generated using the SWISS-MODEL server with the template PDB code of 7zub.1.D (the human AHR LBD domain). The modeled LBD regions for each AHR subform are as follows: truAHR1a (amino acids 282 to 386; with 83.81% sequence identity), truAHR1b (282 to 386; 83.81% identity), truAHR2a (281 to 385; 68.57% identity), truAHR2b (311 to 414; 66.35% identity), and truAHR2c (273 to 377; with 62.86% identity). Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina to predict the binding of TCDD to the LBDs of each AHR subform. The docking grid was centered on the LBD, with grid box dimensions adjusted to encompass the entire binding pocket, measuring 20 × 20 × 20 Å. Following molecular docking, the interactions between TCDD and the LBD of each truAHRs were visualized and analyzed using Protein-Ligand Interaction Profiler (PLIP). The binding cavity of the AHR was identified using CASTp 3.0 with a probe radius of the default value of 1.4 Å.


### Data statistics
  Non-linear logistic concentration-response curve fittings were conducted using Prism GraphPad 9 software (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). The differences between groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA, t-test, and/or non-parametric test in SPSS 26 and Prism GraphPad 9, with *p < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.


## Results and Discussion

### Tiger puffer AHR and ARNT phylogeny
  Studies on fish have shown that, possibly due to tandem gene duplication events before species divergence, fish possess at least two AHR genes (AHR1 and AHR2)10. Furthermore, due to the teleost-specific whole-genome duplication event33, teleost have more copies, including AHR1a, AHR2a, AHR1b, and AHR2b31. These gene duplications offer a unique opportunity to explore the functional diversity of AHR genes, which is crucial for understanding how fish respond to environmental pollutants. The genome of the tiger puffer we study contains few repetitive sequences, making it similar to the human genome and an excellent model for analyzing vertebrate genomes34. This species is of significant value for investigating the structure and evolution of AHR genes in fish. Through a BLAST analysis of the tiger puffer genome available in the NCBI database (GenBank accession number: GCF_901000725.2), we identified two truARNTs. The five truAHRs and the truAHRR sequences were previously identified by Karchner et al35 and were originally deposited in the GenBank database with accession numbers DQ088137, DQ088138, DQ088139, DQ088140, DQ088141, and DQ088142.

  Notably, as illustrated in Fig. 1A, these tiger puffer AHRs can be categorized into the distinct AHR1 and AHR2 subfamilies. As shown in Fig. 2, the genomic localization of these genes offers additional evidence that four AHRs in tiger puffer partake in the AHR1-AHR2 pairs mode, as evidenced by the spatial arrangement of truAHR1a-truAHR2a on Chromosome 1 and truAHR1b -truAHR2b on Chromosome 8. Additionally, truAHR2c is located on Chromosome 6. The comparative genomic analysis between medaka and tiger puffer revealed notable similarities. Medaka has four AHRs, olaAHR1a-olaAHR2a on Chromosome 21 and olaAHR1b-olaAHR2b on Chromosome 2. Notably, the zebrafish AHRs (three AHRs) exhibit a partially divergent genomic organization compared to the tandem configuration observed in tiger puffer. For instance, dreAHR1b and dreAHR2 are tandemly positioned on chromosome 22 and can be activated by TCDD31. In contrast, dreAHR1a is situated on chromosome 16, showing homology to human human AHR (NM_001621.5) on chromosome 7. This analysis of shared synteny indicates that dreAHR1a is an ortholog of human AHR10, 36. While dreAHR1a is not activated by TCDD37, it may be activated by other ligands, such as leflunomide38. This genomic arrangement hints at potential functional differences, suggesting that the functional status of dreAHR1a may be related to its genomic positioning. Noteworthy is the identification of truAHR2c in tiger puffer, located on Chromosome 6; truAHR2c is a subform less commonly reported in other fish species. The phylogenetic investigation of AHRs in zebrafish, tiger puffer, and medaka species suggests that the variations in AHR diversity are largely due to the gene and genome duplications, as well as the selective loss of lineage-specific genes18. The tiger puffer ARNT, a key partner required for AHR transcriptional activation, was also analyzed. Two ARNT homologs were better identified and designated as ARNT1 (chromosome 7) and ARNT2 (on chromosome 13). These homologs were also well-positioned in the phylogenetic tree, clearly classified into ARNT1 and ARNT2 clades (Fig. 1B).

  Given the complex diversity exhibited by AHRs and ARNTs in tiger puffer, further bioinformatics and functional investigations are essential for a comprehensive understanding. The N-terminal region of the AHR contains the highly conserved bHLH and PAS domains, which are crucial for ligand binding, ARNT dimerization, and DNA binding processes. In contrast, the C-terminal segment of the AHR features a glutamine-rich domain and other functional regions involved in transcriptional activation mechanisms37. As illustrated in Fig. 3, the amino acid sequences of five tiger puffer AHRs and one mouse AHR were aligned using GeneDoc. Similar to the mouse AHR, several functional domains are conserved in the N-terminal half of the tiger puffer, including the bHLH, NLS, NES, and PAS domains. The analysis of sequence identity demonstrated varying degrees of conservation between the AHR homologs of the tiger puffer and those of the mouse. The sequence identities were 38.02% for truAHR1a, 39.79% for truAHR1b, 34.57% for truAHR2a, 34.11% for truAHR2b, and 35.71% for truAHR2c. In contrast, the AHR homologs of zebrafish exhibited sequence identities of 33.41% for dreAHR1a, 37.88% for dreAHR1b, and 27.85% for dreAHR2. As shown in Figs. S1–4, further comparisons revealed that truAHR1a exhibited a sequence identity of 35.29% with dreAHR1a, while truAHR1b shared 53% identity with dreAHR1b, and truAHR2b showed 46.18% identity with dreAHR2. These results indicate a greater degree of similarity and conservation between the AHRs of the two fish species compared to those of the mouse. Additionally, when examining the olaAHR, the sequence identities were recorded as follows: 54.89% for truAHR1a-olaAHR1a, 60.56% for truAHR1b-olaAHR1b, 52.31% for truAHR2a-olaAHR2a, and 51.82% for truAHR2b-olaAHR2b (Figs. S1–4). This data suggests a higher level of similarity and conservation among specific AHR forms between these two fish species than when compared to the zebrafish. While the N-terminal region of the AHRs exhibits high conservation (63.77% identity on average when compared to the equivalent region in mouse AHR), the C-terminal region displays significantly less conservation (26.92% identity on average when compared to the corresponding mouse AHR sequence), consistent with previous studies. The phylogenetic analysis and sequence alignment highlighted the conserved and divergent features of AHRs among various species. Additionally, this study sought to investigate further potential variations, including subform-specific expression patterns and sensitivities.


### Tiger puffer AHR and ARNT expression
  AHR expression patterns can vary among different tissues. In the brain of goldfish (Carassius auratus)39 and Atlantic killifish (Fundulus heteroclitus)40, AHR1 expression is always highly expressed, indicating a potential physiological function in the brain. In zebrafish (Danio rerio)41, dreAHR2 plays a role in fin and craniofacial development. White sturgeon (Acipenser transmontanus)42 showed the highest levels of AHR1 and AHR2 expression in the liver and heart, which are known target organs for DLCs. And following exposure to βNF, an increase in AHR1 and AHR2 transcript abundance was observed in all tissues of white sturgeon. Therefore, the varying levels of AHR expression across tissues may indicate distinct physiological roles in different tissues. Based on this, we investigated the expression of AHR in six different tissues of the tiger puffer.

  In tiger puffer, truAHR2a exhibited significantly higher expression compared to other AHR subforms in several tissues, including the liver, gills, and spleen (Fig. 4). This observation is similar to findings in other teleost fish species, such as medaka (Oryzias latipes)32 and Atlantic salmon (Salmo salar)43. The abundance of truAHR2b was always lower than that of truAHR2a, but higher than that of truAHR1a, truAHR1b, and truAHR2c. Notably, AHR2b exhibits differential expression between benthic and limnetic morphs of Arctic charr, with benthic morphs showing higher levels of AHR2b expression36, 44. TruAHR2c is expressed at significantly higher levels than the truAHR1 subfamily in all the tissues. Previous studies on teleost fishes, including Atlantic killifish30, Atlantic cod45, and red seabream46, have demonstrated that AHR1 generally has a greater level in brain, heart, and gonad than in other tissues (means tissue-specific but still lower than AHR2), while AHR2 always has higher overall expression levels almost in all tissues. The Atlantic salmon even retains four AHR2 genes and has at least two AHR1 genes. Moreover, the expression levels of the two AHR1 genes are much lower than those of the four AHR2 genes26, 43. These findings suggest that AHR2 is the predominant form expressed in fish24. Our results also support this, as the expression levels of the truAHR2 subfamily is higher than those of the truAHR1 subfamily. Interestingly, truAHR1a and truAHR1b, both belonging to the AHR1 subfamily, showed greater expression in the brain (Fig. 4D) and are consistent with previous research37, 39, 40, 46. These findings highlight the potential involvement of AHR1 in neurodevelopmental processes, neuroprotection, or the regulation of xenobiotic metabolism in the brain. The conserved expression pattern of AHR1 in the brain across these fish species suggests that at least one member of the AHR family performs an evolutionarily conserved neural funcation39, 40, 46. Although AHR-1 has been clearly shown to regulate neuronal development in C.elegans, the pronounced functional and regulatory differences between invertebrate and vertebrate AHRs leave it unclear which AHR subform fulfils this role in fish. Investigating the neural effects mediated by specific truAHR subforms therefore represents a promising avenue for future research47. The analysis also included the tiger puffer ARNTs, which are essential partners of AHR for transactivation. TruARNT1 exhibits significantly higher abundance than truARNT2 in all six tissues examined, except for the brain. Previous studies in species like Atlantic cod (Gadus morhua)48, common carp (Cyprinus carpio)15, and medaka (Oryzias latipes)32 has also observed elevated expression of ARNT2 in the brain (Fig. 4D). This pattern indicates that the heightened expression of ARNT2 may be evolutionarily conserved to support physiological or developmental functions within the nervous system32.

  The AHR repressor (AHRR), a structural homolog of AHR and ARNT, regulates AHR activity by competing for binding to DREs49. The expression of truAHRR in most tissues generally remained significantly lower compared to truAHR2 subfamily and truARNT1(Fig. 4). In some studies, the expression of AHRR can be induced by TCDD. In killifish, exposure to TCDD exhibited increased AHRR expression, particularly in the spleen, heart, and gut50. According to the above data, the expression levels of the truAHR2 subfamily are higher than those of the truAHR1 subfamily, with truAHR2a showing the highest expression. The expression levels of the other genes are consistent with patterns observed in other fish species, suggesting that the expression patterns of genes in the AHR pathway of tiger puffer have remained relatively conserved.


### Tiger puffer AHR and ARNT’s cooperation potential
  The ARNT1 and ARNT 2 typically function cooperatively with AHRs, and most AHR-ARNT pairs respond to natural ligands or TCDD. However, evidence suggests subtle functional distinctions between them, which may vary based on tissue type or developmental stage51. Consequently, we examined the collaborative potential of the two ARNTs with the five AHRs in tiger puffer. To obtain a comprehensive functional characterization of the AHRs in tiger puffer, we performed a functional screening of the response activities of the five AHRs (truAHR1a, truAHR1b, truAHR2a, truAHR2b, and truAHR2c) and two ARNTs (truARNT1 and truARNT2). We systematically compared these results with the two AHRs (dreAHR1b and dreAHR2) and two ARNTs (dreARNT1 and dreARNT2) in the model organism zebrafish. Note that dreAHR1a is non-functional under TCDD treatment and was not included in this experiment. To achieve this, we conducted dual-luciferase reporter gene assays by introducing expression plasmids into COS-7 cells. We then assessed the transcriptional activation capacity of individual AHR/ARNT pairs or their combinations in comparison to DMSO, after exposure to 100 nM TCDD.

  As shown in Fig. 5, in the transfection mode of ‘only AHR’, all AHRs exhibited significant transactivation in response to 100 nM TCDD. The fold increases were as follows: truAHR1a (2.52×), truAHR1b (3.66×), truAHR2a (3.90×), truAHR2b (6.75×), and truAHR2c (11.79×). In the ‘AHR+ARNT1’ mode, all AHRs were also significantly induced by 100 nM TCDD, with the following fold increases: truAHR1a-truARNT1 (7.69×), truAHR1b-truARNT1 (2.58×), truAHR2a-truARNT1 (13.35×), truAHR2b-truARNT1 (3.13×), truAHR2c-truARNT1 (4.92×), dreAHR1b-dreARNT1 (5.65×), and dreAHR2-dreARNT1 (3.59×). In the ‘AHR+ARNT2’ mode, all tiger puffer and zebrafish AHRs demonstrated effective cooperation with their respective ARNT2, except for the truAHR2b-truARNT2, which exhibited lower signal levels and lacked statistical significance (Fig. 5D). The results for the remaining combinations included: truAHR1a-truARNT2 (12.33×), truAHR1b-truARNT2 (4.85×), truAHR2a-truARNT2 (3.90×), truAHR2c-truARNT2 (99.7×), dreAHR1b-dreARNT2 (9.26×), and dreAHR2-dreARNT2 (10.45×). It is evident that by co-transfecting ARNT1 or ARNT2 with AHRs significantly enhances most ‘AHR+ARNT’ transactivation signals (including DMSO and TCDD treatment) compared to transfecting AHR alone. However, truARNT1 seemed more active than the truARNT2 in inducing an increase in the DMSO background signal and TCDD-induced signal in all the ‘truAHR+truARNT1’ groups. As shown by the groups co-transfecting truAHR1a (12.33×), truAHR1b (4.85×), and truAHR2c (99.7×) with truARNT2, it seems more adaptive to facilitate distinct AHRs to exert efficient TCDD-induced induction fold of TCDD/DMSO, but co-transfecting with truARNT1 (truAHR1a 7.69×, truAHR1b 2.58×, and truAHR2c 4.92×) usually produces a greater DMSO background signal and lower induction folds. It is interesting that by co-transfecting two truAHR2 (truAHR2a and truAHR2b) subforms with truARNTs (Fig.5C and 5D), truARNT1 had higher DMSO and TCDD induction signal and larger TCDD/DMSO induction fold than truARNT2, especially in the truAHR2b+truARNT1 group. In contrast, as indicated in Fig.5F and 5G, in zebrafish, dreARNT2 was found to be more effective than dreARNT1 in inducing both DMSO background signal and TCDD-induced increases in the ‘AHR+ARNT’ combination, and exhibited a greater TCDD/DMSO induction fold. Previous studies have indicated that dreARNT1 serves as a critical partner for dreAHR in mediating TCDD responses in vivo52, 53. Furthermore, there are three mRNA splice variants of the zebrafish homolog of ARNT1—namely ARNT1a, ARNT1b, and ARNT1c—of which only ARNT1b and ARNT1c appear capable of forming functional heterodimers with AHR2. Notably, ARNT1a does not functionally interact with AHR2 in vitro, which may contribute to the observed results52. In the co-transfection of mouse AHR and ARNT, it also shows that the subform of ARNT could influence the magnitude of induction fold15. An in vitro study on chickens found that ckAHR1-ckARNT1 exhibited low transcriptional activation when exposed to TCDD51, but replacing ckARNT1 with cormorant ARNT1 (ccARNT1) restored the response54. Although studies have shown that a qAOP constructed with a single, shared ARNT2 can provide robust cross-species predictions of AHR-mediated early-life-stage mortality25, evidence from our data suggests that species- or tissue-specific differences in ARNT1 and ARNT2 function can modulate ligand-induced transcriptional activation. Accounting for these differences could therefore improve the accuracy of future qAOPs. Additionally, as show in Fig. 5, the TCDD signals induced by all ‘truAHR+truARNT2’ groups are very low. This may be attributed to the low cooperation efficiency between truAHRs and truARNT2, a phenomenon that is also observed in medaka32. One study has demonstrated that ARNT2 can form a dimer with ligand-bound AHR in vitro and is impacted by the activating ligand; however, its capacity to regulate AHR-driven signaling in cell cultures seems restricted.55 In the present study, this is evidenced by the low induction folds observed in all ‘truARNT2+AHRs’ combinations, with no significant activation noted even in the truAHR2b-truARNT2 pairing (Fig. 5D). Studies have shown that in chickens, ckAHR1-ckARNT1 cannot be activated by TCDD, but it can be strongly activated by five natural ligands—6-formylindolo [3,2-b]carbazole, L-kynurenine, kynurenic acid, indoxyl-3-sulfate, and 1,3,7-tribromodibenzo-p-dioxin54, 56. Based on this, it is speculated that truAHR2b-truARNT2 may also be activated by agonists other than TCDD55. However, the signal in truAHR2c-truARNT2 is remarkably high, with the highest TCDD/DMSO induction fold (99.7×, Fig. 5E). Although it is evolutionarily closer to truAHR2b (Fig. 1A), the two exhibit completely opposite activation profiles. This may indicate the functional diversity of different AHRs within the same species. Basing on the greater induction of truAHR2c-truARNT2 (99.7×), as well as the relatively greater sensitivity (Table 2), this may be a good tool for ecological risk assessments.

  The comparative analysis of the cooperative potential of tiger puffer ARNT1 and ARNT2 leads to the conclusion that all truAHRs and truARNTs are functional. Moreover, different ARNT subforms may impact ligand-specific activation or, at the very least, affect the intensity of activation observed for a given AHR. In this study, both ARNTs exhibited different induction activation folds when interacting with the same AHR. Moreover, the same ARNT subform showed varying effects when engaged with different AHRs. Comparisons with zebrafish also revealed significant species-specific differences in AHR-ARNT-mediated TCDD induction. In addition, even not included in this initial exploration, other potential ARNT forms or splice variants may exist and need to considered their function in the future57, 58. To gain a more comprehensive understanding of AHRs and ARNTs in tiger puffer, the sensitivities of each subform were examined and compared, including their interactions with different ARNTs.


### Tiger puffer AHR subform sensitivities
  Structural differences in AHR can result in changes in ligand affinity and transactivation potential, corresponding to variations in AHR sensitivity to ligands among species59. Understanding the differences in sensitivity to DLCs among fish species is crucial for risk assessment. While fish are generally among the most sensitive vertebrates to DLC exposure, significant interspecies variability exists20, 25, 60. Currently, only a limited number of fish species have been extensively studied for AHR sensitivity to dioxins. As a benthic teleost, the tiger puffer is particularly vulnerable to DLC bioaccumulation; however, little is known about the sensitivity of truAHRs. Therefore, to enhance risk assessment for dioxin-like compounds and promote species conservation, this study further investigated the relative sensitivity of truAHRs.

  In this study, we assessed the transactivation potency and sensitivity of the tiger puffer AHR to the classical dioxin congener TCDD and compared these sensitivities with those of AHR in other species. Following exposure to TCDD, the ratio of firefly to Renilla luciferase exhibited a concentration-dependent increase, plateauing at the highest concentrations of compounds (Fig. 6). Given the differences in slopes and maximum responses among the concentration–response curves, multiple data points (EC20, EC50, and EC80) were extracted for further analysis (Table 2). Relative Sensitivity (ReS) is defined as the ratio of the EC50 of dreAHR2-dreARNT1 to the EC50 of the species AHR of interest. Regarding the AHR1 subfamily, the EC50 value for truAHR1a was measured at 0.30±0.10 nM, and for truAHR1b, it was 0.32±0.20 nM. (Table 2 and Fig. 6). With a relative sensitivity of 10.47 (Table 2), truAHR1a exhibited the highest sensitivity among the examined subforms, and within the same order as the truAHR1b (9.81), truAHR2a (3.20), truAHR2b (1.20), truAHR2c (7.30), and dreAHR2 (1.00 for dreAHR2-dreARNT2 pair). Additionally, TCDD demonstrated a significant transactivation effect on the truAHR1a-truARNT1 complex. In contrast, while dreAHR1a is orthologous to mammalian AHR10, it is unrelated to the fish AHR1 subfamily and remains non-functional in response to TCDD exposure36, 61. The EC50 for truAHR1b (relative sensitivity = 9.81) was 0.32 ± 0.20 nM, whereas dreAHR1b showed much lower sensitivity, with EC50 values of 33.30 ± 13.37 nM (relative sensitivity = 0.09) for the dreAHR1b–dreARNT1 pair and 27.05 ± 18.51 nM (relative sensitivity = 0.12) for the dreAHR1b–dreARNT2 pair. Thus, truAHR1b is approximately 80–110 times (≈ two orders of magnitude) more sensitive than dreAHR1b. Similarly, in the common carp, a species within the same family as zebrafish, AHR1b was reported to have an EC50 of 18.79±6.78 nM, which is comparable to that of dreAHR1b15. This indicates that reduced sensitivity of AHR1b has only been observed in these two closely-related cyprinid fish species, highlighting the evolutionary conservation of AHR across species (Fig. 1A shows close AHR phylogenetic relation of common carp and zebrafish). Evidence adding to this conservation is that the red seabream62 (Pagrus major), a species related to Takifugu rubripes within the evolutionary clade Eupercaria (Fig. 1A), also shows greater sensitivity of AHR1 subfamily than the AHR2 as seen in Takifugu rubripes (Table 2). This special conservation feature of red seabream AHR1 in ‘sensitivity’ seems to be just one of those conservation features, in addition to the conservation of greater AHR1 expression in the brain as seen in Takifugu rubripes, Pagrus major, and other fishes beyond the clade Eupercaria37, 39, 40, 46. This could suggest a conserved physiological role of AHR1 across related fish species.

  In the AHR2 subfamily of tiger puffer, the EC50 values for truAHR2a, truAHR2b, and truAHR2c were found to be 0.98±0.63 nM, 2.62±2.48 nM, and 0.43±0.22 nM, respectively (Table 2 and Figs. 6C, 6D, and 6E). The relative sensitivities of truAHR2a (3.20) and truAHR2b (1.20) were comparable to those of dreAHR2, which exhibited relative sensitivities of 0.55 for the dreAHR2-dreARNT1 pair and 1.00 for the dreAHR2-dreARNT2 pair (Table 2). As the most highly expressed AHR2a subform in tiger puffer, truAHR2a showed slightly greater sensitivity than the most abundantly expressed AHR2 subform in zebrafish. Phylogenetic analysis indicated a close evolutionary relationship between olaAHR2b and truAHR2b (Fig. 1A), with truAHR2b relative sensitivity (1.20) being similar to that of olaAHR2b (1.03). Notably, within medaka species, olaAHR2b has the highest sensitivity among subforms, while truAHR2b is the least sensitive subform in tiger puffer. The relative sensitivity of truAHR2c is 7.30, slightly higher than that of truAHR2a and truAHR2b. While this high sensitivity makes truAHR2c a valuable reporter for DLCs screening, no evidence currently links this subform to dioxin-induced toxicity in tiger puffer. Given that knockout studies in other teleosts have established the dominant role of AHR2 family members in dioxin-mediated toxicities, subform-specific functional experiments—such as targeted knock-down or CRISPR/Cas9 knockout—will be required to determine whether truAHR2c actually drives adverse effects in tiger puffer63, 64. These findings indicate potential species-specific variations in the sensitivity of a given subform across different organisms, suggesting that the same subform can exhibit completely opposite sensitivities in different species. The sensitivity differences among different species indicate that results from one species cannot be simply extrapolated to another species. This also underscores the importance of considering species-specificity in toxicological studies.

  The data presented above provide the EC50 values and sensitivity of truAHRs in cooperation with truARNT1, comparing these findings with two model organisms: zebrafish and medaka. This comparison highlights that AHR sensitivity is both species- and subtype- specific. Studies have shown that the EC50 value for Atlantic cod AHR2a is greater than 10 nM, while the AHRs in Atlantic salmon and White sturgeon exhibit significantly higher sensitivity, with EC50 values below 1 nM or as low as 0.1 nM42, 43, 45. Therefore, analysis of EC50 and ReS data indicates that the sensitivity of fish AHRs can vary by one to two orders of magnitude. Previous studies on model fish species have shown that AHR2 is primarily responsible for mediating dioxin toxicity, with AHR1 contributing minimally31, 36, 65. For instance, both zebrafish and medaka AHR2s exhibit greater or similar sensitivity to TCDD, while in truAHR, the truAHR1 subfamily shows significantly greater sensitivity than truAHR2 subfamily. However, this observed sensitivity difference alone cannot substantiate that truAHR1 drives DLC toxicity in vivo, as functional validation remains essential. Moreover, AHR sensitivity varies widely among other fish species, highlighting the specificity and complexity of AHR-mediated responses to DLCs across different species. Consequently, detailed characterization of AHRs in specific fish species is essential for accurate dioxin risk assessments and wildlife conservation efforts, providing a more comprehensive understanding of sensitivity profiles.


### Ligand binding assays and homology modeling of truAHRs with TCDD docking
  As shown in Figs. 7A–E, we assessed the specific binding of [3H]TCDD to five truAHRs (truAHR1a, truAHR1b, truAHR2a, truAHR2b, and truAHR2c) using unlabeled proteins and velocity sedimentation analysis. The five truAHR proteins expressed at similar levels, as determined by labeling with [35S]methionine (not shown). The [3H]TCDD-binding profiles for each AHR exhibited peaks around fraction 15 that was not present in the absence of AHR (unprogrammed lysate), evidence that all truAHRs can specifically bind to [3H]TCDD. The slight differences in peak height among the five AHR subforms likely reflect variations in ligand affinity and slight differences in protein expression in the in vitro transcription and translation system. While there are slight differences in peak height and positioning, the overall trends are consistent, highlighting the commonality in their binding behavior. These findings enhance our understanding of the functional similarities and differences in ligand binding among AHRs.

  The sensitivity of tiger puffer to DLCs may be linked to specific key domains within its AHR. Previous studies have indicated that the sensitivity of avian species to DLCs correlates with the amino acid composition at positions 324 and 380 in the LBD of AHR123, 66. Furthermore, white sturgeon exhibits 3- to 30-fold greater sensitivity to DLC exposure compared to lake sturgeon concerning AHR2 activation, but not AHR1. This variation in AHR2 activation sensitivity is thought to stem from the differing amino acids at position 388, with white sturgeon having Ala-388 and lake sturgeon possessing Thr-38820. Additionally, seabream AHR is more inclined to mediate responses to 2,3,4,7,8-PeCDF, potentially due to the presence of Ile-322 and Ala-378 in rsAHR1, as well as Ile-317 and Ala-373 in rsAHR262. Different ligands also influence the binding interactions with AHR. Research has shown that FICZ (6-formylindolo [3,2-b] carbazole) and TCDD activate avian AHR1 through distinct interaction patterns, making FICZ a more effective AHR1 agonist than TCDD in birds and some mammals67, 68. Therefore, it is essential to study the key domains of truAHR in detail. Understanding the binding patterns and interactions between TCDD and AHR will help explain the observed sensitivity differences. To gain a deeper understanding of the interaction between TCDD and AHR, we explored the molecular mechanisms underlying TCDD binding, identified the key residues involved, and conducted a comparative analysis of the binding modes across different truAHR subforms. Molecular modeling and docking studies revealed similar binding affinities of TCDD to all truAHRs (Figs. 7F–J). The predicted binding affinities for the optimal binding modes ranged from −9.666 to −9.714 kcal/mol. Among the subforms, truAHR1b exhibited the highest affinity (−9.714 kcal/mol), followed by truAHR1a (−9.695 kcal/mol), truAHR2b (−9.694 kcal/mol), truAHR2c (−9.695 kcal/mol), and truAHR2a (−9.666 kcal/mol). Interaction analysis of TCDD with truAHRs revealed consistent hydrophobic and π-stacking interactions across all subforms. In truAHR1a, key hydrophobic residues involved in TCDD binding were Phe-290, Ile-320, and Phe-346, with distances of 3.91 Å, 3.36 Å, and 3.68 Å, respectively. Similar interactions were observed at corresponding positions in truAHR2a, truAHR2b, and truAHR2c, involving conserved residues, though with slight variations in distances (Table S2). Analysis also identified π-stacking interactions in truAHR1a and truAHR1b, with T-stacking at His-286 and P-stacking at Phe-290 (Table S3). These patterns were mirrored in truAHR2a (His-285 and Phe-289), truAHR2b (His-315 and Phe-319), and truAHR2c (His-277 and Phe-281), underscoring the role of π-stacking in stabilizing TCDD binding. The CASTp analysis revealed differences in pocket volumes among the truAHR LBDs. TruAHR1a and truAHR1b exhibited the largest pocket volumes (both are 121 Å3), followed by truAHR2c (119 Å3). truAHR2b (116 Å3), and truAHR2a (115 Å3). The larger binding pockets of truAHR1a and truAHR1b may provide greater ligand flexibility and interaction potential, potentially explaining their increased sensitivity observed in experiments assays. Conversely, the smaller truAHR2a and truAHR2b pockets could restrict ligand conformational freedom, resulting in lower sensitivity despite comparable binding affinities. TruAHR2c, with its intermediate pocket volume, displayed intermediate sensitivity.

  Although the key residues involved in hydrophobic and π-stacking interactions between TCDD and truAHRs are conserved and bind in a similar manner, variations in affinity and sensitivity suggest the influence of additional factors on the response. For instance, differences in amino acid sequences, specific residue, and subtle alterations in the local environment of the binding pocket may affect ligand stabilization and receptor conformation. Thus, factors such as receptor flexibility and specific sequence variations may play a role in determining truAHR sensitivity.


## Conclusion
  Dioxins and DLCs pose serious environmental risks, making it essential to understand their toxicological effects for accurate ecological risk assessments. Investigating AHR pathways across species is crucial for uncovering species-specific sensitivities to DLCs. Current assessments focus on the tiger puffer and a few model organisms, highlighting the need to study AHR functions in a wider range of species. This study characterized five truAHRs along with two truARNTs. Similar to other teleosts, the truAHR2 subfamily had greater abundance, with truAHR2a showing the highest expression across tissues, while the truAHR1 subfamily had lower levels. Additionally, truARNT1 was more highly expressed than truARNT2 in all tissues, indicating a relatively conserved expression pattern. Functional assays revealed that all truAHRs are active. Compared to truARNT2, truARNT1 more effectively promotes TCDD-induced transcriptional activation by AHR. Sensitivity calculations revealed that truAHR1a was the most sensitive to TCDD, displaying much higher sensitivity than observed in zebrafish and medaka. Notably, sensitivity differences across species may range by one to two orders of magnitude. All truAHRs showed specific binding of [3H]TCDD. The peak values indicated differences in binding activity among the truAHRs, but the overall trends were consistent. Docking studies revealed that TCDD binds to truAHRs with similar predicted affinities, involving conserved key hydrophobic residues conferring the molecular contact with TCDD. Despite similar binding affinities, truAHR sensitivity to TCDD varied across subforms, likely due to other factors, demonstrating subform-specific differences. In summary, this research provides a comprehensive functional analysis of tiger puffer AHRs, contributing valuable sensitivity data for fish AHRs. This information can be incorporated into AOP frameworks, facilitating the linkage of experimental data to systems-level biology and thereby improving the accuracy of ecological risk assessments for dioxin-like compounds.
