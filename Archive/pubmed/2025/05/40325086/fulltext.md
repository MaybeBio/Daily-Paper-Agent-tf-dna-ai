# Kratom leaf extracts exert hypolipidaemic effects via the modulation of PCSK9 and LDLR pathways in HepG2 cells


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-00711-1.


## Introduction
  Hypercholesterolemia is characterized by elevated serum levels of plasma cholesterol and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and is a major risk factor for the development of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs)1,2. Statins, also known as hydroxymethylglutaryl coenzyme A reductase (HMGCR) inhibitors, are widely used to treat patients with elevated plasma LDL-C levels. Statins lead to reduced intracellular cholesterol levels and increased synthesis of proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) via the stimulation of nuclear sterol regulatory element-binding protein 2 (SREBP-2) and hepatocyte nuclear-factor 1 alpha (HNF-1α)3. However, increased PCSK9 levels after statin treatment could reduce the lipid-lowering efficacy4. Currently, the PCSK9 inhibitors alirocumab, evolocumab, and inclisiran, which have been approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA), are used as lipid-lowering drugs for the treatment of patients with primary hypercholesterolemia or severe hypercholesterolemia5–7. However, PCSK9 inhibitors are expensive and may lead to an injection site reaction8. Several bioactive compounds and medicinal plants, such as berberine, kaempferol, quercetin, p-coumaric acid, pinostrobin, epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG), allicin, capsaicin, and extracts of pigeon pea (Cajanus cajan (L.) Millsp.) leaves, Welsh onion, Penthorum chinense, and black raspberry, are natural PCSK9 inhibitors9–11 and might be used as alternative lipid-lowering agents used alone or in combination with statins to alleviate hypercholesterolemia.

  Mitragyna speciosa (Korth.) Havil., commonly known as “kratom” in Thailand, is a tropical tree native to Southeast Asia. People in Thailand and Malaysia chew kratom leaves to reduce fatigue and increase their tolerance to long, hard work under heat and sunlight12. However, kratom has been reported to have psychoactive and addictive effects13. In addition, overdose and coingestion of kratom with other substances, e.g., diphenhydramine and O-desmethyltramadol, can cause adverse effects14,15. Nevertheless, kratom also has pharmacological properties and has been widely used for treating opium withdrawal and for pain relief16. Moreover, kratom has several therapeutic properties, including analgesic, antinociceptive, anti-inflammatory, antidiarrhoeal, antidepressant, antioxidant, antibacterial, hypoglycaemic, and hypolipidaemic effects17–19. Thus, the dose, duration, and ingestion of kratom in combination with other drugs should be controlled for safety. A previous study revealed that kratom users had significantly lower total cholesterol (TC) and LDL-C levels than did healthy subjects18. Similarly, other studies have demonstrated that kratom use is associated with a smaller waist circumference, low triglyceride (TG) levels, and elevated high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels20,21. Moreover, an in vivo study demonstrated that TC, TG, and LDL-C levels were significantly decreased in kratom-treated diabetic rats, whereas HDL-C levels were significantly increased22. Kratom leaves consist of a major active compound, namely, mitragynine, as well as flavonoids (e.g., quercetin and rutin), tannins, triterpenoids, and other phenolic compounds23. Previous studies have shown that quercetin downregulates PCSK9 expression24. In addition, quercetin and rutin upregulate LDLR expression24–26. It could be hypothesized that kratom has a hypolipidaemic effect as a result of the bioactive compounds in its leaves. Although evidence and hypotheses support the hypolipidaemic effect of kratom, the underlying molecular mechanism remains unknown. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effects of leaf extracts from red-vein and white-vein (Pho Thong) strains of kratom on hypolipidaemia in HepG2 cells.


## Materials and methods

### Materials
  HepG2 cells were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, VA, USA). Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM), fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin-streptomycin, cell dissociation solution (CDS), and phosphate-buffer saline (PBS) were purchased from Gibco (Grand Island, NY, USA). Mitragynine (Lipomed, Inc., Switzerland), quercetin (ChemFaces, Wuhan, China), and rutin (Acros Organics, NJ, USA) were purchased. Formic acid, acetonitrile, methanol, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT), bovine serum albumin (BSA), and atorvastatin were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Co. (St. Louis, MO, USA). Total RNA Mini Kit was obtained from Geneaid (Taipei, Taiwan). The iScript™ Reverse Transcription Supermix for RT-qPCR, Bradford reagent, nitrocellulose membrane, and Clarity Western ECL Substrate were purchased from Bio-Rad (Hercules, CA, USA). The Luna® Universal qPCR Master Mix was purchased from New England Biolabs Inc. (Ipswich, MA, USA). Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG, BODIPY1-FL-LDL, DRAQ5, and anti-HNF-1α (MA5-17267) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Rockford, IL, USA). Anti-LDLR (ab204941), anti-PCSK9 (ab181142), anti-SREBP-2 (ab194667), anti-β-actin (ab8226), and HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (ab205719) were purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (7074P2), cell lysis buffer, and phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA).


### Plant materials and extraction
  Leaves from the red-vein strain Mitragyna speciosa (Korth.) Havil. were harvested from Tha Sae district, Chumphon Province, Thailand. Leaves from the white-vein (Pho Thong) strain were harvested from Thasala district, Nakhon Si Thammarat Province, Thailand. The plants were identified by Assoc. Prof. Dr. Sukanya Dej-adisai from the Department of Pharmacognosy and Pharmaceutical Botany, Faculty of Pharmaceutical Sciences, Prince of Songkla University, Thailand. Voucher specimens of the red-vein (SKP 165 13 19 01) and white-vein (Pho Thong) (SKP 165 13 19 02) strains were deposited at the School of Allied Health Sciences, Walailak University, Thailand. The plants were dried at 50 °C and ground into a fine powder using a mixer. Subsequently, 20 g of kratom powder was soaked with 200 ml of methanol in bottles for 2 days at room temperature, and this process was repeated three times. Each sample was subsequently filtered through gauze cloth, and the filtered liquid was concentrated using a rotary evaporator (N-1200 Series, Eyela, USA) at 50 °C in a water bath at 37 °C for 1 week. The obtained concentrates of the red-vein and white-vein (Pho Thong) strains were stored at 4 °C.


### Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis
  The bioactive compounds in kratom leaf extracts were identified using LC-MS/MS (Agilent Technologies 6490 Triple Quad LC/MS, CA, USA)27. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (solvent B). The composition of the mobile phase at different time intervals was set as 0–10 min, 5% B; 10–15 min, 95% B; and 15–20 min, 5% B, with a flow rate of 0.5 ml/min. The analysis was performed using the ZORBAX 300Extend-C18 (4.6 × 150 mm, 3.55 μm; Agilent) at 35 °C. Standards and samples were injected with a volume of 10 µl. The standards mitragynine, quercetin, and rutin were analysed. The precursor (m/z) and product (m/z) ions of mitragynine, quercetin, and rutin were 399.20 → 174.10, 303.05 → 153.00, and 611.10 → 303.10, respectively.


### Cell preparation and compound treatment
  The cell line was maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. HepG2 cells were cultured for 24 h in serum-starved medium containing 0.5% FBS. Then, cells were pretreated for 24 h with the following substances: vehicle (0.1% DMSO), leaf extracts from the red-vein strain of kratom (0.125 and 0.250 mg/ml), leaf extracts from the white-vein strain of kratom (Pho Thong) (0.125 and 0.250 mg/ml), and atorvastatin (10 µM). Subsequently, real-time RT-qPCR, Western blotting, and flow cytometric analyses, as well as BODIPY‑LDL uptake assays, were conducted.


### Cell viability assay
  Cell viability was measured via the MTT assay. HepG2 cells were cultured in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well to reach 80–90% confluence. The cells were treated with leaf extracts from the red-vein strain of kratom (0, 0.0625, 0.125, 0.250, 0.50, and 1 mg/ml), leaf extracts from the white-vein strain of kratom (Pho Thong) (0, 0.0625, 0.125, 0.250, 0.50, and 1 mg/ml), and mitragynine (0.0625, 1.25, 2.5, 5, and 10 µM) at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 24 h. After treatment, MTT (1 mg/ml) was added to the cells, which were subsequently incubated at 37 °C for 4 hours. Insoluble formazan crystals were dissolved using DMSO. The absorbance was measured at 560 nm using a microplate reader (Multiskan FC Microplate Photometer, Thermo Scientific, USA).


### Real-time reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis
  HepG2 cells were grown at 7 × 105 cells/well to reach 80–90% confluence in 6-well plates. Total RNA was extracted from the cells using the Total RNA Mini Kit. RNA was converted to cDNA using iScript™ Reverse Transcription Supermix for RT-qPCR. The quantitative real-time PCR analysis of LDLR, PCSK9, SREBP-2, and HNF-1α mRNA expression was performed with an Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). The reaction mixtures contained cDNA, primers (Table 1)28–30, and Luna® Universal qPCR Master Mix. The relative mRNA expression levels were analysed using the 2ΔΔCt method and normalized to the level of GAPDH.

### Western blotting analysis
  HepG2 cells were grown at 1 × 106 cells/well to reach 70–80% confluence in petri dishes. The cells were collected and lysed using cell lysis buffer supplemented with 1 mM PMSF. The protein concentration was determined using the Bradford protein assay. Protein samples were separated using 8% SDS-PAGE and then transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane. These membranes were incubated with specific antibodies, anti-LDLR (1:1,000), anti-PCSK9 (1:1,000), anti-SREBP-2 (1:500) anti-β-actin (1:1,000), and anti-HNF-1α (1:2,000), overnight at 4 °C. After incubation, the membranes were washed and incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (1:10,000) or HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:2,000). The Clarity Western ECL Substrate was applied to the membrane to visualize protein bands, which were subsequently detected and quantified using the ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio-Rad) to capture the chemiluminescent signal. ImageJ software was used for protein band quantification, and protein expression levels were normalized to those of β-actin.


### Flow cytometric analysis of cell-surface LDLR
  HepG2 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 106 cells/well to reach 80–90% confluence in a 6-well plate. The cells were detached using cell dissociation solution (CDS) at 1 ml/well. The cells were washed twice with PBS and incubated with PBS containing 1% bovine serum albumin at room temperature (RT) for 30 min. The cells were blocked and incubated with an anti-LDLR antibody (1:400) at RT for 1 h and then incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:1,000) at RT for 1 h. The cells were resuspended in PBS and analysed by flow cytometry (FACScan, BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). The relative fluorescence intensity was quantified by recording the fluorescence of 10,000 events.


### BODIPY‑LDL uptake assay
  HepG2 cells were grown at 5 × 105 cells/well to reach 50–60% confluence on cover slips in 6-well plates. The cells were washed twice with PBS. The cells on cover slips were subsequently incubated with serum-free media containing 5 µg/ml BODIPY1-FL-LDL at 37 °C for 4 h, followed by nuclear staining with 5 µM DRAQ5 at 37 °C for 15 min in the dark. The cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at RT for 15 min. BODIPY-LDL uptake was visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica SP5 II Laser Scanning Confocal Microscope). ImageJ software was used to quantify the relative fluorescence intensity.


### Molecular docking studies
  The 3D structures of LDLR EGF-A (PDB: 3BPS) and PCSK9 (PDB: 2P4E) were downloaded from the Protein Data Bank (http://www.rcsb.org) and processed using BIOVIA Discovery Studio (Version 2021 Client) to remove water and cofactors. Proteins were optimized using the AutoDock 1.5.6 program by adding hydrogen atoms, assigning Kollman charges, and converting to PDBQT format. The ligands, including mitragynine, quercetin, rutin, and atorvastatin, were obtained from the PubChem database in SDF format and converted to PDB and PDBQT formats using BIOVIA Discovery Studio and AutoDock 1.5.6. Protein-ligand interactions were simulated using AutoDock 1.5.6. The grid box sizes for the X, Y, and Z dimensions and spacing were set as shown in Table 2. The results revealed that lower binding energy values were correlated with stronger protein-ligand interactions, indicating favourable binding.

### Statistical analyses
  Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 23.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Graphs were created using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software LLC, San Diego, CA, USA). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the data. The data were analysed by one-way ANOVA using Bonferroni post hoc correction for parametric factors or the Kruskal-Wallis and Mann-Whitney U tests for nonparametric factors. A P value of < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

### Effects of leaf extracts from red-vein and white-vein (Pho Thong) strains of Kratom and Mitragynine on the viability of HepG2 cells
  To investigate the cytotoxic effects of leaf extracts from red-vein and white-vein (Pho Thong) strains of kratom, as well as mitragynine, HepG2 cells were treated with a vehicle (0.1% DMSO), leaf extracts from the red-vein strain of kratom (0, 0.0625, 0.125, 0.250, 0.50, and 1 mg/ml), leaf extracts from the white-vein strain of kratom (Pho Thong) (0, 0.0625, 0.125, 0.250, 0.50, and 1 mg/ml), and mitragynine (0.0625, 1.25, 2.5, 5, and 10 µM), and cell viability was measured via the MTT assay. No compounds had cytotoxic effects on HepG2 cells (cell viability > 80% at all concentrations) (Fig. 2).

### Effects of leaf extracts from red-vein and white-vein (Pho Thong) strains of Kratom on the expression of LDLR and PCSK9 and the uptake of LDL in HepG2 cells
  To investigate the effects of kratom leaf extracts on LDLR mRNA and protein expression, RT-qPCR and Western blotting analyses were performed, respectively. The results revealed that leaf extracts from the white-vein (Pho Thong) strain of kratom (0.25 mg/ml) but not the red-vein strain of kratom significantly elevated LDLR mRNA expression (Fig. 3A). In contrast, leaf extracts from both red-vein (0.25 mg/ml) and white-vein (Pho Thong) (0.25 mg/ml) strains of kratom demonstrated a significantly increased mature LDLR protein expression of approximately 2.19-fold (p < 0.0001) and 2.12-fold (p < 0.0001), respectively, in comparison with that in the control group (Figs. 4A, S1). The effects of leaf extracts from red-vein and white-vein (Pho Thong) strains of kratom on the cell-surface LDLR level were further investigated by flow cytometric analysis. Leaf extracts from both red-vein (0.25 mg/ml) and white-vein (Pho Thong) (0.25 mg/ml) strains of kratom significantly increased the cell-surface LDLR by 229.9% (p < 0.0001) and 175.3% (p < 0.005), respectively, compared with that of the control group (Fig. 5). The effects of kratom leaf extracts on the activity of LDLR and LDL uptake were also observed by a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) (Fig. 6A). The leaf extracts from red-vein (0.25 mg/ml) and white-vein (Pho Thong) (0.25 mg/ml) strains of kratom significantly increased LDL uptake by approximately 2.86-fold (p < 0.0005) and 2.61-fold (p < 0.005), respectively, compared with the control group (Fig. 6B). These findings suggest that leaf extracts from red-vein and white-vein (Pho Thong) strains of kratom contribute to an increased expression of cell-surface LDLR protein and enhanced LDL uptake into HepG2 cells.

### Effects of leaf extracts from red-vein and white-vein (Pho Thong) strains of Kratom on SREBP-2 and HNF-1α expression in HepG2 cells
  The expression of the LDLR and PCSK9 genes is regulated by SREBP-23,31. Moreover, PCSK9 gene expression is strongly induced by HNF-1α32. To investigate the potential induction of LDLR and PCSK9 expression by these transcription factors, the effects of kratom leaf extracts on the expression of SREBP-2 and HNF-1α were further investigated. The leaf extracts from red-vein and white-vein (Pho Thong) strains of kratom did not affect SREBP-2 or HNF-1α mRNA expression (Fig. 3C and D). However, leaf extracts from red-vein and white-vein (Pho Thong) strains of kratom showed significantly decreased protein expression levels of SREBP-2 (0.57-fold and 0.67-fold, respectively) (Fig. 4C, S1) and HNF-1α (0.29-fold and 0.59-fold, respectively) (Figs. 4D, S1) compared with those in the control group. Moreover, atorvastatin (10 µM), a positive control, significantly increased SREBP-2 and HNF-1α mRNA and protein expression (Figs. 3C and D and 4C and D).

## Discussion
  This is the first study to demonstrate the effects of leaf extracts from red-vein, and white-vein (Pho Thong) strains of kratom on hypolipidaemia via an increase in LDLR expression as well as a decrease in PCSK9 protein expression via the downregulation of the transcription factors SREBP-2 and HNF-1α in HepG2 cells. Leaf extracts from both red-vein, and white-vein (Pho Thong) strains of kratom were also associated with increased LDL uptake into HepG2 cells. This may further lead to a reduction in LDL-C levels. Similarly, other medicinal plants and bioactive compounds, including berberine33, pterostilbene34, aloe-emodin35, piceatannol36, and butein37, have also been found to decrease PCSK9 expression via the downregulation of SREBP-2 and HNF-1α.

  PCSK9 plays a critical role as a posttranslational regulator of cell-surface LDLR. It binds to the epidermal growth factor repeat-A (EGF-A) of LDLR and leads to the degradation of LDLR in lysosomes, resulting in increased levels of LDL-C in the circulation38. In this study, PCSK9 expression was significantly decreased after treatment with kratom leaf extracts. The decreased PCSK9 expression may be due to the downregulation of SREBP-2 and HNF-1α. SREBP-2 and HNF-1α are transcription factors that regulate PCSK9 gene expression by binding to the SRE and HNF1 sites in the PCSK9 promoter, respectively3. SREBP-2 also regulates LDLR gene expression by binding SRE sites in the promoters of these genes31.

  In this study, although SREBP-2 was downregulated, LDLR expression was still increased. It could be speculated that kratom leaf extracts may increase LDLR mRNA or protein stability. Furthermore, a synergistic reduction in HNF-1α and SREBP-2 may strongly repress PCSK9 expression and prevent the lysosomal degradation of LDLR, thereby increasing LDLR levels in hepatocytes. A previous study showed that berberine increased LDLR expression through increased LDLR mRNA stability39. In addition, LDLR degradation can be accelerated by an inducible degrader of LDLR (IDOL), an E3 ubiquitin ligase40. Thus, the effects of kratom leaf extracts on hypolipidaemia in relation to LDLR mRNA stability and the inhibition of IDOL should be further clarified.

  In this study, LC-MS/MS analysis revealed that leaf extracts from red-vein, and white-vein (Pho Thong) strains of kratom consisted of mitragynine, quercetin, and rutin. Our findings revealed that the concentration of mitragynine in kratom leaves ranged from 38.61 to 39.79 mg/g (3.86–3.97% w/w dry leaf weight). Similarly, a previous report revealed that the concentration of mitragynine was 36.70–63.60 mg/g (3.67–6.36% w/w dry leaf weight)41. Another study reported that the mitragynine concentration was 0.31–4.94% w/w dry leaf weight42. The variation in the concentration of mitragynine has been reported to depend on the season and geography42. Mitragynine is a major alkaloid (66% w/w total alkaloids) found in kratom leaves. Nevertheless, other alkaloids, e.g., paynantheine (9% w/w total alkaloids), speciogynine (7% w/w total alkaloids), and 7-hydroxymitragynine (2% w/w total alkaloids), as well as other compounds, e.g., flavonoids, terpenoid saponins, and polyphenols, are also found in kratom leaves23,43.

  In this study, we first showed that mitragynine upregulated LDLR but downregulated PCSK9 protein expression. Molecular docking studies also revealed that mitragynine has a strong binding affinity for the EGF-A domain of LDLR, suggesting its potential role in inhibiting or interrupting the binding between PCSK9 and LDLR. Moreover, quercetin also exhibited the highest binding affinity to the PCSK9 protein according to molecular docking, indicating its ability to inhibit PCSK9 function. However, atorvastatin and rutin had lower binding affinities for both the EGF-A domain of LDLR and PCSK9. These findings suggest that atorvastatin and rutin may have a lower ability to interfere with the binding between PCSK9 and LDLR. In fact, the major mechanism by which statins reduce hepatic cholesterol synthesis is the inhibition of hydroxymethylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR)44. Taken together, these findings suggest that the hypolipidaemic effect of kratom leaf extracts may be a consequence of the bioactive constituents, particularly mitragynine, and quercetin in kratom leaves.

  Mitragynine and 7-hydroxymitragynine are partial agonists of the µ-opioid receptor and antagonists of the κ- and δ-opioid receptors45,46, which have effects similar to those of cocaine and opium47,48. Chewing 1–5 g of raw kratom leaves daily (∼4 leaves daily) had a stimulant effect and could help workers resist fatigue. Chewing 5–15 g of raw kratom leaves daily (∼13 leaves daily) could produce opioid-like effects, e.g., sedation, analgesia, constipation and euphoria. However, chewing more than 15 g of raw kratom leaves daily (> ∼14 leaves daily) can cause adverse effects, e.g., drowsiness, respiratory distress, liver damage, confusion, stupor, and coma49–51. Consequently, the safety or toxicity risk of kratom for medical or pharmacological purposes should be strongly considered. Although kratom has hypolipidaemic effects, the recommended amount of kratom consumed daily to reduce hyperlipidaemia is still unknown. The optimal dosage of kratom leaf consumption for the effects of hypolipidaemia should be further investigated.

  In conclusion, leaf extracts from red-vein and white-vein (Pho Thong) strains of kratom, which contain several bioactive compounds, exhibited hypolipidaemic effects because of decreased PCSK9 and increased LDLR expression through the modulation of SREBP2 and HNF-1α (Fig. 9). These findings suggest that leaf extracts from red-vein and white-vein (Pho Thong) strains of kratom, as well as mitragynine, could serve as potential supplements for ameliorating hypercholesterolemia.
