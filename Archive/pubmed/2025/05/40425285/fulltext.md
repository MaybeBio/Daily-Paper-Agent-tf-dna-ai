# Nrf2‐Mediated Ferroptosis Is Involved in Berberine‐Induced Alleviation of Diabetic Kidney Disease


## ABSTRACT
  Diabetic kidney disease (DKD) is the most common and serious complication of diabetes mellitus. Currently, there is a lack of safe and effective preventive strategies for DKD. The study aimed to explore the preventive effects and potential mechanisms of berberine (BBR) against DKD. In the in vivo experiments, we established a DKD rat model induced by the combination of high‐fat diet and streptozotocin to investigate the preventive effect of BBR on DKD. Subsequently, in vitro experiments using human renal tubular epithelial cells (HK‐2 cells) were performed to further validate the effect of BBR on renal tubular epithelial cell ferroptosis induced by advanced glycation end products (AGEs). In vivo, we found that BBR improved renal function and attenuated inflammatory cell infiltration, podocyte injury, and iron deposition in renal tissue in DKD rats. In addition, in vitro experiments showed that BBR attenuated HK‐2 cell ferroptosis induced by AGEs. We further identified Nrf2 as a direct binding target of BBR by molecular docking and Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR). Then, immunohistochemistry and Western Blot results demonstrated that BBR could activate the Nrf2 pathway, initiate the endogenous antioxidant system, and inhibit the occurrence of AGEs‐induced ferroptosis. Moreover, silencing of Nrf2 by siRNA technology eliminated the protective effect of BBR on AGEs‐induced ferroptosis. Collectively, our results supported that BBR could inhibit oxidative stress and ferroptosis by targeting activation of the Nrf2 pathway, thereby delaying the disease progression of DKD, providing a new scientific basis and perspective for the prevention and treatment of DKD.


## Introduction
  Globally, over 840 million individuals suffer from chronic kidney disease (CKD), which is characterized by proteinuria and decreased estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) (2022). CKD is primarily caused by diabetic kidney disease (DKD) and affects about 40% of type 2 diabetes patients and 30% of type 1 diabetes patients (Alicic et al. 2017). The global incidence of diabetes mellitus (DM) is steadily rising, with a projected increase in DKD cases from 357 to 783 million over the next 24 years. Over 40% of these cases are likely to progress to end‐stage renal disease (ESRD) (Kitada et al. 2017).

  The progression of DKD involves intricate mechanisms, including oxidative stress and persistent inflammation induced by hyperglycemia, enhanced synthesis of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), and local renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system (RAAS) activation (Qin, Peng, et al. 2019).

  Recent evidence reveals that programmed cell death, including ferroptosis, contributes to the pathogenesis of various renal diseases, such as acute kidney injury (AKI) and DKD. Ferroptosis represents an iron‐dependent form of cell death induced by lipid peroxidation (LPO). Notably, studies have shown that nuclear factor erythroid 2‐related factor (Nrf2) is an important upstream gene in ferroptosis regulation. Nrf2 is a key regulator of antioxidant responses and plays a critical role in the protection of kidneys from oxidative stress (Xing et al. 2021). Additionally, Nrf2 regulates tubular iron metabolism and is involved in renal tubular iron handling (Liu et al. 2023). Furthermore, Nrf2 modulates cellular iron metabolism, controls cellular iron levels and Gpx4 production, and protects cells against oxidative damage after ferroptosis (Song and Long 2020).

  Identifying and developing targeted drugs to alleviate DKD holds significance for treating and delaying DKD disease progression. Huanglian, a traditional Chinese medicine, is broadly included in formulations for type 2 diabetes treatment. Its main constituent is BBR, which possesses hypoglycemic, hypolipidemic, and anti‐inflammatory properties (Kong et al. 2022). Systematic analyses have further demonstrated that BBR regulates blood glucose and lipid metabolism (Ju et al. 2018; Ye et al. 2021). Our previous study revealed that BBR inhibits epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) in renal tubular cells from DKD mice (Yang et al. 2017), although the underlying mechanism remains incompletely understood. Considering the significant anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant effects of BBR and the importance of oxidative stress in DKD development, BBR was hypothesized to mitigate DKD progression by targeting Nrf2 to modulate oxidative stress and prevent ferroptosis. Consequently, the molecular binding mode of BBR and Nrf2 was predicted by molecular docking. Subsequently, an AGEs‐induced HK‐2 cell model and a high‐fat‐high‐glucose combined with streptozotocin (STZ)‐induced DKD model were established in rats to test the above hypothesis using pharmacological experiments and molecular assays.


## Materials and Methods

### Rats and Study Design
  Animal experiments were carried out at the Experimental Animal Center of Beijing University of Chinese Medicine (Ethical License for Animal Experimentation No. BUCM‐4‐2021062801‐2071). A total of 40 male SPF‐SD rats (6–8 weeks old, 160–220 g) were provided by Beijing Vitong Lihua Biotechnology, Beijing, China (license No. SCXK [Beijing] 2016‐0006). The rats were housed at 23°C ± 2°C with 45%–65% relative humidity under a 12‐h photoperiod. The rats were kept under standard laboratory conditions and were allowed free access to water and rodent chow.

  Acclimatization was carried out for 7 days prior to study initiation. The 40 rats were randomly divided into the control (n = 10) and DKD model (n = 30) groups. Control rats received standard feed from Beijing Vitong Lihua Biotechnology (No. SCXK [Beijing] 2015‐0015), while the DKD model group was fed a sugar‐and‐fat enriched diet from the same company (No. SCXK [Beijing] ST06100226D). After 8 weeks on the sugar‐and‐fat enriched diet, DKD rats were administered streptozotocin (Pudong Macklin, Shanghai, China) at 35 mg/kg by intraperitoneal injection. Meanwhile, control animals were intraperitoneally treated with sodium citrate buffer solution at 0.1 mol/L (pH 4.2). After 3 days, tail vein blood samples were collected by tail clipping, and blood glucose was assessed with glucose test strips (glucose dehydrogenase method). The rats that maintained blood glucose levels above 16.7 mmol/L and exhibited 24‐h urine protein levels exceeding 30 mg after 4 weeks, as evidenced by extensive proteinuria (++++), were considered successful models (Tesch and Allen 2007). A total of six rats died during the process. Therefore, 24 rats were allocated into 3 groups of 8 each, including the DKD, irbesartan (Irb), and berberine (BBR) groups. The Irb (Sinopharm H2004049, Sanofi Pharmaceutical) group received a daily dosage of 25 mg/kg, while the BBR (Batch No. B21450, Shanghai Yuanye Biological) group received 200 mg/kg/day. Both the control and DKD model groups received 10 mL/kg of deionized water orally every day for 16 weeks. Rats were weighed every 4 weeks, and their physical condition (fur, behavior, activity, diet, etc.) was monitored daily. All animals were housed in metabolic cages, and urine samples were collected for 24 h while fasting but with free access to water.


### Serological Indexes
  After 16 weeks of pharmacological intervention, each rat was anesthetized with 1% pentobarbital sodium solution (40 mg/kg; Sigma‐Aldrich) administered intraperitoneally, followed by euthanasia. Serum and kidney tissues were collected for further analysis. Serum samples were analyzed on an automated biochemistry analyzer from Huaying Biotechnology (MARIN BS300 model, Beijing MARIN Medical Devices) to determine various serum indicators, including renal function markers such as serum creatinine (Scr) and blood urea (urea). Lipid profiles were assessed by quantitating total cholesterol (TC), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL), and triglycerides (TG). Furthermore, oxidative stress markers, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), nitric oxide (NO), and reactive oxygen species (ROS), were quantified.


### Histopathological Analysis of the Renal Tissue
  Following fixation in 10% formalin solution, the kidney tissues were dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 3 μm. These sections underwent staining with H&E (Solarbio, G1140‐500) and Mallory (Solarbio, SW0280).


### Glomerular Podocyte Injury Assessment by TEM
  A total of 1 mm3 of each kidney tissue sample was added to a fixative solution containing 2.5% glutaraldehyde and osmium acid. Specimens prepared after fixation were utilized to observe the kidney's ultrastructure via transmission electron microscopy (TEM).


### Assessment of Lipid Peroxidation and Iron Content
  Kidney tissue specimens were retrieved from a −80°C freezer, and 100 mg each was utilized for tissue homogenization. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was collected, and the relevant components were analyzed with the malondialdehyde kit (MDA, Ruixin Biologics, RXWB0005), lipid peroxide kit (LPO, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, A106‐1‐2), and iron concentration detection kit (Acmec, bc1735) according to the manufacturers' instructions.


### Immunohistochemistry
  Paraffin‐embedded kidney tissue specimens were deparaffinized in xylene and were hydrated with gradient alcohol to water. Sections were then treated with 0.01% Triton X‐100 (LABLEAD, R1000‐100ML) at 37°C (10 min), followed by exposure to 3% H2O2 in methanol (Beijing Chemical Factory, 20150416) for an additional 10 min. After rinsing with PBS, antigen retrieval was performed by microwave heating (15 min). Subsequently, sections were blocked in goat serum for 1 h at 37°C and were incubated overnight with primary antibodies targeting Nrf2 (1:150, Biofriend, No. 16396‐1‐AP), Ho‐1 (1:125, Abcam, No. AB13248), and Gpx4 (1:500, Abcam, No. AB125066). The negative control group was incubated with buffer overnight. The samples were then washed with PBS and incubated with secondary antibodies in the anti‐mouse/rabbit universal immunohistochemical detection kit (Proteintech, No. KIHC‐5) for 30 min at 37°C. DAB was utilized for development, followed by hematoxylin staining (Solarbio, No. G1140‐500). Sections were dehydrated and mounted with neutral gum (Shanghai Sample Model Factory of China, No. 20120301). Positive expression was defined by the presence of brown‐yellow particles. Six randomly selected fields of view were examined under an optical microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) in each sample group.


### Molecular Docking
  The 2D structures of BBR and Fer‐1 were downloaded in the PDB format from PubChem (http://zinc.docking.org/). The structures of these compounds were optimized using the Chem3D software, and their energies were refined using the MMFF94 algorithm. The crystal structures of the Nrf2, Ho‐1, and Gpx4 proteins with ligands were selected from PDB (https://www.rcsb.org/) according to the principle of minimum resolution. Water molecules and small‐molecule ligands were removed from the target proteins and hydrogenated using the AutoDock Tools software (Scripps Research Institute, California, USA). Rotatable bonds in the compounds were defined, and the docking frames were resized according to the protein's crystal structure. The AutoDock Tools software was employed to perform virtual docking to determine the optimal binding free energy conformation of each compound for each protein. Finally, 3D models were visualized using PyMOL Win 1.7.2.1 (Schrodinger Inc., CA, USA), and 2D images were constructed with LigPlot.


### Surface Plasmon Resonance Validation
  Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) technology is widely used in analyzing the binding ability of small molecules and target proteins. A Biacore T200 instrument (GE HEALTHCARE, 8K) was used to perform SPR assay analysis to explore the affinity and kinetic properties of BBR binding to Nrf2. The Nrf2 protein was diluted to 50 μg/mL with sodium acetate, and the protein was coupled to the CM5 Sensor Chip (Cytiva, BR‐1005‐30) at a flow rate of 10 μL/min. Using 10 μM BBR as the initial concentration, BBR was diluted to six concentrations (10, 5, 2.5, 1.25, 0.625, 0.3125 μM) and coupled to the Nrf2 protein through the chip from low to high concentrations (30 μL/min, 150 s). The binding and dissociation constants were determined using the Biacore Insight evaluation software (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA), which globally fit the data to a 1:1 Langmuir binding model.


### Cell Culture and Optimal BBR Concentration for Intervention in the AGEs Damage Model
  The HK‐2 cell line (ATCC, CRL‐2190TM) was maintained in DMEM/F12 (Invitrogen, C11330500BT), comprising 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Los Angeles, CA, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin in a humid environment containing 5% CO2 at 37°C. HK‐2 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded at 5000/well in a 96‐well plate and were exposed to AGEs at 200 μg/mL (Patel et al. 2022) and various concentrations of BBR (1%, 2%, 4%, 8%, and 16%) (Zou et al. 2017). Each treatment group was plated in triplicate. Upon treatment (24 and 48 h), the culture medium was aspirated, and the CCK‐8 solution (Absin, abs50003) was added for incubation for 2 h at 37°C with 5% CO2. A UV spectrophotometer (UY‐2000, Japan) was utilized for absorbance reading at 450 nm.


### Assessment of HK‐2 Cell Viability and Structure
  CCK‐8 was employed to evaluate the blank control, AGEs, and BBR (at the optimal concentration) groups for cell viability. After 48 h of treatment, the cellular morphology and growth characteristics of each group were assessed using an inverted microscope (Olympus, TH4‐200, Japan). In addition, the ultrastructure of HK‐2 cells was assessed by TEM.


### siRNA Transfection of HK‐2 Cells
  Nrf2‐siRNA was transfected into HK‐2 cells 48 h before AGEs stimulation. To suppress Nrf2 expression, Nrf2‐siRNA was synthesized by Sangyo Biotech (Shanghai). The sequence was 5′‐GACATGGATTTGATTGACATACT‐3′, and the sequence of NC‐siRNA was 5′‐CACTTGAATCCG ACGGATTTGCA‐3′. For transfection, HK‐2 cells were placed in six‐well plates until the cells reached 60% to 70% confluence. Invitrogen siRNA transfection reagent was used to transfect Nrf2‐siRNA/NC‐siRNA into the cells at a final dose of 20 nM. Finally, the expression levels of the target proteins in the transfected cells were measured after 48 h.


### Western Blot
  For this experiment, 100 mg of kidney tissue or nested HK‐2 cells were placed in Eppendorf tubes, and RIPA lysis buffer (BIODEE, cat. no. DE5012‐100 mL) and protease inhibitor were added. The samples were homogenized, lysed on ice, and centrifuged to obtain the supernatant, and protein content was calculated using a BCA kit (Prilosec, cat. no. P1511‐1). Protein samples were separated using 5 × SDS‐PAGE loading buffer and subjected to electrophoresis at 100 V for 90 min. The PVDF membrane was then blocked with 5% milk powder for 3 h. The primary antibodies, namely Nrf2, Ho‐1, Gpx4, and β‐actin (CST, USA, cat. no. 4970S), were incubated overnight at 4°C. The next day, the membranes were washed with 1 × TBST and incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h. Detection was performed by enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL). Images were acquired using the ChemiDoc imaging system and analyzed using ImageJ software.


### Statistical Analysis
  IBM SPSS24.0 (Chicago, IL, USA) was utilized for the analysis of data reported as mean ± SEM. One‐way ANOVA was employed to compare multiple groups assuming normal distribution of the data. In case of equal variances among groups (homogeneity of variances), the Least Squares Difference (LSD) test is generally used to assess mean differences among groups. Otherwise (heterogeneity of variances), a more robust statistical test such as Dunnett's T3 test (also known as the Satterthwaite's correction or Welch's t‐test) may be required to accurately evaluate differences among groups. p < 0.05 suggested statistical significance.


## Results

### Effects of BBR on Physiological and Biochemical Indices in the Rat DKD Model
  The BBR and Irb groups initially exhibited a decreasing trend in body weight, but starting from the 8th week of treatment, body weights began to increase in these groups in comparison with the model group (Figure 1a). In the DKD group, 24‐h urine protein amounts were markedly elevated. Both the Irb and BBR groups demonstrated a significant reduction in 24‐h urine protein amounts compared with the DKD group (p < 0.01) (Figure 1b). Scr and urea levels showed a notable increase in DKD rats, whereas the BBR and Irb groups had markedly decreased amounts compared with the DKD group (p < 0.05, p < 0.01) (Figure 1c,d). Similarly, the DKD group exhibited significantly elevated concentrations of TC, TG, and LDL. By contrast, the BBR group showed reduced serum concentrations for these markers in comparison with the DKD group (p < 0.05). The Irb group demonstrated significant decreases in TG and LDL amounts (p < 0.05), while TC levels were not significantly decreased (Figure 1e–g). The above data indicate BBR may attenuate the phenotype of renal impairment in DKD rats.


### BBR Decreases Oxidative Stress, Lipid Peroxidation, and Iron Ion Amounts in the Rat DKD Model
  In comparison with the control group, increased ROS and decreased SOD and NO levels (p < 0.01) were detected in the DKD group. Versus the DKD group, BBR‐treated rats exhibited reduced ROS levels (p < 0.05) and higher NO and SOD amounts (p < 0.05) (Figure 2a–c). Furthermore, renal LOP, MDA, and ferrous ion amounts in DKD rats were markedly increased (p < 0.01). Meanwhile, the Irb and BBR groups showed notable reductions in renal LOP, MDA, and iron ion amounts versus the DKD group (p < 0.05) (Figure 2d–f).


### Renal Histopathological Appearance in Rats With DKD
  In control rats, tubular epithelial cells were tightly packed with no noticeable inflammatory cell infiltration, and no significant abnormal alterations in the ultrastructure of renal cells were detected. DKD rats exhibited collagen fiber accumulation in the kidneys, expanded glomerular mesangial matrix, thickened glomerular basement membrane, moderate infiltration of inflammatory cells, and vacuolar alterations in renal tubular cells. These pathological changes were alleviated in the BBR and Irb groups, with more significant improvements observed in the BBR group (Figure 3a–c). Prussian blue staining in the normal group revealed minimal iron deposition in the renal tissue of rats, whereas DKD rats exhibited a substantial increase in iron deposition. Following drug intervention, reduced iron deposition was detected in the renal tissue (Figure 3d).


### Impacts of BBR on Renal Acls4, Ho‐1, Gpx4, and Nrf2 Protein Levels
  Representative micrographs obtained after immunohistochemical staining of kidney tissues are displayed in Figure 4a. Immunohistochemical analysis revealed markedly decreased Gpx4 (p < 0.01) and increased Nrfr2, Acls4, and Ho‐1 (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01) protein amounts in the kidney tissue of DKD rats versus controls (Figure 4b–e). Following drug intervention, renal Nrfr2, Gpx4, and Ho‐1 levels were elevated in both the BBR and Irb treatment groups, while Acsl4 expression was decreased. The impact was more pronounced in BBR‐treated rats.


### Results of Molecular Docking and SPR Experiments
  Molecular docking analysis revealed strong interactions between BBR and the Gpx4, Ho‐1, and Nrf2 proteins, showing binding energies of less than −5.0 kcal/mol, surpassing the binding affinity of Fer‐1. Specifically, BBR exhibited optimal binding energies of −8.98, −9.79, and −9.45 kcal/mol for Nrf2, Ho‐1, and Gpx4, respectively. In contrast, Fer‐1 showed energies of −6.39, −8.81, and − 8.1 kcal/mol for the respective proteins (Tables 1 and 2; Figure 5a–f). In addition, the affinity and kinetic properties of BBR‐Nrf2 (core protein) were investigated by SPR analysis. The protein coupling sensing map showed that the total amount of human Nrf2 protein coupled was 12984 RU (Figure 5g). The equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) for the binding of BBR to Nrf2 was 5.58 × 10−6 M, indicating a strong binding ability (KD values of ≈10−3 M for weak binding, ≈10−6 M for moderate binding, and ≈10−9 M for strong binding). In this analysis, the association rate constant (Ka) value is 6.45 × 104 (1/Ms) (reference value: 103–106), and the dissociation rate constant (Kd) value is 3.60 × 10−1 (1/s) (reference value: 10−3–10−1). The kinetic fit of BBR to Nrf2 protein revealed that BBR has a strong affinity for Nrf2, and the response value of the affinity was dose‐dependent and showed the kinetic property of “slow binding/fast dissociation” (Figure 5h).


### Effects of BBR on Morphology and Mitochondrial Damage‐Related Phenotypes in HK‐2 Cells Induced by AGEs
  In the CCK‐8 assay, HK‐2 cell viability exhibited a significant decrease after 48 h of exposure to AGEs at 200 μg/mL. Meanwhile, administration of 25, 50, and 100 μM BBR increased viability in HK‐2 cells induced by AGEs (p < 0.05, p < 0.01) (Figure 6a).

  In the control group, HK‐2 cells exhibited an elongated spindle shape, firmly and tightly adhering to the substrate. However, after 48 h of stimulation with AGEs, the cells demonstrated a rounded appearance with reduced volume. Increased interstitial space was observed, with decreased tight junctions, leading to cell detachment. Nonetheless, BBR treatment improved cell morphology, enhanced adherence, and reduced detachment (Figure 6b).

  The control group of HK‐2 cells displayed normal mitochondrial bilayer membrane morphology, with evenly distributed and densely packed mitochondrial cristae and intact mitochondrial membrane structure. After 48 h of treatment with AGEs, the mitochondrial membrane density in HK‐2 cells was increased, leading to fractured, reduced, or even absent mitochondrial cristae, as well as membrane rupture. However, the AGEs+Fer‐1 and AGEs+BBR groups showed significantly improved mitochondrial morphology and a more complete mitochondrial structure compared with the AGEs group (Figure 6c).


### Effects of BBR on AGEs‐Induced Ferroptosis‐Associated Proteins in HK‐2 Cells
  Immunofluorescence indicated an elevation of Gpx4 expression in the control group, whereas exposure to AGEs resulted in reduced Gpx4 levels in HK‐2 cells. Meanwhile, BBR enhanced Gpx4 protein expression (Figure 7a). Immunoblot revealed that HK‐2 cells exhibited increased Acsl4, Nrf2, and Ho‐1 protein levels in response to AGEs stimulation, accompanied by a decrease in Gpx4 expression. Higher Gpx4 protein levels were observed after BBR administration compared with the AGEs group, whereas Acsl4 expression was decreased. Additionally, Nrf2 and Ho‐1 protein levels significantly rose after BBR intervention, emphasizing the need for further mechanistic investigations (Figure 7b,c).

  To elucidate the causal relationship between BBR and the Nrf2 signal pathway, siRNA‐mediated gene silencing was employed to knock down Nrf2 in HK‐2 cells. Thereafter, the effects of these three monomeric compounds on downstream protein expression were assessed. The study demonstrates that BBR markedly enhances the expression of Nrf2, Gpx4, and Ho‐1 proteins with the siNC blank vector. The knockdown of Nrf2 via siNrf2 significantly reduces the ability of BBR to enhance Ho‐1 and Gpx4 protein expression (Figure 7d,e).


## Discussion
  Oxidative stress is intricately linked to the progression and development of DKD. Recent findings suggest that iron accumulation may exacerbate kidney damage in diabetic individuals by enhancing oxidative stress or reducing antioxidant levels (Zou et al. 2017). Ferroptosis, characterized by iron‐induced LPO and elevated ROS levels, poses a significant threat to renal cells due to their vulnerable mitochondrial structure. In diabetic nephropathy mice, the ferroptosis process is associated with increased amounts of MDA (produced by LPO) and decreased antioxidant enzyme levels (Li et al. 2022). The transcription factor Nrf2 has a critical regulatory effect on the antioxidant response to mitigate oxidative stress‐induced kidney damage. Additionally, Nrf2 is pivotal in cellular iron metabolism, controlling iron levels in cells and enhancing glutathione peroxidase 4 (Gpx4) biosynthesis for cell protection from oxidative damage associated with ferroptosis (Adedoyin et al. 2018). Heme oxygenase‐1 (Ho‐1/HMOX1) is another key factor in cellular defense mechanisms that is activated by multiple stressors. Ho‐1 primarily induces heme decomposition into biliverdin, carbon monoxide (CO), and iron ions, potentially elevating the labile iron pool and triggering ferroptosis (Adedoyin et al. 2018; Chang et al. 2018). Two crucial factors involved in ferroptosis are Gpx4 and Acsl4 (Wang et al. 2020). Gpx4 acts as an antioxidant enzyme and is crucial for maintaining cellular redox balance by preventing LPO and eliminating lipid peroxides. Reduced Gpx4 activity results in lipid peroxide accumulation, a pivotal regulator of ferroptosis (Stockwell et al. 2020). Acyl‐CoA synthetase long‐chain family member 4 (Acsl4), on the other hand, is essential for generating lipid peroxides and catalyzing acyl‐CoA synthesis as a crucial step in fatty acid metabolism in vivo. Acsl4 activates long‐chain polyunsaturated fatty acids to catalyze the production of corresponding polyunsaturated acyl‐CoA (PUFA‐CoA), inducing LPO and triggering ferroptosis (Wang et al. 2022; Yuan et al. 2016). Evidence suggests disrupting Acsl4 significantly impedes the conversion of arachidonic acid into phosphatidylethanolamine, thereby reducing cellular susceptibility to ferroptosis and preventing its onset (Ding et al. 2023).

  The alkaloid BBR, found in Coptis chinensis, exhibits potent anti‐inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties (Baska et al. 2021; Belwal et al. 2020; Habtemariam 2020; Majidzadeh et al. 2020; Pang et al. 2015). Numerous reports have also shown BBR can delay DKD progression (Qin et al. 2020; Qin, Zhao, et al. 2019; Yang et al. 2017). Previous research conducted by our team revealed BBR inhibits EMT in renal tubular epithelial cells by suppressing the Notch/Snail signal associated with EMT (Yang et al. 2017). Elevated ROS levels are closely associated with diabetic nephropathy. Antioxidant medications can reverse tissue damage caused by oxidative stress, potentially delaying disease onset and progression (Pirillo and Catapano 2015). Evidence confirms BBR reduces circulating ROS, LPO, and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels in DKD rats, while boosting the production of the antioxidants NO and SOD.

  Molecular docking is a bioinformatics modeling approach focused on the computational simulation of the molecular recognition process. This method predicts the binding conformation of a small molecule ligand to a target binding site, assessing the likelihood of interaction based on the stability of the binding conformation, indicated by the binding energy. Generally, lower binding energy for the receptor indicates stronger binding, with an energy below −5 kcal/mol typically signifying robust binding activity (Wang et al. 2024). In this study, molecular docking revealed that BBR interacts with the ferroptosis‐related proteins Nrf2, Ho‐1, and Gpx4 via van der Waals forces and hydrogen bonds. The calculated free energies of mutual binding were below −5.0 kcal/mol, suggesting a high affinity between BBR and these proteins. This implies potential binding at active sites, potentially modulating their functions and thereby affecting the related biological processes.

  In addition, the affinity and kinetic properties of BBR‐Nrf2 were analyzed by SPR analysis (Zhou et al. 2023). A high‐precision molecular interaction meter (Biacore T200) was utilized for measurement, showing a strong affinity between BBR and Nrf2. The response value of affinity was dose‐dependent, demonstrating kinetic properties of “slow binding/fast dissociation.” Therefore, the clinical administration of BBR requires a certain dose to reach therapeutic concentration, necessitating repeated administration. However, the ultimate clinical delivery of BBR needs to be determined by the half‐life of BBR and other experiments. Nrf2, a transcription factor responsible for activating internal antioxidant response elements, translocates into the nucleus upon activation (Baska et al. 2021). Ho‐1 is a stress protein contributing to defense against oxidative damage and is regulated by Nrf2 transcriptional activity. Nrf2 promotes Ho‐1 production (Dixon et al. 2012), enhancing the synthesis of antioxidants and intracellular antioxidant capacity, thereby suppressing ferroptosis. Gpx4 is crucial for repairing LPO damage in ferroptosis by regulating the conversion of lipid peroxides into alcohols or water, thereby helping cells fight oxidative stress and complete LPO repair (Rush et al. 2021). Acsl4 often serves as a critical indicator of ferroptosis occurrence (Li et al. 2019). In conclusion, our study suggests that the Nrf2 signal pathway axis plays an essential role as a signal pathway for BBR‐targeted treatment of DKD.

  The results of our experiments showed that BBR intervention effectively controlled the body weight of DKD rats, reduced the 24‐h urine protein level, lowered the TC, and LDL levels, and ultimately improved renal function, as evidenced by lowered Scr and urea levels. This effectively enhanced the overall health of DKD rats, which was in line with our previous findings (Yang et al. 2017). Moreover, the underlying molecular mechanisms were explored, showing that Nrf2, Ho‐1, and Gpx4 protein levels were significantly increased, and Acsl4 protein expression levels were significantly decreased in AGEs‐damaged HK‐2 cells and renal tissues of DKD rats after BBR intervention. BBR activated the Nrf2 signal pathway in renal tissue cells, stimulated the production of intracellular antioxidants, and prevented ferroptosis. The in vitro experiments revealed reduced activity, poor morphology, reduced inner mitochondrial membrane, and impaired mitochondrial function in HK‐2 cells exposed to AGEs, which improved after BBR intervention. To validate the role of Nrf2 in the mechanism of action of BBR, Nrf2 expression was knocked down in HK‐2 cells using specific siRNA, which was verified by Western blot. In Nrf2 knockdown HK‐2 cells, a significant attenuation of Ho‐1 and Gpx4 amelioration induced by AGEs in HK‐2 cells by BBR was observed. These results directly support the causal relationship between the amelioration of cellular oxidative stress and ferroptosis by BBR through the Nrf2 signal pathway and significantly enhance the scientific validity and reliability of our conclusions.

  In conclusion, BBR can activate the Nrf2 signaling pathway, improve the antioxidant capacity of cells, attenuate the oxidative stress damage caused by high glucose and AGEs, and resist cellular ferroptosis, thereby slowing down the disease progression of DKD (Figure 8).
