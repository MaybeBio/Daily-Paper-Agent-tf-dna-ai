# Targeting the bile acid receptor TGR5 with Gentiopicroside to activate Nrf2 antioxidant signaling and mitigate Parkinson’s disease in an MPTP mouse model


## Graphical abstract
  Graphical abstract


## Introduction
  Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a prevalent neurodegenerative disorder distinguished by the selective degeneration of dopaminergic (DAergic) neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNpc) and the intraneuronal accumulation of misfolded α-synuclein (α-Syn) [1]. With over six million individuals affected worldwide, PD has become a leading cause of disability associated with neurological disorders, posing a substantial public health challenge [2]. However, there is currently no cure for PD. While dopamine replacement therapy remains a cornerstone of PD treatment, it does not slow disease progression [3], underscoring the critical need for novel therapeutic targets and drug candidates.

  Although the precise etiology of PD remains unclear, oxidative stress is undoubtedly a key driver of DAergic neurodegeneration. Specifically, DAergic neurons in the SNpc are particularly susceptible to oxidative stress due to their high metabolic activity, elevated iron content, and the production of additional reactive oxygen species (ROS) when monoamine oxidases catabolize DA [4]. Longstanding evidence implicates oxidative damage occurs in the early stage of PD, and postmortem studies of PD human also reveal distinct accumulations of lipid peroxidation product and a reduction of reduced glutathione in SNpc [5]. Critically, oxidative stress operates as a vicious cycle: cellular redox imbalance and unchecked ROS production cause oxidative damage of lipids, proteins, DNA and RNA, as well as mitochondrial dysfunction and harmful modification of α-synuclein, which in turn exacerbate oxidative stress and ultimately lead to neuronal death in PD [6]. Furthermore, interventions targeting oxidative damage have shown neuroprotective effects in both genetic and toxin-induced PD models [4,5,7]. The ubiquitous transcription factor Nrf2 plays a critical role in the modulation of oxidative stress response through forming heterodimers with small Maf and binding to the antioxidant responsive element (ARE) to regulate the transcription of its target genes [8]. Moreover, Nrf2 also regulates mitochondrial biogenesis, mitophagy, and mitochondrial integrity [9]. A substantial body of research has established the significant involvement of Nrf2 in PD etiopathology [10,11], and several postmortem investigations have indicated an elevated nuclear translocation of Nrf2 in the cerebral tissues of PD patients [12]. Consequently, modulating the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway presents a promising therapeutic strategy for reducing or preventing neuronal death in PD.

  Transmembrane G protein-coupled receptor-5 (TGR5), a membrane-bound bile acid receptor expressed in the brains of both humans and mice [13], has recently emerged as a critical focus in neuroscience research, due to the potential connections between bile acid signaling and neurological disorders [[14], [15], [16]]. Accumulating evidences demonstrate that TGR5 activation not only enhances Nrf2 pathway [17] but also blocks inflammasome-dependent inflammation [18], thereby exerting beneficial effects in various conditions, including subarachnoid hemorrhage [18], multiple sclerosis [19], depression [20], and Alzheimer’s disease [21]. Notably, an emerging study revealed that a TGR5 agonist improved motor deficits and neurodegeneration in a PD mouse model, proposing that TGR5 activation might offer a promising therapeutic strategy for PD [22]. Nonetheless, further investigation is necessary to fully understand the role of TGR5 in PD pathogenesis and to determine whether its effects are associated with the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway.

  Gentiopicroside (GPS, Fig. 1A) is a secoiridoid compound isolated from the traditional Chinese herb medicine Gentiana manshurica Kitagawa. GPS is capable of crossing the blood–brain barrier and possesses a variety of pharmacological properties, such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-aging, anti-diabetic, and neuroprotective effects [20,23]. Furthermore, it has been observed that GPS significantly activated TGR5 to repress NF-κB pathway and ameliorate diabetic nephropathy [24]. However, the specific effects and mechanisms of GPS in PD remain unexplored. In the current study, we demonstrated that GPS enhanced the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway and improved neurodegeneration in both cellular and animal models of PD via activating TGR5. ## Materials and methods

### Animal
  Male C57BL/6J mice at 10–12 weeks of age were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of the Air Force Medical University, and the Tgr5 knockout (Tgr5-KO) mice were sourced from Cyagen Biosciences Inc. (Suzhou, China). Mice were maintained with unrestricted access to food and water in an environment with a stable temperature of 25 ± 2 °C and a 12 h alternating light and dark schedule. All animal studies were conducted according to the guidelines of the Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee of Air Force Medical University. The experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Ethical Committee of Air Force Medical University (Approval No. 2023-0669).


### MPTP-induced PD mouse model and treatments
  The grouping and modeling of animal experiments are illustrated in Fig. S1A. C57BL/6 mice were randomly divided into four groups (8 mice per group): Control, 1-methyl-4 phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP), MPTP + GPS (25 mg/kg) and MPTP + GPS (50 mg/kg). For establishing PD model, mice were administered intraperitoneally (i.p.) with 30 mg/kg MPTP (T4081, TargetMol) daily for 6 days after 1 week acclimatization [25]. And the control group was treated with the equal volume of saline. One hour after MPTP injection, 25 or 50 mg/kg GPS (dissolved in saline, HY-N0494, MCE, purity: 99.52 %) was given daily via intraperitoneal injection over a 10-day period. After the final treatment, the mice were subjected to open field test, rotarod test and pole test to assess the behavioral alternations.


### Cell culture, treatment, and transfection
  SH-SY5Y cells were provided by Department of Neurosurgery, Tangdu Hospital, The Fourth Military Medical University. And the HEK-293T cells expressing the human TGR5 receptor were obtained from Greater Biotechnology Co., LTD (Wuhan, China). Cells were grown in DMEM with high glucose (11995500, Gibco), enriched with 10 % fetal bovine serum (S711-001S, Lonsera) and 1 % penicillin–streptomycin solution (15140122, Gibco) at 37 °C within a humidified incubator containing 5 % CO2. Following a 6 h pretreatment with GPS, cells were exposed to 1-methyl-4-phenylpyridinium (MPP+, HY-W008719, MCE) for 24 h. For transfection, the mixture of small interfering RNA (siRNA) and X-tremeGENE™ siRNA transfection reagent (4476093001, Roche) was added into SH-SY5Y cells at growth density of 30 %. After a transfection period of 48 h, the expression levels of target gene and protein were detected. The sequences for TGR5 and the nontargeting negative control (NC) siRNAs were designed by Genepharma (Shanghai, China) and shown in Table S1.


### Statistical analysis
  Results were displayed as the mean ± SD. The statistical significance was assessed using one-way or two-way ANOVA, followed by either Tukey’s or Bonferroni’s post-hoc test for intergroup comparisons. All statistical analyses were assessed by GraphPad Prism 5.0 software (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) and significance was accepted at *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01 or ***P < 0.001.

  Detailed methods are described in the Supplementary Materials.


## Results

### GPS improved motor deficits and dopaminergic neurodegeneration in MPTP-induced PD mice
  To evaluate the neuroprotective potential of GPS in PD, subacute intraperitoneal injection of MPTP was employed to produce PD-like symptoms in mice. GPS was administrated as depicted in Fig. 1B, and its dosages were selected based on the documented antioxidant efficacy at 25 mg/kg (i.p.) and the established antidepressant-like profiles at 50 mg/kg (i.p.) from our previous data [26,27]. MPTP injection caused significant impairment of locomotion and coordinative ability, as evidenced by a reduction in the total distance traveled during the open field test, decreased latency to fall in the rotarod test, and increased turn time and total time in the pole test (Fig. 1C, S1B-E). Notably, GPS (25 and 50 mg/kg) treatment obviously attenuated these motor dysfunctions of MPTP-PD mice (Fig. 1C). Next, we analyzed the DAergic neuron numbers in SNpc and striatum (STR) regions of mouse brain through detecting the expression of its critical marker, tyrosine hydroxylase (TH). The result of immunofluorescence staining indicated that MPTP treatment led to a significant reduction in the number of TH+ neurons in SNpc, while the administration of GPS dose dependently reversed this change (Fig. 1D). Additionally, the decrease in TH+ fiber density in STR after MPTP injection was also attenuated by GPS treatment (Fig. 1E, F). In alignment with these findings, western blot analysis demonstrated that GPS treatment upregulated the TH protein levels in SNpc and STR regions of MPTP-PD mice (Fig. 1G–I). Taken together, these results suggested that GPS improved MPTP-induced motor deficits and neurodegeneration.

  To further evaluate the pharmacokinetics and safety of GPS, we analyzed plasma concentration–time curves of GPS in both control and MPTP-induced PD mice after intraperitoneal administration at 50 mg/kg for 10 days (Fig. S2A). No significant differences were observed between the two groups in key parameters: elimination half-life (t1/2: 68.9 ± 0.9 vs. 69.3 ± 0.1 min), peak concentration (Cmax: 31.1 ± 4.2 vs. 27.3 ± 5.6 mg/L), and total drug exposure (AUC0-t: 1326.9 ± 255.0 vs. 1104.6 ± 296.6 mg·min/L) (Fig. S2B), indicating MPTP did not alter GPS pharmacokinetics. Additionally, acute toxicity tests in mice showed no mortality or behavioral abnormalities occurred after a single intraperitoneal injection of GPS at 500 and 1000 mg/kg (Fig. S2C). Systemic histopathological analyses revealed no apparent changes of vital organs in GPS-treated groups (Fig. S2D), further supporting the safety of GPS at selected doses.


### GPS attenuated MPP+-induced neurotoxicity and α-Syn-induced pathology in vitro
  The widely used MPP+ (the toxic metabolite of MPTP)-treated SH-SY5Y cell model was selected for in vitro experiments. First, the cytotoxicity of MPP+ at concentrations of 0.25–4 mM was monitored (Fig. 2A), with 1 mM identified as the optimal dose for establishing the cellular model. GPS treatment up to 400 μM showed no significant cytotoxic effect on SH-SY5Y cells (Fig. 2B). Next, we evaluated the neuroprotective effect of GPS on MPP+-induced neurotoxicity. Cells were pre-incubated with GPS at different concentrations for 6 h prior to MPP+ exposure for 24 h. The Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay showed that GPS administration dose-dependently increased the cell viability in MPP+-treated SH-SY5Y cells (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, Hoechst 33342/PI double staining assay also indicated that 10 and 100 μM GPS treatment significantly reduced the percentage of cell mortality with MPP+ stimulation (Fig. 2D, E).   We next treated primary cortical neurons with α-Syn preformed fibrils (PFFs) to study the effects of GPS on α-Syn pathology [28,29]. After a 7-day incubation with α-syn PFFs (2 μg/mL) and GPS, western blot analysis revealed that GPS treatment at 10 and 100 μM markedly reduced the amounts of insoluble α-Syn monomer and aggregates (Fig. S3). These findings illustrated that GPS inhibited the accumulation of pathological α-Syn, further supporting its potential beneficial effects in PD.


### Effect of GPS on transcript profiling of MPP+-treated SH-SY5Y cells
  Whole-genome RNA-sequencing analysis was conducted to investigate the mechanism associated with the neuroprotective action of GPS in PD. A total of 1918 differential expression genes (DEGs) were identified when comparing control cells to those treated with MPP+ (Fig. 3A). In comparison to the MPP+ group, GPS treatment resulted in the identification of 733 DEGs, including 598 downregulated and 135 upregulated (Fig. 3A). The Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses revealed that GPS increased the expression of genes associated with the G protein-coupled receptor signaling pathway, cAMP signaling, dopamine metabolism and reactive oxygen species metabolic processes (Fig. 3B). Conversely, the genes related to transcription regulation, endoplasmic reticulum stress, signal transduction, and apoptotic process were downregulated following GPS treatment (Fig. 3B). As shown in the volcano plot, GPS led to upregulation of Gpr19 (also known as Tgr5), Gpr158, Gabra3, Nrp1, Nfe2l2 and Hmox1, as well as downregulation of Gpr87, Mafg and Sgk2 (Fig. 3C). Subsequently, the network analysis conducted utilizing GeneMANIA database forecasted the functional interactions among the proteins encoded by the aforementioned genes (Fig. 3E). ### GPS suppressed MPP+-induced oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction
  According to the RNA-sequencing result, the neuroprotective effect of GPS in PD might be associated with the modulation of antioxidant process. Thus, we investigated the impact of GPS on oxidative stress by measuring malondialdehyde (MDA) and ROS levels as indicators of oxidative status, as well as superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity representing antioxidant capacity. Results showed that GPS treatment decreased MDA content and recovered SOD activity in the STR of MPTP-PD mice (Fig. 4A, B) and MPP+-exposed SH-SY5Y cells (Fig. 4C, D). Based on the DCFH-DA and MitoSOX staining (Fig. 4E, F), GPS treatment dose-dependently inhibited the accumulation of intracellular and mitochondrial ROS caused by MPP+ (Fig. 4H, I). Accumulating studies have evidenced that dysregulated ROS production could lead to oxidative stress and cell death through activating the mitochondrial-permeability transition pore and inducing the mitochondrial dysfunction [30]. Therefore, mitochondrial membrane potential was detected using JC-1 fluorescent probe (Fig. 4G), which indicated that GPS treatment reversed the mitochondrial membrane potential collapse induced by MPP+ (Fig. 4J). ### GPS activated Nrf2/HO-1 antioxidant pathway in MPP+-induced SH-SY5Y cells

Thus, the role of GPS in the regulation of Nrf2 antioxidant pathway was prioritized. Additionally, GPS enhanced Nrf2 content in the nucleus and increased the nuclear/cytoplasmic Nrf2 ratio under MPP+ treatment (Fig. 5C, D). Consistently, GPS treatment significantly increased the Nrf2 and HO-1 fluorescence in MPP+-exposed SH-SY5Y cells via immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 5E, F). ### GPS targeted and activated the bile acid receptor TGR5
  TGR5 is a membrane-bound bile acid receptor, its activation has been shown to be neuroprotective in a variety of neurological diseases [21,22]. A previous study reported that GPS could activate TGR5 to repress NF-κB pathway and ameliorate diabetic nephropathy [24]. Interestingly, our RNA-sequencing results indicated strong correlation between TGR5 and the beneficial effects of GPS. Thus, we investigated whether GPS targeted TGR5 to mitigate PD. Firstly, three independent molecular docking were performed using the Glide module of Schrödinger-Maestro [31], and the well-defined bile acid-binding pocket was selected as the docking site [32]. The predicted binding energy between GPS and TGR5 receptor was −7.80 ± 0.87 kcal/mol. As depicted in Fig. 6A, GPS was well inserted into the oval bile acid-binding pocket of TGR5 in a manner similar to INT-777, a novel specific TGR5 agonist. Compared with the interaction mode of INT-777 within TGR5 (Fig. 6B) [32], GPS formed four hydrogen bonds with N93, E169, Y240, S270 residues and extensive hydrophobic interactions with L74, Y89, P92, F161 residues (Fig. 6C). Subsequently, we performed molecular dynamics (MD) simulation to validate the reliability of docking result. The binding diagrams of representing frames in the second half of simulation and the hydrogen-bond heatmaps of all simulated conformations (Fig. S4) revealed the stable interactions between GPS or INT-777 with TGR5 during MD simulation. As shown in Fig. 6D, the root mean squared deviation (RMSD) of TGR5-GPS complex was stable at about 2.7 Å, implying the reliability of the above docking mode under the consideration of protein flexibility. Moreover, the nearly consistent root mean squared fluctuation (RMSF) curves indicated the similar binding patterns of GPS and INT-777 within TGR5 (Fig. 6E).Fig. 6GPS targeted and activated the bile acid receptor TGR5. (A) Molecular docking was performed to investigate the interaction between GPS and TGR5. (B) Local magnification of ligand-binding interface of TGR5-INT-777 or (C) TGR5-GPS complexes. (D) The RMSD and (E) RMSF line plots of TGR5-INT-777 or TGR5-GPS complexes during MD stimulation.   To further validate the direct interaction between GPS and TGR5 protein, we conducted cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) and microscale thermophoresis (MST). CETSA results showed that 100 μM GPS markedly increased TGR5 stability at temperatures ranging from 41 to 55 °C (Fig. 6F, G). In the MST experiment, we observed that increasing GPS concentrations significantly affected the thermophoretic motion of recombinant human TGR5 protein. Further analysis demonstrated that GPS directly bound to TGR5 with the equilibrium dissociation constant (Kd) was 27.5 ± 13.9 μM (Fig. 6H). As a G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR), TGR5 signals through increasing intracellular cAMP and subsequently activating protein kinase A (PKA) and phosphorylating cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB). Then, cAMP measurement was performed in HEK-293T cells expressing the human TGR5 receptor by homogeneous TR-FRET immunoassay (Fig. 6I–K). The specific TGR5 agonist INT-777 significantly enhanced cAMP formation with EC50 value of 1.7 μM (Fig. 6J). Although the activating effect was lower than that of INT-777, GPS behaved as a partial agonist under high concentrations with EC50 value of 51.1 μM (Fig. 6K). These data implied that GPS could target the TGR5 receptor, and its neuroprotective effect might depend on the activation of TGR5.


### GPS ameliorated neuronal damage and oxidative stress via TGR5/cAMP/Nrf2 pathway
  Next, we sought to determine whether GPS regulate neuronal oxidative damage through activating TGR5. We employed specific small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) to silence TGR5 in SH-SY5Y cells, utilizing a nontargeting siRNA as negative control (NC). A TGR5 siRNA-981 (siTgr5-981) had the highest interference efficiency (Fig. 7A) and was subsequently utilized in further experiments. As anticipated, the knockdown of TGR5 reduced the viability of MPP+-treated SH-SY5Y cells, and negated GPS-mediated protection against MPP+-induced neurotoxicity (Fig. 7B). Moreover, silencing TGR5 exacerbated MPP+-triggered ROS generation, and blocked GPS’s ability to inhibit the ROS accumulation (Fig. 7C). Similarly, the restoring effect of GPS on redox homeostasis in MPP+-exposed cells was weakened after transfection with TGR5 siRNA, as evidenced by the SOD activities and MDA contents (Fig. 7D). Additionally, TGR5 knockdown significantly reduced Nrf2 protein expression in MPP+-treated cells, and prevented GPS-induced Nrf2 upregulation and nuclear translocation (Fig. 7E-G). Next, we also used SBI-115, a selective TGR5 antagonist, to pharmacologically block TGR5 activity.   We then evaluated the effects of GPS on the TGR5/PKA/CREB pathway, considering that it increased the cAMP level in TGR5-expressing cells. As shown in Fig. 7E and Fig. S5D, MPP+ treatment led to the decrease in both TGR5 level and CREB phosphorylation, which were rectified by GPS. The pharmacological antagonism of TGR5 by SBI-115 blocked the regulation of TGR5/PKA/CREB cascade by GPS, as evidenced by the level of phosphorylated CREB (Fig. S5D, E). To further verify that GPS activated Nrf2 signaling through TGR5 downstream pathway, a cAMP-dependent PKA inhibitor H89 was also utilized. We observed that H89 (10 μM) hindered the phosphorylation of CREB and canceled the GPS-mediated Nrf2 activation (Fig. S5F, G). Together, these data strongly confirmed that GPS activated Nrf2 antioxidant signaling and ameliorated neuronal damage via targeting the TGR5/PKA/CREB pathway in PD models. Nevertheless, TGR5 silencing or pharmacological inhibition compensatory upregulated HO-1 expression under basic condition (Fig. 7E, S5B). Even with a reduction in Nrf2 expression, GPS still restored MPP+-induced HO-1 downregulation (Fig. 7E, F), suggesting that GPS may activate Nrf2-independent pathways to induce HO-1.


### Tgr5 knockout abolished the neuroprotective effect of GPS in MPTP-treated PD mice
  Based on the above findings, we next employed Tgr5 knockout (KO) mice to further determine whether TGR5 mediates the beneficial effects of GPS against PD. Baseline assessments of Tgr5-KO mice including motor function, oxidative stress, and other bile acid receptor expression ruled out confounding factors (Fig. S6). Behavioral test results indicated that TGR5 deficiency worsened MPTP-induced motor dysfunction, as demonstrated by shorter rotarod latency, reduced open-field locomotion, and prolonged turn time and total time in the pole test (Fig. 8A–D). Likewise, the loss of DAergic neuron and TH+ fiber density stimulated by MPTP was also aggravated by TGR5 deficiency (Fig. 8E). Importantly, the improvement effects of GPS on MPTP-induced coordinative impairment and DAergic neurons loss (Fig. 8E–G) were markedly abolished in Tgr5-KO mice. Additionally, GPS treatment also failed to restore the TH protein levels in both STR and midbrain regions in Tgr5-KO mice (Fig. 8H, I). ## Discussion
  Oxidative stress has generally been described as a major driving force in the pathogenesis of age-related neurodegenerative disorders, such as PD, Alzheimer’s disease and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. The present study provides compelling evidences that GPS exerts neuroprotective and antioxidant effects in PD models by targeting TGR5 to activate the Nrf2/HO-1 signaling. Our findings not only elucidate a novel mechanism underlying GPS’s therapeutic potential but also highlight TGR5 as a critical mediator in PD pathogenesis.

  Oxidative stress results from cellular redox imbalance and the accumulation of ROS, which can severely damage cellular components such as lipids, proteins, DNA, RNA, and mitochondria [6]. Mitochondrial dysfunction, in turn, exacerbates oxidative stress by increasing ROS production, creating a deleterious cycle that ultimately leads to in neuronal death in PD [33]. In this study, we observed that GPS ameliorated DAergic neurodegeneration, inhibited oxidative damage, and prevented mitochondrial membrane potential collapse in PD models. These results are consistent with the existing studies that GPS can scavenge free radicals, reduce oxidative stress and promote mitophagy [34,35], supporting the antioxidant effects of GPS and emphasizing the crucial role of redox imbalance in the pathological process of PD.

  Central to GPS’s mechanism is its activation of the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway. Under oxidizing conditions, Nrf2 escapes degradation by Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1) and occurs nuclear entry. There, it forms heterodimer with small Maf proteins and recognizes the ARE to transactivate antioxidant genes, including HO-1, SOD, glutathione (GSH) and NAD(P)H:quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1) [8]. By promoting Nrf2 nuclear translocation and upregulating downstream targets like HO-1 and SOD, GPS effectively counteracts MPP+/MPTP-induced oxidative damage. Extensive evidences has highlighted the contribution of Nrf2 during the pathogenesis of PD. Specifically, Nrf2 deficiency aggravated DAergic neuronal loss, dendritic dystrophy, α-synuclein aggregation and neuroinflammation [36,37], whereas its overexpression improved PD phenotypes in mice [38]. Our research aligns with the established contribution of Nrf2 in the pathogenesis of PD.

  A key discovery of our study is the direct interaction between GPS and TGR5. As the first known GPCR for bile acid binding, TGR5 is highly expressed in the midbrain and widely distributed in neurons, astrocytes and microglia [39]. Despite the exciting advances in exploring the key role of central TGR5 in recent years [[18], [19], [20], [21], [22],40,41], its effect and molecular mechanisms in PD neurodegeneration remain largely unknown. Herein, we found that GPS stably insets into TGR5′s bile acid-binding pocket through molecular docking and MD simulation, and the direct binding of them was determined based on CETSA and MST experiments. Notably, the silencing, pharmacological inhibition or knockout of TGR5 exacerbated MPTP/MPP+-induced DAergic neuron death and oxidative stress. Meanwhile, the GPS’s effects in Tgr5-KO mice and TGR5-silenced cells were abrogated, underscoring the indispensable role of TGR5 in mediating these benefits. These findings are consistent with pervious study that GPS could activate TGR5 to repress NF-κB pathway and ameliorate diabetic nephropathy [24], and support TGR5′ crucial role in the treatment of neurological disorders.

  TGR5 signals through the Gs-protein mediated classical cAMP/PKA/CREB cascade upon activation. Our cAMP quantification data revealed that GPS increased intracellular cAMP levels in TGR5-expressing cells. Elevated cAMP activates PKA, which phosphorylates CREB at Ser133, enabling CREB to bind CRE sites in target gene promoters [42]. Intriguingly, CREB has been shown to directly interact with Nrf2 and synergistically increase Nrf2 target gene transcription [43,44]. Meanwhile, compromised CREB signaling transduction has been found to be responsible for the down-regulation of Nrf2 [45,46]. The connection between TGR5 and Nrf2 antioxidant signaling has also been established, revealing that TGR5 can activate Keap1/Nrf2/HO-1 pathway and increase nuclear Nrf2 and HO-1 expression [17,22]. Thus, the TGR5/cAMP/PKA/CREB/Nrf2 axis likely underpins GPS’s ability to upregulate Nrf2 and enhance its nuclear translocation in PD models. Our observation that TGR5 or PKA inhibition disrupted CREB phosphorylation and Nrf2 activation further supports this mechanistic hierarchy. Also, GPS-mediated increasing of Nrf2 and phosphorylated CREB protein levels in PD cell model was suppressed by TGR5 or PKA inhibitor, strongly reflecting the involvement of TGR5 downstream cAMP signal in GPS-mediated regulation of Nrf2. However, our findings revealed a complex regulatory layer: while TGR5 knockdown or antagonism abolished GPS-induced Nrf2 activation, it paradoxically upregulated basal HO-1 expression. Moreover, GPS restored HO-1 levels even under Nrf2-suppressed conditions, suggesting the involvement of Nrf2-independent pathways. This duality may reflect compensatory crosstalk between TGR5-driven cAMP/PKA/CREB signaling and alternative pathways, such as activator protein (AP)-1, signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) or hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF)-1ɑ, which can regulate HO-1 transcription independently of Nrf2 [47,48].

  This study identifies GPS as a novel non-bile acid-like TGR5 agonist with superior safety and blood–brain barrier permeability compared to the classic bile acid-derived agonist INT-777, highlighting its therapeutic potential for PD. Nevertheless, three key limitations must be acknowledged. First, while our investigations focus on GPS-mediated oxidative stress modulation, its broader neuroprotective mechanisms remain underexplored. Existing evidences indicate GPS alleviates inflammation through inhibiting SMAD3 phosphorylation and Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)/NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) signaling [49,50], while enhancing mitochondrial homeostasis via ATG32-mediated mitophagy [34]. Notably, TGR5 activation can attenuate neuroinflammatory through canonical cAMP/PKA/CREB-mediated NF-κB/NLRP3 suppression [18,51,52], and suppress cGAS/STING-mediated inflammatory responses by blocking GRP75-dependented endoplasmic reticulum-mitochondrial coupling [53]. These findings suggest GPS may exert multimodal anti-inflammatory and mitochondrial regulatory effects through TGR5 engagement. Second, while MPTP/MPP+ models are widely used, they inadequately recapitulate the chronic pathological features (such as α-synuclein aggregation) of PD. Our preliminary data showing the inhibition of pathological α-Syn oligomers by GPS in α-Syn PFFs-treated primary cortical neurons requires validation in α-Syn transgenic models to strengthen clinical relevance. Third, the partial agonism of GPS at TGR5 suggests potential for structural optimization to enhance potency.

  Future research endeavors should prioritize comprehensive investigations aimed at thoroughly elucidating the multi-pharmacological actions of GPS in neurodegeneration. It is imperative to validate its neuroprotective efficacy in α-Syn transgenic rodents and human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived DAergic neurons. Additionally, the development of GPS derivatives that exhibit full agonistic properties and enhanced brain-targeted efficacy is warranted. These efforts will advance GPS as a disease-modifying therapeutic candidate for PD.


## Conclusions
  Overall, this study provides the first evidence that GPS significantly ameliorates motor deficits and DAergic neuronal loss in an MPTP-induced PD mouse model. Mechanistically, GPS directly targets TGR5 to activate Nrf2/HO-1 pathway, thereby reducing ROS production and alleviating oxidative damage and mitochondrial dysfunction in neurons. The abrogation of the anti-PD effects of GPS in Tgr5 knockout mice underscores the essential role of TGR5 in mediating the neuroprotective effects of GPS. This research not only positions GPS as a potential candidate for PD treatment and for developing novel TGR5 agonists, but also deepens our understanding of the important role of TGR5 in neurodegenerative disorders.
