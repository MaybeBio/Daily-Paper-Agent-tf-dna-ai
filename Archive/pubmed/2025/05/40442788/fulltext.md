# Integrated bioinformatics and network pharmacology to identify and validate macrophage polarization related hub genes in the treatment of osteoarthritis with Astragalus membranaceus


## Background
  Macrophage polarization exacerbates the pathological processes of osteoarthritis (OA). Astragalus membranaceus (AM) can repair chondrocytes and serve as a protective agent for OA. Therefore, the study intended to identify macrophage polarization-related genes (MPRGs) in the treatment of OA with AM.


## Methods
  We utilized data from GSE57218 as training set, while GSE117999 serves as a validation set, all obtained from Gene Expression Omnibus(GEO). The MPRGs were exported from the Molecular Signatures Database. Target genes of AM were obtained by network pharmacology. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified in OA vs. control groups. Then key module genes were acquired through weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) and intersected with DEGs and target genes of AM to obtain candidate genes. Subsequently, the candidate genes were further screened for hub genes by machine learning, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, and expression validation. Further, reverse transcription-quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was applied to verify the mRNA expression levels of hub genes. In addition, the mechanism of these hub genes was investigated through enrichment analysis, immune microenvironment analysis, regulatory network construction, and molecular docking.


## Results
  Ultimately, 1,430 DEGs, 4,577 key module genes, and 486 target genes of AM were intersected to derive 28 candidate genes. After machine learning, ROC curve analysis and expression validation, CREBBP and PIM3 were identified. The mRNA expression of tissue CREBBP and PIM3 was significantly decreased in OA compared with the control group. Furthermore, the enrichment analysis indicated that eight pathways, including oxidative phosphorylation, were simultaneously enriched by two hub genes. Microenvironment analysis revealed negative correlations between both hub genes and 11 differential immune cells. We identified that CREBBP and PIM3 were regulated by 6 miRNAs (e.g., hsa-mir-942-5p) and 79 transcription factors (TFs) (e.g., IRF1). Molecular docking experiments indicated that isoflavone strongly bound to CREBBP, while (3R)-3-(2-hydroxy-3,4-dimethoxyphenyl) chroman-7-ol exhibited significant binding affinity for PIM3, suggesting that these two active ingredients were core components of AM in treating OA via hub genes.


## Conclusion
  This study identified CREBBP and PIM3 as potential focal points for the treatment of OA with AM, providing valuable clues to help treat and predict OA.

## Introduction
  Osteoarthritis is a prevalent degenerative joint disease characterized by the wear and tear of articular cartilage, often leading to bone spurs and inflammation in surrounding joint tissues. It affects approximately 303 million people worldwide. Epidemiologically, OA is more common in individuals aged 45 and older, with a prevalence of about 10% in the hip and 16% in the knee, affecting women more than men. On the other hand, OA is related to disability and premature mortality [1]. Additionally, OA is associated with age, as Advanced glycation end products (AGEs) accumulation accelerates cartilage degradation, chondrocyte apoptosis, and pathological changes, highlighting potential treatment targets [2].

  Although the prevalence of OA is closely related to age, including obesity, genetics, joint damage. OA is heterogenous, and its specific pathogenesis remains unclear [3]. Currently, the drug treatments for OA mainly focus on Paracetamol, anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), corticosteroid injections, and tramadol, widely used likely leading to gastrointestinal and cardiovascular toxicity and little or no alleviated pain sensity, which is usually restricted in aging patients [1]. Another treatment option is total joint replacement after onset, but the surgery is extremely complex and presents a significant financial burden for patients and their families. Additionally, it poses higher risks of mortality and postoperative complications. There are no effective methods to prevent or inhibit the development of OA [4]. Therefore, understanding the pathogenesis of OA can provide potential targets for early intervention and suppression of OA progression, as well as offer new insights for the development of novel drugs and the formulation of personalized treatment strategies.

  Recently, more evidence has shown that low-grade inflammation plays an essential role in the pathogenesis of OA, which may activate the innate inflammatory pathway genes [5]. Synovial macrophages, as key immune cells within the joint, play a crucial role in the inflammatory response. Clearing necrotic debris from the injured area and promoting the resolution of inflammation is essential for maintaining the proper function of the joint cavity [6]. When activated, macrophages undergo reprogramming and are polarized into either classically activated macrophages (CAM, M1 phenotype) or activated macrophages (AAM, M2 phenotype) [7, 8]. Polarized macrophages, through the secretion of a substantial amount of pro-inflammatory cytokines, matrix metalloproteinases, and growth factors, significantly influence the metabolism of chondrocytes [9]. This, in turn, can lead to pathological changes such as joint pain, cartilage damage, and the formation of osteophytes [9]. M1 is associated with producing pro-inflammatory cytokines and plays a dominant role in acute inflammation, whereas M2 exhibits anti-inflammatory properties [10, 11]. M2 demonstrates potential therapeutic value in osteoarthritis treatment by modulating inflammation and promoting bone regeneration [11, 12]. Under abnormal or excessive mechanical stress, chondrocytes and synovial cells may release pro-inflammatory factors to protect the body from mechanical stress, activating local immune processes that lead to low-grade inflammation and an imbalance between M1 and M2 [13, 14]. Therefore, the M1/M2 ratio can indicate the occurrence and development of OA, as exemplified by SP1’s transcriptional activation of HTR2B, which alters the M1/M2 polarization of microglia and subsequently exacerbates traumatic spinal cord injury [15]. Inhibiting the polarization of M1 has become a therapeutic approach for OA, such as targeting TRPV1 to suppress the expression of Rspo2 [16, 17]. Additionally, intra-articular injection of platelet-rich plasma can provide critical factors that balance pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines, targeting macrophage dysfunction to modulate the immune mechanisms within the knee joint [18]. However, the specific mechanisms underlying the role of macrophage polarization genes in osteoarthritis remain unclear.

  Moreover, the traditional Chinese medicine of AM is highly effective and safe in the clinical treatment of OA, which can enhance the immune system and improve microcirculation in the blood-brain barrier [19, 20]. Many modern pharmacological studies have shown that AM has the effects of attenuating the product of inflammatory cytokines, promoting cartilage growth and healing, and improving the ability of chondrocytes to resist damage [21]. Recent pharmacological studies have highlighted the significant role of AM in reducing inflammatory cytokine production and promoting cartilage growth and healing [19]. The components of AM can inhibit ADAM Metallopeptidase With Thrombospondin Type 1 Motif 4 (ADAMTS4), 5 overexpression, and repair damage to chondrocytes by ASN IV administration [22]. Its active components can enhance anabolic processes and block the signaling pathways of inflammatory cytokine Interleukin-17 (IL-17) and similar genes, thereby reducing the expression of catabolic processes in chondrocytes to alleviate pain and treat OA in patients [23, 24]. Additionally, AM has been shown to promote neovascularization, effectively inhibiting the expression of macrophage polarization-related genes (MPRGs), facilitating the transformation from M1 to M2, and demonstrating significant efficacy in the clinical treatment of OA [25, 26]. AM extract presents a selective anti-inflammatory phenotype and reduces sensitivity in the reflection of inflammation [27]. Although there is substantial evidence supporting the beneficial effects of AM in the treatment of OA, its specific mechanisms, particularly in promoting macrophage polarization and regulating related signaling pathways, remain unclear. Therefore, further investigation into the role of AM in OA treatment, especially its regulatory effects on macrophage polarization, is still warranted.

  In this study, we examined OA samples from the GEO database and observed a significant relation between OA and AM. We explored hub genes related to MPRGs in the treatment of OA with AM based on network pharmacology and bioinformatics, to explore the role of hub genes in the treatment of OA with AM. This work is expected to provide new insights into the research on Astragalus treatment of OA, thereby improving the treatment effect of patients.

  In this research, OA samples from the GEO database were analyzed, revealing a significant connection between OA and AM. Through network pharmacology and bioinformatics approaches, we investigated key genes associated with MPRGs involved in the use of AM for OA treatment, aiming to clarify their roles in this process. The findings are anticipated to offer fresh perspectives on applying Astragalus in managing OA, ultimately enhancing therapeutic outcomes for patients.


## Materials and methods

### Source of data
  The GSE57218 [28] and GSE117999 [29] raw data were exported from the GEO database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo). GSE57218 (platform GPL6947, beadchip) was a training set, including 7 chondroid tissue of healthy individuals (control group) and 33 OA samples of chondroid tissue (Supplementary Table 1). GSE117999 (platform GPL20844, Microarray) was a validation set, including 10 healthy individuals (control group) and 10 OA samples of chondroid tissue (Supplementary Table 2). In addition, 35 MPRGs were exported from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb).


### Identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between OA and control groups
  In GSE57218, Differential expression analysis of OA and control samples was performed to obtain DEGs via “limma” package (v 3.54.1) [30] (|log2Fold Change (FC)| > 0.5 as well as p < 0.05) [31]. The “ggplot2” package (v 3.4.1) [32] was employed to plot volcano maps to visualize the top 10 up- and down-regulated DEGs. Under log2FC ranking, the top 10 up- and down-regulated DEGs were selected and visualized by heat map with “ComplexHeatmap” package (v 2.14.0) [33].


### Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)
  In GSE57218, differences in MPRGs between OA and control samples were analyzed using the Wilcoxon test. The single sample Gene set enrichment analysis(ssGSEA) algorithm in “GSVA” package (v 1.46.0) [34] was used to estimate MPRGs-related ssGSEA scores in the OA and control groups based on differential MPRGs. Then the differences in MPRGs-related scores between OA and control samples were analyzed (p < 0.05). After examining and removing outlier samples by hclust function, and leading to MPRGs-related ssGSEA scores, all samples were subjected hierarchical clustering by “WGCNA” package (version 1.71) [35]. Before constructing the co-expression network, to ensure that gene interactions align closely with the scale-free distribution, the threshold value when the scale-free fit index (R2 = 0.9) was close to the threshold and the mean connectivity was near 0 was chosen as the optimal soft threshold (power) via “pick soft threshold” function. A hierarchical clustering tree was then constructed using the dynamic hybrid cut method in the “hierarchical cluster” function, showing the clustering dendrograms for different gene modules. MPRGs-related ssGSEA scores were used as phenotypes to construct correlations (cor) between phenotypes and modules, and correlation heatmaps were plotted. The module with the strongest positive and negative association with the phenotype was taken as the key module (p value < 0.05), and key module genes from these key modules were included in subsequent analyses.


### Identification of the targets of AM
  The core active ingredients of AM were identified from the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology (TCMSP, https://tcmspw.com/tcmsp.php10) database, using oral bioavailability (OB) ≥ 30% as well as drug-likeness (DL) ≥ 0.18 as selection criteria. Subsequently, the target genes of AM core active ingredients were acquired from Swiss Target Prediction (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) database, were recorded as the target genes of AM.


### Identification and functional enrichment analysis of candidate genes
  To identify potential differential genes that AM might target in the context of OA-related MPRGs, the intersection of DEGs, key module genes, and target genes of AM was recorded as candidate genes by the “VennDiagram” package (v 1.7.3) [36]. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis as well as Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) was carried out on candidate genes via “clusterProfiler” package (v 4.2.2) [37] to elucidate their potential biological functions and related pathways (p < 0.05). Furthermore, sorted by the p-values of the candidate genes from the smallest to the largest, the top 5 most significant pathways in biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), as well as molecular function (MF), were displayed, along with the top 10 most significant pathways in KEGG. Candidate genes were imported into “STRING” (http://www.string-db.org/) database to construct a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network (interaction score > 0.4), thereby exploring the interactions among the related candidate genes at the protein level.


### Selection and validation of hub genes
  By employing machine learning models, including support vector machines-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), signature genes were further identified from the aforementioned candidate genes. SVM-RFE models were performed by “e1071” package (v 1.7–13) [38], and the obtained genes were recorded as SVM-RFE signature genes. The “xgboost” package (v 1.7.7.1) [39], as utilized to conduct XGBoost, and the genes identified according to the importance ranking were marked as XGBoost signature genes. Then, the signature genes obtained by the 2 algorithms were intersected via “ggvenn” package (v 1.2.2) [40], and the genes obtained from the intersections were recorded as potential genes. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves of potential genes were analyzed in GSE57218 and GSE117999 by “pROC” package (v 1.18.0) [41], in which potential genes with an area under the curve (AUC) greater than 0.7 in both datasets were identified as candidate hub genes. Then, the expression level of these candidate hub genes was analyzed in both GSE57218 and GSE117999 by Wilcoxon test (p < 0.05). Genes that exhibited significant differences and consistent trends between groups in GSE57218 and GSE117999 were designated as hub genes and visualized using “ggplot2” package (v 3.4.1) [32].


### Construction of nomogram
  Furthermore, nomogram model of hub genes was formed by “rms” package (v 6.5-0) [42]. The predictive accuracy of the nomogram model was assessed via plotting calibration curves (p > 0.05). Decision curve analysis (DCA) curves were charted to evaluate the clinical effectiveness of the nomogram model via rmda package (v 1.6) [43]. Additionally, ROC curve was plotted by “pROC” package (v 1.18.0), and the forecasting accuracy of the nomogram model for OA samples was assessed based on the AUC greater than 0.7.


### GSEA of hub genes
  After obtaining hub genes, to uncover their potential biological functions as well as molecular pathways in OA, “Psych” package (v 2.2.9) [44]was applied to calculate correlation coefficients of each hub gene as well as all other genes in GSE57218 (OA vs. control). The correlation coefficients were then ranked from highest to lowest. Subsequently, the “clusterProfiler” package (v 4.2.2) was utilized to perform GSEA, with p-value threshold set to less than 0.05. The ‘c2.cp.kegg.v7.4.symbols.gmt’ dataset was exported from the MSigDB. Finally, the top 5 pathways for each hub genes were showcased by “ggplot2” package (v 3.4.1).


### Immune microenvironment analysis
  Firstly, the ssGSEA scores of 28 immune cells [45] were estimated using the ssGSEA algorithm of the “GSVA” package (v 1.46.0) in GSE57218. Secondly, the Wilcoxon test was performed to investigate the difference in immune cell abundance in OA as well as control groups (p < 0.05), and the results were visualized by plotting box plots with the “ggplot2” package (v 3.4.1). Finally, Spearman’s correlation analysis was performed by “psych” package (v 2.2.9) to calculate the correlation between differential immune cells as well as hub genes.


### Construction of key MicroRNA (miRNA)-mRNA network, transcription factors (TFs)-mRNA network and molecular docking
  The miRNAs of hub genes were predicted by the experimentally validated micro RNA-target interactions database (miRTarBase, https://www.mirnet.ca) and TarBase (https://dianalab.e-ce.uth.gr/tarbasev9) based on the Network Analyst platform. The intersection of the results predicted by 2 databases was taken as the key miRNA. TFs regulating hub genes were obtained by the Encyclopedia of DNA Elements (ENCODE) database (https://www.encodeproject.org/). The key miRNA-mRNA network and TFs-mRNA network were visualized by Cytoscape Software (v 3.9.1, Updata Date: January 24, 2024) [46]. Subsequently, the 3D structures of the core active ingredients of AM were acquired from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The protein crystal structures of the key genes were obtained from the Research Collaboratory for Structural Bioinformatics Protein Data Bank (RCSB PDB, https://www.rcsb.org/). Then the core active ingredients of AM and protein crystal structures of each hub gene were preprocessed and molecularly docked by the CB-Dock2 tool (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/), and the binding free energy was calculated.


### Expression validation of hub genes
  A total of 5 pairs of articular cartilage tissues were collected at Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital, including 5 OA and 5 control. All participants provided and signed informed consent forms, with ethical approval granted by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital. (Approval Number:2021-001). Firstly, total RNA was extracted from the articular cartilage tissues of 5 OA and 5 control subjects using TRIzol reagent (Ambion, USA). Afterward, RNA concentrations were determined using the NanoPhotometer N50. Following this, mRNA was reverse transcribed to produce cDNA with the SweScript First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit. (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Reverse transcription-quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction RT-qPCR was conducted to assess the mRNA expression levels of CREBBP and PIM3. The amplification reaction conditions were as follows: initial denaturation (95 °C for 1 min), denaturation (95 °C for 20 s), annealing (55 °C for 20 s), and extension (72 °C for 30 s), and this was followed by the execution of 40 cycles. The mRNA relative expression levels were determined using the 2−ΔΔCT method, with GAPDH serving as the internal reference. The primer sequences employed in the experiment are shown in Table 1.

### Statistical analysis
  Bioinformatics analyses were executed in R programming language (v 4.2.2, Update Date: October 31, 2022) [47]. Differences in OA vs. control groups were analyzed using the Wilcoxon test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.


## Results

### Identification of DEGs, key module genes, and target genes of AM
  In GSE57218, 1,430 DEGs were screened in both groups, comprising 651 up-regulated genes (TNC, MXRA5, and TGFBI, etc.) and 779 down-regulated genes (TSPYL2, APOD, and MAGT1, etc.) (Fig. 1A-B, Supplementary Table 3). Subsequently, the differences in MPRGs between the OA and the control group were conducted, revealing 11 differentially expressed MPRGs (Fig. 1C). The results indicated that MPRGs-associated ssGSEA scores were reduced in OA sample (p = 0.00485), suggesting that MPRGs-associated genes were associated with OA (Fig. 1D). After the removal of outliers, a sample clustering tree was plotted, and a soft threshold (power) of 6 (R2 = 0.9) was applied to construct the network topology analysis (Fig. 1E-F). Based on the dynamic hybrid cut method, 11 modules were obtained (excluding the grey module for genes that could not be classified) (Fig. 1G). The MEturquoise modules (cor = 0.48, p = 0.002) and MEmagenta modules (cor = -0.47, p = 0.003) exhibited the highest positive and negative correlations with MPRGs-related ssGSEA scores, respectively, and were thus selected as key modules (Fig. 1H). From these, 4,577 key module genes were obtained.

  Moreover, after identifying 19 core active ingredients of AM, a total of 486 target genes of AM were acquired (Supplementary Tables 4–5).

In GSE57218 and GSE117999, 4 candidate hub genes were identified (AUC > 0.7): IGF1R, CREBBP, CLK1, and PIM3 (Fig. 3D). In which, CREBBP and PIM3 were notably down-regulated in the OA group across 2 datasets (Fig. 3E) Meanwhile, low mRNA expression levels of CREBBP and PIM3 were found in the tissue of OA compared with control group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3F-G).

## Discussion
  OA is a common degenerative joint disease, with low inflammation. Astragaloside IV (AS-IV) can promote wound repair in diabetes by reducing oxidative stress-induced cellular damage, enhancing collagen deposition and the expression of extracellular matrix-related genes, and facilitating the transformation of M1 to M2 [26]. AS-IV affects M2 polarisation by restraining the expression of p-AMPKa in the AMPK signal pathway [48]. After AS-IV treatment, IL-12 and Nos2 are downregulated, while Arg1 and Mrc1 are upregulated, indicating a shift towards an M2 macrophage phenotype. This suggests that AS-IV may activate the CSF-1R signalling pathway, promoting anti-inflammatory responses [49]. However, the mechanism by which AM modulates macrophage polarisation to treat OA remains unclear.

  In this study, we used differential analysis, network pharmacology, WGCNA, and machine learning to identify two MPRGs (CREBBP and PIM3) linked to AM treatment for arthritis. These targets were validated through ROC analysis and expression level checks. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed 11 significant MPRGs in OA, with CREBBP and PIM3 both negatively correlated and enriched in oxidative phosphorylation.

  Further network analysis showed that these two hub genes share seven miRNAs (e.g., has-mir-338-5p, has-mir-942-5p). Molecular docking revealed that CREBBP binds to isoflavanone and PIM3 binds to (3R)-3-(2-hydroxy-3,4-dimethoxyphenyl)chroman-7-ol. These findings highlight CREBBP and PIM3 as potential therapeutic targets, providing new insights into OA treatment with AM.

  Focus on hub genes CREBBP and PIM3. Some studies about OA pathology have shown that CREBBP positively regulates the transcription of RNA polymerase II promoter and is lowly expressed in OA samples, were candidate gene for OA [50]. Crebbp deficiency may result in T cell homeostasis, pancytopenia, and a myeloproliferative phenotype [51]. The mutation and loss of CREBBP are associated with tumour diseases, and overexpression of circCREBBP which is the back splicing of exons of CRBBP, has an anti-fibrosis effect [52]. Other studies have shown circCRBBP is upregulated in OA cartilage which induces degradation of the chondrocytes matrix [53]. TGFβs signal pathway induces phosphorylation of Smad1/5 by circCRBBP control [53].

  Furthermore, PIM3 is a serine/threonine kinase(PIM1,PIM2,PIM3), and inhibite cell apoptosis by inducing phosphorylation to releases Bcl-2 and Bcl-XL [54]. The upregulation of PIM3 can protect from anoxic preconditioning injury by p38MAPK signal pathway [55]. The imbalance of PIM relationships was observed in OA patients, with low levels of PIM1 and PIM2, and high levels of PIM3 [56]. PIM3 catalyzes histone phosphorylation and autophosphorylation [57]. P27kip1 is phosphorylated in the JAK/STAT pathway by PIM3 kinases controlling the transcription and posttranslational expression of p27KIP1 [58]. Lipopolysacharide promotes the overexpression of PIM3 kinases, which inhibit hepatic stellate cell-T6 cell proliferation and apoptosis [59]. PIM kinase isoforms were upregulated, and significantly affected the phosphorylation levels of extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) and cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB) in RA-FLSs [60].

  CREBBP and PIM3 in oxidative phosphorylation display downregulation. Changes in oxidative phosphorylation may affect the function of CREBBP and PIM3, and changes in nutrition and energy status may affect the metabolism of chondrocytes, thereby affecting the progression of OA [61]. In the pathological state of OA, inflammation and oxidative stress lead to a decrease in oxidative phosphorylation, thereby affecting the expression of CREBBP and PIM3, which may aggravate cartilage degradation.

  The analysis of the immune microenvironment involving two hub genes reveals that CREBBP is negatively correlated with natural killer (NK) cells, and PIM3 is negatively correlated with MDSCs. Furthermore, there are significant differences in the expression of 11 immune cell types between the control and comparison groups. These findings suggest that CREBBP and PIM3 play crucial roles in immune regulation, impacting NK cell anti-tumor activity and immune suppression, thus providing new insights for immunotherapy research and warranting further validation and exploration.

  The NK cell is a critical immune system component In the immune microenvironment. It has low cytotoxic activity and high expression of level pro-inflammatory granzyme A in the synovium of OA [62]. The downregulation of CREBBP may affect the activity of natural killer cells, which modulate the progress of OA. Without TLR2 and TLR4, the chondrocyte’s anti-oxidative stress and autophagy flux capacity is reduced, triggering inflammation [63]. CREBBP may help M0 macrophages enter inflammatory environments and mainly promotes their polarization to the M2 phenotype instead of the M1 phenotype. Thus, changes in gene expression could affect immune cell activity [64].

  Predicting TFs of key targets, CREBBP has 9 TFS, and PIM3 has 73 TFs. ZFX(Zinc Finger Protein X-Linked) and IRF1(Interferon Regulatory Factor 1) are protein-coding genes, shared by CREBBP and PIM3. ZXF may play a crucial role in disease progression in postmenopausal patients, making it a potential ideal target for OA treatment or a prognostic biomarker [65]. IRF1 is linked to immune deficiency 117 and OA, and the IRF1/GBP5 axis promotes extracellular matrix degradation and pyroptosis in OA through the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway [66]. This mechanism may help in understanding OA pathology and finding treatment targets.

  CREBBP and PIM3 have high binding activity with isoflavanones by molecular docking. Studies have demonstrated that enantiomers of flavonoids exhibit varying antitumor activities. For instance, Flemingia macrophylla is rich in prenylated flavonoids, with compounds 5 and 7 showing significant cytotoxicity against A549 cells, making them promising candidates for antitumor drug development [67].Additionally, certain isoflavones exhibit potent apoptosis-inducing activity against human leukemia HL-60 cells [68]. Daidzein and its enantiomers, R- and S-equol, inhibit the invasion of MDA-MB-231 human breast cancer cells partly through down-regulation of MMP-2 expression, with effects similar to those of the parent isoflavone daidzein [69]. Therefore, isoflavanones may regulate gene expression by affecting the activity of CREBBP, thereby influencing cell metabolism and growth. The anti-inflammatory effects of isoflavones may be related to regulating CREBBP, as it plays a crucial role in modulating inflammatory factors and cytokines. Besides, PIM3 have high binding activity with (3R)-3-(2-hydroxy-3,4-dimethoxyphenyl)chroman-7-ol is little known. Isoflavones target the TSC1/mTORC1 signaling pathway, inhibiting the activation of osteoblasts and the release of VEGF, thereby suppressing H-type angiogenesis and slowing down abnormal subchondral bone remodeling [70]. S-equol is a soy isoflavone derivative that can reduce oxidative stress and the levels of matrix-degrading enzymes associated with cartilage degeneration, thereby slowing the progression of postmenopausal OA ([71]). Genistein, a kind of isoflavone, has anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties. It reduces TNF-α, IL-1β, and MMP-13 levels in serum and knee joint tissues, helping to alleviate inflammation and oxidative damage in knee osteoarthritis [72]. Based on these research findings, isoflavones show promising potential in the treatment of OA and in alleviating patient discomfort. At the same time, the molecular docking results provide new insights and a foundational basis for developing new drugs to treat OA.

  However, although the study demonstrated the advantages of bioinformatics analysis, quickly translating these findings into practical applications in clinical practice still requires further research and exploration. In addition, additional cell- and animal-based experiments should be designed to verify the biological functions of hub genes further.


## Conclusion
  This study identified two hub genes (CREBBP and PIM3) for OA by bioinformatics methods. Providing a useful reference for the diagnosis and clinical treatment of OA patients and providing a theoretical basis for in-depth research on the pathogenesis of OA.
