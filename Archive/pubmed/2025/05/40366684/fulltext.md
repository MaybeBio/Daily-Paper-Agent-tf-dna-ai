# Unraveling structural transitions and kinetics along the fold-switching pathway of the RfaH C-terminal domain using exchange-based NMR


## Significance
  A growing number of protein domains have been shown to undergo fold switching upon interaction with a partner, yet the mechanism remains unknown. One example is the transcriptional regulator RfaH whose C-terminal domain switches from an α-helical hairpin packed against the N-terminal domain to a β-roll when displaced upon RNA polymerase binding. Here, using NMR, we explore slow (subsecond) and fast (submillisecond to millisecond) structural transitions sampled by the isolated C-terminal domain of RfaH. In addition to the predominant, semistable β-roll structure, four distinct, sparsely populated, transient states are identified, three of which represent intermediates along the fold-switching pathway. The first intermediate (populated at ~25%), while largely unfolded, is characterized by transient helical and β-hairpin elements packed against one another.


## Results and Discussion

### Complete Backbone Chemical Shift Assignments of the Observable Minor State of RfaHCTD.
  RfaHCTD exists in two observable states: a major A state with a population of ~75% and a minor B state populated at ~25% (see SI Appendix, Fig. S1 for a 1H-15N correlation spectrum of RfaHCTD). Resonances of the major state were readily assigned using the 3D scalar through-bond correlation experiments (20). The minor B state is random coil-like with limited chemical shift dispersion. Due to cross-peak overlap and weak intensities, direct resonance assignments of the minor B state using the regular 3D through-bond correlation experiments are challenging. We therefore made use of high-resolution 3D zz-exchange HNH and NNH experiments acquired with nonuniform sampling in the indirect dimensions (see SI Appendix, Experimental Procedures for experimental details and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 for the pulse sequence) to unambiguously assign the amide group chemical shifts (1HN and 15N) for the observable minor state through the observation of exchange cross-peaks with the observable major state (see SI Appendix, Figs. S3 and S4 for examples of strips and projections, respectively, taken from the two 3D zz-exchange spectra). With the 1HN/15N assignments for the minor B state in hand, the complete 13C (13Cα, 13Cβ, and 13C’) and 1H (1Hα and 1Hβ) backbone chemical shifts could be readily obtained from 3D through-bond correlation experiments (see for example, strips taken from 3D HNCA and HN(CO)CA spectra and from 3D HN(CA)CB and HN(COCA)CB spectra shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and B, respectively).


### Transient Secondary Structure within the Observable Minor B State of RfaHCTD.
  The backbone 13C, 1H, and 15N shifts provide a reliable means of delineating secondary structure elements (21–23). From a simple qualitative perspective the backbone 13Cα and 13C’ secondary shifts (difference between observed and calculated random coil shifts) enable one to easily detect helical elements as these are generally characterized by large positive 13Cα secondary chemical shift values (24), and likewise positive 13C′ secondary chemical shifts. Regions of β-strand are generally characterized by smaller negative secondary 13Cα shifts. Plots of the secondary 13Cα and 13C′ shifts for the observable major A and minor B states of RfaHCTD, as well as of the CTD in full-length RfaH are shown in Fig. 2A. The secondary 13Cα and 13C′ shifts for the major A state are fully consistent with the presence of 5 β-strands (denoted as β1 to β5) (Fig. 2 A, Top), while those of the CTD in full-length RfaH indicate the presence of two helices (denoted as α4 and α5). The secondary 13Cα and 13C′ shifts for the observable minor B state of RfaHCTD indicate the presence of a region with high helical propensity denoted as α5* which overlaps with helix α5 in full-length RfaH.

  A more quantitative assessment of secondary structure is provided by the artificial neural network program TALOS-N, trained to reliably extract backbone torsion angles and provide corresponding secondary structure indices from backbone 1HN, 15N, 13Cα, 1Hα, 13Cβ, and 13C′ shifts (23). This more rigorous analysis of backbone secondary shifts confirms the presence of five well-defined β strands for the A state of RfaHCTD (Fig. 3A); two helices for the CTD in full-length RfaH (Fig. 3C); and two strands, β1* (residues 115 to 120) and β2* (residues 127 to 132), and a helix, α5* (residues 136 to 145), for the B state of RfaHCTD (Fig. 3B). β1* and β2* in the B state of RfaHCTD coincide with strands β1 and β2 in the A state of RfaHCTD but their secondary structure indices are reduced by ~20 and ~80%, respectively. Helix α5* in the B state of RfaHCTD starts at one residue later than helix α5 in full-length RfaH and is approximately six residues shorter. Further, the secondary structure index of helix α5* is reduced by ~25% relative to helix α5. It should be noted that the TALOS secondary structure index (23) reports on the probability that a stretch of residues forms a particular secondary structure element but cannot be used in a straightforward manner to determine the occupancy of that element.

  Correlation plots for the differences in 15N backbone chemical shifts between the A and B states of RfaHCTD (15N-ΔδA-B) with the difference in chemical shifts between the A state and either the calculated random coil shifts (15N-ΔδA-random coil) or the shifts in full-length RfaH (15N-ΔδA-full length) are also informative and are shown in Fig. 2 B, Left and Right panels, respectively. Overall 15N-ΔδA-B is more highly correlated with 15N-ΔδA-random coil (correlation coefficient Rall = 0.98) than with 15N-ΔδA-full length (Rall = 0.88) indicating that the B state is indeed largely random coil despite the existence of transient secondary structure elements. However, the agreement Q factor (see Fig. 2 legend for the definition) for the helix α5* residues is significantly higher for the correlation of 15N-ΔδA-B with 15N-ΔδA-full length (Q = 0.27) than with15N-ΔδA-random coil (Q = 0.45), while the reverse is true for the residues comprising the β1*/β2* hairpin loop (Q = 0.98 and 0.24, respectively).

  Thus, the backbone chemical shift data are consistent with a structural ensemble for the B state of RfaHCTD in which two transient strands, β1* and β2*, likely stabilized by the formation of a β1*/β2* hairpin loop, and an α helix (α5*) exchange rapidly (on the submicrosecond time scale) with random coil conformations. Such a time scale is fully compatible with the experimentally determined speed limit of folding for small proteins (27, 28). Helix α5*, while transient, has significant occupancy (up to ~40% from the 13Cα and 13C′ secondary shifts), while the occupancy of the β1*/β2* hairpin loop is significantly lower and the majority of the time occupies a random coil configuration. The upper limit for the occupancy of the β1*/β2* strands in the B state is likely in the 10 to 20% range judging from a comparison of the 15N secondary shifts for the A and B states (SI Appendix, Fig. S6), but cannot be estimated in a straightforward manner from the 13Cα and 13C′ secondary shifts since these are not uniformly negative over the range of φ/ψ values characteristic of β-sheet residues (24).


### Backbone Amide Residual Dipolar Couplings (RDCs) and 15N-Relaxation Data for the RfaHCTD.
  The 1DNH backbone amide RDCs measured for the major A and minor B states of RfaHCTD aligned using phage pf1 (29), as well as 15N-relaxation data (15N-R1, R2 and {1H}-15N heteronuclear NOE) are shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S7.

  One-bond backbone RDCs, measured on weakly aligned macromolecules, provide information on the orientation of bond vectors relative to an external alignment tensor and thus provide a sensitive means of assessing the accuracy of experimentally determined protein folds (30). The 1DNH RDCs for the major A state cross-peaks (SI Appendix, Fig. S7, Top Left) are fully consistent with the β-sheet structure of the major A state: The RDC R-factor (31) calculated for the coordinates of the β-strands of the published ensemble of NMR structure [PDB code 2LCL (4)] is ~15% (the loops were excluded as these are poorly defined in the NMR structure), and the magnitude of the axial component of the alignment tensor (DaNH) and rhombicity have values of −12.7 Hz and 0.45, respectively. Excluding the flexible N- and C-terminal residues with negative {1H}-15N NOE values, the 15N relaxation data are rather uniform across the residues of the major A state with average values of 7.6 ± 0.8 s−1, 1.8 ± 0.1 s−1, and 0.70 ± 0.06 for 15N-R2, 15N-R1, and {1H}-15N NOE, respectively (see SI Appendix, Table S1 and lower left 3 panels in SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Model-free analysis (32) of these average values yields an overall rotational correlation time (τR) of 5.8 ± 0.2 ns with a squared order parameter S2 of 0.83 ± 0.01 corresponding to bond librations. Thus, one can conclude that the major A state is a predominantly rigid, well-folded structure.

  In contrast, the 1DNH RDC values for the minor B state are ~five times lower than those for the major A state (SI Appendix, Fig. S7, Top Right), and one can estimate an approximate DaNH value of ~ 2 to 2.5 Hz from the distribution of 1DNH values (33). Similarly, the overall average values of 15N-R2 (4.5 ± 1.2 s−1) and {1H}-15N NOE (0.32 ± 0.16) (SI Appendix, Table S1, and the two right middle panels of SI Appendix, Fig. S7) are considerably smaller than those for the A state. If one assumes that overall tumbling and bond libration are the same for the A and B states, one obtains, using extended model-free analysis (34, 35), an additional correlation time of ~1 ns for slower motions (that are faster than the rotational correlation but much slower than bond libration on the picosecond time scale), with a corresponding order parameter Sslow2 of ~0.7 for helix α5* and ~0.45 for the remaining residues (including strands β1*/β2*). These data further support the conclusions from secondary backbone chemical shifts that the B state samples an ensemble of structures rapidly exchanging between random coil configurations and states with sizeable (~40%) occupancy of helix α5* but significantly less occupancy (~10 to 20%) for strands β1*/β2*.


### CS-ROSETTA Modeling of the Minor B State of RfaHCTD.
  The CS-ROSETTA program incorporates backbone (1HN, 15N, 13Cα, 1Hα, 13C′, and 13Cβ) chemical shifts into an empirically optimized fragment selection procedure from the Protein Data Bank and has been shown to have a high degree of success in reliably predicting the structures of small proteins from minimal experimental NMR data (26). We therefore used CS-ROSETTA to predict possible partially folded structures sampled by the B state ensemble in solution using the complete set of backbone 1H, 13C, and 15N shifts for the minor B state of RfaHCTD. A total of 10,000 structures were calculated and Cα trace superpositions of the lowest 10 energy structures, best-fitted to either helix α5* or the β1*/β2* hairpin loop, are displayed in Fig. 4, together with ribbon diagrams of the individual structures. (The lowest energy structure is also shown in the right panel of Fig. 3B) Helix α5* is preserved in all 10 structures. The β1*/β2* region also forms a hairpin loop in all the structures, but the entire β-sheet structure is not preserved in all cases. Moreover, the orientation of the β1*/β2* hairpin loop relative to helix α5 is quite variable. That being said, in 8 of the 10 lowest energy structures the β1*/β2* hairpin loop occupies approximately the same region of conformational space relative to helix α5* within a cone of ~50° to 70° (Fig. 4A).


### Slow Conformational Exchange between the Major A and Minor B States of RfaHCTD Characterized by 15N-CEST.
  As noted in the section on backbone chemical shift assignments, the major A and minor B states are in slow exchange on the chemical shift time scale, a regime ideally suited for kinetic analysis by CEST (17, 18). In the case of RfaHCTD where both the major A and minor B states are observable, application of a weak radiofrequency (RF) field at the resonance position of the B state will result in a loss of intensity of the corresponding resonance of the A state and vice versa (Fig. 5 A, Top and Bottom rows, respectively, for selected residues, and SI Appendix, Figs. S8 and S9, respectively, for the complete datasets). Global best-fitting of the appropriate Bloch-McConnell equations (36) for two-site exchange to the complete set of 15N-CEST profiles (obtained by applying a 600 ms duration weak constant wave saturation pulse at a series of equally spaced frequency offsets with RF field strengths of 10, 15, and 25 Hz) recorded for cross-peaks of both the major A and minor B states yields an exchange lifetime (τex) of ~300 ms (with rate constants kAB ~ 0.8 s−1 and kBA ~ 2.4 s−1) and populations pA ~ 75% and pB ~ 25%. The values for the differences in 15N chemical shifts between the B and A states, 15N-ΔωB-A, obtained from the 15N-CEST data are in complete agreement with the differences in 15N chemical shifts (ΔδB-A) obtained directly from the 3D zz-exchange spectra used to assign the minor state 1HN/15N cross-peaks (SI Appendix, Fig. S10A).


### Fast Conformational Exchange Processes Involving the Major A and Minor B States of RfaHCTD Probed by 15N-CPMG Relaxation Dispersion.
  A comparison of the 15N-R2 values obtained from R1ρ and CEST experiments reveals that the latter are significantly higher than the former in distinct regions of both the major A and minor B states (SI Appendix, Fig. S11) indicative of line broadening arising from exchange processes in the intermediate regime on the chemical shift timescale that are faster than the slow reversible A ↔ B conformational transition probed by the 15N-CEST experiment (17, 37). (Note that R1ρ measurements with a spin-lock field of 2 kHz effectively suppress (~90%) exchange processes slower than ~240 µs (=[(2,000 × 2π)2/9]−1/2). To unravel these faster processes we resorted to 15N-CPMG dispersion experiments that generally probe exchange from the mid-microsecond to low millisecond timescale providing differences in chemical shifts exist between the observable and sparsely populated states (14, 19).

  The 1HN/15N cross-peaks of both the major A and minor B states of RfaHCTD exhibit dispersions (Fig. 5B and SI Appendix, Figs. S12 and S13) at spectrometer frequencies of 600 and 900 MHz in which the contribution of exchange line broadening (Rex) to the effective R2eff rate is decreased as a function of the strength of the CPMG field (νCPMG, dictated by the repetition rate of a series of 180° pulses). The shape of the dispersion curves, which is readily calculated using the Bloch-McConnell equations (36), is a complex function of the various exchange rate constants, species populations, differences in chemical shifts, spectrometer frequency, and the kinetic scheme of the underlying exchange processes.

  Simulated 15N-CPMG relaxation dispersion profiles for two-site exchange using the exchange rate constants and 15N-ΔωB-A values (SI Appendix, Table S2) determined from the 15N-CEST experiments (see the previous section) do not replicate the majority of experimentally observed 15N-CPMG relaxation dispersion curves (SI Appendix, Figs. S12 and S13). The normalized χ2 values are 22.6 (SI Appendix, Fig. S14A) and 51.7 (SI Appendix, Fig. S16A) for the 15N-CPMG dispersions obtained for the 1HN/15N cross-peaks of the major A and minor B states, respectively. One can therefore conclude that, in addition to reversible slow (τex ~ 300 ms) A ↔ B interconversion, the major A and minor B states are each involved in additional faster exchange processes. Since the A ↔ B interconversion is slow, one can characterize the underlying scheme of the fast exchange processes involving the A and B states separately by globally fitting their respective 15N-CPMG relaxation dispersion profiles in conjunction with the 15N-CEST data.

  The simplest kinetic model that accounts quantitatively for both the 15N-CPMG relaxation dispersion for the 1HN/15N cross-peaks of the observable major A state and the 15N-CEST profiles [normalized χ2 (all) = 1.02, χ2 (CPMG) = 1.00, χ2 (CEST) = 1.03] is a three-site exchange system (SI Appendix, Figs. S14 and S15), involving the addition of an invisible state, A′, and given by the kinetic scheme A′ ↔ A ↔ B, where the A′ ↔ A process is fast compared to A ↔ B. Other 3-site exchange models such as A ↔ I ↔ B and A ↔ B ↔ B′ have much larger normalized χ2 (CPMG) values (2.9 and 14.8, respectively) and exhibit clear systematic deviations between observed and calculated dispersions (SI Appendix, Fig. S14B and Top panel of SI Appendix, Fig. S14C, respectively).

  In the case of the observable minor B state, a three-site exchange model A ↔ B ↔ B′ has reasonable agreement with both the 15N-CPMG relaxation dispersion and 15N-CEST data [normalized χ2 (all) = 1.24], but some clear systematic deviations between observed and calculated 15N-CPMG dispersions are apparent [χ2 (CPMG) = 2.1; SI Appendix, Fig. S16B] necessitating extension to either linear (A ↔ B ↔ B′ ↔ B″) or branched (A ↔ B in conjunction with B ↔ B′ and B ↔ B″) four-state models. The introduction of the B″ state removes any systematic deviations between observed and calculated 15N-CPMG relaxation dispersion curves [χ2 (CPMG) ~ 1.4] and reduces the normalized χ2 (all) to ~1.1 (SI Appendix, Figs. S16C and S17). No distinction in terms of fits can be made between the linear and branched models.

  With the above analysis in hand, we proceeded to globally fit the 15N-CPMG relaxation dispersion data for the 1HN/15N cross-peaks of both the observable major A and minor B states in conjunction with the 15N-CEST data to linear and branched five-state exchange models [Fig. 5B and SI Appendix, Fig. S18; normalized χ2 (all) = 0.98, χ2 (CPMG) = 1.02/1.03, χ2 (CEST) = 0.95/0.96, where the first and second numbers refer to the branched and linear models, respectively]. The results are summarized in Fig. 6. The 15N-CEST data uniquely inform on the slow A ↔ B exchange process involving the two directly observable species A and B; the 15N-CPMG relaxation dispersion curves obtained for the major A state cross-peaks inform on exchange involving the invisible species A′ and the observable A and B states while those for the minor B state cross-peaks inform on exchange involving the invisible B′ and B″ species and the observable B and A states. The exchange lifetime (τεx) for the A′ ↔ A process is ~500 µs with the invisible state A′ populated at ~0.35%. The occupancies of the B′ and B″ excited states are ~0.25 to 0.35 and ~0.05 %, respectively. τex for the B ↔ B′ process is ~1.2 to 1.3 ms for both linear and branched models. τex for the B ↔ B″ process in the branched model is ~800 µs, while τex for the B′ ↔ B″ process in the linear model is ~1 ms. The choice of linear or branched models has no significant effect on the 15N-ΔωB′-B and ΔωB″-B values (SI Appendix, Fig. S19) and hence does not impact any structural inferences made regarding the nature of the invisible B′ and B″ states.


### 15N Backbone Chemical Shift Characterization of the Excited A′ State.
  A plot of the 15N-ΔωA′-A chemical shift differences as a function of residue is shown in Fig. 7A and mapped on the structure of the five-stranded β-roll A state in Fig. 7B. Overall the values of 15N-ΔωA′-A are small (≤ ~1 ppm) with larger values predominantly localized to a specific region comprising the loop connecting strands β1 and β2 and strand β4. One can conclude that the five-stranded β-roll structure is preserved in A′ and the difference between the A′ and A states likely constitutes a relatively small conformational change in the β1/β2 loop which lies on top of strand β4.


### 15N Backbone Chemical Shift Characterization of the Excited B′ and B″ States.
  Plots of the 15N chemical shift differences, 15N-ΔωB′-B and |15N-ΔωB′-B|, as a function of residue are shown in Fig. 8 for the branched model and in SI Appendix, Fig. S20 for the linear model. As already noted, the values of 15N-ΔωB′-B and |15N-ΔωB′-B| are essentially the same for the branched and linear models (see correlation plots shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S19), and therefore, the choice of model does not impact structural inferences drawn below.

  The values of 15N-ΔωB′-B are small with all but two (residues 127 and 135 located at the beginning of the transient β2* and α5* secondary structure elements, respectively) having values of less the 1.0 ppm and none greater than 2 ppm (Fig. 8, Top). This suggests that the conformational space sampled by the excited B′ state is similar to that sampled by the observable B minor state. However, positive 15N-ΔωB′-B values in the helix α5* region, denoting a downfield shift of the B′ resonances relative to state B, are indicative of a reduced average α-helical population, suggesting that the configuration of state B′ is more random-coil-like than state B.

  In contrast to the small values of 15N-ΔωB′-B, the absolute values of |15N-ΔωB″-B| are much larger (Fig. 8, Lower panel). Note that the small population of state B* (~0.05%) precluded the determination of the signs of the 15N-ΔωB″-B values. The largest |15N-ΔωB″-B| values are observed from residues 134 to 150 suggestive of increased helicity and C-terminal extension of helix α5* to closer approximate helix α5 in full-length RfaH. The second largest |15N-ΔωB″-B| values are seen in the region from residues 115 to 121 which may possibly suggest that the transient β1* strand in state B is partially converted to a transient helical conformation that would correspond to the N-terminal end of helix α4 in full-length RfaH.

  If the structural inferences, based on 15N chemical shifts, regarding states B′ and B″ above, are correct, then it seems likely that the branched model provides a more likely description of the exchange processes than the linear one, even though the two models cannot be distinguished from the global fits to the 15N-CPMG relaxation dispersion and 15N-CEST data. In the branched model, state B′ would represent an off-pathway state that is more random-coil-like than state B, while state B″ constitutes an additional intermediate further along the fold-switching pathway.


### Concluding Remarks.
  In the current work, we have sought to uncover and characterize some of the conformational states sampled during the course of fold switching of the C-terminal domain of RfaH from an all α-helical hairpin in the autoinhibited resting state of RfaH to a five-stranded β-roll structure in the active state bound to ops-paused RNAP. Since exchange between the α and β forms of the CTD cannot be detected for free RfaH, we sought to address this problem by characterizing the structural transitions sampled by the semistable isolated C-terminal domain, RfaHCTD, using exchanged-based NMR spectroscopy. We showed that RfaHCTD samples five distinct states, two of which, A and B, are observable and three (A′, B′, and B″) are sparsely populated (with populations <1%) and hence spectroscopically invisible or “dark” by direct measurement (Fig. 6). The A and B states exchange slowly on the chemical shift time scale (τex ~ 300 ms) while exchange between the observable A and excited A′ states and between the observable B and excited B′ and B″ states occurs on a two orders of magnitude faster time scale (τex ~ 0.5 to 1.3 ms). The predominant A state has an occupancy of ~76 to 77% and comprises a semistable five-stranded β-roll structure, while the B state is populated at ~23% and consists of a rapidly exchanging (microsecond time scale) ensemble of largely unfolded structures that include transient α-helical (α5*) and β-hairpin (β1*/β2*) secondary structure elements that may fold to form a compact ensemble of structures in which the β-hairpin lies on top of the α helix (Fig. 4). This structural ensemble, containing secondary structure elements from both the full-length RfaH autoinhibited and RNA polymerase-bound states (Fig. 1A), suggests that state B could be a true intermediate in the fold-switching pathway. In this context, residues 115 to 120 may contribute to promote strand β1 formation, residues 127 to 132 may destabilize α-helix 4 and promote the formation of strand β2, and residues 136 to 145 may help stabilize α-helix 5 while destabilizing the β3 strand. These hotspots could potentially serve as targets for protein engineering to shift the structure toward one conformation or another. The A ↔ A′ transition likely involves minor conformational changes in a localized loop region (β1/β2) of the β-roll structure (Fig. 7). The B′ and B states are similar to one another in terms of 15N backbone chemical shifts, and the B ↔ B′ transition likely involves a small population reweighting of conformers toward a more random-like configuration, especially in the region corresponding to α5* (Fig. 8, Upper). The transition from B/B′ to B″, however, entails much larger backbone 15N chemical shift changes, especially in the regions corresponding to β1* and α5* in the B state (Fig. 8, Lower). These observations are suggestive of the following: a transition of the transient β1* strand to a nascent helix, which would correspond to the N-terminal end of helix α4 in full-length RfaH; and increased helicity and C-terminal extension of the transient α5* helix to approximate more closely to helix α5 in full-length RfaH. Thus, the B″ state may represent a folding intermediate bridging the B state to the helical hairpin conformation seen in full-length RfaH. Further work will focus on engineering a soluble isolated N-terminal domain of RfaH that interacts directly with the isolated RfaHCTD in order to observe the complete fold-switching pathway from β-roll to helical hairpin structures.


## Experimental Procedures

### Protein Expression, Uniform Isotope Labeling, and Purification.
  Expression, isotope labeling, and purification of RfaHCTD are provided in the SI Appendix, Experimental Procedures.


### NMR Experiments.
  All NMR data were recorded at 25 °C on Bruker 500, 600, 700, and 900 MHz NMR spectrometers equipped with either z- or x,y,z-gradient triple resonance cryoprobes using 1.0 mM isotopically labeled RfaHCTD in 95% (v/v)/5% (v/v) D2O/H2O buffer containing 25 mM potassium phosphate, pH 6.5, 0.5 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), and 1mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride. Spectra were processed using the program NMRPipe (38) and analyzed using the software package XIPP (39). All triple resonance experiments were recorded on a U-[15N/13C/2H]-labeled sample, with the exception of the HBHA(CACB)CONH experiment for which a U-[15N/13C]-labeled sample was used. All other NMR experiments were recorded on a U-[15N/2H] sample. 1H, 13C, and 15N backbone resonance assignments were obtained from analysis of 3D HHN and HNN zz-exchange experiments (see SI Appendix, Fig. S2 for the pulse sequence) and 3D HNCA, HN(CO)CA, HN(CA)CB, HN(COCA)CB, HBHA(CACB)CONH and HNCO through-bond heteronuclear scalar correlation experiments using mixing-time frequency encoding to ensure high resolution in the 15N indirect dimension (40). The 3D zz-exchange (SI Appendix, Figs. S3 and S4) and triple resonance 3D HN(CO)CA and HNCA (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A) experiments were acquired with nonuniform sampling in the indirect dimensions (41), and details are provided in the Experimental Procedures of the SI Appendix. All other spectra were acquired with uniformed sampling in the indirect dimensions. The backbone chemical shifts for the observable major A and minor B states of RfaHCTD have been deposited in the Biological Magnetic Resonance Bank (BMRB accession codes 52718 and 52719, respectively).


### Secondary Structure from Backbone Chemical Shifts Using TALOS and 3D Structure Models from CS-ROSETTA.
  Secondary structure indices for the major A and minor B states of RfaHCTD were calculated from the assigned backbone 1HN, 15N, 13Cα, 13Cβ, 13C’, and 1Hα chemical shifts using the program TALOS-N (23). Models for the 3D structure of the transient, partially folded minor B state of RfaHCTD were computed using CS-ROSETTA (26) on the basis of the same backbone chemical shifts used for the TALOS-N secondary structure prediction. A total of 10,000 CS-ROSETTA models were generated and the l0 with the lowest total energies were selected for analysis (Fig. 4). Structures were rendered with the molecular graphics package PyMOL (http://www.pymol.org/pymol).


### 15N CPMG Relaxation Dispersion Measurements.
  15N-CPMG relaxation dispersion experiments were recorded at 600 and 900 MHz using a pulse scheme with amide proton decoupling to measure the rates of in-phase 15N coherences (42). The relaxation period was set to 60 ms, and the CPMG field strengths (ν = 1/2τCP, where τCP is the time between two consecutive 180° pulses of the CPMG pulse train) were 17, 33, 50, 67, 83, 100, 117, 133, 150, 167, 200, 250, 333, 417, 500, 583, 667, 833, and 1,000 Hz. Continuous wave (CW) decoupling of amide protons during the relaxation period was carried out with a nominal 1H RF field strength of 11 kHz, adjusted for each value of ν as described previously and applied during the entire CMPG period (42). At each spectrometer frequency, 21 datasets were obtained comprising the 19 vCPMG values listed above, a duplicate at ν = 500 Hz (to estimate the uncertainty in the measured R2, eff values), and a reference spectrum without the 60 ms relaxation delay. 210 and 256 t1 complex points in the indirect 15N dimension were collected at spectrometer frequencies of 600 and 900 MHz, respectively, with eight scans per FID and a 1.5 s relaxation delay between scans. At 600 MHz, the data matrix comprised 210* (15N, t1) × 1,922* (1H, t2) complex points with total acquisition times of 105 and 100 ms, respectively. At 900 MHz, the data matrix comprised 256* (15N, t1) × 3,324* (1H, t2) complex points with total acquisition times of 103 and 100 ms, respectively. The complete 15N-CPMG datasets at 600 and 900 MHz were obtained in 35 and 46 h, respectively. The average errors for the R2, eff values, estimated from a single νCPMG value duplicate, are as follows: 0.18 ± 0.04 s−1 (range 0.1 to 0.2) at 900 MHz and 0.12 ± 0.03 s−1 (range 0.1 to 0.2) at 600 MHz for the major A state; and 0.34 ± 0.09 s−1 (range 0.2 to 0.4) at 900 MHz and 0.23 ± 0.05 s−1 (range 0.2 to 0.3) at 600 MHz for the minor B state.


### 15N-CEST Experiments.
  Three 15N-CEST (chemical exchange saturation transfer) experiments (17, 18) were recorded at a spectrometer frequency of 600 MHz in an interleaved manner as described previously (37, 43) with the exchange time TCEST set to 600 ms and 15N constant wave saturation field strengths of 10, 15, and 25 Hz (applied from −1,120 to +1,160 Hz with the 15N carrier set to 118 ppm). For the data collected with a saturation RF strength of 10 Hz, 231 spectra were collected with the saturation pulse applied in 10 Hz increments; for the data collected with a saturation RF strength of 15 Hz, 153 spectra were collected with the saturation pulse applied in 15 Hz increments; and for a saturation RF field strength of 25 Hz, 93 spectra were collected in 25 Hz increments. For each 15N-CEST dataset, a reference spectrum was collected at the same RF field strength but with the saturation frequency applied at −50,000 Hz from the 15N carrier frequency (118 ppm). The data matrix comprised 150* (15N, t1) × 2048* (1H, t2) complex points with total acquisition times of 75 and 189 ms, respectively. Total recording time was ~7.5, ~5, and ~3 d for the data with saturation RF field strengths of 10, 15, and 25 Hz, respectively.


### Quantitative Kinetic Analysis of 15N CPMG Relaxation Dispersion and 15N-CEST Data.
  The 15N-CPMG relaxation dispersion and 15N-CEST data were fit using similar protocols to those described in ref. 44 based on numerical solution of the appropriate Bloch-McConnell equations (36) with rate constants treated as global parameters, and Δω values and intrinsic relaxation rates treated as residue-specific parameters. The relative weights used for the 15N-CEST and CPMG relaxation dispersion data in the global fits were 1.0 and 0.5, respectively. There are two observable states: the folded major one (~75%) denoted as A and the largely unfolded minor one (~25%) designated as B. The 15N-CEST data observed on the 1H-15N cross-peaks of the observable major and minor states (SI Appendix, Figs. S8 and S9) were initially fit separately and together to a two-state exchange model. The calculated populations of A and B and the chemical shift differences ΔωB-A between them were fully consistent with the corresponding populations derived from the integrated cross-peak intensities in a 2D 1H-15N heteronuclear single quantum coherence spectrum and the chemical shift assignments for the A and B states derived from the various 3D zz-exchange and heteronuclear triple resonance spectra (SI Appendix, Fig. S10). In addition to slow exchange (τex ~ 300 ms), dispersions were observed in the 15N-CPMG relaxation dispersion data obtained for cross-peaks of both the observable major (A) and minor (B) states indicative of exchange on the submillisecond to millisecond time scale. Initially, we fit the 15N-CEST together with the 15N-CPMG relaxation dispersion data observed for the major A and minor B state cross-peaks separately. The simplest model required to fit the 15N-CPMG relaxation dispersion data for the cross-peaks of the observable major A state in conjunction with the 15N-CEST data comprised one invisible state (A′) in addition to the two visible species A and B (SI Appendix, Figs. S14 and S15). The simplest model required to fit the 15N-CPMG relaxation data for the cross-peaks of the observable minor B state in conjunction with the 15N-CEST data comprised two invisible states (B′ and B″) in addition to the two visible ones, A and B (SI Appendix, Figs. S16 and S17). Finally, we carried out a global fit of the 15N-CPMG relaxation dispersion data for the observable major A and minor B states together with the 15N-CEST data to two five-state models, linear and branched, comprising species A′, A, B, B′, and B″ (Fig. 4 and SI Appendix, Fig. S18). The signs of 15N-ΔωB-A are unambiguously defined from the 15N-CEST data (as well as from the backbone assignments). This facilitates the determination of the signs of 15N-Δω involving the invisible species obtained from the 15N-CPMG relaxation dispersion data. However, to ensure complete consistency, we made use of the same approach described previously in which the signs of 15N-Δω are defined by carrying out simultaneous fits using several different species as the reference state (ω = 0) (44). Thus, for example, for the simplest three-state model involving the A′, A, and B states, we fit duplicate datasets simultaneously with ωA = 0 in one case and ωA′ = 0 in the other, thereby ensuring that ΔωA′-B is effectively constrained to be equal to ΔωA′-A + ΔωA-B. This procedure is easily extended to the more complex 4- and 5-state models. This approach was successful in determining all the signs of the Δω values with the exception of those involving the B″ state as its population was too small such that the signs of ΔωB″-B and/or ΔωB″-B′ do not impact the 15N-CPMG relaxation dispersion curves to any detectable extent.

  Further, because the population of the B″ state is so small (~0.05%) we investigated the robustness of the |15N-ΔωB″-B| values and rate constants involving the B′ and B″ states by carrying out three sets of calculations (for both the branched and linear models) with the initial values of 15N-ΔωB″-B set to 0, +5, and −5 ppm. The results are reported in the SI Appendix, Figs. S21 and S22 and Table S7 and show that the |15N-ΔωB″-B| values are robust within the uncertainties of their determination, and the overall profiles of |15N-ΔωB″-B| as a function of residue are essentially the same. The values of the rate constants involving the B′/B″ states (Fig. 6) and |15N-ΔωB″-B| (Fig. 8 and SI Appendix, Figs. S19 and S20) reported in the main text were obtained with the initial values of 15N-ΔωB″-B set to 0.
