# Artesunate Promotes Bone Remodeling Through TRAF6-Mediated NF-κB Signaling Under Orthodontic Stress in Diabetic Rats


## Objectives
  To determine the effects of artesunate (ART) on bone remodeling in vivo under orthodontic stress in diabetic rats and explore the underlying mechanisms in vitro.


## Materials and methods
  A rat model of type 2 diabetes mellitus with orthodontic tooth movement was established. The rats received ART and/or metformin (Met) orally. The effects of ART and Met on periodontium changes were evaluated using tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase and immunohistochemical staining. Molecular docking analyses were employed to investigate the mechanisms of ART action. In vitro, the effects of ART on osteogenic and osteoclastic activity were explored by examining TRAF6 and NF-κB expression under hyperglycemic and static pressure conditions via immunofluorescence and Western blotting.


## Results
  ART enhanced bone metabolism despite hyperglycemia, though mechanical stress still induced bone resorption. Treatment with ART alone or in combination with Met promoted osteogenesis. TRAF6, NF-κB and the OPG/RANKL/RANK signaling pathways have been identified as key mediators of these effects. The expression of the osteogenesis-associated factor OPG increased after ART and Met treatment, while that of TRAF6 and the osteoclast-associated factors RANKL and NF-κB decreased.


## Conclusions
  Increased bone resorption and decreased bone formation are characteristics of type 2 diabetes, impacting orthodontic tooth movement. ART administration alone promotes bone remodeling under static pressure and hyperglycemic conditions. These effects are mediated by lowering blood sugar levels, inhibiting osteoclast function, and improving osteogenesis through mechanisms closely associated with the OPG/TRAF6/NF-κB signaling pathway.


## Introduction
  Diabetes is a common disease with a global prevalence of 8.8%-10.5% in individuals aged 20-79 between 2017 and 2021, as estimated by the International Diabetes Federation (IDF).1,2 T2DM is the most prevalent subtype of diabetes mellitus. Individuals with T2DM often exhibit metabolic and endocrine abnormalities, leading to increased bone resorption and reduced levels of essential osteogenic elements, including calcium, phosphorus, and magnesium. As a result, this condition heightens fracture risk.3,4 Over time, patients with T2DM experience increased rates of periodontitis, dental caries, and other oral infections. Blood sugar control helps mitigate diabetes progression and its associated complications.

  Bone resorption due to mechanical stress is common in orthodontics and can compromise long-term dental.5,6 Recent studies indicate that orthodontic force under hyperglycemia increases periodontal bone resorption while delaying bone repair.7 In the periodontal membrane on the tension-exposed side, patients with diabetes exhibit reduced expression of osteogenic markers such as Piezo1, leading to impaired periodontal tissue regeneration.8 Metformin (Met) is the first-line treatment for type 2 diabetes, particularly in individuals with poor dietary control.9,10 Numerous studies have examined how Met affects bone metabolism in diabetes. For example, Stage et al.11 showed that Met suppressed bone resorption and improved blood sugar levels. In a series of in vitro and in vivo experiments, Mai et al.12 determined that Met could reduce the expression of RANKL while upregulating that of OPG in osteoblasts and inhibiting osteoclast activity.

  The ability of artesunate (ART) to regulate inflammatory mediator levels, such as NF-κB, TNF-α, IkB, and IL-6, is well-established. For example, Zeng et al.13 reported that ART reduced the activity of inflammatory osteoclasts in periodontitis-affected tissue following lipopolysaccharide treatment while also decreasing osteolysis through the inhibition of upstream TLR4/TRAF6 and downstream NFATc1 signaling activity. Similarly, Yu et al.14 found that ART reduced NF-κB activity in vitro and in vivo, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic drug for diabetes.

  Recent studies indicate that ART improves salivary dysfunction in NOD/ShiLtj mice by regulating the NF-κB signaling axis via TRAF6.15 These findings suggest that ART and/or Met may exert beneficial effects by modulating the TRAF6/NF-κB axis. Accordingly, this study employed molecular docking to identify ART target proteins associated with bone remodeling, confirming that ART can regulate TRAF6/NF-κB-related pathway activation. By inhibiting NF-κB signaling pathways, ART and Met were found to stimulate osteogenesis and inhibit osteoclast function in a rat model of diabetes and orthodontic tooth movement. While these results suggest a role for TRAF6 in regulating bone metabolism, the mechanisms through which ART, alone or in combination with Met, modulates alveolar bone metabolism under conditions of mechanical force and hyperglycemia remain unexplored.

  This study investigated the efficacy of ART treatment alone or in combination with Met in modulating bone reconstruction-related OPG/RANKL/RANK signaling via the TRAF6/NF-κB pathway.


## Materials and methods

### Experimental animals
  Specific pathogen-free (SPF) male Sprague–Dawley rats, aged 5 weeks (150-180 g), were obtained from the Animal Center of Guangxi Medical University (Nanning, China). The animals were housed in a controlled facility at a temperature of 20-25°C with unrestricted access to food and water. The bedding was changed regularly, and routine cage cleaning was performed. All animal studies were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Guangxi Medical University. At the end of the experiment, the rats were euthanized via intraperitoneal injection of an excess dose of pentobarbital sodium.

  A T2DM rat model was established as previously described16 using a high-fat, high-sugar diet (HFD; Boaigang-B1135DM, Boaigang Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) combined with a low-dose administration of streptozotocin (STZ; Sigma, MO, USA). Following a 1-week acclimatization period, 8 rats were randomly selected for the normal control (NC) group and were fed a standard chow diet, while the remaining 32 animals were assigned to the model group. The HFD was administered for 8 weeks, after which intraperitoneal injections of STZ (35 mg/kg) were given, while the NC group received an equivalent volume of citric acid buffer. If blood glucose levels did not reach FBG ≥11.1 mmol/L within 3 days post-STZ injection, an additional STZ dose (35 mg/kg) was administered to ensure successful T2DM induction. On days 3 and 7 post-STZ injections, blood samples were collected from the tail veins in the morning to assess the fasting blood glucose (FBG) levels, with FBG ≥11.1 mmol/L indicating successful T2DM modeling.17 3 weeks after T2DM modeling, rats in the model group were subjected to a 50-g orthodontic force applied between the maxillary first molars and the incisors using a nickel-titanium coil spring to simulate orthodontic tooth movement.18


### Animal grouping and treatment
  The T2DM model rats were randomly allocated (n = 8/group) into the following groups: DM, ART treatment, Met treatment, and ART+Met treatment. ART (50 mg/kg) and Met HCl (150 mg/kg) were administered intragastricly to the respective groups, while NC and DM group rats received equal volumes of normal saline. After a 3-week course of regular intragastric administration, the orthodontic tooth movement model was established, followed by another week of treatment on the same schedule. At the end of the experiment, the rats were euthanized (Figure 1).Fig. 1Experimental design. STZ was intraperitoneally injected at 8 weeks after HFD feeding. Rats with HFD/STZ treatment respectively received intragastric administration of ART, Met, and ART/Met combination for 3 consecutive weeks at 9 weeks after feeding. A rat orthodontic tooth movement model was then established, and the drug intervention was continued for 1 week.Fig 1


### General analyses
  Weekly assessments were conducted to monitor the body weight, average food consumption, and average water intake of the rats starting from the initiation of the study.


### Biochemical analyses
  The lower third of the tail was disinfected with alcohol before blood collection. Rats were fasted for 10 hours with unrestricted access to water. Blood was then extracted from the tail vein using a needle, and FBG was measured using blood glucose test strips and a glucose meter. Baseline glucose tolerance was determined based on FBG levels. To perform a glucose tolerance test, rats were administered a 20% glucose solution (1 mL/100 g body weight), and blood glucose levels were measured at 30, 60, 90, and 120 min. The area under the glucose tolerance curve (AUC) was then calculated. Following euthanasia, blood samples were collected from the abdominal aorta. These samples were placed in anticoagulant tubes and stored at low temperatures. Hemoglobin A1C (HbA1c) levels were determined using a test instrument according to the manufacturer's instructions.


### Micro-CT scanning
  The maxillae were fixed in paraformaldehyde, rinsed with normal saline and scanned using micro-computed tomography (micro-CT, LCT-200, Hitachi Corporation, Japan) to assess changes in the first molar region. The micro-CT scanning parameters were set as follows: voltage, 80 kV; current, 160 μA; pixel resolution, 24 μm; scanning layer thickness, 24 μm and 360-degree circular scanning. The region of interest (ROI) for this study was the alveolar bone surrounding the first maxillary molars. The scanned data were imported into Latheta V 3.61B software (Hitachi Corporation, Japan) for iso-segmentation analysis, allowing for calculating the calculation of the average bone density, cancellous bone density, and cortical bone density for each ROI. Following imaging, alveolar bone samples were decalcified using a decalcification solution for further analysis.


### Histological analyses
  Samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, decalcified, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 5 μm-thick slices. As per the manufacturer's instructions, the sections were stained using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) using staining kits. Imaging was performed using an Olympus-BX41 microscope (400x; Olympus Corporation, Japan), with 5 fields of view randomly selected from the root of the first molar on the tension-applied side. Red-stained multinucleated osteoclasts were quantified on both the tooth root surface and the corresponding alveolar bone surface.


### Immunohistochemical and immunofluorescent staining
  Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was conducted as previously described,19 using antibodies against OPG (bs-0431R, Bioss Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China), RANKL (orb11190, Biorbyt Ltd., Cambridge, UK), TRAF6 (BS3684, Bioworld Technology, MN, USA), and NF-κB (8242S, Cell Signaling Technology, MA, USA). A total of 10 fields per section (400x) were selected for microscopic analysis. Immunofluorescence staining was performed independently at least twice, and images were analyzed using ImageJ (NIH, USA) to calculate the average integral optical density (IOD)/area value for each field of view. 5 representative images were collected from each group of 3 samples collected for analysis.


## Cell experiments

### Human periodontal ligament fibroblast (HPDLF) culture and identification
  The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Stomatology Hospital Affiliated with Guangxi Medical University, and informed consent was obtained from all subjects and/or their legal guardians. A total of 20 healthy young permanent premolars extracted from individuals aged 11-15 years during orthodontic treatment, were selected for the study. An aseptic scalpel blade was used to remove approximately one-third of the periodontal membrane tissue connected to the base of each tooth. The tissue was evenly placed at the bottom of a T25 culture flask using the tissue block adherent culture method. A total of 1.5 mL of low-glucose Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS) was added to each flask. Experiments were conducted using HPDLFs extracted from passages 3-5.

  For immunostaining, HPDLFs from passage 5 were plated in 12-well plates (5 × 104 cells /well) and grown until 70-80% confluence, after which they were fixed. Immunostaining confirmed that these cells were positive for vimentin and negative for keratin.


### Static pressure and drug treatment of HPDLFs
  A gravity-loaded hydrostatic device was used to apply static pressure to the HPDLFs. The cells were subjected to a 5 g/cm2 of static pressure, after which the device was removed, and the cells were placed in a CO2 incubator for 12 h.

  HPDLFs are divided into 5 groups. CCK-8 assays were employed to determine the recommended dosages and durations of drug administration (Figure. 2). The experimental groups were as follows: (1) a blank control (NC) group, cultured in low-glucose media containing 5.5 mmol/L glucose and 10% FBS; (2) a diabetic stress (DM) group, cultured in high-glucose media containing 25 mmol/L glucose plus 10% FBS and subjected to static pressure; (3) an ART stress group, cultured in high-glucose media with 12.5 μmol/L ART for 48 h and subjected to static pressure; (4) a Met stress group, cultured in high-glucose media with 5 mmol/L Met for 24 h and subjected to static pressure; (5) a combination ART+Met treatment group, cultured in high-glucose media containing 12.5 μmol/L ART for 48 h and 5 mmol/L Met for 24 h and subjected to static pressure.Fig. 2Cell compressive force equipment. The cck8 method was used to measure the cell viability of HPDLF after the intervention of different concentrations of ART or Met for 6, 12, 24 and 48 hours, respectively. The data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation of 3 independent experiments.Fig 2


### Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) analyses
  HPDLFs (passage 5) were seeded in osteogenic induction media in 6-well plates. After 7 days, ALP activity was measured using a commercial assay kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. The plates were air-dried and photographed for analysis.

### Western immunoblotting
  Tissues were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with phosphatase and protease inhibitors, and the proteins were isolated. Western immunoblotting was performed as previously described.21 The relative densities of the bands were analyzed using ImageJ with GAPDH as the loading control, thereby enabling semi-quantitative measurements of protein levels.


### Molecular docking analyses
  The 3D structure of ART was retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), while protein homology models of TRAF6 and NF-κB were obtained from the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/). A lattice file was generated using PyMOL, and an interconnection approach was implemented for molecular docking. The AutoDock 4.2 software was used for molecular docking simulations, with the entire protein structure designated as the docking region. The AutoDock Lamarckian Genetic Algorithm (LGA) was used to calculate binding free energy, producing a scoring function for binding affinity. Finally, the binding sites were identified by determining the appropriate docking region surrounding the bound eutectic ligand.


### Statistical analyses
  GraphPad Prism 7.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) was used for all statistical analyses. Results are expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared using 1- or 2-way ANOVAs with Tukey's post hoc test. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.


## Results

### ART and Met treatment improves the general status of T2DM model rats
  To investigate the effects of ART and Met on the general condition of T2DM model rats, body weight, food intake, and water intake were monitored weekly. Compared to the control animals, the rats in the T2DM model showed a significant increase in body weight after being fed an HFD for 8 weeks. Following successful T2DM modeling using STZ, the rats in the T2DM model groups showed significant reductions in weight starting from week 2. However, body weight in the healthy control group increased steadily. Following a 3-week drug treatment, a successful orthodontic tooth movement model was established in the rats. This resulted in significant weight loss in the model groups, with the most significant reduction observed in the DM group compared to the drug treatment groups. ### ART and Met enhance bone metabolism under orthodontic stress in T2DM rats
  To investigate the effects of ART alone or in conjunction with Met on hyperglycemia and its relationship with bone metabolism during orthodontic stress, FBG and Hb1Ac levels were measured in T2DM model rats. A glucose tolerance test (OGTT) was also conducted. The T2DM model rats exhibited significantly elevated FBG, Hb1Ac, and OGTT values compared to the NC group. Following drug therapy, the ART, Met, and ART+Met groups all showed reductions in all 3 parameters, with the Met and ART+Met groups exhibiting more significant reductions than the ART group (Figure 3D-G).

### ART and Met modulate bone metabolism under orthodontic stress in T2DM rats via the RANKL/TRAF6/NF-κB pathway
  To elucidate the mechanisms by which ART and Met affect bone metabolism, TRAF6/NF-κB pathway protein levels were measured. This pathway is known to be strongly associated with osteoclastic activity. Molecular docking analysis revealed that ART formed hydrogen bonds with the active site residues of OPG (Asp21 and Glu20), RANKL (Lys99), NF-κB (Thr475), and TRAF6 (Lys248 and Ile247). The binding energy of ART with each protein was negative, indicating spontaneous binding. Among them, ART exhibited the highest binding affinity for NF-κB, with a binding energy of -6.8 kcal/mol (Figure 5B). This indicates that ART may exert its effects by regulating the TRAF6/NF-κB pathway (Figure 5A-B). Furthermore, to confirm the molecular docking findings and explore related pathways, IHC staining of the alveolar bone (Figure 5C) was performed to assess the expression levels of proteins involved in osteogenesis and osteoclastogenesis. This revealed that the orthodontic stress-loading group had a broader distribution and higher expression of OPG, RANKL, TRAF6, and NF-κB than the NC group. This was particularly apparent in the apical one-third and root crown one-third regions. Compared to the DM group, RANKL, TRAF6, and NF-κB expression were significantly reduced in all 3 drug-treated groups. ## Discussion
  Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic metabolic disease characterized by persistent hyperglycemia, which is the primary cause of complications in individuals with diabetes.22 Clinical trials have demonstrated that effective blood glucose management can significantly reduce the risk of diabetes-related complications.23,24 DM affects various tissues and organ systems, including bone metabolism.25 Osteoporotic fractures occur more frequently in both T1DM and T2DM patients.26 Braga et al.27 reported that diabetes is associated with increased osteoclast activity and migration, along with suppressed osteoblastic differentiation. However, few studies have focused on approaches to inhibit osteoclast activity and enhance osteogenesis during orthodontic tooth movement in diabetic patients.

  Hyperglycemia inhibits the osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs),28 increases apoptosis of bone cells, and upregulates inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and IL-6, thereby suppressing bone formation and accelerating bone resorption.29 Existing studies indicate that hyperglycemia and the accumulation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs) increase the release of inflammatory factors from adipose tissue, altering osteocyte function.30 Moreover, glycemic control has been associated with periodontal and peri-implant disease in diabetic patients, with severity directly associated with metabolic control.31 Originally developed as an antimalarial drug, artesunate (ART) has demonstrated anti-glycation,32 anti-inflammatory,33 anti-tumor,34 anti-fibrosis35 and other effects. Moreover, ART has recently been found to have hypoglycemic effects and to alleviate diabetic complications.36 For example, our previous study found that ART regulates glucose metabolism via the PI3K/AKT pathway and improves diabetes-related tissue damage.15 Furthermore, ART also affects glucose metabolism through the autophagy- and inflammation-related pathways, reducing bone damage caused by hyperglycemia.37 The findings of this study further support the hypoglycemic effects of ART. In the ART-treated group, FBG and HbA1c levels were significantly reduced, suggesting that ART effectively improves the diabetic state. Moreover, micro-CT analysis and osteogenesis-related marker expression indicated increased bone mineral density and elevated OPG expression in the ART-treated group, suggesting that ART promotes bone remodeling by improving the diabetes-associated metabolic environment. Furthermore, the ART and Met combination treatment showed the most significant reduction in blood glucose and improvement in bone metabolism, indicating a potential synergistic effect between ART and Met. These results collectively suggest that ART’s bone remodeling effects may be partially attributed to its hypoglycemic activity, which improves bone metabolism disrupted by hyperglycemia and enhances bone remodeling capacity.

  Mechanical stress can trigger the production of chemokines. RANKL plays a key role in promoting osteoclastogenesis and the subsequent resorption of alveolar bone and roots.38 The RANKL/OPG ratio regulates the balance between bone formation and resorption. OPG can decrease osteoclast development and prevent bone resorption.39 The OPG/RANKL/RANK pathway is essential for osteoclast differentiation, with RANKL mediating osteoclast activation and differentiation by preventing apoptosis.40 Our in vitro experiments showed that periodontal membrane cells can differentiate into osteoclasts when subjected to mechanical pressure. This occurs due to the increased production of RANKL and the subsequent suppression of OPG expression. This is consistent with previous in vitro research.41 Interestingly, mechanical forces were found to regulate OPG/RANKL expression in the periodontal membrane. This finding provides strong evidence favoring a model where RANKL expression results from biomechanical regulation.

  A. Alqerban42 demonstrated that a pro-inflammatory response occurs in T2DM patients undergoing fixed orthodontic treatment. In all studies comparing diabetic and healthy animals, diabetic animals exhibited greater bone resorption and reduced bone remodeling.43 Significant evidence indicates that DM alters the inflammatory status of the periodontium, promoting osteoclast formation and alveolar bone resorption during orthodontic tooth movement.44,45 These findings are consistent with the results of H&E staining, TRAP staining, and micro-CT scanning in this study (Figure 4B and 5C). The therapeutic effects of ART on bone resorption have been reported, with many studies demonstrating its anti-inflammatory properties and effects on osteoclasts.46,47 In this study, significant decreases in osteoclast counts were observed in the drug-treatment groups compared to the DM group. The highest benefits were observed when both drugs were provided in combination. Further in vitro analyses, including ALP staining and Western blotting (Figure 6A and 7B), demonstrated that the ability of ART to modulate the OPG/RANKL balance was associated with reduced expression of NF-κB. Several dentistry-focused studies have reported a dose-dependent effect of ART on NF-κB-induced osteoclastic differentiation and LPS production during periodontitis treatment.48 Numerous studies have shown that ART may suppress RANKL-induced osteoclast formation and NF-κB activation, indicating its potential as a pharmaceutical treatment for osteolytic disorders.49 Zeng et al.48 and Su et al.50 reported that ART could inhibit NF-κB pathway activity and regulate pathway components, including TNF-α and RANKL. Consistent with these findings, the results of this study indicate that ART reduces bone resorption during orthodontic tooth movement in diabetic patients by modulating RANKL signaling and suppressing NF-κB pathway activity. However, the fundamental mechanisms by which ART promotes bone remodeling, including its direct effects on bone metabolism and its hypoglycemic action, have yet to be fully determined. Further studies, including in vitro assays and biophysical binding studies, are needed to elaborate on the relative contributions of these mechanisms.

  TRAF6 is a key mediator of intracellular signal transduction that regulates many processes, including inflammation and bone remodeling. In this study, molecular docking analysis demonstrated that ART directly binds to TRAF6 and NF-κB, suggesting that ART modulates the OPG/RANKL balance and inhibits osteoclast activity via the TRAF6/NF-κB pathway. These findings align with previous studies reporting that ART attenuates inflammatory osteoclastogenesis by modulating the TLR4/TRAF6 pathway, which plays a critical role in bone resorption and osteoclastogenesis.13 TRAF6 is essential for several RANKL-associated signaling pathways and is closely associated with osteoclast formation.51,52 TRAF6 can promote osteoclast progenitor differentiation, activation, maturation, and/or apoptotic death through pathways involving NF-κB, JNK, protein kinase B, and calcineurin/nuclear factor of activated T cells (CN/NFATcl). TRAF6 is also believed to function as a signal switch regulator within cells. Armstrong et al.53 reported that TRAF6-knockout rats exhibited osteosclerosis due to impaired osteoclastic differentiation. On the other hand, the in vitro results from this study revealed significant reductions in RANKL, TRAF6, and NF-κB mRNA and protein levels in the drug-treatment groups compared to the control DM group. Based on previous findings, ART modulates bone metabolism and inhibits osteoclast formation and activity by interfering with RANK/TRAF6 signaling. For example, Wei et al.48 reported that ART can suppress NF-κB phosphorylation and downstream signaling and prevent LPS-induced bone resorption by inhibiting osteoclast production. This is consistent with the present study, which demonstrates that ART regulates RANKL signaling and exerts downstream effects through a TRAF6/NF-κB-dependent mechanism, at least in part.


## Limitations and future research directions
  While this study provides valuable insights, several limitations remain. The current experimental design focused exclusively on a diabetic (hyperglycemic) model without including a non-diabetic control group treated with ART. Therefore, whether ART improves bone remodeling independently of its hypoglycemic effects remains unclear. Future studies should incorporate a non-diabetic ART-treated control group to distinguish ART's direct effects on bone metabolism from its systemic hypoglycemic actions and address this limitation. Moreover, further studies, including in vitro cell culture assays and binding studies, are required to clarify the relative contributions of ART's hypoglycemic effects versus its direct modulation of bone metabolic pathways.


## Conclusion
  In conclusion, this study demonstrates that ART modulates bone remodeling during orthodontic tooth movement, possibly by inhibiting the OPG/RANKL/RANK and NF-κB signaling pathways through TRAF6-mediated mechanisms (Figure 8). Moreover, ART may enhance bone remodeling in the diabetic state, both directly by targeting key bone metabolic pathways and indirectly through its hypoglycemic effects. While further investigations are required to fully elucidate these mechanisms and confirm ART's therapeutic potential for diabetes-associated bone loss, these findings provide a strong rationale for exploring ART as a novel therapeutic agent to promote bone health and improve orthodontic outcomes in diabetic patients.Fig. 8Mechanism of the effect of ART on bone remodeling during orthodontic movement in type 2 diabetes.
