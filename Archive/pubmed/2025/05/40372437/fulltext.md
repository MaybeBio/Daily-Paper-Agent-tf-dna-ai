# Molecular insights into human phosphatidylserine synthase 2 and its regulation of SREBP pathways


## Significance
  Despite their high homology, the two mammalian phosphatidylserine synthases, Phosphatidylserine synthases 1 and 2 (PSS1 and PSS2), have opposing roles in lipid regulation. While PSS1 inhibition or deficiency reduces phosphatidylserine (PS) levels, thereby blocking Aster-mediated cholesterol transport to activate the SREBP2 pathway, we show that PSS2 deficiency instead suppresses both SREBP-1 and SREBP-2 cleavage by elevating phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) levels in the endoplasmic reticulum. Our biophysical studies reveal the structural basis for PSS2’s substrate specificity, which restricts it to using PE. These findings uncover how closely related enzymes can diverge in function through distinct substrate preferences, highlighting a previously unappreciated layer of regulation in lipid homeostasis and the sterol regulatory element-binding protein (SREBP) pathways.


## Results
  RT-PCR results indicated that the messenger RNA (mRNA) level of PSS2 is ~30% that of PSS1 in wild-type (WT) CHO-K1 cells (Fig. 1A). To investigate whether the knockout of Ptdss1 or Ptdss2 affects the expression of the other gene, we analyzed mRNA levels of both genes in WT, Ptdss1−/−, and Ptdss2−/− CHO-K1 cells. Our results showed that knocking out Ptdss1 in CHO-K1 cells leads to a slight increase in the mRNA levels of Ptdss2. However, knocking out Ptdss2 in CHO-K1 cells did not alter the Ptdss1 mRNA levels (Fig. 1A). The lipidomic analysis showed that the total amount of PS was considerably lower in Ptdss1−/− cells, consistent with a previous finding (15). Conversely, there is no notable change in the amount of PS in Ptdss2−/− cells compared to that in WT cells, demonstrating that PSS1 is a major contributor of PS in WT CHO-K1 cells (Fig. 1B).

  Contrary to our expectations, we observed an increase in Srebp1 and Srebp2 gene expression from the Ptdss1−/− cells (10), while the expression of SREBP genes in Ptdss2−/− cells was suppressed (Fig. 1C). We also performed western blot analysis to analyze the ratio of unprocessed and processed SREBPs in WT and Ptdss2−/− cells (Fig. 1D). Since SREBP-1 transcriptionally activates its own promoter through a positive feedback loop (16), its overall protein expression is also reduced. Nevertheless, consistent with the previous analysis on SREBP-1 inhibition (16), the nuclear form (N) of SREBP-1 is significantly suppressed (Fig. 1E). SREBP-2 cleavage was suppressed in the Ptdss2−/− cells as well (Fig. 1E). Additionally, the expression of downstream genes in the SREBP pathway, but not the other genes (e.g., Scap), was also suppressed (Fig. 1F). Unlike PSS1, which regulates intracellular PS levels (Fig. 1B), abolishing PSS2 does not affect the PS synthesis in CHO-K1 cells, indicating that PSS2 may regulate the SREBP pathway through a different mechanism.

  According to previous reports, an increase in PE levels in the ER can inhibit SREBP cleavage (16). As an ER-associated enzyme that utilizes PE as a substrate, we hypothesize that a decrease in PSS2 expression may reduce SREBP cleavage via altering PE levels in the ER. To test this, we isolated the ER components from WT, Ptdss1−/−, and Ptdss2−/− cells using density gradient centrifugation according to a previously established protocol (Fig. 2 A and B) and analyzed the lipid levels via mass spectrometry (10). The results showed that, compared to WT cells, PE levels were substantially elevated in Ptdss2−/− cells and decreased in Ptdss1−/− cells (Fig. 2C), supporting our hypothesis.

  We next purified human Flag-tagged PSS2 using anti-Flag M2 resin in the presence of 5 mM CaCl2. Gel filtration revealed that PSS2 demonstrated suitable properties in Glyco-dendrimer (GDN). To confirm the activity of recombinant PSS2, we utilized liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) to quantify the synthesized PS. We incubated equal concentrations of PSS2 with 200 mM POPE and 2 mM L-serine at 37 °C for 90 min before the LC-MS analysis. Our result showed that recombinant PSS2 but not its catalytically dead mutant PSS2H200A presented reasonable activity, which is consistent with the previous finding on PSS1 (3) (Fig. 2D).

  We determined the structure of human PSS2 by cryo-EM at 3.3 Å resolution with C2 symmetry. Using the structural prediction from AlphaFold2 as starting coordinates, we built the model of PSS2 according to the density map (Fig. 3A and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 and Table S1). Similar to PSS1, two PSS2 molecules form a homodimer, with each monomer containing 10 transmembrane helices (TMs). TM10 is located far from the protein’s central region and is highly dynamic (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). TMs 4 to 8 form the catalytic cavity in the luminal leaflet, which opens to both the lipid bilayer and the lumen. A PE density was found between the luminal halves of TM4 and TM8 in the cryo-EM map of PSS2 (Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Inside the catalytic cavity, the calcium ion is coordinated by residues Glu225, Glu228, Glu240, and Asp249 (Fig. 3B). Moreover, two PS molecules were found in the cytosolic pocket of PSS2 (Fig. 3A and SI Appendix, Fig. S2), potentially acting as negative regulators of this enzyme (3).

  Structural analysis reveals several differences between the structures of PSS1WT and PSS2. Compared to PSS1WT, the fatty acid chain of PE inserts into the cavity among TMs 7-9, but both fatty acid chains of PE in PSS1WT face the membrane (Fig. 3 B and C and SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Four acidic residues coordinate the calcium ion in the catalytic core (Fig. 3B), which is akin to that in PSS1P269S, a constitutively active PSS1 mutant (17) (Fig. 3D). The luminal part of TM4 moves further away from TM8, which is similar to the movement observed by TM4 in PSS1P269S but not than it does in PSS1WT (Fig. 3E and SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Based on our previous findings on PSS1, we speculate that PSS2 may employ its catalytic histidine (H200) to deprotonate the hydroxyl-group of serine allowing it to attack the ester bond of PE to generate PS (Fig. 3F).

  Why does PSS2 prefer PE rather than PC as its substrate? Since the serine substrate should be engaged by the calcium ion (10) and the recently published serine-bound PSS1 structure demonstrated this (18), we tentatively modeled the serine into the cavity (Fig. 3 F and G). Structural comparison suggests a possibility to explain the substrate specificity of PSS2. The catalytic cavity of PSS2 is much smaller than that of PSS1, since F196 and F332 face into the cavity but the corresponding residues (F168 and F305) in PSS1 face away from each other enlarging the cavity to accommodate PC (Fig. 3H). Residue F324 in TM7 has a hydrophobic contact with I220 in TM5. In contrast, the corresponding residue in PSS1 is W297, which pulls TM5 away from TM7 via hydrophobic contacts. This difference offers more room in PSS1 and allows F305 to assume another position facing away from the catalytic core for the PC engagement (Fig. 3H).

  Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations over a 100 ns timescale reveal that the distance between the Nε2/Nδ1 of the catalytic histidine and the methyl group of the PC head remains ~3.5 to 4 Å, whereas the corresponding distance for the PE head group exceeds 6 Å (Fig. 3I). It is likely that PC introduces steric hindrance with the residues in the catalytic core due to its bulky head group. Furthermore, the PC head group is positioned closer to F196, F224, and F332 in the catalytic core compared to PE (Fig. 3I). These potential steric interferences may disrupt interactions between the phospholipid substrate, the catalytic histidine, and serine, ultimately inhibiting the reaction.


## Discussion
  The transport of LDL-derived cholesterol from the plasma membrane (PM) to the ER is mediated by a group of proteins known as Asters, which are activated by PS through direct interaction (12). Interestingly, previous studies have shown that unlike that in Ptdss1−/− cells, Ptdss2 knockout does not impair LDL-derived cholesterol trafficking from the PM to the ER (11). The mechanism by which PSS1 activity, but not PSS2, regulates Asters-mediated cholesterol transport remains to be explored in future studies.

  In this manuscript, we report that PSS2 plays a critical role in modulating the concentration of PE in the ER to regulate SREBP1 and SREBP2 pathways. Loss of PSS2 leads to repression of both pathways. Building on previous studies, we propose that PSS2 may consume considerable amounts of PE for PS synthesis. As a result, in the absence of PSS2, the accumulated PE in the ER would inhibit SREBP1 and SREBP2 cleavage. Our findings along with previous studies on PSS1 reveal the intricate relationship between intracellular PE, PS, and cholesterol metabolism. Furthermore, the structural and functional studies on PSS2 reveal the working mechanism of PSS2 and highlight its substrate specificity, which may contribute to the regulation of the SREBP pathways.


## Methods and Materials

### Cell Lines.
  Spodoptera frugiperda Sf9 cells (ATCC) were used to generate baculovirus and maintained in Sf-900 III SFM medium (Gibco) at 27 °C with shaking. HEK 293S GnTI−cells (ATCC) were used for protein expression and maintained in Freestyle 293 expression medium (Gibco) with 2% fetal calf serum (FCS) (GeminiBio) at 37 °C and 8% CO2 with shaking. Medium A is a 1:1 mixture of Ham’s F-12 medium and DMEM containing 2 mM L-glutamine. Cholesterol-depletion medium B consists of medium A supplemented with 5% LPDS, 30 µM compactin, and 200 µM mevalonate. All media except Sf-900 III SFM medium are supplemented with 100 U/mL of penicillin and 100 µg/mL of streptomycin sulfate. CHO-K1 cells (Chinese hamster) were maintained in medium A supplemented with 5% FCS. CHO-K1 cells lacking PSS1 (designated as Ptdss1−/− cells) and PSS2 (designated as Ptdss2−/− cells) were generated using CRISPR-Cas9 by the Brown/Goldstein lab (14) and grown in medium A containing 10% FCS and 5% FCS.


### Protein Expression and Purification.
  The complementary DNA of human PSS2 (Protein ID NP_110410.1) was cloned into pEG-BacMam with a C-terminal Flag-tag. Point mutations were introduced into the coding region of PSS2 by site-directed mutagenesis using the QuikChange II XL Site Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Agilent Technologies). The coding region of each plasmid was sequenced to ensure the integrity of the construct. The protein was expressed in HEK-293S GnTI− cells using the baculovirus system. To generate baculovirus, 5 µg bacmid was transfected into 1 × 107 sf9 cells. After total 72 h, the P1 virus was harvested. For the P2 virus, 1 mL P1 was added to 50 mL 3 × 106 sf9 cells, and then, 50 mL medium was added the next day. After a total of 72 h, the P2 virus was harvested for protein expression.

  Protein purification was performed as previously described (10). Briefly, 800 mL of HEK-293S GnTI− cells at a density of 3 × 106 were infected with 50 mL of P2 virus. After 8 h, 10 mM sodium butyrate was added, and cells were then incubated at 37 °C to express proteins. The cells were collected after 48 h of infection and were disrupted by sonication in buffer A (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM CaCl2) supplemented with 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride and 5 mg/mL leupeptin. After low-speed centrifugation (4,000 r.p.m, rotor: A-4-62, Eppendorf, 4 °C, 5 min), the resulting supernatant was incubated in buffer A with 1% (w/v) lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol (LMNG) (Anatrace) for 1 h at 4 °C. The lysate was then centrifuged at high speed (18,000 r.p.m, rotor: F21-8x50y, Thermo Scientific, 4 °C, 30 min) to remove insoluble components, and the supernatant was loaded onto the Flag M2 affinity resin (Sigma-Aldrich). After two tandem washes, the protein was eluted in buffer A supplemented with 100 µg/mL 3xFlag peptide (ApexBio), 0.01% (w/v) LMNG and concentrated by Amicon Centrifugal Filters (Millipore). The concentrated protein was purified by a Superose 6 Increase 10/300 size-exclusion chromatography column (GE Healthcare) in buffer A and 0.005% (w/v) GDN (Anatrace). Finally, the peak fractions were pooled, concentrated, then directly flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. Typically, 800 mL cells can produce 500 µg protein.


### EM Sample Preparation and Imaging.
  First, 3 μL of protein sample at ~8 mg mL−1 (~150 μM) was added to Quantifoil R1.2/1.3 400 mesh Au holey carbon grids (Quantifoil), blotted using a Vitrobot Mark IV (FEI) (15 force, 5 s blotting time and 100% humidity at 22 °C), and frozen in liquid ethane. The grids were imaged in a 300 kV Titan Krios (FEI). For PSS2, raw movie stacks were collected with a Falcon 4i camera at 0.738 Å per pixel and a nominal defocus range of −0.8 to −1.8 μm. The exposure time for each micrograph was 4 s with a total dose of ~60 electrons per Å2.


### Image Processing, 3D Reconstruction and Modeling.
  For PSS2, dark subtracted images were first normalized by gain reference (0.738 Å per pixel). Motion correction and CTF were performed and estimated using CryoSPARC v3.3.0 (19). Auto picking for all datasets was performed with crYOLO-v1.9.3 (20) using the general model with the particle threshold of 0.1. Particle extraction was performed in CryoSPARC v3.3.0 with a box size of 360 pixels and down-sampled to 120 pixels for subsequent two-dimensional classification, multiclass ab initio modeling, and heterogenous three-dimensional (3D) refinement. After that, good particles were re-extracted with 360 pixels and down-sampled to 180 pixels. Nonuniform refinement, CTF refinement, and local refinement of the best class were performed in CryoSPARC v.3.3.0. The masks for the local refinement were generated by the CryoSPARC volume tool. After the local refinement, particles were re-extracted again with box size of 360 pixels. We directly reconstructed it with the Orientation information from the 180-pixel box size local refinement. The resulting map was of better quality compared to the orientation information from the refinement that used the 360-pixel box size particles.

  The initial models were built de novo based on the AlphaFold2-predicted structures and then manually adjusted and refined using COOT (21). Due to the limited resolution, residues 1 to 57 and 437 to 487 of PSS2 were not built. The models were refined in real space using PHENIX (22). For cross-validations, MolProbity (23) was used to validate the final model. Local resolutions were estimated using CryoSPARC. Structure figures were generated using COOT, PyMOL (http://www.pymol.org), and ChimeraX (24).


### ER Isolation.
  The ER isolation was conducted as described previously (10, 25). On day 0, WT, Ptdss1−/−, and Ptdss2−/− cells were set up in medium A at 1 × 106 cells/10 cm dish. On day 3, the cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline and Buffer B (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl) twice and then scraped into 15 mL tubes. All further operations were carried out at 4 °C. The cell pellets were collected after centrifugation at 1,000 g for 10 min and resuspended in 1 mL of ice-cold Buffer B, and protease inhibitors: 50 µg/mL leupeptin (Biosynth), 25 µg/mL Pepstatin A (ChemCruz), 10 µg/mL Aprotinin (Selleckchem), 25 µg/mL phenylmethyl sulfonylchloride (GoldBio) with 15% (w/v) sucrose. Cells were then disrupted by passaging them 13 times through a ball-bearing homogenizer with a 10 µm clearance. The homogenized cells (Fraction A, see fractionation scheme in Fig. 2A) were centrifuged at 7,000 g for 15 min to yield a pellet and a supernatant. The supernatant was diluted to a total volume of 3 mL using Buffer B containing 15% sucrose. A discontinuous sucrose gradient was generated in an ULTRA CLEAR tube (Beckman Instruments) by overlaying the following sucrose solutions, all in Buffer B: 2 mL 45%, 4 mL 30%, 3 mL of the diluted supernatant in 15% sucrose, and 1 mL 7.5%. The gradient was centrifuged at 100,000 g in a TH641 rotor (Thermo Scientific) for 1 h and allowed to slow down without braking, after which two bands of membranes were clearly visible and collected using a Pasteur pipet. The sucrose concentration in the light membrane fraction ranged from 14 to 18%, and 34 to 38% in the heavy membrane fraction (Fraction C, about 800 μL). A discontinuous iodixanol gradient was generated by underlaying, in succession, 2.25 mL of 19%, 21%, 23%, and 25% (v/v) iodixanol (OptiPrep Density Gradient Medium, Sigma-Aldrich), all in Buffer B. This discontinuous gradient was allowed to stand for 1 to 2 h, allowing for diffusion across the interfaces to form a continuous gradient. The heavy membrane fraction from above was loaded at the bottom of this gradient, which was then centrifuged for 2 h at 110,000 g in a TH641 rotor and allowed to slow down without braking. Fractions (0.8 mL each) were collected from the bottom.

  Approximately 5 mL of ER fractions were collected and pooled for further analysis. Then, 2.6 mL was used for lipid analysis via LC-MS, while 2 mL was used to determine ER protein concentration using the Micro BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific). The PE lipid data were normalized to the corresponding ER protein content. For group comparisons, the values in the WT group were set to 1, and data from other groups are presented as relative levels normalized to the WT group.


### Assaying of Base-Exchange Activities.
  First, 200 μM POPE (Avanti) liposomes were prepared by sonication in a buffer containing 50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, and 5 mM CaCl2. Serine-exchange activity was measured by supplementation of 500 nM PSS2 or PSS2H200A into liposomes and 2 mM serine. Assays were performed in a 100 μL volume for 90 min at 37 °C and were terminated by adding 10 μL of 0.5 M EDTA.


### Lipids Analysis by LC-MS/MS.
  Liposomes, ER fractions, or cells (500 k cells of WT, Ptdss1−/−, or Ptdss2−/−) were transferred to fresh glass tubes for liquid–liquid extraction (LLE). Samples were extracted using a modified methyl tert-butyl ether (mTBE) method (26). Briefly, 1 mL of water, 1 mL of methanol, and 2 mL of mTBE were added to the glass tubes containing the sample. The mixture was vortexed and centrifuged at 2,671 g for 5 min. The organic phase was collected to a fresh glass tubes and spiked with 20 µL of a 1:5 diluted Splash Lipidomix standards mixture (Avanti Polar Lipids, Alabaster, AL). Samples were dried under N2 and resuspended in 400 µL of hexane.

  Lipids were analyzed by LC-MS/MS using a SCIEX QTRAP 6500+ (SCIEX, Framingham, MA) equipped with a Shimadzu LC-30AD (Shimadzu, Columbia, MD) high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system and a 150 × 2.1 mm, 5 µm Supelco Ascentis silica column (Supelco, Bellefonte, PA). Samples were injected at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min at 2.5% solvent B (mTBE) and 97.5% Solvent A (hexane). Solvent B was increased to 5% over 3 min and then to 60% over 6 min. Solvent B was decreased to 0% during 30 s while Solvent C [90:10 (v/v) isopropanol-water] was set at 20% and increased to 40% during the following 11 min. Solvent C is increased to 44% over 6 min and then to 60% over 50 s. The system was held at 60% solvent C for 1 min prior to re-equilibration at 2.5% of solvent B for 5 min at a 1.2 mL/min flow rate. Solvent D [95:5 (v/v) acetonitrile–water with 10 mM Ammonium acetate] was infused postcolumn at 0.03 mL/min. Column oven temperature was 25 °C. Data were acquired in negative ionization mode using multiple reaction monitoring. The LC-MS/MS data were analyzed using MultiQuant software (SCIEX). The identified lipid species were normalized to its corresponding internal standard.


### SREBP Processing and Western Blot.
  The details of cell plating and treatment are described in figure legends. Cells were treated with 25 µg/mL of N-acetyl-Leu-LeunorLeucinal (ALLN) (Calbiochem) before being harvested for western blot. Cells were lysed by the addition of PBS containing 0.3% (w/v) SDS and 150 U/mL of Benzonase Nuclease (Millipore-Sigma). Aliquots were mixed with 4× loading dye, after which 20 μL protein was applied to SDS-PAGE. After electrophoresis, the proteins were transferred to nitrocellulose membranes, which were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with one of the following antibodies dissolved in 5% (w/v) nonfat milk: 4 µg/mL anti-SREBP1 (16), 5 µg/mL anti-SREBP2 (Sigma-Aldrich), anti-Actin (1:1,000) (Santa Cruz). For the western blot of the ER fractions (Fig. 2A), samples were boiled at 95 °C for 10 min, mixed with 4× loading dye, and loaded to SDS-PAGE. After electrophoresis and subsequent transfer, the nitrocellulose membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with one of the following antibodies dissolved in 5% (w/v) BSA: anti-TOM20 (1:1,000) (Protein Tech), anti-EEA1 (1:1,000) (Sigma-Aldrich), anti-GM130 (1:1,000) (BD Biosciences), anti-Na+/K+ ATPase (1:5,000) (Abcam), anti-Calnexin (1:1,000) (Bio-techne), anti-NPC1 (1:1,000) (Abcam), and anti-ABCD3 (1:1,000) (Affinity Biosciences). Bound antibodies were visualized by chemiluminescence using Supersignal West Pico Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Scientific) after incubation for 0.5 h at room temperature with goat anti-rabbit IgG conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (1:2,500) (Cell Signaling Technology) or horse anti-mouse IgG conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (1:2,500) (Cell Signaling Technology). The images were scanned using an Odyssey FC Imager (Dual-Mode Imaging System; 2 min integration time) and analyzed using Image Studio lite v5.2.

### Construction of Model.
  The MD simulations were performed as previously described (27, 28). The PSS2 structure was modeled as follows. Hydrogen atoms were constructed using H-build from CHARMM (29). An initial protonation pattern at pH 7 was obtained by calculating the pKa values of all titratable residues; computations were carried out with PROPKA (30). In both protein chains, a disulfide bridge was constructed between Cys174-Cys241. Force field parameters for PE and PC were obtained using the CGenFF server (31). Next, using the CHARMM-GUI (32) and OPM database (33), the protein was inserted in a lipid bilayer consisting of a cytosolic leaflet and an extracellular leaflet. The membrane composition consists of a 10: 4: 2: 1: 1 mixture of 3-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-D-glycero-1-phosphatidylcholine: 3-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-D-glycero-1-PE (POPE): 1-Palmitoyl-2-Oleoyl-inositol: 3-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-D-glycero-1-pqhosphatidylserine. The protein–membrane system was next solvated in water with 150 mM KCl concentration. For the PSS2-PC system, the final model contained K+ (210) and Cl− (117) ions to neutralize charge, and 43,330 explicit TIP3 water molecules (34); the total system (221,984 atoms). For the PSS2-PE system, the final model contained K+ (213) and Cl− (118) ions to neutralize charge, and 43,352 explicit TIP3 water molecules (34); the total system (222,026 atoms). Each system was simulated in a rectangular box of dimension 152.5 Å × 152.5 Å × 103.1 Å.


### Geometry Optimizations and MD.
  The initial geometry of each solvated protein–membrane complex was optimized using CHARMM (29), using the all-atom CHARMM36 parameter set for the protein and lipid molecules (35) and the TIP3P model for water molecules (34). Two rounds of minimization were carried out, each consisting of 100 steps of steepest descent energy minimization, followed by 100 adopted basis Newton–Raphson steps to remove any close contacts. Subsequent equilibration and production simulations were carried out with NAMD (36) using the all-atom CHARMM36 parameter set for the protein and lipid molecules (35) and the TIP3P model for water molecules (34). The solvated protein–membrane complex was simulated with MD at 310 K according to the following protocol: 1) equilibration MD with Langevin dynamics (time step of 1 fs) for 375 ps during which position restraints were held on the membrane atoms and on the protein backbone heavy atoms, followed by Langevin dynamics (time step 2 fs) for 1.5 ns; 2) production MD with Langevin dynamics (time step 2 fs) for 200 ns. To simulate a continuous system, periodic boundary conditions were applied. Electrostatic interactions were summed with the Particle Mesh Ewald method (37) (grid spacing ~0.95 Å; fftx 160, ffty 160, fftz 108). A nonbonded cutoff of 16.0 Å was used, and Heuristic testing was performed at each energy call to evaluate whether the nonbonded pair list should be updated.


### Measurement of Distance between the Lipid Substrate and PSS2 Residues.
  Analyses of the MD trajectories were performed with VMD (Version 1.94) (38). Distances between the lipid substrates PC/PE and histidine side chains were measured between the N atom of the PE head or the N atom plus 1.5 Å (representing the C-H bond length) of the PC head and Nε or Nδ of Histidine; distances between the lipid substrates and phenylalanine side chains were measured between the N atom of PE head or the N atom plus 1.5 Å of the PC head and the Cz atom of phenylalanine.
