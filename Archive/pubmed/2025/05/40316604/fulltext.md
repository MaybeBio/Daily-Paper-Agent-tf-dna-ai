# Computational analysis of lupenone derivatives as potential inhibitor of human papillomavirus oncoprotein E6 associated cervical cancer


## Abstract
  Cervical cancer remains a major global health challenge, largely driven by persistent infections with high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV). Although preventive vaccines have reduced cervical cancer incidence in some settings, effective therapeutic strategies for established HPV-associated malignancies remain limited. High-risk HPV types (particularly 16 and 18) utilize their E6 oncoprotein to promote ubiquitin-mediated degradation of the tumor suppressor p53, thereby facilitating uncontrolled cell proliferation and immune evasion. Targeting E6 has thus emerged as a key strategy to counteract HPV-driven carcinogenesis. In this work, we employed a comprehensive in silico framework—encompassing density functional theory (DFT), ADMET (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity) profiling, molecular docking (including refinement and validation), and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations—to evaluate a series of chemically modified lupenone derivatives as potential HPV oncoprotein inhibitors. Initially, lupenone was modified with different functional groups, and each derivative was screened for drug-likeness via ADMET analysis to confirm pharmacological viability. Concurrently, pharmacophore mapping highlighted key alignments between ligand functional groups and pharmacophoric sites, while DFT calculations elucidated each compound’s electronic structure, conformational stability, and chemical reactivity. Subsequent docking assessments against E6 oncoprotein and molecular dynamics simulations further confirmed structural robustness in several top-performing compounds, indicating minimal conformational fluctuations over time. These findings demonstrate the potential of lupenone derivatives as promising scaffolds for anti-HPV therapy. However, in vitro and in vivo investigations are necessary to confirm their efficacy, toxicity profiles, and clinical relevance in mitigating HPV-related cervical cancer.


## Introduction
  Cervical cancer is the fourth most prevalent cancer in women worldwide, ranking only behind lung cancer (0.7 million cases), colorectal cancer (0.8 million), and breast cancer (2.1 million cases)1. Cervical cancer begins in the cells that line the uterine cervix, specifically the mucus-producing endocervical gland cells and the squamous cells that line the exocervix. Squamous cell carcinomas, the most common type of cervical cancer, typically begin in the “transformation zone,” the area where the exocervix meets the endocervix2. According to the American Cancer Society’s projections, there will be roughly 13,960 new instances of invasive cervical cancer in 2023, along with 4310 new cases of fatalities from the disease3.

  The primary cause of cervical cancer is the human papillomavirus (HPV), which is divided into various genotypes based on their L1 open reading frame and is mostly through sexual contact and causes infections in the cervix, vagina, vulva, penis, and anus. Papillomaviruses are widespread, have been identified in a wide range of species in addition to humans, and are unique to the hosts to which they are found. DNA sequence data demonstrating genomic variations have been used to identify more than 200 different strains of HPV. The HPV is a tiny, non-enveloped, icosahedral, double-stranded DNA virus. Human papillomavirus types 16 and 18 are especially high risk because they have been associated with the development of cervical cancer. Genital warts are caused by HPV types 6 and 11, both of which are low-risk/non-oncogenic HPV strains. Most cases of cervical HPV infection are cleared or suppressed by cell-mediated immunity after a year to two years of infection4.

  HPV inhibits the expression of cellular components that can prevent viral proliferation and replication, including ND10 factor (sp100), IFT1 (p56), IF16, and APOBEC5 and suppresses the innate immune response and the transcription of pro-apoptotic genes (TRAIL and XAF1) via many pathways6. The identification of human papillomavirus (HPV) DNA allows for the early diagnosis of high-risk HPV strains that cause cervical cancer. The clinical behavior and epidemiology of certain HPV types, the characterization of study populations in HPV vaccination trials, and the monitoring of the efficacy of HPV vaccinations all rely on accurate HPV detection7. Conventional methods of HPV detection rely on polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and probe hybridization, typically targeting the L1 gene, the most conserved gene in the HPV genome8. The Hybrid capture method uses a hybridization solution that combines RNA tags with DNA targets to identify HPV. A back-end test similar to an ELISA with an immunological focus complements this. The hybrid capture method may be more effective in identifying women with concurrent squamous epithelial cancers, while PCR is considered a superior analytical method for the identification of HPV9. Since the PCR technique specifically amplifies the DNA target, it is still the “gold standard” for HPV diagnosis10.

  HPV’s transformative capacity arises primarily from the synergistic effects of its E6 and E7 oncoproteins, with E6 serving as a central driver of HPV-induced carcinogenesis. By binding to specific cellular PDZ domain proteins, notably human Discs Large 1 (DLG1), which governs cell polarity and proliferation, E6 disrupts critical regulatory networks, facilitating the progression of cervical squamous intraepithelial lesions into malignant states11. Additionally, E6-induced changes in tissue architecture heighten the invasiveness of neoplastic cells, thereby contributing substantially to tumor development at multiple stages12. In mucosal and cutaneous HPV subtypes, E6 oncoproteins recognize certain targets through acidic leucine (L)–rich motifs that carry the LxxLL consensus sequence (where x denotes any amino acid), which mediates protein–protein interactions underpinning cell motility, adhesion, and gene regulation13. Collectively, these mechanisms underscore E6’s pivotal role in HPV-driven carcinogenesis and reinforce the need for continued research into targeted therapeutic strategies aimed at neutralizing this oncogenic protein.

  Currently, effective therapeutic options for human papillomavirus (HPV) infection remain limited14, and numerous drug candidates fail during development due to unforeseen adverse effects. So, researchers have used computational techniques for the early prediction of drug activity and toxicity to reduce time and resources15.

  Recent evidence suggests that multiple natural compounds can modulate autophagy in cervical cancer through distinct signaling pathways16. Among these, lupenone has garnered attention for its broad pharmacological activities and anticancer potential17,18. A structurally related molecule, lupeol, also exhibits potent anticancer properties19 and potential efficacy against prostate cancer20. Nevertheless, the specific impact of lupenone on HPV-mediated oncogenesis has not yet been explored. So, we prepared several lupenone derivatives by modifying its structure. Accordingly, we employed an integrated in silico studies to evaluate lupenone derivatives as potential HPV inhibitors.


## Methodology

### Calculations of the lupenone compounds

#### Optimization, molecular electrostatic potential and quantum chemical descriptors
  The Lupenone structure was obtained via PubChem21 and were modified by using different functional groups (Supplementary Fig. S1) and then subjected to geometric optimization to prepare them for the docking procedures using Avogrado (http://avogadro.cc), ChimeraX (https://www.cgl.ucsf.edu/chimerax/) and SwissDrugDesign (https://www.molecular-modelling.ch/swiss-drug-design.html).

  The geometry of each ligand was optimized using the CHARMM27 force field. A conformational analysis was performed to generate up to 500 conformers per molecule with an RMSD cutoff of 0.5 Å. The most stable conformers were selected for subsequent quantum chemical calculations by Gaussian 16 Rev. C.01 software (https://gaussian.com/).

  Density Functional Theory (DFT) geometry optimizations and subsequent quantum mechanical computations were carried out employing the hybrid B3LYP exchange–correlation functional paired with the aug-cc-pVTZ (augmented correlation-consistent polarized valence triple-zeta) basis set. The correlation-consistent architecture (cc designation) systematically incorporates higher angular momentum functions to enhance electron correlation recovery, a critical requirement for accurately modeling non-covalent interactions, including hydrogen bonding, van der Waals dispersion, and π–π stacking phenomena. Incorporating diffuse functions (aug designation) is imperative for resolving the spatially extended electron density distributions characteristic of anionic systems, Rydberg states, and intermolecular interactions, as evidenced in hydrogen-bonded systems22.

  Polarization functions (p notation) introduce flexibility to the basis set by permitting orbital deformation, thereby enabling precise modelling of charge redistribution and induced-fit effects in ligand-receptor binding events. This is particularly critical for simulating the polarization-driven orbital distortions induced by charged amino acid residues within the binding site. Considering both ligand polarization energy and solvation-free energy appears essential to computing negative binding energies in protein–ligand complexes, as demonstrated in studies of biomolecular recognition23.

  The charge distribution analysis of a molecule has been done by plotting Molecular Electrostatic Potential (MEP). The electrostatic potential V(r) that is created in the space around a molecule by its nuclei and electrons (treated as a static distribution of charge) is a very useful property for analyzing and predicting the reactivity behavior of molecules24,25.

  Moreover, DFT is a widely used computational quantum mechanical modeling technique with a wide range of applications in molecular optimization and provides detailed information about the behavior of electrons in molecules and their influence on key quantum mechanical parameters and descriptors, including the energies of the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO), the GAP energy (HOMO–LUMO), ionization potential (I), electron affinity (A), chemical hardness (ɳ), softness (σ), chemical potential (μ), electronegativity index (χ), and electrophilicity index (ω)26. #### Drug-likeness and pharmacokinetics predictions
  The pharmacokinetic (PK) properties of drug candidates, particularly absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET), constitute critical determinants of therapeutic viability during drug discovery and development28. To evaluate the drug-likeness of lupenone derivatives under investigation, a systematic drug-likeness screening was conducted using the SwissADME (http://www.swissadme.ch/), pkCSM (https://biosig.lab.uq.edu.au/pkcsm/) and ProTox3.0 (https://tox.charite.de/protox3/) computational platforms. These tools assess physicochemical parameters (e.g., lipophilicity, molecular weight, polar surface area) and ADMET-related descriptors (e.g., bioavailability, blood–brain barrier permeability, hepatic metabolism, hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, cardiotoxicity) in alignment with validated computational protocols.

  Preclinical drug-likeness assessments are pivotal in early-stage drug development, as they enable prioritization of compounds with favorable PK/toxicity profiles while minimizing attrition in later clinical phases29. Notably, inadequate pharmacokinetic characteristics such as poor solubility, rapid clearance, or off-target toxicity account for many clinical trial failures, underscoring the necessity of robust ADMET profiling30.


#### Pharmacophore mapping
  Pharmacophore mapping analysis for the top three ligands was conducted using PharmMapper (https://www.lilab-ecust.cn/pharmmapper/), a reverse pharmacophore screening platform. The protocol was configured with the following parameters: a maximum of 1,000 ligand conformations to ensure comprehensive sampling of flexibility, inclusion of all available targets in the screening set, retention of the top 1,000 matched targets for downstream analysis, and a fit score cut-off value of 0 in advanced settings to maximize sensitivity. All other parameters retained default values.

  PharmMapper employs a repository of 7,162 receptor-based pharmacophore models derived from TargetBank, DrugBank, BindingDB, and the Protein Drug Target Database (PDTD). This database encompasses pharmacophore data for 1,627 unique drug targets, including 459 human proteins. The algorithm systematically maps user-submitted ligand structures (in Tripos Mol2 or MDL SDF formats) against the PharmTargetDB, generating ranked target matches and optimized ligand alignment poses based on binding complementarity.

  In addition, to improve PharmMapper’s accuracy further, we extracted all the pharmacophore model’s ligands from PDB and stored them as a library ligand dataset in mol2 format and calculate these ligands’ PharmMapper fit score. This process generates a nearly 7,000 × 7,000 score matrix.


### Lupenone-E6 complexes calculations

#### Protein preparation and molecular docking (MD)
  The oncogenic human papillomavirus (HPV) E6 oncoprotein is a key driver of HPV-mediated carcinogenesis, principally by targeting the tumor suppressor p53 for proteasomal degradation. Specifically, HPV types 16 and 18 exploit a cellular proteolytic system in which the E6 oncoprotein forms a complex with the E6-associated protein (E6-AP) a 100 kDa cellular factor enabling ubiquitin-dependent proteolysis of p5331. The resulting loss of p53 function leads to uncontrolled cell proliferation and immune evasion, making E6 an attractive target for therapeutic intervention. Consequently, developing small-molecule inhibitors, antibody fragments, and multimodal therapies to disrupt E6 function has gained increasing attention13,32.

  Specifically, the E6 oncoproteins of papillomaviruses target host proteins by interacting with acidic LxxLL motifs. Inactivation of the LxxLL binding pocket has been shown to disrupt the oncogenic activities of HPV’s E6 oncoprotein33. LxxLL-contacting residues are located at equivalent positions in BPV1 and HPV16 E6 sequences, and these positions are generally conserved, suggesting that all mammalian PV E6 proteins contain a LxxLL-binding pocket33.

  Here, to ensure robust molecular docking predictions, both the papillomavirus E6 oncoprotein (PDB ID: 3PY7) and lupenone derivatives underwent rigorous preparatory steps. First, crystallographic water molecules were removed to eliminate non-essential interactions. Then, structural refinements were performed using MolProbity (http://molprobity.biochem.duke.edu/), a web-based tool for biomolecular structure validation and optimization: (1) ramachandran plot analysis, confirming that 92.2% of residues were in favored regions, ensuring correct backbone dihedral angles; (2) rotamer outlier corrections, resolving misoriented asparagine, glutamine, and histidine sidechains to prevent incorrect hydrogen bonding patterns; (3) steric clash analysis, identifying and eliminating atomic overlaps that could distort the protein structure; (4) protonation adjustments to reflect physiological pH (7.4), ensuring proper tautomeric states and hydrogen-bonding networks. Finally, energy minimization using the CHARMm force field, reduces residual strains and atomic clashes, thereby enhancing the structural fidelity of the receptor model.

  Molecular docking was conducted using the SwissDock web server (https://www.swissdock.ch/), which employs the EADock DSS (Evolutionary Algorithm for Docking with Smooth Scoring) and Attracting Cavities 2.0 (AC) algorithms for ligand-receptor interaction prediction. The crystal structure of papillomavirus E6 protein served as the template, where the active site for lupenone derivatives corresponded to the LxxLL-binding pocket, a region essential for the tumorigenic phenotype of E6 proteins33. Thus, the docking search space was confined to a spherical region with a 10 Å radius, centered at coordinates X: 37.1 Å, Y: 33.34 Å, Z: 13.42 Å, to ensure a focused exploration of the ligand-binding pocket. The local/global search exhaustiveness was set to 8 to balance computational efficiency and thoroughness in sampling potential binding poses (Fig. 1). This region corresponds to the conserved interface mediating E6-inhibitors interactions, a critical hotspot for inhibiting oncogenic activity33–35.

  Docking poses were evaluated using two complementary scoring functions: (1) the AC docking score, integrating CHARMM force field energy and FACTS solvation energy terms, and (2) the SwissParam score, estimating binding free energy as a weighted sum of polar and non-polar contributions. While AC docking scores prioritized ligand ranking, SwissParam scores provided a comparative framework for multiple candidates, ensuring a robust selection of the most promising compounds.


### Molecular docking refinement and validation
  The binding affinity between ligands and the E6 oncoprotein was evaluated by applying multiple scoring functions that assess steric and electrostatic complementarity with desolvation penalties36, evaluate surface complementarity through steric and hydrogen bonding terms37,38, balance van der Waals, electrostatic, and hydrophobic interactions while accounting for desolvation39, use statistical interaction potentials derived from known complexes40,41, and refine ligand poses via molecular dynamics and energy minimization42.

  To further refine and validate the optimal docking poses of lupenone derivatives bound to the E6 oncoprotein, we employed the HADDOCK (High Ambiguity Driven biomolecular DOCKing) refinement protocol. HADDOCK2.4 (https://rascar.science.uu.nl/haddock2.4/) is a flexible docking approach that combines experimental restraints, explicit solvent refinement, and a multi-stage simulation pipeline to enhance structural reliability43.

  The entire HADDOCK protocol comprises five key stages. The first stage involves topology and structure generation, where the molecular partners, including the protein and ligand, are prepared for docking. During this stage, non-polar hydrogens are removed to optimize computational efficiency. The second stage consists of rigid-body energy minimization (it0), where the initial molecular orientations are randomized. This is followed by a rigid-body minimization step to explore global binding modes systematically.

  In the third stage, semi-flexible simulated annealing (it1) was performed, wherein semi-flexible residues were automatically defined based on intermolecular contacts within a 5.0 Å threshold. These regions underwent stepwise annealing in vacuo, enabling conformational adjustments while preserving structural constraints in the remainder of the molecule. The fourth stage is the final refinement in explicit solvent, which plays a crucial role in improving the accuracy of ligand–protein interactions. Here, surface contact restraints were applied to maintain the stability of the intermolecular interactions and centre-of-mass restraints were enforced to prevent significant deviations. Short molecular dynamics (MD) simulations in an explicit solvent were performed, incorporating a solvent shell around the complex. A total of 1,250 MD steps at 300 K were conducted, with position restraints applied to heavy atoms that were not part of the critical intermolecular contacts. A subsequent gradual temperature decrease (300 K → 200 K → 100 K) was implemented to refine molecular interactions further while allowing side-chain flexibility at the binding interface. The final stage involves post-processing and analysis, where the refined complexes were clustered based on root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) with a cutoff of 1.5 Å. The top-ranked clusters were selected based on the HADDOCK score, which considers van der Waals interactions, electrostatics, desolvation effects, and restraint energy terms. Additionally, intramolecular and binding free energies were extracted to confirm the stability of the refined structures. Lastly, the best complex for each protein–ligand structure underwent a final round of validation using PRODIGY (Protein binding energy prediction), which predicted better results for Gibbs free energy (ΔG) and atom–atom contacts.


#### Molecular dynamics simulations (MDs)
  The molecular dynamics simulations were performed using the Yet Another Scientific Artificial Reality Application (YASARA) dynamics software with the Assisted Model Building with Energy Refinement (AMBER) 14 force field44. The docked complexes were cleaned up and optimized, including the optimization of the hydrogen bond network. The ligands’ topology files were generated using the General AMBER Force Field (GAFF), and AM1BCC charges were assigned. A cubic simulation cell was created using the TIP3P solvation model with periodic boundary conditions45. To achieve a neutral system, the simulation conditions were set to 0.9% NaCl, 310 K, and pH 7.446. The long-range electrostatic interactions were determined using the particle mesh Ewald (PME) method with an 8.0 Å cutoff radius47. A time step of 2.0 fs was used for the simulation. The primary energy minimization was performed using the steepest gradient algorithm with simulated annealing techniques (5000 cycles). The simulation was carried out for 100 ns, and snapshots of the trajectories were captured every 100 ps48,49. Subsequently, several parameters such as RMSD, Rg, SASA, and hydrogen bonds network were evaluated using the simulated trajectories. Finally, Molecular Mechanics/Poisson-Boltzmann Surface Area (MMPBSA) calculations were profitably applied to assess ligands’ most reliable binding mode.


## Results

### Optimization and conformational analysis of lupenone compounds
  In our study, nine Lupenone derivatives (L-01, L-02, L-03, L-04, L-05, L-06, L-07, L-08 and L-09) underwent thorough optimization and analysis. Initially, the geometries were optimized using a Dreiding-like forcefield, followed by minimization with CHARMm and the SmartMinimizer approach within a distance-dependent dielectric solvent environment.

  For L-01, 702 unique pharmacophore fingerprints were identified. The ligand exhibited 9 distinct conformations, averaging 9.00 per ligand. The average radius of gyration was determined to be 4.46 Å, and the solvent-accessible surface area averaged 645.39 Å2. The pair-wise RMSD values ranged from 0.10 to 1.25 Å, with an average of 0.76 Å.

  The L-02 revealed 1583 unique pharmacophore fingerprints, with 48 conformations averaging 48.00 per ligand. The radius of gyration averaged 4.63 Å, and the solvent-accessible surface area was 714.40 Å2. Pair-wise RMSD values varied between 0.12 and 2.74 Å, with an average of 1.46 Å.

  The L-03 demonstrated 1961 unique pharmacophore fingerprints and 99 conformations, with an average of 99.00 per ligand. It had an average radius of gyration of 4.88 Å and a solvent-accessible surface area of 786.79 Å2. The pair-wise RMSDs ranged from 0.10 to 2.79 Å, with an average of 1.54 Å.

  For the L-04, 2430 unique pharmacophore fingerprints were observed, with 12 conformations averaging 12.00 per ligand. The average radius of gyration was 4.41 Å, and the solvent-accessible surface area was 641.60 Å2. The RMSD values ranged from 0.31 to 1.44 Å, averaging 0.79 Å.

  The L-05 contained 3473 unique pharmacophore fingerprints, with 26 conformations averaging 26.00 per ligand. The radius of gyration averaged 4.37 Å, and the solvent-accessible surface area was 636.15 Å2. Pair-wise RMSD values ranged from 0.12 to 1.47 Å, with an average of 0.77 Å.

  The L-06 revealed 3224 unique pharmacophore fingerprints and 29 conformations, averaging 29.00 per ligand. The radius of gyration was 4.45 Å, and the solvent-accessible surface area was 662.15 Å2. The pair-wise RMSDs ranged from 0.03 to 1.76 Å, with an average of 1.05 Å.

### Quantum calculations of lupenone compounds

#### Energetic characterization and molecular electrostatic potential (MEP) of optimized lupenone derivatives
  The total energy of the nine lupenone derivatives ranged from − 1324.02 to − 1731.91 Ha. These values reflect intrinsic electronic stability in the gas phase, with lower (more negative) energies indicating thermodynamically favorable configurations. However, stability in physiological environments depends critically on solvation effects. For instance, derivative L-03 (− 1731.91 Ha) exhibits the highest gas-phase stability, potentially translating to reduced metabolic degradation in vivo, though this requires validation via plasma stability assays.

  The intramolecular cohesive energy, representing the strength of non-covalent interactions within each derivative, spanned – 94.29 to –123.09 Ha. Compounds with more negative values (e.g., L-03, –123.09 Ha) display rigid scaffolds, which may reduce entropic penalties during ligand-receptor binding by minimizing conformational reorganization50. However, excessive rigidity can reduce induced-fit adaptability, which is often essential for high-affinity protein–ligand interactions, particularly in flexible binding pockets. Conversely, derivatives like L-01 (–94.29 Ha) retain intramolecular stability balanced with moderate flexibility, enabling induced-fit interactions in flexible pockets—a balance critical for high-affinity binding50.

  Dipole moments (2.06–4.00 Debye) revealed distinct polarity profiles, with L-08 (4.00 D) showing the strongest charge separation. High polarity enhances electrostatic complementarity to charged residues in the E6 binding pocket, favoring hydrogen bonding or salt-bridge formation. However, the impact of dipole moment on binding affinity is context-dependent: while polar interactions can strengthen ligand-receptor interactions, excessive polarity may impair membrane permeability, reducing cellular uptake. This suggests a trade-off: while L-08 may excel in in vitro binding assays, its cellular uptake could be limited without prodrug strategies to mask polar groups51.

  The dielectric solvation energy and total solvation energy were calculated to assess the stability of lupenone derivatives in biological environments. The values ranged from − 0.01615 to − 0.04167 eV, indicating that these compounds exhibit favorable solvation in aqueous conditions, which is crucial for bioavailability and systemic circulation. However, high solvation energy also implies increased hydrophilicity, which may reduce passive membrane permeability, potentially limiting intracellular delivery52. Thus, while aqueous stability is beneficial for solubility, an optimal balance between hydrophilicity and lipophilicity is necessary for efficient cellular uptake and target engagement.

  Molecular surface areas (1414.45–1815.94 Å2) and volumes (3336.80–4298.87 Å3) were computed to assess steric complementarity with the E6 active site. Larger derivatives like L-03 (1815.94 Å2, 4298.87 Å3) show potential for multipoint van der Waals interactions in spacious regions but risk steric clashes in constrained subpockets. However, excessive steric bulk can lead to reduced binding efficiency if the ligand does not fit optimally within the receptor’s active site53. In contrast, compact analogues (e.g., L-05, 1414.45 Å2) may better accommodate dynamic regions of E6, albeit with the reduced contact surface (Table 1).

  The molecular electrostatic potential (MEP) surfaces of all optimized lupenone derivatives were computed to analyze the distribution of electron density and predict key regions of electrophilic and nucleophilic reactivity (Fig. 2). ### Molecular dynamics results

#### Root mean square deviation (RMSD) analysis
  To examine structural differences and evaluate the stability, accuracy, and conformational changes of protein–ligand complexes, the Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD) is frequently used. In this study, we analyzed the RMSD profile of complexes between Papillomavirus oncoprotein E6 and ligands C03, C08, and C09 compared to the Apo protein (Fig. 4). The average RMSD values of C03, C08, C09, and Apo were 2.97, 4.05, 3.00, and 2.27 Å respectively. Initially, all the complexes showed similar RMSD values up to 20 ns which suggests that the structures being compared are relatively close in terms of their overall shape and arrangement of atoms. #### The radius of gyration (Rg) analysis
  The radius of gyration (Rg) is an important parameter used to evaluate the overall size of a protein–ligand complex58. It serves as a key metric to analyze the structural fluctuations that occur during MD simulations. By examining Rg, we can quantitatively assess how much the protein’s structure changes the simulation. In essence, Rg provides insights into the magnitude of structural variations the protein exhibits throughout the MD simulation. In our study, the Rg profile of C03 and C08 was almost similar during the 100 ns simulation period (Fig. 5), which indicates that the complexes maintained a folded or globular conformation throughout the 100 ns simulation period, with their components positioned relatively closer to their centre of mass. The similar radius of gyration values suggests no significant structural changes or unfolding events were observed during the simulation. However, the Rg value of all complexes was slightly higher than the Apo protein, whereas their deviation was lower between 25 and 27 Å, indicating that the protein structure experienced minimal expansion or contraction during the simulation (Fig. 5). This implies that the protein’s overall shape and compactness remain relatively stable and consistent.

#### Solvent accessible surface area (SASA) analysis
  The analysis of the Solvent Accessible Surface Area (SASA) is used to measure the portion of a molecule’s surface that is exposed and available for interaction with solvent molecules. This analysis is particularly useful in studying the stability and folding of proteins59. By examining SASA, we can gain valuable insights into how accessible the protein’s surface is to solvent molecules, which helps us understand its stability and how it folds into its functional three-dimensional structure. In our investigation, initially, the SASA profile of C03, C08, and C09 complexes showed increasing trends and after a few ns, all complexes maintained that stability with slight fluctuations which was an almost similar profile with Apo protein (Fig. 6). The similarity in SASA values between the complexes and the Apo protein indicates that they have comparable levels of exposure to the surrounding solvent environment which suggests that they may exhibit similar degrees of flexibility, exposure of functional sites, or potential for interactions. Consequently, it implies that all complexes’ regions responsible for ligand binding are likely to have comparable roles or functions.

## Discussion
  Our computational analyzes of lupenone derivatives show their promising potential as inhibitors of the E6 oncoprotein of human papillomavirus (HPV), a key factor in the pathogenesis of cervical cancer62. The integrated use of density functional theory (DFT) calculations, molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations provided a detailed insight into the electronic properties, binding dynamics and stability of these derivatives when interacting with E6.

  Quantum descriptor analyses showed that molecules such as lupenone derivatives have a balanced profile between electron donating and accepting ability, which is crucial for stable interactions with the target protein. The molecular electrostatic potential maps derived from the DFT calculations provided a better understanding of these interactions and showed that derivatives with ideal electron distribution can interact effectively with the active sites of the E6 protein. Since the investigation is fundamentally static, it does not account for dynamic conformational deviations or clearly includes solvent effects, which may influence the electrostatic and interaction forms under biological circumstances.

  Molecular docking and dynamics analyses provided further clarity regarding the binding pharmacodynamics of lupenone derivatives with the E6 oncoprotein. The HADDOCK-based docking results showed that several lupenone derivatives, particularly 03-E6, 08-E6, and 09-E6 outperformed the positive control, alpha-maltotriose, in terms of HADDOCK score, electrostatic interactions, and desolvation energy. Interestingly, the buried surface area (BSA) varied substantially, reflecting differences in how each ligand projects into the receptor pocket. While alpha-maltotriose showed a moderate BSA (326.81 Å2), complexes 07-E6, 08-E6, and 09-E6 exhibited higher values (> 700 Å2), implicating large contact interfaces that can bolster ligand-receptor stability.

  Notably, the electrostatic energy (Eelec) values of top-performing complexes were significantly more negative than those of alpha-maltotriose, with 08-E6 reaching – 113.17 kcal/mol and 09-E6 at – 88.89 kcal/mol, suggesting robust polar interactions stabilizing these complexes. Likewise, the desolvation energy (Edesolv) negative values indicate that the removal of water molecules from a binding interface releases energy, making this process thermodynamically favorable. Additionally, binding free energies for 03-E6 (– 10.90 kcal/mol), 08-E6 (– 10.98 kcal/mol), and 09-E6 (– 11.00 kcal/mol) exhibit superior binding characteristics compared to the positive control (– 6.27 kcal/mol).

  A deeper analysis of the atomic contacts (ACs) within the binding interface confirmed that top-scoring derivatives exhibit extensive interaction networks. Lupenone derivatives (e.g., 03-E6, 09-E6) show 5 × higher carbon–carbon (CC) contacts than α-maltotriose (3,349 vs. 665), corroborating broader interfacial footprints. Elevated CO (921 vs. 870) and CN (795 vs. 139) contacts in lupenones suggest enhanced polar interactions, aligning with their stronger predicted binding affinities (ΔGpredicted)63,64. Dominant CC contacts in lupenones imply stronger hydrophobic packing in E6’s binding pocket, while increased CO/CN contacts likely stabilize interactions through hydrogen bonding or polar networks63,64.

  Despite the favorable context revealed by predictive analyses, some critical aspects regarding the complexation of lupenone derivatives with the E6 oncoprotein require further clarification64. Binding free energy calculations using the MM/PBSA approach confirmed the docking results, with 03-E6, 08-E6, and 09-E6 exhibiting average binding energies of – 10.90, – 10.98, and – 11.00 kcal/mol, respectively, outperforming the positive control (alpha-maltotriose, – 6.27 kcal/mol). This strong binding affinity, coupled with an extensive network of atomic contacts (notably C–C and C–N interactions, with 03-E6 registering 3,349 C–C contacts compared to 665 in the control), highlights the efficient accommodation of these derivatives within the binding pocket—a key region for disrupting the oncogenic activity of E6.

  The integration of our DFT/MEP analysis with molecular docking provides a comprehensive framework to elucidate the structure–activity relationships of the lupenone derivatives. Notably, L-03, with its most negative total energy (–1731.90635 Ha) and lowest intramolecular cohesive energy (– 123.095 Ha), exhibits a highly stable and rigid molecular scaffold. This intrinsic stability minimizes entropic penalties upon binding, favoring a preorganised conformation that aligns well with the receptor’s binding pocket. This is reflected in its strong docking performance, with a CDOCKER energy of – 64.12 kcal/mol (top 3), interaction energy of – 89.00 kcal/mol (top 3), HADDOCK score of – 54.71 (top 4), and ΔGpredicted of – 10.90 kcal/mol (top 3). Furthermore, its large molecular surface area (1815.94 Å2) and cavity volume (4298.869 Å3) facilitate extensive hydrophobic contacts with E6, while distinct negative potential regions in its MEP profile (localized around oxygen atoms) suggest key hydrogen bonding interactions. Additionally, L-03 exhibits extensive atomic contacts of type C–C (3349, ranking second highest among all derivatives), reinforcing its potential for van der Waals and/or π-stacking interactions within the receptor pocket.

  Molecular dynamics simulations demonstrated that the complexes formed by compounds 03-E6, 08-E6, and 09-E6 maintained structural stability. This stability is supported by the RMSD values and the consistent radius of gyration (Rg) profiles, indicating that the receptor’s structure remained compact without significant deviations—an essential condition for inhibiting p53 degradation and, consequently, halting HPV-driven carcinogenesis (Scheffner et al., 1990; Bissantz et al., 2010; Akash et al., 2023). Additionally, the solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) analysis indicated that the ligand-binding regions of the complexes remained exposed at levels comparable to the apo state, suggesting that these sites remained accessible and functionally relevant.

  Taken together, RMSD, Rg, and SASA each offer complementary yet limited perspectives on a protein–ligand complex’s global stability. Although none of these metrics alone can fully capture the energetic intricacies of binding, their consistency over the simulation indicates that the complexes did not undergo significant unfolding or major destabilization. Consequently, no single “cutoff” for RMSD, Rg, or SASA should be considered absolute; rather, these parameters must be interpreted in conjunction with binding energy evaluations and local interaction analyses (e.g., hydrogen bonding patterns, side-chain rearrangements) to gain a comprehensive view of the system’s structural and energetic integrity. In this regard, the stable hydrogen bond networks observed in complexes 03-E6, 08-E6, and 09-E6, alongside RMSF profiles showing minimal fluctuations, further underscores the robustness of these ligand–receptor interactions and the overall stability of the complexes over time65.

  Dynamic cross-correlation matrix (DCCM) analysis revealed that ligand binding modified the mobility patterns of the protein. While the apo state exhibited strongly correlated movements in certain regions, the presence of ligands especially in 09-E6 led to increased anticorrelated movements, potentially reflecting greater local flexibility. Conversely, 03-E6 and 08-E6 displayed more cohesive dynamics, which may contribute to the stability of their interactions.

  Furthermore, ADMET data provide additional insights into the pharmacokinetic feasibility of these compounds, reinforcing the compatibility of the derivatives with the key criteria for drug-like behavior. While high hydrophobicity—reflected in the high number of CC contacts—enhances binding affinity, it may also reduce aqueous solubility and specificity, posing challenges for oral absorption. This is particularly concerning for orally administered drugs, where inadequate solubility in the gastrointestinal tract can limit systemic absorption52. However, predictive profiles indicate excellent intestinal absorption for most derivatives (except for L-07, which showed a significantly lower absorption rate of 52.38%).

  Variability in the volume of distribution (VDss) among the compounds indicates differences in tissue dispersion, with some (e.g., L-01 at – 0.23 and L-03 at – 0.249) indicating limited distribution. Importantly, none of the derivatives were predicted to inhibit CYP450 1A2 or act as substrates for CYP450 2D6, which minimizes the risk of adverse drug-drug interactions, a crucial factor for clinical translation. Additionally, low predicted clearance values may imply an extended in vivo half-life, which could influence dosage optimization in future studies.

  Furthermore, toxicity predictions summarized in here provide critical insights into the safety profiles of the lupenone derivatives. Hepatotoxicity assessments indicate that most compounds are predicted to be non-hepatotoxic, with scores ranging from 0.70 to 0.82, although L-09 is flagged as active (score 0.69), warranting further evaluation. Neurotoxicity predictions vary, with most derivatives exhibiting active profiles (scores between 0.52 and 0.56), while L-06 and L-07 are predicted to be inactive and L-09 shows a notably higher probability (0.87). Nephrotoxicity is uniformly predicted as inactive (scores ≥ 0.89), suggesting minimal renal risk.

  Respiratory toxicity assessments reveal that several derivatives particularly L-04, L-05, L-06, L-07, and L-09—are predicted to be active, with L-09 displaying the highest likelihood (0.98). In contrast, cardiotoxicity and carcinogenicity predictions are consistently favorable, with all compounds classified as inactive (cardiotoxicity scores ranging from 0.77 to 0.96 and carcinogenicity scores between 0.59 and 0.66). Immunotoxicity emerges as a potential concern, as most derivatives are predicted to be active (scores between 0.85 and 0.97). Reassuringly, both mutagenicity and cytotoxicity profiles are favorable, with all compounds predicted to be inactive (mutagenicity scores between 0.86 and 0.97 and cytotoxicity scores between 0.66 and 0.93).

  Additionally, predictions for BBB-toxicity suggest that most derivatives possess the potential to cross the blood–brain barrier (scores above 0.94), except for L-09, which is predicted to be inactive (score 1.0). Ecotoxicity and clinical toxicity endpoints indicate generally favorable profiles, with ecotoxicity scores ranging from 0.60 to 0.76 and clinical toxicity scores mostly below 0.68, while nutritional toxicity remains minimal across all compounds. Collectively, with high predictive performance (AUC values ranging from 0.84 to 0.98), these toxicity models support an acceptable safety margin for the majority of the lupenone derivatives, though specific endpoints—such as the potential hepatotoxicity of L-09, variable neurotoxicity, and elevated immunotoxicity—should be further validated in experimental studies.

  Our findings align with and build upon the results of Meza-Menchaca et al. and Vani et al. who investigated the binding potential of natural compounds against the HPV16 E6 oncoprotein using molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations. Their studies identified κ-carrageenan, crocetin, ergosterol peroxide, apigenin, and ponicidin as the most promising candidates based on their high binding energy values and stable interaction profiles66,67. Additionally, a large-scale, high-throughput screening study identified 30 potential inhibitors of the E6–E6AP interaction, with seven compounds effectively blocking p53 degradation in HPV-infected cells68. Among these, flavonoid-based compounds, such as gossypetin and baicalein, selectively inhibited the proliferation of E6-expressing cells, offering a promising structural basis for the development of future HPV inhibitors.  Similarly, our lupenone derivatives exhibited strong interaction energies, extensive hydrophobic contacts, and a hydrogen bond network within the E6 binding pocket, supporting their potential to disrupt E6-mediated p53 degradation.

  Overall, the results suggest that lupenone derivatives, particularly 03-E6 and 08-E6, establish stronger and more stable interactions with E6 than the naturally occurring alpha-maltotriose substrate analog. Their combination of high binding affinity, electrostatic complementarity, extensive atomic interactions, and favorable ADMET profiles supports their potential as effective inhibitors of the binding pocket. Further in vitro and in vivo validation will be essential to confirm their efficacy and pharmacokinetic properties, advancing their development as promising therapeutic agents for HPV-associated cervical cancer.


## Conclusion
  This study highlights that chemically modified lupenone derivatives exhibit both favorable pharmacokinetic profiles and high binding affinity towards E6, the principal oncoprotein responsible for p53 inactivation in high-risk HPV types. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations revealed each compound’s electronic structure and conformational stability, while pharmacophore mapping verified that key functional groups effectively align with essential binding features. Subsequent molecular docking reinforced by refinement and validation protocols confirmed that several derivatives can potentially bind with the target E6 oncoprotein (PDB ID: 3PY7) , a critical pathway in HPV-driven malignancies. Finally, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations demonstrated structural robustness and minimal conformational fluctuation of the top-ranked complexes, reinforcing their suitability for further optimization. Despite these promising computational insights, experimental validation is imperative to assess the biological activity, toxicity, and pharmacokinetic properties of these derivatives. In vitro assays should confirm their ability to restore p53 function and inhibit HPV-driven tumorigenesis, while in vivo studies will refine dosing, bioavailability, and safety profiles. Given the persistent global burden of HPV-associated malignancies, particularly cervical cancer, these derivatives represent viable candidates for further preclinical development. If efficacy and safety are substantiated, these molecules could pave the way for novel anti-HPV therapeutic strategies, bridging the gap between prophylactic vaccination and targeted pharmacological interventions.
