# Transcriptome associated with single-cell analysis reveal the role of S-palmitoylation in coronary artery disease


## Abstract
  Coronary artery disease (CAD), a widespread cardiovascular ailment, exhibits a strong association with palmitoylation. This research aimed to elucidate the role of S-palmitoylation in CAD through bioinformatics, providing novel perspectives on the mechanism underlying CAD. By intersecting differentially expressed genes with weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) from the GSE113079 dataset, 534 differentially expressed palmitoylation-related genes (DE-PRGs) were identified. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis, in conjunction with machine learning algorithms and immune infiltration analysis utilizing CIBERSORT, identified CXCL12, KRTAP4-7, and PPP2R2B as pivotal hub genes in CAD progression with significant immune links. Enrichment analyses revealed their predominant roles in immune regulation. Nomogram and ROC curve analyses revealed robust diagnostic predictive capabilities grounded in the three hub genes. A regulatory network involving the transcription factor HDAC2 and miRNA hsa-mir-23a-3p was predicted. Single-cell sequencing (GSE121893) further highlighted endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and macrophages as central cellular interactors, with significant crosstalk among these populations. These findings positioned CXCL12, KRTAP4-7 and PPP2R2B as key palmitoylated hub genes in CAD, thereby offering dual potential as diagnostic markers and therapeutic targets.


## Introduction
  CAD, also known as coronary atherosclerotic heart disease, is a condition caused by the narrowing or obstruction of the coronary artery lumen due to atherosclerotic lesions, leading to myocardial ischemia. It is one of the most common cardiovascular diseases affecting populations worldwide1. CAD originates in the coronary arteries, where an imbalance in lipid metabolism and maladaptive immune responses contribute to the formation of plaques, leading to chronic inflammation in the artery walls. Plaques rupture and persistent lumen blockage lead to acute myocardial infarction, the most severe manifestation of CAD, which is associated with increased mortality. Therefore, identifying key diagnostic genes in peripheral blood that associated with CAD is crucial, as it can provide a scientific basis for diagnosis, disease monitoring, individualized treatment, and prevention, thereby reducing the incidence and mortality of the disease.

  S-palmitoylation is a reversible post-translational modification characterized by thioester-linked palmitate attachment to cysteine residues, emerges as a crucial regulatory mechanism at the lipid-inflammation interface2. This process is dynamically regulated by ZDHHC family palmitoyl transferases and reversed by depalmitoylation enzymes (APTs/PPTs), orchestrating membrane targeting, protein trafficking, and signal transduction. Existing research has established a connection between S-palmitoylation and various human diseases, such as cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, and autoimmune diseases3,4 Notably, recent studies have extended this association to cardiovascular-metabolic comorbidities. For instance, upregulated ZDHHC14 expression has been identified in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) complicated by CAD5. These findings imply that S-palmitoylation may impact the initiation and progression of CAD. However, current research on S-palmitoylation-related genes in CAD remains restricted. Therefore, a thorough investigation into the role of S-palmitoylation in CAD and its underlying mechanisms could offer novel perspectives for the diagnosis and treatment of CAD.

  This study integrated transcriptomic and single-cell sequencing data to explore S-palmitoylation-associated hub genes in CAD, investigate their potential biological processes and pathways, and assess immune microenvironment linked to these genes. Additionally, a nomogram model based on hub genes was developed to predict CAD occurrence. Ultimately, this research aims to elucidate the underlying mechanisms of CAD and provide new perspectives for clinical diagnosis and therapeutic intervention.


## Materials and methods

### Data collection and standardization
  The training set (GSE113079), validation set (GSE23561), and single-cell dataset (GSE121893) originated from The Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://ww.ncbinlm.nih.gov/geo/). The GSE113079 dataset (GPL20115) included 93 samples from CAD patients and 48 healthy control peripheral blood mononuclear cell samples, generated using the Agilent-067406 Human CBC lncRNA + mRNA microarray V4.0. The GSE23561 dataset (GPL10775) included 6 samples from CAD patients and 9 healthy control peripheral blood samples, obtained through the utilization of the Human 50 K Exonic Evidence-Based Oligonucleotide array.

  The heart tissue samples, including 2 CAD and 2 control heart tissue samples, were obtained from GSE121893 (GPL18573), using Illumina NextSeq 500 (Homo sapiens). Thirty S-palmitoylation-related genes (PRGs) were identified from a prior reference6, including 23 palmitoyl acetyltransferase genes and 7 depalmitoyl acetyltransferase genes.


### Identification of key module genes
  Initially, single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) enrichment scores for PRGs were computed for the GSE113079 samples using the “GSVA” (version 1.46.0, 2024.2.3) package7 (p < 0.05), and the results were visualized in a violin plot. Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) was performed using the “WGCNA” (version 1.71, 2024.2.5) package8 on the GSE113079 expression matrix. Hierarchical clustering of all samples was performed using Euclidean distance. The optimal soft threshold power was determined between 1 and 20 to establish the gene correlation threshold. The relationship between the soft threshold (β) and the scale-free network evaluation coefficient (R2) was used to select the ideal soft threshold. The scale-free network was constructed based on this threshold, and genes were classified into distinct modules (minModuleSize = 200, deepSplit = 3, mergeCutHeight = 0.25). The correlation between ssGSEA scores and module eigengenes was analyzed, and a correlation heatmap was generated. Modules most strongly correlated (positively or negatively) with ssGSEA scores were considered key modules, with genes in these modules referred to as key module genes (|cor| > 0.4, p < 0.05).


### Functional enrichment analysis of DE-PRGs and PPI network construction
  Differential expression analysis was performed on GSE113079 using the “limma” (version 3.54.0, 2024.2.2) package9, comparing CAD and control groups to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (adj.p-value < 0.05, |log2FoldChange(FC)| > 1). The distribution of DEGs was visualized via volcano plot and heatmap, created using the “ggplot2” (version 3.4.4, 2024.2.2)10 and “pheatmap” (version 1.0.12, 2024.2.2)11 packages, respectively. The DEGs were intersected with the key module genes to obtain the DE-PRGs. GO and KEGG pathway analysis12 for DE-PRGs was conducted using “clusterProfiler” (version 4.7.1.003, 2024.5.2)13 (p < 0.05). The interactions between proteins corresponding to the DE-PRGs were explored by constructing a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network, using the String database (https://string-db.org/) with a confidence score > 0.4, and visualized using “Cytoscape” (version 3.9.1, 2024.2.2)14. Functional modules in the PPI network were identified using the “MCODE” plug-in (K-core = 2, degree cutoff = 2, max depth = 100, node score cutoff = 0.2). Genes in the functional modules were integrated and recorded as target genes, followed by GO enrichment analysis using “ClueGO”.


### Hub genes identification and ROC curve evaluation
  Lasso regression was a statistical method that constructed a penalty function to obtain a more refined model. It compressed some regression coefficients, performing dimensionality reduction on the data to address multicollinearity, which helped prevent overfitting in multiple regression models. LASSO regression analysis was performed on the target genes using “glmnet” (version 4.1-4, 2024.2.2)15. SVM-RFE was a sequential backward selection algorithm based on the maximum margin principle of SVM. It trained the model on the sample data, then ranked each feature based on its score, removed the feature with the smallest score, and retrained the model using the remaining features. This process was iterated until the desired number of features was selected. Additionally, SVM-RFE analysis was conducted to identify characteristic genes with “caret” (version 6.0–93, 2024.2.2)16. The goal of the Boruta algorithm was to select all features that were related to the dependent variable, rather than selecting a subset of features that minimized the model’s cost function. The significance of the Boruta algorithm lay in its ability to help us gain a more comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing the dependent variable, enabling better and more efficient feature selection. And Boruta analysis was applied to select characteristic genes using “Boruta” (version 8.0.0, 2024.2.2)17. The intersection of characteristic genes identified by these three machine learning methods defined the candidate genes. Violin plots were generated to visualize the expression of candidate genes in two groups from GSE113079, and expression validation was conducted in GSE23561. Genes exhibiting consistent expression patterns across both datasets were designated as hub genes. Diagnostic performance of these hub genes was assessed using ROC curves for both GSE113079 and GSE23561.


### The correlation and co-expression network of hub genes
  In CAD samples from GSE113079, Spearman correlation coefficients for the hub genes were calculated using “psych” (version 2.2.9, 2024.2.2) (|cor| > 0.3, p < 0.05). A correlation heat map of hub genes was generated using “corrplot” (version 0.92, 2024.2.2). GeneMANIA (http://www.genemania.org/) was utilized to predict genes related to the function of the hub genes, and their functions were further analyzed.


### Nomogram construction and immune infiltration analysis
  To evaluate the predictive capacity of the hub genes, a nomogram was constructed using “rms” (version 6.5-0, 2024.2.2)18. The calibration curve was plotted to assess the nomogram’s prediction accuracy, and ROC curves were drawn using “pROC” (version 1.18.0)19 to further evaluate its predictive performance. The area under the curve (AUC) was an indicator that measured the area under the ROC curve, with values ranging from 0 to 1. The closer the AUC was to 1, the better the classification performance of the model.

  The abundance of immune cells in CAD and control groups was evaluated utilizing the CIBERSORT algorithm from GSE113079, aiming to investigate immune infiltration. A violin plot, derived from immune cell expression data, was constructed to pinpoint immune cells that exhibited significant differences between the two groups (p < 0.05). A correlation analysis was conducted to explore the relationships amongst these differential immune cells. Additionally, the correlation between immune cells and hub genes was analyzed, leveraging the immune cell abundance and hub gene expression matrix. Correlation coefficients (R-values) and statistical significance (p-values) were computed, leading to the creation of lollipop charts that visualized the relationships between hub genes and immune infiltrating cells (|cor| > 0.3, p < 0.05).


### GSVA, GSEA, and regulatory network analysis of hub genes
  Gene set differences between CAD and control groups in GSE113079 were assessed using “GSVA” (version 1.46.0, 2024.2.3)7, based on the gene sets (c5.go.v7.4.symbols.gmt and c2.cp.kegg.v7.4.symbols.gmt) from the MSigDB database (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/). Results were visualized using “ggplot2” (version 3.4.4, 2024.2.2). GSEA was further conducted based on hub genes using “clusterProfiler” (version 4.7.1.003, 2024.5.2)13 and the gene set (c2.cp.kegg.v7.4.symbols.gmt) from the MSigDB database (p < 0.05).

  Transcription factors (TFs) targeting hub genes were predicted using chip-seq data from the ENCODE database (https://www.encodeproject.org/) on the NetworkAnalyst platform. Additionally, miRNAs associated with hub genes were predicted using the miRTarBase database (http://mirtarbase.mbc.nctu.edu.tw/php/index.php) on the same platform. TF-mRNA and mRNA-miRNA regulatory networks were then constructed and visualized with “Cytoscape” (version 3.9.1, 2024.2.3)14. The diseases associated with hub genes were predicted using the DisGeNET database (https://www.disgenet.org/).


### Drugs prediction
  Potential drugs targeting hub genes were identified by entering the genes into the DGIdb database (https://www.dgidb.org). The “ggalluvial” package (version 0.12.5, 2024.2.2)20 was employed to generate a Sankey diagram visualizing the drug-hub gene interaction network.


### Single-cell dataset analysis
  For single-cell transcriptome sequencing data, “Seurat” (version 5.0.1, 2024.5.2)21 was used to create Seurat objects. Quality control was performed to select high-quality cells (nFeature_RNA < 200, nFeature_RNA > 20,000, percent.mt > 5%), with results displayed via violin plots. Eigenexpression measurements for each cell were normalized using the LogNormalize method, scaled by a factor of 10,000, and log-transformed. The top 2,000 highly variable genes were selected using “FindVariableFeatures.” Linear dimensionality reduction was applied to determine the optimal dimensionality for cell clustering. Based on the optimal latitude value, cell types were classified. Annotated cell information from the GEO database (https://ww.ncbinlm.nih.gov/geo/) was directly assigned to distinct clusters for Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) mapping.

  UMAP plots were used to explore hub gene expression across different cell types. ReactomeGSA (https://github.com/reactome/ReactomeGSA) was utilized to investigate function and biological pathways, with the top 15 pathways visualized in a heatmap. Cell interactions were analyzed using the “CellChat” package (version 1.6.1, 2024.5.2)22 and the CellChatDB.human dataset from the CellPhoneDB database (https://www.cellphonedb.org/).


### qRT-PCR
  All peripheral blood samples were collected from inpatients at the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University. Patients with CAD were identified as adults who had undergone coronary angiography and were diagnosed with coronary artery stenosis ≥ 50%. The healthy control group consisted of adults who presented with chest pain but had no stenosis detected by coronary angiography.

  Ten blood samples (5 CAD and 5 health control) were used to extract total RNA with the TRizol method. RNA concentration was measured using the NanoPhotometer N50. cDNA was synthesized using the SweScript First Strand cDNA synthesis kit and diluted 5–20 times for qRT-PCR detection, with GAPDH as the internal reference gene. ### Statistical analysis
  Bioinformatics analyses were conducted using the R programming language (v 4.2.2). The Wilcoxon rank sum test was applied to assess differences between two groups, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.


## Results

### DE-PRGs identification
  ssGSEA enrichment scoring revealed that the PRG score in the control group was significantly higher than in the CAD group in GSE113079 (Fig. 1a) (p < 0.05). In hierarchical clustering, all samples were retained for subsequent analysis (Fig. 1b). A soft threshold β of 8 was applied, with R2 set to 0.85 (Fig. 1c). WGCNA identified 10 co-expressed modules, with MEyellow (cor = 0.72, p < 0.05) and MEturquoise (cor = -0.77, p < 0.05) selected as key modules due to their strong positive and negative correlations with the ssGSEA score, respectively, encompassing 2,096 genes as key module genes (Fig. 1d and e). Differential expression analysis in GSE113079 identified 1,341 DEGs, including 930 up-regulated and 411 down-regulated genes (Fig. 1f). By intersecting the DEGs with key module genes, 534 DE-PRGs were identified (Fig. 1g).

### Biological functions and protein interactions of DE-PRGs
  GO enrichment analysis indicated that the DE-PRGs were significantly enriched in muscle system processes, transmembrane transporter complexes, and channel activity (Fig. 2a). KEGG pathway analysis revealed that these DE-PRGs were enriched in the calcium signaling pathway, progesterone-mediated oocyte maturation, and oocyte meiosis (Fig. 2b). The PPI network highlighted strong interconnections among the DE-PRGs, consisting of 104 nodes and 94 edges (Fig. 2c). Using MCODE, three functional modules were identified: module 1 included CXCL12, KITLG, FGFR1, and GDNF (module score = 4.0); module 2 comprised CCNF, PKMYT1, CHEK1, and PPP2R2B (module score = 3.3); and module 3 contained KRTAP4-3, KRTAP4-8, and KRTAP4-7 (module score = 3.0). Merging these modules yielded 11 target genes, which were associated with the protein-coupled purinergic receptor signaling pathway and adenylate cyclase-inhibiting dopamine receptor signaling pathway (Fig. 2d and e).

### Hub genes (CXCL12, KRTAP4-7 and PPP2R2B) identification and their diagnostic value by ROC curve
  Machine learning-based feature selection identified seven candidate genes (CXCL12, GDNF, PKMYT1, CHEK1, PPP2R2B, KRTAP4-8, KRTAP4-7) through LASSO regression (Fig. 3a). Subsequent SVM-RFE and Boruta algorithms retained 11 and 10 characteristic genes, respectively (Fig. 3b and c). Intersection analysis of these gene sets revealed six consensus candidates (PKMYT1, CXCL12, GDNF, KRTAP4-7, KRTAP4-8, PPP2R2B) (Fig. 3d). Among these, CXCL12, KRTAP4-7, and PPP2R2B exhibited significantly elevated expression in CAD samples compared to controls in both the training cohort (GSE113079) and validation cohort (GSE23561), leading to their designation as hub genes.(Figure 3e and f). ROC curve analysis demonstrated robust diagnostic performance for these hub genes, with AUC values exceeding 0.9 in both GSE113079 and the external dataset GSE23561, confirming their potential as highly discriminative biomarkers for CAD detection. (Figure 3g and h).

### Correlation of hub genes and the nomogram conducted for CAD prediction
  Significant correlations were observed among the three hub genes, with the strongest correlation (0.91) between KRTAP4-7 and CXCL12 (Fig. 4a). A functional co-expression network was constructed, integrating these hub genes with 20 interactors functionally enriched in cytokine activity and chemokine receptor binding pathways (Fig. 4b). A nomogram based on these three hub genes was further developed (Fig. 4c). The calibration curve showed a p-value greater than 0.05 and a slope near 1, indicating the nomogram’s robust diagnostic performance (Fig. 4d). The ROC curve yielded an AUC greater than 0.9, further validating the model’s predictive accuracy (Fig. 4e).

The strongest positive correlation was observed between activated NK cells and activated mast cells, while the strongest negative correlation was found between CD8 T cells and monocytes (Fig. 5c). Lollipop charts demonstrated that CXCL12 and KRTAP4-7 were significantly correlated with naive CD4 T cells, monocytes, and resting NK cells (Fig. 5d).

### Enrichment analysis of hub genes
  GSVA, based on the “c5.go.v7.4.symbols.gmt” gene set, revealed that hub genes in the CAD group were associated with calcium-dependent phospholipase A2 activity and cGMP-mediated signaling. In contrast, hub genes in the control group were linked to the SWR1 complex and rRNA-containing ribonucleoprotein complexes exported from the nucleus (Fig. 6a). Furthermore, analysis using the “c2.cp.kegg.v7.4.symbols.gmt” gene set showed that hub genes in the CAD group were associated with dilated cardiomyopathy and ECM receptor interaction, while those in the control group were related to aminoacyl tRNA biosynthesis and non-homologous end joining (Fig. 6b). Additionally, all three hub genes were positively correlated with olfactory transduction and neuroactive ligand-receptor interactions, and negatively correlated with graft-versus-host disease and the spliceosome (Fig. 6c).

## Discussion
  Ischemic heart disease (IHD), encompassing coronary artery disease (CAD) and myocardial infarction, persists as the primary contributor to all-cause and cardiovascular mortality worldwide23. Current therapeutic strategies primarily focus on three pillars: systemic lipid management, antithrombotic strategies, and revascularization procedures. Despite these advances, a substantial residual cardiovascular risk persists in optimally treated patients24, highlighting critical gaps in our understanding of disease pathophysiology. This unmet clinical need underscores the imperative for developing mechanism-driven therapies targeting novel pathological pathways.

  S-palmitoylation, an abundant post-translational lipid modification, plays a pivotal role in various biological processes, including protein localization, stabilization, enzyme activity regulation, and signal transduction2,25. Its reversible nature offers opportunities for regulatory intervention. This modification is catalyzed by palmitoyl acyltransferases, which belong to the ZDHHC family and share a common catalytic domain structure, “Asp-His-His-Cys.” These enzymes are localized in specific subcellular compartments, such as the Golgi apparatus, endoplasmic reticulum, circulating endosomes, and plasma membrane. De-palmitoylation is mediated by three families of enzymes: APTs, palmitoyl-protein thioesterases (PPTs), and the ABHD17 protease family, which contains α/β hydrolase domains. To date, over 3,000 human palmitoylated proteins have been identified26.

  Reports on S-palmitoylation in CAD are limited. Some studies have highlighted its role in ischemic diseases, such as diabetes-related atherogenesis and renal fibrosis induced by ischemic/reperfusion injury5,27. Zhang et al. showed that palmitoylation modification is involved in the membrane localization of macrophage CD36, thereby promoting macrophage phagocytosis of lipids and atherogenesis28. The role of S-palmitoylation in immune regulation may also have an impact on the occurrence and development of atherosclerosis. Lu et al. reported that S-palmitoylation promotes the membrane recruitment of NOD-like receptors 1 and 2, which act as internal sensors in inflammatory cells and mediate the inflammatory process29. Du et al. demonstrated the role of S-palmitoylation in activating gasdermin D, a key process in membrane pore formation and cell pyroptosis30. Furthermore, S-palmitoylation also participates in T cell signaling modulation31, and contributes to endothelial integrity, cell adhesion, migration, and membrane fluidity32. The broad impact of palmitoylation in vascular biology and inflammation underscores its pivotal role in the pathophysiology of CAD.

  In this study, 534 DE-PRGs were identified, whose functions are primarily involved in transmembrane transporter complex activity, ion channel activity, receptor-ligand interactions, and signal receptor activation, suggesting that S-palmitoylation plays a role in these biological processes. KEGG analysis revealed significant enrichment of DE-PRGs in the calcium signaling pathway and parathyroid hormone synthesis and secretion, indicating that S-palmitoylation may be involved in calcium ion regulation. This could be related to coronary calcification, myocardial contractility, or calcium-dependent biological processes such as T cell receptor signaling31. Additionally, DE-PRGs were enriched in arginine and proline metabolism pathways, which are involved in macrophage metabolism, suggesting the potential impact of S-palmitoylation on inflammation in CAD’s onset, progression, and outcomes.

  Our study identified CXCL12, KRTAP4-7, and PPP2R2B as S-palmitoylation-associated hub genes in CAD through three machine learning algorithms (LASSO, SVM-RFE, Boruta) and cross-dataset expression validation. Furthermore, a nomogram model was constructed, and ROC analysis demonstrated its robust diagnostic performance (AUC > 0.9). These findings suggest the potential clinical utility of this model for risk stratification in CAD patients. CXCL12, as a crucial chemokine, regulates leukocyte migration and vascular inflammatory responses by binding to G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) such as CXCR433. Specifically, as a homeostatic chemokine, CXCL12 can induce the migration and activation of hematopoietic progenitor cells, endothelial cells, and leukocytes, typically retaining leukocytes in the bone marrow. CXCL12 also impacts inflammation by regulating glycosaminoglycans, receptors, and dimerization34. CXCL12 is highly important in cardiovascular diseases, exhibiting multiple physiological functions through interactions with the classic GPCR CXCR4 and the non-classic receptor ACKR3/CXCR7. The CXCL12-CXCR4-CXCR7 axis regulates platelet production, thrombosis, and inflammation induced by thrombosis. Furthermore, elevated CXCL12 levels in the blood are associated with an increased incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) in patients with CAD35. Studies have also shown that CXCL12 is identified as a causal mediator related to CAD36. In our study, CXCL12 expression levels were significantly elevated in CAD samples, with a high correlation between CXCL12 and monocytes and CD4 T cells. These results suggest that CXCL12 may affect the occurrence and development of CAD by influencing the functions of monocytes and CD4 T cells. Therefore, CXCL12 may serve as a potential target for coronary artery disease, providing a new basis for early diagnosis and treatment of CAD.

  KRTAP4-7 is a member of the keratin-associated protein (KAP) family involved in cytoskeleton stability. Studies on KRTAP4-7 were limited. In our study, immune infiltration analysis revealed a close association of KRTAP4-7 with monocytes and CD4 T cells, exhibiting a strong correlation with CXCL12. This finding hints at a potential synergistic effect between CXCL12 and KRTAP4-7 in the development of CAD by affecting monocytes and CD4 T cells. These results suggest that KRTAP4-7 may offer a new potential avenue for early diagnosis and treatment of CAD.

  PPP2R2B encodes a specific regulatory subunit of PP2A, which is a heterotrimeric serine/threonine phosphatase involved in numerous regulatory processes, including cell growth and division, as well as transcriptional regulation37.PPP2R2B has previously been implicated in the pathophysiological processes of heart failure and immune evasion38,39. In addition, PPP2R2B has been found to have a significant correlation with the occurrence of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and CAD40. In the current study, PPP2R2B demonstrated a robust correlation with Treg cells and exhibited notable differential expression patterns in endothelial cells when comparing the CAD group to controls in single-cell analyses. It reveals that PPP2R2B may affect the occurrence and progression of CAD by regulating T-cell differentiation and endothelial function. Therefore, PPP2R2B may serve as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target for CAD. In summary, the hub genes we screened may influence the function of immune cells through S-palmitoylation, thereby impacting the occurrence and progression of CAD. Additionally, we plan to integrate these biomarkers with clinical applications in the future through animal experiments, clinical trial designs, drug optimization, and other methods.

  Additionally, scRNA-seq analysis revealed that endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and macrophages exhibited substantial communication in patients with CAD. This cellular interaction may be closely linked to the GRN-SORT1 signaling pathway. The SORT1 gene and its encoded protein, sortilin, have been shown to play a role in lipid metabolism and CAD progression41. Activation of this pathway may be central to CAD pathogenesis, influencing intercellular communication and interactions, and thus contributing to inflammation, immune infiltration, and other processes.

  To our knowledge, this study provides the first systematic exploration of S-palmitoylation-associated hub genes in CAD, identifying three hub genes through integrative machine learning and multi-cohort validation. We further developed a clinically actionable nomogram with high predictive accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity, demonstrating its potential for CAD risk stratification. Functional enrichment and immune microenvironment analyses revealed these genes’ pivotal roles in inflammatory cascades and immune cell infiltration, suggesting mechanistic links to CAD pathogenesis. However, limitations include the absence of functional validation via gain/loss-of-function experiments or preclinical models. Future work will prioritize elucidating the S-palmitoylation-dependent regulatory mechanisms of these hub genes across diverse cell types and translational models, while integrating multi-omics data to refine therapeutic targeting strategies.
