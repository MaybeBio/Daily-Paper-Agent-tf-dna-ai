# Identifying disease progression biomarkers in metabolic associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) through weighted gene co-expression network analysis and machine learning


## Background
  Metabolic Associated Steatotic Liver Disease (MASLD), encompassing conditions simple liver steatosis (MAFL) and metabolic associated steatohepatitis (MASH), is the most prevalent chronic liver disease. Currently, the management of MASLD is impeded by the lack of reliable diagnostic biomarkers and effective therapeutic strategies.


## Methods
  We analyzed eight independent clinical MASLD datasets from the GEO database. Differential expression and weighted gene co-expression network analyses (WGCNA) were used to identify 23 genes related to inflammation. Five hub genes were selected using machine learning techniques (SVM-RFE, LASSO, and RandomForest) combined with a literature review. Nomograms were created to predict MASLD incidence, and the diagnostic potential of the hub genes was evaluated through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. Additionally, Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) networks, functional enrichment, and immune infiltration analyses were performed. Potential transcription factors and therapeutic agents were also explored. Finally, the expression and biological significance of these hub genes were validated using MASLD animal model, histological examination and transcriptomic profiles.


## Results
  We identified five hub genes—UBD/FAT10, STMN2, LYZ, DUSP8, and GPR88—that are potential biomarkers for MASLD. These genes exhibited strong diagnostic potential, either individually or in combination.


## Conclusion
  This study highlights five key biomarkers as promising candidates for understanding MASLD. These findings offer new insights into the disease’s pathophysiology and may contribute to the development of better diagnostic and therapeutic approaches.

## Introduction
  Metabolic associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is a chronic liver syndrome characterized by metabolic disorder-induced lipid accumulation, leading to hepatic inflammation and fibrosis. With a global prevalence of approximately 30%, MASLD is the most common chronic liver disease and can progress to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [1]. Historically referred to as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), MASLD shares a significant overlap in disease spectrum with NAFLD [2]. The early stage of MASLD, known as metabolic associated fatty liver (MAFL, historically non-alcoholic fatty liver or NAFL), is characterized by simple liver steatosis, with minimal to no inflammatory infiltration or fibrosis. Approximately 10–30% of patients with MAFL may progress to metabolic associated steatohepatitis (MASH, formerly non-alcoholic steatohepatitis or NASH). At this stage, the disease is marked by excessive inflammatory infiltration, hepatocyte damage and death, and fibrotic deposition in the liver. MASH is a leading cause of HCC and liver failure, yet no therapeutic drugs for MASH have been approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration [3–5]. Importantly, the risk factors and predictive biomarkers for MASLD progression remain poorly understood, hindering the development of novel diagnostic and therapeutic interventions [6].

  The “Multiple Hit Theory” is a prevailing model for understanding MASLD pathogenesis. This theory suggests that insulin resistance is a critical disease-initiating factor, leading to liver lipid accumulation, inflammation and oxidative stress [7]. Inflammation plays a fundamental role in MASLD progression, driving metabolic dysfunction, liver cell damage and necrosis, and the activation of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), which accelerates fibrosis and cirrhosis [8]. Therefore, identifying inflammation-related biomarkers and thereby developing targeted therapies are crucial for managing MASLD [9]. The advent of next-generation sequencing technologies has significantly advanced the prediction of MASLD biomarkers. However, comprehensive analysis of gene signatures associated with MASLD-associated inflammation remain scarce.

  Our study aims to identify inflammation-related biomarkers in MASLD by analyzing GEO datasets through bioinformatics techniques, with the goal of offering novel insights to enhance clinical diagnosis and treatment strategies. Using the DESeq2 package and weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), we identified 23 inflammation-related genes. We then applied support vector machine-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE), Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO), and RandomForest techniques to refine and select 5 hub genes through a comprehensive literature review. To evaluate the diagnostic performance of these genes, we used receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and nomograms. Finally, we validated our findings with RNA-sequencing transcriptomic assays and immunohistochemical staining.


## Materials and methods

### Acquisition of subjects and datasets
  Data were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) using the search terms “NAFLD” or “MASLD” and “Homo sapiens”. The datasets were selected based on the following criteria: (1) Datasets published within the past six years (2019–2025); (2) Liver biopsy samples from MASLD patients average aged 35–55 years; (3) Availability of RNA-seq raw counts and NAFLD activity scores (NAS) or validated disease staging; (4) Exclusion of cohorts containing patients with comorbid chronic systemic diseases such as diabetes and viral hepatitis; (5) Exclusion of datasets with < 30 samples; (6) Exclusion of cohorts exhibiting > 5-fold inter-group sample size disparities post-stratification. We finally obtained six exploration datasets (GSE126848, GSE130970, GSE162694, GSE174478, GSE207310, GSE225740), one training dataset (GSE167523) and one validation dataset (GSE135251). Three datasets were classified into Control, MAFL and MASH groups by clinical experts. Five datasets were categorized based on the NAS into L_NAS (0–1), M_NAS (2–4) and H_NAS (5–8) groups. Detailed subject information is provided in Table 1. Additionally, we identified 11,236 inflammation-related genes from the Genecard database (https://www.genecards.org/).

### Screening for differentially expressed genes
  RNA-seq analysis was performed with gene expression data provided as raw counts. Genes with zero expression across all samples were excluded. Batch effects across datasets were evaluated through principal component analysis (PCA), with subsequent correction performed by the ComBat algorithm based on sva package (version 3.54.0) [10]. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between groups were identified using the DESeq2 package (version 1.46.0) [11]. The Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) correction was utilized to decrease the false discovery rate (FDR) [12]. A|log2FoldChange| threshold > 0.5 was applied to filter the DEGs. The final DEGs list was generated by intersecting the results from all six exploration datasets. Heatmaps and volcano plots of DEGs were created using the R packages “pheatmap” (version 1.0.12) [13] and “ggplot2” (version 3.5.1) [14].


### Identification of key modular genes
  Clustering analysis was conducted by the WGCNA package (version 1.73) to identify key modular genes [15]. First, an appropriate soft-thresholding power was determined to ensure gene interactions followed a scale-free distribution. A clustering tree was then constructed with the hybrid dynamic tree-cutting algorithm using the topological overlap matrix. Gene modules (≥ 50 genes) with the highest correlation were selected for further analysis. Details on the MEDissThres, soft-thresholding power and target modules for datasets are provided in Table 2.

### Construction of protein-protein interaction network
  We integrated the results of WGCNA, DEGs and inflammation-related genes from Genecard to identify the candidate genes. A Venn diagram of these genes was generated on the Evenn website (http://www.ehbio.com/test/venn/). To construct the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network, STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) was used with a medium confidence threshold > 0.4 and excluding isolated nodes [16]. The PPI network was visualized by the GeneMANIA website (http://genemania.org/).


### Functional enrichment analysis
  Functional enrichment analysis was conducted using the R packages “clusterProfiler” (version 4.13.3), “org.Hs.eg.db” (version 3.19.1) and “DOSE” (version 3.99.1) [17]. The analysis included Gene Ontology (GO) categories—Biological Process (BP), Molecular Function (MF) and Cellular Component (CC). Disease Ontology (DO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis were also conducted. BH correction was applied in these analyses to minimize FDR [17]. The top 15 signaling pathways and diseases ranked by GeneRatio were displayed, with the FDR-adjusted p-value < 0.05.


### Machine learning for hub genes
  Potentially abnormal samples from the datasets GSE167523 and GSE135251 were identified and cleaned by integrating Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (DBSCAN) and PCA-based Mahalanobis Distance. The filtered samples are detailed in Table 3. Machine learning methods comprised LASSO regression (glmnet, version 4.1.8) for feature selection [18], RandomForest (version 4.7.1.2) for nonlinear pattern detection [19], and SVM-RFE (e1071, version 1.7–16; caret, version 7.0.1) for optimal margin identification [20, 21]. Cross-validation (10-fold) is employed to evaluate the generalization performance of the model, and the details can be found in Supplementary Materials. To prioritize high-confidence candidates, genes identified by at least two machine learning methods were selected and shortlisted in Table 4. To balance novelty and biological plausibility, we further filtered these genes based on their association with MASLD in existing literature. Specifically, a PubMed (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) search (updated to March 2025) was performed using the query: (“MASLD” OR “NAFLD”) AND (“Gene Symbol”), and genes with ≤ 5 publications were retained.

### Diagnostic performance analysis of hub genes
  The nomogram-based prediction models were performed to assess the risk of MASH using R package “rms” (version 7.0.0) [22]. The models’ performance was verified with 10-fold CV, as seen in the Supplemental Materials. To evaluate the accuracy and clinical utility, package “pROC” (version 1.18.5) was employed to generate ROC curves for hub genes and clinical characteristics [23]. Additionally, the “ggpubr” package (version 0.6.0) was used to create violin plots depicting the relative expression levels of the hub genes.


### Gene set enrichment analysis
  Samples in GSE167523 were divided into two groups based on the median gene expression levels. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using packages “clusterProfiler” (version 4.13.3) and “enrichplot” (version 1.25.2) to explore the biological pathways associated with hub genes. Hallmark gene sets from the MSigDB (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb) including 50 well-established biological pathways [24], were used to investigate the relationship between genetic functions and biological processes. For single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA), the “GSVA” package (version 2.0.5) was utilized to compute GSEA scores [25]. BH method was applied in GSEA to obtain FDR-adjusted p-value [26]. The “reshape2” package (version 1.4.4) was used to generate box plots. The “tidyverse” package (version 2.0.0) was employed to create a heatmap illustrating the correlations between hub genes and biological pathways.


### Immune infiltration
  The proportion of 22 immune cell types between MAFL and MASH groups of GSE167523 was assessed using the CIBERSORT algorithm via CIBERSORTx website (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/) [27]. After removing cells with zero expression, Spearman correlation analysis with BH correction was performed to explore the relationships between immune cell types and hub genes.


### Prediction of transcription factors and chemicals
  Transcription factors (TFs) associated with the 5 hub genes were predicted by JASPAR TF binding site profile database. To identify potential therapeutic drugs for MASLD, we explored chemicals targeting the hub genes through the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD). All the network diagrams were generated using the NetworkAnalyst platform (https://www.networkanalyst.ca/NetworkAnalyst/).


### MASLD mouse model
  Six SPF-grade male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks) were purchased from the Nantong University Laboratory Animal Center and acclimated for one week. Then the mice were randomly divided into two groups: the normal chow diet (NCD) group, and the high-fat, high-cholesterol diet (HFHC, #TP26304; Trophic Co LTD, Nantong, China) group. Mice in the NCD group were fed an AIN-93G diet, while those in the HFHC group received a diet containing 42% fat and 0.2% cholesterol. All animals were housed in controlled conditions (21–25 °C, 50–60% humidity, 12-hour light/dark cycle) and free access to food and water. After 20 weeks, the mice were euthanized under isoflurane anesthesia and their liver tissues were collected. The study was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee (AEC) of the Nantong University (IACUC20220930-1004). All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, with measures implemented to minimize animal suffering as per the established pain management protocol.


### Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining
  Liver tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and stored at room temperature for 24 h. Following dehydration, the tissues were embedded in paraffin and sectioned into 4 μm slices. The paraffin sections were stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin using a standard protocol and examined under a light digital microscope.


### Oil red O staining
  Fresh liver tissues were OCT embedded, sectioned into 5 μm frozen slices and incubated in the Oil Red O working solution for 20 min. This solution was prepared by dissolving 1 g of Oil Red O in 200 mL of isopropanol and diluting it with distilled water in a 3:2 ratio. Thereafter, the sections were rinsed with distilled water and counterstained with hematoxylin. Lipid accumulation in the liver was then examined under a light microscope.


### Immunohistochemical staining
  For immunohistochemical staining (IHC), paraffin sections were rehydrated using a series of ethanol gradients, and then subjected to antigen retrieve in a heated autoclave. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked using 3% hydrogen peroxide, followed by blocking with 1% bovine serum albumin. The sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody against FAT10 (1:100, #76194, Cell signaling), followed by a 1-hour incubation with the secondary antibody (HRP-Polymer, Biocare Medical) at room temperature. Immunoreactivity was visualized using DAB reagent (34002, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and counterstained with hematoxylin. The stained tissue sections were examined under a light microscope, where positively stained cells were identified by their brownish-yellow coloration.


### Liver transcriptome sequencing
  Total RNA of liver tissues was extracted using the MagPure Universal RNA LQ kit (Magen, Guangzhou, CHN). The quantity and quality of the RNA were assessed with NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies, Wilmington, DE). Strand-specific libraries were constructed using the TruSeq RNA sample preparation kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), and RNA-seq was performed on the Illumina Novaseq (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Raw sequencing data were processed using Skewer, and data quality was evaluated with FastQC v0.10.1. Target transcripts were obtained from the genome assembly mm39 from Ensembl [28], ensuring accurate representation of the liver transcriptome.


### Statistical analysis
  Statistical analysis and graph generation were performed by R 4.4.1 and GraphPad Prism 8.0.2. Student’s t-test with or without Welch’s correction were used to compare differences between groups. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.


## Results

### Integrative gene expression analysis identifies candidate genes in MASLD pathogenesis
  The analytical framework for our study is illustrated in Fig. 1. To identify candidate genes involved in MASLD pathogenesis, we employed WGCNA to pinpoint gene modules with significant correlations across the datasets. A scale-free R² threshold ≥ 0.9 was applied, with detailed parameters such as MEDissThres, soft-thresholding power and target modules outlined in the Supplementary Figures. WGCNA analysis identified 3,992 genes (Fig. 2A). DEGs analysis revealed 56 robust genes (Fig. 2B). Integrating results from WGCNA, DEGs and inflammation-related genes, we refined the list to 23 candidate genes (Fig. 2C).

### Functional analysis revealed enrichments in inflammatory and metabolic pathways
  To investigate the biological roles and pathways associated with the candidate genes, we performed enrichment analyses including GO, KEGG and DO. In the BP category, the genes were strongly associated with chemokine-related activities, such as responses to chemokines and chemokine-mediated signaling pathways (Fig. 2D). CC analysis revealed significant enrichment in endoplasmic reticulum lumen, external side of the plasma membrane and lipid droplets. For MF, the genes were linked to sulfur compounds, glycosaminoglycans, chemokine and cytokine activities. Integrated KEGG pathway analysis across six datasets revealed significant enrichment in cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions, viral protein-cytokine/receptor interactions, and chemokine signaling pathways (Fig. 2E). Finally, DO analysis revealed their associations with inflammatory bowel disease, bacterial infectious diseases and hepatitis (Fig. 2F).


### Identification of hub genes and construction of a predictive model for MASH using machine learning
  To pinpoint key genes among the candidates, we performed a PPI network analysis to assess their interactions (Figs. 3A and B). Next, we applied three machine learning algorithms—LASSO, SVM-RFE, and RandomForest—to refine the list of candidate genes. The LASSO algorithm identified 10 genes (Figs. 3C and D), while SVM-RFE highlighted another set of 10 genes (Figs. 3E and F). RandomForest analysis identified 19 genes (Figs. 3G and H). The three machine learning methods were all subjected to 10-fold CV and demonstrated robust discriminative performance: LASSO achieved superior sensitivity (0.95) and specificity (0.90), followed by SVM-RFE (0.90 sensitivity, 0.83 specificity) and RandomForest (0.85 sensitivity, 0.79 specificity). The genes appeared at least twice among the three methods were detailed in Table 4. Combined with the literature search strategy, we finally identified five hub genes: UBD (also termed FAT10), STMN2, GPR88, LYZ and DUSP8. The nomograms of GSE167523 and GSE135251 were constructed to predict the likelihood of MASH (Fig. 3I and J). Diagnostic accuracy was quantified through 10-fold CV, yielding classification performance of 0.85 (GSE167523) and 0.73 (GSE135251).

### Validation of core genes demonstrates robust diagnostic merit in advancing from MAFL to MASH
  ROC curves were conducted to assess the diagnostic performance of hub genes (Figs. 4A–F). Each hub gene showed strong potential for distinguishing MASH from MAFL in GSE167523, with the following AUC values: DUSP8 (0.897), GPR88 (0.690), LYZ (0.779), STMN2 (0.775) and UBD (0.908). Similar diagnostic performance was confirmed in GSE135251. To further evaluate the predictability of the core genes, we compared them with traditional clinical markers. Fibrosis stage showed varying predictive performance across datasets, with the highest AUC in GSE162694 (0.738), the lowest in GSE174478 (0.546), and intermediate values comparable to hub genes in GSE130970 (0.696) and GSE225740 (0.708). Compared to the hub genes, the predictability of steatosis grade was comparable or superior in GSE130970 (0.816) and GSE225740 (0.761). In GSE167523, DUSP8, LYZ, STMN2 and UBD were significantly elevated in the MASH group compared to the MAFL group, while GPR88 levels were significantly decreased (Figs. 4G–K). These patterns were further validated in GSE135251 (Figs. 4L–P), supporting their diagnostic efficacy across datasets.

### GSEA and SsGSEA uncover biological distinctions and pathway associations between MAFL and MASH
  To investigate the biological distinctions between MAFL and MASH samples, we performed GSEA and ssGSEA analyses. Based on the Hallmark database, ssGSEA revealed 46 significant altered biological processes in MASH compared to MAFL within the GSE167523 dataset (Fig. 5A). Correlation analysis showed that the five core genes were associated with 44 of these processes (Fig. 5B). Among them, DUSP8, LYZ, STMN2 and UBD were positively correlated with most biological processes, while GPR88 displayed negative correlations. A heatmap further demonstrated strong positive correlations among DUSP8, LYZ, STMN2, and UBD, with GPR88 exhibiting negative correlations with the other four genes (Fig. 5C).

  GSEA provided further insights into the pathways influenced by the hub genes. Upregulation of DUSP8 was linked to pathways such as PPAR signaling, proteasome function, steroid biosynthesis, and terpenoid backbone biosynthesis (Fig. 5D). Downregulation of GPR88 was associated with ECM-receptor interactions, focal adhesion, NF-kappa B signaling, and steroid biosynthesis (Fig. 5E). Elevated expression of LYZ showed connections to primary immunodeficiency, rheumatoid arthritis, and ribosome activity (Fig. 5F). Increased level of STMN2 correlated with AGE-RAGE signaling pathway, p53 signaling, ECM-receptor interactions, and focal adhesion (Fig. 5G). Finally, UBD upregulation was specifically enriched in pathways such as allograft rejection, rheumatoid arthritis, and staphylococcus aureus infection (Fig. 5H).


### Immune infiltration analysis reveals associations between hub genes and key inflammatory infiltrates
  To investigate the hepatic immune microenvironment in MASLD, we analyzed immune cell profiles in MAFL and MASH groups using the CIBERSORT algorithm. Compared to MAFL, the MASH group exhibited elevated infiltration of M1 macrophages, monocytes, activated NK cells and regulatory T cells (Tregs), with a significant reduction in resting CD4+ memory T cells (Fig. 6A).

  Spearman correlation analysis revealed notable associations among immune cell types. M1 macrophages correlated positively with resting dendritic cells, and monocytes showed a positive correlation with follicular helper T cells. Activated NK cells were positively associated with M0 macrophages but negatively correlated with resting CD4+ memory T cells (Fig. 6B).

  Correlation heatmaps further highlighted the associations between hub genes and immune cell types. STMN2 showed a positive correlation with resting CD4+ memory T cells, while LYZ correlated negatively with follicular helper T cells. GPR88 exhibited a negative correlation with activated NK cells, and DUSP8 was positively associated with M0 macrophages (Fig. 6C).


### TF-genes-Chemical networks
  To investigate potential TFs driving altered expression of the hub genes, we performed a TF-mRNA network analysis using the JASPAR database. YY1 was identified as a common TF for DUSP8, UBD and STMN2 (Fig. 6D). NFKB1 was linked to DUSP8 and UBD, HINFP to DUSP8 and GPR88, CREB1 to DUSP8 and LYZ, FOXC1 and FOXL1 to LYZ and STMN2, and POU2F2 to GPR88 and STMN2.

  Furthermore, we explored potential chemicals that may interact with the core genes by constructing a chemical-protein network based on the CTD database. The chemicals associated with at least three hub genes included estradiol, cyclosporine, tetrachlorodibenzodioxin, aflatoxin B1, progesterone, nickel, antirheumatic agents and copper sulfate (Fig. 6E).


### Stability validation of hub genes in merged datasets
  To enhance the accuracy and generalizability of the hub genes, we merged the datasets using two strategies. Specifically, GSE130970, GSE225740, GSE174478, GSE135251 and GSE162694 were merged based on NAS (Fig. 7A and B). GSE126848, GSE167523 and GSE207310 were merged based on MASLD staging (Fig. 7G and H). Compared to individual datasets, the hub genes showed similar expression trends (Fig. 7C) and predictive performance (Fig. 7D-F) in the batch-corrected combined dataset. Compared to the NAS strategy, the hub genes showed more significant expression differences and predictability in the combined dataset based on disease staging (Fig. 7I-L).

### MASLD mouse model confirms altered gene expression and pathway signatures
  Furthermore, we established a HFHC diet-induced MASLD mouse model to validate the alterations in gene expression and pathway signatures. After 20-week-feeding, the livers of mice in the HFHC group exhibited pronounced inflammatory cell infiltration, fatty vacuoles and lipid accumulation compared to the NCD group (Fig. 8A). Both body weight and liver weight were higher in HFHC mice, confirming the successful establishment of the MASLD model (Fig. 8B and C). Sample dendrogram and PCA analyses demonstrated clear differences between the NCD and HFHC groups, further supporting the separation of these sample clusters (Figs. 8D and E).

  To examine the underlying molecular alterations, RNA-seq and IHC analyses were conducted. DEGs were visualized in a heatmap (Fig. 8F). A volcano plot highlighted the changes of hub genes, with the dashed lines indicating P = 0.05 and|log2FC| = 1 (Fig. 8G). The mRNA expression of Lyz2 and Ubd, particularly Ubd, was significantly increased in the livers of HFHC-fed mice compared to the NCD group (Fig. 8H). Based on these results, Ubd/FAT10 was selected as the candidate DEG for IHC validation. As predicted, its protein expression was remarkably elevated in the liver of HFHC mice (Fig. 8M). GSEA revealed upregulation of inflammation-related signaling pathways in HFHC mice, along with downregulation of pathways involved in cholesterol homeostasis and oxidative phosphorylation (Figs. 8I–L).


## Discussion
  The prevalence of MASLD is increasing and affecting around 30% of the global population. MASLD includes both MAFL and MASH. There is a significant concern that MASH can progress to cirrhosis and even hepatocellular carcinoma, leading to considerable economic and healthcare burdens. The detailed molecular mechanisms driving the progression from MAFL to MASH are not yet fully understood. Investigating inflammation-related biomarkers is crucial for accurate diagnosis and effective treatment of MASLD.

  In this study, we analyzed RNA-seq data from MASLD patients obtained from the GEO database. We combined DEGs with the WGCNA algorithm to identify potential inflammation-related candidate genes. Through functional enrichment analysis and machine learning algorithms, we identified five key genes—UBD, STMN2, DUSP8, GPR88 and LYZ. To assess their diagnostic and predictive capabilities for MASH, we utilized nomogram diagnostic models and ROC curves. Additionally, we validated the expression changes of these genes experimentally.

  Early studies identified UBD/FAT10 as an IFN-γ-inducible ubiquitin-like protein that is highly expressed in multiple immune cell types, including lymphocytes and dendritic cells, as well as in pathological conditions such as liver and gastrointestinal cancers [29, 30]. Subsequent investigations revealed that Ubd-deficient mice do not exhibit developmental or behavioral abnormalities but instead display improved lifespan and lipid metabolism [31, 32]. Consistent with these findings, FAT10 is upregulated in MASLD livers and shows a positive correlation with NAS [33, 34]. Mechanistically, FAT10 facilitates liver steatosis by inhibiting PPARα-mediated lipolysis. Additionally, FAT10 regulates immune signaling pathways, including NF-κB and JAK/STAT, suggesting its potential role in modulating inflammation during MASLD progression [35–37]. Recent studies further implicate FAT10 in liver fibrosis by suppressing SIRT1 expression in HSCs [38]. Supporting these findings, our RNA-seq and IHC results demonstrate that FAT10 is significantly upregulated in the livers of MASLD mice, positioning UBD/FAT10 as a potential biomarker for MASLD. Collectively, these findings highlight the multifaceted role of FAT10 in driving liver steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis, underscoring its critical involvement in MASLD pathogenesis.

  STMN2, also termed SCG10, is a microtubule-associated protein that is highly expressed in neurons and plays a critical role in axon outgrowth and maintenance. Beyond its functions in neurobiology, STMN2 has also been reported to be expressed in various non-neuronal cells, including HSCs, hepatoma cells, and cirrhotic liver tissues [39, 40]. Transcriptomic analysis of subcutaneous adipose tissue from patients undergoing bariatric surgery identified STMN2 as a downregulated gene following surgery, implicating its potential role in lipid metabolism [41]. A WGCNA analysis based on RNA-seq data from liver tissues of MASH patients identified STMN2 as a core gene associated with MASLD [42]. A cross-sectional study further demonstrated that STMN2 mRNA expression correlated positively with liver histologic features and the severity of insulin resistance in MASH patients [43]. Moreover, STMN2 has been shown to be upregulated during hepatitis C-associated liver fibrosis [44]. While these studies suggest a potential link between STMN2 expression and metabolic dysregulation as well as fibrosis, the molecular mechanisms underlying these associations remain to be elucidated. Notably, we observed minimal expression of STMN2 in normal and MASLD livers in the mouse model, highlighting a potential discrepancy in the pathophysiology of human MASLD and diet-induced animal models.

  DUSP8 is a dual-specificity phosphatase that plays a role in regulating a variety of cellular processes, primarily through the dephosphorylation of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway. Genetic studies have linked DUSP8 genetic variants to the onset of type 2 diabetes, and this association may be attributed to the role of neuronal DUSP8 in regulating carbohydrate intake and maintaining glucose homeostasis [45, 46]. However, Dusp6/8 double-null mice show resistance to HFD-induced obesity, exhibiting reduced liver lipid content and improved glucose tolerance compared to wild-type mice [47]. This contrast suggests that Dusp8 may have tissue-specific effects on energy metabolism. Supporting this notion, DUSP8 has been shown to promote lipogenesis and steroidogenesis by inhibiting the ERK1/2 signaling [48]. Notably, while cohort-wide data indicate that DUSP8 expression is upregulated in the livers of MASLD patients, its mRNA level exhibits significant decrease in the livers of HFHC diet-fed mice. In light of these studies, we hypothesize that the decrease in Dusp8 mRNA level in the animal model may represent a compensatory response, whereas the up-regulation of DUSP8 in human chronic MASLD may be contributing factor leading to aberrant lipid deposition. The construction of a mouse model for liver tissue-specific knockout of Dusp8 may advance our understanding of its metabolic functions within the liver.

  The LYZ gene encodes lysozyme, a crucial antimicrobial protein predominantly expressed in epithelial cells of mucosal surfaces, as well as neutrophils, monocytes, and macrophages [49]. The mouse genome contains two homologs of the human LYZ, Lyz1 and Lyz2. Although hepatocytes exhibit minimal to no lysozyme expression, aberrant LYZ mRNA levels in liver macrophages have been implicated in the progression of MASLD [50, 51]. This is further supported by the findings that targeting macrophage LYZ expression with the natural sugar D-Xylose significantly ameliorates liver lipid accumulation and inflammation [50]. In addition to the liver, upregulated expression of LYZ has been observed in other tissues. A cohort study revealed a significant increase in plasma lysozyme activity and levels in individuals with obesity, which exhibited a negative correlation with fasting glucose levels and the severity of insulin resistance [52]. In addition, studies have revealed a notable elevation in the mRNA and protein levels of LYZ or Lyz2 in adipose tissue from obese patients and HFD-induced obese mice [53]. Given the fact that LYZ mRNA expression is closely associated with tissue macrophage activation, particularly in the liver and spleen, we hypothesize that the upregulation of LYZ expression reflects increased macrophage infiltration and activation in the livers of MASLD patients [54].

  GPR88 is an orphan G protein-coupled receptor predominantly expressed in brain neurons, particularly in striatal medium spiny neurons (MSNs) [55]. It plays an important role in regulating a variety of cognitive functions associated with the dopamine pathway, and its dysfunction has been linked to various psychiatric disorders such as depression and parkinsonism [56]. Emerging evidence suggests that GPR88 plays a role in regulating energy metabolism. Gpr88 knockout mice exhibited suppressed appetite [57]. Another study revealed that Gpr88 ablation led to markedly elevated levels of cocaine and striatum amphetamine-regulated transcript [58], a neuropeptide that regulates appetite and energy balance [59]. Under HFD treatment, striatum-specific Gpr88 knockout mice exhibited lower appetite and fat accumulation, as well as superior glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity when compared to wild-type mice [60]. Despite these findings, the expression and function of GPR88 in peripheral tissues, such as the liver, was poorly understood. Consistently, we found that Gpr88 is barely expressed in mouse liver tissues, and hepatic Gpr88 expression was slightly downregulated in mice fed with HFHC diet.

  Innate and adaptive immune systems play pivotal roles in the inflammatory, insulin-resistant and fibrotic processes underpinning MASLD [61, 62]. We analyzed immune cell infiltration in the livers of patients with MAFL and MASH using a deconvolution algorithm. Our analyses demonstrated elevated levels of M1 macrophages, monocytes, activated NK cells and Tregs in MASH, while resting CD4+ memory T cells showed a notable reduction. In response to inflammatory signals, M1 macrophages can be derived from liver resident macrophages-Kupffer cells or blood monocytes [63]. The accumulation of M1 macrophages serves as a catalyst for MASH progression. As a crucial component of innate immunity, NK cells identify and eliminate target cells through recognizing the cell surface receptors and producing cytokines such as IFN-γ [64]. NK cells appear activated in MASH, despite of conflicting findings from numerous studies [65]. Tregs can secrete anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10, which suppress effector T cell subsets and promote immune tolerance. The liver tissue from MASH patients contained higher levels of activated Tregs, suggesting an active role of these cells in suppressing chronic inflammation and tissue damage [66]. CD4+ T memory cells are primarily responsible for maintaining immune memory for previous infections, which are able to respond rapidly to reinfection. The level of CD4+ central memory T cells was positively correlated with the degree of hepatic lobular inflammation in patients with MASLD, which may serve as a sensitive indicator of pathological progression in MASLD [67].

  Finally, we analyzed the hub genes-related chemicals to provide potential MASLD medicines [68]. A review of the literature revealed that the prevalence of MASLD is higher in men and postmenopausal women than in premenopausal women [69–71], which implies a protective role of estrogen in MASLD development. As the principal estrogens, estradiol and progesterone, have been demonstrated to exert protective effects on the liver and may be associated with reduced MASLD risk [72, 73]. Cyclosporine, an immunosuppressive agent whose biological effects depend on binding to cyclosporin, has been studied for its therapeutic potential of MASLD. Non-immunosuppressive analogs of cyclosporine A and non-immunosuppressive cyclophilin inhibitors have also shown promise in preclinical studies [74, 75]. Epidemiologic studies have found that persistent exposure to endocrine-disrupting chemical 2,3,7,8-tetrachlorodibenzo-p-dioxin (TCDD) is negatively associated with liver health [76]. Exposure to TCDD can induce steatohepatitis, intestinal dysbiosis and disorders of bile acid metabolism, mimicking the pathological features of MASLD [77, 78]. Aflatoxin B1 (AFB1), a potent carcinogen that is frequently present in contaminated crops, has also been implicated in the development of liver diseases. The AFB1-albumin adduct, a blood biomarker of AFB1 exposure, is associated with an elevated risk of HCC and diabetes [79]. Moreover, retrospective studies revealed that elevated urinary nickel levels, elevated serum nickel levels, as well as exposure to soil heavy metals such as nickel, are significant risk factors for MASLD [80–82]. Notably, these associations were observed exclusively in men, highlighting potential gender-specific differences in environmental risk factors for MASLD.

  Our study has several limitations. First, the reliance on publicly available datasets, whose cross-sectional nature precludes us from inferring a causal relationship between core genes and MASLD. Second, these datasets lack uniform clinical metadata, such as BMI, alcohol intake and medication history, which makes it difficult to control for confounders. While the conclusions of this study are based on the analysis of multiple independent datasets, which helps mitigate the impact of batch effects, it is important to note that these effects cannot be entirely eliminated. Nevertheless, the hub genes identified in this study exhibited consistent across both individual and combined datasets. The hub genes showed diagnostic performance comparable to traditional clinical markers such as fibrosis stage and steatosis grade. However, the NAS-based classification, emphasizing inflammatory activity, may compromises the diagnostic precision of these traditional clinical markers across the datasets. Additionally, the validation of hub genes was primarily conducted using a HFHC diet-fed mouse model rather than human specimens. Although several methods exist for constructing MASLD mice models, fully replicating the pathological features and progression of the human disease remains challenging [83]. This is evident from the observed discrepancies in transcriptomic profiles between MASLD clinical specimens and mouse models. Although transcriptomic validation in a mouse model strengthens our findings, further validation using liver biopsy samples is required to confirm the clinical utility of these hub genes. Lastly, this study did not investigate the potential influence of confounding factors, such as diet, medication use, and lifestyle, on the predictive performance of hub genes. These factors are largely unavailable in publicly accessible datasets and would require the establishment of local clinical cohorts for comprehensive analysis. Therefore, the construction of independent local cohorts is urgently needed to fully elucidate the association between confounding factors and gene expression when identifying biomarkers for disease progression.


## Conclusion
  Our study identifies five key genes—UBD, STMN2, LYZ, DUSP8 and GPR88, as potential biomarkers for diagnosing MASLD, either individually or in combination, with notable accuracy. Further experimental research is essential to validate these findings and uncover the molecular mechanisms driving their roles in MASLD.
