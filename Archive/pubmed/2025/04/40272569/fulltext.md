# circCEP70 encoded protein inhibits the progression of hepatocellular carcinoma


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-025-05651-7.


## Introduction
  Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the predominant malignancy of the liver, constituting approximately 80% of primary liver cancers and is a major cause of cancer-related mortality [1, 2]. Its high incidence, aggressiveness, and recurrence rate ensure that HCC will continue to impose a substantial societal burden [3]. Liver cirrhosis is a significant risk factor for HCC, with the cirrhotic microenvironment exerting substantial influence on tumorigenesis [4, 5].

  However, recent advancements in single-cell technology have revealed the complex role of fibroblasts within the cirrhotic microenvironment. Beyond their established role in cancer promotion, certain fibroblasts have been shown to inhibit tumor development, indicating a heterogeneity in their tumor-regulatory functions [6]. For instance, a specific fibroblast subset expressing prolargin protein has been identified with tumor-suppressive activity [7]. Myofibroblastic cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) can also restrict tumor metastasis through the secretion of type I collagen, suggesting the potential for exploiting these cells in therapeutic strategies [8]. In addition, clinical study has revealed that HCC patients with underlying cirrhosis exhibit a lower incidence of early tumor metastasis compared to those without a cirrhotic foundation [9].

  Circular RNA (circRNA) is a type of noncoding RNA that is formed by covalent bonding during transcription, resulting in a single-stranded closed loop RNA molecule [10–12]. In recent years, research has shown that circRNAs are widely involved in various physiological and pathological processes [13], including playing important roles in diseases such as tumors [14–16], the cardiovascular system [17–19], and the nervous system [20, 21]. Therefore, the research potential of circRNAs is enormous, and exploring their regulation of HCC has significant value and research prospects.

  Based on the above background, we address the clinical issues of HCC arising from the cirrhotic liver background and combine the research potential of circRNAs in regulating HCC to propose the scientific question of how circRNAs regulate the biological behavior of HCC in the cirrhotic microenvironment and its underlying mechanism. We attempt to provide new ideas and targets for the research and treatment of HCC. Drawing from previous circRNA sequencing data [22], we identified promising circRNAs and carried out thorough investigations.


## Methods

### Clinical samples
  This study was conducted with abidance by the Declaration of Helsinki and all patients were given informed consent that the residual tissue from surgery could be used for research purposes. With the approval of the ethics committee at West China Hospital, a total of 145 HCC samples were collected from West China Hospital, Sichuan University for research, among which 62 tumor tissues were accompanied by their corresponding normal tissues. The collected tissue samples were promptly frozen in liquid nitrogen after resection and were subsequently utilized for sequencing as well as the extraction of both total RNA and protein.


### Cell culture
  The cell lines utilized in this study comprised HCC LM9, SK-HEP-1, and HEK293T cells. All cell lines were authenticated, and the culture medium employed consisted of high-glucose DMEM (HyClone, Logan, UT, USA), fetal bovine serum (PAN-Biotek, Aidenbach, Bavaria), and a combination of penicillin and streptomycin antibiotics (HyClone) at a ratio of 90:10:1 by volume. Furthermore, measures were taken to ensure the exclusion of bacteria, mycoplasma, and other potential sources of contamination. In each batch, both the experimental and control groups use the same initial number of cells, but the initial cell quantity may be adjusted in different batches to achieve the best display effect. The cells were maintained in a cell culture incubator at a temperature of 37 °C with a 5% CO2 environment.


### Nude mouse model
  Six-week-old male BALB/C nude mice were housed in specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12-h light/dark cycle. All animal experiments conducted adhered to the "Animal Care and Use Guidelines of Sichuan University." In every animal experiment, a control group consisting of 5 nude mice was included. HCC LM9 and SK-HEP-1 cells ranging from 2 to 5 × 10^6 were subcutaneously injected into the mice, 1 × 10^6 cells were intravenously injected into the tail vein, and 2 × 10^6 cells were injected into the liver. Tissue samples are meticulously gathered 1 to 2 months post-procedure, ensuring full compliance with ethical standards.


### siRNA, shRNA and overexpression plasmid
  All siRNAs used in this study were designed and synthesized by RiboBio (Guangzhou, China). The shRNA virus was obtained from GeneChem (Shanghai, China), while the overexpression plasmid was designed by VectorBuilder (Guangzhou, China). Detailed sequence of siRNA and shRNA were provided in Table S1. The plasmids were then packaged into viral particles using HEK293T cells. Following a 48-h incubation period with the infected cells, total RNA was extracted and utilized for qPCR analysis to confirm the effects of knockdown and overexpression.


### qPCR
  Total RNA of clinical tissues or HCC cell lines was extracted using TRIzol (Invitrogen Life Technologies Inc., Germany). RNA was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using a kit (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China), and primers were designed by TsingKe Biotech (Chengdu, China). The specific sequences of the primers used in this study are detailed in Table S2. qPCR analysis was conducted using triplicate using 2 × ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech Co. Ltd.) and the CFX Connect Real-Time System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).


### Western blot
  The antibodies utilized are listed in the supplementary materials (Table S3), anti-CEP70-160aa is a custom-made commercial antibody that recognizes the antigen sequence KEQKTLQATQIF. Additionally, a plasmid overexpressing CEP70-160aa was constructed with a FLAG tag added to its C-terminus. Western Blot was used to detect the expression of CEP70-160aa and the FLAG tag, thereby determining the quality and effectiveness of this custom antibody. Cells were lysed using RIPA buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Nantong, China) supplemented with 1% protease inhibitor (Cell Signaling Technology, USA), and the protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid protein analysis kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Nantong, China). Total protein was separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and subsequently transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (Biosharp, Anhui, China). The membrane was blocked with 5% nonfat milk at room temperature for 1 h, followed by overnight incubation with the primary antibody at 4 °C. After three washes, the membrane was incubated with the secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. Finally, the developed bands were captured using a chemiluminescence imaging system. The expression level of GAPDH served as an internal reference.


### Nucleoplasmic separation of RNA and protein
  A PARIS™ Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used following the manufacturer's instructions to separate the cytoplasm/nucleus of RNA and proteins. Subsequently, qPCR was performed on the RNA obtained from the cytoplasm and nucleus, and WB was performed on the proteins.


### Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)
  The probes used were designed and purchased from RiboBio (Guangzhou, China). The positive control probes were U6 and 18S for the nucleus and cytoplasm, respectively. FISH assays were performed following the manufacturer's protocol using a FISH Kit (RiboBio, Guangzhou, China). Detailed sequences of fish probe were provided in Table S1.


### Immunohistochemistry (IHC)
  Fresh samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h and then gradually dehydrated using a gradient series of ethanol solutions. The samples were embedded in paraffin and sliced, followed by dewaxing and rehydration of the slices with xylene and ethanol. Antigen retrieval was performed by subjecting the samples to high-pressure heating. The slices were incubated with the primary antibody at 4 °C overnight. Finally, all slices were dehydrated, cleared, mounted, and stained using an amino-benzidine-based staining method.


### Immunofluorescence staining
  After the cells were seeded onto a 24-well plate and incubated overnight, they were fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 30 min, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and blocked with 5% BSA solution for 60 min. The cells were incubated with the corresponding primary antibody at 4 °C overnight, washed three times, and incubated with the corresponding secondary antibody at 37 °C for 120 min. The cells were washed three times again and incubated with DAPI solution at room temperature for 10 min. Finally, the cells were observed under a fluorescence microscope.


### Dual-luciferase assay
  This study involved plasmids designed and synthesized by VectorBuilder (Guangzhou, China). The kit used in this study was the Dual Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit-V10.2 (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). After transfecting the target plasmid into adherent 293 T cells for 48 h, the cells were collected, and the experiment was performed according to the kit instructions.


### Coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP)
  This study used the Pierce Crosslink Magnetic IP/Co-IP Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA, USA) for Co-IP experiments following the instructions of the reagent kit for the experimental steps. Briefly, primary antibodies were bound to magnetic beads and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with cell lysates. Elution buffer was used to isolate the protein complexes. The samples were subjected to Western blotting.


### RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay
  This study used the Magna RIP™ RNA-binding Protein Immunoprecipitation Kit (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) for RIP analysis following the instructions of the reagent kit for the experimental steps. Briefly, magnetic beads were first incubated with the target antibody and IgG antibody at room temperature for 30 min. Subsequently, they were incubated with prepared cell lysates at 4 °C overnight, followed by separation of the RNA‒protein complexes using proteinase K at 55 °C for 1 h. The enriched RNA was purified with phenol:chloroform:isoamyl alcohol and subjected to qRT‒PCR.


### Bioinformatic tools
  Bioinformatics tools utilized in this study encompassing multiple biological information websites (details of which can be found in Table S4), as well as software for interpreting the corresponding results. Notably, the analysis results from AlphaFold 3 were visualized using Visual Studio Code.


### Statistical analysis
  All experiments were performed at least three times unless otherwise noted. The experimental data obtained in this study were analyzed and plotted using GraphPad Prism 7, RStudio, Adobe Illustrator 2011, ImageJ, and Bio-Rad. Parametric data were analyzed for significant differences using t tests or Wilcoxon rank sum tests. Correlation analyses were conducted using chi-square tests, Pearson's tests, or Spearman's tests. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan‒Meier method, and the log-rank test was used to assess differences. Univariate and multivariate Cox risk regression models were used to evaluate independent risk factors for prognosis in the survival data. P values less than 0.05 (two-tailed) were considered to indicate statistical significance, data represent mean ± SD.


## Results

### circCEP70 is highly expressed in HCC patients with cirrhosis.
  Three samples from cirrhotic HCC and three from non-cirrhotic HCC were selected from HCC patients for circRNA sequencing (Fig. 1A, Table S5). Our preliminary research confirms that compared with those in normal liver background HCC tissues, 180 circRNAs were significantly downregulated, and 213 circRNAs were significantly upregulated in HCC samples from patients with a cirrhotic background, furthermore, among the differentially expressed circRNAs, the top five most differentially expressed circRNA molecules were selected as candidates, and circCEP70 was highly expressed in cirrhotic HCC and possesses regulatory functions in tumorigenesis [22]. In addition, circCEP70 was also significantly differently expressed between tumors and corresponding normal tissues among 62 pairs of HCC tissues and corresponding normal liver tissues (Fig. 1B), and patients with high expression of circCEP70 had better survival than patients with low expression (Fig. 1C).   circCEP70 is composed of 522 bases and is formed by the circularization of exons 3, 4, 5, and 6 of the CEP70 gene located on chromosome 3. Its splicing site is the starting base "G" of exon 3 and the ending base "G" of exon 6. Sanger sequencing was used to verify that the splicing site of the molecule was consistent with the theoretical sequence (Fig. 1D).

  Actinomycin D and RNase R had no significant effect on the expression of circCEP70, while its corresponding mRNA expression was significantly affected (Fig. 1E and F). Finally, using GAPDH as the internal reference gene, PCR products were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis with divergent primers and convergent primers. The results showed that only cDNA amplified products when divergent primers were used, whereas genomic DNA (gDNA) did not produce amplification. When a convergent primer was used for amplification, both cDNA and gDNA were amplified (Fig. 1G). These results demonstrated that circCEP70 is formed by exon splicing of CEP70 and confirmed its circular nature.

  Nuclear/cytoplasmic fractionation and qPCR experiments indicated that the expression level of circCEP70 in the cytoplasm was significantly greater than that in the nucleus (Fig. 1H). Additionally, fluorescence in situ hybridization validated the nuclear/cytoplasmic fractionation results and showed that circCEP70 was mainly distributed in the cytoplasm (Fig. 1I).

  The GEPIA website showed that the parental gene CEP70 was not differentially expressed between HCC and corresponding normal liver samples (Figure S1A). In addition, there was no statistically significant difference in prognosis between patients with high and low CEP70 expression (Figure S1B).


### U2AF2 regulates the expression of circCEP70
  According to previous research, the differential expression of circRNAs are closely related to RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) [23, 24]. With this background, the circAtlas database revealed that several RBPs, including U2AF2, can bind to the flanking intronic sequences of circCEP70 (Fig. 2A). At the same time, U2AF2 was a differentially expressed gene between cirrhotic and noncirrhotic HCC patients in the GEO database dataset GSE62232 (Figure S2A). B U2AF2 was found to be included in both circAtlas data (blue area) and the GEO bioinformatics data (orange area), we predicted it was associated with circCEP70. C The specific binding sequence of U2AF2 was UUUUUUC, which was found four positions in upstream and one position in downstream of the introns in circCEP70. D RBPuite website was used to predict three high-affinity binding sites (red dots) for U2AF2 in the upstream sequence and two in the downstream sequence of circCEP70, the x-axis represented the position of intron sequence, and the y-axis represented ability score of U2AF2 to bind to the RNA sequence. E qPCR confirmed knocking down of U2AF2(siU2AF2-1, siU2AS2-2) led to increased expression of circCEP70, while overexpression of U2AF2 (ov U2AF2) resulted in reduced expression of circCEP70. F In clinical samples, there was a negative correlation between the mRNA expression levels of U2AF2 and circCEP70.   The specific RNA binding sequence of U2AF2 is UUUUUUC, and when circCEP70 was an immature form, there were four sites upstream and one site downstream of the circCEP70 sequence that matched the binding sequence, these sites may potentially regulate the formation of circCEP70 (Fig. 2C). We then further predicted the binding scores of circCEP70 upstream and downstream sequences with U2AF2 using RBPsuit and found three high-score binding sites upstream and two high-score binding sites downstream (Fig. 2D), indicating that U2AF2 may indeed bind to the intronic sequences flanking circCEP70.

  Subsequently, knocking down U2AF2 resulted in the upregulation of circCEP70 expression, while overexpressing U2AF2 led to the downregulation of circCEP70 expression (Figure S2B C, Fig. 2E). Additionally, a negative correlation was observed between the expression of U2AF2 mRNA and that of circCEP70 in clinical samples (Fig. 2F). These results suggest that U2AF2 can regulate the expression of circCEP70, mainly through a negative regulatory mechanism.

  It has been reported that RNA-binding proteins can regulate circRNA genesis by binding flanking introns [25, 26]. Through coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiments, the U2AF2 protein and the potential upstream and downstream flanking intronic sequences of circCEP70 that may bind to U2AF2 was obtained (Figure S2D). Subsequent qPCR verified that U2AF2 could significantly enrich the upstream and downstream intronic sequences of circCEP70, indicating that U2AF2 can bind to the upstream and downstream flanking intronic sequences of circCEP70 (Fig. 2G), thereby regulating the expression of circCEP70.


### circCEP70 encodes the CEP70-160aa protein
  To investigate the biological function of circCEP70, we used CircPrimer 2.0, the TransCirc RNA Database, and circRNADb, which predicted that circCEP70 contains an internal ribosome entry site (IRES) capable of recruiting ribosomes for translation. Additionally, circCEP70 has an open reading frame (ORF), indicating that it has basic conditions for encoding proteins.

  The main IRES sequence of circCEP70 spans nucleotides 1 to 174 (Figure S3A). The secondary structure of this IRES sequence was predicted to contain multiple stem loops, which may provide structural conditions for ribosome entry (Figure S3B). We then inserted this IRES sequence upstream of the firefly luciferase gene and used Renilla luciferase as an internal control to construct a plasmid (Fig. 3A).   Total protein from HCC LM9 cells overexpressing circCEP70 was extracted, and protein electrophoresis was performed. We named the newly encoded protein of circCEP70 as "CEP70-160aa". Based on the size of CEP70-160aa (about 18.69 kDa) and avoiding overlap with the size of its parent gene (70 kDa), we conducted mass spectrometry analysis within the range of 15–25 kDa. The results showed that several peptide sequences obtained matched the predicted peptide sequences of CEP70-160aa, including MFPVAPKPQDSSQPSDR, QQEEAEWESINVLLMMHGLKPLSLVK, TDLKDLIIFDK, LLVEETSCQQNMIQELIETNQQLRNELQLEQSR, ANDLEQIMESVK, IGELEDESLSRA, and DLQKEQKTLQATQIF. Among them, DLQKEQKTLQATQIF is a specific peptide that includes a splice site, confirming that circCEP70 can encode the CEP70-160aa protein (Fig. 3C).

  To mitigate concerns regarding secondary or non-specific effects from circCEP70 overexpression, we generated a coding promoter mutant, which overexpressing circCEP70 with a mutated CEP70-160aa promoter (ATG), and then the coding promoter mutant(ATG-MUT), an overexpression plasmid for circCEP70(OE-circCEP70), and an overexpression plasmid for the CEP70-160aa linear transcript(OE-ORF) were transfected into the HCC LM9 cell line. Mutating ATG could abolish circCEP70 translation ability. Using a customized antibody, a protein band was detected at about 18 kDa, which corresponds to the predicted size of CEP70-160aa. In addition, when circCEP70 and CEP70-160aa were overexpressed, the protein expression significantly increased, while knocking down circCEP70 resulted in a decrease in protein expression. Following mutation of the coding promoter, the protein expression remained unchanged (Fig. 3D, Figure S3C). Finally, protein bands were also detected at about 18 kDa in clinical samples, and compared to peri-HCC tissue, HCC samples exhibit decreased levels of the protein (Fig. 3E).

  HCC cell lines stably overexpressing CEP70-160aa-Flag were established (Fig. 3F). In addition, AlphaFold 3(AF3)[27] was conducted to predict the structure of CEP70-160aa (Fig. 3G), and immunofluorescence experiments confirmed that Flag-tagged CEP70-160aa was mainly distributed in the cytoplasm (Fig. 3H).

### CEP70-160aa binds to PKM2 and inhibits its nuclear entry
  Through the use of Flag tag antibodies and protein immunoprecipitation methods, proteins that bind to CEP70-160aa were obtained (Fig. 6A). Samples of Flag-tagged and control IgG immune complexes were separated by protein electrophoresis and further analyzed by mass spectrometry, PKM2 ranked highest among the identified proteins (Fig. 6B and C). Protein immunofluorescence demonstrated the same subcellular location of CEP70-160aa and PKM2 (Fig. 6D), in addition, immunoprecipitation experiments verified that CEP70-160aa can indeed interact with PKM2 (Fig. 6E, Figure S7A). At the same time, AF3 was used to predict the binding site of PKM2 and cep70-160aa. The main binding site was located at 76GLU of CEP70-160aa and 516ARG of PKM2. There were three hydrogen bonds less than 3 Å at this site (Fig. 6F), suggesting that there was interaction between PKM2 and CEP70-160AA.   After knocking down circCEP70, overexpressing circCEP70, and overexpressing CEP70-160aa, the mRNA (Figure S7D) and protein levels (Figure S7E) of PKM2 were not affected, indicating that circCEP70 did not regulate the expression of PKM2. Considering that CEP70-160aa is mainly localized in the cytoplasm and can bind to PKM2, immunofluorescence experiments were conducted, and the results revealed that knockdown of circCEP70 resulted in a significant increase in PKM2 nuclear localization compared to that in the control group (Fig. 6H). Conversely, overexpression of circCEP70 and CEP70-160aa led to a significant decrease in PKM2 nuclear localization compared to that in the control group. Furthermore, mutation of the CEP70-160aa promoter to prevent overexpression of CEP70-160aa did not result in a significant difference in PKM2 nuclear localization compared to that in the control group (Figure S8). These experimental results suggest that circCEP70 may prevent the nuclear translocation of PKM2 by encoding CEP70-160aa.


### PKM2 regulated STAT3 phosphorylation in the nucleus
  After knocking down circCEP70 in HCC LM9, transcriptome sequencing (Fig. 7A, Figure S9A) was performed, and it was found that several pathways related to tumor regulation were changed (Fig. 7B, Figure S9B). PKM2 can phosphorylate STAT3 in the nucleus, leading to the formation of Py-STAT3 [28–30], which activates the STAT3 pathway to regulate tumor biology. Therefore, further experiments showed that knockdown of circCEP70 resulted in increases in nuclear PKM2 levels and STAT3 phosphorylation. Overexpression of circCEP70 and CEP70-160aa resulted in decreased STAT3 phosphorylation in the nucleus. Mutation of the CEP70-160aa promoter did not change STAT3 phosphorylation levels in the nucleus (Fig. 7C).   Moreover, knockdown of circCEP70 resulted in a protumorigenic trend with increased expression of multiple downstream target genes of STAT3, while overexpression of circCEP70 and CEP70-160aa led to an antitumorigenic trend in the expression of downstream target genes. As a transcription factor, STAT3 could regulate target genes such as PH3, MMP2, MMP9, Vimentin and so on [31]. There were no significant changes in the regulation of downstream of STAT3 target genes in HCC after mutation of the CEP70-160aa promoter (Fig. 7D). To conclude the investigation of the role of PKM2 nuclear translocation on downstream effectors, we used Tepp-46, which was reported to be a small-molecule compound that can inhibit the nuclear translocation of PKM2 [32, 33]. Following nuclear/cytoplasmic fractionation, WB analysis revealed that tepp-46 blocked the nuclear localization of PKM2, while the level of phosphorylated STAT3 in the nucleus was also reduced (Fig. 7E). Additionally, the activity of downstream target genes was reversed (Fig. 7F).


### Tepp-46 blocks HCC proliferation and migration
  Treatment of tumor cells with the small-molecule compound tepp-46 (100 μM) inhibited their proliferation and migration, indicating that tepp-46 can reverse the enhanced proliferative and migratory abilities of tumor cells in vitro caused by circCEP70 knockdown (Fig. 8A and B). ## Discussion
  The modulation of the tumor microenvironment on tumorigenesis is a complex and multifaceted process. While the microenvironment is widely recognized for its role in promoting tumor growth, a substantial body of evidence has also highlighted its potential to exert inhibitory effects on neoplastic proliferation. In the context of CAFs research, numerous suppressive CAFs have been identified, playing a pivotal role in tumor inhibition including breast cancer patients [34, 35], colorectal cancer [36]. In the hepatic oncology domain, hepatic progenitor cells (HPCs), which arise in regenerative nodules within the cirrhotic microenvironment, have been implicated in enhancing immune surveillance, thereby potentially curbing the incidence and progression of liver tumors [37]. Chiavarina B et al. identified a specific subset of CAFs, termed port_CAFs, which specifically expressing the prolargin protein, and prolargin was identified an angiogenesis modulator and CAF-derived tumor suppressor in HCC, stabilizing prolargin levels in the port CAFs subpopulation might revert their tumor-antagonizing properties [7]. The formation of fibrotic septa has also been posited to impede the dissemination of tumor cells [8, 38]. Clinical studies, such as that by Sasaki K et al., have reported that noncirrhotic HCC patients exhibit a higher early recurrence rate post-tumor resection surgery compared to cirrhotic patients with HCC, suggesting the presence of micrometastases in the noncirrhotic setting, which may also precipitate distant metastasis beyond the liver [9]. Furthermore, it has been observed that noncirrhotic patients present with larger tumor volumes than their cirrhotic counterparts, suggesting a more aggressive proliferative capacity of the underlying tumors in noncirrhotic individuals [39]. In this study, the circular RNA circCEP70 was examined within the context of cirrhosis and was found to mitigate tumor occurrence and development within the cirrhotic microenvironment.

  Recent studies have revealed the extensive involvement of circular RNA in numerous physiological and pathological processes, encompassing tumor [14, 40], cardiovascular diseases [18, 41], and neurological diseases [21, 42]. These RNAs played a pivotal role and exert a profound biological regulatory influence. Among these processes, the regulation of circular RNA in tumor biology has garnered significant attention. It modulates the biological behavior of tumors through various mechanisms, including sponging miRNA [43], protein binding [44], and protein encoding [45]. Notably, despite being a non-coding RNA, circular RNA demonstrates crucial molecular biological functions when encoding proteins. For instance, circFAM53B encoded a peptide whose expression was linked to a substantial infiltration of antigen-specific CD8 + T cells and improved survival rates in patients with breast cancer and melanoma. These encoded peptides could trigger robust anti-tumor immune responses [46]. Another example was circMET, which encoded MET404, a peptide consisting of 404 amino acids. This peptide interacted with downstream proteins to execute biological functions, and its overexpression was associated with a poor prognosis in glioma patients [45]. There are many other similar studies, suggesting that the proteins encoded by circRNAs played important biological role.

  In the present investigation, we deviated from the conventional comparative sequencing paradigm between tumorous and non-tumorous tissues [47, 48] opting instead to analyze tumor tissues with and without cirrhosis to discern differentially expressed circRNA molecules across tumors with varying clinical profiles. Among the differentially expressed circRNA molecules obtained through sequencing, we singled out the candidate circCEP70, predicated on its aberrant expression levels and correlation with clinical parameters. The aberrant activation of circCEP70 observed in this study carried significant implications, as it encompassed the exploration of upstream activators of circCEP70 within the cirrhotic context. This approach may pave the way for the development of targeted regulatory interventions at the nascent stages of circCEP70 expression, thereby offering promising therapeutic targets for the management of HCC.

  Extant literature abounds with studies that have delineated the role of circRNAs as molecular sponges capable of sequestering microRNAs, thereby modulating a plethora of biological processes [43, 49]. Similarly, the current study also addresses the question of whether there was a molecular sponge effect of circCEP70 like another research [50]. Through mutation of the promoter of the ORF of circCEP70, which prevented it from encoding a protein without affecting its microRNA sponge function, we found circCEP70 primarily exerted its biological effects through its encoded protein in HCC.

  In the realm of protein–protein interaction studies, our previous endeavors were confined to preliminary investigations utilizing truncated mutants, which yielded fragmented and imprecise outcomes [51]. Recently, the advent of AF3 has marked a significant advancement in the prediction of biomolecular interactions [27]. This method has demonstrated superior accuracy in forecasting protein–ligand interactions, protein–protein interactions, protein-nucleic acid interactions, and post-translational modifications compared to its predecessor, AlphaFold 2 [52]. By harnessing the capabilities of protein–protein interaction prediction, we have efficiently mapped the precise interaction sites between CEP70-160aa and PKM2, and engineered mutation sites for reverse prediction. Furthermore, we have meticulously designed corresponding experimental validations, the results of which corroborate the AF3 predictions. Our experiments substantiate the efficacy and reliability of AF3 as a potent tool in the domain of circular RNA encoding proteins.

  PKM2 could act as a protein kinase in the nucleus [30], for example, in immune cells, the increase of PKM2 into the nucleus can promote the phosphorylation and activation of STAT3, mediating the proliferation and differentiation of inflammatory cells [28, 53]. In tumor cells, Gao et al. reported that nuclear levels of PKM2 was correlate with cell proliferation in colonic cancer cell line. PKM2 regulates gene transcription by phosphorylating STAT3 [29]. This study confirmed that in HCC cells, when PKM2 entry into the nucleus is inhibited by CEP70-160aa, its entry into the nucleus is reduced, and the phosphorylation level of STAT3 is reduced, which exerts a regulatory effect on its downstream target genes and affects the biological behavior of hepatocellular carcinoma. In CD4 + T cells, TEPP-46 could inhibit nuclear translocation and suppressing CD4 + T cell activation [54]. In this study, we used tepp-46 to block PKM2 entry into the nucleus, and detected the phosphorylation level of STAT3 and the phenotypic changes of tumors, confirming that the downstream STAT3 phosphorylation level changes after the inhibition of PKM2 entry into the nucleus in HCC cells.


## Conclusion
  This study identified circCEP70 as a negative regulator in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) with cirrhotic compared with normal liver background. Its activation was mediated by the binding of U2AF2 to its flanking intron sequences. CircCEP70 exerted its tumor suppressor function by encoding a novel protein, CEP70-160aa, which inhibited the nuclear translocation of PKM2 and the phosphorylation of STAT3. The findings emphasized the importance of circRNAs in the cirrhotic microenvironment and their potential for further research in tumor biology.
