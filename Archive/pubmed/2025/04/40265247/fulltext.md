# Heart Rate Mystery Unveiled: Sex Differences in Human Sinoatrial Node Genes and Female Tachycardia


## Background:
  Despite over a century of clinical electrocardiographic studies showing that females exhibit a faster resting heart rate (HR), the mechanisms underlying sex differences in HR remain unresolved. Moreover, inappropriate sinus tachycardia (IST) primarily affects females, while males are at a higher risk for conduction block and atrial fibrillation (AF). We hypothesized that the sexual dimorphism of genes responsible for sinoatrial node (SAN) pacemaking and signaling pathways may contribute to the sex-differences in HR and susceptibility to arrhythmias.


## Methods:
  Human SAN central pacemaker and right atrial (RA) tissue were isolated from non-diseased ex-vivo donor hearts. Gene expressions were quantified and validated using the transcriptomic panel and quantitative PCR. Gene set enrichment analysis, Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA), and human-specific SAN models were utilized to define regulatory mechanisms and functional impacts of sex-biased gene transcription.


## Results:
  We identified differentially expressed region- and sex-specific genes, with gene sets enriched in HR regulation (e.g., TBX3, HCN1) and metabolism (e.g., ADIPOQ, LEP) pathways in female SAN. In contrast, differential genes and gene sets involved in collagen biosynthetic processes, fibrogenesis (e.g., EGR-1), and immune response (e.g., IL6, CXCL8) pathways were enriched in males SAN and RA. IPA predicted significant roles for TBX3 and estradiol in the sex-specific expression of genes involved in SAN function. Computational simulations showed that the sex-specific SAN differences in If (HCN1) and/or ICa,L (CACNA1D) can explain the faster HR in females, with females having a lower threshold for IST, while males are more vulnerable to sinus arrest.


## Conclusions:
  The human SAN exhibits region-specific sexual dimorphism in pacemaking gene sets. Higher expression of TBX3 and HCN1 in females may underlie their faster HR and increased susceptibility to IST, while enriched gene sets related to inflammation and collagen biosynthesis in males may predispose them to conduction impairments and AF risk.


## Introduction
  Biological sex plays an important role in cardiac electrophysiology1–3, which influences the occurrence and risk factors for various cardiac arrhythmias3–5. For over 100 years, clinical reports consistently show that females have faster resting and intrinsic heart rates (HR) while males have higher HR peaks and larger HR reserves6,7–10. Moreover, inappropriate sinus tachycardia (IST) primarily occurs in females11, while males have a higher risk of AV block, tachy-brady syndrome12, 13 and atrial fibrillation (AF)3. However, the underlying mechanisms of these sex-differences in cardiac arrhythmias prevalence and the function of sinoatrial node (SAN), the human heart pacemaker, remain unclear.

  Human SAN has complex intramural 3D architecture with unique structural, functional, and molecular features that significantly differ from any other cardiac tissues14–16. The intricate 3D structure and the extremely limited ex-vivo human SAN samples restrict the sex-difference studies on SAN function and arrhythmias. Although animal models have been instrumental in investigating mechanisms underlying the SAN function and signaling17, there are conflicting results across species regarding sex-differences in HR and hormonal effects on SAN function18, 19. Furthermore, the structural differences between human and animal SANs20, 21, limit their translational applications to human studies. To overcome these challenges, we have developed a robust, integrative program to investigate the unique structure-molecular mechanisms of human SAN under normal and arrhythmic conditions by accessing both diseased and non-diseased ex-vivo donor hearts22–26.

  Clinical7–10 and animal studies17, 18, 27 suggested multiple mechanisms that may underlie the sex-differences in cardiac electrophysiology including28: (1) gene expression and function of ion channels; (2) autonomic nervous system status and sex hormone levels, in addition to different hormonal effects on autonomic tone and gene transcription; (3) microanatomical structures and cell compositions. Furthermore, a recent study from Olivia et al.1 revealed sex-biased transcriptional variabilities across human organs and cardiac chambers, suggesting tissue-specific gene expression driven by transcription factors. Consistent with this study, the sex-biased genes and enriched pathways have been reported to vary in human atria compared to ventricule.2 Additionally, the sex-biased regulation of fibrosis, inflammation, and metabolic pathways may contribute to the sex-specific arrhythmia susceptibility and cardiac structural and functional remodeling under stress29–31. However, these findings may not extend to specialized cardiac pacemaker-conduction tissues, such as the SAN, highlighting the critical need for sex-specific research on SAN structure and function to identify new targets for HR regulation and arrhythmia treatment.

  In this study, we utilized the ex-vivo human heart to define sex-specific gene expression in SAN primary pacemaker tissue, focusing on the genes involved in HR regulation, inflammation, fibrosis, and metabolism pathways. Our integrated approach combined validated custom transcriptomics, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA), and human-specific SAN computational models to identify distinct sex-specific gene sets and transcription factors, predict sex hormone-regulated pathways, and provide mechanistic insights into sex-specific HR regulation and susceptibility to arrhythmia.


## Methods
  The authors declare that all supporting data are available within the article and its Supplemental Material. Fully described Methods are available in the Supplemental Material.

  Adult non-failing donor human hearts (male = 8, female = 8) were obtained from the Lifeline of Ohio Organ Procurement Organization approved by The Ohio State University Institutional Review Board (IRB). The IRB classified the study on samples from deceased donors as Not Human Subjects Research. The initial collection and data analysis of human samples were blinded to the clinical history. Following rigorous data analysis, we excluded hearts with any arrhythmias and/or active remodeling history (e.g., AF history and active inflammation), and samples with insufficient quality of SAN mRNA. The hearts used for final data analysis included 4 males (52 ± 10 years old) and 5 females (48 ± 16 years old), with no significant age difference between sexes (P > 0.05).

  SAN pacemaker tissue identification and SAN-specific RNA purification were performed according to previous publications22–24 (Figure 1). The gene expression was determined using a customized nCounter® Human Panel (Nanostring Technologies, Inc., Seattle, USA). The total list of 798 genes was used for the final analysis and log2 transformed gene expression of each human sample is provided in Supplemental Data Set S1. To validate the Nanostring gene panel results, quantitative PCR was conducted on the same human mRNA samples for key transcription factors and ion channel genes. The primer assays are listed in the Supplemental Table S1. The sex-specific computational models of human SAN pacemaker cells were modified from Fabbri et.al 32 and our previously published SAN cell models33.


### Statistical Analyses
  Data are presented as mean ± SD. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to determine the normality of the data. Comparison of age between sexes was performed using a two-tailed t-test, and P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Gene transcription was log2 transformed. Comparisons between RA and SAN, and between sexes were made with a linear mixed effect model and a two-tailed t-test. Genes with expression levels yielding P-values <0.05 and absolute fold changes >1.2 were considered significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Additional statistical details are provided in the Supplemental Materials.


## Results

### Extraction and Validation of Human SAN Pacemaker Tissue
  Figure 1 shows the study workflow. Human SAN preparations were dissected from the intact human hearts (Panel 1) and embedded in OCT. To ensure consistent sample collection, we performed serial sectioning of SAN cryo-blocks to define and extract pacemaker tissue exclusively from the central SAN compartment (Panel 2), where leading pacemaker cells primarily reside23. The intramural 3D SAN was identified using Masson’s Trichrome staining and immunolabelling as the Cx43−/α-actinin+ pacemaker cell region encapsulated in dense fibrotic tissue surrounding the SAN artery (Panel 3). Total RNAs were purified from region-specific SAN and RA tissue, which were extracted from the indicated locations using biopsy needles (Panel 4). SAN samples were validated by assessing the expression levels of SAN-specific markers, before sex-specific gene analysis (Panel 5).


### Human SAN-specific Gene Expression
  We first confirmed the expected SAN-specific gene expression compared to the RA across both sexes. The PCA plot shows that samples are clustered by region and sex (Figure 2A). The volcano plot illustrates the distribution of DEGs across the combined sexes, revealing that 197 and 209 mRNAs are highly expressed in the SAN and RA, respectively (Figure 2B). SAN or RA-enriched genes exhibit a sex-biased distribution, with 84 sex-common DEGs in the SAN and 129 in the RA (the full DEG list in Supplemental Data Set S2). Figure 2C highlights the top 25 sex-common DEGs in the SAN versus the RA, presenting validated gene markers for the SAN (e.g., TBX3, TBX18, CACNA1D) and the RA (e.g., NPPA, SCN5A, NKX2.5). Figure 2D illustrates the relative expression of genes associated with SAN development and HR regulation in SAN versus RA, demonstrating a predominantly preserved expression pattern between the SAN and RA in both sexes. However, the SAN to RA ratio of several key genes differed between sexes, with SHOX2 and HCN1 being higher expressed only in female SAN compared to RA. Pearson correlation analysis confirms a significant positive correlation between the expression of SAN transcription factors (e.g., TBX3, TBX18) and key pacemaking ion channels (e.g., HCN1, CACNA1D) (Supplemental Data Set S3).

  To explore how these region-specific genes are involved in specific biological functions, we conducted gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). GSEA revealed 287 sex-common Gene Ontology (GO) pathways in SAN and 336 in RA (Figure 3A). Figure 3B illustrates examples of sex-common GO pathways in SAN versus RA. For instance, gene sets involve in pathways of SAN development and extracellular matrix (ECM) organization are enriched in the SAN, while gene sets related to cardiac conduction and heart contraction pathways are enriched in the RA. TBX3 was identified in multiple GO pathways related to SAN development and pacemaking, suggesting its key role in maintaining human SAN function. Figure 3C displays examples of shared enriched genes (fold change FC>1.2) in region-specific GO pathways for SAN and RA. The complete list of sex-biased GO pathways along with the core-enrichment gene list, is provided in Supplemental Data Set S4.


### Region-Specific Sex-biased Gene Expression
  Next, we defined region-specific sex-biased gene expression in SAN and RA. The volcano plot and heat map in Figure 4A show the distribution of sex-biased gene expression in human SAN, with 25 genes significantly higher in females and 17 genes higher in males (the full list of DEGs and their orders in the heat map are provided in Supplemental Data Set S5). As indicated in the volcano plot and heat map, key pacemaking and transcription factor genes, such as HCN1 and TBX3, were highly expressed in female SAN. In contrast, some genes of pro-fibrotic protein and inflammatory cytokines, such as EGR1, IL6, and CXCL8 were highly expressed in male SAN. Ten out of 13 collagen genes showed significantly higher expression in the SAN compared to the RA. However, the expression levels of several collagen isoforms differed between male and female SAN, with COL6A3 and COL14A1 being expressed higher in females and males respectively.

  We also revealed that sex-biased DEGs in RA exhibit distinct region-specific characteristics compared to those in SAN. Specifically, there were only three region-common DEGs expressed higher in males (CXCL2, CXCL8, and PTGS2) and two region-common DEGs expressed higher in females (COL6A3 and ELOVL6) (Figure 4B, Supplemental Data Set S5).

  Our data did not detect significant sex differences in the expressions of autonomic nervous system (ANS) and sex hormonal receptors in the SAN or RA. These include the beta-adrenergic receptors (ADRB1, ADRB2), cholinergic receptor muscarinic 2 (CHRM2), adenosine A1 receptor (A1R), estrogen receptor (ESR1, ESR2, GPER1), testosterone receptor (AR), and progesterone receptor (PGR).

  In addition to sex-biased DEGs, GSEA revealed the sex-biased GO pathways in female (n=335) versus male (n=584) SAN (Supplemental Data Set S6). Figure 5A illustrates that GO pathways for HR regulation, collagen trimer, glucose and lipid metabolic process, and ATP biosynthetic process are enhanced in female SAN. In contrast, GO pathways related to immune response and collagen biosynthetic process are enhanced in male SAN. Some genes, such as HCN1 and IL6, are involved in multiple sex-specific GO pathways, suggesting their potentially crucial roles in sex-specific HR regulation or disease vulnerability in the SAN. Figure 5B presents examples of core-enrichment genes shared in multiple sex-biased GO pathways.

  In RA, GSEA also showed sex-biased enrichment in 170 GO pathways in females versus 226 in males (Supplemental Data Set S7). GO pathways related to collagen trimer, extracellular matrix, glucose metabolic process, and positive regulation of lipid transport are enhanced in females (Supplemental Figure S1). Meanwhile, male RA has enhanced GO pathways in positive regulation of the collagen biosynthetic process, response to fibroblast growth factor, and inflammatory response.

  We further quantitatively compared the normalized sex-bias distribution of genes within specific functional groups: 1) HR regulation and SAN development; 2) ECM structure and organization; and 3) immune response and signaling pathways. The inclusion of genes in these groups was based on corresponding GO pathways (Supplemental Data Set S8). Figure 5C shows that the log2FC (female/male) distribution of genes in group 1 is significantly positively altered (log2FC=0.18, P= 0.0059), indicating a transcriptional increase in HR regulation and SAN development genes in female compared to male SAN. In contrast, there is a negative shift of log2FC (female/male) distribution of group 3 genes (log2FC= −0.1, p= 5.8e-05), indicating that the expression of genes from immune response and signaling GO pathways is elevated in male SAN. However, in RA, groups 1 and 3 genes showed no difference in log2FC distribution between sexes. The group 2 genes are related to ECM proteins, and they showed increased expression in female RA (log2FC= 0.14, p=0.01). These results further demonstrate the regional and sex-different expression pattern for genes related to SAN structure and function in comparison to the RA.

  To validate the Nanostring transcriptomics gene panel results, we performed qPCR for key transcription factors and ion channels (e.g., TBX3, TBX18, SHOX2, NKX2–5, HCN1, HCN4, CACNA1C, CACNA1D, GJA1, SCN5A). Supplemental Table S2 shows the significantly high correlations (Pearson r >0.7 and p<0.01) between qPCR and nCounter gene panel results for all these gene expressions in SAN and RA.


### Ingenuity Pathway Analysis
  We conducted IPA analysis to investigate the mechanisms underlying regional and sex-biased gene expression patterns as well as predict the regulatory influence of sex hormones on gene expression in the SAN and RA. Figure 6 illustrates the relative gene expression in female versus male and IPA-predicted interactions among major sex hormones and receptors, ANS receptors, key transcription factors, and ion channels related to SAN pacemaking and conduction function. Additionally, based on gene expression data, IPA predicted sex hormone level differences in female versus male SAN. Females showed significantly higher levels of beta-estradiol (E2) (z-score = 1.24, p < 0.01) and progesterone (z-score = 1.83, p < 0.01), but lower testosterone (z-score = −0.32, p < 0.01) than males.

  IPA revealed that elevated E2 levels in females may regulate HR and SAN pacemaking-conduction function through several mechanisms: 1) Higher E2 levels are predicted to increase SAN-specific transcriptional factor TBX3 expression, which in turn enhances the expression of pacemaker ion channel genes (e.g., HCN1) while suppressing atrial cardiomyocyte genes (e.g., GJA5, GJA1, and SCN5A). 2) E2 may upregulate genes that encode pacemaker ion channels, including HCN1, CACNA1D, CACNA1G, and SLC8A1, thereby increase the major contributors (If, ICa,L, ICa,T, and INa/Ca) to SAN diastolic depolarization. 3) E2 may modulate ANS receptors, such as CHRM2, ADORA1, and ADRB2, potentially alter SAN responsiveness to neurotransmitters, thereby contributing to sex-specific HR.

  Supplemental Figures S2 and S3 further demonstrate sex-biased expression patterns of major transcription factors, sex-hormone and ANS receptors, ion channels, inflammatory factors, collagens, and other ECM proteins in the SAN and RA, highlighting a region-specific sexual dimorphism in gene expression.


### Computational Models
  To investigate the functional role of the identified sex-biased expression of pacemaking ion channels, we utilized computational human SAN models. To set up the female SAN cell model, we adjusted four ion currents (If, ICa,L, INa/Ca and INa) with the female-to-male ratios of 1.3, 1.1, 1.2 and 0.9, based on gene expression results (detailed model modification are provided in the Supplemental Methods). To ensure the clinical relevance of the simulations, we applied two criteria for the baseline models: 1) the clinically reported ranges of intrinsic sinus cycle length (SCL) in healthy adult males (645–840ms) and females (594–778ms)9, 10; 2) the estimated female-to-male SCL ratio at 85–96%, which was calculated based on clinical reported resting HR in males and females6–10, 34 (Supplemental Figure S4 and Table S3).

  The higher If and ICa, L in the female SAN model resulted in greater slow diastolic depolarization (SDD) rates at the initial phase (SDD1= 50mV/s vs 48mV/s) and the middle phase (SDD2= 26mV/s vs 21mV/s) compared to the male (Figure 7A), leading to shorter SCLs in the female than male (722ms vs 813ms), and the female-to-male SCL ratio of 0.89.

  We further simulated the sex-specific SAN cell model at altered ICa, L (50% – 200%), and If (50% – 200%) levels to test sex-specific susceptibility to bradycardias/sinus arrest and tachycardia/IST (SCL >500ms, HR >120 bpm)35 (Figure 7B). The simulations showed that the female SAN model is more vulnerable to IST, while the male SAN is more vulnerable to pacemaking failure (sinus arrest). Figure 7B shows shorter SCLs in female than male models at all matching ICa, L and If levels, and the female model reached the IST threshold at lower ICa, L and If levels than the male model (action potential (AP) features are presented in Supplemental Data Sets S9– S10). The male but not female SAN was arrested when ICa, L was set to 60% and If was less than 160%. In contrast, the pacemaking in the female model was arrested only when lowering ICa, L to 50%. The models also indicated that the same proportion of ICa, L and If suppression could lead to greater SCL prolongation in males versus females. For example, 60% ICa, L and 160% If caused SCL to prolong to 2108ms (259% of baseline SCL) in the male and 1059ms (147% of baseline SCL) in the female model (APs are presented in Supplemental Figure S5).

  We also assessed the functional impact of sex-specific RA ion channel expressions by incorporating them into a human RA biophysical model. Only ion channels showing ≥10% sex-biased expression differences, including CACNA1C (ICa,L), KCNQ1 & KCNE1 (IKs), and KCNH2 (IKr), were considered. Supplemental Figure S6 shows representative RA cell AP traces at a 750 ms pacing cycle length. Female baseline RA cells exhibited similar resting potential, AP amplitude, and maximum upstroke velocity, but slightly shorter AP duration at 80% repolarization (APD80 = 294ms vs 305ms), compared to males. Sex-biased expression of cardiac ion channels in SAN and RA is detailed in Supplemental Data Set 11.


## Discussion
  Sex differences in SAN function and arrhythmia occurrence have been noted for over a century6–10, yet the mechanisms underlying these differences remain elusive. In the present study, we provide the first evidence of sexual dimorphism in gene transcription within the human SAN. Specifically, we found that sex-biased gene sets were enriched in the following GO pathways: (1) SAN development and HR regulation genes, such as TBX3 and HCN1, and cardiac metabolic regulation proteins, such as ADIPOQ and LEP were upregulated in females; (2) immune regulation and inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6, CXCL2 and CXCL8, and pro-fibrotic markers, such as EGR1 and FAP were elevated in males. In addition to the SAN, female RA tissue showed sex-specific enrichment of gene sets in GO pathways involved in ECM structure, lipid transfer, and glucose metabolic process, whereas male RA gene set enrichments showed parallel results to those observed in SAN. These regional and sex-biased transcriptional features may offer mechanistic insights into the sex differences in HR and the prevalence of atrial arrhythmias.

  First, the higher expression of TBX3, a SAN-specific transcriptional factor, may contribute to faster intrinsic HR in females. TBX336 along with other transcription factors, such as TBX1837, play a crucial role in SAN development and maintenance by regulating pacemaker cell differentiation and pacemaking gene expression. Hoogaars et al.38 demonstrated through loss- and gain-of-function genetic studies that TBX3 regulates the SAN-specific pacemaker gene expression program and phenotype. IPA analysis of the sex-specific SAN gene expression indicated that TBX3 could upregulate multiple pacemaking ion channels, including HCN1 and HCN4, with HCN4 potentially also being influenced by TBX18 (Figure 6). The opposing expression patterns of TBX3 and TBX18 in the female and male SAN may explain why only HCN1, but not HCN4, exhibits sex-differentiated expression in the human SAN.

  The sexual dimorphism of the HCN1 isoform of the pacemaker If channel may serve as a specific ion channel mechanism for faster HR in females. Among three HCN isoforms and other ion channels assessed in this study, only HCN1 (SAN-specific isoform at the protein level22) was expressed significantly higher in females compared to males (Figures 4 and 6). A study by Feske et al.39 showed that HCN1 knockout leads to 30% HR slowing and sinus node dysfunction (SND) in mice. Recently, Yu et al.40 reported a clinical case linking a novel HCN1 missense variant with SND, and their simulation result showed a 50% decrease in SCL by inhibiting HCN1-If current. These studies confirm the key role of HCN1 in SAN pacemaking, supporting our hypothesis that higher HCN1 transcription in female SAN may lead to faster HR.

  Furthermore, our data showed that CACNA1D was significantly higher in the SAN versus RA in both sexes (Figure 2), and it was indicated as a core-enrichment gene (FC of female/male =1.57) in multiple GO pathways related to HR regulation enhanced in female SAN (Figure 5). This finding is consistent with a previous study on rat SAN, which reported higher CACNA1D in females17. CACNA1D encodes Cav1.3, an isoform of L-type Ca2+ current, which is a major contributor to the depolarization phase of SAN AP41. In humans, CACNA1D variants have been reported to be associated with SND42–44, but the sex-specificity of the SND risk related to CACNA1D has not been reported.

  Our IPA analysis also suggested that the Na+/Ca²+ exchanger (NCX1), encoded by SLC8A1, as another key contributor to SAN automaticity45, could be upregulated by E₂ and TBX3 in the female SAN. This is supported by findings showing that E₂ increased NCX1 expressions and INa/Ca in female but not male iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes 46. To date, no SLC8A1 gene variants have been linked to SND in clinical studies.

  Our finding of sex-biased expression of pacemaking genes in the human SAN may partly reflect the sex hormonal differences between sexes (Figure 6). Sex hormones, such as estradiol (E2) may affect intrinsic HR by increasing the transcription of pacemaking genes in the SAN, as the CACNA1D data shown in a female mouse model18. Our IPA analysis predicted higher levels of E2 and progesterone, but lower testosterone levels in females compared to the male SAN, and identified HCN1 as a shared target for both TBX3 and E2. Since E2 has been shown to upregulate TBX3 expression in stem cells and cardiac myoblast cultures47, 48, elevated E2 levels in the female SAN may similarly enhance TBX3 transcription, thereby increasing the HCN1 expression and accelerating the intrinsic HR.

  Our study also suggests E2-based hormone replacement therapy in females may help preserve SAN function by preventing downregulation of pacemaking gene expression (TBX3 and HCN1), reducing inflammation, and mitigating fibrosis remodeling, hallmarks of failing SND SAN24. A recent Baik et al. study49, analyzed records from 10-million senior Medicare women, found that nonoral E₂-therapy alone was associated with significant reductions in mortality, heart failure, and AF risk, supporting the potential of E₂ treatment in protecting against SND.

  Sex hormones are also known to influence HR and HR variability by modulating the sympathetic-vagal balance tone50 and/or directly regulating the cardiac ANS receptor expressions51. Rosano et al.52 reported that paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardias are more frequent and prolonged when women are in the premenstrual phase, characterized by lower E2 and higher progesterone levels. Burke et al.9 found that resting SCL in adult females was longer during the menstrual phase, when E2 levels in plasma are lower, compared to the follicular or luteal phases when E2 is higher. However, after the double ANS blockade, this difference disappeared, suggesting that female sex hormones may regulate HR through ANS modulation. In our study, although we observed a trend toward higher ANS receptor transcription in the female SAN (Figure 6), differences in ANS and sex hormone receptor expression between sexes did not reach statistical significance. This aligns with Lopes-Ramos et al.53, who reported that sex hormone receptors were not differentially expressed between males and females across human tissues, including left ventricle tissue, though hormone receptors had sex-biased downstream gene targets. Additionally, clinical54 and animal55 studies have demonstrated sex-specific HR response to the beta-adrenergic agonist, isoproterenol, which may also be modulated by E2 level. This suggests that SAN pacemaking cardiomyocytes may exhibit sex-specific differences in the ANS receptor signaling pathway activities and the HR responses to ANS agonists, even when ANS and sex hormone receptor expression levels are comparable.

  Our findings on sexual dimorphism in the SAN pacemaking genes may provide a novel mechanism underlying the >90% female predominance in IST, which is a persistent SAN tachycardia characterized by resting heart rates >100 bpm, primarily affecting young females without structural cardiac diseases. Clinical studies suggest multiple possible IST mechanisms, including an imbalance between sympathetic and parasympathetic ANS inputs56 and altered ANS receptor signaling57 58. However, IST often appears to be involved with increased intrinsic SAN automaticity with an unclear mechanism. We propose that sexual dimorphism in the TBX3-HCN1 and CACNA1D core pacemaking gene pathways could be an intrinsic mechanism driving IST in females. Using sex-specific SAN models, we found a lower tachycardia threshold in females by simulating conditions such as high sympathetic tone with increased If and/or ICa, L currents. These results align with the clinical efficacy of Ivabradine, an If inhibitor targeting both HCN1 and HCN4 isoforms, in managing IST56. Notably, sex-specific efficacy data for Ivabradine in IST treatment are not reported, likely due to the female predominance in tachycardia patients.

  The computational findings on the increased SAN arrest sensitivity in males (Figure 7B) also provide a mechanistic insight into the higher risk of SND in males supported by sex-specific pacemaker implantation rate12. Although SND prevalence has been reported higher in women than men59, 60, these studies did not consider the larger aging population of females. Irnich12 calculated the relative risk of pacemaker implantation in patients reported by the Nowak et al. study13, adjusted for German age-matched population distributions, suggesting that males may have twice the risk of SND compared to females. Since the male model exhibited greater prolongation of SCL and a higher propensity for sinus arrest than the female model when decreasing ICa,L, the male SAN is more prone to pacemaking and conduction impairment during disease and/or aging-induced ICa,L reduction61. These findings are further supported by ex-vivo animal studies showing that the Ca2+ blocker nifedipine caused a greater SCL inhibition in males compared to female rat SAN17.

  Clinical studies have not reported consistent findings on sex-specific differences in non-diseased atrial electrophysiology, including similar refractory periods62. Pecha et al.63 using microelectrode recordings from human RA trabeculae surgically obtained from patients (67 ± 10 years), reported similar APD₉₀ (315ms vs 320ms) in male and female non-AF samples. Similarly, our human RA cell models also showed comparable AP parameters between sexes due to minor sex differences in RA ion channels expressions, with similar APD₈₀ in male and female models (Figure S6). Further inclusion of atrial samples from AF patients into our experimental-computational framework would allow comprehensively evaluate sex-specific ion channel remodeling in AF, and their roles in AF susceptibility64.

  Furthermore, sex-biased expression of inflammatory cytokines and pro-fibrotic proteins may contribute to the higher risk of SND and AF in males3. We found that genes such as EGR1, IL6, CXCL2, and CXCL8 showed higher transcription in male SAN, with multiple GO pathways enriched for immune response and collagen biosynthetic processes (Figure 4–5). CXCL8 and CXCL2 were also highly expressed in male RA, with significant enrichment across multiple male-biased GO pathways involved in the immune response. As the donor’s hearts did not have a known history of inflammation or infection, these gene enrichments in immune response and pro-fibrotic pathways likely represent sexual dimorphism in male SAN and RA. Inflammatory cytokines can serve as pro-fibrotic factors that drive fibrosis remodeling under stress and have been shown to be elevated in AF65. For example, EGR1 is an transcription factor involved in inflammation and fibrogenesis66, 67, which may play a role in AF onset68. Plasma levels of IL6 and CXCL8 have been found to independently predict post-operative AF69, while the level of IL6 can also predict symptomatic SND in middle-aged patients70. In mouse SAN, active inflammation may also directly downregulate HCN4 and lead to SND71. Thus, the elevated transcription levels of inflammatory cytokines and inflammation-regulated pro-fibrotic pathways in males may predispose them to SAN impairments and a higher AF risk in pathological conditions.

  Our current study revealed sex-biased regional transcription patterns of collagen isoforms and other ECM proteins in both SAN and RA (Supplemental Figures S2–S3). Most collagen isoforms, along with elastin (ELN) and fibronectin (FN1), showed higher transcription in both male and female SAN compared to RA, consistent with our previous proteo-transcriptomics findings in nonfailing human heart72. Notably, histological studies on the human SAN did not report sex-differences in SAN structure or fibrotic content in age-matched donor hearts73–77. Similarly, we didn’t find sex-differences in the total SAN ECM-related gene expression (Figure 5C). However, we identified distinct sex-biased SAN expression patterns for specific collagen isoforms, such as elevated COL6A3 in females and COL14A1 in males. The sex-biased ECM compositions and pathways could contribute to varying fibrosis remodeling under specific disease conditions29 or with aging across sexes78. Further research is warranted to elucidate the physiological and pathophysiological roles of sex-specific ECM proteins in the human SAN.

  Our study also revealed sex-biased gene set enrichment in GO pathways involved in metabolic processes. Metabolic pathways are known to differ between sexes and are associated with varying cardiomyopathy risks79, 80 potentially mediated by sex hormones81. Women have higher levels of adiponectin and leptin in serum, both enhance fatty acid, glucose, and lipid metabolic processes82. In alignment with these findings, we observed enhanced gene sets such as ADIPOQ, LEP and IGF-1 in the female SAN (Figure 5). Similarly, glucose and lipid metabolic pathways were also upregulated in the female RA (Figure S1). This increased metabolic activity may support the higher energy demands of SAN and RA cells at faster HR in females. Moreover, glucose metabolic pathways play a critical role in maintaining SAN pacemaking and conduction function83. In male mice, cardiac-specific suppression of IGF-1/insulin signaling downregulated HCN1 and HCN4 expression, leading to SND84, however sex-dependent effects of metabolic pathways on SAN function were not reported.

  Our study has several limitations. First, the human donor heart sample size is limited and potentially influenced by patient lifestyle factors that may affect gene and protein expression. However, we implemented strict inclusion criteria to exclude samples with cardiac diseases, arrhythmias, chronic inflammation, or infections, reducing potential biases despite the small sample size. Second, our gene profiling was conducted on bulk tissue. Additionally, our analysis was based on a targeted gene panel instead of full transcriptomic profiling, since this study is focused on genes associated with HR regulation, inflammation, fibrosis, and metabolism pathways. Future research should explore compartment- and cell-specific gene and protein features in male versus female three-dimensional human SAN pacemaking-conduction complexes. Moreover, functional validation is needed to assess the effects of If -HCN1/4 and ICa,L-Cav1.2/Cav1.3 ion channel blockers on male and female ex-vivo human SAN preparations.


## Conclusion
  The sexual dimorphism in genes responsible for SAN pacemaking, metabolism, and ECM structure as well as inflammation and fibrosis remodeling contribute to sex-specific differences in SAN function and vulnerability to tachy-brady arrhythmias. The high expressions of TBX3 and HCN1 in female SAN may explain the faster intrinsic HR and higher susceptibility to IST in females compared to males. These findings highlight the need for sex-specific therapeutic strategies in SAN arrhythmia management, including TBX3- and/or HCN1-targeted therapies and E2-based hormonal replacement therapy. Future personalized antiarrhythmic therapies should integrate cardiac region- and sex-specific gene expression patterns while considering sex hormone levels to optimize treatment efficacy.
