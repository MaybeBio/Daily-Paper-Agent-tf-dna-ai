# Cold atmospheric plasma drives USP49/HDAC3 axis mediated ferroptosis as a novel therapeutic strategy in endometrial cancer via reinforcing lactylation dependent p53 expression


## Background
  Endometrial cancer ranks among the most common gynecological cancers, with increasing rates of incidence and death. Cold atmospheric plasma (CAP) has become a promising novel therapeutic approach for cancer treatment. Nevertheless, the specific impact of CAP on endometrial cancer remains inadequately characterized.


## Objectives
  This study aimed to investigate the effect of CAP on the progression of endometrial cancer and reveal its specific regulatory mechanisms.


## Methods
  Colony formation, EdU, wound-healing, and transwell assay were used to detect the effect of CAP on endometrial cancer progression. Proteomics is employed to identify potential targets and signaling pathways through which CAP impacts endometrial cancer cells. MDA, lipid ROS, and JC-1 MMP assays were used to detect ferroptosis. Immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry, co-immunoprecipitation, immunofluorescence co-localization, and molecular docking were used to analyze USP49 and HDAC3 interactions. The tumor xenografts model determined that CAP inhibits endometrial cancer growth in vivo.


## Results
  This study observed a significant inhibitory effect of CAP on the proliferation and migration of endometrial cancer cells and reported for the first time that CAP induces ferroptosis in endometrial cancer cells. Mechanistically, CAP activated the transcription of p53 by modulating HDAC3 mediated the histone H3K18 lactylation, resulting in upregulation of p53 driving cell ferroptosis. The interaction between USP49 and HDAC3 was validated through mass spectrometry and co-immunoprecipitation experiments. The regulation of HDAC3 by CAP is contingent upon USP49, wherein the down-regulation of USP49 augments the ubiquitination of HDAC3, consequently diminishing its protein stability. Furthermore, animal models with transplanted tumors corroborated the inhibitory impact of CAP on endometrial cancer in vivo.


## Conclusions
  Our findings illustrate the suppressive effect of CAP treatment on endometrial cancer and uncover a novel regulatory mechanism mediated by CAP. Specifically, CAP modulates the ferroptosis pathway through the HDAC3/H3K18la/p53 axis, presenting a novel therapeutic approach for endometrial cancer treatment.

## Introduction
  Endometrial cancer is a common gynecologic cancer globally, with increasing morbidity and mortality rates [1–3]. Currently, the primary treatment modality for endometrial cancer is surgical intervention, which is often supplemented by radiotherapy and/or chemotherapy based on the histopathological evaluation of the tumor and the clinical presentation of the patients [4, 5]. Furthermore, immunotherapy targeting specific molecular sites is increasingly being adopted as a standard therapeutic approach [6]. Despite advancements in the treatment of endometrial cancer, adjuvant therapeutic options for patients remain constrained, and there has been no significant improvement in 5-year survival rates [7]. Therefore, finding new treatments for endometrial cancer is crucial.

  CAP is a blend of photons, electrons, ions, and free radicals generated through the ionization of a gas at ambient pressure and temperature [8, 9]. Previous studies have demonstrated that CAP possesses antimicrobial and wound-healing-promoting properties and can stimulate stem and progenitor cell proliferation [10–12]. Furthermore, CAP represents a promising cancer therapy demonstrating substantial anti-cancer efficacy across a diverse range of tumors, including lung cancer, pancreatic cancer, gliomas, and various gynecological malignancies, notably ovarian, breast, and cervical cancers [13–18]. CAP’s anti-cancer effects are primarily driven by ROS (O3, •OH, H2O2, •O2-, 1O2) and RNS (NO2-, •ONOO-) that induce apoptosis in cancer cells [19]. Furthermore, CAP can also trigger programmed cell death pathways like autophagy and pyroptosis in tumor cells [20, 21]. Nevertheless, research investigating the impact of CAP on endometrial cancer remains limited. Furthermore, the potential mechanisms underlying CAP’s anti-cancer effects on endometrial cancer have yet to be elucidated.

  Ferroptosis represents a type of programmed cell death that depends on iron, marked by lipid peroxidation induced by ROS and reduced membrane antioxidant activity [22]. Since its discovery, ferroptosis has been identified as a crucial element in the development of cardiovascular diseases, metabolic disorders, neurological conditions, and particularly cancer [23]. However, cancer cells vary significantly in their susceptibility to ferroptosis when dysregulated [24]. For the endometrial cancer, the ferroptosis pathway is regarded as a promising therapeutic approach [25]. Our previous study demonstrated that CAP could inhibit non-small cell lung cancer activity by triggering ferroptosis [26]. However, the exact connection between CAP and ferroptosis in endometrial cancer cells remains undefined.

  ROS are associated with ferroptosis and CAP’s antitumor properties. We hypothesize that CAP may inhibit endometrial cancer cell activity by causing ferroptosis. Prior research has demonstrated that p53 can induce cellular ferroptosis in the presence of ROS stress [27]. In this research, we cultured endometrial cancer cells using a CAP-activated medium and observed that CAP markedly inhibited cell proliferation, migration, and invasion while simultaneously promoting ferroptosis in tumor cells. Furthermore, we explored the precise mechanism underlying CAP-induced ferroptosis activation in endometrial cancer cells. Our findings suggest that the USP49/HDAC3 signaling axis up-regulates p53 expression by enhancing H3K18 lactylation, thereby inducing ferroptosis. This research elucidated how CAP enhances ferroptosis and provided valuable perspectives on possible therapeutic strategies for endometrial cancer.


## Materials and methods

### CAP treatment cell conditions
  CAP-generating equipment comprises a copper plane electrode, a fiberglass dielectric layer, and a copper hexagonal mesh electrode. Waveforms were recorded using an oscilloscope to validate the voltage and current values. Gas flowed three minutes before the atmosphere plasma treatment to stabilize the chamber conditions. During the experiment, a temperature sensor monitored the chamber’s temperature and humidity while the discharge voltage was set to a peak-to-peak value of 8 kV, resulting in a discharge power of 9 W at a frequency of 8 kHz. Cells underwent CAP treatment 0s, 20s, 40s, and 60s according to the time gradient in sequence and then incubated in the incubator for 2 h before being switched to a new complete medium.


### Optical emission spectroscopy and electron spin resonance spectroscopy
  Optical emission spectroscopy (OES) was employed to detect the key excited reactive species formed by atmospheric plasma across the 200–800 nm wavelength spectrum. Electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy spin trapping was employed to detect •ONOO- and 1O2 in the culture medium following atmospheric plasma treatment, with •ONOO- intensity assessed using 2,7-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCFDA) as a probe. TEMP (Sigma–Aldrich Co., Ltd) was employed as a spin trap for 1O2. At the same time, L-His (Shanghai Secondary Military Science Academy) served as a scavenger to verify that the ESR signal originated from 1O2.


### Measurement of the physicochemical traits of solutions treated with plasma
  The ORP and pH of the culture medium were promptly assessed following plasma treatment (0s, 20s, 40s, and 60s) using the multimeter Redox and pH (Mettler-Toledo, Switzerland). The electrical conductivity of the cell culture medium treated with CAP was quantified using an electrical conductivity meter. An O3 detector (Ningbo, China) was used to examine the O3 level. The fluorescence intensity of terephthalic acid, excited at 310 nm and emitting at 425 nm, was used to measure the concentration of •OH. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) was quantified with a Hydrogen Peroxide Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). NO2- concentrations were measured spectrophotometrically. Nitro blue tetrazolium (Solarbio, Beijing, China) was utilized to detect •O2- generated by CAP, with absorbance measured at 580 nm.


### Tissue specimens
  We used 9 human untreated endometrial cancer tissues obtained from The Third Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (Zhengzhou, China). Pathological or cytological evidence confirmed that patients had endometrial adenocarcinoma. The use of clinical samples with patient consent was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (Approval Number: 2020-133-01 ).


### Cell lines and cell culture
  Procell Life Science & Technology Company from Wuhan, China, supplied the HEC-1B and Ishikawa human endometrial carcinoma cell lines. Human endometrial cell (EEC) was purchased from the Keycell Company (Wuhan, China). The culture conditions for HEC-1B and Ishikawa cells included incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in MEM and DMEM (Gibco, NY, USA), respectively, with the addition of 10% FBS (Gibco, NY, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (NCM Biotech, Suzhou, China). The reagent used to inhibit ferroptosis was ferrostatin-1 (MCE, Shanghai, China) treated with 5µM, and to induce ferroptosis was erastin(MCE, Shanghai, China) treated with 5µM.

### Colony formation assay
  A plate colony formation assay quantitatively evaluated cell proliferation capacity. Each well of a 6-well plate initially received 800 cells, which were then incubated for roughly two weeks. During that time, the medium was routinely replaced until conspicuous colony formation was observed. Subsequently, the plate was delicately rinsed with PBS, fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and treated with a 0.1% crystal violet stain. The final step involved counting the colonies to determine the cells’ proliferation potential.


### EdU and cell growth curve
  Following the instructions, we used the EdU assay (Beyotime, China) to assess cell proliferation. A density of 5,000 cells per well was used for CCK-8 assays in 96 well plates. Add 10 µl of CCK-8 solution to 100 µl medium, incubate for 1.5 h at 37 °C, and measure absorbance at 450 nm.


### Wound healing, transwell migration, and invasion assays
  The capacity for cell migration was assessed using the wound-healing assay. Firstly, cells experiencing rapid growth were collected and placed into 6-well plates. After the cells achieved 90% confluence, a sterile 100 µl pipet tip was employed to create a consistent straight scratch in the middle of the well. The scratches were photographed at the start and for 48 h using an inverted fluorescence microscope.

  For transwell migration tests, 5 × 105 cells were added to the upper compartment with serum-free medium, and the lower compartment included 550 µl of media supplemented with 12% FBS. After 24 h, cells were rinsed with PBS, fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 40 min.

  For the invasion assays, 5 × 105 cells were placed into inserts containing Matrige and incubated for 48 h.


### Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) staining and immunohistochemistry (IHC)
  In HE experiments, tissue samples were conserved in 10% formalin with a neutral buffer, encased in paraffin, and cut into 5 μm slices before being stained with hematoxylin and eosin.

  For IHC experiments, paraffin-embedded tissues were subjected to heating at 60 °C for 4 h, deparaffinized, and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval. They were then blocked with 5% BSA for 30 min. The primary antibody was incubated for an entire night at 4 °C, followed by a two-hour incubation with secondary antibodies and DAB treatment.


### Malondialdehyde (MDA) assay
  The Malondialdehyde (MDA) level was measured using an MDA Assay Kit (Cat. No. S0131S, Beyotime, China), where MDA interacted with TBA to produce a pink chromogen exhibiting an absorbance at 532 nm. Whole-cell lysates were obtained by washing the cells with PBS, followed by lysis in RIPA buffer. As a next step, the supernatant was obtained, and protein concentration was quantified using the BCA Kit. The cell lysate supernatant and the MDA detection agent were heated for 10 min at 100 °C. The 200µL of the mixture was dispensed into 96-well plates, and its absorbance at 532 nm was assessed with a microplate reader.


### Evaluation of lipid peroxidation using BODIPY
  BDP 581/591 C11 serves as a fluorescent probe for identifying lipid peroxidation, and its fluorescence changes from red to green after reacting with free radicals around lipids. CAP-treated cells were incubated in 6-well plates at 37 °C for 24 h. The cells were rinsed twice with the fresh medium before a 30-minute incubation with the working solution. After two washes with HBSS, the cells were examined using fluorescence microscopy.


### Mitochondrial membrane potential Measurement(MMP)
  Ferroptosis cells usually exhibit mitochondrial swelling with reduced cristae and dissipation of mitochondrial membrane potential. JC-1 generates green fluorescence as a monomer in the mitochondrial matrix and red fluorescence as a polymer when the membrane potential is high. MMP alterations were measured using a JC-1 MMP assay kit (Solarbio, China). Cells were grown on 6-well plates at 37 °C for 24 h. After removing the medium, 1 ml of JC-1 staining solution was applied. Cells were incubated at 37 °C for 20 min, followed by removing the supernatant and two washes with JC-1 staining buffer. Subsequently, the cells were looked at with a fluorescence microscope, with the MMP represented by the ratio of red to green fluorescence strength.


### Tumor xenografts model
  Female BALB/c nude mice, 4–5 weeks, were sourced from GemPharmatech (Jiangsu, China). All animal experiments have been approved by the Animal Experiment Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (Approval Number: 2020-133-01 ). The mice were split into three random groups, each containing five mice. Following a 3-day acclimation period, a suspension of 1 × 106 HEC-1B cells was injected subcutaneously into one side of each nude mouse. Every three days, groups of mice received injections of PBS, CAP-treated PBS, or CAP-treated PBS + Fer-1. Tumor sizes were assessed every third day from implantation. After approximately 4 weeks, the tumors were removed, and their weight was recorded.


### Co-IP
  Co-IP experiments for plasmid-transfected cells were performed in IP lysis buffer (P0013, Beyotime, China) with added protease inhibitors (P1005, Beyotime, China), and incubated on ice for 30 min. Anti-Flag affinity purification gel (20584ES03, Yeasen, China) was used to incubate the total proteins extracted overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, the mixture was centrifugated at 6000 g for 30 s to discard the supernatant, A 2x loading buffer was added, and boiled for 5 min before conducting Western blot experiments.


### Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP)
  For the Chip experiment, We added 1% formaldehyde to the culture medium at ambient temperature for 10 min. The reaction was halted by adding a 10x glycine solution (P2078, Beyotime, China) and incubating for 10 min. Subsequently, the cells were rinsed twice with PBS. Sonicated nuclear extracts were immunoprecipitated with IgG or Anti-lactylated H3k18 antibody overnight at 4°C. DNA was obtained by DNA product purification kits (DP204, TIANGEN, China), and immunoprecipitated DNA samples were then analyzed by qPCR. Primers used for qPCR analysis of the p53 promoter region: F:5’-TGCTCAAGACTGGCGCTAAA-3’; R:5’-GGAGCTTACCCAATCCAGGG-3’.


### Silver staining
  After electrophoresis, gels were immersed in 100 ml of fixative solution (50% EtOH, 10% ACOH) for 40 min, followed by a wash with 30% ethanol for 10 min. The gel was washed with pure water for 10 min, treated with a silver staining sensitizer for 2 min, and then exposed to silver nitrate for 10 min. Subsequently, the silver-staining chromogen solution was added and incubated for 10 min. Finally, Silver staining termination solution was added and shaken on a shaker for 10 min at ambient temperature.


### Immunofluorescence (IF)
  The cell climbing slices were placed into the 24-well plate, and each well was inoculated with 5 × 104 cells for 24 h. In the next step, cells were immobilized with 4% paraformaldehyde for 40 min, followed by three PBS washes. Cells were permeabilized with PBS and 0.1% TritonX-100 (T8200, Solarbio, China), then blocked with 1% BSA for 2 h. The primary antibody was incubated overnight at 4°C. Slides were incubated for 2 h the following day with fluorochrome-conjugated secondary antibodies protected from light. Coverslips were mounted on glass slides using an anti-fade mounting medium containing DAPI (P0131-5 ml, Beyotime, China), then dried and imaged with a laser confocal microscope.


### Statistical analysis
  This section presents the data as mean ± standard deviations (SD). Two-tailed Student’s T-tests were employed to assess the significance of differences between groups. A one-way ANOVA was used to analyze data from three or more groups. Pearson’s or Spearman’s rank correlation tests were used to analyze correlation studies. Overall survival (OS) was calculated using the Kaplan-Meier method. GraphPad Prism 9.0 software was used to perform the statistical analysis. In all experiments, P < 0.05 was considered significant.


## Results

### Analysis of the physicochemical properties of reactive species in cell culture medium treated with CAP
  A schematic diagram of the CAP generator utilized in the current experiment is presented in Fig. 1A. As shown in Fig. 1B, surface plasmons were formed inside the hexagonal mesh elements. The discharge voltage and current waveforms were captured using an oscilloscope, as shown in Fig. 1C. At a discharge power of 9 W, Optical Emission Spectroscopy (OES) was employed to detect active species in the CAP over a range of wavelengths range of 200 to 800 nm. The findings revealed a substantial presence of nitrogen-containing molecules and oxygen atoms in the CAP, which possess the potential to synthesize further ROS and RNS (Fig. 1D). To assess the physicochemical properties of the CAP-treated media, we initially employed a multimeter to gauge the redox potential and pH of the medium following various CAP treatment durations (0s, 20s, 40s, and 60s). Our results indicate a decrease in pH alongside increases in temperature and conductivity (Fig. 1E-G). In a solution, the Oxidation-Reduction Potential (ORP) reflects the overall oxidative capacity of various substances. As the plasma treatment duration increased (0 s, 20s, 40s, and 60s), the ORP of the medium rose from 321 mV to 381 mV post-treatment (Fig. 1H). This observation suggests the generation of a significant quantity of oxidizing species. Subsequently, We examined NO2-, •ONOO-, 1O2, H2O2, •OH, O3, and •O2- levels to investigate ROS changes in CAP-treated media. The results demonstrate that their levels have risen (Fig. 1I-O). These alterations in ROS are congruent with the previously discussed ORP findings. The results above demonstrate a substantial increase in ROS generation within the liquid medium following CAP treatment.

### CAP suppresses the proliferation, migration, and invasion of endometrial cancer cells in vitro
  We examined the role of CAP in endometrial cancer by assessing its effects on the proliferation, migration, and invasion of HEC-1B and Ishikawa cells. As demonstrated in Fig. 2A, the CCK8 assay revealed a gradual decline in the activity of both cells over the duration of CAP treatment. Interestingly, we used the CCK8 assay to examine the activity of EEC, and the findings showed that CAP had no significant impact on the activity of normal endometrial cells (Fig. 2B). Subsequently, we conducted colony formation and EdU assays to clarify CAP’s role in endometrial cancer cell proliferation. Consistent with our expectations, we observed a considerable reduction in colony numbers (Fig. 2C, D), and the proportion of EdU/Hoechst double-labeled nuclei progressively decreased in endometrial cells with prolonged CAP treatment (Fig. 2E, F). In terms of cell migratory and invasive capabilities, the wound-healing assay results showed a significant decrease in the mobility of endometrial cells (Fig. 2G, H). Similarly, in the transwell assay, the ability of these cells to migrate and invade was diminished with extended durations of CAP treatment (Fig. 2I-L). Collectively, our findings indicate that CAP markedly inhibits the proliferation, migration, and invasion of endometrial cancer cells in vitro.

### CAP facilitates ferroptosis signal pathway in endometrial cancer cells
  To investigate how CAP influences endometrial cancer progression, we conducted proteomic analysis on CAP-treated HEC-1B cells. A comprehensive proteomic analysis identified 7,252 proteins. The differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) are depicted in the volcano and heatmap plots presented in Fig. 3A and B. Among these proteins, 433 exhibited significant changes in expression levels, with 80 proteins up-regulated and 353 down-regulated in CAP-treated cells versus control cells. Functional enrichment analysis indicated that these DEPs were predominantly associated with several tumor-related pathways. Specifically, the analysis of Gene Ontology (GO) terms revealed that the pathways of interest encompassed the DNA replication checkpoint, cell proliferation, and cell cycle checkpoint (Fig. 3C). Additionally, in the context of the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways, notable enrichment was found in areas related to replication and repair, resistance to antineoplastic agents, and ferroptosis (Fig. 3D). Among these pathways, we have identified significant enrichment of ferroptosis signaling pathways. Prior investigations have identified a significant connection between ferroptosis disruptions and endometrial cancer [25]. To explore the connection between ferroptosis and CAP in endometrial cancer, we conducted a Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) to generate an enrichment map of the KEGG ferroptosis signaling pathway, comparing CAP-treated and untreated groups. The analysis indicated significant upregulation of the ferroptosis pathway in the CAP-treated group (Fig. 3E). Subsequently, we queried the FerrDb database for ferroptosis-related proteins and illustrated the overlap with differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) using a Venn diagram (Fig. 3F). To investigate which ferroptosis genes are altered in normal and endometrial cancer tissues, we used mRNA sequencing results from 545 endometrial cancer tissues and 177 normal tissues from the TCGA and GTEX databases to depict the differential expression of these genes in endometrial cancer versus normal tissues. We identified 22 ferroptosis -related genes that were significantly altered. (Fig. S1A).

  Therefore, we hypothesize that CAP may regulate the ferroptosis process in endometrial cancer cells. We examined changes in ferroptosis-related marker proteins to validate this hypothesis in cells treated with CAP. Our study demonstrates that CAP treatment significantly inhibits ferroptosis-related proteins such as ACLS3, SLC7A11, FTH1, and GPX4, with the ferroptosis inducer RSL3 further reducing their expression (Fig. S1B). Interestingly, our investigation also revealed that RSL3 augmented CAP-induced ferroptosis in endometrial cancer cells, as evidenced by mitochondrial membrane potential measurements (Fig. 3G, H). To further elucidate the impact of CAP on ferroptosis, we observed that CAP facilitated the accumulation of malondialdehyde (MDA), a by-product of lipid peroxidation (Fig. 3I), and elevated levels of lipid reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Fig. 3J, K), along with a reduction in mitochondrial membrane potential in HEC-1B and Ishikawa cells (Fig. 3L, M). Besides, these effects were mitigated by the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 (Fig. 3I-M). We examined the expression levels of ferroptosis proteins after Fer-1 addition in HEC-1B and Ishikawa cells.Western blot results showed that CAP inhibited the expression of SLC7A11 and GPX4, while Fer-1 reversed the effect of CAP (Fig. S1C). Collectively, our findings indicate that CAP promotes ferroptosis in endometrial cancer cells.

  To clarify the biological function of HDAC3 in endometrial cancer cells after CAP treatment, we investigated its effects on the proliferation, migration, and invasion of HEC-1B cells in vitro. The findings from the colony formation assay (Fig. 4G, H) and the CCK-8 assay (Fig. 4I) indicated that HDAC3 overexpression mitigated the CAP-induced inhibition of cell proliferation and viability. Meanwhile, cells transfected with HDAC3 demonstrated significantly enhanced migratory and invasive capabilities compared to those treated with CAP (Fig. 4J, K). To further investigate the impact of HDAC3 on ferroptosis, we conducted experiments measuring MDA and lipid ROS. Notably, elevated HDAC3 expression markedly reduced MDA levels and lipid ROS in endometrial cancer cells (Fig. 4L-N). Additionally, HDAC3 overexpression counteracted the effects of CAP on ferroptosis-related protein expression (Fig. 4O). These findings indicate that CAP reduces HDAC3 expression, thereby promoting ferroptosis.


### The deubiquitinase USP49 interacts with HDAC3 and enhances its stability
  To elucidate how CAP reduces HDAC3 protein levels, we performed Co-IP mass spectrometry to identify HDAC3’s interacting proteins (Fig. 5A). Proteins with differential binding were first identified using silver staining, followed by LC-MS analysis of the immunoprecipitated proteins. USP49, a recognized deubiquitinase, was prominently identified among the proteins interacting with HDAC3. Then we used co-IP experiments to verify that there is indeed an interaction between HDAC3 and USP49 in the HEC-1B cell (Fig. 5B). Immunofluorescence further demonstrated the co-localization of HDAC3 and USP49 (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, the three-dimensional complex model for protein docking prediction demonstrated a robust interaction between HDAC3 and USP49 (Fig. 5D). Subsequent RT-qPCR and western blot assays indicated that CAP significantly decreased USP49 expression (Fig. 5E, F). Additionally, Western blot analysis revealed that HDAC3 protein levels were reduced in USP49 knockdown cells and increased in USP49 overexpression cells (Fig. 5G), underscoring the regulatory relationship between USP49 and HDAC3. However, the mRNA levels of HDAC3 did not demonstrate significant changes compared to the control group (Fig. 5H), regardless of whether USP49 was overexpressed or knocked down. Additionally, spearman correlation analysis of the GEO database datasets GSE115810 and GSE56026 indicated no notable link between HDAC3 and USP49 gene expression (Fig. 5I, J).

  We examined USP49’s role in stabilizing HDAC3 protein by treating cells with cycloheximide (CHX), a known protein synthesis inhibitor. Notably, the results revealed that USP49 overexpression mitigated the degradation of HDAC3 compared to the control group (Fig. 5K, L and Fig. S1F). Moreover, the proteasome inhibitor MG132 somewhat counteracted the degradation of HDAC3 due to USP49 knockdown (Fig. 5M, N). These suggest that USP49 regulates HDAC3 protein stability via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. Consequently, we investigated the potential direct influence of USP49 on the ubiquitination levels of HDAC3. Our results indicated that overexpression of USP49 reduced the ubiquitination level of HDAC3 (Fig. 5O). In summary, these results suggest that USP49 boosts the stability of the HDAC3 protein through the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway.


### USP49 promotes endometrial cancer progression by regulating HDAC3 expression
  To further explore the impact of USP49 on HDAC3 in regulating endometrial cancer progression and ferroptosis, we initially examined the GEO database GSE115810 to assess USP49 expression levels in endometrial cancer. As illustrated in Fig. 6A, USP49 expression was markedly elevated in tumor tissues. Additionally, analysis of the TCGA dataset revealed that higher USP49 expression in endometrial cancer is associated with poorer prognoses (Fig. 6B) and predicts reduced overall survival (Fig. 6C). TCGA data further indicated that HDAC3 expression level was positively correlated with the tumor proliferation pathway (Fig. S1D). Following these observations, we performed colony formation and CCK8 activity assays to assess the functional role of USP49 in the proliferation of endometrial cancer cells. The findings from these assays indicated that USP49 overexpression significantly boosted the proliferation of endometrial cancer cells, whereas USP49 knockdown substantially inhibited their proliferation (Fig. 6D-G). The colony formation assay indicated that knocking down USP49 counteracted the cellular proliferation induced by HDAC3 overexpression (Fig. 6H, I). Furthermore, the enhancement of cell migration and invasion capabilities, prompted by HDAC3 overexpression, was also inhibited by USP49 knockdown (Fig. 6J, K). We conducted the lipid ROS and MDA assay to elucidate the regulatory function of USP49 in HDAC3-mediated ferroptosis. Our results indicated that the knockdown of USP49 attenuated the reduction of lipid ROS and MDA induced by HDAC3 overexpression (Fig. 6L, M and Fig. S1G). Furthermore, the suppression of USP49 counteracted the effects of HDAC3 overexpression on proteins implicated in ferroptosis (Fig. 6N, O). In summary, our results found that USP49 influences ferroptosis by regulating HDAC3 expression and promoting the progression of endometrial cancer cells.

### Induction of p53 expression by CAP involves lactylation of histone H3K18 by HDAC3
  HDAC3, a known histone deacetylase, may facilitate the removal of lactosyl modifications from histones [28]. Consequently, we hypothesize that CAP could influence the extent of cellular acylation modifications, thereby modulating cellular signaling pathways. To examine this hypothesis, we conducted Western blot assays on CAP-treated HEC-1B and Ishikawa cells using anti-L-lactyl lysine and anti-acetyllysine antibodies. The study found that CAP treatment significantly elevated cell lactylation levels, whereas acetylation levels showed minimal change (Fig. 7A, B). Similarly, the trend observed in the lactylation of H3K18 corresponded with the changes in overall lactylation levels; however, the variations in H3K18 acetylation levels did not exhibit significant alterations (Fig. 7C).

  CAP can generate significant ROS, leading to ferroptosis, and p53 can initiate ferroptosis under elevated ROS conditions [29]. Therefore, we initially exposed cells to CAP and used western blot and RT-qPCR tests to evaluate p53 protein levels. The findings showed a rise in p53 expression after CAP treatment (Fig. 7D, E). To investigate whether CAP augments p53 expression through histone H3K18 lactylation, we utilized ChIP-qPCR to evaluate the enrichment of H3K18la at the p53 promoter. The results demonstrated significant enrichment of H3K18la in the p53 promoter region (Fig. 7F). Furthermore, as depicted in Fig. 7G and H, overexpression of HDAC3 resulted in reduced enrichment of H3K18la at the p53 promoter, whereas HDAC3 knockdown led to a increased enrichment of H3K18la.

  In light of these findings, given that p53 can be up-regulated by H3K18la, we subsequently investigated the regulatory mechanisms of USP49 and HDAC3 on H3K18la and p53. Our findings showed that overexpressing USP49 or HDAC3 led to reduced protein expression levels of H3K18la and p53, while knocking down USP49 or HDAC3 resulted in increased expression (Fig. 7I). Furthermore, the overexpression of HDAC3 caused a reduction in p53 mRNA levels, whereas reducing HDAC3 expression led to an elevation in p53 mRNA levels (Fig. 7J, K). To elucidate the regulatory effects of HDAC3 on H3K18la and p53 following CAP treatment, we overexpressed HDAC3 after CAP exposure. Western blot analyses revealed that HDAC3 mitigated the CAP-induced increase in H3K18la and p53 levels (Fig. 7L). Furthermore, RT-qPCR results indicated that HDAC3 reversed the CAP-induced upregulation of p53 mRNA levels (Fig. 7M). In addition, we used western blot to detect the expression of USP49, HDAC3, H3K18la, p53, as well as the ferroptosis-related proteins SLC7A11 and GPX4 in endometrial cancer tumor tissues. The observed trends for these proteins were consistent with previously identified regulatory relationships. (Fig. 7N and Fig. S1J). We demonstrated the correlation between USP49, HDAC3,p53, and ferroptosis-related proteins using the TCGA database, and the results showed that USP49, HDAC3, and p53 correlated with most of ferroptosis proteins (Fig. S1E). In conclusion, based on the finding that CAP enhances the ubiquitinated degradation of HDAC3 by downregulating USP49 expression, we further demonstrated that CAP promotes p53 expression by increasing the lactylation level of H3K18.

### CAP suppresses the advancement of endometrial cancer by triggering ferroptosis in vivo
  To further substantiate the hypothesis that CAP impedes endometrial cancer progression by inducing ferroptosis, we conducted supplementary in vivo studies utilizing female BALB/c mice. The mice received implants of HEC-1B cells and were subsequently randomized into three groups: one received PBS injections, another received CAP-treated PBS, and the third received CAP-treated PBS with Fer-1, a ferroptosis inhibitor (Fig. 9A). Tumor volumes were measured every three days over 30 days, and tumor weights were assessed at the conclusion of the study. The group treated with CAP showed a notable reduction in tumor growth compared to the PBS-treated group. Conversely, tumors treated with the combination of CAP and Fer-1 demonstrated a reversal of the inhibitory effects (Fig. 9B-D). To assess the biosafety of CAP, internal organs from mice were harvested and subjected to H&E staining (Fig. 9E, F). The results confirmed the safety of CAP for mice. Western blot analysis and immunohistochemical (IHC) staining corroborated findings from previous studies. In the CAP-treated group, there was a significant reduction in the levels of USP49 and HDAC3, along with an upregulation of H3K18la and p53 expression. The administration of Fer-1 mitigated the effects induced by CAP. Moreover, the levels of proteins linked to ferroptosis, including ACSL3, SLC7A11, FTH1, and GPX4, were decreased in the group treated with CAP, and this decrease was similarly reversed by Fer-1 (Fig. 9G-I). In conclusion, the aforementioned in vivo findings suggest that CAP exerts its anti-cancer effects through ferroptosis induction.

## Discussion
  Endometrial cancer, which is the most widespread gynaecological cancer in developed regions, is seeing a growing number of cases [30]. Despite notable advancements in pharmacological and surgical interventions, the survival rate for patients with endometrial cancer remains suboptimal [31]. CAP exhibits significant therapeutic potential for endometrial cancer as an emerging anti-cancer therapy. This study is designed to evaluate the anti-cancer activity of CAP on endometrial cancer cells. Mechanistically, CAP facilitates the ubiquitination of the HDAC3 protein through the down-regulation of USP49, which in turn up-regulates the lactylation of H3K18. This molecular cascade promotes the expression of p53, thereby inducing ferroptosis. Consequently, these events collectively inhibit the proliferation, migration, and invasion of endometrial cancer cells (Fig. 9J).

  HDAC3, part of class I HDACs, modifies epigenetics by deacetylating histone and non-histone substrates to inhibit gene transcription directly [32]. HDAC3 is pivotal in regulating metabolic growth and development and influencing cell migration and differentiation [33]. High levels of HDAC3 are connected to several types of cancer, such as colorectal cancer, breast cancer, liver cancer, and acute B-lymphoblastic leukemia. They are often related to unfavorable clinical results [34–37]. In the context of prostate cancer, HOXB13 exerts a tumor-suppressive effect by recruiting HDAC3 to inhibit metastasis [38]. This finding implies that HDAC3’s function might vary among different cancer types. Earlier research has shown that HDAC3 is expressed at higher levels in endometrial cancer than in normal endometrial tissue [39]. Recently, an increasing focus has been on studying HDAC3 inhibitors targeting various malignant tumors [35, 40–42]. Notably, Tucidinostat, a small molecule that inhibits histone deacetylase, has received approval for clinical application in treating T-cell lymphoma in China [43]. Furthermore, the application of apicidin, an HDAC3 inhibitor, has significantly reduced endometrial cancer cells’ growth. This research has found a remarkable link between HDAC3 expression and the clinical stage of endometrial cancer. Specifically, higher levels of HDAC3 expression are often linked to a worse prognosis in endometrial cancer patients. Proteomic and bioinformatic analyses have revealed that CAP can down-regulate HDAC3 protein expression. These findings have been substantiated by in vitro experiments, which further indicated that CAP inhibits the proliferation, migration, and invasion of endometrial cancer cells by suppressing of HDAC3 protein expression. Notably, no significant alteration in HDAC3 mRNA expression was observed following CAP treatment, suggesting that CAP may down-regulate HDAC3 protein levels through post-translational modifications.

  In most cells, ubiquitination and deubiquitination are balanced to regulate protein stability [44]. Dysregulation of ubiquitinating and deubiquitinating enzymes is highly prevalent in cancer. USP49, a novel deubiquitinating enzyme, shows increased expression in multiple cancer forms [45]. Nonetheless, the exact function of this interaction in endometrial cancer remains to be understood entirely. Our study explored the HDAC3 and USP49 interaction through mass spectrometry and co-immunoprecipitation. We discovered that USP49 regulates HDAC3 expression when CAP is involved. Through in vitro experiments, we demonstrated that the down-regulation of USP49 results in increased ubiquitination of HDAC3, thereby reducing its protein stability. This regulatory mechanism had not been previously identified. Additionally, in vitro studies revealed that CAP hindered the proliferation, migration, and invasion of endometrial cancer cells by down-regulating USP49. Research conducted previously has shown that decreasing HDAC3 enhances p53 expression through increased acetylation [46]. However, in this study, we observed that HDAC3 primarily functions in lactylation, diverging from the conventional understanding of its role in acetylation. Histone lactylation, a recently identified post-translational modification, promotes gene transcription by lactylating histone lysine residues. HDAC3 has been demonstrated to function as an effective lysine delactylase at the H3K18 site [47]. Notably, ChIP-qPCR experiments have revealed that CAP up-regulates p53 expression by modulating HDAC3 activity to enhance H3K18 lactylation near the p53 promoter. Consequently, this research offers a new understanding of CAP’s essential function in endometrial cancer by regulating the HDAC3-mediated signaling pathway.

  In proteomics, we found that ferroptosis showed significance after CAP treatment. Ferroptosis is a distinct form of cell death marked by iron-dependent buildup and often associated with excess lipid peroxides and ROS [48, 49]. A growing amount of research indicates that triggering ferroptosis in cancer cells could be a promising approach to cancer therapy. Our research noted increased ROS levels in media exposed to CAP. Furthermore, in endometrial cancer cells, we observed increased MDA and lipid ROS and decreased mitochondrial membrane potential. These results indicate that CAP can trigger ferroptosis in endometrial cancer cells. Furthermore, p53 is proven to be essential in the initiation of ferroptosis and can enhance cellular sensitivity to this process. Consequently, we hypothesize that CAP triggers ferroptosis via p53 in endometrial cancer cells. This hypothesis is supported by our subsequent rescue experiments involving p53 knockdown, which corroborated the proposed mechanism. These observations imply that p53 might influence the effect of CAP on endometrial cancer progression by inducing ferroptosis.

  Our experimental results also demonstrated the role of ferroptosis in the inhibition of endometrial cancer cell development by CAP. However, in addition to ferroptosis, a number of metabolic pathways in the proteomics data also showed clear significance. We therefore explored in detail the direct relevance of these metabolic pathways to ferroptosis. First, previous studies have shown that ferroptosis is closely related to lipid metabolism. Ferroptosis depends on the accumulation of lipid peroxides, and polyunsaturated fatty acids increase the sensitivity of GCs to ferroptosis in gastric cancer cells (GCs) [50]. In addition, arachidonic acid (a polyunsaturated fatty acid) supplementation restores the sensitivity of enteric GCs to ferroptosis [51]. Fatty acid desaturase 2 (FADS2) is a key determinant of cellular sensitivity to ferroptosis [52]. In addition, sulfur metabolism and ferroptosis are closely related. Sulfur often participates in biological processes with iron by forming iron-sulfur clusters, which regulate iron homeostasis and susceptibility to ferroptosis [53]. Since iron-sulfur clusters can promote the production of ROS, ROS in turn can activate the ferroptosis pathway [54]. In addition to lipid metabolism and sulfur metabolism, pyruvate metabolism is also involved in ferroptosis. Pyruvate is the source of acetyl coenzyme A in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle [55]. Previous studies have shown that pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 4 (PDK4) drives resistance to ferroptosis. Specifically, PDK4 inhibits pyruvate entry into the TCA cycle by preventing pyruvate oxidation, thereby reducing the rate of fatty acid synthesis and ultimately inhibiting ferroptosis [56]. Together, these metabolic pathways can exert various biological functions by participating in the ferroptosis pathway.

  In our study, CAP demonstrated substantial anti-cancer potential in endometrial cancer, as evidenced by both in vivo and in vitro experiments. Importantly, we were the pioneers in showing that CAP exerts a significant anti-cancer impact on endometrial cancer cells by inducing ferroptosis. Nevertheless, prior to its adoption as a standard anti-cancer therapy, further clinical investigations are necessary to determine its therapeutic efficacy, safety, and optimal treatment protocols. In addition, the precise mechanism underlying CAP-induced ferroptosis remains elusive, and the specific regulatory molecules involved warrant further investigation. Our study demonstrated that the combination of CAP and RSL3 can potentiate the anti-cancer efficacy of CAP, indicating the potential for synergistic use of CAP with other ferroptosis inducers or anti-cancer agents. In summary, our study identifies CAP as a promising therapeutic agent in endometrial cancer progression. It elucidates a CAP-induced molecular mechanism of ferroptosis that is dependent on the HDAC3-regulated H3K18la/p53 signaling axis, which provides a potential therapeutic target for the treatment of endometrial cancer.
