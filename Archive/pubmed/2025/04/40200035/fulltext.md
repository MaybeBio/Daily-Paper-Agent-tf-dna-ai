# Transcriptomics-driven exploration of genetic variation and peptide discovery in the sea anemones Anthopleura midori and Actinia equina

## Results

### (1) Genetic variation and transcriptional regulation
  To unravel evolutionary divergences and functional adaptations between Anthopleura midori (group A) and Actinia equina (group B), we compared genetic variations (single-nucleotide polymorphisms [SNPs] and insertions/deletions [InDels]) and characterized transcription factor landscapes in their tentacle tissues, providing insights into species-specific regulatory mechanisms.

  The Group A had more than 100,000 SNPs, whereas Group B had fewer than 100,000 SNPs. However, the number of InDels in Group B was nearly twice that of Group A (Fig. 1A). In terms of distribution shape, while both groups exhibited nearly normal distributions, the curve for Group A was relatively smoother, whereas the curve for Group B was steeper near the main peak (Fig. 1B). For example, in the A1a sample, InDel lengths in the range of − 5 to 5 were relatively evenly distributed, with minimal fluctuations. In contrast, in the B1a sample, InDel lengths in the same range were highly concentrated near the main peak (InDel length around 0) and rapidly decreased on either side, showing a clear difference. In Group A, the proportion of InDels with a length of 0 averaged around 35–40%, while in Group B, this proportion averaged around 30–35%. For example, A2a had a proportion of 38%, and B2a had 32%. This suggests that Group A has slightly more instances of no insertion or deletion compared to Group B. Insertion lengths in Group A (Anthopleura midori) were more concentrated (0–10 bp) with fewer but tightly clustered events, while Group B (Actinia equina) showed scattered insertions in this range (e.g., B3a had broader 0–9 bp distributions). Deletions in Group B (− 10–0 bp) were more frequent and concentrated (e.g., B1a had 1.5× more events than A1a in − 5–0 bp), contrasting with Group A’s dispersed (− 8–0 bp) deletions. These patterns suggest Group A experienced stable evolution with conserved sequences, whereas Group B’s genetic variation was likely shaped by environmental stress or hybridization, leading to frequent and diverse InDels. Figure 1C shows SNP distributions in six samples (Group A: A1a, A2a, A3a; Group B: B1a, B2a, B3a). Group A samples (Anthopleura midori) exhibited consistent patterns: base A dominated with ~ 2.8 × 10⁴ counts (e.g., A1a), contrasting sharply with T (~ 0.5 × 10⁴), suggesting evolutionary selection favoring A-related SNPs, possibly linked to environmental adaptability. Group B (Actinia equina) showed similar but less pronounced trends (e.g., B1a had ~ 1.4 × 10⁴ A counts), indicating weaker selective pressure for A-based variations. Overall, Group A displayed higher SNP richness and more concentrated A-dominated variation, reflecting greater genetic diversity and potentially stronger adaptive constraints compared to Group B. In summary, the genetic diversity of species Anthopleura midori is reflected in the variation of individual nucleotide sites, which may generate a large number of alleles, laying a strong genetic foundation for adapting to diverse environments. The frequent insertions and deletions in species Actinia equina may lead to significant changes in gene structure and function, possibly giving rise to unique toxin peptides with structures and functions distinct from those of species Anthopleura midori, thus aiding the species in occupying a specific ecological niche.

  To compare the genetic and epigenetic factors involved in toxin production, we compared the transcription factors of species Anthopleura midori and Actinia equina. Figure 2 presents the parameter comparisons. Regarding the composition of transcription factors, both A (Anthopleura midori) and B (Actinia equina) samples share common transcription factors such as TEA(TEA Domain Transcription Factor), SPL (Squamosa Promoter Binding Protein-like), and bHLH(Basic Helix-Loop-Helix)32–34, but also exhibit unique ones, reflecting differences in gene regulation strategies. In terms of expression levels, bHLH in Anthopleura midori samples is close to 400, while in Actinia equina it is around 1200, showing a significant difference. TEA expression in Anthopleura midori samples is slightly higher than in Actinia equina, and both values are relatively low. In terms of physiological function, the high expression of bHLH in Actinia equina may provide advantages in muscle and nervous system development, leading to quicker responses and better motor coordination. In contrast, Anthopleura midori may prioritize tissue repair and nutrient intake through other transcription factors, shaping different physiological functions. Regarding environmental adaptability, if Anthopleura midori is in a stable marine environment, its transcription factors help maintain metabolic stability and growth. However, Actinia equina, when in harsher environments, can rapidly sense and respond to changes due to the high expression of bHLH, enhancing tissue repair and nutrient intake abilities, ensuring survival. From an evolutionary perspective, the shared transcription factors testify to a common ancestor, while the unique markers indicate differentiation paths, providing molecular evidence for reconstructing the evolutionary tree of sea anemones.

### (2) Identification of toxin peptides based on transcriptome sequencing and machine learning
  Building on our previous analysis of genetic variation and transcriptional regulation, we then systematically focused on the identification of novel peptides. Figure 3 provides an overview of the transcriptome sequencing analysis and quality control data for Anthopleura midori and Actinia equina. Figure 3A illustrates schematic diagrams of Anthopleura midori and Actinia equina. The raw read counts were 4,373,454 for A1a, 4,510,242 for A2a, and 5,071,244 for A3a in Anthopleura midori, while Actinia equina showed counts of 4,891,196 for B1a, 4,754,516 for B2a, and 5,013,750 for B3a. The raw base counts were similarly consistent across the samples, ranging from 437,345,400 to 507,124,400 bases for Anthopleura midori and 489,119,600 to 501,375,000 for Actinia equina. The N reads percentage, indicating undetermined bases, remained low at around 0.03% for both species. Additionally, the clean Q30 base percentages—representing the proportion of high-quality bases—were impressively high, ranging from 92.65–93.13% for Anthopleura midori and 92.94–94.21% for Actinia equina in the raw data, with even higher percentages after cleaning. The GC content was slightly higher in Actinia equina, with values ranging from 52.37–53.05%, compared to 42.57–43.28% for Anthopleura midori (Table 1). In the Venn diagram in Fig. 3B, the number of unique new sequences for A1a is 2, for A2a is 2, and for A3a is 4. The number of new sequences common to all three samples is 3. The Venn diagram in Fig. 3C shows that the number of unique new sequences for B1a is 17, for B2a is 2, and for B3a is 7. The number of new sequences common to all three samples is 4. In the analysis, we also identified the conotoxin ‘Eb3-H03 [4969]’ sequences, which in this study are named ‘A3a-c140_c0_g1’, ‘A1a-c14337_c0_g1_’, and ‘B3a-c8682_c5_g5_’ (Supplementary Table 1). This suggests the presence of a shared toxin in the marine ecosystem. Figure 3D display Venn diagrams of unique sequences, showing that Anthopleura midori and Actinia equina each exhibit substantial differences in their toxin sequences, with no shared unique sequences between the two species.

### (3) Mass spectrometry identification of toxin peptides
  To verify the reliability of the toxins, we conducted verification using two samples (Fig. 4). Sequence identification of the peptide segments “SLDFLRDDSER” and “B3a-c29555_c4_g4” (amino acid sequence: QLRSHLMVVQAPRAQR; Supplementary Table 2) highlights their membership in the “A1 A-c14965_c0_g1” family, which is conserved across Anthopleura midori and Actinia equina. The signal peptide for this sequence is “MDIKFALFLLALVSSSLA”. The mature peptide sequence contains 6 cysteine residues, which may stabilize its three-dimensional structure by forming disulfide bonds. However, there are individual differences in the mature peptide region sequence. Evolutionary analysis, sequence alignment, and database searches indicate that it belongs to a new peptide family.

### (4) Structural prediction of candidate cyclic peptides
  To investigate the functional properties of the toxins and identify potential novel natural product candidates, we first employed the software Rosetta to predict their structures, as previously described in reference35. Subsequently, five peptides were selected based on criteria including: computational prediction as toxins, presence of a conserved cysteine-stabilized scaffold, intact “Signal-Pre-Mature” domains for proper biosynthesis, detection across multiple biological samples, and ≤ 6 cysteine residues to ensure structural tractability and avoid excessive disulfide complexity. These criteria ensured the selection of peptides with high functional potential and druggable properties for downstream analysis (Table 2).Table 2 lists five peptides characterized by distinct disulfide bond patterns and molecular weights (1,092.32–2,681.96 Da). Notably, B3a-c29555_c4_g4 is the smallest peptide (9 amino acids) with two compact disulfide bridges (CYS(2)-CYS(8), CYS(3)-CYS(6)), highlighting its potential as a structurally constrained bioactive molecule. Figure 5 shows these peptides labeled as A3a - c15497_c0_g1 (Fig. 5A), B1a - c14302_c0_g1 (Fig. 5B), B3a - c7156_c0_g1 (Fig. 5C), B3a - c29555_c4_g4 (Fig. 5D, also shown in Fig. 5F), and B2a - c37141_c1_g16 (Fig. 5E). The first five structures are presented in colored 3D form, clearly showing their cyclic conformation. Figure 5F highlights the positions of cysteine (CYS) in B3a - c29555_c4_g4 (CYS2–8, CYS3–6).

### (5) Molecular docking of candidate cyclic peptides
  In the field of functional targeting research, most studies on sea anemone peptide toxins have focused on sodium channel toxins36–42 and potassium channel toxins37,40,41,43,44, For example, the toxin of the sea anemone voltage-gated potassium channel has been extensively studied, revealing modes of action including pore blockage, enzyme inhibition, and voltage-dependent modification, with high structural diversity37,45,46, However, there has been limited research on calcium ion channel toxins. To explore the potential value of these cyclic peptides, molecular docking screenings were conducted. We first examined their docking energies and then analyzed their docking positions to identify target peptides for further research. Table 3 shows the docking results with the Cav3.1 (PDB ID:6 KZO) membrane protein, where different peptides exhibited varying binding energies and spatial orientations. For example, peptide B3a - c7156_c0_g1 had the highest binding energy of 402, indicating the most stable binding to the Cav3.1 (PDB ID:6 KZO) membrane protein among the peptides. Its rotation angles were (220°, 52°, 194°), and its translation distances were (166.59 Å, 183.31 Å, 197.26 Å), suggesting a specific spatial orientation and position during binding. On the other hand, peptide B3a - c29555_c4_g4 had a lower binding energy of 304, indicating relatively weaker binding stability with Cav3.1 (PDB ID:6 KZO) membrane protein. Table 4 presents the docking results with the P2Z receptor, where the highest binding energy of peptide B3a - c7156_c0_g1 had an absolute value of only 281, indicating weaker binding stability. However, peptide B3a - c29555_c4_g4 contains only two disulfide bonds, making it easier to chemically synthesize, which suggests a higher feasibility for drug development. Further molecular docking validation is needed to obtain more reliable results.

## Conclusions
  This study analyzed Anthopleura midori and Actinia equina and achieved the following results: 51 toxin sequences were identified, showing significant interspecies and individual variation; Anthopleura midori generates diverse toxins through single nucleotide variations to enhance adaptability, while Actinia equina produces unique toxins through insertion-deletion events; two new gene families were identified and five new cyclic peptides predicted; differences in transcription factors between Anthopleura midori and Actinia equina were hypothesized, with high expression of bHLH in Actinia equina possibly benefiting muscle and nervous system development. Kinetic simulations indicated that peptide B3a-c29555_c4_g4 may regulate the Cav3.1 protein channel. These findings provide important insights into species characteristics and evolutionary mechanisms.


## Discussion
  This study provides a comprehensive and in-depth exploration of two species of sea anemones, A (Anthopleura midori) and B (Actinia equina), focusing on various aspects such as new sequences, genetic diversity, transcription factors, genes and cyclic peptides, and protein-peptide systems. The findings offer valuable and rich information that contributes to understanding the biological characteristics, evolutionary mechanisms, and potential applications of sea anemones. In the study of new sequences, we discovered a total of 51 toxin sequences in Anthopleura midori and Actinia equina, with significant differences between the two species and notable diversity among individuals. These differences in toxin sequences may be due to the distinct ecological environments in which the two sea anemone species exist. In different marine ecosystems, food resources, predator types, and pathogen species vary. To adapt to these environmental pressures, sea anemones have evolved different toxin sequences47,48. For example, sea anemones in certain regions may face more predators and, therefore, need to evolve more toxic and specific toxins to defend against their enemies. The diversity observed between individuals provides a broader genetic foundation for the sea anemone population to cope with environmental changes, increasing the survival probability of the species.

  From the perspective of genetic diversity, Anthopleura midori primarily generates a large number of alleles due to changes at single nucleotide positions, which in turn leads to the production of various functionally diverse toxin peptides. This gives Anthopleura midorigreater flexibility in adapting to its environment. Even a small change in a single nucleotide can alter the structure and function of the toxin peptides49, enabling the sea anemone to cope with different environmental challenges, such as varied food sources and pathogen infections. In contrast, frequent insertions and deletions in Actinia equina have resulted in unique toxin peptides that differ from those in Anthopleura midori, possibly as a strategy for adapting to a specific ecological niche. These unique toxin peptides might give Actinia equina an advantage in competition, for example, in surviving specific light, temperature, or salinity conditions. This finding is significant for understanding ecological differentiation and adaptive evolution in species and offers a new perspective for studying how organisms survive and reproduce in specific ecological niches.

  Transcription factor analysis reveals physiological differences between sea anemones A (Anthopleura midori) and B (Actinia equina). Both A (Anthopleura midori) and B (Actinia equina) samples share common transcription factors such as TEA, SPL, and bHLH49, but bHLH is highly expressed in Actinia equina, potentially giving it an advantage in muscle and nervous system development. This might suggest that Actinia equina is more agile and efficient in behaviors like predation and avoiding predators. On the other hand, Anthopleura midori may rely on other transcription factors that focus on tissue repair and nutrient intake, reflecting the distinct adaptive strategies of Anthopleura midori and Actinia equina in response to different ecological environments during long-term evolution.

  The identification of two new gene families and the prediction of five novel cyclic peptides through transcriptomics and mass spectrometry offer new directions for drug development. The toxins and cyclic peptides of sea anemones often possess unique biological activities and may have therapeutic potential for certain diseases. For instance, some sea anemone toxins exhibit neurotoxicity15 and could potentially be developed into drugs for treating neurological disorders, while the novel cyclic peptides may have antibacterial, antiviral, or anticancer activities50. The discovery of these new genes and cyclic peptides provides a rich resource for developing new drugs and holds the potential for breakthroughs in the pharmaceutical field.

  Kinetic simulations confirmed that the peptide B3a - c29555_c4_g4 interacts with the Cav3.1 (PDB ID:6 KZO) protein-peptide system primarily through hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions, and holds potential as a Cav3.1 (PDB ID:6 KZO) protein channel regulator. Cav3.1 (PDB ID:6 KZO) is an important calcium ion channel protein, closely associated with many physiological processes such as nerve conduction and muscle contraction51,52. This peptide, as a channel regulator, could be used to treat diseases related to calcium ion channel abnormalities, such as arrhythmia and epilepsy52. This discovery provides new targets and ideas for drug development and holds significant clinical application potential.

  However, there are still certain limitations in this study. For example, although we identified differences and diversity in toxin sequences, further in-depth research is needed to understand the specific functions and mechanisms of these toxins. Additionally, more experimental validation is required for the biological activity and medicinal value of the newly identified genes and cyclic peptides. Future research could combine more technical approaches, such as gene editing, cell experiments, and animal experiments, to further explore the biological characteristics and potential applications of sea anemones, contributing to biodiversity conservation and drug development, among other fields.


## Methods

### (1) Transcriptome sequencing and bioinformatics analysis
  Samples were collected from the South China Sea, and tentacles were dissected and stored at − 80 °C. RNA was extracted following the manufacturer’s instructions using TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The transcriptome of the Pomacea canaliculata individual was sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq platform using paired-end sequencing, with a target fragment size of 125 base pairs. Quality control was performed using NGS QC Toolkit53 and Trimmomatic − 0.6054. The assembled sequences were used to predict peptides with the tools Conosort and Conosever55. Peptides containing signal peptides, leader peptides, and mature peptides were retained, while those longer than 100 amino acids were excluded. The sequences were then aligned against the Uniprot database (https://www.uniprot.org) using BLAST, and sequences with a p-value less than 0.001 were removed. This stringent filtering step ensures that only unique peptides, which may be novel, are retained for further in-depth analysis.


### (2) Genetic variation analysis
  After transcriptome assembly, using Actinia equinaas the reference genome56, we employed Samtools software57 to detect SNPs (single nucleotide polymorphisms) and INDELs (insertions and deletions) in the RNA-Seq data. By aligning the assembled transcripts with the reference genome sequence of *Actinia equina* from known databases, potential variant sites were identified. Each type of variant was then subjected to rigorous filtering and annotation to exclude false positive variants, ensuring the accuracy and reliability of the identified genetic variations.


### (3) Proteomics and informatics analysis
  The samples were stored at − 80°C. For sample preparation, the extraction buffer used was Tris-HCl (pH 7.0). Enzymatic digestion was conducted with trypsin at a 1:200 (enzyme: substrate) ratio for 12 h. The sample preparation and analysis strictly followed the Thermo Fisher Scientific EASY-nLC 1000 system and Thermo LTQ Orbitrap Elite mass spectrometer requirements, alongside methods from our prior studies58. The test duration for each sample was set to 50 min. Full Scan Resolution: Set to 60,000–120,000 (m/z 400) for high-precision peptide analysis. MS/MS Resolution: Set to 15,000 (m/z 400) to balance speed and fragment accuracy on the Thermo LTQ Orbitrap Elite. In proteomic data analysis, the Thermo Xcalibur 2.2 sp1.48 software was used to visualize the total ion current chromatograms of the samples. During database searching, the default parameters of the MaxQuant software59 were adopted, and the polypeptide screening library obtained from the transcriptome was used as the database. The mass tolerance for both precursor ions and fragment ions was set to 20 ppm. The dynamic modification was set as methionine oxidation (+ 15.995 Da). The maximum number of modifications allowed per peptide was 4. The mass tolerance for both precursor ions and fragment ions was 20 ppm, and a maximum of 3 missed cleavage sites were allowed. The digestion method was set as trypsin digestion, and the false discovery rate (FDR) was set to 1%. The peptide mass ranged from 600 to 10,000 Da, and the peptide length was between 6 and 100 amino acids.


### (4) Sequence alignment and evolutionary analysis
  We conducted a classification analysis with the polypeptide signal families obtained from the ConoServer database. Through evolutionary analysis, we analyzed the signal sequences of three novel conopeptide precursors identified in this study, the signal sequences of gene superfamilies in ConoServer, and the signal sequences of 35 previously identified superfamilies60–62. These sequences were used as the outgroup for the neighbor - joining phylogenetic tree. The amino acid sequences were aligned using CLUSTALW 2.1 (https://www.genome.jp/tools - bin/clustalw), and all distance values ranged from 0.0 to 1.0.


### (5) Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation
  To screen potential candidate peptides, a research workflow was established, ranging from structure prediction to dynamics simulation, to investigate the interaction mechanism between peptides and target proteins, as well as the stability of their complexes. First, the Rosetta ab initio prediction software was used to construct and optimize the peptide structures35. Rosetta employs a strategy that combines ab initio prediction and fragment assembly, evaluating and ranking numerous conformations based on an energy function. The structure with the lowest energy and highest stability was selected as the preliminary candidate, forming the foundation for further study.

  Next, molecular docking was carried out using the Gramm tool35. This tool defines particles on the surface of the peptide and target protein, searching for the optimal match in three-dimensional space to simulate the binding mode. During docking, factors such as shape complementarity and electrostatic interactions were considered to evaluate binding affinity, and the structure ranked first was selected as the final candidate model, representing the most likely binding mode.

  Subsequently, the docking results were analyzed using LigPlot software64. This software visually presents hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, electrostatic forces, and other aspects of the docking site, providing insights into the binding properties and interaction network between the candidate peptide and target protein. This information is crucial for peptide optimization and drug design.

  Finally, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted to verify the stability and biological relevance of the complex. Before simulation, the AMBER99SB-ILDN force field was used to model the protein, and the system was solvated in a TIP3P water box. Sodium and chloride ions were added to neutralize the charge and simulate a physiological environment. The MD simulation followed these steps: first, a two-stage energy minimization was performed, optimizing water and ions, followed by optimization of the entire system to ensure that the system starts from a low-energy, stable state. Then, a 500 ps equilibration was performed under NPT conditions (300 K, 1 bar) to stabilize the thermodynamic parameters. Subsequently, a 50 ns production simulation was run under the same NPT conditions, with a 2 fs time step and PME method applied to handle long-range electrostatic interactions. During the simulation, data was collected every 1 ps, and parameters such as root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), hydrogen bonds, and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) were analyzed to assess the stability, flexibility, and solvent exposure of the complex. To ensure result reproducibility, the simulation was repeated twice using GROMACS 202065, with random number seeds set to 26 and 56. The results were compared to verify the reliability of the simulation.
