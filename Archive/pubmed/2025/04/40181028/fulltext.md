# BcatrB mediates pyrimethanil resistance in Botrytis cinerea revealed by transcriptomics analysis


## Abstract
  Gray mold, caused by Botrytis cinerea, poses an escalating threat to ginseng production in China. Excessive application of chemical fungicides has resulted in severe resistance development. To elucidate the resistance mechanism of B. cinerea to pyrimethanil, transcriptomic comparisons were conducted between resistant (HRG21) and sensitive (FSG43) strains following exposure to pyrimethanil for 2 and 6 h. GO and KEGG analyses identified differential expression of genes associated with ABC and MFS transporters as well as methionine biosynthesis. qRT-PCR validation confirmed a marked upregulation of ABC (BcatrA, BcatrB, BcatrD, BcatrO, and Bmr3) and MFS (Bchex1 and BcmfsM2) transporter genes in HRG21, whereas in FSG43, ABC (BcatrA, BcatrB, BcatrD, and BcatrO) and MFS (Bchex1) transporter genes were downregulated. No significant transcriptional changes were observed in Mrr1, a transcription factor gene, following pyrimethanil exposure. Molecular docking analysis identified potential binding sites and interaction targets for five key transporters (BcatrB, BcatrA, BcatrD, Bmr3, and Bchex1). Furthermore, exogenous methionine supplementation partially mitigated pyrimethanil toxicity in FSG43. However, amino acid sequences of enzymes involved in methionine biosynthesis (BcmetC, BcStr2, Bcmet2, Bcmxr1, and Bcmxr2) exhibited no variation between FSG43 and HRG21. The results indicate that BcatrB plays a central role in pyrimethanil resistance, while methionine contributes only marginally to resistance mechanisms.


## Introduction
  Botrytis cinerea Pres. ex Fr. [teleomorph Botryotinia fuckeliana (de Bary) Whetzel], a highly destructive necrotrophic phytopathogen1,2, affects over 1,400 plant species during both pre- and postharvest stages, posing a significant threat to numerous economically important agricultural and ornamental crops3. Favorable conditions, such as moderate temperatures and high humidity, accelerate the proliferation of B. cinerea, leading to the rapid generation of abundant asexual conidia from infected plant tissues, including fruits, leaves, and stems4,5. Asian ginseng (Panax ginseng C. A. Meyer), a member of the Araliaceae family, has been an integral component of traditional medicine for millennia, with its dried rhizomes and roots highly valued for medicinal applications6–8. China remains the dominant global producer, contributing approximately 80% of the annual ginseng supply9.

  The temperate and humid climate of the Changbai Mountain region in China provides optimal conditions for both ginseng cultivation and the proliferation of B. cinerea10, contributing to the frequent occurrence of gray mold epidemics11. Under conducive weather conditions, B. cinerea propagates rapidly on residual ginseng plant matter, triggering severe gray mold outbreaks in the absence of timely management strategies12. Infected plants display extensive tissue decay, with progressive deterioration of leaves, stems, and seeds, culminating in significant yield reductions and economic losses13–15.

  Extensive efforts have been directed toward managing gray mold through cultural practices, biocontrol strategies, and resistant cultivars; nevertheless, chemical control remains the most effective approach globally16,17. Despite this, B. cinerea exhibits a high propensity for fungicide resistance due to its rapid lifecycle, genetic diversity, and prolific spore production, classifying it as a high-risk pathogen18,19. Resistance has been reported across multiple fungicide classes, including benzimidazoles, dicarboximides, anilinopyrimidines, pyridinamides, and methoxyacrylates20–22. Prolonged and intensive pesticide application has further accelerated resistance development, diminishing the efficacy of many commercial fungicides and complicating gray mold management21,23. Additionally, the extensive reliance on these fungicides has contributed to environmental contamination and pesticide residues in ginseng products, raising concerns regarding food safety and long-term sustainability24.

  Pyrimethanil, an anilinopyrimidine fungicide (AP) introduced in the 1990s for gray mold management25, remains mechanistically ambiguous despite its extensive application. It is hypothesized to interfere with methionine biosynthesis26,27, with early investigations linking its activity to perturbations in the methionine metabolic pathway, marked by elevated cystathionine levels and a simultaneous decline in methionine concentrations28. Initially, cystathionine β-lyase (CBL) was considered the primary molecular target29, but subsequent findings disproved this assumption30. The role of methionine biosynthesis in AP resistance mechanisms remains unresolved, yet prior studies have demonstrated that pyrimethanil resistance in B. cinerea is governed by a single gene31,32.

  The prevalence of pyrimethanil-resistant B. cinerea strains has risen in ginseng-producing regions of China in recent years12. Previous studies have also identified a substantial proportion of B. cinerea strains exhibiting resistance to pyrimethanil22. Beyond B. cinerea, resistance has been documented in other fungal species, including Penicillium digitatum33,34, Alternaria solani35, and Aspergillus flavus36. In P. digitatum, the inhibitory effects of methionine and homocysteine on mycelial growth under AP treatment are less pronounced than in B. cinerea, implying that the methionine biosynthesis pathway is unlikely to serve as the primary target of APs in P. digitatum33. Additionally, no mutations are detected in the cystathionine γ-synthase (CGS) gene among pyrimethanil-resistant P. digitatum strains34. In Venturia inaequalis, multiple genes associated with demethylation inhibitor resistance have been implicated in reduced AP sensitivity37. However, comparative analysis of key enzymes involved in methionine biosynthesis and sulfate assimilation, including CBL, CGS, methionine sulfoxide reductase (MsrB), and sulfate permease (SP2), reveals no amino acid sequence variations between pyrimethanil-resistant and wild-type (A) flavus strains, indicating that these enzymes are not directly associated with AP resistance in this species36. Moreover, while exogenous amino acid supplementation has been shown to mitigate the toxicity of APs such as cyprodinil and pyrimethanil, the protective effect is markedly stronger in (B) cinerea than in P. digitatum33.

  The development of fungicide resistance is not solely attributed to target site modifications; the overexpression of plasma membrane-localized efflux transporters represents a significant contributing factor38. Characterized by broad substrate specificity, these transporters actively expel cytotoxic compounds from the cell, thereby mitigating fungicidal effects39. This efflux-mediated resistance mechanism, commonly termed multiple drug resistance (MDR), is widely observed across fungal species40. Three principal families of efflux transporters—adenosine triphosphate (ATP)-binding cassette (ABC) transporters39, major facilitator superfamily (MFS) transporters41,42, and multidrug and toxic compound extrusion (MATE) transporters43—are implicated in fungicide resistance. ABC transporters, present in both unicellular and multicellular organisms, mediate substrate transport through ATP hydrolysis44–46. MFS transporters, utilizing proton gradients, facilitate the movement of both endogenous and exogenous toxic compounds47,48. MATE transporters contribute to the translocation of diverse molecules, including specialized metabolites49. Enhanced expression of these efflux transporters has been linked to fungicide resistance in microbial populations50. For instance, in B. cinerea, increased expression of specific transporters is correlated with resistance to fludioxonil5, while the ABC transporter Bcmdl1 has been identified as a determinant of AP resistance51. Furthermore, the ABC transporter BctarA has been associated with diminished susceptibility to cycloheximide and catechol in B. cinerea52.

  The widespread occurrence of pyrimethanil-resistant B. cinerea isolates in ginseng-producing regions has been previously documented22. Notably, these resistant isolates exhibit no significant fitness cost, indicating that resistance is unlikely to stem from mutations in target genes. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) serves as an effective tool for characterizing differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in response to exogenous toxins at the transcriptomic level53,54. In this study, transcriptomic profiles of pyrimethanil-resistant and -sensitive strains were compared under pyrimethanil exposure for 2 and 6 h using Illumina high-throughput sequencing. DEGs linked to pyrimethanil resistance were identified and validated via quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR). Molecular docking analysis was employed to predict interactions between transporters and pyrimethanil. Additionally, the impact of exogenous methionine supplementation on pyrimethanil toxicity in both resistant and sensitive strains was examined, and structural diagrams of key enzymes involved in methionine biosynthesis were constructed. The insights gained into the roles of efflux transporters and methionine biosynthesis in pyrimethanil resistance contribute to a deeper understanding of B. cinerea resistance mechanisms in ginseng cultivation. Furthermore, this study provides essential perspectives on the functional involvement of efflux transporters in pyrimethanil resistance.


## Results

### DEGs induced by pyrimethanil-resistant and sensitive strains
  A total of 24 samples across eight treatment groups underwent sequencing, yielding 158.87 Gb of clean bases, with an average of 8.83 Gb per sample. High sequencing quality was indicated by q30 values surpassing 92%, an overall error rate below 0.03%, and GC content ranging from 46 to 48%. The reliability of the sequencing data was further confirmed through alignment with the B. cinerea genome, ensuring suitability for subsequent transcriptome analysis (NCBI BioProject ID: PRJNA1067281). DEGs were subsequently identified within the transcriptome dataset. To illustrate DEG distribution across different comparison groups, hierarchical clustering analysis was conducted (Fig. 1).

  Fig. 1(A) Hierarchical clustering analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the pyrimethanil-resistant isolate HRG21 and pyrimethanil-sensitive isolate FSG43 following exposure to pyrimethanil for 0, 2, and 6 h. Gene expression levels, measured in FPKM values, range from −1 to 1, with green representing lower expression and red indicating higher expression. (B) Venn diagram depicting the overlap of DEGs across different comparison groups. The shared region highlights common DEGs between the two isolates, offering a visual representation of their distribution and intersection in the pyrimethanil-resistant and -sensitive strains.

  Under the screening criteria of padj < 0.05 and |log2FoldChange| > 1, gene expression levels exhibited no significant differences between FM2 and FH2 in the pyrimethanil-sensitive strain FSG43. In contrast, 687 DEGs were identified in HRG21 between HM2 and HH2. Following 6 h of pyrimethanil treatment, FSG43 displayed substantial transcriptional alterations relative to the control (FM6 vs. FH6), with the number of upregulated genes comparable to that observed in HRG21 (HM6 vs. HH6). However, downregulated genes were less abundant in FSG43 than in HRG21. The number of DEGs in FSG43 (FM6 vs. FM2) exceeded that in HRG21 (HM6 vs. HM2) when comparing 6-hour and 2-hour pyrimethanil treatments. With prolonged exposure, DEGs in pyrimethanil-treated samples progressively increased relative to the control. Notably, HRG21 exhibited a higher DEG count than FSG43 under identical treatment durations (Fig. 2).

### DEGs between samples
  A greater number of DEGs were identified in the pyrimethanil-resistant strain HRG21 compared to the pyrimethanil-sensitive strain FSG43 following pyrimethanil exposure, indicating a broader transcriptional response to pyrimethanil stress in HRG21. These DEGs likely participate in the molecular mechanisms underlying pyrimethanil resistance (Fig. 3). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis further revealed distinct functional classifications and metabolic pathways associated with DEGs in HRG21 and FSG43, which may account for their differential sensitivity to pyrimethanil.

  Fig. 3KEGG74–76 pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs across four comparison groups: HM6 vs. HH6, HM6 vs. HM2, FM6 vs. FH6, and HM6 vs. FM6. Pathway terms are listed on the y-axis, with the gene ratio (proportion of DEGs within each pathway) displayed on the x-axis. Dot size represents the number of DEGs associated with each pathway, while color intensity indicates statistical significance. ABC transporter genes are marked with yellow circles, whereas genes related to amino acid biosynthesis are denoted by blue circles.

  Both HRG21 and FSG43 exhibited substantial enrichment of DEGs involved in transmembrane transport. Within the ABC transporter pathway, 7 DEGs were identified in the FM6 vs. FH6 group, 5 in the HM2 vs. HH2 group, and 5 in the HM6 vs. HH6 group. A notable trend emerged wherein transporter genes linked to fungicide efflux, particularly ABC and MFS transporter genes, displayed upregulation under pyrimethanil stress in HRG21, whereas a general downregulation was observed in FSG43 (Fig. 3). This pattern indicates a strong association between the transporter pathway and pyrimethanil resistance in B. cinerea. Moreover, significant DEG enrichment was detected in the amino acid biosynthesis pathway. In the HM6 vs. FM6 group, 7 DEGs were upregulated and 22 downregulated, while in the HM6 vs. HM2 group, 4 DEGs were upregulated and 23 downregulated.


### GO and KEGG analyses of DEGs
  GO enrichment analysis identified up-regulated genes associated with molecular functions such as heme binding, tetrapyrrole binding, and iron ion binding, whereas down-regulated genes were primarily linked to oxidoreductase activity, coenzyme binding, and ribosome biosynthesis. Pathways related to transmembrane transport exhibited significant enrichment, with DEGs involved in transporter activity and transmembrane transport summarized in (Table S1). Both pyrimethanil-resistant and sensitive strains displayed an overall up-regulation of DEGs, with a slight down-regulation observed following pyrimethanil exposure. Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis revealed that DEGs were predominantly enriched in ABC and MFS transporter genes (Table S2). Following pyrimethanil treatment, ABC transporters exhibited a predominant up-regulation in resistant strains, whereas in FSG43, they showed slight up-regulation or were largely down-regulated. In contrast, MFS transporter expression showed no notable differences between resistant and sensitive strains. These findings indicate that ABC transporters constitute the primary pathway influencing pyrimethanil resistance in B. cinerea.


### qRT-PCR verification of transporter gene expression profile
  Seven ABC and four MFS transporter genes exhibiting significant differential expression were identified from the transcriptome sequencing data. The ABC transporter genes included BcatrB (Bcin13g00710), BcatrD (Bcin13g02720), Bmr1 (Bcin01g05890), Bmr3 (Bcin07g02220), BcatrA (Bcin11g04460), BcatrO (Bcin01g01450), and Bcpdr11 (Bcin02g03630), while the MFS transporter genes comprised Bchex1 (Bcin09g00150), Bchex2 (Bcin13g04820), Bchex3 (Bcin05g07520), and BcmfsM2 (Bcin15g00270).

  qRT-PCR analysis corroborated the transcriptome sequencing results, highlighting the significant involvement of ABC and MFS transporters in B. cinerea sensitivity to pyrimethanil. In the pyrimethanil-resistant strain HRG21, BcatrB, BcatrD, and Bmr3 exhibited substantial upregulation following 2 h of pyrimethanil exposure. By the 6-hour mark, increased expression was observed for BcatrA, BcatrB, BcatrD, BcatrO, and Bmr3, alongside Bchex1 and BcmfsM2. Among these, BcatrB displayed the most pronounced induction, reaching 43.2 and 53.8-fold higher levels than the control at the HM2 and HM6 time points, respectively. Conversely, in the pyrimethanil-sensitive strain FSG43, BcatrA, BcatrB, and Bchex1 underwent significant downregulation after 2 h of exposure. This suppression persisted at 6 h, with BcatrA, BcatrD, BcatrO, and Bchex1 remaining significantly repressed (Fig. 4).

### Molecular docking
  Five key ABC and MFS transporters displaying significant differential expression under pyrimethanil stress were further examined via qRT-PCR. Molecular docking simulations were conducted to predict transporter-pyrimethanil interactions, determine molecular pocket structures, and analyze spatial accommodation. Key amino acid residues associated with pyrimethanil resistance were identified, along with potential binding modes between transporters and pyrimethanil molecules (Fig. 5).

  Fig. 5Molecular docking of ABC and MFS transporters. (A) Three-dimensional structural models of ABC and MFS transporters. (B) Two-dimensional schematics depicting the pyrimethanil molecule, transporter binding pockets, and molecular docking interactions between transporters and pyrimethanil. (C) Identification of key amino acid residues involved in pyrimethanil binding, providing insights into potential interaction mechanisms underlying transporter-mediated resistance.

  BcatrB, the primary transporter implicated in pyrimethanil resistance, exhibited identical amino acid sequences in HRG21 and FSG43. Molecular docking analysis revealed that BcatrB engaged pyrimethanil through van der Waals forces, hydrophobic interactions (Alkyl and Pi-Alkyl), Pi-Pi stacking, and Pi-sulfur bonding. Specifically, pyrimethanil binding within the BcatrB cavity involved van der Waals interactions with ARG80, ALA232, ASN233, PHE236, LEU237, LEU273, MET304, and PHE308. Hydrophobic interactions occurred with PRO220 and ARG276, Pi-Pi stacking with TRY229, and Pi-sulfur bonding with MET68. These interactions established a stable binding conformation between the transporter and pyrimethanil, potentially modulating transporter activity and contributing to resistance mechanisms (Fig. 5).


### Expression of transcription factor gene Mrr1
  A pronounced disparity in BcatrB gene expression, despite identical amino acid sequences in HRG21 and FSG43, indicated that pyrimethanil resistance was predominantly driven by transcriptional regulation and alterations in intracellular BcatrB transporter levels. In contrast, Mrr1 expression remained unchanged before and after pyrimethanil treatment (Fig. 7C).


### The toxicity-relieving effect of methionine
  Exogenous methionine supplementation markedly mitigated pyrimethanil toxicity in the sensitive strain FSG43, though complete restoration to baseline levels was not achieved. In contrast, no significant reduction in pyrimethanil toxicity was observed in the resistant strain HRG21 (Fig. 6A). A similar trend was evident across additional pyrimethanil-resistant strains (GXG4, HDG9, CBG13, and FSG21) and sensitive strains (YCG5, GXG3, HDG11, and KDG5) (Fig. 6B).

A similar downregulation pattern was observed in HRG21 after 2 h of exposure; however, certain genes displayed increased expression at 6 h (Fig. 7A).

## Discussion
  Gray mold, caused by Botrytis cinerea, poses a severe threat to ginseng production in China, significantly reducing both yield and quality of P. ginseng10,12. Chemical fungicides have traditionally served as the most effective and cost-efficient strategy for disease control16,55. However, the increasing prevalence of pyrimethanil-resistant B. cinerea strains has complicated management efforts56. Pyrimethanil, an AP fungicide, is widely employed against pathogenic filamentous ascomycetes, particularly B. cinerea, across fruits, vegetables, horticultural plants, and crops in both pre- and postharvest stages5. Since its introduction in China in 1998 for managing gray mold in tomatoes, its extensive application across various agricultural systems has driven the progressive emergence of severe pyrimethanil resistance in B. cinerea across multiple host plants22,57,58. Notably, approximately 70% of B. cinerea isolates from major ginseng-producing regions in China exhibit resistance to pyrimethanil22. Despite its widespread use, the precise molecular targets of pyrimethanil and the mechanisms governing B. cinerea resistance remain unclear. This study employs RNA-seq, followed by GO and KEGG analyses, to compare the transcriptomes of pyrimethanil-resistant and -sensitive B. cinerea strains. DEGs potentially associated with pyrimethanil resistance were identified, and variations in gene expression between resistant and sensitive strains, as well as pre- and post-exposure to pyrimethanil, indicated a possible role of these DEGs in resistance mechanisms.

  GO and KEGG analyses identified significant enrichment of DEGs associated with transmembrane transporters, particularly ABC and MFS transporters. The upregulation of efflux transporter genes suggests that active efflux serves as a primary mechanism driving pyrimethanil resistance in B. cinerea. Compared to the pyrimethanil-resistant strain, exposure to pyrimethanil at its EC50 concentration induced more pronounced gene expression changes in pyrimethanil-sensitive strains. ABC and MFS transporter genes exhibited predominant upregulation in resistant strains while showing downregulation in sensitive strains upon pyrimethanil exposure. Elevated transporter expression in resistant strains likely enhanced pyrimethanil efflux, lowering its intracellular concentration and contributing to resistance. Previous research has demonstrated that the transcription factor Mrr1 regulates fungicide resistance by inducing overexpression of BcatrB, an ABC transporter gene linked to the MDR1 phenotype in B. cinerea59. Additionally, the MDR2 phenotype is associated with structural rearrangements in the mfsM2 promoter following the insertion of a retroelement-derived sequence, leading to mfsM2 overexpression and subsequent fungicide resistance60.

  BcatrB expression exhibited a 53.8-fold upregulation in the pyrimethanil-resistant B. cinerea strain following exposure, indicating its involvement in the resistance phenotype. However, identical amino acid sequences were observed in both resistant and sensitive strains, suggesting that resistance is not attributable to mutations within the transporter itself. Previous studies have proposed Mrr1 as a potential transcription factor regulating BcatrB expression61. Nonetheless, the current study detected neither amino acid mutations nor significant differences in Mrr1 expression between resistant and sensitive strains, implying that Mrr1 does not serve as a primary regulator of BcatrB. The findings suggest that unidentified transcription factor(s) may modulate BcatrB expression and contribute to B. cinerea sensitivity to pyrimethanil.

  The efflux pump BcatrB mediates the expulsion of 2,4-DAPG, preventing its intracellular accumulation to toxic levels in B. cinerea62. Beyond its role in xenobiotic transport, BcatrB functions as a virulence factor, enhancing fungal tolerance to camalexin63, and modulating B. cinerea sensitivity to fenpiclonil64,65 and fludioxonil66. Its expression is upregulated in response to resveratrol exposure65,67. By actively maintaining low intracellular concentrations of cytotoxic compounds, BcatrB mitigates their harmful effects, reinforcing fungal survival under antifungal stress62. Moreover, the BcatrB gene has been identified as a target for plant-derived compounds, highlighting its potential for natural fungicidal strategies against fungal pathogens68. Functioning as a multi-drug transporter, BcatrB exhibits broad substrate specificity, promoting the efflux of fungicides, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and plant defense compounds such as phytoalexins and phytoanticipins. By limiting intracellular toxin accumulation, BcatrB contributes to reduced cytotoxicity in B. cinerea, reinforcing its role in antifungal resistance phenotypes39.

  ETMs function as inhibitors of efflux pump activity, promoting fungicide accumulation within fungal cells and increasing pathogen susceptibility to fungicides69. Previous studies have indicated that these compounds exhibit high specificity70, predominantly modulating ABC transporter activity71. Future research should incorporate growth assays of both pyrimethanil-resistant and -sensitive B. cinerea strains in media supplemented with ETMs and pyrimethanil to evaluate potential synergistic effects. Currently, direct evidence linking elevated efflux pump activity to fungicide resistance in B. cinerea remains limited. Studies in other fungal species have demonstrated that mutants deficient in ABC or MFS transporters retain partial resistance to fungicides39,47,53. To elucidate the contribution of these transporters to fungicide resistance, targeted gene deletion or overexpression strategies could be employed to examine their biological roles and broader phenotypic effects, including alterations in morphology and virulence.

  Additionally, prior research has demonstrated that exogenous methionine mitigates pyrimethanil-induced inhibition in fungal pathogens34,72. In this study, methionine partially counteracted pyrimethanil toxicity in the sensitive B. cinerea strain but exhibited no measurable effect on the resistant strain. In the pyrimethanil-sensitive strain FSG43, key enzyme genes involved in methionine biosynthesis remained suppressed following prolonged exposure. Conversely, in the resistant strain HRG21, the expression of these genes increased over time but did not fully return to baseline levels. These distinct transcriptional responses likely contribute to the differential sensitivity observed between resistant and sensitive strains. Methionine biosynthesis in the resistant strain may gradually normalize over an extended period, aligning with the observed toxicity mitigation data (Fig. 6A, B). Over four days, the protective effect of exogenous methionine in the resistant strain remained minimal, as disruptions in methionine biosynthesis pathways had already been counterbalanced. Collectively, the restoration of key enzyme gene expression or supplementation with exogenous methionine elevates intracellular methionine levels, ultimately modulating B. cinerea sensitivity to pyrimethanil.

  Comparative analyses have demonstrated that the nucleotide sequences of cysteine sulfide γ-synthase (MetB) genes in pyrimethanil-resistant strains of Sclerotinia sclerotiorum72, A. flavus36, and P. digitatum34 are identical to those in sensitive strains, indicating that these genes are not direct targets of pyrimethanil. In the present study, despite significant differential expression of five key enzyme genes involved in the methionine biosynthesis pathway, no mutations were identified in their amino acid sequences, suggesting that at least two distinct molecular targets contribute to pyrimethanil resistance in B. cinerea. Notably, mutations were detected in the 5’ UTR regions of these genes, implying that while amino acid sequences remain unchanged, transcriptional regulation may be altered.

  Methionine, an essential amino acid for translation initiation, is integral to various physiological and biochemical processes in fungal cells. Although genes such as BcmetC, BcStr2, Bcmet2, Bcmxr1, and Bcmxr2 involved in B. cinerea methionine biosynthesis have been characterized, other key enzymes within this pathway remain unidentified, possibly due to low sequence conservation or additional limiting factors. Identifying these unknown genes would enhance the understanding of methionine biosynthesis in B. cinerea and its contribution to pyrimethanil resistance.


## Materials and methods

### Preparation of B. cinerea strains
  The B. cinerea strains HRG21 and FSG43, analyzed in this study, were isolated from P. ginseng leaves displaying characteristic gray mold symptoms in Huanren, Liaoning Province, and Fusong, Jilin Province, China, respectively. Initial purification was performed through single conidium isolation, followed by cultivation on potato dextrose agar (PDA) at 25 °C in darkness for five days. Mycelial plugs were subsequently excised from the colony margins for further experimentation.


### Fungicide
  Technical-grade pyrimethanil (Shanghai Bi De Pharmaceutical Technology Co. Ltd., China) was dissolved in acetone (Beijing Tong Guang Technology Co. Ltd., China) to prepare a 10 mg/mL stock solution and maintained at 4 °C. Solvent control experiments verified that acetone exerted no significant influence on the mycelial growth of B. cinerea.


### Fungicide resistance test
  The resistance of B. cinerea to pyrimethanil was evaluated using the mycelial growth rate method. Following a three-day incubation on PDA at 25 °C in darkness, mycelial plugs were excised from the colony margins and transferred to the center of PDA plates supplemented with pyrimethanil (10.12 mg/L for the resistant strain HRG21 and 0.15 mg/L for the sensitive strain FSG43). Control plates contained an equivalent volume of acetone. Each treatment was conducted in triplicate. After an additional three-day incubation under identical conditions, colony diameters were measured using the cross-over method10 to determine the mycelial growth inhibition rate.

### Pyrimethanil treatments
  The pyrimethanil-resistant strain HRG21 and the pyrimethanil-sensitive strain FSG43 were cultured on PDA for three days to activate fungal growth. Mycelial plugs from each strain (five per culture) were transferred to 200 mL of potato dextrose broth (PDB) and incubated at 25 °C with continuous shaking at 180 rpm for three days. Subsequently, mycelia from HRG21 and FSG43 were treated with pyrimethanil at their respective EC50 concentrations (10.12 µg/mL for HRG21 and 0.15 µg/mL for FSG43) for 0, 2, and 6 h (Table S3). Control samples received an equivalent volume of acetone in PDB at the corresponding time points. In total, eight RNA extraction samples were prepared: HRG21 treated with pyrimethanil for 2 h (HM2) and 6 h (HM6); untreated HRG21 at 2 h (HH2) and 6 h (HH6); FSG43 treated with pyrimethanil for 2 h (FM2) and 6 h (FM6); and untreated FSG43 at 2 h (FH2) and 6 h (FH6).


### Extraction of RNA and illumina sequencing
  Mycelial samples from each treatment were collected via filtration, freeze-dried, and pulverized under liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was extracted using the Trizol method (Invitrogen Corp., Carlsbad, CA, USA), followed by agarose gel electrophoresis to assess RNA integrity and verify the absence of DNA contamination. RNA quality and concentration were determined using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. All RNA samples were stored at ‒80 °C. High-throughput sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform generated 20 million 150-bp paired-end reads per sample. cDNA libraries were prepared using the NEBNest® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina), and post-quality control, sequencing was performed. Each treatment included three technical replicates, with three biological replicates per sample.


### Reads processing and mapping
  Raw sequencing data generated by the Illumina platform was processed using Casava base recognition software to convert it into fastq format. Low-quality reads were filtered out, and the remaining clean reads were assessed based on Q20 quality metrics.

  Reference genome and gene model annotations were obtained from the NCBI genome database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/15632). The reference genome index was built using HISAT2 v2.0.5, while paired-end clean reads were aligned to the B. cinerea B05.10 reference genome (GenBank accession number: GCA_000143535.4) using STAR v2.0.11 with default settings.


### Analysis of DEGs
  Read counts were quantified using HTSeq v0.6.1 and normalized with edgeR v3.0.8 prior to differential gene expression analysis. To assess DEGs in B. cinerea responding to pyrimethanil exposure, pairwise comparisons were conducted among the following groups: HM6 vs. HH6, HM2 vs. HH2, HM6 vs. HM2, FM6 vs. FM2, FM6 vs. FH6, and FM2 vs. FH2. Expression fold changes were computed using DESeq2 (version 1.16.1), with significance thresholds defined as a normalized p-value (padj) < 0.05 and |log₂ Fold Change| ≥ 1.


### GO and KEGG pathway analyses
  GO enrichment analysis of DEGs was conducted using the ClusterProfiler R package, with GO terms exhibiting a p-value < 0.05 considered significantly enriched. Similarly, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed utilizing the same R package, employing the B. cinerea B05.10 reference strain as the background. A significance threshold of p-value < 0.05 was applied to identify enriched pathways.


### qRT-PCR of RNA-seq data
  For further validation, DEGs identified through RNA-seq, including ABC and MFS transporter genes as well as key enzymes in methionine biosynthesis, were selected for qRT-PCR analysis. Total RNA was extracted and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit with genomic DNA Erase (TaKaRa), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Gene-specific primers for qRT-PCR are detailed in (Table S4). Amplification was conducted on the BIO-RAD CFX96 system with SYBR Green I fluorescent dye detection (TaKaRa) under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 39 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s, concluding with a final denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s. Each reaction was performed in triplicate, with BcActin serving as the internal reference gene. Threshold cycle (Ct) values were determined using default system settings, and relative gene expression levels were calculated as log2 (fold-changes) using the 2–ΔΔCt method73. Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance between means was assessed using independent t-tests (n = 3) in SPSS Version 17.0 (p < 0.05).


### Molecular docking
  Quantitative qRT-PCR analysis guided the selection of ABC and MFS transporter proteins corresponding to DEGs for further investigation. Structural predictions were performed using the AlphaFold2 platform, manually configured with the official AlphaFold2 software package (https://github.com/lucidrains/alphafold2) and relevant databases, following standard guidelines. Upon successful validation with test data, the sequences of the selected genes were used to generate homology models and construct three-dimensional protein structures.

  The two-dimensional structure of pyrimethanil was designed in ChemDraw Version 2020, followed by energy optimization using the SYBYL program. Structural integrity was verified before incorporating polar hydrogen atoms and assigning atomic charges. Energy minimization was executed using the Tripos force field with 1,000 iterations to refine molecular conformation, and the three-dimensional structure of the compound was generated through the Gasteiger-Hückel algorithm.

  The molecular pockets of transporter proteins were generated utilizing the “From Current Selection” module in Discovery Studio Version 2019, followed by molecular docking simulations via the LibDock module to model interactions between each protein and pyrimethanil. Binding modes were systematically examined, and binding energies were computed to evaluate interaction stability. Additionally, a detailed binding site map was constructed, delineating the specific amino acids contributing to these interactions.


### DNA and cDNA manipulation
  For the fungal strains HRG21 and FSG43, trace amounts of mycelium were cultured in PDB for three days, followed by filtration, drying at 37 °C, and cryogenic grinding into a fine powder. DNA extraction was performed using the D3390-1 Fungal Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (OMEGA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA was synthesized from RNA through reverse transcription using a cDNA synthesis kit (TaKaRa).

  To investigate potential mutations associated with pyrimethanil resistance, the amino acid sequences of CGS, CBL, HAT, Msr, fMsr, BcatrB, and Mrr1 from HRG21 and FSG43 were analyzed. PCR amplification was conducted using primers listed in (Table S5). The PCR protocol included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 33 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 57 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 50 s, and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. PCR products were sequenced by Beijing Qingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Sequence alignment and mutation analysis relevant to pyrimethanil resistance were performed using MEGA 7 software.
