# MD Multi-Sector
Selector: Recursive Extraction and
Refinement of Molecular Dynamics Based Sectors Yields Two Sectors
in p53 Tumor Suppressor Protein


## Abstract
  

  Allosteric signaling in proteins allows perturbations at one locale to modulate activity at an orthosteric distant site. This may explain how distal mutations disrupt protein activity and offer pathways for the development of allosteric therapeutics, a novel class of restorative compounds to reactivate native function. Despite the ubiquitous presence of allosteric control in nature and the promises that it holds for treating currently untreatable diseases, quantitative theory of the mechanism of allostery is lacking. Working to fill this critical gap, we have developed a novel method to identify groups of covarying residues which the sector hypothesis suggests are capable of transmitting allosteric signals in proteins. A major problem with sectors computed from covariance measures is the selection relies upon a full covariance matrix rather than on the covariance among the residues posited to be in the sector. We demonstrate a novel method which constructs sectors on the basis of cohesion within the residues in the sector to eliminate the incongruity between the sector idea and the way it is calculated. Furthermore, the refinement can be iteratively applied, enabling the extraction of more than one sector in a well-defined, systematic manner. In this study, we report on the development of MD multi-sector selector and its application to allosteric signaling in the tumor suppressor protein p53. We consider the implications of our findings on our long-term goal of allosterically reactivating mutant p53 as a means of curing cancer, and critically assess the broader applicability of MD multi-sector selector across diverse fields.


## Introduction
  Allosteric signaling, dubbed “the second secret of life,” refers to a perturbation at one locale that produces an effect at a distal site.1 In a classic allosteric protein, an allosteric effector binds to the protein, causing a conformational change at a remote active site. It is a pervasive phenomenon in biology used in nature as a major regulatory mechanism at the molecular level frequently used to control regulatory cascades as a way for cells to respond to their environment.2 A resurgent interest in allostery has arisen with the notion of engineering small molecules as a means of controlling protein activity.3−7 Despite the half century’s worth of research in the field of allostery since the seminal work of Jacob and Monod8 on hemoglobin, the exact mechanism by which an allosteric signal traverses a protein remains elusive. Considerable literature2,9 has been dedicated to a wide variety of models attempting to attribute contributions in a meaningful way that, for example, might lead to quantitative models enabling engineering of allosteric regulators to effect a desired outcome in tweaking the activity of an active site. The problem of allostery, in relation to rational design of protein inhibitors such as antibacterials is especially difficult for several reasons.6 First, the known active site is precisely the site not to target, and which positions will be allosterically active are not known, and second, once an allosteric site is identified, predicting binding effects is difficult. To date, predictive capabilities of such approaches remain an area of active research. Nevertheless, important groundwork has been formulated, providing promise that such a class of drugs may 1 day be rationally designed as a matter of course.

  Sectors, originating in stock market mathematics,10−13 conveys the idea that groups of covarying entities may have some predictive capabilities for a larger trend. Ranganathan and co-workers applied the idea to proteins under evolutionary selection, and it has gained traction as a way to understand allosteric signaling.10 Residues evolutionarily covarying in phylogenetic trees do so because evolutionary pressure selects for their important function, which could be, among other things, allosteric signaling pathways. The “sector hypothesis” puts forth the idea that sector residues convey allosteric information between the site of the allosteric binding and the active site. Customarily the top 20% of covarying residues, selected after a spectral analysis procedure borrowed from an algorithm used to compute sectors in the stock market,14−17 are chosen as the sector. Other methods for computing sectors have been developed, notably from molecular dynamics simulations, as is taken up below.

  Regardless of how sectors are obtained, one may then analyze them to explore the extent to which sectors explain phenomena of interest such as allosteric signaling. Existing approaches can be coarsely partitioned into two categories. First, pathway approaches seek to construct contiguous contact networks from the sector residues through a protein from the point of perturbation (binding of an allosteric effector) to an affected active site accompanied by a conformational change.5,18−21 The signal then propagates along the path of connected residues much like the domino effect. The occurrence of allostery is often detected as a conformational change, however, an increasing number of minority special cases have been observed in which no conformational change is evident,22 bringing a theoretical idea of energy landscapes from the 1980s back to attention. This Cooper and Dryden model23 posits that an allosteric signal could, in principle, propagate by changes at the energetic level. A change in the thermal dispersion about a mean structure exemplifies one way this could manifest,24−26 resulting in a change in ligand binding affinity while maintaining the same average structure. The two ways of signaling are not necessarily mutually exclusive, but historically the pathway models have been more explored, likely because of the greater ease by which a contact network and conformational changes can be conceptualized and experimentally observed.

  Once a sector is obtained, it may be studied within a variety of frameworks. One such framework is from the perspective of graph theory.27−29 Graphs allow for proteins to be represented by nodes corresponding to amino acids, and edges to represent an interaction between them. Such a representation lends itself well to considering pathways in networks. While the primary connectivity of the amino acids is trivial, one may use such representations to create contact maps of residues proximal in space, and then determine whether a contiguous pathway exists between the site of allosteric perturbation and the active site of the protein by examining whether or to what extent residues in the sector are connected in the contact map represented as a graph.

  Calling to mind the ideas of energy landscape, energetic networks represented as graphs may be well suited for capturing the complex phenomena of allostery. Studies on the large MutS protein in which the allosteric signal needs to traverse some 100 Å was an example that motivated us to more closely consider energetic networks.30 The work revealed the surprising result that an allosteric signal was able to be propagated by noncontiguous sector residues just as readily as by sector residues forming a contact network. This was measured as changes in the known essential H-bond network in both the active and allosteric sites when the sector was perturbed by mutations. This raised the question as to how residues apparently not linked into the contact network could be equally effective at procuring such a change. That an energetic network instead of a contact network is responsible is one possible explanation that hearkens back to the Cooper and Dryden idea of the energy landscape.23,30,31 Energetic interactions are more subtle and complex, but can be obtained from force fields when structural information is known, and a graph can then be constructed on the basis of one or more chosen energetic components. Because the nonbonded energetic terms are known to operate long-range, an energetic model could account for a plausible means by which a signal transcends discontinuities in contact networks. Our own recent work in this vein explored creation of networks based on the energetic interactions of residues over the course of molecular dynamics simulations.29,31,31,32 The electrostatic term emerged as having particular significance, and also has the advantage of having long-range effects, which makes intuitive sense when considering questions as to how allosteric effects could propagate in the absence of a contiguous contact network.

  Gaining a greater understanding of how allosteric networks operate could be of paramount importance to medicine, especially for drugging currently undruggable targets.6,7,9,32,33 Allosteric drugs are a way to modulate an active site. Many classical small molecules operate by halting function by occluding an active site. Antibacterials are a key example of this mechanism. From the design point of view, allosteric targeting poses considerably more difficult problems.6 It requires procurement of a binding site for which all that is known is that it can not be the primary active site, and modulation, not disruption, of the activity must be achieved. The sector hypothesis may help with the identification of network residues to target allosteric points of control; it is the most promising lead forward. The use of networks to represent allosteric proteins coupled with network theory constructed from sectors derived from molecular dynamics simulations of proteins provide an enticing way forward to involve machine learning techniques.29,33 We are currently working on generative models to create drugs using such information.

  The tumor suppressor protein p53, the model system for this study, is an allosteric protein involved in the life or death decision making regarding response to DNA insult35−37 (Figure 1). The full-length protein is a 393 amino acid transcription factor with three domains: N- and C-terminal disordered regions, and a β barrel globular DNA binding domain, with the disordered regions accounting for roughly half of the protein. As a transcription factor, it binds to promoters of genes, bringing about apoptosis or DNA repair. The crystal structure PDBID 1TUP34 details the DNA binding domain interface interacting with a cognate DNA sequence. Mutations to this protein from human cancers are catalogued and monitored in an ongoing basis;38−40 p53 mutations account for 50% or more of all human cancers. Hotspot mutations map almost exclusively to the DNA binding domain. While some can be rationalized because of their proximal location to the interface, the majority do not. Thus, we cast the problem loosely as allostery, where the mutations are an allosteric effector modulating the p53-DNA binding interface.

  It follows that curing cancer thus becomes a problem of restoring the original binding interface activity. We hypothesize this could be achieved through the introduction of a second allosteric effector, a drug that would counterbalance the disruption within the network, suggesting sectors could play a yet unrealized role in curing this currently uncurable disease. In fact, a precedence for this mechanism in p53 exists. The Y220C hotspot mutation is a unique example in that it is distal from the binding interface and has been counteracted in vitro and in rodent models by the PK11000 compound to the point of tumor eradication.41 Unfortunately, it is not a viable cure for human use because it covalently bonds to cysteines, opening the possibility of dangerous side effects. Nevertheless, it is the single most important case study in allosteric reactivation of p53 for use in de novo allosteric drug design for reactivation of p53.32 In the absence of at least an operational model of allostery, discovery of a new compound is largely relegated to luck in high throughput screens.42,43 We view sectors as opportune to drive a parallel rational design approach,4,6,33 heightening the importance of developing a practical and theoretically sound means of identifying sector residues in proteins.

  While the phylogenetic approach of Ranganathan et al.13 has advanced the idea of sectors in macromolecules considerably, one shortcoming could be that phylogenetic covariance could reflect results of a mixture of pressures, rather than being reflective of properties of sectors alone. As an alternative means of producing and analyzing sectors to the original phylogenetic approach, Beveridge and co-workers put forth the formulation of molecular dynamics-based sectors that are calculated on the basis of time-averaged motional correlation of residues due to thermal fluctuation as captured by molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. Such models focus on a single molecule having a single sequence, thus eliminating noise that may be present in the genetic approach. MD sectors have been formulated in several different ways. In the first version which we presently refer to as MDS1.0,44 consistent with the notation introduced by Fabry et al.,45 the pairwise distance covariance matrix is computed from coordinate positions of the residues, usually the alpha carbons, over time as ascertained from MD simulations. Two residues are deemed to covary if the positions deviate in any direction over the course of the trajectory, excluding the trivial case of covalently bonded residues. Following the example of the original SCA procedure,10 a spectral decomposition is applied; the rationale for this is to compute the global covariance as distinct from simple pairwise covariance, thereby reporting on how the residue behaves relative to the general corpus of residues. The idea had been borrowed from the financial world where a financial sector reports on a group of businesses relative to the stock market in general.15−17 The resulting matrix is then decomposed into a product of its diagonal eigenvalue matrix and its corresponding eigenvector matrices1where MCM is the motion correlation matrix, D is the diagonal eigenvalue matrix, and V is the eigenvector matrix corresponding to D, and VT is the transpose of V. The resulting eigenvalues represent the magnitude of information obtained from the procedure. That is, the larger the eigenvalue, the more important the linear combinations of variables that created it. The subsequent step involves independent component analysis (ICS) to test for statistical independence between the residues. The eigenvalues are the input to the ICS, which returns Vpica values, the metric ultimately used for selecting membership in a sector. Residues are rank ordered according to their Vpica value, and the top 20% are chosen. Detailed testing across all possible cutoffs confirmed this was a reasonable and robust choice45 allowing for comparison with other sector literature adopting a similar approach. The major difference between SCA sectors and MDS1.0 is the source of the covariance: SCA sectors derive from covariance of many related protein sequences varying in phylogenetic trees over evolutionary time, whereas MDS1.0 sectors originate from motional covariance of a single protein sequence in MD simulations. MD sectors provide a means of rooting the sector in Boltzmann statistical mechanics, with the convenient benefit of being able to seamlessly integrate with Markov State Model theory to gain insight into the conformational dynamics of the thermally accessible substates.

  MDS2.0, the second version of MD based sectors,45 ushered in some relatively minor changes to the algorithm and major improvement to the code base. The procedure in MDS1.0 involved the manual step of removal of a few residues as had been done in the original sequence based SCA due to issues with properties of the alignments; the procedure had been adapted for MD based on the range of motion for MDS1.0 to be as closely similar to SCA sectors as possible. However, we deemed this step to be obsolete since MDs are based on a single sequence, not an alignment, and we preferred to report on the contributions of all residues. While developing the more automated and streamlined code of MDS2.0, the step of manually removing residues from consideration was thus abandoned. A comparison of results from identical trajectories confirmed the results remained essentially the same as the MDS1.0 while producing much more user-friendly and less error prone code.

  A major deficit in the sectors field was that the idea that the residues were forming a cohesive network had never definitively been proven. However, a quantitative means of measuring cohesion was developed, and it was confirmed that, for the cases tested by calculations involving the Vpica value, a term directly related to the aforementioned matrix decomposition, residues in the MD sector(? check) do in fact form networks significantly more cohesive than residues not in the network.45 The cohesion values used to categorize the residues need to be computed by an all-by-all interaction matrix, and the measures of cohesion are measured by the partition of the residues into the top 20% sector and remaining nonsector residues. However, the question remained whether a calculation of the residue membership considering the partitioned subsets could provide more precise information regarding the cohesion; a residue could be miscategorized due to good general correlation to all residues rather than good correlation to the specific sector.

  Given the finding that calculation of residue cohesion based on membership in the sector or nonsector provided useful information regarding the sector, we envisioned an algorithm that iteratively refined the sector membership by swapping borderline cases that had been calculated based on cohesion to all residues rather than just to the sector, which has been manifested as the subject of this paper. With the new iterative refinement algorithm in MD multi-sector selector (MD-MSS, Sectors 3.0) presented herein, we also realized that the algorithm could be recursively applied, potentially giving rise to multiple sectors in the same system. Concomitantly, we have moved the implementation to operate in the high performance computing environment and interface cpptraj,46,47 a powerful trajectory analysis toolbox part of the AMBER suite of programs that is freely available to process simulation trajectories from all major molecular simulation software packages, thus opening its use to a wider audience of practitioners. The covariance is obtained from MD trajectories as a standard cpptraj covariance matrix, which is then decomposed for eigenvalues and eigenvectors to procure the iteratively refined sectors. We report on its application to our latest p53 Tumor Suppressor Protein simulations comparing between wild type, Y220C hotspot mutant, and PK11000 bound rescued mutant simulations. These are of paramount interest because this collection of constructs serves as the key proof of concept for allosteric restoration of proteins. We consider the significance of the finding of multiple sectors in the context of our drug design initiatives.


## Methods

### Molecular Dynamics Simulations of p53 WT, Y220C, and PK11000
Constructs
  MD simulations provide an atomistic level detail of the structure and dynamics of macromolecules consistent with the thermal dispersion of the Boltzmann ensemble of structures.48−50 The AMBER suite of programs has undergone development and testing for several decades, and is now available for use on graphical processing units (GPU), making simulations reaching the microsecond time scale achievable on a routine basis.50−52

  MD simulations were performed on three p53 DNA bound models constructed from wild type p53 DNA binding domain (residues 96 to 289 inclusive) bound to DNA (PDB ID 1TUP(34)) and Y220C mutated DNA binding domain bound to the Y220C compound (PDB ID 5LAP(41)). All contain the identical DNA sequence. Mutagenesis of p53 wild type to Y220C was performed in the tLEaP module in AMBER.

  The protonation states of titratable side chains was computed using H++ and those results were used to match to the corresponding AMBER residue type.53 The program also screens for side chain conformations of HIS, GLN and ASN. p53 is highly sensitive to the protonation state of histidines (delta protonated versus epsilon protonated which correspond respectively to AMBER types HID and HIE). We did observe some variation of H++ protonation states of p53 with other starting structures with only minor differences in coordinates from folded starting structures for other ongoing projects in the lab. Folding algorithms had no knowledge of the zinc cofactor, which was likely the source of the deviant structure, leading H++ to suggest a different protonation state. We observed some local unfolding with these simulations inconsistent with the core domain found in the crystal structure. Thus, we paid particularly close attention to protonation state choice. For this project we ultimately chose to work with protonation states consistent with the crystal structure, which more accurately reflected the spatial constraints imposed by the binding of the essential zinc. We also used variants of cysteines to correctly coordinate zinc (CY2) commensurate with the ZAFF parameter requirements described below and to complex with the PK11000 drug (CYX), when applicable. The amino acids in question and the AMBER residue types chosen for this study are as follows: HIE_115, HIE_168, CY2_176, HIE_178, HE1_179, CYX_182 (for the covalent bond with the drug in the PK11000 simulation, C182 otherwise), HIE_193, HIE_214, HIE_233, CY2_238, CY2_242. Residue His_214 could either be in the epsilon protonated state (HIE_214) or the delta protonated state (HID_214) without consequence, but for consistency with previous studies,32 we retained HIE_214.

  The p53 protein contains an obligatory zinc cofactor. The zinc was parametrized using the Zinc Amber Force Field (ZAFF);54 PK11000 was parametrized using the General Amber Force Field (GAFF).55 Using tLEaP, the Zinc was complexed to residues CY2_176, HE1_179, CY2_238, and CY2_242, consistent with ZAFF parameter usage. Similarly, PK11000 was covalently bonded to residue CYX_182 in tLEaP. The ions08 model56 was used to handle monovalent counterions. The ff14SB58 force field was used for the p53 protein, and TIP3P59 water model was used for the water molecules. The three systems were solvated with an octahedral water box extending a minimum of 12 Å from the solute with periodic boundary conditions, and monovalent counterions were added to electroneutrality.

  Simulation was carried out within AMBER22. Because the project involved structural engineering, we erred on the side of caution with extended energy minimization and equilibration steps and treated all constructs consistently. Energy minimization was performed with 500 steps of steepest descent followed by conjugate gradient for the remainder of 2000 cycles with constraints of 100, 75, 65, 55, 45, 30, 20, 15, 10, 5, and 1 kcal/mol, respectively, throughout. Next, the systems were heated to 300 K which was maintained with Berendsen thermocoupling to a heat bath.60 Equilibration occurred over six rounds of 2 ns each with decreasing restraints of 100, 50, 25, 10, 5, and 1 kcal/mol, respectively. Finally, 1 μs of neutral dynamics was performed on each construct using the parallelized CUDA61 version of particle mesh Ewald molecular dynamics (PMEMD)51 on NVIDIA Graphical Processing Units (GPUs) on Wesleyan University’s High Performance Computing Cluster (HPCC).62 SHAKE was implemented to dampen high frequency hydrogen motions.63 Results from the MD simulations were used to develop the new MD sectors algorithm presented in this paper.


### Trajectory Analysis
  Molecular Visualization of trajectories is carried out using the visual molecular dynamics (VMD)64 program and PyMol.65 Extraction of the correlation matrices was performed with cpptraj46,47 matrix correl and matrix dist commands found in AmberTools of AMBER22. MDS3.0 post analysis scripts were developed in the Anaconda package66 Spyder integrated development environment running python3.11 using the extensions pandas,67 seaborn,68 and matplotlib69 to generate the heatmaps, further correlation, and the sector calculation. The Python script is available in the Supporting Information. After transferring the correlation matrix to a Lenovo Thinkpad P17 with intel core i7 CPU, the local machine, serial process sector analysis of the data for 1 μs of MD completed in about 4 min.


## Results
  Applying the MDS3.0 methodology enables the discovery of sectors in existing and well-known proteins, primarily the DNA binding domain of p53. Systematic approaches to sector definitions allows for replicable results that can be applied to multiple different MD trajectories. Any trajectory can be inputted to discover a cohesive network of proteins. For the current study, three well converged MD trajectories of p53, the tumor suppressor protein, are used to illustrate the calculation of molecular dynamics based sectors (Figure S1). Specifically, the set of three key constructs illustrating proof of concept of rescue are studied: WT, the wild type human p53 DNA binding domain; Y220C, the p53 protein containing the Y220C distal hotspot mutation; and PK11000, the Y220C system bound to the restorative PK11000 compound. We now present the new procedure MD-MSS to obtain one or more sectors of covarying residues as determined by the motional covariance of protein alpha carbons over time from a well converged MD trajectory.


### Calculation of the Correlation and Distance Matrices
  The cpptraj47 program within AMBERTOOLS from the AMBER22 package was used to carry out the distance calculations and correlation calculation; The matrix command was used with dist and correl respectively. For a protein containing N residues, the pairwise correlation matrix C is computed with correl based on correlation c of the pairwise the motions of the alpha carbons for all pairs excluding itself and the immediately connected residues in the 1° protein sequence2

  For these special cases, the correlation is set to 0 with postprocessing. The correl function reports the extent of pairwise correlation cij between the N residues. Similarly, the distance matrix D reports the time averaged pairwise Euclidean distance di,j between the N alpha carbons in Angstroms3

  A sample correlation matrix is shown in the Supporting Information, which shows the values in tabular form, which are then used to construct the heatmaps.

  The heatmap representation of the 196 × 196 motion correlation matrix shows the correlation for the p53 WT construct (N = 196) (Figure 2A) with the trivial case of bonded residues excluded. The corresponding heatmaps for Y220C and PK11000 were also computed (Figure 2B,C). The darkest regions represent pairs of residues that residues are most highly correlated with one another. This pairwise correlation matrix formulates the basis from which the Vpica values used to compute the sector are quantitatively obtained.


### Rank Ordering Correlation of Residues
  The correlation matrix C is then processed to assess a correlation score for each residue. The procedure described by Fabry and Thayer is used.45 Briefly, a covariance score of each residue is ascertained by computing the total covariance score of the residue to all other residues, which is equivalent to summing each row i (or equivalently each column j) to obtain the zeroth order Vpica values which is a column vector4

  The residues n ∈ N can then be rank ordered from greatest to least on the basis of the scores Si. It also follows that the 2-dimensional correlation matrix C could also be rank ordered along both of its axes such that the symmetry of the matrix is preserved. Thus, there exists a rank order mapping operator M that maps C to a reordered matrix R containing the same cell values ci,j of C, but in a noncontiguous order with respect to the original indices of matrix C. The new matrix R thus has the property that the sum of its sequential rows decreases5

  In other words, the operator M contains the mapping of the indices from C to R such that R is rank ordered. The top 20% most correlated residues are defined to be the zeroth order sector, formed on the basis of the correlation of the motions of the residues to all other residues, found at the upper left.

  Figure 3 represents the rank ordered matrix R for the WT, Y220C and PK11000 constructs, with the darkest and most correlated values having clustered around the top left of the matrix. The complete set of residues in rank order for each of the three constructs is provided in the Supporting Information. The initial sector is taken to be the top 20% of these residues in rank order. Note we report here the positions mapped back to p53 standard numbering for the DBD of p53. At this point in the calculation, the overall Vpica value of the sector, which is the first 20% of columns, and first 20% of rows is 25.432.

  In the next iteration, the correlation calculation is repeated, but instead of computing over the entire data set, it is computed among the residues within the sector only. If T is the total set of residues, we define S as the top 20% of the rank ordered matrix6

  Then we recalculate the Vpica on S17

  These represent the Vpica values for the residues in the sector as they interact with each other. The Vpica values for the residues not in the sector is also calculated to represent the interaction of residues that are not in the sector.

  We choose the residues not in S1, that is, N1 = T\S1, the bottom 80% of the rank ordered residues, and again calculate that Vpica.8

  Sample calculations of the procedure are provided in the Supporting Information. Indeed, some residue swaps were retained, revising the sector content such that the residues that are most cohesively covarying among themselves are selected without regard to the extent of correlation to nonsector residues. The new MD sectors algorithm can successfully differentiate between residues that are not just simply correlated well with the entire protein overall, but can select residues that are highly correlated with just the sector itself.

  The recalculation of the coherence follows from the Vpica ratio analysis (VRA).45 The total set of residues T is then divided into the sets S, the sector residues, and N, the nonsector residues. S and N partition T, that is, T = S∪N and S∩N = Ø, and they are chosen by their rank order in R; the number of residues equal to 20% of the number of residues in T are taken in rank order from R. Fabry’s Vpica_in ref (45) is then calculated as the Vpica among residues in the sector set S, reporting on the strength of the interaction of those residues within the sector and within the nonsector. This Vpica value differs from Vpica0 in that it reports on the interaction of the residues within the sector among themselves, and the interaction of the residues among the other nonsector residues. The procedure excludes the cross-terms involving residue correlation interactions between the newly formed sets. The final Vpica value after the final rearrange is 27.785, which is a large improvement because in comparison with the overall correlation of the entire matrix which is 27.800 indicates that the top 20% of residues is almost responsible for the entire positive value of the matrix since it is comprised of both positive and negative correlation values between residues.

  What we noticed when reshuffling the matrices was that while the sector clustered to the upper left, another cluster seemed to emerge in the bottom right (Figure 3). We hypothesized that it could be another sector, disparate from the first one since the residues were doing something quite different from the first set. To test this, we removed the entries from the designated sector 1, and reprocessed the remaining data. In other words, the bottom 80% of the sector residues were processed recursively for the presence of a sector. To seed the process which should have the sector in the upper left, the matrix was rotated to begin the iterative refinement for this sector. The resulting second sector is shown in Figure 4. The improvement is apparent; for example, the overall correlation of the new matrix is 15.623, with the first sector before rearranging being a correlation of 15.269.

  After a second iteration through the MD sectors algorithm, the Vpica value of the sector prior to refinement was 15.623 which improves to a value of 21.141 to indicate greater cohesion. Once again, positive correlation dominates over the negative values. What is of interest, however, is that once the second sector is rearranged, highly correlated pairwise residues still appear on both the top left and bottom right of the rearranged correlation matrix. The new MD sectors algorithm not only points at the potential for a second sector, but the potential of many sectors past the first and second.

  One question that emerged was whether the second sector may be due to anti-correlated motions; if this were the case, one could call to question the legitimacy of deeming it to be truly independent. To test this, we repeated the analysis on the raw data without absolute value of correlations taken, and repeated the refinement process, extracted the first sector, and repeated to extract the second sector. Figure 5 shows the resulting matrices.

  We also wished to consider whether the correlations were simply identifying spatially proximal residues. We generated the average distance matrices over the MD simulation corresponding to the residues in the various stages of rearrangement and sorted them commensurately with each of the matrices so the patterns would be directly comparable. Figure 6 shows the example of this for the second sector of WT, to be compared with the upper right 20% of Figure 4A, the sector colorized by correlation. Distances range from 0 Å along the diagonal to about 40 Å, which approximates the diameter of the protein. Strong correlations between residues separated by considerable distances are present. The contrasting red and blue hues in the heatmap underscore the broad spectrum of distance values among residues within the selected sector of the wildtype p53 protein. This variability highlights the presence of highly correlated residues across the protein, despite differences in their spatial proximity.

  In summary, the overall correlation of WT is 27.800, Y220C is 8.542, and PK11000 is 9.435. High correlation overall corresponds with more positive values around the matrix generally, whereas the lower overall correlation values means that there is a higher variance of positive and negative values. In trajectories with lower overall correlation, the MD sectors algorithm is generally more beneficial, because instead of taking average Vpica values of each column, and ranking the sector in that order, rearranging the matrix can reveal much more highly correlated residues than may be apparent by inspection alone.

  In the case of WT, Y220C, and PK11000, it is found that after the trajectories are run through the MD sectors algorithm, the overall correlation of the sector for WT is 27.785, for Y220C it is 25.640, and for PK11000 it is 21.325. In the case of simply taking average correlation of the entire matrix, as compared to the sector, it seems that mutant and rescued p53 stand to gain the most from multiple sector detection, whereas the wildtype’s sector correlation reveals that the top 20% of residues accounts for a large majority of the positive correlation in the matrix. This is also true for the Y220C and PK11000; however, since the overall correlation is lower, the sector accounts for a larger proportion of the positive correlation in their respective trajectories.

  Past overall correlation between the trajectory and sector, another area of interest is how the residues themselves become selected and rearranged into the sector. Before the algorithm reaches convergence and stops, in all of the example trajectories, the residues that belong in the sector after the first rearrange vary, as the algorithm is still trying to find the residues with the highest Vpica values. However, once the program starts to reach convergence, it is found that the residues that belong within the sector are largely the same, with the first seven almost always being the first seven residues of the matrix. However, after the first seven residues of the sector have passed, which residues belong in the matrix starts to vary more and more. The residues are frequently already what would be considered the top 20% of the matrix, however the residues are ranked differently in terms of order.

  These results are to be expected, because in the rearranged heatmaps, the areas of highest correlation are the top left and the bottom right of the matrices, so to have a residue that is not in one of these areas belonging to the sector would be unexpected. The reason for the sectors always appearing in the top left of the matrix after becoming rearranged is because the algorithm scans the top 20% of columns for what residues would belong in the sector, as the algorithm is comparing every residue in the matrix for the correlation of if the selected residue were in the sector.

  In the p53 trajectories, the new MD sectors algorithm offers up many potential sectors of interest for these proteins in a purely mathematical and replicative way. MD sectors provides great promise in identifying allosteric pathways within a protein, directly applicable in the study of p53 hotspot mutations but also applicable to a wide variety of allosteric systems.

## Discussion
  MD-MSS is a new method allowing for the identification of multiple sectors in a quantitative way for the first time. This follows up on one of the key advancements introduced by Fabry et al.,45 namely the quantitative assessment of sector cohesion, which had previously been hypothesized but not formalized mathematically. Fabry’s methodology established the importance of calculating residue covariance across the entire system to identify putative sectors. However, their approach inherently relies on identifying an initial “zeroth order” sector, which can overlook residues that might exhibit strong covariance with members of a sector but poor covariance overall. This limitation suggests the need for a systematic algorithm to refine sectors iteratively, enhancing their cohesiveness by incorporating such residues. Our study addresses this gap by presenting and validating such an algorithm.

  Furthermore, we extend the conceptual framework by uncovering and mathematically formalizing the presence of multiple cohesive sectors within a system. While the notion that more than one sector could exist has been proposed, no previous work has provided an algorithmic approach to identify additional sectors and quantitatively assess their cohesion. The development and application of this algorithm to identify and characterize multiple sectors constitute the second major novel contribution of this study. Together, these advances significantly enhance our understanding of sector behavior and provide a robust computational framework for future investigations.

  MD-MSS also represents an important advance toward realizing de novo design of allosteric therapeutics. In this work we have detailed the methodology as applied to the tumor suppressor protein p53. The refinement procedure of MD-MSS identified a more cohesive sector than the covariance method alone. Furthermore, iteratively applying the method allowed for the identification of a second sector. It provides a means of quantifying sectors through replicable means, and discover the potential for not one, but potentially many sectors existing within proteins. The current procedure also moved the calculation pipeline to utilizing cpptraj from the AMBER suite, and python code, all of which are freely available and work seamlessly with all major simulation formats.

  To understand whether identified sectors in fact included residues known to be points of control, the sectors were annotated with the hotspots being bolded and the DNA contacting residues underlined.

  One may consider how many sectors is sufficient to extract. Two sectors were selected for their reliability of having high enough correlation values to become feasible sections of data for analysis. Once a sector is obtained, residue sectors can be used to predict how areas of a protein correlate and interact with one another. The idea of interaction between sections of a protein is especially interesting for allostery, where when adding drugs to mutant strains of p53, one can compare the sectors of WT, Y220C, and PK11000 p53 trajectories. The sectors can be used to determine how correlated each trajectory is, and whether residues within the protein differ depending on the construct of p53 being tested. This is a significant advancement made by the MDS3.0 algorithm; Figure 8 shows a Venn diagram of sector residues for each construct. In the case of WT, a quick preliminary calculation showed that overall, after taking the absolute value of the entire trajectory’s correlation and dividing it by the number of residues, the MD sectors algorithm finds that the first sector selected is responsible for about 38% of the wildtype p53’s overall correlation which is significant because it comprises only 20% of the residues.

  The MDS3.0 also offers multiple methods of quantifying sectors, allowing for either the absolute value of a correlation matrix being taken and calculated, or for the algorithm to distinguish between positive and negative correlations within the protein being analyzed. Multiple methods allow for more in-depth analysis, with slight variations between each method due to how sectors become quantified. Another discovery of the MDS3.0 algorithm is its further implications for the study of the p53, specifically the sectors of a Wildtype p53, and a mutant p53 with the PK11000 drug for rescue.   The common residues between the sectors of WT and PK11000 trajectories processed via MDS3.0 are shown. The residues in common may serve as a starting point for potential investigation into research of the Y220C mutation, and how rescued p53 differs from its wildtype counterpart.   Overall, 26 sector residues change from the average rearrange, to the final rearrange. The rank order of residues displays the potential of MDS3.0 (Figure 9).

  Another finding is that after adding the mutation and the drug PK11000, the Vpica values of the PK11000 trajectory drop from the Vpica values of the WT. There seems to be a higher variance of correlation within the Y220C and PK11000 trajectories, with the highest correlated values that end up within the sector, being lower than the values found in WT. The exact reason behind this observation requires further research outside the scope of this paper, but the point being presented is that these substantial observations can be discovered through the updated MD sectors algorithm. Understanding which residues may belong in a sector, and how correlated each residue is to one another may shed light on how residues within a protein react to mutations, and drugs that attempt to rescue the mutated proteins.

  Distance matrices also display an interesting finding between sector residues. The distance between sector residues has a range from 0 to 40 Å, which suggests that the MDS3.0 algorithm is capable of identifying non-specially proximal groupings of residues.

  Understanding sectors within a protein that regulates the important process of cell division has major implications within drug discovery. Although the three trajectories of focus in the paper are of great importance to research of allostery in the context of p53 rescue, the algorithm can be applied to any protein and draw sectors based purely on the inputted AMBER trajectory and desired metric. Because of the versatility of metrics presented in the MDS3.0 algorithm, applications of sector analysis can have benefits to any protein study. Understanding in general how areas of a protein interact with one another can shed light on a vast variety of implications and observations.


## Conclusions
  We have advanced the study of allostery in proteins by introducing the MD-MSS procedure which quantitatively generates and refines sectors of residues. We have demonstrated the procedure on the basis of motional correlations, but this could well be carried out for any pairwise matrix. Furthermore, the algorithm is capable of recursively procuring multiple sectors through iterative refinement. Our sectors identify many important residues in the protein, including hotspot mutations, the PK11000 binding site, and residues directly contacting the DNA, suggesting that the method is successful in capturing features of interest to allosteric signaling in proteins. This is of key significance because identification of residues involved in allosteric control is of paramount importance for the generation of allosteric drugs, particularly when an allosteric binding site is not known. Here, we have demonstrated the utility of the algorithm on the p53 protein with the test case of the Y220C mutant rescued by PK11000. Our results suggest that in cases where an allosteric site is not known, sector analysis may provide candidate binding sites. This procedure can be used for any allosteric system of interest, and provides a way forward for the development of therapeutics for other currently undruggable targets, and may well also find a role in engineering of allosteric systems to procure new functionalities.
