# The yeast CST and Polα/primase complexes act in concert to ensure proper telomere maintenance and protection


## Abstract
  Polα/primase (PP), the polymerase that initiates DNA synthesis at replication origins, also completes the task of genome duplication by synthesizing the telomere C-strand under the control of the CTC1/CDC13-STN1-TEN1 (CST) complex. Using cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of the human CST-Polα/primase-DNA complex as guides in conjunction with AlphaFold modeling, we identified structural elements in yeast CST and PP that promote complex formation. Mutating these structures in Candida glabrata Stn1, Ten1, Pri1, and Pri2 abrogated the stimulatory activity of CST on PP in vitro, supporting the functional relevance of the physical contacts in cryo-EM structures as well as the conservation of mechanisms between yeast and humans. Introducing these mutations into C. glabrata yielded two distinct groups of mutants. One group exhibited progressive, telomerase-dependent telomere elongation without evidence of DNA damage. The other manifested slow growth, telomere length heterogeneity, single-stranded DNA accumulation and elevated C-circles, which are indicative of telomere deprotection. These telomere deprotection phenotypes are altered or suppressed by mutations in multiple DNA damage response (DDR) and DNA repair factors. We conclude that in yeast, the telomerase inhibition and telomere protection function previously ascribed to the CST complex are mediated jointly by both CST and Polα/primase, highlighting the critical importance of a replicative DNA polymerase in telomere regulation.


## Introduction
  Linear eukaryotic chromosomes are capped at their ends by special nucleoprotein structures called telomeres, which promote genome integrity by stabilizing the ends against aberrant re-arrangements [1, 2]. Formation of the telomere nucleoprotein complexes is nucleated by numerous copies of a short repeat sequence, which recruit DNA binding proteins and additional factors to execute the end protection function. Aside from end protection, telomeres need to minimize and compensate for DNA loss incurred due to the failure of the replication machinery to fully copy the DNA template at chromosome ends, i.e. the end replication problem [3–5]. Accordingly, eukaryotic cells have evolved specialized DNA synthesis complexes and regulatory proteins to add telomere DNA onto chromosome ends.

  The two polymerases responsible for adding telomere repeats are telomerase and Polα/primase (PP), which sequentially elongate the two strands of telomeres [6, 7]. First, the telomerase reverse transcriptase uses an embedded segment of a stably associated RNA subunit as template to extend the 3′-end bearing, G-rich strand. The extended G-strand is then converted to double-stranded DNA by PP, a bi-functional enzyme with both primase and DNA polymerase activities [8]. Even at telomeres not extended by telomerase, the replication process results in sizable G-strand single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) that needs to be converted to duplex DNA [3]. PP “fills-in” the telomere C-strand by synthesizing an RNA oligomer using the primase subunits (Pri1 and Pi2) and then extending the RNA using the DNA polymerase (Pol1 and Pol12). Notably, PP is not only essential for telomere maintenance but also for chromosomal replication; it functions as the initiator protein by synthesizing short RNA-DNA oligomers that serve as primers for extension by both the leading and lagging strand polymerases [9]. While the enzymatic activities of PP in telomere maintenance and chromosomal replication are identical, they are regulated through different mechanisms. In genome replication, PP is assembled into the replisome through interaction with the CMG complex [10, 11], whereas at telomeres, PP is physically and functionally coupled to the CST complex [12–14].

  CST (CTC1-STN1-TEN1 in metazoans, plants and some fungi; Cdc13-Stn1-Ten1 in budding yeasts) is an evolutionarily malleable, replication protein A (RPA)-like complex that plays critical roles in telomere regulation; it terminates telomerase-mediated G-strand synthesis and promotes C-strand synthesis by stimulating PP [7, 13, 14]. That is, CST prevents excessive telomere elongation and ensures the addition of double-stranded telomere repeats to chromosome ends. Mutations in CST subunits have been shown to trigger aberrant telomere lengthening and/or shortening depending on the context and the nature of the mutations [15–17]. However, the precise mechanisms of CST in the telomere maintenance pathway remain poorly understood. One unresolved issue is the potential participation of PP in terminating telomerase activity, with some studies indicating that CST alone is sufficient, whilst others supporting the additional requirement for PP [18–20]. Beyond regulating telomere DNA synthesis, the budding yeast CST also plays an essential role in telomere protection; all three subunits are essential and hypomorphic alleles have been shown to trigger DNA damage response and aberrant repair [21–25]. This additional function of yeast CST may have evolved to compensate for the loss of shelterin proteins, which comprise the major protective complex at telomeres in the great majority of eukaryotes [26].

  From the mechanistic perspective, the C-strand synthesis reaction compels interests on its own. Like the PP reaction in chromosomal replication, the enzyme undergoes multiple conformational transitions in order to execute (i) RNA synthesis initiation and elongation, (ii) the handoff of the RNA from the primase active site to the DNA polymerase active site, as well as (iii) the initiation of DNA synthesis and DNA chain elongation [27]. While it is clear that both mammalian and yeast CST can substantially stimulate the synthesis of RNA-DNA products by PP [28, 29], whether and how CST modulates the individual steps of this reaction remain poorly understood. In this regard, even though the budding yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae has offered many insights on telomere regulation in the past three-plus decades [30], it has presented notable challenges in exploring the molecular mechanisms of CST-PP. Most importantly, the ScCST complex has been refractory to recombinant expression and purification, making it difficult to investigate the CST-PP-mediated reactions in vitro. To overcome this challenge, we established an alternative co-expression/purification system for this complex in Candida glabrata [31]. We further purified CgPP and showed that CST stimulates PP by enhancing both the priming and the primase-to-polymerase switch reactions [28]. We also analyzed the determinants of multimerization in CgCST and showed that both the OB1 and OB2 domains of CgCdc13 can promote the formation of higher order complexes [32].

  Herein we report our continued investigation of CgCST-PP. As a point of departure, we leveraged the recent cryo-EM structures of the human CST-PP-DNA pre-initiation complex (PIC), which reveal multiple contacts between CST, PP, and the DNA template [33]. In combination with AlphaFold modeling and comparative sequence analysis, we identified surface residues in CgStn1, Ten1, Pri1, and Pri2 that are likely to promote PIC formation. Indeed, mutations of these residues were found to severely impair the ability of CST to stimulate PP activity in vitro. In addition, the corresponding mutations in Stn1, Ten1, and Pri1 were found to disrupt telomere regulation in vivo, resulting in two distinct phenotypes. One class of mutants exhibited progressive telomere lengthening without evidence of telomere DNA damage, whereas the other class manifested slow growth and multiple telomere aberrations indicative of telomere deprotection. DNA repair factors including Rad52, Rad24, and Exo1 were shown to modulate the growth and telomere defects in the telomere deprotection mutants. Collectively, our findings support the notion that besides C-strand synthesis, the yeast CST and PP complexes act jointly to inhibit telomerase and protect telomeres, underscoring the multiple functions of a replicative DNA polymerase in telomere regulation.


## Materials and methods

### Candida glabrata strain construction and growth
  Standard protocols were employed for the genetic manipulation of C. glabrata. All strains used in this study are derived from the BG14 strain background [34] and are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

  The exo1Δ, rad52Δ, rad24Δ and tertΔ disruption strains were constructed by integrating an NATR cassette flanked by ∼600–700 bp of 5′ UTR and 3′ UTR derived from the corresponding genes [35]. The UTR fragments were generated by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and cloned in between the SacII and HpaI sites, as well as between the AatII and PvuII/BsiWI sites of pBV65. The cassettes were then released from the plasmid via AatII and HpaI cleavage and used in transformation. Correct clones were selected on media containing 100 μg/ml Nourseothricin and identified by PCR genotyping. The FLAG3-tagged TEN gene was constructed by cloning the coding region upstream of the FLAG3 tag in between the BamHI and NotI sites of pSMT3-FLAG3 [36]. The tagged gene was then re-amplified with appropriate primers and cloned in between the XbaI and XhoI sites of pCU-PDC1, pCU-HHT2, and pCU-EGD2 plasmids. PCR mutagenesis was used to generate the various Ten1 mutant expression plasmids. The same strategy was used for the construction of expression plasmids for FLAG3-tagged Pri1 and Pri2 except that the genes were cloned into the pCN-PDC1 vector. The primers used for these constructions are listed in Supplementary Table S2. The Myc5-tagged Ten1 expression plasmids were constructed by replacing the FLAG3-tag of pCU-PDC1-Ten1-FG3 with a Myc5-tag using the NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly protocol: three oligonucleotides for the Myc5-tag and a PCR product from pCU-PDC1-Ten1-FG3 were used in assembly. The primers used for these constructions are all listed in Supplementary Table S2. The resulting Myc5-tagged Ten1 plasmids were transformed into a strain with FLAG3-tagged Pri1 to allow for co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) and chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) analysis.

  For growth analysis of the mutants, liquid cultures of C. glabrata strains were first diluted to OD600 of 1.0 (corresponding to ∼3 × 107 cells/ml) and subjected to serial six-fold dilutions. Four microliters of the diluted cultures were then spotted onto semi-solid media and grown at 30°C for 1–2 days prior to image acquisition. To monitor growth rate during continuous passage in liquid media, 1.5 ml cultures of the strains were inoculated at a starting OD of 0.001 and grown for 22 h at 30°C. The population doublings (PDs) were determined and the cells were used to inoculate fresh media for the next round of growth. The growth rates during passage were presented as [generation time (h)]−1.


### Analysis of telomere lengths and structures
  Southern analysis of telomere restriction fragments (TRFs) was performed using DNAs treated with EcoRI or EcoRI plus AluI as described previously [32]. The blots were hybridized to an oligonucleotide containing three copies of the C. glabrata C-strand repeat (CgTEL-C3; [GCACCCAGACCCCACA]3). For calculation of average TRF lengths, we used the formula mean TRF length = Σ (ODi × Li)/Σ (ODi) because the majority of TRF length variation stems from differences in subtelomeric lengths [37, 38]. The in-gel hybridization assay for detecting G-strand ssDNA has been described [39]. The dried gels were hybridized to a 24-mer probe (CgTEL-C1.5) corresponding to 1.5 copies of the C. glabrata C-strand repeat. C-circle and G-circle assays were performed as before except that deoxynucleotides triphosphates needed for the C. glabrata G-strand and C-strand synthesis were used, respectively [40].


### Protein expression and purification
  The C. glabrata CST, ST and PP complexes were obtained via recombinant co-expression of the relevant subunits in Escherichia coli and affinity purification. For CST, E. coli BL21 (DE3) was co-transformed with pACYC-SUMO-Cdc13-FG (Cdc13 with an N-terminal SUMO and a C-terminal FLAG tag) and pCDF-H10ST (Stn1 with an N-terminal His10 tag and untagged Ten1). For C*ST, E. coli BL21 (DE3) was co-transformed with pDV1BUK-C2-Strep-Cdc13OB234 (encoding Strep-Trx-CL7-SUMO-TEV-Strep-Cdc13OB234) and pCDF-H10ST. All these plasmids have been described previously [31, 32]. Mutations in the STN1 and TEN1 genes were introduced by PCR mutagenesis using appropriate oligonucleotides (Supplementary Table S2). Following induction of the co-transformed E. coli strains by isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), the CST complex was purified from extracts over a Ni-NTA column, treated with ULP1 to remove the SUMO tag, and then further purified through binding and elution from anti-FLAG Sepharose beads (GenScript Biotech. Corp.). The purification of the C*ST complex was the same except that Strep-Tactin chromatography was used as the second affinity step. For PP, the coding regions of the four subunits of the complex (Pol1, Pol12, Pri1, and Pri2) were amplified from C. glabrata lysates using PCR. The polymerase subunits Pol1 and Pol12 were cloned in MCS1 and 2 of a modified pRSFDuet vector, respectively. Similarly, the primase subunits Pri1 and Pri2 were cloned in MCS1 and 2 of a modified pCDFDuet vector, respectively. For the expression of the complex in bacteria, the E. coli BL21 (DE3) strain was co-transformed with pRSF-H10Pol1-Pol12 (Pol1 with an N-terminal His10 tag and Pol12 with a C-terminal Strep-tag-II) and pCDF-Pri1-Pri2 (untagged Pri1 and Pri2 with a C-terminal Strep-tag-II). Following extract preparation, the complex was first enriched through a Ni-NTA column, and then further purified over a Strep-Tactin column. The ST complex was expressed using the pCDF-H10Stn1-Ten1FG3 plasmid (Stn1 with an N-terminal His10 tag and Ten1 with a C-terminal FLAG3 tag). This plasmid was made by replacing the Ten1 fragment in pCDF-H10ST with the Ten1-FG3 fragment from pCU-PDC1-Ten1-FG3 and the complex was purified using Ni-NTA and FLAG affinity chromatography.


### PP activity assays and electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSA)
  The PP activity assays were performed in 20 μl reactions containing 40 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.6, 30 mM potassium acetate, 8 mM magnesium acetate, 5 mM dithiothreitol, 0.05 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 5% glycerol, 0.1 mg/ml bovine serum albumin, ∼5–10 nM PP, CST, or ST at specified concentrations, 1 mM ATP, 10 μM dATP (with 0.5–1 μCi [α-32P]dATP), and 300 ng of poly-dT [28]. For assays with C. glabrata G-strand template, the poly-dT DNA was replaced by 300 ng of CgTEL-G3, and the nucleotides in the reactions included 1 mM ATP, 0.5 mM CTP, 0.5 mM GTP, 50 μM dATP, 10 μM dCTP (with 5 μCi [α-32P]dCTP), and 50 μM dGTP. After incubation at 32°C for 60 m, reactions were terminated with 80 μl STOP solution (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 20 mM EDTA) and 100 μl proteinase K solution [10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 0.5% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), 0.20 mg/ml proteinase K], and incubated at room temperature for 30 m. The products were recovered by ethanol precipitation in the presence of 20 μg GlycoBlue and 30 μg transfer RNA, resuspended in 10 μl formamide loading dye and analyzed by electrophoresis through a 13% polyacrylamide, 7 M urea gel in 1× Tris-Borate-EDTA (TBE) buffer. Signals were acquired by PhosphorImager scanning (Typhoon FLA 7000) and analyzed using PRISM.

  For EMSA, purified CST was incubated with 1–10 nM P32-labeled G-strand probe (CgTEL-G1.5) and 0.5 μg/μl bovine serum albumin (BSA) in binding buffer (25 mM HEPES-KOH, pH 7.5, 5% glycerol, 3 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM EDTA, 1 mM dithiothreitol) at 22°C for 15 min, and then subjected to electrophoresis at 0.8 V/cm through a 6% non-denaturing polyacrylamide gel in 1× TBE, followed by PhosphorImager analysis [41].


### Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) analysis
  For co-IP, the C. glabrata Ten1-Myc5 and Ten1L34A-Myc5 strains with or without FLAG3-tagged Pri1 were cultured to OD600 of 5.0 and the extracts were prepared by lysing cells with glass beads in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% sodium deoxycholate). Two milligrams of each extract in 500 μl of lysis buffer (with 50 μg of BSA and additional NaCl to a final concentration of 300 mM) was subjected to IP using 15 μl of Anti-DYKDDDDK G1 affinity resin (GenScript) at 4°C for overnight. IP samples were washed three times at 4°C for 5 min with rotation in the washing buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 2.5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, 0.1% NP-40). All of the lysis and wash buffers contained protease inhibitors. Bead-bound proteins were eluted by boiling with SDS loading buffer and then subjected to western analysis using Myc antibodies (9E10) (Covance) and FLAG antibodies (Genscript).

  For ChIP analysis, cells were crosslinked at OD600 of 1.0 with 1% formaldehyde for 20 min at 30°C, and the reaction was quenched with 125 mM glycine for 5 min at 30°C. Formaldehyde-fixed or untreated cells were resuspended in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% sodium deoxycholate) and broken by glass beads. The lysates were sonicated 36 times for 5 sec each (constant duty cycle, 30% output) to shear DNAs to a mean length of ∼600 bp. Two milligrams of each extract in 500 μl of lysis buffer (with 50 μg of BSA and 5 μg of sonicated E. coli DNA) was subjected to IP using 15 μl of Anti-DYKDDDDK G1 affinity resin (GenScript) at 4°C for overnight. Five or ten percent of each cell extract was set aside and processed to obtain the input DNA samples. IP samples were washed at 4°C for 5 min with rotation in the following buffers: three times with lysis buffer, once with high salt buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% sodium deoxycholate), once with RIPA buffer (10 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA, 0.25 M LiCl, 0.5% NP-40, 0.1% sodium deoxycholate). All of the lysis and wash buffers contained protease inhibitors. IP samples were eluted with 60 μl of 4 mg/ml 3x FLAG peptide (GlpBio) at 4°C for 5 h with rotation and cross-links were reversed at 65°C for overnight. Samples were treated with RNase A and proteinase K, extracted with phenol-chloroform, precipitated with ethanol, and resuspended in 100 μl of water. The DNA samples were then applied to Hybond-N using a dot blot apparatus, and the membrane was probed with 32P-labeled CgTEL-C3. Hybridization was performed at 56°C.


### Structure predictions
  All protein and protein-DNA complex structural predictions were performed using the AlphaFold 3 (AF3) public server without imposing any post-translational modification and using random seeds [42]. We obtained protein sequences from public databases for Pri1 (Uniprot Q6FKQ9), Pri2 (UniProt Q6FLP8), Cdc13 (UniProt Q6FT40), Stn1 (UniProt Q6FM89), and Ten1 (RefSeq XP_065482704.1). The DNA substrate predicted was composed of 2¼ repeats of the C. glabrata telomeric sequence (5′- [TGTGGGGTCTGGGTGC]2TGTG -3′). All predicted alignment error (PAE) [43] plots were created with af3analysis (https://github.com/nayimgr/af3analysis). All models were visualized using ChimeraX [44] and low-confidence regions (pLDDT < 30) were removed for easier visualization.


### Statistical analysis
  Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0. Comparison of multiple samples and the derivation of P-values were carried out using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test.


## Results

### Identification of Ten1, Pri1, and Pri2 surface residues involved in CST-mediated PP stimulation
  In the human CST-PP-DNA PIC structures, TEN1 is centrally located and makes multiple contacts to PP subunits (Fig. 1A) [33]. In particular, the TEN1 L34 (loop between β-strands 3 and 4) wedges between PRIM1 and PRIM2 and makes sequential contacts to a surface loop and a helix on these proteins, respectively (Fig. 1A and B). While these surface residues are not well conserved between yeasts and mammals (Supplementary Fig. S1), we reasoned that they could have co-evolved to retain interactions in budding yeasts, and proceeded to engineer alanine substitution mutants in C. glabrata TEN1, PRI1, and PRI2 (designated ten1L34A, ten1L34B, pri1L34, and pri2L34) aimed at disrupting the hypothesized contacts (Supplementary Fig. S1). The mutant CST and PP complexes as well as the wild type controls were expressed in E. coli, purified through sequential affinity chromatography (Supplementary Fig. S2A) and subjected to in vitro assays to assess the impacts of mutations on CST-mediated PP stimulation.

  In our previous studies, we utilized PP purified from C. glabrata and CST prepared from a different set of E. coli expression plasmids. We therefore first compared the different preparations in their ability to support the synthesis of poly-rA-poly-dA products from poly-dT templates. Both the native PP complex isolated from C. glabrata and the recombinant PP from E. coli synthesized labeled products that are ATP- and template-dependent (Supplementary Fig. S2B). Both forms of PP were also stimulated by the CST complex to similar extent, indicating that the new expression and purification protocols yielded active protein complexes (Supplementary Fig. S2B). In addition, the PP complexes containing either the Pri1L34 or Pri2L34 mutant protein (PP-Pri1L34 and PP-Pri2L34) remained active in the synthesis of poly-rA-poly-dA, showing that these mutations did not abrogate PP activity (Supplementary Fig. S2C).

  We next examined the effects of the Ten1, Pri1, and Pri2 mutations on the PP-stimulatory activity of CST. Notably, purified CST complexes harboring the Ten1L34A or Ten1L34B mutant proteins (CSTL34A or CSTL34B) were found to contain the three subunits at similar ratios as the wild type complex, suggesting that the mutations do not affect complex assembly (Supplementary Fig. S2A). The mutant complexes also exhibited comparable DNA-binding to a C. glabrata G-strand oligonucleotide as the wild type complex, indicating that they retain as least some biochemical activity (Supplementary Fig. S2D). In parallel PP activity assays, we found that whereas wild type CST produced dose-dependent enhancement of PP activity, CSTL34A and CSTL34B were mostly inactive (Fig. 1C). The stimulatory effect of CST and the loss of stimulatory activity for CSTL34A and CSTL34B were also observed when a G-strand template was used in the PP activity assays (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Fig. S2E). In addition, even though PP-Pri1L34 and PP-Pri2L34 retained substantial basal activity in the absence of CST, neither complex was stimulated upon the addition of CST (Fig. 1E). These results suggest that the structural elements implicated in the formation of the human CST-PP-DNA PIC complex [33] are also required for the regulatory activity of yeast CST. Hence, despite poor sequence conservation at the Ten1-Pri1-Pri2 interface, the human and yeast CST complexes appear to utilize similar mechanisms to promote PP activity.


### The Stn1-Ten1 subcomplex can stimulate PP at high concentrations
  While Stn1 and Ten1 are well conserved in evolution at the level of overall structure, the CTC1/Cdc13 family of proteins exhibit a high degree of evolutionary divergence [7, 14, 45]. Given that PP stimulation is a conserved CST function, we hypothesize that the CTC1/Cdc13 subunit may be dispensable for this activity. To test this idea, we purified a Stn1-Ten1 (ST) subcomplex and compared its stimulatory activity to the full CST complex. Indeed, at high concentrations, ST can stimulate PP nearly as well as CST (Fig. 1F). In standard assays, ∼7 fold more ST than CST is required to achieve 1/2 maximal stimulation. Notably, like CST, the ST complex harboring Ten1L34B (STL34B) failed to enhance PP activity, supporting the notion that ST acts via similar mechanisms as the full CST complex (Fig. 1G).

  We had previously shown that Stn1 alone is capable of stimulating PP [28]. This Stn1-exclusive stimulatory activity, which was observed using Stn1 that carried a His-SUMO tag, is evidently at odds with the Ten1 mutational analysis. One possibility is that the absence of Ten1 may have exposed normally inaccessible portions of Stn1 to engage in fortuitous interaction with PP. Given the consistent effects of Ten1 mutations both in vitro and in vivo (Fig. 1 and see later), the Stn1-exclusive stimulatory activity is unlikely to accurately reflect the in vivo mechanisms of the CST complex.


### AlphaFold modeling supports similarities between the human and C. glabrata CST-PP-DNA PIC complex
  After the above analysis was completed, the AF3 structure prediction algorithm became available [42]. Queries of the AF3 server using CgCST and primase subunits along with a 36-mer C. glabrata G-strand ssDNA yielded models of the CgCST-primase-DNA complex that resemble the human complex regarding the dispositions of Stn1, Ten1, Pri1, Pri2, and DNA (Supplementary Fig. S3A and B). In particular, the CgTen1 L34 loop in the AF3 models is wedged adjacent to the Pri1 and Pri2 interface, just like the human proteins in the cryo-EM structures (Supplementary Fig. S3A and B). In addition, based on the confidence measure developed for AlphaFold (PAE), the interactions between Ten1 and Pri1/Pri2 are estimated to be comparable to those within individual proteins (Supplementary Fig. S3C). Moreover, this PAE-based signal for interaction is present in all five models generated by AF3, adding confidence to the validity of the structural prediction.

  Given the ability of the CgStn1-Ten1 complex to stimulate PP activity in vitro, we also used AF3 to predict the structure of a complex comprising Stn1, Ten1, primase, and DNA. Indeed, AF3 generated models of the CgST-primase-DNA complex that recapitulate the interactions between Stn1, Ten1, Pri1, Pri2, and DNA found in the CgCST-primase-DNA complex (Supplementary Fig. S4A). Again in this case, the PAE-based signals for interactions between Ten1 and Pri1-Pri2 are detected in all five models generated by AF3, supporting the validity of the structural prediction (Supplementary Fig. S4B). Altogether, the results from biochemical analysis and structural modeling support the idea that despite sequence divergence, the yeast and human Ten1, Pri1, and Pri2 orthologs utilize equivalent surface loops to promote PIC formation.

  Interestingly, the Ten1-primase interactions in AF3 models of the CgCST-primase-DNA and CgST-primase-DNA complexes were not predicted by AF3 when the full PP complex (Pri1, Pri2, Pol1, and Pol12) were included in the modeling exercise (not shown). One likely explanation is that because the CST-PP-DNA complex can undergo more conformational changes than CST-primase-DNA, the computational challenges for machine learning-based algorithms are greater [46].


### The ten1L34A and ten1L34B mutants induce distinct telomere defects in vivo
  Having established the importance of the Ten1 L34 loop in stimulating PP activity, we next examined the functions of this loop in vivo by over-expressing FLAG3-tagged Ten1L34A and Ten1L34B proteins (from a plasmid harboring the strong PDC1 promoter) [47]. This strategy allows us to identify mutant proteins that can compete effectively against wild type Ten1 for assembly into the CST complex, but that render the complex defective in telomere regulation. For comparative purposes, we tested three additional mutants with altered residues in other Ten1 surface loops (i.e. Ten1L12, Ten1L23, and Ten1L5C) (Fig. 2A). Viable transformants were obtained from the wild type as well as all the mutant Ten1 plasmids. Western analysis confirmed that the Ten1 alleles encoded by these plasmids were all expressed, but that Ten1L34B and Ten1L5C were present at ∼1/5 of the level of wild type, FLAG3-tagged Ten1 (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. S5A). Notably, the FLAG3-tagged Ten1 detected C. glabrata extracts exhibited the same mobility in sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) as FLAG3-tagged Ten1 purified from E. coli, confirming the identity of the western signals (Supplementary Fig. S5B). Given that the PDC1 promoter is a particularly strong one in C. glabrata, it seems likely that even the reduced Ten1L34B and Ten1L5C levels would still be sufficient to compete against endogenous Ten1. Indeed, while most mutants grew at near wild-type growth rate, the Ten1L34B-overexpression mutant (henceforth referred to as ten1L34B) manifested slow growth consistent with defective telomere function (Fig. 2C).

  Analysis of telomere lengths following ∼75 generations of growth (3 rounds of re-streaking for single colonies) revealed distinct, abnormal telomere patterns in the ten1L34A and ten1L34B mutants; the former showed consistent telomere lengthening (∼300 bp) for the great majority of their TRFs, whereas the latter showed increased telomere lengths as well as heterogeneity (Fig. 2D). In contrast to ten1L34A and ten1L34B, the other Ten1 over-expression strains did not exhibit telomere aberrations. We then monitored telomere dynamics in the ten1L34A and ten1L34B cells for another 150 generations and found that the telomere elongation in ten1L34A cells is progressive, at a rate of ∼4 bp per generation, while the telomere abnormalities triggered in ten1L34B cells occurred early and did not exacerbate over time (Fig. 2E). These initial studies were performed using DNAs treated with EcoRI alone, which resolves the telomere populations into multiple distinct TRF clusters owing to the different lengths of the subtelomeric DNA on different chromosomes. As a complementary approach, we repeated the TRF measurement using DNAs treated with both EcoRI and AluI, which resulted in a single predominant TRF cluster (average length of ∼1.2 kb in the wild type strain) (Supplementary Fig. S5C). Analysis using this protocol showed that in ten1L34A cells, telomeres undergo progressive lengthening, eventually stabilizing at ∼3.0 kb (Supplementary Fig. S5C and D, compare the 525G and 600G samples). In contrast, the TRFs in ten1L34B cells showed greater heterogeneity throughout extended passages and the average TRF lengths exhibited a modest decline in later generations (Supplementary Fig. S5C, right half of the blot). We conclude that even though both mutations impaired CST–PP functional interaction, they result in different abnormalities in vivo.


### The ten1L34A mutant is defective in telomerase inhibition in vivo
  The progressive telomere elongation exhibited by ten1L34A suggests aberrant telomerase regulation, which has been reported for a number of CST mutants [22, 48, 49]. To confirm the telomerase-dependence of telomere elongation induced by Ten1L34A, we first generated several independent tertΔ clones (missing the catalytic component of telomerase) and characterized their telomere dynamics. Consistent with previous findings [50], we found that tertΔ cells manifested progressive telomere shortening, followed by senescence and emergence of survivors after ∼150 generations of growth (Fig. 3A). We then overexpressed Ten1 and Ten1L34A in early generations of tertΔ clones and analyzed telomeres after ∼35 generations of growth. Notably, while Ten1L34A expression induced longer telomeres than Ten1 in the parental strain (BG14), it failed to do so in tertΔ cells, confirming that the abnormal telomere lengthening requires telomerase (Fig. 3B).

  Interestingly, the tertΔ/PDC1-ten1L34A cells exhibited rapid onset of senescence (at ∼25G post construction) despite having comparable telomere lengths as tertΔ/PDC1-Ten1 cells (Fig. 3B and C). Continuous liquid cultures of tertΔ/PDC1-ten1L34A cells resulted in the emergence of faster growing survivors, just like tertΔ/PDC1-Ten1 cells (Fig. 3D). The accelerated senescence and survivor formation for tertΔ/PDC1-ten1L34A cells suggests that Ten1L34A may induce partial telomere uncapping when telomeres are short. To examine this issue further, we asked whether expression of Ten1L34A in tertΔ strain with long telomeres can also trigger rapid senescence. This was accomplished by disrupting the TERT gene in a Ten1L34A-overexpression clone that had been passaged for 225G. This LT/tertΔ/PDC1-ten1L34A (long telomere with tertΔ and PDC1-ten1L34A) strain possessed telomeres that are ∼800 bp longer than the parental strain (BG14) (Fig. 3E, compare the TRFs marked by arrowheads). LT/tertΔ/PDC1-ten1L34A showed a similar telomere attrition rate as tertΔ, confirming that the telomere elongation induced by Ten1L34A-overexpression is telomerase-dependent (Fig. 3E and F). Notably, in contrast to tertΔ/PDC1-ten1L34A, LT/tertΔ/PDC1-ten1L34A can be passaged for >150G without growth defects (Fig. 3G). Hence, the rapid senescence exhibited by tertΔ/PDC1-ten1L34A appears to be restricted to cells with short telomeres.


### The ten1L34B mutant induces telomere deprotection, leading to DDR and aberrant repair
  In contrast to ten1L34A, the slow growth and telomere length heterogeneity exhibited by ten1L34B cells suggest loss of telomere protection rather than defects in telomerase regulation. Consistent with this interpretation, transformation of tertΔ cells using the PDC1-tenL34Bplasmid yielded no viable colonies, indicating that the defects induced in ten1L34B cells do not require telomerase and that ten1L34B and tertΔ are synthetically lethal. Also consistent with defects in telomere protection, the telomeres in ten1L34B cells were more heterogeneous than those in post-senescent survivors, which utilize a recombination-based telomere maintenance pathway. Specifically, prominent TRF bands within the broad telomere smears were more easily detected in survivors than in ten1L34B cells (Supplementary Fig. S6A, marked by arrowheads). This difference was observed in both EcoRI-digested and EcoRI/AluI-digested samples. The ten1L34B mutant also manifested elevated G-strand ssDNA and C-circles, which are again hallmarks of telomere deprotection (Supplementary Fig. S6B and C).

  We next sought to assess the roles of specific DDR and DNA repair factors, including Rad52, Rad24, and Exo1, in mediating the defects of the ten1L34B mutant. These genes were chosen for their strong connections to dysfunctional telomeres in budding yeast [25, 51, 52]. To alleviate the challenges posed by the toxicity of ten1L34B, we constructed Ten1L34B-expression plasmids with the HHT2 and EGD2 promoters, which were reported to be weaker promoters than PDC1 [47]. Based on western analysis, these promoters expressed Ten1 at ∼1/5 and 1/10 of the level produced from PDC1 (Supplementary Fig. S7A). As expected, Ten1L34B expressed from plasmids with different promoters inhibited growth in a dose-dependent manner, further confirming its toxic effect (Supplementary Fig. S7B). We then constructed isogeneic rad52Δ, rad24Δ, and exo1Δ mutants and examined the consequences of expressing wild type and mutant Ten1 with respect to growth and telomere aberrations. For these experiments, Ten1 and Ten1L34A were expressed with the PDC1 promoter, whereas Ten1L34B was expressed using either the HHT2 or the EGD2 promoter.

  We first assessed the effects of rad52Δ, rad24Δ, and exo1Δ on the growth of Ten1-expressing cells on semi-solid media. While none of the deletions caused changes in the growth of cells harboring PDC1-Ten1, deletion of EXO1 substantially improved the growth of cells with HHT2-ten1L34B and EGD2-ten1L34B, underscoring the role of this nuclease in mediating the toxic effects of Ten1L34B (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. S7C). We then analyzed the effects of rad52Δ, rad24Δ, and exo1Δ on the telomere aberrations triggered by Ten1L34B, including telomere lengthening/heterogeneity, ssDNA on the G-strand, and C-circle accumulation (Fig. 4B–D). Notably, these deletions had significant but distinct impacts on the telomere abnormalities, suggesting that all three genes are partly responsible for acting at deprotected telomeres. For example, rad24Δ reduced telomere elongation without eliminating the increased telomere heterogeneity, while rad52Δ and exo1Δ had little effects on telomere length distributions (Fig. 4B). Regarding G-strand ssDNA, rad52Δ triggered a significant increase in this structure, whereas rad24Δ and exo1Δ caused mild reductions, which are not statistically significant (Fig. 4C). The ssDNA signals are mostly resistant to E. coli Exo I treatment, indicating that they cannot be attributed to 3′ overhangs (Supplementary Fig. S7D). We also found that in ten1L34B cells, the deletions of RAD52, RAD24, and EXO1 all decreased the levels of C-circles, which are believed to be markers of telomere DNA damage and telomere recombination (Fig. 4D) [53, 54]. Together, these observations support the notion that the telomere aberrations in ten1L34B cells are produced by the activities of multiple DDR and DNA repair factors.


### The Ten1-binding residues of Pri1 are also required for proper telomere regulation
  The foregoing analysis of ten1 mutants suggests that disrupting the Ten1-Pri1-Pri2 interface in a PIC-like structure can result in loss of telomerase inhibition or loss of telomere protection. To further interrogate this hypothesis, we attempted to over-express in C. glabrata the Pri1L34 and Pri2L34 mutants that were shown to abrogate the stimulatory effects of CST on PP activity in vitro (Supplementary Fig. S1B and 1E). Both mutant proteins as well as wild type Pri1 and Pri2 were fused to a C-terminal FG3 tag, placed under the control of the strong PDC1 promoter, and transformed in the BG14 parental strain. Western analysis showed that both wild type proteins as well as Pri1L34 were expressed at high levels, but that Pri2L34 was undetectable, suggesting that the pri2L34 mutations rendered the protein unstable in vivo (Supplementary Fig. S8A). No significant differences in growth were detected between the strain carrying the vector and those carrying either the wild type or mutant Pri1 and Pri2 overexpression plasmids (Supplementary Fig. S8B). However, further analysis revealed progressive elongation of telomeres in the Pri1L34-overexpression clones, which continued up to at least 300 generations following the introduction of the plasmid (Supplementary Fig. S8C and D). This phenotype is similar to that manifested by ten1L34A cells, reinforcing the notion that disrupting the Ten1-Pri1-Pri2 interface in a PIC-like structure can lead to defective telomerase regulation.


### Disrupting the Ten1-Pri1-Pri2 interface does not abolish the association between CST and PP in cell extracts or the telomere localization of either Ten1 or Pri1
  To gain further insights into how disrupting the PIC-like complex induces telomere abnormalities, we assessed the effects of a disruption mutation on the association of Ten1 and Pri1 in cell extracts, as well as on the telomere localization of Ten1 and Pri1. Because the lower expression of Ten1L34B and the growth defects of this mutant may confound the interpretation of results, we performed this series of experiments using Ten1L34A. In co-IP analysis of Myc5-tagged Ten1 and FLAG-tagged Pri1, similar levels of Ten1-Myc5 and Ten1L34A-Myc5 were pulled down along with Pri1-FG3, suggesting that the mutation does not abolish the interaction between CST and PP (Fig. 5A). In ChIP analysis of cells expressing Ten1-FG3 and Ten1L34A-FG3, a weak but detectable telomere association was observed for Ten1-FG3, and this association was retained by Ten1L34A-FG3 (Fig. 5B). Lastly, in ChIP analysis of the telomere association of Pri1-FG3, similar amounts of telomere DNAs were detected in precipitates from cells co-expressing Ten-Myc5 or Ten1L34A-Myc5, suggesting that the Ten1L34A mutation does not abolish the localization of PP to telomeres (Fig. 5C). Together, these results indicate that the in vivo defects of the Ten1L34A mutant cannot be attributed to the loss of CST-PP association or the loss of either complex from telomeres. Instead, a specific conformation of the CST-PP-DNA complex appears to be important, and this conformation is impaired by the L34A mutation.


### The DNA-binding residues of Stn1 are also required for CST-mediated PP stimulation and for telomere protection
  Having established the critical role of the Ten1-Pri1-Pri2 contacts predicted by the PIC structure and AF3 modeling, we next examined another interaction visualized in the models, namely that between Stn1 and DNA. In the cryo-EM structure and AF3 models, Stn1 not only binds Ten1 (as predicted from numerous prior studies [55, 56]), but also contacts telomere DNA before its entry into the primase complex (Supplementary Fig. S9A and B) [33]. The Stn1–DNA interaction may thus cooperate with other protein-protein contacts to promote PIC formation. In support of this idea, we found that omitting the DNA substrate completely abolished the PAE signals between Ten1 and the Pri1-Pri2 proteins in AF3 models of the CgCST-primase and CgST-primase complexes (cf  Supplementary Figs S9C and S3C; cf  Supplementary Figs S9D and S4B). To interrogate the importance of Stn1–DNA contact experimentally, we designed two Stn1 mutants (Stn1KK: K69E, K74E; Stn1DBM: K69E, K74E, F127E) that, based on previous studies, are predicted to disrupt this interaction [31, 57]. These mutations were first introduced into the ST complex and tested for their effects on PP activity stimulation (Fig. 6A and B). Consistent with the importance of Stn1-DNA interaction, neither SKKT nor SDBMT stimulated PP activity even at the highest concentrations (Fig. 6B).

  Next, we tested Stn1KK as part of the CST complex. For this analysis, we developed an alternative expression strategy for CST to improve yield and homogeneity. Specifically, because full length Cdc13 is prone to proteolysis between OB1 and OB2, we expressed Cdc13OB234 (with an N-terminal Strep-CL7-SUMO tag to facilitate purification) along with His-Stn1 and Ten1 and purified the complex by affinity chromatography (Supplementary Fig. S10A). We verified that this complex with truncated Cdc13 but otherwise wild type sequences (named C*ST) produced dose-dependent stimulation of PP activity (Supplementary Fig. S10B). The magnitudes of stimulation by C*ST are similar to those produced by the original, partially proteolyzed CST preparations, indicating that the proteolysis did not affect the PP activity assay results, and that the Cdc13 OB1 is not required for stimulation (Supplementary Fig. S10B). Importantly, the C*SKKT complex exhibited essentially no stimulatory activity, confirming that the Stn1–DNA contact is required for the regulation of PP by CST (Supplementary Fig. S10C). It is worth noting that the intrinsic affinity of Stn1 for DNA is low. Previous studies of human Stn1DBM showed that this mutation has a small effect on the overall affinity of the CST complex for both telomeric and nontelomeric DNA (approximately two-fold) [57]. Consistent with this finding, we were unable to detect stable binding of the C. glabrata ST complex to DNA (Supplementary Fig. S10D), and the C*SKKT complex exhibited comparable binding to a telomeric oligonucleotide as the C*ST complex (Supplementary Fig. S10E). Therefore, rather than enhancing the affinity of CST for DNA, the low affinity Stn1–DNA interaction may serve mainly to direct the DNA substrate toward the primase active site to promote PIC formation.

  Next, we examined the in vivo consequences of over-expressing the Stn1KK mutant protein. Like Ten1L34B, the expression of Stn1KK from the PDC1 promoter resulted in slow growth as well as elongated and heterogenous telomeres, consistent with telomere DDR and abnormal repair (Fig. 6C and D). Notably, the magnitudes of the growth and telomere abnormalities for Stn1KK are reduced in comparison to those for Ten1L34B, suggesting a milder defect for Stn1KK (compare Fig. 6C with 4A and Fig. 6D with 4B). To gain more insights on the nature of telomere deprotection, we again assessed the effects of rad52Δ, rad24Δ, and exo1Δ on the growth and telomere aberrations of Stn1KK-overexpression cells. This analysis revealed marked similarities in the abilities of these deletions to alter phenotypes of the Stn1KK and Ten1L34B-containing cells. For example, among the deletions, only exo1Δ substantially improved growth, and only rad24Δ reduced the overall lengths of telomeres in Stn1KK cells, just as in the case of Ten1L34B cells (Fig. 6C and D). In addition, like Ten1L34B, all three deletions decreased the levels of C-circles in the Stn1KK cells, albeit that the reduction in rad52Δ is not statistically significant (Fig. 6E). Together, these observations support the notion that Stn1KK induces telomere deprotection through similar mechanisms as Ten1L34B, i.e. by disrupting a PIC-like structure.


## Discussion
  In this study, we identified multiple structural elements in C. glabrata Stn1, Ten1, Pri1, and Pri2 that mediate PP activity stimulation by CST in vitro, and showed that these structures are also required for telomerase inhibition and telomere protection in vivo. These findings suggest that PP plays an integral role in the two major functions previously ascribed to the yeast CST complex (Fig. 7). Our observations have notable implications for the mechanisms, functions, and evolution of the CST–PP complex, which are discussed below.


### Conservation of CST-PP mechanisms
  As discussed earlier, the importance of Ten1-Pri-Pri2 and Stn1-DNA interactions is first suggested by physical contacts observed in the human CST-PP-DNA PIC. That the corresponding structural elements in yeast CST and PP are found to be crucial for PP stimulation (despite sequence divergence) suggests that the mechanisms by which CST regulates PP activity is quite well conserved. CST likely enhances the initiation of RNA priming by favoring an open conformation of PP, in which the primase subunits are poised to capture the DNA template through Ten1-Pri1-Pri2, Stn1-Ten1, and Stn1-DNA interactions [33, 58]. Less clear are how CST regulates other steps of the PP reaction and the extent of conservation. The human CST–PP structure at the “recruitment” stage harbors critical contacts mediated by domains of CTC1 that are not conserved in Cdc13, suggesting that the mechanisms of PP recruitment are likely to be different between metazoans and budding yeasts [59]. According to a cryo-EM structure of the Tetrahymena CST-PP-DNA complex that is proposed to represent the DNA polymerization complex, the CST subunits continue to engage in protein-protein interactions with PP [60]. However, some of the physical contacts in this structure (e.g. interactions between Pol1 and the Cdc13/Stn1 orthologs) are not observed in the human CST-PP-DNA PIC, suggesting alternative modes of regulation. It is also important to point out that not all physical contacts revealed by cryo-EM structures are functionally relevant. For example, the human TEN1 L23 loop is in close proximity to POLA2 in the PIC structure, yet the corresponding loop in C. glabrata Ten1 can be mutated without triggering any apparent defect (Fig. 2C and D). Future structural studies coupled with functional analyses in a variety of systems are needed to provide a more complete understanding of how CST regulates different steps of the PP reaction.


### Termination of telomerase-mediated G-strand synthesis requires the assembly of the CST–PP–DNA complex
  Human CST is known to inhibit telomerase activity in vitro and multiple yeast Cdc13, Stn1, and Ten1 mutations have been shown to induce telomerase-dependent telomere elongation [19, 20]. However, the molecular mechanisms that underlie CST-mediated telomerase inhibition remain unclear. The biochemical and cellular defects of the C. glabrata ten1L34A and pri1L34 mutants suggest that PP plays an integral role in this pathway, i.e. telomerase inhibition requires the assembly of the full CST-PP-DNA complex. First, these two mutants exhibit progressive telomere elongation consistent with a defect in telomerase termination. In the case of ten1L34A, we showed that the elongation is telomerase dependent. Second, both Ten1L34A and Pri1L34 abolished the stimulatory effect of CST on PP without affecting other activities such as CST or PP assembly or the “basal” activity of PP. Indeed, the interaction with PP represents the only physical contacts for the Ten1 L34 loop observed in all available structures of the CST complex [33, 58–61]. Taken together, these lines of evidence argue that the defect in telomerase termination exhibited by the ten1L34A and pri1L34 mutants is due to the disruption of a PIC-like, CST-PP-DNA structure. Notably, the ten1L34A mutant does not abolish Ten1-Pri1 association in cell extracts or eliminate the telomere association of either protein as judged by ChIP. Thus, while the PIC-like structure is critical for function, it is not required for either CST or PP to bind telomeres, and it is not the only mechanism of association between these complexes.

  The accumulated data in budding yeasts suggest that the switch from G- to C-strand synthesis entails the switch from Cdc13-Est1 to Cdc13-Stn1 interaction, with the former supporting telomerase-mediated G-strand elongation and the latter supporting PP-mediated C-strand synthesis [62, 63] (Fig. 7). The failure of the ten1L34A and pri1L34 mutants to efficiently terminate G-strand elongation argues that the interaction between Cdc13 and Stn1 by itself is insufficient to remove telomerase, but that the assembly of the full CST-PP complex is required. The coupling of telomerase termination to the assembly of the CST-PP complex ensures that C-strand synthesis can commence immediately following the removal of telomerase. This may in turn maximize the efficiency of converting the newly synthesized G-strand to double-stranded telomeres.

  Several observations suggest that the CST-PP mediated telomerase inhibition is conserved in yeasts and mammals. First, two S. cerevisiae ten1 mutants that were previously shown to induce telomere elongation (ten1-3 and ten1-13) harbor mutations in or near the L34 loop (Fig. 2A) [22]. These mutations probably cause defects also by disrupting CST-PP interaction. Second, many ts mutants of S. cerevisiae and Schizosaccharomyces pombe PP subunits manifest telomere elongation at permissive or semi-permissive temperatures [18], which could be explained by the failure to form adequate levels of the CST-PP complex. Moreover, a human CTC1 mutation that disrupts Polα interaction as well as a mouse POLA1 ts mutant have been shown to induce telomerase-dependent telomere elongation, suggesting conservation of this pathway in mammalian cells [64, 65]. Notably, however, a study in colon cancer cells revealed a lack of requirement for TEN1 in terminating telomerase activity [15]. Further investigations will be required to establish the extent of mechanistic conservation.


### Yeast CST engages PP to protect telomeres against degradation and recombination
  Yeast CST has long been known to mediate an essential protective function at telomeres [21]. All three subunits of the complex are essential, and many mutant alleles cause dramatic telomere length alterations as well as accumulation of ssDNA, which arises through DDR-induced telomere degradation and recombination [22–25, 66]. The protective function of CST has been ascribed to direct binding of this complex to G-tails to prevent DNA damage recognition and aberrant repair. However, two key findings from our analysis of the ten1L34B and stn1KK mutants indicate that CST does not function alone, but rather enlists PP to provide full protection. First, we showed that both ten1L34B and stn1KK abolished CST-PP functional interaction without affecting CST assembly or its DNA-binding activity. Second, we showed that the telomere defects induced by ten1L34B and stn1KK resemble previously described CST mutants and are suppressed by the same DNA repair gene deletions such as exo1Δ [24, 25], implying that they have similar mechanistic basis.

  Even though a role for PP in telomere protection has not received wide attention, this notion is supported by many previous observations. For example, both yeast and mouse PP mutants can accumulate ssDNA independent of telomerase, consistent with defects in telomere protection or lagging strand synthesis [18, 64]. It has also been shown that the protective function of Cdc13 is especially critical during replication and dispensable in other phases of the cell cycle [51]; this could be rationalized by Cdc13’s interaction with PP in the S phase. Indeed, protection is likely mediated through a combination of two mechanisms: (i) masking of ssDNA and DNA ends by the CST-PP assembly; and (ii) conversion of G-strand ssDNA to ds telomeres. Defects in either mechanism can lead to the accumulation of structures that trigger DDR and DNA repair. It should be noted that CST-PP acts not only at telomeres following extension by telomerase, but also following telomere replication on the lagging strands [3]. Failure to mask the ssDNA or convert ssDNA to double-stranded DNA on the lagging strands can induce DNA damage just as they do at G-strand synthesized by telomerase.

  It is unclear why ten1L34B and stn1KK trigger telomere deprotection while ten1L34A and pri1L34 cause telomere elongation, even though all four mutants disrupt CST–PP functional interaction in vitro. One distinction between the in vitro and in vivo analyses is that the C. glabrata CST and PP complexes used in the biochemical assays were purified from bacteria and may lack important post-translational modifications. If these modifications facilitate CST-PP-DNA PIC formation, then the in vitro results may not accurately reflect the in vivo activities of these complexes. We suggest that in vivo, the ten1L34A and pri1L34 mutants may retain partial ability to mediate PIC formation and support C-strand synthesis. Perhaps the weaker interactions mediated by these mutants result in delayed complex formation, allowing telomerase to synthesize longer G-strands (Fig. 7). The notion that ten1L34A/pri1L34 and ten1L34B/stn1KK represent mutants of different severities is consistent with the phenotypes of ten1 ts mutants [22, 49]. These mutants often exhibit progressive telomere elongation (ten1L34A and pri1L34-like) at permissive temperatures, but manifest telomere deprotection (ten1L34B and stn1KK-like) at nonpermissive temperatures, suggesting that partial and complete disruption of the same activities can give rise to disparate phenotypes (Fig. 7).


### Implications for other CST-PP functions and evolution of the CST complex
  As noted in the introduction, PP plays critical roles at replication origins and in the synthesis of Okazaki fragments on the lagging strands. Current evidence suggests that CST does not participate in these processes. Instead, PP is directed to the replisome through interactions with CTF4 and the CMG complex [10, 11]. Notably, these interactions do not appear to involve the Pri1/Pri2 surface structures that engage with Ten1 in the PIC structure, indicating that the replisome employs very different mechanisms to regulate PP. Instead of directly stimulating RNA priming, the replisome may simply recruit PP to the relevant sites on the template to enable primer synthesis.

  Besides telomeres, PP has been implicated in two other CST related functions: double strand break processing and resistance to replication stress. At double strand breaks, CST–PP is proposed to counteract resection by carrying out fill-in synthesis under the control of 53BP1, RIF1 and shieldin [67, 68]. Under conditions of replication stress, CST is reported to enhance dormant origin firing during recovery, possibly by stimulating PP activity [69]. However, additional mechanisms that invoke interactions between CST and RAD51/MRE11 have also been described [70–72]. Importantly, it is not known whether CST-PP employs the same mechanisms at DSBs and sites of replication stress as it does at telomeres. This issue may be addressed by testing the effects of the Stn1, Ten1, Pri1, and Pri2 mutants we identified in DSB-related pathways.

  As a critical component of the telomere maintenance and protection machinery, the CST complex appears to be extraordinarily malleable, especially regarding the CTC1/Cdc13 subunit. CTC1 is likely to represent the ancestral form of this protein family given its widespread distribution in metazoans and basal branches of fungi [7, 14]. By contrast, Cdc13 is restricted to the Saccharomycotina subphylum of budding yeasts, and itself manifests a high degree of structural plasticity [45]. Interestingly, in many fungal branches, including Basidiomycetes and fission yeasts, a plausible CTC1- or Cdc13-like protein has not been identified despite extensive efforts. While it is possible that distantly related proteins in these organisms may have escaped detection, our findings suggest a plausible explanation for how the CTC1/Cdc13 protein may become dispensable. In particular, we show that high concentrations of ST can stimulate PP activity to the same extent as the CST complex. This in vitro observation echoes previous descriptions of a “Cdc13-independent” pathway of telomere protection. That is, the S. cerevisiae Cdc13 becomes dispensable in cells that over-express Stn1 and Ten1, and the viability of these cdc13 null cells requires normal Pol12 activity [73]. Other viable cdc13Δ mutants that do not require Stn1/Ten1 overexpression have also been described [66, 74]. Collectively, these in vitro and in vivo data suggest that the essential function of fungal Ctc1/Cdc13 may be to facilitate the formation of the ST-PP-DNA assembly by providing additional protein-DNA and protein-protein interactions. Accordingly, it is possible that organisms lacking Ctc1/Cdc13 may have adopted alternative means of accomplishing this task, e.g. by expressing high levels of Stn1-Ten1, by enhancing the affinity of Stn1-Ten1 for DNA, or by using other telomere proteins to recruit ST-PP. Indeed, a recent study of S. pombe implicates Pot1 and Tpz1 as key recruiters of ST and PP in telomere replication [75]. Further analysis of the ST complexes and their interactions with PP in organisms lacking Ctc1/Cdc13 should be informative.
