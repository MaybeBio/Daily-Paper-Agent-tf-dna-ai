# Unveiling Cas8 dynamics and regulation within a transposon-encoded Cascade–TniQ complex


## Significance
  CRISPR-associated transposons have ushered a new paradigm in life sciences, where CRISPR–Cas proteins can guide DNA transposition at desired sites, enabling precise gene insertion. Despite the potential for transformative applications, the molecular mechanisms enabling DNA binding and transposition by these proteins remain poorly understood. This study combines structural reconstruction and molecular simulations to provide a dynamic portrayal of the structural changes in Cas8 within the Vibrio cholerae Cascade–TniQ complex, a key transposition system. Our findings uncover a critical conformational change and illustrate its role at key steps of the Cascade–TniQ biophysical function. These findings offer crucial insights into the molecular choreography underlying CRISPR-associated transposition and aid future innovations in gene insertion technologies.


## Results

### Cas8-Binding Mode.
  To describe the conformation of the Cas8 bundle bound to the Cascade–TniQ complexes, we performed structural modeling, followed by molecular dynamics (MD)-driven cryo-EM fitting. AlphaFold2 (13), an AI-powered method that incorporates biophysical knowledge about protein structure and multisequence alignments, was used to predict the three-dimensional structure of Cas8. AlphaFold2 was combined with PyRosetta (14), preserving the positions of the experimental structures, while adding missing loops and the Cas8 bundle from the AlphaFold2 model (details in SI Appendix).

  Structural modeling reported two main configurations of the Cas8 bundle—i.e., closed and open states (Fig. 1 C and D and SI Appendix, Figs. S2 and S3)—that resemble the available experimental data. In detail, the open state of the Cas8 bundle (Fig. 1D) resembles the orientation observed in the type 1F Cascade from P. aeruginosa obtained by Guo and coworkers (PDB: 6B45) (10). In the closed state (Fig. 1C), the Cas8 bundle assumes a conformation similar to the weak electronic density observed by Halpin-Healy and coworkers [EMD-20350, EMD-20351 maps (8). Furthermore, the closed state is evidenced in the top ranked AlphaFold2 model, followed by the open state, displaying a comparable level of confidence in the prediction (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). This indicates that both states are possible and further reinforces the concept of a conformational change between them (10, 11).

  To refine the position of the closed Cas8 bundle with respect to the weak cryo-EM density observed by Halpin-Healy, we used a MD flexible fitting (MDFF) approach (15, 16). The cryo-EM maps display low-resolution density (from ~6 to 9 Å) at the level of the Cas8 bundle (8). Using these cryo-EM maps as a target, we carried out a flexible fitting of our computational model into the experimental density. The fitted structures refined the missing loops and the Cas8 bundle with respect to the experimental densities (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). In the final structures, the Cas8 bundle binds with its apical portion the Cas7.1 and TniQ.2 proteins, reflecting the experimental data (Fig. 1C). It is also notable that the fitted structure of the Cas8 bundle aligns with a polyalanine model reported by Li and colleagues, derived from weak electron density data (SI Appendix, Fig. S5) (17).


### Conformational Change of the Cas8 Bundle.
  To describe the conformational change between the closed and open states of the Cas8 bundle, we performed free energy simulations using the Umbrella Sampling (US) method (18) (details in SI Appendix). The structural transition was analyzed in the available cryo-EM structures captured prior to R-loop formation (RNA-bound) and in the DNA-bound state, obtained by Halpin-Healy (HH) (8) and Jia (9). Specifically, the RNA-bound systems were based on PDB 6PIG (referred to as RNA-HH) and 6V9Q (RNA-Jia). For the DNA-bound states, we considered PDB 6PIJ (DNA-HH) in which the R-loop is partially formed (i.e., the target DNA binds the guide RNA with 20 base pair complementarities, rather than 32), and 6VBW (DNA-Jia) that displays a formed R-loop (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). The open–closed transition (and vice versa) was rigorously investigated by sampling the populations of the two states along with all intermediate states through converged US simulations (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). The transition was sampled along the difference in RMSD of the heavy atoms’ positions in the Cas8 bundle (Cas8 RMSD) relative to both states, used as the reaction coordinate (RC).

  The free energy profiles for the RNA-bound complexes reveal that in the system structured by Halpin-Healy (RNA-HH), the Cas8 bundle is trapped in a closed state minimum and lacks a stable open state (Fig. 2A). In the system structured by Jia (RNA-Jia), the Cas8 bundle displays a minimum corresponding to the closed state, and a diffusive region leading to a fully open state.

  In the DNA-bound state, the complex obtained by Halpin-Healy (DNA-HH) displays a higher barrier between closed and open states (i.e., ~10 kcal/mol), compared to Jia’s complex (DNA-Jia), in which a ~5 kcal/mol barrier separates the two states (Fig. 2B). Notably, the Halpin-Healy structure is representative of an incomplete R-loop state, whereas the R-loop is formed in Jia’s structure. Hence, when the R-loop is partially formed, the closed state is favored, whereas upon R-loop formation, the bundle assumes both the open and closed states, separated by a ~5 kcal/mol barrier. This indicates that, as the DNA TS binds the RNA forming the R-loop, it lowers the barrier for accessing the open state. This is in line with the hypothesis, based on the biochemical data of a related type 1F Cascade from P. aeruginosa, that conformational changes of the bundle could aid R-loop formation (10, 11).


### Cas7.1 Favors the Conformational Change of the Cas8 Bundle in the RNA-Bound Complex.
  Free energy simulations revealed an appreciable discrepancy in the free energy profiles of the RNA-bound Cascade–TniQ complexes (Fig. 2A). To understand this observation, we analyzed the interactions established by the Cas8 bundle along its conformational change. As a result, the bundle interacts predominantly with the Cas7 subunits (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). In Jia’s RNA-bound system, it mainly interacts with Cas7.1, while it prefers Cas7.2 in the HH’s system. Interestingly, a loop of approximately 10 amino acids within the Cas7.1 subunit (residues 51–60, hereafter referred to as the Cas7.1 loop) exhibits a notable difference in position relative to the Cas8 bundle. In Jia’s RNA-bound system, when the Cas8 bundle assumes a closed state, this Cas7.1 loop binds the Cas8 bundle (Fig. 3A and SI Appendix, Fig. S7). In contrast, this interaction is absent in HH’s RNA-bound complex (Fig. 3B).

  We thereby sought to understand the importance of Cas7.1 binding on the conformational change of the Cas8 bundle. Toward this aim, we performed additional free energy simulations of the RNA-HH and the RNA-Jia systems in the presence of a Cas7.1 bound and unbound to Cas8 in the closed state. The US approach was applied to simulate the open–closed conformational transition of Cas8, as described above (details in SI Appendix and SI Appendix, Fig. S8). These simulations report a reduction in the free energy profile in the RNA-HH system when Cas7.1 binds Cas8, compared to the system with an unbound Cas7.1 (Fig. 3C). A similar trend is evident in the RNA-Jia system, where the free energy profile decreases in the presence of a bound Cas7.1 (Fig. 3C). This indicates that, in the RNA-bound state, the Cas7.1–Cas8 interaction favors the conformational change of Cas8 bundle.

  Analysis of the interactions between the Cas8 bundle and the Cas7 subunits along the free energy profiles reveals that when Cas7.1 is unbound, the interactions between Cas8 and Cas7.2 are stronger than when Cas7.1 is bound (SI Appendix, Figs. S7 and S9). Indeed, when the Cas7.1 loop does not interact with Cas8, the bundle binds the hairpin loops of Cas7.2 (residues 48–63 and 228–243, Fig. 3B), establishing unfavorable contacts that hamper its transition. To investigate how interactions between the bundle and hairpin loops of Cas7.2 contribute to the observed discrepancies in the free energy profiles, we introduced a “flow index.” This metric enabled us to characterize how the Cas8–Cas7.2 interactions affect the tendency of the Cas8 bundle to escape the closed-state minimum. We evaluated the linear interaction energy [LIE, comprising electrostatic and van der Waals energy (20) between the two regions of interest (the Cas8 bundle and the hairpin loops of Cas7.2) and the contacts established between them. Our FI is defined as the normalized product of the LIE with the number of contacts established by the regions of interest (details in Materials and Methods). As the FI assumes more negative values, the interaction between the regions of interest disfavors the transition to the open state, and the free energy profiles display steeper slopes. In the presence of an unbound Cas7.1, the FI decreases as the Cas8 bundle changes conformation (Fig. 3D, gray). This shift reveals that the interactions with the hairpin loops of Cas7.2 in both the HH and Jia RNA-bound systems hamper the bundle transition toward the open state. In contrast, when Cas7.1 is bound (Fig. 3D, purple), the FI increases toward zero, as a sign that the interactions between Cas8 and Cas7.2 are minimal and do not significantly influence the free energy profiles. Overall, these additional free energy simulations indicate that, in the RNA-bound states, the interaction with Cas7.1 promotes the Cas8 transition, mitigating unfavorable contacts with Cas7.2.


### Synchronized Dynamics Reinforce the Role of Cas7.1.
  Cas7.1 plays a critical role in the open–closed conformational transition of the Cas8 bundle within the RNA-bound Cascade–TniQ complex (Fig. 3). To understand how Cas8 dynamically couples with the other neighboring protein subunits (i.e., Cas6, and TniQ) during the open–closed conformational change, we performed a wavelet analysis (21). This analysis decomposes the trajectories of the atoms of interest (i.e., the Cα of Cas8, Cas6, and TniQ) into wavelets to examine the coupling between the wavelet scales of different domains. Wavelet heat maps have been built by plotting the wavelet scale for the Cascade–TniQ domains of interest along the open–closed transition of the bundle (Fig. 4). Short wavelet scales capture fast protein motions, while larger scales correspond to slower motions (details in SI Appendix). In Jia’s RNA-bound complex, in which Cas7.1 is bound to Cas8, we observe a distinctive shift from fast to slow modes as the bundle falls into the closed state (at ~9 Å RMSD, Fig. 4A). This shift couples the dynamics of Cas8 with Cas6, and TniQ. In the RNA-HH system, where Cas7.1 is unbound, the system acquires a cacophonous mix of modes in the wavelet scales (Fig. 4B) and as the bundle changes conformation, its dynamics decouple from those of the neighboring domains. This trend is maintained in the RNA-HH and RNA-Jia systems displaying a bound and unbound Cas7.1, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S10), and demonstrates an important role of TniQ and Cas6 during the Cas8 conformational change. Since the Jia and HH′ systems mainly differ in the Cas7.1 gate orientation, these analyses reinforce the role of Cas7.1 during the bundle conformational change, synchronizing the motions of Cas8 with its neighboring Cas7.6 and TniQ.

  Collectively, our findings indicate that Cas7.1 facilitates the open–closed conformational shift in the Cas9 bundle by serving two distinct functions. First, it reduces unfavorable interactions with Cas7.2 (Fig. 3), contributing an enthalpic effect. Second, it dynamically couples the Cas8 subunit with Cas7.6 and TniQ (Fig. 4), driving a coordinated shift in the conformational states of these subunits.


## Discussion
  The V. cholerae Cascade–TniQ complex has introduced a new paradigm in biology, demonstrating that CRISPR-associated proteins can guide the precise transposition of genes to targeted sites (1, 2). Structural studies of the Cascade–TniQ complex, captured at various stages of R-loop formation, did not resolve the highly dynamic Cas8 bundle (8, 9). This motif was shown to play a role during R-loop formation in similar systems (10, 11), raising questions about how its conformational changes influence the binding of target DNA to the guide RNA. Thus, we aimed to elucidate the dynamics of the Cas8 bundle within the Cascade–TniQ complex. Structural modeling identified two main configurations of the bundle—open and closed states—and suggest an open–closed conformational transition, which we thoroughly investigated using free energy methods.

  Studies of the conformational transition in the RNA-bound states reveal that the Cas8’s shift is facilitated by its interaction with Cas7.1, binding a 10-residue loop (residues 51–60). This interaction mitigates unfavorable contacts with Cas7.2 that would otherwise lock the bundle in a closed state (Figs. 2 and 3). Moreover, when Cas8 binds to Cas7.1, the conformational change of the bundle couples with the dynamics of the adjacent Cas7.6 subunit and the TniQ dimer (Fig. 4). This reinforces the role of Cas7.1, synchronizing the motions of Cas8 with its neighboring domains. These observations suggest that Cas7.1 exerts a pivotal twofold role: it alleviates unfavorable contacts with Cas7.2 and promotes the dynamic coupling of Cas8 with its neighboring subunits. This combined function lowers the energy barrier for the transition to the open state, which primes the complex for DNA binding.

  In DNA-bound states, the Cas8 bundle undergoes a favorable conformational change as the DNA fully complements the guide RNA, forming the R-loop (Fig. 2B). Indeed, when the R-loop is only partially formed, the bundle preferentially adopts a closed conformation. As the R-loop forms, the open state becomes more accessible, facilitating the interaction between the positively charged residues of the bundle and the DNA. In this respect, biochemical experiments of a related type 1F Cascade from P. aeruginosa have demonstrated these interactions to be essential for DNA binding (11). Rollins and colleagues have shown that substituting positively charged residues in the bundle impairs DNA binding, suggesting that conformational changes in this region may facilitate R-loop formation by interacting with the DNA. Our findings support this hypothesis, revealing that as the R-loop forms, the open state of the bundle becomes more thermodynamically accessible. Notably, the Cas8 bundle is enriched with arginine and lysine residues (viz., K278, R279, K291, R295, K298, R306, R310, R311, K312, K314, R322, K352, R353, K362, K369, R370, SI Appendix, Fig. S11). In the closed state, these residues form salt bridges with carboxylate groups in Cas7.1, TniQ1, and TniQ2. Upon transitioning to the open state, many of these positively charged residues become solvent-exposed, while others engage with different regions of Cas8. This solvent exposure enhances their ability to interact with DNA. Hence, as the R-loop proceeds, the ease of the Cas8 conformational change is critical to enable the bundle to engage more effectively with DNA, leading the process to completion.

  In summary, integrating our findings with existing experimental evidence enables us to propose a mechanistic model outlining the role of the Cas8 bundle in nucleic acid binding (Fig. 5). In the RNA-bound state, the interaction between the Cas8 bundle and the Cas7.1 loop promotes the transition to the open state (Fig. 5, path 1). This opening is essential for DNA binding. Upon DNA binding, the open state becomes more accessible as the DNA TS matches the guide RNA (Fig. 5B). The opening of the helical bundle at this stage is essential to enable its positively charged residues to interact with the DNA, facilitating its repositioning as suggested by Rollins et al. (11). This leads to a completely formed R-loop (Fig. 5C). On the other hand, in the absence of a Cas8–Cas7.1 interaction (Fig. 5, path 2), the Cas8 bundle cannot easily transition to the open state (Fig. 5D), which inhibits full R-loop formation.

  The Cas8 conformational change is thereby critical to leading R-loop formation to completion. In this respect, it is insightful to compare our findings with other CRISPR–Cas systems. In the Cas proteins, the formation of the R-loop is facilitated by highly flexible recognition domains (22–24). Notably, CRISPR–Cas systems do not utilize ATP hydrolysis for R-loop formation; instead, the dynamics of the conformational changes in their flexible subunits promote R-loop formation. This leads to a general theme at play in CRISPR–Cas systems, where DNA binding is coupled with large conformational changes of the binding domains.

  To better understand how Cas8 undergoes these conformational changes and its role in the biophysical function of the Cascade–TniQ complex, experimental validation of our dynamic findings would be invaluable. Biochemical experiments, such as site-directed mutagenesis to assess the impact of mutations in the positively charged residues of the Cas8 bundle on DNA binding, as well as investigations into how changes in the interaction between the Cas7.1 loop and Cas8 influence nucleic acid binding, would provide critical validation. Single-molecule experiments would be particularly well-suited to characterize the conformational changes of the Cas8 bundle at various stages of R-loop formation. These approaches have proven instrumental in elucidating the conformational dynamics of the highly flexible HNH domain in CRISPR–Cas9 (25, 26) and could similarly provide critical insights into the dynamics of the Cas8 bundle in the Cascade–TniQ complex. Additionally, solution NMR, particularly relaxation experiments, could reveal intrinsic dynamics of the isolated Cas8 bundle and the role of its positively charged residues. The integration of such experimental data with MD simulations of the full-length protein in complex with nucleic acids has previously advanced our understanding of critical dynamic features in CRISPR–Cas9 (27–29). Employing a similar approach for Cas8 within the Cascade–TniQ complex could yield equally important insights, further advancing our understanding of its functional dynamics.

  Overall, these findings depict a highly dynamic helical bundle in the Cas8 protein, playing an essential role in R-loop formation. These outcomes provide a dynamic and thermodynamic representation of a critical conformational change within one of the largest CRISPR systems and lay the foundation for further experimental investigations to fully elucidate Cas8’s role in R-loop formation. Overall, the insights into Cas8 dynamics presented here enhance our understanding of the transposon-encoded Cascade–TniQ biophysical mechanism and provide valuable contributions to our knowledge of the transposition process.


## Materials and Methods

### Structural Models.
  Computational studies have been based on the cryo-EM structures of the V. Cholerae type 1F Cascade variant in complex with RNA and DNA, from two independent studies. Halpin-Healy et al. (8) reported RNA- (EMD-20350, PDB 6PIG) and DNA-bound (EMD-20351, PDB 6PIJ) complexes at resolutions of 3.50 Å and 2.90 Å, respectively. Jia et al. (9) obtained structures for the RNA- (EMD-21126, PDB: 6V9Q) and DNA-bound (EMD-21146, PDB: 6VBW) complexes at 2.90 Å and 3.20 Å, respectively. To model the Cas8 bundle (residues 277–385) and the missing loops (i.e., ~10 amino acids within the Cas proteins), AlphaFold2 (13) was combined with PyRosetta (14) (details in SI Appendix). This enabled preserving the position of the experimental structures while adding the AlphaFold2 models through structural threading. The obtained structures were embedded in explicit water, adding Na+ ions to neutralize the total charge under physiological conditions. Orthorhombic periodic simulation cells measuring 187 × 216 × 248 Å3 were used, resulting in ~928,000 atoms for each system.


### MD Simulations.
  MD simulations were performed using a protocol tailored for protein/nucleic acid complexes (30), also applied in studies of CRISPR–Cas systems (31–33). The Amber ff19SB (34) force field was employed, incorporating the OL15 (35) and OL3 (36) corrections for DNA and RNA, respectively. The TIP3P (37) model was used for water molecules. An integration time step of 2 fs was applied. To refine the position of the Cas8 bundle in the closed state with respect to the weak cryo-EM density observed by Halpin-Healy (8), an MD flexible fitting (MDFF) approach was used (15, 16). The AlphaFold2/PyRosetta models of the RNA- and DNA-bound states displaying a closed conformation of the Cas8 bundle were flexibly fitted into their respective cryo-EM densities [EMD-20350, EMD-20351 (8). The MDFF bias was applied in stages with progressively higher values of the scaling factor ξ in the range 0.01 to 0.1. Convergence was evaluated by assessing the RMSD relative to the initial structure and the cross-correlation coefficient between the original EM density and a computed density map derived from the atomic model, following the approach described by Trabuco et al. (15) Classical and free energy simulations were performed using the GPU-empowered version of AMBER 22 (38). MDFF simulations were performed using NAMD 2.14 (39). Details are in SI Appendix.


### Free Energy Simulations.
  Free energy simulations were performed using the US method (18). This approach was used to describe the conformational transition between the closed and open states of the Cas8 bundle and compute the associated energetics. In this method, several simulations (US windows) are run in parallel with additional harmonic bias potential applied to selected RCs. In this study, the RC was defined as the difference in RMSD of the backbone atoms’ positions in the Cas8 bundle, relative to both the closed and open states. The initial RC value (~15 Å RMSD) was progressively reduced toward the final target RMSD (0 Å). The RC was discretized into bins of 0.5 Å RMSD increments, spanning centers from 0.5 to 15.0 Å, resulting in 30 windows. Each window was simulated for approximately 60 ns, employing a collective force k of 50 kcal mol−1 Å−2. In total, the US simulations produced a cumulative ensemble of ~1.8 μs per system. Using a simple reweighting scheme to obtain unbiased probabilities, the free energy along the RC was computed. Specifically, the potential of mean force (PMF) profiles were computed using the variational free energy perturbation (vFEP) method (19). Bootstrap analysis was used to estimate the associated errors. Details are in SI Appendix.


### FI.
  To assess how interactions between the Cas8 bundle and the hairpin loops of Cas7.2 contribute to the free energy profiles derived from US simulations, we used a metric termed FI. This metric integrates the linear interaction energy (LIE) (20), encompassing both electrostatic and van der Waals contributions, with the number of contacts formed between the two regions of interest (i.e., residues 278–388 of Cas8 and residues 48–63 and 228–243 of Cas7.2). The FI is defined as the product of the LIE and the number of contacts, normalized by the average number of contacts observed across US simulations. A consistent 4.5 Å cutoff was applied for both contact identification (defined as interactions between heavy atoms) and interaction energy calculations. Since the LIE assumes negative values (SI Appendix, Figs. S7–S9), more negative values of the FI correspond to stronger interactions between the regions of interest. This analysis was conducted along the Cas8 conformational change. For each US window, spanning the RC from 14.5 to 2 Å in 0.5 Å intervals (as described above), we computed the product of the LIE and the number of contacts for each simulation frame. The resulting values were averaged, and SD were calculated to capture fluctuations across frames.


### Wavelet Analysis.
  Wavelet analysis decomposes a signal into a series of wavelets, simplifying its structure similar to the Fourier series. The wavelet transform employs orthogonal basis functions to break down the signal into wavelet coefficients, capturing both frequency and temporal information. In the analysis of MD simulations, continuous wavelet transforms (CWT) are used to decompose a time series into different coefficients characterized in the time scale domain (21). By conducting a significance test, motions are classified as random or significant, identifying long-scale (low frequency) and short-scale (high frequency) vibrational wavelets. In this study, wavelet analysis was applied using the displacement of each atom i from a reference structure (i.e., its position in the first frame) as the input signal. This analysis focused on Cas8 and neighboring subunits (i.e., the Cα atoms of Cas8, Cas6, and TniQ.1-2) during the open–close transition of the Cas8 bundle in the RNA-bound systems (i.e., along the RC of US simulations). All frames were RMS-fitted to the initial frame to remove global translational and rotational motions. The analysis was carried out using the code developed by Heidari et al. (21). Details are in SI Appendix.
