# Cross-interaction with amyloid-β drives pathogenic structural transformation within the amyloidogenic core region of TDP-43


## Abstract
  Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the world’s most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder, characterized neuropathologically by senile plaques and neurofibrillary tangles formed by amyloid-β (Aβ) and tau, respectively. Notably, a subset of AD patients also exhibits pathological aggregates composed of TAR DNA-Binding Protein 43 (TDP-43). Clinically, the presence of TDP-43 co-pathology in AD correlates with more severe cognitive decline and faster disease progression. While previous studies have shown that TDP-43 can exacerbate Ab toxicity and modulate its assembly dynamics by delaying fibrillization and promoting oligomer formation, the impact of Aβ interaction on the structural dynamics and aggregation of TDP-43 remains unclear. Here, we employed all-atom discrete molecular dynamics simulations to study the direct interaction between Aβ42, the more amyloidogenic isoform of Aβ, and the amyloidogenic core region (ACR) of TDP-43, which spans residues 311–360 and is critical for TDP-43 aggregation. We found that monomeric Aβ42 could strongly bind to the ACR, establishing sustained contact through intermolecular hydrogen bonding. In contrast, simulation of ACR dimerization revealed a transient helix-helix interaction, experimentally known to drive the phase separation behavior of TDP-43. The binding of the ACR to an Aβ42 fibril seed resulted in significant structural transformation, with the complete unfolding of the helical region being observed. Furthermore, interaction with the Aβ42 fibril seed catalyzed the formation of a parallel, in-register intermolecular β-sheet between two ACR monomers. Collectively, our computational study provides important theoretical insights into TDP-43 pathology in AD, demonstrating that Aβ42, especially in its fibrillar form, may catalyze the pathogenic structural transformation within the TDP-43 ACR that initiates its aberrant aggregation.


## Introduction
  TAR DNA-binding Protein 43 (TDP-43) is a highly conserved, ubiquitously expressed RNA/DNA-binding protein with numerous functions relating to RNA regulation and metabolism.1,2 Although known to shuttle between the nucleus and cytoplasm, TDP-43 predominantly resides in the nucleus where it performs the majority of its functions.3,4 However, in neurodegenerative diseases such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and frontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTLD), TDP-43 becomes mislocalized from the nucleus and forms amyloid-like aggregates within the cytoplasm of affected neurons.4–6 Although primarily associated with ALS and FTLD, TDP-43 pathology is also present in a large subset of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) patient brains, with abnormal TDP-43 expression and immunoreactivity being reported in up to 57% of AD cases.7–9 Traditional AD pathology consists of senile plaques composed of the peptide amyloid-β (Aβ) and neurofibrillary tangles formed by the microtubule-binding protein tau.10 Interestingly, AD cases featuring the additional presence of TDP-43 pathology show more severe cognitive decline and memory loss than patients with Aβ and tau pathology alone.7,11,12 With AD being the most prevalent neurodegenerative disease worldwide, the presence of TDP-43 pathology in large subset of AD patients has begun to attract attention.13 However, the precise mechanisms underlying the development of TDP-43 pathology and the associated neurotoxicity in ALS and FTLD, as well as in the co-pathology setting of AD, remain unclear.

  Encoded by the TARDBP gene, TDP-43 is a 414-residue-long member of the heterogenous nuclear ribonucleoprotein (hnRNP) family and contains multiple domains responsible for mediating its hnRNP-related functions. Namely, a well-folded N-terminal domain (NTD) promotes dimerization that appears critical for splicing activity.14 The NTD is followed by a flexible region containing the nuclear localization signal (NLS), which is recognized by importin α1/β for nuclear translocation.15 Downstream of the NLS are two tandem RNA recognition motifs responsible for the RNA-binding function of TDP-43, which have been proposed as possibly related to its aberrant self-assembly.16 However, most closely linked to pathological TDP-43 aggregation is its prion-like, low-complexity C-terminal domain (CTD), which spans approximately residues 270–414 and conspicuously contains the vast majority of ALS-linked mutation sites.17,18 The CTD is mostly intrinsically disordered and participates in protein-protein interactions, the disruption of which may be relevant to disease pathophysiology.2,19–23 Furthermore, the CTD mediates TDP-43 liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS), which may serve a physiological purpose in stress granule formation.24,25 Within the CTD, the region spanning residues 311–360 has been shown to be crucial for CTD aggregation and cytoplasmic inclusion formation, and has thus been referred to as the amyloidogenic core region (ACR) of TDP-43.26 Indeed, the residues of this region have been consistently resolved within recently solved structures of TDP-43 filaments extracted from patients with ALS and FLTD.27,28 Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) studies demonstrated that the hydrophobic patch spanning residues 318–343 forms a dynamic helix-turn-helix structure in solution, which is disrupted by certain ALS-linked mutations such as G335D and Q343R.26,29

  The LLPS behavior of TDP-43 appears to be driven by transient intermolecular interactions between residues within its CTD, leading to the formation of dynamic protein droplets.17 In particular, the α-helical region within the ACR drives LLPS through helix-helix contacts, with the self-assembly of TDP-43 being highly sensitive to structural changes within this reigon.30 The conversion of dynamic liquid-like TDP-43 droplets to solid aggregate species constitutes an aberrant liquid-to-solid phase transition (LSPT) event that appears important to the pathophysiology of TDP-43 proteinopathies.25 The molecular events leading up to pathological TDP-43 LSPT remain poorly understood, with possibly influential factors including mutations, persistent stress, and aging.17 Regardless, the α-helix to β-sheet transition within the helical region of the ACR represents a critical event initiating pathological TDP-43 self-assembly into β-shee-trich aggregate species.26 Thus, investigating the physiochemical basis and triggers of this pathogenic structural transformation is of interest for developing an understanding of the etiology of TDP-43 proteinopathies from a molecular perspective. Although dysregulated homotypic interactions are likely of crucial importance in nucleating pathological TDP-43 aggregation, aberrant cross-interactions with other species might also play a role. Amyloid-cross seeding is a well-studied phenomenon whereby one species promotes the aggregation of another, or the two interact synergistically to accelerate self-assembly.31–33 For example, it was shown that α-synuclein, an amyloidogenic intrinsically disordered protein implicated in several neurodegenerative diseases, could cross-seed the aggregation of the TDP-43 CTD.34 Further, it was demonstrated that α-synuclein could interact with droplets formed by the TDP-43 CTD and RNA, triggering gelation and the nucleation of amyloid fibrils.35

  Here, we sought to investigate whether a similar process may be relevant to the development of TDP-43 pathology in AD. Previously, it was demonstrated that TDP-43 could directly interact with Aβ and inhibit its fibrillization while worsening toxicity.36 Additionally, TDP-43 oligomers were found to colocalize with intraneuronal Aβ in AD patients with TDP-43 pathology.36 Using discrete molecular dynamics (DMD) simulations, we previously examined the direct molecular interaction between Aβ and TDP-43, focusing on how the interaction affected Aβ structure and assembly.37 DMD is a rapid and predictive molecular dynamics algorithm used by many groups to study intrinsically disordered proteins and their aggregation.38–40 Notably, DMD, like other MD simulation methods, is limited by the inability to sample the long timescales associated with in vivo aggregation, which are often on the order of hours to days. However, the conformational dynamics that can be observed in silico are useful for understanding crucial molecular events driving aggregation, such as structural transformations and early-stage dimeric or oligomeric species. In the present study, we extend our previous work to assess how interaction with Aβ, specifically its more amyloidogenic isoform Aβ42, affects TDP-43 structure and self-assembly propensity. Using the NMR-solved solution structure of the ACR spanning residues 311–360 of TDP-43, we conducted extensive all-atom DMD simulations to study the interaction between this region and Aβ42, accumulating more than 600 μs of total simulation time across all systems. We found that ACR dimerization was characterized by transient intermolecular helix-helix interactions, whereas binding between ACR and Aβ42 monomers was longer-lived and featured intermolecular hydrogen bonding, occasionally promoting α-helix to β-sheet transition within the ACR. Most strikingly, we observed a strong interaction between the ACR and the elongation surface of an Aβ42 fibril seed, with backbone hydrogen bonding stimulating pronounced structural transformation within the ACR. Our results suggest that cross-interaction with Aβ42 may lower the energetic barrier to ACR unfolding, thereby acting as a catalyst for TDP-43 misfolding and aggregation. Thus, our theoretical study reveals novel insights regarding the cross-interaction between Aβ and TDP-43, providing a potential molecular explanation for the development of TDP-43 pathology in AD.


## Results and Discussion

### ACR dimerization was characterized by transient helix-helix intermolecular interactions
  To establish a control baseline, we first sought to characterize the conformational dynamics of ACR dimerization and the relevant homotypic interactions. We found that the two ACR monomers interacted in a highly dynamic and transient manner, forming dimers that frequently dissociated and re-associated. The time evolution of intermolecular residue contacts (Figure S1) and secondary structure content (Figure S2) averaged across all trajectories demonstrated rapid system equilibration. Single-trajectory analysis revealed that residue-wise secondary structure remained relatively consistent across 1500 ns of simulation time, whereas the number of intermolecular residue contacts fluctuated significantly, corresponding to dissociation and re-association events (Figure 1a,b). Notably, the helical secondary structure content of the input ACR monomer solution structure was largely preserved during dimerization. The “N-terminal” ACR α-helix spanning approximately residues 321–330 proved robust and remained present throughout the entirety of the simulation time (Figure 1a,b). Contrastingly, the “C-terminal” α-helix spanning approximately residues 335–343 was highly dynamic and occasionally absent during dimerization events mediated by interactions between N-terminal helices, as visualized by the 1450 ns snapshot in Figure 1b. To understand how inter-peptide contact impacted helical secondary structure content within the ACR monomers, we computed a two-dimensional potential of mean force (2D PMF) as a function of the number of intermolecular residue contacts and the fraction of total ACR residues adopting helical secondary structure. Interestingly, the helix fraction was approximately equal between basins corresponding to bound and unbound states, and extensive dimerization did result in a clear increase or decrease in helical content (Figure 1c). Notably, any degree of inter-peptide contact rendered states with zero helix content completely inaccessible. The 2D PMF as a function of ACR helix content and β-sheet content featured a single basin corresponding to high helical content and very low β-sheet content (Figure 1d). Thus, our simulations of ACR homodimerizations did not capture the α-helix to β-sheet transformation that initiates pathogenic TDP-43 aggregation, but rather demonstrated the transient homotypic interactions that drive the physiological high-density liquid-like phase behavior of TDP-43 necessary for LLPS.25,30

  Contact map analysis revealed that ACR homodimerization was driven largely by hydrophobic contacts between residues within the N-terminal portion of the ACR sequence (Figure S3). Namely, the two highest contact frequency residue pairs were the in-register contacts made by L330 and A326, with out-of-register contacts involving these residues also occurring with high frequency (Figure S3). Other residues with significant contributions to inter-peptide contact included I318 as well as aromatic residues F316 and W334. Notably, W334 has been previously identified as a critical residue involved in TDP-43 LLPS.41 The most prominent pattern in the homodimer contact map suggested that residues 322–330 maintained helical secondary structure during inter-peptide contact, as evidenced by the spacing between high-frequency, in-register contact residue pairs at positions M322, A326, and L330 (Figure S3). Interestingly, minimal contact occurred between residues within the C-terminal α-helix, likely due to the greater proportion of polar residues and more disordered structure during simulation compared to the that of the highly hydrophobic and robust N-terminal α-helix. Our observation of dynamic interaction between ACR monomers mediated via hydrophobic contacts is consistent with experimental evidence suggesting that LLPS behavior of the TDP-43 CTD is driven by transient intermolecular interactions involving the helical segment of its CTD hydrophobic core.42,43 Our simulation results suggest that the robust N-terminal α-helix is of particular importance to this interaction, and likely serves as the anchoring point of contact between monomers. Thus, we hypothesize that the unfolding of the helix to allow for intermolecular β-sheet formation may be the rate limiting step in the TDP-43 aggregation pathway, initiating the pathological transition from liquid-like droplets to solid aggregates.


### Aβ42 binds the TDP-43 ACR via intermolecular hydrogen bonding
  Senile plaques, one of the neuropathological hallmarks of AD, are extracellular aggregates composed of the peptide Aβ and other protein components.44 Aβ is produced via the proteolytic cleavage of amyloid precursor protein by β- and γ-secretases, ultimately being released into the extracellular space.45 Thus, Aβ aggregation is generally viewed as an extracellular process. However, accumulating evidence suggests that intraneuronal accumulation of Aβ is not only pervasive but also a significant driver of Aβ-related neurotoxicity.46–49 Intriguingly, TDP-43 oligomers have been previously demonstrated to colocalize with intraneuronal Aβ in AD patients.36 Thus, a direct interaction between Aβ and TDP-43 is highly plausible and supported by neuropathological and experimental evidence.36 We previously used a computational peptide array method to screen for interaction between monomeric Aβ and the TDP-43 CTD and identified a binding hotspot located near I318.37 In the present study, we expanded on this work by simulating an Aβ42 monomer with the “full-length” ACR spanning residues 311–360, as opposed to a short 15-residue-long fragment. We observed a strong interaction that was dynamically distinct from ACR homodimerization, with intermolecular hydrogen bonding supporting sustained binding between Aβ42 and the ACR. Contact map analysis revealed that heterodimer formation was driven by binding between the ACR N-terminus, outside the helical region, and the C-terminal hydrophobic core of Aβ42, which spans approximately residues 30–42 (Figure S4). The majority of the highest-frequency contacts were between hydrophobic residues, with L34, M35, and F19 of Aβ42 contributing significantly to binding. Meanwhile, TDP-43 residues F316 and I318 made the most contacts with Aβ42, interacting non-specifically with hydrophobic Aβ42 residues (Figure S4). Other strongly interacting TDP-43 residues were likewise located near the ACR N-terminus, whereas both hydrophobic cores of Aβ42 were hotspots for interaction with the ACR. Interestingly, outside of the ACR N-terminus hotspot, Aβ42 also strongly interacted with certain ACR residues that were found to be important for ACR homodimerization, including L330 and W334 (Figure S4).

  Given the significant hydrophobic character of both sequences, interaction between Aβ42 and the ACR was expected. Interestingly, however, heterodimerization proved stronger and distinct in dynamics from ACR homodimerization. Single-trajectory analysis demonstrated the heterogeneity of heterodimer conformations (Figure 2a–c), which generally featured intermolecular hydrogen bonding between the ACR N-terminus and Aβ42 C-terminal core but varied in the involvement of other residues within the ACR. In some cases, the ACR C-terminal helical region unfolded and formed an intermolecular β-sheet with Aβ42 (Figure 2a,c), whereas the robust N-terminal α-helix was preserved during binding. To assess the effect of heterodimer formation on ACR helix content, we computed a 2D PMF similar to that shown in Figure 1c. Here, two bound-state basins in the energy landscape were present, with one of them representing conformations in which both ACR helices were present (Figure 2d, label I) and the other representing those featuring an unfolded C-terminal helix, resulting in a reduced helix fraction (Figure 2d, label II). In contrast from homodimerization, which necessarily featured at-least partially helical ACR secondary structure, heterodimer formation allowed ACR conformations featuring total helix unfolding to become accessible within the energy landscape (Figure 2d). Furthermore, heterodimerization significantly altered the conformational energy landscape with respect to helix content and β-sheet content. Whereas the β-sheet fraction of the ACR was generally zero in the case of homodimerization (Figure 1d), binding with Aβ42 resulted in a basin featuring a balance of helix and b-sheet content (Figure 2e). Thus, our results generally demonstrated that binding with Aβ42 supported secondary structure transformation within the ACR, most frequently the unfolding of the C-terminal ACR helical region to become either disordered or part of an intermolecular β-sheet with Aβ42.

  Regarding differing interaction dynamics, ACR homodimers underwent frequent dissociation and re-association without achieving sustained inter-peptide contact, while Aβ42–ACR homodimers were more stable and intermolecular hydrogen bonding enabled prolonged binding (Figure 2f). The greater binding strength of the ACR with Aβ42 as opposed to another ACR monomer was further illustrated by a greater overall contact frequency and higher median number of intermolecular residue contacts (Figure 2g). Computing a 2D PMF as a function of system potential energy and residue contacts, we found that the difference in energy between basins corresponding to the bound and unbound states was more substantial in the case of heterotypic interaction with Aβ42, indicating greater binding affinity (Figure S5). Furthermore, the bound state basin was lower in effective free energy for ACR interaction with Aβ42 relative to the corresponding basin for ACR homodimerization (Figure S5), providing additional validation of the greater strength of the heterotypic interaction. Experimental work has demonstrated that transient homotypic interactions between intact helices within the ACR of crucial importance for TDP-43 LLPS.50 Thus, our simulation results suggest that heterotypic interactions with Aβ42 could disrupt the phase behavior of TDP-43 by introducing secondary structure changes, namely helix unfolding, that impede its ability to undergo LLPS. Furthermore, given that homodimerization was highly dynamic compared to heterodimerization with Aβ42, which involved sustained hydrogen-bonded interactions, we hypothesize that partitioning of Aβ42 into a TDP-43 droplet would alter its dynamics and potentially trigger gelation. However, future experimental studies utilizing fluorescence recovery are needed to validate this hypothesis.


### The relative concentration of Aβ42 modulated secondary structure transformation within the TDP-43 ACR
  Having observed intermolecular β-sheet formation between the ACR and Aβ42, a feature that was essentially absent from ACR homodimerization, we simulated a trimer system containing one Aβ42 monomer and two ACR monomers to investigate whether interaction with Aβ42 could nucleate hydrogen-bonded ACR dimerization. Additionally, we simulated an ACR homotrimer system to assess whether increased concentration would result in secondary structure changes not observed in the homodimer system. Furthermore, we simulated a heterotrimer system containing two Aβ42 monomers and one ACR monomers to explore the effect of increased Aβ42 concentration on secondary structure changes within the ACR monomer induced by heterotypic interaction. In general, the results across these trimer systems demonstrated that the extent of secondary transformation within the ACR was dependent on the relative concentration of Aβ42. Simulation of three ACR monomers resulted in the formation of highly dynamic, helix-rich oligomers (Figure 3a,b). The conformational dynamics of homotrimer system were highly similar to those of the homodimer system, with ACR monomers maintaining an N-terminal α-helix while the C-terminal helical region was more prone to becoming unstructured. The secondary structure energy landscape of the homotrimer system, however, was even more tightly constrained (Figure 3c), resulting from more stable C-terminal α-helix formation, which was often bridged with the N-terminal α-helix to form a single α-helix spanning the entire hydrophobic core26 of the ACR. Thus, overall, the ACR homotrimer system further demonstrated the transient, helix-helix interactions known to contribute to TDP-43 LLPS.50

  One Aβ42 monomer being simulated with two ACR monomers resulted in an expansion of the ACR secondary structure energy basin toward decreased helix content and increased β-sheet content (Figure 3f), reflecting helix unfolding events and intermolecular β-sheet formation induced via binding with Aβ42. However, these did not occur to the same extent as in the heterodimer system, with no clearly defined secondary basin corresponding to C-terminal helix unfolding being present. Commonly, only one of the two ACR monomers underwent significant secondary structure transformation via hydrogen bonding with Aβ42, while the other maintained helical structure (Figure 3d). The formation of a parallel intermolecular β-sheet between ACR monomers involving the flexible N-terminal region was also observed and was facilitated via binding between one of the monomers and Aβ42 (Figure 3e). However, due to the favorability of hydrophobicity-driven helix-helix interactions between ACR monomers, helical secondary structure content typically remained high. Thus, although Aβ42 was able to competitively bind one of the ACR monomers due to its greater interaction energy (Figure S5), this typically did not result in both ACR monomers undergoing significant structural transformation. When the stoichiometry was reversed, however, and two Aβ42 monomers were simulated with a single ACR monomer, more dramatic structural change within the ACR was observed. The secondary structure energy basin significantly expanded to include a secondary basin corresponding to unfolding of the C-terminal helical region, with states featuring β-sheet-dominant secondary structure becoming accessible (Figure 3j). The two Aβ42 monomers generally formed a hydrogen-bonded dimer, which could become the core of a larger, multilayer β-sheet involving the flexible ACR N-terminus and the unfolded C-terminal helical region (Figure 3h). Transiently, the unfolded C-terminal helical region formed an intramolecular β-sheet with the flexible C-terminus, which inturn was part of the greater intermolecular β-sheet with the Aβ42 dimer (Figure 3i). Notably, although the energy landscape indicated that states with zero helical content were possible (Figure 3j), the robust N-terminal α-helix generally persisted. Our heterotrimer system results indicate that sub-stoichiometric monomeric Aβ42 may not be able to efficiently catalyze pathogenic structural transformation within the ACR. Rather, aggregate species of Aβ42, which contain higher β-sheet content and are stabilized by extensive intermolecular hydrogen bonding, may be able to more potently induce α-helix to β-sheet transition due to the provision of a more stable template structure with unsaturated hydrogen bond donors and acceptors. Thus, we further hypothesize that these aggregates species may be more apt to disrupt TDP-43 LLPS and trigger pathological LSPT.


### The TDP-43 ACR interacted strongly with an Aβ42 fibril seed
  To investigate the interaction between the TDP-43 ACR and aggregate Aβ42 species, we simulated the ACR monomer with an Aβ42 fibril seed. Due to its recent release, high resolution, and derivation from human AD brain tissue, we selected the fibril structure with PDB ID 7Q4B51 for use in our simulations. Our results showed strong binding between the ACR and the fibril seed, with stable contact being ultimately achieved in all 48 independent trajectories. Amyloid fibrils feature two distinct surfaces, which differ in their physicochemical properties. The “elongation surface” is the interface where monomers or oligomers may attach and integrate into the growing fibril, driven by coordination with the exposed hydrogen bond and donors of the terminal chains. The “lateral surface” refers to the exposed sides of the fibril along its length, which may play a key role in new fibril generation by catalyzing secondary nucleation.52 In our simulations, the ACR displayed a strong preference for the elongation surface of the Aβ42 fibril seed (Figure 4a), driven by strong interactions between mainly hydrophobic residues, including Aβ42 residues L34, L17, and F19, and TDP-43 residues F316, L340, and I318 (Figure S6). Patterns corresponding to intermolecular β-sheet formation were readily identifiable in the contact map computed from the interactions between the ACR monomer and the four total Aβ42 elongation surface chains (Figure S6). The 2D PMF computed as a function of the number of contacts at each surface contained a single bound basin, corresponding to varying numbers of elongation surface contacts with simultaneously few to zero lateral surface contacts (Figure 4b). As visualized by snapshots α-δ in Figure 4b, states within this basin with a greater number of contacts at the elongation surface were characterized by more extensive backbone hydrogen bonding between the ACR monomer and the terminal chains of the Aβ42 fibril seed. For example, as shown by snapshot α in Figure 4b, intermolecular contact was sometimes primarily made by the robust N-terminal α-helix with the elongation surface while most of the monomer remained flexible and unbound. In snapshot δ, by contrast, backbone hydrogen bonding was the driving force of the binding (Figure 4b).

  Analyzing the time evolution of ACR helical secondary structure content and intermolecular hydrogen bonding within individual trajectories revealed that binding the Aβ42 fibril seed induced dynamic but significant structural transformation within the ACR. In one case, the helix fraction was significantly reduced shortly after binding, then rebounded due to the dissociation of a previously hydrogen-bonded segment, before additional rearrangements produced a more stably bound conformation with only the N-terminal α-helix preserved (Figure 4c). In another case, the helix fraction remained high after binding for a majority of the total simulation time before conformational rearrangements resulted in a transient total loss of helix content, driven by extensive backbone hydrogen bonding (Figure 4d). Commonly, the helix fraction fluctuated until a stable conformation was achieved involving residues from the unfolded C-terminal helical region engaging in backbone hydrogen bonding, though the robust N-terminal α-helix, by contrast, remained largely intact (Figure 4g). The energy landscape with respect to the helix fraction and intermolecular residue contacts revealed a predominant basin with its deepest point corresponding to ~140 contacts and an ACR helix fraction of ~0.2 (Figure 4f, snapshot I). Notably, intermolecular contact with the Aβ42 fibril seed induced complete unfolding of the ACR helices (Figure 4f, snapshot II), occurring with much greater probability than in the case of the Aβ42 monomer (Figure 2d). The energy landscape with respect to the helix fraction and β-sheet fraction further illustrated the pronounced structural transformation that occurred during fibril seed binding. The primary basin was shifted in the presence of the fibril seed to conformational space featuring approximately equal helical and β-sheet secondary structure content (Figure 4g, snapshot III). Furthermore, whereas the ACR invariantly possessed some helical residues during homodimerization (Figure 1d), binding with the fibril seed enabled conformations with significant β-sheet content in addition to an absence of any helical residues (Figure 4g, snapshot IV). Overall, these results collectively demonstrated that interaction with an Aβ42 fibril seed potently induced structural transformation within the ACR, including the unfolding of both helical regions.

  To gain deeper insights into the unfolding of the ACR helical regions due to Aβ42 fibril seed binding, we analyzed the mechanism of this transition in the trajectory selected in Figure 4d. In short, the mechanism proceeded through a series of “backbone jumps” in which the successive dissociation and reassociation of ACR residues stimulated helix unfolding, as illustrated by the snapshots shown in Figure 4h. Initially, residues 321–337 of the ACR formed a single α-helix that stretched across the elongation surface of the Aβ42 fibril seed while residues S317, N319, and L340 formed intermolecular hydrogen bonds that stabilized the binding (Figure 4h, snapshot 1). Dissociation of weakly bound C-terminal residues within the α-helix enabled the unfolding of the segment 332SSWGMM337, which formed hydrogen bonds with a fibril seed backbone upon re-association (Figure 4h, snapshot 2). Following this transformation, helical secondary structure within the ACR was reduced to the robust N-terminal α-helix and a transiently helical segment spanning residues 340–347. Following a “jump” of ACR residues between adjacent fibril seed backbones, the robust N-terminal α-helix was reduced to the segment 327QAALQ331, with the preceding residues having formed hydrogen bonds with elongation surface Aβ42 residues following a dissociation event (Figure 4h, snapshot 3). Further dissociation and hydrogen-bonded re-association of residues within the N-terminal helical region led to the complete elimination of helical content within the ACR (Figure 4h, snapshot 4). Meanwhile, the ACR C-terminus remained largely flexible, occasionally contacting the lateral surface (Figure 4h, snapshot 5).

  The complete unfolding of both helices within the TDP-43 ACR due to interactions with an Aβ42 fibril seed is a pathologically relevant observation. Based on our simulation results, Aβ42 aggregate species could catalyze the conversion of the ACR to a more amyloidogenic form, accelerating its pathogenic aggregation. Although different in sequence, the Aβ42 fibril seed could nonetheless serve as a nucleation site for the formation of β-sheet-rich oligomers by the TDP-43 CTD. The additional neurotoxicity by such induced TDP-43 species could be of relevance to the clinical severity of AD cases featuring TDP-43 co-pathology.53 Notably, our simulation results may have been sensitive to initial conditions, namely the particular Aβ42 fibril seed structure used. However, the significant ACR backbone flexibility observed during binding and inherently out-of-register nature of intermolecular β-sheet formation between the ACR and the Aβ42 fibril seed suggest that our results may be generalizable to other fibril structures, supporting the notion that cross-seeding of amyloidogenic species may be a general phenomenon.


### Parallel, in-register β-sheet formation occurred between TDP-43 ACR monomers contacting the elongation surface of an Aβ42 fibril seed
  To further explore whether an Aβ42 fibril seed could serve as a nucleation site for pathogenic TDP-43 aggregation, we simulated two ACR monomers with the fibril seed and assessed for differences in ACR dimerization upon binding to the fibril seed. To maintain unbiased simulations, the two ACR monomers were randomly and independently placed in the simulation box, and no constraints were employed to encourage dimerization. The energy landscape with respect to the number of hetero- and homotypic intermolecular hydrogen bonds revealed a prominent extension of the primary basin toward states featuring a significant number of hydrogen bonds between ACR monomers (Figure 5a, snapshot I). Conformations with fewer homotypic hydrogen bonds despite both ACR monomers binding at the same fibril surface resembled ACR homodimerization in the absence of Aβ42, being rich in helical secondary structure content (Figure 5a, snapshot II). Naturally, due to unbiased starting coordinates, most trajectories featured the two ACR monomers bound to opposite ends of the fibril seed (Figure 5a, snapshot III). Single-trajectory analysis of those in which significant inter-peptide ACR contact did occur revealed that hydrogen bonding with the fibril seed preceded the formation of hydrogen bonds between ACR monomers, which together formed dynamic multi-layer β-sheets with Aβ42 elongation surface chains (Figure 5b,c). Notably, hydrogen-bonded contact between monomers was sustained once achieved (Figure 5b,c), indicating that this interaction facilitated by fibrillar Aβ42 could result in the formation of stable β-sheet rich TDP-43 CTD aggregate species. Furthermore, contact map analysis revealed that helix-helix contacts between ACR monomers were significantly reduced during dimerization in the presence of the fibril seed. As shown by Figure 5d, the contact map between ACR monomers in the control homodimerization setting featured a prominent helix-helix interaction pattern between residues 322–330 of each chain, and notably lacked any pattern denoting intermolecular β-sheet formation. Heterotypic contacts with an Aβ42 monomer during ACR dimerization moderately reduced the frequency of pairwise contacts within this helical region and increased the frequency of parallel, in-register contacts between the N-termini of ACR monomers (Figure 5e).

  Fibrillar Aβ42, however, had a much stronger effect, with the emergence of a clear contact pattern corresponding to a parallel, in-register β-sheet forming between residues 311–321, or some fragment within this range, of the ACR monomers (Figure 5f). Notably, this sequence contains 311MNFGA315, which forms one of the inner β-strands within TDP-43 filaments from FTLD-TDP Type A.28 Furthermore, contact with the Aβ42 fibril seed during ACR dimerization reduced helix-helix interactions between ACR monomers to a much greater degree than did contact with an Aβ42 monomer, with the frequency of key in-register contacts at positions A326 and M330 being greatly reduced (Figure 5f). In addition, numerous out-of-register contact patterns emerged as a result of heterotypic interactions with the fibril seed during dimerization, which were not seen in the control system or with the addition of an Aβ42 monomer (Figure 5f). Visualizing the time evolution of the number of intermolecular residue contacts between ACR monomers for all trajectories revealed that the dynamics of homotypic contacts were significantly altered in the presence of the fibril seed (Figure 5g). Whereas the number of contacts fluctuated rapidly and persistently in the control system, reflecting weak and transient interactions, inter-peptide contact between ACR monomers was more stable and greater in magnitude in the presence of the fibril seed (Figure 5g). These observations were quantitatively reflected by the probability density function of the number of homotypic contacts for each ACR dimer-containing system. Although heterotypic contacts with an Aβ42 monomer did modulate the intensity of certain contact patterns featured in ACR dimerization, the overall distribution of homotypic ACR contacts was not significantly different between the control system and the +Aβ42 monomer system (Figure 5h). In presence of the fibril seed, however, a secondary peak emerged, corresponding to high-contact states enabled by extensive hydrogen bonding between ACR monomers (Figure 5h). Thus, our simulation results demonstrated that fibrillar Aβ42 exerted a potent effect on structural and dynamical features of ACR dimerization. Together, our simulations of an Aβ42 fibril seed with either one or two ACR monomers revealed that cross-interaction could catalyze not only significant structural transformation of ACR monomers, but also the formation of hydrogen-bonded dimers that could serve as initial species in the nucleation of larger, β-sheet-rich aggregates of the TDP-43 CTD.


### Fibrillar Aβ42 most potently induced pathogenic structural transformation within the TDP-43 ACR
  To compare the effects of different species on the structure of the ACR monomer during interaction, we performed intramolecular contact map analysis across systems containing either another ACR monomer, an Aβ42 monomer, an Aβ42 fibril seed, or the monomer alone in a control system. The intramolecular contact map of the ACR monomer in the control system revealed two prominent contact patterns corresponding to the two α-helix-forming regions, linked by lower-frequency contacts corresponding to an extended α-helix spanning both the N- and C-terminal helical regions (Figure 6a). The α-helical nature of the formed helices was confirmed by the distinctive i + 4 spacing present in the intramolecular hydrogen bond map (Figure 6a). To analyze how interactions with other species altered the intramolecular contact map of the ACR monomer, we computed the pairwise frequency difference maps shown in Figure 6b–c. These plots were generated by subtracting the frequency matrix of the control contact map (Figure 6a) from the frequency matrices of the contact maps corresponding to states involving interactions with a given other species. As illustrated by Figure 6b, homodimerization with another ACR monomer slightly increased the pairwise frequencies corresponding to the two helical regions, indicating that homotypic interactions between ACR monomers promoted helical secondary structure. As helical secondary structure within the ACR is important for the LLPS behavior of TDP-43,25,30,50 this result further suggests that our simulations captured well the transient helix-helix interactions mediating the physiological, liquid-like phase behavior of TDP-43. In contrast to the helix-promoting effect of another ACR monomer, interaction with an Aβ42 monomer decreased the frequency of intramolecular helix-forming contacts, though some corresponding to the robust N-terminal α-helix were unaffected (Figure 6c). Interaction with the Aβ42 fibril seed, however, had the most remarkable effects on the ACR structure. As shown by Figure 6d, decreases in pairwise frequencies corresponding to helix-forming contacts were more expansive in scope, extending further along the ACR sequence. Furthermore, the frequency decreases and were often several-fold greater in magnitude compared to those induced by interaction with the Aβ42 monomer, with some contacts being virtually completely eliminated (Figure 6d). Thus, interaction with the fibril seed most potently affected ACR structure, inducing substantive reductions, or sometimes complete elimination, of intramolecular contacts and hydrogen bonds responsible for the native helical structure within the ACR monomer.

  To further comparatively probe the changes in ACR structure induced by interaction with other species, we computed the residue-wise secondary structure propensity of the ACR monomer in the control system and during contact with the other species in the experimental systems. Shown in Figure 6e–h, the results further demonstrated the potency of interaction with fibrillar Aβ42 in stimulating pathogenic secondary structure transformation within the ACR. As shown by Figure 6f, interaction with another ACR monomer during homodimerization resulted in minute increases in the helix propensity of ACR residues 320–336, amounting to a small but significant increase in total helix propensity (Figure 6i) and reflecting the changes seen in the corresponding intramolecular contact map (Figure 6b). Notably, both forms of Aβ42 greatly increased the β-sheet propensity of residues within the ACR N-terminus, resulting from intermolecular β-sheet formation with Aβ42. Interaction with monomeric Aβ42 resulted in small but significant increases in the β-sheet propensity of residues within the C-terminal helical region of the ACR, though these were far exceeded in magnitude by those induced by interaction with fibrillar Aβ42 (Figure 6e). Furthermore, although interaction with monomeric Aβ42 did slightly reduce residue-wise helix propensity within the C-terminal helical region, fibrillar Aβ42 produced a much more potent effect (Figure 6f). Although the effect was small, only interaction with fibrillar Aβ42 was capable of reducing the helix propensities of residues 325AAQAA329 within the N-terminal robust α-helix-forming region (Figure 6f), which adopted coil or bend secondary structure during the binding-induced complete unfolding of the N-terminal α-helix (Figure 6g). Only interaction with fibrillar Aβ42 affected turn content within the ACR (Figure 6h), resulting from the strong tendency of the ACR to form intermolecular β-sheets with the fibril seed elongation surface rather than hairpininducing intramolecular β-sheets.

  The greater scope and magnitude of secondary structure changes induced by fibrillar Aβ42 as opposed to monomeric Aβ42 was reflected by the changes in the overall secondary structure propensities of the ACR monomer. The total β-sheet propensity of the ACR during contact with fibrillar Aβ42 was approximately twice that of during contact with monomeric Aβ42 (Figure 6i). Further, the decrease in total helix propensity of the ACR resulting from interaction with fibrillar Aβ42 was several-fold greater in magnitude than the decrease induced by interaction with monomeric Aβ42 (Figure 6i). Altogether, these analyses demonstrate that fibrillar Aβ42, specifically, was a potent catalyst of secondary structure transformation within the ACR. The conversion of the ACR from a helix-rich form to an unfolded conformation with exposed hydrogen bond donors and acceptors constitutes a pathogenic structural transition to a more amyloidogenic form. Thus, these results further support the hypothesis that fibrillar Aβ42 may promote the pathogenic aggregation of TDP-43 by facilitating the rate-limiting step of helix unfolding, thereby contributing to the development of TDP-43 pathology in AD. Importantly, while we have focused in the present study on the potential contribution of cross-interaction with Aβ to the development of TDP-43 pathology, other proteins known to interact with TDP-43, such as FUS,54 may be of importance in regulating pathogenic conformational transitions within its ACR. Thus, future studies should seek to mechanistically explore how TDP-43 structural conversion might be triggered via interactions with other proteins in the cytoplasmic context of its pathological aggregation.


### Aβ42 monomers interacted with both surfaces of a TDP-43 fibril seed
  For a more comprehensive investigation, we also explored the interaction between Aβ42 monomers and a TDP-43 fibril seed. Although the intracellular colocalization of TDP-43 and Aβ in AD has been reported,36 the molecular events producing this co-pathology in vivo remain unclear, and it is possible that fibrillar TDP-43 pathology develops independently from fibrillar Aβ. Therefore, we sought to understand what effect fibrillar TDP-43 might have on monomeric Aβ in the setting of disease-associated cross-interaction. While numerous AD-associated Aβ42 fibril structures have been solved, it is unclear what specific morphology fibrillar TDP-43 would assume in cases of AD with TDP-43 pathology. Thus, we encountered the limitation of the applicability of the selected TDP-43 fibril structure (PDB ID: 8CG3)28 in the context of AD being uncertain. Nonetheless, we chose this structure due to the pathological distribution of TDP-43 within the brain in FTLD-TDP Type A being most similar to that of NFTs and senile plaques in AD.55–57 We found that monomeric Aβ42 had a strong tendency to interact with the lateral surface of the TDP-43 fibril seed via hydrophobic contacts (Figure 7a). The deepest basin of the 2D PMF as a function of the number of residue contacts at each surface corresponded to the monomer being bound to the lateral surface with very few, if any, contacts at the elongation surface (Figure 7b). As exemplified by snapshot I in Figure 7b, this basin represented conformations featuring the Aβ42 monomer interacting with the lateral surface without intermolecular hydrogen bonds, with the monomer taking on unstructured conformations during the interaction. The Aβ42 monomer was able, however, to engage in backbone hydrogen bonding with the elongation surface of the fibril seed, giving rise to basins II and IV in the energy landscape, corresponding to conformations featuring both lateral and elongation surface contacts and those with predominantly elongation surface contacts, respectively (Figure 7b). In contrast to the hydrophobic interactions that were responsible for binding at the lateral surface, intermolecular β-sheet formation was a key feature of interaction at the elongation surface, as shown by snapshots II-IV (Figure 7b). The time evolution of residue contacts and hydrogen bonds between the TDP-43 fibril seed and the Aβ42 monomer demonstrated that in either case, whether at the lateral surface (Figure 7c) or elongation surface (Figure 7d), the interaction was stable, and the monomer similarly interacted with the fibril seed throughout the entirety of the simulation time subsequent to the initial binding event.

  Using a similar set-up as with our simulations of two ACR monomers with an Aβ42 fibril seed, we also conducted unbiased simulations of two Aβ42 monomers with the TDP-43 fibril seed and assessed for Aβ42 homodimerization accompanied by heterotypic contacts with TDP-43. To understand how contact with each surface the fibril seed affected Aβ42 dimerization, we computed a 2D PMF as a function of the number of heterotypic contacts at a given surface and the number of hydrogen bonds between Aβ42 monomers. We found that the resulting energy landscapes for both fibril seed surfaces were complex and contained several basins. Consistent with the unbiased initial positioning of the Aβ42 monomers, both PMFs featured a basin corresponding to a number of heterotypic contacts but zero homotypic hydrogen bonds, reflecting states in which the monomers were bound to different parts of the TDP-43 fibril seed (Figure 7e, basin α; Figure 7f, basin ε). The elongation surface PMF featured basin γ, which represented conformations in which extensive intermolecular hydrogen bonds linked Aβ42 monomers, which formed a multi-layer β-sheet together with TDP-43 residues at the fibril elongation surface (Figure 7e). Basins δ and η both represented conformations featuring an Aβ42 dimer predominantly contacting the fibril seed lateral surface (Figure 7e, basin δ; Figure 7f, basin η). The disconnected basin θ, which featured an especially high number of hydrogen bonds between Aβ42 monomers, was the result of a trajectory in which a lateral surface-bound dimer was formed via interlocking, alternating β-strands from each monomer (Figure 7f, basin θ).

  To further understand how contact at each surface affected features of Aβ42 dimerization, we computed the residue-wise secondary propensity of Aβ42 monomers that were within a dimer, separating states based on whether contacts at the elongation surface or lateral surface were predominant in a given snapshot. Compared to that of an Aβ42 dimer in a control system with no TDP-43 fibril seed, contact with the elongation surface slightly reduced coil and bend propensity (Figure 7i) in favor of increased β-sheet propensity (Figure 7g) of the segments 2AEFRH6 and 11EVHHQK16 within Aβ42. As conversion of Aβ42 to β-sheet-rich conformations is central to its pathological aggregation,58 our results suggest that interaction with the elongation surface of a TDP-43 fibril seed may have amyloidogenic effects. By contrast, however, predominant contact with the lateral surface, which was favored overall, generally increased coil and bend propensity (Figure 7i) while decreasing β-sheet propensity (Figure 7g) across the Aβ42 sequence. Neither surface greatly affected helix (Figure 7h) or turn (Figure 7j) content within the Aβ42 dimer. Altogether, although our results demonstrated a strong affinity between Aβ42 and a TDP-43 fibril seed, we hypothesize that this interaction is unlikely to be significant in the same way that monomeric TDP-43 interaction with fibrillar Aβ42 may be for the development of amyloid pathology in AD. Although secondary nucleation could possibly occur at the TDP-43 fibril lateral surface, our secondary structure analysis indicated that dimers formed at this surface were less β-sheet-rich than in the control environment. Thus, the high intrinsic amyloidogenicity of Aβ42 may preclude a significant seeding effect by fibrillar TDP-43. Furthermore, classical Aβ pathology likely precedes the development of TDP-43 pathology in AD, which would suggest that cross-interaction between soluble Aβ species and fibrillar TDP-43 is probably rare. Nevertheless, further experimental and neuropathological studies on cross-interaction between Aβ and TDP-43 are needed to explore these hypotheses.


## Conclusion
  The development of TDP-43 pathology in AD is an emerging subject of research that remains poorly understood. Here, using atomistic DMD simulations, we performed an extensive investigation of the cross-interaction between Aβ42, the principal component of plaques in AD, and the TDP-43 ACR, the region within its CTD most crucial to its pathological aggregation. Whereas the ACR alone did not undergo an α-helix to β-sheet transition, consistent with its physiological behavior, interaction with an Aβ42 monomer featured intermolecular β-sheet formation and decreased helical secondary structure content within the ACR. More strikingly, the ACR displayed a high binding affinity for an Aβ42 fibril seed and underwent significant structural transformation when bound to its elongation surface. Unbiased simulations of two ACR monomers with an Aβ42 fibril seed resulted in the formation of parallel, in-register hydrogen bonds between ACR monomers, together forming a multi-layer β-sheet with the fibril seed. Intramolecular contact map and residue-wise secondary structure analysis of the ACR during interaction with other species revealed that fibrillar Aβ42 had a much stronger effect compared to monomeric Aβ42, being capable of inducing complete helix unfolding and structurally converting the ACR to a more amyloidogenic form. Altogether, our simulation results suggest that Aβ42 may act as a catalyst of TDP-43 aggregation, reducing the barrier to its irreversible aggregation by stimulating pathogenic structural transformation within its ACR. We hypothesize that abundant fibrillar Aβ species in severe AD cases may contribute to the development of TDP-43 pathology by interfering with its physiological activity and promoting its aggregation through aberrant interactions with its ACR. Thus, our study provides new theoretical insights on the observed colocalization and apparent interaction between TDP-43 and Aβ in AD and, more broadly, further establishes amyloid cross-seeding as a general phenomenon that may have broad implications in neurodegeneration.


## Methods and Materials

### Discrete Molecular Dynamics Simulations
  DMD is a modified version of traditional molecular dynamics (MD), using stepwise potential functions rather than continuous potentials to govern atomic interactions in simulation.59 Achieving enhanced sampling efficiency over traditional MD by only solving for the trajectories of colliding atoms rather than continuously integrating forces to update velocities, DMD has been widely employed by our group60–62 and others40,63,64 to study protein folding and biomolecular interactions. DMD utilizes collision event-driven dynamics, with a collision occurring when two atoms encounter an energy step according to the discretized potential function governing their interaction. Interatomic interactions, including both bonded interactions (i.e., covalent bonds, bond angles, and dihedrals) and non-bonded interactions (i.e., van der Waals, solvation, hydrogen bond, and electrostatic interactions), were governed in our DMD simulations by the Medusa force field, which has been widely used to study amyloid aggregation and intrinsically disordered proteins.65–68 The Medusa force field parameters for van der Waals and covalent interactions were based on the CHARMM force field.69 The explicit force field parameters describing the Medusa force field are included within the DMD software package. The DMD simulation engine is available at http://www.moleculesinaction.com/. Our simulations utilized implicit solvent to relieve computational burden, with the solvation effect being modeled by the Lazaridis-Karplus effective energy function, EEF1.70 Screened electrostatic interactions were evaluated using the Debye–Hückel approximation with a physiological monovalent salt concentration of ~100 mM, dielectric constant of 80, and Debye length set to 1 nm. Counterbalance ions (Na+ or Cl-) were added if necessary to ensure net zero charge in all systems. Temperature control was achieved via the Andersen thermostat, which was used to maintain a constant simulation temperature of 300 K in all systems.71 The units of mass, time, length, and energy in our simulations were 1 Da, ~50 fs, 0.1 nm, and 1 kcal/mol, respectively.


### Molecular Systems
  The amino acid sequences of the TDP-43 ACR, corresponding to residues 311–360 of wild-type TDP-43, and Aβ42 are shown in Figure S7. Coordinates from an NMR-solved solution structure of TDP-43311–360 (PDB ID: 2N3X29) was used for the initial structure of the ACR monomer in our simulations. For the initial structure of the Aβ42 monomer, atomic coordinates were also adopted from a solution structure solved by NMR (PDB ID: 1Z0Q72). Using the initial ACR and Aβ42 monomer structures described, the following systems were constructed: ACR homodimer (two ACR monomers), ACR–Aβ42 heterodimer (one ACR monomer, one Aβ42 monomer), ACR homotrimer (three ACR monomers), and two heterotrimer systems (1:2 and 2:1 ACR:Aβ42). For each system, simulated across 48 independent trajectories, the monomers received random initial positions and velocities within a cubic box with dimension 10.0 nm, with a minimum initial separation of 1.5 nm. Independent trajectories in all of these systems each lasted 1500 ns, with the exception of the homotrimer system, for which simulations were only ran for 500 ns. Additionally, for control monomer data, the ACR monomer was simulated alone in a cubic box with dimension 10.0 nm across 48 independent lasting 1000 ns each.

  For simulation of the ACR with an Aβ42 fibril seed, we used a recently solved Aβ42 fibril structure (PDB ID: 7Q4B51), the morphology of which was found to be abundant in individuals with sporadic AD. Of note, this structure lacks Aβ42 residues 1–8. However, according to the heterodimer contact map, these residues were apparently of little importance to interaction with the ACR (Figure S4). Using the solution ACR monomer structure and the described Aβ42 fibril seed structure, two systems were constructed, with one containing one ACR monomer randomly positioned at least 1.5 nm away from the fibril seed, and the other containing two ACR monomers, each randomly positioned at least 1.5nm away from the fibril seed and from the other monomer. For both systems, 48 independent trajectories each lasted 1500 ns. Simulations were conducted using a cubic box with dimension 20.0 nm. The box size was significantly increased for simulations with the fibril seed to ensure adequate space for the monomer to receive unbiased initial positioning, and also to ensure that interaction with the fibril seed was not favored due to its sheer volume within the simulation environment. To reduce computational cost, all atoms within the fibril seed were held static during simulation. An identical set-up to what has been described for ACR monomer interaction with an Aβ42 fibril seed was used to study Aβ42 interaction with a TDP-43 fibril seed. For these simulations, though, the fibril seed was modelled using a recently solved structure of TDP-43 amyloid filaments from FTLD-TDP Type A (PDB ID: 8CG328), and ACR monomers were replaced by Aβ42 monomers using the previously noted solution structure for starting coordinates. Otherwise, an identical approach was used, with one- and two-monomer systems each being simulated across 48 independent trajectories lasting 1500 ns. Reference data on Aβ42 dimer secondary structure content was obtained from a control simulation containing two Aβ42 monomers in a cubic box with dimension 10.0 nm, with 48 trajectories each lasting 1000 ns. In all systems, periodic boundary condition was applied to the simulation box.


### Computational Analysis
  Residue-wise secondary structure was computed according to the DSSP algorithm.73 Two atoms formed a contact if their distance was less than the 0.55 nm cutoff. Two residues formed a residue contact if there was at least one atomic contact between them. Figures displaying quantitative data regarding contacts refer to residue contacts. Two monomers were considered as forming a dimer if there were at least five residue contacts between them. Two residues formed a backbone hydrogen bond if the N---O distance was less than 0.35 nm and the N-H---O angle was greater than 150°. Effective energy landscapes were described by the 2D PMF defined as PMF = −kBTlnP(x, y) where P(x, y) was the probability of a conformation with parameters x and y during the simulation time selected for analysis, which was determined based upon the equilibration of the relevant parameters. Equilibration was primarily monitored using the time evolution of intermolecular residue contacts (Figure S1) and monomer secondary structure content (Figure S2), averaged across all trajectories for a given system. Equilibrium analyses were performed using data from simulation time periods corresponding to a plateau in the averaged time evolution graphs of the aforementioned parameters.
