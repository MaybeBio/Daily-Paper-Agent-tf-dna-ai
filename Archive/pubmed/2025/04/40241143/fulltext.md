# CAFs activated by YAP1 upregulate cancer matrix stiffness to mediate hepatocellular carcinoma progression


## Background
  The stiffness of the matrix is closely related to the progression of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Although direct targeting of stromal rigidity in HCC remains a clinical challenge, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are considered key contributors to this process. Given the heterogeneity of CAFs, this study explored the relationship between specific CAF subsets and liver cancer matrix stiffness, aiming to identify novel therapeutic targets for HCC patients.


## Methods
  Single-cell sequencing datasets were leveraged to identify cell types within liver cancer and characterize the transcriptomic profiles of CAFs. Prognostic analysis, utilizing the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) liver cancer datasets, assessed the correlation between matrix stiffness-related genes and HCC patient outcomes. Pseudo-time analysis was applied to trace the developmental trajectories of CAFs. By calculating intercellular communication probabilities and analyzing transcription factor activity, the functions and interactions of different CAF subsets were elucidated. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was used to explore the functional roles of CAFs in distinct Yes-associated protein 1 (YAP1) groups. Finally, cellular experiments and animal experiments were further conducted to validate the hypotheses of this study.


## Results
  This study identified CAF subpopulations based on single-cell sequencing data and analyzed transcriptional changes within these subpopulations. Key findings include the identification of collagen type I alpha 1 (COL1A1), collagen type III alpha 1 (COL3A1), and lysyloxidase (LOX) as pivotal node genes during CAF development. Moreover, the expression of matrix stiffness-related genes was inversely correlated with the prognosis of HCC patients. Notably, the YAP1-positive CAF subpopulation emerged as the primary contributor to matrix stiffness in liver cancer. This subpopulation upregulates the expression of matrix stiffness-related genes and promotes tumor progression by activating signaling pathways such as autophagy and GTPase activity regulation. Cellular experiments and animal studies further validated this conclusion.


## Conclusion
  This single-cell analysis uncovered the functional roles of CAFs in liver cancer. The YAP1-positive CAF subpopulation, in particular, was shown to contribute to matrix stiffness by upregulating the expression of relevant genes and promoting tumor progression through the activation of specific signaling pathways.

## Introduction
  The incidence and mortality rates of liver cancer remain high, posing a significant threat to global health [1, 2]. Approximately 80% of liver cancers emerge from a pre-existing condition of liver fibrosis or cirrhosis [2]. Hepatoma cells are encased within a dense network of extracellular matrix (ECM) deposits. The tension generated by ECM crosslinking results in matrix stiffness, which exerts mechanical stress on hepatoma cells [3]. Matrix stiffness is strongly linked to the initiation and progression of liver cancer. For every 1 kPa increase in liver stiffness, the risk of liver cancer development rises by 4%, and the risk of tumor recurrence after liver resection increases by 16.3% [4]. Hepatocytes are highly sensitive to the mechanical stress induced by matrix stiffness. Liver mechanical stress is an independent prognostic factor for liver cancer recurrence and can significantly influence the efficacy of liver cancer treatments [5, 6]. Currently, elastography imaging techniques can effectively differentiate between tumorous and non-tumorous lesions and predict patient prognosis by detecting the stiffness of patient masses [7, 8]. However, directly targeting and inhibiting the stiffness of the liver cancer stroma in clinical practice remains a significant challenge.

  CAFs are among the most prevalent cell types within the tumor microenvironment and are primary stromal cells influencing tumor progression [9]. During liver cancer development, hepatic stellate cells activate into CAFs, which subsequently synthesize and secrete substantial amounts of extracellular matrix, primarily composed of Collagen I and Collagen III. Concurrently, these cells promote collagen crosslinking by secreting LOX, resulting in a significant increase in matrix stiffness [10]. Our previous research has demonstrated that CAFs can significantly promote liver cancer growth [11]. However, CAFs exhibit substantial heterogeneity, with different CAF subtypes playing distinct roles in tumorigenesis and progression [12–14].

  YAP1, a core component of the Hippo signaling pathway, plays a role in the transcriptional regulation of diverse downstream genes [15]. Reduced Hippo pathway activity triggers the nuclear translocation of YAP1/ Transcriptional co-activator with PDZ-binding motif (TAZ), which subsequently interacts with TEA domain transcription factor (TEAD) to induce target gene expression. Unrestrained YAP1/TAZ activity can lead to rapid tumor growth and tumorigenesis [16]. Multiple studies have indicated that YAP1 participates in the activation of CAFs, maintains their functions, and can serve as a marker for CAF activation [17]. During the progression from liver fibrosis to hepatocellular carcinoma and the advancement of liver cancer, YAP1 in CAFs plays a significant role [18]. Moreover, studies have shown that YAP1 activity can modulate the CAF phenotype to promote the progression of prostate cancer [19]. Nevertheless, it is still unclear whether YAP1 can affect matrix stiffness and ultimately promote HCC progression by regulating CAF function.

  In summary, this study aimed to leverage liver cancer-related single-cell datasets to explore the relationship between YAP1 and CAFs. Specifically, we sought to elucidate the mechanism by which YAP1-activated CAFs upregulate liver cancer matrix stiffness, thereby promoting tumor progression. The insights gained from this research could identify effective therapeutic targets and potential treatment strategies for liver cancer patients.


## Materials and methods

### Datasets of single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq)
  Inclusion Criteria: (a) The dataset must contain single-cell RNA sequencing data from liver cancer tissues. (b) The dataset must provide sufficient metadata to allow us to understand the sample origin, processing methods, and sequencing platform. (c) The dataset must have undergone quality control, and the cell classification results must be clear and reliable. Exclusion Criteria: (a) The dataset contains RNA sequencing data from non-liver cancer tissues or cells. (b) The dataset lacks critical metadata, or the metadata is unclear or incomplete. (c) The dataset has significant quality control issues, such as a high percentage of noise cells, low-quality sequencing data, etc. Based on these criteria, we ultimately selected dataset (GSE149614) from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database for analysis, as it met all our requirements and provided high-quality single-cell RNA sequencing data for liver cancer. The dataset encompasses scRNA-seq data from 21 samples collected from 10 patients with HCC, spanning four distinct anatomical sites: primary tumors, portal vein tumor thrombus (PVTT), metastatic lymph nodes, and non-tumor liver tissue. Specifically, it includes data from 8 samples of non-tumor liver tissue, 10 samples of liver cancer tissue, 1 sample of metastatic lymph node tissue, and 2 samples of PVTT metastatic tissue, all derived from the 10 HCC patients, providing a comprehensive view of the disease across its various manifestations. Additionally, the bulk RNA-Seq transcriptomic data and clinical information for HCC patients were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/).


### Data processing
  Raw sequencing data for scRNA-seq were processed using Cell Ranger. The resulting raw count matrix was imported into R and analyzed using the Seurat (v5.0.1) package. Cells with fewer than 200 detected genes and genes detected in fewer than 3 cells were filtered out. The top 2000 highly variable genes were selected for dimensionality reduction using Principal Component Analysis (PCA). Cells from different sequencing datasets were integrated using Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA). Subsequently, the integrated assay data underwent scaling and PCA, resulting in 50 principal components (PCs). Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) dimensionality reduction was applied, followed by the construction of a Shared Nearest Neighbor (SNN) graph using the first 30 principal components. Clustering analysis was performed at a resolution of 1. The FindAllMarkers function, employing the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, was used to identify marker genes for each cell cluster. Based on the expression of classical marker genes, cell clusters were annotated as follows: fibroblasts (FBs, marked by COL1A1, collagen type I alpha 2 (COL1A2), and Actin alpha cardiac muscle 2 (ACTA2)), B cells (marked by B-cell antigen receptor complex-associated protein alpha chain (CD79A), J-chain (JCHAIN), and Immunoglobulin Heavy Chain Constant Region Gamma 1 (IGHG1)), endothelial cells (marked by Platelet Endothelial Cell Adhesion Molecule 1 (PECAM1), Fc fragment of IgG receptor IIb (FCGR2B), and von Willebrand factor (VWF)), hepatocytes (marked by serpin peptidase inhibitor clade A (SERPINA1), Albumin (ALB), and Hepatocyte Nuclear Factor 4 Alpha (HNF4A)), osteoclasts (marked by CD68, CD14, and CD163), and NK cells (marked by CD3D, CD3E, and natural killer cell granule protein 7 (NKG7)).


### Construction of the CAF atlas
  To elucidate the functions of CAFs, we first conducted CAFs screening based on four marker genes: ACTA2, Fibroblast Activation Protein (FAP), Platelet-Derived Growth Factor Receptor Beta Polypeptide (PDGFRB), and Notch Receptor 3 (NOTCH3). Our selection of these markers was informed by relevant literature reports, which indicate that when used in combination, they can effectively distinguish CAFs from other cell types [20–23]. Then, the top 2000 highly variable genes were selected for dimensionality reduction using PCA. Cells from different sequencing datasets were integrated using CCA. Subsequently, the integrated assay data underwent scaling and PCA, resulting in 50 principal components. UMAP dimensionality reduction was applied, followed by the construction of a Shared Nearest Neighbor (SNN) graph using the first 30 principal components. Clustering analysis was performed on the integrated assay at a resolution of 0.6.


### Copy number variation (CNV) analysis
  CopyKAT combines Bayesian methods with hierarchical clustering to calculate the genome copy number distribution of individual cells and define subclonal structure [24]. CopyKAT is typically used to cluster CAFs based on aneuploidy and diploid levels, thereby revealing cellular heterogeneity. Raw gene expression data was extracted from the Seurat object and formatted into a new gene-cell matrix. Somatic large-scale chromosomal copy number variation (CNV) scores of single cells were calculated using the R package copykat (v1.1.0). The default parameters (id.type="S”; n.gene.chr = 5; KS.cut = 0.1, distance="euclidean”) were used for CNV inference analysis.


### Calculation of intercellular communication probability
  Researchers modeled ligand-receptor-mediated signaling interactions using the law of mass action. Considering the protein-protein interaction nature of ligand-receptor binding, a network propagation technique based on random walks was employed to project gene expression profiles onto a high-confidence, experimentally validated protein-protein interaction network obtained from STRINGdb. The CellChat software was utilized with default parameters.


### Transcription factor (TF) activity analyses
  To identify key regulatory TFs within the candidate cell populations, SCENIC analysis was employed using the pySCENIC software package. The necessary databases for SCENIC were downloaded from the pySCENIC website (https://github.com/aertslab/pySCENIC): a transcription factor database (hg38__refseq-r80__10kb_up_and_down_tss.mc9nr.genes_vs_motifs.rankings.feather) and a motif annotation database (motifs-v9-nr.hgnc-m0.001-o0.0.tbl). The analysis utilized the normalized expression matrix generated by Seurat as input. SCENIC infers regulatory networks through a three-step process: network inference, motif enrichment analysis, and calculation of regulon activity scores. Network inference identifies potential interactions between TFs and their target genes. Motif enrichment analysis assesses the overrepresentation of specific TF binding motifs within the promoter regions of co-expressed genes. Finally, regulon activity scores were used to quantify the predicted activity of each TF within individual cells.


### Identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)
  We calculated the log2 fold change (log2FC) between different groups using the Seurat FindMarkers function. Statistical significance was assessed using a two-sided Student’s t-test with Bonferroni correction. Signature genes were selected based on the following criteria: absolute log2FC > 0.25, adjusted p-value (Bonferroni) < 0.05, and expression in more than 25% of cells within the target cluster. Top differentially expressed genes in each cluster were chosen for in situ analysis to validate cell type identity. Functional and pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes was conducted using clusterProfiler.


### Pathway enrichment analyses
  To explore the functional relevance of the candidate cell clusters, pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs was performed using the R package clusterProfiler (v.4.2.2) and GO gene sets. Enriched terms with an adjusted p-value less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.


### Inference of cell state using the trajectory analysis
  Pseudotime analysis simulates a timeline to reflect cellular differentiation states. Core to this analysis is the identification of specific cellular changes during differentiation through differential expression analysis. Monocle is a widely used tool for pseudotime analysis, employing inverse graph embedding to construct single-cell trajectories [25]. Monocle positions cells along the trajectory and identifies genes differentially expressed along the trajectory using its differential analysis module. In this study, pseudotime series analysis was conducted on different CAF subpopulations. Cell clusters were extracted from the single-cell RNA sequencing data and analyzed using the Monocle (version 2.26.0) algorithm to generate a trajectory. Briefly, a gene-cell matrix of UMI counts was provided as input to Monocle, and the newCellDataSet function was used to create an object with a negative binomial distribution for gene expression. Highly variable genes were selected based on a mean expression threshold of 0.1 and an empirical dispersion threshold of 1 time the fitted dispersion. The DDRTree dimensionality reduction algorithm was employed to plot the trajectory structure in two-dimensional space, and cells were ordered in pseudotime. Finally, the trajectory was inferred using default Monocle parameters.


### Animal experiments
  Animal experiments were conducted in strict adherence to the guidelines set forth by the Animal Ethics Committee of Xi’an Jiaotong University. Cancer cells (Hepa1-6) were subcutaneously inoculated into 4-week-old female C57BL/6 mice. Once the tumors reached a predetermined volume, the mice were randomly assigned to three groups: Group A1, Group A2, and Group A3, with five mice per group. Group A1 received a control treatment consisting of DMSO, Group A2 was administered the YAP1 inhibitor Verteporfin (HY-B0146) at a dosage of 100 mg/kg, once every other day, and Group A3 was treated with the YAP1 inhibitor K-975 (HY-138565) at a dosage of 100 mg/kg, also once every other day. Eight days post-treatment, the mice were euthanized in a humane manner, and the tumor tissues were surgically excised. Subsequently, we successfully isolated the corresponding CAF (cancer-associated fibroblasts) cells from the tumor tissues of all three groups. One aliquot of these isolated cells is reserved for the extraction of RNA and proteins, while the remaining aliquot is utilized for cell culture experiments.

Q-PCR was performed on a BioRad CFX96 system. β-actin was used as an internal reference for the analysis. The primer sequences used in this study are as follows:

### Western blotting and ELISA
  Primary antibodies against COL3A1 (Proteintech, cat.22734-1-AP, 1:1000), YAP1 (Proteintech, cat.13584-1-AP, 1:3000), COL1A1 (Immunoway, cat. YM 4807, 1:1000), COL1A2 (Immunoway, cat.YT1019, 1:1000), LOX (Immunoway, cat. YN 4611, 1:1000) and GAPDH (Proteintech, cat. No. 60004-1-Ig, 1:50,000) were used for Western blotting. ### Statistical analyses
  All data were analyzed and visualized using R (version 4.2.2). Differences between variables were assessed using the Student’s t-test. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value less than 0.05.


## Results

### scRNA-seq analysis reveals the heterogeneous cellular composition within HCC tissues
  After quality control, noise reduction, and normalization of the scRNA-seq data, a total of 69,479 single-cell transcriptome data points were retained for subsequent analysis. Through a stepwise processing and analysis pipeline, 28 distinct cell clusters were identified and visualized using UMAP (Fig. 1A). UMAP was further employed to visualize the distribution of these cell clusters across normal liver tissue, primary liver cancer tissue, PVTT, and metastatic lymph node tissue (Fig. 1B). The top genes characterizing each cell cluster are depicted in a bubble plot, highlighting the significantly higher expression levels of these genes within their respective cell types compared to other groups (Fig. 1C). Based on the expression of canonical marker genes, the 28 cell clusters were categorized into six major cell types: B cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, hepatocytes, myeloid cells, and T/NK cells (Fig. 1D). The results of cell definition are presented in Fig. 1E.

### Screening and clustering of CAFs in scRNA-seq samples
  To further elucidate the functions of CAFs, we first conducted CAFs screening based on four marker genes: ACTA2, FAP, PDGFRB, and NOTCH3. Subsequently, clustering analysis was performed on CAFs, resulting in the identification of five distinct CAF subsets. The markers for these five different CAF subsets are as follows: cluster0-CAF (marked by CD36, THBS4, LPL and ITGLC2), cluster1-CAF (marked by RERGL, KCNA5, CASQ2, and MUSTN1), cluster2-CAF (marked by ITGBL1, MOXD1, SFRP4 and PTGDS), cluster3-CAF (marked by COLEC10, TMEM56, IGFBP3 and CXCL12), and cluster4-CAF (marked by IGLC3, XIST, HLA-DRA and RGS1). Using UMAP, we visualized the distribution of these CAF subpopulations and their aggregation across normal liver tissue, primary liver cancer tissue, PVTT, and metastatic lymph node tissue (Fig. 2A-B). An analysis of cell proportions within each CAF cluster revealed that the cluster1-CAF subpopulation exhibited a higher proportion of normal cells, while the cluster2-CAF subpopulation was enriched in tumor cells (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, the cluster1-CAF subpopulation was most abundant in normal liver tissue (Fig. 2D). Differential gene expression analysis between cluster1 and cluster2-CAF subpopulations identified several significantly differentially expressed genes. RERG-like gene (RERGL), potassium voltage-gated channel, shaker-related subfamily, member 5 (KCNA5), calsequestrin 2 (CASQ2), and Musculoskeletal Embryonic Nuclear Protein 1 (MUSTN1) were significantly overexpressed in cluster1-CAF, with high co-expression observed between RERGL and KCNA5 (Fig. 2E-G). Conversely, integrin subunit beta like 1 (ITGBL1), monooxygenase, DBH-like 1 (MOXD1), secreted frizzled-related protein 4 (SFRP4), and prostaglandin D2 synthase (PTGDS) were significantly overexpressed in the cluster2-CAF subpopulation, with high co-expression noted between ITGBL1 and MOXD1 (Fig. 2H-J). Through literature review, it has been found that the molecules ITGBL1, MOXD1, SFRP4, and PTGDS are closely associated with fibroblast function, fibrosis generation, maintenance of matrix stiffness, the remodeling process of the extracellular matrix and cancer progression [27–33]. In contrast, there are currently limited research reports on the roles of RERGL, KCNA5, CASQ2, and MUSTN1 in the aforementioned physiological or pathological activities. Therefore, we hypothesize that the Cluster2-CAF subpopulation is more closely related to the formation of matrix stiffness.

### Matrix stiffness mediated by CAFs is closely associated with HCC
  Next, copyKAT software was used to conduct a cluster analysis of aneuploidy and diploid levels within CAF cell populations. Figure 3 A-B illustrates the spatial distribution of tumor and non-tumor cells. UMAP visualization was utilized to further explore the distribution of tumor and non-tumor cells (Fig. 3C). Subsequent analysis focused on the proportion of tumor and non-tumor cells within different CAF subpopulations, as well as the proportion of CAF subpopulations within tumor and non-tumor cell groups. Results indicated that the cluster2-CAF subpopulation exhibited the highest proportion of tumor cells, while the cluster4-CAF subpopulation displayed the lowest (Fig. 3D). Among tumor cells, the cluster2-CAF subpopulation was relatively abundant, whereas the cluster4-CAF subpopulation was least prevalent. Conversely, non-tumor cells were enriched in cluster1-CAF and cluster4-CAF subpopulations, with the cluster2-CAF subpopulation showing the lowest proportion (Fig. 3E). The expression levels of COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1, and LOX were examined across different CAF subpopulations. Significantly elevated expression of these genes was observed in the cluster2 and cluster3-CAF subpopulations, while their expression was relatively low in the cluster1 and cluster4-CAF subpopulations (Fig. 3F-I). Further detailed analysis of the cluster2-CAF subpopulation revealed that the expression levels of COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1, and LOX genes were significantly upregulated in tumor-associated tissues compared to corresponding normal tissues (Fig. 3J-M).

### High matrix stiffness is negatively correlated with the prognosis of HCC patients
  Using the GEPIA website, the expression levels of matrix stiffness-related genes, including COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1, and LOX, were compared between liver cancer and normal liver tissues. The results demonstrated significantly higher expression of these genes in liver cancer tissues compared to normal liver tissues (Fig. 4A-D). A liver cancer patient dataset encompassing gene transcriptome data and clinical information was downloaded from the TCGA website. After excluding patients with incomplete information, optimal cut-off values for COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1, and LOX genes were determined using X-tile software. Prognostic analysis of HCC patients was conducted based on the expression levels of these four genes, and the resulting survival curves are presented in Fig. 4E-H. The analysis revealed that higher expression of matrix stiffness-related genes COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1, and LOX in liver cancer patients correlated with poorer prognosis.

### Pseudotime analysis of CAF cell populations
  We conducted pseudo-time analysis on different CAF subpopulations to elucidate specific cellular changes during differentiation. Rigorous gene selection criteria were applied to identify suitable genes for pseudo-time series analysis (Fig. 5A). Subsequently, data processing was performed using the selected genes. Figure 5B and C illustrate the trajectory distributions of different CAF subpopulations in two-dimensional space, with a color gradient representing the differentiation trend along the pseudo-time axis: darker colors indicate earlier stages, and lighter colors indicate later stages of differentiation (Fig. 5D). Analysis of CD36, fatty acid binding protein 4 (FABP4), and lumican (LUM) expression levels along the pseudo-time trajectory revealed that CD36 and FABP4 expression initially increased and subsequently decreased as differentiation progressed, while LUM expression gradually increased (Fig. 5E). Finally, key node genes identified during the CAF differentiation process included COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1, fibronectin 1 (FN1), C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 12 (CXCL12), versican (VCAN), periostin (POSTN), and gap junction protein, alpha 4 (GJA4) (Fig. 5F). The above results suggest that genes such as COL1A1, COL1A2 and COL3A1 play crucial roles in the differentiation process of CAFs.

### Cellular communication among different CAF subpopulations
  To further elucidate the intercellular communication dynamics among different CAF subpopulations, the researchers employed the CellChat package in R to conduct an intercellular interaction analysis. The five CAF subpopulations were found to be interconnected, with strong interaction intensity and a particularly high number of interactions observed between cluster2-CAF and cluster3-CAF (Fig. 6A-C). Subsequently, the expression levels of genes associated with collagen, FN1, Fibroblast Growth Factor (FGF), Laminin, Midkine (MK), Periostin, Thrombospondin (THBS), CXCL, and Vitronectin (VTN) signaling pathways were examined in different CAF clusters. Results indicated that these pathway-related genes were significantly overexpressed in cluster2 and cluster3-CAFs, while their expression was significantly downregulated in cluster1 and cluster4-CAFs (Fig. 6D-L). Further functional analysis revealed that CAF cells within the cluster2 and cluster3 subpopulations acted as both signal transmitters and receivers in the collagen, FN1, FGF, MK, Periostin, THBS, and CXCL signaling pathways. These subpopulations played a significant role in shaping the overall communication within the signaling pathway network (Fig. 6M-R).

### YAP1-activated CAFs upregulate the stiffness of the liver cancer stroma
  Analysis of YAP1 levels in CAF cells revealed that the YAP1-positive CAF cell population exhibited the lowest proportion of normal tissue and the highest proportion of tumor tissue compared to the YAP1-negative CAF cell population (Fig. 8A). Conversely, in normal tissues, the proportion of YAP1-negative CAF cells was relatively high, while the proportion of YAP1-positive CAF cells was relatively low. However, in tumor tissues, the proportion of YAP1-positive CAF cells was relatively high (Fig. 8B). Further examination of COL1A1 and COL3A1 expression levels in CAF groups with different YAP1 statuses revealed significant overexpression of these genes in YAP1-positive CAFs (Fig. 8C-D). Additionally, COL1A1 and COL3A1 were significantly overexpressed in the tumor tissues corresponding to YAP1-positive CAFs (Fig. 8E-F). Secondly, analysis of differentially expressed genes in CAF groups with various YAP1 states revealed significant enrichment in signaling pathways such as focal adhesion, cell-substrate junction, nucleoside triphosphatase regulator activity, GTPase regulator activity, protein serine/threonine kinase activity, actin filament organization, nuclear speckle, RNA splicing, and mRNA splicing (Fig. 8G-H). Analysis of differentially expressed genes in tumor tissues corresponding to CAFs with different YAP1 groupings revealed enrichment in signaling pathways such as ribosomes, cytosolic ribosomes, autophagy regulation, macroautophagy, intrinsic apoptotic signaling pathways, focal adhesion, cell-substrate junctions, and GTPase regulatory activity (Fig. 8I-J). In summary, YAP1-positive CAFs indeed upregulate the matrix stiffness in liver cancer.

### Verification of cell experiments and animal experiments
  To further validate the reliability of our previous findings, we conducted experimental verification. Initially, cancer cells (Hepa1-6) were subcutaneously inoculated into 4-week-old female C57BL/6 mice. Once the tumors attained a specific volume, the mice were randomly allocated to three groups: Group A1, Group A2, and Group A3. Group A1 served as the control and received DMSO treatment, Group A2 was treated with the YAP1 inhibitor Verteporfin, and Group A3 was treated with the YAP1 inhibitor K-975. Following euthanasia, the tumors were excised and individually weighed. The results demonstrated that, compared to the control group, the tumor volumes in the YAP1 inhibitor-treated groups were significantly diminished, and their weights were notably reduced (Fig. 9A, B). Concurrently, the expression levels of COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1, and LOX were downregulated in CAFs treated with YAP1 inhibitors (Fig. 9C-H). Additionally, CAF cells isolated from tumors in the control group were cultured and subsequently treated with K-975 after adherence. After 48 h of culture, the levels of COL1A1 and COL1A2 in the supernatants were assayed. The results indicated that, compared to the control cells, the levels of COL1A1 and COL1A2 in the supernatants of CAF cells treated with K-975 were decreased (Fig. 9I, J).

## Discussion
  The incidence and mortality rates of liver cancer remain high, posing a significant threat to the health of the population. Accordingly, the development of novel therapeutic strategies for liver cancer is of critical clinical importance [2]. CAFs, as a crucial component of the tumor microenvironment (TME), play a significant role in various aspects of tumorigenesis, progression, angiogenesis, immune regulation, and treatment resistance [35–37].

  During ECM remodeling, CAFs not only alter ECM composition but also significantly influence tissue stiffness, a phenomenon known as matrix rigidity [10]. CAF-secreted enzymes, essential for matrix crosslinking, promote ECM remodeling, increase tumor tissue stiffness, and create favorable conditions for tumor cell survival and proliferation [38]. Increased matrix rigidity has been linked to enhanced invasion and migration abilities of various tumor cells [39, 40]. This study identified five CAF subpopulations based on marker gene expression, with Cluster2 and Cluster3 CAF subpopulations exhibiting significantly elevated expression of matrix stiffness-related genes: COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1, and LOX. At the same time, cell communication analysis indicated significant overexpression of genes related to collagen, FN1, Laminin, Periostin, FGF, THBS, CXCL, and VTN pathways in Cluster2 and Cluster3 CAF subpopulations. Further research has found that high levels of COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1, and LOX are negatively correlated with the prognosis of liver cancer.

  The aforementioned results indicate that specific CAF subpopulations can promote liver cancer progression by upregulating matrix rigidity. Moreover, these findings highlight the significant heterogeneity among CAF cells, with different CAF types playing distinct roles in liver cancer development and progression.

  As a transcriptional cofactor, YAP1 plays a significant role in the formation and activation of CAFs. Studies have demonstrated that YAP1 acts as a signaling hub for CAFs in regulating tumor mechanical stress. Particularly in prostate cancer, high YAP1 expression is closely associated with CAF transformation [19]. Research has shown that YAP1 forms a protein complex with the transcription factor TEAD1 to regulate sarcoma (SRC) transcription, thereby influencing the expression levels of SRC downstream cytoskeletal proteins and actin. This process facilitates the transformation of normal fibroblasts (NF) into CAFs [41]. This suggests that YAP1 plays a pivotal role in CAF formation and activation, making it a potential new target for tumor therapy. However, its role in liver cancer CAFs requires further investigation. Further analysis of YAP1 levels in liver cancer CAF cells revealed that the YAP1-positive CAF cell subpopulation exhibited the highest proportion of tumor tissue, with significantly elevated expression levels of COL1A1 and COL3A1. Additionally, differential gene enrichment analysis of CAF cell subpopulations under different YAP1 states showed that the differential genes were primarily enriched in signaling pathways such as focal adhesions, GTPase regulatory activity, and protein serine/threonine kinase activity. The above signaling pathways play crucial roles in the process of tumorigenesis and development [42–44]. In addition, cellular experiments and animal studies have also confirmed that intervening in the expression of YAP1 can down-regulate the expression of stromal stiffness-related genes and inhibit the progression of liver cancer. We further employed the ChIPBase v3.0 database to pinpoint genes regulated by YAP1. The findings revealed that 1,818 genes modulated by YAP1 were filtered from the database, whereas our earlier single-cell analysis distinguished 10,705 differential genes between YAP1-positive and YAP1-negative CAF groups. By intersecting these two gene sets, we uncovered 907 common genes, which we hypothesize are regulated by YAP1 within CAFs. Notably, many of these genes are implicated in the establishment of matrix stiffness, particularly LOX, COL12A1, COL4A1, COL5A3, COL6A3, COL9A3 and so on. These genes are pivotal in the creation and preservation of matrix stiffness.

  In conclusion, this study conducted an in-depth analysis of CAF cell subpopulations in liver cancer and found that YAP1-positive CAFs can upregulate the expression of genes associated with matrix rigidity in liver cancer, thereby promoting liver cancer progression. While this study provides valuable insights, several limitations exist. More comprehensive cellular experiments, animal studies, and clinical trials are necessary to strengthen the level of evidence. The ultimate goal is to identify effective therapeutic targets and develop potential treatment strategies for liver cancer patients.
