# PP1/PNUTS phosphatase binds the restrictor complex and stimulates RNA Pol II transcription termination


## SUMMARY
  The restrictor ZC3H4/WDR82 terminates antisense transcription from bidirectional promoters, but its mechanism is poorly understood. We report that ZC3H4/WDR82 immunoprecipitates with PP1 phosphatase and its nuclear targeting subunit, PP1 phosphatase nuclear targeting subunit (PNUTS), which binds to WDR82. AlphaFold predicts a complex of PP1/PNUTS with the restrictor where both PNUTS and ZC3H4 contact WDR82. A substrate trap, PP1H66K-PNUTS, comprising inactive PP1 fused to the PNUTS C terminus, antagonizes restrictor-mediated termination, whereas PP1WT-PNUTS has less of an effect, suggesting that phosphatase activity is required for termination. One PP1/PNUTS substrate implicated in termination by the restrictor is RNA polymerase II (RNA Pol II) CTD Ser5-P. PP1H66K-PNUTS induces Ser5-P hyperphosphorylation at 5′ ends, presumably by inhibiting dephosphorylation. NET-seq analysis suggests that CTD Ser5 dephosphorylation would promote termination by increasing RNA Pol II pausing. Both inhibition of termination and CTD hyperphosphorylation require the WDR82 binding domain of PP1H66K-PNUTS, which mediates restrictor binding. In summary, the PP1/PNUTS phosphatase associated with the restrictor via WDR82 promotes efficient transcription termination.


## INTRODUCTION
  A large fraction of promoters in multicellular organisms have bidirectional character and give rise to both sense and divergent antisense transcription, which must eventually terminate to prevent interference with neighboring genes and collisions with replication forks. Sense transcription of most protein-coding genes terminates via the “torpedo” mechanism, which couples termination to RNA cleavage at the poly(A) site. This permits loading of the 5′3′ RNA exonuclease torpedo Xrn2/Rat1, which facilitates polymerase release.1–4 In contrast, antisense transcription from promoters and bidirectional transcription from enhancers generate unstable non-coding RNAs that are largely terminated by mechanisms independent of cleavage/polyadenylation.5,6 An important type of poly(A) site independent termination depends on the restrictor, a heterodimer of the WD40 repeat domain protein WDR82 and the Zn-finger protein ZC3H4 that is conserved between Drosophila and humans.6,7 In vivo proximity labeling has identified three factors that localize close to the restrictor: symplekin, a subunit of 3′ end-processing complexes8; ARS2/SRRT, which associates with the mRNA 5′ cap binding complex (CBC), and PNUTS (PP1 phosphatase nuclear targeting subunit).9,10 ARS2 also binds the nuclear exosome implicated in coupled termination and RNA degradation.11,12 How restrictor activity is specifically directed to antisense transcripts remains unclear, but it has been suggested that binding to sense transcripts is antagonized by SET1 histone methyltransferases, which compete with ZC3H4 for binding to WDR82.9,13

  RNA polymerase II (RNA Pol II) CTD (C-terminal domain) phosphorylation and dephosphorylation control dynamic changes within the transcription cycle,14 but how they contribute to elongation-termination transitions is not well understood. The Ser5 residues of the CTD heptad repeats (YSPTSPS) are phosphorylated by TFIIH-associated CDK7 and dephosphorylated by PP1/PNUTS complexed with WDR82 and Tox415–19 and by Integrator/PP2A.20–22 At 5′ ends, Ser5 dephosphorylation by Integrator/PP2A is associated with attenuation of sense transcripts.20–22 At 3′ ends, Ser5 dephosphorylation precedes poly(A) site-dependent termination.23 PP1, PNUTS, WDR82, and ZC3H4 all bind specifically to the Ser5 phosphorylated CTD,15,24–26 and, consistent with a role in co-transcriptional dephosphorylation, PP1 and PNUTS localize to transcribed genes.27–29 How CTD Ser5 dephosphorylation might facilitate termination is unclear.

  PNUTS functions in both poly(A) site-independent and -dependent termination. PNUTS co-purifies with mammalian cleavage polyadenylation complexes,30 and its knockdown inhibits termination in several contexts,31,32 including divergent antisense transcription from bidirectional promoters.6,9 PNUTS binds PP1 via short linear motifs (SLIMs).33 It can positively and negatively affect PP1 activity34 by facilitating substrate recruitment and constraining substrate specificity by occupying one of the substrate binding grooves on the enzyme’s surface.33 Recently, PP1/PNUTs was reported to stimulate release from the promoter-proximal pause site, possibly by dephosphorylation of RNA Pol II-associated elongation factors.35,36 In addition, PNUTS may perform activities independent of PP1, mediated by its conserved TFIIS homology, RNA binding, and WDR82 binding domains.16,37–40 Hence, interpretation of PNUTS knockdown phenotypes is not straightforward. Exactly how PNUTS functions in termination by the restrictor and whether dephosphorylation by PP1 has a role in this process are unclear. It has been suggested that PNUTS plays an indirect role, possibly by slowing RNA Pol II elongation,8,9 as it does at poly(A) sites.27,41 Previous studies have shown that ZC3H4 co-immunoprecipitated with WDR82 but not with PNUTS, leading to the proposal that WDR82 interactions with PNUTS and ZC3H4 are mutually exclusive.42 To date, no specific enzymatic activity of a restrictor-interacting factor is known to be required for termination.

  The subcellular targeting and substrate specificity of PP1 holoenzymes is determined by binding of the catalytic subunit to numerous PP1 interacting proteins (PIPs),43 and this mode of regulation has hampered the development of holoenzyme-specific small-molecule modulators.44 This difficulty can be addressed by the use of chimeric fusions. We have shown previously that a fusion of PP1 with the PIP Phactr retains the specificity of the PP1/Phactr holoenzyme.45 Here, we use a similar approach to assess the function of PP1/PNUTS. Expression of a PP1WT-PNUTS fusion had a relatively modest effect on transcription, but remarkably, inactive PP1H66K-PNUTS46 strongly inhibited termination of divergent antisense RNA Pol II transcription, which was accompanied by CTD Ser5 hyperphosphorylation, presumably because dephosphorylation was inhibited. Moreover, PP1-PNUTS fusions co-precipitated with the WDR82 and ZC3H4 subunits of the restrictor. Both interaction with the restrictor and the dominant-negative effects on CTD dephosphorylation and termination require the WDR82 binding domain of PP1H66K-PNUTS. Together, these results suggest that PP1 phosphatase activity in the context of a complex with PNUTS and restrictor couples dephosphorylation of substrates, likely including RNA Pol II CTD Ser5, to termination.


## RESULTS

### PNUTS binds to the restrictor
  We investigated factors interacting with PNUTS by mass spectrometry-based identification of proteins immunoprecipitated with epitope tagged PNUTS from benzonase-treated nuclear extracts (Figures 1A and 1B). Avitag-PNUTS was modestly overexpressed (2.4×; Figure S1A) in HEK293 cells from a doxycycline (dox)-inducible lentiviral vector. As expected, PNUTS immunoprecipitation (IP) recovered PP1 catalytic (PPP1CA) and regulatory subunits, including the inhibitors PPP1R8, PPP1R2, and PPP1R9B (spinophilin)43 and Tox416 (Figure 1C; Table S1). The RNA Pol II large subunit was also enriched in the PNUTS IP, consistent with previous results.24,25 Remarkably, both the WDR82 and ZC3H4 subunits of the restrictor were among the most highly enriched proteins co-purifying with PNUTS (Figure 1C), in agreement with proximity labeling studies8,9 and IP of endogenous PNUTS.35,47


### Candidate substrates for PP1-PNUTS are linked to restrictor termination
  To investigate the possible role of dephosphorylation by PP1/PNUTS in termination the by restrictor, we used chimeric fusions of PNUTS with wild-type (WT) PP1 and catalytically inactive PP1H66K, in which a key metal binding residue is substituted.25 We have shown previously that a fusion of PP1 with Phactr1 accurately reproduces substrate specificity.25,45 PNUTS and Phactr1 use highly similar sequences to bind PP1; therefore, we designed a PP1-PNUTS fusion that mimics PP1-Phactr1 in maintaining proximity of the PP1 C terminus with elements in PNUTS that interact with it.33,48,49 The chimera containing an N-terminal Avitag, PP1α (1–304), and the C terminus of PNUTS (408–940) (Figure 1A) was inducibly overexpressed in HEK293 cells from a lentiviral vector (Figure S1A). In the chimeras, the conserved PP1 interaction motif RVxF (398SVTW401) of PNUTS is deleted, and the two proteins are connected by a flexible linker (SGSGS). A C-terminal NLS (nuclear localization sequence) in the chimera replaces the putative NLS at residues 157–161 of intact PNUTS.38

  We compared the interactomes of PP1WT- and PP1H66K-PNUTS, seeking candidate substrates that bind more avidly to the inactive mutant.25 IP-mass spectrometry (MS) revealed that, as expected, PP1WT- and PP1H66K-PNUTS pulled down PP1 regulatory subunits and PNUTS interactors, including PPP1R8, PPP1R9B, Tox4, and RIF1 (Figures 1D and 1E; Table S1). The PP1 catalytic subunit was also recovered with PP1H66K-PNUTS, consistent with its auto-dephosphorylation.50 In agreement with previous work,25 the RNA Pol II large subunit POLR2A is significantly enriched for binding to PP1H66K-PNUTS but not PP1WT-PNUTS (Figure 1F). Notably, both restrictor subunits robustly bind PP1H66K-PNUTS and PP1WT-PNUTS, arguing against an indirect interaction between PP1-PNUTS and the restrictor mediated by tethering to RNA Pol II. Other candidate substrates specifically enriched for binding to PP1H66K-PNUTS included the putative WDR82-associated termination factor FIP1L151 and subunits of nuclear exosome complexes that are implicated in restrictor function.9,10 These include the exonuclease MTREX (Mtr4), the ARS2 interacting phospho-protein ZC3H18,52 the NEXT subunit ZCCHC8, the PAXT-associated PABPN1, and other poly(A) binding proteins as well as the core exosome subunits EXOSC3, EXOSC6, and EXOSC8 (Figure 1F; Table S1). Together, these results confirm the RNA Pol II large subunit as a PP1/PNUTS interactor, consistent with the suggestion that it is a substrate, and identify several nuclear exosome-associated proteins as additional interactors and potential phosphatase targets. Spt5 is a PP1/PNUTS substrate at 3′ ends of genes,27,41,53 but was not identified in our IP of PP1-PNUTS fusions. This negative result does not exclude the possibility that Spt5 phosphorylation could be affected by PP1H66K-PNUTS.


### PNUTS association with the restrictor requires its WDR82 binding domain
  The strong coIP of PNUTS and PP1-PNUTS fusions with the restrictor was unexpected, as previous work suggested a loose interaction detected only by long-range proximity labeling and mutually exclusive binding of WDR82 to either ZC3H4 or PNUTS.8,9,42 To address whether the PNUTS-restrictor interaction is direct or indirect, we asked whether it requires the PNUTS domain that binds WDR82 directly16,26 in a manner that is conserved between yeast, plants, and humans.54 IP MS interactome analysis was performed on a fusion protein, PP1H66K-PNUTSΔWBD, comprising PP1(H66K) plus PNUTS (408–940) deleted for the WDR82 binding domain (WBD) residues 455–523 (Figure 1A). Comparison of the interactomes showed that, as expected, WDR82 binding was significantly reduced in the PP1H66K-PNUTSΔWBD mutant relative to the PP1H66K-PNUTS control. Importantly, ZC3H4 binding was also strongly diminished by deletion of the WBD (Figure 2A), and this result was confirmed by western blotting (Figure 2B). Together, these results show that the PP1-PNUTS interaction with restrictor requires the WBD. The most parsimonious explanation for these results is that the PNUTS WBD binds directly to the WDR82 subunit of the restrictor. We cannot absolutely eliminate the alternative possibility that the WBD is required for an indirect interaction with the restrictor mediated by an intermediary; however, for the reasons outlined above, it is unlikely to be RNA Pol II. Association of PP1H66K-PNUTS with EXOSC3, EXOSC6, EXOSC8, and ZC3H18 was also dependent on WDR82 binding (Figure 2A), consistent with interaction between the PNUTS-bound restrictor and the nuclear exosome.9,10


### PPWZ, a predicted complex of PP1/PNUTS/WDR82/ZC3H4
  To gain insight into how PP1/PNUTS might interact with the restrictor, we performed AlphaFold2 modeling. Models of the PP1/PNUTS/WDR82 complex yielded high-confidence predictions of PNUTS residues 397–451 binding to PP1 and residues 453–524 within the WBD binding to WDR82, as indicated by the predicted aligned error (PAE) plot (Figures 2C and S1B). The PP1/PNUTS/WDR82 models are of two classes that differ in the interaction between PP1 and PNUTS. In 19 of 25 models, PNUTS residues 429–452 form an apparently auto-inhibitory helix that occludes the active site of PP1 (Figure 2C) and closely mimics the structure of PP1 inhibitor 2 complexed with PP1.55 This helix overlaps with sequences (376–453) shown previously to stabilize the interaction between PP1 and PNUTS.33 In the other 6 models, which closely conform with the structure of PP1 complexed with a fragment of PNUTS (394–433),33 this helix is free (Figure S1C). The functional significance of these two alternative modes of PP1-PNUTS interaction for regulation of phosphatase activity remains to be investigated.

  We next asked whether a PP1/PNUTS/WDR82 complex was capable of binding to ZC3H4 using AlphaFold. The results in Figures 2E and 2F show that, indeed, a quaternary complex of PP1/PNUTS/WDR82/ZC3H4 (PPWZ) is predicted with high confidence. In the putative PPWZ complex, interaction of PNUTS 520IPLD523 with WDR82 is replaced by the analogous 939IPLD942 motif from Z3CH4 (Figures 2E, 2F, and S2C) without disrupting other PNUTS-WDR82 interactions distributed along a trajectory of 80 residues that zigzags across the surface of WDR82 (Figure 2G). Notably, the contacts between human PNUTS and WDR82 predicted in the PPWZ complex appear to be conserved, as they mimic the model of Arabidopsis PNUTS-WDR8254 (Figure S1D). ZC3H4 is also predicted to interact with WDR82 through its 949LRDPR953 sequence (Figures 2D–2F), in agreement with a previous study.9 In the predicted binary complex of the restrictor, the ZC3H4 motifs 966VPLSKPSF971 and 63ELEDD67 contact WDR82 (Figures S2A, S2B, and S2D), but they are displaced by PNUTS in the PPWZ quaternary complex (Figures 2E–2G). The ZC3H4 63ELEDD67 motif overlaps an element that binds to ARS210; therefore, displacement of this motif from WDR82 in the context of PPWZ might make it available for interaction with ARS2. These results reveal how WDR82 could make simultaneous contact with PNUTS and ZC3H4 in a PPWZ heterotetramer by using a subset of the contacts used in the PP1/PNUTS/WDR82 and WDR82/ZC3H4 complexes (Figures 2D–2G). The additional stabilizing contacts between ZC3H4 and WDR82 that are possible in the absence of competition from PNUTS (Figure S2A) might contribute to the previously reported mutually exclusive binding of WDR82 to PNUTS or ZC3H4. We conclude that the predicted 4-way PPWZ interaction provides a plausible molecular explanation for the WBD-dependent coIP of PNUTS with the restrictor (Figures 1, 2A, and 2B). However, the complete composition, subunit stoichiometry, and specific contacts between subunits in the model await experimental validation.

  SET1 proteins also bind WDR82, and this interaction antagonizes restrictor function.13 AlphaFold modeling predicted that SETD1A 60LQDPR64 binds to WDR82 in a manner similar to ZC3H4 949LRDPR953, consistent with previous reports (Figures 2D, S2E, and S2F).9,13 A second SETD1A sequence, 74FSLPVPKF81, is predicted to compete with the ZC3H4 motif 966VPLSKPSF971 and PNUTS motif 461WVCPRPLVL469 (in the reverse direction) for binding to WDR82. These sequences structurally align to a consensus sequence, Γ×LxPx1-2Ω, which we term the LxxP motif and where Γ is a hydrophobic (L/V/F) and Ω an aromatic (F/W) residue (Figure S2D). These predicted competing interactions could be the basis for mutually exclusive binding of WDR82 with either SETD1A or ZC3H4. In yeast, a similar competition occurs between Set1 and the 3′ processing complex, associated with Pta1 (APT), for binding to the WDR82 homolog Swd2.57


### PP1 phosphatase stimulates restrictor-mediated termination
  We next investigated how transcription is affected in cells expressing WT and inactive PP1H66K-PNUTS fusion proteins. Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) of PP1WT- and PP1H66K-PNUTS (408–940) showed comparable low signals at 5′ ends of genes, consistent with transient chromatin binding. In contrast, full-length PNUTS assayed in a side-by-side ChIP with the same anti-Avitag antibody demonstrated strong peaks of ChIP signal (Figure S3I). We conclude that robust PNUTS chromatin binding requires its N terminus, which includes the TFIIS N-terminal domain (TND). This result is consistent with a recent report that the PNUTS TND is required for binding to the RNA Pol II, Spt6, and Paf complex.35

  To monitor any effects of PP1-PNUTS chimeras on transcription, we performed nascent RNA analysis by Bru-seq (bromouridine sequencing).58 In this procedure, cells are pulse labeled with bromouridine, and then fragmented labeled RNA is immunopurified and sequenced. The Bru-Seq signal was normalized to mitochondrial transcripts that act as an internal control. We quantified the ratio of antisense/sense transcription in the region immediately upstream and downstream of each promoter. We note that a caveat of the dominant-negative approach we adopted is that it requires overexpression so that the PP1-PNUTS fusions can compete with endogenous PP1 complexes. Overexpression of PP1WT-PNUTS elevated antisense relative to sense transcription (Figures 3A–3C), but the antisense transcripts terminated within a few kilobases (Figures 3A, 3B, and S2G). In contrast, catalytically dead PP1H66K-PNUTS caused a much more pronounced increase in antisense/sense transcription (Figures 3C and S2H). Furthermore, the antisense transcripts elicited by PP1H66K-PNUTS extended much farther, often over 10 kb. This effect was evident at individual genes (Figures 3D, 3E, S2H, and S3A–S3C) and enhancers (Figure 3F) and in metaplots of many divergent transcription units (Figures 3G ad S3D). In some cases, inhibition of termination by PP1H66K-PNUTS led to extensive transcription that overlapped adjacent genes in the antisense (Figure 3E) or sense direction (Figure S3A). We identified ~4,900 genes whose divergent antisense transcription was upregulated 2× by PP1H66K-PNUTS and ~2,000 genes upregulated by PP1WT-PNUTS in the region between −5,000 and the TSS (transcription start site) and separated by 5 kb from the nearest upstream gene) (Table S2). At over 3,000 promoters, PP1H66K-PNUTS stimulated antisense transcription that actually exceeded sense transcription in the 5′ portion of the gene (Figure 3C). To quantify the relative extent of divergent transcription, we calculated an antisense elongation index as the log2 ratio of (antisense read density in the region −5,000 to −1,000) / (antisense read density in the region −1,000 to the TSS). Cumulative frequency plots showed that the extent of antisense transcription elongation is significantly increased by inactivation of PP1 in the fusion protein (Figure 3H).

  Together, these results show that inactivation of PP1 in the context of a fusion with PNUTS greatly enhances both the total amount of antisense/sense transcription and the distance traveled by antisense-directed transcription complexes. We therefore conclude that protein dephosphorylation by PP1 positively regulates termination of antisense transcription.


### Dominant-negative PP1H66K-PNUTS mimics PNUTS, ARS2, and ZC3H4 depletion
  Since PP1H66K-PNUTS binds to the restrictor (Figures 1E and 2B), we compared its effects with those of depleting ZC3H4.9,10 Based on nascent RNA POINT-seq data in HCT116 cells,9 we identified 2,207 promoters where ZC3H4 degron depletion elevated antisense transcription ≥2× (Figure S4A). Divergent antisense transcription at these ZC3H4-sensitive promoters was strongly upregulated by PP1H66K-PNUTS in HEK293 (Figure 4A). PP1H66K-PNUTS had an even more potent effect than ZC3H4 depletion on divergent transcription at some loci (compare Figure 4A with Figure S4A). For example, PP1H66K-PNUTS induced antisense transcription at KLF6, which extended over 40 kb (Figure S2H), much farther than reported previously in a ZC3H4 degron and similar to that which occurred when ZC3H4 and symplekin were co-depleted in HCT116 cells.8 At a few genes, including ZC3H6, PP1H66K-PNUTS elevated sense transcription, also consistent with ZC3H4 degrons8,13 (Figures S3A and S3B). Also in common with ZC3H4 inactivation,7,8,59 PP1H66K-PNUTS greatly elevated expression of transposable elements throughout the genome, including SINEs, LINEs, and ERVs (Figures 3I, S3E, and S3F). In contrast to ZC3H4 inactivation though, PP1H66K-PNUTS, but not PP1WT-PNUTS, enhanced readthrough transcription in 3′ flanking regions, consistent with PP1/PNUTS function in poly(A) site-dependent termination6,27,30,41 (Figures S3G and S3H).

  ARS2 is thought to facilitate restrictor recruitment to 5′ ends of genes through interaction with CBC.10 We therefore compared the effects of PP1H66K-PNUTS with those of ARS2 knockdown. Transcription units upregulated by ARS2 knockdown in HeLa cells10 were strongly upregulated by PP1H66K-PNUTS in HEK293 cells, whereas ARS2-insensitive transcription units were not (Figures 4B and S4B). On the basis of the extensive overlap between the effects of ZC3H4 or ARS2 depletion and PP1H66K-PNUTS expression on divergent antisense transcription, we conclude that the PP1H66K-PNUTS substrate trap is a potent inhibitor of restrictor-mediated termination.

  We also compared the effects of PP1H66K-PNUTS with PNUTS degron depletion.9 Notably, antisense transcription was strongly upregulated by PNUTS depletion in HCT116 cells at promoters where antisense transcription is elevated in response to PP1H66K -PNUTS in HEK293 cells (Figure S4C). The fact that PNUTS underexpression inhibits antisense termination in a similar way as PP1H66K-PNUTS overexpression strongly suggests that both interventions work by reducing PP1/PNUTS phosphatase activity rather than through non-specific or indirect effects.


### WDR82 binding is required for inhibition of restrictor termination by PP1H66K-PNUTS
  The preceding results suggest that PP1 catalytic activity promotes termination by the restrictor and that PP1/PNUTS contacts the restrictor via WDR82. To test whether phosphatase-dead PP1H66K-PNUTS must contact WDR82 in order to exert its dominant-negative effect, we performed nascent RNA sequencing in cells expressing PP1H66K-PNUTSΔWBD that lacks the WBD (Figures 1A, 2B, and S1A). Deletion of the WBD effectively abolished the inhibition of restrictor function, resulting in a significantly reduced ratio ofantisense/sense transcription and antisense elongation index relative to the fusion with intact PNUTS 408–940 (Figures 4C–4E and S4D). We conclude that potent inhibition of antisense termination requires association of PP1H66K-PNUTS with WDR82 and therefore probably results from recruitment of the dominant-negative mutant PP1 to the restrictor via this interaction.

  The enhancement of antisense transcription by PP1WT-PNUTS (Figures 3A and 3B) also requires WDR82 binding, as shown by expressing PP1WT-PNUTSΔWBD, which rescued termination of antisense transcription (Figures S4E and S4F). Sequestration of WDR82 by PP1WT-PNUTS therefore contributes to suppression of antisense termination by this fusion protein, but without additional inactivation of the phosphatase, its effect is relatively weak. Competition for WDR82 binding between PP1WT-PNUTS and another factor that limits antisense transcription initiation/elongation could explain these results.

  AlphaFold modeling suggests that the ZC3H4 939IPLD942 motif rather than the identical motif in PNUTS occupies the cognate binding site on WDR82 in the predicted PPWZ complex (Figures 2C–2E). We reasoned that, if the PNUTS IPLD motif is dispensable for interaction with the restrictor in the PPWZ complex, then mutation of this element would not impair the dominant-negative effect of PP1H66K-PNUTS on termination. To test this idea, we mutated 520IPLD523 to GSGS in the PP1H66K-PNUTSΔIPLD fusion and monitored nascent RNA synthesis. Bru-seq showed that PP1H66K-PNUTSΔIPLD inhibited antisense termination only slightly less well than PP1H66K-PNUTSWT (Figures 4F and S4G). We conclude that the PNUTS IPLD motif is not critical for interaction with WDR82, in agreement with the PPWZ model (Figures 2D and 2E).


### CTD Ser5 dephosphorylation by PP1/PNUTS and transcription termination
  The fact that PP1 phosphatase activity promotes termination by the restrictor begs the question of the relevant substrate(s). One candidate is the RNA Pol II large subunit that co-precipitates with PP1H66K-PNUTS (Figure 1E) and is reported to be dephosphorylated at CTD Ser5 residues by PP1/PNUTS.16,17,19,25,60 We tested whether PP1H66K-PNUTS affected Ser5 phosphorylation of RNA Pol II engaged on genes by ChIP sequencing (ChIP-seq). The results showed that PP1H66K-PNUTS caused Ser5 hyperphosphorylation at the 5′ ends of most genes without a substantial effect on recruitment of total RNA Pol II (Figures 5A and 5B), regardless of whether there was extensive antisense transcription (Figures 5A and S5A). Unlike PNUTS degron depletion,35,36 we did not observe a strong effect of PP1H66K-PNUTS on RNA Pol II density at the promoter-proximal pause site relative to the gene body (Figure 5B). In contrast to most protein-coding genes, PP1H66K-PNUTS did not cause Ser5 hyperphosphorylation at histone or U small nuclear RNA (snRNA) genes (Figures 5E, 5F, S5E, and S5F) that produce short non-adenylated transcripts. Similar results were obtained using an independent anti-Ser5-P monoclonal antibody for ChIP (Figure S5G), and conclusions were unaffected by whether the signal was normalized to the mouse cell spike-in or the U snRNA gene internal control (Figures 5A and S5B). We speculate that the distinct behavior of histone and U snRNA genes may be related to their promoter structures, which give rise to more unidirectional transcription. Our results do not exclude the possibility that dephosphorylation of other relevant substrates is also inhibited by PP1H66K-PNUTS, including other CTD residues, Spt5, and the exosome (Figure 1F).

  To determine whether WDR82 binding was required for PP1H66K-PNUTS to inhibit Ser5 dephosphorylation, we conducted anti-Ser5P ChIP in cells expressing PP1H66K-PNUTSΔWBD. This experiment showed no detectable effect of the ΔWBD mutant on CTD Ser5 phosphorylation (Figures 5G and S5H), arguing that inhibition of Ser5-P dephosphorylation by PP1H66K-PNUTS requires WDR82 binding. We speculate that WDR82 helps target the substrate trap by direct binding to phospho-Ser5 heptads in conjunction with ZC3H415,26 (Figure 7). Although the most parsimonious interpretation of our results is that PP1/PNUTS/WDR82 directly dephosphorylates CTD Ser5, we cannot exclude the possibility of an indirect effect.

  To determine whether PP1H66K-PNUTS expression and resulting RNA Pol II Ser5 hyperphosphorylation affected transcription elongation, we conducted Bru-Seq after release from DRB (5,6 dichlorobenzimidazole ribofuranoside), which blocks elongation at the promoter-proximal pause site.61 This experiment showed that PP1H66K-PNUTS caused the wave of RNA Pol II to advance slightly farther into genes 15 min after release from the DRB arrest (Figures 5H, 5I, S5I, and S5J). We could not monitor the speed of divergent antisense transcription in this experiment because the distance traveled by RNA Pol II is confounded by the inhibition of termination. In summary, these observations suggest widespread PP1/PNUTS-dependent dephosphorylation of CTD Ser5 at 5′ ends and that PP1H66K-PNUTS interferes with this process and modestly accelerates transcription.


### Reduced pausing by Ser5 hyperphosphorylated relative to total RNA Pol II
  To investigate the possibility of a mechanistic connection between RNA Pol II Ser5-P dephosphorylation and termination, we monitored transcription by Ser5 hyperphosphorylated RNA Pol II and total RNA Pol II by eNET-seq (enhanced Nascent Elongating Transcript-seq).62 This method can precisely determine the orientation and position of different RNA Pol II isoforms by sequencing of nascent RNA fragments protected by the polymerase.63,64 eNET-seq confirmed that PP1H66K-PNUTS caused Ser5 hyperphosphorylation without substantially enhancing total RNA Pol II recruitment to promoters. It also confirmed that polymerases engaged in divergent antisense transcription when PP1H66K-PNUTS is expressed are indeed Ser5 phosphorylated (Figures 6A–6C and S6A).

  Direct comparison of eNET-seq profiles for total RNA Pol II and the Ser5-P isoform revealed a remarkable difference that has not been emphasized previously and is not obvious from ChIP experiments using the same antibodies. The total RNA Pol II profile exhibits the signature peak of promoter-proximal RNA Pol II that is paused prior to release into productive elongation or premature termination. Ser5-P RNA Pol II eNET-seq, on the other hand, has a quite distinct profile with relatively less proximal and more distal occupancy in both the sense and antisense directions (Figure 6D). In other words, the Ser5-P RNA Pol II subpopulation appears to transition from promoter-proximal to promoter-distal positions far better than total RNA Pol II. Similar results were observed in HEK293 cells with or without dox induction of PP1H66K-PNUTS (Figures 6D and S6B) as well as in parental HEK293 cells (Figure S6C). Examination of the mNET-seq data of Nojima et al.63using independent antibodies revealed a similar contrast between the profiles of Ser5-P RNA Pol II and total RNA Pol II (Figure S6D). We investigated the basis for enhanced elongation by Ser5-P RNA Pol II by quantifying the fraction of reads at pause sites (pausing propensity) and the number of reads/pause (pause strength) in different regions of the gene. Pause sites are defined as positions with >5 reads where the signal is >3 standard deviations above the mean of the surrounding 200 bp window.62,64 This analysis revealed that, relative to total RNA Pol II, the Ser5-P isoform has much reduced pausing propensity and pause strength both in promoter-proximal and distal regions (Figures 6E, 6F, S6E, and S6F). Together these results suggest that the dephosphorylation of CTD Ser5 residues could facilitate transcription termination by increasing RNA Pol II pausing and reducing its capacity to elongate (Figure 7).


## DISCUSSION
  We report that PP1/PNUTS phosphatase activity stimulates termination of divergent antisense RNA Pol II transcription by the restrictor. The most significant observations in support of this conclusion are as follows. (1) PP1/PNUTS co-purifies with both subunits of the restrictor (Figures 1C–1E) in a way that requires the WBD of PNUTS (Figures 2A and 2B). AlphaFold modeling shows that the complex of PP1/PNUTS with WDR82 is compatible with ZC3H4 binding to WDR82 in a putative PPWZ heterotetramer (Figures 2D and 2E). Mutually exclusive binding of WDR82 to either PNUTS or ZC3H4, proposed previously, is not supported by structural considerations. It is therefore possible that WDR82 in other protein complexes might also serve to recruit PP1/PNUTS. (2) A chimera comprising hypoactive PP1H66K fused to PNUTS strongly inhibits restrictor-mediated termination relative to the same fusion with WT PP1 (Figure 3). Inhibition of termination by PP1H66K-PNUTS requires the WBD. The most economical explanation of these results is that the PNUTS WBD directly contacts the restrictor (Figures 2B and 4C–4E) and that the complex of the restrictor with inactive PP1 acts as a dominant-negative inhibitor of termination. Fusions of PP1 with interacting proteins (PIPs) were first developed as substrate binding traps,25 and this work shows that they can also reveal new functions of specific PP1 holoenzymes that have been previously difficult to disentangle.65 (3) CTD Ser5 dephosphorylation by PPWZ may enhance termination by the restrictor. This possibility is suggested by the facts that PP1H66K-PNUTS elicits CTD Ser5 hyperphosphorylation in addition to inhibiting termination and that both activities require the PNUTS WBD (Figures 4C–4E and 5G).

  Our results suggest a functional coupling between restrictor-mediated termination and PP1-mediated dephosphorylation of RNA Pol II CTD Ser5 (Figures 5 and 7), as observed previously in vivo17,18; however, this conclusion is not supported by in vitro assays using purified PP1/PNUTS and Ser5-P peptide substrates.35,36 A possible explanation for this discrepancy is that in vivo PP1/PNUTS activity is modified by association with additional factors that confer substrate specificity. We propose that the restrictor may function as a PP1/PNUTS substrate adaptor for Ser5-P RNA Pol II (Figure 7). This idea is supported by the specific binding of ZC3H4 and WDR82 to the Ser5-P CTD in vitro15,24,26 and restrictor co-localization with Ser5-P RNA Pol II in vivo.8 A rationale for how Ser5 dephosphorylation could facilitate termination is suggested by our finding that the frequency and strength of transcriptional pausing are much reduced for the Ser5-P isoform relative to total RNA Pol II (Figures 6D–6F). These observations are consistent with a recent report of slower elongation when the Ser5 kinase Cdk7 is inhibited.66 Dephosphorylation of Ser5-P would convert RNA Pol II from a faster low pausing state to a slower high pausing state that is presumably more susceptible to termination, since pausing is a universal precondition for termination.67 The molecular basis for how Ser5-P dephosphorylation enhances transcriptional pausing remains unknown. Our results do not exclude additional substrates of PP1/PNUTS that may function in restrictor-mediated termination, including potentially nuclear exosome-associated factors (MTREX, ZC3H18, ZCCHC8, EXOSC3, EXOSC6, and EXOSC8) that were enriched in IP of PP1H66K-PNUTS relative to PP1WT-PNUTS (Figure 1F).

  This work highlights two emerging principles in control of RNA Pol II termination: the involvement of CTD Ser5-P and the function of phosphatases in this process. Ser5-P recognition is central to premature termination of yeast transcription by the Nrd1-Nab3-Sen1 (NNS) pathway. Nrd1 specifically binds Ser5-P CTD heptads68 and works together with the RNA binding protein Nab3 and the helicase Sen1 to terminate short non-coding transcripts.69 The yeast NNS complex and the mammalian PPWZ complex proposed here have a number of features in common: (1) they both bind phospho-Ser5-P RNA Pol II, (2) they bind the nascent RNA, and (3) they interact with the nuclear exosome9,10,70 (Figure 1F), and (4) a phosphatase activity, PP1 in metazoan PPWZ and Glc7 in yeast APT,71,72 is implicated in effecting termination. Moreover, an integral role of PP1/PNUTS/WDR82 phosphatase complexes is shared not only between the metazoan restrictor and yeast NNS mechanisms but also in poly(A) site-dependent termination,27,41,73,74 premature termination within flowering control genes in Arabidopsis,54 and J base-dependent termination in trypanosomes.75 In addition the PP2A phosphatase activity of Integrator, which also dephosphorylates CTD Ser5, promotes premature termination near the 5′ end of genes.20,76,77 CTD Ser5 dephosphorylation and conversion of RNA Pol II to a high pausing state could therefore facilitate termination in multiple contexts.


### Limitations of the study
  The PPWZ complex predicted by AlphaFold is consistent with our coIP results, but we did not determine the complete composition or subunit stoichiometry of the complex, nor did we determine its substrate specificity, which is still very challenging for individual PP1 complexes. The model where CTD Ser5 dephosphorylation is necessary for restrictor-mediated termination remains a hypothesis to be tested further, and other PP1 substrates could function in termination. How Ser5-P phosphorylation affects transcriptional pausing, possibly by modulating RNA Pol II association with positive and elongation factors, remains unknown.

### METHOD DETAILS

#### Lentiviruses and human cell lines
  HEK293 cells were infected with lentiviruses based on pCW57 a gift of Adam Karpf (Addgene 89180) and selected with blasticidin (10 μg/mL) for doxycycline inducible expression of PNUTS and PP1-PNUTS fusions that were codon optimized and synthesized by Genewiz. Doxycycline induction (2 μg/mL) was for ~24 h.


#### Immunoprecipitation
  Nuclear extracts were prepared from induced lentiviral transduced cells (24 h, 2 μg/mL doxycycline) by a modification of the procedure of Baluapuri et al.87 Cells were washed in cold PBS, lysed in 10 mM HEPES pH 7.9, 0.34 M sucrose, 3 mM CaCl2, 2 mM magnesium acetate, 0.1 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP-40, nuclei pelleted and benzonase digested (>150 U/mL) for 40 min at 4° with nutating in 20 mM HEPES pH 7.9, 0.1 mM EDTA, 10% glycerol, 150 mM potassium acetate, 1.5 mM MgCl2,. NaCl was added to 0.3M and nuclei were nutated 30 min at 4°. Insoluble material was pelleted in a microfuge 15,000 rpm 15 min and the supernatant was diluted with an equal volume of 2X RIPA, 2% v/v Nonidet P-40 1% w/v deoxycholate, 0.2% w/v SDS, 50 mM Tris pH 8, 5 mM EDTA.

  Nuclear extract (1–2 mg) was immunoprecipitated with rabbit polyclonal anti Avitag antibody (2 h, 4°) and collected on Protein A Dynabeads, washed 3X rapidly with cold 50 mM Tris-Cl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% NP-40 and eluted with 1% Formic Acid and 28% Acetonitrile, for Mass spectrometry or with Laemmli loading buffer for Western blotting.


#### Immunoblotting
  Immunoblots were developed with HRP conjugated swine anti-rabbit secondary antibody (DAKO, P0217) and ECL Plus (PerkinElmer, NEL103E001EA) or SuperSignal West Dura (Thermofisher 34076).


#### Mass spectrometry
  Samples (4–6 replicates) were reduced, alkylated, and digested using S-Trap micro filters (Protifi, Huntington, NY) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Digested peptides were cleaned using Pierce C18 Spin Tips (Thermo Scientific), dried in a vacuum centrifuge, and resuspended in 0.1% FA in mass spectrometry-grade water. Peptides were loaded into autosampler vials and analyzed directly using a NanoElute liquid chromatography system (Bruker, Germany) coupled with a timsTOF SCP mass spectrometer (Bruker, Germany). Peptides were separated on a 75 μm i.d. × 15 cm separation column packed with 1.9 μm C18 beads (Bruker, Germany) over a 90-min elution gradient. Buffer A was 0.1% FA in water and buffer B was 0.1% FA in acetonitrile. Instrument control and data acquisition were performed using Compass Hystar (version 6.0) with the timsTOF SCP operating in parallel accumulation-serial fragmentation (PASEF) mode under the following settings: mass range 100–1700 m/z, 1/k/0 Start 0.7 V s cm−2 End 1.3 V s cm−2; ramp accumulation times were 166 ms; capillary voltage was 4500 V, dry gas 8.0 L min−1 and dry temp 200°C. The PASEF settings were: 5 MS/MS scans (total cycle time, 1.03 s); charge range 0–5; active exclusion for 0.2 min; scheduling target intensity 20,000; intensity threshold 500; collision-induced dissociation energy 10 eV.

  Fragmentation spectra were searched against the UniProt human proteome database using the MSFragger-based FragPipe computational platform.88 Contaminants and reverse decoys were added to the database automatically. The precursor-ion mass tolerance and fragment-ion mass tolerance were set to 15 and 20 ppm, respectively. Fixed modifications were set as carbamidomethyl (C), and variable modifications were set as oxidation (M), two missed tryptic cleavages were allowed, and the protein-level false discovery rate (FDR) was ≤1%.


#### Bru-seq
  Nascent RNA sequencing by Bru-seq was performed as described58,89 with minor modifications. HEK293 cells + and − doxycycline induction (2 μg/mL, 24 hrs) were incubated with 2 mM Bromouridine in fresh medium + or − doxycycline for 10 or 20 min. Bru-Seq following release from a DRB block89 was carried out by addition of BrU (2mM) 10 min before harvesting. Labeled RNA (50 μg) was fragmented with ZnCl2 (10mM, 70° 8 min, stopped with 10 mM EDTA) and immunopurified in PBS, 0.05% Triton X-100, 1 mM DTT, + RNase inhibitor for 1 h at 4° using 3D4 monoclonal anti-BrdU (3 μg) immobilized on protein G Dynabeads. Beads were washed 3X in the same buffer and RNA was eluted in 5mM DTT at 95° for 3 min. Eluted RNA was rRNA depleted90 and RNA-seq libraries were made with the KAPA RNA HyperPrep kit (KR1350 – v1.16). Sequencing was on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (2 × 150). Reads were mapped to the hg38 UCSC human genome with Bowtie2 version 2.3.2. PCR duplicates were removed using bbtools clumpify and adapters were trimmed using bbtools bbkuk version 39.01. After filtering out rRNA, reads were mapped to the hg38 UCSC human genome with Bowtie2 version 2.3.2.


#### Bru-seq analysis
  Metaplots include all genes longer than 1 kb, separated by > 5 kb, containing at least one read in the region, and were in common between samples plotted. Read counts were normalized to total counts on the mitochondrial chromosome. Antisense/Sense boxplots for antisense signal −5kb-TSS/sense signal TSS-+1.0 kb genes >2kb long and separated by 5kb from other genes. Antisense elongation index is the log2 (antisense read density in the region −5000 to −1000)/(antisense read density in the region −1000 to the TSS). Expression of transposable elements in Bru-Seq datasets was analyzed with SQuIRE using default settings.56


#### AlphaFold modeling
  AlphaFold 2.3.0 with full sequence databases was used to model structure predictions with the multimer setting enabled.81 Full-length proteins fasta sequences from Uniprot.org were used as input unless otherwise indicated. Default five seeds was used per each five model parameters, 25 in total. Top ranked prediction as indicated by pLDDT was used in the study (unless otherwise indicated). Close to identical models were generated by AF2 and AF3.


#### ChIP-seq
  ChIP was performed as described.80,91 Cross-linked human cell pellets were spiked with cross-linked mouse cells prior to sonication with a Covaris ME220. 10 μL of rabbit anti-pan pol II CTD serum 8 μg affinity pure anti-Avitag antibody, 4 μg of 3E8 rat monoclonal anti-Ser5-P CTD together with rabbit anti-Rat IgG or 5 μg of 4H8 mouse monoclonal anti-Ser5-P CTD was used for each IP with 1 mg of cross-linked extract. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (2 × 150). Reads were mapped to the hg38 UCSC human genome with Bowtie2 version 2.3.2. PCR duplicates were removed using bbtools clumpify and adapters were trimmed using bbtools bbkuk version 39.01 Read coordinates were collapsed and centered using deeptools bamCoverage version 3.5.1.

  Pol II ChIP-seq, metaplots include all genes longer than 1 kb, separated by > 2 kb, containing at least one read in the region, and were in common between all ChIP-seq. In some cases read counts were normalized to signal over snRNA genes.


#### POINT-seq analysis
  Reads from GSE228290 and GSE2074219 were mapped to the hg38 UCSC human genome with Hisat version 2.1.0. PCR duplicates were removed using bbtools clumpify and adapters were trimmed using bbtools bbkuk version 39.01 Bigwigs were made using deeptools bamCoverage version 3.5.1. filtering out rRNA, normalizing RPKM.

  Metaplots include all genes longer than 1 kb, separated by > 2 kb, containing at least one read in the region, and were in common between all samples. To calculate the relative signal for each gene, the signal for each bin was divided by the total signal within the region plotted. The mean relative signal was then calculated by averaging the relative signal across the genes plotted for each individual replicate. The replicates were then averaged with the shaded region representing the min and max range for the three replicates, normalized to the +1000 position.

  Adding the replicate signals together and selecting all genes longer than 1 kb, separated by > 5 kb, containing at least one read in the antisense region 5kb–0.5kb upstream of the TSS. We then selected genes that had a greater than 2-fold increase in the antisense signal −TMP/+TMP.


#### eNET-seq
  eNET-seq experiments employed optimized MNase digestion conditions, RNA decapping, and incorporation of 12 base UMIs in library construction.62 Libraries were made with Qiaseq miRNA Library kit (Qiagen 331505). Pol II immunoprecipitation was with polyclonal rabbit anti pan-pol II CTD or rat monoclonal anti-Ser5-P CTD 3E8 (Chromotek) with rabbit anti-rat secondary antibody.


#### eNET-seq read processing
  eNET-seq libraries were made with the QIAseq miRNA UDI Library kit using 12 base UMI’s and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (2 × 150). Adapters were trimmed using cutadapt (v2.3) and reads were aligned to the hg38 human genome using Bowtie2 (v2.3.2). PCR duplicates were removed using UMI-tools (v0.5.4) and read coordinates were collapsed to a single base pair coordinate corresponding to the RNA 3′ end. Reads were filtered to only include those with a mapping quality score ≥10 and to remove reads aligning to snoRNA genes. To generate UCSC genome browser tracks, BigWig coverage files were generated from the aligned/filtered reads. For eNET-seq 5′ metaplots and pausing analysis, reads were further filtered to remove those that did not align within 5 kb of a protein coding gene. Metaplots include all genes longer than 2 kb and separated by > 5 kb. In addition, genes had to contain at least one read and the bottom 10% of genes with the lowest signal were excluded. Each 5′ region was divided into 10 bp bins. Read counts in each bin were normalized by library size and the size of the bin. The relative signal (relative frequency) was calculated separately for each gene by dividing the signal in each bin by the sum of the signal for the entire plotted region. The relative signal was then averaged for each bin and multiplied by 1000 to remove small decimals. The mean relative signal was calculated separately for each biological replicate. The shaded region shows the minimum and maximum mean signal for the replicates and the center line shows the mean for the replicates.


#### eNET-seq read subsampling
  eNET-seq read subsampling was performed as previously described62 To compare pol II pausing for different TSS regions (Figures 6E, 6F, S6E, and S6F), reads were subsampled so each gene had the same number of aligned/filtered reads for all datasets used in the comparison. Subsampling was performed separately for each individual gene and involved identifying the dataset with the fewest reads for each gene. Reads aligning to the gene were then randomly subsampled for the remaining datasets so all samples used in the comparison had the same number of reads for the gene. To compare pol II pausing for gene body regions (Figures 6E, 6F, S6E, and S6F), reads were subsampled for each gene body region (+500 bp − polyA) as described above.


#### Quantification of pol II pausing
  Pause sites were located as previously described.62 Pauses were located by identifying positions where eNET-seq signal was >3 standard deviations above the mean for the surrounding 100 bp on each side, there were at least 5 reads at the pause site, and at least 5 additional reads within the window. Pause sites identified within 2 bp of 5′ and 3′ splice sites were excluded from downstream analysis. The fraction of pause reads was calculated by dividing the total number of reads aligned to pause sites by the total number of reads for the region. Genes were filtered to only include those >2 kb long, separated by > 5 kb, and with at least 1 pause in TSS regions (TSS - +1 kb) and a total read density of at least 1 read/kb within the gene body region (+1 kb − polyA). To assess pause strength, the average number of reads/pause was calculated for genes filtered as described above. In addition, for each region genes were further filtered to only include those with at least 1 pause site for the region.
