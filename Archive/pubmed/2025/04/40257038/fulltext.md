# Comparative transcriptome analysis reveals abscisic acid-induced bHLH transcription factors involved in saikosaponin biosynthesis in Bupleurum chinense DC


## ABSTRACT
  Bupleurum chinense DC. a medicinal plant valued for saikosaponins (SSs) with antipyretic and hepatoprotective properties, faces constrained SS biosynthesis mediated by abscisic acid (ABA) during growth. Basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) transcription factors (TFs) are hypothesized to participate in ABA signaling cascades, but their mechanistic role in SS regulation remains undefined. In this study, 20 differentially expressed BcbHLH genes were identified by transcriptomic profiling of ABA-induced hairy roots, with four MYC-family candidates (BcbHLH1-BcbHLH4) demonstrating ABA-responsive regulatory potential. ABA exposure (100 or 200 μmol/L, 24–72 h) induced dose-dependent SS reduction, while correlation analyses revealed coordinated expression between BcbHLH1-BcHMGR (r = 0.62) and BcbHLH4-BcBAS (r = 0.78), pinpointing these TFs as critical nodes in SS pathway modulation. Tissue-specific profiling showed predominant BcbHLH expression in stems and young leaves, with nuclear localization confirming their transcriptional regulatory organelles. BcbHLH3/4 exhibited transcriptional activation activity in the MYC_N domain, while molecular docking predicted 11th Arginine in the HLH domain as essential for G-box DNA binding. Collectively, our findings suggest that BcbHLH1-BcbHLH4 may serve as potential switches for fine-tuning ABA responsiveness in SS biosynthesis. Strategic manipulation of BcbHLH activity through genetic engineering approaches such as CRISPR-based editing or overexpression could alleviate ABA-mediated biosynthetic repression. Furthermore, precision engineering of the critical functional domain in BcbHLH could enhance promoter-binding activity to target genes and improve SS biosynthesis efficiency. These findings provide a reference framework for harnessing transcriptional regulators to optimize SS production in Bupleurum chinense DC.


## Introduction
  Bupleuri Radix,1,2 a bulk Chinese herbal medicine, is utilized for alleviating exterior syndromes. It exhibits effects such as fever relief, liver soothing, depression alleviation, and yang qi enhancement. According to the Chinese Pharmacopoeia,3 the primary sources of Bupleuri Radix are the roots of the Umbelliferae plants, namely Bupleurum chinense DC. and Bupleurum scorzonerifolium Willd. The primary constituents of Bupleuri Radix encompass saponins, volatile oil, polysaccharides, and other active components.4 Notably, the main active ingredient in B.chinense is saikosaponin (SS), a pentacyclic triterpenoid saponin, serving as a unique quality indicator of Bupleurum genus. Currently, over 90 saikosaponins have been identified in B.Chinense, with Saikosaponin a (SSa), Saikosaponin c (SSc), and Saikosaponin d (SSd) exhibiting definite pharmacological activities.5,6 These include reducing acute lung and liver injury,7,8 and mitigating inflammation and autophagy in osteoarthritis.9,10 SSs hold tremendous potential for future clinical applications. However, their accumulation in raw medicinal materials remains low, and the market is contaminated by the coexistence of authentic and adulterated herbal materials resulting from an absence of a standardized quality system.11 Consequently, there is an urgent necessity to undertake research on the biosynthesis of SSs in order to ensure the quality control of medicinal materials.

  The biosynthesis of SS originates from the plant mevalonate (MVA) and methyl erythritol phosphate (MEP) pathways.12 The upstream pathway controls the synthesis of precursor substances IPP and DMAPP, while the midstream pathway involves the gradual cyclization of GPP and its modification into the triterpene skeleton β-amyrin. The downstream pathway primarily encompasses oxidative modification of β-amyrin, C-C double bond displacement, and glycosylation reactions, which are crucial steps determining the structural diversity of saikosaponins. Several SS biosynthetic enzymes have been characterized in Bupleurum, including HMG-CoA reductase (HMGR), squalene epoxidase (SQE), β-amyrin synthase (β-AS), cytochrome P450 (CYP450), and UDP-glycosyltransferases (UGT).13,14 Additionally, transcription factors involved in SS biosynthesis, such as ERF, WRKY and bZIP,15,16 have also been reported. However, the downstream synthesis pathway and its regulatory mechanism for SSs remain elusive.

  Secondary metabolic pathways in plants are regulated by multiple phytohormones Abscisic acid (ABA) serves as a pivotal plant signal molecule, extensively involved in the regulation of plant growth and development, secondary metabolism, and stress responses.17 In many medicinal plants,18–20 ABA is released to modulate the production of secondary metabolites, thereby enabling resistance to drought stress and other environmental factors. This process entails the participation of a diverse array of plant transcription factors, including members of the basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) family of transcription factors (TFs), which exert extensive influence in the biosynthesis of terpenes, flavonoids, and alkaloids within cells. Overexpression of AabHLHs in A.annua significantly elevated the contents of artemisinin, β-caryophyllene, epi-cedrol, and β-farnesene.21,22 The MYB/bHLH/WD (MBW) repeat complex, which involves bHLH-TFs, regulates anthocyanin synthesis in a modular fashion.23 In Eschscholzia californica, the content of sanguinarine decreased after RNA interference of EcbHLH1–2.24 In the jasmonic acid (JA) signaling pathway, bHLH IIIe, also known as the MYC transcription factor, serves as the core transcription factor.25 It binds to the inhibitor JAZ protein and influences the synthesis of metabolites regulated by downstream genes. SmMYC2a and SmMYC2b could interact with SmJAZ1 and SmJAZ2 in Salvia miltiorrhiza.26

  The bHLH TFs not only play a crucial role in the plant JA signaling pathway but also participate in certain biological processes within the ABA signaling pathway. Studies have found that the expression of the NtPYL4 receptor in the Nicotiana tabacum ABA signaling pathway is regulated by jasmonate.27 In Arabidopsis thaliana, PYL6 is activated by the ABA signal and recruits MYC2 to interact and transmit signals to the JA pathway.28 AtbHLH129 and AtPIF4 are also implicated in ABA signaling.29 As a secondary metabolite, SSs plays a significant role in resistance to adversity and adaptation to environmental changes. Some studies have demonstrated that the synthesis of SSs is regulated by JA and ABA.30–33 However, no studies have documented the regulatory function of ABA in modulating the biosynthesis of SSs within hairy roots, and whether bHLH TFs facilitate signal exchange between the JA and ABA pathways in Bupleurum remains obscure.

  Transcription factors exert their functional effects through a trans-acting mode, involving protein-DNA interactions. The binding domain located at the C-terminal region of bHLH TF amino acids specifically binds to the E-box (5’-CANNTG-3’) cis-element within the promoter region, thereby initiating or inhibiting the transcription of downstream genes. In the case of MYC proteins, the G-box (a palindrome sequence of 5’-CACGTG-3’) typically serves as the preferred binding motif.34 Specifically, AaMYC2 or SmBHLH10 can directly bind to the G-box present in the promoter of biosynthesis genes, promoting the biosynthesis of artemisinin or tanshinone, respectively.35,36 However, research findings concerning Bupleurum genus remain relatively limited. Further investigation into the promoter binding mechanism of BcbHLH TFs to SSs biosynthesis genes is imperative.

  In this study, we analyzed comparative transcriptome sequencing data from ABA-induced hairy roots of B.chinense. The differentially expressed bHLH genes were cloned and subjected to bioinformatic analysis. Subsequently, we observed the correlation between bHLH TFs and the expression of key enzymes involved in the saikosaponin biosynthesis pathway. Furthermore, the tissue specificity and biological characteristics of these TFs. Using homology modeling and molecular docking, we examined the critical binding sites of MYC proteins to the G-box. The findings of this study could offer analytical insights into the regulation of BcbHLH genes on the saikosaponin synthesis pathway, which holds significant research value for expanding the ABA-regulated synthesis network and advancing molecular breeding strategies.


## Materials and methods

### Plant materials
  Seeds of B.chinense were harvested in Xuchang City, Henan Province, China. These seeds were identified as mature seeds of Bupleurum chinense DC. by Professor Changli Liu from Capital Medical University. Following disinfection procedures, the seeds were sown onto agar medium and incubated in the dark at room temperature for a duration of 3 weeks to observe germination (Supplementary Figure S1A). Subsequently, the germinated seeds were exposed to light and cultivated for an additional 6 weeks to obtain sterile seedlings (Supplementary Figure S1B). In a sterile environment, the roots and rhizomes of these seedlings were excised, and the wounded areas were infected with Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain C58C1. After 4 weeks of dark cultivation, adventitious roots emerged (Supplementary Figure S1C). These roots were then excised and subjected to shaking in Gamborg B5 liquid medium for 3 months. During this period, gDNA was extracted for the amplification of RolB gene (with specific primers listed in Supplementary Table S1), and the positive roots were identified as hairy roots (Supplementary Figure S1D).

  The variety “Zhongchai No.2” of Bupleurum chinense DC. was sown and cultivated in the medicinal plant field at Capital Medical University. During the flowering stage, samples were collected from vigorous plants, encompassing roots, stems, tender leaves, and inflorescence tissues, for the purpose of tissue-specific expression analysis.

  Seeds of Nicotiana benthamiana were stored in our laboratory. Prior to sowing, the seeds were soaked in a solution containing 200 mg/L of gibberellin for a duration of 6 hours and subsequently planted in pots. Upon reaching the stage of possessing four true leaves, the plants were transplanted into smaller pots. Tobacco plants that had attained approximately 4 weeks of growth were then utilized for infection experiments.


### Transcriptome sequencing analysis method
  6 bottles of hairy roots of B.chinense were cultivated for a duration of 6 months and subsequently divided into two groups: a control group and a treatment group exposed to 100 μmol/L ABA. Following the addition of ABA, the treated group was returned to the shaker. 24 hours later, samples were collected and stored at  −80°C for further analysis. RNA was extracted from these samples, and its purity was assessed using the Nanodrop spectrophotometer. Additionally, the RNA integrity number (RIN) and the 28S/18S rRNA ratio were evaluated using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer to ensure RNA quality. Subsequent to quality control assessment, the RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA, and libraries of various fragment sizes, both sorted and unsorted, were constructed. High-quality raw sequencing data for transcriptome analysis were obtained by performing full-length cDNA sequencing using the third-generation sequencing platform, PacBio Sequel. The transcriptome sequencing and assembly processes were carried out by Annoroad Gene Technology Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China), as previously described.37,38

  GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using R scripts. GO is structured into three main categories: molecular function, biological process, and cellular component. Through ID correspondence or sequence annotation, genes or proteins can be associated with corresponding GO terms, which denote functional categories or cellular locations. KEGG was employed to annotate the identified genes at the level of biological pathways. Heatmaps were generated utilizing the R package “Heatmap” for visualization.

  The FPKM value for each gene was computed using RSEM to quantify the level of gene expression. Differential expression analysis was conducted using the EdgeR package, with genes exhibiting an absolute log2(fold change) greater than 1 being designated as DEGs. A volcano plot illustrating the distribution of these DEGs was generated using R software.


### Gene cloning and bioinformatics analysis
  Utilizing the full-length information of the screened gene sequences, we designed specific primers using Primer 5.0 software. Full-length PCR amplification was carried out with cDNA derived from the hairy roots of B.chinense as the template. The gene sequences of these target bands were outsourced to Ruibiotech Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China) and then confirmed. The sequences of the full-length specific primers employed for cloning are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

  In bioinformatics analysis, the online tool Protparam (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/) was used to analyze the physicochemical properties of proteins. SOPMA (https://npsa-prabi.ibcp.fr/cgi-bin/npsa_automat.pl?page=/NPSA/npsa_sopma.html) was employed for predicting protein secondary structures. InterProScan (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/search/sequence/) was utilized to search for functional sites within proteins. WOLFPSORT (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/) was applied for predicting the subcellular localization of proteins. The MEME suit tool (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme) was used for the analysis of protein domains and motifs. TB tools II and GraphPad Prism 8.0 were utilized for generating heatmaps or bar charts.

  NCBI blastp (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) was utilized to search for homologous protein sequences across different species. Multiple sequence alignments were conducted using MEGA 7 software, employing the Neighbor-Joining method for constructing the phylogenetic tree, with the bootstrap replications set to 1000. The visualization and aesthetic enhancement of the phylogenetic tree were then performed using iTOLs (https://itol.embl.de/).


### Ultra performance liquid chromatography
  The hairy roots were divided into three groups for ABA39 induction experiments. Group 0 served as the control, Group 100 was treated with 100 μmol/L ABA, and Group 200 was treated with 200 μmol/L ABA. Samples were collected at 0, 24, 72 and 168 hours. 5 mL of a 5% ammonia-methanol solution was added to each sample, followed by ultrasonic extraction for 30 minutes. The resultant extract was then filtered under vacuum to obtain the filtrate. The solvent was subsequently evaporated using a rotary evaporator, and the residue was reconstituted with 1 mL of methanol to achieve a constant volume. This reconstituted solution was transferred to a liquid chromatography vial. The quantification of SSs was performed using a 1260 Infinity II UPLC system (Agilent, USA). The quantification standard curve and sampling method are provided in Supplementary Table S3.


### Quantitative real-time PCR
  Total RNA was extracted from ABA-treated hairy roots harvested at 0, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 72 hours or from fresh tissues of B.chinense using the Eastep Super Total RNA Extraction Kit (Promega, USA). The extracted RNA was then reverse-transcribed into cDNA utilizing the FastKing RT Kit (Tiangen, China). Specific primers were designed using Primer 5.0 software, based on the nucleotide sequences of BcbHLHs, ADF5,40 HMGR41 (GenBank Accession Number EU400217), IPPI (GenBank Accession Number EU400218), FPS (GenBank Accession Number EU400219), and β-AS (GenBank Accession Number EU400200). The sequences of these qRT-PCR primers, which were synthesized by Ruibiotech Company (Beijing, China), are provided in Supplementary Table S4A. The ADF5 gene served as an internal reference for relative quantitation, and the cDNA was diluted 10-fold with sterile water prior to use as the template. qRT-PCR was conducted using KAPA SYBR FAST Universal on a Quant Studio 5 instrument. Gene expression levels were quantified based on cycle threshold (CT) values, and relative gene expression was analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCT method.42,43 The qRT-PCR reaction program is also detailed in Supplementary Table S4B.


### Construction of overexpression vector and subcellular localization
  The amplified target fragments were ligated to the pCambia130044 vector, which had been previously double-digested with BamHI and XbaI, using a seamless cloning kit. The design of the seamless cloning primers is provided in detail in Supplementary Table S5. Subsequently, the overexpression vector, named pCambia1300-GFP-BcbHLHs, was successfully obtained. The plasmid of this overexpression vector was verified by PCR in bacterial solution. Positive bacterial cultures were then selected, and plasmids were extracted for sequencing. Following sequencing confirmation, the recombinant vector pCambia1300-BcbHLHs was transformed into Agrobacterium rhizogenes GV3101 competent cells using the heat shock method. Verification of the transformed cells was conducted again by PCR in bacterial liquid.

  The lower epidermis of the tobacco leaves was infected with the bacterial suspension. Nuclear localization was assessed through DAPI staining, and the infected leaves were observed and photographed using a confocal fluorescence microscope (Nikon Optics, Japan). at a magnification of 400  ×  .


### Transcriptional activation analysis
  The MYC_N domains of BcbHLH3 and BcbHLH4 were cloned into the EcoRI and BamHI double-digested and linearized pGBKT7 vector, resulting in the construction of the pGBKT7-BcbHLH recombinant plasmids. The sequences of homologous primers used for this cloning are provided in Supplementary Table S6. Subsequently, the pGBKT7-BcbHLH constructs were transformed into Y2HGold yeast cells. As controls, the pGBKT7 Empty Vector was transformed into Y2HGold yeast cells as a negative control, while pGBKT7–53 and pGADT7-T were co-transformed into Y2HGold yeast cells to serve as a positive control. The transformed yeast colonies were then screened on SD/-Trp( − Leu), SD/-Trp( − Leu)/ + X-α-gal, and SD/-Trp( − Leu)/ + X-α-gal + AbA plates.


### Protein homology modeling and molecular docking
  The C-terminal binding domain protein sequence of BcbHLH and the DNA sequence of the G-box (5’-CACGTG-3’) were submitted to AlphaFold 3 (https://golgi.sandbox.google.com/) for modeling to predict the conformation of the double-stranded protein-DNA complex. Subsequently, Pymol 2.4.0 was utilized to visualize the hydrogen bonds at the binding interface. DNAMAN was employed for multiple sequence alignment of the binding domain proteins. Following previously established methods,45 the Rotamer tool within UCSF Chimera 1.18 was used to obtain the three-dimensional structure of the protein with mutated key amino acid residues. Rigid docking of the mutated protein with the G-box was then performed using the Genetic Algorithm (GA) implemented in Autodock 4.2, and the binding energy values for each docking result were subsequently output. GraphPad Prism 8.0 was utilized for the visualization and comparison of bar graphs depicting these binding energy values.


### Statistical analysis
  All data presented in this study are expressed as the mean ± standard error (SEM) from three independent replicates. The UPLC chromatographic spectra were generated using Origin 2018 by extracting and plotting the instrumental data. Statistical analysis of the UPLC and qRT-PCR measurement results was performed using SPSS 25.0 software. Multi-group comparisons (≥3 groups) were conducted via ANOVA with post hoc Duncan’s multiple range test, while pairwise analyses between two groups employed two-tailed independent Student’s t-tests. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 for all hypothesis tests. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software.


## Results

### Transcriptome sequencing and analysis
  The Pacbio HiFi platform was used to conduct high-throughput transcriptome sequencing of hairy roots subjected to a 24-hour ABA intervention. Simultaneously, hairy roots without treatment were used as controls (Figure 1a). Comparative analysis of the differential gene transcription profiles was then conducted between the ABA-induced group and the control group. In the volcano plot of differential genes (Figure 1b), the ABA-induced group exhibited a higher number of upregulated genes (2229) compared to the control group, along with a smaller number of downregulated genes (1148). The relative changes in log2FC expression of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were concentrated within the range of  −5 to 10, and the significantly upregulated and downregulated genes accounting for 18.21% (406) and 9.06% (104) respectively. It suggests that more genes were activated in the hairy roots after exogenous ABA stimulation.   For the Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of DEGs, the six terms with the highest confidence level (adjusted p value) are selected from each individual GO category and presented as shown in Figure 1c. In the cellular component pathway, more downregulated genes than upregulated genes were annotated in membrane, organelle, and cell part. In the biological process pathway, the top two pathways for DEGs included cellular process and metabolic process, with a higher proportion of downregulated genes. For the two pathways of response to stimulus and biological regulation, which account for the second highest proportion of DEGs, conversely, there are more upregulated genes than downregulated genes. This implies ABA stimulation caused more genes to be involved in upstream phytohormone signaling and downstream biological response regulation. In the molecular function pathway, DEGs with catalytic activity, structural molecular activity and transcription regulator activity were particularly noteworthy during the screening process, as they may serve as regulatory factors influencing the biosynthesis of saikosaponins through the action of ABA.

  In the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis diagram (Figure 1d), the ABA-induced group had 14 annotated pathways with enrichment rates mostly ranging from 2 to 4. The majority of the red hues in the ABA-induced group were more concentrated in the plant hormone signal transduction, monoterpenoid biosynthesis, and flavonoid biosynthesis pathways. This suggests that the gene activity in hormone response and biosynthesis pathways of hairy roots may have changed after ABA induction.

  To specifically observe the DEGs related to the biosynthetic pathway induced by ABA, a schematic diagram of the SS synthesis pathway is depicted in Figure 1e. From the transcriptome annotation information, 9 significant DEGs upstream and midstream of the synthesis pathway were screened, including 3 HMGRs, 2 IPPIs, 3 FPSs, and one β-AS. Upon ABA stimulation, the transcription abundance of 2 IPPIs increased, while the expression of the remaining 7 genes was downregulated.


### Identification of differentially expressed bHLH genes in ABA-induced transcriptome
  Within the transcriptome, a total of 44 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) belonging to the bHLH gene family were identified. Among these, 18 DEGs were successfully cloned into full-length sequences, as detailed in Supplementary Table S7. These cloned genes were named sequentially based on their cloning order, from BcbHLH1 to BcbHLH20 (note that BcbHLH2 and BcbHLH4 were previously cloned from B.chinense). Similar to the biosynthetic pathway genes, most bHLH genes are downregulated in the ABA-induced group, except for BcbHLH15, BcbHLH16, and BcbHLH18 (Figure 2a).   We examined the phylogenetic relationships among bHLH TFs by generating a phylogenetic tree using the neighbor-joining method (Figure 2b). The tree divided into two groups, with the MYC subfamily encompassing MYC-like protein sequences from Catharanthus roseus, Solanum lycopersicum, Camellia sinensis, and other species, while the other subfamily branches included 24 Arabidopsis and 4 Rice bHLH TFs. BcbHLH1-BcbHLH6 are clustered in the MYC subfamily. BcbHLH7-BcbHLH20 exhibits a close relationship with bHLH proteins in A.thaliana, which are distributed across various subfamilies and exhibit diverse functional roles. As shown in Figure 2c, all the 20 BcbHLH proteins contain a highly conserved HLH_binding domain at their C-termini.

  The physicochemical properties of 20 BcbHLH proteins are predicted (Figure 2d). The number of amino acids encoded ranged from 132 to 643. The theoretical isoelectric points varied between 4.55 and 11.61, with instability indices ranging from 42.27 to 65.15 and hydrophilicity coefficients spanning from  −0.964 to  −0.392. All the bHLH were unstable proteins, but predicted to show good solubility in water. Among the protein secondary structure of 20 BcbHLHs, α-helix and irregular curl account for the largest proportion (Figure 2e). The largest proportion of α-helix was observed in BcbHLH1 at 52.38%, while the smallest proportion of irregular curl was also found in BcbHLH1 at 35.93%. Conversely, BcbHLH20 had the lowest proportion of α-helix (20.11%) and the highest proportion of random coil (69.41%).


### Exogenous ABA induction leads to decrease of saikosaponins in hairy roots
  To evaluate the impact of ABA intervention on the accumulation of SSs in hairy roots, we analyzed the production of SSa, SSc, and SSd following exposure to 100 or 200 μmol/L ABA for 0–7 days (Figure 3). Compared to the control group, a concentration of 100 μmol/L ABA was insufficient to cause a significant decrease in SS levels during the first 3 days of intervention. However, the accumulation of all three SSs significantly decreased after continuous stimulation for 7 days. The content of SSa initially increased and then decreased, reaching 0.12 mg/g after 7 days, which was 0.55 times that of the control group. The content of SSc decreased to 0.06 mg/g, representing 0.13 times that of the control group, while the content of SSd decreased to 0.06 mg/kg, which was 0.11 times the control. In the group induced with 200 μmol/L ABA, the total amount of SSs showed a downward trend compared to the control group. After 7 days of induction, the content of SSa decreased to 0.09 mg/g, which was 0.41 times that of the control group. Similarly, the content of SSc decreased to 0.19 mg/g, representing 0.48 times the control, and the content of SSd decreased to 0.34 mg/g, which was 0.60 times the control. Notably, a slight increase in the content of all three SSs was observed in the blank group at 7 days, which we speculate may be related to metabolic hyperactivity induced by the replacement of the culture medium. In general, the SS content induced by different concentrations of ABA exhibited an overall decreasing trend. Specifically, at the 7-day time point, the decline in saikosaponin content was more pronounced in the 100 μmol/L ABA-induced group compared to the 200 μmol/L ABA-induced group. ### BcbHLHs and saikosaponin biosynthesis genes showed expression correlation under ABA induction
  Based on the results depicted in Figure 3, we focused our investigation on the gene expression levels at a concentration of 100 μmol/L ABA. As illustrated in Figure 4a, we observed the most significant change in the expression level of BcHMGR, which gradually increased from 0 to 12 hours, peaking at 12 hours with a 7.45-fold increase compared to the control group. The expression level of BcBAS gradually rose within 0–6 hours, reaching a peak at 6 hours (2.22 times that of the control group), and was lowest at 24 hours. The expression levels of BcIPPI and BcFPS genes exhibited slight fluctuations, with a gradual decrease in gene expression as the stimulation time extended, dropping to 0.45 times and 0.51 times the levels of the control group, respectively after 72 hours. (c): Expression correlation heatmap between saikosaponin biosynthetic genes and BcbHLHs. Numbers in each color block indicate the r-value of Pearson’s coefffcient (varying from −1 to 1); *P < 0.05.

  Among the 6 BcbHLH genes examined, only BcbHLH1-BcbHLH4 exhibited a response to ABA intervention (Figure 4b), while the expression levels of BcbHLH5 and BcbHLH6 remained unchanged compared to the control group (Supplementary Figure S2). The BcbHLH1 gene reached its peak expression level at the start of ABA treatment, which was 3.15 times that of the control group, and then gradually decreased over time. The BcbHLH2 and BcbHLH4 genes peaked at 12 hours and 6 hours, respectively, with expression levels of 2.01 times and 2.18 times the control group. Subsequently, the expression of BcbHLH3 gene gradually decreased over time, peaking at 3 hours after ABA treatment but only reaching 1.20 times the control level, before decreasing further with time.

  We integrated the expression patterns of four biosynthetic genes and four BcbHLH genes, and calculated the Pearson’s coefficients to analyze the correlation (Figure 4c). The expression patterns of BcbHLH1 and BcHMGR were consistent, with upregulation within 12 h followed by a significant decline. Similarly, BcbHLH4 and BcBAS peaked in expression 6 h after ABA induction and then dropped to a trough at 24 h (all r > 0.6). However, only BcbHLH1 and BcBAS exhibited a slight negative correlation (r =  −0.4). Notably, among these correlations, only BcbHLH4 and BcBAS displayed significant co-expression in temporal dimensions.


### Tissue specificity and subcellular localization of BcbHLH
  To further investigate the functional locales of these four crucial bHLH proteins in the native plant and identify their potential regulatory roles, we evaluated the tissue and subcellular distribution of BcbHLH (Figure 5). The four TF genes were expressed in multiple plant tissues, albeit with differential distributions. Notably, all BcbHLH isoforms exhibited the highest abundance in stems and tender leaves, albeit BcbHLH2 and BcbHLH3 demonstrated even higher expression levels in tender leaves. Only BcbHLH2 showed a slightly elevated expression in roots, whereas in reality, the expression levels of four bHLH genes, were comparatively low in both roots and inflorescences. Collectively, the expression of BcbHLH1-BcbHLH4 was enriched in the vegetative, non-flowering portions of the plant.   According to the prediction results from WoLFPSORT, all BcbHLH proteins are localized within the cell nucleus (Supplementary Table S8). To verify the intracellular localization of BcbHLH proteins, we introduced the full-length CDS of BcbHLH1-BcbHLH4 into the CaMV-35S: EGFP vector. Transient overexpression of BcbHLH-GFP fusion proteins was conducted in N.benthamiana leaves. As shown in Figure 5b, the empty green fluorescent pCambia1300-GFP vector was concentrated in the cell nucleus and somewhat dispersed in the cytoplasm. The fluorescent vectors fused with BcbHLH genes indicated that the green fluorescence was concentrated at the position of the nuclear marker DAPI, with no significant dispersion observed. The subcellular localization of the 4 bHLH TFs was expressed in the cell nucleus, consistent with the predictions from bioinformatics analysis.


### Binding activity prediction of MYC_N TAD and HLH_binding domain in BcbHLHs
  To evaluate the binding activities of the conserved domains within four key bHLH TFs, the positions of conserved domains were predicted and displayed in Figure 6a. All 4 bHLH proteins contain an HLH binding domain approximately 97–122 amino acids in length at the C-terminal. However, only BcbHLH3 and BcbHLH4 possess the MYC_N domain, as the sequences of BcbHLH1 or BcbHLH2 are relatively shorter, potentially indicating an N-terminal truncation.   The MYC_N domain encompasses approximately 180 amino acids, each containing a JID motif and a transcription activation domain (TAD), which are functional in JAZ protein interaction and transcription activation, respectively. Y2H activation assay was used to verify whether TAD has transcriptional activation activity (Figure 6b). The N-terminal domain fragments enclosed in the red frame of Figure 6a were inserted into the downstream of GAL4-BD in the pGBKT7 vector. It was found that 100 ng/ml of aureobasidin A (AbA) could not effectively inhibit the growth of cells expressing BcbHLH3N and BcbHLH4N, with suppression only occurring at 250 ng/ml. The vigorous growth of the positive control strains confirmed the reliability of the method. It suggests that the N-terminal TADs of BcbHLH3 and BcbHLH4 possess intrinsic transcription activation activity.

  We then simulate the binding conformation of BcbHLH dimers with the G-box DNA double helix. At the AlphaFold 3 output interface, the iPTM/PTM scores of the 4 protein complexes were at a high level (Supplementary Figure S3). Figures 6c-d provide insights into the key amino acid residues with the positions of polar hydrogen bonds in binding pocket. Arginine at 11th located within the basic motif region, forms the highest number of hydrogen bonds (approximately 2.8) within a 5Å range with the G-box. We simulated single-point mutations to alanine, isoleucine, and histidine with tool Chimera 1.18. Subsequently, rigid docking of the mutated protein pockets with the G-box was re-performed using Autodock (Figure 6e). The results showed similar changes occurred in the binding energy of 4 hLH_binding domains after R11 mutation. Mutations of R11 to alanine or isoleucine led to decreased binding energies, whereas mutation to histidine was unfavorable for spontaneous binding of the pocket to the G-box.


## Discussion
  In recent years, numerous transcriptome and metabolome studies have been conducted to elucidate the regulatory genes involved in the synthesis of active compounds in Bupleurum.16,46–52 However, there has been limited exploration into the key regulatory factors influencing the synthesis of saikosaponins induced by ABA. In this study, a concentration of 100 μmol/L was chosen as the effective ABA treatment concentration, as it exhibited a more pronounced decrease in saikosaponin content compared to the 200 μmol/L treatment. Subsequent transcriptome sequencing of the hairy roots one day post-ABA treatment revealed a total of 3,377 significantly DEGs compared to the control group, with successful annotation of 24,950 unigenes. These genes were annotated to have functions related to catalytic activity, binding activity, and transcription regulator activity, participating in metabolic processes, hormone stimulus responses, and biological regulatory processes. Enrichment analysis of KEGG pathways identified significant involvement in plant hormone signal transduction and biosynthesis pathways. This indicates that ABA regulation is intricate and encompasses multiple pathways and numerous regulatory factors, necessitating further focused investigation into the components regulating SSs metabolism.

  Triterpenoid compounds in plants are derived from the MVA/MEP pathways.53,54 For saikosaponins, various structural synthesis or modification genes in the MVA pathway collectively determine the rate of their skeletal construction, a process that typically occurs in the cytoplasm.13 HMGR serves as the first rate-limiting enzyme in the MVA pathway, determining the production of the upstream precursor MVA.55 IPPI and FPS are involved in integrating the precursors IPP and DMAPP from both the MVA and MEP pathways to generate the crucial intermediate FPP, controlling the transition rate from upstream to midstream processes.56,57 β-AS,58 an important Oxidosqualene Cyclases (OSC) enzyme in the midstream synthesis of SS, is responsible for the final step in triterpene skeleton construction of β-amyrin, which determines the structural diversity of SS. Previously, we discovered a BcbAS1 gene with the ability to catalyze the conversion of 2,3-oxidosqualene into β-amyrin in B.chinense.59 In this study, we identified 9 biosynthesis enzyme genes and 19 bHLH TF genes responsive to ABA among all the DEGs. We suspected that these genes may be involved in ABA signal response and transduction, holding significant importance for identifying regulatory factors that affect saikosaponin accumulation.

  Next, we performed sequence alignment of these 20 bHLH TFs. All the proteins were found to contain an HLH_binding domain at their C-terminus. A leucine residue at 24th within this domain is highly conserved, where it is closely with reports in Wheat and Arabidopsis.60,61 It may play an important role in the interaction between protein dimerization and other transcription factors. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that BcbHLH1-BcbHLH6 are closely related to MYC proteins from S.lycopersicum, C.roseus, and other species, suggesting their potential involvement in JA and ABA signaling pathways. Furthermore, the tertiary structural framework, predominantly composed of α-helix, determines the activity of the DNA-binding recognition pocket in MYC2.62 We observed an increased ratio of α-helix in the secondary structures of the 6 MYC subfamily proteins (Figure 2d), which may indicate their significant role in maintaining the structural stability of the binding site. The above information is conducive to further exploring the relationship between MYC protein structure and interaction function.

  In medicinal plants, ABA can regulate the secondary metabolism of various natural products, such as flavonoids, terpenoids, and alkaloid.63–66 In this study, interventions with either 100 or 200 μmol/L of ABA for 7 days both resulted in a significant decrease in the content of SSs. This finding aligns with previous research, the exogenous application of ABA to 2-year-old B.chinense plants under drought stress effectively decreased the accumulation of SSs within 7 days, potentially linked to the inhibition of biosynthetic genes.32 Similarly, in Lycoris aurea, it has been reported that 100 μmol/L of ABA can reduce the expression of LaOMT1, leading to decreased alkaloid production.67 Exogenous ABA treatment was found to inhibit the expression of JA biosynthesis and signal transduction genes, decreasing JA levels.68,69 We assume that ABA may inhibit the expression of certain JA pathway genes, leading to reduced downstream SS synthesis. To validate our hypothesis, we assessed gene expression patterns and found that 3 genes of SS synthesis, BcIPPI, BcFPS, and BcBAS, exhibited varying degrees of downregulation within 1–3 days after ABA intervention. The expression impact of BcHMGR seemed even greater. A similar phenomenon was also observed in A.annua, where ABA stimulation could affect the upstream enzyme genes of the monoterpene synthesis pathway, leading to the accumulation of artemisinin.70 Almost all of the bHLH and biosynthetic genes are highly expressed within 12 h but downregulated after 24 h. This fluctuation may be related to some form of metabolic negative feedback,71,72 but the details await further characterization. The decline in the expression levels of all genes preceded the occurrence of SS, potentially linked to the long-term cumulative impact of alterations in SS content. Overall, we focused on four MYC family genes, BcbHLH1-BcbHLH4, which may respond to ABA stimulation, showing synergism with SS synthesis genes and SS accumulation. It is suggested that ABA may affect SS accumulation through JA signal crosstalk.

  Utilizing RT-qPCR, we demonstrated that their transcription levels were highest in stems and tender leaves, consistent with reports in Wheat, Arabidopsis, and Tamarix hispida.73–75 According to the report,34 nearly all MYC proteins reside within the nucleus, attributed to the presence of three or more nuclear localization signal regions within their amino acid sequences. Subcellular localization indicated that the four MYC proteins co-localized with the GFP fusion protein in the nucleus, confirming our previous predictions and suggesting that these four proteins may possess nuclear gene binding ability. The MYC_N domain contains a JID and a TAD, which facilitate interactions with JAZ proteins and recruit transcription initiation complexes for MED25 protein binding.76 In yeast cells, BcbHLH3N and BcbHLH4N were able to activate the reporter genes MEL1 and AbAr, resulting in the formation of blue positive clones on plates. Our findings indicate that BcbHLH3-BcbHLH4 are nuclear proteins with transcriptional activation ability.

  Unfortunately, the public availability of whole-genome HiFi sequencing data for B.chinense remains elusive, thereby hindering our access to the promoter sequences of genes implicated in the biosynthetic pathway of SS. It is another feasible option to predict the C-terminal binding domain function of 4 MYC proteins by molecular modeling. MYC proteins frequently dimerize, encapsulating the DNA strand within a pocket to facilitate cis-element recognition.34,77 To this end, we employed AlphaFold 3,78 a sophisticated platform leveraging homology modeling for high-accuracy predictions of protein-nucleic acid three-dimensional structures and interactions, to simulate the in vitro binding of 4 hLH_binding domain dimers of MYC proteins with the G-box. Notably, the R11 main chain, terminating in a guanidine group, extended into the binding pocket nestled within the α-helix of the basic region, forming the most stable hydrogen bond interaction with the G-box. Among the three mutants (R11A, R11I, and R11H), R11A and R11I exhibited decreased binding energies compared to the wild type (WT), whereas R11H displayed an opposite trend. Referring to previous studies,79 we hypothesize that these mutations disrupted the inherent hydrogen bond network, subsequently altering the hydrophilicity/hydrophobicity balance and tertiary structure conformation within the binding pocket. However, robust validation of this hypothesis awaits further evidence, and future studies may use molecular dynamics to simulate conformational changes. Above all, the aforementioned predictions provide insights into the downstream DNA interactions of the 4 bHLH proteins that are responsive to ABA signaling in B.chinense.

  In summary, we have identified four bHLH TFs associated with saikosaponin biosynthesis that exhibit responsiveness to ABA induction. Intriguingly, the induction of ABA in hairy roots results in a concurrent decrease in both SSs content and the expression of their biosynthetic genes, findings that are consistent with observations made in whole plants.32 This suggests that the roots of B.chinense may constitute direct targets of ABA stimulation, and increased environmental concentrations of ABA frequently exert detrimental effects on SS synthesis. This phenomenon may be linked to the regulatory function of bHLH transcription factors within the roots. However, current research cannot support the direct involvement of these bHLH TFs in regulating the biosynthetic pathway of SS. Future studies will endeavor to elucidate the binding mechanisms of these bHLH TFs to the promoters of biosynthetic genes. This will involve, but is not limited to, obtaining full-length promoter sequences, conducting yeast one-hybrid, dual luciferase, and EMSA experiments, as well as performing overexpression and silencing studies of BcbHLHs in hairy roots. Our objective is to mitigate ABA accumulation or augment biosynthetic gene expression in the roots of B.chinense through genetic transformation and molecular interaction strategies, which holds profound implications for achieving targeted synthesis of SSs in the native plant.


## Conclusion
  In this study, we performed a comparative transcriptome sequencing analysis on hairy roots of B.chinense, resulting in the identification of four MYC family transcription factors, specifically BcbHLH1 to BcbHLH4. These TFs may respond to ABA stimulation signals and influence the in vivo biosynthesis of saikosaponin. Notably, these TFs are nuclear proteins that are highly expressed in stems and tender leaves, with BcbHLH3 and BcbHLH4 exhibiting transcriptional activation activity within their MYC-N domains. Our predictions suggest that the 11th arginine residue may plays a crucial role in the binding of the HLH domain to the G-box. These findings significantly enhance our understanding of the functional roles of bHLH in Bupleurum chinense DC. and introduce new candidate genes for saikosaponin biosynthesis within the ABA signaling regulatory network.
