# Repurposing of epalrestat for neuroprotection in parkinson’s disease via activation of the KEAP1/Nrf2 pathway


## Background
  Epalrestat (EPS), an aldose reductase inhibitor, is used to alleviate peripheral nerve disorder of diabetic patients in clinical therapy. Even though EPS exerted effects in central nervous system diseases, the neuroprotection and underlying molecular mechanism in neurodegenerative diseases, especially Parkinson’s disease (PD), remains obscure. Our study aimed to investigate the potential of EPS suppressed PD progression both in vivo and in vitro.


## Methods
  We used 1-methyl-4-phenylpyridillium ion (MPP+)-treated PD cells and 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP)-treated PD mice to investigate the protective function and molecular mechanism of EPS in PD. EPS was administered three times daily through oral route 3 days before model establishment for 5 consecutive days. Behavioral manifestation of mice was conducted using open field test, rotarod test and CatWalk gait analysis. Immunofluorescence was used to detect dopaminergic (DAergic) neurons survival in the substantia nigra. Subsequently, oxidative stress, mitochondrial function and KEAP1/Nrf2 signaling pathway in PD models were detected through molecular biology methods to assess the effect and downstream mechanisms of EPS on PD. Molecular docking, surface plasmon resonance and cellular thermal shift assay were used to verify the direct binding of EPS and KEAP1.


## Results
  We found that EPS exhibited potent antiparkinsonian activity in PD models both in vivo and in vitro. PD models treated with EPS manifested alleviated oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction. Furthermore, we found EPS activated the Nrf2 signaling pathway which contributed to DAergic neurons survival in PD models. Particularly, we firstly confirmed that EPS competitively binds to KEAP1 and enhanced its degradation, thereby activating the Nrf2 signaling pathway.


## Conclusions
  Collectively, EPS attenuates oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction by directly binding KEAP1 to activate the KEAP1/Nrf2 signaling pathway, further reducing DAergic neurons damage. These findings suggest that EPS has great potential to become a therapeutic for PD as a clinically effective and safe medicine.

## Introduction
  Parkinson’s disease (PD), a common chronic neurodegenerative disease defined a clinical syndrome including motor and non-motor symptoms, is characterized by progressive neuronal degeneration of dopaminergic (DAergic) neurons and misfolded synuclein deposition in the substantia nigra (SN) [1]. With the deepening of the aging population globally, the prevalence of PD is also rising year by year. In 2019, over 8.5 million people worldwide suffered from PD, resulting in a continuous increase in disability adjusted life years [2]. Presently, conception into the pathogenic mechanisms of PD have been stemmed from genetics and molecular pathology. Although significant progress has been considered in the pathogenesis of PD, effective therapeutic treatments are still relatively limited. Clinically, the treatment of PD mainly alleviates symptoms through dopamine (DA) replacement strategies, but lacks medicines targeted neuroprotective or disease-modifying function. In addition, the efficacy is restricted to the side effects after long-term administration [3]. Accordingly, it is imminent to discover new disease modifying drugs to ameliorate the current therapeutic condition of PD.

  Epalrestat (EPS), an aldose reductase inhibitor, has been received approval for use to alleviate peripheral nerve disorder in diabetic patients by decreasing intracellular sorbitol accumulation in Japan, China, and India [4–6]. Researchers also validated that aldose reductase inhibitors play an anti-inflammatory role in the treatment of diabetes peripheral neuropathy [7]. Previous studies on EPS concentrated on diabetic complications, multiple inflammation related diseases and cancer [8–12]. Furthermore, researchers found that EPS increased glutathione (GSH) levels by upregulating nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor (Nrf2) in bovine aortic endothelial cells [13]. The KEAP1/Nrf2 signaling pathway, as one of the main neuroprotective mechanisms, is considered an important regulator of antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and mitochondrial function under stress conditions, which play important roles in the damage of DAergic neurons in PD [14–18]. It has been reported that EPS has antioxidant protective effects on PD cell models [19, 20]. However, there is limited data on the potential mechanism and influence of EPS in protection of DAergic neurons in PD.

  In the present research, we implemented in vivo and in vitro research to explore the function of EPS in PD pathology and progression. Our further research suggested that EPS promoted neuroprotection in PD, manifested by its antioxidant properties and protects mitochondrial function and activating the endogenous defense system to alleviate oxidative damage. Of note, we revealed that EPS improves PD by binding KEAP1 to activate Nrf2 signaling pathway.


## Materials and methods

### Cell culture, drug administration and transfection
  In vitro assays were performed in Neuro-2a (N2a, mouse neuroblastoma) cell line acquired from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (Shanghai, CN). Cells were cultivated in the Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Hyclone, Logan, Utah, US) interpolated into 10% fetal bovine serum (Transerum, Beijing, CN) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Solarbio, Beijing, CN) in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator.

  Cells were exposed with various concentrations of EPS (APExBIO, Cat# B1743, Houston, Texas, US) for 4 h and then cultured with 1-methyl-4-phenylpyridillium ion (MPP+, Sigma Aldrich, Cat# D048, US) for another 24 h. In the experiments using Nrf2 inhibitors, cells were pre-treated with 4 μM ML385 (MedChemExpress, Cat# HY-100523, NJ, US) for 4 h. In protein degradation experiment, cells were incubated with 100 μg/mL cycloheximide (CHX, MedChemExpress, Cat# HY-12320, NJ, US) for 3, 6 and 9 h.

  The small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting Nrf2 (siNrf2 sequences: #1, AGACAUAGAUCUUGGAGU-AAGTT; #2, CCCGAAUUACAGUGUCUUAAUTT; #3, GCCUCUGUCACCAGCUCAAGGTT) were transfected into cells with lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Invitrogen, Cat# 11,668,019, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the specification.

  KEAP1 and KEAP1 mutant (S363A/A414A/S555A/S602A) were ligated into the pcDNA3.1–3 × Flag-C vector (Table S1). The plasmids were transfected into cells with lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, Cat# L3000015, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the specification.


### Cell viability, cytotoxicity and apoptosis assay
  Cell viability was detected using the cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8, APExBIO, Cat# K1018, Houston, Texas, US) assay. Cells were seeded at a density of 6 × 103 cells per well in 96-well plates. After the drug intervention, priming media was discarded and basic medium comprising 10% CCK-8 reagent was supplemented and incubated at 95% air and 5% CO2 and 37 °C for 2 h. The light absorption value at 450 nm was detected by means of a multimode plate reader (PerkinElmer VICTOR Nivo, MA, USA). Cell survival rate was presented as a percentage of compared to the control group value. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) emission of cells was detected using the LDH kit (Beyotime, Cat# C0016, Shanghai, CN) after drug treatment, according to the instruction manual. The light absorption value was detected using a multimode plate reader at 490 nm. The result was represented as the fold-change of LDH compared to the control cells.

  Apoptosis cells were assessed by flow cytometric analysis. The cells were varnished with Annexin V/PI as specification (FITC Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit I, BD Pharmingen, Cat# 556,547, Franklin, NJ, US) and then determined by flow cytometer (CytoFLEX S, Beckman Coulter, CA, US).


### Lipid peroxidation, mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS), mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) and intracellular calcium concentration measurement
  Intracellular lipid peroxidation generated after treatments were measured by C11-BODIPY581/591 and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels. N2a cells were cultured with 10 μM C11-BODIPY581/591 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# D3861, Waltham, MA, US) for the last 30 min at 37 °C incubator, flushed with 0.01 mol/L PBS, and measured fluorescence intensity using flow cytometry under the FITC channel. The MDA level was measured using the MDA Assay Reagent Kit (Beyotime, Cat# S0131S, Shanghai, CN) according to the instruction manual. The results were performed as the fold-change of MDA compared to the control group.

  Mitochondrial ROS was detected using a fluorescent dye mitoSOX Red indicator targeting mitochondrial superoxide in living cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# M36008, Waltham, MA, US). N2a cells were cultivated in 5 μM mitoSOX Red for the last 30 min at 37 °C incubator, flushed with 0.01 mol/L PBS, and measured fluorescence intensity using flow cytometry under the FITC/PE channel. The transition from red fluorescence to green fluorescence of JC-1 (Solarbio, Cat# M8650, Beijing, CN) detected by flow cytometry was used to represent the changes in Δψm.

  Intracellular calcium concentration was measured by flow cytometry using a Fluo-4 AM fluorescent probe (Beyotime, Cat# S1061S, Shanghai, CN). Fluo-4 AM enters the cell and undergoes hydrolysis by intracellular lactonase, producing Fluo-4 that binds with calcium ions and emits green fluorescence measured flow cytometry. Fluorescence intensity in the FITC represented the concentration of intracellular calcium.


### Animals, treatment and delivery of viruses
  C57BL/6 mice (Male, 8–9 weeks old, 22–25 g) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, CN) and placed in specific pathogen-free environment at 23–25 °C with12/12 h light/dark cycle, offering regular diet. All mice experiments were performed following international guidelines and conducted in accordance with the Animal Ethics Committee of Qingdao University (QDU-AEC-2024766).

  Mice were injected intraperitoneally with 25 mg/kg 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP, Sigma Aldrich, Cat# M0896, US) solutions every day for 5 consecutive days to construct a subacute PD model. 25 mg/kg, 50 mg/kg and 100 mg/kg EPS (Yangtze River Pharmaceutical Group, Cat# H20040012, Taizhou, Jiangsu, CN) were administered three times daily through oral route 3 days before model establishment for 8 consecutive days. 60 mg/kg Madopar (Shanghai Roche Pharmaceuticals Ltd., Cat# H10930198, shanghai, CN) and 0.1 mg/kg Rasagiline mesylate (MedChemExpress, Cat# HY-14605, NJ, US) were administered once daily as positive control groups. Control group mice were orally given an equivalent volume of physiological saline solution. Under the condition of ensuring the same number of mice in each group, we used no less than 6–12 mice for behavioral testing. Subsequently, the mouse brain tissue was collected and divided into half brain tissues for molecular biology experiments. 6–8 samples of SN tissue were preformed to immunofluorescence detection, 6–12 samples of SN tissue were preformed to immunoblotting, ELISA, MDA, and ATP detection, and 12 samples of striatum tissue were preformed to DA, DOPAC, and HVA detection. The samples for immunoblotting, ELISA, MDA, and ATP detection were all SN region tissue protein extracts and normalized to the protein level. The animal mortality rate in all animal experiments is less than 5%.

  Mice were anesthetized using isoflurane and secured in a stereotaxic instrument (RWD Life Science, 68,025, Shenzhen, CN). A heating pad was used to maintain the body temperature at 37 °C, and eye ointment was applied to prevent dryness. The scalp was shaved and disinfected with betadine. A midline incision was made to expose the skull, and the bregma and lambda points were identified for coordinate calibration. A burr hole was drilled at the target coordinates (anteroposterior (AP) =  − 3.0 mm, mediolateral (ML) =  ± 1.4 mm, and dorsoventral (DV) =  − 4.2 mm from bregm). A 0.5 mm diameter drill bit was used to thin the skull and generate a 0.5 mm diameter burr hole. A glass electrode needle (proximal outer diameter: 1.14 mm, proximal inner diameter: 0.53 mm, RWD Life Science, Shenzhen, CN) with conical tip outer diameter < 0.5 mm slowly lowered to the target depth. The image of the glass electrode used in the stereotaxic surgery procedures was provided in Fig S7. The viral solution was injected at a rate of 0.2 µL/min using a microinjection pump, with a total volume of 0.5 µL. After injection, the needle was left in place for 5 min to prevent backflow. Adeno-associated virus (pAAV-TH promoter-EGFP-mir155(Nfe2 l2)-SV40 PolyA, serotypes: AAV9, titer: 4.65E + 13v.g/mL) were purchased from Shanghai GeneChemCo., Ltd. Immediately after surgery, animals were placed on a heating pad to maintain body temperature at 37 °C until fully recovered from anesthesia. Postoperative antibiotics were administered to minimize pain and prevent infection. Animals were monitored continuously for 2 h post-surgery and daily for 7 days to assess recovery. The surgical site was inspected daily and cleaned with antiseptic if necessary. All animals were housed in a clean, temperature-controlled environment with ad libitum access to food and water, and bedding was changed regularly to maintain hygiene. The virus was injected to interfere with target proteins for 21 days, subsequent experiments will be conducted.


### Behavioral parameters

#### Open field test (OFT)
  Autonomic movement of mice were estimated by OFT (RWD Life Science, 63,008, Shenzhen, CN). Continuously record the mouse’s movement trajectory for 10 min in an open device measuring 50 cm × 50 cm × 50 cm. Smart 2.0 tracking analysis system was used for parameter analysis including total distance travelling.


#### Pole test
  Bradykinesia in mice was detected by pole test. This test manifested in recording the time it takes for the four paws of a mouse to reach the ground from the roof of a 50 cm tall wooden pole. Each mouse undergoes training the day before the test and measured at least five times to avoid error.


#### Rotarod test
  A rotarod apparatus (RWD Life Science, LE8205, Shenzhen, CN) was done to check limb coordination. Mice were placed on a rotating rod with acceleration mode of from 4 to 40 rpm. Each mouse undergoes training the day before the test and measured at least three times to avoid error.


### CatWalk gait analysis
  The CatWalk XT (Noldus Information Technology, WUR, NL), an automated gait analysis system, can measure dynamic and static gait patterns of mice. Visual walking mode can qualitatively describe stage gait. When the mice passed through the tunnel from left to right in a darkened room, their footprints on the glass panel were detected by the camera and transmitted to the computer. Footprints can be analyzed and calculated in different ways using CatWalk XT gait analysis including Run Duration, Run Average Speed, Paw Statistics (Body Speed, Stand, Swing, Step Cycle, Duty Cycle, Stride Length). Each mouse was preceded by a training day before the test. Each trial with three runs and separated by a 10 min long interval.


### High-performance liquid chromatography with electrochemical detection (HPLC-ECD) for striatal DA, DOPAC, and HVA
  DA, 3,4-dihydroxy phenylacetic acid (DOPAC) and homovanillic acid (HVA) levels in the striatum tissue of mouse brain were estimated to be using HPLC-ECD (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Dionex Ultimate 3000, Waltham, MA, US). The method details refer to previous studies [21]. The concentration was calculated by constructing a calibration curve using data of standard samples and normalized to the striatum weight.


### Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)
  N2a cells were treated according to experimental requirements, fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C. Midbrain SN tissue of mice perfused with 2.5% glutaraldehyde were cut into 1 mm3 blocks and stored in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C. Further samples were dehydrated in acetone and cut with an ultramicrotome and varnished using uranium acetate and lead citrate. The mitochondrial ultrastructure was observed under Tecnai 10 microscope (FEI, Hillsboro, OR, US).


### Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
  Superoxide dismutase (SOD), Catalase (CAT) and GSH in the supernatant collected from cell lysate and mouse brain homogenate were assayed by corresponding ELISA kits (Mlbio, Cat# ml643059, Cat# ml037752, Cat# ml063305, Shanghai, CN). The signal at a wavelength of 450 nm by a multimode plate reader. The concentrations were calculated by drawing a standard curve.


### NADPH and ATP determination
  N2a cell lysate and mouse midbrain SN homogenate supernatant was collected after treatment. NADPH levels were detected with NADP/NADPH-Glo™ Assay kit (Promega, Cat# G9081, Madison, WI, US) according to the instruction manual. Luminescence values were executed at 340 nm in multimode plate reader. ATP levels were measured with Molecular Probes™ ATP assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# A22066, Waltham, MA, US) according to the instruction manual. Luminescence value was assayed at 570 nm in multimode plate reader. NADPH or ATP concentrations were evaluated using a calibration curve and normalized to the protein level.


### Prediction of potential targets, network construction and bioinformatics annotation
  Targets of EPS were collected from TCMSP (https://tcmspw.com/), BatmanTCM (http://bionet.ncpsb.org.cn/batman-tcm/), ETCM (http://www.tcmip.cn/ETCM), SEA (https://sea.bkslab.org/), SwissTarget Prediction (http://swisstargetprediction.ch/), TargetNet (http://targetnet.scbdd.com/) and HERB (http://herb.ac.cn/). PD-related targets were collected from GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/) and OMIM databases (https://omim.org/). Venny 2.0 software is used for analysis the intersection of EPS and PD targets. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis was administered using the STRING database (https://string-db.org/). Then, Cytoscape 3.8.2 software was employed to calculate the core regulatory network and establish a visual composite target network. The target genes acquired from the PPI network were used for GO functional annotation and KEGG pathway analysis. DAVID database (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) was applied in the data processing. Two GEO datasets (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo) were used as the validation cohort (GSE26927 and GSE30). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was implemented with GSEA 4.1.0 software. All data used in this analysis was available from public databases, and ethical permission and informed consent had been provided in previous study.


### Molecular docking analysis
  The 3D structure of EPS (CID: 1549120) and transformation format were downloaded from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The structure of KEAP1 (ID: Q14145), Nrf2 (ID: Q16236) and GCLC (ID: P48506) were predicted using AlphaFold from the Uniprot dataset (https://www.uniprot.org/). The crystal structure of HO-1 (ID: 6EHA), NQO1 (ID: 4 CET) were acquired from the PDB dataset (https://www.rcsb.org/). Pymol software was performed to molecular docking pretreatment on protein Q14145, Q16236, P48506, 6EHA and 4 CET, dehydrates and hydrogenates for the protein molecule. AutoDock Tools-1.5.7 software was performed to transfer the EPS and protein Q14145, Q16236, P48506, 6EHA and 4 CET to *pdbqt format. The Grid function of AutoDockTools1.5.7 software was applied to determine the structural domains (active pockets) of protein receptors that combine with drug component ligands. Based on the parameters obtained from Grid, molecular flexible docking was performed using the DOCK function and independently docked fifty times. Select the docking model with the smallest binding energy. Finally, Pymol software was used to draw a visualizing pattern diagram between the components and the core target protein. Molecular docking calculations were performed three times to calculate mean values and standard deviations.


### Surface plasmon resonance (SPR)
  SPR assays were measured with the Biacore X100. KEAP1 protein was immobilized on a sensor CM5. The compounds were refluxed at a rate of 10 μL/min for 60 s subsequent by 150 s for detachment over fixed protein in 1 × PBS/0.1% DMSO running buffer at 25 ℃. EPS was detected for binding at 0.0183 to 150 μM. The recorded data was standardized, and the affinity between EPS and KEAP1 assessed using calculated equilibrium dissociation constant (KD).


### Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)
  Cell lysate was repeatedly freeze-thawed three times in liquid nitrogen and extracted by centrifugation at 4℃. The supernatant fluid was separated into two equal aliquots, one aliquot was treated with EPS (100 μM) and the other aliquot was treated with DMSO (Control) for 30 min. Then, the cell lysates were divided into seven equal aliquots. Each aliquot was heated for 3 min at varying temperatures (37, 42, 47, 52, 57, 62, and 67 ℃) followed by cooling for 3 min. Sensations obtained by centrifugation at 20,000 × g, 20 min, and 4 ℃ were used for immunoblotting analysis.


### Identification of EPS and MPP+ by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS)
  EPS (100 μM) was incubated with MPP+ (100 μM) for 24 h at 37 °C. EPS, MPP+ and the EPS-MPP+ complex were analyzed by LC–MS/MS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Orbitrap IQ-X Tribrid, Waltham, MA, US). Transport a mobile phase consisting of 30% water (0.1% formic acid) and 70% acetonitrile at a constant flow rate of 0.4 mL/min.


### Immunofluorescence staining
  N2a cells were seeded on a coverslip in 24-well plates and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 30 min. Afterwards, cells were flushed three times with 0.01 mol/L PBS and treated with 10% goat serum (Solarbio, Cat# SL038, Beijing, CN) soluted in 0.1% Triton X-100 for 1 h, and then incubated with the Nrf2 Rabbit pAb (ABclonal, A11159, Wuhan, Hubei, CN) at 4 °C overnight. Cells were flushed again with 0.01 mol/L PBS and stained with Alexa Fluor™ 555 goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) highly cross-adsorbed secondary antibody (Invitrogen, Cat# A-31572, CA, US) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (Beyotime, Cat# C1006, Shanghai, CN). The images were observed with a confocal microscope (Lecia, STELLARIS 5, Wetzlar, DE). 110–130 cells from each group to calculate the average value of fluoresence indensity for statistical analysis.

  Brain tissues were isolated and fixed in 4% PFA for 24 h at 4 °C following by soaked in 20%, 30% sucrose solution gradually for 24 h, then, cryoprotected at −80 °C. Frozen midbrain was excised into 20 μm sections with cryostat microtome to identify SN region for immunofluorescence staining. Brain slices were blocked with 10% goat serum for 1 h and incubated with Rabbit polyclonal anti-TH (1:500, Millipore, Cat# AB152, Billerica, MA, US), Mouse polyclonal anti-TH (1:500; Millipore, Cat# MAB318, Billerica, MA, US), and Nrf2 Rabbit pAb (ABclonal, A11159, Wuhan, Hubei, CN) overnight at 4 °C. Alexa Fluor™ 488 goat anti-Mouse IgG (Invitrogen, Cat# A21202, CA, US) and Alexa Fluor™ 555 goat anti-Rabbit IgG (Invitrogen, Cat# A31572, CA, US) were mixed with sections and cultured for 1 h. The images were observed with immunofluorescent microscopy (Olympus, BX63, JPN) and confocal microscope. The number of TH+ neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNpc) was counted using stereological quantification. Each group consists of 6 samples, with 20–40 cells selected from each sample to calculate the average value of fluoresence indensity for statistical analysis.

### Immunoblotting

### Statistical analysis
  All data are presented as mean ± SEM. All data analyses were executing GraphPad Prism 8.0.2. Student’s t-test, one-way or two-way ANOVA with Turkey’s multiple-comparisons test are used for statistical assessment. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.


## Results

### EPS improved motor function and attenuated the injury of DAergic neurons in MPP+/MPTP-treated PD model
  To determine the protective properties of EPS on PD, 25 mg/kg, 50 mg/kg and 100 mg/kg EPS were administered to PD model based on C57BL/6 mice via intraperitoneal injection of MPTP, and then multiple behavioral tests were implemented (as illustrated in Fig. 1A). OFT test showed 50 mg/kg and 100 mg/kg EPS treatment could increase the travel distance of MPTP-treated PD mice (Fig. 1B). Rotarod and pole test showed the time spent was drastically improved in PD mice after 50 mg/kg and100 mg/kg EPS intervention (Fig. 1C, D). Moreover, we found that TH protein expression level in the SN of the midbrain substantially increased in PD mice treated with 50 mg/kg and 100 mg/kg EPS (Fig. S1 A, B). Thus, we chose 50 mg/kg EPS as the therapeutic dose for subsequent experiments. The gait analysis result indicated that EPS treatment led to an increase in average running speed and a time decrement in running duration, stand, swing, step cycle (Fig. 1E). In brief, motor dysfunction in PD mice were attenuated by EPS treatment manifested as a significant expansion of traveling range in the open field, restoration of motor coordination and bradykinesia, and regain of abnormal gait. Then, we used Madopar and Rasagiline mesylate as positive treatment controls to evaluate the therapeutic effect of EPS. Animal behaviors were tested after administering three oral medications for treatment. The specific experimental process was shown in Figure S1 C. OFT, rotarod and pole test results indicated that EPS, Madopar and Rasagiline mesylate could improve motor behavior disorders in PD mice, and there was no statistically significant difference in treatment effects among them (Fig S1D-G). These results demonstrated that EPS presents a prospective ability to reverse motor dysfunction of PD mice. To explore the loss of DAergic neurons in MPTP-treated PD mice, immunofluorescence staining and immunoblotting results showed EPS intervention for 8 days could ameliorate the MPTP-induced DAergic neuron loss and increase TH protein expression (Fig. 1F-I, Fig S1H). We also found that EPS could deteriorate the decrease Bcl-2/Bax protein ratio caused by MPTP in the SN. (Fig. 1H, J). To exclude the possibility that EPS affects the metabolic changes of MPTP by affecting MAO-B, we detect the expression levels of MAO-B protein in the midbrain SN and striatum regions of mice, and the results showed that EPS did not affect MAO-B protein (Fig S1I-K). Moreover, we measured the contents of DA, DOPAC and HVA in the striatum tissue of mice and found a significant depletion at the levels of DA and its derivatives in MPTP-treated PD mice, while EPS could compensate for these levels (Fig. 1K-M). EPS reduced MDA accumulation and increased SOD, CAT and GSH levels in the SN, further confirming its antioxidant effect in PD animal model (Fig. 2F-I). Collectively, EPS could eliminate excessive ROS and effectively regulate intracellular redox balance effectively, thereby reducing MPP+/MPTP-induced oxidative damage. We collected a total of 9102 targets related to PD using the"Relevance score"parameter for screening from the Genecards and OMIM database. ## Discussion
  Parkinson’s disease, as an incurable high burden neurological disease with a steadily rising global incidence rate [26]. Current PD therapies primarily alleviate symptoms by compensating for DA loss but are limited by side effects, a lack of long-term efficacy, and a shortage of disease-modifying treatments, highlighting the need for novel therapeutic targets [27]. Therefore, new disease-modifying drugs are stand in need to impede the progression of diseases. Given the high costs, time, and safety challenges of new drug development, drug repositioning has emerged as a promising approach, leveraging low-risk compounds with established safety and toxicological profiles [28]. Risk factors for PD include oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction provides a mesmerized opportunity to repurpose existing drugs to treat PD, if those drugs contain PD modifying features. As a medicine approved for clinical practice, EPS has been used in treating diabetes peripheral neuropathy [5, 6]. Although existing studies have prompted EPS to have a protective effect on various disease cell models [11, 13, 29], it is rarely reflected in central nervous system (CNS) diseases, especially PD. In current research, we found that EPS alleviated oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, further improving symptoms in PD mice. EPS diminished KEAP1 and activated Nrf2 neutralized this oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage. To sum up, we inferred that the EPS would be a perspective drug for PD.

  Recent evidences have increasingly discovered that oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage were significant in the DAergic neurons injury of PD [18]. Oxidative stress is the main procedure of aging in the body, directly damaging the CNS, especially neurons, credited to excessive oxygen consumption and lack of antioxidants [30]. Systematic cross-sectional characterization of human autopsy of PD brains announced that overproduction and incorporation of free radicals led to the excessive accumulation of cytotoxic substance that induce oxidative stress damage in the CNS [31, 32]. Cytoplasmic sustained oxidant stress can further compromise the mitophagy pathway and intensify mitochondrial functional damage. Meanwhile, mitochondrially generated ROS also impair lysosomes and proteasomes, giving rise to the overproduction of misfolded α-synuclein [33, 34]. The autopsy results of human PD demonstrated severe mitochondrial DNA aberrations and deletions specifically in the SN tissue [35–37]. Meanwhile, a few studies have found that mitochondrial dysfunction generates chronic ROS and DAergic neurodegeneration [22, 38]. Hence, the potential application of antioxidant effects and restoration of mitochondrial function drugs for treating neurological disease like PD requires widespread attention. According to the understanding of the pathophysiology, etiology, and molecular processes of PD, we constructed the MPP+/MPTP-treated PD model. In subsequent experiments, we observed that the improvements in behavioral disorders and increased of DAergic neurons in PD model treated with EPS. Meanwhile, we also confirmed that the antioxidation of EPS resisted oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction. In consideration of the available result, we speculate that EPS administration alleviated the oxidative damage and mitochondrial function by MPP+/MPTP, thus reducing the attack to DAergic neurons.

  Subsequently, we conducted a thorough analysis of the potential mechanisms of EPS antioxidant properties. Based on network pharmacology, bioinformatics annotation, and experimental validation, we hypothesize and confirm that EPS modulates Nrf2 signaling in PD by targeting KEAP1. Furthermore, we confirm that EPS directly binds to the ASN414 site of KEAP1, enhancing our understanding of EPS promoting Nrf2 activation. Pathology research manifested that Nrf2 expression was essential impaired in nigral DAergic neurons of postmortem samples of PD patients [39]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that the knockout of Nrf2 exacerbates the damage to DA neurons, while the overexpression of Nrf2 alleviates disease symptoms in various PD models [40–43]. Under physiological condition, Nrf2 binds to KEAP1 protein and remains in the cytoplasm. Correspondingly, KEAP1 undergoes conformational changes and disconnects from Nrf2 under oxidative stress conditions, promoting Nrf2 translocated to nucleus and combined with antioxidant response element (ARE) to regulating the transcriptional expression of detoxification genes, thereby preventing oxidative damage, mitochondrial damage and ATP depletion [44]. Our study found that EPS promotes Nrf2 nuclear translocation and activates the expression of GCLC, HO-1, and NQO1 in PD model. However, we found that MPP+ treatment also increased Nrf2 level and its downstream target genes compared to the control group in vitro. We speculated this phenomenon as the endogenous activation of Nrf2 signaling in cells under MPP+ stimulation to resist oxidative stress, but this protective process did not reverse the damage. In addition, we observed that EPS intervention activates the Nrf2 signaling pathway under physiological conditions in vitro without inducing cellular damage. This suggests that EPS, as an antioxidant, targets pathological mechanisms while maintaining redox homeostasis under physiological conditions. Meanwhile, we observed that this phenomenon was attenuated in vivo, likely due to the simplified conditions of in vitro experiments compared to the complex physiological environment in vivo. Therefore, under the oxidative stress environment induced by MPP+/MPTP, the strong antioxidant effect of EPS reversed oxidative damage. Furthermore, we found that Nrf2 silencing or pharmacological inhibition exacerbated MPP+-induced cytotoxicity and attenuated the neuroprotection of EPS. Similarly, knocking down Nrf2 resulted in more severe motor disorders and degeneration of DAergic neurons in PD model mice, while offsetting the therapeutic effect of EPS. Here, we proposed that Nrf2 was extremely important in the neuroprotective effect of EPS in PD models both in vitro and in vivo. When attempting to corroborate whether EPS attenuated oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction by the KEAP1/Nrf2 signaling pathway, we found that knocking down Nrf2 partially counteracts the antioxidative and protecting mitochondrial effects of EPS in MPP+/MPTP-treated PD model. In summary, these results recommended that EPS exerts neuroprotective effects by attenuating oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage, where these processes are mediated by Nrf2.

  Previous studies have also shown that promising results obtained by activating Nrf2 in drug therapy for PD. The insulin-like growth factor II has been confirmed to counteract MPP+-induced damage by increasing nuclear translocation of Nrf2 in DAergic neurons cell culture and progressive PD mouse models [45]. Recent research has found that pyridoxine promoted GSH synthetize through PKM2-mediated Nrf2 activation and enhanced the resistance of nerve cells to MPP+ toxicity [46]. In MPP+/MPTP-treated PD model, tetramethylpyrazine nitrone has authenticated effectiveness and the therapeutic mechanism was related to the PGC-1α/Nrf2 signaling pathway [47]. Moreover, Researchers have found that inflachromene exhibited significant potency in repressing the DAergic neurons loss and microglia activation by targeting KEAP1/Nrf2 signaling [48]. Artemisinin is an antimalarial drug that has been used in neutralized MPTP-induced neurotoxic effects and improved DAergic neurons loss by activating Nrf2 [49]. Carvacrol exerts antioxidant effects on dopaminergic neurons by regulating the Nrf2/HO-1/NLRP3 axis in rotenone-induced PD mice model [50]. Phloretin, as a phenolic compound with reported anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities, has recently been found to improve pathological injury and motor function in PD mice model by regulating the mTOR/Nrf2/p62 pathway [51]. Dimethyl fumarate (DMF), as an Nrf2 activator, can activate the Nrf2-TIGAR-LAMP2/Catepsin D pathway to regulate autophagy and cell apoptosis, thereby improving rotenone induced DAergic neurodegeneration [52]. Another study found that DMF improves mitochondrial autophagy and neuronal damage by regulating the Nrf2-BNIP3-PINK1 axis [53]. Notably, Nrf2 activators have been confirmed to ameliorate performance in PD and exert neuroprotective effects. This preclinical experimental evidence supports that Nrf2 inducers can be recommended as an available therapeutic target against PD. Considering the latent pharmacological advantage of EPS in PD, the study was projected to estimate the therapeutic effect and mechanism of EPS in PD model.

  Although we have proposed candidate molecules and mechanisms that mediate the effects of EPS (Fig. 8), further detailed research is needed to clarify the therapeutic effect of EPS as an adjuvant drug. Presently, no neuroprotective drugs have been approved for PD treatment. ## Conclusion
  Our study revealed that an unconventional function of EPS as a specific Nrf2 activator in enormous antioxidant, mitochondrial protective and anti-apoptotic activities. EPS stabilized the redox balance, improved mitochondrial damage, and reduced the degeneration of DAergic neurons in PD, attributed to its regulation of KEAP1/Nrf2 signaling.
