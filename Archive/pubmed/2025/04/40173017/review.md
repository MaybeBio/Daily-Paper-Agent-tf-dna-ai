## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text including abstract, introduction, materials and methods, results, discussion, and supplementary figure/table legends as provided.
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific content, technical soundness, and support of claims based solely on the supplied manuscript text. No external data, figures, or supplementary files were available for inspection beyond what is described in the text.
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors report that RdfS, a winged helix-turn-helix (wHTH) recombination directionality factor from the Mesorhizobium japonicum R7A integrative and conjugative element ICEMlSymR7A, binds DNA as an oligomer, forms tetramers in solution and in crystals, and uses a unique N-terminal α1 helix to stabilize oligomerization that is essential for ICE excision and conjugative transfer in vivo. They propose an indirect readout mechanism for DNA recognition of large, poorly conserved regions.
- **Visible evidence base** X-ray crystallography (PDB 8DGL), analytical gel filtration, SEC-SY-SAXS, EMSA, SPR-based DNA footprinting, β-galactosidase reporter assays, quantitative PCR excision assays, conjugation assays, and molecular dynamics simulations. All data are described in the text but no primary figures or tables were provided for direct inspection.
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Primary figures, supplementary figures, supplementary tables (including SAXS collection parameters and strain/plasmid lists), and detailed experimental protocols in the supplementary data were not available. This limits independent verification of data quality, statistical analyses, and reproducibility of methods.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript presents a compelling and well-integrated structural and functional study of RdfS, a wHTH RDF with a unique N-terminal helix that mediates oligomerization critical for ICE excision and transfer. The combination of crystallography, biophysical solution analyses, in vitro DNA-binding assays, and in vivo functional assays provides a strong multi-pronged approach. The proposed indirect readout mechanism is plausible and well contextualized within the broader wHTH literature. However, several technical concerns, particularly regarding the interpretation of SAXS data, the lack of quantitative binding affinity measurements, and the indirect nature of the in silico DNA-binding model, temper full confidence in the central claims. The manuscript is well written and will interest researchers in mobile genetic elements, site-specific recombination, and bacterial gene regulation.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers studying integrative and conjugative elements, prophage biology, site-specific recombination, and tyrosine recombinase directionality factors will find the functional dissection of RdfS directly relevant. The structural biology community, particularly those interested in wHTH proteins and protein-DNA recognition, will value the novel oligomerization mechanism. Additionally, those studying nucleoid-associated proteins and indirect readout DNA recognition may find the proposed model of interest.
- **Major strengths** 1) The study addresses a significant gap in understanding how RdfS coordinates excision and conjugation, with clear prior genetic evidence motivating the structural work. 2) The combination of crystallography, solution biophysics, and in vivo assays provides complementary evidence. 3) The identification of a unique N-terminal α1 helix and its functional importance is a novel and significant contribution. 4) The proposed indirect readout mechanism is well reasoned and supported by the lack of a consensus binding motif and the tolerance to single nucleotide substitutions. 5) The writing is clear and accessible, with appropriate context provided for nonspecialists.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **Concern ID** R1-M1
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Technical soundness of SAXS analysis
  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that SEC-SY-SAXS analysis of the 6H-RdfS-attP_8 complex indicates a tetrameric assembly (4 RdfS: 1 DNA) with a mass of approximately 85.7 kDa (or 71.5 kDa after adjustment for nucleic acid electron density).
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section "RdfS oligomerizes alone in solution and together with DNA"; Supplementary Table S3 (location not provided)
  - **Concern** The molecular weight estimates from SAXS are reported as 85,655 Da and 83,125 Da, which are substantially higher than the expected 76.5 kDa for a 4:1 complex. The authors attribute this to the higher electron density of nucleic acids, but the correction applied is not described in sufficient detail. The credibility interval (75,300-86,950 Da) is broad and overlaps with the expected mass only at its lower bound. Without a clear description of the Bayesian assessment priors, the exclusion of alternative stoichiometries (e.g., 5:1 or 6:1) is not rigorously established. Additionally, the Kratky plot analysis indicating flexibility is qualitative, and the authors do not report Rg or Dmax values that would allow independent assessment of the particle shape.
  - **Why it matters** The stoichiometry of the RdfS-DNA complex is central to the proposed model of oligomeric DNA recognition. If the stoichiometry is not 4:1, the molecular dynamics model and the proposed mechanism of cooperative binding would require revision. The current evidence does not definitively rule out higher-order assemblies.
  - **Resolution test** Provide a detailed description of the SAXS data analysis, including the specific Bayesian prior used, the Rg and Dmax values, and a comparison of the experimental scattering profile to theoretical profiles for 3:1, 4:1, 5:1, and 6:1 models. Show that the 4:1 model is statistically preferred over alternatives. Alternatively, provide an orthogonal method (e.g., analytical ultracentrifugation or native mass spectrometry) to confirm the solution stoichiometry.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M2
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Support for the indirect readout mechanism
  - **Claim pointer** The authors propose that RdfS recognizes larger DNA regions with low primary sequence conservation through an indirect readout mechanism, based on the absence of a consensus motif and the lack of individual nucleotides critical for binding.
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section "RdfS binds the attP site upstream of intS and upstream of promoters PrdfS and PrdfG"; Figure 3E (location not provided)
  - **Concern** The conclusion that no individual nucleotide is critical for binding is based on single-base-pair substitution experiments within a 40-bp oligonucleotide. However, the authors do not report whether combinations of substitutions were tested, nor do they provide quantitative binding affinities for the mutant oligonucleotides. The observation that some substitutions reduced binding to 50-60% of wild-type suggests that certain positions contribute more than others, but the threshold for "critical" is not defined. Furthermore, the lack of a consensus motif could also be explained by recognition of a degenerate sequence motif that was not captured by the limited mutagenesis, or by binding that is primarily shape-based but still sequence-dependent at multiple positions simultaneously. The indirect readout model is plausible but not uniquely supported by the data presented.
  - **Why it matters** The proposed indirect readout mechanism is a central conceptual contribution of the manuscript. If the mechanism is not rigorously established, the broader significance of the work is diminished. The distinction between indirect readout and recognition of a degenerate, multi-position sequence motif has important implications for predicting RdfS binding sites across genomes.
  - **Resolution test** Provide quantitative binding data (e.g., Kd values from EMSA or fluorescence anisotropy) for wild-type and mutant attP_8 variants, including combinations of substitutions. Demonstrate that binding affinity does not correlate with the presence of any specific sequence feature. Alternatively, perform a more comprehensive mutagenesis (e.g., saturation mutagenesis across the entire 40-bp region) and show that no single position or simple combination of positions is essential. If possible, test binding to DNA with altered shape (e.g., bulged or bent DNA) to directly test the indirect readout hypothesis.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M3
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** In vivo relevance of the α1 helix
  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that removal of the α1 helix (RdfS13-89) abolishes RdfS-mediated ICEMlSymR7A excision and conjugative transfer, while DNA binding in vitro is only reduced, not eliminated.
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section "The N-terminal helix of RdfS is not essential for DNA-binding in vitro but is essential for in vivo activation of ICEMlSymR7A excision and conjugative transfer"; Figure 5F (location not provided)
  - **Concern** The in vivo assays demonstrate that RdfS13-89 cannot complement the rdfS deletion for excision or conjugation. However, the authors do not report whether the RdfS13-89 protein is stably expressed in M. japonicum R7A at levels comparable to wild-type RdfS. If the truncated protein is poorly expressed or degraded in vivo, the loss of function could be due to reduced protein levels rather than a specific requirement for the α1 helix in oligomerization or DNA binding. The authors also do not test whether RdfS13-89 can still bind to the attP site in vivo (e.g., via ChIP or a reporter assay), which would distinguish between a defect in DNA binding versus a defect in a post-binding step such as oligomerization or interaction with IntS.
  - **Why it matters** The conclusion that the α1 helix is essential for in vivo function is a key finding, but the mechanism (oligomerization, DNA binding, or protein stability) is not fully resolved. This distinction is important for understanding how RdfS coordinates excision and conjugation.
  - **Resolution test** Perform Western blot analysis to confirm equivalent expression levels of RdfS and RdfS13-89 in M. japonicum R7A. If possible, perform a chromatin immunoprecipitation assay to assess in vivo DNA binding of RdfS13-89 to the attP site. Alternatively, test whether overexpression of RdfS13-89 can restore excision in a strain where IntS is also overexpressed, to determine if the defect is in cooperative function with IntS.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that RdfS forms tetramers in solution in the absence of DNA is supported by gel filtration and SAXS, but the SAXS molecular weight estimates are imprecise and the possibility of higher-order assemblies is not rigorously excluded.
- The claim that the α1 helix is required for stable oligomerization in vivo is inferred from in vitro truncation experiments and in vivo functional assays, but direct evidence for altered oligomerization in vivo is lacking.
- The proposal of an indirect readout mechanism is plausible but not definitively established, as alternative explanations (degenerate sequence recognition, multi-position sequence dependence) are not fully excluded.
- The claim that RdfS binding to attP_8 activates transcription from PintS is supported by reporter assays, but the magnitude of activation (53%) is modest and the physiological relevance is not discussed in depth.
- The statement that the α1 helix is "absent from all other structurally characterized wHTH-domain proteins" is a strong claim that relies on the completeness of the structural database and should be verified against the most recent PDB entries.
- The speculation that RdfS may regulate additional genes involved in conjugation is not directly tested and is presented as a hypothesis, which is appropriate, but it should be clearly labeled as such.

## Assessment against Nature-style criteria
- **Originality** The manuscript presents a novel structural mechanism for RdfS function, specifically the role of a unique N-terminal α1 helix in oligomerization. This is a distinct contribution to the field of wHTH proteins and RDFs, which have not been extensively characterized structurally. The proposed indirect readout mechanism, while not unprecedented, is applied here in a new context.
- **Scientific importance** The work addresses a fundamental question in mobile genetic element biology: how excision and conjugation are coordinated. The finding that RdfS oligomerization is critical for both processes has implications for understanding the regulation of ICE transfer, which is relevant to horizontal gene transfer and the spread of antibiotic resistance and symbiotic traits. The study is likely to be of interest to a specialized but significant community.
- **Interdisciplinary readership** The manuscript bridges structural biology, microbiology, and molecular genetics. The structural findings will appeal to biochemists and structural biologists, while the functional assays will interest microbiologists studying mobile genetic elements. However, the level of detail in the structural analysis may be challenging for nonspecialists, and the broader implications for bacterial evolution and horizontal gene transfer could be more explicitly articulated.
- **Technical soundness** The experimental approaches are generally appropriate and well executed, but several technical concerns limit the strength of the conclusions. The SAXS analysis lacks sufficient detail to rigorously confirm the tetrameric stoichiometry. The in vitro DNA-binding assays are qualitative (EMSA) or semi-quantitative (SPR without kinetics), and no binding affinities are reported. The molecular dynamics simulations are described only briefly, and their conclusions are not experimentally validated. These issues do not invalidate the work but reduce the level of confidence in the central claims.
- **Readability for nonspecialists** The manuscript is generally well written and logically organized. The introduction provides appropriate background on ICEs and wHTH proteins. However, some sections, particularly the description of the structural analysis and molecular dynamics, assume familiarity with specialized techniques. The abstract is clear and accessible, but the discussion could be more explicit about the broader significance of the findings for understanding horizontal gene transfer and bacterial evolution.
