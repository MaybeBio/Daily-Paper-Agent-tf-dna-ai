# The Role of HIF-1α/BNIP3 Pathway in Hippocampal Neuronal Autophagy in Epilepsy


## Abstract
  Epilepsy (EP), a prevalent neurological disorder, lacks a comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying epileptogenesis. Multiple studies highlight that hippocampal (Hip) neuronal injury may serve as an essential underlying factor in EP onset and progression. Accumulating evidence shows that autophagy (Apg) has an essential function in supporting cell survival and sustaining cellular and tissue homeostasis. This investigation aimed to examine and further validate hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF)-1α/BNIP3 signaling in the Hip neuronal Apg in a lithium chloride-pilocarpine (LiCl-Pilo)-induced EP rat model. We retrieved the GSE143272 dataset and generally validated Apg-related genes (ATGs) expression and its diagnostic roles in EP through differential expression analysis, protein–protein interaction (PPI), enrichment analysis, and four machine learning (ML) models. Afterward, the predictive performance of the ML models was evaluated via a nomogram model and validated with an external dataset (GSE63808). Finally, the function of the HIF-1α/BNIP3 pathway in regulating Apg through HIF-1α inhibition was systematically validated in the LiCl-Pilo-induced chronic EP rat model. Our data identified eleven ATGs as differentially expressed. Among them, HIF-1α and BNIP3 were found to be closely associated, exhibiting a negative regulatory interaction. The ML model XGB exhibited superior performance in differentiating EP patients. Subsequent experimental validation confirmed elevated Apg markers and HIF-1α levels, along with decreased BNIP3 levels. Inhibition of HIF-1α upregulated HIF-1α/BNIP3-modulated Apg and attenuated Hip neuron damage and mitochondrial damage induced by LiCl-Pilo-induced EP. Collectively, these outcomes suggest that HIF-1α/BNIP3 signaling might be involved in neuronal damage by mediating Hip Apg, thereby regulating epileptic seizures.


## Introduction
  Epilepsy (EP) constitutes a frequent neurological disease on a global scale, affecting people of all ages, races, social classes, and geographical locations. Typically, EP is marked by abnormal neuronal discharges or dysfunctions that may affect the entire temporal lobe or specific areas within it, leading to localized epileptic seizures. Hippocampal (Hip) neuron damage can, in turn, exacerbate seizures. Thus, the signaling pathways related to this damage are regarded as promising targets for reducing seizures or delaying the progression of EP. However, the pathogenetic mechanisms of EP-induced neuronal damage have not been completely elucidated. Therefore, clarifying the molecular pathways responsible for this neuronal injury would facilitate the creation of innovative preventive and therapeutic approaches. Recently, autophagy (Apg) has been revealed to have an association with various diseases, including EP [1]. Recent investigations have illustrated that multiple forms of cell death, including Apg, apoptosis, and necrosis, occur in Hip regions. These processes contribute to neuronal damage and promote the development of EP [2–4]. Reducing neuronal apoptosis and Apg can protect Hip neurons [5]. Consistently, kainic acid induces neuronal damage that is exacerbated by Apg inhibitors but alleviated by Apg inducers. [6, 7]. Notably, Apg contributes to EP pathogenesis. Nevertheless, Apg-associated EP genes are mostly unidentified and require additional investigation.

  The hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF)−1α participates in several pathophysiological processes, including tumor growth and metastasis, stem cell proliferation and differentiation, angiogenesis, inflammation, neuronal apoptosis, and hypoxic injury [8–11]. Recent studies have revealed HIF-1α activation in both epileptic rat models and human patients with EP [11–15]. The inhibition of HIF-1α decreased the spontaneous recurrent seizure (SRS) rate in the lithium chloride-pilocarpine (LiCl-Pilo) rat model via VEGF and EPO signaling pathways [16]. Research conducted by Zhang W et al. has shown that HIF-1α/FUNDC1 signaling modulates renal tubular cell mitophagy by inhibiting apoptosis and reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation [17]. Additionally, HIF-1α has been elucidated to govern Apg levels and mitochondrial homeostasis. Huang B et al. [18] have indicated that HIF-1α facilitates human aortic smooth muscle cell migration and proliferation by enhancing Apg in hypoxic circumstances. The Bcl-2/adenovirus E1B 19 kDa-interacting protein 3 (BNIP3), a part of the Bcl-2 protein family, serves as a target gene of HIF-1α and is differentially regulated by HIF-1α under hypoxic circumstances. The HIF-1α pathway is a potent activator of BNIP3-modulated Apg. Although recent investigations have elucidated the essential function of HIF-1α/BNIP3-mediated Apg in regulating ovarian functionality and fertility in mice [19], its role in modulating Apg in EP and the underlying mechanism of neuronal damage associated with Apg in Hip neurons remains unknown. Therefore, we suggest the scientific hypothesis that the HIF-1α/BNIP3 pathway enhances epileptogenesis and EP development by facilitating Hip neuronal Apg.

  Pilocarpine (Pilo) is widely used in experimental neuroscience to model human neurological disorders, including EP, neuropsychiatric conditions, and neurotoxicity. The LiCl-Pilo coadministration protocol significantly reduces Pilo-induced mortality while maintaining a high induction rate of status epilepticus (SE), thereby providing a stable and reproducible model for studying SE-related neuronal injury [20]. Rats induced by LiCl-Pilo exhibit spontaneous seizures, Hip structural damage, and neuropathological features—including hippocampal sclerosis and neuronal degeneration—alongside electroencephalography (EEG) abnormalities that mirror clinical EP manifestations. This model recapitulates key characteristics of human hippocampal sclerosis, as validated by Lévesque et al. [21], thereby serving as a reliable platform for investigating epileptogenesis and developing novel therapeutic interventions [22, 23]. Thus, herein, we conducted an integrated bioinformatic analysis aimed at identifying differentially expressed Apg-related genes (DE-ATGs) in patients with EP. Subsequently, we constructed various machine learning (ML) algorithms, and the prognostic performance of the ML models was evaluated via a nomogram model and validated with an external dataset (GSE63808). Lastly, the levels of the essential differentially expressed genes (DEGs), as well as the function of the DE-ATGs, were systematically validated in the LiCl-Pilo-induced chronic EP rat model.


## Material and Methods

### Experimental Design

#### Data acquisition
  Two microarray datasets, GSE143272 and GSE63808, were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The GSE143272 dataset (GPL10558 platform, PMID: 30826443, 32054883), consisting of 50 and 34 blood samples obtained from healthy subjects (control group) and medication-free individuals with EP (EP group), respectively, was selected as the training set. Whole blood samples from 34 EP cases included idiopathic (n = 13), cryptogenic (n = 9), and symptomatic (n = 12) subtypes. The GSE63808 dataset (GPL6947 platform, PMID: 25615886), selected for validation, includes 129 Hip specimens obtained from patients undergoing selective amygdalo-hippocampectomy for mesial temporal lobe epilepsy. Patients experiencing only simple partial seizures were excluded. This current study utilized the R language (version 4.1.3).


#### Differentially Expressed Analysis of ATGs
  Herein, 222 genes were retrieved by accessing The Human Autophagy Database (http://www.autophagy.lu/index.html). By employing the “limma” R package [24], we adjusted the expression profile of the training set GSE143272 to screen differentially expressed disease-related genes. Moreover, the batch effect was eliminated, and the background was corrected. Genes with an adjusted p-value (adj-P) < 0.05 and log fold change | (log FC) |> 0.25 were identified as DEGs. Finally, the intersection between DEGs and Apg-related genes (ATGs) was defined as DE-ATGs and further analyzed in the following steps. The “ggplot2,” “VennDiagram,” and “pheatmap” R packages were employed to construct the volcano plot, Venn diagram, and heatmap, respectively.


#### Functional Enrichment Analysis of DEGs
  The potential functional enrichment of DEGs was primarily carried out using GO and KEGG enrichment analyses in R software with the aid of the “GOplot” and “clusterProfiler” packages. A p-value < 0.05 was deemed indicative of significant enrichment.


#### Protein–Protein Interaction (PPI) Analysis
  Correlation analysis of DE-ATGs was performed via the R package “corrplot” to reveal gene–gene correlations. The STRING online tool (https://cn.string-db.org/cgi/input.pl) was utilized to assess the PPI among DEGs. Using the cytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape software (version 3.8.1), we identified the top 10 hub genes within the PPI network.


#### Construction of Predictive Model and Nomogram Analyses
  For further validation of DE-ATGs’ diagnostic potential in EP, four ML models were constructed according to S. Li and Y. He et al. [25, 26]: support vector machine (SVM), random forest (RF), eXtreme gradient boosting (XGB), and generalized linear model (GLM). SVM is a potent categorization tool that functions by maximizing the separating margin between classes [27]. RF is a decision tree-based ML algorithm that demonstrates high predictive accuracy in classification tasks, leveraging bootstrap aggregation and randomized selection of predictors to enhance performance. XGB is an ensemble supervised learning algorithm depending on gradient-boosted trees that can reach increased prognostic precision with reduced computational demands in numerous practical applications [28, 29]. GLM is a statistical model that relates the expected value of a response variable to a linear combination of predictor variables [30]. These four ML methods were employed to determine significant prognostic genes among the DE-ATGs in EP. To produce the residual distribution of the four models, the R package “DALEX” was utilized. We deployed the R package “pROC” for the visualization of the areas under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (area under curve, AUC). The diagnostic model’s effectiveness was assessed in the GSE63808 dataset by employing ROC curve analysis. Additionally, a Spearman correlation analysis was conducted within the GSE63808 dataset, focusing on the primary predictive genes for EP to investigate the relationships between model-related genes and EP patients. In this analysis, the top five factors were considered the essential genes for prediction. A nomogram was created using these predictors to identify EP clusters using the “rms” R package. The “total score” was determined by summing the values assigned to each predictor. Decision curve analysis (DCA) and calibration curve analysis were utilized to evaluate the nomogram’s clinical value and predictive capability, respectively. A p-value < 0.05 was deemed significant.


#### Animals
  Healthy male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (200 ± 20 g, from the Experimental Animal Center at Guangxi Medical University) were habituated for 7 days under specific temperature, humidity, and lighting conditions (20–25 °C, 12-h light–dark cycle), with unlimited food and purified water before the trial commenced. The ethical committees of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University (2024-E123-01) approved all procedures. A total of 55 Sprague-Dawley rats were used for the establishment of the epilepsy model. Among these, 45 animals successfully developed status epilepticus (SE). Of the SE-induced rats, 11 died during the acute phase and 4 died during the chronic phase. Consequently, 30 rats that exhibited SE survived both phases and were included in the subsequent analyses. Additionally, a control group consisting of 32 Sprague-Dawley rats was included, of which two died. In total, 87 animals were used in this experiment. At the end of the experiment, rats were euthanized via an overdose of sodium pentobarbital. Death was confirmed by cessation of heartbeat and respiration, and all procedures adhered to ethical guidelines to minimize suffering.


#### LiCl-Pilo-Induced Chronic EP Model
  According to a previous report [20], the EP rats were intraperitoneally given LiCl (127 mg/kg, Sigma-Aldrich) 18 h prior to injecting Pilo (30 mg/kg) (MedChemExpress). Before Pilo administration by 30 min, atropine (1 mg/kg, intraperitoneal) (Sigma-Aldrich) was used to minimize peripheral cholinergic effects. As saline served as the solvent, control rats received an equivalent volume of normal saline concurrently with the initial atropine treatment. After Pilo injection, behavioral observations were conducted, and the seizures induced by Pilo were assessed according to Racine’s scale [31]. SE was characterized by uninterrupted seizures persisting for a minimum of 30 min, with no complete recovery between seizures. Diazepam (10 mg/kg, IP) (Kelun, China) was administered 30 min after the onset of sustained Racine stage IV–V seizures to stop convulsions. Animals in stage IV or above were selected for further testing. Intensive video monitoring began on day 10 post-SE to detect SRS onset. Hippocampal tissues were collected 14 days after the confirmed onset of SRS in each animal, ensuring a consistent chronic epileptic state across all LiCl-Pilo groups (total post-SE duration: ~ 4 weeks). Control rats were sacrificed and tissues were harvested at the same time points.


#### Stereotactic Brain Injection
  Rats were anesthetized with 3% sodium pentobarbital (30 mg/kg, IP). Anesthetic depth was monitored by loss of pedal withdrawal reflex and maintained throughout surgical procedures. The scalp was sanitized with 75% alcohol before being shaved. Subsequently, the skull’s surface was cleaned with 0.05% hydrogen peroxide to fully expose the coronal suture, sagittal suture, and fontanel. After setting the stereotactic apparatus to zero, the target location was identified as the right dorsal Hip, specifically positioned 1.00 mm posterior to the bregma, 2.00 mm lateral to the right of the midline, and 4.00 mm beneath the dura, followed by injecting KC7F2 (10 μg/kg, 0.5 μL/min) over 5 min into the lateral ventricle using a 5-μL syringe. The scalp was sutured, and the rats were kept warm until they woke up and returned to their cages. KC7F2, an inhibitor of HIF-1α protein translation, was provided by Selleck.cn (China). Because DMSO was used as the solvent, control rats received an equivalent volume of normal DMSO. Finally, the LiCl-Pilo EP model was constructed 24h after KC7F2/DMSO injection. The timeline for SRS monitoring and tissue harvesting in this group was identical to that of the pilocarpine-only group, as detailed in the previous section.


#### EEG Monitoring and Recording
  Rats were used to establish an EP model with LiCl-Pilo. When the rats exhibited seizures of grade IV or above, they were fixed in a rat restrainer. Electrodes were placed on the rats’ frontal, temporal, and forelimb regions. EEG recordings were performed using a digital video EEG monitoring system (Model: Neusen.U08, Boruikang Technology (Changzhou) Co., Ltd.). The electrodes were positioned and identified as follows: Fp1-Avf for the left frontal region, Fp2-Avf for the right frontal region, C3-Avf for the left central area, C4-Avf for the right central area, T3-Avf for the left temporal region, T4-Avf for the right temporal region, O1-Avf for the left occipital region, and O2-Avf for the right occipital region. When spontaneous seizures were recorded on video, EEG recordings were performed again.


#### Primary Neuron CultureIn Vitro
  The primary neuron culture protocol in this study was performed according to previously described procedures [32]. Before the experiment, serum-containing and serum-free neurobasal media were prepared using the following ratios: (1) serum-neurobasal medium: DMEM/F12 medium (88%, A4192001) + fetal bovine serum (10%, A3160902) + GlutaMAX™ Supplement (1%, 35050061) + penicillin–streptomycin (1%, 15140-122, all from Gibco). (2) Serum-free neurobasal medium: NEUROBASAL-A MEDIUM 1 × (96%, 10888022) + B27 supplement (2%, 17504044) + GlutaMAX™ Supplement (1%, 35050061) + penicillin–streptomycin (1%, 15140-122, all from Gibco). On the night before the experiment, 12-well culture dishes were prepared by adding poly-D-lysine (Gibco, A3890401) to each well, and then, a 10-h incubation was conducted at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere. On the day of the experiment, the Hip tissue of newborn SD rats was carefully separated in pre-cooled D-Hank’s solution (Thermo Fisher, #14185052), sliced, and digested with 0.125% trypsin at 37 °C for 10–15 min. The serum-neurobasal medium was applied to prevent digestion when the digestive fluid in the centrifuge tube became turbid or the tissue mass became filamentous. This was followed by centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 5 min, discarding the supernatant, and adding an appropriate volume of the serum-neurobasal medium. The undissociated tissue was filtered using a 40 nm nylon mesh strainer. Hip neurons were enumerated with a hemocytometer and plated in 2 mL of serum-supplemented neurobasal medium. After 10 h, the serum-comprising medium was eliminated, followed by replacing it with a serum-free neurobasal medium.


#### Hematoxylin-Eosin (H&E) and Nissl Staining
  To prepare for H&E staining, the rats were subjected to transcardial perfusion using cold saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) (Solarbio). Subsequently, at room temperature, the rat brains were fixed in PFA, subjected to gradient dehydration using ethanol, and then paraffin-embedded. The tissues were sliced into continuous 4 μm coronal sections and dried in an oven at 60 °C for 5 h, followed by H&E staining. For the Nissl staining procedure, coronal sections of the Hip were first dewaxed using xylene. The sections were then immersed in ethanol before being stained with 1% toluidine blue. After staining, dehydrating the sections was conducted with ethanol, then cleared using xylene, and finally sealed with neutral gum.


#### Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM)
  Cold saline perfusion was administered to the rats before they were perfused with a solution of 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 4% PFA in 0.1 M phosphate buffer at pH 7.4. The infusion was stopped when the muscles of the head and limbs of the rat became stiff, and the tail stood up in line with the trunk. Then, the brain tissues were promptly dissected. The freshly harvested Hip (1 mm3) was processed on ice and fixed overnight with pre-cooled 2.5% glutaraldehyde. After fixation with osmium tetroxide, dehydration in a series of graded alcohols, and infiltration with acetone and 812 embedding agents, the tissue samples were sliced into 60–80 nm ultrathin sections using an ultramicrotome. The sections were observed using a Hitachi HT7800 TEM. For quantification, ImageJ was utilized to analyze and measure the mitochondrial ultrastructure and area.


#### Immunofluorescence (IF) Labeling of Sections and Co‑localization Analysis
  The IF staining was carried out, as previously outlined [33]. For IF staining, we employed the subsequent primary antibodies: rabbit anti-HIF-1α (1:200, CST, 36,169), rat anti-BNIP3 (1:200, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-56167), rat anti-GFAP (1:2000, Servicebio, GB12096), and mouse anti-MAP (1:2000, Abcam). The secondary antibodies included fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled goat anti-rat IgG(H + L) (1:500, GB23303) and Cy3-labeled goat anti-rat IgG (1:300, GB21303, both from Servicebio). These antibodies were incubated at 4 °C for 1 h, followed by re-staining with DAPI. ImageJ 1.51w was used for co-localization analysis. After transforming the two 24-bit images from the red and green channels into two 8-bit images, the “Co-localization” command was executed to generate the results. Yellow pixels in the final images represented co-localized pixels.

  Neuronal IF co-localization analyses were conducted starting 8 days after culture, at which time neurons gradually formed neural network structures. The cultured neurons were subjected to three washes with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), fixed with PFA for 15 min, and then subjected to antigen retrieval using an antibody eluent. After being fixed with PFA for another 15 min, 0.1% Triton X-100 (Solarbio) was used for a 10-min neuron permeabilization, and 5% bovine serum albumin was employed for a 1-h blockage. The incubation of neurons was conducted at 4 °C overnight with an anti-HIF-1α antibody (1:200, CST, #36169). Subsequently, a red fluorescent secondary antibody (CST, #8889) was applied, and incubation was continued at 37 °C for 45 min. The incubation procedure for the anti-BNIP3 antibody (1:200, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-56167) was conducted in the same manner, followed by incubation with a green fluorescent secondary antibody (CST, #4408) for 45 min at 37 °C. Finally, a DAPI staining solution was applied, followed by a 5-min incubation at room temperature.


#### Real-Time Quantitative Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-qPCR)
  RNAiso Plus (Takara Biotechnology, Dalian, China) was utilized to isolate total RNA from the Hip of rats. As per the protocols, complementary DNA synthesis was conducted via the PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara Biotechnology, Dalian, China). The primer sequences for HIF-1α, BNIP3, and GAPDH were as follows: HIF-1α forward primer: GGATGGAATGGAGCAGAAGACAA; reverse primer: TGCCTTGTATGGGAGCATTAACT; BNIP3 forward primer: TGGATATGGGATTGGTCAAGTCG; reverse primer: GTTGTCAGACGCCTTCCAATGTA; and GAPDH forward primer: GACATGCCGCCTGGAGAAAC; reverse primer: AGCCCAGGATGCCCTTTAGT. Finally, RT-PCR was performed using a StepOne Plus thermocycler. The mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method, normalizing the relative mRNA expression of the target genes to GAPDH.


#### Western Blotting (WB)
  The rat Hip in each group was homogenized to extract protein using a modified RIPA buffer (R0010, Solarbio, Beijing, China) with a protein phosphatase suppressor (All-in-one) (P1260, Solarbio). A BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0012S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was utilized to assess the protein level of each sample and calculate its uptake. WB was performed according to a previously established protocol [34]. Protein samples were electrophoresed on SDS-PAGE gels, and then transferring to PVDF membranes (Millipore) was conducted, which were kept overnight in an incubator at 4 °C with the subsequent specific primary antibodies: rabbit anti-HIF-1α (1:800, CST, #36169), rabbit anti-BNIP3 (1:800, CST, #3769), rabbit anti-LC3B (1:1000, Abmart, T55992), rabbit anti-SQSTM1/p62 (1:1000, Sigma, SAB5700845), and rabbit anti-Tubulin (1:10000, Proteintech, 11224-1-AP). IRDye 800CW goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (1:10000, LI-COR, D11215-03), which was utilized as the secondary antibody. Lastly, scanning the membranes was conducted using an Odyssey Infrared Imaging System (LI-COR Biosciences) operating on the 700–800 nm channels to visualize the detected proteins. ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) was utilized for the quantification of the band densities, with β-tubulin or GAPDH serving as internal controls.


#### Co‑Immunoprecipitation (Co‑IP)
  The extraction of native proteins from the Hip was performed via the Minute™ Protein Extraction Kit (Invent Biotechnologies, SD-001/SN-002). The protein level was measured as shown above. The Co-IP experimental procedures were conducted as per the guidelines of the Immunoprecipitation (IP) Kit from Sangon Biotech (C600689). The antigen-antibody complex was formed by co-incubating the native total protein with the anti-HIF-1α antibody (1:30, CST, 36169) at 4 °C overnight. After centrifugation at 1000 g for 2 min, protein A/G Plus-Agarose was rinsed four times with PBS. The antigen-antibody complex was then added and incubated at 4 °C overnight. For IP, the agarose beads were sequentially rinsed with 1 × IP buffer followed by 0.1 × IP buffer. Subsequently, 1 × sample buffer was added, and the mixture was denatured by heating in a metal bath at 100 °C for 10 min. Then, a 30-s centrifugation was conducted at 4 °C and 12,000 g. The resulting protein samples were used for SDS-PAGE electrophoresis. A 5% skim milk was utilized for the blockage of PVDF membranes, which were then incubated with primary antibodies, such as rabbit anti-HIF-1α (1:800, 36169) and anti-BNIP3 (1:800, 3769S, both from CST), followed by secondary antibody incubation. Visualizing the immunoreactive bands was conducted as mentioned in the WB procedure.


#### Statistical Analysis
  GraphPad Prism (version 6.07), SPSS 26.0 (SPSS Inc., USA), and R software (version 4.3.1) were employed for data processing, statistical analysis, and graph visualization. Gene expression levels of the control and EP groups were compared via Student’s t-test. Correlation analysis was conducted using the Spearman test, while differences among groups in the datasets were compared using the Wilcoxon test. The data obtained from RT-qPCR and WB experiments are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (x ± SEM). To analyze and compare variations between the control and EP groups, a t-test was utilized. For multiple group comparisons, one-way ANOVA was conducted, followed by an LSD test for post-hoc analysis of multiple means. If the variances of continuous variables were unequal, Welch’s correction and Tamhane’s post-hoc test were applied. A p-value < 0.05 was deemed significant.

### Enrichment Function Analysis of DE-ATGs
  Both GO and KEGG functional analyses of the DE-ATGs were employed for exploring the probable functional relation of these DE-ATGs. Herein, 566 DEGs were uploaded for the analyses, and the results (Fig. 2D) showed that DE-ATGs participated in the biological processes (BP) of the regulation of positive cysteine-type endopeptidase activity, response to cytokine stimulus, cytokine-mediated signaling pathway, and necrotic cell death. The cellular components (CC) enrichment analysis results manifested that DE-ATGs mainly contributed to membrane rafts, membrane microdomains, inflammasome complexes, and autophagosomes. The identified molecular functions (MF) terms were cysteine-type endopeptidase/peptidase activity, ubiquitin protein ligase binding, and cysteine-type endopeptidase activity contributing to the apoptotic signaling pathway. At the same time, the KEGG analysis (Fig. 2E) represented that DE-ATGs were crucial in signaling pathways, including Apg, mitophagy, legionellosis, NOD-like receptors, and HIF-1 signaling pathway.

## Discussion
  Typically, EP occurs when brain networks are irregularly formed or disturbed by structural, infectious, or metabolic disorders, and some or all of the brain becomes abnormally synchronized with neuronal firing. The pathogenetic mechanisms of EP have not been completely elucidated. Previously recognized mechanisms of EP include gene mutations that regulate certain ion channels such as Na⁺, K⁺, and Ca2⁺ channels [35–38], as well as abnormal neural network formation. Clinical and experimental evidence suggests that prolonged seizures can lead to neuronal death and glial cell proliferation, thereby forming new networks that promote the initiation and spread of EP, reinforcing neuronal damage and creating a vicious cycle [39–41]. The mechanisms of action of currently used antiseizure medicines are achieved by interacting with a variety of cellular targets, such as voltage-gated ion channel modulation, enhanced γ-aminobutyric acid inhibition, and suppression of glutamate receptor-mediated synaptic excitation, among others [42]. Characterizing the molecular pathways contributing to seizure-triggered neuronal death may uncover innovative therapeutic approaches to protect the brain against prolonged or repetitive seizures.

  Bioinformatics analysis significantly contributes to the diagnosis and prediction of diseases, advancing our comprehension of disease progression through genome-level and comprehensive bioinformatics approaches. Further, due to the easy availability of blood samples, research on blood biomarkers for diagnosing EP and discovering therapeutic targets has broad application prospects. Herein, we screened DE-ATGs in the blood of EP individuals and combined several experimental validations to reveal their potential implication in neuronal damage in the LiCl-Pilo-provoked EP rat model. Accordingly, we obtained 11 DE-ATGs by intersecting DEGs and ATGs, including FADD, BNIP3, CTSD, ERBB2, GNAI3, HIF-1α, CASP8/4, MAP1LC3A, NLRC4, and DAPK2, suggesting that DEGs are essential in EP development. This study also analyzed their potential biological functions via GO and KEGG enrichment analyses, indicating several enriched terms in Apg and mitophagy pathways. The enrichment function findings depicted that these DEGs participated in different response processes such as Apg-animal, NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, legionellosis, and mitophagy-animal. Although Apg is crucial in neuronal death induced by EP [43–45], its disorders may cause EP.

  Conversely, an imbalance in Apg could also be a contributing factor to EP, potentially exacerbating the condition and creating a vicious cycle. The PPI is essential for the study of systems biology; each protein forms an interaction PPI network by interacting with one or more other proteins inside or outside the cell. Among the key node proteins (genes) obtained through PPI network screening, we found that a number of Apg-related proteins have been clearly shown to correlate with EP or epileptic comorbidities. For instance, TNFR1 may participate in the pathogenesis of refractory temporal lobe EP by mediating the combination of CASP8 and FADD [46]. Multi-omics analysis of heart tissue from LiCl-Pilo epileptic rats has elucidated that ERBB2 is a key regulator of epileptic-related cardiac changes [47]. These studies confirm the credibility of this research method. Nonetheless, the roles of HIF-1α and BNIP3 in EP have not yet been reported.

  Typically, HIF-1 is a crucial transcription factor consisting of HIF-1α/β subunits, with HIF-1α serving as the primary functional subunit, primarily accountable for controlling cellular adaptation to hypoxic conditions [48]. Furthermore, HIF-1α contributes to tumor development/metastasis, angiogenesis, inflammation, hypoxia-induced damage, neuronal apoptosis, and stem cell growth and differentiation [8–11], many of which intersect with the pathogenesis of EP. Additionally, HIF-1α stability and activity are regulated by numerous factors, including oxygen concentrations, intracellular iron levels, and the stimulation of alternative signaling pathways [48]. Moreover, HIF-1α is capable of inducing Apg via numerous pathways. Excessive fluoride exposure leads to HIF-1α signaling enhancement, thereby triggering Apg and activating Wnt/β-catenin signaling [49]. In alveolar type II epithelium infected with influenza A virus, downregulation of HIF-1α reduces glycolytic pathways, thereby activating AMPKα-mediated Apg [50]. Emerging evidence suggests that the HIF-1α/HO-1 pathway exacerbates oxidative stress in Hip neurons by mediating ferroptosis, contributing to epileptogenesis [15]. Animal studies demonstrate that pharmacological blockade of this pathway significantly reduces neuronal damage and improves PTZ-induced epileptic seizures [51]. Our experimental results provided empirical support for these prior findings by demonstrating that HIF-1α inhibition effectively mitigated neuronal damage and reduced the number of SRS. However, while Lucchi et al. [52] demonstrated HIF-1α co-localization with hypoxic foci (identified by pimonidazole) and microglial heme oxygenase-1 expression in peri-hemorrhagic regions following status epilepticus, indicating that vascular disruption contributes to secondary hippocampal damage; our study reveals a parallel autophagy-dependent pathway where HIF-1α/BNIP3 drives mitochondrial dysfunction independent of vascular pathology. Collectively, HIF-1α may drive hippocampal neurodegeneration via both vascular and autophagic routes, with pathway dominance likely determined by SE severity, strain susceptibility, or therapeutic interventions. To our knowledge, this study provides the first mechanistic evidence that the HIF-1α/BNIP3 pathway regulates epileptic seizures via modulation of Apg of Hip neuronal.

  The BNIP3, a Bcl-2 family member, is a target gene of HIF-1α, which is differentially regulated by HIF-1α under hypoxic conditions. Recently, research has revealed that HIF-1α and BNIP3 participate in hypoxia adaptation and facilitate Apg in specific disease conditions, including ovarian dysfunction [19], cardiomyocyte death [53], and EP-associated cognitive impairment [54]. Investigations have established that HIF-1α signaling is a robust inducer of BNIP3-modulated Apg. HIF-1α/BNIP3-modulated mitochondrial Apg protects renal tubular epithelium [55]; zinc oxide nanoparticles provoke osteosarcoma cell death via the HIF-1α/BNIP3/LC3B-modulated Apg pathway [56]. The HIF-1α/BNIP3-induced Apg pathway has a promotional or inhibitory role in diverse diseases. Nevertheless, HIF-1α/BNIP3 function in EP is unclear. Here, we observed an elevation in HIF-1α levels and a decline in BNIP3 levels through bioinformatics analysis. Our following in vivo trials in LiCl-Pilo-induced EP rats further indicated significantly elevated HIF-1α expression and diminished BNIP3 expression levels in the Hip tissue. Our findings suggest that HIF-1α and BNIP3 may be among the mechanisms of neuronal damage and epileptogenesis after EP. These outcomes may offer novel targets and approaches for treating EP.

  The loss of Hip neurons is pivotal in EP pathogenesis. Consistent with previous studies [57, 58], increased neuronal loss was also observed in the Hip of LiCl-Pilo receiving animals in this experiment. Studies on neuronal damage and its relationship with EP have focused on both apoptosis and necrotic cell death. Aberrant neuronal electrical activity may trigger post-epileptic apoptosis [59]. The neuronal apoptosis extent increased significantly during seizures [60, 61]. Necrotic cell death, an atypical cell demise, is commonly linked with conditions like hypoxia, acute ischemia, and inflammatory responses. In the context of EP, ischemia and hypoxia are frequent pathological and physiological changes resulting in neurodegeneration and neuron necrotic death [46]. Typically, Apg plays dual roles in cells, with its effects varying based on the stage of disease progression, changes in the cellular microenvironment, and differences in treatment interventions. External stimuli can trigger autophagic processes that are essential for removing damaged mitochondria, thus safeguarding cells from additional harm. It was shown that the autophagic pathway is activated after a seizure [62, 63]. During EP, impaired Hip neurons exhibit progressive Apg dysfunction, which may weaken their neuroprotective effect [64]. On the contrary, inhibition of Apg significantly reduces neuronal damage; e.g., Guan leaf coneflower (Hypericum perforatum L.) attenuates neuronal damage in the CA3 region after EP by enhancing antioxidant capacity and inhibiting excessive Apg [65]. Apg is currently associated with neuronal function through a variety of mechanisms, of which homeostatic synaptic plasticity regulation is an important potential mechanism [66]. Therefore, targeting Apg dynamic homeostasis regulation may become a new strategy for EP treatment.

  This evidence implies a connection between EP and Apg, highlighting that controlling the autophagic process might be an effective treatment strategy. These findings are further supported by our animal experiments, which show that significantly elevated Apg levels in Hip neurons of epileptic rats contribute to EP progression. Nevertheless, the precise molecular mechanisms driving this process remain to be further explored.

  Investigations have demonstrated that HIF-1α signaling is a robust inducer of BNIP3-modulated Apg. The HIF-1α/BNIP3-induced Apg pathway promotes or suppresses different diseases [19, 53, 55, 56]. The function of HIF-1α/BNIP3 signaling in regulating Apg in the Hip and its contribution to EP development remains unclear. KC7F2 is a small molecule compound with a hemiamine-centered structure, which exerts its ability to inhibit HIF-1α protein synthesis at the translational level by inhibiting 4E-binding protein and the phosphorylation of S6K [67]. KC7F2 was initially developed as a cancer therapeutic target. Recent studies have found that KC7F2 can effectively intervene in HIF-1α protein expression and play a role in different areas of disease mechanisms [16, 68, 69]. Our experimental results found that from 24 h after the injection of KC7F2 into the lateral ventricle of rats, the HIF-1α level decreased most significantly, unlike the control; at this time, the BNIP3 level increased most significantly, which was correlated with HIF-1α expression. The experimental data and the IP results further confirm our hypothesis that HIF-1α and BNIP3 are co-localized and that HIF-1α negatively regulates BNIP3 expression. Overall, inhibition of HIF-1α expression resulted in reduced Apg, alleviated neuronal damage, and prolonged seizure latency. This finding offers innovative targets and approaches for EP treatment. Suppressing the stimulation of the HIF-1α/BNIP3 pathway and controlling Apg provide possible methods for treating EP.

  Artificial intelligence has gained significant traction in clinical settings recently. Recent years have seen an increasing reliance on ML models that utilize demographic and imaging metrics for predicting disease occurrence. ML models have demonstrated robust predictive performance in diagnostic investigations of tuberculosis subtypes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and Alzheimer’s disease [25, 70, 71]. Herein, as per the residual and AUC, the ML model of XGB performed well in predicting EP. Five crucial variables (BNIP3, ERBB2, CTSD, CASP8, and CASP4) were then chosen to build the XGB model. A previous study has demonstrated that BNIP3 may be involved in aggravating cognitive deterioration via the PARP1/BNIP3/AIF/Endo G signaling pathway in PTZ-induced kindling mice [54], indicating BNIP3’s potential role in EP pathogenesis. The Erbb receptor belongs to the tyrosine kinase family and is related to cardiotoxicity. An investigation utilizing comprehensive whole transcriptome and proteome analyses in a temporal lobe EP rat model revealed that Erbb2 and Erbb3 may be involved in seizure-induced cardiac alterations [47]. Cathepsin D (CTSD), a prominent lysosomal enzyme, contributes to protein and organelle degradation through the Apg-lysosomal pathway [72, 73]. Moreover, CTSD is recognized for its role in neuronal loss and removal of degenerative substances in several experimental contexts [74]. Research indicates that LC3-positive puncta and cytosolic aggregates with p62 increase in the thalamus of CNS-specific CTSD-knockout mice, indicating the potential involvement of CTSD in the Apg-lysosomal pathway in neurons [75]. In another in vivo experiment, CTSD levels and activity in the Hip of rats treated with kainic acid rose over time compared to control animals injected with saline. Caspase-8 is an apical caspase that becomes active due to the initial inflammatory response caused by seizures and the release of TNF-α, leading to DNA damage [76]. Nonetheless, the role of caspase-4 is still not understood and requires more investigation. The results of the above study showed that the five key genes extracted using the XGB model related to EP prediction fit with the findings of previous studies, and this result further confirms the validity and reliability of the XGB model.

  Mitochondria represent the main source of intracellular ROS and are especially vulnerable to oxidative stress. Excess ROS triggers oxidative stress and promotes altered mitochondrial Apg when mitochondrial dysfunction occurs. Particularly, the brain is vulnerable to oxidative stress owing to its high oxygen consumption and the buildup of lipid peroxides. Therefore, mitochondrial Apg abnormalities (uncontrolled or insufficient) due to oxidative stress-induced mitochondrial defects are highly likely to exacerbate the progression of EP. Abnormal mitochondrial morphology and reduced expression levels of ROS, superoxide dismutase, and peroxidase GPX4 have manifested in EP animal models [15, 77]. Here, LiCl-Pilo epileptic rat Hip tissues showed altered mitochondrial morphology and structure, and the mitochondrial area was reduced; after inhibiting HIF-1α/BNIP3 signaling, the Apg level in the Hip was diminished in the KC7F2 group relative to the DMSO group, neuronal edema was reduced, and the mitochondrial area was enlarged in the SEM. Therefore, we hypothesize that pharmacological inhibition of the HIF-1α/BNIP3 signaling axis confers neuroprotection through attenuation of oxidative neuronal damage.

  In summary, HIF-1α/BNIP3 signaling enhances the Hip Apg level, and excessive Apg exacerbates Hip neuronal damage and EP progression, whereas inhibition of HIF-1α/BNIP3 reduces neuronal damage by suppressing the level of Apg. This discovery provides novel targets for treatment and potential mechanistic research of EP. Furthermore, the technique used for detection depending on ATG expression in peripheral blood offers a cost-effective and easily acceptable approach for identifying patients with prodromal symptoms of EP. It shows promise for near-future clinical application. However, some limitations in this research should be acknowledged. First, BNIP3 is a key DEG for diagnosing EP. Other DEGs, including CTSD, ERBB2, CASP8, CASP4, MAP1LC3A, and DAPK2, could possibly serve as potential diagnostic biomarkers for EP; however, further research is needed on these genes. Second, ATGs must be updated with discovering novel genes; thus, this study has room for development. Third, the potential relationship between the HIF-1α/BNIP3 pathway and Apg has not been thoroughly investigated. It has been shown that BNIP3 may induce Apg by disrupting the interaction of Beclin 1 with Bcl-2 and Bcl-XL, thus necessitating further studies. Finally, upcoming investigations should comprise gathering more clinical samples to examine the association between the HIF-1α/BNIP3 pathway and epileptogenesis in human subjects with EP.
