## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text including abstract, introduction, results, discussion, methods, and supplementary figure/table references.
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific validity of claims, methodological soundness, interpretation of results, and alignment with stated conclusions. No assessment of editorial fit or publication policy.
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors assess whether AlphaFold2, AlphaFold2-Multimer, and Boltz-1 can distinguish experimentally validated leucine zipper dimers from non-dimerizing pairs. They report that AlphaFold2-Multimer predicts high-confidence structures for the FosB homodimer, which is known not to form in vivo, and that confidence metrics (pLDDT, PAE, ipTM) do not differentiate probable from improbable dimers across a large dataset of human leucine zippers. They conclude that current deep learning methods are not well suited to predict dimerization specificity in this protein family.
- **Visible evidence base** Full text, figures referenced (Figures 1-7), tables (Table 1), supplementary figure references (Supplementary Figures 1-7), and methods descriptions. Raw data files are referenced but not provided.
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Raw data files (Supplementary Files L-zip dimer pairs AlphaFold confidence metrics.xlsx and L-zip dimer pairs Newman Ranks and structures.xlsx) are not available for independent verification. Supplementary figures are referenced but not viewable in this submission. Specific AlphaFold version and parameter details for the large-scale analysis are partially described. Statistical analysis details for AUC-ROC calculations are not fully specified.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript addresses an important and timely question: whether AlphaFold-family methods can reliably predict leucine zipper dimerization specificity. The authors use a well-characterized experimental dataset (Newman and Keating, 2003) and a focused case study (FosB/JunD) to demonstrate that high-confidence structural predictions do not correlate with experimentally determined dimerization propensity. The core observation, that AlphaFold2-Multimer produces structurally plausible but biologically incorrect dimers with high confidence scores, is clearly documented and reproducible from the described methods. However, the manuscript has several weaknesses that limit the strength of its conclusions. The analysis of the large-scale dataset relies on a simplified binary classification of "true" versus "false" dimers that may not capture the quantitative nature of the original experimental data. The comparison between AlphaFold2 and Boltz-1 is confounded by different input sequence lengths and the use of different databases. The discussion of why AlphaFold fails to account for electrostatics is speculative and not directly tested. The manuscript would be strengthened by a more rigorous quantitative analysis, direct comparison of prediction confidence against experimental signal strength, and a clearer articulation of what specific improvements in methodology would be needed to overcome the identified limitations.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Structural biologists, computational biologists, and researchers using AlphaFold for protein-protein interaction prediction would be the primary audience. The results are also relevant to the broader community studying transcription factor networks and combinatorial control, as the authors demonstrate a specific case where AlphaFold predictions could be misleading. Researchers developing or benchmarking protein structure prediction methods would find the systematic evaluation of confidence metrics valuable. The manuscript provides a cautionary example that is directly useful for anyone interpreting AlphaFold outputs for coiled-coil systems.
- **Major strengths** The manuscript addresses a question of high practical importance with a well-designed case study. The use of the Newman and Keating (2003) dataset provides a large, experimentally validated benchmark that is appropriate for the question asked. The authors clearly demonstrate that multiple confidence metrics (pLDDT, PAE, ipTM) fail to discriminate between known dimers and non-dimers, which is a robust and important finding. The inclusion of Boltz-1 as a newer method strengthens the conclusion that this is a general limitation of current approaches, not specific to one implementation. The writing is clear and the figures are well-designed to support the main claims.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **Concern ID** R1-M1
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Data analysis and interpretation
  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that "both approaches have a high false-positive rate (83.5% for AlphaFold2 and 63.1% for Boltz-1) when using ipTM" and that "ipTM scores have almost no discrimination power (AUC-ROC = 0.566) between true and false binders."
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section "Analysis of an array of validated human L-zip proteins shows a high false positive rate of dimer formation", Figure 7a-b
  - **Concern** The analysis dichotomizes the Newman and Keating (2003) data into "true dimers" (rank >4) and "false dimers" (rank <3), discarding the quantitative information in the original experimental measurements. The Newman and Keating data are based on a quantitative dimerization assay with continuous signal intensities, and the rank thresholds used here are arbitrary. This binary classification may misrepresent borderline cases and inflate or deflate the reported false-positive rates. Furthermore, the AUC-ROC analysis treats the prediction task as binary classification, but the underlying experimental data are ordinal. A more appropriate analysis would correlate predicted confidence scores with the continuous experimental signal, or use rank-based correlation measures. The reported false-positive rates are also dependent on the chosen threshold for "high confidence" predictions, which is not clearly justified.
  - **Why it matters** The central quantitative claim of the manuscript, that AlphaFold methods cannot discriminate true from false dimers, rests on this analysis. If the binary classification is inappropriate or the thresholds are arbitrary, the reported false-positive rates and AUC-ROC values may not accurately reflect the methods' performance. This could either overstate or understate the limitations of AlphaFold for this application.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should re-analyze the data using the continuous experimental signal from Newman and Keating (2003) where available, or use ordinal regression or rank correlation approaches. They should also report the distribution of predicted confidence scores across the full range of experimental ranks, not just the dichotomized groups. Sensitivity analysis with different rank thresholds should be provided.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M2
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Methodological consistency
  - **Claim pointer** The authors state that "we used AlphaFold v2.3.2 to predict all the potential dimer pairs" for the large-scale analysis, and that "Boltz-1 v0.41 was run with default settings plus the inclusion of the --write_full_pae option."
  - **Evidence pointer** Methods section, paragraph on large-scale dimer prediction
  - **Concern** The manuscript does not clearly specify whether the AlphaFold2 large-scale analysis used the same ColabFold settings as the case study (msa_mode "mmseqs2_uniref_env", model_type "alphafold2_multimer_v3", template_mode "pdb100") or different settings. The methods state that AlphaFold v2.3.2 was used with "the small BFD database," which differs from the ColabFold setup used for the FosB/JunD case study. This inconsistency makes it difficult to determine whether the observed failure to discriminate dimers is due to the method itself or to specific implementation choices. Additionally, the input sequences for the large-scale analysis are described as "the residue sequences listed in the Supplementary Material of Newman and Keating's (2003) paper," but it is not clear whether these include the full leucine zipper domains or only the heptad repeat regions, and whether the sequence lengths are comparable between the AlphaFold2 and Boltz-1 runs.
  - **Why it matters** The comparison between AlphaFold2 and Boltz-1 is a key part of the manuscript's contribution. If the two methods were run with different input sequences, different databases, or different parameter settings, the observed differences in performance could be attributed to these confounds rather than to the methods themselves. The reproducibility of the results depends on clear specification of all computational parameters.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should provide a complete specification of all computational parameters for both AlphaFold2 and Boltz-1 runs in the large-scale analysis, including input sequence definitions, database versions, model versions, and any non-default settings. They should also confirm that the input sequences were identical for both methods, or explain any differences and justify why they do not affect the comparison.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M3
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Interpretation and generalizability
  - **Claim pointer** The authors conclude that "current deep learning models for protein structure prediction are not well suited to distinguish between functionally relevant and irrelevant L-zip dimer pairs."
  - **Evidence pointer** Discussion section, final paragraph
  - **Concern** The manuscript demonstrates that confidence metrics from AlphaFold2 and Boltz-1 do not correlate with experimentally determined dimerization propensity for the specific dataset tested. However, the conclusion is stated in very broad terms that may overgeneralize. The Newman and Keating (2003) dataset is limited to human leucine zippers and uses a specific experimental assay. The authors do not test whether other computational approaches, such as co-evolutionary analysis, molecular dynamics, or dedicated protein-protein interaction predictors, might perform better. The claim that the methods are "not well suited" could be interpreted as a definitive statement about the methods' general utility, when the evidence only supports a more limited claim about the specific test case. Additionally, the authors do not discuss whether the failure is specific to coiled-coil systems or might extend to other protein-protein interaction classes.
  - **Why it matters** The manuscript's title and framing suggest a general assessment of AlphaFold validity for leucine zipper constructs. If the conclusion is overstated, it could mislead readers into dismissing AlphaFold for applications where it may be adequate, or conversely, into over-trusting the negative result without considering alternative approaches. A more nuanced discussion of the scope and limits of the conclusion would strengthen the manuscript.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should temper the conclusion to reflect the specific scope of their analysis, and discuss what additional evidence would be needed to support a broader claim. They should also consider whether any alternative computational approaches could be tested with the same dataset to provide a more complete picture.

  - **Required correction** Report 95% confidence intervals for the AUC-ROC values, or provide a bootstrap analysis to quantify uncertainty.

  - **Issue** The authors attribute the failure of AlphaFold to account for electrostatics, but they do not directly test this hypothesis. The observation that confidence metrics are high for electrostatically unfavorable dimers is consistent with the hypothesis, but alternative explanations (e.g., insufficient training data for such dimers, or limitations in the multiple sequence alignment) are not discussed.
  - **Required correction** Acknowledge alternative explanations and consider whether any additional analyses could distinguish between them.

  - **Issue** The manuscript does not discuss prior work on evaluating AlphaFold for coiled-coil or protein-protein interaction prediction, beyond citing the original AlphaFold papers. There is a growing literature on benchmarking AlphaFold for multimeric interactions that could provide context for the authors' findings.
  - **Required correction** Add a brief discussion of related benchmarking studies and how the present results compare.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** The primary technical failing is the lack of a clear, quantitative link between the continuous experimental measurements from Newman and Keating (2003) and the binary classification used for the large-scale analysis. The manuscript must demonstrate that the dichotomization does not materially affect the conclusions. Additionally, the inconsistency in computational settings between the case study and the large-scale analysis must be resolved and fully documented. Without these clarifications, the central quantitative claims cannot be fully evaluated.
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** 
  - **Originality** The manuscript addresses a question that has been discussed anecdotally but not systematically tested with a large experimental dataset. The use of the Newman and Keating (2003) benchmark is a valuable contribution. However, the finding that AlphaFold confidence metrics do not correlate with binding specificity is not entirely unexpected, and the manuscript does not propose a novel solution or methodological advance. The originality is moderate.
  - **Scientific importance** The question of whether AlphaFold can predict protein-protein interaction specificity is of high importance to the structural biology and computational biology communities. The manuscript provides a clear negative result that is likely to be widely cited. However, the importance is somewhat limited by the narrow focus on leucine zippers and the lack of a proposed path forward.
  - **Interdisciplinary readership** The manuscript is likely to appeal to structural biologists, computational biologists, and researchers studying transcription factor networks. The writing is accessible to a broad scientific audience, and the figures are clear. However, the technical details of the analysis may limit appeal to non-specialists.
  - **Technical soundness** The case study is technically sound, with appropriate use of established methods and clear presentation of results. The large-scale analysis has methodological weaknesses as described above, particularly regarding the binary classification of experimental data and the consistency of computational settings. The statistical analysis is basic and could be strengthened.
  - **Readability for nonspecialists** The manuscript is generally well-written and the main conclusions are clearly stated. The introduction provides sufficient background for non-specialists to understand the biological context. However, the methods section assumes familiarity with AlphaFold and related tools, and some terms (e.g., ipTM, pLDDT) are not fully explained for a general audience.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The core observation, that AlphaFold2-Multimer predicts high-confidence structures for a known non-dimer, is clearly demonstrated and is an important cautionary example. The large-scale analysis has the potential to be a valuable benchmark, but the methodological issues described above must be addressed before the quantitative claims can be accepted. The manuscript would be suitable for publication in a specialized structural biology or computational biology journal, but would likely not meet the broad-interest threshold for Nature.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that "current deep learning models for protein structure prediction are not well suited to distinguish between functionally relevant and irrelevant L-zip dimer pairs" is broader than the evidence supports, as only two methods (AlphaFold2 and Boltz-1) were tested on a single dataset.
- The claim that "both approaches have a high false-positive rate" depends on the arbitrary binary classification of the Newman and Keating (2003) data and the chosen confidence thresholds, which are not justified.
- The statement that "removing the MSAs or the templates from the calculation made no meaningful difference" is based on visual inspection of structures and confidence metrics, but no quantitative comparison is provided.
- The assertion that the FosB homodimer "cannot form stable homodimers due to repelling charges" is presented as established fact, but the evidence for this is based on prior literature and the Newman and Keating (2003) data, which is not described in detail.
- The claim that "it remains unclear what the biologically relevant oligomeric structure of ΔFosB is" is appropriately cautious, but the discussion of this point is speculative and not directly tested.
