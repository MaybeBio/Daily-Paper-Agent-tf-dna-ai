# In silico analysis of the functional implications of drug resistance associated mutations in Mycobacterium tuberculosis


## Abstract
  The tuberculosis mutation catalogue published by World Health Organization (WHO) lists a large number of mutations based on the statistical significance of their association with resistance or susceptibility to various drugs. However, the mechanism by which they confer resistance to drugs is often not understood. To address these gaps, we combined known resistance associated mutations from the WHO catalogue and newly discovered mutations by explainable artificial intelligence (XAI). In order to decipher the mechanistic basis of drug resistance, we examined where these mutations occur in three dimensional (3D) structures of key drug targets, measured their proximity to drug binding sites and compared their abundance in drug resistant as well as drug susceptible Mycobacterium tuberculosis (M.tb) strains. In parallel, we analyzed the functions of 112 newly identified drug resistance associated genes and compared them to known resistance genes, finding that most novel genes fall into different functional categories, though six share families with known resistance genes. We mapped coding mutations in all 112 novel genes to their functional domains, predicted 3D structures using Alphafold3 and evaluated their effects on protein stability. Notably, our study highlights that mutations in ribosomal proteins (RpsN1, RpsN2) and the transporter PstB may introduce new resistance mechanisms, such as altered drug interactions or increased drug efflux. Whereas, analysis of non coding mutations revealed that most are located at transcription factor binding sites, potentially affecting gene regulation. The current analysis provides valuable insights for the design of experiments to decode mechanistic basis of drug resistance tuberculosis.


## Introduction
  The emergence of drug resistant tuberculosis (TB) presents a significant challenge to global health, highlighting the urgent need for a comprehensive understanding of the genetic mechanisms, including mutations, insertions, deletions and frame shift (fs), that confer resistance to antitubercular drugs [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) has taken significant steps to address this issue by cataloguing mutations associated with drug resistance in Mycobacterium tuberculosis (M.tb). WHO has listed over 17,000 mutations occurring in selected regions of the M.tb complex associated with resistance to 14 anti-tuberculosis drugs [2]. Even though WHO carefully curated each mutation by interpreting whether it is associated with drug resistance or not, considerable gaps are still there in our understanding of the full range of mutations linked to drug resistance. The role of numerous mutations linked to resistance remains inadequately understood, resulting in difficulties in accurately predicting susceptibility or resistance to different drugs [3], [4]. Non-synonymous mutations in the protein-coding region can have a significant impact on protein structure and function, influencing its interactions with drugs, proteins, and DNA. On the other hand, mutations in noncoding regions and even synonymous mutations in coding regions can alter the expression of protein coding genes. While in silico predictions of the functional implications of noncoding mutations is a challenging task, experimental studies involving gene expression analysis provide valuable information on the sequence motifs in the noncoding regions, which are binding sites for regulatory proteins. The databases like the TB Genome database [5] have integrated ChIP-seq and expression data for 50 transcriptional regulatory factors in M.tb, to provide information on the binding sites of these regulators, which are associated with processes such as hypoxic response, lipid catabolism, lipid anabolism, and cell wall lipid production. Hence, information from TB Genome database can be used to map drug resistance associated noncoding mutations onto binding sites of these transcriptional regulatory factors. This analysis can help in deciphering how mutations in the noncoding regions can potentially alter the expression of key genes.

  The relationship between genetic variations and their structural implications on the three-dimensional (3D) protein structure is complex and challenging to elucidate. In the context of M.tb, distinguishing between drug susceptible and drug resistant populations based solely on genetic abundance or statistical analysis does not provide a clear understanding of how specific mutations affect drug binding. Therefore, a comprehensive approach that integrates genetic data with structural biology is necessary to determine whether these mutations have a substantial effect on folding stability of resistance associated proteins and binding of drugs to their targets. The analysis of the interactions of the drugs with their targets can provide insights into how resistance-associated mutations affect drug binding [6]. On the other hand, mutations in regulatory proteins, such as transcription factors and transporters or efflux pumps, can also affect the activity of the drugs by altering the expression of the drug target or the influx/efflux of the drugs. Numerous studies have been published that focus on the structural analysis of the rifampicin in resistance determining region in M.tb [4], [7], [8], [9]. Rifampicin is a first-line antibiotic that targets bacterial beta subunit of RNA polymerase (RpoB), inhibiting its activity and disrupting the transcription process essential for bacterial growth and survival. These studies investigate specific mutations within the rifampicin resistance determining region (RRDR), including Asp441Val, Ser456Trp, Ser456Gln, Arg454Gln, His451Gly, and His451Pro [10]. These mutations can alter the interaction between rifampicin and RpoB by reducing the binding affinity for rifampicin, ultimately resulting in drug resistance. In addition, research on fluoroquinolones, particularly moxifloxacin, has revealed critical insights into how these drugs interact with DNA gyrase, a key enzyme in bacterial DNA replication [11], [12]. For instance, a study demonstrated that moxifloxacin interacts with Ala90 residue within the DNA gyrase complex, highlighting important interactions that influence the moxifloxacin efficacy against M.tb [12], [13]. Majority of such studies have analysed functional implications of known resistance associated mutations, which have been listed in WHO mutation catalog for tuberculosis. However, functional basis of new mutations associated with drug resistance of various antibiotics have not been analysed in such studies.

  In a recent work [14], we used explainable AI (XAI) techniques to develop TB-AMRpred software, which can identify new mutations not listed in WHO catalog that significantly contribute to drug resistance for 13 antibiotics. TB-MRpred takes either whole genome fasta file or NGS read file as input and predicts the resistance or susceptibility status of the input sample for 13 antibiotics. If the input sample is predicted to be resistant for a given antibiotic, using XAI method TB-AMRpred also identifies the mutations (with respect to the H37Rv reference genome [15]) which contribute significantly to the resistance for the corresponding antibiotic. In addition to the known resistance associated mutations which are mentioned in WHO catalog, it can also predict novel mutations associated with drug resistance. This approach involved development of a XGBoost or ANN classifier for prediction of genotypic drug resistance and identifying mutations which contribute maximally to the classification, based on SHAP analysis. For example, we detected 55 streptomycin resistant M.tb isolates that did not harbour any known resistance associated mutations. Hence, knowledge based tools such as TB-Profiler which use WHO mutation catalog were unable to classify them as drug resistant isolates. Using our XAI approach, we identified novel mutations, including Asp55Asn in Rv0012, Lys31Glu in Rv0893c, and Thr61Met in pstB, which were potentially associated with streptomycin resistance in these isolates. Incorporation of these newly identified mutations as resistance markers in TB-AMRpred enabled accurate classification of all 55 isolates as streptomycin resistant, in agreement with their phenotypic DST result. Therefore, it is crucial to examine the functional impact of both well known drug resistance mutations listed in WHO mutation catalog and new mutations identified by novel AI/ML approaches like TB-AMRpred.

  Thus, in this study, our primary objective is to perform a functional comparison between drug resistance associated known and newly discovered genes through Gene Ontology (GO) [16]. This analysis will aid in clarifying the various mechanisms by which drugs influence M.tb strains and explain the possible functions of proteins related to drug resistance. Afterwards, our goal is to project all known mutations linked to drug resistance onto the 3D structures of relevant proteins. We primarily focused on providing detailed information about known drug resistance associated coding mutations occurring in drug targets, specifying whether these mutations are located within the drug-binding pocket or away from the binding pocket. Such structural mappings can facilitate straightforward interpretation of the effects on resistance associated mutations on drug binding. We have mapped all known and newly identified drug resistance (DR) associated mutations predicted by XAI method and mapped them onto 3D structures of RpoB, KatG, InhA, EmbB, GyrA, and GyrB, which are targets for rifampicin, isoniazid, ethambutol, and moxifloxacin, respectively. This data is made available through the MutMapTB resource, accessible at http://www.nii.ac.in/MutMapTB_database.html. Furthermore, detailed structural analysis and docking studies were also carried out to decipher the mechanistic basis of drug resistance arising from a notable mutation, the RpoB F433dup variant. In addition, the effect of the DR associated mutations on protein stability was also analysed. For non-coding mutations, we analysed if these mutations were present in nucleic acid stretches associated with binding of transcription factor and other regulatory proteins and their possible impacts on the expression of adjacent genes.


## Materials and methods
  Fig. 1 provides an overview of the workflow used to analyse both known and novel drug DR associated mutations. Initially, Gene Ontology (GO) mapping was performed for known drug resistance genes listed in the WHO catalogue and also for novel genes predicted by our XAI model. This enabled identification of shared biological functions. Subsequently, we conducted a mutation-based analysis for both gene sets. Known mutations were taken from the WHO catalogue, while additional novel mutations were incorporated from our XAI predictions. For mutations occurring in drug target proteins, each mutation was mapped onto the corresponding protein structure, and its spatial proximity to the bound drug molecule was determined. For mutations located in non-target (accessory) proteins, we examined their occurrence within functional domains and evaluated their impact on protein stability. The same analyses were extended to novel mutations predicted by the XAI approach. ### Compilation of resistance associated genes predicted by XAI and WHO mutation catalogue

Hence, new drugs like Bedaquiline, Delamanid and Pretomanid etc, with limited data have not been included in the current study. ### Gene ontology analysis for functional annotation of known and novel drug resistance associated genes
  We utilised the Gene Ontology Resource to collect Gene Ontology (GO) terms for molecular functions associated with both novel and known DR associated genes, aiming to determine if they share similar protein families/functional categories and identify a potential link between the novel genes and corresponding drug resistance (Supplementary Methods).


### Analysis of the 3D structural environment of resistance associated mutations in coding regions
  Out of the drug targets mentioned in Table 1 for 10 anti-tubercular drugs, we checked the availability of 3D crystal structures in PDB. We then mapped all the known resistance-associated mutations (labelled “Assoc w R” by the WHO), along with novel resistance-associated mutations predicted by our XAI method, onto drug-bound 3D crystal structures of the targets. To analyse the mutations that significantly affect structural interactions with drugs or within the binding pocket, we set a cutoff distance of 5 Å between the nearest ligand atom and the nearest atom of the mutated residue. This 5 Å range indicates that the ligand is close enough to engage in interactions where van der Waals forces and hydrogen bonding can play a crucial role in stabilizing the drug-protein complex. Conversely, if the distance exceeds 5 Å, the mutated residue may influence ligand binding indirectly or through allosteric mechanisms. This means that while the mutated residue might not directly interact with the drug, it could still affect the folding stability and overall conformation of the protein, which can potentially alter the drug binding (Supplementary Methods)


### Docking of a duplicated (dup) phenylalanine (phe) at position 433 of the RpoB protein
  As a case study, we investigated the binding interactions of Rifampicin with both wild-type RpoB and the RpoB F433dup mutant. We utilized the crystal structure of RpoB (PDB: 5UH6) and performed redocking analysis for the wild-type protein using AutoDock Vina [17]. Subsequently, we modelled the 3D structure of the RpoB F433dup mutant using SWISS-MODEL [18] and conducted molecular docking analysis to evaluate its binding with Rifampicin. Further details on the computational protocols, including receptor and ligand preparation, as well as docking parameters, are provided in the Supplementary Methods.


### Abundance analysis of resistance associated mutations occurring at drug targets and evaluation of their significance for association with drug resistance
  After identifying known DR associated mutations and their proximity to drug ligands, we investigated the abundance of these mutations in both drug resistant and drug susceptible M.tb populations. We analyzed 24,067 M.tb genomes from BV-BRC [19] and CRyPTIC [20] databases (Supplementary File 2), which included laboratory based drug susceptibility testing (DST) results for Isoniazid, Rifampicin, Ethambutol, and Moxifloxacin, for which the drug target 3D structures were available out of the 10 drugs mentioned previously. Using the SNIPPY [21] variant calling pipeline, we identified genetic variations and compared the abundance of known drug resistance mutations at target sites across drug resistant and susceptible strains. This analysis helped us to clarify the relationship between mutations that are strongly associated with drug resistance and their potential presence even in drug susceptible populations of M.tb (Supplementary Methods)


### Database implementation
  The MutMapTB database has been developed to provide public access to spatial and genetic abundance data on drug resistance and susceptibility for all known mutations in antitubercular drug targets with available 3D crystal structures and drug binding site information, including KatG, InhA, EmbB, GyrA, GyrB, and RpoB (Supplementary Methods).


### Mapping mutations to protein domains
  After mapping and analyzing known mutations occurring in drug targets, a systematic analysis was conducted for mutations in known DR associated genes other than drug targets (accessory proteins), as well as for newly identified mutations in novel genes. These mutations were mapped to their corresponding protein domains using to HMMscan tool [22] to determine whether they reside within any functional or nonfunctional domains. This approach enables us to identify mutations that may disrupt essential protein functions, thereby contributing to our understanding of their biological significance. (Supplementary Methods)


### Assessing structural stability impacts of XAI predicted drug resistance mutations in accessory and novel proteins using 3D structures
  After mapping mutations onto protein functional domains, we assessed their impact on protein stability to understand how they might contribute to drug resistance. Destabilizing mutations within functional domains can compromise structural integrity, potentially hindering drug-protein, protein-protein, or protein-DNA interactions. We used available crystal structures or AlphaFold predicted structure for all the accessory as well as novel proteins and employed mCSM software [23] to analyze the effects of mutations on stability based on ΔΔG values. This analysis provides valuable insights into the relationship between mutations and protein stability in the context of drug resistance. (details are in Supplementary Methods)


### Analysis of the resistance associated mutations in the non-coding regions
  Additionally, both known non-coding mutations in the WHO list and novel non-coding mutations predicted by our XAI method were further analysed. To map non-coding mutations, we utilized the “TB Genome Database” specifically its comprehensive regulatory networks derived from transcriptomic and ChIP-seq data for 50 Mycobacterium regulatory proteins. When predicted non-coding mutations occur within these 50 regulatory protein binding site regions, we inferred that they may affect regulatory binding. This alteration could upregulate drug-inactivating enzymes or downregulate drug-activating enzymes by modifying the binding affinities between transcription factors (TFs) and DNA, thereby altering the recruitment of transcriptional complexes and potentially increasing drug resistance. (Supplementary Methods)


## Results

### Gene Ontology analysis for functional annotation of known and novel drug resistance associated genes
  The XAI approach implemented in TB-AMRpred had identified resistance associated mutations for each of the 13 antitubercular drugs. While some of them were present on 11 known DR associated genes (e.g., katG, inhA, embB, rpoB/rpoC, pncA, 16 s rRNA, eis, rpsL, gidB, gyrA/gyrB, ethA) for these antibiotics, other mutations were mapped to 112 novel genes (Supplementary File 3). GO analysis revealed molecular function for 11 known genes and 72 novel genes, cellular component for 4 16S RNA genes, and no function could be assigned for 36 novel DR associated genes. As can be seen, the novel genes represent a more diverse range of functional groups. For first-line drugs, many genes exhibiting hydrolase and transporter activity were identified (Supplementary Figure S1A). In contrast, for second-line drugs, genes with oxidoreductase activity were notably prominent (Supplementary Figure S1B). No overlapping functions were found between novel and known genes for second-line drugs.   The known rifampicin resistance genes, such as rpoB and rpoC, exhibit DNA binding activity, while novel genes like amiC, Rv0272c, and stp have amidase, lipase, and transporter activities, respectively (Supplementary Figure S1 and Table 2). Since, earlier studies have reported over expression of stp in rifampicin resistant isolates [24], [25], stp could be an efflux pump which can potentially enhance efflux of drug leading to resistance.

  Similarly, for ethambutol, the known resistance associated gene embB is target for ethambutol and has arabinosyltransferase activity required for cell wall biosynthesis. New genes associated with ethambutol resistance, such as zwf2, stp, Rv1279, Rv2670c, and Rv0272c, showed functions including glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase activity, transporter activity, flavin adenine dinucleotide binding, ATP binding, and lipase activity. Mutations in stp could play an important role in ethambutol resistance similar to the case of rifampicin. However, it is unclear how mutations in other new genes result in resistance to ethambutol.

  XAI method predicted RpsN1 and RpsN2 as novel proteins associated with streptomycin resistance (as shown in Supplementary Table S1). Uniprot analysis revealed that RpsN1 and RpsN2 function in the assembly of the 30S ribosomal complex similar to RpsL, which is a known streptomycin resistance associated protein. Streptomycin functions by disrupting protein translation through binding to the 16S rRNA. Two additional novel streptomycin resistance associated genes, bkdC and Rv0893c, were identified, both exhibiting the same methyltransferase activity as gidB, which is known to facilitate stable binding of streptomycin to the 16S rRNA [26]. The gidB gene encodes a methyltransferase that modifies the nucleotide G527 of 16S rRNA which is a required step for effective streptomycin binding. Mutations in gidB disrupt this methylation process, weakening the ability of streptomycin to bind to the rrs region and leading to low-level resistance. Similarly, newly identified methyltransferase genes such as bkdC and Rv0893c may compensate for the loss of gidB function. Based on abundance analysis of 2650 streptomycin resistant samples (as confirmed by laboratory DST results), 1458 samples carried mutations in Rv0893c and bkdC along with gidB mutations. Additionally, 104 samples harboured mutations in Rv0893c and gidB; only 2 samples carried a mutation in Rv0893c alone, and no samples contained a mutation in bkdC alone (Details are in Supplementary File 3). Since mutations in Rv0893c and bkdC mostly co-occurred with gidB mutations, this pattern suggests that their combination may enhance the moderate levels of resistance observed when present individually. Similarly, two novel genes Rv3813c and Rv3762c were found to be associated with pyrazinamide resistance. Both possess hydrolase activity similar to the known resistance gene pncA, which is responsible for converting pyrazinamide into its active form. Frequent mutations in pncA are known to result in pyrazinamide resistance by disruption of its hydrolase activity. A framedhift mutation such as p.Gln125fs in Rv3813c (predicted as pyrazinamide resistance by XAI) likely disrupts its hydrolase activity completely. Hence, inactivation of this hydrolase by mutations might lead to pyrazinamide resistance. Whereas, p.Tyr345His mutation in Rv3762c (predicted as pyrazinamide resistance by XAI), located within the hydrolase domain, suggests a possible impact on enzymatic function. However, due to limited structural data, particularly about the active site, we could not perform a detailed analysis. Our hypothesis that p.Tyr345His affects hydrolase activity in Rv3762c, is based on its position and the known importance of this domain, but experimental validation or high-resolution structures are needed to confirm its role. Thus, GO analysis for all 13 drugs revealed how the newly identified resistance associated genes could possibly lead to drug resistance. However, further experimental functional assays of these novel genes are needed to validate our hypothesis.

### Mapping of resistance associated mutations onto 3D structures of drug targets
  Table 1 shows the list of 11 known DR associated genes for the 10 antibiotics which acts on 8 known drug targets. Out of these 8 drug targets 3D structures of drug-target complexes were available for four drugs only, namely isoniazid, ethambutol, moxifloxacin and rifampicin. Targets for these four antibiotics consist of five proteins. The GyrA and GyrB heterodimer is the target for moxifloxacin. KatG activates the pro-drug isoniazid and converts it to the active form, which binds to InhA. EmbB and RpoB are targets for ethambutol and rifampicin, respectively. Hence, we mapped the known and novel DR associated mutations (as listed in Table 4) onto 3D structures obtained from RCSB-PDB for these four drugs. Out of the 210 known DR associated coding region mutations, 59 are present on these five drug targets. It is interesting to note that, the novel mutation identified by our XAI approach consisted of a frameshift mutation, G124fs, in the katG gene. Similarly, in the InhA, XAI method predicted P251A as a novel mutation. In the case of rifampicin, XAI method predicted a novel double mutation, MD434IY, in RpoB.

  Supplementary Figure S2A shows the number of point mutations and insertions/deletions which are present on each of the five drug targets. The 53 coding point mutations were mapped on these five crystal structures using PyMOL software and the distance of the mutating residue from the bound drug was computed. The mutation sites were categorized based on their proximity to the ligand. Mutations within 5 Å from the bound drug were categorized as binding pocket residues, and those beyond 5 Å were annotated as distal residues away from drug binding site. It was interesting to note that almost half of the mutations, 26 out of 53 mutations on drug targets were present in the binding pockets of drugs.

  For instance, Met306 residue in the EmbB protein is mutated to Ile, Leu, or Val (M306I/L/V) with a distance of 4.2 Å from the ligand as shown in Fig. 2. Although this residue does not participate in polar interactions with Ethambutol, it has non-bonded van der Waals interactions with the drug. For InhA, which is a drug target for both Isoniazid and Ethionamide, S94A is located at a distance of 3.8 Å from the NADH binding site in the structure 1ENY (Supplementary Figure S2B). It is important to note that the 3D structure for InhA does not contain binding information for the Isoniazid-NADH or Ethionamide-NADH adducts, it only includes data for NADH binding. However, since the binding pocket remains largely unchanged, this information allows us to infer the proximity of the closest amino acid residues to the binding sites of isoniazid-NADH and ethionamide-NADH. Similarly, in GyrA, A90V is located 3.6 Å from the ligand, while in GyrB, E501D is located at a distance of 3.5 Å from the bound Moxifloxacin molecule (Supplementary Figure S3A and S3B). It is important to note that we found that out of 23 coding mutations on RpoB, 17 mutations were present in the drug binding pocket, while 6 mutations were away from the bound drug (Supplementary Figure S4). This alteration may disrupt isoniazid binding, potentially contributing to isoniazid resistance in M.tb. Similarly, N432_D435delinsH mutation in the RpoB protein results in the deletion of four residues, N432, F433, M434 and D435, which are in close contact with the bound drug (Supplementary Figure S6). Consequently, the deletion of these four residues is likely to disrupt the binding interaction with rifampicin, potentially impacting the efficacy of this critical antibiotic. Additionally, a duplication at T444dup was observed in the RpoB protein. It is important to note that the distance between the T444 residue and the rifampicin molecule is approximately 9.2 Å (Supplementary Figure S6). Another insertion mutation, p.F433dup in RpoB, was found in the binding pocket of rifampicin, which can potentially affect drug binding.


### Docking study of Phe433dup in RpoB
  Since we found that almost all resistance-associated mutations in RpoB occurred at the binding pocket and were well studied [10], except the Phe433dup variant, which is found in the WHO catalogue and predicted by our XAI method. Therefore, as a case study, we have selected the Phe433dup variant for detailed analysis to determine its effect on drug binding. Since wild-type Phe433 residue is a binding pocket residue that interacts with rifampicin, it plays a critical role for effective binding of the drug. As illustrated in Figs. 4A and 4B, the insertion of an additional Phe at position 433 creates a bulge on the binding surface, potentially introducing steric hindrance that could interfere with rifampicin binding. In order to compute the binding energy score of rifampicin to wild type rpoB, rifampicin was removed from the crystal structure of rpoB-rifampicin complex and was redocked in the binding pocket using Autodock Vina software [17]. The redocking analysis of the wild type RpoB (PDB: 5UH6) with rifampicin indicated a binding affinity of −10.8 kcal/mol (As shown in Fig. 5A). Our findings revealed that Rifampicin engages in polar interactions with several key residues, including Gln432, Phe433, Arg448, Ser450, Arg459, and Asp487. One hydrogen bond was formed with Arg448 at a distance of 3.1 Å, while another hydrogen bond was formed with Gln432. Furthermore, three electrostatic interactions were detected with Arg459, Asn487, and Ser450 at distances of 3.9 Å, 3.5 Å, and 2.6 Å, respectively. In contrast, docking studies involving the Phe433dup RpoB mutant and Rifampicin showed that the insertion of an extra phenylalanine residue at position 433 altered the binding pose of Rifampicin as shown in Fig. 5B. This mutation resulted in the loss of four polar interactions: two with Phe433 and one each with Arg459 and Arg448. However, the F433dup RpoB mutant was able to form new hydrogen bonds with Asn487, Ser450, Gln429, and Gln432 at distances of 2.7 Å, 3.0 Å, 2.3 Å, and 2.4 Å, respectively, as illustrated in Fig. 5B. Consequently, this leads to a decrease in binding affinity from −10.8 kcal/mol for the wild-type RpoB-Rifampicin complex to −8.3 kcal/mol for the Phe433dup RpoB mutant-Rifampicin complex. This decrease in binding affinity has also been validated by re-scoring the complexes with ML-based scoring function SG-ML-PLAP [28]. The predicted affinity was consistent across three independent models, with the average SG-ML-PLAP score decreasing from 6.91 (wild-type) to 6.09 (Phe433dup), further validating the significance of the docking results, which showed a difference of −8.3 vs −10.8 kcal/mol. ### Implementation of the MutMapTB web server for structural analysis of resistance mutations
  MutMapTB web server was implemented to provide a comprehensive visualization of mutations associated with drug resistance for ethambutol, isoniazid, moxifloxacin, and rifampicin on 3D structure of their respective drug targets (Fig. 7). This platform allows not only the visualization of resistance mutations on 3D structure of drug targets, but also provides information on the abundance of those mutations in a dataset of 24,067 M.tb isolates with known DST profile through interactive graphs. ### Mapping coding mutations in accessory and novel drug resistance associated proteins
  After analysing 59 coding mutations in drug target proteins, we further examined 156 known mutations in drug resistance associated accessory proteins (154 predicted by both WHO and XAI and 2 new mutation in known gene predicted by XAI) and 119 mutations in novel resistance associated proteins identified by XAI approach as in Table 3. All 156 known mutations were present in five known resistance associated or accessory proteins: GidB, RpsL, PncA, EthA, and RpoC. In contrast, the 119 new mutations were distributed across 112 completely new genes.

  Using Pfam for domain mapping, we found that out of 156 known coding mutations,122 were located within the functional domains of five resistance-associated accessory genes (Supplementary File 3). Of these, 36 frameshift or deletion mutations occurring within functional domains are straightforward to interpret, as they are likely to disrupt protein function. The effect of the point mutations on the stability of the accessory proteins was analysed using mCSM software [23] and crystal structures of the corresponding proteins. As shown in Fig. 8, for the streptomycin resistance associated protein GidB, all six point mutation sites were found within the ‘GidB rRNA methyltransferase G domain.’ This domain is responsible for methylating 16S rRNA, which facilitates streptomycin binding. Further analysis using mCSM revealed that all six mutations had a destabilizing effect on protein stability (ΔΔG < 0 kcal/mol). This result suggests that these destabilizing mutations may disrupt the methyltransferase activity of GidB and increase streptomycin resistance in M.tb. For PncA, all 108 point mutations were mapped to the ‘Isochorismatase domain,’ which is responsible for catalyzing the deamination of nicotinamide to nicotinic acid and is likely involved in converting the pyrazinamide prodrug to its active form. Fig. 8 shows that 99 of these mutations have a destabilizing effect on the PncA protein, indicating disruption of the Isochorismatase domain function and conferring pyrazinamide resistance. For the other two proteins, EthA and RpoC, mutations were found to destabilize the ‘Pyridine nucleotide- disulphide oxidoreductase’ and ‘RNA_pol_Rpb1_2 RNA polymerase Rpb2’ domains, respectively. These destabilizing mutations are likely to impair domain function and lead to ethionamide and rifampicin resistance, respectively.Fig. 8Predicted protein stability (ΔΔG) for point mutations across functional domains. Each panel represents a distinct protein domain, showing the ΔΔG values of mutations (on the y-axis) specific to that domain. Individual mutations are plotted on the x-axis, and the horizontal dashed red line at y = 0 indicates the stability threshold. Mutations with negative ΔΔG values are predicted to be potentially destabilizing, whereas those above zero are stabilizing.Fig. 8

  Within this set of 119 novel mutations, we identified 52 mutations located specifically within known functional protein domains belonging to 49 proteins. For the remaining 37 proteins, we used predicted structures obtained from the AlphaFold database. To assess the quality of these predicted structures, we examined their average pLDDT scores. As shown in Fig. 9, almost all 35 proteins had high-quality structures with pLDDT scores greater than 70, while only two showed lower structural quality. However, none of the predicted models were of very low quality. Therefore, a total of 49 protein structures (37 AlphaFold-predicted and 13 crystal structures) were included in the protein stability analysis. Although the direct mechanisms by which these novel proteins contribute to drug resistance remain unclear, insights into possible indirect resistance mechanisms were obtained by mapping mutations onto functional domains and analyzing the mutation effects on protein stability. It has been found that, out of 52 mutations occurring in functional domains, 38 had a destabilizing effect on protein stability, suggesting that these mutations may completely disrupt protein function. (Supplementary File 3). One such mutation is p.Ala336Asp in the PepN protein, which is located within the Peptidase_M1 domain responsible for protease activity. The destabilizing effect of the p.Ala336Asp mutation may disrupt this protease function. ### Mapping non-coding mutations to regulatory binding sites to elucidate their role in drug resistance
  We investigated the impact of non-coding mutations associated with drug resistance in M. tuberculosis on expression of other genes by mapping them onto known transcription factor (TF) binding regions using the TB-Genome database [5]. We curated 17 non-coding mutations linked to drug resistance, out of which 15 were mentioned in the WHO catalogue and 2 were identified by our XAI method. Remarkably, our analysis revealed that 16 out of the 17 curated non-coding mutations overlapped with known TF binding regions. Detailed information for the remaining mutations is provided in Supplementary Table S3, including the upstream gene, its functional annotation, and its corresponding drug resistance association in M.tb. The two novel mutations were found upstream of the eis and pncA genes: a G→A mutation at position 2715,346 (14 bp upstream of eis), and an A→G mutation at position 2289,253 (12 bp upstream of pncA). Notably, the G→A mutation at 2715,346 lies within the binding region of the Rv0047c regulator (spanning 2.7152–2.7156 Mbp), as indicated by a prominent ChIP-seq peak in the TB-Genome database (Figs. 10A and 10B). This suggests that the mutation may play a role in Rv0047c binding, potentially leading to a change in eis expression. For the second novel mutation upstream of pncA (A→G at 2289,253), no overlap with currently known regulatory binding sites was found. However, this does not rule out a regulatory role,it may reflect incomplete annotation of TF binding sites in current databases. To further generalize our findings, we extended this analysis to 50 additional novel non-coding mutations predicted by our XAI model. To the best of our knowledge, none of these 50 mutations or their associated upstream genes had been previously linked to drug resistance.   One such example is a G→T mutation at position 1371,567, located 210 bp upstream of the mrp (Multidrug Resistance Protein) gene. Several studies have shown that overexpression of mrp, a member of the ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter family, is associated with drug resistance in other organisms and identified as a high-confidence drug target [31]. However, there is currently no direct evidence linking mrp overexpression to drug resistance in M.tb. Our XAI-predicted G→T mutation at this site, however, shows strong potential for involvement in pyrazinamide resistance. The mutation at position 1371,567 lies within multiple TF binding sites, including Rv0324, Rv0081, Rv0302, and Rv1353c, mutation could affect TF binding, leading to overexpression of mrp. This hypothesis warrants further experimental validation.

  Another novel non-coding mutation predicted by our XAI model to be associated with pyrazinamide resistance is C→CATGTACAA at position 3430,358, located 29 bp upstream of the mmr (Multidrug resistance protein Mmr) gene, which encodes an efflux pump. Previous studies have shown that overexpression of mmr can lead to drug resistance in M.tb [32], [33]. Although the exact mechanism of mmr involvement in pyrazinamide resistance is not yet fully understood, our findings support the hypothesis that this upstream insertion mutation may significantly contribute to pyrazinamide resistance. This mutation lies within the binding regions of multiple transcription factors—Rv0023, Rv2324, Rv1255c, Rv0324, Rv3133c, Rv3249c, Rv3066, Rv2621c, Rv3597c, Rv1033c, and Rv0081—which may function as negative regulators of mmr. The mutation may interfere with their binding, leading to mmr overexpression. A gene expression study introducing this C→CATGTACAA mutation upstream of mmr under pyrazinamide treatment would help validate this hypothesis.

  Of the 50 novel non-coding mutations identified by our XAI method, eight did not correspond to any known transcription factor binding sites. This limitation likely arises from the TB-Genome database, which currently includes only 50 transcription factors, restricting the detection of all possible regulatory regions. Moreover, these mutations might affect gene expression through alternative regulatory pathways—such as modifying ribosome binding sites or other control elements—rather than altering transcription factor interactions. With the expansion of transcription factor datasets in the future, some of these mutations may be found to have additional regulatory functions.


## Discussion
  Our study offers a detailed functional and structural overview of mutations linked to drug resistance in M.tb, incorporating GO annotation, analysis of functional domains, and the effects of mutations on protein stability. This study employs a multi-dimensional strategy to enhance the understanding of various resistance mechanisms by integrating known drug resistance genes with 112 newly identified genes predicted by XAI and mapping their mutations onto available crystal structures or structures predicted by AlphaFold. A key insight of this research is the functional diversity observed between known and newly identified resistance-associated genes. Although traditional resistance genes, such as rpoB, katG, inhA, and embB, primarily play roles in fundamental metabolic or enzymatic functions, numerous recently discovered genes exhibit a wider range of activities, including amidase, lipase, and various transporter roles. GO analysis revealed that the newly identified genes are associated with a wider spectrum of molecular functions and biological processes, suggesting that alternative or compensatory pathways may contribute to the development of resistance phenotypes. Notably, two novel methyltransferases, bkdC and Rv0893c, were found to co-occur with the known streptomycin resistance associated methyltransferase gene gidB. This co-occurrence pattern indicates that bkdC and Rv0893c may participate in related functional pathways or provide a compensatory effect that enhances resistance when combined with gidB mutations. On the other hand, genes such as stp and pstB associated with various drug resistances (e.g., rifampicin, ethambutol, tetracycline) probably demonstrate their influence via efflux-mediated mechanisms, a well-recognised resistance pathway in bacteria, including M.tb. [34].

  The structural mapping of 59 known drug resistance-associated coding mutations, including five novel predictions from our XAI model, onto five primary drug target proteins (e.g., RpoB, KatG) allowed us to delineate the relevance of the mutations to drug binding. Notably, approximately 50 % of the mutations were located in drug targets that line the drug binding pocket, indicating a direct impact on binding affinity and, consequently, drug efficacy. This was exemplified by a detailed study of a known drug target mutation, RpoB F433dup, where structural modelling and docking analyses showed reduced rifampicin binding affinity due to steric hindrance introduced by the phenylalanine duplication. These findings underscore the utility of structural analyses in interpreting the effects of mutations. Furthermore, mutations in novel proteins, particularly those located within conserved functional domains, were assessed using mCSM-based protein stability predictions, which revealed destabilising effects on protein structure and function. Considering limitations of tools like mCSM, it is best that experimental studies be used for further validations [35]. Studies indicate that various functional gene deletions or disruption may occur to maintain the long term survival and drug tolerance of M.tb under drug-induced stress conditions. For instance, M.tb can undergo metabolic shifts, reduce growth rates and promote efflux pump activity, which together contribute to a drug-resistant phenotype without necessarily acquiring drug resistance mutations [27]. Additionally, specific genetic changes, such as the deletion of certain genes, have been shown to enhance the ability of a bacteria to withstand antibiotic pressure, thereby facilitating its persistence during treatment [28], [29]. Another key contribution of our study is the mapping of 67 non-coding mutations, including novel XAI-predicted variants, to known transcription factor (TF) binding sites. A majority (88 %) overlapped with regulatory regions curated in the TB-Genome database. Mutations in the upstream areas of the eis, mmr, and mrp genes suggest upregulation, possibly leading to efflux-mediated resistance, which aligns with previous observations that promoter mutations can modulate drug susceptibility. These results emphasise that non-coding mutations, although frequently disregarded, can significantly influence gene expression without directly altering the protein-coding sequence. Such mutations may enhance drug resistance by affecting transcription factor binding or promoter activity, resulting in the upregulation of resistance genes or efflux pumps, thereby contributing indirectly but notably to resistance against drugs. Although they could be significant, non-coding regions are still largely unexamined in M. tb due to the limited functional annotation of regulatory components. The existing TF binding site information, derived from ChIP-seq studies, encompasses only around 50 transcriptional regulators, which likely does not capture the entire regulatory network of M. tb. This study underscores the need for more comprehensive mapping of regulatory binding sites of transcription factors in M. tuberculosis to better elucidate how non-coding mutations contribute to drug resistance. Nonetheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. Nonetheless, several limitations should be noted. First, while AlphaFold and mCSM provide valuable computational predictions, these require experimental validation. Second, many newly identified genes lack adequate functional characterisation. Third, our regulatory annotations are limited by the available transcription factor binding site data, which may underestimate the impact of certain non-coding mutations. Finally, while SHAP values effectively reveal how mutations influence model predictions, they do not constitute statistical proof of causality and do not account for confounding factors such as population structure. These limitations highlight the importance of integrating computational approaches with experimental and statistical validation to establish the genuine biological significance of identified mutations.

  In summary, this comprehensive study highlights the potential of integrating computational structural biology, machine learning predictions, and functional genomics to enhance our understanding of M. Tuberculosis drug resistance. By revealing new resistance mechanisms and broadening the list of potential resistance genes and mutations, our research lays the groundwork for future experimental studies and the identification of therapeutic targets. In addition, the MutMapTB web server developed in this study is a comprehensive resource for the structural analysis of resistance-associated mutations for the first four-line drugs.


## Conclusion
  This study presents a comprehensive framework that integrates genetic, structural, and regulatory data to elucidate the complex mechanisms underlying drug resistance in M.tb. Utilizing both high-confidence mutations listed by the WHO and new variants predicted through explainable AI, we discovered various resistance-related genes with different molecular roles. The structural mapping of these mutations, particularly their proximity to drug-binding sites, can provide valuable insights into how mutations impair the effectiveness of antibiotics. However, more detailed insights can be provided by additional computational analysis and experimental studies. Domain mapping and protein stability assessment indicated that numerous new mutations arise in functional domains of non-essential proteins, leading to destabilization of protein structure. Non-coding mutations were found to affect regulatory elements, especially the regions where transcription factors bind, indicating that gene expression variation serves as a secondary resistance mechanism. Overall, this comprehensive strategy expands the range of recognized resistance factors and highlights the importance of merging XAI, structural biology, and functional genomics to inform future diagnostics and targeted treatment advancements for drug resistant TB.
