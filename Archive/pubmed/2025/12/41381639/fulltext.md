# In silico-driven identification of potent CDK9 inhibitors through bioisosteric replacement and multi-stage virtual screening


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-31230-8.


## Introduction
  Cancer remains one of the leading causes of premature mortality worldwide1. Among cancer types with the highest mortality rates, lung cancer leads (18.7%), followed by colorectal (9.3%), liver (7.8%), and breast cancer (6.9%), accounting for a total of 9.7 million deaths in 20222. Despite advancements in prevention strategies, early detection, and more effective treatment options, the global cancer mortality rate has increased by approximately 28% over the past decade3,4. Additionally, estimates indicate that in the next ten years, approximately 75% of cancer-related deaths will occur in middle- and low-income countries, mainly due to the inability to acquire new medical technologies, in contrast to high-income countries5.

  Cancer is a disorder characterized by genetic and epigenetic alterations that drive uncontrolled cell proliferation, resistance to cell death, sustained angiogenesis, and invasive behavior6. Cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs), which are members of the serine/threonine kinases family, regulate a wide array of biological processes in eukaryotic cells through phosphorylation. They oversee critical processes such as cell division, DNA replication, and repair7,8. By interacting with cyclins, CDKs orchestrate progression through the various phases of the cell cycle. Dysregulation of CDK activity, often resulting from genetic mutations or altered expression, can disrupt normal cellular proliferation and contribute to tumorigenesis7.

  The members of the CDK family, along with cyclins, are classified based on their evolutionary origins and functional roles, primarily categorized into two groups: those involved in cell cycle progression (e.g., CDK 1–7, 14–18)9 and those that serve as transcriptional regulators (e.g., CDK 7–13, 18–20)10. Among the latter, CDK9 stands out as a pivotal kinase that plays a central role in the regulation of transcription. In the context of cancer, CDK9 is frequently overexpressed or hyperactivated, making it a significant target for the development of novel inhibitors with therapeutic potential11,12. The reaction catalyzed by CDK9 consists of the phosphorylation of Ser2 within the C-terminal domain of RNA polymerase II, a modification that is crucial for facilitating the elongation phase of RNA synthesis13, as illustrated in Fig. 1.Fig. 1Schematic representation of the transcriptional role of CDK9. The CDK9–Cyclin T1 (P-TEFb) complex phosphorylates Ser2 of the C-terminal domain (CTD) of RNA polymerase II, promoting pause release and enabling transcriptional elongation and mRNA processing.

  This post-translational modification effectively alleviates the pause typically observed in RNA polymerase II activity following the initiation phase, thereby allowing for uninterrupted RNA synthesis and promoting efficient gene expression. The predominant isoform of CDK9 is a protein consisting of 372 amino acids11,14. Its N-terminal lobe (residues 16–108) features five β-structures and one α-helix, while the C-terminal lobe (residues 109–330) contains seven α-helices and four β-strands15. An ATP-binding motif is located between these lobes, and inhibitors targeting this site have been developed to modulate CDK9 activity12.

  Selective CDK9 inhibitors, including NVP-216, JSH-15017, AZD-457318, BAY-125115219, and BAY-114357220 have demonstrated in vitro IC50 values ranging from 0.5 to 13 nM. Moreover, several of these compounds have shown significant effectiveness in xenograft mouse models with a variety of tumor cells11. Currently, only BAY-1251152 (for advanced hematological cancers21) and AZD-4573 (for refractory hematological cancers22) are undergoing active clinical trials. Pyrimidine-based ligands are recognized as some of the most active kinase inhibitors identified to date. This heterocyclic scaffold has emerged as a critical structural element in the development of potent inhibitors of several kinase families23. In particular, numerous CDK9 inhibitors contain a pyrimidine core that plays a central role in anchoring the ligand within the ATP-binding site of the kinase, typically flanked by additional substituents that support binding and selectivity24. However, non-pyrimidine-based inhibitors, such as flavopiridol—a flavonoid-related molecule—also exhibit potent inhibition of this enzyme25, confirming that the chemical space for inhibitors of this enzyme is much wide26.

  Recent in silico studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of computational strategies for identifying selective kinase inhibitors, particularly those targeting CDK9. Approaches combining virtual screening, pharmacophore modeling, and molecular dynamics simulations have successfully retrieved promising candidates from natural-product libraries27. Similar workflows combining large-scale virtual screening with binding free energy calculations have also identified potent synthetic scaffolds with improved CDK9 affinity through rational structural optimization28. These advances collectively reinforce the reliability of computer-aided approaches for prioritizing and refining CDK9 inhibitors before experimental validation.

  In this study, a combination of ligand-based and structure-based drug discovery protocols was used to identify new CDK9 inhibitors. In a first stage a library of bioisosteric analogs was generated by replacing the pyrimidine core of 134 inhibitors with known Ki values. A pharmacophore-based virtual screening, followed by a structure–activity relationship (QSAR)-based screening, was employed to identify the most promising candidate. This molecule served as the parent structure for two additional rounds of bioisosteric substitution. In the first round, replacements were introduced on one terminal moiety of the parent scaffold, and in the second round, additional bioisosteric substitutions were generated on the opposite moiety of each first-round derivative. This hierarchical approach enabled a systematic and directional expansion of structural diversity while preserving the essential pharmacophoric features. The three top-ranked ligands, which are not closely related structurally, were evaluated as representatives of more than 270 promising bioisosteric derivatives, all of which exhibited predicted Ki values comparable to those of several potent inhibitors reported to date. Density functional theory (DFT) analyses were conducted to explore their electronic properties and to evaluate their chemical reactivity. Additionally, molecular dynamics simulations and binding free energy calculations were performed to evaluate the stability and interaction profiles of these compounds within the ATP-binding site. These results point to these ligands and the broader set of bioisosteres as promising candidates for continued research and development in CDK9-targeted drug discovery. ## Methodology

### Selection of active compounds and generation of decoys
  A search on BindingDB database29 in September 2024 identified 134 inhibitors of human CDK9, with Ki values ranging from 2 to 12,000 nM, all determined under comparable experimental conditions (Table S1). These structures were downloaded in SDF format and all were used to create a bioisosteric library. In addition, the most active compounds were selected to build the pharmacophore hypothesis and for QSAR modeling.


### Building of in silico library from CDK9 inhibitors by bioisosteric replacement
  To create the bioisosteric library from the 134 inhibitors, the MolOpt server (Shanghai, China) was utilized30. This web-based tool employs data mining and generative modeling to facilitate the substitution of molecular substructures with bioisosteric groups. In the first stage, each inhibitor was provided as input, and the generative AI model was selected to perform substitutions on the pyrimidine core present in 133 of the 134 active compounds. The remaining ligand, BDBM50563322, which features a central pyridine ring, was also included and subjected to a similar substitution. After removing duplicate structures, a total of 17,633 unique compounds remained. The top-ranked compound, identified through pharmacophore-based and QSAR-based virtual screening, was then subjected to further bioisosteric modifications. These were applied sequentially to the flanking moieties of its core scaffold—first on one side and then on the opposite—structural regions that are frequently represented among known inhibitors, leading to the creation of a dataset comprising 66,966 novel molecules.


### Preparation of compounds for in silico analyses
  All the compounds were prepared for in silico analyses using the RDKit version 2023.09 (Available at: https://www.rdkit.org)31, Pandas version 1.3.5 (Available at: https://pandas.pydata.org), and NumPy version 1.20.332 libraries for the generation of 3D structures and file editions. Subsequently, the protonation state was assigned at pH 7.4, and energy minimization for geometry optimization was performed using the MMFF94 force field with the steepest descent algorithm, followed by the conjugate gradient algorithm with default parameters, employing Open Babel-3.1.1 software33. All the structures were then converted to MOL2 and SDF formats.


### Pharmacophore modeling, validation, and screening
  To construct the pharmacophore model, a set of 54 inhibitors and 224 decoys was used as input for the three-dimensional pharmacophore signature approach implemented in the PSearch software34. The decoys were generated from each inhibitor using the Directory of Useful Decoys, Enhanced (DUD-E) web server (San Francisco, USA)35, after which duplicate decoys were identified and removed to ensure a non-redundant dataset. The pharmacophore signature protocol implemented in PSearch does not rely on molecular alignment; instead, it derives spatial signatures from a structurally diverse set of active compounds, while incorporating decoy molecules to minimize bias and assess the ability of the model to distinguish actives from inactives. In the strategy labeled as "1" in the protocol of the authors, active compounds are grouped into a single training set, and an F05 value of 0.8 or higher is considered satisfactory for model selection.

  To further validate this pharmacophore model, a larger database comprising 134 inhibitors and their corresponding decoys (726 unique decoys) was screened using the Pharmit web server (Pittsburgh, USA)36. The pharmacophore features were applied in the screening with a radius (r) of 1. The compounds were ranked based on their root mean square deviation (RMSD) values, which quantify the average distance between the pharmacophoric features of the model and those of each aligned ligand. Lower RMSD values indicate a better spatial fit and were used to evaluate the model ability to discriminate true inhibitors from decoys.

  With these results, the reliability of the pharmacophore model for subsequent screening was analyzed with the Screening Explorer server (Paris, Francia)37, which calculates metrics such as the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) and the enrichment factor (EF). The AUC-ROC quantifies the overall ability of the model to correctly classify active and inactive compounds, while the enrichment factor measures how effectively the model identifies true actives within the top-ranked subset compared to random selection. Following model validation, the bioisosteric libraries were screened to identify structures that matched the pharmacophore features, using an RMSD threshold of < 0.5. Additional filters, available in Pharmit, were applied to select structures that complied with drug-like properties according to the Lipinski rules38.


### Quantitative structure–activity relationship modeling
  Properly validated Quantitative Structure–Activity Relationship (QSAR) models can reliably predict biological activity by correlating molecular descriptors with experimental data, often providing greater accuracy than other in silico methods that rely solely on structural information39. In this study, the development, validation, and selection of the most suitable QSAR model were performed using QSARINS software version 2.2.4 (Varese, Italy)40. The software employs a genetic algorithm (GA) to iteratively select molecular descriptors associated with the biological activity, and the final QSAR model was built using multiple linear regression (MLR). Model performance was rigorously evaluated using statistical metrics to ensure both explanatory power and predictive reliability.

  A total of 124 inhibitors were included in the final model, while 10 were excluded for being outside the applicability domain during QSAR model construction. The ligands were divided into 92 inhibitors for the training set and 32 for the test set, using the “response” setup in QSARINS to retain a broader range of activities. The experimental Ki values of these molecules, expressed in nanomolar concentrations, were converted to pKi values. Molecular descriptors were calculated using PaDEL-Descriptor version 2.21 (Singapore, Singapore)41 (2D descriptors) and OCHEM database online version 4.3.156 (Hamburg, Germany)42 (OEState, ALogPS, Mold2, CDDD, JPlogP and SIRMS descriptors). Descriptors with constant values (> 80%) and those that were more than 95% pairwise correlated were eliminated. For variable selection, the GA parameters were: population size of 10, maximum generations of 10,000, and a mutation rate of 20.


### QSAR model validation
  To ensure the reliability of QSAR results, a model must not only show a solid correlation between experimental and predicted values, but also pass a rigorous internal and external validation to confirm its predictive power. To further guarantee the internal stability and robustness of the models, cross-validation techniques were applied, including Q2 leave-one-out (LOO), Q2 leave-many-out (LMO), and the Concordance Correlation Coefficient for cross-validation (CCCcv). For the LMO method, 2,000 iterations were performed to enhance the statistical reliability of the results, each of which excluded 30% of the data. In addition, permutation tests using the Y-scrambling procedure were conducted to discard the possibility that the best model was developed by chance. This involved randomizing the response values and performing 2,000 iterations with 30% of the data scrambled. To assess external predictivity, the performance of the model was evaluated using Q2 ext-F1, Q2 ext-F2, Q2 ext-F3, and CCCext, incorporating molecules from the test set that were not used during model generation. The applicability domain (AD) of QSAR model was evaluated using the leverage approach in the Williams plot, which identifies data points lying outside the AD of the model.

  For QSAR-based virtual screening, molecular descriptors from the selected model equation were calculated for each bioisostere. These descriptors were then input into the QSARINS-Chem module43. This tool calculates the activity of each ligand by applying the selected descriptors to the QSAR model. The Insubria graph40 was used to visualize and assess whether these compounds fell within the AD defined by the training data.


### Similarity analyses
  Structural similarity of the molecules in the datasets was evaluated using Data Warrior software version 060402 (Allschwil, Switzerland)44, employing the FragFp fingerprint algorithm to analyze the molecular relationships between the bioisosteres. A similarity cutoff of 80% was applied to identify compounds with shared substructural motifs. FragFp fingerprinting was chosen for its ability to efficiently detect fragment-based similarities by encoding key substructural features and functional group patterns, enabling meaningful comparisons of ligand scaffolds within the dataset. Specifically, it relies on a dictionary of 512 predefined fragments optimized to occur frequently in organic molecules while minimizing redundancy. This design improves the ability of the descriptor to distinguish relevant structural features across diverse scaffolds, and the use of wildcard atoms in many fragments allows the recognition of chemically intuitive similarities despite small atomic variations44.


### Molecular docking analysis
  After the QSAR-based virtual screening, the binding modes of the most promising ligands in complex with CDK9 were analyzed through molecular docking. To select the most suitable software for these analyses, we assessed the capability of LeDock-1.1 (Beijing, China)45, Protein–Ligand ANT System-1.2 software (PLANTS) (Konstanz, Germany)46 and RxDock (a fork of RDock)47 to reproduce the crystallographic pose of the co-crystallized ligand in the ATP-binding motif of the enzyme. The crystal structure corresponding to CDK9 was obtained from the RCSB PDB database48 (PDB: 3BLQ, chain A15) in August 2024. The target was prepared using the PropKa-3.0 software49, with the pH set to 7.4 to assign protonation states to the ionizable amino acids within the ATP-binding motif, while keeping all other parameters at their default values. The structure was subsequently processed using LePro-1.0 (Beijing, China), SPORES 1.3 (Konstanz, Germany), and Rbdock script with default parameters, and saved in MOL2, PDB, and SDF formats for PLANTS, LeDock, and RxDock analyses, respectively. All simulations were conducted within a 12.5 Å radius, centered on the co-crystallized ATP, with coordinates of 50.0, -15.0, and -10.0 for the x, y, and z axes. An RMSD threshold of 2.0 Å was established to ensure effective clustering, with default settings applied for all other parameters.


### Molecular dynamic simulations
  All molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of the complexes with the enzyme were executed using GROMACS-2024.150, employing an all-atom CHARMM36m force field51. The water model utilized for all systems was the transferable intermolecular potential 3P (TIP3P), within a periodically corrected triclinic box, ensuring a minimum edge distance of 1.0 nm. To simulate physiological conditions, Na+ and Clˉ ions were adjusted to a concentration of 0.15 mol/L. The steepest descent algorithm was applied to reduce initial steric clashes, reaching a minimum energy threshold of 500 kJ/mol. The equilibration phase comprised two stages: the first involved equilibration for 1 ns gradually increasing the system temperature until 310 K in the NVT ensemble using V-rescale thermostat; the second was conducted in the NPT ensemble, equilibrating for 3 ns at a pressure of 1 bar controlled with C-rescale barostat. The production runs were executed for 200 ns changing the barostat to Parrinello-Rahman. Long-range electrostatic interactions were analyzed using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) algorithm52 and the hydrogen bond constraints were applied using the LINCS algorithm53.


### Binding free energy calculations
  Binding free energy calculations were performed using the MD trajectories to quantify the total interaction energy and to analyze the residue-level contributions stabilizing each complex. For total binding free energy calculations, the MM-PBSA methodology54 was employed, utilizing a single trajectory and performed with the gmx-MM-PBSA software (version 1.6.0)55. From the MD results of all runs, 400 snapshots were extracted for total energy and amino acid energy decomposition analysis. All settings followed the recommended default values of the software. The computed ΔG_binding values include both polar and non-polar solvation contributions. Normal-mode entropy estimations in MM-PBSA introduce large statistical uncertainty and may even compromise overall accuracy for most systems, as shown in large-scale benchmarks56–58. Therefore, entropy was omitted in accordance with standard practice in comparative MM-PBSA analyses.


### Density functional theory calculations
  Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were carried out using ORCA version 5.0.1 (Germany)59 with the B3LYP/6-311G (d, p) method for the optimization of the selected structures, while the electron properties of global reactivity were conducted with the model PBE0/6-311G (d, p). During the process of optimization, no internal coordination limits or symmetry was applied. The objective of these analyses was to ascertain: global and local reactivity descriptors, optimal geometric parameters, and frontier molecular orbital (FMO). The output files were examined utilizing UCSF-Chimera software version 1.18 (San Francisco, USA)60 and ORCA scripts.


## Results and discussion

### Bioisosteric library generation and pharmacophore-based virtual screening
  In the search for CDK9 inhibitors, several promising molecules have been developed based on scaffolds such as flavonoid, aminopyrimidine, pyridines (or triazines), purines, pyrazolo[1,5-a]pyrimidine, pyrazole, aminothiazole, azaindole and pyrrolo[2,3-d]pyrimidine26. In fact, the pyrimidine core is the most common scaffold among the CDK9 inhibitors used as active compounds in this work, appearing in 133 out of 134 molecules, whereas a pyridine ring serves as the core structure in the inhibitor BDBM50563322 (Binding DB Reactant set ID 51393854) (Fig. S1 and Table S1). To identify promising new inhibitors and expand the current knowledge on structural diversity for potential CDK9 inhibitors, bioisosteric substitutions of these core structures were performed in a first step. Using the MolOpt server, these replacements generated 24,504 compounds from the 134 inhibitors (Table S2). However, some substitutions resulted in cores that still incorporated pyrimidine rings (such as purine-like bicyclic systems), and it was therefore decided to exclude them from further analysis, resulting in a final set of 17,633 bioisosteres (Table S3).

  To enhance the likelihood of identifying potent CDK9 inhibitors, the dataset was subsequently screened using a pharmacophore model developed in this study. A validated pharmacophore defines the spatial arrangements and key chemical features shared by active molecules, allowing for a more accurate, focused selection of potential inhibitors. Active compounds and decoys were used to construct ligand-based pharmacophore models through the signature method (SM) with PSearch software34. Following this protocol, a validated model with seven pharmacophoric features was obtained, achieving a high accuracy value of 1.0 and a satisfactory recall of 0.89 (Table S4). This pharmacophore includes three regions where aromatic and hydrophobic features are spatially superimposed, located at the following coordinates: (-1.83, -1.7, -0.87), (3.23, − 2.28, − 1.75), and (6.62, − 1.24, 0.23). Additionally, there is one hydrogen bond acceptor at the coordinates 2.02, − 2.6, − 2.31. The inter-feature distances defining the pharmacophore geometry are 4.047 Å, 4.292 Å, and 6.711 Å, as represented on Fig. 3A.Fig. 3Pharmacophore model representation and validation results (A) Pharmacophore 3D representantion: cyan spheres represent three overlapping aromatic and hydrophobic features, while the yellow sphere indicates a hydrogen bond acceptor feature. The numerical values shown between the features correspond to the inter-feature distances expressed in Å (B) Validation results based on AUC-ROC and (C) Predictive curves.

  An additional validation step was performed by testing the ability of the generated pharmacophore to distinguish inhibitors from decoys, using a new database generated for this purpose. This validation set included 857 compounds, comprising 131 inhibitors and 726 decoys. It is important to note that three active compounds from the original set of 134 could not be included in the analysis as they could not be processed by DUD-E for decoy generation. The results indicate that the pharmacophore model effectively differentiates active compounds from decoys, as reflected by an AUC ROC of 0.993, indicating high performance for classification, Fig. 3B. The BEDROC score of 0.912 and the RIE value of 5.683 (Table S5) confirm that active compounds were consistently ranked at the top of the screening results, indicating a strong early enrichment and demonstrating the robustness of the model for virtual screening. This trend was also evident in the predictiveness curve (Fig. 3C), further supporting the ability of the model to prioritize relevant candidates. These results collectively validate the predictive power of the pharmacophore and support its applicability for identifying promising CDK9 inhibitors.

  Virtual screening with this pharmacophore identified 3,753 compounds (Table S6) from the bioisosteric dataset (out of 17,633) that matched the defined features. The resulting library displays a markedly expanded structural diversity compared to the 134 active compounds shown in Fig. 4A. As indicated by the FragFp similarity analysis, the 3,754 bioisosteric derivatives (Fig. 4B) span a broader distribution of chemical features, including scaffolds containing one to seven aromatic nitrogen atoms and two to seven aromatic rings.Fig. 4Structural diversity representation of active molecules and bioisosteric library. ### Development, selection and validation of a QSAR model
  To perform QSAR-based virtual screening of the 3,754 previously filtered compounds, the development, selection, and validation of the best QSAR model was carried out using QSARINS software. The selected model, based on four descriptors, shows a R2 of 0.836, indicating a good correlation between experimental and predicted values; this coefficient reflects the proportion of variance in the experimental data explained by the model. The scatter plot shows that the data points closely follow the diagonal line, reflecting good agreement between predicted and experimental values, further validating the accuracy of the model (Fig. 5A). The resulting equation from the model is as follows: pKi =  − 4.9273 + (− 1.2163 × cddd_194) + (1.4228 × cddd_508) + (32.7015 × D248) + (19.3064 × ETA_dEpsilon_D). To clarify the components of this equation, the nature of the descriptors employed in the model is detailed below.Fig. 5QSAR modeling validation. (A) Scatter plot of experimental vs predicted pKi by MLR and (B) Scatter plot of experimental vs Q2-LOO (Leave-One-Out) validation.

  Continuous data-driven descriptors (cddds) are molecular representations derived from deep learning models. Trained on large molecular datasets, they capture features that reflect chemical and biological properties, offering a more robust framework for modeling molecular similarities than traditional descriptors61. The D248 (EXP3 Path-distance/ Walk-distance) descriptors, calculated by tools such as Mold262, characterize the topological features of a molecule. Path-distance measures the shortest bonding route between atoms, while Walk-distance considers random walks along atomic connections, allowing atoms to be revisited. The EXP3 function applies an exponential transformation to these distances, emphasizing variations in atomic connectivity. The ETA_dEpsilon_D descriptor, part of the Extended Topochemical Atom (ETA) family of descriptors, captures the differences in electronegativity and topological properties of a molecule63. With the exception of cddd_194, the other three descriptors in the model are positively correlated with the activity of these inhibitors.

  The internal validation of the model through cross-validation (CV) analyses yielded significant results, with a high Q2-LOO (Leave-One-Out) value of 0.8154 (Fig. 5B) and a comparable Q2-LMO (Leave-Many-Out) value of 0.8104. These metrics evaluate the predictive robustness of the model by testing its ability to reproduce the activity of compounds excluded during training. Specifically, the Leave-One-Out procedure removes one compound at a time, while the Leave-Many-Out approach omits multiple compounds in each iteration, both reflecting how reliably the model can generalize to unseen data. These results highlight the robustness and stability of the model across different subsets of data, supporting its reliability for future predictions64. Since our objective was to screen and predict the pKi values of bioisosteres—structurally related analogs derived from known CDK9 inhibitors—the validation results confirm the model robustness and generalization ability within this consistent chemical series.

  External validation is a critical step in QSAR modeling, used to evaluate the predictive performance of the model on an independent dataset and to confirm its generalizability beyond the training set. In this analysis, the model achieved Q2-F1, Q2-F2, and Q2-F3 values of 0.786, 0.7848, and 0.7913, respectively, which are all well above the commonly accepted threshold of 0.5 for reliable external prediction. These three formulations of the predictive squared correlation coefficient assess the degree of linearity between predicted and experimental activities while accounting for possible systematic deviations in scale or intercept. In addition, the external concordance correlation coefficient (CCCext = 0.8907) measures both precision and accuracy of prediction, further supporting the strong agreement between predicted and observed data and confirming the robustness of the model for new molecules65 (Table S7).

  In addition, to determine whether this model was obtained by chance, the Y-scrambling test was performed. Models derived from these scrambled data showed significantly lower R2 and Q2 values compared to our selected model (Fig. 6A). This substantial difference confirms that the proposed model captures a genuine structure–activity relationship rather than a spurious correlation, reinforcing its reliability for predicting pKi values.

  The applicability domain (AD) was quantitatively assessed using the leverage method, where the boundary of the domain of the model is determined by the leverage threshold (h*), which is calculated using the formula 3(p + 1)/n, where p is the number of model descriptors and n is the total number of compounds in the training set. This threshold helps identify compounds that are outside the chemical space of the model, with those having leverage values greater than h* potentially indicating extrapolation beyond the reliable prediction range of the model. As shown in the Williams plot in Fig. 6B, both the training set and test set molecules are within the AD (Hat ≤ h* = 0.163), except for 50224851, which is slightly an outlier (Hat = 0.164). This compound was nevertheless retained in the modeling process due to the presence of key structural features shared with other inhibitors in the training set.


### QSAR-based virtual screening
  When the 3,754 molecules matching the pharmacophore model were screened using the obtained QSAR model, 79% (2,967 structures) were found to fall within the AD and also exhibited pKi values within the activity range of the training set (Fig. 7A and Table S8). The top-ranked ligand was 50224760_85 (5-(methylamino)-4-methyl-3-[1-(phenyl-sulfonyl)-1H-pyrazol-4-yl]-thiazole-2-carboxamide. This compound was obtained by replacing the pyrimidine ring of the parent compound 50224760 with a pyrazole carbonyl linker (Fig. S2). Compound 50224760_85 exhibited a predicted pKi value of 8.3503 and fell within the AD (Fig. 7B), thus being selected for further analyses. Importantly, the parent compound 50224760 is one of the most potent inhibitors among the 134 active compounds, with a Ki value of 2 nM.Fig. 7Insubria plot of the 3754 molecules obtained after pharmacophore-based screening (A), showing that the vast majority of compounds (red spheres) exhibit leverage (Hat) values below the threshold (vertical black line at h* = 0.163). (B) Insubria plot indicating that all molecules in the training set (yellow spheres) and compound 50224760_85 (red sphere) fall within the leverage range.

  Upon examining the structures of the 134 molecules in the dataset, it was found that although a pyrazole moiety is present in 10 of them, its location does not align with the core scaffold chosen for bioisosteric substitution at this stage. Rather, it appears as a peripheral group decorating alternative core frameworks. Notably, AT7519, a compound with a pyrazole core, has advanced through clinical phases due to its activity as an inhibitor of several CDKs, including CDK1, CDK4, CDK5, CDK6, and CDK9, though it is particularly recognized for its efficacy against CDK266. In fact, it is precisely the reduced specificity of CDK inhibitors that contributes significantly to severe adverse effects, highlighting the need for ongoing efforts to identify more selective candidates and further pursue the search for new inhibitors67.

  Additional observation of the structure of the 134 inhibitors reveals that 120 of them carry a thiazole group adjacent to the pyrimidine or pyridine cores, whereas 35 inhibitors feature a phenylsulfonamide group occupying an equivalent position within the scaffold. Furthermore, both thiazole and phenylsulfonamide moieties are simultaneously present in 24 of these inhibitors, demonstrating the prevalence of these groups in these inhibitors. In fact, 50224760_85 also presents both the thiazole and phenylsulfonamide groups flanking its central scaffold. Considering these structural trends, the search for new and chemically distinct candidates was further expanded through additional rounds of bioisosteric replacement. By systematically introducing substitutions first on the thiazole moiety of this structure and subsequently on the phenylsulfonamide group of each resulting bioisostere, a total of 66,966 new structures were generated (Table S9).

  Pharmacophore-based screening of this dataset identified 27,444 promising structures, which were subsequently evaluated using the QSAR model to predict their pKi values. The results revealed that 14,264 molecules fell within the AD and pKi range established by the training set. Within this second generation of bioisosteres, 67% (9,569 structures) exhibited promising inhibitory activity, with predicted Ki values ranging from 53 to 2 nM (pKi from 7.2758 to 7.7503) (Table S10). In fact, 273 of these molecules have predicted Ki values between 2 and 2.5 nM (Table S11), comparable to those reported for several potent CDK9 inhibitors to date68. These results reflect the efficiency of the sequential bioisosteric replacement strategy and subsequent pharmacophore- and QSAR-based screenings in identifying a large subset of derivatives with predicted nanomolar affinity toward CDK9.

### Molecular docking, molecular dynamic and binding free energy calculations
  To ensure reliable molecular docking of the selected molecules in complex with CDK9, the performance of RxDock, PLANTS, and LeDock was first assessed by evaluating their ability to reproduce the crystallographic binding pose of ATP. RxDock exhibited the best performance with an RMSD (Root Mean Square Deviation) of 1.4 Å between the crystallographic and the docked pose (Fig. S5A), followed by LeDock and PLANTS, with RMSD of 1.6 Å and 1.9 Å, respectively (Fig. S5B-C). Docking results obtained with RxDock showed that the top-ranked compounds consistently occupy the ATP-binding site of the enzyme (Fig. S6A), but 9724 and 31801 (Fig. S6C and S6D, respectively) exhibit greater overlap with the ATP backbone compared to 9550, Fig. S6B.

  Given the inherent limitations of molecular docking in predicting the stability and dynamics of ligand-target interactions, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed to gain deeper insights into these aspects. MD results indicate that all the bioisosteres stabilize the enzyme more effectively than the parent compound (50224760), as evidenced by RMSD values consistently below 0.2 nm throughout most of the simulations (Fig. 10A), a range typically associated with highly stable ligand–protein complexes77. In addition, the binding free energies estimated by MM-PBSA were more negative, with an average of − 28 kcal/mol (≈ − 117 kJ/mol), than that of the 50224760 (Fig. 10B), indicating a greater potential to act as competitive CDK9 inhibitors.   The interacting residues highlighted by our simulations coincide with conserved structural and catalytic motifs that govern CDK9 activity and inhibitor binding. The β3-strand lysine (Lys48) is critical for ATP anchoring and positioning through a salt bridge with the catalytic glutamate; its disruption weakens ATP binding and kinase function26. The gatekeeper residue Phe103 defines the hydrophobic pocket adjacent to the hinge, where π–π and hydrophobic interactions are commonly exploited by selective CDK9 inhibitors24. The hinge cysteine (Cys106) provides a hydrogen-bonding anchor for many ATP-competitive inhibitors80, whereas the DFG-motif aspartate (Asp167) participates in Mg2⁺ coordination and contributes electrostatically to inhibitor binding15. In addition, hydrophobic residues such as Val33 and Leu156 line the ATP pocket and stabilize bound ligands through dispersion forces12, while Asn154 has been reported to form polar interactions that support ligand recognition26. Residues Ile25, Lys151, and Asp109, not previously emphasized in structural studies, appeared here as additional contributors that may reflect secondary stabilizing effects specific to this ligand series. Together, these interactions involve catalytically and structurally relevant regions of CDK9, providing a mechanistic rationale for the high stability and predicted inhibitory potential of the identified bioisosteric ligands.

  Taken together, the stability of RMSD trajectories, the steady evolution of total binding free energies throughout the simulations, and the consistent per-residue energy contribution profiles across key residues suggest that all simulated complexes achieved equilibrium and maintained convergence, confirming the reliability of the MD analysis.


## Conclusion and perspectives
  This study employed a combined approach of bioisosteric replacement, pharmacophore modeling, and QSAR-based virtual screening to identify novel CDK9 inhibitor candidates. The resulting dataset comprises hundreds of promising compounds with predicted Ki values comparable to those of the most potent reported inhibitors. As prove of this, three ligands 9550, 9724, and 31801 with predicted Ki values of 2 nM show in molecular dynamics simulations formation of stable complexes with CDK9. The total binding energy calculations demonstrate a strong affinity for complex formation, supported by favorable interactions with key residues in the ATP-binding motif of CDK9, as revealed by interaction decomposition analyses. The high stability and favorable binding free energy of these bioisosteres in complex with CDK9, which exceed those of the control, further reinforce the reliability of the QSAR-predicted Ki values, confirming their potential as highly active molecules. The electronic properties calculated by DFT suggest that 9724 and 9550 show reactivity, with further validation needed to confirm these results. In contrast, ligand 31801 appears to be electronically more stable and less reactive. Overall, the results obtained are consistent and robust within the applied computational framework; nevertheless, experimental validation through in vitro assays with purified CDK9, alongside cytotoxicity evaluations, is required to confirm the inhibitory activity and biological relevance of the compounds identified in this study.
