# Designing synthetic regulatory elements using the generative AI framework DNA-Diffusion


## Abstract
  Systematically designing regulatory elements for precise gene expression control remains a central challenge in genomics and synthetic biology. We introduce DNA-Diffusion, a generative artificial intelligence framework that uses machine learning trained on DNA accessibility data from diverse cell lines to design compact regulatory elements with cell-type-specific activity. Here we show that DNA-Diffusion generates 200-base-pair synthetic elements that recapitulate endogenous transcription factor binding grammar while exhibiting enhanced cell-type specificity. We validated these elements using a 5,850-element STARR-seq library across three cell lines. Additionally, we demonstrated successful endogenous gene modulation using EXTRA-seq, reactivating AXIN2, a leukemia-protective gene, in its native genomic context. Our approach outperforms existing computational methods in balancing functional activity with cell-type specificity while maintaining sequence diversity. This work establishes DNA-Diffusion as a powerful tool for engineering compact, highly specific regulatory elements crucial for advancing gene therapies and understanding gene regulation.


## Introduction
  Gene regulation is a complex process where elements like promoters and enhancers modulate gene activity, defining cell identity. A key goal in genomics and synthetic biology is to harness these elements for gene expression control and to correct disease-related misregulation. While large-scale projects1-7 have annotated millions of regulatory elements, synthesizing them de novo to achieve desired gene expression remains a critical challenge. Generative artificial intelligence (AI) has revolutionized various fields and offers a powerful approach to design sequences, enabling precise control (Fig. 1a-b, Supplementary Note 1).

  Diffusion models8-10, a class of generative AI, have shown success in generating complex data like images and proteins11-14, making them a promising tool for designing biological sequences (Supplementary Note 2). However, current AI methods for designing regulatory elements15-23 face significant limitations. They often lack user control over activity levels, perform inconsistently across different cell types, and most critically, have not been validated in their native genomic context, relying instead of artificial episomal assays (Supplementary Note 3).

  To overcome these challenges, we introduce DNA-Diffusion, a conditional diffusion model for designing context-specific DNA regulatory sequences. Its key innovations include: (1) a single model that generates cell-type-specific elements without requiring separate models for each cell type or additional post-processing steps; (2) tunable control to balance signal strength and sequence diversity; and (3) the ability to generate sequences that preserve natural regulatory grammar while exhibiting enhanced and specific activity. This approach is vital for applications like targeted gene therapies24, where compact and highly specific elements are necessary for delivery systems like AAVs, which have limited cargo space25. Given the limitation of using endogenous enhancers in therapeutic applications26, DNA-Diffusion generates tailored, compact 200-bp elements, expanding the toolkit for precision medicine (Supplementary Note 4).

  We rigorously validated DNA-Diffusion through a multi-faceted approach. Extensive in silico analyses confirmed the sequences had appropriate TF binding motifs and regulatory patterns. We also experimentally assessed the transcriptional activity of 5,850 synthetic sequences using large-scale STARR-seq27,28. Critically, we validated their regulatory function in native genomic context using EXTRA-seq29, successfully modulating AXIN2, a leukemia therapeutic target30, at its native locus in human B cells. Finally, comprehensive benchmarking against existing methods revealed DNA-Diffusion’s superior signal intensity, specificity, and sequence diversity balance. DNA-Diffusion is a diffusion model for regulatory element design validated through both large-scale reporter assays and direct endogenous gene modulation, bridging the gap between computational design and therapeutic application.


## Results

### DNA-Diffusion is a conditional diffusion model that generates cell-type-specific regulatory elements
  DNA-Diffusion is a conditional diffusion model that operates in the space of DNA sequences. Sequences are encoded using a strategy akin to one-hot encoding, but each nucleotide has a support range of [−1,1] to facilitate the injection of Gaussian noise centered around zero. The model’s backbone is a denoising U-Net31 architecture with two embedding layers for cell type labels and diffusion timesteps respectively, enabling it to learn a cell-type-specific denoising function (Fig. 1c, Methods). The model receives three inputs during training: DNA sequences, a timestep, and a cell-type label. After training, a cell-type label can be used as input to generate novel, cell-type-specific sequences. To train the model, we leverage the comprehensive DNase I hypersensitive site (DHS) index dataset curated by Meuleman et al.32, which contains 733 biosamples from 438 cell and tissue types. We focused our initial efforts on three well-characterized and distinct cell lines: GM12878 (B-lymphocyte), K562 (leukemia), and HepG2 (hepatocellular carcinoma). These lines were chosen for their diverse biological origins, distinct germ layer lineages, ease of culture, and the rich availability of existing genomic data for model interpretability and evaluation.

  We assigned a categorical class label for each cell type and selected sequences spanning 200 bp around the summit of cell type-specific DHS peaks (Methods). To ensure model generalizability and prevent data leakage, we partitioned the dataset using a chromosome-based stratified sampling strategy: chromosome 1 was held out as the independent test set, chromosome 2 was used for validation, and the remaining chromosomes were used for training. During training, the model learns to reverse a forward process where varying levels of Gaussian noise are progressively added to the encoded sequence representations. The model’s objective is to learn a function that can effectively denoise a sequence at each step by predicting the patterns of introduced noise (Fig. 1d). After successful training (Supplementary Fig. 1), the model can synthesize novel DNA sequences from random noise through a reverse process, conditioned on a specific cell type label and a number of steps (Fig. 1e). With this model we generated 100,000 synthetic 200-bp DNA sequences for each of the three cell types and evaluated their genomic and functional characteristic across various metrics: sequence uniqueness, TF motif composition, predicted accessibility, and the predicted capability to drive gene expression in a cell-type-specific manner (Fig. 1f,g).


### Diffusion-generated sequences exhibit motifs present in endogenous sequences without memorizing existing sequences
  To verify the uniqueness of the DNA-Diffusion sequences and confirm that they were not mere copies of the training sequences, we used BLAT33 to conduct a comparative sequence alignment between the generated DNA-Diffusion sequences and the sets of training, test, and random genomic coordinates.

  DNA-Diffusion sequences showed minimal sequence identity to the training set, with only 4.7% having at least one alignment hit of ≥20 bp, substantially lower than that of randomly selected genomic coordinates (23.1%) and test DHSs (14.5%), indicating genuinely novel DNA sequences without overfitting (Fig. 2a, top). The within group alignment for generated sequences (25.3%) was higher than for the training sequences (14.5%), which is expected as the model learns to combine common functional TF motifs (Fig. 2a, bottom).

  After accounting for repetitive elements that inflated random sequence alignments (Supplementary Fig. 2a,b), and using a more stringent ≥30 bp threshold, only 0.6% of DNA-Diffusion sequences aligned to the training set (Supplementary Fig. 3a, Supplementary Note 5). These alignment results demonstrate that DNA-Diffusion generates diverse sequences without memorizing training data.

  Having confirmed novelty, we assessed whether the sequences captured regulatory grammar using MOODS34 to scan for TF bindings motifs from the JASPAR35 database. To compare the motif compositions, we calculated the Jensen-Shannon (JS) divergence between the motif distributions of generated sequences and the endogenous test set. This yielded a low mean JS divergence of 0.1 across the three cell types, revealing a high degree of similarity and suggesting that the model learned an appropriate regulatory vocabulary for each cellular context (Fig. 2b). In fact, the model appeared to distill and enrich for the most informative cell-type-specific motifs. For example, HNF4A36, a liver-specific pioneer factor, was present in approximately 47% of generated HepG2 sequences, compared to only 29% of endogenous HepG2 training sequences (Fig. 2c). We observed a similar enrichment for key immune-related factors like IRF1 in GM12878 sequences (55% generated vs. 38% training) and hematopoietic factors like GATA1-TAL137 in K562 sequences (41% generated vs. 31% training). Conversely, the model learned to exclude TF motifs specific to other cell types, suggesting it creates sequences with enhanced in silico specificity. This enrichment extended to TF motif co-occurrences, with IRF1-STAT1::STAT2 appearing in 24% of generated GM12878 sequences versus 12% in training, while preserving endogenous spacing and orientation (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. 4, Supplementary Note 6).

  We discovered that the motif enrichment can be controlled by the classifier-free guidance (CFG) parameter38, which allows for tunable amplification of the conditioning signal during generation (Methods). By adjusting the CFG scale, we can modulate the density of these cell-type-specific TF motifs consistent with theory that suggests diffusion models tends to concentrate probability mass on high-likelihood regions of the learned distribution39. Sequences generated without guidance (CFG scale 0) closely matched the training set’s motif abundance, while a higher scale (CFG scale 2) produced even greater enrichment than the default (Supplementary Fig. 5). This finding demonstrates that diffusion naturally emphasizes functional determinants of cell-type-specificity while de-emphasizing non-functional or shared elements, providing a powerful, interpretable lever to balance biological plausibility with functional optimization.


### DNA-Diffusion-generated sequences demonstrate cell-type-specificity, accessibility, and cell-type-specific activation of cis genes in silico
  To predict the functional potential of our synthetic sequences prior to costly experimental testing, we employed state-of-the-art deep learning “oracles” that predict genomic activity from sequence alone (Fig. 3a). We systematically replaced endogenous sequences at specific DHS sites with our generated sequences to determine if they could maintain or alter chromatin accessibility and gene expression in their respective cell types in silico.

  We focused on regulatory regions of three well-characterized cell-type-specific genes: GATA1 (K562), HNF4A (HepG2), and CD19 (GM12878) (Supplementary Fig. 6; Supplementary Table 1). Using ChromBPNet40, which predicts chromatin accessibility at nucleotide resolution, we first tested sequences at a GATA1 regulatory element previously validated by CRISPRi41. When K562-specific DNA-Diffusion sequences were inserted, they were predicted to maintain or enhance in silico accessibility specifically in K562 cells, achieving a significantly higher median z-scored normalized DNase I value (1.12) than training K562 sequences (0.92) (p < 0.01, Wilcoxon rank-sum test), while showing reduced activity in non-target cells (GM12878: −0.62; HepG2: −0.74) (Fig. 3b). Conversely, GM12878 or HepG2-specific sequences induced in silico accessibility at this otherwise silent locus specifically in their target cell types, again with stronger signal than their training counterparts. These patterns held true when testing insertion at enhancers from other loci, such as those for HNF4A and CD19 (Supplementary Figs. 7,8).

  Next, to assess in silico transcriptional potential, we used a model trained to predict MPRA activity42. DNA-Diffusion sequences consistently showed high predicted cell-type-specific activity, with distributions that closely mirrored those of the endogenous training sequences (Fig. 3c), indicating the model learned to generate sequences with in silico transcriptional potential. We extended this analysis using Enformer43, a powerful transformer-based model that predicts thousands of genomic tracks, including gene expression (CAGE signal), from a large 200-kb sequence context. Enformer’s predictions for DNase I accessibility at the GATA1 enhancer mirrored the ChromBPNet results (Fig. 3d). We then examined the predicted impact on in silico gene expression by looking at the Enformer CAGE signal near the GATA1 TSS. The insertion of cell-type-specific DNA-Diffusion sequences at the validate enhancer site led to a significant increase in predicted promoter activity for each respective cell type (p < 0.01, Wilcoxon rank-sum test), even in GM12878 and HepG2 cells, where the gene is normally inactive (Fig. 3e). Several generated sequences exceeded the in silico performance of both training data and wild-type enhancers (Fig. 3f) and contained known transcription factor binding sites (Fig. 3g). Furthermore, using an in silico tiling strategy, we analyzed replacement at all the 200-bp windows within the GATA1 locus and found an intronic region that significantly enhanced reactivation (Supplementary Fig 9, 10; Supplementary Note 7).

  Although we emphasize that creating an accessible region in silico does not necessarily indicate active gene expression, this analysis reveals a significant limitation of using Enformer as a predictor of gene expression. Using the GATA1 enhancer as an example, in silico perturbation of its CRISPRi-validated enhancer fails to reduce GATA1 expression based on the K562 CAGE signal prediction (Fig. 3f). This limitation complicates efforts to examine potential off-target effects resulting from the introduction of new regulatory elements, consistent with previous studies documenting similar constraints44,45.

  Despite this limitation, these sequences consistently demonstrated predicted accessibility levels on par with or higher than DHS regions and were also able to induce the intended in silico cell-type-specific expression of the target genes. For loci with existing regulatory activity in specific cell types (e.g., the distal GATA1 enhancer in K562), we incorporated the wild-type enhancer sequence (marked with a star in Fig. 3b,d,e) as a reference point. Our analysis revealed that many synthetic sequences not only match but exceed the chromatin accessibility and gene activation potential of the wild-type element across all computational oracles. Specifically, our DNA-Diffusion sequence set contains 483 GM12878 sequences, 507 HepG2 sequences, and 553 K562 sequences that surpass the wild-type sequence across all in silico assays. This finding is particularly significant when contrasted with the training dataset, which includes only 2 GM12878, 1 HepG2, and 1 K562 sequences that outperform the wild-type sequence in K562. These synthetic sequences can activate GATA1 to higher in silico levels than the endogenous wild-type sequence achieves in K562 and can do so even in cell types where GATA1 is normally silent (HepG2 and GM12878). This pattern holds consistently across all three actively expressing loci (GATA1, HNF4A, and CD19). This evidence suggested that our model could generate highly active and specific regulatory elements capable of precise gene modulation in silico.


### Selection and characterization of synthetic sequences with different regulatory potential and cell type specificity
  The ability to generate a vast number of sequences allows for the selection of elements with specific desired characteristics. We developed a framework to select DNA-Diffusion sequences along two dimensions: “signal intensity” and “signal specificity” (Fig. 4a). The product of these metrics yields a single Intensity-Specificity (IS) score (Methods). Given the complexity of using oracles to predict CAGE expression scores for genes that are already expressed in our tested cell lines, as demonstrated in our previous analysis of GATA1, HNF4A, and CD19, we also analyzed TNFSF18. This gene presents both experimentally verified and computationally predicted low expression and low accessibility values across all three cell types while still providing differential prediction across all the target assays (DNase I, CAGE) (Supplementary Fig. 11, 12). To validate these metrics, we compared our in silico derived score with score derived based on experimentally measured chromatin accessibility data, finding strong correlations for signal intensity (r = 0.57-0.67), signal specificity (r = 0.70-0.77), and the combined IS score (r = 0.65-0.74 across multiple gene loci, Supplementary Fig. 13, Supplementary Table 3, Methods).

  Using the IS score, we selected the top 100 sequences per cell type from both the DNA-Diffusion and training datasets at the unbiased TNFSF18 locus (Supplementary Table 4). These results showed a compelling advantage of the synthetic sequences, which achieved higher IS scores across all three in silico oracles (ChromBPNet DNase, Enformer DNase, Enformer CAGE) compared to the best training DHS sequences (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Fig. 14). This highlights the model’s capability to discern sequence features unique to specific cell types and generate sequences that optimize both signal intensity and specificity beyond what is typically found in the genome.

  We then investigated the TF motif composition of sequences classified as high signal vs high specificity (Fig. 4a). In HepG2 sequences, the HNF1A motif was enriched in high signal sequences (56% vs. 23% in high specificity), while the FOXC2 motif was enriched in high specificity sequences (69% vs. 45% in high signal), suggesting a learned regulatory logic for tuning these properties (Fig. 4c,d). Similar patterns were observed in GM12878 with immune-related TF motifs like REL and MEF2C and GATA family motifs in K562. TF expression analysis revealed that many motifs enriched in high-specificity and high-signal sequences are predominantly expressed in a cell-type-specific manner (Supplementary Fig. 15). These findings suggest a sequence-based regulatory logic distinguishing these two categories of sequences, underscoring the nuanced relationship between TF motifs and their regulatory impact in specific cellular contexts.


### Using STARR-seq to evaluate the regulatory activity of DNA-Diffusion sequences
  To validate the predictions obtained for the DNA-Diffusion sequences, we performed Self-transcribing Active Regulatory Region Sequencing (STARR-seq)27,46 on 5,850 DNA elements in K562, HepG2, and GM12878 cells to measure the transcriptional activity of each element in an episomal context (Fig. 5a).

  STARR-seq quantitatively measures the ability of DNA sequences cloned into reporter plasmids to drive transcription, determined by the ratio of the abundance of mRNA transcripts derived from the DNA fragments relative to their representation in the original DNA pool (Fig. 5a). Although this assay does not directly probe chromatin accessibility, its quantitative output has been shown to correlate positively with accessibility data47, validating its suitability for evaluating the functional activity of DNA- Diffusion sequences. Our library design included multiple endogenous control sets and DNA-Diffusion sequences selected using various strategies (Supplementary Table 5e, Supplementary Note 8).

  The results robustly confirmed our computational predictions despite the known shortcomings of the predicted oracles we used44,45. Endogenous DHS sequences established experimental baselines, showing higher activity in their native cellular contexts. Randomly sampled DNA-Diffusion sequences accurately mirrored this behavior, demonstrating that the model captures the quantitative regulatory activity of the sequences it was trained on (Fig. 5b). DNA-Diffusion sequences selected for high in silico intensity or a high IS score outperformed strong, previously validated positive control elements. Despite the DNA-Diffusion model not being trained on expression data, the top-ranked generated sequences consistently outperformed the strongest validated genomic sequences experimentally. Importantly, incorporating specificity in addition to signal intensity for the selection of generated sequences did not compromise their transcriptional potential. STARR-seq activity correlated strongly with in silico Enformer DNase I scores, with Pearson correlation coefficients reaching 0.74 in both K562 and HepG2 (Fig. 5c). The lower correlation in GM12878 (0.42) was consistent with known transfection efficiency challenges in this cell line48.

  To link function to sequence grammar, we performed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis49 (GSEA) on TF motifs within a sequence set ranked by their experimentally observed regulatory activity levels (Supplementary Table 6, Methods). This confirmed that high transcriptional activity was significantly associated with the presence of the expected cell-type-specific TF motifs (e.g. GATA1-TAL1 in K562, HNF4A in HepG2, and REL in GM12878) (Fig. 5d, Supplementary Figs. 16, 17).

  These results underscore DNA-Diffusion’s potential to generate functionally active, cell-type-specific regulatory elements beyond naturally occurring genomic counterparts. Our generative framework enables efficient selection of sequences demonstrating high regulatory activity and specificity, significantly expanding the repertoire of experimentally validated regulatory elements for diverse biological applications.


### DNA-Diffusion sequences can reactivate a human leukemia protective gene in an endogenous context
  Building upon our previous findings that highlighted DNA-Diffusion’s capability to generate cell-type-specific chromatin accessibility and precisely modulate gene expression both computationally and experimentally, we sought to test its efficacy within an endogenous cellular context. Specifically, we targeted the locus of Axin-related Protein 2 (AXIN2), a gene often silenced in leukemic B-cells. Interestingly, a naturally occurring protective genetic variant (rs143348853), located approximately 2.5 kb upstream of the AXIN2 transcription start site within a distal regulatory element, can reactivate AXIN2. This variant enhances the binding affinity of MEF2-family transcription factors, modestly increasing AXIN2 expression and importantly correlating with significantly improved prognosis chronic lymphocytic leukemia30 (Fig 6a,b).

  To evaluate the capacity of DNA-Diffusion sequences to endogenously modulate AXIN2 transcription, we employed a MPRA-like system, called EXTRA-seq3, which utilizes CRE-recombinase mediated cassette exchange and long-read sequencing to measure the gene’s transcriptional output in response to enhancer sequences (Fig. 6c, Supplementary Table 7a-d).

  We tested a library of 100 sequences in a B-cell leukemia cell line (MEC-1), including 60 GM12878-specific DNA-Diffusion selected to span a range of predicted activities, and included various positive and negative controls (Fig. 6d, Methods). DNA-Diffusion sequences designed for GM12878 effectively reactivated endogenous AXIN2 expression, with multiple sequences surpassing the activity of the natural protective variant and achieving activation levels comparable to strong positive controls from other studies (Fig. 6e, Methods). Sequences designed for non-B-cell contexts (K562 and HepG2) showed no significant activity, confirming the cell type-specificity of the synthetic sequences in an endogenous setting.

  For practical applications where it is necessary to select and validate a small set of sequences with a given design goal, we assessed which computational metrics best predicted functional outcomes. CAGE signal intensity was the best ranking metric to select sequences with 6 out of the top 10 sequences exhibiting log2FC greater than the protective variant and obtaining results comparable to the positive controls. The CAGE IS Score (enhancer site) performed similarly, identifying 5 of its top 10 ranked sequences that met this threshold. Remarkably, all top 3 sequences ranked by CAGE IS Score group strongly reactivated AXIN2 (Fig. 6f, Supplementary Fig. 18).

  Post-hoc analysis revealed that the Enformer prediction of CAGE signal at the enhancer insertion site had the strongest correlation to measured transcriptional activity, with a Spearman rho of 0.70 (Fig. 6g, Supplementary Fig. 19). DNase I accessibility predictions showed little to no correlation, suggesting that chromatin accessibility alone is not a strong predictor of activation potential in this context.

  Additionally, we discovered motifs enriched in sequences that, despite showing high levels of predicted chromatin accessibility, failed to reactivate AXIN2. These factors include RUNX2 (Runt-related transcription factor 2), known for its role in osteoblast differentiation50 and LEF1 (lymphoid enhancer binding factor-1), which mediates the Wnt pathway in CLL cells51. RUNX2 upregulation has been shown to act as a repressor in patients with acute lymphocytic leukemia and plays a key role in disease progression52 (Fig. 6h). This finding suggests that factors beyond chromatin accessibility, such as the presence of other regulatory elements or the recruitment of additional transcriptional machineries, may be necessary to drive AXIN2 expression.

  These findings demonstrate the potential of DNA-Diffusion to design sequences with therapeutic potential, showing their effectiveness in an endogenous setting. By identifying TFs that are more likely to be effective and optimizing the generation to incorporate or exclude specific TF “words”, we can fine-tune DNA-Diffusion sequences for enhanced performance. This optimization approach opens exciting opportunities for targeted gene regulation and the development of therapeutic strategies.


### Benchmarking DNA-Diffusion against state-of-the-art methods for the generation of synthetic regulatory elements
  We systematically benchmarked DNA-Diffusion against other leading approaches for regulatory element design including GANs and CNN-based regressors17,22,53. We retrained these models on our dataset and generated 1,000 sequences per cell type for each model (Methods, Supplementary Note 9). We developed an in silico validation framework to evaluate all methods on three key metrics: signal intensity, cell-type-specificity, and motif diversity (Fig. 7a, Methods). For signal intensity and specificity assessment, we used Enformer DNase I predictions at the TNFSF18 locus, chosen to minimize potential bias from pre-existing cell-type-specific expression patterns. To quantify motif diversity, we developed a metric based on the Gini coefficient, where a score of 1 indicates highly diverse motif usage.

  Results revealed fundamental trade-offs between generation approaches. Optimization-focused approaches like CODA generate sequences with higher predicted activity (average median IS score of 0.168), but this came at the cost of reduced motif diversity (average median score of 0.24), as the model converged on a few optimal TF motifs (Fig. 7b,c). CODA also incorporates a motif penalization procedure that enables enhanced sequence diversity generation, with sequential rounds yielding progressively degrading IS scores: Round 1 (0.065) and Round 2 (0.001). However, this penalization requires manual, cell-type-specific tuning and expertise in TF motif composition.

  In contrast, standard DNA-Diffusion (with moderate CFG = 1) produced a more balanced output (IS score 0.04, diversity 0.53), outperforming GANs (IS score 0.006, diversity 0.59) and matching the activity of DeepMEL (IS score 0.041, diversity 0.58) while maintaining comparable diversity to endogenous elements (IS score 0.03, diversity 0.58). Increasing CFG to 7 tuned DNA-Diffusion to outperform all other methods on signal intensity and specificity (IS score 0.215), effectively operating as a powerful sequence optimizer when desired, albeit with a corresponding drop in diversity (diversity score 0.36). This allows to bridge the gap between sequences that resemble endogenous elements and those that aim to maximize activity.

  To systematically evaluate how these metrics translate to practical sequence selection for experimental validation, we examined model performance under simulated validation conditions. We selected and analyzed each model’s top-performing sequences (100 per cell type) based on minimal Euclidean distance from the optimal point (1,1) in the predicted signal intensity/specificity space and their motif diversity (Supplementary Table 8, Supplementary Note 9).

  A unified performance metric, represented by the area of a radar plot summarizing all three axes, confirmed that standard DNA-Diffusion offers the best overall balance of activity, specificity, and motif diversity (area score 0.71) while DNA-Diffusion with CFG7 had the highest area score overall (0.84) (Fig. 7d).

  Taken together, DNA-Diffusion distinguishes itself through its versatility, enabling the generation of sequences that closely mirror endogenous sequence patterns or optimized for activation. While CODA and DeepMEL can generate sequences exceeding their training data’s in silico signal potential, DNA-Diffusion’s tunability through CFG allows it to excel across different design objectives without requiring manual intervention for each cell type.


## Discussion
  We developed DNA-Diffusion, a generative AI framework that designs compact regulatory elements with tunable cell-type specificity. Through experimental validation, we demonstrated that these designed sequences can modulate AXIN2, a disease-relevant gene, in its native genomic context, suggesting potential therapeutic applications.

  DNA-Diffusion differs from prior methods in several ways. Consistent with theoretical predictions54, the model learns to generate potent elements in an end-to-end fashion, without requiring manual cell-type-specific tuning. Classifier-free guidance allows users to balance the generation of endogenous-like elements with sequences designed for maximum activation potential. Benchmarking revealed this versatility is notable, as DNA-Diffusion achieves a superior balance of signal intensity, specificity, and motif diversity, increasing the likelihood of generating functionally robust elements with minimized off-target potential.

  High-throughput STARR-seq assays confirmed that DNA-Diffusion can generate several elements that are more active and specific than endogenous controls. More importantly, the EXTRA-seq experiment at the AXIN2 locus provides a proof-of-concept for the therapeutic potential of this technology. By demonstrating that computationally designed sequences can reactivate a protective gene in a disease-relevant context, outperforming a natural genetic variant, this work provides a foundation for precision gene therapies.

  To our knowledge, this is the first DDPM-based DNA diffusion model validated with both large-scale reporter assays and direct modification of an endogenous locus. While this marks a substantial advance for the field, our model has limitations. Since DNA-Diffusion was trained to reproduce the accessibility distribution and diversity of endogenous DHS regions, our approach requires a second step to select sequences with higher regulatory activity using oracles. As shown in Figure 5b, randomly sampled DNA-Diffusion sequences exhibit similar activities to endogenous DHS elements, with enhanced signals emerging only after computational pre-selection. In addition, its reliance on binarized labels for DHS peaks could be improved by incorporating continuous measures of chromatin activity. Recent diffusion models and large language models have explored this idea and have demonstrated promise in in silico studies, warranting experimental validation to confirm their efficacy19,21,55-57. Finally, we expect this approach will benefit from extension to a broader range of cell types and from creating active learning cycles where experimental results are used to progressively refine the model.

  In conclusion, DNA-Diffusion is a robust platform that holds immense promise for developing gene therapies and deepening our understanding of the DNA regulatory code and grammar.


## Methods

### Data preprocessing for training
  We selected training data from an annotated index of DNase I Hypersensitive Site (DHS) regions in the human genome32, focusing on data for K562, GM12878 and HepG2 cell lines based on ease of laboratory culture, tissue diversity and availability of additional annotation and data. A detailed description of the curation and filtration methods are provided in Supplementary Methods 1.


### Model Architecture
  Our DNA-Diffusion model extends the foundational Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model (DDPM) framework8, with architectural implementations informed by the Annotated Diffusion Model. We implemented several fundamental adaptations to optimize the architecture for nucleotide sequence generation. In contrast to conventional image-based diffusion models that operate on square inputs and pixel values, our model processes DNA sequences using a dimensional tensor structure of (batch, 1, 4, 200), wherein the dimension of 4 corresponds to the four nucleotides (A, C, T, G) and 200 represents the sequence length. The U-Net architecture preserves the essential structural elements from the original DDPM framework while reconfiguring channel dimensions specifically for DNA sequence representation, projecting encoded sequences to dimensions that optimally balance nucleotide dependency modeling with computational efficiency. We provide a more detailed description of the components of the U-Net backbone in Supplementary Methods 2.

  For model training, we implemented cell type-specific conditioning with DNA-appropriate modifications to enable targeted regulatory sequence generation. The conditioning protocol incorporated cell type labels with a 10% random masking rate during training to facilitate classifier-free guidance during the sampling phase. These conditioning labels were concatenated with timestep embeddings and integrated into the ResNet58 blocks, while attention layers59,60 exclusively processed the noised input sequences. During the reverse diffusion process, conditional and unconditional samples were jointly processed with a guidance scale parameter of 1.0. Through this methodological approach, we generated a comprehensive dataset of 100,000 sequences per cell type, yielding a total of 300,000 generated regulatory sequences for subsequent analysis and validation. We note that utilizing a guidance scale of 1 generates class conditional outputs with moderate guidance and 0 generates class-conditional outputs with no additional guidance.


### Training process
  The main diffusion model was implemented in PyTorch 2.0.0 and trained on 4x 40GB NVIDIA A100 GPUs using a batch size of 960 for 2,000 epochs requiring approximately 10 hours to complete. Subsequent sequence generation using a single PCIE A100 GPU proceeds at a rate of approximately 15 sequences per second. For distributed computing across the nodes, we employed Slurm Workload Manager. We used the Adam61 optimizer with a learning rate of 1e-4 along with PyTorch default values for other hyperparameters: β1 = 0.9 and β2 = 0.999. Linear noising schedule was used with βstart = 0.0001 and βend = 0.005 .


### In-Silico Validation

#### Sequence similarity search
  We performed the BLAT33 similarity search using two strategies. In the first strategy, we used the training sequences as the database and searched for matches using the generated, test, and randomly selected genomic coordinate sequences. The random sequences were obtained using the BedTools62 random function, which selects genomic regions without bias toward their sequence composition. After the initial selection, we removed sequences intersecting with DHS sites used in our training and test datasets.

  For the analysis that included only alignments without overlap with genomic repetitive elements, we used all repetition types in the hg38 RepeatMasker63,64 available in UCSC genome browser database to filter out hits that intersected with the training DHS regions. This intersection was created using command bedtools intersect with a bed file containing the alignments coordinates regions relative to the train sequences and another file with repetitive regions.

  In the second strategy, we considered alignments within groups using the same sequence set as both the database and the query (e.g., Train x Train, Random x Random, and Generated x Generated). The BLAT parameters used in both strategies were: q=dna, tileSize=11, stepSize=5, -minMatch=1, repMatch=2253, minIdentity=100, noHead=True, and minScore=20 or 30


#### Motif Scanning
  To scan motifs the MOODS34 1.9.4.1 package was used using the vertebrate motifs from the JASPAR 2024 database35 (n = 880 PWMs). The parameters used in MOODS were a p-value of 0.0001 and a pseudocount (--ps 0.0001). We observe the appearance of key cell type-specific TF motifs around step 30 that resolve more granularly until the final timestep 50 (Supplementary Fig. 20). We provide all scanning (Supplementary Data 1) and co-occurrence (Supplementary Data 2) results as supplementary data.


#### Assessing DNA chromatin accessibility with ChromBPNet
  Chromatin accessibility is a hallmark of regulatory activity for a DNA sequence. We used the ChromBPNet40 model as a biological oracle to assess the cell-type specificity of generated diffusion sequences’ chromatin accessibility. Details of ChromBPNet’s architecture are noted in Supplementary Methods 3.

  We used ChromBPNet models trained on ENCODE1,2,4 experiment data ENCSR000EPC, ENCSR000EMT, and ENCSR000ENP to predict base-pair chromatin accessibility of DNA-Diffusion sequences in K562, GM12878, and HepG2 contexts respectively. Each ChromBPNet model’s input was a one-hot encoded 2114 bp DNA sequence (A = [1,0,0,0], C = [0,1,0,0], G = [0,0,1,0], T = [0,0,0,1]).

  To evaluate how DNA sequences generated by the diffusion model affect chromatin accessibility, we inserted them within known or putative regulatory regions for the following genes: GATA1, CD19, HNF4A, TNFSF18 and calculated the mean of the predicted base-resolution chromatin accessibility score.

  Given a generated sequence designed for a particular cell type, we ideally expect that the ChromBPNet model trained to predict accessibility in that cell type will yield greater chromatin accessibility predictions relative to the other two ChromBPNet models, indicating greater regulatory potential. However, the three ChromBPNet models used were trained on experimental data with different read depths: ENCSR000EPC had a read depth of 245 million, ENCSR000EMT had a read depth of 68 million, and ENCSR000EMT had a read depth of 323 million. Thus, we observed that models trained on deeper data typically predicted higher chromatin accessibility compared to the other two models for a generated sequence, irrespective of the cell type specificity of a sequence - leading to inaccurate comparison of model outputs. To combat this potential confounder, we applied quantile normalization (see Section 5. Normalization and scaling of the data).


#### MPRA Predictor
  We trained a model42 to predict MPRA activity using MPRA data from GM12878, K562, HepG2, SK-N-SH, and A549 cells (accession IDs are available in Supplementary Table 3). The assayed sequences are 200-bp long and most of them are derived from human genomic segments containing the reference and alternate alleles for variants from UK Biobank65 and GTEx66. These sequences are cloned upstream of a reporter gene and this construct is delivered to host cells using transient transfection. Each sequence’s MPRA activity is measured as the log2 of the ratio of the number of mRNA molecules produced using the construct to the number of constructs delivered to the host cells. From ENCODE, we obtained 318,734, 636,185, 750,298, 750,084, and 318,734 MPRA activity measurements from GM12878, K562, HepG2, SK-N-SH, and A549 cells, respectively. The training procedure of the MPRA predictor is described in Supplementary Methods 4.

  The trained models were accurate and achieved Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients of 0.4527, 0.7285, 0.7682, 0.7745 and 0.6682 on the test sets for GM12878, K562, HepG2, SK-N-SH, and A549 cells respectively. It achieved Pearson’s correlation coefficients of 0.4479, 0.8115, 0.8257, 0.8199 and 0.7218 on the test sets for GM12878, K562, HepG2, SK-N-SH, and A549 cells respectively.


#### Assessing DNA chromatin accessibility and gene expression with Enformer
  We replaced the endogenous sequence of putative regulatory regions with DNA-Diffusion or other endogenous sequences and considered Enformer predictions using a window of 393,216 base pairs around the region used for replacement. The resulting Enformer43 predictions offered an approximate 128-bp resolution window for DNase I and CAGE tracks across all three cell lines. We averaged the extracted predictions across enhancer and promoter regions to generate a single value representing the predicted signal at each genomic coordinate.


#### Selecting random genomic regions and promoter regions
  Random sequences were obtained using the BedTools random function, which selects random genomic coordinates of 200 bp. Promoter regions of protein-coding genes were extracted from GENCODE v4267 and extended ±500 bp from the TSS start site to generate same-length intervals.


### Normalization and scaling of the data
  Since Enformer (DNase I and CAGE), ChromBPNet (DNase I) and the MPRA-predictor were trained using data with different sequencing depths, their baseline predictions exhibited a wide dynamic range, making it difficult to directly compare the difference in raw prediction scores across cell lines. Using quantile normalization, we adjusted these predictions and rescaled the data to a common range. This process helped to minimize the differences and biases, allowing for more accurate comparisons and interpretations of the data across the loci and cell types.

  For ChromBPNet predictions in all loci, we normalized the three cell lines’ accessibility predictions, generating a similarly distributed set across K562, HepG2, and GM12878. Unlike ChromBPNet, which uses different models, Enformer outputs a separate prediction signal track for each cell type and data modality. Ultimately, all predictions were normalized together using the following groups: DNA-Diffusion, Training, Validation, Test, Promoters, Random Genome Regions, and the positive and negative control sequences previously validated based on MPRA assays (see Section 6.1 Selection of sequences for normalization and experimental validation). Signal intensity and specificity scores obtained from all oracles were normalized between 0 and 1 (Supplementary Methods 5).


### Episomal experimental validation of DNA-Diffusion sequences with STARR-seq

#### Selection of sequences for normalization and experimental validation
  To create a set of sequences that captured a broad range of potential outcomes in STARR-seq experiments, we generated a non-biased reference set by randomly sampling 400 sequences from the DNA-Diffusion model using a guidance scale of 1.0, without applying any pre-selection criteria. We also selected 400 endogenous DNase I hypersensitive (DHS) elements at random from the 47,873 sequences obtained from the DHS index dataset to serve as a baseline control.

  For K562 positive sequences, 1,365 sequences were selected from the PINTS (Peak Identifier for Nascent Transcript Starts) Database68 based on prior validation by CRISPR, MPRA, or STARR-seq assays. For HepG2 positive sequences, 112 sequences were similarly chosen using HepG2 sequences from the PINTS database. For GM12878 positive sequences, an initial set of 12,946 sequences identified by High-resolution Dissection of Regulatory Activity (HiDRA)69 were intersected with the ENCODE DNase I hypersensitive sites (DHS) index to retain only regions with evidence of chromatin accessibility. The resulting DHS-overlapping sequences were ranked by HiDRA activity scores, and the top 4,000 were selected as high confidence. To define the final set of sequences for STARR-seq validation in each cell type, we averaged the predicted regulatory scores from Enformer (DNase I and CAGE models) and ChromBPNet (DNase I model) across the positive sequences selected in the previous step. The top 100 sequences with the highest averaged predicted activity were retained for experimental validation.

  To select a set of negative sequences, we curated a negative control dataset starting with 7,000 sequences from the PINTS database. All sequences were previously functionally tested via Massively Parallel Reporter Assays (MPRA) or STARR-seq27,46, although definitions of inactivity varied across source studies. These sequences were designated putative "non-enhancers" as they were pre-filtered to exclude overlaps with ENCODE enhancer candidate cis-Regulatory Elements (cCREs). While predominantly derived from K562 cell line experiments, this initial set was treated as cell-type agnostic. To generate cell-type-specific negative controls, we employed ChromBPNet and Enformer computational models to predict regulatory activity for each sequence within the three target cell types. For each cell type, the 50 sequences exhibiting the lowest predicted activity scores were selected as the final negative control set.

  To expand the number of DNA-Diffusion sequences evaluated in our library, we selected a total of 1,000 sequences from a previously generated pool of 300,000 sequences (100,000 per cell type: K562, GM12878, and HepG2), all created using a CFG guidance scale of 1.0. From this pool, we prioritized sequences that spanned a broad range of predicted DNase I accessibility intensities and cell-type specificities, as inferred from Enformer predictions. This strategy ensured that the selected sequences represented a diverse spectrum of chromatin accessibility profiles across the three cell types.

  For more details about the experimental protocol and post-processing of experimental results see Supplementary Methods 6.


### In Vitro Validation of DNA-Diffusion and Control Sequences within the AXIN2 Locus

#### Selecting candidate sequences for downstream experimental validation
  We evaluated a total of 105 sequences using the AXIN2 endogenous replacement experiment (Supplementary Methods 7.1-7.4) using the following sequence groups: GM12878 DNA-Diffusion, GM12878 Positive, shuffled GM12878, Negative Controls, K562 DNA-Diffusion and HepG2 DNA-Diffusion (Supplementary Table 7e). We also included the alternative and reference enhancer allele sequences as described below.

  The sequences were inserted into a region situated 2.5 kb upstream of the AXIN2 TSS. The putative chromatin accessibility or CAGE changes induced by the introduction of these sequences at the AXIN2 locus was assessed using the Enformer model. Cell-type-specificity values the normalization procedures followed instructions previously described (Methods Section 5).


#### GM12878 DNA-Diffusion:
  We utilized the DNA-Diffusion model to sample 10,000 GM12878 cell type-specific sequences. These sequences were analyzed using Enformer to evaluate their accessibility by predicting the DNase I values at the replacement site upstream of the AXIN2 TSS. From an initial pool of 10,000 candidate sequences, 60 sequences were selected across a range of predicted signal intensity and cell type-specificity values. The selection strategy was designed to include a diverse set of sequences, enabling investigation of how different values for accessibility and AXIN2 expression translate to experimentally observed effects on AXIN2 expression.


#### GM12878 Positive and Negative Controls:
  A set of positive control GM12878 sequences, previously validated experimentally using HiDRA, was obtained and filtered to keep only regions intersecting with GM12878 ENCODE DHS peaks. From this subset, 10 sequences with high predicted accessibility were selected for the final positive control group.

  The negative control set of GM12878 sequences was selected from 7,097 sequences previously classified as non-enhancers in the PINTS database. These sequences exhibit minimal or no regulatory activity in MPRA and STARR-seq experiments and did not overlap genomic coordinates of candidate cis-regulatory elements (cCRE v3 hg38) from the SCREEN database70. The predicted DNase I activity at the replacement regions was determined for these sequences. A subset of five sequences was selected based on having the lowest values for DNase I signal intensity and DNase I specificity. In addition, we also included 5 shuffled (single nucleotide shuffle) sequences by shuffling previously selected DNA-Diffusion GM12878 sequences with the highest accessibility and AXIN2 promoter reactivation predicted values.


#### K562 and HepG2 DNA-Diffusion:
  We generated 1,000 K562-specific and 1000 HepG2-specific DNA-Diffusion sequences. Enformer was employed to predict the accessibility for these sequences.

  For each respective cell type’s 1,000 sequence set, a subset of five sequences was selected based on their predicted high performance (high signal intensity and cell type-specificity) in K562 or HepG2 cells, while exhibiting minimal activity in GM12878 cells. The selection process involved ranking the sequences according to their Enformer predictions, considering the DNase I signal at the AXIN2 TSS upstream replacement region. The signal intensity and specificity values were evaluated, and sequences with the highest values for DNase I signal and specificity were chosen. The specificity constraint was crucial for selecting sequences that displayed activation restricted to K562 or HepG2 cells, with minimal evidence of accessibility and AXIN2 promoter reactivation in GM12878 cells.


#### Enformer predictions and visualization of the AXIN2 locus sequences
  To simulate the endogenous experiment, a custom version of the hg38 genome was generated for in silico prediction. This modified genome incorporated the insertion of several elements: LoxP sites, random barcodes, a fixed site, the endogenous UTR, the endogenous sequence, a 200-bp insertion region, a second fixed site, and Lox2272 (Supplementary Table 7a-d). The integration of these components into the genome allowed for a more accurate representation of the experimental conditions when performing in silico predictions.


#### Visualization of the AXIN2 locus using non-protective and protective alleles
  The epigenetic tracks (ATAC-seq, H3K27ac, and H3K4me3) from two individuals, one homozygous for the reference allele and the other homozygous for the alternative allele (rs143348853), were compared. Genetic variant information for these individuals was obtained from the 1000 Genomes Project71, and the corresponding epigenetic data from the European Nucleotide Archive-ENA (ATAC-seq) and ArrayExpress72 (H3K4me3 and H3K27ac). BAM files were converted to the hg38 genome version using CrossMap v0.7.073 and transformed into BigWig tracks using DeepTools v3.5.474 with the following parameters: --binSize 10, -- normalizeUsing RPKM, and --extendReads.


#### Transcription Factor Enrichment Analysis
  To identify transcription factor (TF) motifs associated with gene expression changes, the generated sequences were scanned using MOODS. The resulting motif hits were ranked in descending order based on their corresponding log2FC values. The prerank function from GSEApy (v1.1.3)75 was then employed to correlate the presence of each TF motif with its associated log2FC value with the follow parameters (permutation_num=500, min_size=1, max_size=5000). This analysis allowed for the systematic assessment of the relationship between the occurrence of specific TF motifs and the observed changes in gene expression.


#### Quantification of endogenous AXIN2 expression
  For each of four replicates, gDNA and mRNA counts were quantified by sequencing the 12-nt barcodes corresponding to the designed DNA-Diffusion synthetic sequences. The DNA replicates were merged by barcode; replicates with no reads for a particular barcode had the count set to 0. The RNA replicates were merged following the same procedure. DNA barcode counts were then merged with the corresponding RNA counts by barcode, and barcodes sequenced only in mRNA reads (i.e. at least one read across four replicates vs. no reads across five DNA replicates) were omitted from further analyses.

  The full listing of paired 12-nt barcodes with designed DNA-Diffusion synthetic sequences was filtered to include only barcodes with a minimum of three reads in the mapping step. The DNA and RNA barcodes were then assigned to their corresponding synthetic sequences using this mapping; barcodes with no assigned synthetic sequence were omitted. For each barcode, two key statistics were calculated: the log2 ratio of the sum of RNA counts to the sum of DNA counts, and the difference between this ratio and the median ratio across all barcodes for the corresponding synthetic sequence (“ratio_diff”). Outlier barcodes were removed if “ratio_diff” was greater than five times the median absolute deviation of “ratio_diff” across all barcodes within the same decile of total RNA counts. After filtering per replicate, any RNA count corresponding to a DNA count of 0 was set to 0. The transcriptional activity was analyzed using the “mpra” package76, following the same methodology mentioned in the STARR-seq session (Supplementary Table 7e).


### Training and Benchmarking Computational Regulatory Element Generators
  CODA/Malinois17, DeepMEL218, and a WGAN18 were trained using the original DNA-Diffusion dataset with adaptations to the training. For a detailed explanation of each models specific training pipeline see Supplementary Methods 8.
