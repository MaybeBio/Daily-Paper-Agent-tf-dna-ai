# Biomimetic liposomal co-delivery of quercetin and carnosine for enhanced transdermal penetration and NRF2/HES1-mediated antioxidant photoprotection


## Abstract
  Quercetin and carnosine, natural antioxidants with anti-aging and photoprotective potential, face therapeutic limitations due to poor skin permeability. We engineered EV-QuerCar, a liposomal-based nanoparticles (LPNs) system co-encapsulating both compounds, to enhance transdermal delivery and evaluate its structural and functional efficacy. EV-QuerCar was prepared biomimetically using thermophilic bacterial lipids via ultracentrifugation, thin-film hydration, and high-pressure homogenization. Physicochemical properties, transdermal penetration, cellular uptake, and cytotoxicity were tested in vitro. Molecular docking and qPCR assessed pathway targeting. EV-QuerCar exhibited uniform size (94.70 ± 0.71 nm), high encapsulation efficiency (90.07%), sustained release, and 3.21-fold enhanced transdermal absorption compared to free compounds. Cellular uptake in fibroblasts increased time-dependently (up to 5.48-fold at 4 h). Molecular docking and qPCR confirmed interactions with NRF2 and HES1 targets, significantly upregulating their mRNA levels (2.29- and 3.35-fold, respectively) in UV-exposed human fibroblasts. EV-QuerCar improves skin permeability and antioxidant efficacy, highlighting its cosmeceutical promise.


## Introduction
  Skin aging is a progressive process that is time-dependent on the biological and exogenous factors. The skin aging exposome is defined as the combination of external and internal factors and their interactions, which result in the biological and clinical signs of skin aging1–3. To counteract the harmful effects caused by various stimuli, NRF2 is well known as one of the main orchestrators of the cellular xenobiotic and oxidative stress response4–6. As a master transcriptional factor, nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) plays a key role in the regulation of an extensive panel of antioxidant response elements (ARE)-related genes including superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), glutamate-cysteine ligase catalytic subunit (GCLC) and glutamate–cysteine ligase regulatory subunit (GCLM), NAD(P)H: quinone oxidoreductase-1 (NQO-1) and heme-oxygenase-1 (HO-1)7,8. Most research focuses on NRF2 as an effective target in maintaining redox homeostasis8–10.

  Originating from harmful and excessive free radicals, a shortage of NRF2 leads to the dysregulation of skin fibroblasts, resulting in reduced collagen I biosynthesis, and diminished collagen fibril deposition11. Moreover, the research by single-cell RNA sequencing (SC-RNAseq) has revealed that the molecular changes underlying human skin aging are associated with cell-type-specific senescent genes. In fibroblast cells, the growth controlling transcription factor hairy and enhancer of split-1 (HES1) was identified12. HES1 is a key downstream target of Notch signalling pathway, which regulates fibroblast activation and collagen release13,14. Therefore, both NRF2 and HES1 in skin fibroblasts are essential in skin homeostasis maintenance and aging protection.

  Non-toxic natural ingredients have been widely investigated due to their potential benefits in treating aging-related disorders. Carnosine (beta-alanyl-L-histidine), recognized as a natural antioxidant, excitable vertebrate tissues from oxidative stresses, safeguards skin functionality against UV-radiation and oxidant-induced injuries, and manifests supplementary biological activities, including the chelation of heavy metals and anti-glycating properties15–18. As an endogenous dipeptide and an important metabolite in vertebrates, carnosine is present in several human tissues and can be synthesized endogenously in glial cells and myocytes19–22. Carnosine also exhibits the ability to provide skin photo-protection against a broad range of solar-simulated radiation (SSR, 300–750 nm)23. In addition, carnosine can activate the NRF2 pathway, leading to the upregulation of some ROS-detoxifying enzymes, such as the glutathione S-transferase (GST) protein family24.

  Quercetin [2-(3,4-dihydroxyphenyl)-3,5,7-trihydroxy-4 H-1-benzopyran-4-one], a hydrophilic flavonoid abundant in fruits and vegetables, has drawn attention as a potential natural, non-toxic ingredient. Its notable roles include exerting anti-oxidative, anti-inflammatory, and anti-aging effects25–28. Quercetin is a pioneering bioactive ingredient used in skin senotherapeutics and has emerged as a compelling pharmacological candidate for attenuating cellular senescence26,29,30. At a molecular level, quercetin directly targets multiple signal transduction pathways, including the Notch-HES1 pathway31–33. The downregulation of HES1 caused by UV radiation in fibroblasts was reversed by the supplementation of quercetin12.

  Both carnosine and quercetin exhibit promising physiological activities, but their topical applications face challenges. On one hand, the stability of carnosine is compromised due to its rapid hydrolysis by carnosinase, as well as the influence of environmental factors such as temperature, pH, and metal ions34,35. On the other hand, the poor bioavailability of quercetin limits its oral and topical application in pharmacology. To enhance the skin penetration and stability of quercetin, various drug delivery systems have been explored, including liposomes36, nanoparticles37, micelles38, and inclusion complexes39.

  Liposomal-based nanoparticle (LPNs) typically consist of hydrophobic and hydrophilic structures. Their amphiphilic structure can exist in either a gel phase or a liquid crystalline phase40. The characteristics enable LPNs to encapsulate both hydrophobic and hydrophilic drugs, thereby providing the possibility for the delivery of a variety of active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs). For instance, melittin-LPNs have been reported with a core of melittin/aspartate, a lipid membrane as the middle layer, and PEG as the outer shell, which can significantly improve the delivery efficiency of melittin in the body and inhibit the proliferation of tumour cells41. Another example is the loading of carboplatin (CTX) into CTX-LPNs, which are formed by the self-assembly of polyblock copolymers. This formulation can significantly enhance the penetration and bioavailability of CTX within tissues42. Furthermore, LPNs also greatly enhance the stability of the delivered drugs. Research has reported that siRNA encapsulated in a lipid-polymer hybrid nanoparticle, composed of trehalose, dextran, and leucine as carriers, significantly improves the long-term stability of siRNA compared to unencapsulated siRNA43.

  The aim of this study was to develop a transdermal delivery system designed to co-encapsulate carnosine and quercetin, termed EV-QuerCar, thereby enhancing their stability and penetration. In addition, the study leveraged the physiological effects of these compounds and explored their combined impact on key regulatory targets. Specifically, the system targets the NRF2 pathway for its antioxidant properties and HES1 for its anti-aging effects. The co-encapsulation of carnosine and quercetin within LPNs presents a novel and promising strategy for combating dermal oxidation and aging.


## Materials and methods

### Materials
  Carnosine was obtained from Symrise AG, Germany. Quercetin, β-alanine, Fluorescein Isothiocyanate (FITC), acetic acid-sodium acetate buffer, and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)-grade solvents such as methanol were purchased from Aladdin Chemistry Co., Ltd, China. L-histidine and medium protease were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd, China. Tyramine (p-hydroxyphenylethylamine) and sodium cyanoborohydride were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd, Beijing, China. Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) was purchased from Sevier, China. Thermophilic bacteria, as well as its culture medium and extraction buffer, were kindly provided by Moda Biological Co., LTD., Zhejiang, China.


### Preparation and characteristics of EV-QuerCar
  A biomimetic approach to prepare LPNs was developed in this study. The biomimetic LPNs were derived from thermophilic bacteria using the method we previously reported44,45, which were cultivated in a culture medium at 55 °C for 24 h in a shaking incubator (ZQLY-380B, Changzhou Jintan Liangyou Instrument Co., Ltd., China).

  After cultivation, the bacteria were collected through high-speed centrifugation at 4,000 g for 2 h using a centrifuge (KH20R-II, Hunan Kaida Scientific Instrument Co., LTD., China). The resulting precipitate was dissolved in an extraction buffer and subjected to ultrasonic disruption using a cell crusher (JY92-IIN, Shanghai Huxi Industrial Co., LTD., China) at 65 W for 30 min. Subsequently, the debris from the thermophilic bacteria was removed by high-speed centrifugation at 12,000×g for 30 min. The supernatant was then centrifuged using an ultra-fast centrifuge (Type 100 Ti, Beckman Coulter, USA) at 145,000×g for 1 h to isolate the bacterial culture precipitate. Finally, the biomimetic nanoparticles solution was obtained by resuspending the precipitate in 5 mL of 0.01 M PBS buffer, pH 7.4.

  The biomimetic LPNs were synthesized using a process that involved spin-evaporation film-ultrasonic hydration followed by high-pressure homogenization. The APIs were formulated by measuring out carnosine and quercetin, with a weight ratio of 10:1. The biomimetic nanoparticle solutions were first diluted with ultrapure water and agitated to obtain suspension, then dissolved in anhydrous ethanol and slowly infused at an infusion rate of 10 milliliters per hour, with continuous stirring maintained throughout.

  The next step was to decant the mixture into a rotary evaporation flask (RE-2000B, Hangzhou Rui Jia Precision Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., China) under a temperature of 45 ℃ until the mixture was entirely dried. After dispensed in water, the dispersion was then subjected to high-pressure homogenization using a high-pressure homogenizer (Noozle Fluid Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at a pressure of 20,000 psi, repeated three times. Finally, a high-speed centrifuge was employed to spin at 4000×g for 30 min to eliminate unencapsulated APIs. Through this process, the APIs were encapsulated in biomimetic nanoparticles, resulting in the formulation of EV-QuerCar. The concentration of APIs in EV-QuerCar is around 5% (w/v).

  The particle size, PDI, and zeta potential of the EV-QuerCar were evaluated using a Zetasizer Nano-ZS90 instrument (Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK). The morphologies of the EV-QuerCar were observed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEM-1010, JEOL Ltd., USA). EE was determined using the ultrafiltration-centrifugation technique. The ultrafiltration membrane had a retention value of 50 kDa in molecular weight. The concentrations of EV-QuerCar and free APIs were determined by HPLC46,47, respectively. ### In vitro release and transdermal penetration kinetics of EV-QuerCar
  Abdominal skin tissues were taken from 6-week-old Wistar rats (purchased from Hangzhou Hangsi Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). The transdermal properties of the EV-QuerCar were investigated using the Franz diffusion cell method with an RYJ-12 transdermal diffuser (Shanghai Huanghai Drug Testing Instrument Co., Ltd.). Briefly, the Wistar rat skin tissue was fixed between the donor and the receiver compartments of the Franz cell, with the stratum corneum side oriented toward the donor compartment. 1 mL of a 2 mg/mL free APIs solution and 40 mg/mL of an EV-QuerCar suspension, each containing the same amount of APIs, were measured and added to the donor compartment. They were then uniformly applied to the rat skin, respectively.

  The Franz diffusion cells were maintained at 37 °C using a recirculating water bath, and the PBS buffer (pH 7.4) in the receptor chambers was continuously stirred at 500 rpm. At 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 8, 12, 18, 24, and 36 h, 1 mL aliquots were withdrawn from the receiver compartment for HPLC analysis to quantify the amount of APIs per unit skin area. The concentration and transdermal penetration rate of the APIs were determined using an API standard curve.

### FITC-labeled apis and FITC-labeled EV-QuerCar
  For visualized results under a fluorescence microscope, the experimental fluorophore FITC was utilized to label the free APIs. Briefly, 50 µg/mL APIs and 1 mg/mL Ev-QuerCar dissolved in a PBS solution (pH 7.4) were labeled by adding FITC, respectively. The mixture was stored overnight at 4 °C to allow for the labeling process to occur. Subsequently, the mixture was centrifuged using an ultrafiltration membrane to retain the molecules larger than 1 KDa, then collect pellets for purification. The pellet or precipitate was resuspended in PBS, and the ultrafiltration process was repeated three times to ensure the purification of the FITC-labelled APIs and FITC-labelled Ev-QuerCar, respectively.


### Fluorescence microscopy observation of skin penetration
  Fresh abdominal skin from three-month-old pigs was obtained from the Hangzhou Institute of Medicine, Chinese Academy of Sciences, for the experiment. The skin was cut into circular pieces of approximately 3.14 cm². Subsequently, equal amount of FITC-labelled APIs and FITC-labelled Ev-QuerCar, was added to the apparatus and placed in the dark for 30 min. The skin was then removed and thoroughly rinsed with water to ensure no surface fluorescence was left. After drying, the skin was sectioned and examined under a fluorescence microscope (Eclipse Ti-S, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) to evaluate penetration.


### Cell culture
  MEF L929, HDF and Human Keratinocyte Cells (HaCaT) were purchased from the Shanghai Institute of Biological Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Fetal bovine serum (FBS) and Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM) were purchased from Gibco, USA. Penicillin-Streptomycin-Amphotericin B additive was purchased from Sevier, China. MEF L929, HaCaT, and HDF cells were cultured in DMEM medium containing 10% FBS and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin-Amphotericin B. SYBR and reverse transcription reagents were purchased from Vazyme (Nanjing, China). RNAiso Plus was purchased from Takara (Beijing, China). Paraformaldehyde was purchased from Biosharp Life Sciences (Hefei, China), and DAPI (4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole) was obtained from Beyotime Biotechnology, (Jiangsu, China). All cells were cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator.


### Cell viability assays
  Cell viability was examined by the MTT (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) assay. MEF L929 cells were seeded in 96-well plates (1 × 10^5 cells/well) and cultured for 24 h. Cells were washed twice with PBS buffer and incubated for another 24 h with APIs, blank-LPN, EV-QuerCar, and culture medium only (negative control), respectively. Subsequently, cells were washed with PBS buffer and incubated with MTT reagent (5 mg/mL in PBS, pH 7.4) for 4 h. The precipitated formazan was dissolved with 100 µL DMSO and quantified by measuring the absorbance at 570 nm in a plate reader (Molecular Devices, LLC, USA).

### In vitro cellular uptake assay
  MEF L929 cells were seeded into 6-well plates (2 × 105 cells/well) and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. Next, 1 µg/mL of FITC-labeled EV-QuerCar was loaded, and further incubated at 37 °C for different time points (1 h, 2 h, and 4 h). FITC-labeled APIs, containing the same concentration of APIs as FITC-labeled EV-QuerCar, were used as a control. DAPI staining solution was added to stain the cell nuclei. Fluorescence distribution within the cells of different groups was observed and photographed using a fluorescence microscope (Eclipse Ti-S, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).


### RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)
  First, to examine the effects of EV-QuerCar on both HaCaT and HDF cells, HaCaT cells were irradiated with UVB at a dose of 10 mJ/cm² for 5 min, while HDF cells were irradiated with UVA at a dose of 1 J/cm² for 5 min, respectively. Total RNA was isolated from cells using TRIzol™ Reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Real-time qRT-PCR was performed on a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, California, USA) using the SYBR-Green-based method. Relative expression levels of mRNA were determined after normalization to Glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH).


### Molecular docking
  AutoDockTools-1.5.7 is a software for molecular docking using the principle of semi-flexibility. The structures of carnosine and quercetin were initially constructed using GaussianView, followed by optimization utilizing the M062X functional in conjunction with the LANL2DZ basis set within Gaussian 16. The three-dimensional structure files of core target proteins were retrieved and downloaded in PDB format via the RCSB PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org/). AutoDock 4.2.6 was also used to evaluate the binding affinity of the protein to the components and to select the lowest energy conformation in the docking simulation.

  In-depth analysis of the specific interaction patterns was conducted using PyMOL 2.3.0. Through PyMOL, high-quality 3D and 2D structures of small molecules and proteins were created, and the corresponding protein residues and binding bonds were displayed.


### Statistics
  Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (n = 3) and analyzed using the student’s -test for two groups or one-way ANOVA for multiple groups. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered significant. All computations were carried out using the statistical software GraphPad Instat (Graphpad Software, San Diego, CA).


## Results

### Characteristics of EV-QuerCar
  The comprehensive characterization of EV-QuerCar is essential for developing effective strategies and ensuring its nanotechnological and physicochemical stability. The average particle size of the blank nanoparticles was 86.85 ± 0.68 nm, with a PDI of 0.18 ± 0.12 and a zeta potential of -22.85 ± 1.21 mV. EV-QuerCar demonstrated a PDI of 0.16 ± 0.04, a zeta potential of -21.68 ± 1.07 mV, a DL (w/w) of 5.11 ± 0.22%, and an EE (w/w) of 90.07 ± 3.67%. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis confirmed the optimized formulation’s hydrodynamic diameter of 94.70 ± 0.71 nm (Fig. 1b). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images revealed monodisperse, spherical nanoparticles with a diameter of approximately 100 nm (Fig. 1a), consistent with the DLS-derived size distribution. Furthermore, EV-QuerCar displayed a slightly acidic pH in the range of 5.5–6.0, aligning with the skin’s natural surface pH. This pH compatibility is advantageous for topical delivery applications.

### In vitro release and transdermal penetration properties of EV-QuerCar
  The transdermal delivery efficiency of EV-QuerCar was evaluated via in vitro diffusion assays through rat abdominal skin (Fig. 2a). The results show that both the unencapsulated APIs and EV-QuerCar exhibit uniform permeation rates from 0 to 8 h. However, EV-QuerCar maintains a sustained release beyond 8 h, only plateauing after 24 h, whereas the unencapsulated APIs plateau significantly earlier. Over 36 h, the cumulative skin permeation rate of EV-QuerCar reached 91% (w/w), compared to 67% (w/w) for the free APIs, demonstrating a 1.4-fold enhancement in transdermal efficiency. Similarly, EV-QuerCar demonstrated a higher cumulative release rate than the unencapsulated APIs over 36 h (Fig. 2b), which is likely attributed to the improved solubility of the APIs upon encapsulation in EV-QuerCar and its presence in an amorphous form, facilitating faster diffusion and release in the solution. Confocal imaging (Fig. 2c) visually revealed that, compared to the unencapsulated APIs, EV-QuerCar penetrated more effectively through the skin barrier. Quantitative analysis showed a 3.21-fold increase in mean fluorescence penetration for EV-QuerCar relative to the free APIs (Fig. 2d).

### Cell viability
  In vitro cell viability was assessed by comparing APIs, blank-LPN, and EV-QuerCar against MEF L929 cells (Fig. 3a). The concentrations used for both APIs and EV-QuerCar were maintained consistently, following a gradient increase from 10 to 20, 40, 80, and 100 µmol/L. The blank-LPN is a version of EV-QuerCar that does not contain APIs. The results demonstrated that the cell viability of all groups was above 85%, indicating that the APIs, LPN carriers, and EV-QuerCar are all biocompatible with cells.

### EV-QuerCar enhances time-dependent cellular uptake in MEF L929 cells
  In vitro uptake assays conducted in MEF L929 cells revealed time-dependent accumulation of FITC-labeled EV-QuerCar and free APIs (Fig. 3b-c). Fluorescence imaging demonstrated significantly higher intracellular fluorescence intensity for EV-QuerCar compared to free APIs at 1, 2, and 4 h, with the signal intensity directly reflecting drug accumulation. At equivalent concentrations, EV-QuerCar exhibited 2.27-fold greater uptake than free APIs at 1 h, 3.90-fold at 2 h, and 5.48-fold at 4 h. These results underscore the superior time-dependent accumulation of EV-QuerCar over free APIs.


### Molecular docking between apis and target proteins
  To ascertain whether the active ingredients, quercetin and carnosine, would interact directly with the core targets, we assessed their binding affinity with the aids of molecular docking analysis. The protein structures corresponding to complex (PDB ID: 2FLU), KEAP-1 monomer (PDB ID: chain A of 2FLU) and HES-1 (PDB ID: 2MH3) were retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB). As can be seen from Table 1, the molecular binding affinities of the selected active ingredients to the core target proteins, including Keap1-NRF2 complex, KEAP-1 monomer and HES1 were all < -5.0 kcal/mol, suggesting that the active ingredients had an excellent affinity for the targets and that they readily form a stable binding conformation. PyMOL software was used to visualize the corresponding protein residues in stick representation and identify different residues with different colors at the same time (Fig. 4; Table 1). Therefore, both quercetin and carnosine might be effective ligands for the selective targets. To further understand the binding affinity of the two combined ingredients to their respective targets, combined docking scores were obtained. The binary complex of KEAP1-NRF2 and quercetin, chosen for its higher monomer binding affinity, served as the substrate for molecular docking with carnosine. The docking results showed that carnosine could accurately occupy KEAP-1’s active site within the complex, achieving a binding energy of -5.9 kcal/mol (Fig. 4g). In addition, carnosine was also docked with the KEAP1–quercetin binary complex without NRF2, yielding a binding energy of -5.4 kcal/mol (Fig. 4h). Similarly, the simultaneous interaction of quercetin and carnosine with the HES-1 protein was demonstrated, with a binding energy of -5.2 kcal/mol (Fig. 4i). This outcome indicates that both molecules can interact cooperatively with either Keap1-NRF2 complex or Keap-1 monomer itself or HES-1 proteins, without exhibiting any antagonistic effects between the two compounds.

### EV-QuerCar exhibits dual antioxidant and anti-aging efficacy in skin cells
  Further investigation into the antioxidant and anti-aging effects of EV-QuerCar in HDFs was conducted. EV-QuerCar significantly upregulated Nrf2 expression in UV-exposed HDFs, demonstrating 4.6-fold and 2.3-fold increases compared to untreated (NC) and UV-treated controls, respectively (Fig. 5a). UV irradiation alone induced a 1.7-fold Nrf2 upregulation (vs. NC), confirming its role as a stress-responsive biomarker. Post-treatment, EV-QuerCar further amplified Nrf2 activity, highlighting its capacity to enhance redox regulation under oxidative stress. Concurrently, EV-QuerCar elevated HES1 expression (Notch signaling) by 1.9-fold and 3.4-fold compared to NC and UV group, respectively (Fig. 5b), linking its anti-aging effects to transcriptional homeostasis.

  In UV-treated HaCaT keratinocytes, EV-QuerCar upregulated Nrf2 downstream antioxidant enzymes: GCLC (1.2-fold), GCLM (1.1-fold), and SOD1 (1.3-fold) compared to NC (Fig. 5c-e). These results confirm EV-QuerCar’s dual-cell efficacy in mitigating UV-induced oxidative stress activating Nrf2-mediated antioxidant defenses (GCLC, GCLM, SOD1) and HES1-dependent anti-aging pathways.

## Discussions and conclusions
  Skin, being the outermost human organ, is frequently affected by exposome factors and, at molecular level, by harmful free radicals48. It possesses a natural defense mechanism that is intricately related to the structure and function of its constituent layers. The stratum corneum layer acts as a natural barrier to transdermal delivery and topical application of APIs48. Effectively delivering appropriate antioxidant and anti-aging substances or APIs to protect the skin against the destructive actions of exposome factors remains a significant challenge in the field of skin care.

  Carnosine, an endogenous dipeptide, and quercetin, a hydrophilic flavonoid, exhibit not only antioxidative activities by neutralizing the products involved in lipid peroxidation but also potential anti-aging effects17,18,24. In this study, carnosine and quercetin were selected as antioxidant and anti-aging substances, with the aim of targeting key regulatory genes, NRF2 and HES1, respectively. With the aid of computational biology, the affinity of carnosine and quercetin to the target proteins was initially virtually screened by molecular docking, confirming their potential target-specific abilities. Next, due to the poor bioavailability, permeability, and instability of both carnosine and quercetin48, their topical applications are limited. Consequently, this study developed an innovative biomimetic LPNs formulation capable of co-encapsulating the two ingredients and demonstrating enhanced transdermal delivery.

  A biomimetic approach to prepare LPNs, which are composed of phospholipids derived from thermophilic bacteria, water, and ethanol, are renowned for their enhanced transdermal permeability. This permeability is achieved through the fluidization of the lipid domains within the stratum corneum, which possess strong flexibility and deformability49. The transdermal absorption facilitated by LPNs involves inter-lipid penetration, fusion with skin lipids, and the release of APIs at different regions following the fluidization of the stratum corneum50,51. In a separate study, an improved in vitro skin permeation of testosterone was observed when utilizing ethosomes, a result that is consistent with our findings. Our observations revealed that the absorption of EV-QuerCar is significantly higher than that of free APIs after 36 h (Fig. 2). Additionally, the transdermal penetration of EV-QuerCar, as observed using FITC-loaded LPNs under a fluorescence microscope (Fig. 2c), also demonstrated an augmented transdermal absorption facilitated by LPNs carriers. The use of rat and porcine skin in ex vivo studies is common due to their availability and structural similarities to human skin. However, species differences in skin permeability can significantly impact the relevance of the data obtained. For instance, rat skin is thinner and has a higher density of hair follicles compared to human skin, which can lead to differences in permeability. Human skin models, including reconstructed epidermis and diseased or barrier-compromised tissue, therefore provide more clinically relevant information for evaluating permeability and optimizing topical formulations. Subsequent studies should prioritize such human-relevant models to confirm the present findings.

  The internalization of LPNs loaded with APIs primarily occurs through endocytic processes, including clathrin-mediated endocytosis, caveolae-mediated endocytosis, and micropinocytosis52. The hydrophobicity of a small molecular drug is a pivotal factor in its permeation across biological cell membranes. Given the superior hydro-phobicity of quercetin (LogP = 1.8) compared to carnosine (LogP = -3), quercetin was selected for labeling with FITC. This labeled quercetin was further used to formulate FITC-EV-QuerCar, which served to indicate the cellular uptake of EV-QuerCar and APIs, providing insights into their intracellular dynamics. Our findings revealed increased fluorescent signals in MEFs following treatment with FITC- EV-QuerCar compared to FITC-APIs (Fig. 3a and b), suggesting that the use of LPNs can enhance the cellular uptake of hydrophobic small molecules, such as quercetin. These findings can be explained by the interaction between membrane anchoring moieties, such as cholesterol and fatty acids present on the surface of LPNs, with the hydrophobic tail regions of phospholipids within the cell membrane53. As a result, this interaction could facilitate the enhanced cellular internalization of the entire EV-QuerCar. The marked enhancement aligns with EV-mediated mechanisms, such as receptor-driven endocytosis and evasion of lysosomal degradation, which bypass limitations of passive diffusion seen in free APIs. Such efficiency surpasses ligand-modified systems (e.g., folate-PEG NPs), highlighting EV-QuerCar’s bioinspired advantage for targeted delivery without synthetic modifications. This positions EV-QuerCar as a promising platform for therapies requiring rapid, sustained intracellular payload retention.

  The dermis is primarily composed of fibroblasts. Recent single-cell RNA profiling of human dermal cells has revealed that restoring HES1 expression plays a pivotal role in protecting HDFs from aging-related damage12. Our findings illustrate a significant reduction in HES1 expression following UV exposure (Fig. 5b). Notably, our data also shows that treatment with EV-QuerCar significantly restored HES1 expression, suggesting that EV-QuerCar treatment has the potential to shield the skin from UV-induced cellular senescence, thereby enhancing the resilience of HDFs (Fig. 5b). NRF2, a member of the leucine zipper transcription factors, is involved in antioxidant response elements in the promoter region of genes associated with DNA repair, apoptosis prevention, and redox regulation7. Our findings show a noteworthy enhancement in Nrf2 expression in HDF after treatment with EV-QuerCar (Fig. 5a). Additionally, our results reveal a significant upregulation in the expression of the antioxidant genes GCLC, GCLM, and SOD1 in keratinocytes after treatment with EV-QuerCar (Fig. 5c-e). This strongly suggests that EV-QuerCar effectively activates NRF2 target genes, thereby inducing both antioxidant and anti-aging effects for skin protection. Although transcriptional up-regulation indicates pathway engagement, future studies will confirm changes in protein expression by Western blotting in UV-stressed models.

  In a separate experiment, the effectiveness of EV-QuerCar in improving the stability of a cosmetic product was demonstrated. As a potent antioxidant, the stability of quercetin is a concern due to its tendency to cause color changes, which can adversely affect cosmetic products. For the first time, EV-QuerCar was introduced into a Repair and Revitalizing Eye Cream. A comparative experiment was conducted with an equal amount of unencapsulated quercetin in the same base. After stirring evenly, both formulations were stored in a constant temperature chamber at 50 °C, and the color change was recorded after a week. The formulation with unencapsulated quercetin showed a significantly darker color compared to the formulation with encapsulated EV-QuerCar. The color of the EV-QuerCar formulation remained almost unchanged. The result demonstrated that the stability of quercetin was greatly im-proved when encapsulated in LPN carriers.

  In summary, our findings demonstrate the enhanced transdermal delivery and increased cumulative release rate of the combined use of carnosine and quercetin, achieved through the use of LPN as carriers. This is evident in the improved transdermal absorption and heightened cellular uptake. Notably, EV-QuerCar has exhibited promising antioxidative and anti-aging effects in both HaCaT and HDF cells. Building on these findings, future studies will focus on in vivo pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiling to validate EV-QuerCar’s antioxidant and anti-aging efficacy in preclinical models, including biodistribution, sustained release kinetics, and systemic toxicity assessments. In addition, skin irritation and biocompatibility testing will be prioritized using reconstructed human epidermis models and in vivo dermal tolerance assays to ensure translational safety. Finally, to optimize therapeutic synergy, dose-response studies will systematically evaluate quercetin-carnosine ratios, leveraging computational modeling to identify the maximal ratio for oxidative stress mitigation. Consequently, the potential of EV-QuerCar, which combines both carnosine and quercetin, positions it as a novel therapeutic solution for combating dermal oxidation and aging.
