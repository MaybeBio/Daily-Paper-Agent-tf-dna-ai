# CircRNF10 sequestrates β-catenin by a dual regulatory circuit of direct degradation and a miR-1275/DKK3-mediated inhibition in driver gene- negative lung adenocarcinoma


## Background
  Circular RNAs (circRNAs) exert critical regulatory functions in tumor biology by modulating pathways associated with oncogenesis or tumor suppression. Despite substantial progress in elucidating their roles in several malignancies, the contribution of circRNAs to the pathogenesis of driver gene-negative lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), a molecular subtype lacking actionable genetic alterations and exhibiting limited response to existing targeted or immunotherapeutic strategies, remains poorly defined.


## Methods
  The expression of circRNF10 in driver gene-negative LUAD was analyzed using circRNA microarray analysis followed by RT-qPCR validation. A series of functional assays were performed both in vitro and in vivo to evaluate the effects of circRNF10 on tumor cell behavior, including proliferation (EdU incorporation), migration (wound healing), and invasion (transwell assays), as well as tumor growth in a murine model. To elucidate the underlying molecular mechanism, we employed a combination of computational and experimental approaches, including AlphaFold3-based structural prediction, in vitro transcription, biotin-labeled RNA pulldown, RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP), and dual-luciferase reporter assays.


## Results
  In this study, we identified a previously uncharacterized circular RNA, circRNF10, which is markedly downregulated in driver gene-negative lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and positively associated with favorable clinical outcomes. Functional analyses revealed that circRNF10 overexpression suppresses LUAD cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro and in vivo, primarily through inhibition of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. Mechanistically, circRNF10 directly interacts with β-catenin via its cyclization site, thereby promoting β-catenin degradation. Moreover, circRNF10 functions as a competing endogenous RNA by sequestering miR-1275, thereby alleviating the miR-1275-mediated suppression of DKK3, a potent inhibitor of the Wnt pathway. Our findings further confirm that circRNF10 promotes β-catenin degradation through direct interaction and modulation of the miR-1275/DKK3 signaling cascade.


## Conclusions
  Collectively, our findings highlight circRNF10 as a tumor suppressor in driver gene-negative LUAD and suggest that restoring circRNF10 expression represents a promising therapeutic approach for this refractory subtype.

## Introduction
  Lung cancer is the most frequently diagnosed cancer and the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, accounting for an estimated 1.8 million deaths or 18.7% of all cancer-related deaths in 2022 [1]. Among its histological subtypes, lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most prevalent, representing more than 40% of all lung cancer cases and posing a significant public health burden [2]. A subset of LUAD, known as driver gene-negative LUAD, is characterized by the absence of oncogenic mutations in common driver genes such as KRAS, ALK, EGFR, and ROS1. This subtype accounts for approximately 36% of LUAD cases and exhibits limited responsiveness to current first-line therapies, including anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies [3, 4]. Thus, the search for new therapeutic targets in patients with driver gene-negative LUAD continues to be a critical challenge.

  Circular RNAs (circRNAs), a class of noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs) characterized by their covalently closed loop structures, are important regulatory molecules in the oncogenesis and chemotherapy resistance of various cancers, including leukemia, colon cancer, breast cancer, and lung cancer [5, 6] underscoring their potential as biomarkers for diagnosis and prognosis, as well as therapeutic targets [7, 8]. Increasing data indicate that circRNAs, frequently altered in tumors, exert their oncogenic or tumor-suppressive functions through microRNA (miRNA) sponging, functional peptide coding, and protein interactions [9]. In LUAD, specific circRNAs have been linked to tumor progression. For example, hsa_circ_0013958 [10] is associated with the TNM stage and metastatic status, suggesting its potential as an early diagnostic biomarker. Others, such as circUSP7 [11], promote immunosuppression and resistance to immunotherapy [12], suggesting novel avenues for therapeutic intervention [7]. Despite these advances, the regulatory roles of circRNAs in driver gene-negative LUAD remain largely unexplored. Therefore, identifying dysregulated circRNAs and elucidating the mechanisms by which they influence tumor behavior could help address the current gaps in treatment efficacy for driver gene-negative LUAD.

  In the present study, we investigated circRNF10, a circRNA significantly downregulated in driver gene-negative LUAD. Mechanistically, circRNF10 suppresses β-catenin expression via direct protein interaction and sponging of miR-1275. Through this dual mechanism, circRNF10 attenuates Wnt/β-catenin signaling, thereby inhibiting the proliferation, migration, and invasion of driver gene-negative LUAD cells. These findings link our prior observation of aberrant Wnt/β-catenin activation in driver gene-negative LUAD [13] with a novel circRNA regulatory mechanism. Collectively, our findings highlight circRNF10 as a potential prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target for improving the outcomes of patients with this LUAD subtype.


## Results

### Driver gene-negative LUAD exhibited low tumor-specific expression of circRNF10
  To investigate the expression landscape of circular RNAs (circRNAs) in driver gene-negative lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), we first conducted a circRNA microarray analysis using matched tumor and adjacent normal tissue samples. This approach allowed us to comprehensively profile circRNA expression in the absence of common oncogenic drivers. The microarray analysis identified a total of 10,970 distinct circRNAs. Among these, 5,106 circRNAs were found to be highly expressed, while the remaining 5,864 exhibited low expression levels, indicating a diverse and heterogeneous circRNA expression pattern in driver gene-negative LUAD (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Subsequent statistical analysis identified 147 circRNAs with significantly altered expression levels. Of these, 101 were upregulated and 46 were downregulated in driver gene-negative LUAD tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues (common reference criteria: log2-fold change > 1 or < −1, p < 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 1a). ### CircRNF10 suppressed tumor growth and metastasis in vitro and in vivo
  We established H2126 and H2085 cell lines, which were stably transfected with lentiviruses encoding a circRNF10 overexpression plasmid (OE-circRNF10) or transfected with a trio of small interfering RNAs (si-circRNF10), to investigate the role of circRNF10 in tumor progression (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 1d-g). A series of functional assays was performed to evaluate the modulatory effects of circRNF10 on cell growth, migration, and invasion. First, the results of the EdU and CCK8 assays showed that circRNF10 overexpression inhibited the proliferation of both cell lines (Fig. 2b-c, Supplementary Fig. 1 h-i). In contrast, circRNF10 silencing strongly promoted H2085 cell proliferation (p < 0.001) without significantly affecting H2126 cells (Supplementary Fig. 2a-b). In addition, we found that circRNF10 expression was negatively correlated with circulating tumor cell (CTC) counts in 61 LUAD patients (p < 0.001, R = −0.510) (Fig. 2d). Further wound healing and Transwell assays demonstrated that circRNF10 inhibited cell migration and invasion in vitro (Fig. 2e,2f, Supplementary Fig. 1j-k, 2d-f). The tumor-suppressive function of circRNF10 was further validated in vivo using both a subcutaneous xenograft model and a metastatic tumor model in BALB/c-nu/nu mice. In these models, overexpression of circRNF10 led to a marked reduction in tumor growth and metastasis. At the endpoint of the experiment, the tumor size in the circRNF10 overexpression group was significantly smaller than that in the control group (Fig. 2g-h, Supplementary Fig. 2 g). Further supporting this finding, the tumor growth curve demonstrated that circRNF10 overexpression effectively inhibited the tumor growth rate (Fig. 2i). Similarly, and in line with the reduced tumor size, the tumor weight in the circRNF10 overexpression group was also lower than that in the control group (Fig. 2j). Immunohistochemical analysis of Ki-67, a proliferation marker, revealed significantly lower Ki-67 scores in tumors derived from circRNF10-overexpressing cells, indicating reduced proliferative activity (Supplementary Fig. 2 h). Moreover, in the metastatic model, the number of metastatic lung nodules was substantially decreased in mice with elevated circRNF10 expression (Fig. 2k, Supplementary Fig. 2i). ### CircRNF10 directly bound to β-catenin and facilitated its degradation
  Given that circRNF10 functions as a tumor suppressor, we next aimed to explore the detailed molecular mechanism underlying this function. Bioinformatics analyses of the GSE115002 dataset revealed that Wnt/β-catenin signaling is frequently hyperactivated in driver gene-negative LUAD (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 3a-b). A high β-catenin level was strongly correlated with a poor prognosis (Supplementary Fig. 3c).   The Cancer-Specific CircRNA database revealed that the conformation of circRNF10 includes defined microRNA response elements (MREs) and RNA-binding protein (RBP) binding sites. Notably, the absence of a discernible open reading frame (ORF) in exon 13 and exon 14 suggested that it lacks peptide-coding ability (Fig. 3b). Therefore, we focused on its potential roles as a miRNA sponge or in interactions with other proteins. Using the AlphaFold3 prediction algorithm, we constructed a binding model between circRNF10 and β-catenin. The predicted alignment error (PAE) plot revealed a reliable correlation between the theoretical and actual models for amino acids 148 through 740 (Fig. 3c), which was further verified by an immunofluorescence (IF) colocalization analysis of circRNF10 and β-catenin (Fig. 3d). This predicted interaction remained stable even when various physiological ions (including Na+, K+, Mg2+, Cl+ and Ca2+) were incorporated into the simulation, mimicking the intracellular environment (Supplementary Fig. 3 g). Furthermore, RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) and RNA pulldown assays provided persuasive evidence that circRNF10 could directly bind to β-catenin (Fig. 3e-f). Neither the overexpression nor the knockdown of circRNF10 altered total β-catenin mRNA levels (Supplementary Fig. 3 h), suggesting that circRNF10 may directly influence β-catenin protein degradation through a RBP-mediated mechanism.

  Further structural analysis revealed that the 240–80 bp region of circRNF10 is in close spatial proximity to the 300–500 amino acid domain of β-catenin, enabling direct interactions between specific RNA bases and amino acid residues within this domain (Fig. 3g). We constructed a full-length circRNF10 and various truncated versions using a T7 promoter-driven in vitro transcription to characterize the predicted binding sites (Fig. 3h, Supplementary Fig. 3i). Only the truncated circRNF10 fragment spanning 240–80 bp, along with the full-length circRNF10 sequence, was able to bind β-catenin, confirming that the 240–80 bp region serves as the essential binding domain responsible for this interaction (Fig. 3i-k). H2085 cells transfected with the circRNF10 240–80 bp mutant (circRNF10mut) or OE-circRNF10 plasmid showed that OE-circRNF10 could indeed significantly reduce the cytoplasmic accumulation and nuclear translocation of β-catenin, whereas the circRNF10mut plasmid had only a slight effect (Fig. 3l). These results suggest that circRNF10 can directly bind to β-catenin via a specific domain, thereby facilitating its degradation.

  Next, we investigated the functional significance of the circRNF10-β-catenin axis in tumor progression through the direct modulation of β-catenin expression. Silencing β-catenin attenuated the proliferative, migratory, and invasive capabilities of both cell lines (Supplementary Fig. 3j, 4a-d). Conversely, β-catenin overexpression augmented these malignant phenotypes, which were significantly mitigated by circRNF10 upregulation (Fig. 4a-c, Supplementary Fig. 3 k, 5a-c). Interestingly, we observed that LUAD cells transfected with circRNF10mut plasmids, which disrupt the binding site for β-catenin, exhibited only a partial restoration of their aggressive phenotypes. This finding suggests that the direct interaction between circRNF10 and β-catenin contributes to, but does not fully account for, the tumor-suppressive effects of circRNF10. ### Preclinical evidence for circRNF10-mediated Wnt pathway inactivation
  We established a β-catenin-activated xenograft model by the intratumoral injection of the Wnt agonist KY19382 (0.1 μM, q3d) to further validate the role of circRNF10 in vivo. As predicted, the overexpression of circRNF10 significantly attenuated KY19382-induced tumor growth, as indicated by the reduced tumor volume and weight (Fig. 4d-f, Supplementary Fig. 5 d). At the molecular level, IHC confirmed decreased β-catenin expression and a lower Ki-67 proliferation index in the subgroup treated with both KY19382 and OE-circRNF10 when compared to the subgroup treated with KY19382 alone. Notably, the subgroup treated with KY19382 alone did not present significant changes in DKK3 expression (Supplementary Fig. 5e). In line with this, high-power microscopy confirmed comparable differences in β-catenin expression (Supplementary Fig. 5f). Collectively, these findings strongly indicate that circRNF10 mediates its tumor-suppressive effects predominantly through inhibition of the β-catenin pathway.

  In the clinical cohort, an inverse correlation between the expression levels of circRNF10 and β-catenin was observed (Fig. 4g). Patients were stratified into four subgroups according to their circRNF10 and β-catenin levels: circRNF10low/β-cateninlow, circRNF10high/β-cateninlow, circRNF10low/β-cateninhigh and circRNF10high/β-cateninhigh. The circRNF10high/β-cateninlow subgroup exhibited the best PFS and OS, whereas the circRNF10low/β-cateninhigh subgroup presented the poorest prognosis (Fig. 4h). ### CircRNF10 Modulates the miR-1275/DKK3 axis to inhibit canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling in LUAD

DKK3 showed the most significant and consistent increase in expression in cells expressing the OE-circRNF10 plasmid (Fig. 5a right). In turn, circRNF10 silencing reduced DKK3 mRNA levels (Fig. 5b), with no significant decrease on RSPO3 expression (Supplementary Fig. 6a).   CircRNAs can act as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) by sponging miRNAs, thereby disrupting miRNA-mediated target suppression and adding an extra layer of posttranscriptional regulation [7]. In this study, a RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay demonstrated that circRNF10 was significantly enriched within AGO2 (Argonaute 2) protein complexes. This finding confirms that circRNF10 has a strong capacity to bind microRNAs, suggesting its role as a competing endogenous RNA that can interact with and potentially regulate miRNA activity (Fig. 5c). An integrated analysis using the miR2Disease, PhenomiR, TargetScan, and miRanda databases identified 65 miRNAs potentially targeting the DKK3 mRNA. Through subsequent filtering based on miRNA sequencing of LUAD tumor and adjacent normal tissues [14], along with qPCR validation, six miRNAs with increased expression were ultimately selected (Fig. 5d). Among them, only miR-1275 was negatively correlated with circRNF10, which was consistent across the OE-circRNF10 groups (Fig. 5e, Supplementary Fig. 6f-h). The cytoplasmic colocalization of circRNF10 and miR-1275, as demonstrated by FISH (Fig. 5f), was further confirmed by the significant reduction in co-localization signal upon mutation of the miR-1275 binding site (Fig. 5f). Moreover, dual-luciferase reporter assays showed that the miR-1275 mimic only significantly decreased the luciferase activity of the WT reporter vector, strongly supporting its specific binding to circRNF10 or DKK3 (Fig. 5g). Collectively, these findings indicate that circRNF10 acts as an effective miR-1275 sponge to rescue DKK3 expression and suppress canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling.


### miR-1275-dependent activation of β-catenin mediated by the DKK3 protein
  The upregulation of miR-1275 significantly promoted the proliferation, migration, and invasion of both LUAD cell lines, accompanied by a marked decrease in DKK3 mRNA levels and almost no change in β-catenin mRNA levels (Fig. 6a-d, Supplementary Fig. 6i-j, 7a-e). Similarly, both the extracellular and intracellular DKK3 protein levels decreased after miR-1275 upregulation.   To delineate the respective contributions of the dual regulatory pathways to β-catenin degradation, we constructed additional expression plasmids: one with a mutated miR-1275 binding site in circRNF10, and another with mutations in both the miR-1275 and β-catenin binding sites. These constructs were transfected into H2085 cells. Notably, β-catenin levels showed no significant difference between cells expressing the double mutant (miR-1275/β-catenin binding sites mutated) and those expressing the control plasmid. In contrast, expression of either the β-catenin binding site mutant or the miR-1275 binding site mutant resulted in a partial reduction of β-catenin levels. As expected, wild-type circRNF10 overexpression induced a further, more substantial decrease in β-catenin (Fig. 7g). These results clearly demonstrate that both the direct circRNF10/β-catenin interaction and the indirect circRNF10/miR-1275/DKK3 axis independently contribute to the degradation of β-catenin.


## Discussion
  Due to their unique structural stability, regulatory versatility, and involvement in key oncogenic and tumor-suppressive pathways, circRNAs are being increasingly recognized as important contributors to tumor biology. While further research is needed to fully elucidate their mechanisms of action and therapeutic value, circRNAs represent a promising area for the development of novel cancer treatment strategies [15–17]. For example, in breast cancer and glioblastoma, targeting circRNAs has demonstrated remarkable efficacy in inhibiting tumor progression [18, 19]. Recent investigations have revealed deregulated circRNA expression profiles in LUAD, further underscoring their prospective role in cancer therapy [20–22]. However, in the specific molecular subtype of lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) lacking known driver gene mutations, referred to as driver gene-negative LUAD, it remains unclear whether its circRNA expression profile has been thoroughly characterized, and whether these unique or differentially expressed circRNAs could serve as viable clinical targets. Here, we identified a circRNA, circRNF10, as a pivotal regulator that mediates tumor-suppressive effects on tumor growth and metastasis in driver gene-negative LUAD. Our investigation elucidated the molecular mechanism by which circRNF10 inhibits driver gene-negative LUAD progression through a direct interaction with β-catenin and the modulation of the miR-1275/DKK3/β-catenin axis. The downregulation of circRNF10 led to the pronounced degradation of β-catenin, thereby exacerbating tumor progression (Fig. 8). Our research indicated that circRNF10 has significant potential as a novel therapeutic target for driver gene-negative LUAD.Fig. 8Schematic diagram illustrating the proposed molecular mechanism by which circRNF10 regulates tumor progression in driver gene-negative LUAD

  In addition to its marked downregulation in driver gene-negative LUAD tissue, circRNF10 is significantly positively related to the clinical prognosis. This finding aligns with prior evidence indicating that circRNF10 is a prognostic biomarker across malignancies [23–25]. However, its prognostic significance displays a striking tumor-derived specificity: a high level of circRNF10 is strongly correlated with a favorable prognosis for patients with breast cancer [23, 24], whereas in patients with glioblastoma, increased circRNF10 expression is correlated with a poor prognosis [25]. This discrepancy may be attributed to the environmental differences caused by the circRNF10-regulated signaling molecules. For example, in breast cancer, circRNF10 has been reported to suppress tumor progression via the DHX15/NF-κB pathway or the miR-934/PTEN/PI3K-Akt axis [23, 24], whereas in glioblastoma, it has been demonstrated to maintain stemness through HSPB1/IGF2BP3-mediated ferroptosis inhibition [25]. These studies indicate that exploring the interaction mechanism between circRNF10 and its downstream target molecules may contribute to a deeper understanding of the pathogenesis of driver gene-negative LUAD.

  In the present study, circRNF10 was found to suppress the invasion and metastasis of lung cancer cells in vitro, and Wnt/β-catenin signaling was shown to be activated in driver gene-negative LUAD [13]. The activation of the canonical Wnt pathway via Wnt-FZD2/LRP5/6 signaling disrupts a destruction complex (APC/Axin/GSK3-β/CK1), allowing β-catenin nuclear translocation and the transcriptional activation of downstream oncogenes such as c-Myc and Cyclin D1. Unlike its parental linear mRNA, circRNF10 contains critical splicing-derived β-catenin-binding domains located in RNF10-JP (junction point, approximately 97 bp), as confirmed by Al-guided proteomic mapping [26]. Interestingly, we found that circRNF10 cannot only bind to β-catenin but can also affect the general degradation of β-catenin. Our finding aligns with the established role of circRNA-protein interactions in post-transcriptional regulation, where binding can directly influence the stability and cellular levels of target proteins [27–29]. Thus, RNF10-JP could be a novel small RNA that may be an ideal candidate for nucleic acid drugs in driver gene-negative LUAD.

  The DKK family consists of four main members (DKK1, 2, 3, and 4) and a unique DKK-3-related protein termed Soggy (DKKL1, or SGY-1) [30]. Some reports indicate that DKK3 activates noncanonical WNT-β-catenin signaling [31]. Here, we first showed that in driver gene-negative LUAD, DKK3 can mediate the regulatory effect of circRNF10 on β-catenin degradation. Mechanistic studies revealed that DKK3 suppression drives LUAD progression through various molecular pathways [32, 33]. Because miR-1275/DKK3 is involved in the regulatory effect of circRNF10 on the proliferation and mobility of lung cancer cells, DKK3 could be useful as a potential combination treatment target to compensate for the insufficient clinical efficacy of nucleic acid drugs targeting RNF10-JP. On the other hand, a deeper understanding of miR-1275/DKK3/β-catenin signaling will also assist in elucidating the mechanisms of secondary resistance to RNF10-JP nucleic acid drugs.

  CircRNAs have received increasing amounts of attention in the cancer treatment field because of their strong stability and high binding affinities for target proteins [17, 34, 35]. Indeed, in our nude mouse model, circRNF10 overexpression markedly reduced the tumor volume and tumor weight in one mouse, even achieving tumor-free survival, highlighting its effective therapeutic efficacy. Similar preclinical studies on the therapeutic effects of different circRNAs on liver [36], breast [24], gastric [37], and other cancer types have also reported favorable outcomes, whereas the persistent existence of tumors in the subgroup with tumor suppressor circRNA overexpression may partially reflect the incomplete inhibition of circRNAs in the corresponding downstream signaling pathway or the bypass of signaling pathways to some extent [38]. Here, we demonstrated that although circRNF10 overexpression exerted significant tumor-suppressive effects in vivo by inhibiting tumor growth, some residual tumor growth was still observed in a subset of nude mice, potentially due to the compensatory regulation of Wnt/β-catenin downstream targets such as Myc [39–41]. KY19382 [42] forms hydrogen bonds with Asp133 and Val135 in the ATP-binding site of the GSK3β enzyme to inhibit its activity [43] and perturbs amino acid residues (S265, L321, R322 and V325) within the Dvl PDZ domain to disrupt the CXXC5–Dvl interaction in the Wnt/β-catenin pathway [44, 45]. When KY19382 was administered, the tumor-suppressive effect of circRNF10 was reversed with increased β-catenin expression levels, further confirming the compensatory functional importance of Wnt/β-catenin regulation. Concurrently, the analysis of our clinical samples revealed that circRNF10high and β-cateninlow exert synergistic effects on the prognosis, with β-catenin expression further stratifying the prognosis of patients into high-risk and low-risk subgroups based on circRNF10 expression. Thus, combining β-catenin inhibitors in clinical practice may improve the potential benefits of RNF10-JP-based nucleic acid therapeutics.

  However, some limitations in this study should be further addressed in the future. First, all the clinical samples were obtained from a single center; thus, whether circRNF10, functioning as a prognostic marker or potential treatment target, possesses wider application value needs to be validated in an external multicenter study. Second, since the tumor model was established with a human cell line, we have not determined whether circRNF10 has similar effects on patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models, which can better simulate the complexity and heterogeneity of human tumors [46]. Third, while circRNAs are known to be capable of binding RBPs and functioning as ceRNAs [47], the role of circRNF10 in encoding peptides, another important function of circRNAs [48, 49], was not explored in the present study. Fourth, although co-immunoprecipitation experiments indicated a potential DKK3-LRP6 interaction (data not shown), further functional experiments demonstrated that DKK3 does not modulate LRP6's expression, localization, or phosphorylation. Therefore, we propose that DKK3 likely acts as a decoy receptor for LRP6, which blocks WNT1 binding. The exact mechanism of DKK3-mediated β-catenin regulation, including the potential functional involvement of LRP6 or other receptors, requires further elucidation.


## Conclusion
  In conclusion, our study demonstrated that circRNF10 plays an important role in regulating the progression of driver gene-negative LUAD through the coordinated targeting of the canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway. This coordinated modulatory mechanism, which involves both circRNF10-mediated direct sequestration and miR-1275/DKK3-mediated indirect sequestration of β-catenin, paves the way for circRNF10 to be considered a novel therapeutic target for driver gene-negative LUAD.


## Materials and methods

### Clinical samples
  From the First Affiliated Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University (SYSUFH, Guangzhou, China), eight pairs of primary driver gene-negative LUAD tissues and paired adjacent normal tissues were obtained via surgical resection and immediately preserved in liquid nitrogen at the Biobank. None of them had experienced any treatment before surgery. Five pairs were used for circRNA microarray analysis, while the remaining three pairs were used for the technical validation of the microarray-identified candidates via real-time quantitative reverse transcription-PCR‒PCR (RT‒qPCR). Next, in situ hybridization (ISH) was performed to determine the expression characteristics of the above-screened circRNAs in a total of 144 pairs of paraffin-embedded LUAD tissues and matched adjacent normal tissues. The driver gene-negative sub-phenotypes of these tissues were classified based on the corresponding gene spectrum from NGS. Our study received approval from the Institutional Research Ethical Committee of SYSUFH, and written informed consent was obtained from all participating patients.


### Cell lines
  All human LUAD cell lines (A549, PC9, H2126, H2085, Calu-3, H1975, and H1623) and the human bronchial epithelial cell line Beas-2b were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). Beas-2b cells were maintained in bronchial epithelial growth medium (BEGM, USA). The driver gene-negative cell lines (H1623, H2126, and H2085) were cultured in RPMI 1640 basic medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA). DMEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin served as the culture medium for A549, PC9, and H1975 cells. Calu-3 cells were cultured in MEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. All the cell lines were confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination and were maintained in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2.


### Real-time quantitative reverse transcription-PCR
  Total RNA was extracted from the cell lines or tissues using RNAex Pro RNA extraction reagent (Accurate Biology, China) based on the TRIzol method. For mRNA or circRNA amplification, the RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA with Evo M-MLV Reverse Transcription Reagent Premix (for qPCR) (Accurate Biology, China). Similarly, the miRNA analysis involved reverse transcription using a miRNA first-strand cDNA synthesis kit (Accurate Biology, China). A SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit II (Rox Plus) (Accurate Biology, China) was used to detect the amplification signal in all RT‒qPCRs. The miRNA expression levels were normalized to those of U6 small nucleolar RNA (snRNA), while the mRNA levels were normalized to those of the housekeeping genes GAPDH and β-actin. The sequences of all the primers utilized here are presented in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.


### Cell transfection
  Driver gene-negative cell lines (H2126 and H2085) were transfected with siRNA sequences or miRNA mimics using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA). The specific siRNA target sequences and miRNA mimics are listed in Supplementary Table 3. The siRNA sequences or miRNA mimics were incubated with Lipofectamine 3000 in Opti-MEM (Gibco, USA) for no less than 15 min. Then, the cells were transfected for 12 h in basal culture medium, followed by a transition to complete medium. All subsequent assays were performed 48 h after transfection.


### RNase R digestion
  Total RNA (10 μg) from H2126 and H2085 cells was treated either with RNase R (Geneseed, China) or with no RNase R as a control. After they were incubated with RNase R for 15 min at 37 °C, followed by 10 min at 70 ℃, reverse transcription and RT‒qPCR were conducted to amplify the remaining circRNA. Agarose gel electrophoresis was performed to observe the distribution of the amplified target circRNAs.


### Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization (FISH)
  The probes (the detailed sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 4) with FAM- or Cy3-labelled circRNF10 and Cy3-labelled miR-1275 were synthesized by GenePharma (China). They were utilized to determine whether circRNF10 could absorb miR-1275 in H2126 and H2085 cells by analyzing their different fluorescent signal patterns. According to the manufacturer’s protocol, the probes were incubated overnight for hybridization. Images were captured using a Zeiss LSM710 confocal laser scanning microscope (Germany).


### RNA-binding protein immunoprecipitation assay
  RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) experiments were performed using an RNA Immunoprecipitation Kit (Geneseed, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. H2126 and H2085 cells (at least 2 × 107 cells per sample) were lysed in RIP buffer supplemented with protease and RNase inhibitors provided in the kit. Magnetic beads were conjugated with anti-AGO2, anti-β-catenin, or anti-IgG (control) antibodies at 4 ℃ for 3 h to capture the target protein. Lysates were subsequently incubated with the beads overnight at 4 °C, followed by the elimination of nonspecific binding by rinsing with 1 × washing buffer five times. Captured protein complexes, possibly coupled with diverse circRNAs were eluted and separated into proteins and circRNAs by one wash with 5 × washing buffer to obtain the target circRNF10. The target proteins were analyzed by Western blotting, and circRNAs were examined using RT-qPCR and agarose gel electrophoresis.


### In vitro transcription assay
  The GV658 plasmid (GeneChem, China) was used to synthesize diverse T7 promoter-driven plasmids containing different circRNF10 fragments for in vitro transcription. These plasmids were subsequently linearized with the BspQI restriction enzyme (NEB, China) to serve as a template for subsequent in vitro transcription. First, the Ribo™ RNAmax-T7 Biotin Labelling Transcription Kit (RiboBio, China) was used to label RNAs transcribed from the above plasmid-derived linear DNA template through 4-h incubations with a reaction system containing T7 Enzyme Mix, Biotin RNA Labelling Mix, and T7 Reaction Buffer at 37 ℃. After successful transcription, the remaining template DNA was removed by DNase I digestion at 37 ℃ for 30 min for RNA purification. Eventually, the purified RNAs were enriched from the above reaction system using ethanol precipitation. The corresponding RNA primers and their product lengths are listed in Supplementary Table 5.


### RNA pulldown assay
  The following experimental procedures were systematically performed to investigate the interaction between circRNF10 and β-catenin. Initially, H2126 and H2085 cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with a 1% protease inhibitor cocktail using ultrasonication. Nonspecifically bound proteins were removed from lysates using streptavidin-coated control magnetic beads from the Magnetic RNA‒Protein Pull-Down Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) by incubating the mixture at 4 ℃ for 30 min. Then, biotin-labelled circRNF10 probes (50 pmol) or in vitro transcribed biotinylated circRNF10 fragments were immobilized on streptavidin-conjugated magnetic beads by rotation for 1 h at room temperature. After the RNA-bound magnetic beads were incubated with the cell lysates, nonspecifically bound proteins were removed using control magnetic beads at 4 ℃ for 4 h, and the remaining proteins that were not bound to the RNA were further removed using washing buffer. The RNA-bound proteins were subsequently eluted in Evolution Buffer at 37 ℃ for 15 min and analyzed by Western blotting with an anti-β-catenin antibody.


### Luciferase reporter assay
  Dual-luciferase reporter plasmids containing wild-type (WT) or mutant (MUT) putative miR-1275 binding sites within circRNF10 or the DKK3 mRNA were constructed by Geneseed Biotech (China) to validate whether miR-1275 directly targets circRNF10 and the DKK3 mRNA. Here, putative miRNA binding sites were predicted using the RNAhybrid algorithm (v2.2). H2085 cells (3 × 105 cells per well) were cotransfected with 1 μg of plasmid (WT-circRNF10, MUT-circRNF10, WT-DKK3, or MUT-DKK3) and 100 nM miR-1275 mimics or negative mimic control (NC) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). After 48 h, the transfected cells were lysed and analyzed using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Abbkine, China). Briefly, 30 μL samples of cell lysate were transferred to opaque 96-well plates. Then, Firefly Luciferase Reagent (100 μL) was added, and the absorbance at 560 nm was measured using an automatic microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Subsequently, 100 μL of Renilla Luciferase Stop & Glo Reagent was added to quench firefly luciferase activity, followed by the measurement of the absorbance at 480 nm. Relative luciferase activity was calculated as the firefly/Renilla signal ratio.


### Statistical analysis
  GraphPad Prism 8.4 and R 4.3.2 were used for graph generation and statistical analyses. The differential expression analysis of RNA sequencing data was performed using DESeq2 (for read count data) or limma (for raw data), with significance thresholds defined as |log2-fold change|> 1 and adjusted p value < 0.05. The chi-square test was used to evaluate the associations of different expression patterns of circRNF10 and β-catenin with the clinicopathological characteristics. Kaplan‒Meier survival curves and the log-rank test were used to evaluate differences in overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) among groups. Pearson’s correlation analysis was employed to assess linear associations among variables. For all sequential variables, which are presented as the means ± standard deviations (S.Ds), the statistical significance of differences between two independent groups was evaluated using Student's t test, whereas comparisons among three or more experimental groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s or Turkey’s post hoc test. Variables that change over time are analyzed using repeated measures ANOVA or linear mixed models. P values reported in the graphs were two-sided, with a p-value of < 0.05 considered statistically significant. The significance levels were set as follows: ns for p ≥ 0.05, * for p < 0.05, ** for p < 0.01, and *** for p < 0.001.
