# High-throughput screening identifies FDA approved drug mitiglinide as a novel pyroptosis inhibitor and therapeutic agent for osteoarthritis


## Graphical abstract
  Schematic representation of the mechanism of mitiglinide attenuating OA through Nrf2/HO-1/NLRP3 pathway.

  


## Introduction
  Osteoarthritis (OA), the most prevalent degenerative joint disorder, is characterized by the progressive deterioration of cartilage, inflammation of the synovial membrane, and remodeling of subchondral bone [1]. With an aging global population, OA imposes a growing socioeconomic burden [2], yet current therapeutic strategies—primarily analgesics and anti-inflammatory drugs—offer only symptomatic relief without halting disease progression [3].

  Pyroptosis has been implicated in driving cartilage destruction [4,5]. ​​Activation of NLRP3, a key inflammasome component, and the resulting caspase-1-mediated release of pro-inflammatory cytokines amplify inflammation and accelerate extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation, positioning pyroptosis as a promising therapeutic target in OA [6,7].

  This study aims to identify pharmacological inhibitors of chondrocyte pyroptosis to mitigate OA progression. The FDA-approved drug library provides clinically approved drugs with validated bioactivity and established safety profiles [8]. Prior studies confirm FDA-approved drugs attenuate disease progression through pyroptosis suppression [9,10]. To date, limited studies have systematically investigated the repurposing potential of FDA-approved drugs for therapeutic intervention in OA.

  High-throughput screening (HTS) of existing FDA-approved drug libraries is a strategic approach to rapidly identify candidates with dual anti-inflammatory and chondroprotective properties [[11], [12], [13]]. Thus, using an in vitro pyroptosis model (LPS-primed and nigericin-stimulated chondrocytes) [[14], [15], [16]], we performed HTS of 1,228 FDA-approved drugs and identified mitiglinide as the optimal candidate for rescuing pyroptosis-induced cell death. Mitiglinide, a rapid-acting insulin secretagogue approved for type 2 diabetes, ​​displays properties of antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity​ [17]. Nevertheless, its role in OA pathogenesis and mechanisms targeting chondrocyte pyroptosis remain uncharacterized.

  Nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) mediates ECM degradation, inflammatory response, and pyroptosis in the pathogenesis of OA. Pathway activation induces IκB kinase (IKK)-mediated phosphorylation of IκB proteins, enabling P65 nuclear translocation from the cytoplasm, thereby initiating the transcription of pro-inflammatory mediators [18,19]. The nuclear translocation of P65 promotes pyroptosis by transcriptionally upregulating inflammasome components and inflammatory factors, namely IL-1β and IL-18. This enhances the activation and cleavage of Caspase-1, converting it into its bioactive form [20]. Furthermore, as a key transcription factor, P65 upregulates the transcription of ADAMTS5, MMP13, iNOS, and COX2, thereby collectively promoting ECM degradation and the initiation of inflammation [20,21]. Current research confirms that Nrf2 activation induces HO-1 transcription via binding to the ARE [22]. Expression of HO-1 effectively suppressed the nuclear translocation of p65, thereby inhibiting the transcription of genes related to ECM degradation, inflammation, and pyroptosis [[22], [23], [24]]. Consequently, the Nrf2/HO-1 axis attenuates NF-κB-driven ECM degradation [25], inflammatory responses, and pyroptosis activation [26], thereby mitigating OA progression [27,28].

  This study represents the first effort to screen an FDA-approved drug library specifically targeting chondrocyte pyroptosis and provides inaugural evidence that mitiglinide alleviates OA progression through suppression of pyroptosis and cartilage matrix degradation, mediated by the Nrf2/HO-1 signaling cascade.​ Our findings underscore drug repurposing as a viable strategy for developing disease-modifying OA therapeutics.


## Materials and methods

### Bioinformatics analysis
  Using the DESeq2 tool in R, gene expression datasets from the GEO database were analyzed to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs). DEGs were filtered using standards of |log2FoldChange| > 1 with adjusted P-value < 0.05. The identified DEGs were then cross-referenced with published pyroptosis-related gene sets to determine overlapping genes. Hierarchical clustering was performed using R's heatmap package to visualize expression profiles of the overlapping gene set, with color gradients in the heatmap reflecting expression changes of pyroptosis-associated genes during OA progression.


### Reagents
  Mitiglinide (purity 98.82 %) was purchased from MedChemExpress (MCE, Shanghai, China). Lipopolysaccharide (LPS, purity ≥ 98 %) and collagenase-II were procured from Solarbio (Beijing, China). Nigericin (purity ≥ 98 %) was sourced from Aladdin Biochemical Technology (Shanghai, China). Indomethacin (purity ≥ 99 %) were acquired from MedChemExpress (New Jersey, USA). Anti-p65 (#8242), anti-phospho-IκBα (#2859), and anti-IκBα (#4812) antibodies were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Beverly, MA, USA). Anti-Collagen II (ab34712), anti- IL-1β (ab254360), anti-Aggrecan (ab216965), anti-ADAMTS5 (ab41037), anti-MMP13 (ab194726), anti-COX2 (ab179800), and anti-Phospho-P65 (ab194726) antibodies were obtained from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). Anti-iNOS (18985–1-AP), anti-GAPDH (60004–1-Ig), anti-Lamin B (12987–1-AP), anti-Nrf2 (16396–1-AP), anti-HO-1 (66743–1-Ig), anti-Caspase-1 (CASP1, 22915–1-AP), anti-ASC (10500–1-AP), and anti-IL-18 (10663–1-AP) antibodies were sourced from Proteintech (Wuhan, China). Anti-NLRP3 (ET1610-93), anti-Gasdermin D (GSDMD) (HA721144) antibody was purchased from HuaBio (Hangzhou, China).


### Isolation and primary culture of mouse chondrocytes
  From the knee joints of 3–5-day-old C57BL/6 mice, chondrocytes were harvested. Cartilage tissues were chopped into minute fragments, rinsed thrice with PBS, and subjected to digestion with 2 mg/ml collagenase II at 37 °C for 4 h. Subsequent to digestion, the cell suspension underwent centrifugation (800 g, 5 min), PBS rinsing, with final resuspension in serum-supplemented DMEM/F12 (10 % FBS) incorporating 1 % bactericidal agents (penicillin/streptomycin). Under 5 % CO2 and 37 °C conditions, cells were incubated. Secondary chondrocytes (passage 2) were utilized in subsequent experiments, with daily replacement of the culture medium.


### High-Throughput screening of the FDA-Approved drug library
  For drug repurposing discovery, primary mouse chondrocytes were utilized in screening 1,228 FDA-approved drugs via high-throughput methodology.​ We inoculated 96-well plates with chondrocytes (8,000/well) to ensure monolayer attachment, followed by 24 h incubation.​​ Following PBS washing, cells were divided into three experimental groups: Blank control group, Stimulation group (LPS (100 ng/mL) + nigericin (10 μM)), Drug treatment group (LPS (100 ng/mL) + nigericin (10 μM) + candidate drug (10 μM)). After a 24-hour incubation period, cell survival rates were assessed with CCK-8 reagents.​ Absorbance measurements were acquired, and drug rescue rates were computed via the formula below (n = 3): Rescue Rate (%) = [(Ac − Ab) / (Aa − Ab)] × 100. Aa represents Absorbance of untreated chondrocytes (blank control group). Ab represents Absorbance of LPS + nigericin-treated chondrocytes (stimulation group). Ac represents Absorbance of LPS + nigericin + drug-treated chondrocytes (drug treatment group).


### Cell viability analysis
  Chondrocyte viability was evaluated by seeding cells at a density of 8,000 per well in 96-well plates and incubating for 24 h. After PBS rinsing, cells were incubated in basal medium containing gradient concentrations of mitiglinide for 24 or 48 h. Following PBS washes, cells in CCK-8-supplemented DMEM/F12 (10 %) were incubated 2 h, then read at 450 nm on a microplate reader.


### Calcein/PI staining
  24-well plates received chondrocyte seeding (105 cells/well) followed by 24-h culture. After PBS washing, cells were divided into: untreated controls, LPS (100 ng/mL) + nigericin (10 μM)-stimulated group, and LPS (100 ng/mL) + nigericin (10 μM) + mitiglinide (10 μM) treatment group. Live/dead staining via Calcein-AM/PI was conducted in basal medium at 37 °C under light-free conditions for 30 min post-24-h culture. Viable cells fluoresce green due to Calcein-AM esterase activity, while dead cells exhibit red fluorescence from PI nuclear staining. Fluorescence imaging was conducted via an inverted microscope system.


### Determination of intracellular ROS levels
  Post-treatment chondrocytes were PBS-flushed and incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA in serum-free medium (37 °C, dark) for 30 min. ROS levels were assessed by fluorescence microscopy utilizing ImageJ software for intensity analysis.


### Western blot analysis
  Chondrocytes were subjected to mitiglinide dose gradients alongside co-stimulation with LPS (100 ng/ml) and nigericin (10 μM) for 24 h. Cell lysis proceeded for 30 min in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) containing 1 mM PMSF, phosphatase/protease inhibitor cocktails. For supernatant collection, lysates underwent 30-min centrifugation at 12,000 × g and 4 °C. Protein aliquots (30 μg/lane) were electrophoretically resolved on 10–12.5 % SDS-polyacrylamide gels and electroblotted onto PVDF membranes. Membranes blocked with 5 % skim milk for 2 h at room temperature (25 °C) underwent overnight cold incubation (4 °C) with designated primary antibodies: p-p65, p65, p-IκBα, IκBα, COX-2, iNOS, Collagen II, Aggrecan, ADAMTS5, MMP13, Nrf2, HO-1, GSDMD, Caspase-1 (CASP1), ASC, IL-18, NLRP3 (all 1:1,000 dilution), GAPDH, Lamin B (1:3,000 dilution). Following three washes with Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1 % Tween-20 (TBST), membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5,000 dilution) for 2 h at room temperature. Chemiluminescent protein signals were documented on a ChemiDoc™ MP platform (Bio-Rad), with quantitative densitometry executed in Image Lab™ (v3.0, USA).


### Immunofluorescence analysis
  Chondrocytes were inoculated at a density of 5 × 104 cells per glass-bottom plate and incubated for 24 h to facilitate adherence. Subsequent to the removal of the growth medium, cells underwent triple PBS rinsing followed by 15-min fixation in 4 % paraformaldehyde (PFA) at room temperature. Following fixation, three 15-min PBS washes were performed to remove residual fixative. Cellular permeabilization was achieved using 0.1 % Triton X-100 in PBS (5 min, 25 °C), then incubated with primary antibodies for 24 h at 4 °C. After three PBS washes, cells were immunolabeled with Alexa Fluor® 488-conjugated secondary antibodies under light-protected conditions (2 h, RT). Finally, stained samples were mounted after three final PBS washes. Fluorescent images were obtained utilizing an IX83 inverted fluorescent microscope equipped with a 20 × objective lens.


### Network pharmacology analysis
  PubChem served as the source for mitiglinide's molecular structure and SMILES encoding. OA-related targets were systematically curated from three authoritative databases: GeneCards, OMIM, and DisGeNET. To elucidate the therapeutic mechanism of mitiglinide against OA, Venn diagrams were constructed using the R package VennDiagram (v3.1.10) to identify overlapping targets between drug and disease. STRING database was utilized to generate PPI networks for overlapping genes. The PPI network was imported into Cytoscape (v3.9.1) and analyzed via Maximal Clique Centrality (MCC) algorithm in CytoHubba plugin, selecting the top 50 hub genes. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses were conducted using the following R packages: DOSE (v3.26.0) for disease ontology, clusterProfiler (v4.8.1) for enrichment calculation, enrichplot (v1.20.0) and ggplot2 (v3.4.2) for visualization, Supporting packages: colorpace (v2.1–0), stringi (v1.7.12).


### Molecular docking
  The two-dimensional structure of mitiglinide was retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). This 2D structure was subsequently converted to a three-dimensional configuration using ChemOffice software and saved in MOL2 format. For molecular docking receptors, protein targets were screened through the RCSB PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org/), with crystal structures of higher resolution being selected. The corresponding PDB IDs were as follows: NFE2L2 (PDB_00007k2j), MMP13 (PDB_00004jpa), and CASP1 (PDB_00001rwm). Protein preparation including removal of water molecules and phosphate groups was performed using PyMOL 2.6, with results saved as PDB files. Both protein and ligand structures were processed with AutoDock 1.5.6-proteins were hydrogenated and dehydrated, while small molecule ligands were hydrogenated and assigned rotatable bonds, followed by determination of docking grid coordinates. Molecular docking was carried out using AutoDock Vina to explore protein–ligand interactions. The optimal conformation was identified by comparing docking scores among the results. Finally, Discovery Studio 2019 and PyMOL 2.6 were employed to visualize the docking results, generating both 2D interaction diagrams and 3D analytical plots showing compound interactions with key residues.


### Molecular dynamics simulation
  All-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted using GROMACS 2022.3 under periodic boundary conditions, wherein the protein–ligand complex was solubilized in a cubic box with TIP3P water molecules extending 1.2 nm from the solute surface. Ligand topology was created utilizing GAFF2 parameters through ACPYPE v2022.2. Electrostatic interactions were treated using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method and Verlet cutoff scheme. Subsequently, the system underwent 100,000-step equilibration in both NVT (constant particle number, volume, and temperature) and NPT (constant particle number, pressure, and temperature) ensembles, with a coupling constant of 0.1 ps over a 100 ps simulation duration. Van der Waals and Coulombic interactions were calculated with a 1.0 nm cutoff distance. Finally, molecular dynamics simulations were executed with GROMACS 2022 under isothermal-isobaric conditions (310 K, 1 bar) for a 100-ns production run.


### Cellular thermal Shift Assay
  Following isolation, chondrocytes underwent 24-h culture in complete growth medium containing 10 μM mitiglinide. Post-treatment cells underwent ice-cold PBS washing followed by RIPA buffer lysis containing 1 mM PMSF and Roche protease inhibitor cocktail for protein preservation.​ Lysates were aliquoted and exposed to thermal stress (50–70 °C, 4°C increments) for 3 min, with all samples maintained on ice before treatment. Parallel lysate batches underwent three liquid nitrogen freeze–thaw cycles. Following centrifugation at 20,000 *g (4 °C, 20 min), the supernatants were combined with Laemmli buffer, denatured at 95 °C for 5 min, and analyzed using immunoblotting.


### siRNA transfection
  A validated Nrf2-targeting siRNA (sequence: 5′-GCAAGAAGCCAGATACAAA-3′) was procured from Ruibo Biotech (Guangdong, China). We experimented according to the instructions. Knockdown efficiency (>80 %) was verified by immunoblotting at 48 h post-transfection.


### Osteoarthritis animal model
  The Animal Center of Wenzhou Medical University provided forty 8-week-old male mice of C57BL/6 strain. Mice were kept in a clean, ventilated animal room with controlled temperature (24–26 °C) and a 12 h light/dark cycle, with food and water available ad libitum. The DMM model was surgically induced in the right hind knee joint. Indomethacin was selected as the positive control drug. Through random allocation, eight mice were assigned to each of five groups: Sham, DMM, DMM + Mitiglinide (1.5 mg/kg), DMM + Mitiglinide (3 mg/kg) and DMM + Indomethacin (3 mg/kg) groups. The left knee joints in all groups underwent sham surgery (simple arthrotomy) as internal controls. Post-surgery, Experimental groups received daily oral gavage of mitiglinide (1.5 or 3 mg/kg) dissolved in sterile saline. The positive control group was administered indomethacin (3 mg/kg/day) via the same route. As vehicle controls, both the DMM-induced and Sham-operated groups were given equal saline volumes. After 8-week intervention, euthanized mice underwent knee joint isolation for biochemical and histopathological assessment.


### Ethics statement
  All experiments involving animals were conducted according to the ethical policies and procedures approved by the ethics committee of 10.13039/100007835Wenzhou Medical University (WYDW2025-0292).


### X-ray imaging analysis
  The Xpert 80 platform was employed to evaluate cartilage degradation and calcification under dual-energy conditions (50 kV/160 kV).


### Histopathologic staining
  Following euthanasia, mouse knee joints were fixed, decalcified, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. Tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) and Safranin O-Fast Green (S-O). We captured histopathological images via conventional light microscopy. OA severity was graded using the Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) histopathological scoring system.


### Immunohistochemical analysis
  Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis quantified tissue expression profiles of Aggrecan, GSDMD-N, and Nrf2. Prepared knee joint sections were deparaffinized in xylene, subjected to 3 % hydrogen peroxide for 10 min, rinsed thrice with PBS-T (0.1 % Tween-20), and heat-induced epitope retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Following a 2-hour incubation with 10 % goat serum for blocking, slices were treated with primary antibodies against Aggrecan, GSDMD-N, and Nrf2 (1:100 dilution) at 4 °C overnight. Following three PBS-T washes, sections were incubated with species-matched HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature, then visualized using DAB chromogen and counterstained with hematoxylin.


### Statistical analysis
  All experimental procedures were triplicated. Results are presented as mean ± SD. GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, USA) facilitated statistical analyses employing Student's t-test and one-way ANOVA, with P < 0.05 defining statistical significance.


## Results

### Pyroptosis contributes to osteoarthritis progression and mitiglinide is identified as a potent pyroptosis inhibitor
  Pyroptosis, a gasdermin-mediated lytic cell death, exacerbates tissue damage by unleashing IL-1β/IL-18 in inflammatory cascades [29]. To validate whether pyroptosis occurs during OA progression, we conducted bioinformatics analysis of sequencing data from public databases. DEGs between OA patients and controls were first identified from the GSE114007 dataset. A volcano plot revealed 2,169 DEGs, comprising 936 downregulated and 1,311 upregulated genes (Fig. 1A). These DEGs were subsequently cross-analyzed with published pyroptosis-related gene sets [30]. Venn diagram analysis identified 78 pyroptosis-related differentially expressed genes (PRDEGs) implicated in OA pathogenesis (Fig. 1B). Hierarchical clustering analysis visualized through a heatmap demonstrated upregulated expression of pyroptosis-associated genes (e.g. NLRP3) in OA cohorts (Fig. 1C). To further validate the occurrence of pyroptosis in vivo, we performed immunohistochemical analysis of GSDMD-N expression in articular cartilage. Quantitative assessment revealed elevated GSDMD-N levels in 4-week and 8-week OA models compared to sham controls (Fig. 1D-E). In the IL-1β-induced osteoarthritic environment in vitro, treatment with a pyroptosis inhibitor markedly alleviated the degradation of the ECM and suppressed chondrocyte death (Fig. S1).   To alleviate pyroptosis occurring during OA progression, we established an in vitro pyroptosis model using LPS (100 ng/mL) and Nigericin (10 μM)-treated chondrocytes, with concentrations validated through CCK-8 viability assays and Western blot confirmation (Fig. 1F-H). Subsequent HTS of 1,228 FDA-approved drugs identified 10 candidates demonstrating > 50 % rescue efficacy against pyroptosis-induced chondrocyte death (Fig. 1I). To comparatively assess the therapeutic efficacy of the top 10 candidates, we conducted secondary screening to determine their optimal concentrations (Fig. 1J). Our objective was to identify novel drugs with pyroptosis-inhibitory properties for OA treatment. Accordingly, we implemented the following screening strategy: 1. All drugs from the FDA-approved drug library were ranked based on their pyroptosis rescue efficacy at the same concentration. 2. Drugs demonstrating a rescue rate exceeding 50 % were subjected to secondary screening to determine their optimal effective concentrations and were re-ranked accordingly. 3. To ensure mechanistic novelty, we first excluded drugs previously reported as validated pyroptosis inhibitors in other disease models, such as rucaparib [31], doxycycline [32], and pirfenidone [33]. 4. Subsequently, to maintain novelty in OA therapeutic context, we further excluded drugs with established ECM modulation studies, including dopamine [34], doxycycline [35], and pirfenidone [36]. In summary, following this systematic exclusion process, we selected mitiglinide—which ranked second in our screening—as the focus of our subsequent investigation.


### Mitiglinide suppresses LPS/Nigericin-Induced pyroptosis in chondrocytes
  From the HTS, mitiglinide was suggested as one of the most potent pyroptosis inhibitors in chondrocyte. In the following study, we aimed to validate its effect on pyroptosis. Fig. 2A illustrates the molecular structure of mitiglinide. The cytotoxicity of mitiglinide (0, 2.5, 5, 10, 20, 40, and 80 nM) on chondrocytes was evaluated using CCK-8 assays. Results demonstrated no significant reduction in cell viability or marked cytotoxicity after 24- and 48-hour exposure at concentrations below 10 μM (Fig. 2B). ​​Consequently, a mitiglinide dose range of 0–10 μM was employed for follow-up studies in LPS/nigericin-stimulated chondrocytes.​ Calcein-AM/PI dual staining is routinely employed to assess cell viability, therefore we utilized this method to further evaluate the protective effects of mitiglinide on chondrocytes. Results demonstrated that under nigericin stimulation, mitiglinide significantly reduced the proportion of PI-positive cells in LPS-primed chondrocytes (Fig. 2C-D). Current studies have demonstrated that excessive ROS production is a key factor in activating pyroptosis [37], therefore intracellular ROS levels in chondrocytes were evaluated utilizing the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe.   Subsequent investigations revealed the inhibitory role of mitiglinide in pyroptosis. Western blot analysis demonstrated that LPS and Nigericin treatment significantly upregulated pyroptosis-associated proteins (NLRP3, GSDMD-N, C-CASP-1, ASC, IL-18 and IL-1β), while mitiglinide dose-dependently suppressed this activation (Fig. 2G-H). Immunofluorescence analysis of GSDMD-N expression showed concordance with western blot results (Fig. 2I-J). In summary, mitiglinide dose-dependently attenuates LPS and nigericin induced chondrocyte pyroptosis.


### Mitiglinide attenuates extracellular matrix degradation and inflammation in chondrocytes
  ECM is essential for maintaining joint homeostasis, and its degradation along with inflammatory activation critically drives OA progression [38]. Therefore, to investigate the effects of mitiglinide on ECM metabolism, we evaluated the expression levels of key proteins that involve in ECM metabolism, such as Collagen-II, Aggrecan, MMP-13, and ADAMTS-5. Western blot analysis revealed that mitiglinide treatment may lead to increased expression of ECM anabolic proteins (collagen-II and aggrecan), and decreased expression of ECM catabolic proteins (MMP-13 and ADAMTS-5) (Fig. 3C-D). Immunofluorescence analysis of Collagen-II and MMP-13 expression corroborated the Western blot findings (Fig. 3E-H). Safranin O and Alcian blue staining of chondrocytes further confirmed the efficacy of mitiglinide in alleviating ECM degradation (Fig. 3I-J). ### Nrf2 is suggested as the target of mitiglinide
  The aforementioned studies have demonstrated mitiglinide's modulation of pyroptosis, ECM metabolism, and inflammatory responses. To investigate the underlying mechanisms, we employed network pharmacology to explore potential targets of mitiglinide in alleviating chondrocyte pyroptosis in OA. The chemical structure of mitiglinide was obtained from relevant databases, identifying 125 candidate targets. Subsequently, 2,037 OA-related targets were screened. Venn diagram analysis revealed 52 overlapping targets between mitiglinide and OA (Fig. 4A, S2). The overlapping genes were then imported into the STRING database, selecting Homo sapiens as the species to obtain protein–protein interaction (PPI) network data. Identified targets including TLR4, STAT3, MMP9, NFKB1, BCL2, MMP2, NFE2L2, CASP1, and MMP13 provided mechanistic clues for exploring mitiglinide's pharmacological mechanisms. (Fig. 4B). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that mitiglinide exerts its therapeutic effects on OA primarily through the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway and Toll-like receptor signaling pathway (Fig. 4C).   To further elucidate the molecular targets of mitiglinide, molecular docking was performed between the top 30 targets from the PPI network and mitiglinide to evaluate binding affinities. Molecular docking revealed robust mitiglinide-NFE2L2 interaction, evidenced by strong binding affinity (−9.0 kcal/mol), along with moderate binding affinities with MMP13 (−4.29 kcal/mol) and CASP1 (−4.04 kcal/mol). Molecular docking revealed hydrogen bonding between mitiglinide and Nrf2 at Val465/Val418 residues (Fig. 4G-H).

  To evaluate the dynamic binding stability of the protein–ligand complex, we performed 100 ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of the NFE2L2-mitiglinide complex. Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD) analysis revealed that the system reached equilibrium after 5 ns, with fluctuations stabilizing within 1.1 Å, indicating high conformational stability. Further characterization of the Radius of Gyration (Rg) and Solvent Accessible Surface Area (SASA) showed minor variations during the simulation, suggesting that the mitiglinide −NFE2L2 complex underwent only minor conformational changes during molecular dynamics simulations. Hydrogen bond occupancy analysis demonstrated persistent interactions between mitiglinide and NFE2L2, with 1–3 bonds maintained for > 75 % of the simulation time. This finding demonstrates robust hydrogen bonding interactions between mitiglinide and NFE2L2.Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF) values for backbone residues remained below 2.3 Å, confirming that the complex demonstrated robust structural stability. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that mitiglinide forms a stable complex with NFE2L2(Fig. 4I). ## Discussion
  OA, the leading degenerative joint condition, involves cartilage degradation, synovial pro-inflammatory activation, and osteochondral junction remodeling​ [1,41]. Driven by aging populations, OA imposes a growing global health burden [42]. This study demonstrates that mitiglinideexerts therapeutic effects on OA by suppressing chondrocyte pyroptosis through activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 signaling axis, thereby attenuating NLRP3 inflammasome activation and ECM degradation. The pathological significance of pyroptosis in OA is underscored by our bioinformatics analysis, which identified 78 pyroptosis-related PRDEGs enriched in OA cartilage. Among these, NLRP3 inflammasome components exhibited marked upregulation. Pyroptosis is a form of inflammatory programmed cell death characterized by NLRP3 inflammasome activation, caspase-1-dependent cleavage of GSDMD, and subsequent release of pro-inflammatory cytokines [43]. This process leads to plasma membrane pore formation, amplifying inflammatory responses and tissue damage. Our study (Fig. 1), along with results from other groups, showed that pyroptosis activation drives cartilage destruction and ECM degradation in OA [44,45]. However, although some molecules have been reported to suppress pyroptosis in non-chondrocytes [10,46], a systematic screening for pyroptosis-targeting drugs remains uninvestigated. Thus, we established an in vitro pyroptosis cell model and carried out a HTS screening to find out new pyroptosis inhibitors.

  The repurposing of FDA-approved drugs offers a transformative strategy in modern pharmacotherapy, leveraging existing compounds with well-characterized safety profiles to expedite novel therapeutic development [47]. These drugs possess validated pharmacokinetic properties, structural diversity, and multi-target engagement capabilities that enable rapid translation across diseases. By establishing a screening platform using an FDA drug library, we identified a list of FDA approved drugs that may potently suppress pyroptosis in chondrocytes. We excluded drugs that have already been published to have the capability to suppress pyroptosis as well as alleviate OA, and mitiglinide was selected for further investigation.

  Mitiglinide is a rapid-acting insulin secretagogue initially approved by the FDA for type 2 diabetes management. Our study confirmed that mitiglinide may indeed suppress pyroptosis in chondrocytes, as indicated by the biochemical experiment results (Fig. 2), it may also suppress ECM degradation in chondrocytes (Fig. 3), which suggests that it may also have the potential of OA therapy. Mechanistically, mitiglinide targets the Nrf2/HO-1 signaling cascade, while concurrently suppressing NF-κB-mediated inflammation and NLRP3 inflammasome activation.

  It is noteworthy that mitiglinide, as an anti-diabetic drug, carries a potential risk of inducing hypoglycemia if administered to non-diabetic patients [48]. Moreover, through oral administration, the effects of mitiglinide may be limited by first-pass metabolism, resulting in reduced intra-articular bioavailability. Consequently, we propose that the future direction of the investigations should consider cartilage-targeted delivery systems employing local injection, e.g. encapsulating the drug within sustained-release carriers for intra-articular injection. This approach not only enhances intra-articular drug concentration and bypasses the limitations of oral administration, but may also potentially prevent hypoglycemic risk in normoglycemic OA patients.

  However, for diabetic OA patients, mitiglinide might be an ideal drug. Current evidence indicates that diabetes is a potential risk factor for OA [49,50], and a subset of patients presents with both conditions. Therefore, oral administration of mitiglinide may not only treat diabetes, but also alleviate OA progression.

  Additionally, our screening generated a list of drugs with potential pyroptosis-inhibitory activity. Although some drugs have already been reported to be pyroptosis inhibitors, such as rucaparib [31], doxycycline [32], and pirfenidone [33], most of others were previously unknown as pyroptosis inhibitors. Thus, we provide a list of new pyroptosis inhibitors. As pyroptosis is a widely involved pathogenic mechanism, these drugs may have the potential for the therapy of pyroptosis related diseases, such as sepsis, SARS-CoV-2 infection, and other infectious diseases.

  Given the high efficiency of screening, we adopted a uniform concentration (10 μ M) when establishing the in vitro screening model. Therefore, drugs with a rescue rate below 50 % do not necessarily imply an absence of inhibitory effects on pyroptosis. If their concentrations are optimized, some novel pyroptosis inhibitors may emerge. Additionally, since pyroptosis can generate inflammatory factors (such as IL-18 and IL-1β), which may lead to ECM degradation, these drugs can inhibit both pyroptosis and ECM degradation. Although our experiments only validated the regulatory effect of mitiglinide on ECM, we speculate that other pyroptosis inhibitors may similarly possess the ability to suppress ECM degradation and exhibit therapeutic potential for OA, and pyroptosis inhibitors such as dopamine [34], doxycycline [35], and pirfenidone [36] have already been proved to suppress ECM degradation.


## Conclusion
  In summary, through HTS of an FDA-approved drug library, we identified a list of novel pyroptosis inhibitors. Mitiglinide was found to suppress pyroptosis through direct binding and activating Nrf2 and its downstream pathways, it may also alleviate OA in vitro and in vivo. We propose that the listed pyroptosis inhibitors may have the potential for the therapy of pyroptosis related diseases, including but not limited to OA. Meanwhile, as mitiglinide is a hypoglycemic drug, it may better be taken in diabetic patients. For those non-diabetic patients, further local delivery system should be developed to bypass the side effects of its oral administration.
