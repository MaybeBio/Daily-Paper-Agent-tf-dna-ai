# Identification and experimental validation of biomarkers associated with macrophages in myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-27301-5.


## Introduction
  Myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury (MIRI) is a critical and challenging pathological component in the management of acute myocardial infarction, which must not be overlooked1. As a major complication of clinical interventions such as thrombolysis, percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), and coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), its high incidence poses a significant public health threat2. The pathogenesis of MIRI arises from the restoration of blood flow following coronary artery occlusion, initiating a cascade of pathological events, including excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, intracellular calcium overload, inflammatory cascades, and endothelial cell damage. These processes ultimately result in sustained myocardial tissue injury and functional impairment, significantly contributing to the increased mortality associated with cardiovascular diseases3,4. Clinically, MIRI diagnosis relies on manifestations, electrocardiogram (ECG) changes, and biomarker detection. However, current treatment approaches, such as pharmacological interventions, PCI, and CABG, exhibit substantial limitations, including inadequate myocardial perfusion and a high risk of vascular restenosis. Moreover, the absence of precise therapeutic targets represents a significant bottleneck in improving patient outcomes5,6. Therefore, a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms driving MIRI and the development of novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies are urgent medical priorities.

  Macrophages, crucial innate immune cells, are highly versatile and heterogeneous, playing key roles in tissue homeostasis, immune surveillance, and inflammatory responses. These cells are capable of phagocytosing cellular debris, pathogens, and apoptotic cells, while secreting a variety of cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors that regulate immune responses and tissue repair7,8. In the context of MIRI, macrophages have been identified as central regulators of inflammation and tissue damage. Accumulating evidence indicates that macrophage polarization, typically classified into M1 (inflammatory) and M2 (repair/anti-inflammatory) states, plays distinct roles in the progression and resolution of MIRI9. However, despite significant advancements in understanding macrophage interactions with MIRI, the precise molecular mechanisms governing these processes remain largely unclear. Further investigation into macrophage-specific signaling pathways and their contributions to MIRI pathogenesis is critical for the development of targeted therapeutic strategies10.

  This study aims to utilize transcriptomic data to identify and validate macrophage-related biomarkers in MIRI, thereby elucidating its complex mechanisms. Immunoinfiltration analysis and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) were performed to explore the relationships between these biomarkers and the immune microenvironment. Additionally, this study examined their involvement in biological pathways, potential therapeutic targets, and drug-binding interactions, providing a novel theoretical framework for the development of therapeutic and diagnostic strategies for MIRI.


## Materials and methods

### Data acquisition
  MIRI transcriptome datasets were sourced from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The 24-hour time point was identified as critical for MIRI-induced inflammatory responses and macrophage polarization. At this stage, significant changes in the expression of pro-/anti-inflammatory factors and complete M1/M2 macrophage differentiation were observed, making it an ideal time for capturing immune and gene expression alterations11. Consequently, samples treated with MIRI for 24 h were selected for analysis in this study. GSE160516 served as the training set, comprising 4 mouse heart tissue samples treated with ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) for 24 h (IR24h group) and 4 samples from the sham-operated group as controls. All other samples were excluded. GSE225105 was used as the validation set to assess gene expression, with only 3 mouse heart tissue samples from the I/R treatment for 24 h selected for the IR24h group and 3 samples from the sham-operated group for the control group. All other samples were excluded. The GSE146285 dataset, a single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset (sequencing platform: GPL19057), provided 4 Sham_1day samples as controls and 9 MIRI samples. Additionally, 39 macrophage-related gene (MRG) sets were retrieved from the Molecular Signature Database (MSigDB) using “macrophage” as the keyword and Gene Ontology (GO) as the filtering criterion, resulting in a total of 295 MRGs12. The biomaRt package was used to convert Homo sapiens genes into their mouse gene counterparts (v 4.2.2).


### Differential expression analysis
  The limma package (v 3.54.1)13 was employed to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the IR24h and control groups in the GSE160516 dataset, applying a |log2 fold-change (FC)| > 1 and adjusted P < 0.05 as thresholds. The ggplot2 package (v 2.6.4.3)14 was used to generate a volcano plot for DEGs, highlighting the top 10 upregulated and downregulated genes. The ComplexHeatmap package (v 2.15.1)15 was subsequently used to create a heatmap of these DEGs.


### Candidate genes acquisition and enrichment analysis
  Candidate genes were identified by intersecting DEGs with MRGs using the ggvenn package (v 0.1.9)16. Functional enrichment analysis of the candidate genes was performed using the clusterProfiler package (v 4.10.1)17, including GO and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichments, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Visualizations, including volcano and box plots, were generated using the ggplot package (v 2.6.4.3).


### Biomarkers acquisition
  To examine protein interactions among the candidate genes, a Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) network was constructed using the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING) database (https://string-db.org/), with a confidence score threshold of 0.4. Following this, four algorithms—Maximum Clique Centrality (MCC), Degree of Maximum Neighborhood Component (DMNC), Maximum Neighborhood Component (MNC), and Degree—were applied to score the candidate genes using the CytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape (v3.9.1). The ggvenn package (v 0.1.9) was used to identify the intersection of the top 25 genes from each algorithm, and the resulting genes were designated as candidate biomarkers. To assess the correlations among candidate biomarkers, correlation analysis was performed using the psych package (v 2.2.9)18, based on all samples from the GSE160516 dataset (|correlation coefficient (cor)| > 0.30 and P < 0.05). The expression of candidate biomarkers was then compared between the IR24h and control groups in the GSE160516 and GSE225105 databases using the Wilcoxon test (P < 0.05). Biomarkers exhibiting consistent expression trends across both datasets were selected as final biomarkers. Additionally, the expression levels of these biomarkers at different time points (6 h, 24 h, and 72 h) were analyzed using the Wilcoxon test (P < 0.05).


### Functional correlation and enrichment analyses of biomarkers
  To explore the functional similarity between biomarkers, the GOSemSim package (v 2.18.1)19 was used to calculate similarity scores (a score > 0.5 indicated high similarity). Box plots were generated using ggplot2 (v 2.6.4.3) to visualize the results. GeneMANIA was employed to predict genes related to the functions of biomarkers and their involvement in functional pathways. The correlation coefficients between each biomarker and all genes in the GSE160516 dataset were computed using the corrplot package (v 0.92)20. These coefficients were then ranked, generating a ranked list of genes corresponding to each biomarker. The “mh.all.v2023.2.Mm.symbols.gmt [Hallmarks]” file from the MSigDB database was used as the reference gene set. GSEA was conducted using the clusterProfiler package (v 4.10.1), with thresholds of |Normalized Enrichment Score (NES)| > 1, false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.25, and P < 0.05. After filtering with P < 0.05, the top 5 most significant pathways were visualized for further analysis.


### Immune infiltration analysis
  For a comprehensive assessment of immune cell infiltration in both the IR24h and control groups, immune infiltration analysis was performed on all samples from the GSE160516 dataset. The single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA) algorithm was used to calculate the infiltration scores of 28 distinct immune cell types. Immune cell types were based on previous literature21. The Wilcoxon test was applied to compare infiltration scores between the IR24h and control groups. P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, and immune cells with significant differences (P < 0.05) were identified. Correlation analysis was then conducted using the psych package (v 2.2.9) to examine relationships between differentiated immune cells (|cor| > 0.30 and P < 0.05).


### Regulation network analysis
  To identify the TFs and miRNAs regulating the biomarkers, predictions were made using the NetworkAnalyst database (https://www.networkanalyst.ca/), and the miRNA-mRNA-TF network was visualized with Cytoscape (v3.9.1). To explore potential associations between the biomarkers and other diseases, the Connective Tissue Disease (CTD) database was employed for analysis. The top ten diseases with the highest predicted scores for each biomarker were identified, and the biomarker-disease network was visualized using Cytoscape (v3.9.1).


### Drug prediction and molecular Docking
  For drug predictions targeting the biomarkers, the Drug-Gene Interaction Database (DGIdb) was used to identify drugs linked to the biomarkers, and the drug-biomarker network was visualized in Cytoscape (v3.9.1). To assess the binding affinity between biomarkers and drugs, the drug most strongly correlated with the biomarkers was selected (CTD as an option). The three-dimensional structure of the drug was retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), and the protein structure of the biomarker was obtained from the PDB database (https://www.uniprot.org/). Molecular docking was then performed using the CB-Dock database. A docking score < -5 kcal/mol indicated a high binding affinity between the drug and the biomarker-encoded protein, confirming the strong interaction between the drug and its target.


### Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)
  The levels of these biomarkers were validated via RT-qPCR. A total of 5 pairs of MIRI22 and control samples were obtained from the Department of Cardiology at The First Hospital of Nanchang, following strict sampling protocols. Inclusion criteria for participants included a confirmed diagnosis of MIRI based on clinical symptoms, ECG findings, and cardiac biomarker levels, with ages between 40 and 70 years and no history of other significant cardiovascular diseases. Exclusion criteria included chronic infections, autoimmune diseases, or malignancies. Blood samples were collected in ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA) tubes after an overnight fast in the morning, centrifuged at 1,500×g for 10 min at 4 °C to separate plasma, and stored at -80 °C until analysis. Tissue samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen within 30 min of collection and stored at -80 °C. All participants provided written informed consent. This research protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanchang First Hospital (Approval Number: gx-00018), and the study was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant ethical guidelines. Total RNA was extracted from 10 samples using TRIzol (Ambion, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription into cDNA was performed using the SweScript-First-strand-cDNA-synthesis-kit (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). RT-qPCR was conducted with the 2xUniversal Blue SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Primer sequences used for PCR are provided in Supplementary Table S1, with GAPDH serving as the internal reference gene. The qPCR assay was performed on a CFX Connect Real-time Quantitative Fluorescence PCR Instrument (Bio-Rad, USA), with the following conditions: pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 1 min, denaturation at 95 °C for 20 s, annealing at 55 °C for 20 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s, repeated for 40 cycles. Relative mRNA quantification was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCT method.


### ScRNA-seq data analysis
  The Seurat package (v 5.1.0)23 was used to process the scRNA-seq data. Quality control was first performed to filter out low-quality cells, excluding those with fewer than 300 genes or genes detected in fewer than three cells. The number of genes detected per cell (nFeature) was set to range between 320 and 3000. Cells with a mitochondrial gene proportion (percent.Mt) greater than 5% were removed, and the nCount_RNA (sum of expression levels of all genes detected per cell) was limited to 25,000. The FindVariableFeatures function was then applied to identify the top 2,000 genes exhibiting the highest expression variability as highly variable genes. Multi-sample integration was performed using IntegrateData, followed by principal component analysis (PCA) based on the overall gene expression in each cell. The variance percentage represented by each principal component was assessed, and a PCA elbow plot was generated. Principal components preceding the elbow point were selected for further analysis.

  Unsupervised clustering analysis was conducted on the filtered cells using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions in Seurat with a resolution of 0.2. Cell clustering maps generated by the tSNE method were used to define distinct cell clusters. DEGs within these clusters were identified using the FindAllMarkers function (thresholds: logfc.threshold = 0.25, min.pct = 0.25, only.pos = TRUE). Mouse heart-related cluster cells and corresponding markers were downloaded from the CellMarker 2.0 database (http://117.50.127.228/CellMarker/) for annotating the cell clusters, assigning them to different cell types. The results were visualized through tSNE, and the distribution of biomarkers across the annotated cells was presented.


### Statistical analysis
  Bioinformatics analyses were performed using R (v 4.2.2). The Wilcoxon test was employed to assess differences between groups (P < 0.05). The ggplot2 package (v 2.6.4.3) was used to generate volcano plots and box plots, while the ComplexHeatmap package (v 2.15.1) was used to create heatmaps. Venn diagrams were visualized using the ggvenn package (v 0.1.9).


## Results

### Acquisition of candidate genes and investigation into their biological functions
  In the GSE160516 dataset, a total of 1,931 DEGs were identified, with 1,284 genes upregulated and 647 genes downregulated in the IR24h group (Fig. 1A-B). By intersecting the DEGs with 295 MRGs, 88 candidate genes were identified (Fig. 1C). Functional enrichment analysis indicated that these candidate genes were strongly associated with 2,266 GO terms and 184 KEGG pathways. Notably, GO terms included “macrophage activation” (BP), “membrane raft” (CC), and “cytokine receptor binding” (MF) (Fig. 1D, Supplementary Table S2). KEGG pathways primarily involved “cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction” and “osteoclast differentiation” (Fig. 1E, Supplementary Table S3).

  Fig. 1Candidate gene acquisition and functional research. (A) Differential gene volcano plot; (B,C) differential co expressed genes in GSE160516 dataset; (D) Differential gene GO analysis; (E) Differential gene KEGG analysis.


### Identification of Tlr7, Tlr1, Tlr6, and Il33 as biomarkers
  Further investigation of the candidate genes’ interactions was conducted by constructing a PPI network (Fig. 2A). Additionally, 12 candidate biomarkers—Myd88, Tlr7, Tlr1, Ccl3, Casp1, Il33, Ccr2, Ccr5, Tlr6, Aif1, Nlrp3, and Lgals3—were screened using four algorithms (Fig. 2B-F). Correlation analysis showed significant associations between the candidate biomarkers (|cor| > 0.30, P < 0.05) (Fig. 2G). In the GSE160516 dataset, Myd88, Tlr7, Tlr1, Ccl3, Casp1, Il33, Ccr2, Tlr6, Aif1, Nlrp3, and Lgals3 were all significantly upregulated in the IR24h group compared to the control group (P < 0.05). In the GSE225105 dataset, Tlr7, Tlr1, Tlr6, and Il33 were similarly upregulated in the IR24h group, showing a consistent expression trend with the biomarkers in the GSE160516 dataset. These four genes were thus identified as potential biomarkers (Fig. 2H-I). The expression of the 12 biomarkers varied at different time points. Among them, Tlr7 exhibited the highest expression at 72 h, followed by 6 h and 24 h. Tlr1 showed the highest expression at 72 h, then 24 h, and 6 h. Tlr6 expression gradually increased from 6 h to 72 h, while Il33 had the highest expression at 6 h, followed by 72 h and 24 h (Supplementary Figure S1). Friends analysis revealed that the similarity scores of Tlr7, Tlr1, Tlr6, and Il33 were all greater than 0.5, indicating a strong functional similarity between these biomarkers (Fig. 2J). A total of 20 genes associated with the functions of these biomarkers were predicted using the GeneMANIA database, which linked them to pathways such as interleukin-8 production and leukocyte activation, both involved in inflammatory responses. These results suggest that the biomarkers play a significant role in inflammation and disease progression (Fig. 2K).

(A) PPI protein mapping of candidate genes; (B–F) Selecting of candidate biomarkers Myd88, Tlr7, Tlr1, Ccl3, Casp1, Il33, Ccr2, Ccr5, Tlr6, Aif1, Nlrp3, and Lgals3; (G) Correlation analysis of candidate biomarkers; (H–I) Screening of candidate genes Tlr7, Tlr1, Tlr6, and Il33; J: Friends analyzes Tlr7, Tlr1, Tlr6, and Il33; K: GeneMANIA database predicts 20 genes related to biomarker function.


### Explored biomarker enrichment pathways and their associations with immune cell infiltration
  GSEA enrichment analysis indicated that Tlr7, Tlr1, Tlr6, and Il33 were associated with 102, 100, 90, and 81 biological pathways, respectively (Fig. 3A-D, Supplementary Tables S4-7). Specifically, Tlr7 was mainly linked to “basal transcription factors” and “ribosome,” while Tlr1 was primarily associated with the “ribosome.” Tlr6 was predominantly connected to “oxidative phosphorylation,” and Il33 showed a similar association with “oxidative phosphorylation.” Additionally, “ribosome,” “oxidative phosphorylation,” and “spliceosome” were common pathways enriched for all four biomarkers. Furthermore, stacked plots demonstrated the infiltration abundance of 28 immune cell types in both the IR24h and control groups (Fig. 3E). Significant differences were observed in the abundance of 25 immune cell types between the IR24h and control groups, including macrophages, M1 macrophages, and M2 macrophages (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3F). Correlation analysis revealed significant positive correlations between Tlr7, Tlr1, and Tlr6 with macrophages, M1 macrophages, and M2 macrophages (|cor| > 0.3, P < 0.05) (Fig. 3G). These results suggest that immune cells may interact with biomarkers, contributing to disease progression.

  Fig. 3Explored biomarker pathways & immune cell infiltration associations. (A–D) GSEA enrichment analysis of candidate genes Tlr7, Tlr1, Tlr6, and Il33; (E) Infiltration abundance of 28 immune cells in the IR24h and control groups; (F) Differences in the abundance of 25 immune cells between the IR24h and control groups; (G) Correlation analysis of Tlr7, Tlr1, and Tlr6 with macrophages, M1 macrophages, and M2 macrophages.


### Molecular regulatory networks probe regulatory mechanisms of biomarkers
  A total of 7 TFs and 61 miRNAs targeting the biomarkers were predicted. For instance, Tlr7 was regulated by both miR-15a-5p and MYC, while Il33 was regulated by mmu-miR-15a-5p and TBP. Additionally, Il33 was also regulated by mmu-let-7c-5p and CHD1 (Fig. 4A). The biomarkers were all associated with inflammation, necrosis, weight loss, prenatal exposure delayed effects, and chemical/drug-induced liver injury (Fig. 4B), suggesting their significant role in the MIRI process.

  Fig. 4Probe biomarker regulatory mechanisms in mol. networks. (A) The regulatory network of TF targeting biomarkers and 61 miRNAs; (B) The regulatory role of biomarkers.


### Drug prediction and molecular Docking suggested biomarkers as potential therapeutic targets for MIRI
  Using the DGIdb database, 24 drugs were predicted to target these biomarkers. Of these, 19 were linked to Tlr7, and 5 were correlated with Il33 (Fig. 5A). Acetaminophen, related to Il33, was also identified in the CTD database as the highest-rated drug. Molecular docking analysis revealed that the protein encoded by Tlr7 formed a bond with IMIQUIMOD through hydrogen bonding, exhibiting a binding energy of -5.7 kcal/mol. Similarly, Il33 interacted with Acetaminophen via hydrogen bonding, with a binding energy of -6.3 kcal/mol. Generally, lower binding energy indicates stronger stability in drug-protein binding, confirming IMIQUIMOD and Acetaminophen as significant therapeutic options for MIRI (Fig. 5B-C, Supplementary Table S8).

  Fig. 5Drug pred. & mol. docking suggest biomarkers as MIRI therapeutic targets. (A) DGIdb database predicts candidate gene drug associations; (B) Tlr7 and Il33 molecular docking results.


### Analysis of biomarker levels
  RT-qPCR analysis indicated that Tlr7, Tlr1, and Tlr6 were significantly upregulated in the MIRI group, consistent with bioinformatics results. However, Il33 showed no significant difference between the two groups (Fig. 6A-D).

  Fig. 6RT-qPCR detection of biomarkers.A-D: Statistical analysis results of Tlr7, Tlr1, Tlr6, and Il33.


### scRNA-seq results
  Initial scRNA-seq data contained 13,073 genes and 1,424 cells, but after quality control, 11,168 cells and 13,073 genes were retained (Supplementary Figure S2A-B). A total of 2,000 highly variable genes were identified (Fig. 7A), and the top 20 principal components were selected for further analysis (Fig. 7B-C, Supplementary Figure S3). Unsupervised clustering analysis with a resolution of 0.2 identified 6 distinct cell clusters (Fig. 7D), which were annotated into 5 major cell types based on marker gene expression: pericytes, M2 macrophages, M1 macrophages, endothelial cells, and cardiomyocytes (Fig. 7E-F). Notably, high expression of Tlr7 and Tlr1 was observed specifically in M2 macrophages, with Tlr1 showing high expression in the IR group of M2 macrophages (Fig. 7G, Supplementary Figure S4).

## Discussion
  MIRI is a complex pathophysiological process that frequently arises during myocardial infarction treatment, resulting in significant tissue damage and dysfunction24. MRGs are integral in modulating immune responses and tissue repair, positioning them as key targets for understanding and treating MIRI25. This study identified macrophage-associated biomarkers in MIRI and explored their underlying molecular mechanisms. Through the integration of transcriptomic data with MRGs, four biomarkers—Tlr7, Tlr1, Tlr6, and Il33—were identified. Enrichment analysis highlighted their involvement in essential biological pathways, including “ribosome” and “oxidative phosphorylation“26. Molecular regulatory networks were constructed to elucidate interactions between these biomarkers and other cellular components, complemented by immune infiltration analysis to assess their effects on the immune microenvironment. Molecular docking studies further indicated potential therapeutic targets, such as IMIQUIMOD and Acetaminophen, for MIRI treatment. These findings provide valuable insights into the pathogenesis of MIRI and suggest promising avenues for the development of diagnostic markers and therapeutic strategies.

  This study identified four macrophage-associated biomarkers in MIRI—Tlr7, Tlr1, Tlr6, and Il33. Tlr7, a pattern recognition receptor, plays a critical role in immune response by detecting single-stranded RNA, particularly from viruses like influenza and coronaviruses27. While extensively studied in viral infections, its involvement in MIRI has not been previously reported. Our findings suggest that Tlr7 may contribute to the inflammatory response following reperfusion, possibly by activating macrophages and other immune cells28. Tlr1, another Toll-like receptor family member, forms heterodimers with Tlr2 and Tlr6 to recognize pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) from bacteria and other microbes29. Although Tlr1 is known to be involved in various infectious diseases, its role in MIRI remains underexplored. Our results suggest that Tlr1 may be upregulated in response to ischemic stress, driving macrophage activation and subsequent tissue damage30. Tlr6, which also forms heterodimers with Tlr2, detects bacterial components and triggers immune responses31. Similar to Tlr1, Tlr6’s role in MIRI has not been extensively studied. Our findings suggest that Tlr6 may play a critical role in the inflammatory cascade following reperfusion, likely by enhancing macrophage activation and cytokine production32. Il33, a member of the IL-1 cytokine family, acts as an “alarmin,” released during cellular damage or stress33,34. Although Il33 is involved in various inflammatory and allergic diseases, its role in MIRI has been less explored. Our study highlights Il33 as a potential biomarker for MIRI, suggesting its contribution to immune modulation and tissue repair following ischemia-reperfusion injury35. These biomarkers exhibit dynamic expression patterns at different MIRI time points. Tlr7 and Tlr1 reach peak expression at 72 h, indicating their involvement in initiating early immune responses36. Tlr6 expression progressively increases from 6 to 72 h, while Il33 shows high expression in the acute phase at 6 h, remaining elevated at 72 h. This suggests a reparative function for Il33 and a synergistic, time-lagged effect with the Tlr family. These findings lay the groundwork for staged MIRI therapy: inhibiting Tlr7/Tlr1 early to block inflammation, regulating Tlr6 in the chronic phase to enhance repair, and balancing Il33’s roles at different stages36. Future studies focusing on dynamic gene expression, model development, and interaction analysis could advance precision medicine strategies for MIRI.

  Functional enrichment analysis revealed that the biomarkers Tlr7, Tlr1, Tlr6, and Il33 are associated with several biological pathways, including “ribosome,” “oxidative phosphorylation,” and “spliceosome.” The “ribosome” pathway, essential for protein synthesis, is implicated in various cellular processes and diseases. Previous studies suggest that disruptions in ribosome function can lead to altered protein expression and cellular responses, potentially contributing to the pathophysiology of MIRI37. Our findings reinforce this idea, suggesting that dysregulation of ribosome-related processes may significantly influence the development and progression of MIRI. The “oxidative phosphorylation” pathway, integral to energy production, is also linked to MIRI. Oxidative stress, a major factor in ischemia-reperfusion injury, results in the generation of ROS that damage cellular components38. Our study suggests that Tlr6 and Il33 may modulate oxidative phosphorylation, potentially influencing the balance between ROS production and antioxidant defense mechanisms. Targeting these pathways could reduce oxidative damage and promote tissue recovery, offering therapeutic potential for MIRI management39. Immune cell infiltration is critical in MIRI pathogenesis, with significant differences observed in the infiltration levels of various immune cells, especially macrophages, between MIRI and control samples. Macrophages, key players in the inflammatory response, exist in distinct polarization states (M1 and M2), which influence tissue damage and repair9. Our findings demonstrate that Tlr7, Tlr1, and Tlr6 are positively correlated with macrophage infiltration, indicating that these biomarkers may modulate macrophage activity and contribute to the inflammatory environment in MIRI40. This correlation emphasizes the central role of immune responses in MIRI. Understanding the interactions between biomarkers and immune cells could provide deeper insights into the mechanisms driving tissue damage and repair, potentially facilitating the development of targeted therapies that modulate immune responses and improve outcomes for patients with MIRI41. Notably, RT-qPCR analysis revealed that Tlr7, Tlr1, and Tlr6 were significantly upregulated in the MIRI group compared to the control group, with statistical significance (P < 0.05). This suggests that these biomarkers play critical roles in MIRI pathogenesis. However, while Il33 also showed a trend of upregulation in the MIRI group, the difference in its expression did not reach statistical significance. This expression pattern may reflect the complexity of the immune response in MIRI, highlighting the diverse mechanisms by which different biomarkers contribute to inflammation and tissue damage.

  This study predicted seven TFs and 61 miRNAs targeting MIRI-associated biomarkers, creating a complex molecular regulatory network. For Tlr7, the TF MYC activates transcription by binding to its gene promoter region, amplifying the inflammatory response. Conversely, miR-15a-5p post-transcriptionally represses Tlr7 expression by targeting its mRNA 3’-UTR, thereby counteracting excessive inflammation. In MIRI, this balance is disrupted, resulting in Tlr7 overexpression and exacerbated myocardial injury42. For Il33, the TF TBP drives transcription by participating in the initiation complex assembly. Mmu-miR-15a-5p negatively regulates Il33 expression, preventing excessive inflammatory signaling. CHD1 and mmu-let-7c-5p fine-tune Il33 expression via chromatin remodeling and mRNA degradation, respectively, balancing inflammation and repair43. These findings enhance our understanding of MIRI pathogenesis and suggest new therapeutic targets, such as intervening in MYC/miR-15a-5p regulation of Tlr7 or developing Il33-targeted therapies, providing both mechanistic insights and clinical value.

  In conclusion, this study successfully identified four macrophage-related biomarkers—Tlr7, Tlr1, Tlr6, and Il33—associated with MIRI using bioinformatics approaches. The Tlr and Il33 genes have significant clinical applications in MIRI. For early diagnosis, these biomarkers can be detected using various molecular techniques, such as qRT-PCR, immunohistochemistry, and ELISA, with blood or tissue samples. Cellular-level methods like flow cytometry and single-cell sequencing enable early detection of MIRI and analysis of cellular subgroup expression. Additionally, combined analysis of multiple biomarkers (e.g., Tlr7 + Il33) can create prognostic models to assist in clinical stratification44. In terms of therapeutic interventions, targeting the Tlr pathway with inhibitors and the Il33/ST2 pathway with biologics offers novel anti-inflammatory and immune-modulatory strategies. Combining these approaches with local delivery via nanoparticle carriers enhances treatment precision and minimizes systemic toxicity. However, clinical translation faces several challenges, including the lack of standardized detection methods (with insufficient sensitivity and consistency across techniques), inter-individual heterogeneity leading to varied expression patterns, high costs associated with advanced technologies (such as single-cell sequencing and targeted drug development), and safety concerns (including risks of immune suppression and cross-species translation differences). Future research should focus on validating biomarker efficacy through large-scale studies and establishing standardized detection protocols45. The development of non-invasive detection methods and myocardial-targeted therapies, coupled with multi-omics data, will be crucial to advancing personalized medicine. Addressing these challenges will help overcome barriers and accelerate the translation of basic research into clinical practice. However, this study has some limitations. The small RT-qPCR sample size may have introduced bias, and additional samples should be collected in the future to validate the results. Moreover, as the study primarily relied on bioinformatics analysis without experimental validation, further experiments are needed to confirm the mechanisms and clinical relevance of the identified biomarkers. At present, RT-qPCR on myocardial tissue-level samples only reports the overall expression of the target gene and cannot pinpoint its cellular origin. In the future, we will sort distinct cardiac cell subpopulations by flow cytometry and perform RT-qPCR on each subset to directly characterize the gene’s expression profile in specific cell types.


## Conclusions
  This study identified four macrophage-related biomarkers—Tlr7, Tlr1, Tlr6, and Il33—that are associated with MIRI. Through an extensive bioinformatics analysis, including differential expression analysis, PPI network construction, functional enrichment, immune infiltration analysis, and molecular docking, the potential roles of these biomarkers in MIRI pathogenesis were elucidated. The findings suggest that these biomarkers are involved in key biological pathways such as “ribosome” and “oxidative phosphorylation” and may serve as targets for developing novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Predicted drugs, IMIQUIMOD and Acetaminophen, show promise as therapeutic agents for MIRI. Although further experimental validation is required, this study offers valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms of MIRI and presents promising avenues for future research and clinical applications.
