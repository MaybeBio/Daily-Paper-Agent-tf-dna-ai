# Decoding cross-talk between Mpox and HIV: insights from transcriptomics and network-based analyses


## Background
  At present, the Monkeypox virus (MPXV) outbreak has become a significant public health concern, and its severity is increasing based on different clades globally, which, combined, are leading to severe complications. However, it was observed that MPXV infection was highly expressed in individuals with Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV), and the mechanism of cross-talk has not been explored.


## Methods
  This study employed a network-based methodology to examine the role of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in HIV and monkeypox (Mpox) infections. The commonly shared DEGs in Mpox and HIV were examined, followed by their functional enrichment, co-expression, transcriptional factor, potential correlation with disease, and promising drug-associated network. Additionally, the docking analysis was accomplished to gain the interaction site of the compound towards these target genes.


## Results
  The GEO datasets of Mpox and HIV were examined, wherein a total of 570 Mpox and 642 HIV DEGs were identified. Among them, 41 DEGs were common, and functional enrichment and pathway analysis revealed their involvement in vital biological functions and pathways. Further, the PPI and co-expression analysis of these genes was conducted, which revealed 8 key genes or hub genes, which included SOCS3, NFKBIA, IFIT1, CCNB2, PLK3, PTGS1, ZFP36L2, and RGS16. Moreover, transcription factors (TFs) and miRNAs of the hub genes were also examined, which offered new insight into potential therapeutic targets. The gene-disease network also revealed several other diseases linked to the common and hub genes. Subsequently, the docking analysis revealed that the selected compound actively bound to the target genes and could be a potential lead to combat this.


## Conclusions
  This study unveils the shared mechanism between HIV and Mpox based on the hub genes perspective, offering new insights into co-infection and highlighting potential possibilities for simultaneous therapeutic intervention in both diseases.

## Introduction
  The emerging and re-emerging pathogenies present a challenging situation for human health worldwide [1]. Mpox has been declared a public health issue due to its recent outbreak caused by the MPXV virus. The Mpox is a re-emerged virus first reported in 1970 [2, 3]. MPXV is a 200 kb long double-stranded DNA virus belonging to the Poxviridae family and is under the Orthopoxvirus genus [4–6]. Initially, infections were reported mostly in Africa; however, as per the 56th situation report (https://www.who.int/publications/m; Accessed on September 10, 2025), it is reported that all clades were continuing to circulate in several countries. Moreover, the WHO (World Health Organisation) Global Mpox Trends (https://worldhealthorg.shinyapps.io/mpx_global/#sec-down; Accessed on September 10, 2025).

  shows a total of 1,58,547 laboratory confirmed cases, followed by the 410 confirmed deaths, and involved reporting 138 countries globally. This virus shows fever, rash, etc., symptoms and can spread from person to person [7]. Compared to previous epidemics, the recent outbreaks revealed a high occurrence due to close and sexual contact, resulting in predominantly men who have sex with men. Surprisingly, approximately 28–47% of confirmed cases are also living with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) [8]. The Mpox also leads to potential complications in sexually transmitted infections (STIs), syphilis, encephalitis, and Mycoplasma genitalium through co-infection cases as well [8–12]. Apart from the STIs, it has also been noted that people infected with Mpox are known to face multiple neurological disorders, such as myalgia, seizures, encephalitis, etc [13]. A systematic review conducted by Deb et al. reviewed a total of 18 cases, predominantly involving male patients, wherein the Mpox-associated neurological disorder was observed. Reported diagnoses included acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (50.0%), encephalitis/meningoencephalitis (38.9%), isolated transverse myelitis (5.6%), and transverse myelitis with encephalitis (5.6%), supporting the association of Mpox with neurological disorders [14]. The connection of this infection with the majority was found with the STDs, and Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome (AIDS) is one of them, caused by HIV infection, which deteriorates the immune system by depleting CD4 + T cell counts. This reduction in immune response activity leads to the progression of HIV infection, increasing susceptibility to other infections, and resulting in higher mortality [15–17]. One such case is the correlation between HIV infection and neurological complications. Jadav et al. in their study, highlighted the infiltration of HIV in the central nervous system (CNS) through several mechanisms. The study also highlighted the role of viral proteins like gp120, Tat, and Vpr in oxidative stress, neurotoxicity, and other problems, thereby exploring the impact of HIV in the CNS and its influence on neurodegeneration [18]. Moreover, their HIV infection is merely associated with sexual contact, similar to Mpox, which shows the progression within the coinfection mechanism [15]. Mainly, the HIV-infected patient has lower CD4 + counts, which is helpful for the Mpox to immunocompromise the patient’s immune system and leads to widespread transmission followed by severe co-infection complications [19–21]. It has also been noted that patients who had high CD4 showed most of the Mpox infections with outcomes comparable to those without HIV [3, 22]. Apart from the complication, the study found a significant challenge behind the confection mechanism for the concern, as previously reported [8, 23], which indicates its severity will lead to high infection followed by mortality due to the interplay of cross-talk within the molecular mechanism of this pathogenesis and transmission process [3]. Moreover, due to HIV-related complications, the risk of hospitalisation increases in Mpox infections [24] and coinfected leads to higher mortality rates [25]. Subsequently, studies were performed to unveil the cross-talk mechanism [3, 26]. Despite the association with coinfection, the molecular machines were poorly explored, and currently, no proper therapeutics are available to reduce this infection completely. Unveiling the interplay mechanisms between these Mpox and HIV infections through the expressed gene could serve as potential therapeutic targets to combat and mitigate the co-infection of both pathogens, targeting common pathways. However, as the number of emerging cases increased frequently, this study aims to identify the intersection of the DEGs among the Mpox and HIV datasets. The shared genes were examined for their functional enrichment analysis, followed by their co-expression to explore the potential hub gene that could serve as a possible drug target. Furthermore, the possible drugs associated with these genes and gene correlation with the other vital diseases were also examined to provide potential therapeutic strategies.

### Collection of the target dataset
  To unveil the underlying mechanisms of cross-talk between the Mpox and HIV, the initial data were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (NCBI GEO) database [27]. Two microarray datasets containing disease and control data for HIV (GSE6740) and Mpox (GSE36854) were utilized. The HIV dataset belonging to the platform Affymetrix Human Genome U133A Array (GPL96 [HG-U133A]) consisted of individuals with acute, chronic progressive, and non-progressive infections with low or undetectable viral loads and uninfected individuals. Similarly, the Mpox dataset of platform Agilent-014850 Whole Human Genome Microarray 4 × 44 K G4112F (GPL4133) contained expression data from individuals with cowpox, monkeypox, vaccinia virus, and uninfected controls. Only transcriptional profiles from infected patients and uninfected controls were considered for further investigation, while other groups were excluded. The selected data contains the required well-defined annotations and is relevant to the study objective. Moreover, previous studies were successfully performed using these data [28, 29].


## Screening of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)
  The DEGs among the Mpox and HIV datasets were screened and analysed via Limma [30] and the GEOquery [31] preprocessorCore [32]and affy [33] packages available in R software, considering p-value < 0.05 and log Fold Change >0.5, as reported earlier [34]. For the HIV dataset, the celfile was downloaded from NCBI GEO, followed by the data normalization using the affy package, and finally the DEGs analysis using the limma package. Subsequently, for Mpox data, the GEOquery package in R was utilized to retrieve the data, and the preprocessorCore package was utilized for quantile normalization followed by DEGs using limma [35]. Moreover, the selected parameters will ensure the identification of biologically relevant genes and avoid the risk of over-filtering the relevant genes among the pre-defined sets of genes. Further, the identified DEGs were subjected to ggplots2 [36] and pheatmap [37, 38] to generate volcano plots and heatmap, respectively. Finally, the common DEGs among the Mpox and HIV datasets were screened via Venny 2.1.0 [39] and were further analyzed.


## Gene ontology and KEGG pathway analysis
  The identified common genes underwent functional enrichment analysis to unveil their vital biological roles and possible pathways. The GO and KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) pathway analysis was accomplished via the SRplot considering the default parameters, and the results were demonstrated via dot plots and bar plots [40]. SRplot functions by integrating the inbuilt modules of clusterProfiler and pathview, which facilitate pathway enrichment, assessment, and visualization [41–43].


## Identification of hub genes via network construction
  The possible interaction among the genes was achieved via STRING database [44] following their confidence score. This approach utilized common DEGs derived from both HIV and Mpox infections to predict interactions among proteins. Following the network construction, visualization and analysis were computed via Cytoscape 3.10.2 [45] along with its plug-in, Cytohubba [46]. Further, to identify key genes based on various topological criteria (seven: Closeness, Degree, EPC, MCC, MNC, Radiality, and Stress) were employed similar as previously reported [3] considering their average score.


## Co-expression analysis of the hub genes
  The co-expression network was accomplished via GeneMANIA [47] to unveil the relationship among the identified hub genes and their relationship with HIV and Mpox pathogenesis following the default parameters. GeneMANIA is useful for computing the functional interactions between subject’ genes and the associated genes involved in the same disease. The co-expression network revealed various functional relationships, including physical interactions, co-expression patterns, co-localization, pathways, etc [48]., to unveil the genes and their activity. Moreover, the co-expression investigation of the HIV and Mpox infection genes will lead to an understanding of how these genes contribute to each other and their activity.


### Identification of transcription factors (TF) and MiRNAs associated with common DEGs
  Transcription factors (TFs) that recognize and bind to specific DNA sequences play a key role in transcription, gene expression, and many more [49]. In viral infections such as HIV and Mpox, TFs can significantly influence the host’s immune response and viral replication mechanisms. Here, Enrichr [50] provides the list of TFs and miRNA according to the given gene list. To investigate the involvement of TFs in common DEGs, the Enrichr Transcription Factor Protein-Protein Interactions (PPIs) library was employed to identify TFs associated with these DEGs and hub genes, considering the default parameters. Further, Cytoscape 3.10.2 [45] was employed to analyse and visualise the TF-gene interaction. Further, the target gene and microRNA (miRNA) interactions were analyzed via the MiRTarBase [51], which provides experimentally validated miRNA-target interactions (MTIs) [49]. Moreover, relevant miRNAs were identified in the Enrichr miRTarBase 2017 library, and their network was analysed via Cytoscape software version 3.10.2 [45].


## Identification of potential drugs towards the hub genes
  The potent drug towards the hub genes was investigated via Enrichr and the Drug Signatures Database (DSigDB), considering the default parameters. The hub genes were subjected to Enrichr, incorporating various resources to analyze the gene set [52]. Drug Signatures Database (DSigDB) is one of the resources that can be accessed via Enrichr in the Drug/Disease function and helps identify potential drugs that target the key hub genes [53].


## Identification of associated disease with hub genes
  To elucidate the potential health implication of the common DEGs and hub genes, DisGeNET under the Diseases/Drugs function of the Enrichr platform was utilized considering the default parameters. DisGeNET contains comprehensive information and their association with various human diseases, which aided in the investigation of gene-disease relationships and enabled the identification of disease and various other health conditions linked with the hub genes [49].


### Molecular docking
  The identified compounds were further utilised for the molecular docking analysis to examine their interaction towards the fusion key core gene target. The target structures were retrieved from the PDB database [54] and the AlphaFold database [55]. These structures were further optimised and prepared using the Protein Preparation Wizard, and a grid box was generated with the Receptor Grid Generation module, considering the binding site identified by SiteMap and the available cocrystal ligands in the Schrödinger Suite [56–58]. Moreover, the identified compound was collected from the PubChem database in sdf format [59]. Furthermore, these collected compounds were prepared via the LigPrep module available in Schrödinger Suite [56, 60]. Following this, the docking analysis was accomplished via the Glide module available in Schrödinger Suite, considering the SP method [61, 62].


## Results

### Collection of the target dataset
  The collected dataset GSE6740 for HIV resulted in a total of 40 samples comprising transcriptional profiles of CD4 + and CD8 + T cells from infected patients having different stages of infection and uninfected control group. Further A total 10 sample datasets of the patients with acute HIV infection (GSM154936, GSM155179, GSM155180, GSM155181, GSM155182, GSM155183, GSM155184, GSM155185, GSM155186, GSM155187) and 10 sample datasets of uninfected patients or control (GSM155228, GSM155229, GSM155230, GSM155232, GSM155233, GSM155234, GSM155235, GSM155236, GSM155237, GSM155238) were retrieved. Similarly, the collected dataset GSE36854 for Mpox resulted in a total of 8 samples comprising Cowpox, vaccinia, Mpox viruses, and normal or uninfected samples. Among them, 4 samples having Mpox-infected samples (GSM903058, GSM903062) and normal or uninfected samples (GSM903055, GSM903059) were retrieved and processed further.


### Screening of DEGs in HIV and Mpox
  The DEGs in the HIV and Mpox datasets were analyzed via Limma and the GEOquery package (Fig. 2). In the HIV dataset (GSE6740), the investigation revealed a total of 642 DEGs (Upregulated: 345 and Downregulated: 267), whereas in the Mpox datasets (GSE36854), 570 DEGs (Upregulated: 347 and Downregulated: 223). Further, the distribution of the top 50 DEGs from both datasets, GSE6740 (Fig. 2A) and GSE36854 (Fig. 2B) were visualized via a heatmap. Moreover, a volcano plot of upregulated and downregulated significant genes was analyzed via R software using the ggplot2 package, wherein the upregulated genes were denoted with red dots, and blue dots represented downregulated genes (Fig. 2C; HIV and Fig. 2D; Mpox) [36, 63]. Moreover, the log fold value of the up- and down-regulated genes in both samples, along with gene symbols, were listed in the Supplementary Tables 1–4.

GO analysis typically includes three main categories that describe gene functions: biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). The analysis revealed significantly enriched terms, with 191 terms in BP (Supplementary Table 6), 20 in CC (Supplementary Table 7), and 21 in MF (Supplementary Table 8). Additionally, the enriched genes in terms of BP, CC, MF and KEGG plots were obtained through the SR plot, which contained the combined inbuilt modules of clusterProfiler and pathview [40, 43].

The CC showed enrichment in P-body, mRNA cap-binding complex, npBAF complex, COPI vesicle coat, RNA cap-binding complex, Fanconi anaemia nuclear complex, and DNA helicase complex (Fig. 3B). However, the MF showed its involvement in mRNA 3’-UTR AU-rich region binding, AU-rich element binding, protein kinase regulator activity, kinase regulator activity, acting on NAD(P)H, heme protein as acceptor and inositol phosphate phosphatase activity (Fig. 3C).

### Identification of hub genes via network construction
  A set of 41 common DEGs with HIV and Mpox, with a combined score of >1.50 and their PPI, was generated using the STRING database. This network comprised 41 nodes and 106 edges, with an expected number of edges calculated at 57, resulting in a PPI enrichment value of 3.74e-9 (Fig. 4A). The network underwent further analysis and visualization using Cytoscape software, which revealed a refined PPI having 37 nodes and 531 edges (Fig. 4B). To identify the most significant hub genes, seven topological criteria were applied as previously reported [3]. By analyzing the intersection of the top 15 genes (Supplementary Table 10) from each criterion through a Venn diagram (Fig. 4C), eight common hub genes were identified, i.e., SOCS3, NFKBIA, IFIT1, CCNB2, PLK3, PTGS1, ZFP36L2, RGS16 (Table 1), which significantly relevant to the biological process of the infection of the individual infection, which may leads to the critical pathogenesis of co-infection. Moreover, the key hub genes were classified based on the up and downregulated activity in Table 1. These genes were subsequently visualized within Cytoscape software, which involved 8 nodes and 16 edges, respectively (Fig. 4D).

## Discussion
  In recent years, many research studies have revealed possible connections between various diseases, creating a vacuum for possible investigations [3, 66, 67]. The re-emergence of Mpox poses a global challenge, leading to multiple outbreaks. Observations from these outbreaks indicate that many Mpox cases occurred among individuals with HIV, suggesting a potential connection between the two infections [67, 68]. Moreover, the Mpox and HIV infection are known to significantly affect the CNS, with some individuals developing neurological complications as a result of the infection [69, 70]. With little to no knowledge about co-infection and its molecular gene aspects, the relationship between these two illnesses is, nevertheless, little investigated. In this investigation, the common DEGs among HIV and Mpox were identified and were further explored to unveil the cross-talk mechanisms. A total 41 common genes among the subjected dataset were identified in this investigation. Moreover, a similar study by Li et al. reported 25 [26] and Cai et al. 59 genes [3] common genes. The functional enrichment analysis revealed various associated biological processes and pathways of those common genes. Further, PPI was constructed, which revealed the top 8 hub genes that had a strong relation with both HIV and Mpox, providing a new perspective on the coinfection between HIV and Mpox. Moreover, a similar study by Li et al. reported 7 [26], and Cai et al. 5 [3] key genes. The investigation demonstrated that among the potential common genes, 8 hub genes (SOCS3, NFKBIA, IFIT1, CCNB2, PLK3, PTGS1, ZFP36L2, RGS16) were found to be more vital in the interplay interaction within the Mpox and HIV screened in this study. Moreover, to unveil these interplay mechanisms, Suppressor of Cytokine Signaling 3 (SOCS3) is a primary negative regulator of the cytokine signalling pathway (JAK-STAT), which suppresses the immune responses in case of HIV-infected individuals SOCS3 was seen to be highly expressed, which might lead to compromise of the immune system, allowing the progression of HIV infection [71, 72]. Also, a study found that in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), SOCS3 downregulation may contribute to prolonged inflammation, immune dysregulation, and lung tissue damage [73]. Moreover, the study by Devalraju et al. highlights that SOCS3 overexpression in HIV-TB co-infections inhibits pro-inflammatory cytokine signalling, impairing immunological responses and T cell energy. This suggests that SOCS3 may be a promising target for treatment [74]. NFKBIA encodes for an inhibitor of the NF-κB (Nuclear Factor Kappa B) pathway, significantly regulating the immune response. NFKBIA’s control of NF-κB may impact the host’s reaction to HIV and poxviruses since both viruses rely on NF-κB pathways to weaken host immunity [75–77]. Moreover, the negative regulation of NFKBIA raises NF-κB activity and aids in the development of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) [78]. Interferon-Induced Protein with Tetratricopeptide Repeats 1 (IFIT1) is a part of the immune response system of the human body, which helps to inhibit the replication of viral RNA. However, in individuals with HIV infection, it has been noted that IFIT1 is upregulated and vital in stopping the replication of HIV (Nasr et al., 2017) and is also involved in the vaccine virus mechanism [79]. However, it was found that IFIT1 is a crucial gene in CNS-TB, with significant expression in the central nervous system, and plays a part in developing tuberculous granulomas by promoting the inflammatory response and IFN-γ-mediated signaling [80]. CCNB2 (Cyclin B2) is an essential gene that encodes for CDK1 activation when the cell cycle is regulated, especially when the G2 phase ends and mitosis begins. CCNB2 dysregulation in HIV and Mpox may have an impact on replication and may lend support to HIV persistence. Replication conditions are optimized by viruses such as MPXV by manipulating host cell cycle regulators [81, 82]. Moreover, the over-expressed gene CCNB2 was over-regulated connecting both diseases, with its expression linked to poor survival in pancreatic cancer (PC) [83]. Polo-like Kinase 3 (PLK3) controls the cell cycle, DNA damage, and signalling pathways triggered by stress. Controlling apoptosis and cellular stress responses during viral infection can impact immune-mediated clearance and is linked to both HIV and monkeypox. By regulating the cell cycle and apoptosis in MPXV, PLK3 may impact replication and pathogenesis [84, 85]. However, Zhu et al. reported that PLK3 is linked to aggressive gliomas, affecting tumour growth, T-cell immunity, and neutrophil attraction, and highlights that blocking PLK3 could improve glioma treatment, especially with immunotherapy [86]. The enzyme Prostaglandin-Endoperoxide Synthase 1 (PTGS1), commonly referred to as COX-1, is involved in the production of prostaglandins, which can regulate inflammation and preserve physiological processes. It might influence long-term inflammation in HIV and regulate inflammation in MPXV [87, 88]. Moreover, the COX1 expression in cervical biopsies of HIV/HPV coinfected women was significantly lower than in controls, suggesting a potential role in immune suppression and cervical cancer progression [89]. Zinc Finger Protein 36 Like 2 (ZFP36L2) is an RNA-binding protein that controls the stability of mRNA and is essential for the growth of immune cells and inflammatory reactions. Similar to its effects on inflammation in other viral infections, it may influence the immune response in HIV and the host response to monkeypox by regulating the breakdown of pro-inflammatory cytokines [90–92]. A GTPase-activating protein called RGS16 controls GPCR signalling, modulating inflammation and immunological responses. It may affect HIV’s ability to enter cells by altering chemokine receptor signalling. Further research is necessary; however, RGS16 may influence how the immune system reacts to viral infections in monkeypox [93–95]. Based on these genes’ possible correlation and classification in the different mechanisms, hub genes regulate immunological responses, inflammation, and cell cycle processes and can lead to possible therapeutic targets. GO and KEGG analyses of common HIV and Mpox genes revealed their involvement in key biological processes and pathways, particularly RNA metabolism, which may regulate immune cells. These genes localize to critical cellular structures like the P-body and are associated with kinase regulation and RNA binding, essential for post-transcriptional control and signal transduction. They also contribute to immune response modulation and viral replication, as seen in pathways like hematopoietic cell lineage, Th1/Th2 differentiation, insulin resistance, and viral processes (e.g., Hepatitis C, Influenza A). Targeting these shared pathways could offer promising therapeutic strategies. The gene co-expression investigation revealed a remarkable gene relationship between the TFs and miRNA. The top 10 TFs (NFKB1, EMX2, STAT6, HOXD13, LMX1B, LYL, THAP11, HIF1A, CREB1, and PBX3) were selected according to their lowest P values and the top 10 miRNA (hsa-miR-30e-5p, hsa-miR-30d-5p, hsa-miR-30b-5p, hsa-miR-30c-5p, hsa-miR-30a-5p, hsa-miR-346, hsa-miR-4724-5p, hsa-miR-8062, hsa-miR-126-5p, hsa-miR-18a-5p) also selected according to the lowest P values. Since miRNAs post-transcriptionally regulate gene expression by degrading mRNA or blocking translation, TFs influence the expression of target genes, and their research in common DEGs is essential to examine mechanisms that underlie variations in gene expression in illnesses and identify prospective targets for therapeutic intervention [96]. Molecular docking of the key common genes with selected drug molecules highlighted promising drug-gene interactions. Among them, Irinotecan hydrochloride showed binding activity against SOCS3 and ZFP36L2, Genistein against NFKBIA, PLK3, and PTGS1, Bepridil against IFIT1, Etoposide against CCNB2, and Ibuprofen against RGS16. Ibuprofen is a commonly used analgesic and is used during fever, which also possesses anti-inflammatory properties [97]. Yadav et al. reported that severe pain associated with Mpox infection is often managed with ibuprofen, making it an effective option for symptomatic relief [98]. Genistein is another drug with anti-inflammatory and anti-cancer effects, and is also used in menopause [99]. Choi et al. explored the effect of genistein against oxidative stress and NF-kappaB activation. Their analysis highlighted that giensein has mild anti-oxidative properties for reducing oxidative stress and NFkappaB activation [100]. Etoposide is another drug used to stop DNA synthesis and exhibits anti-tumour activity [101]. The study by Sengar et al. developed a novel chemotherapy combining etoposide with three other drugs, cyclophosphamide, vinblastine, and prednisolone, commonly called CVEP, to treat HIV and showed promising results [102]. Moreover, Irinotecan hydrochloride is an FDA-approved anti-cancer drug that stops the rapid cell growth [103]. Bepridil is a calcium channel blocker that is generally used for hypertension and chest pain [104]. However, their action against the said genes or HIV and Mpox has not yet been explored, making it a potential novel drug for crosstalk treatment.

  Finally, this investigation offers valuable insights into co-infection by identifying important hub genes shared by HIV and Mpox that control the immune response, inflammation, and viral replication. The regulatory roles of miRNAs and transcription factors suggest potential targets for therapeutic intervention. To potentially develop new treatments for HIV-Mpox co-infections, future research should concentrate on verifying these targets and examining how therapeutic drugs, such as genistein and 8-azaguanine, could be employed to combat this co-infection based complication.


### Limitation and future scope
  At present, HIV and Mpox co-infection cases are leading to severe complications and are a public health concern. This study employed an integrated transcriptomic and network-based approach and found valuable insights into the co-infection mechanisms, followed by the key target and the associated drug based on their binding interaction and their docking score. Although the study demonstrated remarkable insights, it has certain limitations. The designed study and its findings rely on the limited dataset’s different tools, databases, and computational validation steps. Future steps should include the experimental validation of the identified cross-talk gene and its potential drug, followed by clinical trials to confirm the overall finding and its effectiveness in dealing with the co-infection mechanisms.


## Conclusion
  This study primarily focused on finding the common key gene between Mpox and HIV. As it has been noted that individuals with HIV were susceptible to contracting Mpox, this study revealed 8 hub genes (SOCS3, NFKBIA, IFIT1, CCNB2, PLK3, PTGS1, ZFP36L2, RGS16), key genes and their associated pathways and biological functions behind their interactive cross-talk mechanism which contributed collectively to infection susceptibility and progression, led to severe complications in the host. The contribution of these genes in the immune response modulation, as well as vital RNA metabolic processes, may aid as a potential biomarker for co-infection diagnosis, and their involvement in the biological pathways suggests that they could be a potential therapeutic target and help combat Mpox and HIV infection simultaneously. Furthermore, the identified promising drugs, based on their interactions and docking analysis, demonstrated significant activity and can serve as potential leads to inhibit these key genes and simultaneously combat this severe complication.
