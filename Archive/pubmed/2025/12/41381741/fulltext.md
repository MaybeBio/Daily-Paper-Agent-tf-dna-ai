# Decoding cell-class specific roles of non-coding variants in human retina


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-30821-9.


## Introduction
  Inherited retinal disorders (IRDs) are a heterogeneous collection of diseases, affecting over 2 million individuals worldwide1. To date, over 300 genes have been implicated in IRDs (https://RetNet.org/)2. Many of these genes are expressed in just a subset of retinal cell classes, shaping the genotype-phenotype correlation of each disorder within the cell classes most directly affected3–5. As many as half of all IRD cases cannot be explained by variants in protein-coding genes alone6. Well-studied disorders such as Blue Cone Monochromacy, Non-syndromic Congenital Retinal Non-Attachment, and Aniridia with Foveal Hypoplasia have demonstrated that non-coding genetic variants can cause retinal diseases7–9. Much more common retinal disorders like Age-Related Macular Degeneration have also been associated with non-coding variants10. In additional cases without explicit genetic diagnoses, other non-coding variants could be contributing to disease. It is therefore important to characterize non-coding variants to improve upon our understanding of the genetic mechanisms underlying retinal diseases with strong genetic components.

  The vast majority of the human genome is composed of non-coding regions. This landscape contains cis-regulatory elements (CREs) such as promoters, enhancers, silencers, insulators and boundary elements, all of which shape the regulation of gene expression in cell-type-specific manners11–14. Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS) implicate non-coding genomic regions and CREs in genetically complex retinal diseases such as Age-Related Macular Degeneration, Glaucoma, and Macular Telangiectasia type 212,15,16. Genetic variants within CREs have additionally been associated with altered retinal gene expression by expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) analyses17,18. Despite these analyses and associations, a lack of full non-coding characterization across complex tissues and limitations of existing tools contribute to a challenge in systematically interpreting individual variants within CREs, especially in the context of distinct cell classes.

  The function of CREs is determined through the complex interaction of DNA sequences and regulatory factor activity, in particular protein binding, such as that of transcription factors (TFs)19–21. These factors act together to regulate appropriate transcription and yield a complex epigenomic and expression profile for a given cell class21. DNA-protein interactions can be characterized by assays such as ATAC-seq—which identifies regions of genomic accessibility—and protein binding assays (ChIP-seq, CUT&RUN, CUT&Tag)22,23. These assays can be used individually or together to identify and characterize candidate CREs. These identified elements are extremely diverse in accessibility, binding profiles, and activity, and may act in cell class and developmentally specific manners24, as has been demonstrated previously in retinal contexts to have profound impacts on cell fate25–27. This diversity of function makes the interpretation and characterization of such elements particularly challenging. Recent advancements have improved analyses by introducing single cell-resolution characterization28. Additionally, efforts have been made to characterize regulatory elements active in the retina and in other tissues through computational methods16,29–31. However, in-depth and widespread characterization of non-coding elements remains a complex task. Resources that begin to tackle this challenge by providing concise summaries of regulatory DNA in distinct retinal cell classes are needed.

  Recently, machine learning based approaches have been trained with high-throughput -omics datasets to predict the inferred value or function of genetic sequences and variants, including on non-coding regulatory regions32–34. We have previously applied a gapped k-mer support vector machine (gkm-SVM/dSVM)-based approach to retinal candidate CREs identified through bulk-tissue assays for DNA accessibility35. This approach was capable of distinguishing key features within retinal CREs in a tissue-specific manner. However, as the retina is a heterogeneous tissue made up of numerous cell classes36, this model has limited applications in terms of disease modeling. In particular, in the case of less abundant cell populations in the retina, a bulk tissue-based approach would be unable to identify crucial sequences or differentiate between cell classes within the retina.

  In this study, we applied a gkm-SVM/dSVM approach trained with our retinal single nucleus ATAC-seq (snATAC-seq37 datasets38 and an additional RPE ATAC dataset39 to generate cell-class-aware models. We generated 18 models trained on high confidence peak calls. These models were determined to be 91–97% accurate and highly cell class specific. We additionally performed in silico saturation mutagenesis on holdout data to generate variant impact prediction (VIP) scores for 22,983 candidate CREs using deltaSVM. These VIP scores were able to distinguish the predicted impact in crucial sequences in a cell-class specific manner. To demonstrate functional relevance, we compared the predictions of our model against bulk and single cell Massively Parallel Reporter Assays (MPRAs), demonstrating a high degree of accuracy by AUROC in model VIP scores predicting variant impact. Further, this comparison demonstrated the power of our model to predict variant impacts across conserved sequence motifs. Lastly, we generated a series of predictions on retinal disease-associated GWAS hits including for AMD in cell classes of interest. This analysis will allow us to focus on relevant cell classes for IRDs and prioritize disease-associated variants in future analyses.


## Results

### Models built from SnATAC are accurate and specific
  Previous modeling from our group demonstrates the effectiveness of gapped k-mer Support Vector Machine (gkm-SVM) modeling on bulk ATAC-sequencing profiles of the human retina35. To create more granular predictions for unique cell classes of the retina, we utilized snATAC-seq data generated by our group38. These data were collected across three developmental timepoints and from adult human retinas, yielding 18 distinct clusters of developing and mature cell classes in the human retina (Fig. 1A). Additionally, we integrated ATAC-seq data from adult macular retinal pigment epithelium (RPE)39. The top 25,000 ATAC peaks scored by MACS2 from each of these clusters were used to train cell-class-specific gkm-SVM models. These top 25k peak sets were split such that 80% of the peaks were randomly selected for model training for each cell class. The remaining 20% of peaks were cross referenced against the outgroups from other cell classes and combined to create a universal test set (Fig. 1B). Positive training data was utilized together with negative training datasets of equal number of peaks of equal size with GC matching. The resulting models trained with gkm-SVM produced distinct models representative of high confidence putative regulatory regions.

(B) Model training paradigm for building gkm-SVM models from snATAC clusters in (A), and downstream analysis of variant sequences by deltaSVM. (C) Receiver Operator Characteristic (ROC) curves from five-fold cross validation of all models built from cell classes as defined in (A). (D) Model-to-model scoring of snATAC-defined CREs.   To demonstrate the accuracy of these models, the training data was used in five-fold cross validations of each model. These cross-validations were used to plot Receiver-Operator Characteristic (ROC) curves, mapping true and false positivity rates of each model (Fig. 1C). All models performed extremely well, with Area Under the Curve (AUROC) values above 0.90 for each (Figure S1). To visualize model specificity, we scored holdout test peak sets for each cell class by each cell-class-specific model (Fig. 1D). Comparing average scores for each peak set by model shows how models identify the most related sets of peaks, with self-recognizing-self as the highest scoring peak sets on average, as well as clustering of related cell classes. For instance, hierarchical clustering of cell class scoring by model gathers classes of rods, cones, and bipolar cells as well as precursors into related clusters, reflective of the similarity of accessible sequences and cis-regulation in each of these classes.

  Using each model, a set of all possible 11-mers was scored for downstream applications. This scored set of vocabulary terms highlights the features driving each model. By subsetting the top 1% of scored vocabulary, we assessed known transcription factor (TF) binding motifs by similarity to JASPAR position weight matrices and identified which motifs are most highly enriched in the top vocabulary for each model. These motif calls were filtered by those TFs which are known to be expressed at any developmental stage of the human retina using snRNA-seq data38 (Table S1). Using these motif counts, it is possible to highlight the top enriched motifs contributing to each model (Fig. 1E). This analysis identifies critical factors with varying degrees of cell class specificities. In particular, some TF families such as OTX, PITX, and MEIS are highly enriched throughout the top vocabulary of a given cell class consistent with their known roles in retinal cell-class-specific development. By contrast, other models have more diverse vocabularies, yielding lower average counts per TF motif across the top scored 11-mers (Table S1) perhaps consistent with greater diversity of cell types within a given cell class.


### Variant impact predictions identify critical sequence motifs
  To further test the specificity of our newly generated models, we used them to predict the cell-type-specific consequences of disrupting consensus transcription factor binding motifs. First, using our 20% outgroup sequences, we performed in silico saturation mutagenesis and generated variant CRE sequences that contained all possible single nucleotide variants (SNVs). We then applied our trained models together with the deltaSVM pipeline40 to generate Variant Impact Prediction (VIP) scores for each model for all possible mutations. These VIPs demonstrate the predicted cell-class-specific impact of a given sequence variant on a CRE. A more negative VIP indicates a variant that is predicted to disrupt the CRE, whereas a positive score indicates a variant that is closer to a calculated consensus CRE sequence for the corresponding cell class. VIPs approaching zero likely have little impact on a sequence.

  To evaluate the relevance of these cell-class-specific VIP scores compared to functionally important features of CREs, we compared VIP score with TF motifs known to be important in specific cell classes. We identified all occurrences of these specific consensus transcription factor binding motifs within the original holdout CREs, and averaged VIP scores for all possible variants within a +/-30 bp window of each motif for each model. Scanning across the core sequence of a motif and its surrounding sequence context, we observed cell-class-specific consequences of motif disruption for a variety of factors with known impacts across retinal cell classes and development (Fig. 2A-F). SNVs along the core motifs of SOX2 and LHX2 (critical in retinal progenitor and glial fates) demonstrate VIP scores reaching their most negative impact near the center of the motif, with a high degree of specificity for progenitor and Müller glial cells consistent with the expression and activity of these TFs (Fig. 2A&D). Similarly, TF motifs consistent with ONECUT family binding are scored most negatively in mature, developing and precursor horizontal cells (Fig. 2B). TF motifs such as those compatible with OTX2 and CRX (Photoreceptor and Bipolar cells) show even stronger VIP scores, with some differences in predicted impact across their sequences, despite a high degree of similarity between the two motifs. These factors additionally demonstrate extreme specificity for bipolar and photoreceptor cell classes and, corresponding to the specificity of expression of OTX2 and CRX in these cell classes (Fig. 2C&F). In contrast, motifs of more general transcription factors expressed and well characterized in many retinal cell classes exhibit reduced specificity in model scoring predictions (Fig. 2E). This is reflective of how each cell class utilizes these transcription factors, and thus functionally, disruption of a motif may be detrimental to one model but not the other.

(G,H) Heatmaps of average Variant Impact Prediction (VIP) score for disruptions of motifs of interest (columns) by cell class (rows).   This trend is further demonstrated by the range of VIP scores by cell class within the core motif sequence for a wide variety of TF motifs (Figure S2A). While there are a large number of motifs for which single nucleotide disruption leads to more impactful (more negative) VIP scores across multiple models (Fig. 2G), many other motifs show specific, though potentially moderate effects (Fig. 2H). Among stronger TF motifs, glial-specific TFs appear to have strong VIPs especially for JUN and FOS factors with a high degree of specificity, while common Bipolar and Photoreceptor regulators such as OTX2 and CRX show a more broad range of model impacts due to their role in regulating a larger branch of cell classes. VIP scores highlight still other motifs that are predicted to be more essential in mature cells than developing cells such as RORA and MEF2A/C/D (Figure S2B). Altogether, these scores highlight the specificity and utility of these models to predict the cell-type-specific impact of regulatory variants.

  For the purposes of comparing these VIP scores to other publicly available data, and comparisons to Deep Learning techniques for variant predictions, we sought to identify the correlation between our predictions and those of the DeepSEA Sei Model41. To work within the upper limit of DeepSEA’s interface, we selected a single region containing the Rhodopsin promoter and identified VIP scores for all SNVs for Mature Rods as well as Muller Glia. For these same variants, we accessed DeepSEA predictions, and isolated predictions from Retinal DNAse data. Comparing these predictions to VIPs from our models, we found that DeepSEA is most aligned with our Rod model with a Pearson correlation of 0.48, compared to 0.29 for the correlation to Muller VIPs(Figure S2C, D). This is expected, as the retinal DNAse-Seq data used by DeepSEA is from the bulk retina and therefore rod dominated. Differences between these predictions likely arise from the nature of training models on heterogeneous tissue populations and single cell classes, thus demonstrating the necessity for training on more homogenous cell populations. To compare these two sets of predictions back to experimental data demonstrating the impact of variants on regulatory activity42. We generated Rod VIPs and DeepSEA predictions for SNVs in the mouse Rho regulatory element CRE3, and compared these predictions to MPRA data previously published by Kwasnieski et al. These comparisons demonstrated that the Rod VIPs were more predictive (Pearson 0.44) than the DeepSEA Retina DNAse predictions (Pearson 0.39). This highlights the value in these models trained on highly cell-class specific accessibility data.

  In consideration of combinatorial variants, we selected the most positive, negative, and neutral variants contained within a putative CRE found in an intron of HTRA1 a gene associated with AMD. For each of these variants, we performed additional saturation level mutagenesis, creating three series of combinatorial variants (Figure S3). Largely, the subsequent VIP scores are additive (Figure S3A), with the exception of variants within +/- 5 bp of the initial variant, in which case, it is likely that both variants are disrupting the same motif (Figure S3B).


### Models predict CRE-directed expression changes in a class-specific manner
  We further assessed the predictive power of our models using existing data from functional studies of CRE variants. Zhao et al. characterized the impact of variants in CRX motifs within the mouse Gnb3 promoter using a massively parallel reporter assay in single retinal cells. An analysis of the human GNB3 promoter with our models demonstrates similar predictive variant impacts at putative CRX binding sites (Fig. 3A).Characterization of the same variants as in Zhao et al. with our models demonstrates that cell-class-specific VIP scores mirror changes in expression of reporter libraries driven by this same promoter. Correlation of VIPs to variant reporter expression is highest in bipolar cells with a Pearson correlation of 0.575, with correlations of 0.49 in rod photoreceptors, while cell classes like Müller glial cells with low expression of Gnb3 demonstrate little to no correlation (0.072) between VIP scores and variant reporter expression (Fig. 3B&C, Figure S4). This is consistent with higher expression of the gene in bipolar cells and rods, thus showing that the models are most predictive on elements active in the cell class of interest. These correlations between expression disruption and VIP are particularly clear in comparisons of individual and combinatorial deletions of CRX motifs from the Gnb3 promoter sequence (Fig. 3D&E). Notably, the VIP scores for deletions have a much broader range, due to the larger variant size from a full enhancer deletion as compared to those for SNVs. In each dataset, the second of five CRX motifs is identified as an extremely valuable sequence, with individual and combinatorial deletions of the sequence reflective of its importance to gene regulation. These correlations demonstrate the ability of these models to predict the impact of more complex variants beyond individual SNVs. Additionally, these results demonstrate the models’ abilities to predict the impact of variants at one conserved locus across two species, given that all models were trained on human CRE sequences, but Gnb3 expression analysis was performed with the mouse promoter sequence in the mouse retina. This is consistent with recent human-to-mouse comparisons of other tissues using gkm-SVM43.

## A resource for cell-class-specific human retinal regulatory variant predictions
  The analyses described above suggest that this cell-class-specific variant impact scoring strategy using the GKM-SVM/deltaSVM workflow trained on human retinal snATAC-seq data has several biologically relevant features. We therefore extended these scores to include a more inclusive set of 39,437 putative retinal CREs as defined by both our original ATAC-seq regions, as well as our top 10,000 peaks of retina-associated TF ChIP-seq data by MACS2 score44. This resource entitled “Cell-class-Specific Variant Impact Scores in Ocular Non-coding Sequences” (csVISIONS) is available on the UCSC genome browser to query and to compare with human retinal DNA-accessibility and gene expression (http://genome.ucsc.edu/s/CherryLab/csVISIONS_TrackHub). It is our hope that these predicted impact scores can assist other researchers in identifying and interpreting variants of interest within non-coding retinal regulatory elements.


## csVISIONS can be used to screen variants of interest for IRDs
  In order to demonstrate potential use cases for our browser, we explored a number of IRD-associated variants within the existing predictions. We identified 6 non-coding variants in the literature that have been characterized in association with Retinitis Pigmentosa (RP)45,46. These variants are associated with the promoter regions of TMEM216 (two variants at c.-69)46, and EYS (four variants from c.-451 to c.-456)45. Through navigating to these regions in csVISIONS, we see that this promoter is accessible in rods—the likely impacted cell class—and that disruption of these regions is likely to have a negative impact (Fig. 4A, B). We are also able to compare these scores to other cell classes in the neural retina and identify whether these motifs are more universally important, or cell class specific. For the purposes of a more detailed view of these variants, we additionally identified the exact VIP scores for each of these SNVs, and found the rod model to be most predictive of variants with a characterized impact on disease in the case of RP (Fig. 4C).

### GWAS predictions narrow variant search windows
  The value of these models becomes further apparent upon the intersection of Genome-Wide Association Studies and disease-associated variants. Studies of this nature have identified thousands of potential variants associated with complex retinal diseases, often concentrated in non-coding regions of the genome16,47. Candidate regulatory variants can be narrowed down in scope based on many factors, such as DNA accessibility16 (Fig. 5A). However, this still leaves a daunting task of identifying which variants functionally contribute to phenotypes. By looking at sequence context and identifying candidate CREs where a disease-associated variant changes a predicted binding motif, the search for relevant variants can be further narrowed, but this still does not guarantee a functional change (Fig. 5A).

Filtration by snATAC-derived accessibility in any cell class, accessibility in cell classes of interest by retinal disorder, and presence in a change of motif at the site of variant. (B) Tracks of snATAC-seq38 and summed saturation mutagenesis VIP scores in region surrounding five variants identified for AMD (highlights). snATAC and VIP featured for Cones, Rods, Bipolar cells, Muller Glia, and RPE. (C) Inset from (B). Specific scoring surrounding AMD-associated SNVs rs36212732 and rs36212733 and JASPAR-predicted transcription factor binding sites.

  To predict which variants are most likely to have an impact on a cell class of interest, we generated VIP scores for all variants in an aggregated table of GWAS-identified sequence variants across four retinal diseases (Table S2). Using this scoring, we can identify regions of interest and variants with higher predicted impacts that are more likely to contribute to disease. For example, within the intronic region of ARMS2, there are putative CREs which are accessible in photoreceptors, horizontal cells, and RPE. This region contains at least 5 variants associated with Age-Related Macular Degeneration (AMD) (Fig. 5B). We have performed in silico saturation-level mutagenesis and generated VIP scores for all SNVs across the full accessible region. From this scoring, it is possible to identify regions of very negative VIP scores, indicating sequences where disruption may have a significant impact on CRE function. The scoring surrounding rs36212732 and rs36212733, highlights the differences in VIP scores (Fig. 5C). While both SNVs are within an accessible region in cell-classes of interest and fall within a predicted TF binding motif, only rs36212733 has a predicted negative impact. Both of these variants are potentially associated with AMD, but VIP scoring indicates that only one likely has a strong impact on its own.


## Discussion
  The identification of causal non-coding variants in complex tissues presents a unique challenge in characterizing disease mechanisms and providing genetic diagnoses for IRDs. Variants have been identified across the genome in many non-coding regions, and the classification of a disease-associated variant as functional can be resource intensive, which is compounded when many variants must be tested across many cell classes12,15. In this study, we utilized single nucleus DNA accessibility data to train machine learning models on individual retinal cell classes. This level of resolution allows for distinct characterization of retinal cell classes and their regulatory mechanisms. We demonstrate that by using high-confidence DNA accessible regions, we are able to generate models that are both accurate, and cell-class-specific.

  Comparisons of these models helps to demonstrate both their specificity and relatedness. As each model scores representative CREs and sequence variants, it is clear that each model values different sequences based on the sequence motifs that contribute to it. This can highlight the differences between each class, as well as relatedness between models that may have similar regulatory logic48. Hierarchical clustering of model scoring of peak sets demonstrates this cell class relatedness.

  The models presented here specifically score sequences using a gapped k-mer support vector machine learning model, and the applied delta SVM algorithm. While additional deep learning modeling architectures are also capable of variant prediction, the gkm-svm approach efficiently and robustly trains models, and allows for the high degree of specificity shown in this article. A specific advantage of SVM-based models is that they are interpretable and effective with smaller datasets. A drawback compared to CNN or Transformer-based deep learning approaches is that SVM models are computationally expensive for larger datasets. Nonetheless, prior comparisons of these algorithms demonstrate the comparable nature of the predictions from gkm-svm and other prediction models49. We provide additional evidence for this by comparing our gkmSVM/delta SVM approach to DeepSEA, a CNN-based approach.

  A recognized limitation in developing any predictive model stems from the inherent heterogeneity and accessibility of high-quality training data. In the case of this study, rare cell classes with lower snATAC-seq coverage present a challenge. This sparsity of input features can potentially compromise the robustness and generalizability of the model’s predictions. Therefore, while our model performs reliably on well-represented cell classes, interpretation regarding predictions in more rare cell classes must proceed with caution. Future predictions could incorporate deeper sequencing of rare cell classes covering more cells, as well as diving into tissues beyond the neural retina and RPE. For example, additional models trained on snATAC-Seq data from cell classes of the choroid16,50,51 could further elucidate non-coding variants in AMD. While our current approach utilizes snATAC-Seq data, there are clear advantages from building models that incorporate additional genomic modalities such as chromatin conformation − 3D architecture, snRNA-Seq, and others as shown by DeepSEA41 and more recently by AlphaGenome52. However given these limitations, the current models presented in our study confidently identify regulatory regions and critical sequences which yield a valuable resource for the prediction of variants of interest in neural retina disorders.

  The driving features of each of these models can be discovered through explorations into the contributions of individual sequence motifs. In particular, TF binding motifs are highlighted by both scored model vocabularies, and by VIP scoring. Through the summarization of TF motifs called in top scored 11mer vocabulary, we identify the motifs of TFs that contribute highly to each model. Many of these identified top factors, such as OTX2, MEIS1, and LHX2 are well known to be involved in retinal cell fate specification53. The impact of disruption to these highly impactful motifs can be observed by identifying calls for these sequences across the genome and comparing average VIPs for disruptions in and around those motifs. This identifies the TF motifs which generate strong-negative VIP scores when disrupted, and easily demonstrates the emergent features of models, as well as model relatedness based on combinations of motif VIP scoring. For the interpretation of VIPs from each of these models, unless direct TF binding can be confirmed via binding assay data, it is critical to consider the genomic landscape of the prediction. Native accessibility, and expression of putative binding factors in the cell class of interest must be taken into consideration when assessing the potential regulatory impacts of a predicted variant of interest.

  It is necessary to ensure that these variant impact prediction scores are accurate. Comparisons to massively parallel reporter assays performed with cell-class-specific resolution54 reveal a high degree of similarity between changes in reporter expression from sequence variants to VIP scoring of the same variants. This holds true in multiple cell classes that express the construct. However, this scoring becomes less predictive when a construct is lowly expressed. This is to be expected, as major changes to expression are unlikely when the reference construct is lowly expressed, if at all, as small variants are less likely to induce a high degree of expression. In cell classes where the reference construct is highly expressed, changes are more consistent and measurable. This is replicated in our models’ scoring of variants, which further identifies sequence motifs of interest that contributed most highly to the function of a CRE.

  The value of this type of predictive scoring is apparent in larger screens for disease-associated variants. Many GWAS and eQTL studies have identified hundreds of variants associated with retinal diseases and expression of retinal disease-linked genes, many of which are present in non-coding sequences across the genome16,17,55. Prioritizing the risk of each variant becomes a challenge, as it is not always clear which sequence is likely to contribute to development or disease. Some filtration of these variants is possible through the identification of DNA sequence accessibility in cell classes of interest, and it is possible to identify if a TF motif is disrupted in the region. However, our models are able to quickly and efficiently screen variants of interest based on what degree they are predicted to disrupt a candidate CRE. By identifying variants of interest in this way, it is possible to streamline the characterization of variants in sequences of interest.

  Ultimately, this study demonstrates that it is possible to create valuable, accurate models of cis-regulatory sequences in distinct cell classes of the retina. These models will be useful in future efforts to characterize cell class-specific regulation and identify non-coding variants of interest in disease modeling. Pre-screening of candidates will allow for researchers to narrow down candidate lists to reduce the complexity of future assays and focus on the most likely functional variants.


## Methods
  Code used in for pre-processing data, training models, generating VIP scores, and comparing to MPRAs can be found at github.com/TCherryLab/snATAC-ML-2025/.


### Training data
  Retinal snATAC-seq data generated by our lab is available through GEO (GSE183684) and described in Thomas et al.38. Single nucleus ATAC-seq was performed on developing and mature human retinas across four distinct timepoints from 9 donors. A total of 74,933 nuclei were analyzed with ArchR to generate the distinct cell class clusters used to identify accessible domains for training data38. Macular RPE data was obtained from GEO (GSE99287) and is described in Wang et al.39. Bulk ATAC-seq was performed on adult human RPE with peaks called by MACS239. For snATAC-seq, pseudobulk peak calls derived from individual cluster calls were performed as previously described38. Macular RPE ATAC-seq fastqs were used to call peaks as previously described35. Summits defined by MACS2 were extended +/- 150 bp to define candidate CRE regions. Peaks were sorted by MACS2 peak score, and retinal cell classes were compared to each other to determine universally accessible regions likely used for housekeeping gene regulation. Universally accessible regions were removed from the peak sets, and the top 25k summits selected from the remaining peaks were used for model generation. For selection of training data, regions for each cell class were shuffled, and 20k regions were randomly selected, with remaining peaks set aside as for later use as outgroup test sets. For a uniform test set, all 80% training sets were cross-compared against the outgroup sets, to ensure no model was trained on outgroup data. Negative training data was selected from non-accessible background genomic regions and GC-matched to the positive training data as previously described35.


### Model training
  Models were trained with ls-gkm gkmtrain56 as previously described. Genomic coordinates for training data described above were converted to fasta format with bedtools and used to train a model using LS-GKM gkmtrain with the following gkmtrain hyperparameters: L = 11, k = 7, d = 3, C = 1, t = 2, and e = 0.005. Validation and sequence scoring similarly follows as previously described35 using 5-fold cross validation from gkmtrain, and sequence scoring with gkmpredict.


### TF motif identification
  Vocabulary sequences and GWAS variants were scored for sequence motifs via the JASPAR PFM database (10th release)57. Motif lineplots were generated through the identification of genomic locations of specific motifs within the Homer database or using scanmotifgenomewide from Homer58. For further detail on motif analysis, see http://github.com/TCherryLab/snATAC-ML-2025/.


### MPRA analysis comparison
  Bespoke dSVM scoring for individual models was performed to match specific SNV and indel variants tested by MPRAs in Zhao54. For further detail on comparisons, see http://github.com/TCherryLab/snATAC-ML-2025/.
