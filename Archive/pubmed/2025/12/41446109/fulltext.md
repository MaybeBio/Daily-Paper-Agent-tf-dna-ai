# Comprehensive perturbation of transcription factors in human cardiomyocytes reveals the regulatory architecture of congenital heart disease


## Abstract
  Over 100 genes have been implicated in congenital heart disease (CHD), yet the genetic basis for >50% of CHD remains unexplained. A key challenge is to define the regulatory architecture of CHD genes. Here, we systematically perturb 1983 transcription factors (TFs) during cardiomyocyte differentiation of human stem cells. Our analysis links TFs to gene expression phenotypes across scales and nominates TFs in cardiac cell fate commitment, dosage sensitivity, and transposable element regulation. By deriving TF-gene regulatory networks from experimental perturbation, we gain insights into how developmental networks are structured, use this map to interpret the regulatory architecture of CHD, and deorphanize TFs with roles in CHD that have been under-sampled by genetic studies. To extend these networks to include enhancer-TF-gene linkages, we also perturb 981 putative enhancers of TFs. Finally, we develop a deep learning transformer model to accurately predict perturbed TFs from altered transcriptomes, which we apply to interpret CHD patient transcriptomes. This reference map represents a foundational platform to model the functions of TFs and aids in interpreting CHD variants and mechanisms.


## Introduction
  The human genome encodes ~2000 transcription factors (TFs) that regulate diverse gene expression programs, notably for lineage specification1. Since cell fate commitment is essential for normal development, genetic mutations of TFs have been associated with developmental defects such as congenital heart disease (CHD). For example, GATA4, NKX2–5, and TBX5 have well-established roles in cardiac development in both mice and humans2–4, and mutations in these TFs cause CHD5–8. More broadly, human genetic studies have implicated dozens of TFs and epigenetic regulators in CHD9, suggesting that altered gene regulation is an important component of this disease. Furthermore, mutations in several chromatin regulatory proteins have been linked to CHD by comprehensive exome sequencing of parent-offspring trios10. However, the genetic basis for 54% of familial and 80% of sporadic CHD cases remains unknown11, indicating that disease mechanisms remain incompletely understood.

  Traditional strategies to define TF function have relied on computational inference of TF-gene regulatory networks. These correlative approaches often use gene co-expression, chromatin accessibility, TF binding, and motif enrichment to model the relationship between TFs and the genes they regulate. However, accurate inference of gene regulatory networks (GRNs) remains a well-recognized computational challenge12,13. An alternative approach is to experimentally perturb TFs and directly measure regulated genes, an approach accomplished in yeast14. While single-cell screens have recently been developed to perform genome-scale perturbations in human cells (Perturb-Seq)15, they have been more challenging to implement in stem cell-based models of human development16–19.

  Here, we systematically perturb TFs and map their transcriptional activities to gain insights into cardiac development and disease. Using an optimized Perturb-Seq platform in human iPS cell-derived cardiomyocytes (CMs)20, we characterize the impact of 1983 TFs during cardiac differentiation. We perform multiscale analysis linking TFs to transcriptome-wide phenotypes, altered activity of gene programs, and changes in expression of individual genes, which we use to derive TF-gene regulatory networks from experimental perturbation. Our analysis gives insights on how developmental networks are structured and the regulatory architecture of CHD. We use these maps of TF function to nominate understudied TFs in cardiac cell fate commitment and deorphanize TFs relevant to CHD that have been undersampled in human genetic studies. We also demonstrate the utility of this unique resource to experimentally identify dosage-sensitive TFs and to define regulatory relationships between TFs and transposable elements. Next, we extend this cardiac regulatory network to include enhancers that regulate TFs and impact cardiac cell fate commitment. Finally, we apply these perturbation maps to develop Percoder, a machine learning model that predicts underlying TF perturbations given transcriptomes from patient-derived cardiomyocyte disease models.


## Results

### Systematic perturbation of 1983 human TFs in cardiac differentiation
  To comprehensively survey the impact of transcription factors on cardiac phenotypes, we perform Perturb-Seq on 1983 TFs (1747 DNA binding proteins and 236 epigenetic regulators) during iPSC differentiation to cardiomyocytes (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). After filtering, this high quality dataset consists of 765,351 cells (ave 3588 transcriptome unique molecular identifiers (UMIs), ave 1004 sgRNA UMIs, ave 1.9 significant sgRNAs called per cell)(Fig. 1b). We confirm on-target repression efficiency (Extended Data Fig. 1a) and replicate reproducibility among libraries (Extended Data Fig. 1b–c). Our differentiation protocol yields cell states across cardiomyocyte differentiation, from early CMs (FN1+) to late CMs (TNNT2+) (Extended Data Fig. 1d). Expectedly, we observe reduced cell recovery for well-known regulators of cardiac development including GATA4, NKX2–5, HAND2, ISL1, and TBX5 (Extended Data Fig. 2a–b).


### Transcriptome-wide phenotypes prioritize 209 TFs in cardiomyocyte differentiation
  Perturb-Seq enables the analysis of TF functions at multiple transcriptional resolutions. At the level of transcriptome-wide phenotypes, we adopt a principal components-based energy distance metric to identify TFs that impact global cell state (Fig. 1c)21. This analysis identifies 209 regulators with consistent sgRNAs that are significantly different from non-targeting negative controls (Extended Data Fig. 3 and 4a–c, Supplementary Tables 3–7). While most significant regulators are relatively highly expressed, a handful of lowly expressed TFs also exhibit significant impact on the transcriptome (Fig. 1d). Notably, significant regulators are highly enriched in known CHD genes (p=4.4E-14), and are enriched in TFs with de novo variants (p=1.7E-4) (Fig. 1e)10,22,23.

  Using energy distance to visually embed and cluster TFs, we identify several notable functional modules (Fig. 1f). Expectedly, we identify clusters representing the hypoxia pathway (Cluster 1: HIF1A, ARNT)24, DNA damage (Cluster 24: TP53, RB1)25,26, transcriptional coactivators (Cluster 27: CREBBP, EP300)27, and the WNT pathway (Cluster 20: TCF7, PIN1)28,29 (Fig. 1f). Interestingly, Cluster 2 contains regulators highly enriched in known CHD TFs, including TBX5 2,3, SOX4 30, CHD731, and SMAD2–410,32,33 (Fig. 1g). This observation indicates that CHD TFs share similar transcriptional impacts, and suggests potential shared mechanisms. In addition, Cluster 7 represents regulators with known functions in heart development (Fig. 1h), including MEF2A34, MEF2C35, and ISL136.

  Through guilt-by-association, we can implicate lesser studied TFs from Clusters 2 and 7 to CHD and heart development. For example, CHAMP1 is a member of Cluster 7 (development) and human genetic studies have identified one CHD patient with a 1-MB deletion including CHAMP1 and one CHD patient with an early termination de novo variant in CHAMP1 (Extended Data Fig. 4d)22,37,38. Our Perturb-Seq analysis thus further prioritizes CHAMP1 as a regulator of cardiac differentiation and CHD. To test this hypothesis, we knocked down CHAMP1 and observed delayed cardiac differentiation by TNNT2 staining (Fig. 1i). A similar analysis implicates the High-Mobility Group TF HMG20A (a member of Cluster 2) in cardiac cell fate commitment39, as well as several zinc-finger containing genes (ZBTB12, ZC3H15, ZNF384)


### Deorphanization of CHD TFs regulating cardiac differentiation
  To increase the resolution of transcriptional phenotypes, we apply consensus, non-negative matrix factorization (cNMF)40 (Fig. 2a) to define 250 gene programs (GPs) and 241 associated TF perturbations across perturbed transcriptomes (mean 7.2 TFs per GP, mean 6.9 GPs per TF, padj = 0.001) (Extended Data Fig. 5a, Supplementary Tables 8 and 9, Supplementary Note 2). Gene Program 1 (GP1) is highly enriched in CHD genes9 (padj = 1.6e-06), including MYH7, MYH6, and MEIS2, as well as cardiac development and differentiation ontologies (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Tables 10 and 11). We identify 24 TF-perturbations that down-regulate GP1 (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Table 12), many of which have been genetically associated with CHD and have well-known roles in cardiac development. These TFs have distinct differentiation phenotypes. For example, knockdown of TBX20 or NKX2–5 causes severe stalling of differentiation, while GATA4 knockdown cells exhibit an attenuated phenotype (Fig. 2d). Independent qPCR and immuno-staining experiments confirm the roles of these TFs in regulating markers of cardiac differentiation (Fig. 2e, Extended Data Fig. 5b–c). Consistently, the findings from Perturb-Seq analyses in our in vitro model of human development are also recapitulated by in vivo knockout studies from the International Mouse Phenotyping Consortium41 (Extended Data Fig. 5d). These results indicate that altered regulation of cardiac differentiation is a common mechanism for CHD pathogenesis.

  Four TFs (ZSCAN10, GATA4, BPTF, CHAMP1) regulating GP1 have been implicated in CHD cohort sequencing studies (Supplementary Table 13)10,22,23, but below the threshold of statistical significance as these studies are still underpowered. For example, BPTF, ZSCAN10 and CHAMP1 are not commonly mutated in CHD patients, but there have been isolated instances of de novo mutations identified in CHD patients (missense for ZSCAN10 and BPTF, and nonsense for CHAMP1) (Extended Data Fig. 4d). Importantly, these results are corroborated by independent TNNT2 immuno-staining experiments (Fig. 1i, Extended Data Fig. 5e). Since mis-regulation of cardiac differentiation is a common feature for CHD pathogenesis, these results can be used to deorphanize several de novo TF mutations associated with CHD.

  Beyond GP1, GP28 is also significantly enriched for CHD genes including HAND1, FOXC2, and PRDM6 (padj = 1.4e-03) (Fig. 2f, Supplementary Table 14), with functional enrichment for genes of the extracellular matrix and collagen-containing structures (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Table 10). Our analysis indicates GP28 is regulated by TFs that are associated with CHD (AFF4, ZFPM2, and GATA4) as well as TFs whose roles in CHD remain uncharacterized (SOX11, GTF2I, and MTF2). These results further demonstrate the potential of this approach to potentially deorphanize novel TF regulators of CHD.


### Co-regulation analysis reveals novel interactions between TFs and chromatin regulators
  We noticed groups of TFs and chromatin regulators that often co-regulate multiple gene programs more than would be expected by chance (Fig. 2g). For example, we found that HIF1A and ARNT, both well-known regulators of the hypoxia response, co-regulate 29 gene programs (Fig. 2h). This analysis also identifies frequent co-regulation of the MEF2 family of TFs (MEF2A, MEF2C, and MEF2D) with the members of the Polycomb Repressive 1 Complex (PRC1) (PCGF6, PCGF2, and RNF2) (Supplementary Table 15). These TFs regulate the expression of multiple gene programs associated with CHD (Supplementary Table 9). In general, chromatin regulators do not directly bind DNA and thus require recruitment by context-dependent TFs, which makes target gene identification particularly challenging. Interestingly, we notice a partitioning of gene programs regulated by the distinct complexes of PRC1: PRC1.2 (containing PCGF2 and RNF2) generally regulates a distinct set of gene programs from PRC1.6 (PCGF6). In contrast, TF usage is more varied: MEF2D only co-regulates PRC1.2 programs while MEF2A and MEF2C co-regulates with both PRC1 complexes (Fig. 2i). To test if MEF2A coregulates genes with RNF2 (member of PRC1.2) we performed qPCR in individual MEF2A or RNF2 CRISPRi CMs. We confirm that HEY1 is modestly upregulated and NKX2–5 is downregulated when MEF2A or RNF2 is repressed during CM differentiation (Fig. 2j, Extended Data Fig. 5f). To understand the molecular mechanism behind co-regulation of the downstream genes, we performed co-immunoprecipitation of Myc-RNF2, and confirmed co-localization and physical interaction between RNF2 and MEF2A, at least in a heterologous overexpression system (Fig. 2k,l). Overall, this network analysis suggests possible molecular mechanisms of coordinated gene regulation by TFs and chromatin regulators.


### The regulatory architecture of CHD TFs and genes
  We next construct a gene regulatory network (GRN) with 25,615 TF-gene edges connecting 1316 TFs to 7122 unique genes that are differentially expressed upon TF knockdown (ave 19.5 edges per TF; ave 3.6 edges per gene.) (Fig. 3a–b, Supplementary Table 16). This network is consistent with a scale-free network with power-law exponent of 2.04 (Extended Data Fig. 6a), which has implications for the hub-like structure of GRN in cardiomyocytes.

  Next, we assess the properties of Perturb-Seq-derived GRNs to understand how CHD genes are regulated. Applying reverse pagerank on CHD genes (Fig. 3c), we identify ISL1 as a highly influential regulator of CHD genes36,42, followed by known cardiac regulators (TBX20, NKX2–5), and other CHD genes (SOX1143, ZFPM244). These TFs regulate a significant number of CHD genes, both positively and negatively (Fig. 3d). Many well-known CHD genes are connected by this network. Consistent with a scale-free network, while some CHD genes like MYH7 are regulated by as many as 71 TFs, others like GJA1 are only regulated by 6 TFs (Fig. 3e). Each of 50 CHD genes is regulated by on average 7.4 TFs, thus implicating TFs that could influence CHD by modifying the expression of CHD genes. For example, TBX20 and NKX2–5 regulate 18 and 12 CHD genes, respectively. Indeed, the top TFs genetically associated with CHD convergently regulate multiple CHD genes (Fig. 3f). For example, MYH7 is regulated by ISL1, TBX20, and NKX2–5. This regulation of multiple CHD genes underscores the impact of TFs in CHD pathogenesis by regulating cardiomyocyte developmental programs.

  50 TFs have been associated with CHD9. To understand how the TF-TF regulatory network is structured to control the expression of developmental and disease genes, we examine the GRN subnetwork connecting these 50 CHD TFs (Extended Data Fig. 6b)9. The TF-TF network is consistent with a small world network (Extended Data Fig. 6c), highlighting their pivotal roles of central regulatory hub genes(HAND1, NKX2–5, PITX2, and TBX20), in cardiac development and CHD45. Network motifs further reveal TF crosstalk and compensatory mechanisms (Extended Data Fig. 6d). For example, NKX2–5 and TBX20 co-regulate multiple downstream TFs including MYOCD, which we independently confirm (Extended Data Fig. 6e). Consistent with studies in mice46,47, we find that three regulators (HAND1, NKX2–5, and PITX2) form a feedback loop, representing a self-sustaining regulatory module that maintains stable expression patterns in heart development.

  In summary, experimentally derived GRNs reveal a scale-free network with hub structure regulating CHD genes (Fig. 3j).This regulatory architecture where many TFs can converge to regulate CHD genes highlights the multiple paths to disease pathogenesis.


### Regulation of transposable elements by TFs during cardiac differentiation
  Our unbiased TF Perturb-seq dataset can also capture non-canonical transcripts, including transposable elements (TEs)48. Given recent evidence suggesting that TEs modulate gene expression through enhancer-like activity49 and cause developmental disease by retrotransposition50,51, we sought to identify TF-TE regulatory interactions. Thus, we examined how knockdown of TFs, especially Zinc-finger TFs, impacts the expression of TEs during cardiomyocyte differentiation52. Highlighting the sensitivity of Perturb-Seq to capture TF-TE regulatory interactions, we recover the published finding that knockdown of the KRAB zinc finger TF ZNF91 results in derepression of the SVA family of TEs53 (Extended Data Fig. 7a). Consistently, these TF-TE interactions are supported by published TF binding at TEs54 (Extended Data Fig. 7b). These results suggest that Perturb-Seq can efficiently capture TE regulation. Next, we systematically identify 144 TF-TE regulatory relationships as those TEs that are differentially expressed upon TF knockdown (Fig. 3g, Supplementary Table 17) (FDR = 10%). Consistent with the role of Zinc finger TFs in repressing TEs during differentiation, knockdown of TF hits generally results in TE de-repression (Fig. 3g, red). Examples with potential relevance to cardiac development include CHAMP1 regulating MER61A, and RCOR2 regulating LTR2B, which we validate by qPCR (Fig. 3h). Consistently, these regulated TEs are down-regulated during normal in vivo human development in the PCW4 embryo55, with higher expression in early cardiomyocytes (MYH7-) compared to late cardiomyocytes (MYH7+) (Fig. 3i). In addition, regulated TEs are enriched in evolutionarily young (Haplorhini and Simiiformes) stages (Extended Data Fig. 7c). Finally, we find that almost all Zinc finger TF hits regulate a single TE (Extended Data Fig. 7d) and that most TEs are regulated by a single TF (Extended Data Fig. 7e), consistent with their specialized role for targeting TEs54. One notable exception is the WNT pathway transcription factor TCF7, whose knockdown leads to the up-regulation of 54 TEs (Extended Data Fig. 7d), including multiple LINEs (Extended Data Fig. 7f), and HERV-H which functions as an insulator in cardiomyocytes56.


### Identifying dosage-sensitive TFs of cardiac cell fate commitment
  CHD TFs can be dosage sensitive, which is exemplified by Holt-Oram Syndrome caused by TBX5 haploinsufficiency. Indeed, using the LOEUF metric of haploinsufficiency from gnomAD57, we find that TF hits from Perturb-Seq are significantly enriched in dosage sensitive TFs (Fig. 4a). However, while LOEUF scores haploinsufficiency, it lacks resolution of cell type. Thus, we adopt a framework to distinguish dosage sensitive from dosage buffered TFs using Perturb-Seq data. We define dosage sensitive TFs as those where small changes in TF expression cause large changes in transcriptional phenotypes, compared to dosage buffered TFs that require large changes in TF expression (Fig. 4b). We distinguish these two possibilities by whether TF-phenotype relationships fit linearly (sensitive) or exponentially (buffered)58. (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Table 18). This analysis is made possible because we targeted each TF with multiple sgRNAs, each of which may have differential CRISPRi repression efficiency. This analysis confirms that TBX5 is dosage sensitive while RCOR2 is dosage buffered, which is validated by independent qPCR experiments (Fig. 4d). Expanding this analysis across all testable TFs where sgRNAs span a large dynamic range of repression, we identify 5 TFs that are dosage sensitive in CMs (ARID4B, RB1CC1, SOX4, TBX5, and ZNF608) (Fig. 4e). Notably, SOX4 mutations cause Coffin-Siris syndrome with heart phenotypes and SOX4 haploinsufficiency has been implicated in developmental disorders30. Consistently, these TFs also have low LOEUF scores, while dosage buffered TFs tend to have higher scores.


### Enhancer regulatory networks connect non-coding CHD variants to TF phenotypes
  Since enhancers regulate TFs and can contain CHD variants, a more comprehensive enhancer-TF-gene regulatory network can give further insights into cardiac differentiation and CHD. Thus, we prioritized Perturb-Seq experiments of 1267 enhancers, of which 981 are in the regulatory neighborhoods of 107 TFs (Fig. 5a, Extended Data Fig. 8, Supplementary Tables 19 and 20). Energy distance analysis identifies 116 genes and 194 enhancers as regulators that are significantly different from non-targeting negative controls (Fig. 5b, Supplementary Tables 21–23). Clustering and embedding analysis shows that enhancer perturbations phenocopy promoter perturbations on a transcriptome-wide level (Fig. 5c, Extended Data Fig. 9), including two enhancers regulating HOXB4 (Fig. 5d, Extended Data Fig. 10a) and three enhancers regulating TBX5 (Fig. 5e).

  Accurate identification of enhancers is challenging because of the low detection sensitivity of scRNA-Seq. However, for enhancers of TFs, perturbing strong enhancers phenocopies promoter perturbation because both induce robust transcriptome-wide phenotypes. This is also true for weaker enhancers (Fig. 5f): CRISPRi of Element293 moderately represses TBX5 and displays modest transcriptome-wide similarity with promoter perturbation, as compared with stronger enhancers (Element 290/291/294) (Fig. 5g). These observations motivate us to develop a new approach to increase enhancer target gene sensitivity by examining the similarity of transcriptome-wide phenotypes (Extended Data Fig. 10b–c). This analysis identifies 207 enhancers that regulate the expression of 64 TFs (Fig. 5h–i, Extended Data Fig. 10d, Supplementary Table 24), compared to only 76 enhancers (77.6% overlap) identified from traditional differential expression analysis (Fig. 4h, Extended Data Fig. 10e). Enhancer perturbations also facilitate better characterization of TF dosage sensitivity by achieving a broad spectrum of repression levels. We observed that the dosage-sensitive TF TBX5 also exhibits a continuous, linear relationship between its expression levels and transcriptome-wide phenotypes (Extended Data Fig. 11a–b, Supplementary Table 25). In contrast, TBX20 displays a non-linear, dosage-buffered pattern (Extended Data Fig. 11c–d). These findings underscore the critical role of enhancers regulating dosage-sensitive TFs in development and disease, consistent with previous reports on the distal TBX5 enhancer 19,59.

  We illustrate one example of how this enhancer-TF-gene network can help interpret CHD variants. We identify an enhancer (Enhancer781) associated with a non-coding CHD variant60 about 4-kb from the gene for the transcription factor MEIS1. Perturb-Seq of Enhancer781 and the MEIS1 promoter share modest transcriptome-wide similarity (Fig. 5j), with upregulation of FBN2 and KDR and downregulation of TBX18 and GAP43, which we independently validate (Fig. 5k). Importantly, a previous study characterized a non-coding variant at Element781 that significantly modulates activity in an enhancer reporter assay61 (Fig. 5l). Together, these data confirm that this non-coding variant contributes to CHD pathogenesis by modifying the activity of Element781 and the expression of the transcription factor MEIS1, resulting in altered expression of its downstream genes and programs (Fig. 5m).


### Perturbation modeling nominates TFs driving altered regulatory networks in patients
  One application of experimentally-derived TF-gene networks is to interpret transcriptomes from CHD patients. Thus, we developed Percoder (a portmanteau of Perturb-Seq + Decoder) to predict underlying TF perturbations given perturbed (but unlabeled) transcriptomes (Fig. 6a, Extended Data Fig. 12a). Percoder learns the transcriptional phenotypes of TF knockdown as a distribution over a set of cells using a Set transformer architecture62, which we use to predict perturbation labels (See Supplementary Note) (Extended Data Fig. 12b). To test how accurately Percoder can learn perturbation labels from the transcriptome, we trained on 209 significant TF perturbations. We found that 48.7% of cell sets are correctly classified by cross validation (102x better than random). Notably, 193 out of 210 perturbations achieve AUC > 0.75 (Fig. 6b; left), with strong perturbations like sgNKX2–5 or sgGATA4 yielding near-perfect accuracy (Fig. 6b; right). Instances of misclassification are biologically meaningful examples where TFs have shared functions: sgARNT and sgHIF1a (components of the hypoxia pathway), MEF2A and MEF2C (cardiac MEF2 family), EP300 and CREBBP (histone acetyl-transferases), and TBX5 and GATA4 (cardiac TFs and known interactors) (Fig. 6c). Finally, we also test Percoder’s generalizability on out-of-sample data using the enhancer perturbation dataset (Fig. 6d and 5a), where 86 shared TFs were perturbed (Fig. 6e). Using a conditional variational autoencoder to calibrate for batch effects in a shared embedding space (Fig. 6f, Extended Data Fig. 12c and 13)63 (see Supplementary Note 1), we achieve prediction accuracy far exceeding random chance (Fig. 6g). Overall, these results indicate that Percoder can effectively learn diverse perturbation responses with out-of-sample data.

  Next, we tested Percoder to predict dysregulated TFs from patient-derived iPSC-cardiomyocytes (iPSC-CMs) disease models. First, we analyze single-cell RNA-Seq datasets of iPSC-CMs derived from patients with Hypoplastic Left Heart Syndrome (HLHS)64. Percoder identified YAP1 as a top candidate dysregulated TF, consistent with the original published study identifying a defective YAP-regulated antioxidant response in these patients (Fig. 6h, Supplementary Table 26). Importantly, the original study also observed significant dysregulation of genes in the hypoxia pathway in patients, which is also consistent with Percoder’s prediction of ARNT and HIF1A as likely dysregulated TFs in patient transcriptomes (Fig. 6i). We also apply Percoder to a single-cell RNA-Seq dataset of iPSC-CMs modeling a GLYR1 P496L point mutation found in CHD patients65 (Fig. 6j). While the original study identified differentially expressed genes in heart development and mitotic cell cycle in the iPSC-CMs, Percoder goes further by predicting that these dysregulated networks could be driven by TBX20, RB1, and ATMIN (Fig. 6k, Supplementary Table 27). Analysis of scRNA-Seq data from iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes of a Hypoplastic Right Heart Syndrome patient66, also identifies the myogenesis gene MTF1 as a potential driver (Extended Data Fig. 12d, Supplementary Table 28)67. Overall, these results highlight the utility of Percoder to interpret patient-derived transcriptomes to predict underlying perturbed transcriptional regulators, which could be targets for intervention.


## Discussion
  In this study, we experimentally perturbed and measured transcriptome-wide phenotypes for ~2000 human transcription factors during cardiomyocyte differentiation to illuminate their roles in cardiac gene regulation, development, and disease. Our analysis systematically catalogs the impact of each TF on the transcriptome, gene programs, and individual genes. This reference map of experimentally-defined regulatory interactions in cardiomyocytes represents a foundational platform to model the functions of TFs, as well as to interpret the impact of genetic variants and cellular perturbations on cardiac differentiation and CHD-relevant transcriptional phenotypes.

  Human genetic studies have implicated transcriptional regulators in CHD. Early genome linkage studies implicated GATA4, NKX2–5, and TBX5 in familial CHD2,7,68. Consistent with their well-studied roles in cardiac development, these TFs exhibit significant transcriptional phenotypes in our knockdown studies. More recently, exome studies have identified de novo mutations for dozens of genes in sporadic CHD cases10,22,23. Many of the genes reaching statistical significance are transcription factors and epigenetic factors including CHD7, KDM5B, KMT2A, PITX2, SMAD2, and SMAD6. Notably, our CRISPRi studies show that these genes are necessary for proper cardiac cell fate commitment. However, as CHD cohort sequencing studies are undersampled, many genes with de novo mutations in a small number of patients have not reached statistical significance. By attributing experimental evidence to the functions of TFs, our functional datasets nominate additional TFs with roles in CHD, including: AHDC1, CHAMP1, CRAMP1L, EP300, KDM5B, KMT2A, LHX2, TADA2B, and TBX18. For example, exome sequencing identified a de novo nonsense mutation of CHAMP1 in a CHD proband22 and our analysis shows that CHAMP1 knockdown significantly delays cardiomyocyte differentiation and phenocopies MEF2A and MEF2C knockdown (both of which are CHD TFs). This example highlights the utility of our resource to deorphanize TFs with roles in CHD and to interpret the significance and mechanisms of CHD patient variants.

  The lack of experimentally-derived gene regulatory networks, especially in biological systems relevant to cardiac development and CHD, has been a bottleneck in the field. Our comprehensive experimental mapping of TF-gene interactions has several implications. First, regarding the regulatory architecture of CHD, we find that some CHD genes like MYH7 act as “hubs” in the regulatory network and are regulated by many TFs, suggesting convergent molecular routes to CHD. In addition, these TFs can have varying impact on the expression of a CHD gene. For example, small changes in NKX2–5 have a large impact on MYH7 expression, whereas even large changes of TBX20 have a minor impact on MYH7. Second, regarding potential applications of experimentally defined regulatory networks, we find that machine learning models trained on these networks can be used to accurately predict perturbed TFs from unseen transcriptomes, even those from patient-derived cells. We anticipate that future maps of networks in other developmental states will expand our ability to predict the underlying TFs that are dysregulated in diseases across cellular contexts.


## Methods

### Experimental details

#### Cell culture
  Human pluripotent stem cells (WTC11 and H9) are cultured as previously described20. In short, H9/WTC11 cells were maintained under feeder-free conditions in mTeSR plus medium (STEMCELL Technologies) and incubated in 37 ⁰C 5% CO2. For Perturb-Seq, we used the WTC11-CLYBL-dCas9-KRAB cell line69.


#### Cardiac differentiation
  WTC11 and H9 are differentiated to cardiomyocytes as previously described19,20. In short, 3 or 4 µM CHIR99021 supplemented CDM3 (RPMI 1640; 0.5 mg/ml human albumin, ScienCell OsrHSA; 211 µg/ml L-ascorbic acid 2-phosphate) for 48 hr followed by 2 µM Wnt-C59 supplemented CDM3 for 48 hr with subsequent media changes every 2 days with CDM3 alone. On day 12 post-differentiation, samples were washed with DPBS and dissociated to single cells with Accutase.


#### Design of sgRNA library
  sgRNA sequences are from Replogle et al70. 6 gRNAs per region are used for each transcript. We included 600 non-targeting gRNAs and 600 targeting gRNAs as a control in the final library (Supplementary Table 1). For enhancer Perturb-Seq, we designed gRNA using FlashFry71. 10 gRNAs per target are used (Supplementary Table 19)72.


#### Perturb-Seq
  Library construction, virus packaging, transduction of virus, single cell RNA-Seq is performed as previously reported20. Briefly, DNA oligos (Twist Bioscience) are cloned into LentiGuide(10X)-BFP-Puro (LW203), and electroporated in Endura Duo competent cells (Lucigen). For virus packaging, 8µg of these plasmids are transfected in HEK293T with the 6µg of PMD2.G and 2µg of psPAX2 (Addgene ID: 12259 and 12260). The media is changed the next day and viruses are collected after 48hr of media change. These viruses are transfected to the WTC11-CLYBL-dCas9-KRAB cells at 80% confluency using the standard protocol, followed by BFP-positive sorting and 2 weeks culture. These cells proceed to the cardiac differentiation. For scRNA-Seq, cells were loaded onto a Next GEM Chip N with hash-tag oligo superloading20,73. Raw data were processed according to the manufacturer (10x Genomics, User Guide, CG000513).


#### RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis and RT-qPCR.
  RNA extraction was done using Trizol according to previously published protocols20. cDNA was synthesized using lunascript RT (NEB) according to manufacturers instructions. qPCR was performed using SYBR-green master mix and specific primers to detect transcript levels. Biorad’s CFX 384 real time qPCR machine was used to quantify transcript levels.


#### Immunostaining, Immuno-precipitation and western blotting
  For immunostaining, differentiated cardiomyocytes were grown on matrigel coated coverslips. Immunostaining was performed as previously described74. For immunoprecipitation and immunostaining in Figure 2, HeLa cells were transiently transfected with PCS2-Mef2A-GFP, PCS2-Myc-RNF2 constructs using Lipofectamine 3000 according to manufacturers instructions. MYC co-immunoprecipitation and immunoblotting was done as reported previously75. Input indicates 10% of lysates on western blot.


### Analysis details

#### scRNA-seq data pre-processing, filtering low-quality cells and doublets
  We used our in-house preprocessing pipeline Perturb-Seq-Processing-Pipeline available on GitHub (https://github.com/Hon-lab/Perturb-Seq-Processing-Pipeline/). In short, we mapped the transcriptome to hg38 with Cell Ranger (version 7.0.0, 10x Genomics). Cells with low UMI count or higher than 20% mitochondrial content were removed, as well as genes with fewer than 1 counts. Also, gRNAs and HTO are assigned to cells using FBA76. Singlet cells (only 1 HTO) with at least 1 gRNAa are used in the following analysis. For visualization, we applied UMAP based on 50 principal components from principal component analysis (PCA).


#### Energy distance overview
  To compare the effect of perturbations and associations between perturbations, we use energy distance analysis15,21,77 . To balance computational cost and precision, we applied principle component analysis(PCA) (described above), and kept the top 50 principle components for the downstream analysis.

#### Removing outlier gRNAs
  The first step of analysis is removing outlier gRNAs within each target. Although CRISPRi has less off-target activity, we observed some examples of off-target activity gRNAs and weak gRNA, that affect associations between perturbations (Extended Data Fig. 4a). Some methods filter out cells based on target gene expression level78 or differential expressed genes79. However, these methods are not appropriate for our setting because (1) gene expression is highly changing over time in differentiating dataset, and (2) cell states are not uniform even with non-targeting gRNAs and (3) quantification of transcription factor expression is sometimes difficult especially for transient or lowly expressed but important genes. Therefore, we propose a new algorithm based on energy-distance. Assumptions of this algorithm are (1) cells with the same gRNA have the same off-target and on-target (repression efficiency) effects (2) cells with outlier gRNAs have a different distribution in the embedding space. These assumptions motivate us to filter gRNAs based on metrics which do not depend on target gene expression level. This algorithm consists of three steps.


##### Filtering gRNA based on # of gRNAs and cells
  If less than 20 cells are assigned to a gRNA, this gRNA is not considered in the following analysis because it would be noisy to use a small number of cells in the analysis. After this filtering based on cell number, if only 1 or 2 gRNAs are left after drop-off or filtering out gRNAs with a small number of cells, all gRNAs are kept.If not (if the number of gRNAs are more than 2), proceed to step 2.

If p-value of DISCO is more than 0.05, gRNAs per target is judged as all equally distributed. In other words, each gRNA behaves similarly. If p-value is less than 0.05, these gRNAs are likely to contain outliers within these, therefore proceed to the “Finding outlier gRNAs” step.


##### Finding outlier gRNAs
  The assumption here is that “clean” gRNAs, which show no off-target activity and strong repression of targeted regions, are distributed similarly. Thus, the energy distance between cells with distinct clean gRNAs will be smaller than between outlier gRNAs. This idea is similar to the Local outlier factor analysis of k-nearest neighborhood method, with some modification: (1) to calculate p-value in an unbiased way and (2) to detect outlier gRNA in small numbers of samples (max 6 gRNAs per target).


#### Detecting significant hit regions
  After removing outlier gRNAs we performed a permutation test using energy distance as described15,21. In short, we calculate energy distance between cells with non-targeting control gRNAs and cells with targeting gRNAs. For 1 batch, we randomly subsampled 2000 non-targeting cells to faster computation, and 1000 permutation was performed per batch. We repeated this step for 20 batches to avoid a bias effect on choice of background non-targeting cells. In total 20000 permutations were tested per target. To note, p-values are consistent among different sets of 2000 non-targeting cells (Supplementary Table 4). In this study, perturbations whose permutation-based p-value is less than 0.05 are referred to as significant hits.


#### Association analysis, clustering, and embedding of perturbations
  Within significant TF hits, we calculated energy distance between perturbations (Extended Data Fig. 3). Clustering of these significant hits are performed in the following method. First, distance matrices from energy distance analysis are embedded in high dimension space with Isomap (implemented by sklearn.manifold.Isomap). This step is important because biologically associated TF pairs such as EP300 and CREBBP (both are histone acetyltransferase), HIF1A and ARNT (both are hypoxia pathway genes) significantly show smaller distances than other TFs, whereas larger distances between perturbations generally have little information, and Isomap is able to retain information of local structure in distance space. After embedding in high dimension space with Isomap, we clustered perturbations using affinity propagation (implemented by sklearn.cluster.AffinityPropagation) in Isomap embedded space to find clusters in an unbiased way without defining the number of clusters.


#### Comparison between CHD genes and de novo variants
  The list of congenital heart disease genes are obtained from CHDgene9. Among CHD genes, we extracted overlapped genes with our tested TFs, and calculated p-value of enrichment using a hypergeometric test. For de novo variants, we obtained the list of de novo variants from multiple studies10,22,23.


#### Transposable element (TE) analysis
  scRNA-Seq transcriptome reads were mapped with scTE48. Differentially expressed TEs were detected by pySpade80. The phylogenetic distribution and family of each TE is annotated from Dfam 3.381. ZNF561 ChIP-Seq data shown in Fig. 4E is downloaded from the ENCODE consortium (https://www.encodeproject.org/experiments/ENCSR350GZC/).


#### TF dosage analysis
  We performed energy distance analysis at the gRNA level compared to 2000 randomly selected cells with non-targeting gRNAs. We excluded gRNAs with less than 30 cells assigned. Next, we identified testable transcription factors for dosage analysis using the following criteria: (1) at least 3 gRNAs are analyzable after the previous filtering step (2) dynamic range of expression level (maximum normalized expression level - minimum normalized expression level) is more than 0.2 (3) Pearson correlation coefficient between normalized expression and energy distance is less than −0.5. (4) Constraint information is available in the gnomAD database. After these filtering steps, 24 TFs remained. To note, the bottleneck of this filtering step is dynamic range for each TFs because we observed that most gRNAs work efficiently. We fit this expression-energy distance plot with two different two-parameter functions that satisfy (0,0) potentially (non-targeting control). For linear fitting, 𝑦 = (𝑥 − 1) + 𝑏 is used. For curve fitting, we used 𝑦 = (𝑒(−𝑏(𝑥 − 1)) − 1) is used. In this formula, y is energy distance and x is relative expression. Data are fit by least squares method using curve_fit function in scipy.optimize library. Details can be found in the code, deposited on pubmed (see code availability). For the comparison between dosage sensitivity and the gnomAD database, we used transcripts marked as “canonical” in the gnomAD57 v4.1.0 database.


#### Gene programs analysis
  Gene program analysis was performed with consensus non-negative matrix factorization (cNMF)40. cNMF was run for 100 iterations for top 2000 highly variable genes using 0.1 as clustering metric. The optimal k (k=250) was identified based on unique gene ontology terms82, significant perturbations identified, and recovery of a rare perturbation (REST) as a positive control.


##### Significant perturbation calculation.
  cNMF assigns a score for each cell for every program. The mean score was calculated for each target for each program. Similarly the mean score was calculated for non-targeting control. Log2 fold change was calculated between targeting and non-targeting for every program. Mann-Whitney U test was performed to determine significance and Benjamin-Hochberg for adjusted p-value. A cutoff P-value of 0.001 was used based on the olfactory receptor genes as negative control83.


#### Public mouse datasets
  Mouse heart phenotype data was obtained from International Mouse Phenotype Consortium41. We queried the website (https://www.mousephenotype.org/) for individual genes to identify the earliest heart phenotype in knockout mice.


#### Identification of TF-TF interactions from gene program analysis
  TF-gene interactions were quantified across 250 gene programs. To assess the significance of observed interactions, we applied a bipartite shuffle algorithm, which preserves the number of nodes and edges while randomizing connections. To determine the best-fit distribution for modeling the null distribution of TF interactions, we used the Fitter package to compare multiple candidate distributions (normal, gamma, exponential, beta, lognormal, Poisson, and binomial). The most frequent best-fit distribution across all tested interactions was identified and used to estimate p-values. Adjusted p-values were calculated using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, with a significance threshold of 0.05. To ensure biologically meaningful interactions, we applied two additional filters: (1) Interactions with an observed count <2 were removed; (2) Interactions between two promoters of the same gene were excluded.


#### Differential expression (DE) analysis
  pySpade (version 0.1.6) is used to calculate differential expressed genes80. pySpade ‘process’ function takes the output files from our in-house pre-processing pipelines. The randomization method of the DErand function is set to be ‘equal’ for all the datasets. Complementary cells are used in the background sets except time-course datasets. Non-target cells are the background in time-course datasets. All hits reported are FDR < 0.1, with fold change >20% as cutoff, and genes expressed in at least 5% of cells.


#### Motif scanning
  To search for transcription factor motifs in the promoters of DE genes, the JASPAR database (2024) is used to scan the TF motifs within the promoter region of DE genes84. The promoter regions are defined as +/−250 bp of transcription start site.


#### Network Construction and visualization
  To construct the CHD-centric gene regulatory network (GRN), CHD-associated genes were identified as previously described and subsequently classified as either transcription factors (TFs) or non-TFs.

  For the sub-network regulating non-TF CHD genes, we utilized the global hit table generated by pySpade. Non-TF CHD genes were first identified from the gene_names column. These genes were then mapped to their corresponding regulatory regions (from the region column), which were associated with specific TF perturbations. This set of putative interactions was filtered to retain only those where a binding motif of the perturbing TF was present within the promoter region of the target non-TF CHD gene.

  The resulting TF-to-gene interactions were used to construct a directed graph using the NetworkX library85 (version 2.6.3). The network was then visualized using the Kamada-Kawai layout algorithm.
