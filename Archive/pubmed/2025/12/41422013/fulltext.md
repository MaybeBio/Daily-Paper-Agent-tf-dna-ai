# Integrative single-cell transcriptomic analysis reveals immunomodulatory hub genes and candidate compounds for HIV-associated chronic inflammation


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12985-025-03030-z.


## Introduction
  The human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) is a retrovirus that attacks the immune system, specifically targeting CD4+ T cells, which are vital for defense against infections and diseases [1]. The depletion of these cells by HIV progressively weakens the immune system, ultimately leading to acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), characterized by heightened susceptibility to opportunistic infections and malignancies [2]. Despite significant advances in understanding HIV virology and pathogenesis, the virus remains a global health crisis, with 37.7 million individuals living with HIV as of 2020 (UNAIDS, 2020) [3]. The persistent challenges in the management of HIV highlight the urgent need for innovative diagnostic and therapeutic strategies [4].

  Recent advancements in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) have improved our ability to examine HIV infection at single-cell resolution [5]. This methodology enables profiling gene expression within individual cells, providing insights into HIV-host cell interactions. Through this approach, researchers can identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with HIV infection. The integration of multi-omics data, encompassing transcriptomics, proteomics, and epigenomics, has further advanced our understanding of regulatory networks underlying HIV pathogenesis [6]. These complementary approaches illuminate different aspects of viral-host interactions: transcriptomic analyses reveal gene expression changes associated with HIV infection, proteomic studies identify protein interactions, and epigenomic data uncover regulatory modifications linked to disease progression. Functional enrichment analyses, including Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway analyses, can be used to examine DEGs to identify their involvement in biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions, potentially revealing disruptions in immune response, viral defense, and apoptosis pathways in HIV infection. Pathway analysis tools further characterize dysregulated signaling pathways, including T-cell activation, cytokine signaling, and interferon-stimulated gene (ISG) responses. While interferon activation can suppress viral replication, excessive stimulation may contribute to chronic immune activation, accelerating disease progression [7].

  Protein-protein interaction (PPI) [8] network analysis can identify key host genes involved in cellular responses to HIV infection, highlighting pathways that may offer therapeutic opportunities. Complementing this approach, regulatory network analyses reveal transcription factors and microRNAs that modulate gene expression during HIV infection, providing insight into the transcriptional control of these pathways. The identification of these regulatory elements is crucial for developing targeted therapies that enhance antiviral defenses while avoiding exacerbation of chronic immune activation, a key challenge in HIV treatment [9, 10].

  Despite effective antiretroviral therapy (ART), many HIV-infected individuals experience persistent immune activation with elevated inflammatory mediators, significantly impacting their long-term health [11]. This chronic inflammation contributes to serious non-AIDS comorbidities, including cardiovascular disease, neurocognitive decline, and metabolic disorders [12]. Large-scale transcriptomic analyses of immune cells from HIV-positive individuals have provided insight into this phenomenon, consistently revealing inflammatory signatures characterized by elevated expression of interferon-stimulated genes, immune checkpoint regulators such as PD-1 and LAG3, and proinflammatory cytokines [13]. While these transcriptomic studies identify which genes are differentially expressed, integrating this data with protein-protein interaction (PPI) mapping provides further functional insights by highlighting the interactions and modules through which these genes operate. This integrated approach allows us to identify hub proteins within these networks that may serve as strategic targets to reduce systemic inflammation in HIV patients.

  In this study, we developed a pipeline by integrating two publicly available datasets to identify consistent differentially expressed genes across independent HIV studies. We constructed protein-protein interaction networks, highlight critical hub genes, and performed regulatory interaction network analyses to uncover transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulators. This integrative approach connects single-cell and regulatory datasets to actionable insight, culminating in the identification of candidate genes and repurposable drugs that may mitigate chronic inflammation in HIV patients. By unifying multi-omic data analysis, network modeling, and molecular docking, our approach efficiently distills transcriptomic datasets into potentially relevant therapeutic targets and candidate compounds. These computational findings could serve as starting points for targeted experimental studies, representing a step towards bridging the gap between large-scale expression studies and therapeutic applications.


## Results

### HIV heterogeneity and identification of DEGs
  We conducted single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from HIV-positive individuals and healthy controls using the publicly available dataset GSE157829 [14]. This dataset was selected for its investigation of immune cell exhaustion in chronic HIV infection across diverse PBMC populations. The study included six HIV-infected donors undergoing antiretroviral therapy (ART) with varying viral loads: three with high viral loads (>100,000 RNA copies/mL plasma) and three with low viral loads (<20 RNA copies/mL plasma). Following data preprocessing and dimensionality reduction, we visualized the results using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP), as shown in Fig. 1. The UMAP plot reveals distinct clustering of immune cell types, with each cluster annotated according to cell identity. The separation between clusters indicates distinct gene expression profiles among different cell types, highlighting cellular heterogeneity within PBMCs and identifying specific cell populations affected by HIV infection. While the original GSE157829 study [14] identified nine major immune cell clusters and eight T-cell subclusters using t-SNE dimensionality reduction, our UMAP analysis identified seven distinct clusters. This difference in cluster number most likely results from our use of different analytical tools specifically, we used UMAP instead of t-SNE for visualization and applied a clustering resolution that groups cells more broadly based on their gene expression similarities, whereas the original study used a method that detected more subtle distinctions between closely related cell populations.   While we acknowledge the limitations of comparing different technological platforms, the convergence of DEGs across these datasets strengthens confidence in their biological relevance to HIV pathogenesis. This cross-platform validation approach helps filter out platform-specific noise and highlights genes consistently differentially expressed in HIV infection, providing a more focused set of candidates for further investigation.


### Investigation of functional enrichment and pathways in DEGs
  We performed functional analysis of pathway enrichment (Figure S3) and gene ontology (GO) (Figure S4) using the DAVID functional annotation tool [18], focusing on the identified DEGs. We examined the top enriched GO terms across Biological Process (BP), Cellular Component (CC), and Molecular Function (MF), as well as pathways from KEGG [19], Reactome [20], and WIKI databases [21]. We selected the top 15 disease-related pathways for further investigation based on their p-values and enrichment scores, prioritizing those with the highest statistical significance and biological relevance. Our analysis showed the involvement of multiple biological processes critical to HIV pathogenesis, including defense response to viruses, innate immune response, and apoptotic regulation. The cellular component analysis highlighted various subcellular locations, including the cytosol, cytoplasm, endoplasmic reticulum membrane, and mitochondrion. Key molecular functions associated with the DEGs included identical protein binding, double-stranded DNA binding, and integrin binding.

  KEGG pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment in pathways also associated with other viral infections, including Epstein-Barr virus, COVID-19, and Hepatitis C, reflecting common gene annotations linked to immune responses. Reactome pathway analysis highlighted processes such as the Immune System, Cytokine Signaling in the Immune System, and the Innate Immune System. Similarly, WIKI pathway analysis showed involvement in networks related to SARS-CoV-2 signaling, Measles virus infection, and the TGF-beta signaling pathway. Overall, the alignment of our findings with known viral-host interaction pathways supports that the identified DEGs reflect biologically meaningful immune-related responses rather than statistical noise.

### Performance measure of the identified hubs using ROC analysis
  To assess whether our computationally identified hub genes could discriminate between HIV-infected samples and healthy controls, we evaluated their performance as potential biomarkers using ROC curve analysis of ISG15, STAT1, MX1, BCL2, and TNFSF10. Among these, three genes demonstrated strong discriminatory ability: STAT1 showed the highest performance (AUC = 0.863), followed by MX1 (AUC = 0.845) and ISG15 (AUC = 0.729) (Figure S5). In contrast, BCL2 and TNFSF10 (both with AUC = 0.5) showed no discriminatory ability between HIV-infected and healthy individuals, effectively ruling them out as potential diagnostic biomarkers despite their prominence in the network analysis.


### Drug repurposing through molecular docking simulation
  To identify potential therapeutics targeting our identified hub proteins, we conducted molecular docking simulations using 33 compounds from the DrugBank database [31]. Drug repurposing offers significant advantages over de novo drug development, including reduced development timelines, lower costs, and established safety profiles for compounds already in clinical use [32]. Our selection criteria prioritized compounds with known anti-inflammatory properties, potential immunomodulatory effects, and favorable pharmacokinetic profiles. The compound library included diverse therapeutic classes: established antiretroviral agents (nucleoside/non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors and integrase inhibitors such as Abacavir, Efavirenz, Nevirapine, and Dolutegravir), plant-derived flavonoids and polyphenols (Apigenin, Luteolin, Quercetin, Curcumin, and Resveratrol), and additional antiviral drugs (Remdesivir, Sofosbuvir, Favipiravir, and Ribavirin). This broad selection was intended to cover compounds with reported antiviral and host-targeted activities relevant to HIV pathogenesis. For each compound, we performed molecular docking simulations against the hub gene products STAT1, ISG15, and MX1, followed by ADMET analysis to assess pharmacokinetic profiles and clinical applicability.

  After assessment of binding parameters, including binding energy, interaction types, and binding site compatibility, two compounds emerged as potentail candidates: Dolutegravir (PubChem CID: 54726191) [33] and Luteolin (PubChem CID: 5280445) [34]. Dolutegravir is an FDA-approved HIV integrase strand transfer inhibitor currently used in antiretroviral therapy, while Luteolin is a naturally occurring flavonoid found in various fruits and vegetables with demonstrated anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties [35].

  These compounds demonstrated significant binding affinities to our hub proteins STAT1, MX1, and ISG15, components of the type I interferon signaling pathway [36, 37]. In chronic HIV infection, persistent upregulation of these genes contributes to sustained inflammation and immune exhaustion, making them potential targets for therapeutic intervention. The binding affinities of Dolutegravir and Luteolin were −10.2, −9.1, and −8.5 (kcal/mol) for STAT1, MX1, and ISG15, respectively. While these values suggest energetically favorable interactions in silico, their ability to modulate protein activity in biological systems requires experimental confirmation.

  Molecular interaction analysis revealed predicted binding mechanisms for each protein-ligand pair, though these interactions remain to be validated through experimental studies. Dolutegravir was predicted to form a conventional hydrogen bond with STAT1 at ASN357:HD21 (distance: 2.28 Å), suggesting stable binding within a hydrophobic pocket (Fig. 5A). This interaction could potentially interfere with STAT1 dimerization or DNA binding. ## Discussion
  Despite advances in antiretroviral therapy, HIV remains a global health challenge due to persistent immune dysfunction and chronic inflammation [44]. HIV primarily targets CD4+ T cells, gradually compromising immune function and leading to AIDS-related complications. While single-cell technologies can improve our understanding of HIV pathophysiology, developing effective treatments requires deeper insights into the molecular mechanisms driving infection and disease progression.

  Here, we examined gene expression profiles associated with HIV, encompassing gene activities, pathways, and regulatory elements such as transcription factors (TFs) and microRNAs (miRNAs). The utilization of scRNA-seq data from peripheral blood cells offered an avenue for identifying distinct cell types and gaining further insights into HIV pathogenesis than possible with bulk RNA-seq approaches. Our clustering analysis revealed significant heterogeneity in HIV across various dimensions. The single-cell approach demonstrated differential expression of STAT1 and ISG15 predominantly in exhausted CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, a nuance masked in bulk RNA-seq datasets. The hub genes identified in our protein-protein interaction network, ISG15, STAT1, MX1, BCL2, and TNFSF10 play central roles in antiviral response and immune regulation [26, 27].

  STAT1 mediates responses to interferon signaling [45], crucial for antiviral defense [46] and immune responses against tumors [47]. Its activation leads to transcription of genes fundamental to antiviral and antitumor activities, highlighting the importance of balancing its protective functions against potential overactivation. ISG15 functions in ubiquitin-like protein conjugation, modulating antiviral signaling cascades and innate immune responses. This activity has been implicated in controlling HIV replication, suggesting that enhancing ISG15-mediated modification could inform new antiviral strategies [48]. MX1 influences cellular growth and differentiation through transcriptional repression of Myc target genes, which has been noted to contribute to its tumor-suppressive role across diverse cancer types [49, 50]. These three genes (STAT1, ISG15, and MX1) have also been noted to mediate antiviral responses to HIV in multiple immune cell types, including CD4+ T cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells [51, 52]. TNFSF10 selectively induces apoptosis in cancer cells while sparing normal tissue [53], demonstrating potential in challenging therapeutic contexts [54]. BCL2 inhibits pro-apoptotic proteins such as BAX and BAK, conferring resistance to cell death under stress, a mechanism frequently exploited in lymphoma and other malignancies and a validated target for BH3-mimetic drugs [55, 56]. The expression of these genes across various cell types and their role in multiple signaling pathways suggest that these genes are important components of antiviral immunity, cellular homeostasis, and inflammatory regulation. We examined if these hub genes could serve as potential biomarkers for HIV infection progression and treatment response, and our ROC analysis suggests that STAT1, MX1, and ISG15 may have discriminatory abilities.

  Our regulatory network analysis revealed ten miRNAs related to these hub genes: hsa-miR-155-5p, hsa-miR-29c-3p, hsa-miR-124-3p, hsa-miR-133a-3p, hsa-let-7b-5p, hsa-miR-34a-5p, hsa-miR-23b-3p, hsa-miR-16-5p, hsa-miR-129-2−3p, and hsa-miR-26a-5p. We also identified ten transcription factors: TFAP2A, SREBF1, POU2F2, RELA, PPARG, FOXC1, STAT1, GATA2, NF-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} 				\usepackage{amsmath} 				\usepackage{wasysym}  				\usepackage{amsfonts}  				\usepackage{amssymb}  				\usepackage{amsbsy} 				\usepackage{mathrsfs} 				\usepackage{upgreek} 				\setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} 				\begin{document}$$\kappa$$\end{document}B1, and STAT3. These TFs orchestrate gene expression in response to cellular signals, and their dysregulation has been implicated in immune responses, inflammation, and cancer [57, 58]. Our analysis further demonstrated the involvement of these hub genes and their regulatory networks in potential comorbidities associated with HIV progression, including weight loss, bipolar disorder, gastrointestinal conditions, and cardiovascular complications [42, 43]. This suggests that there maybe an interplay between HIV infection and systemic health.

  Molecular docking studies identified Dolutegravir and Luteolin as compounds that may interact with our identified hub genes. Dolutegravir formed conventional hydrogen bonds with ASN357 in STAT1 and stabilized binding via hydrophobic interactions in ISG15. These interactions suggest potential for modulating protein functions that could affect immune activation pathways in HIV infection. ADMET analysis demonstrated favorable pharmacokinetic profiles for both compounds. Dolutegravir exhibited excellent intestinal absorption (95.4%) and was non-hepatotoxic, though its classification as a hERG II inhibitor suggests potential cardiotoxicity risk. Luteolin showed moderate oral absorption (88.5%) with a favorable safety profile, without hERG inhibition or hepatotoxicity. Targeting STAT1, MX1, and ISG15 may offer approaches for addressing interferon signaling and inflammation observed in HIV progression. While inhibiting host antiviral genes presents potential risks, selective modulation using small molecules such as Dolutegravir and Luteolin could potentially impact pathways associated with inflammation. Notably, Luteolin has previously been suggested to have therapeutic effects in other chronic inflammatory conditions, including rheumatoid arthritis, osteoarthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, and chronic atrophic gastritis [59].


## Limitations
  Our study has several important limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. The integration of data from different technological platforms (scRNA-seq and microarray) introduces potential biases due to inherent differences in sensitivity, dynamic range, and data processing methods. Additionally, the primary scRNA-seq dataset was derived from a limited sample size (6 HIV-infected donors and 1 healthy control), which may not capture the full spectrum of biological variability in HIV infection. While our computational predictions identified candidates for biomarkers and potential therapeutic compounds, these findings represent preliminary insights rather than clinically validated targets. As with any computational study, our findings would benefit from experimental validation. The hub genes, regulatory networks, and potential therapeutic compounds identified through our analyses should be considered as candidates for further investigation in appropriate experimental models before any conclusions about their clinical utility in HIV infection can be drawn. Future work building upon these computational foundations will be essential for translating hypothesis generated via bioinformatics based studies into meaningful advances in HIV diagnosis and treatment.


## Conclusion
  HIV remains a significant global health challenge, necessitating the development of innovative diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. In this study, we employed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and bioinformatics analyses to identify differentially expressed genes in HIV-infected CD4+ T cells. From an initial broad set of candidates, we narrowed our focus to three key genes: STAT1, MX1, and ISG15, which emerged as potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets. These genes demonstrated diagnostic potential based on receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with STAT1 showing the strongest discriminatory power. Our regulatory network analysis identified transcription factors and microRNAs that may contribute to HIV pathogenesis through regulation of these hub genes. Molecular docking simulations identified two compounds, Dolutegravir, an established antiretroviral, and Luteolin, a natural compound with immunomodulatory properties, that may potentially interact with these targets. Both compounds showed favorable pharmacokinetic profiles in our ADMET analysis, suggesting their potential suitability for further investigation.

  To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to integrate scRNA-seq and molecular docking to identify STAT1, ISG15, and MX1 as both potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets that could guide future research into modulating inflammatory pathways in HIV infection. By integrating scRNA-seq analysis, network modeling, and molecular docking, this framework transforms transcriptomic data into defined molecular targets for experimental investigation. While our integrative analytical pipeline requires further refinement, this initial application demonstrates its potential value in systematically identifying disease-relevant biomarkers and therapeutic leads from transcriptomic data, offering a streamline approach for future investigations across diverse pathological conditions.


## Methods

### Collection and processing of scRNA-seq data
  We analyzed publicly available scRNA-seq data (GSE157829) comprising peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from six HIV-infected patients and one healthy donor [14]. The dataset, sequenced on Illumina HiSeq 2500, was downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database and processed using Seurat package V3.1.1 [60] in R V3.6.1. For quality control, we retained cells expressing a minimum of 200 unique genes and filtered for genes expressed in at least three cells. The filtered data underwent log-transformation normalization before subsequent analysis. To evaluate data quality, we assessed metrics using violin plots (Figure S1), including nFeature_RNA (number of detected genes per cell), nCount_RNA (total molecules detected per cell), and percent.mt (percentage of mitochondrial gene reads). This assessment was used to identify if potential outliers and batch effects were present prior to futher analysis. The single-cell approach provided critical advantages over bulk RNA sequencing by resolving cell-type-specific gene expression patterns, enabling the identification of distinct regulatory mechanisms across CD4+ T cells, monocytes, and other immune cell populations involved in HIV pathogenesis.

  To strengthen the biological relevance of our findings and minimize dataset-specific biases, we integrated multiple publicly available datasets from the GEO database. While GSE157829 [14] served as our primary resource for identifying DEGs, we employed GSE6740 [15] for independent validation of our DEG and diagnostic performance. This multi-dataset approach was done to improve the reliability and generalizability of the identified genes.


### Clustering and DEG identification
  Dimensionality reduction was first performed using principal component analysis (PCA), followed by Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) for visualization. Cell clustering was performed using the Louvain algorithm with a resolution parameter of 0.8, based on the top 15 principal components. Marker genes for each cluster were identified using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with adjusted p-value < 0.01 and log fold change >1. The expression levels of established marker genes were assessed using Seurat’s VlnPlot function to assist in cluster annotation [61]. DEGs between clusters were analyzed using the Seurat package in R.


### Functional enrichment and pathway analysis of DEGs
  Gene set enrichment analysis for gene ontology (GO) functional enrichment and pathway analysis of DEGs was carried out using the DAVID functional annotation tool (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/tools.jsp) [18]. We considered pathways and GO terms with a Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) adjusted p-value < 0.05 to account for multiple hypothesis testing. Additionally, pathway and GO analyses were employed to annotate and categorize the enrichment study of biological processes, cellular components, molecular functions, and gene clusters. This analysis helped in identifying the biological processes and pathways that are significantly impacted in the context of HIV infection.


### PPI network analysis of DEGs
  Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks provide a visualization of potential physical or functional associations between proteins, helping researchers identify which proteins may interact with each other. In this study, we used the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes (STRING v11.0) [8] database to construct a network of predicted protein interactions among our differentially expressed genes. We visualized and analyzed this network using Cytoscape (version 3.7.1). To identify key proteins within this network, we applied eleven topological analysis methods (Betweenness, Degree, Closeness, Bottleness, MCC, MNC, DMNC, Stress, EPC, Radiality, and Clustering Coefficient) using the Cytoscape plugin cytoHubba [22] (Table 1). These centrality measures are deterministic computations derived from the network topology, producing consistent rankings for a given network configuration. Utilizing multiple complementary measures enhances the robustness of the analysis by reflecting different dimensions of node importance within the PPI network. Additionally, GeneMANIA (https://genemania.org) [30], was employed to examine a co-expression network for the identified hub genes.


### ROC analysis of the hub genes
  Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to evaluate the discriminatory potential of the identified hub genes (ISG15, STAT1, BCL2, MX1, and TNFSF10) in distinguishing HIV-infected individuals from healthy controls. For each gene, expression values were used as predictors, and HIV infection status (infected vs. healthy) was used as the binary outcome variable. ROC curves were constructed and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to assess the classification performance. The SRplot web server [62] was used to generate the ROC plots.


### Regulatory interaction network analysis of hub genes
  Using NetworkAnalyst 3.0 [29], we created a regulatory interaction network (Hubs-miRNAs, Hubs-diseases, and Hubs-TFs). This tool featured six interaction databases for gene-miRNAs, including TarBase, miRTarbase, and miRecords, as well as six databases for gene-TFs, including ENCODE, JASPAR, and ChEA, and six databases for gene-diseases, including DisGeNET. For the Hubs-miRNAs, Hubs-diseases, and Hubs-TFs interaction networks, we used the databases TarBase, JASPAR, and DisGeNET, respectively.


### Lipinski’s rule analysis with SwissADME
  To evaluate the drug-likeness of potential therapeutic compounds targeting HIV-related immune regulators, we analyzed a curated set of 105 compounds. The drug-likeness properties were assessed using SwissADME [38], with compound structures provided as SMILES representations. SwissADME calculated a comprehensive range of molecular descriptors and pharmacokinetic parameters, including logP, polar surface area, molecular weight, number of heavy atoms, hydrogen bond donors and acceptors, hydroxyl groups, rotatable bonds, and molecular volume [38].

  The platform also evaluated bioavailability scores and assessed potential biological targets including GPCRs, ion channels, enzymes, and nuclear receptors, along with reporting violations of Lipinski’s Rule of Five. After comprehensive screening against Lipinski’s criteria for oral bioavailability, 33 compounds were selected for further investigation, as shown in Table S1. For these 33 compounds, ligand optimization and preparation were conducted using PyRx software with mmff94 force field integration to ensure optimal conformations for docking simulations [63].


### Protein and ligand preparation for molecular docking
  The crystal structures of ISG15 (PDB ID: 1Z2M) [64], STAT1 (PDB ID: 1YVL) [65], and MX1 (PDB ID: 3SZR) [66] were obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank [67]. Water molecules were removed using Discovery Studio, and energy minimization was performed with the GROMOS96 43B1 force field in SWISS PDB Viewer [68]. Our ligand selection followed a dual strategy: (1) inclusion of FDA-approved HIV drugs such as Dolutegravir as reference ligands and positive controls; and (2) selection of natural compounds like Luteolin based on documented immunomodulatory and antiviral properties. This approach aimed to identify compounds capable of modulating immune exhaustion pathways rather than directly inhibiting viral enzymes. All ligand molecules were downloaded in SDF format from the PubChem database and converted to PDBQT format using Open Babel [69] for compatibility with docking software.


### Molecular docking and interaction analysis
  Virtual screening was conducted using AutoDock Vina integrated within PyRx to examine interactions between ligands and target proteins. In the docking process, ligands were treated as flexible while target proteins remained rigid to reduce computational complexity. Ligands were prioritized based on docking scores, binding affinities, and molecular interactions with key residues, with preference given to those exhibiting the lowest RMSD values and most negative docking scores. The docked poses were examined using Discovery Studio tools to analyze hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and binding energy profiles. This analysis focused on binding residues and interaction types that might influence protein function.


### ADMET analysis
  Compounds demonstrating high binding affinity were subjected to ADMET (Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion, and Toxicity) evaluation. SwissADME software [38] was employed to assess pharmacokinetic properties using canonical SMILES provided by PubChem for selected ligands. The analysis included evaluation of drug-likeness parameters, bioavailability, and potential metabolic interactions. Additionally, toxicity prediction tools from pkCSM were utilized to evaluate potential safety concerns [70], including hepatotoxicity, cardiotoxicity, and other relevant toxicity endpoints. This ADMET assessment helped identify compounds with favorable pharmacokinetic profiles and minimal toxicity risks.
