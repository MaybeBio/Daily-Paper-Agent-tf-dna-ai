# ProMoHGT: a heterogeneous graph transformer with graph contrastive learning for robust microbial protein function prediction


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12383-2.


## Introduction
  Proteins are central executors of life’s processes: they drive metabolism through enzymatic catalysis, regulate gene expression as transcription factors or histone modifiers, function as receptors, kinases, or adaptors in signal transduction pathways, and maintain cellular shape and motility through dynamic remodeling of cytoskeletal filaments such as actin, microtubules, and intermediate filaments [1–4]. Precise annotation of these diverse functions is essential for elucidating molecular mechanisms, identifying disease targets, and advancing drug discovery and bioengineering. In environmental and clinical microbiome studies, accurate prediction of protein functions across microbial species can shed light on community interactions, reveal antibiotic resistance mechanisms, and facilitate the development of novel biotherapeutics. However, more than 99% of sequences in the UniProt database lack experimentally validated functional annotations, severely limiting functional interpretation from single strains to complex microbial consortia [5]. To standardize functional descriptions, bioinformatics has established controlled vocabularies and classification systems such as the Gene Ontology (GO) [6], Enzyme Commission (EC) numbers [7], and KEGG pathways [8]. In the context of this study, protein function annotation specifically refers to the prediction of GO terms—covering molecular function, biological process, and cellular component—and EC numbers. These two systems are among the most widely used, experimentally curated, and validated frameworks, providing standardized resources for evaluating computational methods. While GO and EC terms do not exhaustively capture all aspects of protein function, they represent the most practical and reliable standards currently available for large-scale prediction and benchmarking [9]. Given the high cost and time demands of experimental assays, ontology-based computational methods have become indispensable for large-scale, high-throughput protein function annotation. Consequently, there is an urgent need for efficient and accurate in silico prediction frameworks, particularly those tailored to microbial proteins.

  Early efforts in protein function annotation were grounded in the homology principle, inferring the roles of query sequences by measuring their similarity to proteins of known function. The foundational tools include the global-alignment algorithm FASTA [10], the local‐alignment heuristic BLAST [11], and its iterative extension PSI‐BLAST [12]. FASTA rapidly computes overall sequence similarity when the query and target lengths are comparable, whereas BLAST excels at detecting high‐scoring local alignments within shorter sequence fragments. PSI‐BLAST further enhances sensitivity to distant homologs by constructing a position‐specific scoring matrix from initial alignments and iteratively refining it. Although these homology‐based approaches perform exceptionally well for proteins with high sequence conservation, their predictive accuracy and coverage remain limited when confronted with proteins exhibiting low sequence similarity or considerable structural diversity.

  With the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence, a variety of machine-learning techniques have been widely adopted for protein function prediction. Cai et al. [13] developed SVMProt, an online tool based on support vector machines that, by training on representative proteins and Pfam seed families, classifies primary sequences into 54 functional families with accuracies ranging from 69.1% to 99.6%, thereby complementing traditional sequence-alignment methods in cases of remote homology or functional divergence. Nguyen et al. [14] introduced a Naïve Bayes framework that incorporates protein–protein interaction network topology to boost recall; when evaluated on the HPRD and BioGRID datasets, this method significantly increased recall without sacrificing precision. ProMK [15] achieves selective integration and noise suppression of high-throughput, multi-source proteomic data through iterative optimization of multi-kernel weights and multi-label classifier losses, outperforming existing multi-kernel multi-label approaches on both public benchmarks and synthetic datasets. Furthermore, several other machine-learning–based strategies have been proposed for protein function annotation [16, 17].

  In recent years, deep learning has transformed protein function prediction by unifying diverse biological data. Kulmanov et al. [18, 19] used CNNs to fuse sequence features, cross-species interaction networks, and GO’s hierarchical structure, outperforming BLAST in CAFA benchmarks. Gligorijević et al. [20]’s DeepFRI applied graph convolutional networks to combine language‐model embeddings with experimental and modeled structures, adding class activation mapping for residue‐level annotation. Yuan et al. [21]’s SPROF‐GO is an alignment‐free method that leverages pretrained embeddings, self‐attention pooling, and label propagation to boost AUPRC by 14.5% (MF), 27.3% (BP), and 10.1% (CC), generalizing well to nonhomologous and novel species. More recently, Wu et al. [22]’s CFAGO integrated single‐species PPI networks and multiple protein attributes via multi‐head attention, achieving at least 7.6% (micro‐AUPRC), 6.9% (macro‐AUPRC), and 11.7% (F-max) improvements over state‐of-the‐art network methods on human and mouse datasets. Together, these advances highlight deep learning’s capacity to integrate heterogeneous data and markedly improve prediction accuracy, coverage, and interpretability.

  The recent breakthrough of AlphaFold2 in high-accuracy tertiary structure prediction has enabled the rapid generation of vast numbers of reliable protein structures, offering unprecedented opportunities for structure-based functional annotation [23–27]. Concurrently, protein language models such as ESM and ProtBERT [28–32], pretrained in a self-supervised manner on billions of sequences, efficiently capture both contextual residue relationships and evolutionary signals, yielding embeddings rich in biological information for downstream tasks. The integration of these two advances—high-throughput structural modeling and deep sequence embedding—provides a powerful foundation for elucidating protein function, uncovering latent sequence–structure correlations, and driving next-generation annotation methodologies.

  Most existing protein function prediction methods face three major limitations. First, few models are specifically designed for microbial proteins. Instead, they often rely on data from humans or model organisms, which prevents them from fully capturing the unique evolutionary dynamics and sequence diversity of microbial proteomes. Second, many approaches depend on only one type of information, such as sequence homology, network topology, or structural features, and thus lack a unified and efficient mechanism for integrating multiple data sources. Third, current models do not adequately account for long-range residue interactions or the global topological context of proteins, which limits their ability to identify comprehensive functional modules.To address these challenges, we constructed the first benchmark dataset dedicated to microbial protein function annotation and developed ProMoHGT, a multi-pathway heterogeneous Transformer framework (overview in Fig. 1). ProMoHGT integrates three complementary sources of information: (i) sequence embeddings from the ESM-2 language model, which capture evolutionary conservation and contextual residue relationships; (ii) spatial proximity graph features derived from AlphaFold2-predicted structures, which describe three-dimensional residue adjacency; and (iii) physicochemical encodings of amino acids, which represent charge, polarity, and other chemical properties. At the core of the framework, a heterogeneous Transformer employs learnable super-nodes that interact with all residue nodes through multi-head self-attention, enabling the aggregation of long-range dependencies and global topology while preserving local detail. To further enhance robustness and mitigate overfitting, ProMoHGT combines supervised learning with graph contrastive regularization, which enforces representation consistency across perturbed views of the same protein graph. This hybrid training strategy substantially improves both accuracy and generalization in microbial protein function prediction.

## Materials and methods

### Dataset construction
  We constructed a high-quality microbial protein function annotation dataset by adapting the protocol of Gligorijević et al. [20]. In contrast to comprehensive protein databases [33], which were last comprehensively updated between 2019 and 2022 and contain many unverified or electronically inferred annotations (IEA), our dataset is updated to 2025 and includes only experimentally supported microbial proteins curated under stringent quality control. All protein chains were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) and clustered at 95% sequence identity to eliminate redundancy. For every sequence, we preferentially used its experimentally determined structure from the PDB; when no experimental structure was available, a predicted structure was generated using AlphaFold2. Residue-level graphs were then constructed to capture three-dimensional conformations.

  Functional annotations were obtained from SIFTS [34] and UniProtKB [35] and transferred only when the corresponding PDB and UniProtKB sequences shared at least 90% identity with ≥ 70% coverage. We retained only annotations supported by experimental evidence codes (EXP, IDA, IPI, IMP, IGI, IEP, TAS, IC), excluded IEA annotations, and filtered GO terms by information content to ensure specificity. Only GO terms represented by at least 50 non-redundant Swiss-Prot or PDB chains were kept, and EC numbers were restricted to levels 3 and 4 of the hierarchy. Functional annotations were transferred prior to dataset partitioning. After annotation transfer, all sequences were globally clustered at 95% sequence identity using CD-HIT, and entire clusters were assigned to a single subset to prevent any sequence pairs with high identity (≥ 90%) from being distributed between the training and test sets. The subsequent stratification by sequence identity thresholds (40%, 50%, 70%, 95%) was performed only within the test set for evaluation purposes.The non-redundant dataset was clustered at 95% identity and divided into training (80%), validation (10%), and test (10%) subsets. The test set contained only experimentally determined PDB structures with validated annotations and was further stratified based on sequence identity thresholds (40%, 50%, 70%, and 95%). Specifically, we used CD-HIT [36] to compute pairwise sequence identity across all protein sequences in the test set. Based on these identity values, the test set was stratified into four distinct homology-based subsets, ensuring that the protein samples in each subset represented different levels of sequence similarity. This stratification allowed us to rigorously evaluate model generalization across a range of homology levels.For the GO term prediction task, the label distributions were as follows: Molecular Function (MF: 267 in training / 257 in validation / 264 in test), Biological Process (BP: 621 / 595 / 616), and Cellular Component (CC: 92 / 92 / 79). For the EC number prediction task, the dataset contains 273 EC classes. After splitting, the training set covers all classes, the validation set covers 221 EC classes, and the test set covers 225 EC classes, ensuring representative label distributions across subsets.This rigorous assembly yielded a comprehensive and reliable microbial benchmark that underpins robust protein function prediction and analysis.


### Graph construction
  To capture geometric information, each protein is represented as an undirected graph G = ( V, E), where V denotes the set of nodes (residues) and E the set of edges. We first derive per-residue feature embeddings and then infer the graph topology from the protein’s contact geometry.

  In the feature-extraction stage, two complementary types of sequence descriptors are computed for every amino acid residue. The first comprises classical physicochemical properties—such as hydrophobicity, polarity, charge state, and molecular weight—which characterize each residue’s chemical attributes and interaction preferences [37]. The second consists of high-dimensional semantic embeddings obtained from ESM-2, a large-scale protein language model based on the Transformer architecture [38]. Trained on millions of sequences, ESM-2 captures rich evolutionary and structural signals, yielding continuous vector representations for each residue.

  Next, to define the graph edges, we obtain spatial coordinates for the Cα atoms of all residues. When an experimentally determined structure is available in the PDB [39], we directly extract its coordinates; otherwise, we predict the structure de novo using AlphaFold 2 to generate comparable Cα positions. From these coordinates, we compute the pairwise Cα–Cα distance matrix and connect any two residues whose Cα distance falls below 10 Å, thereby constructing the binary adjacency matrix that encodes the protein’s three-dimensional topology.

### Model training settings
  In our experiments, we implemented a deep learning framework composed of two graph convolutional layers, a hierarchical graph Transformer block, and two fully connected layers. The model was trained for up to 300 epochs with an early stopping strategy (patience = 10) to prevent unnecessary iterations. For optimization, we employed the Adam algorithm with default hyperparameters (learning rate = 1 × 10⁻³, β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.999, ε = 1 × 10⁻⁸). A constant learning rate of 1 × 10⁻⁴ combined with a weight decay of 1 × 10⁻³ was used to ensure stable convergence while mitigating the risk of overfitting. All non-linear activations were implemented as LeakyReLU with a negative slope of 0.2, which alleviates gradient vanishing and accelerates optimization. Model weights were initialized using the Xavier scheme to preserve unit variance between inputs and outputs, thereby facilitating efficient convergence in the early training stages. In addition, we applied a dropout rate of 0.2 to randomly deactivate neurons during training, which effectively enhances generalization performance.To demonstrate that this training schedule is appropriate and does not lead to overfitting, we provide the training loss curves and evaluation metric trajectories in the Supplementary Materials (Figures S1 and S2).


### Baseline methods
  In this study, we compare ProMoHGT against four representative baselines. DeepFRI [20] employs a graph convolutional network to model protein structures as graphs, integrating sequence embeddings from a pre-trained protein language model (PLM) with structural features derived from experimental assays and homology modeling. By operating at the residue level, DeepFRI achieves superior precision and recall relative to conventional sequence- or structure-based approaches, and its graph-based message-passing inherently attenuates homologous noise. SPROF-GO [21] pursues an alignment-free, sequence-only strategy: it first extracts high-dimensional sequence features via a PLM, then applies self‐attention pooling to highlight critical residue regions, and finally incorporates homologous protein data and a label‐diffusion algorithm to detect functionally coherent communities. Cross-species validation demonstrates that SPROF-GO generalizes better than existing sequence-based and graph-based methods across the three Gene Ontology domains (BP, MF, CC). MSF-PFP [44] addresses heterogeneous biological data, comprising sequence, 3D structure, protein–protein interactions, and gene expression, through a three-tier feature extraction scheme at the global, local, and instance levels. These multi-scale features are seamlessly merged via a multi-source fusion network to perform multi-label classification of biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components. Extensive experiments show that MSF-PFP outperforms state-of-the-art techniques in overall F1 score and AUPRC, particularly under conditions of network sparsity and imbalanced expression. Finally, DeepMVD [45] introduces a multi‐view deep framework with separate branches dedicated to global semantics (e.g., sequence context and conservation), local motifs (e.g., functional sites and domain activations), and higher‐order abstractions. An adaptive fusion mechanism dynamically weights each view’s contribution, delivering high‐accuracy predictions for GO BP, MF, and CC, while systematic ablations confirm the individual and combined importance of each module.


### Evaluation metrics
  Consistent with prior work, we evaluate model performance using two widely adopted metrics in protein function prediction: Fmax and the area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC). The Fmax score is defined as the highest F1 value, which is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, achieved across all classification thresholds, thereby identifying the optimal trade-off point between precision and recall. AUPRC quantifies the area under the precision–recall curve, offering a sensitive measure of precision changes at varying recall levels and thus better reflecting performance on rare function labels. By combining Fmax and AUPRC, we simultaneously capture the model’s overall best-threshold effectiveness and its fine‐grained precision behavior across different recall regimes, yielding a comprehensive assessment of classification quality.


## Results

### Homology-based partitioning and performance evaluation of test set
  To comprehensively assess the generalization capability of the model across varying homology levels, we evaluated its performance on two fundamental protein function prediction tasks: Gene Ontology (GO) term prediction (encompassing Molecular Function (MF), Biological Process (BP), and Cellular Component (CC)) and Enzyme Commission (EC) number prediction. Using CD-HIT, we computed pairwise sequence identity across all protein sequences in the test set and stratified the samples into four homology-based subsets using identity thresholds of 40%, 50%, 70%, and 95%. Subsequently, we measured model performance within each subset by calculating the Fmax score and the area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC). This systematic approach elucidates the relationship between sequence similarity and predictive performance.

Notably, even in the most challenging low-homology regime (< 50%), the model maintained robust performance, with Fmax > 0.65 and AUPRC > 0.70, suggesting its ability to leverage both high-similarity homology signals and subtle structural/evolutionary features in divergent sequences.This multi-tiered homology-partitioned evaluation not only quantifies performance trends across similarity ranges but also provides actionable insights for optimizing the model’s generalization capability, particularly for distantly related proteins.

## Disccussion
  The ProMoHGT framework represents a significant advance in microbial protein function prediction by seamlessly integrating sequence, structural, and physicochemical information within a hierarchical graph Transformer architecture. In extensive benchmarks spanning high to remote homology levels (95% down to 40% sequence identity), ProMoHGT consistently surpasses state-of-the-art methods such as DeepFRI, SPROF-GO, MSF-PFP and DeepMVP on both Gene Ontology (GO) and enzyme commission (EC) classification tasks, suffering only a marginal ~ 2% drop in molecular function Fmax at the lowest identity threshold. Ablation studies underscore the paramount importance of explicit structural features, which when withheld cause Fmax and AUPR to fall by 0.038–0.046, as well as the added value of contrastive representation learning and rich sequence embeddings. In the representative case study, ProMoHGT further validated its superiority on three experimentally annotated microbial proteins, ArcA from Escherichia coli, CodY from Bacillus subtilis, and SPT16 from Saccharomyces cerevisiae. Compared with DeepFRI, SPROF-GO, MSF-PFP, and DeepMVD, ProMoHGT consistently achieved the highest F1 scores (0.721, 0.745, and 0.752), not only significantly improving overall predictive accuracy but also accurately recovering key functional annotations such as DNA binding, transcription activation, chromatin remodeling, and metabolic regulation. These results highlight the value of integrating sequence, structural, and physicochemical features within a graph Transformer framework coupled with contrastive learning, demonstrating ProMoHGT’s robustness across different sequence homology levels and its practical potential in microbial functional genomics research. To fully realize its potential, future work should validate ProMoHGT across experimentally annotated microbial proteomes and explore the incorporation of complementary data modalities, such as gene expression profiles and protein–protein interaction networks, to further bolster its predictive power and generalizability.


## Conclusions
  Proteins are fundamental to biological processes, yet microbial proteins present unique annotation challenges due to rapid evolution, structural diversity, and limited experimental data (over 70% uncharacterized in UniProt). To overcome these challenges, we developed ProMoHGT, an advanced algorithm that integrates ESM-2 sequence embeddings, AlphaFold2 structural graphs, and physicochemical properties through a hierarchical graph Transformer. This innovative architecture, featuring super-node-enhanced attention and contrastive learning, achieves state-of-the-art performance in predicting Gene Ontology terms and Enzyme Commission numbers, particularly for low-homology proteins. It effectively captures distant evolutionary relationships and structure-function patterns that elude traditional methods.

  Looking forward, ProMoHGT defines a new paradigm for microbial proteomics with three major strengths: (1) a hierarchical design that scales to massive metagenomic datasets, (2) dynamic graph embeddings that link structure to function, and (3) contrastive learning for broad species robustness. Next steps include speeding up inference with sparse attention, extending to pathway-level predictions via multi‐tier ontology learning, and modeling conformational changes through temporal graph networks. Our benchmark dataset lays the groundwork for future method development. By bridging sequence, structure and function, ProMoHGT opens the door to systems‐level microbiome analysis and structure‐guided enzyme engineering.
