# PathHDNN: a pathway hierarchical-informed deep neural network framework for predicting immunotherapy response and mechanism interpretation


## Background
  Immunotherapy has revolutionized the treatment of cancer and tremendously prolonged the overall survival of patients; however, only parts of patients could derive durable clinical benefit from it. Predicting clinical responses to immunotherapy and interpreting the response mechanism remain major challenges.


## Methods
  Here, we presented a pathway hierarchical-informed deep neural network (PathHDNN) framework to predict the therapeutic responses of cancer patients and identify key pathways associated with immunotherapy efficacy. PathHDNN was trained by encoding established biological pathway hierarchically knowledge into a neural network architecture through embedding the somatic mutations and copy number variations. During training, PathHDNN optimized only the edge weights connecting hierarchically related pathways, while maintaining the fixed network topology derived from biological pathway hierarchy, thereby preserving biological interpretability throughout the learning process.


## Results
  We assessed PathHDNN on multiple immunotherapy cohorts and found it outperformed other state-of-the-art machine learning methods and established immunotherapy biomarkers. Moreover, through interpreting the trained model, PathHDNN effectively identified critical pathway features associated with the therapeutic response in melanoma cohort, particularly noting the critical roles of p53-mediated tumor suppression and JAK2-mediated immune regulation in the course of responsiveness to immunotherapy.


## Conclusions
  In conclusion, PathHDNN provided an effective pathway hierarchical-informed framework for accurately predicting patient responses to immunotherapy and identified key pathways yielding valuable insights into the biological mechanisms underlying treatment responses, which is essential for advancing precision medicine.

## Background
  Immunotherapy, especially immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has achieved remarkable and sustained tumor remission in several common solid tumors, tremendously improving treatment efficacy and cancer survival. Unlike traditional therapies such as chemotherapy, immunotherapy provides a significant advantage by harnessing the immune system’s ability to selectively target and eliminate cancer cells, offering durable responses with fewer off-target effects on healthy tissues. However, only a minority of cancer patients experienced durable clinical benefit from these treatments [1–3]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop effective methods for predicting the clinical response to immunotherapy and interpreting the biological mechanisms underlying ICI treatments, facilitating the customization of personalized treatments.

  Recently, advancements in molecular profiling technologies have enabled scientists to identify molecular biomarkers for predicting cancer treatment outcomes. Biomarkers such as PD-L1 expression [4], tumor mutational burden (TMB) [5, 6], microsatellite instability (MSI) [7, 8], and mismatch repair gene deficiency (dMMR) [9, 10] have shown strong correlations with immunotherapy response, providing valuable decision-making biomarkers for clinicians. Despite advances in biomarker discovery, they still fail to consistently predict the response to immunotherapy across all cancers. For instance, McGrail et al. reported that high TMB does not reliably predict immunotherapy benefit across different cancer types [11], while Bai et al. found that some patients who respond to immunotherapy exhibit low PD-L1 expression levels [12]. Such inconsistencies may arise due to the complex, dynamic interplay between the immune system and tumors, where the predictive value of certain biomarkers fluctuates over time. Therewith, several computational methods have been developed to improve the identification of immunotherapy responders. For example, NetBio, developed by Kong et al., effectively improved the accuracy of ICI response predictions [13], which use immunotherapy targets (PD1, PD-L1, CTLA4) as seed genes for network propagation. Similarly, a model proposed by Chowell et al. integrated multiple ICI-related biomarkers (e.g., TMB, MSI status) with demographic and clinical data to substantially improve clinical decision-making in immunotherapy [14].

  Despite these improvements, the inherent complexity and heterogeneity of cancer continue to challenge the performance of existing predictive signatures. Deep learning algorithms are being increasingly incorporated into genomics workflows to address these limitations and enhance immunotherapy biomarker discovery. Traditional deep learning models often provide superior predictive capabilities, but their fully connected dense architectures can lead to issues like overfitting, computationally expensive, and reduced interpretability. To achieve a balance between predictive accuracy and interpretability, customized deep learning models have been designed inspired by biological systems to effectively handle complex biomedical challenges. For example, DCell [15] and DrugCell [16] explicitly linked the architecture of deep neural networks to cellular functions to predict cellular responses to therapeutic chemical compounds. IRnet adopted a flattened version of pathways to predict cancer immunotherapy response by embedding the whole genome transcriptome into pathways [17]. However, most biological processes particularly signal transduction pathways, exhibit cascading behavior where initial signals trigger a series of downstream events thereby amplifying the effects. This underscored the need for models that can effectively capture these hierarchical and cascading dynamics in the context of immunotherapy to predict treatment response.

  In this study, we introduced PathHDNN, a pathway hierarchical-informed deep neural network framework designed to predict immunotherapy response. By using somatic mutations and copy number variations as inputs, the PathHDNN was trained by encoding established biological pathway hierarchically knowledge into a neural architecture and then it optimized the pathway hierarchies by assigning context-specific weights that reflect their relevance to immunotherapy outcomes. Based on multiple immunotherapy cohorts, we found PathHDNN achieved higher prediction accuracy and outperformed other state-of-the-art machine learning and deep learning algorithms in predicting ICI responses. Moreover, the interpretability of PathHDNN enabled us to identify panels of critical genomics alterations and pathways, shedding light on the biological mechanisms underlying treatment response, which is essential for advancing precision medicine and offering new directions for therapeutic interventions.


## Methods

### Data collecting and processing
  In this study, we first collected somatic genomics alterations (including somatic mutations and copy number variations [CNVs]) of melanoma patients from various immunotherapy clinical cohorts. A total of four melanoma cohorts were used in the study which were downloaded from the cBioPortal for Cancer Genomics database (https://www.cbioportal.org/) [18], and the detailed information of these cohorts were listed in Additional file 1: Table S1. For the Miao cohort, 144 melanoma patients treated with anti-PD1 were obtained from Miao et al. study [19], which involved in the experiments on the anti-PD1 prediction model. For the Hugo cohort, we obtained 37 melanoma patients receiving anti-PD1 treatment as the external validation cohort for the PD1 prediction model which was obtained from Hugo et al. study [20]. It is well-known that the underlying mechanisms of action for different ICI treatments are not the same. Therefore, we also downloaded the Van Allen cohort from Van Allen et al. study [21] to develop the anti-CTLA4 prediction model, including 105 melanoma patients. To further validate the performance of this model, another immunotherapy cohort was downloaded from Synder et al. study [22], including 55 patients with anti-CTLA4 treatment. Importantly, patients from the Hugo and Synder cohorts were not included in the training data, and there was no sample overlap among all cohorts as per cBioPortal metadata. This rigorous train-validation strategy utilizing independent sample cohorts was designed to ensure robust evaluation and assess model generalization capability across diverse patient populations. Patients’ clinicopathologic information including response to immunotherapy, gender, brain metastases, lung metastases, bone metastases, M stage, and UV induced mutations were gathered accordingly. The patients in the above cohorts were categorized as complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), or progressed disease (PD) based on the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST). Individuals who achieved either CR or PR after immunotherapy were classified as responders, otherwise as non-responders.

  For somatic mutations, the alterations were initially aggregated at the gene level. Considering that most mutations in tumors are often passenger events, we focused exclusively on non-silent mutation (including missense, nonsense, insertion, deletion, and splice mutations) for further analysis. The copy number alterations for each gene were determined based on the segment-level copy number calls (amplifications and deletions) as defined by GISTIC2.0 [23]. Next, mutations or CNVs events in genes were represented as binary variables. Specifically, genes with somatic mutations or copy number alterations (either amplifications or deletions) were given a value of 1, while genes without such alterations were assigned a value of 0. To comprehensively represent cancer genotypes, only genes with mutations or copy number variations observed in at least three samples were retained as somatic genomics alteration signatures for model construction.

  Furthermore, we also extracted pathway information from the Reactome database [https://reactome.org/] [24]. Reactome systematically describes the relationships among biological entities (such as molecular, pathways, and high-level processes) through a hierarchical structure. This hierarchical framework facilitates a comprehensive understanding of cellular functions, enabling researchers to trace how discrete molecular interactions are coordinated to form broader, more complex biological systems. Therefore, in this study, we constructed a pathway hierarchical graph using the pathway information obtained from the Reactome database for further analysis.


### Construction of the PathHDNN architecture
  The architecture of the biologically-informed neural network was originally developed as P-NET by Elmaraeby and his colleagues for prostate cancer discovery [25], which incorporates biological processes with the concept of sparse modeling based on neural network. Inspired by P-NET, we present PathHDNN, a novel pathway hierarchical-informed deep neural network framework, to predict the response of immunotherapy. First, the pathway hierarchical network was derived from the Reactome pathway database, encompassing detailed priori knowledge between biological entities such as molecules, pathways and high-level processes. The detailed steps are as follows:

  Subnetwork Extraction: A subnetwork was first extracted from the Reactome pathway database through a systematic hierarchical traversal process. Starting at the gene level, we progressively incorporated parental pathways based on overlapping genes, where overlapping genes refer to genes that are simultaneously present in multiple pathways. Specifically, a parental pathway was included if it contained at least one gene already present in the current subnetwork level. This process continued until reaching the highest-level pathways, which represent top-level biological processes in the Reactome hierarchy (e.g., “Metabolism”, “Signal Transduction”, “Immune System”) with no parent pathways in the database structure.Pathway network generation: A comprehensive pathway hierarchical network was then generated by merging the above subnetworks with an output node linked to the highest-level nodes, while the number of output nodes represents the predicted sample classes.Backward traversal: Staring from the output node, we then traversed the network backward at most N layers. If a terminal node was reached before completing the N layers, a copy parent node was created that replicated the terminal node’s connectivity and properties. This approach ensured uniform network depth across all branches and prevented information loss from shorter pathway branches.Node pruning: Nodes that were not traversed during the backward pass were systematically eliminated to optimize and streamline the network architecture.Genes integration: Finally, genes of interest were linked to their corresponding terminal nodes along the pathway hierarchical network, establishing the complete gene-to-pathway connectivity structure for downstream analysis.

  In this study, a six-layer pathway hierarchical network was constructed. Then, with the genomics features (mutations and CNVs) as input, the PathHDNN framework was trained by implementing the above pathway hierarchical network as a neural network. Compared to a fully connected network, the number of weights in the first layer of PathHDNN was sharply decreased, thereby enhancing training efficiency. The second layer was designed to represent the gene-pathway relationships curated from the Reactome database. These links were then encoded into a mask matrix M, which was then multiplied by the weight matrix W to effectively zero any connections do not present in the Reactome database. For the subsequent layers, the similar approach was employed to supervise the connections between sequential layers to ensure they reveal the actual hierarchical relationships established in the Reactome database. ### Interpretation of the PathHDNN model
  To further clarify the decision-making process of PathHDNN and identify important genes or pathways influencing immunotherapy response prediction, SHapley Additive exPlannations (SHAP) algorithm was employed to interpret the PathHDNN model. SHAP is a backpropagation-based attribution algorithm that assigns sample-specific importance scores to each feature, quantifying its contribution to the model’s prediction. In our PathHDNN implementation, SHAP values were computed using the DeepExplainer algorithm, which naturally respects the sparse connectivity imposed by our biological mask matrices. The gradient-based attribution inherently follows the existing connections defined by the Reactome pathway hierarchy, with gradients flowing only through non-zero connections in the masked weight matrices. This ensures that SHAP values reflect the actual information flow within the biologically constrained network architecture rather than artificial connections that would exist in fully connected networks. For each pathway layer, SHAP values are computed based on direct contributions to the final prediction through established biological connections, rather than simple aggregation along sparse pathways. In this study, the importance scores were calculated for each node across all gene and pathway layers. For a given sample, the importance score Sn for node n was estimated as the difference between the current model output and the expected model output (based on the background samples): Sn = f(x)-E(f(x)). ### Evaluation of the PathHDNN model
  We evaluated the accuracy of the PathHDNN model in predicting the benefits of immunotherapy. Beside the training cohort, the model was tested in the independent cohorts for which genomics variations data was available. Metrics including the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), F1 score, Harrell’s concordance index (C-index), Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) were calculated to evaluate the prediction performance of the PathHDNN model. In addition, the Kaplan-Meier analysis and log-rank test were performed to assess whether the PathHDNN model could be used to evaluate the power of prognostic classification. The “survival ROC” package was used to perform the time-dependent ROC analysis.


## Results

### Validation of the PathHDNN framework
  In this study, we introduced a pathway hierarchical-informed deep neural network framework (named PathHDNN) that utilizes established biological pathway knowledge to tailor the neural network architecture for predicting cancer immunotherapy response. The overall study design was depicted in Fig. 1. Specifically, the model processed individual genomics variation profiles by mapping them onto a layer of nodes that represent specific genes through weighted links. In the subsequent layers of the network, pathways were encoded with progressively increasing levels of abstraction: the lower layers depicted detailed molecular pathways, while the higher layers represent more broader and complex biological processes. And the connections between different layers were rigorously constrained to reflect established hierarchical relationships among encoded features, genes, and pathways, thereby ensuring that the network is inherently structured for optimal interpretability.

#### Anti-PD1 PathHDNN model
  Initially, the PathHDNN model was trained on a melanoma cohort of 144 patients receiving anti-PD1 treatment. Using somatic mutation and CNV data as inputs, a six-layer anti-PD1 PathHDNN model was constructed. The distribution of nodes per layer in the model was presented in Additional file 1: Fig. S1A. This sparse model contained a total of 11, 955 trainable parameters across different layers, significantly less than a conventional full connection deep neural network with the same number of nodes. By comparing the classifications predicted by the PathHDNN model and the ground truth, it was demonstrated an excellent predictive performance for ICI response, achieving an AUROC of 0.989, an F1 score of 0.943 and a C-index of 0.989 (Fig. 2A and Additional file 1: Fig. S1B). Notably, a significant concordance was observed between the predicted and truth classifications (chi-squared test P < 2.2e-16, Fig. 2B), correctly classifying all immunotherapy responders and 84.71% of non-responders. Next, we also assessed the temporal dynamics of the PathHDNN’s prognostic performance through time-dependent ROC curve analysis. At 1 year, PathHDNN obtained an AUROC of 0.682, which progressively increased as the follow-up period extended, reaching 0.946 at 4 years (Fig. 2C). This trend indicates an improvement in the prognostic capability of the PathHDNN over longer time horizons. Melanoma patients were then classified into “predicted responders” and “predicted non-responders” groups based on the median value of PathHDNN model’s predicted scores. And a significant difference in overall survival (OS) was noted between “predicted responders” and “predicted non-responders” groups (median OS, 26.23 months VS 12.10 months, log-rank test P < 0.0001, Fig. 2D), in which “predicted responders” exhibit more favorable prognoses. Additionally, we explored whether somatic mutations or CNVs could independently predict immunotherapy response by training single-genomic-variation PathHDNN models (mutation-based or CNV-based PathHDNN) on each data type separately. The analysis showed that both mutation-based and CNV-based PathHDNN models performed a moderate predictive ability for ICI response (Fig. 2A and Additional file 1: Fig. S1B). Interestingly, the mutation-based model outperformed the CNV-based model, with an accuracy of 0.910 (Fig. 2A and Additional file 1: Fig. S1B) compared to 0.705 (Fig. 2A and Additional file 1: Fig. S1B), suggesting mutations data were more informative than copy number variation data. However, neither mutation-based nor CNV-based PathHDNN model could rival the predictive accuracy of the PathHDNN model that developed based on both somatic mutations and CNVs data.

  Subsequently, we performed external validation of the predictive ability for the anti-PD1 PathHDNN model using another independent melanoma cohort with patients treated with anti-PD1 (Hugo cohort). The anti-PD1 PathHDNN model also revealed exceptional predictive performance and significantly outperformed mutation-based and CNV-based PathHDNN models in predicting immunotherapy benefits (Fig. 2E and Additional file 1: Fig. S1C). Moreover, the model accurately classified 80% of immunotherapy responders and 88.24% of non-responders (Fig. 2F), underscoring its ability to generalize to unseen samples with robust predictive performance. Notably, time-dependent ROC and Kaplan-Meier survival analyses further substantiated the superior prognostic power of the PathHDNN model (Fig. 2G-H).

  Furthermore, we evaluated the prognostic performance of the anti-PD1 PathHDNN score against other clinicopathologic factors, including gender, brain metastases, lung metastases, bone metastases, M stage, and UV-induced mutations. The PathHDNN model consistently outperformed all these clinicopathologic factors in predicting patient survival outcomes (Additional file 1: Fig. S2A). Subsequently, Cox regression analysis found that brain metastases, bone metastases and the PathHDNN model were significantly associated with patients’ prognoses, with the PathHDNN having the lowest hazard ratios of 0.202 (P < 0.001, Fig. 2I). Furthermore, multivariate Cox regression analysis revealed that the PathHDNN model was the sole independent prognostic factor for OS in the training cohort (P < 0.001, Fig. 2I). Similarly, in the validation cohort, the PathHDNN remained a significant independent prognostic factor for OS (multivariate Cox analysis, P < 0.001, Additional file 1: Table S2 and Fig. S2B).


#### Anti-CTLA4 PathHDNN model
  Previous research has demonstrated that the underlying mechanisms of action differ among various types of immune checkpoint inhibitors, underscoring the necessity of developing ICI-specific models to maximize therapeutic efficacy. Consequently, an anti-CTLA4 PathHDNN model with 6, 356 nodes and 16, 231 trainable parameters was developed (Additional file 1: Fig. S3A) using the Van Allen cohort (N = 105) of melanoma patients undergoing anti-CTLA4 treatment and subsequently tested the model’s predictive power in the Snyder cohort (N = 55). Specifically, the trained anti-CTLA4 PathHDNN model exhibited noteworthy predictive performance in predicting immunotherapy benefits, achieving an AUROC of 0.912, an F1 score of 0.74, and a C-index of 0.912 (Fig. 3A and Additional file 1: Fig. S3B-G). A significant overlap was found between the actual immunotherapy benefits and the outcomes predicted by the model (chi-squared test P < 3.682e-5, Fig. 3B). And the “predicted responders” exhibited a remarkably better prognoses than “predicted non-responders” (median OS, 16.233 months VS 7.467 months, log-rank test P = 0.017, Fig. 3C). Additionally, the anti-CTLA4 PathHDNN model also displayed a high predictive accuracy in the validation cohort and precisely classified 85.71% of immunotherapy responders (Fig. 3D-E). Despite survival analysis showed that the model’s prognostic performance was less robust (Fig. 3F), the PathHDNN model remained a significant independent prognostic factor after adjustment by clinicopathologic variables in both training and validation cohorts (Fig. 3G and Additional file 1: Table S2).

#### Cross-cancer validation of PathHDNN
  To evaluate the generalizability of our PathHDNN framework beyond melanoma, we conducted cross-cancer validation using three independent immunotherapy-treated cohorts from distinct cancer types: bladder cancer (BLCA, N = 27), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC, N = 12), and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC, N = 56), all treated with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors. Subsequently, the pre-trained anti-PD1 PathHDNN model, originally developed using melanoma data, was directly applied to these three cohorts without additional training or parameter adjustment. The cross-cancer validation demonstrated that our model maintains robust predictive capability across these histologically diverse tumor types, achieving AUROC values of 0.786 for the BLCA cohort, 0.800 for the HNSCC cohort, and 0.750 for the NSCLC cohort (Additional file 1: Fig. S4). The robust performance across these diverse tumor types suggested that PathHDNN successfully identifies fundamental response patterns associated with anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy that are conserved across different cancer contexts.


### PathHDNN learns mechanism that mediate immunotherapy
  Having assessed the predictive capabilities of the PathHDNN model, we proceeded to learn mechanisms that mediate immunotherapy. To explore the specific features, pathways and biological processes most contributing to the model’s predictions, the anti-PD1 PathHDNN model was first analyzed using the SHAP method [https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.5555/1756006.1756007]. SHAP is a feature attribution method that estimates the Shapley values for each node within the network relative to the outcome prediction. Among aggregate molecular alterations, our analysis revealed that the contributions (SHAP values) of somatic mutations were significantly larger than that of CNVs in both responder and non-responder groups (Fig. 4A), which partly explain why mutation-based model outperformed CNV-based PathHDNN model in predicting ICI response (Fig. 2A). Previous research has demonstrated that both somatic mutations and CNVs serve as drivers of tumorigenesis, yet their contributions to cancer progression and immunotherapy outcomes differ significantly. Specifically, somatic mutations are often regarded as primary drivers of neoantigen formation, which is crucial for the effectiveness of ICI treatment. Conversely, CNVs involve alterations in the number of gene copies, leading to genomics instability, which has a more intricate and less well-defined relationships with immunotherapy outcomes.

  To evaluate the relative importance of specific genes contributing to the model prediction, we inspected the genes layer and used the SHAP attribution method to obtain the total importance score of genes. Highly ranked genes included TUBA3C, TP53, JAK2, EP300, COL1A1, HLA-B, and CDKN2A, which are significantly associated with tumorigenesis and under a significant genomic alteration pressure in melanoma patients [26, 27] (Fig. 4B-C and Additional file 1: Table S3). For example, TP53 mutations play a fundamental role in the initiation, progression, and therapeutic resistance of many cancers by disrupting the normal tumor-suppressive functions of the p53 protein, leading to increased cell survival, proliferation, and genomic instability [26, 27]. Our results also showed that nodes in each layer with a high SHAP value in the anti-PD1 PathHDNN model were largely related to signaling pathways and immune system, such as interferon signaling, signaling by interleukins, adaptive immune system, and oncogenic MAPK signaling (Additional file 1: Fig. S5).

  To further understand how the input information is transformed along the pathway hierarchical network, we then interrogated the interpretable architecture of PathHDNN, which enable our understanding of the state and importance of involved biological entities. As mentioned, the candidate marker TP53 was identified as one of the most important genes in the PathHDNN and has many known implications in immune checkpoint inhibitor [26, 27]. Therefore, we traced the relevance of TP53 somatic variations to a series of related cascading biological pathways. In the hierarchical network, we found that TP53 exert its influence primarily through its mediated transcriptional regulation and interleukin signal transduction, which cascade to affect the cell cycle, various signaling pathways, and the immune system (Fig. 4B). Specifically, TP53 regulates the expression of genes involved in cell death, DNA repair, and the cell cycle by forming a tetramer, which subsequently leads to apoptosis, enhanced DNA repair, and cell cycle arrest. In cancer, TP53 mutations often disrupt or interfere with its tetrameric structure, resulting in TP53 inactivation and diminishing its tumor-suppressive function, thereby facilitating malignant transformation. Within the subnetwork originating from TP53 mutation, we also observed its influence on the G2/M transition, leading to cell cycle arrest and modulating patient responses to immunotherapy (Fig. 4B). These results interpret the mechanism of action for TP53 mutation on immunotherapy. Additionally, JAK2 was also identified as one of the most important gene in the PathHDNN model, where it plays a crucial role in key biological processes, including immune regulation, cell apoptosis, differentiation, and proliferation [28–30]. After tracing the subnetwork originating from JAK2, we discovered that it mostly contributes to immunotherapy benefit by RAF/MAPK cascade signal, modulating interferon gamma and interleukin signaling, and ultimately affecting overall immune system function (Fig. 4B), consist with previous studies. Gamma interferon (IFN-γ) enhances T cell anti-tumor activity by promoting immune recognition and destruction of tumor cells [31, 32]. However, tumors can evade immune responses by up-regulating the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway, and PD-1 inhibitors target this pathway to restore T cell function and boost the IFN-γ-mediated immune response [33]. Consequently, high levels of IFN-γ are frequently correlated with improved response to PD-1 inhibitors, making it an important biomarker for predicting immunotherapy efficacy [34, 35]. Furthermore, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) performed on the gene ranked list based on SHAP values indicated that immune checkpoint inhibitors influence patients’ responses to immunotherapy by modulating key biological processes, such as cell cycle, PI3K-AKT signaling, and Notch signaling (Fig. 5). These findings suggested that both p53-mediated tumor suppression and JAK2-mediated immune regulation play a critical role in the course of responsiveness to PD-1 inhibitors therapy.

  In the context of the anti-CTLA-4 PathHDNN model, the key mutation genes and pathways were also identified (Additional file 1: Fig. S6 and S7). Specifically, somatic mutations provided more information for cancer immunotherapy benefit prediction compared to copy number variations (Additional file 1: Fig. S7A). Furthermore, nodes in each layer with high SHAP values were remarkably related to critical signal transduction processes, such as signaling by interleukins, Axon guidance, and cellular responses to stimuli (Additional file 1: Fig. S6). Notably, several top-ranking genes identified in the PathHDNN model, such as COL4A3, COL1A2, NRAS, ATR, and BRCA2, were established biomarkers for immunotherapy and have been shown to undergo relatively high alteration pressure in melanoma [36, 37] (Additional file 1: Fig. S7B-C and Table S4). Specifically, previous study indicated that COL4A3 and COL1A2 are essential collagen proteins in the basement membrane and extracellular matrix, respectively, playing crucial roles in cell adhesion, migration, and tissue structure. By modulating the extracellular matrix and tumor microenvironment, these proteins may indirectly influence melanoma progression and response to immunotherapy [36, 37]. The same results were observed when we examined the biological processes associated with COL4A3 and COL1A2 mutations in the pathway hierarchical network (Additional file 1: Fig. S7B and S8), indicating that investigating the critical biological pathways influenced by these gene alterations could yield deeper insights into the mechanisms of action of immunotherapy.


### Comparison of PathHDNN with state-of-the-art methods
  To further assess the effectiveness of PathHDNN for cancer immunotherapy response prediction, it was benchmarked against several widely used conventional machine learning approaches, including random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), XGBoost, and fully connected neural network (FNN). All methods were trained and tested using the same genomic features to ensure a fair comparison of their predictive power (Detailed hyperparameters provided in Additional file 1: Supplementary Text and Table S5). Among these genomic feature-informed methods, PathHDNN consistently delivered the highest performances across C-index, F1 score, and MCC (Fig. 6A-C). Specifically, both anti-PD1 and anti-CTLA4 PathHDNN attained a C-index exceeding 0.8 for the binary classification of immunotherapy responders vs. non-responders across the training cohorts (Liu and Van Allen cohorts) and validation cohorts (Hugo and Snyder cohorts) (Fig. 6A), while other methods consistently yielded C-indexs below 0.7 in the validation cohorts, highlighting their limitations in immunotherapy response prediction. Besides, PathHDNN also achieved relatively higher F1 score and MCC compared to other methods (Fig. 6B-C). Notably, we further established pathway-informed baseline models by utilizing the biological pathway variation matrix as model input. And our results demonstrated that pathway-informed baseline models exhibited improved performance for predicting immunotherapy response compared to genomic feature-informed models, but remained consistently inferior to PathHDNN (Additional file 1: Table S6-8).

  We then compared the PathHDNN models with several published immunotherapy biomarkers, including cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAF) [38], CD274 express levels [39], CTL.flag [40], M2 type of tumor-associated macrophages (TAM.M2) [41], tumor immune dysfunction and exclusion (TIDE) [42], TMB [43], as well as signatures proposed by Wang et al. [44] and Long et al. [45]. These biomarkers have previously demonstrated potential in predicting immunotherapy response in cancer patients [38, 44, 45]. Surprisingly, PathHDNN outperformed nearly all of them across all metrics (Fig. 6A-C and Additional file 1: Table S6-8), indicating that PathHDNN significantly improved the accuracy of predicting immunotherapy benefits.

  We further conducted comparisons with recently published state-of-the-art deep learning approaches. We implemented seven representative deep learning approaches: DeepCC [46], BPformer [47], HyperTMO [48], IRnet [49], MOGAT [50], MOGDX [51], and MOGONET [52]. All models were trained using identical preprocessing, feature sets, and optimization procedures across the same training and validation cohorts with consistent train-validation splits (Detailed information and hyperparameters of these approaches were provided in Additional file 1: Supplementary Text and Table S5). And our results showed that PathHDNN consistently outperformed these deep learning models across C-index, F1 score, and MCC (Fig. 6A-C and Additional file 1: Table S6-8).

  We finally performed statistically significant test of comparison of PathHDNN with the published biomarkers, conventional machine learning, and deep learning models. The bootstrap resampling methodology was implemented to evaluate the significant differences of C-indexs, F1-scores and MCCs of PathHDNN and other models with 20 repeats generated empirical distributions. In training and validation cohorts, PathHDNN achieved significant improvements in predicting immunotherapy response over almost all other biomarkers and models across these metrics (Wilcoxon rank sum test, p < 0.05, Additional file 1: Fig. S9A-C).


### Pathway hierarchical network analysis of PathHDNN
  To comprehensively evaluate PathHDNN’s architectural stability, we conducted systematic analyses for both pathway network depth optimization and topological perturbation effects. Specifically, we constructed PathHDNN models with different hierarchical depths ranging from 3 to 10 layers and assessed their predictive performance on independent validation cohorts. Our results demonstrated model’s optimal performance was at six layers, with progressive degradation beyond this threshold, notably the anti-PD-1 PathHDNN achieving an AUROC for 0.52 training at 10 layers (Additional file 1: Fig. S10A). These findings suggested that the six-layer configuration achieves optimal balance between biological comprehensiveness and computational efficiency, effectively modeling pathway hierarchical complexity without unnecessary architectural redundancy.

  Additionally, we also evaluated network topological importance by systematically removing 2%, 4%, 6%, 8%, and 10% of pathway nodes from the hierarchical architecture. For each removal, PathHDNN models were retrained and validated on independent cohorts. The results revealed that the predictive performance of the model declined sharply in the validation cohorts (Hugo and Snyder cohorts) (Additional file 1: Fig. S10B), indicating the critical importance of intact biological knowledge encoding for maintaining model performance.


## Discussion
  Immunotherapy has revolutionized the treatment of cancer; however, only parts of patients could derive durable clinical benefit from it [1, 2]. This underscored the urgent need for predicting immunotherapy response and learning the mechanism of immunotherapy. Nowadays, biologically-informed neural networks offer an interpretable solution by capturing complex biological processes to improve the prediction accuracy for driver genes and cell states [15, 53]. Here, we proposed PathHDNN, an advanced framework that encodes established biological pathway hierarchically knowledge into neural network architectures, to predict the immunotherapy response of cancer patients and interpret mechanism. Unlike conventional “black-box” deep learning models, this framework optimized pathway hierarchies into a computational model by assigning weights to reflect their importance to immunotherapy outcomes. Using somatic mutations and copy number variations as inputs, PathHDNN had elucidated the internal relationships among biological pathways affected by genomics variations, thereby offering a more nuanced representation of the biological determinants of immunotherapy response predictions.

  The superior performance of PathHDNN across diverse ICI treatment cohorts may reveal previously unrecognized biological mechanisms underlying treatment responses. The consistent improvements in predictive accuracy, coupled with the model’s capacity to identify biologically relevant features, indicated that pathway hierarchy architecture can capture essential regulatory relationships that conventional feature representations fail to model effectively. Through SHAP analysis, we have uncovered novel mechanistic insights demonstrating that genes such as TP53, NRAS, BRCA1, EP300, and MAP2K1 function within interconnected regulatory networks that collectively orchestrate treatment responsiveness. Moreover, visualizing the architecture of PathHDNN also provided a hierarchical view of the relevant pathways, offering valuable insights to guide future research on the mechanisms of immunotherapy response. For example, the identification of p53-mediated tumor suppression and JAK2-mediated immune regulation as critical pathway nodes provides a systems-level understanding of immunotherapy mechanisms, revealing that therapeutic efficacy depends on the coordinated function of multiple biological processes rather than individual molecular targets.

  The clinical translational potential of PathHDNN extends beyond prediction accuracy to encompass mechanistic understanding that can inform rational combination therapy design and patient stratification strategies. Future research directions should focus on validating the mechanistic hypotheses generated by PathHDNN through experimental studies, expanding the framework to incorporate additional data modalities such as transcriptomic and proteomic profiles, and developing real-world implementation strategies that can integrate pathway-informed predictions into clinical decision-making workflows. The successful translation of PathHDNN into clinical practice will require careful validation in prospective clinical trials, development of standardized protocols for genomic data preprocessing and pathway annotation, and establishment of interpretability standards that enable clinicians to understand and trust model predictions.

  In conclusion, PathHDNN, a pathway hierarchical-informed deep neural network, accurately classified immunotherapy responders versus non-responders, and which is available at Github (https://github.com/hanjunwei-lab/PathHDNN). The visualization of the trained model generated novel hypotheses for mechanisms of immunotherapy response and provided insights with potential for clinical translation in molecularly stratified melanoma patient populations.


## Conclusions
  This study demonstrates that PathHDNN, a pathway hierarchical-informed deep neural network, can effectively predict immunotherapy response in cancer patients by integrating somatic genomic alterations with curated biological pathway hierarchies. PathHDNN provides robust predictive performance across multiple immune checkpoint inhibitor-treated cohorts while offering mechanistic insights into key pathway interactions, including p53-mediated tumor suppression and JAK2-driven immune regulation. The interpretability of the model enables the generation of testable hypotheses, supports patient stratification, and informs rational combination therapy design. The source code is publicly available at GitHub (https://github.com/hanjunwei-lab/PathHDNN), facilitating reproducibility and further methodological development.
