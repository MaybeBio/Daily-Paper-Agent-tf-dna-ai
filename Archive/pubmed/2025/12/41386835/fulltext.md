# Phenotyping analysis of rice cv. Nipponbare overexpressing OsMADS56 gene in drought tolerance, plant height, and yield components


## Graphical abstract
  Graphical abstract


## Introduction
  MADS-box is a transcription factor (TF) family that has been the focus of considerable research due to its essential regulatory function in various groups of eukaryotes, including the growth and development of flowering plants.1 The term MADS, an acronym for Mini chromosome maintenance1, Agamous, Deficiens, and Serum response factor, originates from four species of yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae), plants (Arabidopsis thaliana and Antirrhinum majus), and human (Homo sapiens), respectively.2 The members of this gene family are classified into two types: type I includes ARG80/serum response factor (SRF)-like genes from animals and fungi, also known as M−type genes in plants, and type II includes myocyte enhancer factor 2 (MEF2)-like genes from animals and yeast, as well as MIKC-type genes from plants.1, 2

  A MIKC-type MADS-box protein is comprised of four distinct domains in sequence: a MADS (M), an intervening (I), a keratin-like (K), and a C-terminal (C) domain.3 The M domain has a highly conserved sequence of 60 amino acids that function in DNA binding, dimerization, and accessory factor interactions.4 The subsequent three domains following the M domain are the I region, which exhibits a lesser degree of conservation and contains approximately 30 amino acids; the K domain, which is moderately conserved and contains approximately 70 amino acids and is primarily involved in heterodimerization; and the C domain, which is highly variable and can vary in length while displaying involvement in transcriptional activation and higher order complex formation.2

  It is widely acknowledged that all prominent MADS-box genes implicated in floral development and initiation are classified as 'classical' MIKCc-type genes.5 Besides floral development, they have been shown to play key roles in regulating defence and developmental processes, such as activating cellular strategies in plant response to environmental stresses (biotic and abiotic) at the cellular level.6 Numerous studies have investigated the roles of the MADS-box gene family in response to various abiotic stresses. For example, salt-alkali resistance and cold stress in tomatoes are positively regulated by SlMBP8 and SlMBP117 and the SlMADS23-like genes,8 respectively. In the case of Zea mays, ZMM7-L is involved in reproductive development, and several responses related to abiotic stresses, such as cold, salt, and polyethylene glycol (PEG) stresses. It has been hypothesized that ZMM7-L may regulate the quickening of the life cycle and the inhibition of seed germination in response to abiotic stresses.9 Significant regulations of RhMADS24, RhMADS25, RhMADS39, and RhMADS44 expressions were observed in Rhododendron hainanense in response to temperature and drought stresses.10

  Arora et al. (2007)2 investigated the expression profile of rice's MADS-box genes during various stages of development, including panicle, seed, and vegetative growth, as well as under abiotic stress conditions. They identified 75 MADS-box genes, which were subsequently classified into five distinct groups based on their phylogenic relationships: MIKCc (38 genes), MIKC* (5 genes), Mα (13 genes), Mβ (9 genes), and Mγ (10 genes). The investigation further encompassed the MADS-box gene expression profiles during cold, salt, and dehydration stresses. Four genes (OsMADS18, 22, 26, and 27) exhibited a greater than twofold increase in expression during cold and dehydration stresses. In comparison, three other genes (OsMADS2, 30, and 55) demonstrated a twofold decrease in expression during dehydration and salt stresses.

  In another study, OsMADS26 was involved in the rice's response to biotic and abiotic stresses. It was found to regulate disease resistance and drought tolerance responses negatively. The suppression of OsMADS26 expression led to enhanced rice resistance to diseases and water scarcity.11 Several studies investigated the functions of OsMADS56 in rice by overexpression and knock-out/knock-down analysis. They found that the overexpressed OsMADS56 rice lines showed improvement in rice response against abiotic stresses such as salt12 and heat13 tolerance and its phenotype changes in both vegetative and generative stages of rice, including plant height,14 flowering time,15 and grain size.16 In this study, we isolated the OsMADS56 gene and overexpressed the gene in the rice cultivar Nipponbare. We studied the OsMADS56 overexpression related to rice tolerance during drought stress and other phenotypes under normal conditions, including plant height, flowering time, and grain characteristics such as length, width, and yield, which can influence rice productivity.


## Materials and methods

### Plant materials and its cultivation
  This study employed Oryza sativa ssp. Japonica cultivar Nipponbare. The rice plants were grown hydrophonically in a growth chamber or medium pot with soil in a glasshouse. The growth chamber was set at 28 °C/25 °C (day/night), with humidity levels of 70 %/80 % and a light intensity of 5,000/0 (day/night) in a 12-hour photoperiod. Meanwhile, during rice cultivation in the glasshouse, standard cultivation practices were employed, including insect pests and disease controls.


### Cloning of the OsMADS56 gene and sequence analysis
  To amplify the OsMADS56 gene, a specific primer pair, namely MADS56F/MADS56R, was designed based on OsMADS56 reported in the database (Os10g0536100). Total RNA from leaves of rice plant [extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen)] was utilized as a template for amplification of first-strand cDNA of OsMADS56 [cloned AMV First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Invitrogen)]. The cDNA was then subjected to PCR amplification of full-length OsMADS56 gene using KAPA2G Robust DNA polymerase (KAPA Biosystems) with reaction conditions as follows: one cycle of pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of three steps consisting of denaturation (95 °C for 30 sec), annealing (55 °C for 15 sec), and elongation (72 °C for 30 sec), finally followed by one last cycle at 72 °C for 5 min. The amplified fragment was extracted using Silica Bead DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Thermo Scientific) and cloned into Escherichia coli DH5α. The fragment was then sequenced (1st BASE DNA Sequencing services, Singapore), analyzed by DnaMan1, and identified by basic local alignment search tool (BLAST)X from National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/blast).


### Establishment of OsMADS56 overexpressed transgenic rice plants
  The OsMADS56 gene was constructed in the pCAMBIA1305.1 expression vector by inserting the gene into the BglII and BstEII restriction sites of the vector to overexpress the gene under the control of a constitutive promoter Cauliflower Mosaic Virus 35S (CaMV35S). The BstEII site in the OsMADS56 gene was mutated by substituting C60 to A to eliminate the BstEII site. PCRs did nucleotide substitution (Table 1). The constructed plasmid was subsequently introduced into the Agrobacterium tumefaciens LBA4404 strain. The A. tumefaciens strain harbouring the constructed plasmid was spotted onto embryogenic calli of the rice cultivar Nipponbare to facilitate the transformation of the OsMADS56 overexpression construct as previously described by Hiei et al. ### Southern-blot and quantitative real-time (RT)-PCR analysis
  The copy number of constructed OsMADS56 inserted into the rice genome was quantified by Southern blot hybridization. The genomes of first-generation (T0) transgenic rice plants were extracted using the Cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method with some modifications. About 10 µg of transgenic rice genomic DNAs were digested with BamHI restriction enzyme and electroporesed on 0.8 % gel agarose (75 V, overnight). The digested genomic DNAs were then blotted onto the nitrocellulose membrane (Amersham Hybond N+, GE). Labeled hpt gene was used as a probe to detect the inserted OsMADS56 construct following the manufacturer's instruction of Amersham Gene Images AlkPhos Direct and Detection System (GE Healthcare).

  Quantitative (q) RT-PCR of selected T1 transgenic rice line was performed to analyze overexpression of OsMADS56. Four tissues of root, stem, leaf blade, and leaf sheath were collected from T1 transgenic rice seedling grown for three weeks. Two other tissues (panicles and leaf sheath) were collected from the mature T1 transgenic line. The six tissues were subjected to RNA extraction using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), and 10 ng of total RNAs were used as the template for qRT-PCR (KAPA SYBR FAST One-Step qRT-PCR kit, KAPA Biosystems) and EcoReal Time PCR System (Illumina). The OsMADS56 gene expression quantification was normalized against the actin housekeeping gene. Quant Prime qPCR primer design tool (http://www.quantprime.de) was employed to design the qPCR primers (Table 1).


### Abscisic acid (ABA) sensitivity assay
  Abscisic acid (ABA) sensitivity was tested for the WT and T2 OsMADS56 overexpressed transgenic plants. Seeds of both tested lines were grown in normal ½ Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium in bottomless 96-well plates for 5 days. The germinate seedlings (in triplicate, 8 plants each replicate) were transferred to ½ MS medium supplemented 0 and 3 µM ABA. After 8 days of ABA treatment in a growth chamber, each seedling was measured for height, root length, and fresh weight..18


### PEG-induced dehydration simulating drought treatment
  For the preliminary drought response analysis, the WT and T2 OsMADS56 overexpressed transgenic lines were grown hydroponically in bottomless 96-well plates using Hoagland solution for three weeks. The three-week-old WT and T2 OsMADS56 overexpressed transgenic lines seedlings (12 plants each) were transferred into Hoagland solution containing 20 % (w/v) PEG6000 for 6 d for simulating drought stress treatment. The PEG stress-treated plant recovery was performed in Hoagland solution for 7 d, and the survival rate of each plant was recorded.19


### Statistical analysis
  Statistical analyses were performed by applying one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with a confidence interval ≥ 95 %, followed by data visualizations using Graphad Prism version 10.4.2 and Microsoft Excel.


## Results

### Sequence analysis of the isolated OsMADS56 full-length gene
  A 702 bp fragment of the full-length OsMADS56 gene (OsMADS56_N) encodes 233 amino acids and was successfully isolated from wild-type rice cv. Nipponbare and cloned in the pGEMT-Easy vector. A BLASTX analysis of the amplified fragment revealed that it exhibited 100 % identity to the Oryza sativa Japonica Group MADS-box 56 transcription factor (NP_001390992.1). The multiple sequence alignment of the amino acid of isolated OsMADS56 in comparison to those of NP_001390992.1 and other MADS56 sequences of other monocots such as identified from GenBank showed varying identities (69.43 % to 98.71 %) (Fig. 1). As previously described by Arora et al. (2007),2 the alignment result indicates the predicted presence of plant-specific MIKC-type MADS-box proteins. These proteins exhibit a conserved N-terminal region of the 60 amino acid MADS-box domain. This region is followed by an intervening region of approximately 30 amino acids, which shows less conservation. A moderately conserved Keratin-like domain, comprising approximately 70 amino acids, is present. This domain is primarily involved in heterodimerization. The C-terminal region, which plays roles in transcriptional activation and higher-order complex formation, is highly variable in length. ### Abscisic acid (ABA) sensitivity of OsMADS56 overexpressing rice line M76.7
  Given the pivotal role of ABA in abiotic stress response, we investigated the reaction of the OsMADS56 gene to exogenous ABA in three plants of T2 generation of M76.7 transgenic line overexpressing OsMADS56 gene OX5, OX7, and OX9. The sensitivity of the OsMADS56 overexpressed line to exogenous ABA was examined at the post-germination stage (Fig. 4a-4d). Both the transgenic and wild-type (WT) plants exhibited similar sensitivity to exogenous ABA (Fig. 4a). No significant differences were observed in relative shoot length, root length, and fresh weight between the transgenic and WT lines in response to exogenous ABA (Fig. 4b-4d). ## Discussion
  Transcription factors (TFs) represent a critical component of plant molecular components, functioning as pivotal elements within regulatory networks that plants employ in response to environmental stressors. These factors regulate the expression of multiple target genes, thereby influencing various physiological processes and environmental responses in plants.20, 21 The MADS-box domains of transcription factors (TFs) regulate various developmental processes, including seed germination vegetative, and reproductive growth. These processes encompass floral development and senescence. Additionally, they play a role in regulating stress tolerance in response to both abiotic and biotic stimuli. These proteins contain a conserved MADS domain, 60 amino acids long, and located at the N-terminal end. They bind to the target genes by recognizing the CArG-box DNA motif (CC[A/]6GG). The presence of the MADS domain can distinguish the two types of MADS-box TFs. In type I, the MADS domain is followed by an extended highly variable carboxy-terminal domain. In contrast, in type II, which is also known as MIKC type, the conserved MADS domain is followed by three other conserved domains intervening, keratin-like, and carboxy-terminal domains.6 In the case of plants, the MADS-box genes have been found in a broad range of monocot and dicot plant species.21 Here, we aligned the MADS-box 56 of O. sativa cv. Nipponbare with other monocots species. The four conserved MIKC domains of MADS-box 56 were identified in the multiple amino acid sequence alignments of OsMADS56 isolated from rice cv. Nipponbare compared to the OsMADS56 of rice cv. Japonica and Indica also to the MADS56 of other five monocots, including O. glaberrima, S. italica, S. bicolor, T. aestivum, and Z. mays collected from the database (Fig. 1a).

  Besides its role in grain development, the MADS-box family of genes plays a pivotal role in regulating plant flower architecture, flowering induction, vegetative development, and biotic and abiotic stresses.6, 10 In rice, OsMADS26 has been demonstrated to play a negative regulatory role in disease resistance and water deficit.11 From 75 MADS-box genes in rice, four MADS-box genes (OsMADS18, 22, 26, and 27) were up-regulated more than two-fold during cold and dehydration stress treatments. Conversely, three other genes (OsMADS2, 30, and 55) exhibited more than two-fold downregulation during dehydration and salt stress.2 Recently, OsMADS56, which has been demonstrated to regulate rice flowering time15, grain size16, and plant height14, has also been involved in the salt stress response12 and heat tolerance.13 Accordingly, the present study aimed to isolate and overexpress the OsMADS56 gene to evaluate its role in rice response to drought stress and validate its function in rice plant development.

  A phylogenetic tree was constructed which compared the MADS56 from rice cultivars (Japonica and Nipponbare) and 5 other monocots to the OsMADS18, 22, 26, and 55, which were identified to be up- or down-regulated during cold, salt and dehydration, treatments (Fig. 1b). It reveals that the MADS56 genes were grouped in one cluster separated from OsMADS22 and 55 in one cluster and OsMADS18 and 26 in other cluster. Arora et al. (2007)2 performed the phylogenetic analysis of 75 rice MADS-box genes; OsMADS56 is clustered with 37 other MADS-box genes (including OsMADS18, 22, 26, and 55) in the MIKCc-type genes. Unlike OsMADS18 (which belongs to the subgroup of SQUA-like proteins) and OsMADS22, 26 and 55 (which belong to the subgroup of AGL12-like proteins), OsMADS56 belongs to the subgroup of TM3-like proteins.2 Like SQUA-like proteins, TM3-like proteins may function in different aspects of flower initiation or vegetative identity.5

  To investigate the function of the OsMADS56 gene related to drought stress and other phenotypes related to the OsMAD56 role in rice development, we established OsMADS56 overexpressed transgenic rice plants. Transgenic rice lines with single copy T-DNA insertion designated as M76.7 were used for subsequent analysis under normal and PEG-induced dehydration conditions. Previously, Hong et al. (2023)14 reported that transgenic rice lines overexpressing MADS56 have shorter height compared to wild type at the maturity stage. They concluded that MADS56 is a rice plant height regulator that underlies qphSN10.2.

  Expression analysis of OsMADS56 in WT under normal conditions showed that expression levels varied in six collected tissues of root, stem, leaf blade, leaf sheath, panicle and flag leaf (Fig. 3). This revealed a diverse function of OsMADS56 in rice development as previously observed by Hong et al. (2023)14 through qRT‐PCR analysis, MADS56 has ubiquitously expressed in rice vegetative and reproductive tissues throughout plant development. The present study found OsMADS56 to be most highly expressed in the flag leaf, which may be related to the classification of OsMADS56 as a member of the TM3-like protein subgroup.2 This subgroup has been shown to function in various aspects of flower initiation.5 On the other hand, the expression of OsMADS56 was overexpressed in the transgenic line M76.7, where the expression level in six representative tissues from root, stem, leaf blade, leaf sheath, panicle, and flag leaf was higher than in WT (Fig. 3).

  The T2 generations of M76.7 were divided into three segregating lines, namely OX5, OX7, and OX9. The OX5, OX7, and OX9 were first subjected to an exogenous ABA sensitivity assay (Fig. 4) to investigate the transgenic rice overexpressing OsMADS56 to cope with abiotic stress responses such as drought. ABA-regulated genes are commonly understood to contribute to plant tolerance in response to dehydration. The ABA-induced genes are enhanced to express proteins involved in stress tolerance, including regulatory proteins such as TFs. In contrast, the ABA-repressed gene products are enriched for proteins corresponding with growth. These data indicate a central role for ABA in plant tolerance to abiotic stress.22 Furthermore, plants regulate their ABA content to respond to external and internal stimuli, which is essential for defending against stress and assimilating stress signals.21 Previously, transgenic rice overexpressing OsMYB48-1, tolerant to drought and salinity stress, showed a significant increase in sensitivity to exogenous ABA compared to WT.18 In this study, we observed that exogenous ABA treatment did not enhance the sensitivity of transgenic rice overexpressing OsMADS56 in response to exogenous ABA compared to WT plants. During drought stress, plants induce numerous genes with diverse functions to acquire drought stress tolerance, including the ABA-dependent and the ABA-independent transcriptional regulatory systems. ABA-RESPONSIVE ELEMENT BINDING PROTEINS (AREBs)/ABRE-BINDING FACTORS (ABFs) are stress-responsive TFs induced by ABA that are associated with the gene expression process related to stress tolerance. Conversely, the ABA-independent transcriptional regulatory system of seed plants includes an ABA-unresponsive subclass I SnRK2, which regulates stable gene expression in an ABA-independent way through the mRNA decay pathway and plant growth during drought stress.23

  To investigate the role of OsMADS56 related to drought stress, we simulated drought stress using the polyethylene glycol (PEG)-based method. Polyethylene glycol (PEG) can cause water deficiency and physiological disorders in plants, resulting in reduced water absorption and loss of cell swelling.24 The effects of PEG stress and soil drought on plants are similar.25, 26 Both can dehydrate plant tissues and affect all levels of plant organization. These stresses can also alter water relationships, biochemical processes, membrane structure, and organelles. Therefore, PEG is often used to mimic drought and is a common method for studying plant responses to water stress.26 The PEG-based method has also been employed in recent reports to study the response of rice to drought stress.27, 28, 29 In this study, a simulated drought stress treatment using PEG6000 revealed that two transgenic lines, OX7 and OX9, exhibited higher tolerance to PEG-drought stress than WT plants, though the difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 5). However, this result needs to be confirmed using a soil-based method because the PEG- and soil-based methods involve different processes of water loss. PEG-induced drought occurs rapidly in plants, and water loss in the PEG solution is caused by osmotic potential. Meanwhile, in soil drought, the water accumulation is lost from the soil through gradual processes, which lead to less absorption of the soil by the planted material due to the lack of water, thus causing stress to plants.26 The higher tolerance of OX7 and OX9 compared to WT during PEG-drought corresponded to the recent findings on the role of the OsMADS56 gene in response to other abiotic stresses, including salt and heat stress. Cui et al. (2024)12 reported that OsMADS56 overexpressed lines enhanced salt tolerance by up-regulating ROS-scavenging enzyme activities to reduce ROS accumulation during salt stress. He et al. (2024)13 reported that the transgenic rice plants overexpressing MADS56 showed significantly greater heat tolerance than the wild-type plants. In contrast, the MADS56 knockout mutant showed a significant decrease in heat tolerance.

  To further evaluate the role of the OsMADS56 gene in rice development, we examined two segregating populations of the transgenic lines overexpressing OsMADS56 (OX7 and OX9). The first distinct phenotype observed was the PH of OX7, which was shorter than WT, and conversely, the PH of OX9, which was higher than WT (Fig. 6a and 6c). MADS56 has been reportedly involved in plant hormone signalling, particularly in GA biosynthesis.16 Hong et al. (2023)14 further confirmed the function of MADS56 in PH. They found that the transgenic rice lines overexpressing MADS56 were significantly shorter at maturity than the wild type. MADS56 (qphSN10.2) interacted epistatically with a GA signalling regulator, GAMYB (qphSN1.4). They observed that in the MADS56 overexpressing lines, the transcript level of seven GA biosynthetic genes was significantly reduced, whereas the expression of GA deactivating genes was increased considerably. These results suggest that MADS56 expression associated with the GA homeostasis regulates the rice PH. Hong et al. (2023)14 also confirmed that MADS56 physically interacts with MADS57 to co-regulate the expression of the gibberellin (GA) metabolic genes OsGA2ox3 and Elongated Uppermost Internode1 (EUI1). MADS57 is known to be a positive regulator of rice PH in the GA-mediated regulatory pathway.30 The next phenotype related to the OsMADS56 gene was the grain size, which is essential in determining the grain weight and yield in rice. The OX7 has significantly higher grain length and width than WT and OX9 (Fig. 6b, 6d and 6e). Here, we observed that the PH variation corresponded to HD and NP. The OX7 with lower PH had earlier HD and higher NP, NG, and GY compared to WT (Fig. 7). Previously, Zhan et al. (2022)16 studied the grain length QTL GL 10 encoding MADS56 which positively regulates grain length and weight. Similarly, they observed that GL10 overexpression significantly increased several features, including grain length and weight, earlier HD, and grain number per plant. Similarly, Zuo et al. (2022)31 observed a reduction in OsMADS56 expression in the knockout OsMADS56 rice mutant, leading to a decrease in grain size. Conversely, overexpressing OsMADS56 resulted in increased grain weight and size.


## Conclusions
  In conclusion, an OsMADS56 overexpressed rice line (OX7) showed improved tolerance to PEG-drought stress, and the OsMADS56 gene may not be ABA-dependent since the overexpressed lines did not show enhancement in sensitivity to ABA compared to WT. Consistently, the OX7 had a shorter PH than WT, confirming the role of OsMADS56 in rice PH regulation, which may be regulated by modulating GA homeostasis. Other phenotypic variation were observed in OX7, which had earlier HD, higher NP, NG, and GY than WT. It showed an improvement in OX7 productivity compared to the WT. These findings may serve as a foundation for further research, specifically investigating the potential for enhancing rice tolerance to drought stress and increasing rice productivity through the overexpression of OsMADS56.
