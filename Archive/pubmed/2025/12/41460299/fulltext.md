# Skin delivery and anti-inflammatory effects of the anesthetic propofol against psoriasiform lesions through KEAP1/Nrf2/HO-1 pathway activation


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-025-05986-1.


## Introduction
  Psoriasis is one of the most common inflammatory skin diseases and is characterized by erythematous plaques covered with silvery scales [73]. The pathogenesis of psoriasis is associated with multiple factors associated with genetic, immune, environmental, and oxidative stress responses [20]. This chronic autoimmune disorder affects 125 million people worldwide and 2‒4% of people in Western countries [17]. Seventy-three percent of psoriasis patients have comorbidities such as cardiovascular events, ischemic stroke, diabetes, obesity, and arthritis [68]. An elevated risk of melanoma and squamous cell carcinoma is also found in psoriasis patients [12]. The conventional therapies for psoriasis include corticosteroids, phototherapy, retinoids, vitamin D analogs, and biologics. These approaches have several drawbacks, such as short-term efficacy, easy relapse, intolerable adverse effects, and high cost [52]. Owing to the increasing incidence of psoriatic disease, new strategies with effective and safe options for treating psoriasis are urgently needed. One of the approaches used to develop novel therapies is drug repurposing. The advantages of drug repurposing for new indications are the fast development time, low cost, and high success rate [59].

  Propofol (2,6-diisopropylphenol) is a γ-aminobutyric (GABA) receptor agonist that is one of the most commonly used anesthetics for inducing and maintaining anesthesia and sedation during surgery [15]. In addition to its application as an anesthetic agent, propofol is reported to have diverse bioactivities, such as immune modulation, analgesia, neuroprotection, and antioxidant effects [62]. Propofol can mitigate inflammation with potential effects on sepsis, ischemia, acute pulmonary injury, liver injury, and COVID-19 [79]. At the cell level, propofol can regulate the immune response of lymphocytes, macrophages, and neutrophils [71]. The oxidative stress evoked in epidermal keratinocytes is also prevented by this anesthetic drug [72]. These beneficial effects on inflammation suppression suggest the potential for its development as a candidate for treating psoriasis. A total of 78% of psoriasis patients are recognized as having mild-to-moderate disease [49]. The first-line choice for mild-to-moderate psoriasis management is topical therapy. Owing to its presence in plasma and dose-dependent side effects after intravenous propofol administration, topical delivery is favorable for developing an antipsoriasis approach to achieve skin targeting and avoid systemic effects.

  Li et al. [34] combined intravenous scopolamine and propofol to treat recalcitrant psoriasis in ten patients. However, the therapeutic efficacy and antipsoriatic mechanisms of topically applied propofol are still unclear. The aim of this study was to assess the antipsoriatic activity of propofol via skin absorption. Psoriasis is considered a T-cell-mediated disorder that depends upon the interplay between several cells, including keratinocytes, macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells [26]. All of these cells play vital roles in psoriatic pathogenesis to induce epidermal hyperplasia and massive immune cell infiltration. It has been demonstrated that > 80% of the genes whose expression is upregulated in psoriatic lesions are involved in the stimulation of keratinocytes and macrophages [57]. To comprehensively explore the anti-inflammatory activity of propofol, we employed keratinocytes, macrophages, and neutrophils as cell models. The possible pathways of the anti-inflammatory effect of propofol were also elucidated in the present work. The prerequisite of successful topical therapy is sufficient skin delivery into the lesion site. We also examined the skin absorption of propofol via in vitro and in silico platforms. Finally, the safety of topical propofol in the skin was evaluated.


## Materials and methods

### Animals
  Eight-week-old male BALB/c mice were acquired from the National Laboratory Animal Center in Taipei, Taiwan, for the purposes of neutrophil isolation, in vitro permeation tests (IVPTs), imiquimod (IMQ)-induced psoriasis models, and skin irritation assessments. Additionally, one-week-old pigs were sourced from PigModel Animal Technology in Miaoli, Taiwan, specifically for IVPT. All animal experimentation protocols received approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Chang Gung University and were carried out in compliance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals established by the institution.


### Preparation and culture of the cells
  In this investigation, human keratinocytes (HaCaT cells), differentiated monocytes (THP-1 cells), and primary neutrophils derived from mouse bone marrow were utilized. HaCaT cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 100 U/ml of penicillin–streptomycin, under conditions of 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. The THP-1 cell line, which serves as a macrophage model, was maintained in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and penicillin–streptomycin. Differentiation of THP-1 cells into macrophage-like cells was achieved by the addition of 100 ng/ml phorbol myristate acetate (PMA) for a duration of 36 h, followed by a 10-h incubation in fresh medium. Mouse bone marrow was harvested from the femurs through flushing with a 23-gauge needle, and the resulting cell suspension was filtered using a 70-μm mesh filter to retain only single cells. The filtrate was subsequently centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min. Mouse neutrophils were isolated using an EasySep Mouse Neutrophil Enrichment Kit (STEMCELL Technologies), following the manufacturer's protocol.


### Cytotoxicity was determined by 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) and a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8)
  HaCaT cells (2 × 104 cells/ml) or differentiated THP-1 cells (1 × 106 cells/ml) were treated with propofol at concentrations ranging from 0 to 100 μM in the culture medium for a duration of 24 h. Following this incubation, MTT reagent (0.5 mg/ml) was introduced and the cells were further incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. The viability of the cells was assessed by measuring the photometric absorbance at 570 nm using a microplate reader. In the CCK-8 assay, viable cells were evaluated by adding culture medium containing WST-8 solution for 1 h, after which the absorbance at 450 nm was measured with a microplate reader. Cell viability was expressed as a percentage relative to the control group.


### Proinflammatory mediator analysis by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
  HaCaT cells (1.5 × 105 cells/ml) and differentiated THP-1 cells (5 × 105 cells/ml) were cultured in 24-well plates. To induce inflammatory conditions, HaCaT and THP-1 cells were activated with tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α at a concentration of 20 ng/ml and imiquimod (IMQ) at 10 μg/ml, respectively. Additionally, propofol was administered at concentrations ranging from 0 to 100 μM, and dexamethasone was added at a concentration of 1 μM to the wells. IMQ at 10 μg/ml was also utilized to stimulate mouse neutrophils at a density of 6 × 105 cells/ml. Following a 24-h incubation period, the cells were harvested, and the levels of cytokines and chemokines were quantified. The concentrations of interleukin (IL)−1β, IL-6, IL-8, CXC motif chemokine ligand (CXCL)1, and TNF-α released from the cells were assessed using commercial ELISA kits (BioLegend), in accordance with the manufacturer's protocols.


### Western blotting
  The expression levels of mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs), protein kinase B (Akt), and signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT)3 in HaCaT and differentiated THP-1 cells were evaluated using immunoblotting techniques. The cells underwent lysis with a lysis buffer, and the total protein concentration was determined using a Bradford assay at a wavelength of 595 nm. Following this, the proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and subsequently transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary antibody diluted to 1:1000. After the incubation period, the membrane was washed with Tris-buffered saline. Protein detection was performed using a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody diluted to 1:5000, in conjunction with a Western Lightning Plus-ECL detection kit. Signal detection was carried out using a ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad). A densitometry assay was performed to quantify the protein concentrations, with glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) serving as an internal control.


### Antioxidant assay
  HaCaT cells (6 × 105 cells/ml) were treated with propofol (100 μM) for 3, 6, 12, or 24 h to examine the antioxidant effects on keratinocytes. Proteins related to oxidative stress were quantified by immunoblotting. The primary antibodies used here were anti-kelch-like ECH-associated protein (KEAP)1, anti-nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor (Nrf)2, anti-heme oxygenase (HO)−1, anti-catalase (CAT), anti-superoxide dismutase (SOD)2, and anti-GAPDH. The other protocols were the same as those used for western blotting.


### Scavenging assay by 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) and 2,2’-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS)
  The stable nitrogen-centered free radicals DPPH and ABTS were employed to assess the scavenging ability of propofol [31]. Propofol (0‒100 μM) was added to DPPH buffer (100 μM in ethanol) or ABTS buffer (7 mM in 2045 mM K2S2O8) for 15 min. The changes in the absorbances of DPPH and ABTS were recorded with a spectrophotometer at 517 and 734 nm, respectively. α-Tocopherol (0‒30 μM) was used as the positive control for comparison.


### Reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection by confocal microscopy
  Intracellular ROS in HaCaT keratinocytes were detected by confocal microscopy to observe the presence of alkyl radicals in keratinocytes after propofol treatment (100 μM). The TNF-α-activated cells (1 × 105 cells/ml) were treated with propofol at 37 °C for 4 h. Then, the cells were incubated with 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFDA) at 25 mM and Hoechst 33342 for 30 min. The keratinocytes were washed three times with serum-free medium to remove the excess DCFDA outside the cells. Formaldehyde was used to fix the cells for observation via confocal microscopy (TCS SP8 X AOBS, Leica).


### Chemotaxis assay
  A chemotaxis assay was conducted to assess the stimulation of keratinocytes by conditioned medium derived from differentiated THP-1 cells. The conditioned medium was obtained from THP-1 cells that had been activated with imiquimod (IMQ) at a concentration of 10 μg/ml and subsequently treated with 100 μM propofol for a duration of 2 h. Following this, the conditioned medium was introduced to keratinocytes at a density of 6 × 105 cells/ml in a 96-well plate. The plate was incubated at 37 °C for 15 min prior to the immunoblotting assay. The protein levels of STAT3 in HaCaT cells were measured to evaluate the chemotactic interaction between keratinocytes and macrophages.


### Macrophage migration assay
  The chemotaxis of macrophages induced by the conditioned medium from TNF-α-activated keratinocytes was examined via the Transwell method. The chemotaxis assay was conducted in a Transwell plate (Millicell, Millipore) with a pore size of 3 μm. The conditioned medium was collected from HaCaT cells stimulated with TNF-α with or without propofol at 100 μM for 24 h. Differentiated THP-1 cells (5 × 104 cells/ml) were placed in the upper chamber of the Transwell. The conditioned medium from HaCaT (500 μl) cells was added to the lower chamber. After being incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in the presence of 5% CO2, crystal violet (1%) was added to stain the migrated macrophages in the lower well, and images were taken via optical microscopy (DMi8, Leica).


### In vitro permeation test (IVPT)
  The administration of propofol through pig and mouse skin was conducted utilizing a Franz cell apparatus. The dorsal skin of the subjects was positioned between the donor and receptor compartments, with the effective permeation area of the cells measuring 0.79 cm2. In addition to employing intact skin, various modified skin types were prepared as permeation barriers, including stratum corneum (SC)-stripped, delipidized, deproteinized, and desebumized skins, following methodologies established in prior research [38, 75]. The donor compartment was filled with 0.5 ml of a propofol solution (3 mM) in a 30% ethanol/pH 7.4 buffer. We prepared a fresh solution with 3 mM propofol (10 ml). This solution was shaken for 30 min at room temperature, then immediately used in the IVPT experiment. The receptor compartment (5 ml) contained a 30% ethanol solution buffered to pH 7.4. The temperature of the receptor compartment was maintained at 37 °C, with a stirring rate of 600 rpm facilitated by a stir bar. At predetermined time intervals, aliquots of 0.3 ml were extracted from the receptor medium. Upon completion of the 24-h experiment, the skin was excised from the Franz cells to assess the extent of propofol accumulation within the skin reservoir. The skin samples were weighed, sectioned, and homogenized using a MagNA Lyser (Roche) in methanol. The resultant homogenate was subjected to centrifugation and filtration to isolate the supernatant. Quantification of propofol was performed using high-performance liquid chromatography (Hitachi) to determine both skin deposition (nmol/mg) and flux (nmol/cm2/h). Additionally, differential stripping and cyanoacrylate skin surface casting techniques were employed to investigate the accumulation of propofol within hair follicles [33]. The dermal/transdermal selectivity index (S value) was calculated by dividing the skin deposition by the flux.


### In silico docking of propofol with SC lipids
  The superimposition of propofol with SC lipids, including ceramides, palmitic acid, cholesterol, and cholesteryl sulfate, was computed to understand the ligand-binding interaction. Discovery Studio 2021 (Dassault Systems) software was used to estimate the docking by measuring the negative CDOCKER energy.


### In vivo psoriasiform mouse model
  IMQ cream (Aldara, 3 M) was used to induce psoriasiform lesions on the mouse back according to a previous study [61]. The dorsal hair was shaved 2 days before the start of the experiment. The experimental groups included (i) the control group of healthy mice without any treatment,(ii) the IMQ group of the mice receiving 62.5 mg of 5% IMQ cream plus water 2 h postinduction, once daily for five consecutive days; (iii) the propofol group of the mice receiving IMQ plus 150 μl of propofol (3 mM) 2 h postinduction, once daily for five days; and (iv) the positive control group of the mice receiving IMQ plus dexamethasone ointment (0.1%, Sinphar Pharmaceutical) 2 h postinduction, once daily for five days. Each experimental group comprised six animals. The skin colors a* and b* were quantified every day via a colorimeter (CD100, Yokogawa). The definition of a* and b* is specified in Commission Internationale de l’Eclairage standard. a* presents the green–red axis, whereas b* represents blue-yellow axis. For dermatological evaluation, a* can reflect cutaneous erythema. b* in skin reflects pigmentation. Transepidermal water loss (TEWL) was also measured every day via a tweezer (MPA580, Courage and Khazaka). The phenotype of the skin surface was visualized via a digital camera and microscope (AM7515M2T, Dino-Lite) at the end of the experiment. After the termination of the experiment, the mice were euthanized and the skin was excised for cytokine/chemokine analysis and histological examination. For the cytokine/chemokine assay, the excised skin was homogenized using a MagNA Lyser for further ELISA.


### Histological observation
  Dorsal skin sections taken from various groups were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The skin was preserved with 10% buffered formaldehyde before being embedded in paraffin wax. The paraffin-embedded samples were sliced and stained with H&E for microscopic observation. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis was performed by incubating the skin slices with anti-Ki67, anti-F4/80, or anti-Ly6G antibodies for 1 h, followed by incubation with biotinylated donkey anti-goat IgG for 20 min.


### In vivo skin irritation test
  Propofol (150 μl of 3 mM in 30% ethanol/pH 7.4 buffer) or dexamethasone (150 mg of 0.1% ointment) was topically applied to the back skin of healthy mice daily for 5 days. The microscopic appearance of the skin surface was monitored. Erythema (a*), skin surface pH, and TEWL were quantified for five consecutive days. After sacrifice, the excised skin was harvested for H&E-stained histology.


### Statistical analysis
  The data are presented as the means and standard errors of the means (SEMs). The data of the treatment groups were compared via the unpaired t-test or Kruskal‒Wallis test and post hoc Dunn’s test. The 0.05 (*), 0.01 (**), and 0.001 (***) levels of probability were regarded as statistically significant.


## Results

### Propofol mitigates proinflammatory effectors in activated keratinocytes
  To avoid the cytotoxic effect of propofol on keratinocytes, we investigated the impact of propofol (0‒100 μM) on keratinocyte survival. Cell viability was monitored by both MTT and CCK-8 assays. The MTT data revealed that propofol did not cause cytotoxicity at concentrations less than 50 μM (Fig. 1A). Propofol at 100 μM significantly reduced cell viability to 93% (p < 0.05); however, this cytotoxicity was still negligible. In the case of CCK-8, propofol had no cytotoxic effect on HaCaT cells at any of the tested doses (1‒100 μM). The keratinocytes were treated with 1, 10, 50, or 100 μM of this drug in the following experiments. To explore whether propofol inhibited the inflammatory response of keratinocytes induced by TNF-α, we first evaluated its effect on cytokine/chemokine expression via ELISA. TNF-α markedly increased the protein expression of IL-6, IL-8, and CXCL1 in HaCaT cells (Fig. 1C to E). A low concentration of propofol (1 μM) had no effect on the inflammatory response of keratinocytes activated by TNF-α. At higher concentrations (10‒100 μM). Propofol significantly inhibited these proinflammatory mediators in a dose-dependent manner. Propofol at 100 μM inhibited IL-6, IL-8, and CXCL1 overexpression by 94%, 95%, and 84%, respectively. ### Propofol has no effect on MAPK, Akt, or STAT3 signaling
  Given the potential of propofol to arrest proinflammatory effectors in keratinocytes, we aimed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the immunomodulating effect of this anesthetic. The MAPK, phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt, and Janus kinase (JAK)/STAT3 pathways are known to be involved in downstream signaling in TNF-α-stimulated keratinocytes. An immunoblotting assay was conducted to detect signaling pathway-related proteins to verify whether propofol regulated these signaling cascades. TNF-α intervention significantly increased the phosphorylation of MAPKs, including p38, extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK), and Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), as determined by immunoblotting (Fig. 2A; uncut images of triplicate samples are shown in Suppl. Figure 1). Propofol at 100 μM did not significantly affect the upregulation of MAPKs. The results of the quantitative analysis of MAPK proteins confirmed that propofol failed to regulate this molecular signaling pathway (Fig. 2B to 2D). We also analyzed the expression of Akt. The increase in p-Akt expression induced by TNF-α was not attenuated by propofol (Fig. 2E and 2 F; uncut images of triplicate immunoblotting data are shown in Suppl. Figure 2), suggesting that the Akt pathway is not involved in the reduction in inflammation induced by this drug. Continuous STAT3 phosphorylation is a potential target in psoriatic pathogenesis. To examine the effect of propofol on inflammatory signaling in psoriasis, we estimated the expression level of STAT3 in stimulated keratinocytes. ### Anti-inflammatory activity of propofol via redox balance
  Defective redox homeostasis in epidermal keratinocytes plays a critical role in psoriatic inflammation. KEAP1 is a sensor of oxidative stress. The major antioxidants in the skin include Nrf2, HO-1, CAT, and SOD2. To clarify the mechanisms by which propofol affects psoriasis-like keratinocytes, the components of the redox pathway were investigated. After treatment with propofol, keratinocytes presented obvious downregulation of KEAP1, along with an increase in the levels of enzymatic antioxidants (Fig. 3A; uncut images of triplicate immunoblots are shown in Suppl. Figure 4). The results of the quantitative western blot analysis revealed a decrease in KEAP1 at 6 and 12 h after treatment with propofol (Fig. 3B). In contrast, the antioxidative index Nrf2 was increased by approximately 1.7-fold after propofol treatment at 6 and 12 h (Fig. 3C). In the case of HO-1 and CAT, propofol considerably increased the levels of both enzymatic antioxidants by 2‒2.5-fold at 24 h (Fig. 3D and 3E). We also detected increased expression of SOD2 following propofol intervention (Fig. 3F).   The antioxidant activity of this anesthetic was further evaluated via the chemical models of DPPH and ABTS. α-tocopherol was used as the positive control in this experiment. Compared with the DMSO control, both propofol and α-tocopherol dose-dependently increased DPPH reduction (Fig. 3G), indicating the ability of propofol to scavenge radical species directly. A 47% DPPH reduction could be achieved by treatment with propofol at 100 μM. In parallel with DPPH, propofol decreased ABTS in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 3H). Reactive oxygen species (ROS) production in HaCaT cells was visualized by DCFDA staining in confocal microscopy. Compared with that of the control, the green fluorescence of DCFDA in the cells was greater following TNF-α stimulation (Fig. 3I). We observed that ROS generation was strongly attenuated by propofol treatment. The level of ROS in the control cells without TNF-α activation was not changed by propofol. The quantification of the fluorescence confirmed this result (Fig. 3J). Overall, the results of the oxidative stress evaluation suggested that propofol reduced inflammatory responses in keratinocytes by inhibiting ROS and increasing the activity of antioxidative enzymes.


### Propofol inhibits proinflammatory effectors and the migration of activated macrophages
  Anesthetic agents such as propofol can directly modulate the functions of immune cells. We also examined whether propofol suppressed the inflammatory response in psoriasis via the regulation of immune cells, including macrophages and neutrophils. The viability of macrophage-like cells (differentiated THP-1) was not affected by propofol at the concentrations tested (1‒100 μM) according to the results of the MTT and CCK-8 analyses (Fig. 4A and B). Macrophages overexpress Toll-like receptors (TLRs). IMQ is a TLR7/8 agonist that triggers the production of proinflammatory mediators by macrophages. IMQ significantly increased the levels of IL-6 and IL-8 in macrophages by 44- and 21-fold, respectively (Fig. 4C and D). We found that propofol at 1‒100 μM dose-dependently attenuated the overexpression of IL-6 and IL-8. The dose of 100 μM reversed the overexpression of both effectors to the level of the baseline control. The TNF-α level was increased by IMQ (Fig. 4E), whereas 50 and 100 μM propofol suppressed the TNF-α level by 32% and 49%, respectively. However, low drug concentrations (1 and 10 μM) did not change TNF-α expression after IMQ stimulation. In the positive control, dexamethasone inhibited the overexpression of IL-6 and TNF-α but not that of IL-8. The immunoblotting of MAPKs revealed no inhibition by propofol treatment (100 μM) in IMQ-activated THP-1 cells (Fig. 4F; uncut images of immunoblotting in triplicate are shown in Suppl. Figure 5).   Chemotaxis plays a crucial role in the interaction between immune cells and skin host cells, specifically keratinocytes, within the pathological cycle of psoriasis. To investigate the influence of propofol on the communication between macrophages and keratinocytes, we evaluated the effects of conditioned medium derived from IMQ-activated macrophages on the proliferation of HaCaT cells through immunoblotting techniques. The results indicated that the conditioned medium from the activated macrophages significantly elevated the levels of phosphorylated STAT3 (p-STAT3), which serves as a biomarker for keratinocyte proliferation (refer to Fig. 4J and K; the complete immunoblotting images in triplicate are presented in Suppl. Figure 6). STAT3 phosphorylation in conditioned medium-exposed cells was unchanged after propofol treatment, suggesting that propofol failed to prevent macrophage chemotaxis. To investigate the role of the interaction between macrophages and keratinocytes in chemotaxis, we examined the migration of macrophages in response to conditioned medium from activated HaCaT cells. The extravasation and infiltration of macrophages into lesional sites in psoriasis is a critical response of the chemoattractants of keratinocytes. In the Transwell experiments, macrophage migration induced by the conditioned medium from keratinocytes was significantly reduced in the presence of propofol (Fig. 4L). Compared with the TNF-α-stimulated control, 100 μM propofol resulted in a 40% decrease in migration (Fig. 4M).


### Propofol mitigates proinflammatory effectors in activated neutrophils
  In addition to macrophages, neutrophils were employed as an immune cell model to test the anti-inflammatory effect of propofol on psoriasis. To determine whether propofol regulates neutrophil function, the levels of the proinflammatory mediators IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in mouse neutrophils activated by IMQ were measured. The generation of IL-1β and IL-6 was increased in neutrophils incubated with IMQ, and propofol inhibited this increase in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 5A and B). IMQ also stimulated the expression of TNF-α, whereas propofol suppressed its overexpression (Fig. 5C). Unlike the concentration-dependent inhibition of IL-1β and IL-6, propofol at concentrations of 10, 50, and 100 μM resulted in comparable suppression of TNF-α. This finding indicated that 10 μM of this drug was sufficient to induce a maximal effect to restrain TNF-α upregulation in neutrophils. The positive control dexamethasone also inhibited these mediators. Notably, the cytokine expression of the propofol group at higher doses tended to be lower than that of the baseline control group. ### Topical propofol easily permeates the skin
  Satisfactory skin absorption of topical drugs is necessary to achieve therapeutic outcomes in the skin. The skin permeation of propofol was evaluated in vitro by the Franz cell following the OECD 428 guideline. In this absorption setting, the amount of permeants found in the skin deposit is considered to be locally available in the skin reservoir. On the other hand, the permeants found in the receptor reflect diffusion into the deeper skin strata and/or systemic circulation. Propofol (3 mM) was deposited in intact pig skin at a level of 1.19 nmol/mg after 24 h of delivery (Fig. 6A). Barrier-defective skin was also utilized to determine the degree of propofol deposition in inflamed skin and explore the penetration pathways across the skin. All of the barrier-defective skins presented greater propofol accumulation than did the intact pig skin, with the SC-stripped skin exhibiting the highest degree of deposition. The degree of drug deposition in the SC-stripped skin was 3.7 times greater than that in the intact skin. The delipidized (3.68 nmol/mg), deproteinized (3.15 nmol/mg), and desebumized (3.31 nmol/mg) skins presented comparable levels of propofol deposition. Propofol deposition in intact and IMQ-sensitized mouse skin was also analyzed to determine its absorption in psoriasiform skin.   Flux is an indicator of transport to the receptor according to the calculation of the slope from the cumulative amount-time curve. Like skin deposition, propofol flux increased following barrier disruption (Fig. 6B). The SC-stripped and desebumized skins presented the highest flux, followed by the deproteinized and delipidized skins. A cellulose membrane was also used as a diffusion barrier to determine the release of propofol from the vehicle. The flux (release rate) of propofol through the cellulose membrane was greater than that across intact and defective skins, demonstrating the barrier feature of the skin. In psoriasiform mouse skin, the flux of propofol was twofold greater than that in intact skin.

  The S value is an index used to compute the selectivity between skin targeting and the risk of circulation absorption. A higher S value indicates preferable drug retention in the skin with a lower risk of systemic adverse effects. Compared with intact pig skin, defective skin presented greater S values for propofol absorption (Fig. 6C), suggesting that it is an ideal drug for targeting the inflammatory skin reservoir. Both mouse skin deposition and the flux of propofol were increased after IMQ treatment, resulting in comparable S values between healthy and psoriasiform skin. Hair follicles also act as an important route for drug transport. We found that topical propofol was able to be delivered into these appendages with a moderate uptake of 1.80 nmol/cm2 (Fig. 6D).

  In silico molecular docking was conducted to understand the ability of propofol to interact with SC lipids. The docking simulation predicts the pathways of drug penetration via the SC layer. A higher negative CDOCKER value indicates a stronger affinity of the permeants with the lipids. Ceramides II, III, and VI are rich in human SC lipids. Ceramides III and VI exhibited greater affinity for propofol than ceramide I did (Table 1), suggesting that the preferred routes of propofol delivery involve interactions with ceramides III and VI. The scores for palmitic acid, cholesterol, and cholesteryl sulfate were positive or not determined. The superimposition of propofol with the lipids indicated a possible affinity of the drug molecule to the lipids (Fig. 6E). The docking poses of propofol exhibited different conformations in various SC lipids. This superimposition suggested possible partitioning of propofol molecule into SC lipid bilayers. ### Topical propofol ameliorates psoriasiform lesions in vivo
  To explore the therapeutic role of propofol in psoriasis, a mouse model of IMQ-induced lesions treated with topical propofol was used. No animals died during the experiment and their activity remained good. The phenotype and microscopy images of the skin surface revealed redness, desquamation, and acanthosis after IMQ stimulation (Fig. 7A and B). These signs are the typical symptoms of psoriasis. The topical application of propofol and dexamethasone resulted in a distinct decrease in terms of psoriatic traits. The skin surface colors related to erythema (a*) and yellow (b*) were also monitored for seven days. Cutaneous erythema significantly increased following treatment with IMQ for four days (Fig. 7C). The erythema level decreased from Day 5 to Day 7 due to the coverage of white scales on the red skin. Propofol and dexamethasone could reduce skin redness and scaling, as evidenced by a* detection. The b* level of the skin was greatly increased by IMQ treatment (Fig. 7D), suggesting a change in the original skin color. This change was significantly lessened after the topical delivery of propofol. Dexamethasone resulted in a greater reduction in IMQ-induced b* elevation than did propofol. Skin barrier function, as determined by TEWL, greatly increased after IMQ intervention, indicating that barrier damage occurred (Fig. 7E). Propofol did not function to restore the barrier properties. ### Propofol causes insignificant skin toxicity
  Topical propofol and dexamethasone were administered to healthy mouse skin for 5 days to examine skin tolerance. The gross and close-up images of the skin surface revealed no alterations in the skin surface after compound treatment for 5 days (Suppl. Figure 7 A and 7B). For histological visualization, the skin morphology remained intact without significant disruption by compound management (Suppl. Figure 7 C). The epidermal thickness was not affected by the compounds according to the calculation from the histology (Suppl. Figure 7D). The a* recorded from the propofol-treated group approximated the value of the control group over five days (Suppl. Figure 7E). Dexamethasone treatment slightly increased the degree of skin erythema. The compound treatment did not change the skin surface pH or TEWL (Suppl. Figure 7 F and 7G). The increase in TEWL after topical treatment with the vehicle control and compounds could be due to the hydration effect of the vehicle (30% ethanol in pH 7.4 buffer), resulting in an increase in the water content in the SC.


## Discussion
  The intravenous anesthetic propofol is widely applied in surgical anesthesia induction and postoperative care because of its rapid response and short duration. Propofol has been reported to have anti-inflammatory properties through various signaling cascades. This drug may be beneficial for treating cutaneous inflammation, such as psoriasis. The management of mild-to-moderate psoriasis continues to rely on topical therapy. Localized therapy involving the use of propofol to target the skin is favorable for avoiding the systemic response of the anesthetic. We aimed to develop a topical propofol treatment for mitigating skin inflammation evoked by psoriasis. The cell-based results demonstrated that propofol reduced the inflammatory response in stimulated keratinocytes, macrophages, and neutrophils. Our data suggest that this protection against immune malfunction is carried out through antioxidant activity. Propofol alleviated IMQ-induced hyperplasia and immune cell infiltration in mice. The results of this study indicate the protective capacity of topical propofol in psoriasiform inflammation.

  Psoriasis is considered mainly an autoimmune disorder of keratinocyte dysfunction [48]. Keratinocytes are a predominant component of the epidermis and have immune functions. They play a key role in the initiation and maintenance of psoriasis. Keratinocyte stimulation in psoriasis leads to the production of cytokines and chemokines. Overwhelming cytokines/chemokines are hallmarks of psoriatic inflammation and can be indicators of disease severity in psoriasis patients [74]. Together, the proinflammatory effectors IL-6, IL-8, and CXCL1 activate keratinocyte proliferation and inflammation in psoriasis, resulting in the recruitment and stimulation of immune cells to amplify inflammation [4]. For example, IL-6 overexpression in psoriatic keratinocytes can promote T helper (Th)17 cell differentiation [57]. IL-8 and CXCL1 release from psoriatic keratinocytes attract neutrophils to the lesion site [80]. During psoriasis initiation, dendritic cells are activated to produce TNF-α to stimulate keratinocytes. Our data revealed that the levels of the proinflammatory markers IL-6, IL-8, and CXCL1 were increased after TNF-α challenge, and propofol significantly blocked this increase to nearly the baseline control level. Previously, the effects of propofol on proinflammatory mediators were controversial. Several studies have reported that the serum level of IL-6 is increased in healthy volunteers and critically ill patients receiving propofol [6, 62]. In contrast, propofol represses inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, in sepsis [58]. Our data revealed the anti-inflammatory activity of propofol against activated keratinocytes.

  The MTT and CCK-8 assays revealed that propofol did not elicit keratinocyte cytotoxicity, suggesting that the inhibition of keratinocyte activation by propofol was not attributable to cell death. There is abnormal activation of several signaling pathways in psoriatic keratinocytes. The candidate signaling pathways include the MAPK, PI3K/Akt, and JAK/STAT3 pathways [19]. MAPKs include p38, ERK, and JNK, which are associated with the inflammatory response. Previous studies [30, 64] have demonstrated that propofol inhibits inflammation in alveolar epithelial cells and amnion-derived cells through MAPK signaling. This was not the case since propofol did not affect MAPK phosphorylation in activated keratinocytes. PI3K/Akt is a critical transduction pathway involved in cellular proliferation and differentiation. The aberrant activation of PI3K/Akt leads to epidermal hyperplasia and inflammation in psoriasis [76]. JAK/STAT3 is also involved in the response of keratinocytes to cytokines, including proliferation and proinflammatory effector release [16]. STAT3 is acknowledged as a major mediator of IL-6 function in many cells [8]. Propofol reportedly regulates PI3K/Akt and JAK/STAT3 in various types of cells, such as endothelial cells and cancer cells [63, 67]. However, we did not detect the involvement of Akt and STAT3 signaling in propofol-treated keratinocytes. Propofol may act via different mechanisms in various types of cells. In our case, we demonstrated that MAPKs, Akt, and STAT3 were not involved in the molecular signaling in the anti-inflammatory activity of propofol.

  The pathogenesis of psoriasis is related to defects in the antioxidant system with increased development of ROS [14]. Oxidative stress biomarkers are associated with the severity of psoriasis. Psoriasis patients have elevated ROS and free radical production and reduced antioxidant capability. The ROS-mediated signaling pathway in keratinocytes is important for the immunoregulation of psoriasis [77]. TNF-α causes ROS formation in keratinocytes, which in turn increases cytokine/chemokine generation [50]. One of the principal oxidative stress sensors in psoriatic keratinocytes is KEAP1. This biomarker binds to Nrf2 in normal cells to retard its activation. To trigger the antioxidant pathway, KEAP1 undergoes a conformational change to liberate Nrf2 to promote the expression of a series of antioxidant enzymes and detoxifying proteins [45]. Nrf2 is a transcription factor that governs the cellular defense against ROS. Nrf2 deficiency in psoriatic lesions leads to increased levels of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 [46]. With respect to psoriasis attenuation, KEAP1 inhibition and Nrf2 activation can decrease the inflammatory response through balance to maintain cellular homeostasis [28]. Our results revealed that propofol markedly reduced KEAP1 levels and increased Nrf2 expression in human keratinocytes. KEAP1/Nrf2 may affect the molecular signaling involved in propofol-induced anti-inflammatory effects. Nrf2 can prevent the increase of ROS in keratinocytes [65]. Confocal images of DCFDA confirmed that there were fewer ROS in propofol-treated HaCaT cells than in those stimulated with TNF-α alone. Nrf2 activation can further hinder the production of proinflammatory cytokines via redox control. As a regulator of the redox response to inhibit keratinocyte activation, KEAP1/Nrf2 may be a potential therapeutic target for psoriasis. Propofol is reported to have antioxidant activity to decrease oxidative stress in macrophages, neurons, and cancer cells [18, 69]. Nrf2 activation in psoriasis is associated with the ERK, Akt, and STAT3 pathways [21, 39]. This effect was not found in the present study, suggesting that other mechanisms dominate the antioxidant activity of propofol in keratinocytes. Further studies are needed to elucidate the specific signaling pathways involved.

  After dissociation from KEAP1, Nrf2 enters the nucleus to activate the target gene HO-1. HO-1 is an antioxidant involved in the prognosis of psoriasis. Increased HO-1 expression in keratinocytes is favorable for mitigating psoriatic inflammation [25, 78]. The enhanced HO-1 expression in propofol-treated keratinocytes indicated that stimulating the antioxidant effect via Nrf2/HO-1 was a feasible approach to attenuate proinflammatory mediators in psoriasis. CAT and SOD2 are also natural defense enzymes that are responsible for reducing ROS. Deficiencies in CAT and SOD2 in psoriasis cause aberrant inflammation [5]. We found that both CAT and SOD2 were upregulated by propofol treatment. Overall, propofol effectively decreased ROS and increased antioxidant enzymes, subsequently leading to cytokine/chemokine downregulation in keratinocytes.

  In the chemical model for antioxidant activity evaluation, propofol could decrease DPPH and ABTS. This manifested a direct scavenging effect of propofol. The cellular protective mechanism of propofol was partially attributed to direct ROS scavenging effects or ligand‒enzyme interactions. The direct scavenging of propofol could be due to the phenolic moiety in its structure, such as α-tocopherol [29]. There are two isopropyl groups at the ortho positions of the phenol structure for propofol. The high electron transfer ability of this mesomeric effect could strengthen the antioxidant action, similar to the structures of the antioxidants Trolox and butylated hydroxytoluene [44].

  Psoriasis involves the complex interplay between keratinocytes and immune cells. In addition to keratinocytes, further investigations are needed on the interaction and role of the immune system in the antipsoriatic effect of propofol. Among immune cells, macrophages and neutrophils appear to be important for governing the immune response in psoriasis [56]. Propofol has been shown to have a modulatory effect on the inflammatory process of macrophages and neutrophils [44]. With respect to macrophages, propofol inhibits macrophage activation and chemotaxis during infection, inflammation, and tissue repair [1]. The communication between macrophages and keratinocytes in psoriatic inflammation is mediated by cytokines/chemokines. Macrophages are involved in the production of cytokines/chemokines following the activation of TLRs. The levels of the inflammatory markers IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α were increased in macrophages after IMQ challenge, and propofol significantly reduced this increase. Macrophages extensively express GABA receptors to regulate the immune response. Since propofol is a GABA agonist, it was reasonable that this drug triggered GABA signaling to control the inflammatory reaction. Propofol has also been reported to reduce TLR expression in macrophages, inhibiting proinflammatory cytokines [79].

  The cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α in macrophages can be decreased by propofol in experimental models of endotoxic inflammation, sepsis, and myocardial infarction [24, 35, 37, 47]. Psoriatic patients are known to have macrophage infiltration that favors the M1 phenotype to secrete high levels of IL-6 and TNF-α [32]. In a mouse model, macrophage depletion decreased psoriasiform inflammation to downregulate IL-6 and TNF-α to normal levels [27]. IL-8 is a chemokine largely produced by activated macrophages [51]. We also showed that propofol reversed the increase of IL-8 released from activated macrophages. Our macrophage data demonstrated that MAPKs were not involved in the cytokine/chemokine inhibition by propofol. There are inconsistent results concerning the effects of propofol on macrophage MAPKs. Hsing et al. [24] demonstrated that propofol did not affect MAPKs in lipopolysaccharide-activated RAW264.7 cells, whereas Chiu et al. [11] reported that propofol inhibited lipoteichoic acid-activated RAW264.7 cells through MAPKs. This difference may be dependent on the stimulation type and experimental setup. Further investigations are needed to elucidate this signal transduction pathway.

  The recruitment of macrophages to inflammatory sites is a major pathological feature of psoriasis. Keratinocytes, as host cells, secrete IL-8 and CXCL1 to promote the migration of macrophages. We found that propofol inhibited keratinocyte activation to block subsequent macrophage migration in a Transwell study. The targeting and removal of macrophages can be a strategy to decrease the severity of psoriatic inflammation [35, 37]. Propofol achieved this aim to ameliorate macrophage activation in psoriasis. TNF-α released from macrophages can initiate keratinocyte hyperproliferation [57]. We confirmed that activated macrophages increased p-STAT3, a proliferation biomarker, in keratinocytes. Nevertheless, propofol intervention in macrophages failed to inhibit STAT3 phosphorylation in keratinocytes. The effect of propofol on the macrophage–keratinocyte interaction was unidirectional.

  Neutrophils are also key immune cells in the initial phase of psoriasis development [13]. The involvement of neutrophils in psoriasis makes them a target for therapeutic treatment. Our data revealed a decrease in the levels of overexpressed IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in IMQ-stimulated mouse neutrophils after propofol intervention. The approach of targeting neutrophils also likely affects other myeloid-lineage cells, such as macrophages [42]. We confirmed that propofol could target activated macrophages and neutrophils to relieve cytokine/chemokine upregulation. Propofol effectively inhibits the release of proinflammatory mediators in the neutrophils of patients with aortic cross-clamping- and cardiopulmonary bypass-induced inflammation [53, 54]. The anti-inflammatory activity of propofol against neutrophils has been established previously [10, 70]. Propofol can inhibit neutrophilic inflammation by blocking formyl peptide receptor 1, calcium mobilization, and Akt. Neutrophils can have a local effect on keratinocyte function to establish a feedback loop in psoriatic lesions [41]. Thus, the suppression of neutrophilic inflammation by propofol may also prevent the occurrence of a vicious loop between immune and resident cells.

  Pig skin was used as the permeation barrier to evaluate propofol absorption because of its similarity with human skin [22]. Propofol can easily deliver into pig skin, especially barrier-defective skin. Small molecule permeants such as propofol are expected to have high penetration rates. The molecular weight of propofol (178 g/mol) is preferable for achieving facile absorption on the basis of the 500 g/mol rule [7]. The lipophilic permeants have relatively high skin absorption due to their favored partitioning into SC lipid bilayers. Propofol, with an octanol/water partition coefficient (log P) of 3.8, can be categorized as a lipophilic drug with acceptable skin delivery ability. The docking simulation results indicated a possible interaction of propofol with ceramides in SC, whereas no or negligible affinity was found for interactions with palmitic acid, cholesterol, and cholesteryl sulfate. The steric hindrance of the two isopropyl moieties might contribute to the unfavorable orientation toward these SC lipids. In general, the number of hydrogen bonds in the permeant structure is inversely correlated with skin absorption since hydrogen bonds impede transport through the SC [60]. Drugs with a hydrogen bond acceptor number ≤ 3 and a donor number ≤ 2 can be recognized as ideal permeants [40]. There is only one hydrogen bond acceptor and one donor in the structure of propofol. This anesthetic fits the profile of an ideal permeant.

  Propofol penetration increased in all of the barrier-defective skins tested. The propofol deposition and flux after lipid and protein removal were comparable or slightly lower than those of the SC-stripped skin. This finding indicated that both the intercellular and transcellular routes affected propofol diffusion across the SC. The follicular route was also crucial for propofol absorption since we found a moderate accumulation of propofol molecules in hair follicles. The follicular infundibulum can form a drug reservoir for further distribution to viable skin because of the weaker barrier function of the follicular epithelium than the SC. Sebum removal significantly increased the deposition and flux of propofol, suggesting an important step of sebum partitioning for the permeation of propofol into the skin. Barrier-defective skin could be a model of inflammatory skin. Delipidized skin simulates the condition of psoriasis [9]. Propofol deposition is relatively high in delipidized skin compared with intact skin, suggesting that a satisfactory amount of propofol enters the skin to treat psoriasis. The ability of topical propofol to achieve high absorption in the skin and avoid systemic absorption to escape a sedative effect is important. Topically applied propofol is valid for psoriatic skin targeting with limited systemic delivery on the basis of the higher S value in delipidized skin than in other skin types.

  Topical corticosteroids are a primary therapy used to treat psoriasis by inhibiting inflammation and hyperplasia. The in vivo profiles in this study revealed that dexamethasone drastically mitigated psoriasiform signs and cytokines/chemokines in lesional skin. Topical propofol had a comparable effect with this positive control. These results provide direct evidence that propofol effectively alleviated psoriasiform inflammation. SC disturbance by IMQ sensitization can promote drug absorption into psoriasis-like skin [43]. Our IVPT data verified greater propofol delivery into IMQ-treated skin than into healthy mouse skin, contributing to the accumulation of sufficient amounts of propofol in psoriasiform skin to inhibit inflammation. The vehicle itself has a critical effect on skin penetration since the first step involves permeant partitioning from the vehicle to the SC [22]. The vehicle of 30% ethanol/buffer could be an ideal formulation for propofol delivery. Cytokines stimulate keratinocyte proliferation and differentiation in psoriasis, resulting in the induration and white peeling of the skin surface. The inhibitory effect of propofol on cytokines could mitigate scaling on psoriasiform skin, as visualized by the appearance of the skin surface. The cytokines/chemokines in psoriasiform lesions are derived from diverse types of cells, including keratinocytes, lymphocytes, macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells [51]. The multiple targets of propofol for blocking the activation of different cells have led to efficient treatment of inflammation. Cytokine/chemokine-stimulated proliferation results in epidermal hyperplasia, which is a hallmark of psoriatic plaques [3]. The keratinocyte proliferation rate decreases following increased HO-1 [78]. The ability of propofol to increase the level of the antioxidant enzyme HO-1 could be a mechanism of proliferation inhibition, as evidenced by the histological profiles of H&E and Ki67.

  Epidermal hyperproliferation and oxidative stress in psoriasis are consequences of an imperfect skin barrier [5]. Nrf2 acts as an antioxidant to regulate barrier function under stress conditions [46]. However, the increase in Nrf2 expression induced by propofol did not reverse psoriasiform barrier abnormalities, according to the TEWL data. This could be due to previous evidence that long-term unrestricted Nrf2 activation may be a negative consequence of barrier properties [55]. However, further studies are needed to elucidate the effects of propofol. Macrophages make up 60% of all infiltrating immune cells in psoriatic lesions [32]. IL-8 and CXCL1 overexpression in activated macrophages is involved in neutrophil migration to lesions. Psoriatic keratinocytes are highly proliferative and produce IL-8 and CXCL1 to recruit macrophages and neutrophils to lesion sites [68]. The ROS formed in keratinocytes also act chemotactically on macrophages and neutrophils [2]. [35, 37] demonstrated that macrophages and neutrophils closely interact with each other in psoriasis. Our IHC results revealed that neutrophil infiltration into the dermal region was high. Propofol was successful in slowing the infiltration of immune cells via the inhibition of chemokines and oxidative stress in keratinocytes and the crosstalk between keratinocytes and inflammatory cells.

  The first-line treatment for mild-to-moderate psoriasis is topical therapy, such as corticosteroids. However, topically applied corticosteroids have several side effects, such as atrophy, telangiectasias, and adrenal suppression. Our skin tolerance test also revealed increased erythema in dexamethasone-treated skin. Topical propofol may be an alternative candidate for treating psoriasis because of its anti-inflammatory efficacy and cutaneous safety. Previous studies [23, 66] have suggested that propofol has a nonanaesthetic effect of analgesia to relieve inflammation-induced acute pain. This analgesic potential may be beneficial for alleviating psoriasis-associated pain. Topical drugs are intended to target the skin nidus and evade systemic absorption to reduce systemic effects. The possible risk of topical propofol entering the circulation should not be ignored. Propofol in circulation causes sedative and immunomodulatory responses. Clinically, the blood concentration of 17‒62 μM propofol is considered the range at which anesthesia can be induced or maintained [36]. The accumulation of propofol in the receptor compartment of IVPT patients could be expected to be due to its possible absorption into the circulation. The propofol concentration in the receptor after a 6-h treatment on delipidized skin was 21 μM. This concentration is approximately the lower limit of the concentration that induces anesthesia. The IVPT receptor volume was only 5 ml, which was much smaller than the volume of the blood pool (approximately 5 l). We anticipated a lower propofol concentration than 21 μM after topical delivery under clinical conditions because of the dilution effect. Nevertheless, the development of novel formulations for topical propofol causing greater skin targeting and minimal systemic absorption is important to increase its clinical usefulness. There are limitations in the present study. Although animals are commonly used to assess the therapeutic efficacy of the drugs, they are still questionable to fully mimic the condition of human. Laboratory animals do not share identical skin structure and psoriasis pathogenesis with human. Caution should be exercised to directly extrapolate the animal results to psoriatic patients. We employed the cultured cells in the cell-based study. Primary cells may be more feasible to predict the in vivo or clinical outcomes. The present study also lacked the evidence to examine the effect of propofol on Nrf2 nuclear translocation. Further study is needed to elucidate the detailed mechanisms of this drug for mitigating psoriatic inflammation.


## Conclusions
  The current investigation sought to evaluate the antipsoriatic properties of propofol and the mechanisms that underlie its effects. Findings indicate that propofol effectively reduces the overexpression of cytokines and chemokines in activated keratinocytes, macrophages, and neutrophils, which may be linked to the modulation of the redox response. Additionally, propofol was found to disrupt the interaction between keratinocytes and macrophages, suggesting a potential interruption of the pathological cycle associated with psoriasis. The favorable absorption characteristics of propofol present a promising avenue for its topical application in the treatment of psoriatic lesions. Furthermore, the in vivo imiquimod-induced psoriasiform model in mice revealed a reduction in lesional inflammation following the topical application of propofol. This anesthetic effectively arrested proinflammatory mediator overexpression and immune cell migration at the psoriasiform site. The present investigation provides evidence for the potential benefits of propofol in the treatment of psoriatic inflammation. We developed a novel therapeutic approach that has safety and strong efficacy against psoriasis.
