# Molecular dynamics simulations reveal DNA gate opening mechanisms for M. smegmatis topoisomerase 1A


## Abstract
  Type 1A topoisomerases relax torsional strain in DNA via a strand passage mechanism in which a protein-mediated DNA gate must open during the enzyme’s catalytic cycle. This gate-open conformational state of the enzyme has been challenging to observe via experimental methods. In this study, we first used equilibrium molecular dynamics simulations to probe the structural properties of the gate-closed state for the DNA-free apo system and a system with an ssDNA bound at the DNA binding site. For both systems, we followed the equilibrium simulations with umbrella sampling simulations. Umbrella sampling allowed us to bias the protein to adopt a gate-open state to study the properties of this conformation, as well as the pathways leading to it. We observed that several electrostatic interactions contribute to the closed-state stability of the protein, which were broken during the gate opening. The gate opening composed of three major domain motions that were determined from simulation trajectories and principal component analysis. Finally, umbrella sampling results combined with the weighted histogram analysis method allowed us to reconstruct the free energy profiles of gate opening for all simulations.


## Significance
  Multi-drug-resistant bacterial strains necessitate newer ways of targeting bacteria. Type 1A topoisomerases are potential antibacterial drug targets. Current strategies are to either block the protein binding to DNA or prevent the cut DNA being relegated during the catalytic cycle. A possible alternative to these methods would be to trap the protein at an open conformation. Structural information about the transient open state is crucial to this approach. Results of this study provide mechanistic details of gate opening. This information furthers the understanding of the topoisomerase catalytic cycle that will aid in antitopoisomerase drug design efforts.


## Introduction
  DNA topoisomerases are important enzymes that resolve topological issues in DNA including knots, precatenanes, excess supercoils, and similar. These DNA structures occur as a result of various cellular processes such as DNA replication and transcription (1). Topoisomerases are classified into two major families, Type 1 and Type 2 depending on whether they cleave one or both strands of DNA for their catalytic activity (2). Type 1A topoisomerases are members of the type 1 topoisomerases, and use a strand passage mechanism in which an intact ssDNA strand—commonly referred to as the T-strand—is passed through a cut in the other strand, called the G-strand. The protein must undergo a large conformational change that moves the cut ends of the G-strand apart to create an opening for the T-strand to pass through. Type 1A topoisomerases share a conserved N-terminal structure formed by domains 1–4 (D1–D4) that exhibit a toroidal padlock type structure (Fig. 1 A). The C-terminal domains (gray, Fig. 1 A and B) however vary between different type 1A topoisomerases (4). During protein activity, the G-strand binds across D1 and D4 domains while a catalytic tyrosine from D3 cleaves the ssDNA forming a 5′ phosphotyrosyl linkage with the cut end. To pass a T-strand through this cut, a conformational change presumably occurs where D3 moves away from D4. Based on the proposed protein activity models, in a gate-open state, D3 moves away from both D1 and D4 (5).   The type 1A topoisomerase mechanism of activity has been well studied using several biochemical (7,8) and biophysical techniques (9,10,11). Single molecule experiments have shown that these proteins relax excessive supercoils via a strand passage mechanism in steps of one excess turn at a time (9). Other studies have shown that the proteins must undergo significant conformational changes during the reaction cycle (12,13). There have been many structural studies throughout the years looking at type 1A topoisomerases (3,14,15). Structures of intermediate states had been elusive until a recent structural study on Human Top 3β (16) revealed an open conformation. This elusive nature of the open state suggests that this state is highly transient and less stable than the closed state. Single-molecule experiments have also contributed to the understanding of the protein mechanisms regarding the gate-open state. It has been demonstrated that the application of force can bias the protein to dwell longer at these transient gate-open states (17). Individual protein-mediated DNA gate openings were directly observed using single-molecule magnetic and optical tweezers (17,18). These experiments demonstrated that the protein domains undergo large conformational changes upon DNA cleavage with DNA gate opening >5 nm (17,18) under application of piconewton forces.

  Little is known about type 1A topoisomerase structures at different stages of the reaction cycle and the mechanisms by which they occur. The transient nature of the open state makes visualizing the conformational dynamics challenging. Therefore, a dynamic study is essential to be able to capture such processes. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations are one way to study such mechanisms. MD simulations are regularly employed to probe protein conformational changes, domain reorganization, the folding-unfolding pathway, and much more (19). MD simulations can incorporate different biasing techniques to force a protein to explore alternative conformational states as well.

  In this work, we used MD simulations to probe the dynamic features of the M. smegmatis type 1A topoisomerase (Msm Top1A) protein structure. Msm Top1A was chosen because it is a well-studied relative of Mycobacterium tuberculosis Top1A—a potential antitopoisomerase drug target (20,21). A better understanding of mycobacterial Top1A structural dynamics will aid in such drug discovery efforts. Using equilibrium simulations, we identified stable and mobile regions of the protein as well as residues important for the closed-state stability. Using umbrella sampling (US) simulations, we explored the structural changes that occur during gate opening. We computed one- and two-dimensional free energies of gate opening as well as identified important domain motions during this process. Principal component analysis results support the domain motions as being the major functional motions of the gate opening. Taken together, the findings of this study shed light on the energetics and dynamics of the elusive protein-mediated DNA gate opening.


## Materials and methods
  Starting structures for all systems were based on Msm Top1A structure from the Protein Data Bank (PDB: 6PCM (3)). The protein crystallographic structure as solved lacks the last domain D8 and an unstructured tail. This slightly truncated structure was used in this study as we don’t expect these missing domains to influence gate opening. Missing residues within the truncated protein structure were modeled using Modeller via ChimeraX (22). Among the Modeller-generated structures, the structure with the lowest normalized Discrete Optimized Protein Energy Score (z-DOPE) was selected as the starting structure to build simulation systems. Comparison with Alphafold (23,24) generated structures showed minimal differences in the core domain organization except for loop placements, whose positions vary dynamically during a simulation (Video S1). Systems were prepared using VMD. Apo protein and protein with ssDNA at the active site binding pocket were both solvated with TIP3P water molecules in a water box with at least 10 A∘ of padding in all directions. Charges were neutralized and ion concentration set to 150 mM NaCl. The total atom count for each system was ∼186k and 206k respectively. For simulations with ssDNA at the active site pocket (+ssDNA), the longer ssDNA strand from the crystal structure was trimmed, leaving only eight nucleotides with sequence 5′-TTCCGCTT-3′ in place to resemble a precleavage-stage protein-DNA complex.

  Video S1. Apo system core domain fluctuations during equilibrium simulations

  All MD simulations were atomistic in scale and run using NAMD 2.14 or NAMD 3.0 (25,26). Systems were energy-minimized for at least 10,000 steps using a conjugate gradient algorithm. Energy minimization was followed by isothermal-isobaric (NPT) equilibration at 1 bar and 310 K using Langevin dynamics. Equilibration in the canonical ensemble (NVT) followed NPT equilibrations. Interactions were governed by CHARMM36 (27) force field. Nonbonded interactions were truncated smoothly at 12A∘ with a switching function activating at 10A∘. All electrostatics were treated by particle mesh Ewald method. For both systems, each replica was equilibrated for 0.5 μs. Runs were terminated once the root mean-square deviation (RMSD) values for the core domains of the protein (D1-D4) had stabilized (ΔRMSD<2A∘). Both RMSD and time-averaged RMSD per-residue (root mean-square fluctuation, RMSF) calculations were done using Python MDAnalysis libraries (28,29). Two further conformations were generated for each system where the unstructured loop was moved out of the cavity. These systems are referred to as apo_1 and +ssDNA_1 respectively.

  PyMol was used to generate the electrostatic potential map of the protein as well as the figure showing the protein surface painted with the respective colors in Fig. 3 A–D. The total nonbonded interaction energies between residues were calculated using VMD NAMDEnergy plugin with ϵr = 70. The approximate D3 swiveling angle is calculated as the angle between Cα residues of Y339 (the active site tyrosine), S516, and E523 (D4 interface helix residues). The latter two residues are found on the interface alpha-helix on the opposite side of the gate from the active site tyrosine. A unit vector through S516 to E523 can be represented by r1ˆ, and from S516 to Y339, it can be written as r2ˆ. The angle between the two unit vectors is then calculated as cos(θ)=r1ˆ·r2ˆ for all frames of the MD trajectories. The helix containing S516 and E523 (D4 α3) does not lose its structure throughout the MD simulation and is one of the most stable regions on the protein. To measure the slide of D3 against D1, trajectories are aligned to the starting structures based on the D1 regions (residue numbers 18 to 143). The displacement of D3 center of mass (COM) against the starting position is computed for the trajectories, and this displacement is defined as the slide of D3 against D1.

  For gate opening, simulations were run using US (30,31). For all runs, restraining potentials of the form U=12kx2 were used with spring constant k=5kcal/(mol·A∘2). All US runs used a single reaction coordinate (RC) as shown in Fig. 6 A. The RC is the distance between the COM of the cyan (residue numbers 297 to 430) and green regions (residue numbers 1 to 211 and 513 to 638). This RC was based on previous simulations and structural information (12,17,32,33). For standard US simulations, the RC was split into 1−A∘ or 0.5−A∘ windows, which were used to steer the system from a closed to an open conformation. RC space was sampled every 2 ps. Each window was sampled for at least 2, 4, or 10 ns in all simulations. Replica exchange umbrella sampling (REUS) simulations were conducted using NAMD 3.0.1 (25). Each replica was centered around a 1 A∘ window using the restraining potential mentioned above. The replicas progressed from an RC value representing a gate-closed conformation to a gate-open conformation. Exchange attempts were made every 2 ps. Each replica (or window) was simulated for 8 ns for both systems. 1D and 2D free energy calculations were done using the Weighted Histogram Analysis Method (WHAM) code from (34).

  All principal component analysis (PCA) for the N-terminus Cα residues was done using ProDy libraries (35,36,37). Twenty lowest-energy uncorrelated modes were retained from each analysis. VMD’s Normal Mode Wizard plugin was used to visualize all the modes. All trajectory snapshots except the electrostatics figure were generated using VMD (38) as well. NCBI Blast was used for multiple sequence alignment (39) and visualized using Jalview 2.11.4.1 (40). All plots were generated using Matplotlib (41).

All replicas were simulated for 0.5 μs each. The protein RMSD for all simulations was calculated as mentioned in materials and methods.   The per-residue RMSF plots in Fig. 2 B highlight some important motions in the protein structure. First, all curves demonstrate the high mobility of C-terminus domains in the equilibrium simulations. For both systems, the fluctuations for the C-terminal tail domains (gray, Fig. 1) are higher past D4 residues (residue number 625). Likewise, the first ∼14 residues on the N-terminus are also highly mobile due to a lack of secondary structure. Another region that shows higher fluctuations for both systems is the unstructured loop that exists in the vicinity of the toroid cavity. For apo, we see about a twofold increase in RMSF for residues comprising the loop vs. residues in the vicinity of the loop. For +ssDNA, the fluctuations of the loop are lower, but still higher than the neighboring residues. In general, the RMSF values are lower for the unstructured loop in the +ssDNA system than in the apo system.

  This unstructured loop is predicted to have a regulatory role during supercoil relaxation (15). When the loop protrudes into the toroid cavity, it blocks the entrance into the toroid and alters the surface electrostatics of the cavity rim. This loop is highly negatively charged as well. Fig. 3 A–D shows that in its absence, the cavity rim is electrostatically positive, which is favorable for DNA binding. Its presence in the cavity, however, reduces the space available for DNA and also alters the charge of the interior. This was previously observed using M. tuberculosis Top1A where the presence or absence of the loop in the cavity altered the electrostatics in a similar manner (15). This is significant in that both the loop and the ssDNA cannot simultaneously exist in the cavity. Multiple sequence alignment in Fig. 3 E shows that the loop is conserved for mycobacterial type 1A topoisomerases but not other species. ### Stability of the gate-closed conformation
  The majority of type 1A topoisomerases structures that are currently available are of the protein in the closed form either as apo or in complex with ssDNA strand(s). Previously, single-molecule experiments using magnetic tweezers found that the E. coli type 1A topoisomerases that were bound to ssDNA and formed covalent complexes were able to stay in a gate-closed state against as much as 12 pN of opposing force (17). This suggests that the protein highly favors a clamp-closed state and is stable in this conformation. In our simulations, the core domains RMSD and RMSF show minimal fluctuations as well. To assess the stability of the gate-closed state, we computed the total nonbonded energy (sum of pairwise electrostatic and van der Waals energy) of interaction between charged residues that formed contacts across the gate interface. The interaction energy for all identified pairs throughout equilibration runs was calculated using the VMD NAMDEnergy plugin. Since these interactions are known to be overstabilized by common force fields, we use them here for quantitative comparisons of the interacting pairs only and not for exact energy determination (43).   From the time evolution and the distribution of nonbonded energies in Fig. 5, several interesting features are apparent. First, we see that residue K537 (D4 α4) forms strong interactions with E322 (D3 α2) in both systems (see Fig. S3 for the helices labeling). The proximity of the interacting residues is shown in Fig. 4 A, and the strength of this interaction is plotted in Fig. 5 A. This is a strong contact that likely contributes to the stability of the closed state. This contact exists in the front face of the protein.

  Residues R377, K380, D583, E522, and E523 also form interactions that are important in stabilizing the gate-closed structure. These residues form contacts on the back face of the protein. R377 and K380 from a D3 loop interact and form contacts with D4 residues E522, E523, and D583. In both systems, E522 (D4 α3) is able to form contacts with both R377 and K380. Likewise, other contacts such as K380-D583 (D4 α6) and R377-E522 are found to be of comparative strengths in both systems and all replicas. Across the D1-D3 interface, E333 forms strong contacts with K25 and K28 in the apo system but not in the +ssDNA system. Such a variation is observed for the R341-D108 interaction as well (Figs. S1 and S2), where both residues are strictly conserved. These differences probably arise due to the interaction of ssDNA with charges in its vicinity. A small separation of D1 and D3 domain occurs at the interface (∼3A∘ in +ssDNA vs. no gap in apo) due to lack of these contacts.

  We observed interaction of the same charged residue with multiple oppositely charged amino acids at several locations on the protein as well. As mentioned above, residue K380 interacts with multiple residues from D4 at the same time in the gate-closed state. Likewise, E522 also interacts with both R377 and K380 in both systems. E522 is a conserved glutamic acid among different topoisomerases (Fig. 4 D). Similar to findings in (15) (E527 for Mtb Top1A), E522 is likely important for Msm Top1A for activity and/or structure. In the interface between D1 and D3, E333 from D3 also forms interactions with two residues from D1 (Fig. 4 E). These redundant interactions between D3 and D1/D4 suggest a fail-safe mechanism to maintain the closed-state structural integrity. In this manner, the protein might maintain its gate-closed structure even accounting for issues such as mutations.

  Sequence alignments were done to determine if the residues involved in these contacts are conserved across species. Sequence conservation expressed in percentages are displayed in Fig. 4 D. All residues are conserved between topoisomerases of the two mycobacteria. These residues all formed stabilizing interactions across the gate interface and contributed to the closed-state stability for Msm Top1A. However, some residues that were not strictly conserved between species had opposite charged pair analogs such as in Homo sapiens topoisomerase 3α (Hs Top3α). In Hs Top3α crystal structure (PDB: 4CHT), residues K345 and E543 are positioned close to one another in front of the protein similar to E322 and K537 for Msm Top1A (44). We speculate that K345-E543 is an interaction that contributes to the stability of the protein structure for Hs Top3α as well. Therefore, rather than strictly looking at the sequence conservation, it may be of interest to look at co-evolution of pairs of residues for different type 1A topoisomerases. This would help identify other interaction pairs that are important for a specific type 1A topoisomerase.


### Umbrella sampling to sample the gate-open conformation

#### Umbrella sampling determines important domain motions during gate opening
  To further probe the stability of the gate-closed complex, we conducted several standard US simulations of the apo protein (apo) and the ssDNA bound protein (+ssDNA) system. US simulations were conducted from equilibrated structures as described in materials and methods. The RC was the distance between the COM of D3 and D1+D4 regions as described in materials and methods and shown in Fig. 6 A. Increasing the RC distance forces the protein to explore the gate-open state. In the US simulations, both systems were steered from the closed to an open state by increasing the RC distance by 40A∘. The gate is considered “open” when D1 residues detach from D3, and a gap arises between the D3 and D4 residues. For the activity of the protein, this gap must be large enough for a strand of DNA to pass through. Evidence has shown that type 1A topoisomerases open up during catalysis via a large conformational change and can fit even a dsDNA inside the cavity (12,15).Figure 6Umbrella sampling setup and results. (A) Reaction coordinate (solid blue line) for all US simulations is the distance between center of mass (COM) of the cyan region and the green region shown in solid blue spheres. The two regions contain D3 and D1+D4 residues respectively. ssDNA (blue) sits in a binding groove that spans D1 and D4. (B) Mean free energy curves generated using US results and WHAM. Thick lines represent averages, and translucent bands represent standard error of mean for each system. (C) Protein in a conformation where D1, D3, and D4 are in contact with one another. (D) During US runs, D1 detaches from D3 first. This is seen as a separation between the respective domains. (E) Finally, D3 detaches from D4 as further contacts are broken.   For all US simulations, the pairwise nonbonded energies for contacts across the gate interface were computed as well. Contacts that stabilized the closed state broke as the gate opened. An example of interactions breaking during an US simulation is shown in Figs. S6 and S7. In some US simulations, occasionally, there was formation of new interactions that were not present during equilibrium runs. Time evolution of two such interactions during a US run is shown in Fig. S8. Such interactions can alter the gate opening mechanisms by affecting the domain motions themselves. Still, all such interactions must break before the gate can fully open. In apo, D1 and D3 are close to each other due to contacts between E333 and K25, resulting in a strong contact at the beginning of the simulation. The same interaction has negligible strength in the +ssDNA system, which is readily broken. The presence of loop interactions in the cavity appeared to prevent reforming of strong K380-D583 contacts once broken in the apo system. This is one of the stronger contacts between D3 and D4. An earlier detachment of this contact would assist in gate opening.

  In these simulations, gate opening was seen to follow the same general motions. The first large motion was the movement of D1 down and away from D3 (Fig. 6 D). This motion generally occurs within the first ∼10A∘ of the RC distance. Without the movement of D1 away from D3 first, D2 could stretch sideways (as seen from front or back) (Fig. S5). This stretch could cause the gate to stay closed despite the interface helices being separated, which opens the toroid cavity for DNA (12).

  After D1 moves away from D3, the next motion is the detachment of D3 from D4 (Fig. 6 E). At this point, interactions across the gate interface involving residues such as K380, R377, and K537 break. This allows the gate to fully open. For systems with the unstructured loop in the cavity, this loop must also detach from any interactions across the gate interface. The loop usually sits over the cavity opening just above the two interface alpha helices (as shown in Figs. 1 A, 6 C and D) and, therefore, must move away from the interface before the opening is accessible. On average, separation of D3 and D4 takes place along the next ∼10A∘ of RC distance. The separation of D3 beyond this point is referred to as “wider gate opening” where the RC increases further, representing a wide open DNA gate. It is important to note that these larger motions are correlated, and other smaller scale motions will be influenced by these motions during the evolution of the RC.


#### Free energy curves show variability in the gate opening process
  Results of the standard US simulations for both systems suggest that the protein gate can open through different conformational pathways. To quantify these differences and probe the stability of the gate-closed conformation, we computed the free energy profiles of gate opening using WHAM (34). The free energy profiles from different simulations are shown in Figs. S9 and S10. In all cases, the free energy is funnel shaped with a deep well corresponding to the closed state and a plateau for the open state. However, the barrier heights are different between simulations. The funnel shape is consistent with the assumption that the open state is transient, and the closed state is highly favored. An unbiased simulation starting from the protein in an open state rapidly reverted back toward the closed state within 0.2 μs, demonstrating the high stability of this protein in a closed state. Despite the differences in the free energy curves, the protein generally follows the major domain motions identified in the previous section. A swivel of D3 was also observed during gate opening.

  To improve convergence between different simulations, we turned to REUS simulations. In REUS, collective variable (or RC) exchange attempts are made between neighboring umbrella sampling windows, which allows for better sampling efficiency of the conformational space compared with standard US simulations (45). For REUS, we conducted four simulations from each system and focused only on the first 20 A∘ of RC distance since most of the differences in free energy curves occurred during the initial stages of gate opening. In comparison to the standard US simulations, REUS resulted in lower free energy barriers to gate opening and better convergence for both simulation systems. The agreement between mean free energy curves for both apo and +ssDNA systems is thus improved (Fig. 6 B). Although there is better agreement between the free energy profiles for the REUS simulations, both systems had one simulation with a different free energy profile than the others (Fig. S11). Longer simulations than those conducted for this study may be needed to ensure that convergence has been fully achieved.

  Based on the standard US and REUS outputs, we propose that the protein is able to go from a closed conformation to open conformation through multiple pathways. Even with improved sampling, a minority of systems appeared to follow a different path. Biological processes such as protein folding have been shown to follow multiple pathways (46,47,48,49). It is reasonable to assume that large-scale protein conformational changes may also occur along different pathways, which any single simulation could not fully sample during our simulation times.


#### Gate opening involves swivel of D3
  Standard US simulations showed that gate opening comprises several other motions on top of the separation of D3 from D1 and D4 domains. This was also suggested by the diversity in the free energy curves. To probe this further, we used PCA. PCA is a dimensionality reduction procedure that allows one to extract the functional motions of a molecule from correlated motions as is the case in MD simulations (50,51,52). Although the PCA results are affected by the US bias, the analysis allowed us to identify key domain motions orthogonal to the biased RC motion (53).

  We used the ensemble of protein conformations derived from each standard US simulation for PCA. For each simulation, the analysis found several components, but the top three components were retained as they were enough to describe most of the fluctuations of the system (>85%) (Fig. S15, Note S1). Projection of the simulation trajectory on these components allows one to visualize the motions directly. The three components that were able to describe the majority of the protein’s motions in these simulations were identified as 1) the detachment and downward motion of D1 away from D3, 2) the detachment of D3 from the D1+D4 interface, and 3) swiveling of D3 out of the plane of the protein (Fig. 7). Between different simulations, there was significant overlap of the components showing that similar motions were captured by the first three components in all such simulations (Table S1, Note S2). For the closed-state equilibrium simulations, PCA resulted in small-scale motions only (Fig. S13). In the open state, the swivel of D3 also appears along with other motions of domains D1 and D3 (Fig. S14).Figure 7A few principal components describe most of the motions. (A)–(C) show the three components that describe the majority of the system fluctuations as identified using principal component analysis (PCA) on an US run output. These three components from PCA resemble the major motions of the protein as it undergoes conformational changes from the gate-closed to a gate-open state.


#### D3 swivel is an important process in gate opening
  An important orthogonal motion identified from the standard US simulations and PCA is the swiveling of D3 during gate opening. Conformations seen during our US simulations (Fig. 8 A and B) are similar to the structure of a topoisomerase fragment previously observed in a swiveled state (54). A recent cryo-EM gate-open structure of Hs Top3β also shows substantial swivel of D2 and D3 together (16). To investigate the extent of the swivel and its importance on gate opening during our simulations, we computed 2D free energies. 2D free energies as a function of swiveling angles and RC distance during REUS for both apo and +ssDNA systems were calculated using WHAM (34). Swivel angles were calculated as mentioned in materials and methods and as shown in Fig. 8 C. Fig. 8 D shows that significant swiveling occurs during gate opening for both systems. Free energy landscapes for individual simulations are shown in Fig. S12. D3 is generally seen swiveling outward from the front face of the protein (see Fig. 1 A for the front face definition). This swiveling motion can occur either by just a rotation from the D2–D3 hinge region or along with a rotation of D2 and D3 together through the D2–D4 hinge.   REUS allowed for wider sampling of this orthogonal degree of freedom (Figs. 8 D and S12). The protein samples a wider range of angles in the apo system but is more constrained when the protein is bound to ssDNA. Likewise, as seen in one ssDNA simulation, swiveling in the other direction resulted in the system following an alternative pathway. These results point to the fact that swivel of D3 is significant in this process. The importance of D3 swiveling motion is also consistent with a proposed revised supercoil relaxation mechanism (13) and the recent structure (16). In the first mechanism, it was proposed that D3 moves down toward D1 and D4 to capture the T-strand. This process necessitates the hinges between D2 and both D3 and D4 be flexible. From Fig. 8 D, we see that the hinges are quite flexible to allow such a motion. They allow for swiveling similar to observations from (54) and (16), in excess of 20° previously seen in (42) but comparable to 60° reported in (16). As proposed in (15), our study demonstrates that the dissociation of D3 from D1 and D4 is an important step in gate opening. This dissociation is facilitated by the swiveling of D3. Although the large-scale motions are consistent, the precise pathway the system takes to opening may vary.


#### Limitations
  Although the results of our study shed light on conformational dynamics of the protein, these are not without caveats. First, running the simulations longer would increase the robustness of the results. The use of all-atom simulation system was prohibitive for us to significantly extend simulation times for all simulations. We were unable to see complete convergence even with increasing simulation time. Significantly longer simulations might demonstrate that the different pathways do eventually converge to one and the same, but in our studies, this was not achieved. To facilitate this, coarse-grained approaches might be adopted as timescales can be increased by orders of magnitude (55). Another potential limitation is the RC itself. The RC was chosen to correspond to experimental measurements of gate opening (17), but the choice may not have been optimal. Recently, it has been shown that machine learning is a promising tool in improving the choice of RC (56,57). Another option might be to run multidimensional US with an additional RC such as the swivel as referenced in Fig. 8. Together, these improvements could clarify the gate opening pathway and reproduce similar free energy curves faithfully in different simulations.


## Conclusions
  Type 1A topoisomerases undergo large conformational changes on short timescales. This requires dynamic experimental and computational methods to resolve the intricate details along their reaction cycle. The MD studies presented here have revealed possible structural mechanisms of gate opening—a major elusive state along the catalytic cycle.

  Msm Top1A activity via strand passage requires an opening between the D1, D3, and D4 interface domains. The interface is stabilized by electrostatic interactions between charged residues, all of which are conserved in mycobacteria and some across species (Fig. 4 D). During biased runs using US, these interactions are broken. This breaking of interdomain electrostatic interactions results in a gate-open structure that is consistent with several findings and predictions (2,7,15,16,17). Our results indicate gate opening involves not only the separation between domains but also a swiveling of D3, congruent with other experimental findings (13,16,54). The diversity in free energy curves and landscapes suggests that the protein could possibly take different pathways to get to the gate-open conformation. However, lack of convergence could also be a factor in this diversity, as well as the choice of RC, which might not be optimal in this study. Despite the lack of convergence, the broader motions described above are consistent between simulations.

  Mutagenesis studies targeting the important residues and their interactions from this study might lead to further insights. Specifically, supercoil relaxation experiments and force-dependent kinetics experiments would clarify the significance of these interactions as they pertain to protein activity. The approach described in this article could also be generalized to other type 1A topoisomerases—especially Homo sapiens type 1A topoisomerases. Structural studies into Hs topoisomerase 3α and 3β have garnered much interest in recent years, and MD simulations could facilitate a similar study of structural transitions that occur during gate opening. These investigations could pave the way for further research into the general mechanisms and structural details of proteins belonging to the type 1A topoisomerase subfamily.
