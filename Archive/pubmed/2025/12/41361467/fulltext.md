# L-Fucose alleviates inflammation, pyroptosis and mitochondrial injury in obesity-related cardiac injury via TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway


## Background
  Obesity-related cardiac injury is a serious global condition associated with excessive fat intake, which is strongly linked to chronic low-degrade inflammation. Pyroptosis, a pro-inflammatory form of cell death, has been implicated in obesity recently. L-Fucose (Fuc), a sulfated polysaccharide with anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, remains unexplored in the area of pyroptosis and obesity-related cardiac injury.


## Methods
  Six-week-old wide type C57BL/6 mice were subjected to high-fat diet (HFD) feeding for 20 weeks to induce obesity with or without Fuc gavaging, followed by assessment of metabolic related indicators. Echocardiography, histopathological analysis and enzymatic assays were used to assess cardiac function. The level of pro-inflammatory cytokines was detected by ELISA, qRT-PCR and CD68 staining. Pyroptosis-related protein including NLRP3, GSDMD, pro-caspase-1, cleaved-caspase-1, IL-1β, and ASC were evaluated by western blot. Oxidative stress was detected by ROS level. Cardiac mitochondrial structure was assessed by electron microscopy and mitotracker staining. Mitochondrial function was measured by mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) and oxygen consumption rate (OCR) assay. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted to look for the potential target of Fuc. Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) and western blot were used to investigate the interaction between Fuc and its target. Palmitic acid (PA) induced lipotoxicity model in AC16 cells was used to further confirm the protective effects of Fuc and its downstream signaling pathway against inflammation, pyroptosis and mitochondrial injury in vitro.


## Results
  In vivo, Fuc treatment alleviated obesity-related weight gain, hyperlipidemia, elevated systolic blood pressure and cardiac diastolic dysfunction. It also reduced the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and pyroptosis-related proteins, the level of oxidative stress, and mitochondrial abnormalities in the myocardium. Moreover, the vitro lipotoxicity model further confirmed the protective effect of Fuc. The molecular docking analysis identified TLR4 as the key target of Fuc. Fuc reduced the level of TLR4 and its downstream proteins MyD88 and p-NF-κB, while activating TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway by TLR4 agonist Neoseptin3 reversed the protective function of Fuc in vitro.


## Conclusions
  These findings demonstrated that Fuc mitigated cardiac inflammation, pyroptosis and mitochondrial injury by inhibiting the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic agent for obesity-associated cardiac injury.

## Introduction
  Recently, the rising prevalence of obesity has made it a global health issue [1]. Obesity is one of the main risk factor of cardiovascular diseases (CVD) including myocardial infarction (MI), atherosclerosis and heart failure (HF), significantly increasing the burden on healthcare system [2, 3]. Thus, it’s crucial to develop new approaches for the intervention and management of obesity-related cardiac dysfunction. Obesity-related cardiac injury involves complicated mechanisms including oxidative stress, inflammation, various forms of programmed cell death, oxidative DNA damage and autophagy disruption [4–8], but the precise mechanism of it remains obscure. Among them inflammation serves as a key contributor to the pathophysiology of obesity-induced cardiac injury. Nowadays, chronic cardiac inflammation has become a major threat to the health of people. Accumulating evidence has suggested that excessive lipid accumulation, especially the saturated fatty acid (SFA) in adipose tissue and myocardium, leads to the activation of chronic low-grade inflammatory response [9–11]. It can induce the expression of inflammatory factors, activate specific inflammatory pathways and trigger an inflammatory cascade in cardiomyocytes [11], ultimately leading to myocardial remodeling and cardiac dysfunction in obesity-associated myocardial injury [12, 13].

  Pyroptosis, defined as a specific form of programmed inflammatory cell death, is marked by the creation of pores in plasma membrane, cellular swelling and rupture, and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and cytoplasmic components [14]. Recent studies have highlighted its extensive involvement in the early pathological stages of CVD [15]. Canonical pyroptosis is started by the triggering of inflammasomes such as AIM2, NLRP3 and NLRC4, which subsequently contributes to the activation of caspase-1. Activated caspase-1 cuts GSDMD, separating its active N-terminal segment from the inactive C-terminal segment. The N-terminal segment forms gasdermin pores on the plasma membrane, resulting in cell lysis. Concurrently, caspase-1 converts IL-1β and IL-18 precursors into their cleaved forms, which are secreted into the extracellular environment to amplify inflammatory responses [16]. Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), a critical innate immune receptor, is essential for inflammatory response in metabolic disturbances such as hyperlipidemia and diabetes [17, 18]. Studies have shown that the initiation of TLR4/myeloid differentiation maker 88 (MyD88)/nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathway enhances the transcription of inflammasome complexes and promotes post-translational modifications, ultimately initiating pyroptosis [19].

  Fucoidan, a sulfated polysaccharide derived from brown algae and sea cucumbers, contains Fuc as its primary bioactive component [20]. In vivo, Fuc is a natural constituent of many glycans that are presented at the cell membrane of mammalian cells [21]. Fuc has been extensively studied due to its protective effect against obesity, tumor, and CVD by mitigating oxidative stress and inflammatory response [22, 23]. Recent study also found Fuc protected renal tubular epithelial cells from NLRP3 mediated pyroptosis [24]. However, whether Fuc can similarly exert protective effects on cardiomyocytes in obesity-related cardiac inflammatory and pyroptosis remains unexplored.

  In this study, we first reported that Fuc alleviated HFD-related cardiac injury through alleviating inflammation, pyroptosis and mitochondrial injury through TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway. Our findings may offer new perspectives for developing therapeutic strategies of obesity-related cardiac injury.


## Materials and methods

### Experimental animals
  Male wide type C57BL/6 mice (six-week-old) were divided to four groups (n = 10 each group): normal control (NC), NC with Fuc (NC + Fuc), HFD and HFD with Fuc (HFD + Fuc). The two NC groups were fed a normal chow diet, while the other two groups were fed a 60% HFD (D12492, Research Diets, USA) for 20 weeks. Fuc (300 mg/kg/d, F11093, Aladdin@, China) was gavaged every day in NC + Fuc and HFD + Fuc groups, whereas the other two groups received dd water. The selection of Fuc dose and duration was based on previous studies [25, 26] and preliminary experiments conducted in our laboratory. At the final stage of the experimental period, the animals were euthanized. Heart tissues and serum samples were obtained for subsequent analysis. All animal protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (Shanghai, China).


### Blood pressure (BP) measurement
  Systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were measured in conscious mice without invasion by using the CODA® high throughput system (Kent Scientific, USA). Mice were positioned individually on a platform and measurements were taken under stable conditions. For each session, a minimum of three readings was obtained [27]. To minimize measurement artifacts, mice underwent a three-day acclimatization period prior to recording.


### Glucose and insulin tolerance test
  Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) and insulin tolerance test (ITT) were conducted during the 20th week. For OGTT and ITT, mice were deprived of food for 16 h or for 6 h before receiving an oral glucose dose (2 g/kg) or an intraperitoneal injection of insulin (0.75 U/kg) respectively [28, 29]. Blood specimens were obtained from the caudal vein. A glucometer (ACCU-CHEK Performa Nano, Roche, Germany) and test papers (Accu-Chek Active test strips, Roche, Germany) were used to measure glucose level at specific time points.


### Metabolic analysis
  Level of triacylglycerol (TG), cholesterol (CHO), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c) was assessed on an ADVIA® 2400 automated biochemical analyzer (Siemens, Germany) with commercial assay kits (S03027, S03042,S03025,S03029, Leidu, China).


### Echocardiographic evaluation
  Echocardiography was performed on mice anaesthetized with 2% isoflurane using the Vevo 3100 imaging system (VisualSonics, USA). Long- and short-axis images were captured in B-mode, while M-mode was employed for precise measurements. Doppler measurements included peak inflow velocities across the mitral valve during early and late diastole, peak myocardial relaxation velocity at the mitral annulus in early diastole via tissue Doppler, and early filling deceleration time. To ensure stable conditions during imaging, isoflurane was maintained at 1.0-1.5%, with adjustments made to sustain heart rates between 450 and 550 bpm.


### Histopathological analysis
  The hearts of anesthetized mice were excised and fixed overnight at 4 °C in 4% paraformaldehyde solution. After dehydration and clearing, the tissues were embedded in liquid paraffin and sectioned into 5 μm slices using a microtome after the paraffin blocks were cooled and solidified. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed to observe histopathology. Sirius Red and Masson staining were used to assess the content of perivascular and interstitial fibrosis. CD68 immunostaining was used to analyze myocardial inflammation. For hypertrophy assessment, fresh myocardial tissue was embedded in OCT compound and rapidly cooled on dry ice. The frozen tissue was then sectioned into 5 μm slices using a cryostat and fixed before being subjected to FITC-WGA staining. Fluorescence imaging was performed using an Olympus fluorescence microscope (Tokyo, Japan).


### Measurement of cardiac injury biomarkers
  Level of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and creatine kinase isoenzyme (CK-MB) was quantified using the ADVIA® 2400 automated biochemical analyzer (Siemens, Germany) with commercial assay kits (C058, Changchun huili, China; S03023, Leidu, China).


### Detection of inflammatory cytokines
  Inflammatory cytokines from mouse serum and cell supernatants were measured using ELISA kits (88-7013-88, 88-7324-88, 88-7064-88, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The concentrations of these cytokines were determined based on standard curve calculations.


### Detection of MDA, SOD and GSH/GSSG
  Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was tested using a commercial assay Kit (A003-1, Jiancheng Biotechnology, China). Serum malondialdehyde (MDA) level and the ratio of reduced glutathione (GSH) to oxidized glutathione (GSSG) were quantified with commercial assay kits (G4306, G4304, Servicebio, China), following the manufacturer’s protocols.


### Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)
  Fresh left ventricular (LV) tissue was cut into 1 mm³ blocks and fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 4 h at 4 °C, followed by secondary fixation with 2% osmium tetroxide for 2 h. The tissue was then dehydrated in a graded ethanol series, embedded in resin, and sectioned into ultrathin slices. After staining, the sections were examined using a TEM (Hitachi, Japan).


### DHE staining
  DHE dye solution (Yeasen, China) was applied to freshly frozen heart slices. The sections were incubated in the darkness for 30 min, followed by two rinses with cold PBS. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) level in myocardial tissue was visualized using fluorescence microscopy (Olympus, Japan).


### Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)
  TRIzol reagent (Beyotime, China) was used to extract total RNA from LV myocardial tissue. RNA concentration and purity were assessed with Nanodrop One (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The RNA was then transferred into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (RR037A, Takara, Japan). The qPCR reaction mixture was prepared and amplified on a qPCR instrument (Bio-rad, USA).


### Western blot analysis
  Heart tissue and AC16 cell samples were lysed on ice for 30 min using radioimmunoprecipitation (RIPA) lysis buffer supplemented with phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) and phosphatase inhibitors. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C and the supernatant containing the total protein was collected. Protein concentration was quantified using the Bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (P0010, Beyotime, China). Loading buffer was then added to the samples, heating at 95 °C for 10 min to ensure complete protein denaturation. The loading amount of the protein sample was 20 µg. SDS-PAGE was conducted to separate proteins and polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (Bio-Rad, USA) was used to transferred proteins. After blocked with milk for 2 h, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. Secondary antibodies conjugated to horseradish peroxidase were applied for 2.5 h at room temperature. Immunoblotting results were captured using a Bio-Rad calibrated densitometer, with normalization to loading control β-actin or GAPDH. The specific primary and secondary antibodies used were: anti-NLRP3 (ab263899, Abcam, 1:1000), anti-GSDMD (ab209845, Abcam, 1:1000), anti-caspase1 (ab179515, Abcam, 1:1000), anti-IL-1β (#31202, CST, 1:1000), anti-ASC (ab309497, Abcam, 1:1000), anti-TLR4 (19811-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:1000), anti-MyD88 (#4283, CST, 1:1000), anti-p-NF-κB p65 (#3033, CST, 1:1000), anti-NF-κB p65 (#8242, CST, 1:1000), anti-IκBα (#2859, CST, 1:1000), anti-βactin (ab8227, Abcam, 1:1000), anti-GAPDH (ab9485, Abcam, 1:1000), HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) (A0208, Beyotime, 1:1000).


### Cell culture and regents
  Human cardiomyocyte AC16 cells was purchased from Shanghai Fuheng Biotechnology co.,LTD (catalog number: CVCL_4U18, EXPASY) and were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F12) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin in an incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. In line with the procedures previously mentioned, a cell lipotoxicity model was created by PA incubation [30]. According to the experimental design outlined in the figure legends, cells were co-treated with 3–30 mM Fuc and 300 µM PA (KC002, KunChuang, China) for 36 h after cell adhesion. Prior to PA treatment, cells were pretreated for 2 h with Nigericin (5 µM), MCC950 (5 nM), or Neoseptin3 (10 µM) [31–33]. Nigericin (pyroptosis agonist), MCC950 (NLRP3 inhibitor), and Neoseptin3 (TLR4/NF-κB pathway agonist) were obtained from MedChemExpress (USA).


### Cellular viability detection
  Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) (C0038, Beyotime, China) was used to assess cell viability in order to determine the optimal drug concentration. LDH release assays (C0016, Beyotime, China) was used to assesse the level of necrosis. Absorbance changes were recorded at wavelengths of 450 nm and 490 nm respectively.


### ROS assessment
  ROS assay kit (S0033, Beyotime, China) and MitoSox red probe (M36007, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) were used to quantify Intracellular and intramitochondrial ROS level respectively. Cells were stained with DCFH-DA (10 µM) and MitoSox red probes (5 µM) for 30 min at 37 °C in the darkness. After incubation, the cells were washed twice with serum-free medium to remove residual dye. Images were captured by a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan).


### Mitochondrial structure
  To evaluate alterations in mitochondrial structure, cells were stained with MitoTrackerTM Red (M46752, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 1 mM for 15 min at 37 °C. Single-cell images were acquired by a confocal microscope (Leica, Germany) and analyzed with ImageJ software (version 1.47v) to calculate the average mitochondrial length.


### Mitochondrial membrane potential measurement
  JC-1 assay kit (C2006, Beyotime, China) was used to evaluate MMP of cells. The cell samples were stained following the manufacturer’s protocol and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C before observation under a fluorescence microscope. In mitochondria with intact membrane potential, JC-1 aggregates in the mitochondrial matrix, producing intense red fluorescence, whereas in depolarized mitochondria, JC-1 remains in the cytoplasm as monomers, emitting green fluorescence due to reduced membrane potential. We also used flow cytometry (Agilent, USA) to further quantify the ratio of JC-1 aggregates to monomers.


### Oxygen consumption rate assay
  OCR was measured using the Seahorse XFe96 Analyzer (Agilent, USA) with the Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit and the Seahorse XF Glycolysis Stress Test Kit (103015-100, 103020-100, Agilent, USA). Prior to the experiment, the probe plate was hydrated with 200 µL of sterile water and incubated overnight at 37 °C. The next day, the water was replaced with 200 µL XF calibration solution, followed by hydration for 45–60 min at 37 °C in a CO₂-free incubator. Cells were washed twice with assay medium and maintained in 180 µL of detection solution. Oligomycin, FCCP, and Rot-AA were added sequentially at final concentrations of 1.5 µM, 1.5 µM, and 0.5 µM, respectively. OCR data were recorded and analyzed using Seahorse Wave software.


### Molecular Docking
  Molecular docking and virtualization were conducted using AutoDock Tools version 4.2.6. The crystal framework of the TLR4-MD-2 complex (PDB ID: 3FXI) was retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank, while the structural information for Fuc (CID: 17106) was sourced from the PubChem Compound Database. The optimal docking conformation was analyzed and visualized with Discovery Studio 2024 Client and PyMOL version 2.4.


### Molecular dynamics simulations
  A 50,000 ps MD simulation was conducted using the minimum energy conformer of the TLR4-Fuc complex with GROMACS 2024 [34], applying the CHARMM36-march2019 force field. The TIP3P water model was employed for solvation, and ligand (Fuc) parameters and topology files were generated via the PRODRG server. The system was positioned in a simulation container with sizes of 10 × 10 × 10 Å³, and sufficient ions were introduced to ensure electroneutrality. Energy minimization was performed until the force converged to 10 kJ·mol-1·nm-1, followed by 100 ps equilibration phases under constant volume and temperature (NVT) and constant pressure and temperature (NPT) conditions, with a 2 fs time step. Particle mesh Ewald (PME) method was used to calculate long-range interactions. Subsequently, a 5000 ps production MD simulation was performed with equilibrated ensembles using identical cut-off values. Temperature was maintained at 300 K and pressure was remained 1 bar respectively. Snapshots of the simulation trajectory were recorded every 1 ns.


### Cellular thermal shift assay
  The cells were digested with trypsin and then resuspended in pre-cooled PBS containing protease inhibitors. The samples were evenly distributed into eight PCR tubes and subjected to a temperature gradient protocol using a PCR thermocycler. This protocol included heating for 3 min, incubating at room temperature for 3 min, and rapid freezing in liquid nitrogen for 3 min. This cycle was repeated three times to achieve complete cell lysis. The lysates were then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm at 4 °C for 15 min, and the supernatant was subjected for western blotting.


### Statistical analysis
  All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For statistical comparisons, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used for two-group analyses, while one-way ANOVA followed by the Bonferroni post hoc test was employed for comparisons among multiple groups. When data did not follow a normal distribution, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied to evaluate differences between two groups. Analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 9.4.0. *P < 0.05. NS means no significance between groups.


## Results

### Fuc ameliorated the body weight gain, hyperlipidemia and SBP in HFD fed mice
  Figure 1A depicted the chemical structure of Fuc, while the details of the animal study protocol were outlined in Fig. 1B. After 20 weeks, HFD group exhibited a markedly greater body weight compared to ND-fed mice, whereas the mice receiving Fuc significantly reduced HFD-induced body weight gain (Fig. 1C-D). Consistently, we found that Fuc improved blood lipid profiles by reversing the HFD-induced elevation in plasma CHO, TG, and LDL level, as well as the reduction in HDL level (Fig. 1E). However, Fuc failed to alleviate the increased level of fasting blood glucose or improved glucose/insulin tolerance (higher area under the curve of the mice in HFD group), hinting that Fuc had no obvious effect on improving glucose homeostasis (Fig. 1F-H). Since arterial hypertension is strongly related to obesity [34], we measured BP at the beginning and at the end of the experiment. Intriguingly, we found that both SBP and DBP were significantly elevated after 20 weeks of HFD feeding, and Fuc significantly reduced SBP but didn’t alter DBP of HFD mice (Fig. 1I). We could conclude that Fuc treatment effectively ameliorated body weight (BW) gain, hyperlipidemia and SBP of obese mice.

### Fuc treatment alleviated HFD-induced cardiac dysfunction and remodeling
  Obesity leads to chronic structural heart changes including cardiac hypertrophy, further impairing the cardiac performance and accelerating the progression of heart failure [35]. Echocardiogram results indicated that 20 weeks of HFD feeding significantly impaired the cardiac diastolic performance, including reduced E/A ratio and enhanced E/e’. This impairment was effectively reversed by Fuc treatment (Fig. 2A, B). However, the cardiac systolic function was slightly impaired in HFD fed mice including decreased ejection fraction (EF) and left ventricular fractional shortening (LVFS), both of which were not alleviated by Fuc treatment (Fig. 2C, D). Other echocardiographic measurements could be seen in Supplementary Table 1. Additionally, HE and WGA staining revealed disrupted myocardial fiber organization and cardiac hypertrophy in HFD group. Masson and Sirius red staining showed that HFD feeding increased a significant deposition of fibrotic tissue in the left ventricle. And treatment with Fuc reverted all the parameters in morphometric and histological analysis above (Fig. 2E-G). Along the same line, HFD also significantly upregulated the level of cardiac injury biomarkers including LDH and CKMB in serum, and the responses were mitigated by Fuc (Fig. 2H, I), hinting that Fuc treatment could alleviate HFD exacerbated cardiac damage. These findings suggested that Fuc exerted a cardioprotective effect in HFD feeding mice.

### Fuc reversed inflammation, pyroptosis, and mitochondrial injury in cardiac tissues of HFD mice
  In the progression of cardiac injury in obesity, infltration of infammatory cells, pyroptosis and oxidative stress have been observed in vivo [36, 37]. Previous study has verified that Fuc protects against gastric ulcer injury through inflammation and pyroptosis [38]. In order to access the anti-inflammatory and anti-pyroptosis properties of Fuc in HFD-induced obesity, we conducted ELISA and transcriptional analysis to qualify the inflammatory markers TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-1β in serum and cardiac tissues respectively. As expected, Fuc supplementary decreased the level of TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-1β in HFD mice (Fig. 3A, B). Furthermore, immunoblotting was used to assess the expression level of NLRP3, GSDMD, pro-caspase-1, cleaved-caspase-1, IL-1β, and ASC across four experimental groups. The findings revealed a substantial rise in pyroptosis-related protein production in the HFD group compared to the ND group, which was significantly reduced by Fuc treatment (Fig. 3C, D). Moreover, The result of IHC revealed that Fuc downregulated CD68 level in heart of HFD group (Fig. 3E). These evidences suggested that Fuc attenuated inflammation and pyroptosis induced by lipid overload. Our results also indicated that Fuc exerted significant inhibitory effects on ROS production and oxidative stress activation in obesity. This was evidenced by a notable reduction in cardiac ROS level, serum MDA concentrations and the GSH/GSSH ratio, alongside increased SOD activity following Fuc treatment (Fig. 3F, G). To further validate oxidative stress-induced damage and subcellular organelle alterations in HFD fed mice, TEM was performed on myocardial tissues. The result showed that compared with ND group, HFD mice exhibited significant mitochondrial swelling, increased mitochondrial number and area, fragmentation or absence of cristae architecture and disorganization of sarcomere arrangement. However, these changes were dramatically attenuated by Fuc treatment (Fig. 3H). Collectively, these findings confirmed that Fuc effectively alleviated pyroptosis, inflammation and mitochondrial damage occurring in HFD mice.

### Fuc alleviated inflammation, pyroptosis and mitochondrial injury induced by PA in cardiomyocytes
  We established an PA-induced lipotoxicity model in AC16 cells to further confirm the protective effects of Fuc in vitro. CCK8 analyses were conducted to assess the optimal modeling concentration and duration of PA and to detect the safe dose of Fuc in AC16 cells. As it was showed in Fig. 4A, we chose a dose of 300 µM PA incubating 36 h for subsequent experiments, which caused 50% reduction of cell viability. Cell viability was significantly suppressed when Fuc was administered at a final concentration of 100 mM. Herein, we chose 3, 10 and 30 mM Fuc as low, middle, high concentrations for our research (Fig. 4B). Our findings revealed that PA treatment markedly increased LDH release into the cell supernatant, which could be attenuated by Fuc dose dependently (Fig. 4C). PA exposure also elevated the level of pyroptosis markers including NLRP3, GSDMD, pro-caspase-1, cleaved-caspase-1, IL-1β, and ASC. However, Fuc supplementation significantly decreased the expression of these proteins in AC16 cells. Furthermore, additional treatment with MCC950, a NLRP3 inhibitor, didn’t further strengthen Fuc’s pyroptosis-inhibitory effects, whereas co-treatment with Nigericin, a pyroptosis agonist, partially diminished its inhibitory impact (Fig. 4D, E). PA also induced inflammatory factors TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-1β level over-expression in cell supernatant and Fuc impressively inhibited the inflammatory responses in cardiomyocytes (Fig. 4F). Compared with control group, PA significantly decreased the basal, ATP-linked, and maximal respiratory capacity of mitochondria, indicating a damaging effect on mitochondrial respiratory function. However, Fuc treatment could dose-dependently reverse this effect (Fig. 4G, Supplementary Fig. 1). Immunofluorescence analysis was performed to assess mitochondrial morphology and evaluate the role of Fuc in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis. PA exposure fragmented mitochondria in AC16 cells, reducing mitochondrial length, whereas Fuc supplementation preserved mitochondrial network integrity (Fig. 4H, I). PA also reduced JC-1 monomer level, indicating a disruption in mitochondrial membrane potential, while Fuc treatment restored membrane potential by increasing JC-1 monomer level (Fig. 4J-L). For ROS staining, PA increased both mitochondrial and cytoplasmic ROS level in the cardiomyocytes while Fuc decreased both of them (Fig. 4M). All data revealed that Fuc could alleviate inflammation, pyroptosis and mitochondrial injury induced by PA in cardiomyocytes.

### TLR4 was a potential downstream target of Fuc in HFD-related cardiac dysfunction
  To explore the underlying molecular mechanism for the protective effect of Fuc, we performed molecular docking to look for the potential target of it. The structure of Fuc was retrieved from PubChem and analyzed using PharmMapper tools to identify relevant targets. 212 targets were initially acquired, and their names were standardized using the UniProt database. Additionally, 3672 obesity-related genes were collected through GeneCards, DisGeNET and OMIM databases after a series of filtering criteria. 110 common targets were overlapped through intersection (Fig. 5A-B). Molecular docking analyses were conducted using Auotodock Tools. Based on the docking scores, Fuc demonstrated a binding affinity to human TLR4 (-5.4 kcal/mol; Supplementary Table 2). Structural analysis revealed that Fuc binds to an active pocket in the lower central region of TLR4, forming hydrogen bonds with key residues, including GLU-225, ARG-227, HIS-199, and MET-201, which contributed to protein structural stability (Fig. 5C). The 2D diagram showed the surrounding interactions of Fuc (Fig. 5D). To account for ligand-protein dynamics, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted to validate the docking results. These simulations demonstrated stable interactions between Fuc and the target protein over time. Root mean square deviation (RMSD) was utilized to evaluate system stability, revealing fluctuations between 0 and 30,000 ps and consistent stability between 30,000 and 50,000 ps (Fig. 5E). The RMSF of Fuc were maintained between 0.50 and 2.50 Å, which was consistent with the result of RMSD. To further verify the docking results, a CETSA experiment was conducted to evaluate stability of target under compound interaction. The level of TLR4 protein decreased when temperature increased but it was preserved in the presence of Fuc treatment, suggesting that Fuc effectively protected TLR4 from heat-induced degradation (Fig. 5F, G). Furthermore, the expression of TLR4 was also dose dependently inhibited by using different concentrations of Fuc (Fig. 5H, I). These findings confirmed a stable interaction between Fuc and TLR4, highlighting its potential as a regulatory target. Briefly, TLR4 signaling was a potential Fuc target.

### Fuc treatment inhibited TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling activated by HFD in vitro and PA in vivo
  TLR4 recognizes pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), subsequently activating NF-κB and MAPKs in a MyD88-dependent way. This activation drives the transcription of genes linked to inflammatory responses and pyroptosis [39, 40]. So we conducted Immunoblot to test the level of TLR4/Myd88/NF-κB signaling cascade in vitro and in vivo. The results demonstrated that HFD feeding increased the expression of TLR4, MyD88 and phosphorylation of NF-κB and IκBα while Fuc treatment restrained these changes.(Fig. 6A, B). The result in PA-induced AC16 cells was consistent with HFD mice, and Fuc significantly inverted these changes dose dependently (Fig. 6C, D). Our data illustrated that the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling was upregulated in high-fat environment and Fuc could directly inhibit it.

### Fuc attenuated inflammation, pyroptosis and mitochondrial injury in PA-treated cardiomyocytes through inhibiting TLR4/MyD88/NF-kB pathway
  We have already proved that TLR4/MyD88/NF-kB pathway was activated in a high-fat environment in both heart tissues and cardiomyocytes, which could be reversed by Fuc supplementary. To further investigate, Neoseptin3, a specific TLR4 agonist, was used to assess whether Fuc mitigated myocardial abnormalities through TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB cascade in vitro. Western blot analysis was conducted to measure TLR4, MyD88, p-NF-κB and NF-κB concentrations. PA exposure significantly improved the expression of these proteins except for NF-κB, but this upregulation was markedly reduced by Fuc treatment. However, co-treatment with Neoseptin3 partially reversed this inhibition (Fig. 7A, B). Fuc also reduced the level of NLRP3, GSDMD, pro-caspase-1, cleaved-caspase-1, ASC and IL-1β in PA group, but the addition of Neoseptin3 partially reversed this reduction (Fig. 7C, D). ELISA was then conducted to assess the level of TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6 in supernatant after PA injury. Fuc reversed the increase of inflammatory cytokines induced by PA but Neoseptin3 canceled such kind of reduction (Fig. 7E). Fuc alleviated PA-induced effect in mitochondrial OCR and fragmentation, but this protective effect was partially reversed by the addition of Neoseptin3 (Fig. 7F-H). Likewise, Fuc increased JC-1 monomers level in AC16 cells treated by PA whereas Neoseptin3 co-treatment decreased JC-1 monomers and exacerbated mitochondrial oxidative damage (Fig. 7I). In addition, Fuc lessened mitochondrial ROS ulteriorly in PA-treated AC16 cells but this reduction was partially mitigated by the addition of Neoseptin3 (Fig. 7J). Taken together, our results indicated that activating TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway by Neoseptin3 could partially reverse the beneficial effect of Fuc in vitro, so we could conclude that Fuc regulated inflammation, pyroptosis and mitochondrial injury through TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway in PA-induced AC16 cells. ## Discussion
  Long-term obesity is strongly associated with cardiac remodeling. The accumulation of fat, particularly the excessive storage of visceral adipose tissue (VAT) and pericardial fat, leads to elevated cardiac output (CO) and workload, prompting LV enlargement to accommodate heightened energy demands [41], leading to LV hypertrophy, cardiac fibrosis and diastolic dysfunction, ultimately progressing to overt heart failure [36].

  Growing evidence demonstrates that Fuc can effectively mitigate metabolic disorders including obesity. For instance, Fucoidan from Fucus vesiculosus improved lipid profile, glucose homeostasis and insulin resistance in mice with metabolic disorders [42]. Also, S.fusiforme Fucoidan ameliorated diet-induced obesity (DIO) in mice by enhancing thermogenesis in adipose tissue and modulating gut microbiota to improve metabolic parameters [43]. Consistently, our present study indicated that Fuc significantly alleviated HFD-induced body weight gain, hyperlipidemia and increased systolic blood pressure of mice. More importantly, studies also have found that Fuc could exhibit cardioprotective effects, including anticoagulant, antithrombotic, and anti-inflammatory activities, as well as modulation of vascular cell behaviors [22], in which Fuc was reported to inhibit acute cardiotoxicity induced by doxorubicin [44], alleviate LV hypertrophy and cardiac damage in diabetic mice [45], and reduce angiotensin II-induced heart remodeling through the regulation of USP22/Sirt1 expression [46]. But its effect on HFD-induced cardiac injury hasn’t been explored yet. Therefore, we developed a diet-induced obesity model to investigate the cardioprotective effects of Fuc in obese mice. Our finding revealed that an oral gavage of 300 mg/kg Fuc for 20w significantly improved cardiac diastolic function including increased E/A and reduced E/e’. Furthermore, Fuc reduced cardiac fibrosis, alleviated LV hypertrophy and mitigated myocardial damage. Our findings were the first to establish that Fuc enhanced cardiac structure and function in obese mice.

  A notable feature of obesity cardiomyopathy is the chronic activation of inflammation [47]. Adipocytes and resident immune cells in dysfunctional adipose tissues become activated and release a wide range of secretory factors including adiponectin, leptin, resistin, nitric oxide, interleukins, IL-1, TNF-α and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1). Additionally, diverse immune cells are recruited to adipose tissues, where they adopt an active inflammatory phenotype, contributing to the production and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines [48]. These secretory factors result in the development of CVD through autocrine, paracrine, or endocrine mechanisms. Pyroptosis, a type of programmed cell death strongly linked to inflammation, also serves as a key factor in the pathophysiology of obesity cardiomyopathy [15]. Studies have shown that the level of ASC, caspase-1 and NLRP3 were elevated in HFD mice, while genetic inactivation of caspase-1 or NLRP3 suppressed inflammasome initiation in adipose tissue and liver of obese mice [49]. The Nod-like receptor (NLR) family, including NLRP3 inflammasome, serves as innate immune sensors that recognize “danger signals.” This recognition activates caspase-1, triggering the secretion of IL-1β and IL-18 and resulting in canonical pyroptotic cell death. Evidence indicated that increased body weight in patients, sheep and mice was related to the initiation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in heart and other organs [50, 51]. Our experiments demonstrated that Fuc downregulated the level of inflammation related factors induced by HFD or PA damage in cardiomyocytes. Furthermore, Fuc inhibited the release of pyroptosis proteins markers including NLRP3, ASC, GSDMD, cleaved-caspase-1 and IL-1β. This inhibitory effect could be partially reversed by the pyroptosis agonist Nigericin but not by the NLRP3 antagonist MCC950, indicating that Fuc exerted a protective effect against pyroptosis in the myocardium of obesity.

  Mitochondrial injury is a kind of ‘‘point of no return’’ in GSDMD activated pyroptosis. The N-terminal pore-forming fragment of GSDMD (GSDMD-NT) rapidly interacts with cardiolipin on mitochondrial membranes, disrupting both the inner and outer mitochondrial membranes (OMMs). This disruption results in increased mitochondrial ROS production, loss of transmembrane potential, and the secretion of cytochrome c and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) into the cellular matrix [52, 53]. Previous study also observed that elevated expression of GSDME in renal tubular epithelial cells, where the GSDME-N fragment not only triggered pyroptosis but also localized to mitochondria, causing mitochondrial damage and cytochrome c release [54]. Therefore, we conducted TEM and mitotracker staining to observe the change of mitochondrial morphology within myocardium undergoing pyroptosis and observed that Fuc treatment significantly alleviated mitochondrial injury, mitochondrial membrane potential depolarization, and reduced OCR under lipid overload, suggesting its involvement in modulating mitochondrial functions.

  To elucidate the potential mechanism of Fuc regulation in obesity-related cardiac injury, we analyzed multiple public databases and performed molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations. Our findings identified TLR4 as a potential target of Fuc, which was further validated through CETSA experiments. Additionally, our data revealed that TLR4 protein expression was elevated following PA treatment, while Fuc treatment dose-dependently reduced its expression. All these data indicated that TLR4 was a downstream regulation factor of Fuc. As a key member of the Toll-like receptor (TLR) family, TLR4 acts as a primary pattern recognition receptor (PRR) and exerts a critical role in the inflammatory cascade response associated with DIO.

  Saturated free fatty acids (FFAs), such as PA, activate TLR4 signaling pathways, triggering the generation of inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α and NF-κB [55, 56]. TLR4 is also an essential upstream regulator of the NLRP3 inflammasome complex. Through interacting with its downstream adaptor protein MyD88 at the plasma membrane, TLR4 promotes the activation of transcription factor NF-κB [40, 57]. This signaling is instrumental in triggering pyroptosis in a variety of cell types, leading to ROS and cytokine production [58]. So we then investigated whether TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway was participated in obesity-induced inflammation and pyroptosis in myocardium. We detected the level of TLR4, MyD88, p-NF-κB and p-IκBα in HFD mice and in PA-induced AC16 cells. The results showed that the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway was upregulated under the circumstances of lipotoxic exposure and Fuc reversed this change both in vivo and in vitro. Moreover, Neoseptin3, a TLR4 agonist, was involved to enhance the TLR4 pathway activity in PA-induced AC16 cells. We subsequently observed that TLR4 agonist aggravated inflammation, pyroptosis and oxidative stress damage in PA and Fuc co-administered AC16 cells, which indicated that Neoseptin3 accession could reduce the protective effect of Fuc. Collectively, our results illustrated that Fuc could mitigate HFD-induced inflammation, pyroptosis and mitochondrial injury in cardiomyocytes via downregulating TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway.

  This study also has several limitations that warrant consideration. These findings highlight the potential of Fuc as a novel therapeutic candidate and TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway as a potential target for preventing obesity-related cardiac injury. Further clinical studies are warranted to evaluate its translational applicability in human populations. While we investigated a protective effect of Fuc in classical pyroptosis pathway, the role of non-classical pyroptosis pathways was also important but it was not explored in our study. When it comes to identify the potential downstream target of Fuc, although we used molecular docking experiment, CETSA and western blot to effectively validate the TLR4 target, RNA sequencing was still not conducted to further elucidate potential regulatory pathways of Fuc. Besides, although inflammation is a key factor in obesity-related myocardial damage, we cannot exclude the possibility that Fuc may exert cardioprotective effects through other mechanisms beyond its anti-inflammatory properties. In addition, we only demonstrated that HFD could induce pyroptosis and mitochondrial damage in cardiomyocytes, the specific molecular mechanism and relationship between them are also a question to be addressed in the future.

  In conclusion, our findings demonstrated that Fuc offered significant protection against HFD-induced cardiac dysfunction. This study further revealed that Fuc mitigated inflammation, pyroptosis and mitochondrial injury in cardiomyocytes by modulating the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling. Collectively, our results underscore the clinical potential of Fuc in alleviating inflammatory damage associated with HFD-related cardiac injury and provide valuable insights for new therapeutic strategies.
