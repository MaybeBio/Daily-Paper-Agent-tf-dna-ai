# A Novel Palmitoylation-Related Molecular Signature for Predicting and Therapeutically Targeting Alzheimer’s Disease


## Graphical Abstract

## Introduction
  Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by memory impairment and cognitive deficiencies [1, 2]. As global populations continue to age, AD has become an increasingly urgent public health concern with significant socioeconomic implications [3, 4]. Although amyloid-beta (Aβ) deposits and hyperphosphorylated tau aggregates are two of the neuropathological hallmarks of AD, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying the disease’s development remain elusive [5, 6]. Furthermore, current therapeutic interventions for AD remain limited in both efficacy and availability [1]. Hence, elucidating the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms and identifying novel therapeutic targets for AD represent critical priorities in biomedical research.

  Posttranslational modifications (PTMs) are important regulatory processes endowing the complexity of the cellular proteome by enabling diverse functional alterations in proteins [7]. Among various PTMs, palmitoylation—a lipid-based modification involving the attachment of palmitate (a lipid moiety) to cysteine residues through a labile thioester linkage—plays a crucial role in the dynamic regulation and functional modulation of proteins [8]. This PTM is widely implicated in the regulated trafficking and functional adaptation of membrane proteins and membrane-associated signaling molecules. Maintaining a balance between protein palmitoylation and depalmitoylation is paramount for effectively controlling these processes [9–11]. Palmitoyl acyltransferases, which belong to the DHHC family, are major players in the regulation of palmitoylation and several of which are expressed in neurons [12–14]. Extensive research has established protein palmitoylation as a key regulatory mechanism in maintaining normal brain physiology [15–17]. Dysregulated palmitoylation has been associated with pathological mechanisms underlying various neurological disorders such as cancers, neurodegenerative diseases, and psychiatric disorders [18–23]. Recently, increasing attention has been directed toward the role of palmitoylation in neurodegenerative disorders, particularly in the pathogenesis of AD.

  Palmitoylation is starting to show signs of being a pathogenic process in AD, primarily through its ability to influence Aβ production and modulate effector proteins involved in Aβ- and tau-mediated synaptic toxicity [15, 24–26]. Emerging evidence further indicates that dysregulated palmitoylation exacerbates pathological alterations in these proteins, leading to subsequent disruptions in cellular processes [15, 16, 27]. Moreover, impaired function of palmitoylating and depalmitoylating enzymes has been linked to the pathogenesis of neurological disorders [28, 29]. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms and functional consequences of palmitoylation in AD remain insufficiently understood. A better understanding of palmitoylation in AD pathogenesis may lead to the development of innovative medications to prevent or postpone the disease.

  To investigate the potential role of palmitoylation in AD, we obtained expression profiles of AD patients and non-demented (ND) controls from the publicly available GEO database. Differential expression genes (DEGs) screening and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) were performed. Subsequently, palmitoylation-related genes associated with AD (AD-PRGs) were obtained from the intersection of DEGs and the palmitoylation-related genes (PRGs) set. Seven key AD-PRGs were finally determined by protein–protein interactions (PPI) network and three machine learning algorithms. We also evaluated and validated the expression levels and risk associations of these key genes. And the putative palmitoylation sites of these genes were predicted using the deep learning tool GPS-Palm. Additionally, a potential transcription factor (TF)-miRNA regulatory network was constructed to explore the upstream regulatory mechanisms of the seven key genes. Finally, by screening the DrugBank database, we identified and assessed potential drugs targeting these key AD-PRGs. This study provides novel insights into the role of palmitoylation in AD and may contribute to the development of future therapeutic strategies.


## Methods

### Data Acquisition and Preparation
  Seven transcriptomic microarray datasets related to AD were obtained from the publicly accessible GEO platform (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo). The obtained datasets were as follows: GSE5281 (GPL570 platform; 87 AD and 74 ND controls), GSE29378 (GPL6947; 31 AD and 32 ND controls), GSE36980 (GPL6244; 33 AD and 47 ND controls), GSE122063 (GPL16699; 56 AD and 44 controls), and GSE132903 (GPL10558; 97 AD and 98 ND controls) [30–34]. Additionally, two datasets both annotated by the GPL4372 platform were incorporated: GSE33000 (310 AD and 157 controls) and GSE44770 (129 AD and 101 controls) [35, 36]. And these datasets comprise samples from six brain regions: the hippocampus, frontal cortex, temporal cortex, entorhinal cortex, posterior cingulate cortex, and primary visual cortex. Each dataset underwent background correction and normalization using the “limma” R package (version3.62.2) in R software (version4.4.1). The datasets GSE5281, GSE29378, GSE36980, GSE122063, and GSE132903 were then integrated into a training set using the R package “sva” (version3.50.0) to mitigate batch effects [37]. And principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the “factoextra” R package (version1.0.7) [38]. The key AD-PRGs that distinguish AD patients from ND controls were subsequently identified through the training set. Moreover, datasets GSE33000 and GSE44770 served as the validation set to confirm the expression levels of the identified key palmitoylation-related genes associated with AD across different groups.


### Palmitoylation-Related Genes
  A total of 1728 genes with a relevance score of ≥ 1.019 (the median score) were integrated from the GeneCards database using the keyword “palmitoylation” (Table S1). This palmitoylation-related gene set incorporates palmitoyl acyltransferases (ZDHHC1-24, excluding ZDHHC10), depalmitoylases (including acylprotein thioesterases, palmitoyl protein thioesterases, and α/β hydrolase domain proteins), identified substrates of palmitoylation, and crucial lipid metabolism regulators (e.g., fatty acid synthase and acyl-CoA synthetase) that affect palmitate synthesis.


## Differential Expression Genes Analysis
  DEGs between AD and ND control samples in the training set were identified using the “limma” package (version3.62.2), with significance thresholds set at |log2 fold change|> 0.5 and adjusted p-value < 0.05 [37]. The expression patterns of these DEGs have been presented as a volcano plot and a hierarchical clustering heatmap using the R “ggplot2” (version3.5.1) and “pheatmap” (pretty heatmaps; version 1.0.13) packages [39, 40].


### Functional Enrichment Analysis of DEGs
  GSEA [41] was carried out with the R package “clusterProfiler” (version4.14.6), using gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB; version2023.1) as references, including “c2.cp.kegg.v2023.1.Hs.entrez.gmt,” “c5.go.bp.v2023.1.Hs.entrez.gmt,” “c5.go.cc. v2023.1.Hs.entrez.gmt,” and “c5.go.mf.v2023.1.Hs.entrez.gmt.” The results were visualized using the “enrichplot” (version1.26.6) R package.


### Extraction of Palmitoylation-Related Genes Associated with AD (AD-PRGs)
  The “VennDiagram” package (version1.7.3) [42] was utilized to identify the intersection of PRGs and DEGs, and the overlapping genes were pinpointed as candidate genes associated with palmitoylation in AD. The candidate genes subsequently underwent enrichment analyses via the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO), with an adjusted p-value of less than 0.05.


### Screening of Hub AD-PRGs
  The STRING database (version12.0; http://www.string-db.org/) was utilized to construct a PPI network to investigate the interactions among AD-PRGs. The PPI network was visualized using Cytoscape software (version 3.10.1) after the removal of isolated nodes. Significant gene clusters were identified via the cytohubba-MNC (Maximum Neighborhood Component) and cytohubba-MCC (Maximal Clique Centrality) plugins.


### Machine Learning for Risk Feature Gene Identification and Key AD-PRGs
  Three machine learning techniques were implemented to detect for risk genes. First, a least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) algorithm was executed using the “glmnet” R package (version4.1–8) to select genes crucial for discriminating AD patients from controls [43]. Simultaneously, the support vector machine-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) method was applied via the “e1071” package (version1.7–14) to ascertain the optimal set of discriminatory genes[44]. Complementarily, we employed Random Forest—technique notably effective for high-dimensional genomic data, due to its ability to withstand noise and accurately quantify feature importance [45]. In this implementation, 500 decision trees were constructed to enhance model stability. Genes with an importance score exceeding 15 were retained as characteristic features. The consensus genes that intersected with the outputs of all three algorithms were identified as the key AD-PRGs. Expression levels of these genes were subsequently validated across both training and external validation cohorts.


### Construction of AD-PRGs Risk Model
  To enhance the clinical utility of our findings, we constructed a prognostic nomogram integrating key AD-PRGs using the “rms” R package (version6.7-1) [46]. The reliability of the model was rigorously evaluated through a comprehensive validation framework, which included (1) calibration curve analysis to assess prediction accuracy, (2) decision curve analysis (DCA) to quantify clinical utility, and (3) receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve evaluation. Complementary ROC analysis was performed using the “pROC” package (version1.18.5) to assess the diagnostic performance of individual genes. Finally, external validation was conducted on independent GEO datasets (GSE33000 and GSE44770) to evaluate model robustness, reproducibility, and generalizability.


### Quantitative Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction (qRT-PCR)
  Total RNA was extracted from the cerebral cortex of 6-month-old male wild-type (WT) and 3 × Tg AD model mice (Beijing Viewsolid Biotech Co., Ltd.) using RNA isolater Total RNA Extraction Reagent (R401-01, Vazyme). RNA concentration was measured with a Nanodrop ONE spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (R323-01, Vazyme). Quantitative PCR was performed with gene-specific primers (Table 1) using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q711-02, Vazyme) on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method with GAPDH as the internal reference. ### Single-Gene GSEA of Key AD-PRGs
  Single-gene GSEA is a computational method that examines the functional roles of key AD-PRGs by leveraging gene expression profiles within biological and disease contexts [47]. The gseKEGG function from the “clusterProfiler” package (version4.14.6) was used to assess the correlation between each key gene and all other genes, followed by ranking genes based on correlation strength. These ranked lists served as test gene sets, which were then evaluated against KEGG signaling pathway gene sets. Enrichment levels were assessed using thresholds of |NES|> 1, adjusted p-value < 0.05, and q < 0.05 to elucidate associated physiological and pathological mechanisms.


### Prediction of Palmitoylation Sites in Key AD-PRGs
  To further investigate potential roles of palmitoylation in the seven key AD-PRGs, we predicted their modification sites using the deep learning tool GPS-Palm (version1.0; https://gpspalm.biocuckoo.cn/). This computational approach efficiently identifies palmitoylation sites beyond experimental limitations. GPS-Palm incorporates three innovations: (1) a data quality discrimination method that assigns weights to sites based on experimental data, (2) sequence-to-image encoding, and (3) parallel convolutional neural networks for processing multi-feature inputs. The predictor achieves exceptional performance (AUC = 0.855), representing a > 31.3% improvement over existing tools, with human- and mouse-specific versions attaining AUCs of 0.900 and 0.897, respectively [48, 49].


### Interaction Analysis of Key AD-PRGs with Aβ and Tau via GRAMM and PISA
  Corresponding amino acid sequences were retrieved from UniProtKB (https://www.uniprot.org/) based on gene identifiers. The three-dimensional protein structures were acquired from the Protein Data Bank (PDB; https://www.rcsb.org/) using the following accession IDs: BAG3 (O95817), TJP1 (Q07157), MET (P08581), NOTCH1 (P46531), SYT1 (P21579), CALM1 (P0DP23), Aβ (P05067), and Tau (P10636). Protein–protein interactions were predicted using GRAMM (Global RAnge Molecular Matching; http://vakser.utk.edu/resources/gramm), and the binding energies of the resulting complexes were evaluated with PISA (Proteins, Interfaces, Surfaces and Assemblies; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/msd-srv/prot_int/ pistart.html). Finally, the top-scoring conformation was selected according to its calculated binding affinity. All resultant structural models were visualized and analyzed using PyMOL (version 3.0.5).


### Construction of TF-miRNA Regulatory Networks for Key AD-PRGs
  Potential miRNAs that regulate the key AD-PRGs were predicted using the miRTarBase (version 9.0). Subsequently, TFs targeting these AD-PRGs were identified through JASPAR binding motif analysis, as implemented in NetworkAnalyst (version 3.0). Finally, an integrated miRNA-TF regulatory network was constructed and visualized with Cytoscape (version 3.10.1).


### Screening of Potential Therapeutic Drugs and Molecular Docking
  Potential therapeutic drugs targeting key genes were identified using DrugBank (version 6.0, https://go.drugbank.com), an integrated cheminformatics and bioinformatics repository documenting over 10,000 drugs. Ligand 3D structures were retrieved from DrugBank, while corresponding receptor protein structures were acquired from the Protein Data Bank (PDB; https://www.rcsb.org/). Molecular docking simulations were performed in AutoDock Tools software (version 1.5.6) to characterize binding interactions and affinity profiles between ligands and target receptors. Finally, the optimal result docking diagrams were visualized using PyMol software (version 3.0.5).


### Statistical Analysis
  R 4.4.1 software was used for all bioinformatics statistical analysis and visualization. Data are presented as the mean ± SD, and comparisons between groups were performed using an unpaired Student’s t-test. All statistical analyses of experiments were performed by GraphPad Prism 10. Significance was defined as p < 0.05.

The integrated training dataset comprised 599 samples, consisting of 295 ND controls and 304 AD brain specimens; detailed sample characteristics are summarized in Table S2 and Table S3. Following preprocessing and batch effect correction, 592 DEGs were identified, with 251 upregulated and 341 downregulated in AD compared to controls. The docking results indicated strong interactions between these key AD-PRGs and both pathogenic proteins, with TJP1 exhibiting particularly high binding affinity. Furthermore, the surface pattern diagrams of these seven proteins in combination with Aβ and tau are also shown in Fig. S3 and Fig. S4, which further demonstrated the binding sites of these proteins in complex with Aβ and tau.

## Discussion
  Aberrant protein palmitoylation and dysfunctional DHHC-family palmitoyltransferases are implicated in AD pathogenesis [26, 28]. Specifically, impaired palmitoylation dynamics have been mechanistically linked to dysregulated Aβ production, tau-mediated neurotoxicity, synaptic impairment, and disrupted calcium homeostasis in AD [15, 27, 50]. However, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated, hampered by technical challenges such as the absence of a conserved sequence motif for predicting modification sites, a lack of commercial antibodies, and the high hydrophobicity of palmitoylated proteins [8]. These limitations collectively restrict comprehensive mechanistic and functional studies of palmitoylation.

  In this study, we conducted a comprehensive bioinformatic analysis of palmitoylation-related DEGs in AD and controls using data from the GEO database.

  Enrichment analysis revealed that these genes were primarily associated with the synaptic vesicle cycle, citrate cycle (TCA cycle), and propanoate metabolism among KEGG pathways, and with the regulation of transmembrane transport, negative regulation of the G protein-coupled receptor signaling pathway, and synaptic vesicle exocytosis in GO terms. PPI network analysis identified ten hub genes, which were subsequently refined to seven key AD-PRGs (CALM1, VAMP2, SYT1, MET, BAG3, TJP1, and NOTCH1) using three machine learning algorithms. A diagnostic model constructed from these seven key genes demonstrated high accuracy across both training and external validation datasets, thereby substantiating their essential role in AD pathogenesis. Furthermore, when evaluated against several recently published AD transcriptomic signatures—including those related to oxidative stress, autophagy, aging, and the immune microenvironment—our seven-gene model achieved highly competitive performance. It yielded AUCs of 0.907 and 0.865 in two independent validation sets, matching or surpassing the performance of previously reported multigene classifiers (see Table 2) [51–54]. Each individual gene also exhibited considerable predictive power, with AUCs consistently above 0.7. Importantly, our diagnostic model demonstrated consistent efficacy across brain regions with distinct anatomical origins. Despite being derived from multi-regional data, the model maintained high and comparable AUCs in two external cohorts: one exclusively from the prefrontal cortex (AUC = 0.865, GSE33000) and another encompassing prefrontal cortex, occipital cortex, and cerebellum (AUC = 0.907, GSE44770). Additionally, we explored the interactions of Aβ and tau with key AD-PRGs using a computational docking technique. Our investigation revealed putative interactions, supporting their potential as therapeutic targets.   A diverse array of palmitoylated proteins has been identified within the central nervous system, including scaffolding proteins, ion channels, signaling proteins, enzymes, cell adhesion molecules, and numerous G-protein-coupled receptors [17, 55, 56]. Notably, approximately 41% of all known synaptic proteins can undergo palmitoylation, indicating that the modification plays a crucial role in neural function [57]. And synaptic impairment is one of the earliest pathological changes observed in the AD patients [58, 59]. In our study, the single-gene GSEA revealed a significant collective association between the seven key AD-PRGs and synaptic vesicle cycling pathways. Specifically, CALM1, VAMP2, SYT1, and MET exhibited a positive correlation with this pathway, whereas BAG3, TJP1, and NOTCH1 demonstrated a negative correlation. These findings suggest that palmitoylation may play a principal role in AD pathology through dysregulation of synaptic processes.

  Among these key genes, VAMP2 (vesicle-associated membrane protein 2) and SYT1 (synaptotagmin 1) are central regulators of presynaptic neurotransmitter release [60] VAMP2, a vesicle-associated SNARE (soluble NSF attachment protein receptor) protein, mediates intracellular vesicle fusion [63], while SYT1 acts as a calcium sensor for exocytosis [64]. These two proteins work together within the SNARE complex to mediate calcium-triggered vesicle fusion and neurotransmitter release. The diminished expression of VAMP2 and SYT1 in the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex is associated with cognitive impairment in AD [15, 64]. SYT1 could interact with presenilin-1 (PS1), and enhancing PS1–SYT1 binding has been investigated as a synaptic therapeutic strategy for AD prevention [65]. Of particular functional relevance, palmitoylation promotes the co-clustering of VAMP2 and SYT1 on vesicle membranes—a modification that spatially organizes the fusion machinery and is essential for efficient neurotransmitter release[61, 62, 66]. Furthermore, calcium ions mediate essential neuronal functions including neurotransmitter synthesis, release, and the regulation of membrane excitability [67]. The gene CALM1 (calmodulin 1), which encodes calmodulin, regulates the subcellular localization and activity of calmodulin in neurons. It also affects tau phosphorylation, a process whose dysregulation results in the accumulation of pathological phosphorylated tau [68, 69]. However, the specific impact of palmitoylation on the function and regulation of CALM1 remains to be fully elucidated.

  The receptor tyrosine kinase MET (mesenchymal epithelial transition) is activated by hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), initiating pleiotropic signaling during brain development that promotes neurogenesis, survival, synaptogenesis, and plasticity. Consequently, the HGF–MET axis contributes to activity-dependent regulation of learning and memory in the adult brain [70, 71]. Wang et al. further demonstrated that palmitoylation of MET at Cys801 by ZDHHC15 is essential for its O-glycosylation, dimerization, and activation, underscoring the critical role of this lipid modification in MET signaling [72]. In contrast, NOTCH1 (neurogenic locus notch homolog protein 1) signaling—a pathway fundamental to brain development and adult function—is implicated in synaptic plasticity, memory, and olfaction [73]. Reduced NOTCH1 activity disrupts these processes, while its overexpression has been linked to sporadic AD, suggesting that NOTCH1 imbalance may contribute to AD pathophysiology [74, 75]. Beyond these signaling pathways, structural and chaperone proteins also play critical roles in AD. TJP1 (tight junction protein 1), initially identified in the blood-brain barrier (BBB), is essential for maintaining barrier integrity. Aβ peptide impairs TJP1 function and compromises BBB integrity, contributing to AD pathogenesis [76, 77]. In neurons, TJP1 localizes to synaptic junctions, including those between mossy fiber terminals and CA3 dendrites [78]. Meanwhile, BAG3 (BAG family molecular chaperone regulator 3), a multifunctional 575-amino acid protein, regulates processes such as apoptosis, autophagy, and cytoskeletal organization via its protein-interaction domains [79]. It has been reported that BAG3-mediated selective macroautophagy is crucial for the clearance of aggregated proteins associated with AD, and the expression of BAG3 is significantly elevated in AD [80, 81].

  Palmitoylation can modulate the expression, stability, trafficking, or membrane localization of these proteins through direct or indirect mechanisms [9–11]. For instance, ZDHHC15-mediated palmitoylation of MET at Cys801 and Cys1025 enhances its membrane localization and downstream signaling, including the RAS/MAPK pathway [72, 82]. Similarly, ZDHHC17-catalyzed palmitoylation of VAMP2 at Cys103 promotes its membrane-binding affinity and synaptic vesicle recycling efficiency [83]. To explore the potential roles of palmitoylation on these seven key AD-PRGs further, we predicted the palmitoylation sites on these genes using the online software GPS-Palm. The results indicated several palmitoylation sites on six genes that were highly conserved in different species with the exception of CALM1. Critically, all six genes are independently supported by literature to be involved in key AD pathological processes, including synaptic dysfunction (VAMP2, SYT1) [61, 66], disrupted cellular signaling (MET, NOTCH1) [70, 74], impaired protein clearance (BAG3) [80], and blood-brain barrier breakdown (TJP1) [77]. The presence of conserved palmitoylation sites across these functionally diverse AD-related genes suggests that this post-translational modification may represent a common mechanism fine-tuning their disease-associated functions. Although there is no current evidence that CALM1 is palmitoylated, palmitoylation may indirectly affect the membrane location and activity of CALM1 by influencing other calmodulin-family proteins, such as GAP43 [84, 85].

  To elucidate the regulatory interplay among the seven key genes, TFs, and miRNAs, a comprehensive gene–TF–miRNA regulatory network was constructed. This network incorporated 33 TFs and 23 miRNAs interacting with the hub genes. Notably, the transcription factor GATA2 was identified as a common regulator of six key genes—CALM1, VAMP2, SYT1, MET, TJP1, and NOTCH1—suggesting its potential significance in both the pathogenesis and treatment of AD [86]. Moreover, 14 medications that target the key AD-PRGs were sourced from the DrugBank database for this investigation. Among these, seven of these candidates are FDA-approved for other indications (trifluoperazine, halofantrine, capmatinib, cabozantinib, tepotinib, crizotinib, brigatinib, and botulinum toxin type B), providing a compelling foundation for drug repurposing. For instance, trifluoperazine, a calmodulin inhibitor, may counteract calcium signaling dyshomeostasis in AD [87], while botulinum toxin type B represents a novel approach to modulate synaptic vesicle release in neurodegeneration [88]. These therapeutic medicines were discovered to have favorable binding interactions with and strong docking efficiency with the key AD-PRGs through molecular docking. Additionally, investigational agents like the Notch inhibitor LY3039478 were selected based on high binding affinity and pathway relevance, offering mechanistically grounded hypotheses for future testing [89]. Nevertheless, additional validation and research are required to properly clarify the underlying mechanisms and verify their therapeutic potential.

  Our study identified seven key palmitoylation-related genes that could potentially predict and serve as therapeutic targets for AD through bioinformatic analysis of sufficiently large cohorts. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The reliance on publicly available transcriptomic datasets from GEO may introduce technical artifacts such as batch effects or unaccounted confounders. Additionally, the scarcity of detailed clinical metadata in these databases may limit the applicability to broader AD populations. Lastly, while our analyses imply functional roles related to synaptic function, transcription factors, and miRNA interactions, the specific mechanistic contributions of these genes—particularly the functional consequences of their highly conserved palmitoylation—remain a computational inference. Future experimental studies using site-directed mutagenesis and acyl-biotin exchange assays are needed to definitively establish the causal link between their palmitoylation status and AD phenotypes.


## Conclusions
  In conclusion, our study has deepened the understanding of palmitoylation-related molecular mechanisms in AD. We have identified and validated a series of key genes—CALM1, VAMP2, SYT1, MET, BAG3, TJP1, and NOTCH1—that may serve as promising diagnostic markers and therapeutic targets for AD. Furthermore, our findings underscore the importance of considering synaptic function in the context of AD pathogenesis. The involvement of key AD-PRGs such as VAMP2 and SYT1, which are crucial for synaptic vesicle recycling and neurotransmitter release, respectively, highlights the potential synaptic dysfunction in AD. By elucidating the regulatory interplay among these genes, TFs, and miRNAs, our study paves the way for future research to explore the synaptic basis of AD and identify novel therapeutic strategies targeting these pathways. Despite these promising findings, it is crucial to note that further experimental validation is required to fully understand the roles of these genes in AD and their potential as therapeutic targets. Overall, our study contributes to the growing body of knowledge on the molecular mechanisms underlying AD and offers new insights into the potential diagnostic markers and therapeutic targets for this devastating disease.
