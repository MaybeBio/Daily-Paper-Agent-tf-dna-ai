## Introduction
  Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most common type of lung cancer, despite a decreasing mortality trend was observed due to improved medical care, it remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1,2]. Despite the increased understanding of genetic and epigenetic events occurring during tumorigenesis and tumor progression, the molecular driving force remains elusive. Technological development in single-cell sequencing enabled recent research to globally characterize the cellular complexity of solid lung tumors [[3], [4], [5]]. The intertumoral heterogeneity and diversity of cancer cell states challenge the effectiveness of current cancer therapies [6].

  A small subpopulation of malignant cells possessing stem cell properties, contributing to tumor initiation, therapeutic resistance, and metastatic dissemination, have been identified in many tumor types and termed as “cancer stem cells” (CSCs) [7]. Therapies focused on targeted eradication of CSCs are warranted due to their potential to increase treatment efficacy and decrease cancer relapse. However, the identification and lineage hierarchy of CSCs in lung adenocarcinomas is far from well elucidated.

  Controversy is still open about the choice of appropriate markers to isolate CSCs. Cluster of differentiation-133 (CD133, prominin-1, PROM-1) was initially and widely used as a marker specific for human CSCs, including lung adenocarcinoma stem cells [8]. Aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH) represents intracellular isoenzymes that convert activated cyclophosphamide to inactive excretory carboxyphosphamide and might produce NADH as an energy source for ATP production, which has been used for selecting stem-like tumor cells in NSCLC cell lines [9,10]. ALDH activity is mainly associated with ALDH1A1 expression [11]. Previous literature has reported that increased expression of CD133 and ALDH1A1 is associated with detoxification, proliferation, and drug resistance, as well as poor survival in NSCLC patients, [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16]] whereas the relationship was not observed in other research [17,18]. Intriguingly, controversy exists among different researchers in finding the difference in tumorigenic and clonogenic activity between tumor cells with positive and negative stem cell markers [[19], [20], [21], [22], [23]].

  Besides, emerging evidence since then has characterized that different tumor cell subpopulations showed temporal heterogeneity within a tumor, which has correlated with tumor progression, dissemination, and immune infiltration, including LUAD [24,25]. However, the contribution of CSCs to tumor progression has been controversial. Current molecular profiles could not fully capture the variety of changes in LUAD CSC properties fostering ensemble effects in gross tumorigenic behaviors. Technologies that profile tissues at single-cell RNA (scRNA-seq) resolution have allowed the interrogation of global gene expression among individual cells in a single tumor region [26,27]. The unbiased characterization of individual LUAD stem cells might provide a fundamental understanding of the lineage hierarchy and indicate the most potential oncogenic subtypes.

  In this study, with profiles of individual human LUAD cells through scRNA-seq, we defined the stem-like cell population and investigated the trajectory of heterogeneous transcriptional profiles within the CSC population. We identified novel markers with progenitor and oncogenic properties during lung adenocarcinoma progression, leading to further tumor heterogeneity. We stratified tumor cells into three transcriptional subtypes based on stemness signature and constructed a risk model, which has implications for both prognosis and therapeutic assignment.


## Materials and methods

### Clinical specimens
  Considering the complex spatial distribution of heterogenous CSCs and their interactions remaining unresolved, [28,29] we resected tissues of both tumor interior and tumor edge to reduce the bias. Four LUAD patients who underwent surgery with informed consent were selected, and paired samples were obtained immediately after resection for scRNA-seq analysis. Clinical aspects of this study and tissue acquisition procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Hospital Fudan University.


### Tumor dissociation and single-cell sample preparation
  Dissected tissues were wiped off necrotic areas and cut into small pieces. Tumor dissociation was disposed with the Tumor Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Tissue pieces were transferred to a gentle Macs C Tube in digestion buffer and mechanically dissociated three times with the gentle MACS™ Dissociator (30 min incubation at 37 °C at each dissociation step interval). Tumor-derived cell suspensions were passed through a 40 μm filter and centrifuged at 300 × g for 7 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was removed completely and tumor cells were resuspended in RPMI. Erythrocytes were removed using Red Blood Cell Lysis Solution (10X) (Sigma-Aldrich), and 1X lysis solution was added to the cell suspension and incubated away from light for 15 min. A Dead Cell Removal Kit (Miltenyi Biotec) was used to remove dead cells. Tumor cells were assessed using Trypan Blue to ensure cell viability over 90 %.


### Flow cytometry and sorting
  We used an ALDEFLUOR KIT (StemCell Technologies, Vancouver, BC, Canada) to isolate tumor cells with high ALDH enzymatic activity [30,31]. Briefly, cells were suspended in an Aldefluor assay buffer containing a non-toxic fluorescent ALDH substrate (BAAA-DA, 1 μmol/l per 1 × 106 cells), which could be degraded by ALDH into fluorescent BODIPY-aminoacetate and remains inside cells, then incubated at 37 °C for 45 min. Negative control was treated with DEAB, a specific ALDH1 inhibitor used to control background fluorescence and set ALDEFLUOR-positive FACS gate. The gate was defined as less than 0.1 % of DEAB-treated cells. Cells above the threshold were sorted as ALDH1 positive populations, and fluorescence intensity is proportional to ALDH activity. Immediately after ALDEFLOUR staining, CD133 (APC, #566,596, BD Pharmingen) and CD45 (PerCP-Cy5.5, #564,105, BD Pharmingen) antibodies staining were performed on ice for CD133 and CD45 identification. Single stained controls of CD133 and CD45 were made with separate aliquots of cells. Cell debris and doublets were discriminated to differentiate between viable and dead cells before sorting. Dead cells were eliminated by staining with 7-aminoactinomycin D (7-AAD). Flow cytometry analysis was conducted with a FACS Aria IIIu Flow Cytometry (BD Bioscience, USA). Gating was used for sorting out CD45-(CD133+ or ALDH1+) population. Tumor cells were assessed again using Trypan Blue to ensure cell viability over 90 %.


### scRNA-seq data processing
  Single-cell RNA-seq libraries were prepared with Chromium single-cell 3′ Reagent v3 Kits. The concentration of single-cell suspension was adjusted to 1000 cells/μl and loaded on the Chromium Single Cell Controller Instrument (10 × Genomics) to generate single-cell gel beads in emulsions (GEMs). About 10,000 total cells were added to each channel with an average recovery of 8000 cells. The remaining procedures were performed according to the standard manufacturer’s protocol [4].

  The sequencing data were processed with the Cell Ranger software pipeline (version 4.0.2), mapping reads to genomes and transcriptomes using the STAR algorithm [32]. Unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) were required to generate normalized aggregate data across samples by the R package Seurat (version 2.3.4), producing a gene-barcode matrix [33]. Likely multiplet captures and low-quality cells were excluded (UMI/gene number out of the limit of mean value ± 2 fold of standard deviations; >10 % counts of mitochondrial genes). After applying the above QC criteria, single cells were included in downstream analyses. Finally, we generated a filtered matrix containing the normalized count. All of the sequencing data is available when asked for the first authors or the corresponding authors.


### Copy-number alterations (CNVs) analysis and identification of malignant cells
  Cells with CNV alterations were recognized as tumor cells, and vice versa. CNVs were assessed with inferCNV R package to distinguish malignant tumor cells from non-malignant cells [34]. Immune cells, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells were selected as references to eliminate the individual somatic CNV and re-calculated with scRNA-seq expression levels. The inferCNV analysis was performed with the cutoff value of 0.1 and hidden Markov model (HMM) based settings. The CNV score was figured out as a quadratic sum of the CNV region. Then, we removed potential non-malignant cells and retained malignant tumor cells. We generated a heatmap to illustrate the relative normalized CNV level across each chromosome. CNV results were combined with tSNE-based malignant classifications, and the final list of malignant tumor cells belonged to the intersection of these two sets.

  To perform clonality analysis, CSCs were run with inferCNV again to infer tumor subclones and corresponding CNVs. The “Subcluster” method was utilized on CNVs. CNVs were converted to simplified p- or q-arm level change (gain or loss) according to their location with the genomic GRCh38 cytoband information. Subclones containing identical arm level CNVs were collapsed and clonality trees were restructured to represent subclonal CNV architecture. Intra-tumor evolutionary trees were automatically generated by UPhyloplot2 algorithm (https://github.com/harbourlab/UPhyloplot2) [35]. The arm-level CNV calls and the percentage of cells in each subclone were visualized. The arm length was proportional to the percentage of cells plus a spacer (circle diameter + 5 pixels).


### Recognition of stem cells
  A predictive model using one-class logistic regression (OLCR) on the pluripotent stem cell samples (ESC and iPSC) from the PCBC dataset was built to calculate a stemness index based on mRNA expression. A CSC signature was obtained and applied to score each malignant tumor cell in our study, with Spearman correlations calculated between the model’s weight vector and the cell sample’s expression profile. Leave-one-out cross-validation by withholding each CSC sample in turn and external dataset validation were applied. This method was previously described in Malta, T. M et al’s work [36]. Stem or non-stem malignant cells were defined based on stemness indexes, which ranged from low (0) to high (1) stemness.

  We generated classical marker genes, MYC EZH2 HOXA9 E2F3 MTOR CSR GCNP PRC2 PRS14 E2F1 CAHOY NFE2L2 PKCA EIF4E ATF, of stem cells based on previous literature and calculated the stem/non-stem index.


### tSNE visualization
  The average expression and dispersion were calculated for each gene, and highly variable genes across single cells were identified. Significant principal components (PCs) were determined using JackStraw. Combined with cluster annotations, cells were visualized in 2-dimension using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (tSNE) with selected PCs. The likelihood ratio test was used to perform differential gene expression analysis, which could detect changes in mean expression and in the percentage of expressed cells simultaneously. A novel computational method R package, SingleR, was applied to infer the cell origin and types independently and then checked manually [37,38]. Based on the presence of known marker genes the clusters were divided into epithelial cells (EPCAM, KRT8, and KRT18), fibroblasts (COL1A1, DCN, COL1A2, and C1R), endothelial cells (CD31+), myeloid cells (LYZ, MARCO, CD68, and FCGR3A). The stemness gene signature was referred as follows: ALDH1A1, CD44, PROM1, NANOG, KIT, NES, KLF4, CD55, AL- CAM, NOTCH4, WNT7A, PDPN.


### Identification of marker genes and enrichment analysis
  Cluster-specific marker genes were analyzed with normalized expression values by running the FindAllMarkers and FindMarkers functions in the Seurat package under default parameters. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were selected with FDR < 0.05 and log fold change of 1 or more by edgeR package [39]. The calculation of pathway activity was previously described. GSEA analysis was applied to compare metabolic profiles between CSCs of different stages and identify metabolic pathways enriched in genes with the highest variability, with an adjusted p < 0.05 considered statistically significant [40,41].

  Non-parametric and unsupervised gene set variation analysis (GSVA) converted the expression matrix into a path enrichment score format, and then lmFit analysis of the limma package was used to obtain relative pathways [42].


### SENIC analysis
  The pySCENIC (version 0.10.3) algorithm was run on a normalized expression matrix of high-quality CSCs to identify active TFs across different tumor stages as described (https://github.com/aertslab/pySCENIC) [43]. Single cells were assigned an AUC score for each regulon to label its activation status. The results were visualized through a heatmap with hierarchical clustering [44]. Targeted genes of individual TFs were extracted for expressional plotting and enrichment analysis.


### Developmental pseudotime analysis
  We performed pseudotime trajectories of CSCs across tumors using Monocle 2 package (version 2.22.0) in R [45]. Highly variable genes were selected using built-in DipersionTable function to describe the variance of each gene’s expression according to the mean, with a cutoff of 0.1. The architecture of the pseudotime trajectory was plotted using the DDRTree dimensionality reduction algorithm.


### Cell culture
  Human lung adenocarcinoma cell line A549 was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) and maintained at the Thoracic Surgery Laboratory of Fudan University. All cell lines underwent genotypic authentication prior to experimental use. A549 cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM; Gibco-Life Technologies) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS; Every Green, Zhejiang, China), 100 μg/mL Penicillin-Streptomycin-Gentamicin Solution (Beyotime, China). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified 5 % CO₂ atmosphere with routine passage at 80–90 % confluence.


### Animal studies
  All animal experiments were performed in strict accordance with the approbation of the Animal Ethics Committee at Zhongshan Hospital, Shanghai, China. Sorted tumor cells were resuspended in serum-free medium and inoculated subcutaneously into the dorsal flank of 3–5-week-old female nude mice, and wounds were sterilized with 75 % alcohol. Nude mice were randomly divided into CD133+/ALDH1+ group (n = 6) and CD133-ALDH1- group (n = 6). In the preliminary experiment, 105 of CD133-ALDH1- cells transplantation was required to induce tumor in nude mice, and then the dosage of 105 was selected. After injection, the length and width of tumors were measured every week, and tumor volumes (mm3) were calculated using the equation Length x Weight2 x 0.5. The mice were sacrificed under deep anesthesia with pentobarbital before tumor resection.


### Quantitative RT-PCR
  Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and then the RNA was reverse transcribed using the PrimeScript RT Master Mix Perfect Real Time kit (TaKaRa, Dalian, China) to obtain the cDNA. Using the cDNA as the template, a real-time PCR assay was performed using designed pairs of primers. The 20 μL real-time PCR reaction included 0.5 μL of cDNA template, 0.25 μL of Primer F, 0.25 μL of Primer R, 10 μL of RNase-free dH2O, and 8 μL of 2.5 × RealMasterMix (SYBR Green I). The reaction conditions included a pre-denaturation step at 95 °C for 10 s, 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, and 60 °C for 60 s. After the reaction, the data were subjected to statistical analysis.


## Results

### scRNA-seq identified CSCs and malignant cells in LUAD
  To explore stem cell diversity in LUAD, we first depleted immune cells with pan-immune marker CD45 staining (Fig. 1A) and isolated CSCs with high ALDH1 enzymatic activity and positive CD133 expression from human resected tissues of LUAD by flow cytometry (Fig. 1B). We identified diverse expression of ALDH1 and CD133 across samples. We observed a population with a higher mean percentage of CD133 expression in patients with advanced stages, and double positive populations (CD133+/ALDH1+) were only detected in a small number of cells (Fig. 1B).   To validate if CD133+/ALDH1+ tumor cells harbor stem-like characteristics, including tumor-initiating properties, compared to CD133-ALDH1- cells, we performed qRT-PCR, cell motility assay (IBC) and CDX models. The size and weight of CD133+ or ALDH1+ cell-derived tumors were significantly larger than those of both CD133- and ALDH1- cells (Fig. 1C).

  We then used the single-cell data for further analyses. Details of samples used for scRNA-seq were shown in supplementary Table 1. After computational selection and filtering, we distinguished malignant cells from non-malignant cells by following approaches. First, we inspected marker genes from clustering analysis with previously described transcriptional signatures and identified 4 major cell populations in all samples, which were further validated and modified by SingleR and AUCell. Aside from epithelial cells, clustering displayed three small populations of cell types: fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and macrophages (Fig. 1D). Second, we inferred copy number variations (CNVs) and identified extensive amplifications and deletions within most cells. CNV is less likely to occur in normal cells as a hallmark of tumorigenic cells like progenitor or immature cells. T-SNE plots showed distributions of 14 cell types in the re-clustering of epithelial cells, with CNVs found in clusters 0, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 11, 12, and 13. We performed t-SNE visualization of malignant cells and observed significant tumor heterogeneity (Fig. 1E-F, Supplementary Fig. 1A).


### Expression profiles vary among CSCs and other subpopulations
  To delve into the specific regulation of CSCs, we performed a detailed analysis of DEGs between CSCs and other tumor cells. Transcriptome profiling revealed significant differential expression of biomarkers in CSC subpopulations, including CD133 and ALDH. Notably, S100A9 was significantly overexpressed in CSCs (Fig. 2A, 2C).   To validate stem cells' stemness in mRNA expression, we calculated the stemness indices of different subpopulations. Since cluster 1, 2, and 8 had significantly higher stemness indices than others, we defined these clusters as CSCs (Fig. 2F) in this study. Subpopulation dynamics during tumorigenesis were further assessed. We found that CSC cluster 8 was shared among all stages, while the proportion was significantly higher in advanced tumors than in early tumors. Cluster 2 was mainly found in advanced invasive LUAD tumors and showed different expression patterns (Fig. 2G). These results indicated that CSC heterogeneity existed across tumor stages and cells of cluster 8 might represent major persistent CSC subpopulation. As CSCs consisted of three distinct subpopulations, top marker genes showed that cluster 8 exhibited more stem-like features. Centromere protein CENP-F and CENP-W were remarkably overexpressed in cluster 8, which were necessary for cell division. Genes involved in cell cycle regulatory machinery (e.g. CCNB1), anaphase promoting complex regulation (e.g. CDC20) and G1/S-phase transition modulation (e.g. PTTG1, CDKN3) were highly expressed in cluster 8. Cluster 2 occurred in advanced tumors and showed different expression pattern, we found that remarkable systematic differences were linked to S100A gene family, including high expression of S100A6, S100A8, S100A9 and S100A13. We found that the expression level of S100A9 was elevated in both cluster 2 and 8 (Fig. 2H). To functionally characterize differences among these subpopulations, pathway analyses were performed (Fig. 2I). This confirmed our observation as terms related to positive regulation of cell division, cell cycle and APC/C—CDC20 pathway were significantly enriched in cluster 2&8. Moreover, the related genes were enriched in the NF-κB signaling pathway.

### Gene regulatory networks underline lineage specification
  To elucidate transcriptional states and the underlying molecular mechanisms regulating the differentiation of LUAD CSCs, we performed a single-cell regulatory network inference and clustering (SCENIC) analysis. We identified a set of transcriptional factors (TFs) with high activity varying across different tumor stages, many of which were associated with stemness in various cancers. In addition, gene sets targeted by individual TFs were estimated (Fig. 4A) . We noticed that MYC was overexpressed in early-stage CSCs while moderately expressed in advanced tumors, suggesting its multiple roles in tumor development. Targeted genes of MYC were enriched in TAP63 and Notch signaling pathways (Fig. 4B). XBP1 was upregulated in advanced tumors and reported to regulate viability, apoptosis, and stemness among cancer stem-like cells. Targeted genes of XBP1 were upregulated in transportation along the microtubule, negative regulation of cell differentiation, and regulation of protein stability (Fig. 4C). As a well-known TF regulating cell differentiation, the expression of GATA6 in CSCs decreased with tumor stage. GATA6 was reported to exert a tumor-suppressive effect by promoting cell differentiation and suppressing inflammatory and cancer-related pathways in pancreatic cancer. ### Metabolic characteristics of CSCs
  To further investigate the metabolic landscape of CSCs and the potential metabolism-based treatment strategy, we compared metabolic activities across tumor stages which allow a more in-depth analysis of regulatory pathways. Early stage tumor CSCs exhibited lowest metabolic pathway activity, while most metabolic pathways were upregulated in CSCs of higher tumor stage. CSCs in advanced LUADs were found to have higher activity of both glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation. Marker genes of glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation among different stages were listed (Fig. 4D-4E). Several pathways like glutathione metabolism and lactylation were unregulated in tumors with higher stage (Fig. 4F).


### CSCs promote LUAD progression through S100A9-XBP1-pNF-κB-CXCL13 axis
  Further, we explored the potential mechanisms underlying the increased LUAD stemness by CSCs. Cell motility assay confirmed that co-culture with CSC cells promoted the invasion and migration ability of LUAD cells (Fig. 5A-5B). Considering the critical role of S100A9 in promoting LUAD progression in previous studies, we knocked down S100A9 in CSC cells and sequenced the knockdown S100A9 and control cells to look for downstream molecules that may be regulated by S100A9. The results showed that in addition to the previously mentioned S100A9 and XBP1, the chemokine CXCL13 was also highly expressed in control CSC cells (Fig. 5C). CXCL13 promotes the release of inflammatory factors by binding to its receptor CXCR5, which is aberrantly expressed in a variety of malignant tumors and is closely associated with tumor proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and treatment. Enrichment analysis showed that the NF-κB pathway was the most significantly altered before and after the S100A9 knockdown (Fig. 5D). Subsequently, we knocked down or overexpressed S100A9 in CSC cells. We found that knockdown of S100A9 resulted in decreased expression of XBP1 and CXCL13 expressed by CSC cells, and similarly decreased activation levels of pNF-κB. While overexpression of S100A9 produced the opposite changes (Fig. 5E). Whereas after knocking down of XBP1, there was no significant change in S100A9 expression, CXCL3 expression and pNF-κB activation significantly decreased (Fig. 5F). In contrast, after the use of NF-kB inhibitors, there was no significant change in the expression of S100A9, the XBP1 expression was slightly increased, and the expression of CXCL13 was significantly reduced (Fig. 5G). Therefore, we suggested that S100A9 within CSCs activates XBP1 and pNF-kB, which subsequently further promotes the secretion of CXCL13. ### Multi-genomic analysis of different molecular subtypes in TCGA
  CSC lineage transition might contribute to intratumor heterogeneity within the tumor cell population and be related to LUAD prognosis. We took the intersection of CSC marker genes and DEGs between different states in lineage analyses and identified a total of 730 genes. The genes with significant clinical prognostic value in TCGA database were screened and utilized for molecular typing. Subsequently, we included 181 marker genes with significant expression and prognostic correlation. According to the cophenetic coefficient plot, NMF consensus analysis provides solid statistical support for three clusters (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Further, various dimension decreasing and consensus clustering methods like PCA and tSNE plot identified the optimal number of clustering (Supplementary Fig. 2B, 2C). The heatmap plot illustrated the basic expression pattern among the three clusters. Survival analysis showed a significant difference in the clinical outcome of the three clusters (Supplementary Fig. 2D), with a favorable prognosis for C1 and a poor prognosis for C2 and C3.

  The different molecular clusters have different clinical features. Patients with poor prognosis in the C3 subtype had higher T and M stages, and the C1 and C3 subtypes differed significantly in terms of T stage, pathological stage, gender, and smoking group. Over half of the C3 subtype patients are male, in contrast to C1 and C2. The age among the three subtypes did not show a statistical difference. The proportion of patients in later stages (III and IV) showed a significant upward trend among C1, C2, and C3 subtypes, consistent with their prognosis risk (Fig. 6A).Fig. 6Genomic differential analysis of different molecular subtypes C1, C2, C3; (A) Heatmaps showing differential signaling pathways between molecular subtypes; (B-F) Violin plots showing differences in tumor malignancy between molecular subtypes; (G-I) Comparison of differential genes between C1 and C2, C2 and C3, as well as C1 and C3, respectively; (J) Violin plots comparing stromal scores, immune scores, ESTIMATE scores, and tumor purity scores between molecular subtypes; (K) Box plots showing immune cell differences between subtypes.Fig 6

  We further explored the genomic heterogeneity among these three molecular subtypes. The C3 subtype shows a significantly higher genomic alteration in gene mutation level and copy number level, as Fig. 6B - 6F shows the boxplot of aneuploidy score, fraction alteration, numbers of segments, homologous recombination defect, and mutation load scores.

  The mechanism embedded in the different molecular subtypes might be investigated by analyzing distinct activated pathways. We identified DEGs between different subtypes (Fig. 6G-I) and performed pathway enrichment analysis to determine whether different signaling pathways were distributed in patients with these subtypes. Ten pathways, like the cell cycle and DNA repair process, were more significantly enriched in the C1 subtype, while the C3 subtype is enriched in the lipid metabolism pathways. In addition, the immune landscape and immune cell composition characterization in different molecular subtypes also demonstrated significant differences. To further elucidate the diversity in the immune microenvironment of different subtypes, we evaluated the immune cell infiltration degree in these three identified molecular subtypes. We also utilized the ESTIMATE algorithm to access the general immune infiltration score. Notably, we found that the ImmuneScore of three subtypes decreased in the order of C1, C2, and C3, while the Tumorputity increased gradually (Fig. 6J). Finally, we inspected the expression of genes involved in immune checkpoint pathways (Fig. 6K), which showed that almost all of the immune molecules in the C3 subtypes were significantly lower than those in the C1 and C2 subtypes. These results suggest that immune landscape characterization provided complementary value to the above-identified molecular subtypes and a new insight into the immune landscape of LUAD.


### Risk model based on sc-rick-score and its application
  Since the above-identified molecular subtypes showed differences in multi-omics background, we shrink the aforementioned scRNA-seq marker genes by a significant correlation between expression and prognostic survival and the LASSO—COX algorithm. Setting the TCGA cohort as our training model, we conducted the risk model based on the LASSO—COX procedure when the lambda was set as 0.0657 for minimal deviance (Fig. 7A, 7B). Hence, we obtained the nine-gene risk model. The sc-risk-score model formula was as follows: sc-risk-score = 0.165*LDHA + 0.185*ADM + 0.124*HTATIP2 + 0.037*RHOV + 0.205*SEC61G + 0.038*KRT18 + 0.199*DNAJB4 - 0.236*CXCR4 - 0.207*MYLIP. The sc-risk-score analysis was evaluated by dividing the training cohort into high- and low-sc-risk-score groups. As shown in Fig. 7C, the KM plot showed that the higher group had a significantly poor prognosis. The AUC of the time-dependent ROC curve is 0.71, 0.7, and 0.7 at 1-, 3-, and 5-years overall survival (Fig. 7D). Next, we selected the GSE31210 cohort as the validating dataset, which emphasized the robustness of our model due to concordant results in validating dataset. Similarly, the KM plot showed that the higher group had a significantly poor prognosis (Fig. 7E, 7F). ## Discussion
  With the high recurrence and metastasis rate of lung adenocarcinoma, the specific mechanism of tumor progression needs to be explored, and novel therapeutic paradigms are warranted [46]. In this study, we investigated the cellular phenotypes of purified CSCs in human lung adenocarcinoma samples and identified a subpopulation with high stem-like properties. We defined the relationship between CSCs and progressive processes by certain stages and illustrated the transcriptomic heterogeneity of CSCs at the single-cell level in LUAD. Strikingly, our analysis also revealed three transcriptional subtypes and constructed a wonderful prognostic risk model based on CSC gene signatures.

  In the past decades, research on lung CSCs has focused on identifying and ablating CSC markers. Although an extensive list of lung adenocarcinoma CSC markers has been identified, it is unclear whether these markers identify the overlapping, stochastic, or hierarchical subpopulations of tumor cells, and several markers have been reported not specific to CSCs. Isolation of CD133 or ALDH1 populations has been shown in previous literature to be a bona fide method to obtain CSCs, which were used to screen out stem-like cells in this study. According to previous studies, CD133 was present in a small number of lung adenocarcinomas, usually limited to < 1 % of cells. The CD133 positive NSCLC cells exhibited significantly increased clonogenic, tumorigenic, and drug resistance abilities compared to CD133 negative NSCLC cells [47,48]. However, inconsistent primary CD133 antibody clones and subsequent poorly performing might lead to conflicting results and even CD133 negative expression in tumor samples [11,49]. In this study, ALDH+ cells generally comprise 2 %−30 % of the total EpCam+ NSCLC cells as reported before [19]. Although ALDH isoforms are widely expressed in epithelial cancer cell lines, [10] CSC activity mostly depends on ALDH1A1 expression [19]. One meta-analysis by Dong W et al. reported that ALDH1 expression was correlated with a decrease in disease-free and overall survival in 1926 lung cancer patients [50]. Researchers have realized that CSCs are not a homogeneous cell subpopulation [51]. Enrichment of CSCs by one marker only isolates a fraction of stem-like cells among the bulk population. Besides, one subpopulation of CSCs might express several CSC markers. Sullivan JP et al. found that CD133 and ALDH1A1 identified different NSCLC subpopulations by IHC expression [19]. At the single-cell level, a comparison of stemness genes and pathways revealed significantly higher expression in the CD133 and ALDH double-positive group compared to the single-positive group. This observation indicated that CD133, combined with other markers of stemness like ALDH1A1, might be more reliable in identifying CSCs. Work by Alamgeer M et al. also showed that the coexpression of CD133 and ALDH1A1 selects the worst prognostic group from stage I NSCLC patients [52].

  We defined different stages of lung adenocarcinoma in vivo and identified a persistent and growing CSC subpopulation through scRNA-seq. LUAD has a molecular complexity with diverse genomic aberrations, while it is not yet clear whether any sequential genetic changes of CSCs could be observed in the tumor evolution cascade. The plasticity between multiple CSC and non-CSC subpopulations has been demonstrated in LUADs [53]. Accumulating evidence indicates that CSCs are in a dynamic and plastic state, even in different stages of the tumor. Intriguingly, Li L et al. reported that CD133+ lung CSCs are highly enriched in suspensive tumors compared with progressive tumors [54]. Li H et al. found that overexpression of CD133 in lung tumors was markedly associated with early stage and poor differentiation grade [47]. In Masciale V et al.’s work, despite a similar median percentage of CSCs in early and locally advanced stages, a positive correlation between CSC frequency and risk of relapse was observed only in patients with locally advanced stages [55]. Our data allowed us to put stage-specific CSC populations on a robust trajectory and observe a high stemness subpopulation that persistently existed with an increasing proportion along the progression. NF-κB pathway was critical for this subpopulation. We found that these persistent CSCs highly expressed stemness-related genes and drug-resistant genes. Besides, more aggressive behaviors and higher heterogeneity of CSCs were shown in advanced tumors. These clues revealed that CSCs are prone to remain in a state of quiescence in early-stage tumors, which underlined the critical role of CSCs in initiating lung adenocarcinomas. Increasing heterogeneity of CSCs paves the way for malignant proliferation. This was accompanied by previous findings that CSCs tend to transform their self-renewal mechanism from symmetric cell division to asymmetric cell division with tumor progression, [56] which might reduce the CSC pool and tumor stemness. Meanwhile, a small CSC subpopulation constantly possesses high stem-like characteristics, which might be attributed to lineage priming.

  The existence of multiple CSC subpopulations and the high plasticity of lung CSCs are challenges for the development of effective targeted therapies. Numerous intrinsic regulators are found to effectively promote tumor-initiating potential in lung adenocarcinoma [57]. This study is the first to identify a persistent and increasingly expressed lung CSC subpopulation with tumor progression. We found that a higher degree of CSC flexibility was noticed throughout development, with novel CSC subpopulations occurring to confer more malignant stem behavior. We found that increased expression of the S100 gene family corresponds with a higher stage of tumor progression. Simeonov, K. P. et al. have revealed that the expression of S100 genes is propagated across distinct metastatic subpopulations through single-cell lineage tracing [58]. Cancer cells could produce S100A proteins with pro-angiogenic activity. S100As are also considered autocrine, paracrine, and even circulatory long-distance signaling molecules. 1q21.3-encoded S100A7/8/9 were involved in a reciprocal feedback loop driving tumor growth [59]. We hypothesized that CSCs might function through S100A9-pNF-κB axis. Multiple lines of evidence support our findings. S100A8-S100A9 complex contributes to the immune suppression of tumor microenvironments and the formation of pre-metastatic niches [60]. S100A9 could also be recruited to numerous promoters and enhancers as a transcriptional coactivator, leading to breast cancer transformation [61]. Other pathways were also involved along the progression trajectory, including EMT, Wnt, and OXPHOS.

  In addition, the prognostic role of CSC marker genes was validated using the data from the TCGA database. We divided LUAD patients in TCGA into three subtypes by CSC marker genes, and significant prognostic differences were observed among patients of different subtypes. As the type with a poorer prognosis, the C3 subtype shows higher mutation frequencies of KEAP1 and copy number levels. The results indicated that the diverse transcriptome of each single cell could reflect its tumor biology. We could predict the response to immunotherapy or targeted therapies by stratifying patients into groups based on identified gene signatures [62].


## Limitations
  Only malignant cells were included in the analysis, while cell state transition and stemness properties might be regulated under microenvironment stimuli. Investigations are required to elucidate the complex interactions between CSC regulation and microenvironment cues. Secondly, we acknowledged that using only CD133 and ALDH1 to isolate CSCs could not provide the best precision for purifying purposes. Previous studies have reported that CSC marker-negative tumor cells could either proliferate into a status of mixed CSCs, indicating the function of other CSC markers, false negatives in marker-negative cells, or transition from marker-negative to positive cells. However, previous literature and this work both showed reliable and representative values of these two markers. Besides, considering that transcription of CSC marker genes is sometimes not synonymous with the expression of proteins, more multi-omics investigations should be warranted to offer a comprehensive understanding of CSC regulation. Studies incorporating more LUAD samples are needed to further elucidate clinical clues of this concept.


## Conclusion
  In summary, this study revealed the molecular composition and plasticity of CSCs in LUAD, which underlined the mechanisms of LUAD evolution. Our work provides a blueprint to molecularly identify and extensively characterize CSC-like cells other than merely relying on pre-defined surface markers. Furthermore, our findings provided essential insights into the molecular mechanism and clinical setting of CSCs and allowed targeted therapeutic intervention by regulating CSCs to suppress tumor progression.
