# In silico functional, structural, and pathogenicity assessment of single nucleotide polymorphisms in the human SOX9 gene


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-30462-y.


## Introduction
  SNPs are the most prevalent and robust type of genetic variation associated with disease development, which helps guide the identification of the fundamental processes underlying disease progression. Approximately 1% of the most prevalent genetic variations in the human genome have been identified in human SNPs1. SNPs introduce genomic changes by altering intergenic regions, coding sequences known as exons, and non-coding sequences referred to as introns2. Coding-region SNPs fall into two categories: non-synonymous SNPs (nsSNPs), which modify the sequence of amino acids in the encoded protein, and SNPs that are synonymous, which do not change the encoded protein sequence3. The encoded amino acid sequence is unaffected by synonymous mutations, which result from the degenerate nature of the genetic code. These synonymous SNPs are considered functionally silent and contribute little to evolutionary dynamics, even though they may affect RNA splicing4. Missense SNPs, a significant class of nsSNPs, predominantly impact protein conformation, thermodynamic stability, and biological activity5. Due to the non-coding nature of intronic regions, their SNPs have a negligible impact on disease processes. Nonetheless, introns, which comprise almost half of the non-coding genome, play a crucial role in genome evolution6.

  In humans, the transcription factors are the crucial mediators for disease pathogenesis. Within these, the SOX gene family plays a significant role in regulating cell fate, differentiation, and tumour development7. The SRY is vital for testis development by binding to a testis-specific enhancer of SOX9 and activating its expression during embryogenesis8. Our target gene, SOX9, is located on human chromosome 17 and DNA-binding HMG box domain PF005059. Mutations in the DNA-binding HMG box domain PF00505 are significantly involved in disease progression10. The SOX9 is essential for cartilage growth and regeneration; gene therapy employing SOX9 and chondrogenic SOX Trio may increase the effectiveness of cartilage repair10. In addition to its importance in liver development, homeostasis, and the treatment of disorders, SOX9 may also serve as a biomarker for the prognosis and progression of tumours11. Since SOX9 is essential for the growth and differentiation of pancreatic cancer cells, its knockdown inhibits the cell cycle and apoptosis processes while activating signalling pathways12. Depending on the type and setting of the cancer, SOX9 can function as both an oncogene and a tumour suppressor, making it a crucial and complex player in cancer. SOX9 is often overexpressed in malignancies such as breast13, pancreas12, liver14, lung15, gastric16, and oesophageal cancers17, which accelerate tumour growth by improving cell proliferation, stemness, metastasis, epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and chemotherapy resistance. However, SOX9 plays a significant role in the development of neuroglioblastoma and brain metastasis, and its involvement in multiple cancers makes it a key target for our study.

  Missense SNPs, a functional class of widespread genetic variants, modulate disease risk by altering gene function and serve as pathogenic biomarkers18. Highly conserved missense SNPs with protein-destabilizing effects serve as promising disease biomarkers, where single amino acid changes alter structure–function and contribute to pathogenesis19. Non-synonymous mutations in SOX9 could compromise its functional interactions through structural perturbations at critical binding sites. Despite its importance, the functional consequences and pathogenic mechanisms of naturally occurring SOX9 missense SNPs in human diseases, particularly cancer, remain unexplored. Therefore, this study aimed to systematically identify and characterize deleterious missense SNPs in SOX9 using a comprehensive in silico approaches20. We integrated multiple pathogenicity prediction tools, stability analysis algorithms, and structural modelling approaches to evaluate their potential to disrupt SOX9 conformation and function. Furthermore, MD simulation was performed to assess the structural effects of the highest-impact variants21. Using R programming, PCA, DCCM, and FEL analyses were performed from the MD simulation output, providing insight into how SNP-driven SOX9 dysfunction may contribute to cancer development and offering a framework for future experimental validation and therapeutic targeting20. Our research is based on multiple computational predictions, and experimental validation in cellular and animal models is needed to explore the effects of the identified SNPs. Further research should explore other mutations and integrate techniques like CRISPR-Cas9 and proteomics for deeper insights into SOX9-related cancer mechanisms and therapeutic targeting.

### Protein sequence and missense SNPs retrieval
  The protein sequence of the SOX9 gene (in FASTA format) and its associated SNPs were obtained from the NCBI dbSNP repository (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/snp/) and the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) database, respectively22. Because missense SNPs cause amino acid alterations that can result in protein variations, they have become the focus of attention. Significant structural changes could result from such modifications, potentially lowering binding affinity and impacting protein function.


### Functional annotation of SNPs
  To evaluate the potential functional impact of missense SNPs in the SOX9 gene, we employed multiple sequence-based pathogenicity prediction tools, including SIFT, PolyPhen-2, SNAP, PhD-SNP, SNPs&GO, and MutPred2. Subsequently, I-Mutant was used to assess the effects of these variants on protein stability.


### Deleterious missense SNPs prediction using SIFT
  A web-based bioinformatics tool called Sorting Intolerant From Tolerant (SIFT) (https://sift.bii.a-star.edu.sg/) is used to separate harmful missense SNPs from those that are tolerated23. SIFT performs homology-based sequence analysis by aligning homologous protein sequences and computing the normalized probability for every potential amino acid substitution. Missense SNPs are categorised as harmful in SIFT predictions if their score is less than or equal to 0.05, and as tolerated if their score is larger than 0.05.


### Damaging missense SNPs prediction using PolyPhen-2
  Polymorphism Phenotyping v2 (PolyPhen-2) (http://genetics.bwh.harvard.edu/pph2/) is a web-based tool used to assess the potential impact of missense SNPs on protein structure and function24. Several features are included in the analysis, including phylogenetic relationships, structural factors, and sequence conservation. Based on their expected impact, PolyPhen-2 divides SNPs into three groups: potentially harmful (score: 0.45–0.95), probably harmful (score: 0.95–1.00), and benign (score: 0.00–0.45). The PolyPhen-2 server was used to analyse the functional implications of mutations, which were assessed using the proteins FASTA sequence, the affected amino acid position, and the introduced residue.


### Functional effect prediction of missense SNPs using SNAP
  We applied the neural network-driven computational tool SNAP (Screening for Non-Acceptable Polymorphisms) (http://www.rostlab.org/services/SNAP), which is designed to predict the functional consequences of individual amino acid substitutions in protein sequences25. By comparing the solvent accessibility of the native and mutant proteins and examining changes in secondary structure, SNAP assesses the possible impacts of missense SNPs. Every variation is categorised as neutral or having an effect. The SNAP server received the native SOX9 proteins FASTA sequence as input for this analysis. In SNAP analysis, SNAP score > 0 indicates a predicted functional effect (deleterious) and scores ≤ 0 indicate a neutral (N) effect.


### Disease association prediction of missense SNPs

#### PhD-SNP
  The study incorporated the PhD-SNP web-server (http://snps.biofold.org/phd-snp/phd-snp.html), a bioinformatics resource for predicting the damaging effects of human SNP mutations that classify single-nucleotide variants as either disease-related or neutral26. It utilises an algorithm based on Support Vector Machines (SVMs), which have been specifically trained to distinguish between harmful and benign alterations. To predict the biological impact of each mutation, the target proteins FASTA sequence was used, and the corresponding amino acid change was uploaded to the PhD-SNP server. Variants with scores > 0.5 were considered deletorious (D), while those with scores ≤ 0.5 were considered neutral (N).


#### SNPs&GO
  The SNPs&GO server (http://snps-and-go.biocomp.unibo.it/snps-and-go/) predicts whether a missense mutation is neutral or linked to a disease27. To increase prediction accuracy, it combines data from evolutionary studies, protein sequences, and functional annotation. Every variation is given a confidence value, with a score > 0.5 indicating a likely effect linked to the disease-associated (Dis) and a score < 0.5 indicating a neutral (N) impact. To determine each SNPs potential for pathogenicity, SNPs&GO was fed the FASTA sequence and the particular amino acid substitution.


### Conservation analysis
  The ConSurf web server (http://consurf.tau.ac.il/) was used to identify evolutionarily conserved residues within the query protein28. ConSurf determines conservation scores for every amino acid position and builds a phylogenetic tree using homologous sequences. The protein structure or function often depends on highly conserved residues, and mutations at these locations can have detrimental consequences. This investigation revealed the functional significance of locations impacted by missense SNPs. In conservation analysis, residues scoring ≥ 7 were considered as highly conserved, while those scoring ≤ 3 were considered variable and less critical.


### Stability and flexibility prediction of missense SNPs on SOX9 protein

#### I‑Mutant2.0
  The I-Mutant2.0 web server (https://folding.biofold.org/cgi-bin/i-mutant2.0.cgi) was used to evaluate the free energy change (ΔΔG) and measure the impact of missense mutations on protein folding stability29. This method, which utilizes SVM methodology, predicts the influence of single residue replacements on protein stability parameters. When ΔΔG is more than 0 kcal/mol, stability is increased; when ΔΔG is less than 0 kcal/mol, stability is decreased. The investigation sheds light on whether particular mutations could cause the structure of the SOX9 protein to become unstable.


#### MUpro
  The MUpro server (http://mupro.proteomics.ics.uci.edu/) was used to predict the effect of missense mutations on protein stability30. MUpro determines how residue alterations affect the structural stability of proteins using neural network-based techniques as well as Support Vector Machine (SVM) algorithms. A lower stability is indicated by a confidence score less than zero, whilst a higher stability is suggested by a number greater than zero. The structural alterations caused by mutations are further validated by this dual-approach prediction.


### Protein three-dimensional modelling and Ramachandran plots
  The three-dimensional (3D) structures of the wild-type (WT) SOX9 protein and its mutant forms were predicted using the Protein Homology/Analogy Recognition Engine v2.0 (Phyre2)31 online server (http://www.sbg.bio.ic.ac.uk/phyre2) with default parameters. For homology-based modelling, the FASTA sequences of the WT and mutant proteins were utilized to Phyre232. PyMOL software was then used to visualise and analyse the resultant structural models, enabling a structural comparison of mutation-induced conformational changes. The VADAR is a freely accessible web-based platform that facilitates the quantitative evaluation of protein structural quality with default parameters33. It enables the assessment of various structural parameters at both the individual residue and whole-protein levels by utilizing PDB-formatted protein and mutant files as input. In the present analysis, the accessible surface area was calculated and subsequently compared between the native and mutant protein models.


#### Prediction of harmful mutations using MutPred2
  MutPred234 (http://mutpred2.mutdb.org/) is a web server that predicts the molecular processes underlying amino acid alterations associated with disease. MutPred2 analyses fourteen distinct structural and functional features to produce a general probability score indicating the likelihood that a variant will cause disease. The top five changed qualities are also provided, along with a p-value (p), which represents the statistical confidence that particular structural or functional features are affected. The pathogenic potential and molecular basis of missense SNPs are clarified with the use of this technology. Missense with MutPred2 score ≥ 0.5 were considered potentially disease-causing, and those with scores < 0.5 as likely benign.


#### Visualization of selected mutations using the mutation3D server
  The Mutation3D server (http://mutation3d.org/) is a technique for functional prediction and visualization that is used to examine how amino acid substitutions (AAS) are distributed spatially within protein structures35. Using a 3D clustering technique, it identifies groups of mutations, which helps locate areas that may be structurally or functionally significant. In addition to evaluating the relative spatial proximity of variants, mutation3D may be used to analyse the structural context of various kinds of mutational datasets in protein models.


#### Molecular dynamics simulation analysis
  The Desmond v6.3 program Schrödinger, 2020–3 was used in a Linux environment to perform a 100-ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulation, assessing the structural stability of the native SOX9 and mutant (D85H) protein36. The three-site transferable intermolecular potential computer simulation made use of the (TIP3P) water model37. To neutralise Na+ and Cl- ions, the system was introduced to the system to bring the salt content down to 0.15 M, within an orthorhombic simulation box defined with a 10 Å buffer from the protein surface. For all computations, the OPLS3e force field was used38. Using a natural pressure and temperature (NPT) ensemble, energy minimisation and equilibration were carried out while keeping the temperature at 300 K and the pressure at 1.01325 bar constant. Key structural metrics, such as radius of gyration (Rg), hydrogen bond interactions, RMSD and RMSF, or root mean square deviation and fluctuation, respectively, were examined to evaluate the proteins stability and dynamic behavior.


#### Principal Component Analysis (PCA), Dynamical Cross-Correlation Matrix (DCCM), and Free Energy Landscape (FEL) analysis
  The trajectories output from the 100-ns MD simulation were used to generate the PCA, DCCM, and FEL analyses. The simulation trajectories were visualized in VMD software. The PCA analysis was conducted to visualize the prevalent collective motions of complexes. In contrast, the DCCM retrieved the correlated collective motions of each residue from the protein. Both the PCA and DCCM calculations were done by the Bio3D package from R Studio39. The trajectory (.dcd) was aligned to the reference structure (.pdb) based on Cα atoms. The results were visualized from the PCA plots and DCCM heat maps to interpret the dynamic behaviour of the system. The FEL analysis was carried out using the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) from the PCA, which represent the most significant motions during the simulation40. The FEL plots were generated using the Bio3D package in R, and both 2D contour and 3D surface plots were created to visualize the conformational states and energy minima explored by the protein–ligand complex over time.


## Gene–gene and protein–protein interaction networks

### GeneMANIA
  The GeneMANIA41 program (http://www.genemania.org) was used to construct a gene–gene interaction network, providing insight into the functional relationships and potential biological roles of the SOX9 gene with default parameters. GeneMANIA integrates multiple proteomic and genomic datasets to predict gene function and identify genes that are functionally linked. The results were obtained from a variety of sources, including shared protein domain similarity, co-expression, co-localization, pathway data, and genetic relationships, using the human SOX9 protein sequence as input. This approach provided insights into the molecular pathways that may be linked to disease mechanisms and made it easier to identify genes that are functionally connected.


#### STRING
  A Search Tool for Finding Interacting Genes and Proteins (STRING)42 database (https://string-db.org/) was employed to build the protein interaction network for SOX9 with default parameters. STRING visualises the functional relationships between proteins by combining anticipated and known interactions from several sources. Correlations between SOX9 and other proteins in the human proteome were identified through the PPI network, suggesting potential functional interactions.

#### RMSF analysis
  The RMSF values were calculated to evaluate structural flexibility variations in specific amino acid residues (Fig. 5B). The highest peak fluctuations were found in the wild-type and mutant proteins D85H, which were located at MET1, ASN2, PHE7, GLY20, HIS227, GLY248, VAL306, PRO373, ALA419, and PRO509; and MET1, ASN2, LEU3, SER23, GLY32, GLN351, ALA372, GLN491, GLU499 and GLN500; amino acids respectively. We observed the highest average fluctuation amplitudes in the wild-type, at 9.32 Å, relative to the D85H variant, at 7.38 Å (Supplementary Table S1). Additionally, the maximum and minimum fluctuations for these proteins were 24.04 Å and 3.58 Å (wild-type), and 18.55 Å and 2.32 Å (D85H) (Supplementary Table S1). The reduced flexibility in RMSF for mutant (7.38 ± 3.95 Å) and WT (9.32 ± 3.80 Å) indicates significant structural deviation from WT (Supplementary Table S1).


#### Radius of gyration (rGyr) analysis
  The radius of gyration (rGyr) measures the spatial distribution of atoms relative to a protein axis, providing key insights into macromolecular structural dynamics and changes in protein rigidity. To assess the protein stability, both wild-type and mutant, their rGyr values were analysed over a 100-ns MD simulation. The Rg value of wild protein and a selected missense SNPs range from 30.05 to 46.867 Å, and 26.013 to 46.912 Å, respectively (Fig. 5C; Supplementary Table S1). The average Rg values for wild-type protein and D85H were 33.76 Å and 28.67 Å, respectively (Supplementary Table S1). Rg showed a lower mean but higher dispersion for D85H relative to WT (WT: 33.76 ± 2.99 Å; D85H: 28.67 ± 4.01 Å), indicating altered structural compactness (Supplementary Table S1). The mutated protein structures exhibited instability during the 100-ns simulations, as indicated by their reduced range of fluctuations.


#### Hydrogen bonds analysis
  Hydrogen bonds play a crucial role in maintaining the stability of protein binding. The number and pattern of hydrogen bonds influence key protein properties, including structural integrity and molecular interaction capabilities. The number of hydrogen bonds present in both the wild and mutant protein ranges from 149 to 331 and 142 to 318 in a 100-ns simulation time, respectively. Altered hydrogen bond counts for WT (149–331) and WT (142–318) indicate significant structural deviation from WT. The wild-type protein exhibited greater hydrogen bond specificity and reduced flexibility compared to its mutated counterpart, likely attributable to the increased structural instability observed in the mutant (Fig. 5D).

#### DCCM Analysis
  To evaluate the impact on the internal dynamics of SOX9, DCCM plots were generated for the 100-ns MD trajectories of the wild form and its three mutants (Fig. 6B). In these matrices, correlated motions are represented by positive values (blue, ranging toward + 1). In contrast, anti-correlated motions are represented by negative values (magenta, ranging from –1). The DCCM analysis showed a higher mean residue correlation in WT (r =  + 0.62) compared to D85H (r =  + 0.38), reflecting reduced coordinated motion in the mutant. Regions exhibiting strong positive correlations suggest coordinated movements, where residues move in the same direction, potentially reflecting structurally or functionally linked domains. In contrast, areas of strong negative correlation indicate that residues move in opposite directions, which may correspond to hinge regions or flexible loops involved in conformational transitions. In the wild form (Fig. 6B; iii), prominent correlations, anti-correlations, structural integrity, and functional predictions are observed as strong, balanced, stable, and active, respectively. Similarly, mutant D85H (Fig. 6B; iv) showed a weakened prominent correlation, less anti-correlation, moderately affected structural integrity, and partially impaired functions.


#### FEL analysis
  The FEL analysis was calculated from 100-ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulation trajectories. The figures are illustrated in 3D surface plots (Fig. 6C) using the PC1 and PC2 derived from PCA results. The energy landscapes reflect the conformational space and thermodynamic stability of each system. The 3D plots of FEL analysis for SOX9 showed a scattered landscape with a minimum free energy of 0 kcal/mol, covering PC1 from –400 to 0 and PC2 from –200 to 400, indicating high structural flexibility (Fig. 6C; v; Supplementary Table S1). The mutant D85H exhibited a free energy of 0 kcal/mol, and covering PC1 from –100 to 400 and PC2 from –300 to 100, indicates a stable conformation with reduced flexibility (Fig. 6C; vi; Supplementary Table S1).

## Discussion
  DNA-binding activity in SOX proteins is mediated through their HMG box domain (PF00505), which recognizes and interacts with particular nucleotide sequences, particularly in interactions with TCF proteins. These regulators are involved in a wide range of developmental and tissue-specific processes43. A crucial transcription factor implicated in several biological processes is SOX9, including chondrogenesis, sex determination, and stem cell maintenance. It regulates key target genes such as COL2A1 (essential for cartilage formation) and AMH (involved in male sexual development). Additionally, SOX9 plays an important role in testis differentiation and neural crest cell development44. SOX9 is encoded by a gene located on human chromosome 17q24.345. The SOX9 transcription factor is critically involved in the developmental processes of the breast, and its aberrant expression has been strongly associated with breast cancer pathogenesis46. SOX9 is implicated in multiple malignancies, where it promotes stem-like properties, EMT, and therapy resistance often through activation of Wnt/β-catenin signalling. In prostate cancer, SOX9 drives Wnt pathway activation and confers sensitivity to Wnt inhibition; in gastric cancer, its upregulation cooperates with canonical Wnt signalling and correlates with poor prognosis; and in non-small-cell lung cancer, SOX9 induces EMT via the Wnt/β-catenin axis15,47–51. Comparable oncogenic roles have been reported in breast and pancreatic cancers, while colorectal studies demonstrate a dose-dependent oncogenic effect, highlighting its context-specific regulation. Further SOX9 genes overexpression drive stem-like properties, epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, and therapy resistance. These oncogenic roles are frequently associated with activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signalling pathway52. The missense SNPs might have a great role in destabilizing SOX9 structure and disrupt its protein–protein interactions, which could, in turn, dysregulate this pathway and contribute to cancer development.

  Multiple missense SNPs documented genetic variations affecting the SOX9 gene are archived in the dbSNP database. For clarity on how these genetic variants alter protein stability and drive disease mechanisms, a comprehensive bioinformatics investigation was performed to pinpoint functionally significant missense SNPs. We found possible pathogenic mutations by using in silico structural and functional studies, along with diverse computational instruments for predicting deleterious SNPs. Functional impacts were inferred using SNAP, PhD-SNP, SNPs&GO, and MutPred2, which integrate sequence conservation, structural attributes, and functional annotations to predict function-altering variants.

  Among 5,029 SNPs analyzed in the SOX9 gene, 1,158 were missense variants, suggesting a substantial potential for altering protein structure. To identify the most damaging SNPs, computational tools such as SIFT, PolyPhen-2, SNAP, PhD-SNP, and SNPs&GO were utilized. SIFT predicted nine deleterious SNPs, and PolyPhen-2 predicted seven as probably damaging. However, not all deleterious SNPs may contribute to disease pathogenesis, despite their potential to alter protein function (Table 1). Discrepancies in disease association predictions across SNAP, SNPs&GO, and PhD-SNP likely stem from differences in their underlying algorithms. Nevertheless, four missense SNPs (D85H, A158T, K173E, and D441N) were consistently predicted as deleterious, probably damaging, and disease-associated across all tools, indicating a potential contribution to pathogenic mechanisms (Table 1). The identified missense SNPs (D85H and A158T) lie within the high mobility group DNA-binding or transactivation domains and are predicted to destabilize SOX9, potentially disrupting DNA binding and protein interactions. Such alterations could dysregulate Wnt/β-catenin-driven transcription, thereby promoting epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition and cancer progression.

  Further analysis revealed that these four high-impact SNPs reside within conserved regions of the SOX9 gene, suggesting they play a critical role in disease development by disrupting protein function. Mutations in evolutionarily conserved regions are known to have more severe functional consequences compared to those in non-conserved regions53. The persistence of these variants across generations highlights their potential to alter protein function significantly, providing key insights into their pathogenic role (Fig. 2). The stability of proteins is crucial for sustaining the biological activities and functions of biomolecules. Protein function can be compromised by pathogenic missense mutations that interfere with appropriate protein folding and decrease stability. Due to their critical involvement in inter-protein bonding and three-dimensional structural maintenance, a distinct missense SNP, D85H in the SOX9 gene, exhibits severe destabilizing effects.

  Amino acid substitutions can profoundly disrupt the three-dimensional conformation and functional properties of the resulting protein54. Crucial structural components of proteins include alpha helices and beta strands. Alpha-helices are distinguished by intramolecular hydrogen bonding, whereas beta-strands create beta-sheet topologies. These secondary structures differ significantly in their ability to tolerate mutations55. Alpha-helices have more inter-residue interactions, and they are more resistant to mutations. In contrast, β-strand mutations often result in smaller amino acid volumes, which can lead to the protein structure becoming unstable56. Mutations can alter the characteristics of disordered protein regions and seriously alter helix-helix interactions57. Ramachandran plots validate the structural reliability of the wild-type and mutant protein structure, with 86% of residues in favoured regions and having an expected mean range of φ and ψ angles33. These variants may lead to significant perturbations in the proteins native conformation and biological activity.

  MutPred2 analysis identified missense SNP D85H as deleterious, indicating that these mutations likely induce significant structural and functional alterations in the affected proteins (Table 4). A mutation in the residue alters the behaviour, function, or reactivity of the mutant protein by interfering with intramolecular and intermolecular interactions58. In the PF00505 domains, the distribution of the mutation D85H indicates a significant contribution to protein functional modification and the progression of disease pathogenesis (Fig. 4). Protein domains are regions that carry out specific functional tasks within a protein structure. Their biological activity can be drastically changed or disrupted by mutations that arise in these domains59. Consequently, when overexpressed in the human genome, the proteins associated with this mutation may have adverse consequences. The effects of missense SNPs on protein stability, residue fluctuations, and structural compactness are evaluated using molecular dynamics simulations. This method effectively finds new mutations in the target protein. RMSD and RMSF values that fall between 1 and 3 Å are considered stable for protein structures60. The average flexibility of both wild-type (WT) and mutant protein structures is assessed by RMSF analysis, which reflects the structural instability of the proteins. The increased fluctuations observed in mutant variants compared to WT indicate reduced stability in the proteins conformation (Fig. 5B). The higher (rGyr) values observed in the mutant show that the protein structure is different from the wild-type (WT), which may have partially unfolded or dissociated. Ironically, though, it seems that this mutant higher hydrogen bond count leads to structural instability rather than stabilisation61. According to our findings, the D85H variant within the HMG DNA-binding domain (PF00505) shows structural destabilization (changes in RMSD, RMSF, rGyr, and increased number of H-bonds), which might reduce DNA-binding affinity, disrupt cofactor interactions, and dysregulate Wnt/β-catenin signalling43,62.

  To ensure the reliability and consistency of MD simulation outcomes, we employed PCA, DCCM, and FEL analyses of SOX9 wild and three respective mutants63,64. PCA provided insights into the significant movements in the protein structure. Whereas the DCCM analysis helped to understand how different residues in the protein moved relative to each other. FEL provides a thermodynamic perspective by mapping the conformational states against their free energy levels. From the results, all the selected lead mutants exhibited fewer stable results when compared with the native protein.

  The functional network analysis using GeneMANIA demonstrated strong interactions between SOX9 and twenty functionally associated genes. The genes that showed the most significant connections were RUNX2, COL9A1, COMP, MATN1, and NR5A1 (Fig. 7A). RUNX2 is a central osteogenic transcription factor that regulates the complexity and interactions within pathways65. Two interdependent SOX9 dimers activate a COL9A1 collagen gene enhancer, which enables SOX9 to activate transcription in chondrogenesis and sex determination66. In patients with colon cancer, the gene COMP, which is connected to M2 macrophage infiltration, serves as a critical prognostic biomarker and predictor of immunotherapy efficacy67. MATN1 gene polymorphisms are associated with a reduced risk of mandibular retrognathism68. NR5A1 plays a crucial role in cell survival, proliferation, and metabolic regulation, and can also reprogram cells to produce steroids69. SOX9 effects are amplified through its interactions with at least 20 other genes and regulatory elements, further influencing cancer risk and severity12,70,71.

  Ten proteins were found to interact with SOX9 by the STRING network analysis72–77, and each one showed remarkably high confidence values of 0.987 or higher (Fig. 7B)1. SOX9’s particular relationship with these proteins was confirmed by comparative analysis, which identified eight shared interaction partners between the GeneMANIA and STRING databases (SOX6, GATA4, DMRT1, NR5A1, PRKACB, PRKACA, RUNX2, and COL2A1). The D85H variant, located in the HMG DNA-binding domain of SOX9, may disrupt its sequence-specific DNA recognition, potentially weakening activation of chondrogenic targets (COL2A1, COL9A1, COMP, and MATN1) and reducing its repression of CTNNB1-driven Wnt/β-catenin signaling. Previous studies revealed that SOX9 mutations can disrupt its structural stability or degradation control, thereby impairing its DNA binding, transcriptional activity, and interaction networks62,78. Consistent with previous research, our findings suggest that destabilizing SOX9 missense may disrupt its structural stability and binding interfaces, thereby weakening its regulatory interaction networks.

  Among Han Chinese women, a particular SNP, rs73354570, has been closely associated with postmenopausal osteoporosis and differences in bone mineral density, suggesting that it may have an impact on disease risk79. SNPs rs8072254 and rs1859961, located in an enhancer region that controls SOX9, alter gene expression in prostate cancer and are associated with a higher risk of contracting the disease, likely due to interference with the binding of transcription factors and androgen receptors53. In our findings, SNP rs368623884 was the most significant for the progression of diseases (Table 1).

  The specific biochemical pathways by which the most significant missense variant, D85H, contributes to disease development are yet unknown, despite several SNPs in the SOX9 gene being associated with distinct disease causes. SOX9 is known to have connections with several genes; the existence of these missense mutations in PF00505 domain proteins may be a major pathogenic factor, disrupting critical pathways in DNA replication and cell cycle regulation, as suggested by protein–protein interaction analyses from GeneMANIA and STRING databases. These results are further supported by our MD simulation data, PCA, DCCM, and FEL analyses. We recommend that, by thoroughly examining the impact of missense mutations in SOX9 on protein composition and function, programmable synthetic genetic circuits may enable the development of tailored treatments for individuals who possess these particular SOX9 variants. This study is limited by its exclusive reliance on in silico predictions; future work will validate the prioritized variants through site-directed mutagenesis, functional assays, and clinical correlation analyses. To fully utilize this potential, though, two things must happen: (1) the detected mutations must be thoroughly characterised; and (2) synthetic biology, machine learning, and artificial intelligence techniques must be applied synergistically across computational, cell-based, and animal model systems.


## Conclusion
  As our analysis revealed, out of 5,029 SNPs in the SOX9 gene, 1,158 were missense mutations. Then, to determine whether nsSNPs are detrimental, likely harmful, effective, disease-associated, extremely harmful, and destabilizing and could change the structure and function of the SOX9 protein, several exact bioinformatics studies were carried out. Following several analyses, it was shown that the mutation D85H in domain PF00505 reveals that it was a highly harmful disease-causing mutant. The higher hydrogen bond and rGyr in mutant proteins compared to WT, along with consistent variations in RMSD and RMSF values during MD simulation, indicate that the mutation may change the stability and structure of the WT protein and might have a significant impact on the pathogenesis of the disease. Considering the impact of missense SNPs and their associated pathways, identifying high-impact SOX9 variants could enable personalized therapeutic strategies like risk stratification, structure-guided inhibitor design, allele-specific RNA or gene-editing therapies, and the use of Wnt/β-catenin pathway inhibitors to mitigate the adverse effects of these mutations on affected individuals or populations. This study is based on computational predictions; therefore, experimental validation and multi-omics integration are needed to confirm these findings and guide future therapeutic development.
