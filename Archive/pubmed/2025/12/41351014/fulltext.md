# CXCL12 deficiency promotes colorectal cancer progression and reduces anti-PD-L1 immunotherapy efficacy through MDSC regulation


## Introduction
  Several reversal strategies associated with CXCL12, especially the use of CXCR4 antagonists, have been proposed for colorectal cancer (CRC). These strategies have shown some efficacy in clinical trials, but the mechanisms underlying how CXCL12 deficiency contributes to reduced anti-PD-L1 blockade efficacy, a critical barrier to immunotherapy success, remain unclear, highlighting the need for deeper mechanistic exploration and optimized interventions.


## Methods
  In this study, the single-cell transcriptome sequencing analysis, in vitro cell experiments and in vivo animal experiments were integrated to identify CXCL12-related RNA binding proteins (RBPs) with causal links to CRC, define key cell types driving resistance, and validate mechanistic insights.


## Results
  Twelve CXCL12-related RBPs showed causal relationships with CRC. Machine learning methods and diagnostic analysis identified CPEB3, DDX39B, and SIDT2 as biomarkers of CRC. Monocytes were selected as the key CRC cell type based on their biomarker distribution. Single-cell transcription factor analysis screened out two CRC-related transcription factors: MEIS2 and TCF4. In vitro cell experiments and in vivo animal experiments indicated that CXCL12 silencing promoted the migration and invasion capacities of CRC tumor cells. Notably, CXCL12 overexpression combined with PD-L1 antibodies showed the lowest cell viability and invasion capacity, indicating that CXCL12 enhances rather than inhibits anti-PD-L1 therapeutic efficacy Moreover, CXCL12 was negatively correlated with the proportion and number of myeloid-derived suppressor cells.


## Discussion
  Our identification of CPEB3, DDX39B, and SIDT2 as CRC revealed that CXCL12-related CRC biomarkers, combined with mechanistic evidence linking CXCL12 to MDSC regulation and anti-PD-L1 resistance, provides a novel framework for understanding immunotherapy failure in CRC. These findings might aid in establishing clinical CRC treatment strategies guiding the development of CXCL12-targeted combination strategies to overcome anti-PD-L1 resistance and improve CRC immunotherapy outcomes.


## Introduction
  Colorectal cancer (CRC), one of the most common malignant tumors of the digestive tract, has a high incidence rate and mortality rate worldwide [1]. At present, CRC treatments include surgical resection, neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, postoperative chemoradiotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy, with surgery remaining the main treatment. However, CRC continues to have a high recurrence rate and a mortality rate of 5 years after surgery [2, 3]. Therefore, a comprehensive insights into the molecular processes behind CRC formation and evolution are required to refine prognostic strategies.

  Distinct genetic and epigenetic events are now known to characterize the development of CRC [4]. Importantly, emerging evidence now indicates that the progression of CRC is significantly influenced by RNA-binding proteins (RBPs), which are primarily involved in RNA-related processes such as processing and transport [4–6]. For example, RBPs have been associated with the clinical outcomes of CRC patients [7], and have been shown to promote cell proliferation and tumor development in CRC [8], Other evidence now indicates that RBPs can function as tumor suppressors in CRC by modulating JAK/STAT signaling [9]. All of these studies now point to an important role of RBPs in CRC, while also implying that RBP-related genes (RBP-RGs) could serve as biomarkers of CRC and that they may participate in the migration and migration of cancer cells.

  Tumor cell migration is the main cause of death in colon cancer patients [10, 11]. The chemokine CXCL12, a key cellular regulator, drives the invasion and migration of CRC. Notably, most previous studies on CXCL12-mediated CRC progression and therapy resistance have focused on traditional cytokine signaling cascades, such as the PI3K/AKT/mTOR, MEK/ERK, and Wnt/β-catenin pathways, primarily investigating transcriptional regulation mechanisms. These studies have advanced the understanding of the role of CXCL12, but they largely overlook the RNA metabolism processes that are tightly controlled by RBPs. This gap limits a comprehensive understanding of CXCL12-mediated biological effects, especially in the context of anti-PD-L1 blockade resistance, where post-transcriptional fine-tuning of key molecules may play a decisive role [12]. However, while CXCL12 is abundant in common migration sites of CRC, like lymph nodes and liver, the CXCL12 receptor CXCR4 is highly expressed in CRC tissues [13], suggesting that the CXCR4/CXCL12 signaling axis likely contributes to CRC migration process, and upon binding to CXCR4. CXCL12 triggers multiple downstream pathways that enhance tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion [14, 15]. These signaling pathways include, but are not limited to, the PI3K/AKT/mTOR, MEK/ERK, and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways. Activation of these pathways enhances tumor cell viability while promoting tumor angiogenesis and immune escape, thereby accelerating tumor growth and migration [16–18].

  Several reversal strategies associated with CXCL12 have been proposed and studied, including CXCR4 antagonists, signaling pathway inhibitors, immunotherapies, and gene therapy. These strategies have shown some efficacy in clinical trials, but further studies and optimization are needed. Given the aforementioned gap in post-transcriptional regulation research of CXCL12 signaling, and the established role of RBPs in CRC progression, exploring CXCL12-related RBPs represents a novel entry point to uncover unrecognized mechanisms of CXCL12-mediated anti-PD-L1 blockade resistance, one that complements and extends beyond traditional cytokine signaling studies. Therefore, the aim of the present study was to use single-cell transcriptome sequencing analysis to investigate the regulatory role of RBP-related genes showing associations with CXCL12 in the progression of CRC, focusing on the possible effects on PD-L1 expression and the recruitment of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) in CRC.


## Materials and methods

### Data extraction
  The TCGA and GTEx datasets for 33 types of cancer were acquired from the UCSC Xena platform. Additionally, RNA sequencing profiles along with clinical data (including survival information) of CRC patients were rettrieded from the Xena and GEO databases. The GSE14297, GSE21510, GSE23878, GSE81558, GSE89076, and GSE90627 datasets were used to study the expression of CXCL12. The TCGA-CRC dataset, which contains 469 CRC and 349 healthy control (HC) samples was used as the training dataset [19], The GSE39582 dataset and GSE87211 dataset were used to verify the availability of a survival risk model, among them, the GSE39582 dataset contains 566 CRC samples with survival information and 19 HC samples, and the GSE87211 dataset contains 203 CRC samples with survival information and 160 HC samples [20]. The GSE146771 dataset, which contains 10 CRC samples and 10 HC samples, was used for the single-cell correlation analysis [21]. Genetic variation data for CRC (GCST90428118) were obtained from the GWAS Catalog database, which included 4988 CRC samples and 310272 HC samples [22]. The cis-eQTL of exposure factors was downloaded from the eQTLGen Consortium database [23]. Besides, a total of 1,542 RBP-RGs were obtained from previous studies [24].


### Expression and prognosis analyses of CXCL12
  The expression of CXCL12 was first analyzed between tumor tissues and tissues adjacent to cancer for 33 types. The expression of CXCL12 in CRC-related datasets (GSE14297, GSE21510, GSE23878, GSE81558, GSE89076, and GSE90627) was then analyzed. Kaplan-Meier (K-M) curves (overall survival period [OS] curves) were then plotted to determine the prognostic value of CXCL12 using the “survminer” R package (version 0.4.9).


### Screening of the biomarkers of CRC
  Differentially gene expression analysis on 469 CRC and 349 HC samples in the TCGA-CRC dataset were perfomed firstly, applying a threshold of |log2FC| > 1 and adj.p.value < 0.05 using the “DESeq2” R package (version 1.48) [25]. The CXCL12-related genes were then analyzed using “Spearman”, applying |Cor| > 0.6 and p < 0.05. Then, candidate genes were then obtained by intersecting the DEGs, 1,542 RBP-related genes, and CXCL12-related genes using “venn” (version 1.11).

  The candidate genes with a causal relationship with CRC were screened using Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis. The SNPs screened with FDR < 0.05 and MAF > 0.01 were pretreated using the SMR1.03 software, and the SNPs with linkage disequilibrium (LD) were removed with r2 = 0.1, kb = 500, and F-statistics < 10. The two-sample MR analysis was carried out using “TwoSampleMR” R package (version 0.5.6), and the effective causal relationships between exposure factors (candidate genes) and outcome variables were verified using “Steiger directionality.”

  Based on the candidate genes with causal relationships with CRC, the importance rankings of the target genes were calculated using the following three machine learning algorithm (LASSO, SVM-RFE, and RF). The target genes were then obtained by intersecting these three groups of characteristic genes.

  As a final step, the ROC curves of these target genes in the TCGA-CRC, GSE39582, and GSE87211 datasets were drawn to find the biomarkers of CRC (AUC > 0.7). In addition, the nomogram was constructed and the corresponding calibration curve was drawn using the “RMS” R package (version 6.5–0) to diagnose the risk of CRC.


### Construction of a survival risk model for CRC
  The risk score was calculated by: Riskscore = ∑βiXi, and the risk curve, K-M survival curve, and ROC curve were conducted to evaluate the prognostic ability of the model. In addition, external validation was performed using the GSE39582 and GSE87211 datasets.


### Cell type annotation and screening to identify key CRC cell type
  The GSE146771 dataset represents immune cell-enriched samples from colorectal cancer tissues and adjacent normal tissues, providing an ideal resource to investigate the immune microenvironment composition and CXCL12-mediated immune cell interactions, rather than tumor cell-intrinsic properties. To further dissect the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying CXCL12-mediated primary resistance to anti-PD-L1 blockade in CRC following our pan-cancer and RBP-related gene analyses, we leveraged scRNA-seq data from the GSE146771 dataset to resolve the expression patterns of CXCL12 and its associated regulatory factors at the single-cell level.

  Quality control of the GSE146771 dataset was performed using the “Seurat” R package (version 4.0.0). Specifically, cells with mitochondrial gene content > 20%, detected genes < 200, or detected genes > 6000 were filtered out to exclude low-quality cells and potential doublets. After quality control, the cells were normalized using the “LogNormalize” method (scaling factor = 10,000), and highly variable genes (HVGs) were identified with the “FindVariableFeatures” function (selection.method = “vst”, nfeatures = 2000) [26].

  The cells were then clustered using the “FindNeighbors” and “FindClusters” functions (resolution = 0.5) after scaling the data and performing principal component analysis (PCA, npcs = 50). Cell clusters were visualized using UMAP with the “RunUMAP” function configured to use the first 50 PCA dimensions (npcs = 50) and a minimum distance of 0.3 to optimize cluster separation. The cell types were annotated using the CellMarker database, with a minimum correlation coefficient of 0.7 as the threshold for reliable annotation. The proportions of different cell types in the CRC and HC groups were calculated and compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The functional enrichment of the differential cells and the biological pathways involved were explored using ReactomeGSA.

  The distributions of CXCL12 and biomarkers in different cell types were visualized using UMAP plots. The cells with significantly different CXCL12 expression were defined as the key cells. The sub-cell clusters of key cells were further identified using “FindNeighbors” and “FindClusters” (resolution = 1), and visualized using UMAP. The cell differentiation trajectory in the key cells was constructed using Monocle2, and the “differentialGeneTest” function was used to explore gene dynamics during cell differentiation. To further calculate the expression and pairing of key cell receptors and ligands, the interactions between different cells were inferred, and the cell-cell interactions were explored using “CellChat” with the CellChatDB database. The Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method was used to adjust the raw p-values, with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 set as the statistical significance threshold.


### Single-cell transcription factor analyses
  A random forest tree based on the single-cell gene expression matrix was constructed using transcription factors (TFs) as the input and CXCL12 as the output. The importance score (IM) between each TF and CXCL12 was calculated to obtain the TF-gene coexpression module. Each motif was accumulated for all genes in that module based on the gene-motif database and using “RcisTarget.” The target genes of each significantly enriched motif were predicted, and the gene regulatory network module (Regulons) containing TFs and target genes was constructed. Finally, the activity of Regulons was quantified using “AUCell.”


### Verifying the expression and regulation of CXCL12 in vitro
  Human normal colorectal cells (FHCs) and human CRC cells (HT-29 cells) were used for in vitro validation of the expression of CXCL12 and other biomarkers of CRC. The changes in the gene and protein expression levels of CXCL12 and other biomarkers in the FHC and HT-29 cells were determined by quantitative Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (qRT-PCR) and Western Blot (WB), respectively. The corresponding cell models showing silencing and overexpression of CXCL12 (FHC-NC, FHC-siCXCL12, FHC-oeCXCL12, HT-29-NC, HT-29-siCXCL12, and HT-29-oeCXCL12) were designed, and the expression levels of CXCL12 and other biomarkers were determined by qRT-PCR and WB. All primer pairs were designed to target the coding regions of target genes, with GAPDH as the internal reference gene.   To examine the regulation of CXCL12, TF silenced and overexpressing HT-29 cell models were constructed, and the CXCL12 gene and protein expression levels were determined. The intracellular colocalization of CXCL12 with upstream TFs, and the transcription regulation of CXCL12 were explored using a dual fluorescent Allele-Specific Expression (ASE) reporter gene technique.


### Verifying the function of CXCL12 in vitro
  The effects of silencing and overexpression of CXCL12 on CRC tumor cell proliferation, migration and invasion abilities were examined using CCK-8 assays, 5-acetylene-2’-deoxyuracil nucleoside (EdU) staining, clone formation, and Transwell experiments.


### Verifying the expression and function of CXCL12 in vivo
  In vivo, female immunodeficient BALB/c nude mice (6–8 weeks) were used to validate the expression of CXCL12 and other biomarkers of CRC. The mice were randomly divided into a negative control group (n = 8, WT), a positive control group (n = 48, Model), and an experimental group (n = 48, siCXCL12-Model). Mice in the experimental group were injected subcutaneously with CXCL12 silencing HT-29 cells (1 × 107 cells/mouse), the positive control mice received subcutaneous injection of HT-29 cells (1 × 107 cells/mouse), and the negative control group received an equal dose of saline. The body weight of each mouse was recorded every week. Tumor volume was measured weekly using calipers and calculated as (length × width2)/2. After 6 weeks, all mice were sacrificed and mouse serum and colorectal tissues were collected for analysis. Colorectal tissues and lymph nodes were also examined using HE staining. Tumor tissue samples were taken for Ki-67 immunostaining to determine cancer cell proliferation as an assessment of the effect of CXCL12 overexpression on the proliferative capacity of colorectal cell cancer cells. Tumor tissues from each group of mice were subjected to grinding and digestion to obtain single-cell suspensions. Impurities and red blood cells were removed by centrifugation and washing, leaving preparations of relatively pure cancer cells. The expression levels of CXCL12 and other biomarkers were first subjected. CCK-8 tests, EdU staining, clone formation, and Transwell experiments were also analyzed for the effects of CXCL12 silencing and overexpression on in vivo CRC tumor cell proliferation, migration and invasion ability of CRC tumor cells.


### In vitro exploration of the mechanism of CXCL12-induced anti-PD-L1 primary resistance
  HT-29 cell with high CXCR4 expression was used to explore the mechanism of CXCL12 expression induces anti-PD-L1 primary resistance. The experimental groups were as follows: Con (untreated), CXCL12si (CXCL12 silencing group), CXCL12oe (CXCL12 overexpression group), Con + anti-PD-L1, and CXCL12oe + anti-PD-L1. The proliferative capacities of the cells in each group were determined using the CCK-8 assay. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells were also cocultured with these cell groups, and the proportions and changes in the numbers of the key cell type were determined using flow cytometry. MDSCs were identified and quantified using flow cytometry with the following markers: monocytic MDSCs (M-MDSCs) were defined as CD14+ HLA-DRlow/-CD11b+ cells, and granulocytic MDSCs (G-MDSCs) were defined as CD15+ CD11b+ HLA-DRlow/- cells. The proportions and absolute numbers of these MDSC subsets were analyzed using FlowJo software.


### Statistical analysis
  Data are presented as mean ± SD. For comparisons among ≥3 groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test was used when assumptions were met; otherwise, Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparison test was applied. Multiple testing corrections were performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method. All in vitro experiments were performed with ≥3 biological replicates and 3 technical replicates per condition. Normality was assessed by Shapiro–Wilk test, homogeneity of variance by Levene’s test. ROC curves were constructed and AUC values with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using the DeLong method. Survival models were evaluated using C-index with bootstrap correction and calibration curves. Two-sided tests were used with α = 0.05 as the significance threshold.


## Results

### The expression of CXCL12 was associated with the risk of CRC
  CXCL12 was significantly highly expressed in the following four cancers: DLBC, GBM, LAML, and TGCT. Conversely, its expression was significantly low in the following 21 cancers: ACC, BLCA, BRCA, CESC, CHOL, COAD, ESCA, HNSC, KICH, KIRC, KIRP, LIHC, LUAD, LUSC, MESO, PAAD, PRAD, READ, SKCM, STAD, and UCEC (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1A). Notably, CXCL12 also showed significantly low expression in the GSE14297, GSE21510, GSE23878, GSE81558, GSE89076, and GSE90627 CRC datasets (p < 0.01) (Fig. 1B–G). Significant differences in overall survival were also evident between these two groups in the TCGA-COAD dataset (p = 0.0145) (Fig. 1H–I). ### Monocytes were identified as the key cell type of CRC
  A quality control evaluation of the GSE146771 dataset was conducted and the top 2,000 highly variable genes (HVGs) were screened for standardization (Fig. 4A–B). The cells were then classified into 29 clusters and identified as 6 cell types (CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, B cells, monocytes, NK cells, and hematopoietic stem (HSC) cells) (Fig. 4C). Notably, all 6 cell types were significantly higher in the CRC group (p < 0.001) (Fig. 4D–E). Another feature worth noting was that CXCL12 was highly expressed only in the monocytes, and its expression was significantly different in the CRC and HC samples. Thus, monocytes were selected as the key cell type of CRC (Fig 4F–G).

  Annotation of the monocyte cell cycle enabled their classification into 7 subtypes. Notably, most of the marker genes were highly expressed during the late differentiation stages (Fig 4H). Furthermore, cell communication was weakened in the CRC group, establishing monocytes as the cell communication center for CRC. The most significant ligand receptor between HSC and CD8+ T cells was MIF-(CD74+CXCR4), whereas ANXA1–FPR1 was the most significant ligand receptor between HSCs and monocytes in CRC (Fig 4I).

### In vivo inhibition of CXCL12 increases the CRC cell proliferative capacity

Compared with HC mice (negative control), the Model mice showed destruction of gland structures, obvious cell heterogeneity, large stained nuclei, imbalances in the proportions of nuclei and cytoplasm, and a high degree of tissue infiltration. Comparison of the lymphoid tissue sections from the positive control group (HT-29-NC injected nude mice) revealed greater migration of tumor cells in the experimental group (HT-29-siCXCL12 injected nude mice) (Fig. 8B). The Ki-67 expression levels were also higher in the experimental group than in the control group, as was the cell proliferation capacity, confirming that inhibiting CXCL12 promotes in vivo cancer cell proliferation (Fig. 8C). ## Discussion
  This study reveals that CXCL12 deficiency, rather than overexpression, contributes to reduced anti-PD-L1 blockade efficacy in CRC using single-cell transcriptomics, and we first discuss key observations from our preliminary CXCL12 expression analysis across 33 pan-cancer types to provide context for subsequent CRC-specific findings. Our pan-cancer analysis revealed inconsistent abnormal CXCL12 expression. This heterogeneity stems from that CXCL12 mainly secreted by stromal cells to regulate cell migration, angiogenesis, and immune recruitment, is upregulated by activated stromal cells in lDLBC/GBM for tumor progression, while reduced stromal activation or increased CXCL12 antagonists lower its levels in CRC/BRCA. On the other hand, it also depends on tumor-specific molecular mechanisms. Different tumors harbor distinct genetic mutations and epigenetic modifications that regulate CXCL12 expression. For example, in CRC (COAD), mutations in genes like APC and KRAS can alter the Wnt/β-catenin and MAPK signaling pathways, which have been reported to downregulate CXCL12 transcription. In contrast, in GBM, mutations in EGFR may activate the PI3K/Akt pathway, upregulating CXCL12 expression. Besides, the expression of CXCL12 may also be influenced by the clinical stage of the tumor and sample collection methods. In our study, we analyzed tumor tissues and adjacent non-tumor tissues from multiple datasets, and the differences in sample homogeneity across different tumor types may further contribute to the inconsistent CXCL12 expression.

  The development of CRC is a well-understood phenomenon that is characterized by distinct genetic and epigenetic events [4]. Importantly, emerging evidence has shown that RBPs play critical roles in the progression of CRC, implying that RBP-related genes could serve as CRC biomarkers and participate in the process of proliferation and migration of cancer cells [4, 6]. In this study, 15 CXCL12-related DEGs were associated with RBP-related genes. Three genes (CPEB3, RBM20, and RBM6) positively associated with CRC outcomes, and nine genes (DDX39B, IGHMBP2, MYEF2, NANOS1, RBPMS2, SAMHD1, SIDT2, TDRD9, and ZGPAT) were negatively associated with CRC outcomes. This is the first demonstration of a direct relationship between these genes and the developmental outcomes of CRC, and the further linking of CXCL12-related RBPs with the development outcomes of CRC is a novel finding.

  Further analysis of seven of the target genes (CPEB3, DDX39B, NANOS1, RBM20, RBPMS2, SIDT2, and ZGPAT) revealed that CPEB3, DDX39B, and RBM20 participated in the pathological progression of CRC, in agreement with previous studies [27–29]. The role of Pl3K-Akt signaling pathway in CRC is mainly reflected in the regulation of cell growth and proliferation and the promotion of tumorigenesis. On the one hand, some studies have shown abnormal activation of PI3K/AKT signaling pathway in CRC and a resultant promotion of rapid cancer cell growth and proliferation [30]. On the other hand, mutations or aberrant expression of certain genes in the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway have been strongly associated with the prognosis of CRC. For example, the proportion of PIK3CA mutations in CRC is about 17%, and patients with PIK3CA mutations may have a shorter survival [31].

  A similar role in CRC prognosis is also likely for genes associated with the Ras signaling pathway—another pathway also mainly associated with CRC tumor growth and migration [32, 33]. Notably, mutations in RAS genes are the most common mutation types in CRC, with KRAS accounting for 44.7% and NRAS accounting for 7.5% of the mutations associated with CRC [33, 34]. The present study findings provide novel evidence that CXCL12 also plays an important role in regulating the Pl3K-Akt and Ras signaling pathways that are important for the development of CRC.

  CXCL12 functions as a chemotactic cytokine that mediates cellular migration through CXCR4 receptor binding [35, 36]. This interaction initiates G protein-coupled signaling through a series of molecular events, followed by subsequent dissociation of the G protein into GTP-Gɑ, GTP-Gβ, and GTP-Gγ subunits. These subunits further activate downstream the Ras/ERK, PLC-β, and the PI3K/AKT pathways [14, 15, 35, 37]. Based on this existing knowledge, we considered that the Pl3K-Akt and Ras signaling pathways would be key pathways affecting CRC from the perspective of RBPs. However, the unexpectedly high AUC values ( > 0.7) for CPEB3, DDX39B, and SIDT2 in all our training and validation datasets led us to designate these as biomarkers and to construct a prognostic model for CRC based on CXCL12.

  The three selected biomarkers function in translation mediated by CPEBs. The roles of CPEBs have been well characterized and include the regulation of the cell cycle, cellular senescence, and synaptic plasticity [38, 39]. CPEB3 expression decreases in colorectal cancer tissues in a pattern that is closely associated with poor patient prognosis [9], which in agreement with our findings. Furthermore, the previous authors showed that CPEB3 downregulation promoted CRC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion both in vitro and in vivo, while up-regulation had an inhibitory effect that was associated with abnormal JAK/STAT signaling activation in CRC [9]. Other researchers have pointed out that CPEB3 inhibits the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) via IL-6 R/STAT3 signaling that disrupts the crosstalk between CRC cells and tumor-associated macrophages [27].

  Similarly, DDX39B has been identified as a biomarker associated with poor CRC prognosis [28, 40]. Emerging evidence suggests that DDX39B facilitates CRC progression through multiple mechanisms, such as it drives tumor migration and EMT via the DDX39B/FUT3/TGFβR-I axis [41], and it enhances proliferation by directly interacting with the CDK6/CCND1 complex [42]. Although no relevant studies have sought a role for SIDT2 in CRC, significant reductions in SIDT2 expression have also been reported in a variety of cancers and are associated with poor cancer prognosis. These effects have also been associated with proliferation, angiogenesis, and tumor cell migration and invasion [43–45]. Interestingly, our in vitro cell and in vivo experiments aimed at modulating CXCL12 expression also produced very similar results.

  In the present study, monocytes were selected as the key cell type of CRC based on the distribution of the identified biomarkers (CPEB3, DDX39B, and SIDT2). Monocytes are important components of the immune system, as they are involved in systemic immunity and inflammatory responses [46]. Inflammatory responses are closely related to the occurrence and development of tumors in CRC, and monocytes can influence tumor cell growth, invasion, and migration by releasing cytokines and other inflammatory mediators [47]. In the present study, our results showed significantly increased cell communication between monocytes and CD8+ T cells, between monocytes and NK cells, and between monocytes and HSC cells in CRC. Notably, in CRC, MIF-(CD74+CXCR4) was the most significant ligand-receptor functioning between HSC and CD8+ T cells, whereas ANXA1–FPR1 was the most significant ligand-receptor functioning between HSCs and monocytes. Other researchers have suggested that the upregulation of MIF-(CD74+CXCR4) by other genes could further inhibit its anti-tumor immunity [48]. Similarly, the MIF-(CD74+CXCR4) axis has been proposed to produce neutrophil extracellular traps, which are considered to be the cause of inflammatory periodontal lesions [49]. The ANXA1–FPR1 axis has also been related to the growth and invasion of tumor cells [50, 51]. Other studies have shown that the preoperative lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio is closely associated with prognosis in patients with CRC, with a low value indicating poor prognosis [47, 52].

  The monocyte count is also associated with other inflammatory response indicators, such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio and the systemic immune inflammatory response index (SII), which have value in the prediction and prognostic evaluation of CRC [53]. Together, the findings of these studies imply that the communication between the key cell type that responds to CXCL12 and other immune cells plays an important role in the proliferation and migration of CRC tumor cells. For example, PD-L1 serves as an inhibitor in the immune system that induces immune resistance through its interactions with its ligand PD-1 [54]. Despite immune resistance, anti-PD-L1 has generated resistance to tyrosine kinase inhibitors in certain cancers by activating the TGF-β/Smad/EMT axis [54, 55].

  As a cellular chemokine, CXCL12 plays a key role in the tumor microenvironment, especially in CRC, and because it is able to regulate PD-L1 monoclonal antibody primary resistance through multiple mechanisms, understanding this process is important for understanding the challenges of cancer immunotherapy and searching for potential reversal strategies [12, 56, 57]. The CXCL12–CXCR4 axis stimulates tumor proliferation, invasion, and migration of tumor cells in CRC [13]. Elevated CXCL12 expression correlates with advanced disease stages and unfavorable clinical outcomes. This ligand-receptor interaction promotes oncogenic behavior through activation of critical pathways including PI3K/AKT/mTOR, MEK/ERK, and Wnt/β-catenin signaling, while simultaneously modulating immune cell activity within the tumor microenvironment [16–18]. In our study, our examination of the proliferative capacity and migration capacity of tumor cells, and the proliferation and migration capacities of the cells supplemented with CXCL12 and PD-L1 antibodies, revealed significantly higher activities in the cells supplemented with PD-L1 antibodies and CXCR4 inhibitor, which are in agreement with the results reported in the literature. However, CXCL12 is also able to influence immune cell infiltration and polarization in the tumor microenvironment, thereby regulating PD-L1 expression and function.

  Studies have shown that CXCL12 mediates immune evasion through multiple mechanisms. It facilitates recruitment and activation of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and other immunosuppressive populations that secrete IL-10 and TGF-β to suppress effector T cell function. Meanwhile, CXCL12 can also upregulate PD-L1 expression on malignant cells, creating an immune-inhibitory milieu that compromises T cell-mediated cytotoxicity [58, 59]. In our tumor-cell–centric CRC model, loss of CXCL12 promoted MDSC expansion and blunted anti-PD-L1 efficacy. In contrast, in other tumor types where CAF-derived CXCL12 dominates, the effect may be opposite.

  Notably, the role of CXCL12 in immunotherapy resistance is not unique to CRC, but its mechanism of action varies across cancer types, which highlighting both commonalities and tissue-specific differences. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), a cancer also characterized by strong immunosuppression and poor immunotherapy response, CXCL12/CXCR4 signaling primarily drives stromal desmoplasia. CXCL12 secreted by cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) recruits CXCR4+ immunosuppressive cells and restricts the infiltration of effector T cells into the tumor core, thereby mediating anti-PD-1/PD-L1 blockade resistance [13, 14]. Unlike CRC, where CXCL12 directly regulates monocyte differentiation into MDSCs, CXCL12-mediated resistance in PDAC is more dependent on stromal-tumor crosstalk rather than direct modulation of immune cell differentiation [60]. In non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), CXCL12 further differs by promoting EGFR-TKI resistance alongside anti-PD-L1 resistance. It activates the PI3K/AKT pathway to upregulate PD-L1 expression while simultaneously inducing EGFR-independent survival signals [61]. By contrast, in breast cancer, CXCL12/CXCR4 signaling mediates resistance to PD-L1 inhibitors by enhancing EMT and cancer stem cell (CSC) properties, with minimal reliance on MDSC recruitment, unlike the MDSC-centric mechanism we observed in CRC [62]. These cross-cancer comparisons underscore that while CXCL12 is a conserved driver of immunotherapy resistance, its downstream effectors and cellular targets are highly context-dependent. This context dependence is largely shaped by tissue-specific microenvironmental features of CRC. The TME of CRC has a unique immune cell composition compared to others, CRC contains a higher proportion of monocytes and MDSCs, providing a larger cellular pool for CXCL12-mediated differentiation and immunosuppression. These tissue-specific differences and cross-cancer comparisons have important implications for clinical translation. For example, CXCR4 antagonists have shown limited efficacy in PDAC due to its stromal barrier [15], but our findings suggest they may be more effective in CRC by blocking CXCL12-mediated monocyte differentiation into MDSCs, CXCR4 antagonists could reverse anti-PD-L1 resistance without needing to disrupt a dense stroma. However, our results also highlight that CRC-specific factors should be considered when designing CXCL12-targeted therapies, emphasizing the need for patient stratification based on tissue-specific biomarkers. Taken together, in our tumor-cell-centric model, CXCL12 acts protective; its loss aggravates malignancy and expands M-MDSCs, thereby blunting anti-PD-L1 efficacy. This differs from stromal-CAF-derived CXCL12 reported in PDAC; hence, cell-of-origin and tissue context explain the opposite effects. Our findings suggest that CXCL12 deficiency, rather than overexpression, promotes primary resistance to anti-PD-L1 blockade through enhanced MDSC recruitment and subsequent immunosuppression.

  In summary, our study establishes a novel mechanism wherein CXCL12 deficiency in colorectal cancer cells promotes malignant progression through two interconnected pathways [1]: direct enhancement of tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion capacities, and [2] indirect promotion of MDSC accumulation, which creates an immunosuppressive microenvironment that blunts anti-PD-L1 therapeutic efficacy. This dual mechanism explains why CXCL12 restoration, rather than inhibition, represents a promising therapeutic strategy to enhance anti-PD-L1 immunotherapy in CRC.


## Conclusions
  Several reversal strategies associated with CXCL12 in CRC were proposed and studied here, including the use of CXCR4 antagonists, signaling pathway inhibitors, immunotherapies, and gene therapy. CXCL12 deficiency promotes primary resistance to PD-L1 antibodies by enhancing tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and migration, while CXCL12 restoration enhances anti-PD-L1 therapeutic efficacy, by affecting immune cell infiltration and polarization, and possibly through multiple mechanisms, such as interactions (possibly synergistic) with PD-L1 in CRC. Therefore, interventions aimed at restoring CXCL12 expression and related signaling pathways might be effective strategies to enhance anti-PD-L1 immunotherapy efficacy that could improve the efficacy of PD-L1 antibodies as immunotherapy for CRC.
