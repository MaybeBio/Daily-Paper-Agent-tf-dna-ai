# Identification and experimental validation of biomarkers associated with paraptosis in meningioma


## Abstract
  Research suggests that programmed cell death may play a key role in meningioma, yet the mechanisms involving paraptosis-related genes (PRGs) remain unclear. This study aimed to identify biomarkers for meningioma that were associated with PRGs, providing new targets and strategies for therapeutic intervention. Meningioma-related datasets (GSE43290 and GSE77259) were retrieved from public databases. Initially, candidate genes were identified by overlapping PRGs with differentially expressed genes (tumor vs control). Biomarkers were then ascertained through protein–protein interaction (PPI) analysis and gene expression analysis. To further investigate the biological mechanisms of these biomarkers, functional enrichment, regulatory network, drug prediction, molecular docking, and reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction analyses were conducted. The design and implementation of this study strictly adhered to internationally recognized research standards and relevant regulations: Clinical procedures and sample collection complied with the Declaration of Helsinki, Chinese medical regulations, and the ethical review standards of the First Hospital of Lanzhou University. Data management followed GDPR requirements for anonymization and storage. The study identified ITPR3, MAPK1, and MAPK8 as biomarkers for meningioma. ITPR3 was found to be upregulated in tumor samples, while MAPK1 and MAPK8 exhibited significantly reduced expression in tumor samples. Meanwhile, reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction analysis confirmed these findings. Evidently, biomarkers were predominantly enriched in the “olfactory transduction” pathway. Regulatory network analysis predicted 130, 138, and 123 transcription factors targeting ITPR3, MAPK1, and MAPK8, respectively. Notably, SMAD3 was found to co-target all 3 biomarkers. Additionally, interactions involving MAPK1-“hsa-miR-130a-3p”-MIR17HG, MAPK8-“hsa-miR-32-5p”-NORAD, and ITPR3-“hsa-miR-506-3p”-MALAT1 were identified. Furthermore, drug prediction analysis suggested that tamoxifen could co-target these 3 biomarkers, with ITPR3 and tamoxifen exhibiting the strongest binding energy of −9.0 kcal/mol. ITPR3, MAPK1, and MAPK8 are biomarkers for meningioma, all targeted by SMAD3. Tamoxifen could treat meningioma by affecting paraptosis pathways, offering a promising basis for targeted therapy development.


## 1. Introduction
  Meningiomas represent the most prevalent primary tumors of the central nervous system in adults, constituting approximately 40.8% of all primary CNS tumors in the United States and 56.2% of “nonmalignant” primary brain tumors.[1] These tumors primarily originate from arachnoid cap cells located in the arachnoid granulations. Although the majority of meningiomas are histologically benign, their incidence increases with age, and a subset of tumors demonstrates aggressive behavior, including local invasion and recurrence, which can significantly impair neurological function and quality of life.[2] The clinical presentation of meningiomas varies depending on their anatomical location and may manifest as headaches, seizures, visual disturbances, or other focal neurological deficits. Current standard treatments include surgical resection, radiotherapy, and pharmacological interventions. In individuals with recurrent or progressive meningiomas, current therapeutic strategies demonstrate limited effectiveness in managing invasive or recurrent lesions, as indicated by elevated recurrence rates and the emergence of therapeutic resistance. These difficulties may be ascribed to tumor heterogeneity and the restrictive function of the blood–brain barrier in impeding drug delivery.[3] Given these challenges, the identification of molecular biomarkers linked to the pathogenesis and progression of meningiomas is crucial.[4] Such biomarkers not only provide insights into the underlying mechanisms driving tumor development but also offer potential targets for personalized therapeutic strategies, ultimately contributing to improved clinical outcomes for patients with meningiomas.[5,6]

  Paraptosis is a distinct form of programmed cell death characterized by cytoplasmic vacuolization, mitochondrial swelling, and endoplasmic reticulum dilation, differing from apoptosis as it is caspase-independent and lacks typical apoptotic features such as chromatin condensation or DNA fragmentation.[7] Biologically, paraptosis plays a crucial role in cellular homeostasis, defense against malignant transformation, and regulation of cellular responses to stress, with its mechanisms primarily involving dysregulation of intracellular ion homeostasis, mitochondrial dysfunction, and protein aggregation. Unlike apoptosis, which is well-studied and widely recognized, paraptosis remains relatively underexplored, particularly in the context of tumorigenesis.[8] Recent research has indicated a possible association between paraptosis and cancer, suggesting that paraptosis-related genes (PRGs) may play a role in influencing tumor cell proliferation, survival, invasion, and resistance to conventional therapies.[9] Nonetheless, the precise function and underlying mechanisms of PRGs in the context of meningioma are not yet fully understood. The swift progression of contemporary bioinformatics technologies has underscored the critical importance of integrating multi-omics data-encompassing genomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics-in meningioma research for the identification of paraptosis-related biomarkers. This integrative approach not only elucidates the mechanistic role of paraptosis in the tumorigenesis and progression of meningioma but also enhances early disease diagnosis and aids in the identification of novel therapeutic targets.

  This study employs a novel integration of comprehensive bioinformatics and experimental validation to identify biomarkers associated with paraptosis in meningioma. By utilizing meningioma-related datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, we performed differential expression analysis, protein–protein interaction (PPI) analysis, and gene expression analysis to identify potential molecular targets implicated in the development and progression of meningioma. The expression levels of these biomarkers were subsequently validated in clinical samples using reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). To elucidate their functional roles, we conducted functional enrichment analysis to identify key pathways, constructed detailed regulatory networks involving transcription factors (TFs) and noncoding RNAs, and performed drug prediction analyses to identify potential therapeutic compounds. These analyses reveal the molecular basis of meningioma, offering new insights into its pathophysiology and suggesting novel strategies for better treatment, particularly for invasive or recurrent cases.


## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1. Data source
  Transcriptome data for meningioma were gained from GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). In particular, the GSE43290 dataset (GPL96 platform) included 47 meningioma tumor and 4 control tissue samples. While the GSE77259 dataset (GPL6244 platform) consisted of 14 meningioma tumor tissue samples, and the data of 3 control tissue samples were from http://www.affymetrix.com/support/technical/sample_data/gene_1_0_array_data.affx. Additionally, a total of 66 PRGs were acquired from the literature[10] (Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R126).

  During data processing, raw data were first downloaded using the getGEO tool, from which the expression matrix and sample information were extracted. After converting probe IDs in the expression matrix to gene symbols, the na.omit function was applied to directly remove probes containing missing values. For duplicate genes that appeared after conversion, the probe with the highest mean expression level was retained. Finally, group labeling was completed according to sample types.


### 2.2. Principal component analysis (PCA) and differential expression analysis
  PCA was conducted to examine the dispersion and correlation between tumor and control samples in GSE43290 dataset. After, differentially expressed genes (DEGs; tumor vs control) were ascertained via “limma” package (v 3.52.4)[11] (|log2 Fold change (FC)| > 0.50 and P < .05). Subsequently, the acquired DEGs were clearly visualized through a volcano plot and heatmap. The volcano plot was generated with “ggplot2” (v 3.3.6),[12] in which the top 10 upregulated genes and top 10 downregulated genes ranked by log2 FC were labeled. Meanwhile, the heatmap was produced employing the “ComplexHeatmap” package (v 2.18.0)[13] to display the expression patterns of the above-mentioned top 10 upregulated DEGs and top 10 downregulated DEGs in tumor samples and control samples.


### 2.3. Function analysis of candidate genes and identification of candidate biomarkers
  Candidate genes were ascertained by intersecting DEGs and PRGs employing the “ggvenn” package (v 0.1.10; https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=ggvenn). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses were then executed to explore the functions roles and pathways related to these candidate genes, utilizing the “clusterProfiler” package (v 4.7.1.3)[14] (adj. P < .05). Additionally, to examine protein interactions among the candidate genes, a PPI network was built employing STRING database (https://string-db.org/) with a confidence threshold of 0.4. After removing outlier genes, the PPI network was clearly visualized employing Cytoscape software (v 3.9.0).[15] Notably, key module within the PPI network was ascertained by clustering employing the MCODE plugin in Cytoscape (v 3.9.0; degree cutoff = 2, k-core = 2, node score cutoff = 0.2, and max. depth = 100). Ultimately, the genes within the key module were defined as candidate biomarkers for meningioma.


### 2.4. Determination and function analysis of the biomarkers
  To further evaluate and verify the expression of candidate key genes, based on candidate biomarkers, the Wilcoxon test was employed to perform gene expression analyses between tumor and control samples in the GSE43290 and GSE77259 datasets. Genes demonstrating statistically notable differences (P < .05) and consistent expression patterns across both datasets were ascertained as biomarkers. Subsequently, GeneMANIA (https://genemania.org/) was employed to ascertain genes functionally linked to these biomarkers, facilitating the construction of a gene–gene interaction network. Furthermore, to further explore the biological roles linked to the biomarkers, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was carried out on the GSE43290 dataset. In particular, Spearman coefficients between the biomarkers and other genes were calculated and subsequently ranked in descending order employing the “psych” package (v 2.2.9).[16] The “c2.cp.kegg.v7.0.symbols.gmt” gene set was gained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, http://software.broadinstitute.org/gsea/msigdb) as the background set. GSEA was subsequently performed employing the “clusterProfiler” package (v 4.7.1.3), with significance criteria established at an adjusted P-value of <.05 and an absolute normalized enrichment score (|NES|) >1. The top 5 enriched pathways were ascertained and ranked according to their adjusted P-values in ascending order.


### 2.5. Chromosomal localization and subcellular localization analyses
  To determine the chromosomal locations of the biomarkers, the “RCircos” package (v 1.2.0)[17] was applied for visualization. Furthermore, to explore the subcellular distribution situation of the ascertained biomarkers, FASTA files of the biomarker sequences were retrieved from the Gene database on the NCBI website (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gene/). The mRNALocater database (http://www.rnalocate.org/) was then employed to determine the subcellular localization of these biomarkers.


### 2.6. Regulation network analysis
  ChEA3 database (https://amp.pharm.mssm.edu/ChEA3) was applied to predict TFs targeting the ascertained biomarkers. The top 20 TFs with the highest scores were selected to construct and visualize a TF-biomarker network using Cytoscape (v 3.9.0). Additionally, the miRDB (http://mirdb.org) and TargetScan (http://www.targetscan.org) databases were applied to fully predict miRNAs that targeted the biomarkers. Key miRNAs were identified by overlapping predictions from both databases using the “ggvenn” package (v 0.1.10). Subsequently, the starBase database (http://starbase.sysu.edu.cn/index.php) was chosen to predict lncRNAs targeting the key miRNAs (clipExpNum > 25). These relationships were organized to construct and visualize a lncRNA-miRNA–mRNA network employing Cytoscape (v 3.9.0).

  Furthermore, the DisGeNET database (https://www.disgenet.org/) was applied to predict diseases associated with the biomarkers, and a biomarker-disease network was built and clearly visualized employing Cytoscape (v 3.9.0). As treatment strategies for meningioma based on biomarkers might impact multiple organs and tissues, the BioGPS database (https://biogps.org) was employed to obtain mRNA expression data of the biomarkers across various organs and tissues. A biomarker-tissue/organ network was then visualized employing Cytoscape (v 3.9.0).


### 2.7. Drug prediction and molecular docking analyses
  Drug-Gene Interaction Database (DGIdb, http://www.dgidb.org) was employed to fully predict drugs targeting specific biomarkers, with a combined score threshold of >10,000. A biomarker-drug interaction network was then built and visualized employing Cytoscape (v 3.9.0). To assess the binding potential of the identified drugs to the biomarkers, drugs with the highest combined score for each biomarker were selected for further molecular docking analysis. The 3D structures of the biomarkers (serving as receptors) were mined from Protein Data Bank (PDB, https://www.rcsb.org/), while the 3D molecular structures of the drugs (acting as ligands) were retrieved from PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Following this, molecular docking was carried out employing CB-Dock (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock/php/blinddock.php), and binding affinities were calculated. The final results were visualized employing PyMOL software (v 3.0.3). Generally speaking, a binding energy lower than −5.0 kcal/mol is typically regarded as a strong indicator of an effective binding conformation.


### 2.8. The reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)
  To better validate the expression situation of biomarkers in clinical samples, RT-qPCR analysis was performed. Specifically, a total of 10 samples (5 controls and 5 cases) were acquired from the clinic in The First Hospital of Lanzhou University. All participants were given informed consent. The study had the approval of the First Hospital of Lanzhou University ethics committee (approval number: LDYYLL2025-801). Total RNA was fully extracted from the 10 tissue samples employing the TRIzol reagent (Ambion, Austin) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Subsequently, RNA concentrations were clearly measured with a NanoPhotometer N50. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was fully synthesized via reverse transcription employing the SureScript First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit, with the reverse transcription process conducted on an S1000TM Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad). Primer sequences are detailed in Table S2 Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R126. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) assays were carried out employing the CFX Connect Real-Time Quantitative Fluorescence PCR Instrument (Bio-Rad) under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95°C for 1 minute, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 20 seconds, annealing at 55°C for 20 seconds, and extension at 72°C for 30 seconds. The relative quantification of mRNA levels was determined employing the 2−ΔΔCT method. Ultimately, the RT-qPCR results were initially exported to Excel and subsequently imported into GraphPad Prism 5 (https://www.graphpad.com/) for statistical analysis and visualization.


### 2.9. Statistical analysis
  Statistical analyses were conducted using R (v 4.2.2). Differences between groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon test (P < .05). For RT-qPCR analysis, a t-test was employed for statistical comparisons (P < .05). Notably, **** represented P < .0001, *** represented P < .001, ** represented P < .01, * represented P < .05, and ns represented P > .05.


## 3. Results

### 3.1. Candidate genes were ascertained
  PCA analysis revealed a clear distinction between tumor and control samples in the GSE43290 dataset (Fig. 1A). In this dataset, a total of 5753 DEGs were identified, including 439 up-regulated and 5314 down-regulated genes in tumor samples (Fig. 1B, C). Subsequently, 26 candidate genes were selected by intersecting the 5753 DEGs with 66 PRGs (Fig. 1D). Overall, this analysis successfully highlighted candidate genes that might play significant roles in the pathophysiology of meningioma.


### 3.2. Function and pathways of candidate genes were explored
  The 26 candidate genes were enriched in 99 GO terms, including 90 biological processes and 9 molecular functions, as well as 112 KEGG pathways. Noteworthy GO terms included “cellular response to biotic stimulus” (biological process) and “cysteine-type endopeptidase activity involved in apoptotic process” (molecular function), etc (Fig. 2A). In the KEGG analysis, these candidate genes were predominantly involved in pathways related to “pathways of neurodegeneration – multiple diseases,” “lipid and atherosclerosis,” and “prion disease” (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, after excluding 9 outlier genes, a PPI network was constructed (Fig. 2C). Within this network, HSPA5 was found to interact closely with several genes, including PDCD6IP and HSPB8. The key module of the PPI network was identified using the MCODE plugin, and the 10 genes in this key module were considered as candidate biomarkers (Fig. 2D).


### 3.3. ITPR3, MAPK1, and MAPK8 were deemed as biomarkers
  In the GSE43290 and GSE77259 datasets, 3 genes-ITPR3, MAPK1, and MAPK8-demonstrated statistically significant expression differences (P < .05) with consistent trends, as illustrated in Figure 3A and B. Notably, ITPR3 was upregulated in tumor samples, while MAPK1 and MAPK8 were markedly downregulated in these samples. Based on these findings, ITPR3, MAPK1, and MAPK8 have been proposed as potential biomarkers for meningioma, highlighting their significance in the advancement of targeted therapeutic strategies. Notably, RT-qPCR validation demonstrated that ITPR3 exhibited significantly elevated expression levels in tumor samples, whereas MAPK1 and MAPK8 were markedly more expressed in control samples (P < .05; Fig. 3C).

  Furthermore, GeneMANIA analysis identified 20 genes functionally associated with these biomarkers, uncovering significant interactions, including those between MAPK1 and MAP3K1, MAPK8 and MAP2K7, as well as ITPR3 and JUN. These interactions were involved in important processes such as the “Fc receptor signaling pathway” and the “stress-activated MAPK cascade” (Fig. 3D). GSEA further revealed that ITPR3 was significantly enriched in pathways related to “olfactory transduction” and “basal transcription factors” (Fig. 3E), MAPK1 in pathways related to “lysosome” and “ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis” (Fig. 3F), and MAPK8 in pathways related to “neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction” and “olfactory transduction,” etc (Fig. 3G). These findings highlighted the potential involvement of ITPR3, MAPK1, and MAPK8 in key biological pathways, providing valuable insights into their roles in the progression of meningioma.


### 3.4. The localization of biomarkers was explored
  Chromosomal localization analysis indicated that ITPR3 was located on chromosome 6, MAPK1 on chromosome 22, and MAPK8 on chromosome 10 (Fig. 4A). Additionally, subcellular localization analysis revealed that ITPR3 and MAPK8 were mainly present in the cytoplasm, while MAPK1 was predominantly localized in the nucleus (Fig. 4B). These results provided important insights into the genomic and cellular positioning of these biomarkers, enhancing our understanding of their functional roles.


### 3.5. Regulatory networks of biomarkers were constructed
  A total of 130, 138, and 123 TFs were predicted to target ITPR3, MAPK1, and MAPK8, respectively. Utilizing the top 20 TFs with the highest scores, a TF-mRNA interaction network was constructed, which revealed that SMAD3 co-targets all 3 biomarkers (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, the miRDB and TargetScan databases were queried, resulting in the identification of 267 and 166 miRNAs targeting these biomarkers, respectively. The intersection of these miRNAs led to the identification of 57 key miRNAs (Fig. 5B). Additionally, 11 long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) targeting these key miRNAs were predicted, facilitating the development of a lncRNA–miRNA–mRNA network (Fig. 5C). For instance, interactions such as MAPK1-“hsa-miR-130a-3p”-MIR17HG, MAPK8-“hsa-miR-32-5p”-NORAD, and ITPR3-“hsa-miR-506-3p”-MALAT1 were identified (Fig. 5C).

  Furthermore, a disease-biomarker network was constructed based on disease associations from the DisGeNET database, which identified 6, 59, and 17 diseases linked to ITPR3, MAPK1, and MAPK8, respectively. Notably, 5 diseases, including stomach neoplasms, schizophrenia, hyperalgesia, trigeminal neuralgia, and extrahepatic cholestasis, were co-associated with MAPK1 and MAPK8 (Fig. 5D). Regarding tissue expression, all 3 biomarkers were observed to be expressed in CD34+ cells, cardiac myocytes, and testis seminiferous tubules (Fig. 5E). Collectively, these findings provide significant insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying meningioma and suggest that these biomarkers may be instrumental in the development of targeted therapeutic strategies.


### 3.6. Molecular docking analysis was performed using the targeted compounds and biomarkers
  A comprehensive search of the DGIdb database revealed the identification of 8, 57, and 57 pharmacological agents targeting ITPR3, MAPK1, and MAPK8, respectively. Notably, the identical set of 57 compounds was found to target both MAPK1 and MAPK8. Furthermore, 8 compounds, including tamoxifen, and menadione were identified as co-targeting all 3 biomarkers (Fig. 6A). Significantly, tamoxifen achieved the highest aggregate score across the 3 biomarkers, positioning it as a promising candidate for additional research. Consequently, tamoxifen was chosen for molecular docking analysis. The docking results indicated that tamoxifen demonstrated a favorable binding energy of −9.0 kcal/mol with ITPR3, with critical interactions involving the residue Lys236 (Fig. 6B). For MAPK1, tamoxifen showed a binding energy of −5.9 kcal/mol, with key interactions involving the residue Asp173 (Fig. 6C). Similarly, in the case of MAPK8, tamoxifen displayed a binding energy of −6.2 kcal/mol, with key interactions involving the residue Ser307 (Fig. 6D). Overall, these findings highlighted the potential of tamoxifen as a candidate for targeted therapies involving ITPR3, MAPK1, and MAPK8, offering valuable insights for future research and clinical applications.


## 4. Discussion
  Meningiomas pose significant diagnostic challenges due to their frequent incidental detection and heterogeneous biological behavior, with some cases exhibiting malignant potential despite being histologically classified as typically benign tumors. The discovery of novel biomarkers that facilitate early detection and serve as therapeutic targets is both a research priority and a technical challenge in the field of neuro-oncology. Among the various mechanisms of programmed cell death, apoptosis and paraptosis have emerged as critical regulatory nodes in tumor biology. This study addresses the existing knowledge gap by systematically characterizing paraptosis-associated biomarkers through bioinformatics analysis and experimental validation. In doing so, it elucidates the pathogenic role of paraptosis dysregulation in the genesis of meningiomas and identifies new targets for diagnosis and treatment. Utilizing extensive bioinformatics analysis complemented by in vitro validation, we have identified, for the first time, 3 pivotal biomarkers – ITPR3, MAPK1, and MAPK8 – that demonstrate significantly altered expression patterns in meningioma tissues compared to normal controls. These findings contribute to a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying meningioma and facilitate the advancement of novel therapeutic strategies. ITPR3 is upregulated in tumors, while MAPK1 and MAPK8 are downregulated. As a calcium channel protein on the endoplasmic reticulum, ITPR3 is vital for intracellular calcium signaling and cancer-related processes like apoptosis and tumor cell survival.[18] Its upregulation is linked to poor prognosis in cancers such as bladder, colorectal, and pancreatic cancer. In bladder cancer,[19] ITPR3 promotes proliferation and metastasis via the NF-κB/CD44 pathway; in colorectal cancer,[20] it aids metastatic colonization with RELB; and in pancreatic cancer, its overexpression is tied to poor survival. PTEN and FBXL2 regulate ITPR3 degradation, and stabilizing ITPR3 in PTEN-deficient cancers may enhance sensitivity to photodynamic therapy.[21,22] ITPR3 is increasingly seen as a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target due to its role in calcium homeostasis and cancer progression.

  MAPK1 (ERK2) serves as a pivotal intracellular signaling molecule that is integral to cellular processes such as proliferation, differentiation, migration, and apoptosis, and it holds a significant role in the pathogenesis of various cancers. The hyperactivation of MAPK1 is intricately linked to tumor initiation, metastasis, and invasion, particularly in malignancies such as breast, pancreatic, and bladder cancers.[23–25] MAPK1 facilitates tumor cell growth and metastasis through the activation of downstream effectors, including BTRC and IQGAP1. Additionally, MAPK1 contributes to the tumor microenvironment by modulating immune and inflammatory responses.[26] Given its critical functions, MAPK1 represents a promising therapeutic target, with inhibitors demonstrating potential in curtailing tumor growth and metastasis. Furthermore, MAPK8 (JNK1), another essential member of the MAPK family, plays a crucial role in mediating cellular responses to stress, inflammation, apoptosis, and differentiation. MAPK8 is activated by signals like inflammatory cytokines and stress, playing a role in cellular processes such as proliferation and survival. In cancer, it aids tumor progression by influencing cell cycle control, metastasis, and chemotherapy response.[27] For instance, in colorectal cancer, the PVT1/miR-152-3p/E2F3/MAPK8 axis enhances tumor growth by activating MAPK8.[28] In bladder cancer,[29] MAPK8 collaborates with other MAPKs to affect TFs, impacting cell cycle arrest and proliferation. Targeting MAPK8 could inhibit tumor growth, reduce metastasis, and improve cancer treatment efficacy.

  Although ITPR3, MAPK1, and MAPK8 have been extensively investigated in various other cancers, our study identifies distinct expression patterns and potential functional roles for these genes in meningioma, highlighting the significance of tissue-specific research. These observed differences may stem from the unique molecular landscapes or microenvironmental interactions characteristic of meningioma, thereby emphasizing the necessity for further research to elucidate their mechanisms and therapeutic potential. In terms of mechanisms, functional enrichment analysis indicates a significant involvement of ITPR3 and MAPK8 in the olfactory transduction pathway and MAPK1 in the lysosome pathway, suggesting that these pathways may be critical in the progression of meningioma. The olfactory transduction pathway, traditionally associated with odor detection, involves G protein-coupled receptors and downstream calcium signaling.[30] Dysregulation of calcium homeostasis, mediated by key components such as ITPR3, has been implicated in tumor growth, invasion, and resistance to apoptosis.[31] The lysosomal pathway plays a pivotal role in intracellular degradation and autophagy, significantly impacting tumor progression by modulating cellular stress responses and survival mechanisms. MAPK1, as a key component, may affect tumor cell survival and metastasis through lysosome-mediated autophagy and apoptosis. This study underscores the involvement of the lysosomal pathway in meningioma invasion and recurrence, with a particular emphasis on lysosomal proteases and their inhibitors. Meningioma invasiveness is linked to lysosomal proteases Cathepsin B and L,[32] with elevated levels, especially Cathepsin B, distinguishing invasive from noninvasive cases. While Cathepsin B activity correlates with cell invasiveness, its expression doesn’t predict prognosis. However, its inhibitor, Stefin B, is a key predictor of recurrence. Alkaline phosphatase activity increases with meningioma grade, aiding diagnosis.[33] Endogenous cysteine protease inhibitors like Stefin A, Stefin B, and Cystatin C are more active in benign meningiomas but decrease in malignant ones.[34] The imbalance between proteases and inhibitors likely drives increased invasiveness. The lysosomal pathway contributes to meningioma invasion and recurrence through this protease-inhibitor balance, offering crucial insights for future diagnostic and treatment research.

  To better understand the roles of these biomarkers, we analyzed their subcellular localization. ITPR3 and MAPK8 are mainly in the cytoplasm, indicating a role in cytoplasmic signaling, while MAPK1 is mostly in the nucleus, suggesting involvement in transcriptional regulation and DNA damage response. These patterns provide insights into their functions and potential for targeted therapies. Additionally, TF analysis identified SMAD3 as a common TF co-targeting all 3 biomarkers, which could shed light on their transcriptional regulation. SMAD3, in particular, is crucial in meningioma growth, invasion, and grading via the TGF-β signaling pathway.[35] Phosphorylated SMAD3 (p-SMAD3) has been identified in nearly all meningioma specimens, with elevated expression levels noted in higher-grade tumors, such as grades II and III, underscoring its importance in tumor progression.[35,36] TGF-β signaling, which is mediated via SMAD3, frequently exhibits alterations in high-grade meningiomas, characterized by a marked reduction in the expression of TGF-β receptor III (TGF-βRIII), potentially contributing to enhanced tumor invasiveness.[37] Although TGF-β is capable of activating SMAD3 and downstream signaling molecules such as p38 MAPK and p44/42 MAPK, its growth-inhibitory effects are often diminished in higher-grade tumors, indicating a disruption of its regulatory functions.[38] SMAD3 may serve as a central regulator of ITPR3, MAPK1, and MAPK8, influencing calcium signaling and balancing cell proliferation and stress responses. Disruption of this network in meningioma could lead to tumor progression. Targeting SMAD3 to restore its tumor-suppressive function, possibly by enhancing TGF-βRIII or modulating SMAD3 activation, offers a potential treatment strategy. Investigating the interactions between ITPR3, MAPK1, and MAPK8 may reveal new tumorigenesis mechanisms and therapeutic targets for meningioma.

  This study highlights ITPR3, MAPK1, and MAPK8 as promising meningioma biomarkers and suggests tamoxifen as a potential treatment targeting these markers. Known for its use in breast cancer treatment,[39] tamoxifen shows strong binding to these biomarkers, suggesting its potential repurposing for meningioma therapy. Molecular docking reveals tamoxifen’s specific binding to key residues in these proteins, indicating it may modulate their activity. Additionally, tamoxifen appears to reduce meningioma risk, especially in breast cancer patients on long-term or high-dose treatment, likely due to its inhibition of estrogen receptors and modulation of growth factor pathways.[40,41] Immunohistochemical analysis of ER and PKC can identify patients who may benefit. Further studies are required to refine its use and develop personalized treatments.


## 5. Limitations
  Despite the partial external validation supporting our findings, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Chief among these is the reliance on publicly available genomic data from the GEO database, without subsequent validation in independent clinical cohorts. This may constrain the generalizability and translational relevance of our results. To address this, future studies involving larger prospective clinical samples are warranted to further verify and refine our conclusions. Furthermore, integration of multi-omics approaches – such as metabolomic and proteomic profiling – could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the molecular pathogenesis of meningiomas. Such efforts would help elucidate underlying mechanisms and identify novel therapeutic targets, thereby enriching the biological context of our findings. Finally, the identification of biomarkers such as ITPR3 opens promising avenues for targeted drug development. Advancing these molecules toward personalized therapeutic strategies could enhance treatment precision and improve clinical outcomes for patients with meningioma.


## 6. Conclusion
  This study identified TPR3, MAPK1, and MAPK8 as novel biomarkers for meningiomas, highlighting their roles in disease pathways like “olfactory transduction” and “lysosome.” SMAD3 was found to regulate these biomarkers, and tamoxifen emerged as a potential treatment based on drug prediction and molecular docking. These insights could enhance personalized meningioma treatments, though further multi-omics analyses and clinical validations are needed to understand molecular mechanisms and discover more therapies. This research advances meningioma study and targeted therapy development.
