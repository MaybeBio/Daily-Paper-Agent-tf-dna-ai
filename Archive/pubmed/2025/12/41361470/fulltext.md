# PD-L1 mediates M1 polarization of macrophages via the AIM2/IL-18/STAT1 signaling axis during sepsis


## Background
  Sepsis, a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by dysregulated host responses to infection, has emerged as a leading cause of mortality in ICU patients. Macrophages, crucial effector cells in innate immunity, play pivotal regulatory roles in sepsis pathogenesis. While Programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), a key immune checkpoint molecule, is traditionally believed to exert immunosuppressive effects through membrane anchoring, its involvement in macrophage polarization during sepsis remains unclear. This study investigated the spatial distribution of PD-L1 in macrophages and its regulatory effects on inflammatory responses during sepsis.


## Methods
  This study investigated PD-L1’s regulatory role in macrophage polarization through RNA sequencing, Immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry, molecular docking, and site-directed mutagenesis, with preliminary validation in C57BL/6 mice.


## Results
  Using GEO database analysis combined with qRT-PCR and Western blotting, we confirmed elevated PD-L1 expression in sepsis and M1-polarized macrophages. Laser scanning confocal microscopy demonstrated dual localization of PD-L1, appearing both on the plasma membrane and intracellularly within M1 macrophages. RNA sequencing revealed PD-L1’s promotion of M1 polarization through enhanced AIM2 expression in the NOD-like receptor pathway. Integrated analyses employing mass spectrometry, molecular docking, site-directed mutagenesis, and Western blotting demonstrated PD-L1 binding to AIM2, which augmented expression of downstream effector molecules (IL-18 and IFN-γ) and potentiated STAT1 activation. Silencing AIM2 by siRNA or IL-18 antagonism reversed PD-L1-induced M1 markers (IL-27, IL-6, iNOS/NO). PD-L1 was further shown to exacerbate pathological progression in septic mouse models.


## Conclusions
  Our study demonstrated that sepsis-induced PD-L1 overexpression in macrophages exacerbates pathological progression by upregulating AIM2 expression, binding to AIM2 to enhance IL-18 production, which activates STAT1 to drive M1 polarization.

## Introduction
  Sepsis is a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, characterized by rapid progression and high fatality rates, and has become one of the leading causes of death in ICU patients. Reports indicate there were approximately 48.9 million new sepsis cases worldwide in 2017, resulting in 11 million sepsis-related deaths, with mortality rates exceeding 80% in some underdeveloped countries/regions. Annual treatment costs for sepsis patients reach 14 billion United States dollars [1–3]. These findings confirm sepsis as a major global health threat lacking effective therapies, underscoring the crucial need to investigate its pathogenic mechanisms and develop therapeutic strategies.

  Dysregulated inflammatory response constitutes the core pathophysiological mechanism underlying sepsis development. As central regulators, macrophages (Mφ) coordinate inflammatory responses through M1/M2 polarization - two pivotal events in septic inflammation. Early-phase sepsis triggers M1 polarization with excessive IL-6 and NO secretion, initiating inflammatory activation. Uncontrolled M1 polarization amplifies inflammatory cascades, potentially progressing to systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), though its regulatory mechanisms remain incompletely characterized [4–8]. Although numerous regulatory mechanisms of M1 macrophage polarization have been reported, the precise pathological basis for its dysregulation during sepsis remains unelucidated due to system complexity. Current intervention strategies targeting established pathways continue to show suboptimal therapeutic efficacy [9–11].

  The programmed death-1 (PD-1)/PD-L1 axis functions as a critical negative regulatory pathway in immune homeostasis. PD-L1, a type 1 transmembrane glycoprotein predominantly expressed on tumor cells, vascular endothelial cells, dendritic cells, and macrophages [12], induces T cell exhaustion through PD-1 binding-mediated dephosphorylation of TCR/CD28, establishing immunosuppressive states [13]. In addition to its immunosuppressive functions, PD-L1 has also been reported to play a positive regulatory role in inflammatory responses. Studies have shown that PD-L1 is consistently highly expressed in peripheral blood monocytes of sepsis patients, and its expression level is an independent risk factor for 28-day mortality [14–17]. PD-L1 knockout significantly improves survival rates in mice subjected to sepsis models such as CLP or hemorrhage + CLP, although the specific molecular mechanisms remain unclear [18, 19]. While some research indicates that PD-L1 can activate interferon signaling pathways and transcription factors (e.g., SPI1/MAFB) to promote pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion in human pluripotent stem cell (hPSC)-derived macrophages [20], this has not been validated in sepsis models. Additionally, PD-L1 exacerbates lung injury through the HIF-1α pathway, but this primarily involves lung epithelial and vascular endothelial cells rather than immune cells [21]. As mentioned, it is well-established that macrophages play a critical role in the pathological progression of sepsis; however, the regulatory mechanisms of PD-L1 in macrophages under septic conditions have not been fully elucidated. Therefore, this study aims to systematically investigate the molecular mechanisms by which PD-L1 mediates the pathological process of sepsis in macrophage models, filling current research gaps.

  To address this concern, we integrated GEO clinical data, RNA sequencing, mass spectrometry and molecular docking methods, confirming that PD-L1 is significantly upregulated in inflammatory macrophages and mediates the increase of inflammatory factors such as IL-27, IL-6, and iNOS/NO via the AIM2/IL-18/STAT1 axis, thereby promoting M1 polarization of macrophages and positively regulating the pathological progression of sepsis. These findings are expected to provide new theoretical foundations and therapeutic targets for sepsis management.


## Materials and methods

### Reagents
  LPS (Escherichia coli 0111:B4) and thioglycolate were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Recombinant Human IFN-γ (Cat# 11725-HNAE) was obtained from Sinobiological (Beijing, China). Recombinant mouse IFN-γ (Cat# 50709-MNAH) was obtained from Sinobiological (Beijing, China). Trypsin, fetal bovine serum (FBS), RPMI-1640 medium and penicillin-streptomycin solution were acquired from Gibco (Carlsbad, CA, USA). The antibodies used in this study were listed in Supplemental Table 1. The treatment conditions are summarized in Supplemental Table 2.


### Mice
  Male C57BL/6 mice (6–8-week-old, 20–22 g) including wild-type (PD-L1WT/mice) and PD-L1 knockout (PD-L1KO/mice) strains were purchased from SPF Biotechnology (Beijing, China) and Gempharmatech (Jiangsu, China), respectively. Animals were maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions at 19–23 °C with 40–70% humidity and a 12 h/12 h light-dark cycle in the Experimental Animal Center of Army Medical University (Chongqing, China; Animal Use License SYXK(Yu) 20170002), with free access to food and water. All experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Army Medical University (Approval No. AMUWEC20247118). Cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) was applied to induce the polymicrobial sepsis model as previous study described [22]. The validation data was shown in Supplemental Fig. 1.


### Cell culture
  The human monocytic wild-type THP-1 cell line (PD-L1WT) was purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; Manassas, VA, USA). Engineered THP-1 variants including negative control (PD-L1NC), PD-L1-overexpressing (PD-L1HI), PD-L1-knockout (PD-L1KO) and PD-L1-mutant (PD-L1Δ35–90 with FLAG-tag) cell lines were generated by Genechem (Shanghai, China). The validation data was shown in Supplemental Fig. 2. Cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (100 U/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin). For macrophage differentiation, cells were treated with 100–200 nM phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) in complete medium for 24–48 h. Following PMA induction, cells were cultured in fresh PMA-free medium for 24 h to obtain differentiated macrophage-like cells (THP-1-M).


### GEO data analysis
  All gene expression datasets were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) in MINiML format. Intersected gene symbols across multiple platforms were identified for cross-dataset consistency. Batch correction was performed using the removeBatchEffect function (limma package, R v4.0.3) with datasets/platforms as independent batches. Statistical comparisons between groups utilized the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (p < 0.05). Hub genes were filtered by Venn diagram intersection (Venny 2.1) and visualized using Cytoscape v3.10.1.


### RNA-seq and GSEA analysis
  Total RNA was extracted from treated cells with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) and subjected to RNA sequencing on the Illumina platform (Majorbio, Shanghai, China). Raw sequencing reads were aligned to the Mus musculus reference genome GRCm39 using STAR v2.7.10b. Transcript quantification including reads count, RPKM (reads per kilobase per million), and FPKM (fragments per kilobase per million) were calculated via Majorbio’s proprietary pipeline. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DEGs-seq software with thresholds of |log2(foldchange)| ≥ 1.0 and adjusted p-value (padjust) < 0.001. Functional enrichment analyses (KEGG pathways and GSEA) were performed on the Majorbio Cloud Platform using DEGs and log2FC-ranked gene lists.


### Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR)
  Total RNA was isolated using the RNApure High-Purity Total RNA Kit (Bioteke, Beijing, China). Genomic DNA removal and cDNA synthesis were performed with the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Kyoto, Japan). Quantitative PCR reactions (20 µl total volume) included 1 µl cDNA, 5 µl TB Green Premix Ex Taq (Takara), and 0.4 µM primers. Amplifications were conducted on a CFX96 Touch™ system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) with triplicate technical replicates. Cycle threshold (Ct) values were normalized to β-actin and analyzed via the 2−ΔΔCt method. The qRT-PCR primers were listed in Supplemental Table 3.


### Western blotting
  Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) containing 1 mM PMSF for 30 min on ice. Lysates were centrifuged (12,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C) and supernatants mixed with 5× Laemmli buffer (pre-chilled). Samples were denatured by boiling (100 °C, 10 min) and stored at −80 °C until use. Proteins were resolved on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and electrotransferred to 0.22 μm PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore). Membranes were blocked with QuickBlock™ buffer (Beyotime) for 1 h at RT, then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After TBST washing, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied (1 h, RT). Signal acquisition was performed using Clarity™ ECL (Bio-Rad) and quantified via Image Lab™ software (v6.1).


### Cytokines assessment
  IL-27, IL-6, IL-18, and IFN-γ concentrations in cell supernatants or plasma were quantified using ELISA kits (Boster Biological Technology; Wuhan, China) with absorbance measurements at 450 nm. Nitric oxide (NO) levels were assessed in parallel samples using a commercial NO assay kit (Beyotime; Shanghai, China), following manufacturers’ protocols.


### Laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM)
  Cells (2 × 10⁵/well) were seeded in confocal imaging dishes (Nest Biotechnology, Wuxi, China) and processed sequentially: fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (30 min), permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-100/PBS (10 min), and three PBS washes. Blocking was performed using QuickBlock™ buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at room temperature (15 min). Primary antibodies (1:200) were applied overnight (4 °C), followed by Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:400; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) under light protection (1 h). Nuclear staining used DAPI (5 µg/mL; Beyotime) for 10 min. Imaging was conducted using an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope after triplicate PBS washes at each step.


### Flow cytometry
  The cell suspension (approximately 3 ml) was collected into a 15 ml centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min to harvest the cells. The cells were stained using an antibody against the target protein (RT, for 15 min). After staining, the cells were washed twice with 1 ml PBS and centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min to be collected. Finally, the cells were resuspended in 200 µl PBS and analyzed by flow cytometry.


### Immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS)
  Immunoprecipitation (IP) lysates were prepared with IP-specific lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), and protein concentrations were quantified using a BCA assay kit (Beyotime). Protein A/G magnetic beads were pre-incubated with anti-PD-L1 antibody or isotype control (16 h, 4 °C) before lysate incubation (16 h, 4 °C). Immunoprecipitated complexes were analyzed by LC-MS/MS (Q Exactive HF-X, Thermo Fisher Scientific; EASY-nLC 1200 system). Candidate interactions were validated via immunoblotting.


### Molecular docking
  Three-dimensional protein structures of PD-L1 and AIM2 were predicted using AlphaFold (v2.3.2). Molecular docking simulations were performed with GRAMM web service (v2.0) followed by binding interface analysis using PyMOL Molecular Graphics System (v2.4.1). Molecular interaction maps were generated through comprehensive evaluation of hydrogen bonding networks and electrostatic complementarity.


### RNA interference
  STAT1- and AIM2-specific siRNAs, scramble siRNA controls, and Lipofectamine 2000 were purchased from GenePharma (Suzhou, Jiangsu, China). For transfection, 2 × 10⁶ cells/well in 6-well plates were incubated with siRNA-Lipofectamine 2000 complexes (120 nM) for 36 h. Post-transfection cultures received fresh RPMI-1640 medium for subsequent analyses.


### Caspase-1 activity assay
  Cells were enzymatically dissociated with trypsin and gently resuspended in the cell supernatant. The cell suspension was centrifuged at 600 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was carefully aspirated. The pellet was washed once with PBS. Subsequently, the cells were lysed on ice for 30 min using a pre-chilled lysis buffer (50–100 µl per 2 × 10⁶ cells). The lysate was centrifuged at 16,000–20,000 × g for 10–15 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was transferred to a chilled tube. A reaction mixture was prepared with 80 µl assay buffer, 10 µl supernatant, and 10 µl of the caspase-1-specific substrate Ac-YVAD-pNA (2 mM). After incubation at 37 °C for 1–2 h, the absorbance was measured at 405 nm. The concentration of liberated pNA was determined from a standard curve and normalized to the total protein content of each sample. Caspase-1 activity was expressed as fold change relative to the NC group.


### Mouse serum and somatic cell isolation
  Male C57BL/6 mice were subjected to CLP. Blood was collected via retroorbital venous plexus under anesthesia 12 h post-CLP. Serum was separated by centrifugation (2,000 × g, 15 min, 4 °C), while monocytes were isolated by Solarbio peripheral blood monocytes isolation reagent kit (Beijing, China). Serum IL-27, IL-6, AST, ALT, BUN, cTnI, and NO levels were analyzed by ELISA and Griess assay. Total RNA extracted ffrom monocytes was reverse-transcribed for quantitative real-time PCR analysis of IL-27, IL-6, and NOS2 mRNA expression. Peritoneal cells were isolated from mice through peritoneal lavage with 4 mL PBS and subjected to flow cytometry and peritoneal macrophages preparation.


### Statistical analysis
  For the GEO dataset analysis, the Wilcoxon test was used. For bar charts, the data are expressed as the mean ± SEM from 3 independent experiments. The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was verified by Levene’s test. Multi-group comparisons were analyzed by one-way ANOVA with Fisher’s LSD post hoc test. Intergroup differences were evaluated using two-tailed Student’s t-test. Survival rates were compared by Mantel-Cox log-rank test. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS v16.0 (IBM Corp.) and GraphPad Prism v6.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.


## Results

### Upregulated PD-L1 expression in sepsis and its aberrant cytoplasmic-nuclear distribution in THP-1-derived macrophages
  To investigate PD-L1 expression dynamics in sepsis, we analyzed clinical datasets (GSE134347 and GSE65682) from the GEO database. Transcriptomic profiling revealed significant PD-L1 upregulation in septic patients (Fig. 1A). In vitro stimulation of THP-1-derived macrophages with LPS + IFN-γ– a classical M1 polarization protocol – demonstrated coordinated increases in both PD-L1 mRNA (Fig. 1B) and protein levels (Fig. 1C). Flow cytometry also confirmed enhanced PD-L1 expression in LPS + IFN-γ-induced THP-1-M (Fig. 1D). LSCM further revealed atypical cytoplasmic-nuclear PD-L1 localization alongside membrane distribution in stimulated cells, with Pearson colocalization coefficient showing a significant increase in nuclear PD-L1 following LPS and IFN-γ stimulation (Fig. 1E). We further examined the expression of PD-L1 in THP-1-M cells under individual LPS, IFN-γ, and their combined stimulation via Western blotting. The results demonstrated that the combined stimulation of LPS and IFN-γ resulted in the highest total protein expression level of PD-L1 (Supplemental Fig. 3 A). Moreover, across different cellular fractions (particularly in the cytoplasmic and nuclear components), the expression was highest when induced by the combination of LPS and IFN-γ (Supplemental Fig. 3B). These findings preliminarily reveal the expression pattern of PD-L1 in THP-1-M cells under classical M1-polarizing stimulation with LPS + IFN-γ, while also suggesting the presence of an unconventional cytoplasmic-nuclear localization pattern of PD-L1 in this context, which differs from its commonly recognized membrane-bound distribution.

### RNA sequencing identified PD-L1 as a driver of M1 macrophage polarization
  To investigate the role of PD-L1 in M1 macrophage polarization, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis was performed on LPS + IFN-γ-induced PD-L1-overexpressing THP-1 macrophages (PD-L1HI/THP-1-M) and negative control cells (PD-L1NC/THP-1-M). The results demonstrated that, in addition to the elevated expression of classical M1-type proinflammatory genes (e.g., IL-6 and NOS2, encoding inducible nitric oxide synthase, iNOS), the proinflammatory cytokine IL-27—an emerging biomarker predictive of clinical outcomes in sepsis—was also significantly upregulated (Fig. 2A-C). Retrospective validation by qRT-PCR and ELISA further confirmed elevated expression levels of IL-27, IL-6, and NOS2/NO in PD-L1HI/THP-1-M (Fig. 2D, E). In contrast, LPS + IFN-γ-stimulated PD-L1KO/THP-1-M exhibited markedly reduced expression of IL-27, IL-6, and NOS2/NO compared to PD-L1WT/THP-1-M (Fig. 2F, G). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that PD-L1 overexpression under inflammatory conditions enhances M1 polarization of THP-1-derived macrophages, a role distinct from its classical immune-suppressive function.

### Transcriptome sequencing reveals PD-L1 drives M1 macrophage polarization via activation of the NOD-like receptor pathway
  To elucidate the mechanistic basis of PD-L1-driven M1 macrophage polarization, we initially performed KEGG pathway analysis on RNA sequencing data. Under LPS + IFN-γ stimulation, the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway ranked highest among all activated pathways in conventional THP-1-M (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, single-gene GSEA analysis of three independent datasets (GSE57065, GSE28750, and GSE95233) from the GEO database identified the NOD-like receptor pathway as the most significantly PD-L1-associated activated pathway, demonstrating a positive correlation with PD-L1 expression (Fig. 3B, C). Consistent with these findings, GSEA analysis of LPS + IFN-γ-stimulated PD-L1HI/THP-1-M and PD-L1NC/THP-1-M RNA-seq data confirmed PD-L1-dependent activation of the NOD-like receptor pathway (Fig. 3D). Collectively, these multi-modal analyses strongly implicate PD-L1 as a regulator of M1 macrophage polarization through modulation of the NOD-like receptor pathway.

### Multi-omics profiling reveals PD-L1 enhances AIM2 expression and physically interacts with AIM2 in the NOD-like receptor pathway
  To delineate PD-L1’s regulatory mechanism on the NOD-like receptor pathway, we conducted IP-MS on LPS + IFN-γ-stimulated THP-1-M. IP-MS identified 311 proteins bound to PD-L1 in the PBS group and 289 in the LPS + IFN-γ group (excluding IgG-associated proteins). Venn analysis revealed 208 LPS + IFN-γ-specific PD-L1-interacting proteins (Fig. 4A), suggesting their stimulus-dependent association. Further intersection of these 208 proteins with LPS + IFN-γ-induced M1 differentially expressed genes (M1 DEGs) and sepsis-associated DEGs from datasets GSE57065, GSE28750, and GSE95233 (sepsis DEGs) identified AIM2 as the sole overlapping molecule (Fig. 4B). AIM2, a component of the NOD-like receptor pathway, cooperates with NLRP3 via the ASC adaptor to activate caspase-1-dependent inflammasome signaling, driving IL-18 and IL-1β maturation, pyroptosis, and innate immunity [23]. These findings collectively suggest that PD-L1 modulates the NOD-like receptor pathway through AIM2. To test this hypothesis, the 208 candidate proteins identified by mass spectrometry were subjected to GO enrichment analysis. Notably, AIM2 ranked prominently within the inflammatory response gene set (Fig. 4C). MS/MS spectra and Co-IP confirmed direct PD-L1-AIM2 binding (Fig. 4D, E). RNA-seq and retrospective qRT-PCR/Western blotting validated PD-L1-dependent upregulation of AIM2 (Fig. 4F, G). Molecular docking using AlphaFold-derived full-length PD-L1 and AIM2 models generated five high-scoring complexes via GRAMM docking service. The top-ranked model predicted seven hydrogen bonds and one salt bridge between PD-L1 (residues 35–90) and AIM2 (Fig. 4H-J). These findings collectively suggest that PD-L1 regulates M1 macrophage polarization through AIM2-mediated modulation of the NOD-like receptor pathway. Furthermore, we examined caspase-1 activity and the expression of cleaved caspase-1 upon PD-L1 overexpression. The results consistently demonstrated that under LPS and IFN-γ stimulation, PD-L1 could enhance caspase-1 activity and promote the expression of cleaved caspase-1 (Supplemental Fig. 4), suggesting that PD-L1 mediates the activation of the AIM2 inflammasome.

### PD-L1 overexpression enhances expression of AIM2 downstream effector IL-18 and augments STAT1 activation
  As previously reported, IL-18 and IL-1β are critical downstream effectors of AIM2-mediated signaling [24]. We thus investigated PD-L1’s regulatory role in their expression. RNA sequencing revealed that PD-L1 overexpression significantly upregulated IL-18 (but not IL-1β) (Fig. 5A), prompting focused investigation on IL-18. Retrospective validation via qRT-PCR and ELISA confirmed PD-L1’s positive regulation of IL-18 (Fig. 5B). In our previous study, we demonstrated that PD-L1 enhances the expression of IL-27, IL-6, and iNOS/NO production. Notably, among the transcriptional regulators governing these molecules, STAT1 emerged as one of the few transcription factors capable of coordinately regulating all three molecular outputs, particularly under co-stimulatory conditions involving both LPS and IFN-γ. Mechanistically, AIM2 is known to enhance IL-18 expression, which subsequently elevates IFN-γ to activate STAT1 [23, 25]. Our qRT-PCR, ELISA and Western blotting analyses corroborated PD-L1’s promotion of IFN-γ expression and STAT1 activation (Fig. 5C, D). To further validate PD-L1’s regulatory impact on STAT1, we analyzed datasets GSE57065, GSE28750, and GSE95233. Single-gene GSEA identified 111 PD-L1-correlated genes. Core gene analysis via Cytoscape highlighted STAT1 centrality within this cohort. Subsequent GO enrichment of 40 core genes showed 26 were functionally linked to JAK-STAT pathway activation (Fig. 5E-G). Collectively, these findings suggest PD-L1 upregulates AIM2 to amplify IL-18 expression, which triggers IFN-γ-dependent STAT1 activation, thereby polarizing macrophages toward an M1 phenotype.

### RNAi, cytokine neutralization, and mutagenesis demonstrated PD-L1 drives macrophage M1 polarization through the AIM2/IL-18/STAT1 signaling axis
  To further validate whether PD-L1 regulates macrophage polarization through the AIM2/IL-18/STAT1 signaling axis, we employed multiple approaches including AIM2/STAT1-specific siRNAs, IL-18 neutralizing antibody, and truncated PD-L1 mutants disrupting the PD-L1/AIM2 binding interface (residues 35–90). As shown in Fig. 6A, AIM2 knockdown (compared to scramble siRNA control) effectively reversed PD-L1 overexpression-induced hyperactivation of STAT1. Similarly, IL-18 neutralization (with appropriate isotype control) significantly attenuated PD-L1-mediated upregulation of IFN-γ and STAT1 activation (Fig. 6B, C). Disruption of PD-L1/AIM2 interaction through binding interface truncation likewise abolished PD-L1-induced STAT1 activation (Fig. 6D). We further examined how these interventions affected inflammatory polarization markers. Notably, AIM2/STAT1 silencing, IL-18 blockade, and PD-L1/AIM2 binding interface truncation all substantially suppressed PD-L1 overexpression-driven upregulation of IL-6, IL-27, and NOS2 expression (Fig. 6E-H). These collective findings demonstrate that PD-L1 orchestrates STAT1 activation through AIM2/IL-18 signaling, thereby modulating the expression of key M1 polarization markers in macrophages. The sequential evidence from genetic perturbations, cytokine neutralization, and protein interaction analysis establishes the PD-L1/AIM2/IL-18/STAT1 axis as a critical regulatory pathway controlling macrophage polarization.

### PD-L1 genetic ablation confers survival benefits and multi-organ protection in murine sepsis models
  To validate the pathophysiological relevance of PD-L1-mediated regulation observed in vitro, we established a CLP model using PD-L1 knockout mice (Fig. 7A). PD-L1 deficiency significantly improved survival outcomes compared to wild-type controls following CLP induction (Fig. 7B). Systemic inflammatory profiling revealed substantial downregulation of circulating IL-6, IL-27, and NO in PD-L1 knockout mice. Parallel analysis of monocytes demonstrated coordinated suppression of IL-6, IL-27, and NOS2 mRNA expression (Fig. 7C, D). Critical organ functional assessments showed PD-L1 ablation attenuated CLP-induced elevation of serum biomarkers for hepatic injury (ALT/AST), renal dysfunction (BUN), and myocardial damage (cTn1) (Fig. 7E). Furthermore, we performed flow cytometry on peritoneal cells collected 24 h after CLP modeling in wild-type and PD-L1 knockout mice. The flow cytometry results demonstrated that PD-L1 knockout significantly reduced M1 polarization of peritoneal macrophages. Although no statistically significant difference was observed between the two groups, the proportion of M2-type cells showed a notable increasing trend (Supplemental Fig. 5). These in vivo findings collectively confirm the generalized pro-inflammatory nature of PD-L1 signaling across experimental systems, demonstrating its dual regulatory capacity in both cellular immunity and multi-organ pathophysiology during sepsis.

## Discussion
  Macrophage M1 polarization plays a dual and critical role in the pathological progression of sepsis: it serves as a defensive mechanism to eliminate pathogens during early stages, yet its excessive activation drives cytokine storms and multi-organ failure. In the initial phase of infection, pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs, e.g., LPS) or pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IFN-γ, TNF-α) induce M1 polarization, characterized by high expression of pro-inflammatory mediators (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) and NADPH oxidase-dependent reactive oxygen species (ROS) production to directly eradicate pathogens. Concurrently, M1 macrophages enhance phagocytic capacity, antigen presentation, and Th1 immune activation, thereby limiting systemic infection. However, persistent M1 polarization establishes a pro-inflammatory positive feedback loop. Excessive release of cytokines (e.g., IL-6 and TNF-α, whose levels correlate positively with clinical organ injury scores) amplifies systemic inflammation via TLR4/NF-κB and JAK/STAT signaling pathways, directly contributing to tissue damage. Metabolic reprogramming in M1 macrophages, particularly HIF-1α-mediated aerobic glycolysis, sustains their pro-inflammatory state, while microcirculatory dysfunction and coagulation activation exacerbate hypoxia-induced injury [26–31]. Despite advances in understanding regulatory mechanisms of M1 polarization, the complexity of its dysregulation in sepsis remains incompletely elucidated. Current therapeutic strategies targeting these pathways have yet to achieve satisfactory clinical efficacy.

  PD-L1, the principal ligand of the immune checkpoint molecule PD-1, plays a pivotal role in maintaining immune homeostasis by suppressing T cell activation and cytokine secretion.

  During inflammatory responses, PD-L1 prevents excessive immune activation and tissue damage, while its aberrant expression is also implicated in immune evasion. As critical expressers of PD-L1, macrophages exhibit regulatory mechanisms closely tied to the inflammatory microenvironment. In tumor or infection-related microenvironments, inflammatory cytokines markedly upregulate PD-L1 expression on macrophages. For instance, IFN-γ secreted by activated T cells enhances PD-L1 expression via the JAK-STAT pathway, while IL-10 and IL-27 promote its expression through the Akt/PI3K and STAT3 pathways, respectively [32]. Under hypoxic conditions, HIF-1α directly binds to the PD-L1 promoter, driving its expression and inhibiting T cell activation [33]. In chronic inflammatory conditions such as arthritis, the PD-L1/PD-1 axis suppresses effector T cell function, facilitating inflammation resolution and self-tolerance [34, 35]. In this study, we investigated the inflammatory regulatory role of PD-L1 in THP-1- derived macrophages. THP-1, a human-derived acute monocytic leukemia cell line with intrinsic monocytic characteristics, was selected to optimally recapitulate the inflammatory responses of human peripheral blood monocytes during sepsis and to align with validation using GEO datasets. We first demonstrated through GEO dataset analyses and in vitro experiments that PD-L1 is highly expressed in macrophages under septic conditions or M1-polarized states, with an unconventional intracellular distribution pattern (Fig. 1), suggesting compartment-specific roles of PD-L1 in inflammation regulation. RNA sequencing revealed that PD-L1 overexpression promoted M1 polarization in macrophages, characterized by increased synthesis of inflammatory markers including IL-27, IL-6, and iNOS/NO, whereas PD-L1 knockout suppressed these markers (Fig. 2). These findings indicate a predominantly pro-inflammatory role of PD-L1 in THP-1-derived macrophages, potentially overriding its canonical membrane-associated anti-inflammatory effects. Bioinformatic integration of RNA sequencing data and GEO datasets preliminarily identified activation of the NOD-like receptor pathway by PD-L1 (Fig. 3). Subsequent immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry coupled with molecular docking analyses further revealed that AIM2, a classical pro-inflammatory molecule within the NOD-like receptor pathway, exhibited enhanced expression and physical interaction with PD-L1 upon PD-L1 overexpression (Fig. 4). Retrospective analysis of RNA sequencing data demonstrated elevated expression of IL-18, a key downstream effector of AIM2, accompanied by upregulated IFN-γ (Fig. 5A-C). Accumulating evidence has established that IFN-γ triggers STAT1 pathway activation [36–40]. Building on our previous findings demonstrating PD-L1-mediated upregulation of IL-27, IL-6, and iNOS/NO production, mechanistic analyses revealed that STAT1 emerged as a unique transcription factor capable of coordinately regulating these three inflammatory mediators under combined LPS and IFN-γ stimulation. Based on this compelling evidence of STAT1’s integrative regulatory role, we prioritized investigating its activation status as the central mechanistic node underlying PD-L1-driven proinflammatory responses. Western blotting and GEO dataset validation confirmed PD-L1-driven STAT1 activation (Fig. 5D-G), supporting a putative PD-L1/AIM2/IL-18/STAT1 signaling axis governing M1 polarization. To validate this axis, systematic interventions were performed using siRNA-mediated silencing, neutralizing antibodies, and truncation mutagenesis. STAT1 silencing abolished PD-L1-induced pro-inflammatory effects, underscoring its role as a terminal regulator. Similarly, AIM2 knockdown, disruption of the PD-L1-AIM2 binding interface, or IL-18 neutralization (concomitantly blocking PD-L1-driven IFN-γ upregulation) reversed both the pro-inflammatory cytokine surge and STAT1 activation caused by PD-L1 overexpression (Fig. 6). These findings collectively establish the PD-L1/AIM2/IL-18/STAT1 axis as a critical driver of macrophage M1 polarization. To extend the relevance of in vitro observations, we evaluated PD-L1 deletion in a murine sepsis model. PD-L1 knockout significantly improved survival rates, attenuated systemic inflammation, and alleviated histopathological damage in vital organs (heart, liver, and kidney) of septic mice (Fig. 7), corroborating the pro-inflammatory role of PD-L1 identified in cellular studies.

  As previously mentioned, although PD-L1 has been reported to have a pro-inflammatory role in various models and cells—particularly as it is highly expressed in septic patients and serves as an independent predictor of mortality risk [14–21]—some studies have also reported that PD-L1 can negatively regulate inflammatory responses. Tang et al. found that in LPS-induced bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs), PD-L1 inhibited M1 polarization via the JAK-STAT3 pathway [41]. In glioblastoma, PD-L1 promotes an immunosuppressive microenvironment by driving tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) toward M2 polarization and suppressing effector T cells, thereby promoting tumor progression and resistance to immunotherapy. Wei et al. also reported that exogenous addition of PD-L1 promoted macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype [42]. Similarly, Zhang et al. found that treating CLP-induced septic mice with anti-PD-L1 antibodies increased levels of TNF-α and IL-6 and decreased IL-10 in peripheral blood [43]. The team also treated peripheral blood monocytes from patients with septic shock with PD-L1 antibodies and obtained results consistent with the animal experiments [44], confirming the anti-inflammatory effect of non-intracellular PD-L1. Therefore, the dual regulatory role of PD-L1 in inflammation may be explained by its subcellular localization. Gao et al. previously reported that deacetylation at site 263 in the cytoplasmic tail of PD-L1 by HDAC2 mediates its translocation from the plasma membrane to the intracellular compartment, challenging the conventional belief that PD-L1 is primarily located on the cell surface and expanding the functional understanding of PD-L1 to different subcellular levels [45].

  From the observed inflammatory responses, whether through exogenous PD-L1 promoting M2 polarization or neutralization of membrane-bound PD-L1 facilitating M1 polarization, the evidence suggests that membrane-bound PD-L1 inhibits inflammation. However, our study found that in macrophages stimulated with LPS combined with IFN-γ (LPS + IFN-γ), PD-L1 was present not only on the membrane but also intracellularly (Fig. 1E). This study further demonstrated that overexpression of PD-L1 overall promotes the inflammatory response, which, together with the aforementioned literature, suggests that PD-L1 may possess both anti-inflammatory and pro-inflammatory roles. Given the coexistence of membrane-bound and intracellular PD-L1, we hypothesize that under these conditions, intracellular PD-L1 exerts a pro-inflammatory effect that may override the anti-inflammatory function of membrane-bound PD-L1 (supported by evidence: antagonizing membrane PD-L1 enhanced baseline inflammation [43, 44]).

  It is worth noting that we compared PD-L1 expression under stimulation with LPS alone versus LPS + IFN-γ. The results showed that upon LPS + IFN-γ induction, both the total expression level and intracellular distribution of PD-L1 were significantly greater than with LPS alone (Supplemental Fig. 3). Although Tang et al. also reported that PD-L1 inhibits inflammation, their experiments used BMDMs induced with LPS alone. Thus, we speculate that under Tang’s experimental conditions, the intracellular distribution of PD-L1 might be limited, resulting in weaker pro-inflammatory effects and allowing the anti-inflammatory role of membrane-bound PD-L1 to dominate, leading to an overall anti-inflammatory outcome. Therefore, while our findings may appear contradictory to some conclusions, they actually reveal a new perspective: PD-L1 may have dual regulatory functions in macrophages—membrane-bound PD-L1 mediates anti-inflammatory effects (effective at low levels), while intracellular PD-L1 may promote inflammation (requiring relatively higher levels). The ultimate effect on inflammation depends on the expression level and subcellular distribution (membrane vs. intracellular) of PD-L1, which may vary under different stimulation conditions (e.g., LPS vs. LPS + IFN-γ) and across different macrophage types. In fact, consistent with our study, some reports have also indicated that PD-L1 may upregulate the expression of inflammatory cytokines in macrophages [20], further supporting the context-dependent role of PD-L1 under different stimuli and in different macrophage subtypes.

  While our integrated multi-omics approach delineated PD-L1’s regulatory role in macrophage inflammatory responses, this study has limitations requiring future clarification. Contrary to the canonical view of PD-L1 as a membrane-anchored anti-inflammatory mediator, we identified its intracellular localization and overall pro-inflammatory functional dominance. While this study did not directly investigate the intracellular trafficking mechanism of PD-L1, we postulate that the intracellular pool of PD-L1 likely serves as the principal driver of macrophage M1 polarization, thereby orchestrating the predominant pro-inflammatory phenotype. Systematic spatial mapping of PD-L1 will be prioritized to dissect its compartment-specific regulatory effects. Notably, we report the first evidence of direct PD-L1-AIM2 interaction mediating downstream pathway activation. However, our truncation analysis targeting predicted binding domains lacked single-residue resolution. Future studies should precisely map the binding epitopes to inform therapeutic targeting. Given AIM2’s involvement in the NOD-like receptor pathway and its known crosstalk with NF-κB/MAPK cascades, further exploration of PD-L1’s regulatory effects on these inflammatory networks is warranted. Future studies will also systematically investigate PD-L1’s multi-dimensional regulation of NOD-like receptor-mediated immune responses, including but not limited to pyroptosis, apoptosis, and efferocytosis processes.


## Conclusions
  Our integrated findings demonstrate that macrophage PD-L1 expression is significantly upregulated under inflammatory conditions, exhibiting positive correlations with pro-inflammatory mediators including IL-27, IL-6, and iNOS/NO. Through multi-omics integration, we identified PD-L1 as a critical facilitator of AIM2 upregulation and established their direct interaction, which orchestrates IL-18 secretion to activate the IFN-γ/STAT1 signaling cascade. The pathophysiological relevance was further corroborated in vivo, where PD-L1 knockout markedly attenuated sepsis-induced multiorgan dysfunction. These results collectively delineate a previously unrecognized PD-L1/AIM2/IL-18/STAT1 regulatory axis driving macrophage M1-type polarization during sepsis. These results lay the groundwork for innovative therapeutic strategies targeting macrophage polarization dynamics during sepsis.
