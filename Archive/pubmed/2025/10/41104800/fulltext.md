# CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 Enhance Acquired Drought Tolerance by Mediating Flavonoid Biosynthesis in Camellia sinensis


## ABSTRACT
  Drought priming is a critical agronomic strategy for enhancing plant drought tolerance, yet the optimal priming intensity and transcriptional regulatory mechanisms underlying subsequent drought responses in the tea plant ( Camellia sinensis ) remain poorly characterised. In this study, we systematically evaluated tea plants exposed to recurrent drought stress under varying priming intensities. Results demonstrated that moderate drought priming specifically conferred superior drought tolerance compared to non‐primed controls. Integrated metabolomic and transcriptomic profiling identified flavonoid biosynthesis as the key pathway associated with priming‐induced drought resilience. Exogenous flavonoid application and overexpression of six biosynthesis genes (CsCHS, CsCHI, CsFLS, CsDFR, CsANS and CsANR) functionally validated flavonoids' role in drought adaptation. Notably, transcriptional regulators CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 were identified as hub transcription factors demonstrating transcriptional activation potential towards flavonoid biosynthesis. Combinatorial transient overexpression and silencing assays revealed that both CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 coordinately upregulate flavonoid biosynthesis genes, redirecting metabolic flux towards flavonoid accumulation to enhance drought tolerance. Multimodal validation through yeast one‐hybrid assays, dual‐luciferase reporter systems and electrophoretic mobility shift assays, as well as molecular docking, confirmed or simulated direct binding of CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 to promoter regions of flavonoid biosynthesis genes for transcriptional activation. These findings establish a synergistic regulatory model where CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 cooperatively enhance flavonoid accumulation through transcriptional reprogramming, thereby conferring acquired drought tolerance. This study provides mechanistic insights for developing adaptive cultivation practices and advances molecular breeding strategies for drought‐resilient tea cultivars.


## Introduction
  Drought represents a critical environmental stressor that significantly impairs plant growth, development and productivity (Sato et al. 2024). Under current climate change scenarios, rising global temperatures and altered precipitation patterns are triggering more frequent, intense and prolonged drought events globally (Sharma et al. 2022). This poses particular challenges for tea plants ( Camellia sinensis ), an economically vital crop predominantly cultivated in tropical and subtropical regions (Wei et al. 2018). Projections indicate substantial increases in drought frequency and severity across tea‐growing areas, significantly elevating the risk of recurrent short‐term drought episodes. These conditions present formidable obstacles to tea plant growth and sustainable tea production. So far, research on drought stress in tea plants has mainly focused on the physiological response patterns under single drought stress and the identification and biological function analysis of related genes. Without comparing drought‐tolerant and nondrought‐tolerant tea plants, it is difficult to explore the key genes and regulatory mechanisms to enhance drought tolerance. Thus, clarifying the drought tolerance mechanism of the tea plant and developing a new, effective strategy for cultivating and breeding tea plants is crucial for the tea plant to withstand the current and future climate models.

  Drought priming (also termed hardening, conditioning or training) has emerged as a crucial adaptive strategy for plants to enhance drought tolerance (Hilker et al. 2016). This process involves pre‐exposure to moderate stress that induces physiological, metabolic and genetic modifications, creating a ‘stress memory’ mechanism (Lämke and Bäurle 2017; Oberkofler et al. 2021), enabling plants to mount more rapid and robust responses to subsequent drought events while enhancing both immediate adaptation and transgenerational resilience (Galviz et al. 2020; Sharma et al. 2022). For tea plants, elucidating the regulatory mechanisms of priming‐acquired drought tolerance is critical to developing effective strategies for enhancing drought resilience. Substantial evidence indicated that plants possess drought stress memory, a phenomenon mediated through transcriptional reprogramming and sustained alterations in transcription factor activity. These molecular adjustments regulate key protective proteins and signalling metabolites, enabling enhanced responsiveness to subsequent drought events. This adaptive mechanism facilitates more robust and rapid stress responses, ultimately mitigating drought‐induced damage (Ganie et al. 2024; Sharma et al. 2022). Among these, the flavonoids, a diverse class of over 6000 phenylpropanoid‐derived compounds, play particularly vital roles as potent antioxidants. They effectively scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby reducing oxidative damage induced by drought stress (Dong and Lin 2021). For example, the β‐glucosidase 17 contributed to flavonoid accumulation to enhance  Medicago truncatula  drought and salt tolerance (Du et al. 2024). Additionally, the accumulation of flavonoids promoted the drought tolerance of apples and Populus ussuriensis (Li et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2020). In tea plants, the tea polyphenol was found to mediate CsMYB77 regulation of CsPOD44 to enhance root drought resistance (Xu et al. 2025). Although studies have shown that flavonoids are the key metabolites of plants to resist drought stress, the function and transcription regulation of flavonoids in tea plants have not been well clarified under drought stress, especially under drought priming.

  Generally, the biosynthesis of flavonoids in plants is regulated by encoding different key enzyme genes, and these genes are also regulated by many transcription factors (TF). The YABBY TF is plant‐specific and is a small family of TFs that consists of an N‐terminal C2C2 zinc‐finger domain and a C‐terminal helix–loop–helix domain (YABBY domain) (Bowman and Smyth 1999). YABBY was first thought to be limited to regulation on leaf polarity establishment and flower organ development in plants (Han et al. 2024). However, some studies have reported the function of YABBY in secondary metabolism, especially in flavonoid biosynthesis and response to drought stress (Cui et al. 2024; Kayani et al. 2022; Xiong et al. 2023; Zhao et al. 2017). Additionally, the R2R3‐MYB TFs are the central factors that have been systematically documented as direct activators or repressors in flavonoid biosynthesis. For example, the MYB114 belonging to Subgroup 6 (SG6) R2R3‐MYB family can directly bind to the promoter regions of flavonoid biosynthesis genes and activate or repress their expression to regulate flavonoid biosynthesis in numerous species (Li et al. 2020; Stracke et al. 2007; Tirumalai et al. 2019). Due to the species‐dependent functional exertion of TFs, the expression of the same TFs varies in different species. Although the function of YABBY and MYB114 in flavonoid biosynthesis and drought stress response has been studied in other plants, the relevance of YABBY and MYB114 to tea plant flavonoid biosynthesis and acquired drought‐tolerance formation remains obscure.

  In this study, we set the different degrees of drought priming of tea plants and filtered the moderate drought priming that could endow tea plants with more tolerance to drought stress. The multiomics were deployed to investigate the internal precise mechanism of acquired drought tolerance of tea plants formed by drought priming. Flavonoids were identified as the key active metabolites in enhancing acquired drought tolerance. Moreover, the regulatory network of flavonoid synthesis governing the drought tolerance in tea plants was constructed. These results provide an important theoretical basis for the breeding and genetic improvement of drought‐tolerant tea cultivars.


## Results

### Moderate Drought Priming Enhanced the Acquired Drought Tolerance of Tea Plant Under Drought Stress
  To study the effect of drought priming on tea plants, we first subjected them to an initial drought stress treatment (Figure 1a and Figure S1a). During the initial drought treatment cycle, we systematically evaluated physiological parameters and examined leaf anatomical features in tea plants subjected to varying intensities of drought stress (Figure 1b–j). After confirming that the tea plants were under drought stress, the water supply was restored. These tea plants, which experienced mild (T1), moderate (T2), and severe (T3) drought stress after the restoration of the water supply, were named R‐T1, R‐T2 and R‐T3, respectively. The tea plants that were supplied with normal water during the same period were named CK. We found that tea plants did not easily recover after severe drought stress, as manifested by leaf damage (Figure 1b) and lower leaf relative water content (LRWC) (Figure 1c), chlorophyll fluorescence (Figure 1d) and the maximum efficiency of the Photosystem II photochemistry (F v /F m) value (Figure 1e). Furthermore, analysis showed that while R‐T1 tea plants had recovered to some extent, R‐T2 and R‐T3 maintained relatively high levels of MDA, H2O2 and O2⋅−, with R‐T3 exhibiting the highest levels (Figure 1f–h). Additionally, the survival rate of tea plants after rewatering in different drought‐priming degrees was counted, and the results showed that the tea plants in R‐T3 had a significantly higher mortality rate than in R‐T1 and R‐T2 (Table S1). The result of the tea leaf anatomical structure showed that the cuticle thickness of tea leaves increased significantly under C1‐T1 and C1‐T2 and remained at a high level after rewatering compared with the corresponding CK. In contrast, the thickness of the upper epidermis, palisade tissue, spongy tissue, lower epidermis and mesophyll decreased with intensifying drought and recovered in R‐T1 and R‐T2, especially in R‐T1 (Figure 1i,j). Considering the irreversible and severe damage caused by severe drought to tea plants, we chose mild and moderate drought as the degree of drought priming for further analysis.

  To further explore the mechanisms underlying tea plants' physiological responses to recurrent drought, we subjected tea plants that had experienced varying degrees of drought to a second round of drought stress, using nonprimed plants as controls (Figure 1a and Figure S1b). As the drought stress intensified, the phenotype of all tea plants displayed a gradually worse status (Figure 1k), and the LRWC, chlorophyll fluorescence level, F v /F m values (Figure 1l–n), and stomatal aperture (Figure 1o,p) steadily declined. The MDA, H2O2 and O2⋅− content and the levels of H2O2 and O2⋅− as revealed by DAB and NBT staining of tea plants increased with the drought intensity in repeated drought events (Figure 1q–t). Under drought stress, while all tea plants exhibited phenotypic and physiological impairments, the tolerance capacity was more pronounced in tea plants that had undergone moderate drought priming, which resulted in less damage to tea plants experiencing moderate drought priming when subjected to varying degrees of drought stress, especially when faced with moderate drought stress (Figure 1k–t). Combining tea plant phenotype information and physiological measurements, we confirmed that drought priming can enhance the drought tolerance of tea plants, particularly in moderate drought priming. Thus, R‐T2 samples were used to compare with tea plants that have not experienced drought priming when facing new drought stress. The tea plants that had experienced moderate drought stress were named R‐CK. When R‐CK was subjected to mild (T1) and moderate (T2) drought stress, the samples were named C2‐T1 and C2‐T2, respectively. Those samples were selected further to explore the formation mechanism of acquired drought tolerance.


### Flavonoids Are the Key Imprinted Metabolites in Drought Priming in Tea Plants Under Drought Stress
  A comprehensive untargeted metabolomics approach was employed to compare drought‐responsive metabolites in tea plants with and without prior drought priming when subjected to recurrent drought stress. The peak intensity in the samples' total ion chromatogram (TIC) and the tight clustering of QC samples in the principal component analysis (PCA) plot indicated that the mass spectral data were highly reproducible and stable (Figure S2a,b). To identify key metabolites contributing to drought‐tolerant memory in tea plants, we analysed differential metabolite accumulation and detected 313 different accumulated metabolites (DAMs) in drought‐primed plants under stress compared to controls (Table S3). The PCA of DAMs was performed to visualise the metabolic variations in tea plants, demonstrating the high distinction of metabolic traits among the samples (Figure 2a). In 313 DAMs, flavonoid metabolites showed a higher proportion of all DAMs with 15.65% (Figure 2b). Subsequently, metabolites highly accumulated in experienced drought‐priming groups (R‐CK, C2‐T1 and C2‐T2, especially in C2‐T2 compared with CK‐T2) were further identified to ascertain the key metabolites that contribute to the formation of drought‐tolerant memory in tea plants. In total, 187 metabolites accumulated to higher levels in R‐CK (64 upregulated DAMs), C2‐T1 (57 upregulated DAMs), and C2‐T2 (94 upregulated DAMs) than in their control (CK‐CK, CK‐T1 and CK‐T2) (Table S4 and Figure S2c). Among the upregulated DAMs, flavonoids accounted for the highest percentage of 16.04% (Figure 2c). Further analysis found that those flavonoid metabolites displayed specificity accumulated in different drought stress degrees, and most of the flavonoid metabolites were highly accumulated in C2‐T2 (Figure 2d), implying that these flavonoid metabolites may be key metabolites in experienced drought‐priming tea plants and play an important role when they are faced with drought stress again.

  To confirm the changes in flavonoids, we determined the total flavonoid content of tea plants that experienced drought‐priming versus those that had not when exposed to drought stress again and found that the total flavonoid content of tea plants that had undergone drought‐priming continued to increase as the degree of drought worsened. In contrast, the flavonoid content of tea plants that had not experienced drought priming showed a decreasing and then increasing trend. Moreover, drought‐primed tea plants exhibited significantly higher flavonoid content compared with nonprimed plants under both mild and moderate drought stress conditions (Figure 2e). To determine whether flavonoids could enhance the resistance of tea plants to drought stress, we randomly selected four flavonoids, including epicatechin, epicatechin gallate, saponarin and astragalin, to evaluate their effect on the drought tolerance of tea plants. The tea plants sprayed with flavonoid solution and purified water were subjected to drought stress for 24 h (Figure 2f). Under drought stress, the tea plants treated with the flavonoid solutions showed improved status compared to the control group, displaying a better phenotype (Figure 2g), higher chlorophyll fluorescence and F v /F m values (Figure 2h,i) and lower levels of MDA, H2O2 and O2⋅− (Figure 2j–l). Furthermore, we found that exogenous spraying of flavonoids on tea plants experiencing drought stress also alleviated the damage caused by drought stress (Figure S3). These results indicated that flavonoids are the key imprinted metabolites in drought priming in tea plants, enhancing drought tolerance.


### High Expression Levels of Six Flavonoid Biosynthetic Genes Resulted in the Accumulation of Flavonoids in Drought‐Primed Tea Plants Under Drought Stress
  To investigate the molecular mechanisms of moderate drought‐priming enhanced drought tolerance in tea plants, we collected the transcriptome profiles of tea plants from R‐CK, C2‐T1, C2‐T2, CK‐CK, CK‐T1 and CK‐T2 (Table S5). The results of PCA show that the biologically repeated samples are clustered together, while the samples between groups are scattered (Figure S4a), which shows that the data is reliable and can be used for subsequent analysis. A total of 7760 DEGs were identified (Figure S4b). KEGG enrichment analysis of DEGs in CK‐CK versus R‐CK, CK‐T1 versus C2‐T1 and CK‐T2 versus C2‐T2 revealed significant enrichment in flavonoid biosynthesis pathways, particularly in the CK‐T2 versus C2‐T2 comparison (Figure S4c). These results aligned with the metabolic profiles, indicating that the enhanced drought tolerance in primed tea plants primarily stems from transcriptional regulation of flavonoid biosynthesis. A total of 125 DEGs were annotated as phenylpropanoid biosynthesis‐related genes in both drought‐primed and control tea plants (Figure S4d). Among these, 119 DEGs were associated with flavonoid biosynthesis. Notably, 84 genes exhibited higher expression in drought‐primed plants compared to controls, indicating enhanced flavonoid biosynthesis activity in primed plants (Figure S4d). Analysis of flavonoid biosynthesis flux revealed that recurrent drought‐stressed tea plants exhibited transcriptional reprogramming through marked activation of memory genes, resulting in enhanced flavonoid production (particularly in C2‐T2) compared to single‐stress counterparts (Figure 3a). Moreover, when suffering from drought stress, the six encoded enzyme genes for synthesising flavonoid metabolites, including CsCHS (CsTGY09G0000882), CsCHI (CsTGY01G0003643), CsFLS (CsTGY04G0001430), CsDFR (CsTGY02G0000792), CsANS (CsTGY14G0001296) and CsANR (CsTGY08G0000373), had a significantly higher expression level in C2‐T2, explaining the accumulation of those flavonoid metabolites in C2‐T2. The qRT‐PCR validation results corroborated these findings, showing strong concordance with transcriptome data (Pearson's correlation coefficient R > 0.8) (Figure S5). Additionally, although some genes did not show significant accumulation in both R‐CK and C2‐T1 compared with corresponding controls, they were highly expressed in tea shoots (Figure S6a,b) and maintained high expression levels under drought stress compared with other genes, suggesting the importance of these genes in tea plants. Thus, the high expression levels of CsCHS, CsCHI, CsFLS, CsDFR, CsANS and CsANR in drought‐primed tea plants resulted in the high accumulation of flavonoids to enhance the drought tolerance of tea plants.

  Given the pivotal roles of flavonoids in drought resistance and the centrality of CsCHS, CsCHI, CsFLS, CsDFR, CsANS and CsANR in flavonoid biosynthesis, our study adopted a systemic approach to investigate drought tolerance mechanisms. Rather than focusing on individual flavonoid metabolites, we quantified total flavonoid pools to holistically evaluate their drought‐adaptive functions. To functionally validate these key biosynthetic genes, we subjected transgenic overexpression tea plants to drought stress, testing the hypothesis that enhanced flavonoid accumulation mediated by these genes confers acquired drought tolerance. The expression of these genes and the content of flavonoids were drastically increased in overexpression‐transformed tea leaves (Figure 3b–d). After 24 h of drought stress, the tea plants of the CK group were most severely damaged, while CsCHS, CsCHI, CsFLS, CsDFR, CsANS and CsANR overexpressing tea plants performed better under drought stress (Figure 3b). The chlorophyll fluorescence and F v /F m values in six genes related to flavonoid synthesis overexpressing tea plants were higher (Figure 3e,f). The levels of MDA, H2O2 and O2⋅− (measured directly and via histochemical staining) were lower in the overexpressing plants than in CK (Figure 3g–j), suggesting reduced oxidative damage under drought stress. Additionally, we monitored the expression levels of six flavonoid biosynthetic genes in overexpression lines under drought stress. We found that the expression levels of six genes were kept higher than CK (Figure 3k), confirming that those genes played an important role in enhancing the tolerance of tea plants to drought stress by regulating flavonoid accumulation.


### Identification of a Regulatory Network Involved in the Maintenance of Acquired Drought Tolerance of Tea Plants
  To investigate the regulation mechanisms and establish the regulatory network related to flavonoid biosynthesis during the tea plant's drought memory generation, the co‐expression network of six flavonoid biosynthesis‐related genes using 18 transcriptome profiles of tea plants at different drought treatments was constructed via weighted gene co‐expression network analysis (WGCNA). The 10 modules (blue, tan, green, magenta, greenyellow, red, cyan, turquoise, salmon and yellow) were classified by hierarchical clustering and dynamic branch cutting, with gene numbers in each module ranging from 30 to 559 (Figure 4a,b). Further analysis found that genes encoding TFs that preferentially correlated with CsCHS, CsCHI, CsFLS, CsDFR, CsANS and CsANR were primarily gathered in MEcyan, MEturquoise and MEsalmon modules (Pearson > 0.8; Figure 4b). The 35 TFs were identified as positively correlated with six flavonoid biosynthesis‐related genes in the MEcyan, MEturquoise and MEsalmon modules, respectively (Table S6). These TFs belong to 21 families (Figure 4c). Additionally, the PlantRegMap (https://plantregmap.gao‐lab.org/binding_site_prediction.php) was used to map the TF‐binding site of six flavonoid biosynthesis‐related genes, and  Arabidopsis thaliana  was used as a reference species (Table S7). Our analysis revealed that the promoters of CsCHS, CsCHI, CsFLS, CsDFR, CsANS and CsANR contain binding sites for at least 13 TFs, excluding LIM, NF‐YC and TUB. Notably, drought‐primed plants exhibited significantly higher expression of four key TFs (CsbHLH [CsTGY01G0002824], CsYABBY [CsTGY03G0002716] and two CsMYBs [CsTGY15G0000202, CsTGY08G0001209]), particularly during the second drought cycle (C2‐T2) (Figure 4d). These findings suggest that these hub TFs actively regulate flavonoid pathway gene expression to maintain acquired drought tolerance in tea plants. Subsequently, we validated the expression patterns of those four TFs using qRT‐PCR and found that the expression of the four TF genes was higher in the plants undergoing moderate drought‐priming than in the control (Figure 4e). An analysis of the expression levels of these four TF genes in various tissues of the tea plant found that CsbHLH, CsYABBY and two CsMYBs had high expression in tea shoots (bud and young leaf) (Figure S7a,b). Therefore, we focused on CsbHLH, CsYABBY and two CsMYBs and indicated that these four TFs potentially perform the hub roles in regulating the expression of six flavonoid biosynthesis genes in the maintenance of acquired drought tolerance of tea plants.

  The full‐length cDNA sequences of CsbHLH, CsYABBY and two CsMYBs contain 1125, 648, 582 and 885 bp open reading frames (ORFs), respectively. The phylogenetic trees were constructed based on AtbHLH, AtYABBY and AtMYB from Arabidopsis, and the results showed that CsbHLH, CsYABBY and two CsMYBs had their closest evolutionary affiliation with AtbHLH18, AtYABBY1, AtMYB114 and AtMYB91, respectively (Figure S7c–f). Therefore, we named CsbHLH as CsbHLH18, CsYABBY as CsYABBY1, CsMYBs as CsMYB114 and CsMYB91. Multiple sequence alignment of amino acid sequences of CsbHLH18, CsYABBY1, CsMYB114 and CsMYB91 TFs showed that CsbHLH18 consists of a typical helix–loop–helix domain, CsYABBY1 contains a C2C2‐zinc finger domain in the N‐terminus and a YABBY (i.e., helix–turn–helix) domain in the C‐terminus, and the CsMYB114 and CsMYB91 contained the conserved R2 and R3 DNA binding domains at the N‐terminus (Figure S7g–i).

  To investigate the subcellular localisation of CsbHLH18, CsYABBY1, CsMYB114 and CsMYB91, the GFP fusion constructs 35S::GFP and 35S::CsbHLH18/CsYABBY1/CsMYB91/CsMYB114 were fused with DAPI and transiently transfected into onion, with 35S::GFP used as the control. The GFP fluorescence of CsbHLH18, CsYABBY1, CsMYB114 and CsMYB91 was observed in the nucleus and overlapped with the nuclear localisation signal from DAPI. In contrast, the GFP fluorescence of the control was distributed throughout the cell (Figure S8a). The full‐length CsbHLH18, CsYABBY1, CsMYB114 and CsMYB91 CDS sequences were fused with the GAL4 DNA‐binding domain of pGBKT7 and a pBD vector to construct pGBKT7‐TFs and pBD‐TFs, respectively, to identify those responsible for its transcriptional activation in yeast cells and tobacco. Yeast growth assays showed that the yeast cells harbouring the positive control and full‐length CsbHLH18, CsYABBY1, CsMYB114 and CsMYB91 grew normally on both SD/‐Trp and SD/‐Trp‐His‐Ade medium, and X‐α‐Gal was activated, whereas the empty pGBKT7 only grew on SD/−Trp medium (Figure S8b). Dual‐LUC reporter assays in tobacco showed that the LUC fluorescence signals and LUC/REN values driven by CsbHLH18, CsYABBY1, CsMYB114 and CsMYB91 significantly activated the expression of the LUC reporter compared with pBD‐empty (Figure S8c–e). Taken together, CsbHLH18, CsYABBY1, CsMYB91 and CsMYB114 serve as a nuclear‐localised transcription activator.


### Overexpression of 
CsYABBY1
 and 
CsMYB114
 in Tea Plants Enhances Drought Tolerance and ROS Scavenging Ability by Controlling Flavonoid Biosynthesis
  To verify the functionalities of four TFs in the maintenance of acquired drought tolerance in tea plants, the CsbHLH18, CsYABBY1, CsMYB114 and CsMYB91 were overexpressed in tea plants, and their transgenic status was confirmed using RT‐PCR (Figure 5a). The expression levels of CsbHLH18, CsYABBY1, CsMYB114 and CsMYB91 in the CsbHLH18‐OE, CsYABBY1‐OE, CsMYB114‐OE and CsMYB91‐OE tea plants were significantly higher than those in the control (Figure 5b). Subsequently, the flavonoid contents of the four TFs‐OE tea plants were detected. The flavonoid content was extremely significantly accumulated in CsbHLH18‐OE, and the CsYABBY1‐OE and CsMYB114‐OE tea plants to an extremely significant level (p < 0.01). However, although the flavonoid content in CsMYB91‐OE leaves of tea plants was increased, it was not at a substantial level (Figure 5c). Thus, considering the function of flavonoids in tea drought tolerance, CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 were chosen to investigate further the function of TFs in regulating flavonoid biosynthesis for the maintenance of the tea plant's acquired drought tolerance.

  To assess whether CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 were the key factors driving acquired drought tolerance in tea plants, the CsYABBY1‐OE and CsMYB114‐OE tea plants were subjected to drought stress for 24 h. After 24 h of drought treatment, the damage to the phenotype in these TFs overexpressing tea leaves was lighter than in the control (Figure 5d) and showed higher chlorophyll fluorescence and F v /F m values (Figure 5e,f). In addition, quantitative measurements of MDA, H2O2 and O2⋅− content were conducted to evaluate the impact of drought stress on transgenic tea plants. Under drought stress, the CsYABBY1‐OE and CsMYB114‐OE leaves of tea plants had significantly lower MDA, H2O2 and O2⋅− content than the control (Figure 5g–i). Next, the tea leaves H2O2 and O2⋅− levels were detected by staining with DAB and BNT, respectively. The results revealed that H2O2 and O2⋅− accumulation levels of CsYABBY1‐OE and CsMYB114‐OE leaves of tea plants were lower than those in the control under drought stress (Figure 5j). We also found that CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 overexpression resulted in the upregulation of five (CsCHS, CsCHI, CsFLS, CsANS and CsANR) and six (CsCHS, CsCHI, CsFLS, CsDFR, CsANS and CsANR) flavonoid biosynthesis genes, respectively (Figure 5k,l). These results suggested that CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 may be important in enhancing tea plant‐acquired drought tolerance by ROS scavenging ability under drought stress through controlling flavonoid biosynthesis.


### CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 Could Promote the Expression of

,

,

,

,

  Y1H assay was employed to investigate the binding of CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 to these targets (Figure 6a). When pGADT7‐YABBY1 was cotransformed with CsCHSp, CsCHIp, CsFLSp, CsANSp and CsANRp‐pAbAi constructs, the yeast cells grew normally on SD/−Leu medium with aureobasidin A (AbA), while the control and those cotransformed with CsDFRp showed no signs of growth. Additionally, when pGADT7‐MYB114 was cotransformed with CsCHSp, CsCHIp, CsFLSp, CsDFRp, CsANSp and CsANRp‐pAbAi constructs, the yeast cells grew normally on SD/−Leu medium with AbA, while the control showed no signs of growth. The above results of Y1H assays demonstrated that CsYABBY1 could directly bind to CsCHS, CsCHI, CsFLS, CsANS and CsANR gene promoters, and CsMYB114 could directly bind to CsCHS, CsCHI, CsFLS, CsDFR, CsANS and CsANR gene promoters (Figure 6a). A dual LUC reporter system was used to clarify the CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 effects on the activity of their target gene promoters. The results showed that co‐expression of 35S::CsYABBY1 with ProCsCHS::Luc, ProCsCHI::Luc, ProCsFLS::Luc, ProCsANS::Luc and ProCsANR::Luc (Figure 6b,c) and co‐expression of 35S::CsMYB114 with ProCsCHS::Luc, ProCsCHI::Luc, ProCsFLS::Luc, ProCsDFR::Luc, ProCsANS::Luc and ProCsANR::Luc (Figure 6d,e) led to a significant increase in luminescence intensity and LUC/REN values compared with the controls, which confirmed the Y1H results.

  Previous studies have shown that the YABBY TFs recognise the consensus binding sequences defined as 5′‐ATTNATAA‐3′ and 5′‐AATNATTA‐3′ elements of their target genes (Kayani et al. 2022). To further comprehensively scan the CsYABBY recognition site in the promoter sequences of six flavonoid biosynthesis genes, MEME suite v5.5.7 was used to scan the putative YABBY binding sites from these genes' promoters. The result showed that six flavonoid biosynthesis‐related genes' promoter regions were identified with at least two YABBY binding sites (Table S8). More MYB binding sites existed in the promoter regions of flavonoid synthesis genes (Table S7). Furthermore, the core YABBY and MYB binding cis‐elements with the top or second‐lowest q value in flavonoid biosynthesis gene promoters were selected to perform molecular docking (Table S9). Solvation energy effect (Δi G) was used for the molecular docking to evaluate the intermolecular binding efficiency. Negative Δi G corresponds to positive protein affinity between receptors and ligands. The molecular docking results showed that DNA‐binding sites in five or six flavonoid biosynthesis‐related genes' promoter regions could effectively bind to the CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 receptor protein, respectively (Figure S9). Their Δi G value ranged from −11.5 to −19.6 kcal/mol and −15.6 to −30.3 kcal/mol, respectively (Table S9), suggesting that the CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 could bind to the promoter region of five (CsCHS/CsCHI/CsFLS/CsANS/CsANR) and six (CsCHS/CsCHI/CsFLS/CsDFR/CsANS/CsANR) flavonoid biosynthesis genes to regulate their expression. Then, these TF‐binding sites in the promoter regions of the flavonoid biosynthesis genes were selected for the EMSA assay. The EMSA assay results showed that CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114, respectively, bound to the core cis‐elements of CsCHS/CsCHI/CsFLS/CsANS/CsANR (Figure 6f) or CsCHS/CsCHI/CsFLS/CsDFR/CsANS/CsANR promoters (Figure 6g). These results collectively demonstrated that CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 positively regulated the expression of core structural genes involved in flavonoid biosynthesis.


### Silencing the Expression of Either 
CsYABBY1
 or 
CsMYB114
 Could Reduce Flavonoid Content in Tea Leaves and Impair Their Drought Tolerance
  The silencing of both CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 was performed using AsODNs to investigate and verify their role in flavonoid biosynthesis and tea plant‐acquired drought tolerance. The specific AsODN probes for CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 were designed, and the AsODN‐CsYABBY1 and AsODN‐CsMYB114 were injected into the first to third mature leaves below the bud of tea plants for 48–72 h, and sODN‐treated leaves were used as controls (Figure 7a). After being injected for 48–72 h, the expression level of CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 was significantly suppressed in CsYABBY1‐ and CsMYB114‐silenced leaves compared to control leaves, respectively (Figure 7b). Notably, suppression of CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 significantly reduced the expression of five (CsCHS/CsCHI/CsDFR/CsANS/CsANR) and six (CsCHS/CsCHI/CsFLS/CsDFR/CsANS/CsANR) flavonoid biosynthesis genes, respectively (Figure 7c,d). The flavonoid content significantly decreased in CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 silenced leaves relative to control leaves (Figure 7e). These results implied that CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 contributed to tea plant flavonoid biosynthesis through regulating flavonoid biosynthesis‐related genes. Subsequently, CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114‐silenced and sODN‐treated tea plants were treated for 24 h with drought stress. After 24 h of drought stress treatment, the tea leaves of CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 silenced suffered more serious damage (Figure 7f,l). The chlorophyll fluorescence (Figure 7g,m) and F v /F m values (Figure 7h,n) were significantly decreased, and the MDA, H2O2 and O2⋅− content were significantly increased in CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114‐silenced tea leaves compared to control leaves under drought stress (Figure 7i–k,o–q). Taken together, those results suggested that CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 acted as positive regulators of flavonoid biosynthesis, thereby enhancing the acquired drought tolerance of tea plants.


## Discussion

### Drought Priming Enhances the Acquired Drought Tolerance of Tea Plants by Eliciting Multilevel Responses
  Drought stress is one of the most devastating environmental factors that seriously limits plant yield and quality (Sato et al. 2024). Lots of research has investigated the intricate adaptation and coordinated drought stress response mechanisms in multiple species to attempt to elucidate the mechanisms by which plants respond to drought stress to establish more feasible strategies for plant improvement (Dey and Bhattacharjee 2020; Dong et al. 2021; Jin et al. 2023; Kong et al. 2023; Li, Ren, et al. 2024; Li, Zhou, et al. 2024; Shao et al. 2023). Recently, advances in elucidating the mechanism of the plants' pre‐exposure to drought events (drought priming) provide new strategies for enhancing drought tolerance (Backhaus et al. 2014; Ding et al. 2012; Schulze et al. 2021). In this study, we exposed tea plants to different levels of drought stress in advance and subjected them to simultaneous drought treatments with tea plants that did not experience drought stress and found that tea plants that experienced drought priming showed stronger drought tolerance (Figure 1). These results were consistent with the findings that drought priming enhances drought tolerance in coffee (Menezes‐Silva et al. 2017), potato ( Solanum tuberosum ) (Banik et al. 2016) and tea plant (Shao et al. 2023).

  The capacity of the acquired drought tolerance conferred by drought priming is derived from the plant's interconnected and elaborate regulatory mechanisms involved at multiple levels, including modifications at morphological, physiological, metabolic, transcriptional, translational and even epigenetic levels (Ganie et al. 2024). Our research supports this conclusion. After experiencing drought priming, the tea plant's phenotype and related physiological and biochemical indices involved in the self‐protection mechanism of the tea plant were enhanced and improved when the tea plant was exposed to repeated drought stress compared with the control (Figure 1), although the related indices did not seem to be fully restored to a perfect state during the stage of restoration of water supply to the tea plant after drought priming. Notably, the drought tolerance obtained from drought priming presents more excellently in the tea plants undergoing moderate drought priming than mild drought priming, especially when tea plants were again subjected to moderate drought stress (Figure 1), suggesting that compared to the mild drought priming, the moderate drought priming left a profound imprint on the tea plant, which enabled it to retrieve the imprinted information quickly and respond faster when it was exposed to drought stress again.

  Additionally, the available imprinting information tends to initiate faster recruitment and efficient production of salubrious metabolites, triggering the changes in the plant metabolic profile and allowing it to behave more tolerantly in the face of stress (Jacques et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2022). In tea plants, we found significant changes in the metabolic profiles of tea plants that had undergone moderate drought priming under different levels of drought stress, and some metabolites represented by flavonoid compounds were highly accumulated in tea plants that had undergone drought priming (Table S3 and Figure 2a–e). Generally, the changes in physiological and metabolic processes formed by stress priming are governed by transcriptional and epigenetic mechanisms (Avramova 2015; Ganie et al. 2024). Evidence shows that plants possess transcriptional stress memory, with memory genes activated upon recurrent drought stress (Ding et al. 2012, 2013, 2014). Similarly, we found that tea plants that had been exposed to drought priming generated a large number of transcriptional reprogramming events when they were exposed to the stress again and that many genes showed faster responsiveness or a differentiation of performance from those that were exposed to the drought stress for the first time (Figure S4a,b). Further analysis found that flavonoid biosynthesis pathways were one of the significantly enriched pathways of DEGs (Figure S4c) and that the six encoded synthesis of flavonoid metabolite core genes upregulated considerably in experienced drought priming tea plants under drought stress, especially in moderate level drought stress (Figures S4d and S5, Figure 3a), suggesting that the transcriptional reprogramming events of those genes triggered the accumulation of protective metabolites that enhance acquired drought tolerance in tea plants. Collectively, our results support that drought priming can confer an acquired drought tolerance on tea plants, an ability that allows for a faster and stronger response when tea plants are again faced with drought stress, allowing them to maintain a constant alert state across multilevel responses.


### Flavonoids Are the Key Imprinted Metabolites to Enhance the Acquired Drought Tolerance of Tea Plants
  Flavonoids are characterised as critical antioxidants that play an important role in plant defence against drought stress by reducing oxidative damage induced by a burst of ROS (Xu et al. 2015; Yildiztugay et al. 2020; Zhan et al. 2019). It is worth noting that the flavonoids in plants that have been exposed to stress in advance have significant potential in coping with subsequent stress. When pre‐exposed to pulsed UV‐B, although the total duration was the same, the plants accumulated more UV‐B protective flavonols (Höll et al. 2018). The same phenomenon was observed in the present study. In the face of post‐drought stress, tea plants that experienced drought priming accumulated more flavonoids, especially under moderate drought stress (Figure 2d,e). Exogenous spraying of these flavonoids significantly alleviated drought‐induced damage, especially in ROS scavenging (Figure 3f–l). In Arabidopsis, the flavonoid structural genes such as F3H and DFR have been proven to increase flavonoids to reduce the accumulation of ROS and enhance drought tolerance (Nakabayashi et al. 2013). To elucidate the function of flavonoid structural genes in flavonoid biosynthesis to enhance drought tolerance, the CsCHS, CsCHI, CsFLS, CsDFR, CsANS and CsANR were overexpressed in the tea plants. Consistent with previous studies, the overexpression of six flavonoid structural genes significantly increased the flavonoid content in tea plants, alleviating oxidative damage caused by ROS accumulation and thus enhancing the drought tolerance of tea plants (Figure 3). Therefore, flavonoids are considered one of the core metabolites in the formation of drought tolerance memory. This is achieved because tea plants retain information from early exposure to drought stress, allowing for rapid retrieval when exposed to drought and the rapid initiation of flavonoid synthesis‐related genes, which leads to enhanced flavonoid synthesis and drought tolerance in tea plants.


### CsYABBY1/CsMYB114‐Flavonoid Biosynthesis Genes Module Plays a Crucial Role in Promoting Flavonoid Biosynthesis and Enhancing ROS Scavenging Capacity, Thereby Enhancing Acquired Drought Tolerance of Tea Plants
  The development of acquired drought tolerance in plants involves a complex regulatory network, in which transcriptional regulation by TFs plays a crucial role (Ding et al. 2013; Liu et al. 2016). Previous studies have shown that many TFs regulated flavonoid biosynthesis, including MYB, WRKY and CCCH. (Li et al. 2020, 2022). Our study revealed significant correlations between the expression levels of numerous TFs and six key flavonoid biosynthesis genes (CsCHS, CsCHI, CsFLS, CsDFR, CsANS and CsANR) under drought stress (Table S6 and Figure 4a–d), and CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 were identified as key TFs involved in the formation of acquired drought tolerance of tea plants, regulating the formation of flavonoids by manipulating the expression of six hub genes in the flavonoid synthesis pathway (Figure 4). The CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 were confirmed to be able to directly bind the promoter and activate the expression of CsCHS, CsCHI, CsFLS, CsDFR, CsANS and CsANR (Figure 6). Overexpressed CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 could significantly improve flavonoid content and the expression of flavonoid biosynthesis genes (Figure 5a–c,k,l). In our study, CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 were proven to be transcriptional activators, which can activate the expression of downstream genes (Figure S8). Therefore, we reasoned that CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 promote flavonoid biosynthesis by activating the flavonoid biosynthesis genes. Secondary metabolites are crucial for plant viability, coordination and regulation in environmental stress (Kutchan 2001). Previous studies have revealed that plant YABBY and R2R3‐MYB participate in the stress response of plants (An et al. 2018; Zhao et al. 2017). In our study, overexpression of the CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 activated flavonoid biosynthesis and resulted in higher chlorophyll fluorescence and F v /F m values (Figure 5e,f) and a lower level of MDA, H2O2 and O2⋅− under drought stress (Figure 5g–j). The transcriptional inhibition of CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 based on AsODN technology further proves this conclusion (Figure 7). Therefore, we demonstrated that the CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 increased the acquired drought tolerance of tea plants by directly binding the flavonoid biosynthesis genes to enhance flavonoid accumulation.

  In summary, we clarified that moderate drought priming could significantly improve the drought tolerance of tea plants. The metabolome and transcriptome have been united in revealing that drought priming can confer acquired drought tolerance to tea plants, a trait that is largely attributed to the retention of some information, mainly related to flavonoid biosynthesis, upon early exposure to drought stress. Additionally, we excavated CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 as potential regulatory factors that play a crucial role in the acquired drought tolerance of tea plants. CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 were found to collaboratively enhance drought tolerance by directly binding to the promoters of five (CsCHS/CsCHI/CsFLS/CsANS/CsANR) or six (CsCHS/CsCHI/CsFLS/CsDFR/CsANS/CsANR) flavonoid biosynthesis genes, respectively, resulting in increased flavonoids to enhance antioxidant scavenging ability against drought‐induced ROS in tea leaves (Figure 8). Our research will provide an important theoretical basis for the development of an effective drought tolerance enhancement system for tea plants. CsYABBY1/CsMYB114‐mediated drought tolerance provides new insights into the molecular mechanism of the formation of acquired drought tolerance of tea plants. However, the establishment of acquired drought tolerance in tea plants is also regulated by epigenetic inheritance, and the relevant regulatory mechanisms need to be clarified in the future.


## Materials and Methods

### Plant Materials
  Well‐grown and consistent 1‐year‐old ‘Tieguanyin’ ( C. sinensis ) tea plants grown in a greenhouse of Fujian Agriculture and Forestry University, Fuzhou, Fujian Province, China (E 119°14′, N 26°05′) were used as the experimental materials. The average temperature and humidity of the greenhouse during the treatment period were 30°C ± 2°C and 80% ± 5%, respectively. The cultivated  Allium cepa , a kind of Chinese onion cultivar with red skin purchased from Yonghui Superstores Co. Ltd. (Fuzhou, China), was used for subcellular localisation. All N. benthamiana used in this study were grown in the greenhouse of Fujian Agriculture and Forestry University. The Nicotiana benthamiana first germinated on soil, then was transplanted and grown under 25°C with a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod for use in transient expression analysis.


### Chemicals and Reagents
  The epicatechin (≥ 98%), epicatechin gallate (≥ 98%), and saponarin (≥ 98%) were purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Polyethylene glycol (PEG) 6000 and Astragalin (≥ 98%) were purchased from Solarbio Science and Technology Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China) and Shanghai Canspec Scientific Instruments Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China), respectively.


### Experimental Design of Tea Plant Drought Priming Treatment
  For drought treatments, tea plants were divided into control and treatment groups (Figure S1). In the first round of drought stress (Cycle 1), the tea plants in the control group were supplied with normal water (CK) to maintain a SRWC of field capacity of 80% ± 5% until further treatment. Tea plants were withheld of water according to the previous method, with minor modifications (Guo et al. 2017; Tian et al. 2022) in the treatment group, as described in Methods S1. For the subsequent second round of drought treatment (Cycle 2), the tea plants in the control group were subjected to well‐watered, mild, and moderate drought stress (CK‐CK, CK‐T1, and CK‐T2) as controls in the second round of drought treatment; R‐T1 and R‐T2, in which the tea plants recovered with water in the treatment group were regarded as experiencing drought priming, were subjected to well‐watered, mild and moderate drought stress (R‐T1‐CK/T1/T2 and R‐T2‐CK/T1/T2), respectively. Each test treatment consisted of 30 pots. The tea shoots, including one bud and three leaves, were collected for further analysis.


### Measurement of Drought‐Related Indicators
  The second‐third leaf of tea plants in the control and treatment groups was harvested for measurement of drought‐related indicators. Detailed protocols are described in Methods S2.


### Leaf Anatomical Structure and Stomatal Bioassay
  The leaf anatomical structure of tea leaves in the first round of drought stress and re‐watered tea plants was observed, and the anatomical analysis of those samples was performed, as described in Methods S3. The tea leaves of the experienced drought‐primed and control tea plants under drought stress (Cycle 2) were collected for the stomatal bioassay, as described in Methods S3.


### Metabolomic Analysis
  According to the results of drought priming degree screening, the tea shoots (one bud and three leaves) of R‐T2 (renamed as R‐CK), R‐T2‐T1 (renamed as C2‐T1) and R‐T2‐T2 (renamed as C2‐T2) in the treatment group experienced drought priming, and CK‐CK, CK‐T1 and CK‐T2 in the control group under drought stress were collected for metabolomic analysis, as described in Methods S4.


### Flavonoid Exposure Experiment
  To test the effects of flavonoids on tea plants under drought stress, the epicatechin, epicatechin gallate, saponarin and astragalin were randomly selected and dissolved in pure water. Pure water was used as the control. Subsequently, the tea plants were treated as described in Methods S5.


### Transcriptome Sequencing and Analyses
  The samples mentioned above, including R‐CK, C2‐T1, C2‐T2, CK‐CK, CK‐T1 and CK‐T2, were collected for total RNA extraction, detection, sequencing and analysis, as described in Methods S6.


### RNA Extraction, Genomic DNA Extraction and qRT‐PCR Analysis
  RNA and genomic DNA extraction, first‐strand cDNA synthesis and reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction were performed as previously reported (Tian et al. 2024). The quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT‐PCR) was performed using SYBR fluorescence with gene‐specific primers. Unless otherwise specified, all primers used in this study are listed in Table S2. The 2−ΔΔCT formula was used to calculate relative expression levels, and β‐actin was used as an internal reference gene. All analyses consisted of three biological replicates.


### Genes Overexpression in Tea Plants
  The CDS fragments of six flavonoid biosynthesis genes (CsCHS, CsCHI, CsFLS, CsDFR, CsANS and CsANR) and four TFs (CsbHLH18, CsYABBY1, CsMYB114 and CsMYB91) were inserted into the pCAMBI1301 vector. The details of CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 overexpression in tea plants are described in Methods S7.


### Phylogenetic Tree Construction
  The amino acid sequences of CsbHLH, CsYABBY and two CsMYBs were used to construct phylogenetic trees with AtbHLH, AtYABBY and AtMYBs of  Arabidopsis thaliana , respectively, based on the NJ (Neighbour‐joining) method in the software MEGA 10.0 and a bootstrap test with 1000 iterations.


### Transcriptional Activation Analysis
  The subcellular localisation in  A. cepa  epidermis and transcriptional activity in yeast cells and tobacco leaves of CsbHLH18, CsYABBY1, CsMYB114 and CsMYB91 were examined. Detailed protocols are described in Methods S8.


### Molecular Docking
  For the molecular docking, the CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 were used as receptors, and the DNA‐binding sites of CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 in six flavonoid biosynthesis genes were regarded as ligands, as described in Methods S9.


### Protein‐DNA Interaction Assays
  The yeast one‐hybrid assays (Y1H), dual‐LUC reporter assay, and electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) were used to test the binding of CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 to the CsCHS, CsCHI, CsFLS, CsDFR, CsANS and CsANR promoters. Y1H was performed as described in Methods S10. Dual‐LUC reporter assay and EMSA were performed as described in Methods S11 and S12, respectively.


### Silencing of 
CsYABBY1
 and 
CsMYB114
 in Tea Plants Based on AsODN
  The expression of CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 was repressed based on antisense oligonucleotides (AsODNs)–mediated gene suppression. The CDS sequences of CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 were used to design the probe using SOLIGO online software (http://sfold.wadsworth.org/cgi‐bin/soligo.pl) and operated as previously reported (Tian et al. 2024) to silence the expression of CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 in tea plants. The details of CsYABBY1 and CsMYB114 expression suppression in tea plants are described in Methods S13.


### Data Statistics and Analysis
  Every test was performed with at least three separate biological replicates, and the data were represented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), analysed by GraphPad Prism 9.50. Different letters or asterisks represent statistical significance detected by analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Duncan's multiple range test using IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA). Lowercase letters and * represent p < 0.05, and uppercase letters and ** represent p < 0.01.
