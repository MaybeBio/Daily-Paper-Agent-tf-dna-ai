# Identification and validation of aryl hydrocarbon receptor-associated hub genes in ulcerative colitis via integrated bioinformatics analysis


## Objective
  Ulcerative colitis (UC), a chronic inflammatory bowel disease, continues to pose substantial challenges in both diagnosis and treatment. The aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) plays a pivotal role in intestinal immune regulation; however, its core regulatory network in the progression of UC remains largely undefined. This study aims to identify core UC-related genes associated with AhR and to validate their expression in dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced murine models, thereby elucidating potential mechanisms underlying UC progression.


## Methods
  Using the GSE75214 and GSE87466 datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, immune cell infiltration was quantified via the CIBERSORT algorithm. Candidate genes were identified through differential expression analysis, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) module selection, and construction of an AhR co-expression network. Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) were performed, followed by construction of a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network using STRING and application of two machine learning algorithms to identify AhR-associated hub genes. Finally, the expression of key genes was validated in DSS-induced UC mouse models using real-time quantitative real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR).


## Results
  A total of nine AHR-related shared genes were identified, which were significantly enriched in immune response, amino acid metabolism, and oxidative stress pathways. Through integration of the PPI network and machine learning approaches, three central hub genes (PPARG, IL1B, and IDO1) were identified. Immune infiltration analysis revealed pronounced immune dysregulation during the progression of UC, which may contribute to disease development. Animal experiments confirmed the expression of these three key genes in colonic tissues, and hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining revealed extensive infiltration of inflammatory cells, consistent with the bioinformatics findings.


## Conclusion
  PPARG, IL1B, and IDO1 were identified as potential key genes closely associated with AHR, suggesting their possible involvement in modulating AhR signaling in UC, thereby offering a theoretical basis for disease diagnosis and therapeutic strategies.

## Introduction
  Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic inflammatory bowel disease characterized by diffuse inflammation of the colonic and rectal mucosa. Its pathological features include mucosal ulceration, glandular destruction, and inflammatory cell infiltration [1]. At the same time, typical clinical manifestations encompass abdominal pain, diarrhea (often accompanied by mucus and bloody stools), weight loss, and fatigue. UC can lead to severe complications, such as dehydration and electrolyte imbalance, and its chronic course substantially elevates the risk of colorectal cancer, profoundly impairing patients’ quality of life [2]. Epidemiological data indicate marked geographical variation in UC incidence, with higher rates observed in North America and Europe, reaching 35–45 cases per 10,000 individuals [3].

  The pathogenesis of UC is multifactorial, involving the synergistic interplay of genetic, immune, microbial, and environmental factors related to toxicants. With increased exposure to environmental toxicants during industrialization, the incidence of UC has been rising steadily over recent years [4]. In recent years, researchers have increasingly recognized that the pathogenesis of UC is influenced by multiple factors, among which the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) plays a critical role in intestinal immune regulation and inflammatory responses. AhR is a ligand-activated transcription factor that, upon ligand stimulation, translocates into the nucleus, where it forms a complex with the aryl hydrocarbon receptor nuclear translocator (ARNT) to regulate downstream gene expression. Its canonical activation pathway involves ligand binding, followed by the AhR-ARNT complex binding to specific DNA response elements, thereby inducing the expression of cytochrome P450 family enzymes such as CYP1A1 [5]. AhR also plays essential roles in immune and inflammatory processes by promoting anti-inflammatory responses, for instance, through the induction of cytokines such as IL-22 and the inhibition of the NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby alleviating inflammation and oxidative stress [6]. Activation of AhR depends on metabolites produced by the gut microbiota, particularly tryptophan derivatives, which serve as central regulators of the intestinal epithelial barrier by upregulating tight junction proteins, strengthening intercellular adhesion, and modulating the production of antimicrobial peptides, such as β-defensin 1, to protect the gut against pathogenic invasion [7]. Additionally, AhR modulates gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT), influencing the differentiation and migration of regulatory T cells (Tregs) [8].

  Current research primarily focuses on harnessing natural compounds to activate the AhR to ameliorate clinical symptoms, restore barrier integrity, and modulate immune responses in patients with UC. Emerging studies indicate that AhR agonists, including phytochemicals such as quercetin and kynurenine, as well as combination regimens, exhibit considerable therapeutic potential for UC by suppressing pro-inflammatory pathways, promoting intestinal barrier repair, and modulating gut microbiota composition [6]. These findings underscore the translational promise of AhR as a therapeutic target. Despite preliminary evidence elucidating the role of AhR in UC, its precise molecular mechanisms and regulatory networks remain to be fully delineated. Systematic dissection of AhR-associated pathways and their regulatory networks in UC pathogenesis will help uncover the key molecular underpinnings of immune homeostasis disruption and epithelial injury, thereby providing a theoretical foundation for targeted therapeutic strategies.

  In recent years, the advancement of microarray and transcriptomic technologies has provided powerful tools for elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying various diseases [9]. Bioinformatics-driven differential expression analysis and network mining have emerged as indispensable approaches in disease research. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), by constructing module-based networks from gene expression patterns, surpasses conventional correlation analyses in its ability to uncover complex patterns of coordinated regulation [10]. Moreover, emerging machine learning algorithms demonstrate a strong capacity for feature selection in high-dimensional transcriptomic datasets, enabling the efficient identification of key biomarkers and enhancing the robustness of predictive models.

  In this study, we integrated the GSE75214 and GSE87466 datasets from the GEO database as the testing cohort, constructed co-expression modules via weighted gene co-expression network analysis, and systematically screened for key candidate genes associated with the AhR pathway in ulcerative colitis by combining differential expression analysis with machine learning algorithms, thereby providing a theoretical foundation for subsequent functional studies and therapeutic target development.


## Materials and methods

### Data preprocessing
  The microarray datasets used in this study were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, v5.12; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), a publicly accessible repository for microarray and gene profiling data. Two primary transcriptomic datasets, GSE75214 and GSE87466, were included in the analysis. The GSE75214 dataset, generated on the GPL6244 platform, comprised 97 UC patients and 11 healthy controls [11]. The GSE87466 dataset, based on the GPL13158 platform, included 87 UC patients and 21 healthy controls [12]. After performing normalization to ensure data integrity and batch consistency, four additional UC datasets (GSE38713, GSE47908, GSE53306, and GSE37283) were selected as independent validation cohorts [13–17]. ### Differential gene expression analysis
  Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between UC patients and healthy controls were identified using the GSE75214 dataset (97 UC patients and 11 controls) and the GSE87466 dataset (87 UC patients and 21 controls). Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the samples from both datasets using the “ggplot2” package in R (v4.5.0) to evaluate global expression differences across samples. DEG analysis was performed using the “limma” R package, applying a threshold of P < 0.05 and |log2 fold change (FC)| >1 for significance. The filtered DEGs were visualized as volcano plots and heatmaps using the “ggplot2” and “pheatmap” packages.


### Construction of co-expression networks and module identification
  The GSE75214 and GSE87466 datasets were subjected to WGCNA to identify co-expression modules associated with UC. The analysis was conducted using the “WGCNA” package in R. Initially, hierarchical clustering was applied to assess whether outlier samples with aberrant gene expression values should be removed. Subsequently, the goodSamplesGenes function was employed to construct a scale-free co-expression network, and the “pickSoftThreshold” function was used to determine the optimal soft-thresholding power (β). Subsequently, a weighted adjacency matrix was calculated based on Pearson correlation coefficients and transformed into a topological overlap matrix (TOM). During module detection, the minimum module size was set to 30 genes, and the module merging threshold (cut height) was fixed at 0.25. Ultimately, the module most significantly associated with the disease was identified, in which genes exhibiting similar expression patterns were clustered together, and the module eigengenes (MEs) showing the strongest correlation with UC were extracted for subsequent analyses [18].


### Identification of AhR-related genes
  Genes associated with the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) were retrieved from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/) and the Reactome database (https://curator.reactome.org/). In the GeneCards database, “Aryl Hydrocarbon Receptor” was queried, with the filtering criteria set as protein-coding genes and a relevance score greater than 4 [19]. In addition, genes annotated within the AhR signaling pathway were integrated from the Reactome database [20, 21]. By combining the results from the two databases, a comprehensive AhR-related gene set was obtained for subsequent analyses.


### Screening and functional analysis of AhR-related DEGs
  A Venn diagram was generated using the online platform Bioinformatics (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/) to determine the intersection among DEGs, AhR-related genes, and genes from WGCNA modules, thereby identifying AhR-related DEGs [22]. Subsequently, functional enrichment analyses were performed on the candidate genes using the “clusterProfiler” package in R, encompassing Gene Ontology (GO: biological process [BP], cellular component [CC], and molecular function [MF]) analysis [23–25], Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis [26–28], and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) [29, 30]. Functional annotation and pathway visualization were employed to systematically elucidate the multilayered functions and critical signaling pathways associated with these candidate genes.


### Construction of the PPI network
  A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network associated with the aryl hydrocarbon receptor in ulcerative colitis was constructed using the STRING database (v12.0, https://string-db.org/), with the organism restricted to Homo sapiens and the confidence score threshold set at ≥0.4 to enhance reliability [31]. The PPI network results were imported into Cytoscape software (v3.10.3) for visualization and further analysis. Within Cytoscape, subnetworks were identified using the MCODE plugin, and the highest-scoring subnetwork was considered to potentially represent a functionally relevant cluster. In addition, the CytoHubba plugin was applied to compute network topology, and the top five hub genes were prioritized according to the Maximal Clique Centrality (MCC) algorithm.


### Machine learning approaches and hub gene selection
  Building upon the hub genes identified through Cytoscape, we further applied two machine learning approaches—support vector machine recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) and random forest (RF)—to refine the selection of UC-related AhR hub genes. SVM-RFE analysis was performed using the “e1071” package in R, with fivefold cross-validation applied to calculate the average root mean square error (RMSE) for selecting the optimal subset of feature genes [32]. Random forest analysis, based on random sampling with replacement, was applied to differentially expressed genes, ensuring robust predictive accuracy even in the presence of missing data or imbalanced distributions [33]. The RF algorithm was implemented using the randomForest package, and model performance was evaluated through five-fold cross-validation. Overlapping biomarkers identified by both algorithms were regarded as the most promising AhR-related genes in UC. These candidate genes were subsequently intersected with the core genes identified through Cytoscape analyses (MCODE and CytoHubba) to finalize the hub gene set. To evaluate their diagnostic performance, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted, and the area under the curve (AUC) for each hub gene was calculated using the “pROC” package.


### Immune infiltration analysis
  The CIBERSORT algorithm was used to evaluate immune cell infiltration differences between UC tissues and normal controls [34]. The LM22 reference matrix, comprising 22 immune cell subtypes, was employed to estimate the relative abundance of infiltrating immune cells [35]. The infiltration profiles were visualized using heatmaps and frequency histograms generated with the “ggplot2” package, illustrating the abundance distributions and intergroup differences of various immune cell populations. In addition, correlations between the identified AhR-related hub genes and the 22 immune cell subtypes were evaluated and visualized [36]. This analysis offers new insights into the cellular composition of the immune microenvironment in UC.


### Construction of miRNA (or TF)–hub gene interaction networks
  Target gene–miRNA and transcription factor (TF) interaction networks were generated using Network Analyst (v3.0, https://www.networkanalyst.ca/). The parameters used were as follows: organism, Homo sapiens; ID type, official gene symbol; gene–miRNA interaction database, TarBase v9.0; and TF–gene interaction database, ChEA. The resulting networks were imported into Cytoscape for further visualization and topological analysis [37].


### External validation
  A dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced UC model was established using male C57BL/6 mice aged 8–12 weeks and weighing 20–25 g. The mice were randomly allocated into a DSS group (n = 3) and a normal control (NC) group (n = 3) using a random number table. Under identical feeding conditions, mice in the DSS group were allowed free access to 2.5% DSS solution for 7 days, whereas normal control mice received distilled water. Body weight, stool characteristics, and rectal bleeding were monitored daily, and a disease activity index (DAI) was calculated to assess disease progression. Significant weight loss, increased DAI, and the presence of hematochezia were considered indicators of successful model establishment [38]. On day 8, the mice were euthanized, and their colons were excised and measured for length. The colonic tissues were divided into two portions: one was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to assess histological damage; the other was rapidly snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for RNA extraction and subsequent RT-qPCR detection of the hub genes (PPARG, IL1B, and IDO1). Total RNA was extracted and purified using Trizol Reagent (Lifetech, 15596026), and first-strand cDNA was synthesized via reverse transcription using the FastKing RT Kit (with gDNase). Primers used for the experiments were designed using Beacon Designer 7.90 (Table 2). ### Statistical analysis
  Statistical analyses and data visualization were conducted using GraphPad Prism (v10.1.2) and R software (v4.5.0). To compare the expression levels of hub genes between the UC and control groups, two-tailed independent-sample t-tests were performed. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, with thresholds defined as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001.

### Predicted microRNAs (miRNAs) and transcription factors (TFs) targeting the hub genes
  Regulatory networks for the hub genes PPARG, IL1B, and IDO1 were constructed using the Network Analyst platform. Predicted miRNA–gene interactions indicated that hsa-miR-25-3p, hsa-miR-34a-5p, hsa-miR-129-2-3p, and hsa-miR-130a-3p may regulate the expression of these three hub genes by directly binding to their target sequences (Fig. 8A). Furthermore, transcription factor (TF) prediction identified SWI/SNF-related matrix-associated actin-dependent regulator of chromatin subfamily A member 4 (SMARCA4) and BTB domain and CNC homolog 1 (BACH1) as putative regulators of PPARG, IL1B, and IDO1 (Fig. 8B). These findings suggest that the identified miRNAs and TFs may contribute to immune dysregulation and inflammatory progression in UC by modulating the expression of these hub genes.


### External validation using independent cohorts and DSS-induced colitis model
  To confirm the robustness of the identified hub genes, we validated the differential expression of PPARG, IL1B, and IDO1 across four independent GEO datasets (GSE38713, GSE47908, GSE53306, and GSE37283) (Fig. 9A). All three genes exhibited consistent and significant differential expression patterns in UC samples.

  Subsequently, in vivo validation was performed in a dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced acute colitis mouse model. Compared with normal controls (NC), DSS-treated mice exhibited hallmark colitis phenotypes, including a markedly elevated disease activity index (DAI; Supplementary Figure 10), pronounced weight loss (Supplementary Figure 11), and a shortened colonic length. To further assess intestinal injury, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed on colonic tissues from both groups (Fig. 9B). Histological analysis revealed intact epithelium, well-organized crypts, and the absence of inflammation, oedema, or hemorrhage in NC mice. In contrast, DSS-treated mice displayed severe pathological injury, characterized by disrupted epithelial integrity, ulceration with discontinuous epithelium, goblet cell depletion, degeneration and necrosis of mucosal epithelial cells, diffuse crypt loss, marked interstitial fibrosis, submucosal edema and hemorrhage, and extensive inflammatory cell infiltration extending to the muscularis. These findings indicate pronounced immune dysregulation and structural disruption during the pathogenesis of UC.

  Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) further demonstrated downregulation of PPARG (P < 0.001) and upregulation of IL1B (P < 0.01) and IDO1 (P < 0.0001) in DSS-treated mice compared with NC controls (Fig. 9C; Supplement table 9). These results were consistent with our bioinformatics findings, reinforcing the roles of PPARG, IL1B, and IDO1 as AhR-related hub genes in UC.


## Discussion
  Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic, relapsing inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), characterized by diffuse mucosal inflammation and persistent epithelial barrier disruption in the colon [40]. Despite advances in therapeutic strategies—including aminosalicylates, corticosteroids, immunomodulators, and biologics—a substantial proportion of patients fail to achieve sustained remission, highlighting that the molecular pathogenesis of UC remains incompletely elucidated and novel diagnostic and therapeutic targets are urgently needed [2].

  In recent years, the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) has emerged as a pivotal regulator of intestinal immune homeostasis and barrier integrity [41]. It is increasingly recognized as a key player in the pathogenesis and therapeutic modulation of UC. Previous studies have demonstrated that AhR activation modulates T cell differentiation—particularly regulatory T cells (Tregs)—and dendritic cell function, promotes the expression of anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-22, and suppresses NF-κB signaling, thereby alleviating intestinal inflammation [42, 43]. Moreover, AhR ligands—including tryptophan-derived metabolites and polyphenolic compounds—have been shown to enhance the expression of epithelial tight junction proteins (e.g., ZO-1 and Claudins), preserve barrier integrity, restore gut microbiota balance, and facilitate mucosal healing [5, 44]. Collectively, these findings suggest that systematic identification of AhR-associated hub genes may provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying UC and aid in the development of optimized diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. To address this, our study employed in-depth bioinformatic analyses of publicly available GEO transcriptomic datasets to explore AhR-related regulatory networks in UC systematically.

  The Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), maintained by the National Center for Biotechnology Information, is a globally accessible repository that archives and disseminates a wide range of high-throughput genomics datasets, including gene expression profiles, epigenomic data, and genomic variations. In this study, we integrated six UC-related transcriptomic datasets from GEO, applying weighted gene co-expression network analysis, differential expression analysis, and AhR-related gene enrichment to identify nine hub genes that are significantly associated with AhR signaling [10]. Gene Ontology, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes, and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis revealed that these genes are primarily involved in immune responses, inflammatory processes, and amino acid metabolism pathways, all of which are tightly linked to canonical AhR-mediated signaling functions. The subsequent integration of protein–protein interaction network modeling and machine learning approaches enabled the identification of three key hub genes—PPARG, IL1B, and IDO1—whose biological roles closely align with established mechanisms of UC pathogenesis. Validation in a DSS-induced murine UC model further substantiated these findings: IL1B and IDO1 were markedly upregulated, whereas PPARG was significantly downregulated, consistent with bioinformatic predictions, underscoring their pivotal roles in immune dysregulation and mucosal injury in UC.

  Through immune infiltration profiling, we further delineated the dysregulated immune microenvironment within the colonic mucosa of UC patients, which was corroborated by histopathological evidence from a DSS-induced murine model. H&E staining revealed marked epithelial disruption, crypt loss, and extensive inflammatory cell infiltration in DSS-treated mice, findings that closely mirrored the activation of pro-inflammatory cell subsets identified by immune deconvolution. Previous studies have linked immune dysregulation in UC to epithelial barrier disruption, microbiota dysbiosis, and host genetic susceptibility [45]. The UC immune landscape was characterized by a pronounced imbalance between pro- and anti-inflammatory subsets, with marked enrichment of M0/M1 macrophages, activated dendritic cells, activated CD4 memory T cells, and neutrophils in UC tissues, whereas M2 macrophages, regulatory T cells (Tregs), and resting mast cells predominated in healthy controls [46]. These findings suggest that exaggerated M0/M1 macrophage polarization promotes NF-κB activation through the secretion of IL-6 and TNF-α, thereby recruiting neutrophils to amplify inflammation. Meanwhile, aberrant activation of effector T cells drives excessive IL-17 production, directly impairing epithelial barrier integrity. Concomitant depletion of Tregs further undermines immunoregulatory restraint. Moreover, the three hub genes identified were tightly associated with these immune subsets: downregulation of PPARG inversely correlated with elevated M1 macrophages and neutrophils, implicating impaired anti-inflammatory regulation; IL1B showed positive correlations with activated dendritic cells, M1 macrophages, and neutrophils, underscoring its role as a pro-inflammatory driver; and IDO1 correlated positively with M1 macrophages and activated T cell populations, suggesting its potential contribution to metabolic-immune crosstalk that exacerbates inflammatory amplification. The concordance between murine histopathology and immune infiltration profiling underscores that macrophage polarization and T cell hyperactivation represent central axes of UC immunopathogenesis, with PPARG, IL1B, and IDO1 emerging as pivotal molecular nodes that orchestrate this dysregulated network.

  Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARG), a nuclear transcription factor, serves as a master regulator of adipogenesis by activating downstream targets to facilitate lipid storage [47]. PPARG exerts multiple roles in intestinal inflammation by regulating fatty acid oxidation and mitochondrial function. Reduced PPARG expression disrupts cellular energy metabolism in the intestine, thereby exacerbating oxidative stress and tissue injury [48]. Recent evidence has revealed that activation of PPARG can significantly ameliorate gut microbiota composition, enriching beneficial genera such as Bifidobacterium while suppressing pathogenic taxa, including Enterobacteriaceae and Staphylococcus, thereby alleviating T2DM-associated dysbiosis and mucosal inflammation [49]. Additionally, PPARG attenuates inflammatory responses by repressing the NF-κB pathway, partly through downregulating PTGS2, thereby limiting the release of pro-inflammatory mediators [50]. A recent study by Li et al. demonstrated that activation of PPARG promotes the polarization of macrophages from a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, thereby alleviating colonic inflammation [51]. In terms of therapeutic applications, Xie et al. reported that a novel triazole derivative can directly activate PPARG, thereby suppressing the PI3K/Akt and NF-κB signaling pathways and alleviating symptoms of ulcerative colitis in mice [52]. Additionally, Liu et al. demonstrated that natural products, such as Aspergillus cristatus-fermented black tea, can mitigate intestinal inflammation by activating the PPARG–NF-κB axis [53]. Collectively, these studies support the role of PPARG as both a diagnostic biomarker and a potential therapeutic target in ulcerative colitis. In both colonic tissues from UC patients and DSS-induced murine models, PPARG expression was markedly downregulated, exhibiting a negative correlation with disease severity. Furthermore, when incorporated into multi-gene diagnostic models, PPARG enhances the accuracy of distinguishing UC patients from healthy controls [51]. In this study, PPARG was markedly downregulated in both UC patients and DSS-induced colitis mice, with ROC curve AUCs of 0.984 (GSE75214) and 0.924 (GSE87466), highlighting its promise as a diagnostic biomarker. Integrated with enrichment analyses, PPARG downregulation may destabilize TNF and IL-17 signaling, weakening its suppressive control over pro-inflammatory cell subsets and thereby facilitating the infiltration of M1 macrophages and neutrophils, which amplifies mucosal injury. The expression level of PPARG may serve not only as a potential diagnostic biomarker for ulcerative colitis but also as a theoretical rationale for the therapeutic application of PPARG agonists in UC.

  The interleukin—1β (IL1B) gene encodes a pivotal pro-inflammatory cytokine that plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of ulcerative colitis. Previous studies have demonstrated that IL1B is initially synthesized as an inactive precursor and requires cleavage by Caspase-1, activated via the NLRP3 inflammasome, to generate its mature, biologically active form. IL1B is synthesized as an inactive precursor requiring cleavage into its mature form by caspase-1, a process initiated through NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Tripartite motif (TRIM) family proteins tightly regulate this process via ubiquitination, while excessive activation results in IL-1B overproduction, aggravating intestinal mucosal injury [54]. Mature IL-1B induces miR-200c-3p upregulation, which targets occludin mRNA for degradation, thereby disrupting epithelial tight junctions and increasing intestinal permeability. Clinical studies have demonstrated a significant correlation between elevated IL-1B/miR-200c-3p levels and reduced occludin expression in the intestinal mucosa of UC patients [55]. Additional studies have revealed a close association between IL1B regulation and autophagy. Wu et al. reported that NRBF2-deficient mice exhibit impaired autophagosome-lysosome fusion due to defective RAB7 activation, resulting in defective clearance of apoptotic cells and subsequent secondary necrosis that releases damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), further activating IL1B [56]. Therapeutically, recent studies have demonstrated that inhibition of the NLRP3 inflammasome or direct reduction of IL-1B levels can markedly alleviate clinical symptoms in UC patients. Sun et al. confirmed that oxymatrine ameliorates colonic injury by suppressing the NLRP3 pathway and reducing the expression of active caspase-1 and cleaved IL-1B [57]. Similarly, Ekhtiar et al. reported that ferulic acid and quinic acid dose-dependently decrease IL-1B levels and exert anti-inflammatory effects via activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway [58]. Collectively, these studies indicate that IL1B plays a central role in driving inflammation in the pathogenesis of UC, and that targeting its associated pathways may serve as a promising therapeutic strategy. Our analysis revealed marked upregulation of IL1B in UC tissues and DSS-induced colitis models, with ROC curve AUCs exceeding 0.95 across both datasets, underscoring its diagnostic potential. Functional enrichment analyses linked IL1B to the TNF, IL-17, and cytokine–receptor interaction pathways, with its elevation positively correlated with activated dendritic cells, neutrophils, and M1 macrophages, collectively driving a pro-inflammatory milieu and barrier disruption. These findings suggest that IL-1B amplifies UC-associated inflammation by reinforcing NF-κB activation and perpetuating the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines [55].

  Indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1) is a cytosolic heme-containing enzyme that catalyzes the rate-limiting step of tryptophan (Trp) catabolism, converting it into kynurenine (Kyn) [59]. Broadly expressed in immune cells (e.g., macrophages) and intestinal epithelial cells (IECs), IDO1 serves as a central node that integrates immune regulation, gut microbiota homeostasis, and metabolic balance. Accumulating evidence suggests that IDO1 regulates immune tolerance and inflammation by depleting local tryptophan while generating kynurenine derivatives, such as kynurenic acid (KYNA), which activate the AhR pathway to promote regulatory T cell (Treg) differentiation and suppress effector T cell activity, thereby tempering excessive immune responses [60]. Pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IFN-γ and TNF-α, markedly upregulate IDO1 expression [61]. In turn, KYNA, produced by IDO1, acts to suppress inflammatory signaling, establishing a negative feedback loop. Hyperactive IDO1 rapidly depletes local tryptophan while generating abundant kynurenine and its downstream metabolites. Accumulated Kyn, as a potent endogenous ligand, activates AhR expressed on diverse immune cell populations. Such activation may skew T cell differentiation toward pro-inflammatory phenotypes and sustain M1-like macrophage polarization within the inflamed microenvironment. Furthermore, Kyn-mediated AhR activation in IECs disrupts epithelial barrier integrity and enhances chemokine production, thereby recruiting additional neutrophils and exacerbating mucosal injury [62]. Other studies have reported that IDO1 expression is significantly upregulated in UC patients and promotes disease progression by inducing macrophage polarization toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype via the endoplasmic reticulum stress-associated GRP78-XBP1 pathway. Oral administration of the IDO1 inhibitor 1-MT markedly ameliorated symptoms in mouse models of colitis, including downregulation of IDO1 expression, suppression of the GRP78-XBP1 pathway, and reduction in the proportion of M1 macrophages [63]. These findings highlight IDO1 as a potential diagnostic biomarker and a promising therapeutic target for UC. In our study, IDO1 was significantly upregulated in DSS-induced colitis, with an AUC of the ROC curve exceeding 0.94. Enrichment analyses suggested its involvement in tryptophan metabolism, as well as in TNF and IL-17 signaling pathways. Immune infiltration profiling revealed positive correlations between IDO1 expression and M0/M1 macrophages, activated dendritic cells, and activated T cell subsets. These findings suggest that, within the inflammatory milieu of UC, immune cells (dendritic cells, macrophages) and IECs may upregulate IDO1 in response to pro-inflammatory cytokines. These findings suggest a dual role of IDO1 in immunometabolism and the amplification of inflammatory responses. Targeting IDO1 with inhibitors or modulators may offer novel strategies for immunometabolic intervention in ulcerative colitis.

  This study integrated multiple GEO transcriptomic datasets and employed WGCNA, differential expression analysis, PPI network construction, and machine learning approaches to identify three AhR-associated hub genes—PPARG, IL1B, and IDO1. Validation in a DSS-induced murine model confirmed their dysregulated expression, highlighting their critical roles in immune imbalance and mucosal injury in UC. At the same time, we acknowledge certain limitations in this study. First, the transcriptomic data were obtained from publicly available databases, lacking large-scale clinical samples, and the inherent sample heterogeneity and batch effects may affect the robustness of the results. Moreover, gene expression validation was only performed in DSS-induced acute colitis mouse models, and functional mechanistic studies, as well as clinical sample verification, were lacking. These limitations indicate that future studies should focus on investigating the functional mechanisms of hub genes and leverage single-cell sequencing combined with spatial transcriptomics to elucidate the roles of core genes in specific immune cell subsets. Simultaneously, strategies targeting PPARG, IL1B, and IDO1 for UC intervention should be explored, and clinical samples should be collected to validate the diagnostic and therapeutic value of these hub genes in multicenter clinical cohorts, thereby facilitating their translational application [10].


## Conclusion
  Through a combination of bioinformatics analyses and experimental validation, this study systematically identified three AhR-associated hub genes—PPARG, IL1B, and IDO1—that are closely implicated in UC. These genes exhibited consistent expression patterns in both UC patients and DSS-induced murine colonic tissues, with ROC curves confirming their potential as diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Immune infiltration analysis and histological evaluation further revealed that macrophage polarization and T cell activation imbalance represent central pathological features of the UC colonic microenvironment, in which PPARG, IL1B, and IDO1 likely contribute by modulating AhR-related inflammatory and metabolic pathways. Collectively, this study not only provides novel insights into the immunopathogenesis of UC but also offers a theoretical foundation for its clinical diagnostic application and targeted therapeutic strategies.
