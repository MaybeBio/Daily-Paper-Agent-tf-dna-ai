# Celebrating the 40-Year Milestone: NF-ĸB in Oncoimmunity


## Abstract
  Since its discovery by Dr. David Baltimore nearly 40 years ago, and in light of his recent passing, NF-κB has once again come into sharp focus as a central regulator of diverse cellular processes, including immune responses, inflammation, and cell survival. Its dysregulation is implicated in a wide range of human diseases, with mounting evidence highlighting its pivotal role in cancer and oncoimmunity. This review provides an integrated overview of NF-κB family members and their canonical and noncanonical signaling pathways, emphasizing how context-dependent activation orchestrates complex cellular outcomes. Within the tumor microenvironment (TME), NF-κB regulates crosstalk among cancer cells, immune subsets, and stromal components, promoting proliferation, metastasis, and immune evasion. We summarize FDA-approved and orphan-designated drugs targeting NF-κB, along with emerging therapeutics in clinical and preclinical development. Innovative strategies, including tumor-targeted delivery, immune checkpoint combination, nanocatalytic, and epigenetic modulation, are redefining NF-κB-directed therapy. Looking ahead, future efforts should focus on understanding context-specific NF-κB signaling, optimizing combination therapies, improving drug delivery and bioavailability, and identifying predictive biomarkers for patient stratification. Moreover, the emerging integration of artificial intelligence holds promise to accelerate discovery and personalize NF-κB-targeted therapies. Collectively, FDA-approved agents, experimental compounds, and novel strategies underscore NF-κB modulation as a versatile and promising avenue for cancer and immune disease therapy.


## Introduction
  With the passing of the great scientist Dr. David Baltimore in September, 2025, we have lost a phenomenal mind of our century. His legacy is linked not only to the discovery of reverse transcriptase, which rewrote the central dogma, but also to contributions that laid the conceptual foundation for the discovery of the nuclear factor (NF)-κB - pioneering work that opened a new era in immunology and inflammation-related diseases and set the stage for modern immunotherapy.


### Discovery of NF-κB:
  The discovery of NF-κB exemplifies how insights from fundamental virology and transcriptional mechanisms can illuminate the complex signaling networks that govern cellular behavior. NF-κB was discovered in 1986 by Ranjan Sen and David Baltimore at the Whitehead Institute for Biomedical Research at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) [1]. In their study of transcriptional regulation of the immunoglobulin κ-light-chain gene in B lymphocytes, they identified a nuclear protein complex that bound specifically to the κ-enhancer DNA sequence. Initially considered B cell-restricted, NF-κB was soon recognized as a ubiquitously expressed transcription factor that governs diverse biological processes, most notably immune regulation, inflammation, and cell survival. Its discovery opened the door to extensive research into NF-κB signaling pathways, subunits, and regulatory mechanisms, firmly establishing it as one of the most influential transcription factors in the history of modern biology and medicine.

  Since its discovery, NF-κB has captured the attention of researchers worldwide, with pioneering work by Karin, Verma, Ghosh, Hoffmann, and Stark illuminating the remarkable complexity of its regulation. Their studies revealed how NF-κB activity is finely tuned through inhibitor of κB (IκB) kinase (IKK)-mediated activation, IκB inhibition, and post-translational modifications such as lysine methylation [1–13]. The key contributions of these researchers are summarized in Table 1, which also highlights major awards and landmark citations recognizing their work (A complete list of abbreviations used in this review is provided at the end).


### NF-κB in human diseases and scope of this review:
  NF-κB serves as a central hub in controlling immune and inflammatory responses, and when its regulation goes awry, it can drive a wide array of human diseases – from cancer and autoimmune disorders to chronic inflammation, metabolic and cardiovascular conditions, and neuroinflammation disorders (Fig. 1) [14].

  In cancer, constitutive NF-κB activity promotes cell survival, proliferation, angiogenesis, and immune evasion, supporting both tumor progression and a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment [15]. Beyond oncology, NF-κB also integrates metabolic and inflammatory signals, contributing to obesity-associated inflammation, insulin resistance, and type 2 diabetes [16]. In the nervous system, chronic NF-κB activation drives glial activation and neuroinflammation, linking it to Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and other neurodegenerative conditions. In chronic inflammatory and autoimmune disorders such as rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, and multiple sclerosis, persistent NF-κB activation drives excessive cytokine production and breaks immune tolerance, leading to tissue damage [17]. In infectious diseases, NF-κB serves as a frontline defense by sensing microbial products through pattern-recognition receptors [18], yet many viruses, including human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), exploit this pathway to enhance replication [19]. Finally, rare genetic immunodeficiencies caused by mutations in NF-κB regulators (e.g., NF-κB essential modulator [NEMO], IκBα) underscore its essential role in immune homeostasis and development [15].

  Collectively, NF-κB functions as a critical regulator of host defense but also acts as a pathogenic driver when aberrantly activated, underscoring its importance as a therapeutic target across multiple disease states. This review uniquely integrates NF-κB signaling with onco-immunology, offering a comprehensive perspective on its impact on tumor-immune interactions and therapeutic potential.


## Overview of the NF-κB Family and Pathways
  The NF-κB family and its signaling pathways have been comprehensively described in prior reviews [15, 20, 21]; in this article, we provide a concise schematic overview to highlight their core features and establish the foundation for the discussions that follow.


### NF-κB Family:
  The NF-κB family is a small but versatile group of transcription factors, evolutionarily conserved across opisthokonts, with origins predating the divergence of animals and fungi [22]. This family comprises five members in mammals (Fig. 2): RelA (p65), RelB, c-Rel, NF-κB1 (p50/p105), and NF-κB2 (p52/p100) [20, 21]. All share a characteristic Rel Homology Domain (RHD) region at the N-terminus, which mediates DNA binding, dimerization, and IκB interaction [20, 21].

  NF-κB family members can be functionally divided into two subclasses: i) Class I (NF-κB1/p50 and NF-κB2/p52): Generated by proteasomal processing of the precursors p105 and p100, respectively. They lack a transactivation domain (TAD) but contain a glycine-rich region (GRR) and C-terminal ankyrin repeats that keep them inactive until processed [23]. ii) Class II (RelA, RelB, c-Rel): These proteins possess C-terminal TADs that directly activate transcription. RelB also contains an N-terminal leucine zipper domain important for dimerization specificity [23].

  Combinatorial dimerization generates transcriptionally active or repressive complexes, such as p65:p50 (canonical activator), RelB:p52 (non-canonical activator), and p50:p50 homodimers (often repressive). The stoichiometry and dynamics of these dimers fine-tune NF-κB responses to diverse stimuli [6, 7].


### NF-κB Pathways:
  As illustrated in Fig. 3, NF-κB signaling is classified into canonical and non-canonical pathways. The canonical pathway mediates rapid, transient responses to pro-inflammatory stimuli such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, interleukin (IL)-1, and antigen receptor activation. In resting cells, NF-κB dimers are sequestered in the cytoplasm by IκB inhibitors, primarily IκBα, which mask the nuclear localization signal (NLS) [24, 25]. Upon stimulation (e.g. IL-1), signaling cascades activate the IKK complex (IKKα, IKKβ, NEMO). Activated IKKβ phosphorylates IκBα at S32/S36, triggering polyubiquitination and proteasomal degradation [3]. This releases NF-κB dimers, predominantly p65:p50, enabling nuclear translocation and transcription of NF-ĸB target genes, including cytokines, chemokines, adhesion molecules, oncogenes, and anti-apoptotic factors, etc. Oscillatory feedback loops mediated by resynthesis of IκBα ensure tightly controlled activation kinetics [6, 26].

  The non-canonical pathway is slower and stimulus-specific, regulating lymphoid organogenesis, B cell maturation, and adaptive immunity. It is triggered by receptors such as cluster of differentiation 40 (CD40), B-cell activating factor receptor (BAFF-R), and lymphotoxin β receptor (LTβR), leading to stabilization and accumulation of NF-κB-inducing kinase (NIK), which phosphorylates IKKα, resulting in phosphorylation and partial proteasomal processing of p100 to p52 [27, 28]. The resulting RelB:p52 heterodimers translocate to the nucleus and activate NF-ĸB target genes controlling developmental and adaptive immune processes. Feedback regulation via NIK degradation ensures pathway homeostasis [29].

  Together, the NF-κB family is a tightly interconnected network that coordinates rapid inflammatory responses with longer-term immune programming. Whereas the canonical pathway drives immediate activation of RelA:p50 dimers, the non-canonical pathway sustains transcription through RelB:p52 dimers. Their interplay enhances signaling robustness while simultaneously creating therapeutic vulnerabilities [6, 23, 26].


## NF-κB in Oncoimmunity

### Tumor Microenvironment Overview:
  As summarized in Table 2 and Fig. 4 [30–73], the tumor microenvironment (TME) is a dynamic ecosystem of immune and stromal cells that interact to shape tumor progression. NF-κB signaling serves as a central orchestrator, promoting a pro-tumor niche. Immune cells, including T cells (CD8⁺ cytotoxic, CD4⁺ helper, regulatory T cells [Treg]), tumor-infiltrating B cells (TIL-B cells), natural killer (NK) cells, dendritic cells (DCs), tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs), and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), can either attack tumors or adopt immunosuppressive states depending on local cues [36–39, 42, 44–50, 53–55, 74, 75], while stromal cells such as cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), endothelial cells (ECs), pericytes, and mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) provide structural support, secrete signaling factors, and remodel the extracellular matrix. Key functions and NF-κB-regulated factors of each TME cell type are detailed in the same table and figure.


### Chronic Inflammation and Tumor-Promoting Immunity:
  Chronic inflammation is now recognized as a key enabling factor of cancer development, driving initiation, promotion, progression, and metastasis. Early observations by Virchow in the 19th century proposed that leukocyte infiltration at sites of persistent inflammation could precede malignant transformation, a concept that has since been validated by molecular studies [76]. Epidemiological evidence shows that 15~25% of all cancers are associated with chronic infection or inflammation, with risk factors such as smoking, obesity, chronic infections, and autoimmune disorders creating a persistent pro-inflammatory environment [77, 78].

  Within the TME, immune and stromal cells release cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors that activate key oncogenic signaling pathways such as NF-κB, signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), and cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2)/prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), creating a feed-forward loop of inflammation and tumor promotion [79]. NF-κB activation drives the transcription of IL-6, TNF-α, and other pro-inflammatory mediators that sustain proliferation and survival signals, while STAT3 cooperates with NF-κB to promote immune suppression and angiogenesis [76].

  COX-2 and its downstream effector PGE2 play pivotal roles in bridging inflammation with tumor progression. PGE2 acts through EP receptors to activate β-catenin, phosphoinositide 3-kinase PI3K/protein kinase B (AKT), and NF-κB signaling, which collectively enhance tumor cell proliferation, migration, and immune evasion [79]. Elevated COX-2 expression has been observed in colorectal, breast, and gastric cancers and correlates with poor prognosis, while pharmacological inhibition with non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or EP antagonists reduces tumor incidence and improves therapy response [79].

  The IL-6-STAT3 axis is another critical mediator linking chronic inflammation to cancer. IL-6 functions as both an NF-κB target gene and a driver of STAT3 phosphorylation, forming a positive feedback loop (“IL-6 amplifier”) that perpetuates inflammatory signaling and promotes tumorigenesis [80, 81]. Persistent IL-6 signaling supports cancer stem cell renewal, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and metastasis, while simultaneously suppressing anti-tumor immunity through recruitment of MDSCs and induction of Tregs [80–82].

  Importantly, chronic inflammation also contributes to therapeutic resistance. Cytokine-driven activation of survival pathways enhances DNA repair, inhibits apoptosis, and remodels the TME to protect tumor cells from chemotherapy and radiotherapy [79]. As a result, targeting inflammatory mediators and pathways has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy. Combinatorial approaches that integrate COX-2 inhibitors, cytokine blockade, or immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) aim to disrupt the inflammatory circuits sustaining tumor growth and re-activate anti-tumor immunity [79, 80–82].


### NF-κB in Tumor Cells:
  As noted above, chronic inflammation is tightly linked to tumor initiation and progression. Many secreted factors, most of them NF-κB-inducible genes, including proinflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors, play central roles in shaping the TME. Tumor cells are not only embedded within the TME, composed of diverse immune and stromal populations, but also actively shape its formation and function. In turn, the TME influences both tumor cells and every other cell type within this complex ecosystem.

  In tumor cells, NF-κB is a key driver of survival and proliferation, primarily through the upregulation of anti-apoptotic, pro-survival, and cell cycle-regulating genes (Table 2, Fig. 4). Constitutive NF-κB activation is observed in many cancers and is often driven by oncogenic mutations or chronic inflammatory signals. This persistent activation enables tumor cells to evade apoptosis by inducing B-cell lymphoma 2 (BCL-2) family members, cellular inhibitor of apoptosis proteins (c-IAPs), and survivin, which collectively inhibit caspase activation and apoptosis pathways [23, 83]. Furthermore, NF-κB enhances cell proliferation through transcriptional control of cyclin D1, cellular myelocytomatosis oncogene (c-Myc), and growth factor signaling components, supporting uncontrolled tumor growth [14].

  Recent advances highlight how NF-κB signaling contributes to therapy resistance and stemness phenotypes in tumors. For instance, Dai et al. [84] developed a novel clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)-Cas13a-based therapeutic approach, HOpe for Promoter-Expression (HOPE), that leverages NF-κB-specific promoters to selectively target oncogene transcripts (telomerase reverse transcriptase [TERT], polo-like kinase 1 [PLK1], kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog [KRAS], Myc) in cancer cells, leading to effective tumor suppression in vivo without harming normal tissues [84]. This strategy underscores the dual nature of NF-κB, while it is essential for normal cellular homeostasis, its persistent activation is a driver of tumorigenesis and thus a promising therapeutic target.

  Hypoxic conditions in the TME also synergize with NF-κB signaling to promote tumor cell survival. Chronic and cycling hypoxia activate both NF-κB and hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF)-1α pathways, which cooperate to induce genes involved in angiogenesis (vascular endothelial growth factor [VEGF]), immune evasion, and metabolic adaptation, allowing tumor cells to thrive under low-oxygen conditions [83].

  Additionally, noncoding RNAs including microRNAs (miRNAs), long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), and circular RNAs (circRNAs) have been shown to modulate NF-κB activity in cancer, either enhancing or repressing its signaling. Aberrant ncRNA regulation can further boost NF-κB-mediated EMT, metastasis, and resistance to chemotherapy [83, 85]. These findings point to NF-κB as a master regulator of tumor cell fate, making it a central node for therapeutic intervention.


### NF-κB in Immune Cells:
  NF-κB is a master regulator of immune responses, orchestrating gene programs that control inflammation, cell survival, and context-dependent immune activation or suppression. Depending on the cellular and tissue environment, its activity can drive rapid defense against pathogens, maintain immune balance, or, in pathological settings like chronic inflammation and cancer, contribute to immunosuppression and tumor progression. To understand these diverse effects, it is useful to examine NF-κB’s role within the two major arms of immunity - innate and adaptive cells, highlighting how its regulation shapes their distinct functions (Table 2, Fig. 4).


#### NF-κB in innate immune cells
  Among innate immune cells in the TME, DCs are critical mediators of anti-tumor immunity, and NF-κB signaling plays a central role in regulating their maturation, antigen presentation, and T-cell priming. Tumor-derived factors, including IL-10 and VEGF, can attenuate canonical NF-κB and STAT3 signaling in DCs, leading to defective major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II expression, impaired antigen presentation, and reduced T-cell activation [86]. Re-activating NF-κB in these DCs restores IL-12 production and rescues their capacity to elicit robust anti-tumor T-cell responses, suggesting that DC-targeted NF-κB modulation could synergize with cancer vaccines and immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy [86].

  However, the function of DCs is highly context-dependent. Several studies have shown that noncanonical NF-κB signaling, mediated by RelB:p52, promotes immune regulation rather than activation. For instance, CD40 ligation induces the expression of the tolerogenic enzyme indoleamine-2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) through noncanonical NF-κB, thereby suppressing T-cell activation and promoting Treg development [87]. Similarly, RelB-silenced DCs exhibit reduced expression of MHC II and costimulatory molecules (CD80/CD86), impaired allostimulatory capacity, and expansion of Tregs, consistent with a tolerogenic phenotype [88]. Collectively, these findings underscore the dual and context-dependent nature of NF-κB signaling in DCs, acting as a double-edged sword that can either potentiate anti-tumor immunity or promote immune tolerance depending on the tumor milieu and signaling balance.

  Beyond DCs, NF-κB also plays a pivotal role in shaping the development and immunosuppressive function of MDSCs in the TME. Activation of NF-κB in MDSCs promotes the expression of immunosuppressive molecules, including inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), arginase-1, IL-10, and transforming growth factor (TGF)-β, which inhibit T cell responses and facilitate tumor progression [89]. NF-κB also drives the secretion of chemokines such as C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2) and C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 12 (CXCL12), recruiting additional suppressive cells, including Tregs, and establishing an immunosuppressive TME that promotes tumor growth and metastasis [80]. These examples highlight NF-κB as a critical regulator of MDSC-mediated immunosuppression and a potential therapeutic target in cancer. While NF-κB can transiently enhance immune activation under acute conditions, in the chronic inflammatory TME, its role in MDSCs is overwhelmingly pro-tumor, driving immunosuppression, angiogenesis, and tumor progression [75].

  Beyond MDSCs, NF-κB signaling is essential for NK cell function within the TME. NF-κB activity in NK cells regulates both anti-tumor and immunosuppressive functions. On one hand, NF-κB can induce the production of cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β, which suppress NK cell cytotoxicity and promote an immunosuppressive TME, facilitating tumor progression [40]. On the other hand, NF-κB enhances NK cell activation by upregulating activating receptors and pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IFN-γ and TNF-α, which enhance tumor cell killing [41]. Consequently, NF-κB acts as a central regulator of NK cell function in the TME, with its activity tipping the balance between immune suppression and anti-tumor immunity.

  Another crucial innate immune population in the TME whose functions are strongly influenced by NF-κB signaling is TAM. TAMs are highly plastic: they can either support tumor progression (alternatively activated macrophage [M2]-like, pro-tumor) or enhance anti-tumor immunity (classically activated macrophage [M1]-like, anti-tumor). NF-κB acts as a critical regulator in both contexts, integrating tumor-derived and inflammatory signals to influence macrophage polarization and function. Activation of NF-κB in TAMs primarily leads to the expression of immunosuppressive cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β, pro-angiogenic factors like VEGF, and matrix remodeling enzymes including matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs). These factors contribute to immune evasion, enhanced vascularization, and tumor invasion. Additionally, NF-κB activation in TAMs has been shown to induce programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression on tumor cells via the NF-κB and STAT3 signaling pathways, facilitating tumor immune escape [90]. A recent study showed that in cholangiocarcinoma, M2-like TAMs activate the atypical protein kinase Cι (aPKCι)/NF-κB signaling pathway, inducing EMT and chemoresistance, further illustrating NF-κB’s role in tumor progression [91].

  Although TAMs are primarily pro-tumor, they can occasionally adopt an anti-tumor M1-like phenotype, producing TNF-α and IL-12, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) and nitrogen species, and presenting tumor antigens via MHC II to activate T cells. It was reported that NF-κB activation can reprogram TAMs from M2- to M1-like states, enhancing cytotoxicity and T cell activation [42]. Thus, NF-κB dynamically regulates TAM polarization in the TME, shaping immune surveillance, tumor progression, and responses to immunotherapy.

  Just as NF-κB shapes TAMs polarization, it also dictates whether TANs assume pro- or anti-tumor roles. NF-κB signaling in TANs exhibits context-dependent functions within the TME. In the pro-tumorigenic N2 phenotype, NF-κB activation promotes the secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines or chemokines such as IL-8, IL-10, TGF-β, VEGF, and CCL-2, facilitating tumor growth, angiogenesis, and immune suppression [41]. Conversely, in the anti-tumor N1 phenotype, NF-κB drives the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-12, TNF-α, and ROS, enhancing CD8⁺ T cell and NK cell cytotoxicity against tumor cells [92, 93]. For example, Tang et al. demonstrated that CD74high neutrophils enhance anti-tumor T cell responses through antigen cross-presentation. Activation of CD74 with an agonist further promotes T cell activation and improves the efficacy of anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) or tyrosine kinase inhibitor osimertinib therapies in non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) mouse model [92]. These findings underscore the pivotal role of NF-κB in determining TAN polarization and function, influencing the balance between tumor progression and immune surveillance in the TME.

  In summary (Table 2, Fig. 4), NF-κB serves as a central regulator of innate immune cells in the TME, directing DC maturation or tolerogenic activity, modulating NK cytotoxic versus regulatory functions, shaping TAM and TAN polarization, and sustaining primarily immunosuppressive MDSC states. By integrating inflammatory and tumor-derived signals, NF-κB determines whether these cells adopt anti- or pro-tumor roles, dynamically influencing immune responses, tumor progression, and therapeutic outcomes.


#### NF-κB in adaptive immune cells
  Beyond its pivotal role in shaping innate immune responses, NF-κB extends its influence into adaptive immunity, where it governs how T and B cells develop, activate, and sustain effective immune defense (Table 2, Fig. 4).

  NF-κB plays a pivotal role in the activation, differentiation, and function of T effector cells, including CD8⁺ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTL) and CD4⁺ T helper (Th) cells. Upon antigen recognition through the T-cell receptor (TCR), NF-κB signaling is activated, leading to the expression of genes essential for T cell activation and survival. This activation promotes the production of cytokines such as IL-2, IFN-γ, and TNF-α, which are crucial for effective anti-tumor immunity [48]. Studies using NF-κB-deficient T cells demonstrated that impaired T cell-NF-κB activation leads to failure of tumor rejection despite normal T-cell priming and infiltration, largely due to defective effector differentiation and reduced IFN-γ/TNF-α production [51]. Restoring NF-κB activity in T cells enhances cytotoxic function and promotes durable tumor regression, underscoring the therapeutic potential of targeting this pathway for T-cell reinvigoration [50, 52].

  Notably, within the TME, NF-κB’s impact on T effector cells is highly context-dependent. Sustained NF-κB activation can promote T cell exhaustion, characterized by upregulation of immune inhibitory receptor–ligand pairs such as PD-1/PD-L1, and T cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain-containing protein 3 (TIM-3) - a key mediator of T cell exhaustion, as well as reduced cytokine production, ultimately impairing anti-tumor immunity [49]. These dynamics highlight how NF-κB signaling can both promote and restrain T cell function, underscoring its dual role in shaping immune responses within tumors.

  While NF-κB activation drives the effector functions of CTL and Th populations, its role in Tregs is more nuanced, primarily sustaining their immunosuppressive activity. Tregs are critical for maintaining immune tolerance and preventing autoimmunity. In the TME, Tregs often accumulate and exert suppressive functions that facilitate tumor progression. NF-κB signaling, particularly through the c-Rel subunit, is essential for Treg development and function. c-Rel is involved in the transcriptional regulation of forkhead box p3 (Foxp3), the master regulator of Treg differentiation, and is required for the suppressive activity of Tregs [57]. For the groundbreaking discoveries elucidating peripheral immune tolerance and the central role of Tregs and Foxp3 gene, Drs. Mary E. Brunkow, Fred Ramsdell, and Shimon Sakaguchi were awarded the 2025 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in October, 2025 [59]. In the TME, NF-κB activation enhances Treg-mediated immunosuppression through IL-10 and TGF-β, thereby dampening anti-tumor responses [94]. Accordingly, targeting NF-κB signaling in Tregs has been proposed to strengthen anti-tumor immunity by alleviating Treg-driven suppression.

  Beyond T cells, NF-κB also shapes the function of TIL-Bs, influencing their activation, differentiation, and contribution to immune responses. NF-κB signaling in TIL-Bs has context-dependent effects on tumor progression. On one hand, NF-κB activation promotes the differentiation of regulatory B cells (Bregs), which secrete immunosuppressive cytokines such as IL-10, IL-35, and TGF-β. These cytokines suppress cytotoxic T cell and NK cell activity, creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment that supports tumor growth [54]. On the other hand, NF-κB enhances the antigen-presenting function of TIL-Bs by increasing MHC II and costimulatory molecule expression, thereby promoting T cell activation and potentially strengthening anti-tumor immunity [55]. Moreover, NF-κB activation in TIL-Bs can lead to the production of antibodies that contribute to tumor surveillance and elimination [56]. Thus, NF-κB serves as a critical regulator in determining whether TIL-Bs function to suppress or promote tumor progression, depending on the specific subsets and signals present within the TME.

  Overall, NF-κB orchestrates adaptive immunity by balancing the context-dependent activity of effector T cells, Treg-mediated suppression, and TIL-B cell function within the TME, highlighting potential opportunities to modulate this pathway for cancer immunotherapy.


### NF-κB in Stromal Cells:
  As noted above, the TME also contains stromal cells - non-malignant, non-immune connective and support cells that constitute the tumor stroma. These include CAFs, ECs, pericytes, and MSCs. Within the TME, NF-κB critically regulates the signaling pathways and functional activities of these stromal populations. In this section, we will examine these roles in greater detail (Table 2, Fig. 4).

  It is worth noting that NF-κB signaling is a key mediator of interactions between CAFs and tumor cells, influencing senescence, metastasis, and drug resistance. CAFs can adopt a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) in response to pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, secreting factors that promote tumor progression and therapy resistance, a process dependent on NF-κB [60]. In metastasis, NF-κB-activated CAFs release IL-6, CXCL12, and other mediators that drive EMT and enhance tumor invasiveness [61]. CAFs also contribute to drug resistance by triggering NF-κB-dependent survival pathways in tumor cells [62]. Heterogeneous CAF subsets, including myofibroblastic CAFs (myCAFs), inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs), and antigen-presenting CAFs (apCAFs), display distinct NF-κB-regulated functions, affecting extracellular matrix remodeling, immune modulation, and tumor proliferation [63]. These interactions underscore the therapeutic potential of targeting NF-κB-CAF signaling in cancer.

  ECs play a pivotal role in the TME, with NF-κB signaling serving as a central mediator of EC activation. NF-κB activation in ECs promotes angiogenesis and immune cell recruitment, processes critical for tumor progression. NF-κB activation enhances EC motility and vessel formation, as demonstrated by studies on IKKα-driven angiogenesis in tumor-associated endothelium [64]. Additionally, NF-κB induces the expression of adhesion molecules such as intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1) and vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM-1) in response to TNF-α, facilitating leukocyte recruitment through TNF receptor 1 (TNFR1)-dependent signaling [65]. Chronic cytokine stimulation sustains NF-κB activity via autophagy-mediated IκBα degradation, maintaining adhesion molecule expression [66]. Furthermore, NF-κB-dependent induction of matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP-2) and MMP-9 enhances extracellular matrix remodeling and angiogenic sprouting [67]. Collectively, these mechanisms underscore NF-κB as a key regulator of endothelial function in the TME.

  Another key stromal cell type, MSCs, represents an important component of the TME. NF-κB activation in MSCs drives the production of pro-inflammatory and pro-tumorigenic factors, including IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α, CCL2, CXCL1, CXCL12, VEGF, fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF2), MMP-2, MMP-9, TGF-β, and IDO, while also regulates their migration, differentiation, and immunomodulatory functions, thereby influencing tumor progression. Activation of NF-κB promotes MSC-mediated EMT, enhancing migration and invasiveness to facilitate metastasis [68]. It also modulates immunosuppressive activity by controlling cytokine secretion and T cell responses [69], and regulates MSC accumulation at tumor sites, supporting tumor growth [70]

  A less frequently discussed cell type in the TME is the pericyte. NF-κB activation in pericytes within the TME plays a pivotal role in regulating vascular stability, inflammation, and immune cell recruitment, thereby influencing tumor progression. Pericytes, as mural cells, contribute to blood vessel support and function. Upon NF-κB activation, pericytes secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α, and chemokines like CCL2 and CXCL1, which recruit immune cells to the TME and modulate inflammation [71]. Additionally, NF-κB signaling in pericytes induces the production of MMP-9 via IL-33, facilitating extracellular matrix remodeling and tumor cell invasion [72]. This activation also enhances ECs proliferation and migration, promoting angiogenesis and tumor growth [73]. Thus, NF-κB in pericytes serves as a critical mediator of vascular dynamics and immune modulation in the TME.

  In short, NF-κB coordinates the pro-tumorigenic functions of stromal cells in the TME, including CAF-driven cytokine secretion, endothelial angiogenesis and adhesion, MSC-mediated matrix remodeling and immunosuppressive signaling, and pericyte-supported vascular stabilization, highlighting stromal cells as key contributors to tumor progression.


### TME Crosstalk and Systemic Impacts - Orchestrating Immune Escape and Tumor Growth:
  Within the TME, tumor, immune, and stromal cells engage in continuous crosstalk, exchanging signals that shape tumor progression and immune responses. NF-κB plays a central role in regulating these interactions. The TME functions as a dynamic network where cells communicate through a complex web of cytokines and chemokines. This “cytokine/chemokine cocktail” enables cells to sense local cues and respond in ways that can either promote tumor growth or support anti-tumor immunity. Tumor cells can exploit these signals to recruit immunosuppressive populations, inhibit effector lymphocytes, and remodel the stroma for survival, while immune and stromal cells can mount context-dependent anti-tumor responses. The balance between tumor-promoting and tumor-suppressive signals ultimately dictates disease progression, highlighting opportunities to strategically reprogram the TME toward effective tumor elimination (Table 2, Fig. 4).

  To thrive within the TME, tumors employ highly sophisticated strategies. For example, immune evasion is a hallmark of cancer and a major barrier to effective immunotherapy. The concept of cancer immunoediting describes tumor-immune interactions across three phases: elimination, equilibrium, and escape, with the last phase characterized by tumor variants that develop mechanisms to avoid immune destruction [40, 95]. Tumor cells achieve immune escape through multiple mechanisms, including downregulation of antigen presentation, secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines, recruitment of regulatory immune cells, and activation of inhibitory immune checkpoints [40, 95].

  NF-κB plays a pivotal role in immune evasion by orchestrating the expression of cytokines and immune checkpoint molecules that remodel the TME into an immunosuppressive niche. Constitutive NF-κB activation in tumor and stromal cells upregulates IL-10, TGF-β, and VEGF, which inhibit DCs maturation, suppress cytotoxic T-cell activation, and promote angiogenesis [30, 96]. Tumor-derived soluble phosphatidylserine (sPS) further engages phosphatidylserine receptors (PSR) on macrophages, driving an anti-inflammatory M2-like polarization and increasing production of IL-10, TGF-β, and PGE2, thereby expanding TAMs and enhancing immune tolerance [79, 97].

  Viral immune evasion strategies provide valuable insight into tumor biology. Many viruses suppress NF-κB activation by directly targeting IKKs, IκBα, or the p65:p50 dimer, enabling persistent infection and immune evasion [98]. Tumors exploit similar pathways, upregulating NF-κB inhibitors or activating checkpoint molecules such as PD-L1 via NF-κB-dependent transcription, resulting in T-cell exhaustion [99].

  ICB targeting PD-1/PD-L1 and cytotoxic T-Lymphocyte associated protein (CTLA-4) has revolutionized cancer therapy, but responses remain limited to a subset of patients due to redundant escape mechanisms including upregulation of alternative inhibitory receptors (Lymphocyte-activation gene 3 [LAG-3], TIM-3, T cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM motif domains [TIGIT]) and recruitment of MDSCs [86]. Combination approaches that pair NF-κB inhibition with checkpoint blockade or therapies targeting TAMs and MDSCs are being explored to reprogram the TME and improve ICB efficacy [96].

  These findings highlight immune evasion as a dynamic, multi-layered process in which NF-κB serves as a central hub. While early neoplastic cells can be recognized and eliminated by cytotoxic T cells and NK cells, most established tumors evade immune surveillance [100–102]. The balance between pro-inflammatory and immunosuppressive signals in the TME determines tumor-immune interactions and disease progression. Healthy lifestyle factors, such as regular exercise and a balanced diet, can support immune function and help maintain effective tumor surveillance [103].

  Beyond its local TME functions, NF-κB exerts systemic effects that significantly impact cancer patients. Chronic NF-κB activation drives systemic inflammation, contributing not only to cancer cachexia - characterized by muscle wasting, adipose tissue loss, and metabolic dysregulation [55, 104–106], but also to anemia, thrombosis, and chronic fatigue through dysregulated cytokine production and altered hematopoiesis [107]. Elevated NF-κB signaling in circulating immune cells and stromal compartments amplifies pro-inflammatory cytokine release (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6), further exacerbating metabolic disturbances, systemic inflammation, and impaired anti-tumor immunity. These local and systemic effects highlight NF-κB as a central mediator linking tumor-localized signaling to whole-body physiological consequences, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic target to modulate tumor progression and alleviate cancer-associated systemic complications.


## Therapeutic Implications
  NF-κB influences tumor progression, immune regulation, and therapy resistance, making it a key cancer target. Strategies to modulate NF-κB, including small molecules, proteasome/kinase inhibitors, biologics, and immunotherapies, have shown variable clinical outcomes [108, 109] and are discussed below.


### Proteasome Inhibitors:
  As presented in Table 3 [112], the first-in-class proteasome inhibitor bortezomib (Velcade, PS-341) revolutionized multiple myeloma (MM) treatment by preventing IκBα degradation, thereby blocking NF-κB nuclear translocation and transcriptional activation [113]. Next-generation proteasome inhibitors, including carfilzomib (irreversible epoxyketone), ixazomib (oral boronic acid), and oprozomib, a designated orphan drug, have improved pharmacological profiles and are being investigated for broader oncologic applications [109–111]. Despite their efficacy, development of resistance and dose-limiting toxicities such as peripheral neuropathy remain challenges, motivating the search for combination strategies and novel inhibitors with higher specificity [113].


### Cytokine/ Receptor - Targeted Inhibitors:
  In addition to proteasome inhibition, cytokines and their receptors represent effective therapeutic targets. NF-κB can be activated by multiple cytokines, and in turn, its activation stimulates the production and secretion of numerous cytokines (Table 3) [112], including IL-1, IL-2, IL-6, TNF, receptor activator of NF-κB ligand (RANKL, also known as TNF superfamily member 11 [TNFSF11]), TNF receptor superfamily member 8 (TNFRSF8), CSF1, granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF, also known as CSF2), VEGF, and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), among others, many of which are present in the TME [74]. Drugs targeting these cytokines or their receptors have been developed to downregulate NF-κB signaling, with indications spanning various cancers (e.g., colorectal, lung, renal, glioblastoma), immune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and psoriasis (PSO), and ocular disorders including neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD). Most FDA-approved agents are monoclonal antibodies, including Canakinumab (IL-1), Basiliximab (IL-2), Sarilumab (IL-6), Infliximab (TNF), Denosumab (RANKL), and Bevacizumab (VEGF), while a smaller portion are small-molecule inhibitors, such as Pexidartinib (CSF1), Sunitinib (VEGF), and Pazopanib (VEGF/PDGF) [112]. Together, these approaches underscore the central role of cytokine signaling in NF-κB-mediated diseases and their therapeutic potential.


### Immune Checkpoint - Targeted Inhibitors (PD-1/PD-L1):
  Beyond targeting cytokines and their receptors, modulation of immune checkpoint pathways has rejuvenated the field of cancer therapy by introducing innovative strategies to regulate NF-κB signaling and enhance antitumor immunity. PD-1 and its ligand PD-L1 are pivotal immune checkpoints that maintain T-cell activation and tolerance within the TME [99]. Interestingly, both PD-1 and PD-L1 can be transcriptionally regulated by NF-κB, while engagement of PD-1 with PD-L1 suppresses NF-κB signaling and the subsequent production of proinflammatory cytokines. This forms a dynamic feedback loop in which NF-κB not only induces checkpoint molecule expression but is also inhibited by their interaction. Monoclonal antibodies targeting PD-1 (e.g., Nivolumab, Pembrolizumab) or PD-L1 (e.g., Atezolizumab, Durvalumab, Avelumab) (Table 3) [112] effectively disrupt this inhibitory circuit, restoring NF-κB activity in effector T cells, enhancing cytokine production, and promoting robust antitumor immunity. These ICIs have revolutionized cancer treatment, yielding durable clinical responses across diverse malignancies, including melanoma, NSCLC, and renal cell carcinoma (RCC), by reactivating endogenous immune surveillance through NF-κB-dependent mechanisms [112].


### NF-κB Downstream Effector - Targeted Inhibitors:
  In addition to immune checkpoints and cytokine signaling, NF-κB regulates a broad array of downstream genes that drive tumor cell survival, proliferation, and resistance to apoptosis. Among these, BCL-2 and Cyclin D1 are key NF-κB target genes that mediate anti-apoptotic and cell-cycle progression pathways, respectively. Overexpression of BCL-2 family proteins allows cancer cells to evade programmed cell death, a defining hallmark of malignancy. As shown in Table 3 [112], Venetoclax, a selective BCL-2 inhibitor approved in 2016, effectively disrupts this survival mechanism and has demonstrated significant clinical benefit in chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) and acute myeloid leukemia (AML). Likewise, Cyclin D1 forms a functional complex with cyclin-dependent kinases CDK4 and CDK6 to regulate the G1/S transition of the cell cycle [114, 115]. Inhibiting this complex with CDK4/6 inhibitors, such as Palbociclib (approved in 2015), Ribociclib (2017), and Abemaciclib (2017), has revolutionized therapy for hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer [112]. Collectively, these agents exemplify how targeting NF-κB downstream effectors can suppress tumor progression by blocking essential pro-survival and proliferative signaling cascades.


### NF-κB Kinase - Targeted Inhibitors:
  Building on strategies that target NF-κB downstream effectors, another approach involves directly inhibiting kinases that regulate NF-κB signaling (Table 3) [112]. Several small-molecule inhibitors targeting these kinases have been developed for cancer and immune-mediated diseases. Auranofin, an FDA-approved drug since 1985 for RA, inhibits IKKβ and lipoxygenase, thereby modulating NF-κB activity. CA-4948 and R289 are orphan-designated drugs that block interleukin-1 receptor-associated kinases IRAK1/4, suppressing NF-κB–mediated proinflammatory signaling and are indicated for AML or myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) [112]. Amlexanox, approved in 1996 for aphthous ulcers and asthma, inhibits TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1, also known as IKKε), attenuating NF-κB activation. Most recently, EYD-001, an orphan drug approved in 2024 for systemic sclerosis, targets transforming growth factor-β-activated kinase 1 (TAK1), inhibiting TAK1-mediated NF-κB signaling [116]. Collectively, these kinase-targeted agents illustrate the therapeutic potential of directly modulating NF-κB-regulating enzymes across diverse diseases.


### NF-κB-Targeted Immunomodulatory Agents:
  Complementing strategies that target the proteasome, cytokines, immune checkpoints, downstream effectors, and kinases, immunomodulatory agents provide an additional approach to regulate NF-κB signaling while enhancing antitumor immunity and modulating the TME (Table 3) [112]. Thalidomide, approved in 1998, and Lenalidomide, approved in 2005, inhibit NF-κB signaling, thereby promoting immune-mediated tumor cell killing and reshaping the TME. Thalidomide is primarily used in MM, whereas Lenalidomide has broader indications, including MM and MDS [112]. These agents highlight the potential of combining NF-κB modulation with immune activation to achieve durable therapeutic responses in hematologic malignancies.


### Natural Compounds and Repurposed Drugs:
  Building on strategies that directly or indirectly target NF-κB signaling, natural compounds and repurposed drugs provide additional approaches to modulate this pathway. Natural products such as curcumin, resveratrol, epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG), and parthenolide also inhibit IKK activation or block NF-κB DNA binding, although their clinical utility is limited by bioavailability issues [109, 117]. Similarly, several FDA-approved drugs not originally developed as NF-κB inhibitors, including aspirin, statins, sulfasalazine, and metformin, exert indirect NF-κB inhibitory effects, offering potential for drug repurposing [108, 109]. While these agents have been extensively reviewed elsewhere [109–111] and will not be covered in detail here, they underscore the wide range of chemical strategies available to modulate NF-κB signaling and highlight opportunities for future therapeutic exploration.


### Investigational and Emerging NF-κB-Targeted Agents:
  Building on FDA-approved and orphan-designated NF-κB inhibitors, numerous investigational compounds are being explored to modulate NF-κB signaling at multiple levels. While thousands of additional candidates exist in preclinical or clinical development, their efficacy remains uncertain and has been reviewed elsewhere [109–111]; here, we focus on several representative examples.

  For example, several agents target NF-κB downstream effectors, including FL118 and YM155, which inhibit survivin to downregulate NF-κB activity, and SurVaxM, a survivin-targeted vaccine that elicits immune-mediated antitumor responses in glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) [118]. Cytokine-targeted approaches include HuMax-IL8 (BMS-986253), a monoclonal antibody against IL-8. Other strategies directly modulate NF-κB activation through kinases and regulatory complexes, such as the NEMO-binding domain (NBD) peptide, which inhibits IKKγ/NEMO and IKKβ interactions [119], and MLN4924, a neural precursor cell expressed, developmentally down-regulated 8 (NEDD8)-activating enzyme (NAE) inhibitor. Preclinical inhibitors targeting IKKβ (MLN120B, TPCA-1) and NIK (CW15337, NIK SMI1) further expand the pipeline [109–111]. The epigenetic enzyme protein arginine methyltransferase 5 (PRMT5), which mediates methylation of NF-κB, has also gained attention as a promising therapeutic target. Multiple inhibitors are in clinical [120–122] or preclinical stages (e.g., PR5-LL-CM01) [123, 124], while repurposed agents such as candesartan and cloperastine also exhibit potential inhibitory effects [125]. Additionally, nanocatalytic medicines and novel immunomodulatory strategies have emerged to potentiate NF-κB-dependent antitumor immunity. Nanocatalysts locally reprogram TME by generating ROS or inducing immunogenic cell death, which activates NF-κB in immune cells, enhances antigen cross-presentation, and promotes T-cell infiltration [126]. Combination therapies pairing NF-κB activation with immune checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., anti-PD-1/PD-L1) or Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors to rebalance cytokine signaling are being investigated to maximize immune clearance while minimizing inflammatory toxicity [127, 128]. Collectively, these investigational agents illustrate the diverse strategies under development to modulate NF-κB signaling, offering promising avenues for cancer and inflammatory disease therapies.


## Conclusion and Future Perspective
  While several NF-κB-targeted therapies have received FDA approval or orphan designation, the pipeline of investigational agents far surpasses these approved drugs, encompassing proteasome inhibitors, kinase modulators, downstream effector-targeted drugs, immunomodulatory agents, epigenetic enzyme inhibitors, and nanocatalytic medicines. Many remain in preclinical or early clinical stages, underscoring both the therapeutic promise and the challenges of safely modulating NF-κB in tumor and immune contexts.

  Future efforts should focus on understanding context-specific NF-κB signaling, optimizing combination therapies, improving drug delivery and bioavailability, and identifying predictive biomarkers for patient stratification. Artificial intelligence (AI) can accelerate these advances by guiding drug discovery, predicting synergistic combinations, and integrating multi-omics data to enable precision targeting. Collectively, FDA-approved drugs, emerging therapeutics, and the prospective application of AI-driven strategies highlight NF-κB modulation as a versatile and promising avenue for cancer and immune disease therapy.
