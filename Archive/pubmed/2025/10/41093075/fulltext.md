# Sp100A isoform promotes localization of the histone chaperone HIRA to PML nuclear bodies


## Abstract
  PML nuclear bodies (PML-NBs) are dynamic subnuclear structures important for chromatin dynamics and anti-viral defense. In this study we investigate the role of Sp100 isoforms in promoting localization of the H3.3 histone chaperone HIRA to PML-NBs in human keratinocytes. Sp100 knockout (KO) cell lines were generated in normal keratinocytes using CRISPR-Cas9 technology and shown to display typical skin development, normal PML-NB formation, and a normal interferon response as determined by RNAseq. However, the histone chaperone HIRA and its associated complex members (Ubinuclein-1 and ASF1A) failed to localize to PML-NBs in the absence of Sp100, even after interferon stimulation. Exogenous expression of the four main isoforms of Sp100 showed that the Sp100A isoform is the primary driver of HIRA localization to PML-NBs; predictive structural analysis of the various Sp100 isoforms using several AI methods (AlphaFold 3, Chai-1, and Boltz-2) plus molecular dynamics simulation of Sp100B highlighted how interspersed structural domains and intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) could influence this localization. Analysis of Sp100A proteins with mutations in seven distinct functions showed that the SUMO-interacting motif (SIM) plays an important role in HIRA localization to PML-NBs. These findings highlight the functional diversity of the Sp100 isoforms in modulating chromatin dynamics at PML-NBs and further discern the molecular features underpinning the relationship between histone chaperones and PML-NBs. We conclude that HIRA recruitment to PML-NBs is not required for immediate interferon signaling but as suggested by others, is potentially required for maintaining epigenetic memory by delayed H3.3 incorporation at interferon-stimulated genes.


## Results

### Generation of Sp100-deficient NIKS cell lines
  NIKS is a spontaneously immortalized keratinocyte cell line that has retained the ability to terminally differentiate into a stratified epithelium and is often used in the study of HPV infection (32) and has been used to generate human skin grafts. Previous studies in our laboratory showed that levels of keratinocyte differentiation markers were slightly altered by siRNA downregulation of Sp100 (33). Therefore, to study the role of Sp100 in keratinocyte biology, we generated Sp100 knockout (KO) NIKS cell lines using CRISPR technology. We designed five CRISPR Cas9 guide RNAs to generate deletions in exons 3, 6 and 8 of the SP100 gene (Fig. 1B). The guide sequences were cloned into the PX458 plasmid, which also expresses GFP, and the resulting plasmids were electroporated into NIKs cells. Our screening strategy is outlined in Figure 1C: 72 hours post-transfection, GFP-positive cells were selected by flow cytometry and plated onto irradiated feeder plates to allow recovery and expansion of the cells. NIKS cells have a plating efficiency of ∼10%, and so we plated 100 cells into each well in multiple 12-well dishes with the expectation of ∼ ten clones per well, which would allow effective screening for knockout clones by immunofluorescence. The Sp100 antibody used to confirm that Sp100 protein expression was eliminated recognizes all four isoforms and was raised against amino acids 312 to 411 of Sp100A (encoded by exons 9–13). After an initial screen of 36 clones by combined PML and Sp100 immunofluorescence, seven clones were expanded for characterization. These consisted of five Sp100 knockout clones with no Sp100 expression (Sp100 KO 1–5), and two clones derived from control cells (Vector 1–2) that had been transfected with the parental PX458 plasmid and underwent the same sorting and selection procedures. These clones were expanded into cell lines, and the absence of Sp100 was verified by immunofluorescence (Fig. S1A) and Western blot (Fig. S1B).


### Genotype of the NIKS Sp100 KO clones
  To genotype the cell clones, the regions targeted by CRISPR were PCR amplified, ligated into the PCR2.1 TOPO cloning vector and analyzed by Sanger sequencing. A minimum of eight clones were selected per exon for each cell line. As shown in Table S1, each KO clone had mutations in exon 3 and exon 8, but not in exon 6. Sp100 KO 1, 2 and 3 cell lines harbored various deletions around the guide RNA that targeted exon 3 and also contained 35 bp and 36 bp deletions in exon 8. Sp100 KO 4 and 5 had an identical 158 bp insertion at the guide site in exon 3 that is derived from 28S rRNA. These cells also had additional 35 bp and 36 bp deletions in exon 8, similar to those in KO cells 1, 2 and 3.


### The absence of Sp100 does not affect keratinocyte differentiation
  We have previously analyzed the role of Sp100 in HPV31 containing CIN612 9E keratinocytes using siRNA technology and observed an approximate three-fold increase in the late differentiation marker filaggrin after calcium induced differentiation (33). To determine if the NIKS Sp100 KO cells can differentiate normally into a stratified epithelium, we generated organotypic rafts from the NIKS vector and Sp100 KO cells. As shown in Fig. S2, the SP100 KO cells formed a stratified epithelium that was indistinguishable from that formed by vector control or parental NIKS cells. We conclude that stable knockout of this protein does not affect keratinocyte differentiation.

### Sp100 is required for localization of HIRA to PML-NBs
  The histone chaperone HIRA associates with PML-NBs in response to IFN, in a cell line specific manner, and McFarlane et al. and Kleijwegt et al. report that this is dependent on Sp100 (17, 18). Our Sp100 CRISPR knockouts are in NIKS-normal diploid keratinocytes. These cells are valued as being “normal”, are carefully cultured using a keratinocyte-fibroblast coculture system, and, as shown here, can be differentiated into a full-thickness skin equivalent. Most cell lines used in similar studies have defects in innate immunity and/or are not completely devoid of Sp100 (were dependent on siRNA knockdown).

  To determine whether IFN induced localization of HIRA to PML-NBs also occurs in NIKS keratinocytes, and whether Sp100 is required for this localization, we assessed the colocalization of HIRA with PML-NBs in NIKS, control, and Sp100 KO cells before and after stimulation with IFN. ### Sp100 is required for localization of HIRA complex members to PML-NBs

Others have shown that all members of the HIRA complex, in addition to ASF1a, localize to PML-NBs in response to various stimuli (12, 13, 15, 18, 34). As shown in Figure 3 and by others (17, 18), HIRA localization to PML-NBs occurs in a Sp100-dependent manner. However, the dependency on Sp100 for localization of other HIRA complex members is unknown. To evaluate this, we assessed the colocalization of HIRA and Ubinuclein-1 (Fig. 4A) or HIRA and ASF1a (Fig. 4D) with PML-NBs in Vector 2 and Sp100 KO3 cells, before and after IFN treatment. As shown in Figure 4A, both HIRA and Ubinuclein-1 localized throughout the nucleoplasm in Vector 2 cells, with almost no association with PML-NBs in the absence of IFN treatment (Fig. 4B). However, they were observed together at PML-NBs in all cells following stimulation with IFN. ### The Sp100A isoform promotes HIRA localization to PML-NBs in NIKS Sp100 KO cells
  Sp100A, Sp100B, Sp100C, and Sp100HMG are the four main isoforms encoded by alternatively spliced transcripts of the Sp100 gene (Fig. 1A). All four isoforms share N-terminal sequences with Sp100A, but Sp100B, Sp100C, and Sp100HMG proteins contain additional domains within the C-terminus that confer the ability of Sp100 to interact with DNA and specific modifications on chromatin (20). The N-terminal region of all isoforms contains a dimerization region that could promote the formation of homo- and hetero-dimers.We and others have shown that exogenous expression of each isoform results in differential association with PML-NBs and localization patterns within the nucleus (25, 35, 36). However, these experiments were performed in cell lines containing endogenous Sp100 proteins that could affect the distribution of exogenous Sp100 proteins. As such, we evaluated the nuclear localization of the four Sp100 isoforms, in addition to a mutated isoform of Sp100B containing a W655Q amino acid substitution in the SAND domain (Sp100BQ) in Sp100 KO3 keratinocytes.

  Sp100 KO3 cells were transfected with either a control EYFP expression vector or expression vectors encoding individual Sp100 isoforms as YFP-fusion proteins (27). Representative immunofluorescence images of transfected cells treated with or without interferon are shown in Figure 5A, and Fig. S4, respectively, and the protein levels of each isoform were confirmed by Western blot analysis (Fig. S5). As shown in Figure 5, A and B, all four Sp100 isoforms localized to PML-NBs, but with varied frequencies. Sp100A and Sp100HMG were associated with PML-NBs in all expressing cells, both in the absence or the presence of IFN. However, Sp100B and Sp100C localized with only with a subset of PML-NBs per nuclei in only ∼40 to 60% expressing cells. It has been shown previously that mutation of the SAND domain in Sp100B results in its relocalization, predominantly to PML-NBs (37), and our observations confirm this; Sp100BQ localized to PML-NBs in all expressing cells. Stimulation with IFN increased the association of each Sp100 isoform with PML-NBs. In addition to localization at PML-NBs, we also observed small patches of Sp100 nuclear accumulation or aggregation of the longer isoforms that did not colocalize with PML, as others have reported (25, 36). Thus, individual Sp100 isoforms differentially associate with PML-NBs in the absence of endogenous Sp100 protein.Figure 5Sp100A promotes HIRA localization to PML-NBs.   We next evaluated which Sp100 isoform was responsible for the relocalization of HIRA to PML-NBs. Only the expression of Sp100A resulted in a high frequency (∼70% cells) with HIRA and Sp100 colocalization at PML-NBs. The number of PML-NBs per nucleus showing colocalization increased slightly to 75% cells following stimulation with IFN, concomitant with increased numbers of PML-NBs per nucleus containing Sp100A (Fig. 5, B and C). Despite frequent association of Sp100HMG with PML-NBs (Fig. 5B), HIRA was rarely associated with PML-NBs (at most 1–3 PML-NBS per nucleus in ∼ 6.5% cells). When Sp100B and Sp100C were associated with PML-NBs, HIRA association with PML-NBs was almost never observed. As shown in Fig. S5, exogenous expression of the Sp100 isoforms did not alter HIRA or PML protein levels. As described above, the Sp100B protein with a mutation in the SAND DNA-binding domain localizes primarily to PML-NBs. Despite this, Sp100BQ expression resulted in minimal re-localization of HIRA to PML-NBs (∼11% cells had HIRA at 1–3 foci). Of note, Sp100A is expressed at higher levels than the other isoforms (Fig. S5), but examination of Sp100 YFP intensity at individual PML-NBs indicated that HIRA association did not always correlate directly with YFP-Sp100 levels. We conclude that Sp100A is primarily responsible for the re-localization of the histone chaperone HIRA to PML-NBs.


### Sp100A SUMO interacting motif is important for HIRA localization at PML
  To determine what functions of Sp100A are required for its association with PML-NBs and promotion of HIRA localization to PML-NBs, we generated a series of expression vectors that encoded Sp100A proteins mutated in previously characterized functions, as shown in Figure 6A (22, 38, 39, 40). The mutations targeted the SUMO interacting motif (mSIM) (40), the site of SUMOylation (mSUMO), the nuclear localization signal (mNLS), the HSR domain (ΔHSR) which is required for nuclear body targeting and dimerization (38), the HP1 binding region (mHP1) (22), alanine and aspartic acid substitutions of the S180 phosphorylation site (39), and deletion of the last 3 amino acids (ΔKED) that are unique to the Sp100A isoform. Each mutation was substituted into the EYFP-Sp100A expression vector. With the exception of ΔKED, these mutations have been described previously, but have been characterized by different groups, in different experiments, conditions, and cell lines. Here we rigorously compare the phenotype of each mutated protein (in the presence and absence of interferon) in our NIKS keratinocyte cell lines. To assess the intracellular localization of the mutated EYFP-Sp100A proteins, the expression vectors were transfected into Sp100 KO3 cells. Transfected cells were either untreated or treated with 25 ng/ml IFNα at 24 h post-transfection and fixed 24 h post-stimulation with IFN. Cells were analyzed by immunofluorescence and confocal microscopy for Sp100 and HIRA intracellular localization (Fig. 6B). As shown in the representative images in Figure 6B, and summarized in the Table in Figure 7A, most mutated proteins (ΔKED, mSUMO, mHP1, and S180 phosphorylation mutations) localized to the PML-NBs in ∼60 to 70% cells, similar to the wildtype Sp100A protein. As expected, the EYFP protein localized throughout the cell, Sp100A ΔHSR localized throughout the nucleoplasm, Sp100A mSIM showed both accumulation at PML bodies as well as a nucleoplasmic distribution, and Sp100A mNLS was mostly cytoplasmic with a small amount entering the nucleus and localizing to PML-NBs.   To confirm that the mutated EYFP-Sp100A proteins were expressed at similar levels and were of the expected molecular weight, Sp100 protein levels were analyzed by immunoblot (Fig. 7B). Each protein was of the expected size, and all were expressed at comparable levels, although the levels of mNLS, mSUMO, and ΔKED were ∼ 50% lower. SUMOylated Sp100A could also be observed and was absent in the mSUMO and ΔHSR proteins, as expected. Notably, the levels of SUMOylated Sp100A were lower in the mNLS and mSIM proteins.

  To assess the impact of each Sp100A mutation on HIRA localization to PML-NBs, HIRA localization was evaluated in cells with moderate expression of the EYFP-Sp100A proteins (Fig. 6B) and is displayed as % cells with HIRA and Sp100A at PML-NBs in Figure 7C. As expected, HIRA did not localize to PML-NBs in NIKS Sp100 KO3 cells expressing YFP alone, even after interferon treatment (Fig. 7C). HIRA localized to PML-NBs with Sp100A wildtype, ΔKED, mSUMO, mHP1, S180A and S180D in >60% cells with or without IFN treatment. Of the remaining mutated Sp100A proteins, for the most part ΔHSR did not localize to PML-NBs and correspondingly did not promote HIRA localization to the bodies (We do observe a very small amount of the ΔHSR protein enriched in PML-NBs but with no enrichment of HIRA). Most of the mNLS Sp100A protein was cytoplasmic but small amounts colocalized with PML-NBs, and in 7 to 10% these cells HIRA colocalized at PML-NBs. Sternsdorf and colleages have shown that the K447E NLS mutation also greatly reduces SUMOylation of Sp100A (38) and this could explain the low efficiency of Sp100-dependent HIRA localization at PML-NBs. However, we and others, find that Sp100A with a mutation in the SUMO site localizes similar to wildtype indicating again that this cannot be the complete explanation for the mNLS phenotype.

  Most notable is the mSIM Sp100A protein, which localizes to the PML-NBs with or without IFN treatment (there is also a low level of nucleoplasmic signal), but only promotes HIRA colocalization after IFN treatment. This implies that IFN treatment is modifying Sp100A in some way that enables it to promote HIRA colocalization or alternatively is modifying or inducing other proteins to localize to PML-NBs and co-operate with the mSIM protein to rescue HIRA localization. These results indicate that while the SUMO interacting motif is important, SIM alone is not entirely responsible for the Sp100-dependent HIRA recruitment to PML-NBs. A potential concern is that the mSIM mutated protein is present at lower levels (∼0.54 fold) than the wild-type protein (Fig. 7B). However, other mutated proteins (e.g. ΔKED) are also expressed at levels similar to mSIM, yet they are able to promote HIRA localization to PML-NBs in the absence of interferon. Furthermore, interferon treatment does not increase the level of the transiently expressed mSIM protein but HIRA localization to PML-NBs is increased from 0 to ∼70% of mSIM expressing cells.

  To investigate this further, we generated a different mSIM mutated Sp100 A protein (I324E/V325A/I326G), as well as both mSIM mutations in combination with the mSUMO mutation. This resulted in phenotypes that were identical to the original mSUMO (I325K/I325K) mutated protein (Fig. S6). Therefore, the Sp100A SIM motif is the primary contributor to Sp100A-mediated localization of HIRA to PML-NBs.


### Structural prediction of the Sp100 isoforms and location of Sp100A mutations
  To gain some insight into the Sp100 functions required for recruitment of HIRA to PML-NBs we generated 3D models of each isoform using a pipeline of well-established methods, including AlphaFold3 (41), Boltz-2 (42), and Chai-1 (43) as outlined in detail in Methods, in the legend of Fig. S7, and in ModelArchive https://modelarchive.org/doi/10.5452/(Sp100A:ma-7mo0m; Sp100B:ma-0hla7; Sp100C:ma-ew0pl; Sp100HMG:ma-xw697).

  It is well-established that the N-terminal region of Sp100 mediates dimerization (22, 38, 44) and residues 33 to 149 have been defined as a PROSITE HSR domain (PS51414). This is well supported by our bioinformatics analysis, AI modeling, and molecular dynamics simulations showing that dimerization of Sp100 is mediated by the N-terminal HSR domain, followed by a largely disordered region. Several of these methods produce models with enough order in the region between residues 153 and 602 (the region between the HSR domain and the SAND domain in the three longer isoforms (Fig. S7) to justify further exploration. However, none of the initial AI models have an overall pLDDT greater than 50 (poor overall), with only the well-defined domains (HRS, SAND, and HMG) having pLDDT>70 and the N-terminal domain (HRS) dimerizing in a recognizably similar manner in most models, independently of the method used. However, molecular dynamics simulations suggest a semi-stable region between residues 380 and 480, which may represent a contact domain. Example images are shown in Fig. S7, and the final structures are available on ModelArchive.

  Each Sp100 isoform contains the same N-terminal 483 amino acids, yet only the Sp100A protein promotes HIRA recruitment to PML-NBs. The flexible parts connecting the main domains of each isoform allow for various alternative relative dispositions of the domains, suggesting a dynamic arrangement and the possibility that the C-terminal domains in the longer isoforms could sterically hinder interactions with the N-terminal regions important for Sp100A re-localization of HIRA.To examine the Sp100A mutations further, they were mapped onto the predicted dimeric model Sp100A structure (Fig. 7D). With the exception of the ΔHSR mutation that removes most of the structured dimerization and PML-NB targeting regions, all other mutations mapped to the C-terminal regions of the protein predicted to be unstructured and disordered.

  AIUPRED was used to annotate and predict the disordered regions described above (Fig. S8). We hypothesize that the combination of Sp100 structural domains and disordered regions plays a role in Sp100 function. PML-NBs can form biomolecular condensates dependent on liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS), and many of the constituent proteins contain intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs), which can present flexible interaction motifs to partner proteins (45). We further explored the dynamic characteristics of Sp100B by molecular dynamics using YASARA (46) as described in detail in the legend of Fig. S9 and in see ModelArchive Sp100B entry ma-0hla7.

  Fig. S9 shows a comparison of the root mean fluctuations (RMSF) for the 400 ns after equilibration. The RMSF, compared to the results from AIUPRED and VoroMQA, is shown. The correlation with expected disorder appears to be preserved, and the presence of a semi-stable region between residues 380 and 480 is confirmed. However, the overall dynamics suggest that domains can move relative to one another, except the HSR domain dimer, which remains stable throughout the entire simulation. A movie (see ModelArchive Sp100B entry ma-0hla7 and Supplementary Movie 1) shows a simplified rendition of the observed flexibility, morphing the frames' extreme displacements relative to the lowest energy conformation, with the protein colorized by RMSF values. The dynamic cross-correlation matrix derived from analyzing the molecular dynamics trajectory further supports this interpretation (see ModelArchive Sp100B entry ma-0hla7).


### RNA-seq analysis shows no significant changes in basal or interferon-stimulated gene expression in the Sp100 KO cells
  We show that the Sp100A isoform is absolutely required to relocalize HIRA to PML-NBs and it has been proposed that this is important for interferon signaling. To investigate changes in the transcriptome of Sp100 KO cells, and their response to IFN treatment, RNA was collected in triplicate from Vector 2 and Sp100 KO5 cells that were untreated, or treated with IFN for 12 or 48 h, Figure 8A. Principal component analysis (PCA) of RNA-seq data showed similar basal transcription profiles between Vector 2 and Sp100 KO5 cells, with samples clustering based on interferon treatments rather than genotype (Fig. 8B). Detailed analyses showed that there were no major differences in the transcriptome of each cell line, either in the absence or presence of interferon, or at 12 or 48 h post-treatment. Minor differences in expression of keratinocyte differentiation markers or ISGs are due to NIKS cell clone-to-clone variation. IFN treatment of both cell lines resulted in a massive induction of interferon-stimulated genes, but there was no significant difference in the response between the Sp100 positive Vector 2 cells and Sp100 deficient KO5 cells (Fig. 8C). ## Discussion
  In this study we generated CRISPR Sp100 KO cell lines from the immortalized keratinocyte cell line, NIKS, and showed that they could differentiate into a stratified epithelia like the parental NIKS cells. The absence of Sp100 did not perturb PML-NB formation nor recruitment of Daxx to PML-NBs. Recent studies have shown that the recruitment of the histone chaperone HIRA to PML-NBs is dependent on the Sp100 protein (17, 18). Thus, the NIKS Sp100 KO cell lines are valuable to study the interplay of HIRA and Sp100 in human keratinocytes.

  The histone chaperone, HIRA localizes to PML-NBs in response to viral infection or transfection with plasmid DNA (15, 16, 17), both of which induce secretion of IFNs (47, 48). Similar to previous studies, we observed that IFN treatment caused re-localization of HIRA to PML-NBs in NIKS keratinocytes and this is independent of viral infection or cellular senescence (17, 18). We show that HIRA (and other components of the chaperone complex, Ubinuclein-1 and ASF1a do not localize to PML-NBs in NIKS keratinocytes in the absence of Sp100, even in the presence of interferon.

  The Sp100 KO NIKs cells present a unique opportunity to evaluate the roles of the different Sp100 isoforms in the absence of endogenous Sp100 proteins. Previous studies have shown that each isoform localizes with varying degrees to PML-NBs in cells that express endogenous Sp100 (25, 35, 36). The Sp100 isoforms can form homo- and hetero-dimers through N-terminal sequences, and this could influence the localization of each isoform. In the Sp100 KO keratinocytes, there are no endogenous isoforms; we find that the Sp100A isoform associates predominantly with PML-NBs and the longer isoforms associate partially with PML-NBs. The longer forms contain chromatin and DNA binding domains that can direct their association to other regions of the nucleus, and in support of this, we show that mutation of the SAND DNA binding function in the Sp100B isoform results in a protein that predominantly localizes with the PML-NBs. We also show that only the Sp100A isoform could re-localize HIRA to PML-NBs. This finding is novel and unexpected, because each isoform contains the same amino acid sequences and N-terminal domains as Sp100A (except for the last three amino acid residues). The Sp100A protein is expressed at higher levels than the other isoforms, but this does not explain why it is the only isoform to re-localize HIRA as the mutated Sp100B W655Q protein localizes strongly to PML-NBs but does not change HIRA localization. It is possible that Sp100A is differentially post-translationally modified compared to the other isoforms, or that interaction between N-terminal and C-terminal Sp100 domains sterically hinder the ability of the longer forms to re-localize HIRA.

  We show that Sp100A is the predominant isoform responsible for HIRA localization to PML-NBs. Expression of Sp100A proteins with previously characterized mutations indicates that (as also shown by others (18)) mutation of the major SUMO site of Sp100 at K297 does not affect Sp100A localization to PML-NBs nor recruitment of HIRA. Not unexpectedly, deletion of the HSR PML-NB targeting domain abrogates both functions. However, mutation of the SIM motif does not interfere with Sp100A’s PML-NBs localization but does abrogate HIRA recruitment to the nuclear bodies in the absence of interferon treatment. While this could be postulated to be due to Sp100A recruiting HIRA through SUMO-SIM interactions, Kleijwegt and colleagues have shown that mutation of putative SUMO sites in HIRA residue K809 does not affect its accumulation at PML-NBs (18) and no direct protein interaction could be detected between HIRA and Sp100 (17). In contrast, the authors show that mutation of the SIM2 motif in HIRA (225VLRL228) abrogated its localization to PML-NBs (18).

  In our study, treatment of cells expressing Sp100A mSIM with IFN resulted in localization of HIRA to PML-NBs. IFN treatment results in SUMOylation of many proteins (49), particularly at PML-NBs. We postulate that upon IFN stimulation the global increase in SUMOylation, particularly at PML-NB sumoylation hubs, greatly enhances the SUMO-SIM interactions among many proteins and that these interactions can compensate for the inability of the Sp100A mSIM to promote HIRA localization to PML-NBs. It may be possible to dissect these interactions more completely by generating combinatorial mutations in the Sp100A and HIRA proteins.

  The concentration of macromolecules at nuclear bodies can greatly increase the efficiency of biochemical processes. PML-NBs are thought to form biomolecular condensates that might be dependent on liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) (3, 50). These condensates are driven by multivalent interactions such as those between proteins that are SUMOylated and those with SUMO interaction motifs (SIMs) (51). Many of these proteins also contain IDRs, which can present flexible interaction motifs to partner proteins (45). Our structural predictions of the Sp100 isoforms in Figure 7 and Figs. S7–S9 show that sequences outside the HSR, SAND, PHD, bromodomain, and HMG domains are predicted to be unstructured and are likely IDRs. Of note, most functional motifs mapped to the Sp100A protein localize to these regions.

  Despite the absence of Sp100 proteins and lack of localization of HIRA to PML-NBs, the NIKS Sp100 KO keratinocytes described in this study show normal growth and differentiation in cell culture. However, Sp100 proteins have potent pro-viral and anti-viral responses and thus the NIKS Sp100 KO cells will be valuable for the study of viral infection of keratinocytes in monolayer culture or in a stratified epithelium.

  PML-NBs are central to cellular innate immunity, and they play an important role in anti-viral responses. Interferon stimulation results in rapid transcriptional activation of thousands of ISGs. These gene loci localize adjacent to PML-NBs after interferon treatment, and HIRA has been shown to deposit H3.3 at the 3′ end of these genes (18). However, this deposition occurs well after the rapid induction of ISG transcription, and HIRA localization to PML-NBs is not absolutely required for the IFN response (this study and (17, 18)). Nevertheless, the HIRA-dependent deposition of H3.3 and concomitant acquisition of H3K36me3 generates chromatin marks on memory ISGs (52). This generates epigenetic memory at ISGs that enables a more rapid transcriptional response upon repeated interferon stimulation (52).

  There are three prevailing hypotheses about the role of HIRA at PML-NBs (1): Upon interferon signaling, the PML-NBs act as nuclear depots to balance the availability of HIRA and H3.3 across the nucleus; and/or (2) HIRA localization at PML-NBs directly promotes transcription of ISGs; and/or (3) HIRA localization at PML-NBs is required for delayed H3.3 incorporation at ISGs to generate epigenetic memory of the interferon response. We have used our Sp100 KO keratinocyte cells to show that HIRA recruitment to PML-NBs absolutely requires the Sp100A protein, but immediate interferon signaling is unaffected; thus, we can rule out hypothesis 2. Our Sp100 KO cells should prove useful for future studies examining the location of Sp100 and HIRA in nuclear depots and their role in long-term epigenetic innate immune memory.


## Experimental procedures

### Cell culture
  NIKS cells (RRID:CVCL_A1LW) were grown in Rheinwald-Green F medium (3:1 Ham’s F-12/high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium [DMEM], 5% fetal bovine serum [FBS], 0.4 μg/ml hydrocortisone, 8.4 ng/ml cholera toxin, 10 ng/ml epidermal growth factor, 24 μg/ml adenine, 2 mM glutamine, 6 μg/ml insulin, 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 μg/ml streptomycin) on a layer of lethally irradiated J2/3T3 mouse fibroblast cells. J2/3T3 fibroblasts were grown in DMEM, 10% newborn calf serum, 2 mM glutamine, 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 μg/ml streptomycin. Parental NIKS cells were profiled by KaryoStat Karyotyping (Thermo Fisher). All cell lines tested negative for mycoplasma contamination.


### Organotypic rafts
  Organotypic raft cultures were generated by plating NIKS keratinocytes on a collagen dermal equivalent containing NIH 3T3 fibroblasts as described previously (53). Rafts were lifted to the air-liquid interface and cultured for 15 days in raft culture medium (3:1 DMEM/F12, 10% fetal calf serum [FCS], 0.4 μg/ml hydrocortisone, 0.01 nM cholera toxin, 5 μg/ml transferrin). Rafts were fixed in 3.7% formaldehyde, embedded in paraffin and sections stained with hematoxylin and eosin.


### Generation of Sp100 knockout cell lines using CRISPR
  Guide RNAs (gRNAs) were designed using CHOPCHOP, https://chopchop.cbu.uib.no/, a webtool for designing target sites for CRISPR Cas9 mutagenesis, and the UCSC Genome Browser (54) to check for specificity. The top five gRNAs that targeted all isoforms of Sp100 were selected. The oligos used were 5′-AGGATGTTCACGGAAGACCA-3′ (exon 3); 5′-CTTTCTTCATCACAGGGCAACGG-3′ (exon 8), 5′-TGCTGTCCAAGTGAATAATGGGG-3′ (exon 8), 5′-CAAGTGAATAATGGGGATGCTGG-3′ (exon 8) and 5′- ACTCTTTTCGAAGCCTGACTTGG-3′ (exon 6). The oligonucleotides were cloned into pSpCas9(BB)-2A-GFP (PX458, Feng Zhang Addgene plasmid # 48138). The resulting five plasmids were mixed in equal ratios and electroporated into NIKS cells using the Amaxa Human Keratinocyte Nucleofector Kit (Lonza). Vector control cells were electroporated with pSpCas9(BB)-2A-GFP without the gRNA sequence. Positively transfected cells were isolated by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) for GFP expression at 72 h post-transfection. The GFP-positive cells were plated on a 10 cm plate of lethally irradiated J2/3T3 fibroblasts and allowed to recover and expand for 8 days. Cells were plated at 100 cells per well of a 12-well plate, and the resulting cells were screened for the absence of Sp100 by immunofluorescence staining with the Sp100 antibody, rabbit anti-Sp100 HPA016707 (Sigma). Pools that showed no expression of Sp100 by immunofluorescence were expanded into cell lines.


### Plasmids
  YFP-Sp100 isoform expression vectors in pEYFP (pEYFP-Sp100A, pEYFP-Sp100B, pEYFP-Sp100C, pEYFP-Sp100HMG, and pEYFP-Sp100B W655Q) were acquired from Susan Janicki and have been previously described (27). To generate mutations in Sp100A, gene fragments containing point mutations or deletions in Sp100A sequences were synthesized by Synbio. The mutated fragments were cloned between the BamHI/Mfel or EcoRI/BamHI sites in pEYFP-Sp100A. The S188D and S188A mutations described by Dong et al. (39) correspond to serine residue 180 in the sequence of Sp100A used in this study (NP_001193631.1). An additional SIM mutation, mSIM2 (I324E/V325A/I326G) was generated in Sp100A as well as each mSIM mutation in combination with the mSUMO mutation using standard PCR mutagenesis methods. The sequences were verified by long-read sequencing technology (Plasmidsaurus).


### Antibodies
  The Research Resource Identification (RRID number is given for all antibodies in the methods below. The key antibodies in this study against Sp100 and HIRA proteins were validated in our laboratory by siRNA or CRISPR knockdown.


### Transfection of EYFP-Sp100 expression vectors
  NIKS Sp100 KO cells were seeded at a density of 2.9 × 104 cells/cm2 onto a monolayer of irradiated J2/3T3 cells in a 12-well plate containing glass coverslips or in a 6-well plate and cultured overnight. Cells were transfected with 400 ng control EYFP expression vector or EYFP expression vectors encoding Sp100A, Sp100B, Sp100C, Sp100HMG, Sp100B-W655Q, or Sp100A mutations complexed with 3:1 Fugene6 transfection reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were stimulated with 25 ng/ml Human Interferon Alpha 2a (PBL Assay Science), as indicated. Cells were fixed on coverslips or lysed for protein extraction, as indicated.


### Immunofluorescence
  Cells were fixed at room temperature in 4% paraformaldehyde/1x PBS for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.1% or 0.5% Triton-X 100 in PBS, blocked in 5% normal donkey serum, and stained with the following primary antibodies for either 1 h at 37 °C or overnight at 4 °C: Sp100 rabbit polyclonal [RRID: AB_1857398] (Sigma; HPA016707, 1:500), Sp100 mouse monoclonal [AB_2722351] (Development Studies Hybridoma Bank; PCRP-SP100-1B9-s, 5 μg/ml), PML [PG-M3] mouse monoclonal [RRID: AB_628162] (Santa Cruz; sc-966, 1:50-1:100), PML [A-20] goat polyclonal [RRID: AB_2284064] (Santa Cruz; sc-9862, 1:50), PML [H-238] rabbit polyclonal [RRID: AB_2166848] (Santa Cruz; sc-5621, 1:100), PML [G-8] mouse monoclonal (Santa Cruz; sc-377340, 1:50-1:100), Daxx rabbit polyclonal [RRID: AB_1078625] (Sigma; HPA008736, 1:100), HIRA [WC119] mouse monoclonal [RRID: AB_1977097] (Millipore; 04-1488, 1:100), Ubinuclein-1 rabbit polyclonal [RRID: AB_2684420] (Sigma; HPA061029), ASF1a [C6E10] rabbit monoclonal [RRID: AB_2289918] (Cell Signaling; 2990S, 1:100), and GFP-Booster Alexa Fluor 488 alpaca [RRID: AB_2827573] (Chromotek; gb2AF488-50:50 μl, 1:1000). Fluorescent secondary antibodies conjugated with Alexa 488, Alexa 594, Rhodamine Red-X, or Alexa 647 (Jackson Immunoresearch; 1:100) were applied using standard procedures. The coverslips were stained with 300 nM DAPI and mounted with Prolong Gold.


### Confocal microscopy
  Images were collected with a TCS-SP8 laser scanning confocal imaging system using a 63X oil immersion objective (numerical aperture 1.4). 2D images are from a single optical slice unless otherwise mentioned. All images were processed using Leica LAS AF Lite (Leica Microsystems, version 2.6.0) or Leica Application Suite X (Leica Microsystems, version 3.7.0.20979) software or Imaris (version 10.2, Bitplane). Images were pseudo-colored where indicated.


### Image analysis
  Manual Scoring: The average number of visible PML-NBs per nuclei was counted for a minimum of 30 nuclei per replicate. The percentage of visible PML-NBs per nuclei containing Daxx was assessed for a minimum of 30 nuclei per replicate. The association of various proteins (HIRA, HIRA + Ubinuclein-1, HIRA + ASF1a, and Sp100) with visible PML-NBs was scored, grouped into four categories according to the number of positive PML-NBs (0 foci, 1–3 foci, 4–9 foci, and 10+ foci), and reported as the total percentage of nuclei associated with each category for a minimum of 30 nuclei per replicate. HIRA association with PML-NBs in cells containing EYFP-Sp100 isoforms was evaluated as described above but only nuclei where Sp100 was associated with PML-NBs were scored. The sample size per condition is at least two biological replicates. Counts were not blinded.

  For assessment of the mutated Sp100A proteins, cells with moderate YFP expression were identified, and the intracellular location of the mutated Sp100A proteins was scored. HIRA localization to PML-NBs was assessed by manual counting of HIRA accumulation (yes/no) at PML-NBs in cells with Sp100A localizing to PML-NBs. Between 35 and 250 cells were scored for each condition per replicate. The sample size per condition is at least two biological replicates. Counts were not blinded.


### Western blot
  Feeders were removed prior to protein extraction. Cells were lysed in SDS extraction buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl [pH 6.8], 5% [wt/vol] SDS, 10% [vol/vol] glycerol), sonicated, and heat denatured. A BCA protein assay kit (Thermo-Pierce) was used to determine total protein concentration. Prior to loading the gel, NuPAGE LDS Sample Buffer© and DTT (50 mM) were added to the protein samples, which were heated at 70 °C for 10 min 12 to 15 μg total protein was separated on 4 to 12% or 8% bis-Tris polyacrylamide gels (Invitrogen) from one or two technical replicates per biological replicate. Proteins were transferred overnight onto 0.45 μm polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Millipore) and proteins detected by immunoblotting. The antibodies used were Sp100 rabbit polyclonal [RRID: AB_1857398] (Sigma, HPA016707; 1:1000 or 1:2000), PML rabbit polyclonal [RRID: AB_873108] (Bethyl; A301-167A, 1:1000), Daxx rabbit polyclonal [RRID: AB_1078625] (Sigma; HPA008736, 1:1000), HIRA [WC119] mouse monoclonal [RRID: AB_1977097] (Millipore; 04-1488, 1:500 overnight at 4 °C), Ubinuclein-1 rabbit polyclonal [RRID: AB_2684420] (Abcam; ab101282-1001, 1:1000), ASF1a [C6E10] rabbit monoclonal [RRID: 2289918] (Cell Signaling; 2990S, 1:1000 overnight at 4 °C), GFP rabbit polyclonal [RRID: AB_303395] (Abcam; ab290, 1:2500), GAPDH [6CS] mouse monoclonal [RRID: AB_627679] (Santa Cruz; sc-32233, 1:1000), and α-Tubulin [DM1A] mouse monoclonal [RRID: 477583] (Sigma; T6199, 1:1000) and [B-5-1-2] mouse monoclonal [RRID:AB_477579] (Sigma; T5168, 1:10,000). Species-appropriate secondary antibodies conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (Pierce) were used at a 1:10,000 dilution and detected with SuperDura Extended Western Detection Reagent (Thermo-Pierce). The chemiluminescent signal was collected with a Syngene G:Box. Syngene Gene Tools software (version 4.3.9.0 or 4.3.17.0) was used to quantitatively assess protein levels. Raw protein levels were obtained by subtraction of a background region and normalization according to their associated GAPDH level.


### RNA extraction
  Total RNA was isolated with the RNeasy Mini-RNA extraction kit (Qiagen). One microgram of total cellular RNA was reverse transcribed with the Transcriptor First-Strand Synthesis kit (Roche).


### RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)
  Keratinocytes were seeded onto 10 cm plates of irradiated J2/3T3 fibroblasts at 3.5 × 104 cells per plate and harvested for RNA 4 days later. Cells were untreated or stimulated with 25 ng/ml Human Interferon Alpha 2a (PBL Assay Science) for 12 or 48 h prior to harvesting. Feeders were removed prior to RNA extraction. Total RNA from three replicates was isolated using the RNeasy Mini-RNA extraction kit (Qiagen). RNA concentration and integrity was determined using the Quibit Fluorometer (Invitrogen) and Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent Technologies), respectively. Polyadenylated RNA was sequenced (2 × 76 bp paired-end reads) using the Illumina NextSeq (CCR Sequencing Facility, FNLCR/NCI/NIH). RNA-seq data was aligned to the human hg19 reference genome using STAR (version 2.6.1). K-means clustering was performed using iDEP 2.01 (55) and heatmaps were generated using Morpheus, https://software.broadinstitute.org/morpheus. RNA-seq datasets are accessible through GEO Series accession number GSE290877.


### Protein structural prediction and molecular dynamics simulation
  Disordered regions of Sp100 were annotated with AIUPRED. Multiple AI methods (AlphaFold 3, Chai-1, Boltz-2) generated candidate models; well-resolved domains (HSR, SAND, HMG) showed pLDDT > 70 whereas global confidence was < 50. We constructed meta-/homology models by combining high-confidence AI regions with experimentally determined templates from the PDB, allowing rotational but not translational symmetry for the N-terminal HSR dimer. All candidate models were relaxed in YASARA 2025.1.13 (YASARA2 side-chain optimization ×3; em_run minimization), then finalized with the YASARA Protein Modeling macro. Model quality (|Z-score| < 2.0) was obtained for all final models and model characteristics were assessed with VoroMQA and ProQ3 (with B-factor annotations in deposited files); For dynamics, the selected Sp100B model was simulated for 1000 ns in YASARA (AMBER14/TIP3P, vdW 8 Å, PME, MTS 1.25/2.5 fs, NPT 298 K/1 atm, 0.9% NaCl), discarding the first 250 ns. RMSF over the subsequent 400 ns agreed with disorder predictions and revealed a semi-stable region (≈380–480) and a stable HSR dimer. Supporting details and models are available have been deposited in ModelArchive (modelarchive.org) with the accession codes ma-7mo0m (Sp100A), ma-0hla7 (Sp100B), ma-ew0pl (Sp100C) and ma-xw697 (Sp100HMG).
