# Conformational Transition of a Polycationic Hinge Domain contributes to DNA Binding


## Abstract
  Nuclear receptors (NRs) are multidomain, ligand-activated transcription factors that play critical physiological roles. While the structured DNA-binding domain (DBD) and ligand-binding domain (LBD) have been well characterized, the function of intrinsically disordered regions—such as the hinge between the LBD and DBD—remains unclear. To illuminate the role of the hinge, we conducted five-microsecond molecular dynamics simulations of thyroid hormone receptor (TRα) alone versus bound to DNA. We reveal that DNA binding induces a significant structural change in the hinge region (helical to unwound coil), with a potentially important role in the regulation of TRα activity. Previously, hinge helicity has been reported to drive oligomerization and the consequent inhibition of coactivator binding, and such DNA-induced transition may promote TR activation. Protein-DNA binding is found to be multivalent and reveals the direct interaction of the hinge with the DNA minor groove in addition to the canonical recognition helix of the DBD with the major groove. Furthermore, the poly-Arg segment of the hinge has a direct and significant influence on DNA conformation. This interaction promotes a bent DNA phosphate backbone, which might further contribute to the protein-DNA recognition. On a global scale, DNA binding induces a “closed-to-open” conformational change thus reducing direct DBD-LBD interactions, which corroborates previous calorimetric binding studies. Overall, our results provide insight into the mechanism of DNA recognition and the resulting conformational dynamics of the TRα-DNA complex.


## Introduction
  Transcription factors consist of distinct domains with assigned functions. For example, a DNA-binding domain is responsible for recognizing a specific nucleic acid sequence while a regulatory domain senses specific environmental conditions. However, the action of the protein is more than a linear sum of individual activities from each modular domain. The interactions between domains appear to fine-tune the precise action of these modular proteins. A prominent example is the nuclear receptor (NR) family of transcription factors, which presents several longstanding puzzles related to activation mechanisms—including ligand sensing and DNA recognition (1,2). Notably, there are only a few canonical 6-base-pair DNA response element sequences that are targeted by the 48 classes of human NR families and hundreds of others across species. For instance, thyroid hormone receptors, retinoid X receptors, and estrogen receptors all preferentially bind to the AGGTCA motif, while androgen, glucocorticoid, progesterone, and mineralocorticoid receptors recognize AGAACA(2–4). Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain this apparent DNA readout degeneracy, including DNA shape differences influenced by flanking sequences, recruitment of specific partner proteins, heterodimerization or oligomerization of NRs, and the involvement of additional domains that assist the canonical recognition site and contribute to binding specificity (3,5,6).

  Overall, conformational dynamics and protein-polynucleotide interaction are recognized as being essential for multidomain signaling proteins to achieve their functions (7,8). The direct structural consequence and subsequent implications of protein-nucleic acid interaction have been increasingly appreciated in recent decades, especially in the areas of intrinsically disordered domains, multivalent interactions, folding-binding coupling, and cross-domain allostery (9–16). As a superfamily of ligand-dependent transcription factors, the NR superfamily senses various small molecules (from hormones, metabolites, to xenobiotics) and responds by turning on and off transcription of target genes (1). The current study focuses on the thyroid receptor (TR), which plays a crucial role in regulating several biological processes, including thyroid hormone-controlled metabolism, cell differentiation, and intercellular communication (17).

  NRs share the overall modular domain arrangement of four domains, i) the disordered amino-terminal domain (NTD), ii) the DNA-binding domain (DBD), iii) the disordered hinge, iv) the ligand-binding domain (LBD) (18). Of these, the DBD and LBD have well-characterized globular structures. The LBD contains a ligand-binding pocket that activates the protein upon agonist binding. The NTD and the hinge domain are predicted to be more flexible and are less understood, although these disordered domains also play important biological roles (5,19,20).

  For example, in addition to the direct activation mechanism by the binding of coactivators (21) and other transcription factors, studies indicate that allosteric conformational dynamics is important for NR activation due to hinge-mediated communication between LBD and DBD, which in turn affects the DNA-DBD interactions (22,23). The mechanism of ligand sensing and allosteric activation has potential applications in drug discovery and protein engineering.

  Intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) within proteins may be an important form of regulation for transcription factors. In fact, about 40% of all eukaryotic proteins contain IDRs of 50 amino acids or more (15). The percentage for transcription factors is much higher, and in fact all human NRs, except CAR, have both disordered NTD and disordered hinge within their structural topology (24,25). The physiological functions of IDRs include inducing protein-rich transcriptomic condensates and liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) (26–29). However, for a subset of transcription factors, it is plausible that LLPS is not a manifestation of biological needs but is related to the fact that these IDRs contain malleable and transient structures that, upon various environmental cues, bind to distinct regulators. In addition, certain parts of the IDRs have sequence motifs to these regulators, and the similarity leads to self-attractions and aggregations.

  In addition to conformational dynamics, allostery is an important mode of biomolecular interaction and regulation of functional activity within NRs. While ligand binding initiates conformational changes within the LBD that control interactions with coregulators, NR ligands also affect the interactions between DBD and DNA. Besides ligand allostery (22), there is also DNA-binding allostery suggesting that DBD interacts with LBD differently in response to DNA binding. The effects of DNA binding on NR conformations have been frequently debated in recent years (30–37). Previous studies have largely focused on the mean conformational changes upon DNA binding, while much less is understood about the associated dynamics and allostery.

  Although both DBD and LBD are highly conserved in sequence and structure and have a well-defined role, the disordered hinge varies significantly between NRs sequence-wise(18). It is still unclear if they share the same function (38–40). The uncertainty of the hinge conformation also leads to a debate on the overall conformation of this modular protein, especially on how the DBD and LBD are spatially related to each other (30–33), since there are various suggestions on the association between the DBD and the LBD of NRs (34–37). One view suggests a tight DBD:LBD association, such as those reported in PDBs 3DZU (PPAR:RXR) (35) and 5UAN (RAR:RXR) (34), while another view reported fewer direct interactions between these two structural domains, such as EMD-1985 (VDR: RXR)(37), PDB 4UMM (USP:EcR) (41), and PDB 4NQA (LXR:RXR) (42). There is further indirect evidence on how an NR may change its conformation (particularly how the DBD and LBD interact) upon binding to DNA. Based on biophysical measurements using isothermal calorimetry (ITC), the hypothesis of a conformational switch triggered by DNA binding was also proposed (23).

  The high variability of the hinge among NRs suggests that its exact action might be different for each receptor. The hinge might participate in several biophysical actions, from orienting DBD and LBD to modulating the DNA-protein interaction. We aim to explore the specific role of the TRα hinge and the DNA-binding aspect of this multidomain protein dynamics, as illustrated in Figure 1a. In addition to facilitating how the DBD and LBD interact in a specific and biologically meaningful way, the hinge also exhibits unstructured characteristics, which is known to play an important role in regulating transcription factors (43).

  Previously, TRα hinge was reported to be crucial for the binding of agonist ligand, T3, at the LBD (39,44). In this work, we first constructed a structure model of the complex TRα(T3):DNA, where the agonist ligand T3 (3,5,3’-triiodothyronine) is bound to the LBD. TRα includes the DBD, the hinge and the LBD, as shown in Figure 1. We then conducted computer simulation and analyzed the dynamic trajectories. As a comparison, we also studied TRα (T3) in the absence of DNA. The simulation results suggest that DNA binding significantly affects the receptor conformational dynamics and vice versa, DNA dynamics is also affected by the protein-DNA interaction. We also observed that protein-DNA interactions are not limited to DBD, since the hinge-DNA interaction is also prominent. Our results are applicable to both TRα and TRβ as the DBD-hinge-LBD sequences are highly conserved and the primary difference between TRα and TRβ lies in the NTD, which is not included in this study. The protein dynamics being studied can provide insight into the allosteric communication between three domains and how NRs, as multidomain transcription factors, are regulated in general.


## Systems and Methods

### Modeling the protein-DNA complex
  To model the conformational changes induced by DNA binding, we constructed two systems (a protein-DNA complex and the protein alone), where the protein is in the same initial conformation with and without DNA. Currently, there are no experimental structures available for full-length TR. The TRα we modeled is the DBD-hinge-LBD of chicken TRα (cTRα, isoform α1, UniProt ID P04625). cTRα is used instead of hTRα (UniProt ID P10827) or TRβ (UniProt ID P10828) because it aligns with a previous experimental biophysical and structural study (23) and additionally, components of the cTRα models (LBD and ligand, DBD and NTD) were constructed previously when we examined in simulations the cross-LBD allostery (45) and NTD-DNA interaction(5), respectively.

  Since structural information is missing for the full-length TRα, we used homology modeling based on the existing interaction of another NR system. For the protein-DNA complex, we need to consider four independent aspects since there is no direct structural information available for them: (1) the internal structure of DBD, (2) the relative position and orientation between the DBD and LBD domains, and (3) the conformation of the hinge region that connects the DBD and LBD, and (4) the DNA and DBD interaction.

  We first built DBD of cTRα using the structural template of human thyroid receptor beta hTRβ (PDB: 2NLL (46), chain B, of 89 residues, 90% identical and 94% positive between hTRβ-DBD and cTRα-DBD) for our target amino acid sequence of chicken TR (residues 49–136) (47). There are two zinc finger binding sites in the DBD, along with eight cysteine residues (highlighted in Figure 1b). These cysteine residues are set to be in the deprotonated state CYM, whereas all the other cysteines are in the normal protonated state CYS. The LBD structure of cTRα bound with the ligand T3 was created using the existing structure (PDB: 3UVV, chain A), with missing loops added as described in a previous simulation study of the LBD dimer complex. We only used residues 161–405 of 3UVV, since the N-terminus of 3UVV is part of the hinge region and we did not use the conformation of 3UVV, as it does not align with the DBD/LBD conformation template.

  To properly orient the DBD and LBD relative to each other, the corresponding orientation of DBD and LBD of the retinoic acid receptor (RAR) (PDB: 5UAN, chain B) was used as a template. Among the available structures, hRAR was chosen due to its high homology with cTRα (40% identical) and positioning of the two subunits was done using the structure alignment script in Pymol (48). The NTD (residues 1–48 using the standard cTRα index) was not included because the crystal structure of this region could not be resolved. Next, we modeled the hinge region (40) spanning residues 124–160 that joins two structured domains (DBD and LBD). We constructed a few structure models of this region using AlphaFold (49) and ModLoop (50). In comparison, AlphaFold2 or AlphaFold3 was unable to provide an acceptable starting conformation of this disordered hinge region for an obvious reason, as shown in the supporting information (SI) Figure S1. Nevertheless, it is interesting to note that AlphaFold2 hints that helical structure(s) persists at this disordered region when it is DNA-free. AlphaFold2 models consistently show a long α-helix (residues 126–154) that separates the DBD and LBD domains. This unconstrained α-helical conformation of the hinge domain prevents the two domains from forming contacts, which would be inconsistent with the crystal structures of 5UAN and some other structures.

  In contrast, the model generated by ModLoop does not pose the same physical interaction problem. ModLoop successfully generated a flexible loop (residues 127–160) that allows for the DBD and LBD to form an interface in an orientation consistent with other homologous NRs. The reason for these differences is that AlphaFold is a data-driven structure predictor whereas ModLoop models disordered regions and relies more on local interactions. In this case, the PDB 2NLL, while providing a useful model of the DBD, contains a long α-helix in the hinge region that points away from where the LBD would reside in our system. Similarly, the starting residues of the PDB 3UVV point away from the DBD of our model. Data driven models such as AlphaFold2/3 output models of the disordered, polycationic hinge region based on these two incompatible structures that would preclude the investigation of DBD-LBD interactions, thus making a local structural model such as Modloop more practical for our study. Protonation states for the LBD are inherited from Ref. (45) and we also checked the newly added DBD and the hinge region using H++. Conventional protonation states were assigned. In summary, the DBD of the cTRα is structurally homologous to hTR (PDB: 2NLL) and the LBD is identical to a large part of the PDB: 3UVV, while the relative orientation between the DBD and LBD is fitted from a template from RAR (PDB: 5UAN) and the linker region between is modeled for this study.

  There are a few mobility assay-based experimental reports on high affinity dsDNA sequences for TR’s DBD, the so-called TR-specific DNA response elements (DREs) (3,51,52). Although there are 48 types of human NRs, only a handful of DREs are being used, one so-called 6-bp “half-site” being 5’-AGGTCA-3’/5’-TGACCT-3’ for TR (51,52). The half-site AGGTCA forms the well-known direct repeat motif AGGTCA NNN AGGTCA where “NNN” symbolizes a spacer of 1 to 10 bps of unspecified nucleotides for dimeric binding as the motif provides two AGGTCA sequences that interact with NR dimer complexes where each sequence binds to one NR. In addition, a 5’-flanking sequence TA (51) was added to the motif and finally we surrounded the core sequence with the filler of poly(A) nucleotides upstream and downstream. As shown in Figure 1, the result is a 24-bp dsDNA, with the top strand being 5’-AAATA AGGTC AGAAG GTCAG AAAA-3’ and its complementary strand. This dsDNA sequence has a high affinity for the DBD of TRα1, which was also tested in biophysical experiments (23). The DNA structure was constructed using AMBER tool NAB (53). Alignment of this DNA response element with DBD was done using the position of DNA in the crystal structure 2NLL as a template, where hTRβ binds the canonical thyroid response element (TRE), 5’-AGGTCA-3’.

  The complete construction of the protein-DNA system is shown in Figure 1b. The TRα construct (total 357 residues) contains three domains using the standard index (or internal index): (i) DBD, 49–123 (internal indices1–75), (ii) hinge, 124–160 (76–112) and (iii) LBD, 161–405 (113–357). Using the internal index, the ligand T3 is residue 358, two zinc ions are residues 359 and 360. One DNA strand spans residues 361–384, and the complementary strand spans residues 385–408 with the binding box at 366–371 and 398–403 (AGGTCA). Our structural model preserves several critical protein-DNA interactions between the recognition helix and the response element reported in (46), such as TRα Arg76:3G-4T (at the 3rd and 4th positions of the binding box, hydrogen bonding between the sidechain of Arg and the bases of G-T), which corresponds to the internal residue index 28:368–369, and to the DNA-binding structure template hTRβ (2NLL) indexing Arg328:515G-516T.


### Atomistic molecular dynamics simulation
  The first system, the protein-DNA complex system, was established through the LEaP program. The TRα-DNA complex was solvated in a 10-Å box of 33,396 water molecules. 49 Na+ counterions were added to produce a neutral charge for the entire system with a total size of 107,527 atoms. For the TRα-alone setup there are a total of 74,776 atoms, including 23,003 water molecules and 3 Na+ counterions. A ligand (T3) associated with LBD and Zn2+ ions associated with a zinc-finger motif (54) are also included. The forcefield used matches the previous study of the heterodimeric system involving TR (T3), where amber14SB is used for protein and TIP3P for water. Additionally, the DNA forcefield used is bsc0.

  Two 5-microsecond computer simulations were performed on Anton2 and STAMPEDE2/3, one for the TRα-DNA complex and one for TRα alone. Continuous simulations were chosen instead of multiple replicas of short-time simulations. (55). This approach enables improved exploration of the dynamics and conformational space, which is particularly important for simulations involving IDRs, whereas advanced sampling methods (56) might not be practical for handling large systems. The convergence is discussed in the Results section below based on post hoc analysis. Snapshots of the trajectory were stored every 0.1 ns during the simulation (total 50,000 frames for each system). All simulations used a Nose-Hoover thermostat with a set temperature of 300 K at a pressure of 1 bar. The time step was 2.5 fs for Anton2 simulations and 2.0 fs for STAMPEDE2/3. Our analysis in the following focuses on the microsecond timescale which we were able to approach with available resources.


### Statistical analysis of conformational dynamics
  To describe the internal conformational dynamics of the protein and the protein-DNA interaction, we focus on using residue-residue contact interactions in addition to conventional analyses. The starting point of contact analysis is to render each snapshot in a corresponding contact matrix, where a binary value of 1 or 0 is assigned to the interaction between two residues. We define that a contact is formed (aij = 1) when any atom in residue i is within a distance cutoff (4.2 Å) of any atom in residue j. Otherwise, no contact is formed (aij = 0). The distance cutoff value was selected based on previous studies (57,58). Note that the cutoff is used only for the analysis in the current study and we do not rely on the cutoff to construct the energy function or drive coarse-grained models, where the choice of the value can be more critical. In fact, a sensitivity study comparing a slightly more stringent 4.0 Å vs. a more tolerant 4.2 Å shows no significant differences (59). The contact matrix has a similar mathematical structure to the distance matrix, and the main difference is that the former discerns the contact energetics while the latter emphasizes long-distance connection and loses shorter-range sensitivity. An ensemble average will lead to the mean contact matrix which is also referred to as a contact heatmap. Furthermore, the collective variable analysis of these contact matrices through linear methods, such as principal component analysis (60), and nonlinear ones, such as variational autoencoder (61), lead to major dynamic modes of biomolecular structure and dynamics expressed by contact degrees of freedom (58).

  As demonstrated and compared in previous studies, two types of contact-based PCA reveal distinct features. (62,63): Edge-based PCA (or explicit principal component analysis, ePCA) of proteins examines the dynamic fluctuations of individual contacts, such as conformational switch (64,65) and allosteric binding (45,66,67), while site-based PCA (or implicit principal component analysis, iPCA) focuses on the basic consensus of structural features, such as structural elements and domains of largely disordered or partially folded proteins (62,68). In this work, we mainly used iPCA to characterize the residual structural consensus of the hinge with and without DNA.

  As for the dynamic feature, instead of using ePCA, we opt for using a more versatile nonlinear analysis based on machine learning algorithms. While ePCA identifies collective modes as linear combinations of individual contacts, capturing concerted motions of contact formation and disruption, nonlinear methods such as neural networks with activation functions are better suited to accurately describing large-scale nonlinear motions exhibited in the disordered hinge. As a contact matrix shares the same mathematical structure as a (symmetrical) 2D image, where various advanced AI-based image processing algorithms are being developed, we use a specific convolutional neutral network (CNN) algorithm, and the architecture of the CNN is the widely used variational autoencoder (VAE) (61). The VAE scheme was used to compress the hinge contact matrices into a small number of collective variables that capture the dominant patterns of contact formation and disruption. This nonlinear dimensionality-reduction step allows us to better represent the large-scale motions of the disordered hinge compared with linear approaches such as ePCA. Because the VAE scheme contains both a recognition (discriminative) model for encoder and a generative model for decoder, the learned collective variables can be decoded back into representative contact maps, providing an interpretable link between the low-dimensional features and the underlying structural changes (SI Figure S2). The detailed parameters of our VAE model are listed in SI Figure S2. One advantage of VAE over many other nonlinear methods, especially those classification methods such as UMAP (69) and t-SNE (70), is that one can easily compute the average on the constructed collective variables and reverse them to a representative contact map using the decoder step, as illustrated in SI Figure S2.

  In this work, we also used analysis tools that are typically used for characterizing abstract graphs. When we compare mean structural changes using mean contact matrices, sometimes a direct comparison is difficult to visualize and we may want to extract residue-residue contact interaction and express the information as residue-associated properties, which essentially is a 2D to 1D rendering. To achieve this, we use a set of protein structure network analysis metrics that convert the two-dimensional contact interaction information to linear information associated with the residues. The concept of a protein structure network (PSN) (71,72) is based on the mean contact matrix. The formation of a link in PSN is decided by a threshold parameter δ and we construct a link between two residues (nodes) for PSN if the corresponding contact frequency is larger than δ (= 0.02) (59). Once the network (adjacency matrix) is defined, we apply additional node-based network metrics to extract the network features. The specific ones that we used here include eigenvalue centrality, clustering coefficient and square clustering coefficient (73,74).


## Results and Discussion

### The hinge-DNA interaction assists in the association of DBD and DNA
  A highly unusual feature of the TR hinge is its polyelectrolyte character, which we highlight based on our observation that, within a short 16-residue segment 132KRKLIEENRERRRKE147E, immediately preceding the coactivator-like motif 148MIKS152L, 13 out of 16 residues are charged. They strongly interact with DNA as observed from an average contact heatmap (Figure 2a) of hinge-DNA interaction and from the corresponding 3D view (Figure 2b). Our results are reported using internal protein and DNA indices. Our protein system contains 357 residues, and the actual index can be obtained by adding 48 to the internal index (e.g., residue 1 of the internal index corresponds to residue 49 on the chicken index). The double-stranded DNA system contains 48 nucleotides (1A-24A, 48T-25T), including the 6-bp binding box residues. For convenience, the binding box residues are denoted with an underline and a double underline when they are on the complementary strand, e.g., residue 1A of strand I is paired with residue 1T of strand II. In Figure 2a, the result shows that DNA binds strongly to not only the DBD region (residues 49–123) but also the hinge region (residues 124–160).

  The prominent features of the DBD include two zinc-fingers, each followed by an amphiphilic helix (54). The DNA recognition helix interacts with the major groove with specificity, while the scaffold helix interacts with the DNA backbone nonspecifically (75). With a peptide loop in between, a “helix-loop-helix” fold forms the core of DBD (76). The dominant interaction comes from the DNA recognition helix (Arg76) forming contacts with position 3 of the DNA binding box region, 3G, with their contact frequency is greater than 90%. Furthermore, the adjacent β-strand (His62-Tyr63) of the DBD forms contact with the important 5’-flanking sequence (TA) (51) and the first half of the binding box (AGG).

  The scaffold helix and the adjacent loop (Arg102-Arg109) of the DBD also form additional strong contact with the DNA binding box on the complementary strand (5G, 6T) and the 5’-flanking C (399, 398, and 397 internal index), and they target the DNA backbone atoms through hydrogen bonding. On the other hand, an arginine-rich region of the hinge (Arg140, Arg142, Arg143, Arg144) can be seen interacting strongly with the middle of the DNA binding box, 2G to 5C, suggesting that the hinge region also reinforces the interaction between the DNA recognition helix and the binding box. Noting that disordered hinges not only make a strong contribution to the binding energy but also directly affect the conformation of DNA. As shown in Figure 2b, DNA bending occurs at the phosphate backbone upon insertion of arginine sidechains (Arg140, Arg142, and Arg143). The average distance between sequential phosphates (Pi–Pi+1 and Pi–Pi+1) is reduced to less than 6.2 Å, compared to the typical 6.5–7.0 Å spacing observed in canonical B-form DNA in the absence of guanidinium group interactions (77,78). This protein-induced DNA deformation has important implications for DNA recognition, suggesting that interactions between the hinge and the DNA backbone—particularly in flanking regions such as the 3′ end in this case—can augment the canonical 6-base-pair motif. While this assisting interaction involves the phosphate backbone rather than base-specific contacts, variations in hinge sequence and structure among different nuclear receptors may fine-tune the nature and extent of DNA engagement. These subtle differences in DNA shape could contribute to receptor specificity beyond well-studied factors such as spacer length and half-site orientation (79).

  Furthermore, we found that the hinge region also interacts with DNA outside of the binding box region. Specifically, a loop region of the hinge (Lys150-Arg155) inserts itself into the minor groove of DNA (16G to 20G, 31G to 35T) and forms contact with both the DNA bases and the backbone atoms. The other loop region of the hinge (Arg144-Lys145) forms nonspecific contact with the DNA backbone, further anchoring the hinge to the DNA. The strong interaction between the hinge and the minor groove throughout our simulation indicates that the hinge plays an important role in mediating the binding of the DBD to the major groove and stabilizing the conformation necessary for protein activation. Both interactions, TRα hinge–DNA minor groove and TRα DBD–DNA major groove, exhibit the multivalent nature of TRα-DNA binding.

  Unlike the respective DBD and hinge interaction with DNA, the LBD has little direct contact with DNA, except for one relatively strong interaction (contact formation of approximately 20%) between Lys355 (helix H9) and the backbone atoms of 38T and 39G. In addition to characterization of the structure using contacts, we also performed molecular mechanics (MM) plus desolvation free energy analysis (MM/GB-SA), where generalized Born (GB) and surface area (SA) account the polar and nonpolar solvation components, respectively (80). The binding energy is ΔGMM/GB-SA = –99.56 kcal/mol and the per residue of the interaction is shown in SI Figure S3 where the results are largely consistent with what has been revealed using contact heatmaps. This consistency is not surprising since the contact itself, as a nonlinear distance function, is an approximate energy measurement.


### Allostery and interdomain interaction modulated by DNA binding
  We first present how the overall structure of the TRα conformation ensemble changes in response to DNA binding from a contact heatmap viewpoint, as shown in Figure 3a. Additional accompanying detailed information on both contact- and distance-based metrics can be found in SI Figure S4. As shown in Figure 3a (and SI Figure S4), the overall contacts between LBD and DBD decrease after DNA binding by roughly 50%, while the contacts between LBD and the hinge increase by approximately 30%. The 5-μs simulation also reveals a notable overall movement between the DNA-free and DNA-bound TRα. We note that, upon DNA association, TRα adopts a more open conformation (overall distance features are illustrated in SI Figure S5). In addition, the direct contact interaction between DBD and LBD also decreases significantly due to DNA binding, supported by the contact heatmaps of Figure 3a and SI Figure S4. This finding is also consistent with a previous study which reported that DNA binding reduces the DBD-LBD interactions (23).

  Most structural changes revealed by our simulations are from interactions between domains and within the hinge, and only minor internal structural changes within the DBD or LBD exist. Although there was no comparison of conformations between the DNA-free and DNA-bound DBD for TRα, earlier studies examined the DNA-DBD interaction of other NRs such as the glucocorticoid receptor (GR) and the retinoid X receptor (RXR). The contact heatmaps for both structural domains maintain a consistent level of internal contacts. Our results (subtle direct change in DBD upon DNA binding) are consistent with a recent report on the crystal structure of the GR DBD-DNA complex, where the DBD of GR in the DNA-free state is similar to that of the DNA-bound form (81). However, earlier NMR studies performed on the DBD of GR present more dramatic conformational changes in the D loop region and mutations that mimic these DNA binding allostery were also observed (81,82). The difference might be tied to the oligomerization status, i.e., the monomer system (which lacks the dimerization interface), that is being simulated here.

  In addition to the 2D heatmap representation in Figure 3a, we rendered the 2D information into linear annotations at the residue level for easier comparison, as shown in Figure 3b. To achieve this, we applied network properties to extract structural changes at the per-residue level. As mentioned in the Method section, we first render the 2D heatmap into a protein structure network (PSN) and then calculate node-based metrics of the PSN. Here, we opt to use a single threshold PSN with a cut-off range [0.02, 1] instead of PSN* with a range [0.02, 0.98]. Although the previously defined PSN* (a dual threshold network that ignores the permanent contacts as well as those infrequent contacts) is more sensitive to conformational changes than PSN using centrality measurements (59), PSN is more connected, allowing for the calculation of well-defined local clustering coefficients (73,83). As shown in Figure 3b, node-based metrics, eigenvector centrality (EC) (73) and local clustering coefficients (LCC3 and LCC4) (74) are displayed as functions of residues. With DNA bound, the major changes in the LCC3 and LCC4 values are concentrated on the hinge, while the values in the DBD and LBD regions do not change significantly. For the hinge region, an LCC3 peak originally between 90 and 100 disappears and splits into two peaks. LCC4 confers the observations from LCC3, but in a more subdued display. All three analyses indicate that drastic conformational rearrangements upon TRα binding to DNA originate from the hinge region.

  Additionally, to better understand how these contacts affect protein dynamics in response to DNA binding, we also examined protein flexibility throughout the 5-μs simulations of DNA-free and DNA-bound TRα, as shown in Figure 3c. DNA binding to the TRα generally increases the stability of the protein, as evidenced by the consistently lower B-factor values throughout most of the DNA-bound protein structure (compared to the DNA-free structure). In fact, the entire DBD and most of the LBD show diminished flexibility, with the exception of two functionally important regions for dynamic structural change. One of these regions is the loop region located after helix 1 of the LBD, which not only increases flexibility but also loses contact with the DBD when it binds to DNA. This is supported by the comparison of the contact map in Figure 3a, as we can see a delocalization and weakening of the contact between this loop and the DBD. It becomes clear from the protein dynamics analysis that the increased flexibility of these regions can be important for mediating structural changes in response to DNA binding.

  Coupled with the information from the B-factor and contact dynamics, we can identify the major dynamic regions of the protein system. For the protein without DNA, there are two relatively dynamic regions: one is the DBD-LBD interface through the hinge. The second region is in a distal part of the LBD, which includes the ligand-binding pocket and the specific loop located after helix 1 of the LBD (residues 191–209). For the DNA-bound TRα, we also observe two major dynamics regions. One region is still at the LBD and DBD interface and is comparable to that of the DNA-free TRα. The second region is at the interface between the DNA recognition helix and the major groove of DNA.

  Our results suggest that DNA binding decreases fluctuations at the ligand-binding site. In turn, this enhances the effectiveness of the ligand-binding domain in holding the ligand and consequently promotes the LBD active conformation and its ability to recruit coactivators. This finding is consistent with an earlier discovery that estradiol induces changes of binding kinetics and promotes the estrogen receptor-DNA interaction (22). Both demonstrate that there is an allosteric communication between the protein-DNA interface and the protein-ligand interface.


### Helix-to-unwound coil transition of hinge segments upon DNA binding secure activation
  Although there are subtle changes within the DBD and LBD, the hinge adopts different conformations when DNA is bound. Unlike highly structured domains, where the focus is often on capturing dominant conformational changes, the primary goal in analyzing a diverse structural ensemble—such as that of an intrinsically disordered region (IDR)—is to identify consensus structural elements. As shown in earlier studies, site-based principal component analysis (iPCA) is particularly suited for unstructured polymers like IDRs that contain loosely defined secondary structures, whereas edge-based PCA (ePCA) is more appropriate for revealing dominant dynamic fluctuations in well-folded, highly structured proteins (62,63). We usually only apply ePCA for highly structured proteins as there is no ambiguity about the native structure; whereas iPCA can be useful for largely unstructured polymers (but with residual structural features) such as genome folds or intrinsically disordered proteins (68).

  The internal contact of the hinge is shown in Figure 4a. We further applied site-based contact PCA (iPCA) on this patch and obtained structural features as the dominant principal component PC1. The iPCA for IDP is analogous to the A/B compartment analysis performed on Hi-C data, which depicts structural elements in semiflexible polymer chains. In the IDP content, the top eigenvectors are shown as bar graphs in Figure 4b. Here, each strong peak or valley indicates a cluster of sequence neighboring residues for coherent contacts with other (unspecified) residues. Meanwhile, the nodal set (residues at the x intercept) can be viewed as the boundary between clusters. We found two subdomains (residues 76–89, 90–110 using the internal index) of the hinge when it is DNA-free. Upon DNA binding, the second subdomain further splits into two near residue 96 (internal index; corresponds to Gly144).

  In addition to the analysis of the subdomain structure from the contact viewpoint, it is interesting to inspect the changes in the secondary structure from helix to unwound upon DNA binding, as shown in Figure 4c. Here, we sorted 8 DSSP-derived secondary structure states (84) into four larger classes: helix (green: G, H, and I), strand (red: E and B), turn/loop (black: S and T) and others. Note that we do not refer to this transformation as the classic helix-to-coil transition, since coil often refers to a random structure. Rather, we use “unwound” or “extended coil” to emphasize that DNA provides specific constraints on the conformation of the hinge region, leading to the unwinding and then the flattening of the initial helical structure of the hinge. The simulation results reveal that DNA binding induces an intricate conformational change at the secondary structure level for the hinge region. For example, segments 139NRER143R and 145KEE148M have a helical conformation in its DNA-free form and change into a disordered region upon DNA binding. Additionally, while the 149IKSLQH155R segment remains mostly helical, it also shows an increased propensity to form turns after DNA binding.

  Our result in Figure 2a also indicates a high level of contact interactions between this region and the minor groove. The 148MIKS152L region of TRα was previously reported to be helical under certain conditions and potentially involved in a significant protein-protein interaction that can lead to protein aggregation, since the region interacts with the AF2 region of another receptor’s LBD and triggers oligomerization or phase separation (47). Interestingly, the 148MIKS152L region is considered an LXXLL-like motif, and the AF2 region of RXR usually binds to the well-known LXXLL motif of a coactivator (85). Thus, this oligomerization can be viewed as an inhibition mechanism since the coactivator binding site at the AF2 is blocked. Thus, the change in conformation near this location upon DNA binding can have an implication on TR regulation. Specifically, DNA binding leads to a disordered hinge and reduces inter-protein contact, consequently, fewer oligomerizations occur, more receptors are open to coactivator interactions, and finally, transcription becomes more active.

  In addition to the mean structure features revealed in Figure 4, we also study the structural fluctuations of these ensembles. Since the hinge is quite disordered, instead of looking for dynamic modes using linear methods such as ePCA, we directly apply a machine learning algorithm. As stated in the Method section, the variational autoencoder (VAE) is selected as it can both perform nonlinear transformations of input information (residue-residue contacts) to collective degrees of freedom (latent variables) and produce conformations based on latent variables using the decoder scheme. To construct the machine learning model, we used the conformations of both TR alone and TR in the complex, total 2 × 1,000 frames (at the frequency of 1 per 5 ns) as the training set, The snapshots projected onto the latent variable are shown in Figure 5a and a total of 2,000 are shown. The latent variable space is shown in Figure 5b. The dynamic evolution of the hinge is shown in Figure 5c with time being color-labeled. Because there are little significant internal changes of the DBD and LBD and most activities come from the hinge, the time-dependent plot Figure 5c represents the process of this closed-to-open dynamics. Advanced transition path-based methods could be used to further assess the dynamics quantitatively; however, the present projection provides a clear qualitative picture. Notably, without DNA, the conformations of the hinge appears to be at the upper-right corner of Figure 5a, and one can interpretate such conformations as the hinge having helical fragments in the unbound state (based on near-diagonal contacts formed in the upper-right corner of contact maps shown in latent space display, Figure 5b), which become unwound upon DNA binding, as reflected in the shift in the latent space. This transition is consistent with the helix-to-unwound transition described earlier in Figures 4b and 4c, reinforcing the observation that DNA binding induces structural disorder in the hinge.

  Figure 5c also indicates that the time scale of this helical transition appears within one microsecond for the unbound TR hinge. From this and other explicitly time-dependent analyses, such as global root-mean-square plot rms(t) (SI Figure S6), one can conclude that each system is relaxed into a relatively stable state during the simulation. The system will eventually explore other conformations on a longer time scale, but they appear to be stable during this simulation period. Our report of DNA induced helix-to-unwound transition is relatively robust as we have discovered qualitatively similar transition of an alternative system where 5-μs simulation of the same protein with a different DNA response element was paired (data available upon request).

  Although the current work on IDR is focused on the hinge region, it is interesting to make a connection to another IDR of nuclear receptors, the N-terminal domain (NTD). As the other well-known disordered domain for this superfamily of proteins, the NTD has been associated with ligand-independent activation (19,86). It was recently reported that NTD may affect the 5’-flanking sequence (further upstream at approximately 20 bp) binding with selectivity (5). This IDR-DNA interaction could be an important general theme for many transcription factors. It turns out that there are similar LXXLL-like motifs existing in both the NTD and the hinge regions for various NRs (19,85), and we may regard both of these IDR regions as one continuous, long IDR with a structured domain (DBD) embedded in the middle.


## Concluding Remarks
  Based on a set of 5-μs simulations of TRα (DBD-hinge-LBD) with DNA, we were able to sample how this multi-domain protein interacts with DNA. We found that the interaction is multivalent since both interactions (DBD–major groove and hinge–minor groove) make a significant contribution to the association, which in turn can be important for DNA recognition. Notably, both the recognition helix and the hinge region interact directly with nearly every base pair within the AGGTCA binding box of the TRE. At the global level, we found that the direct DBD–LBD contact interaction decreases upon DNA binding, which is consistent with previous experimental findings. The structural ensemble of hinge is strongly altered by DNA binding and the DNA structure is also distorted by the poly-Arg of the hinge. We report a helix-to-unwound transformation of the hinge upon DNA binding that can be significant for TR activation. The helicity of this region could lead to its attraction to the AF2 region of another receptor, which in turn leads to not only oligomerization or even condensate formation, but also the inhibition of coactivator binding.
