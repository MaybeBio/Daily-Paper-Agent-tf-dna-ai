# Atractylodes macrocephala compounds attenuate pediatric epilepsy neuroinflammation through multitarget regulation of the NF-κB pathway


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19492-8.


## Introduction
  Pediatric epilepsy, a widely prevalent neurological disorder, is intricately linked to inflammatory responses within the central nervous system (CNS). Recent research has underscored the critical role of neuroinflammation in both the onset and progression of epilepsy. A variety of factors, encompassing infections, trauma, and genetic predispositions, can activate neuroglia and immune cells, resulting in the excessive release of proinflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α). These cytokines compromise the integrity of the blood–brain barrier, heighten neuronal excitability, and ultimately precipitate epileptic seizures1. Prolonged neuroinflammation may also inflict structural damage and induce functional modifications in neurons, thereby aggravating the trajectory of epilepsy. In light of these discoveries, therapeutic approaches that specifically target inflammatory responses present a novel avenue for managing epileptic seizures. This is particularly pertinent for patients with refractory epilepsy, who often display markedly elevated levels of inflammatory markers2. Consequently, the suppression of neuroinflammation, especially through the modulation of the nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) signaling pathway, holds promise as an efficacious strategy for the treatment of pediatric epilepsy3. In addition, Shokr (2025) found that HMGB1, as a key inflammatory mediator, can aggravate epilepsy-related neuroinflammation by activating the NF-κB pathway4, further supporting the importance of targeting the NF-κB pathway in epilepsy treatment.

  Recently, AM has demonstrated considerable potential in regulating CNS inflammation, especially in the treatment of pediatric epilepsy. Epilepsy is not merely a disease of neuronal hyper-excitability; rather, complex neuroinflammatory responses underlie its pathophysiology. The activation of neuroglia, which release a plethora of proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α), exacerbates neuroinflammation5. Studies have revealed that AM extracts can inhibit inflammatory responses by regulating multiple signaling pathways. For instance, AM polysaccharides reduce the production of inflammatory cytokines by inhibiting the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway, thereby alleviating neuroinflammation6. Furthermore, atractylenolides have been found to inhibit apoptosis and oxidative stress by activating the MAPK/extracellular regulated protein kinases (ERK) signaling pathway7. Among them, atractylenolide III (ATR-III) inhibits Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) in mouse microglia, suppressing LPS-induced inflammatory responses8. Recent research further indicates that ATR-III significantly inhibits TLR4 expression, at least partially leading to the inactivation of p38MAPK and c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) pathways. Consequently, ATR-III markedly reduces the mRNA expression and protein levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), and cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) in LPS-induced microglia3,9. Although the precise mechanism by which ATR-III mitigates neuroinflammation via the NF-κB signaling pathway remains to be fully elucidated, numerous studies have demonstrated that active constituents derived from traditional Chinese medicines can effectively suppress the production of proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β and TNF-α, through the regulation of the NF-κB signaling pathway10. This finding offers a potential therapeutic strategy for the management of neuroinflammation-associated diseases, such as pediatric epilepsy. By attenuating neuroinflammation and safeguarding neurons, Atractylodes macrocephala (AM) presents a novel therapeutic avenue for the holistic treatment of pediatric epilepsy and lays a robust theoretical groundwork for the development of antiepileptic drugs derived from natural products.

  In summary, these research findings underscore the potential of further investigating the application of AM and its active ingredients in the treatment of neuroinflammation and epilepsy, with a particular focus on their capacity to alleviate CNS inflammatory responses through precise modulation of the NF-κB signaling pathway. Nevertheless, given the multitude of active ingredients and broad range of targets associated with AM, traditional pharmacological approaches face challenges in comprehensively elucidating its underlying mechanisms. Modern network pharmacology analysis offers a promising means to further explore the multitarget mechanisms of AM, thereby providing novel insights and a foundation for future research endeavors.


## Materials and methods

### Network pharmacology-based screening of active compounds and targets of AM
  The information on active compounds and their potential target proteins in AM was obtained from multiple authoritative databases, including the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology Database and Analysis Platform (TCMSP, https://tcmspw.com/tcmsp.php), Encyclopedia of Traditional Chinese Medicine (ETCM, http://www.tcmip.cn/ETCM/index.php/Home/), and Bioinformatics Analysis Tool for Molecular mechanism of Traditional Chinese Medicine (BATMAN-TCM, http://bionet.ncpsb.org.cn/batman-tcm/). Based on the pharmacokinetic parameters provided by these databases, compounds were screened. Oral bioavailability (OB) was used to measure the absorption and utilization capacity of compounds, with compounds having an OB value greater than 30% being selected for study. Drug-likeness (DL) reflects the similarity of compounds to known drug properties, and compounds with a DL value greater than 0.18 were also included in the screening criteria. Additionally, the target compound-associated proteins retrieved from TCSMP were further standardized using UniProt (http://www.uniprot.org) for uniform annotation and standardized analysis to ensure data consistency and scientific rigor.


### Screening of targets related to pediatric epilepsy
  Targets related to pediatric epilepsy treatment were obtained from multiple authoritative databases, primarily the Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM, https://omim.org/) and GeneCards Database (https://www.genecards.org/). During the target screening process, known target information related to pediatric epilepsy was first retrieved and integrated to ensure comprehensive and accurate data. Subsequently, the potential targets of AM screened through the aforementioned network pharmacology methods were intersected with the disease targets of pediatric epilepsy. The targets identified in this intersection were considered as key therapeutic targets of AM that may act on pediatric epilepsy. These potential targets provide a scientific basis for further mechanistic studies and drug development.


### Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network
  The intersected targets obtained from the screening were imported into the STRING database (http://string-db.org) for the construction and functional association analysis of the PPI network. The STRING database provides PPI network based on known physical interactions and functional correlations, integrating experimental results, computational predictions, and literature mining to reveal potential biological mechanisms. The generated PPI network was visualized and analyzed for topological structure using Cytoscape 3.7.0 software. Key parameters such as Degree were used to screen nodes in the network analysis, where the Degree value represents the number of interactions with a certain protein, used to identify key targets that may play a core regulatory role in the inflammatory response related to AM treatment in pediatric epilepsy. Furthermore, modular analysis was conducted using the CytoHubba plugin, identifying highly interconnected functional submodules in the network through algorithms (e.g., MCC, Degree ranking) to further explore the relationship between key active components and core targets, revealing the potential molecular mechanisms of the active components of AM in multi-target and multi-pathway synergistic effects.


### Construction of the compound target network
  This study constructed a compound target network between the active components of AM, their targets, and pediatric epilepsy using Cytoscape 3.7.0 software to reveal its potential drug action mechanism. First, the screened data on active components of AM and their corresponding target data were integrated with information on targets related to pediatric epilepsy and imported into Cytoscape software for network construction and visualization analysis. In the network diagram, the central node represents pediatric epilepsy, while external nodes represent the active components of AM and their targets. The interactive relationships between nodes reveal the multi-target regulatory characteristics of AM on the disease. Further topological analysis was conducted to identify key targets in the network, providing theoretical support and data for elucidating the multi-pathway synergistic mechanism of AM and its active components in the treatment of pediatric epilepsy.


### GO and KEGG analysis
  GO and KEGG analyses were performed on the target genes using an online data analysis and visualization platform (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn) with p < 0.05. The top ten enriched Biological Process (BP), Cellular Component (CC), and Molecular Function (MF) GO terms and KEGG pathways were displayed.


### Molecular docking validation of major active ingredients-core targets interactions
  In this study, the top five core compounds ranked by degree (Degree) value in the AM ingredient-target-pediatric epilepsy interaction network were selected. The MOL2 format structural files of these compounds were downloaded from the PubChem database, and the three-dimensional structures (PDB format) of the core target proteins were obtained from the PDB database. The CB-Dock2 tool (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/php/index.php) was utilized for molecular docking, which automatically identified the active sites of the target proteins and calculated the binding energies. Docking results with lower scores and stable conformations were screened out. Subsequently, the molecular docking results were visualized using PyMOL software to display the three-dimensional binding mode between the compounds and targets, with a focus on analyzing key interactions such as hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions.


### Cell culture and modeling
  For this experiment, the BV2 mouse microglial cell line was purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Cat#GNM45). The cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM medium (Gibco, Cat#11965092, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Cat#10099141, USA) and maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. After the cells reached 80% confluence, a neuroinflammatory model was established by treating them with 1 µg/mL LPS (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat#L4516, USA) for 24 h11. LPS treatment activates microglia and induces an inflammatory response, thereby mimicking the inflammatory response in the CNS of pediatric epilepsy and providing an in vitro model for subsequent intervention experiments with AM extracts. ATR-III (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat#A2987, USA; purity ≥ 98% by HPLC, as certified by the manufacturer), with the following chemical composition and structural features: molecular formula C₁₅H₂₀O₃, exact molecular weight 248.31749 Da, belonging to sesquiterpene lactones; its structure contains a γ-lactone ring (five-membered cyclic ester bond), one hydroxyl group (-OH), and carbon–carbon double bonds, with chiral characteristics (data from Encyclopedia of Traditional Chinese Medicine, ETCM database), was used in different concentration treatment groups: low-dose (25 µM), medium-dose (50 µM), and high-dose (100 µM) groups12. Additionally, an NF-κB inhibitor control group was set up using BAY 11-7082 (5 µM) as a positive control. Cells in each group were treated with corresponding doses of ATR-III or the NF-κB inhibitor BAY 11-7082 (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat#B5556, USA) in the LPS-induced inflammatory model and incubated for 24 h before analysis to assess the regulatory effect of ATR-III on LPS-induced inflammation.


### Cell viability assay
  To evaluate the impact of ATR-III on LPS-induced cell damage, the cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) method was used to detect cell viability, with the CCK-8 kit purchased from Dojindo (Cat# CK04, Japan). Microglial cells (BV2) were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5000–10,000 cells per well and cultured until they reached 70–80% confluence. The experimental groups included a control group, a model group (LPS-treated group, 1 µg/mL), and low, medium, and high-dose ATR-III groups (25 µM, 50 µM, 100 µM, these concentrations were prepared by diluting a 10 mM DMSO stock solution of ATR-III with high-glucose DMEM medium containing 10% FBS, ensuring the final DMSO concentration was ≤ 0.1% to avoid cytotoxic interference) as well as a BAY 11–7082 (5 µM) group12,13. After treatment at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h, CCK-8 solution was added, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 1–4 h. The absorbance values were then measured using a microplate reader at a wavelength of 450 nm to calculate cell viability.


### Western blot experiment
  Western blot was performed to detect the inhibitory effect of ATR-III on the NF-κB signaling pathway. Treated BV2 cells were lysed, and proteins were extracted. Proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) electrophoresis and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. Immunoblotting was conducted using antibodies against P65 (Beyotime Biotechnology, #AF0246, Rabbit, 1:1000), P-P65 (Beyotime Biotechnology, #AF5875, Rabbit, 1:1000), IκBα (Beyotime Biotechnology, #AG2741, Mouse, 1:1000), P-IκBα (Beyotime Biotechnology, #AF5851, Rabbit, 1:1000), with β-actin (Cell Signaling Technology, #4967, Rabbit, 1:1000) serving as the internal control. Protein bands were detected by incubating with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (Sangon Biotech, D111018/D111024, 1:2000) and visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence kit (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China). Protein bands were detected using an ECL luminescence system and quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software.


### Real-time fluorescent quantitative polymerase chain reaction detection
  Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was used to detect the mRNA expression levels of inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β) and mitochondrial function-related genes (αsubunit of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ coactivator-1 (PGC-1α), cytochrome c oxidase subunit 4 (COX4)). The qPCR kit (Reverse transcription: TaKaRa, Cat# RR037A; Quantitative PCR: TaKaRa, Cat# RR820A, Japan) was used for reverse transcription and quantitative mRNA amplification. Specifically, RNA from cells in each group was extracted (using RNAiso Plus, TaKaRa, Cat#9109, Japan) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA, which was then amplified using specific primers. The reaction conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. The results were analyzed using the 2-ΔΔCt method, with β-actin serving as the internal reference gene. The expression levels of inflammatory cytokines in the control group were used for standardized comparisons. #### Detection of mitochondrial membrane potential
  Mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed via JC-1 staining14. Following the treatment of BV2 cells, a solution of JC-1 dye (Beyotime, Cat# C2006, China) was added, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Fluorescence microscopy or flow cytometry was utilized to detect the red fluorescence of JC-1 (aggregated at higher mitochondrial membrane potentials) and green fluorescence (dispersed at lower membrane potentials). Changes in mitochondrial membrane potential were reflected through the red/green fluorescence ratio, thereby evaluating the impact of ATR-III on mitochondrial function.


#### Statistical analysis
  All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 software and GraphPad Prism 8.4. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. Differences between experimental groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Student’s t-test. The symbol ‘#’ indicates a significant difference (p < 0.01) between the LPS group and the control group; the asterisk ‘*’ indicates a significant difference in the AM treatment group compared to the LPS group(*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001).

### ATR-III improves LPS-induced cellular damage by inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway
  ATR III is the main active constituent of Atractylodes rhizome, and its structure is shown in Fig. 4A. Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 method. The results in Fig. 4B indicate that the cell viability of the model group (LPS-treated) significantly decreased at different time points (0, 24, 48, 72 h) (p < 0.01), suggesting that LPS-induced inflammation caused significant cellular damage. The AM extract-treated groups showed a dose-dependent improvement in cell viability, particularly in the high-dose group, where cell viability was significantly higher than that of the model group (p < 0.01). Meanwhile, the cell viability of the NF-κB inhibitor control group was similar to that of the high-dose AM extract group, further supporting the potential mechanism of AM extract in improving cell survival by inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway. Figure 4F–H evaluated the activation status of the NF-κB signaling pathway in glial cells. The results showed that the phosphorylation levels of the p65 subunit and IκBα in the model group (LPS-treated) were significantly increased, indicating that the NF-κB pathway was significantly activated (p < 0.01). However, the AM extract-treated groups showed a dose-dependent inhibition of NF-κB signaling pathway activation, especially in the high-dose group, where the phosphorylation levels of p65 and IκBα were significantly decreased, approaching those of the control group. The results of the NF-κB inhibitor group were consistent with those of the high-dose AM extract group, further confirming that AM extract can reduce inflammation by inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway. ## Discussion
  This study systematically investigated the mechanisms by which AM and its major active component, ATR-III, alleviate CNS inflammation in pediatric epilepsy by modulating the NF-κB signaling pathway. The results indicated that ATR-III significantly inhibits the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway, reduces the expression of proinflammatory cytokines, and ameliorates LPS-induced microglial inflammation. Additionally, ATR-III improves inflammation-induced energy metabolic disturbances by preserving mitochondrial function, including restoring mitochondrial membrane potential, enhancing ATP production, and regulating the expression of mitochondrial function-related genes (PGC-1α and COX4). This suggests that ATR-III not only inhibits the NF-κB signaling pathway during the anti-inflammatory process but also exerts neuroprotective effects through mitochondrial functional preservation, thereby mitigating further neuronal damage.

  Currently, there is a lack of research on the use of AM in the treatment of pediatric epilepsy, providing significant scope for exploration in this study. This study aimed to elucidate the multitarget mechanism of action of AM active components in regulating CNS inflammation in pediatric epilepsy through the NF-κB signaling pathway. To this end, we initiated our investigation with pediatric epilepsy and, through target intersection analysis, found that the targets of AM active components and pediatric epilepsy-related targets significantly converged on neuroinflammation-related BPs and signaling pathways in GO and KEGG enrichment analyses, further validating the scientific and practical significance of this study. GO enrichment analysis revealed that the targets of AM active components are primarily involved in inflammatory-related BPs such as membrane potential regulation, postsynaptic potential regulation, and glutamate metabolism, which are closely related to neuronal excitability and neural network balance. KEGG pathway analysis further revealed that AM may exert anti-neuroinflammatory effects through key pathways such as the NF-κB signaling pathway, glutamatergic synapse, GABAergic synapse, and retrograde endocannabinoid signaling, emphasizing the central role of these pathways in the occurrence and development of neuroinflammation. Based on this, five core targets were further screened in this study and combined with the top five key targets ranked by degree values in the network diagram for molecular docking validation. The molecular docking results showed that ATR-III exhibited low binding energy and high binding stability with the core targets, indicating its good target affinity and potential biological activity. This result further confirms the crucial role of ATR-III as the main active component in regulating neuroinflammation at the molecular level.

  Among the various active components of AM, ATR-III has been most extensively studied in terms of its regulatory mechanisms related to neuroinflammation, demonstrating clear effects. The therapeutic role of ATR-III in CNS inflammation has been validated. For instance, ATR-III improves rat spinal cord injury by modulating microglial/macrophage polarization18, ameliorates cognitive dysfunction in rats with intracerebroventricular streptozotocin through the activation of the PI3K/AKT/GSK3β pathway19, and improves cerebral ischemic injury and neuroinflammation by inhibiting JAK2/STAT3/Drp1-dependent mitochondrial fission20. These studies fully demonstrate the anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects of ATR-III in neuroinflammation. However, these studies were primarily based on direct validation of experimental hypotheses without systematically screening potential targets and pathways using network pharmacological methods. The advantage of this study lies in the systematic revelation of the potential molecular mechanisms of AM in treating CNS inflammation related to pediatric epilepsy through network pharmacological and molecular docking analyses by screening the active components, targets, and pathways of AM. This integrated research approach combining network pharmacology and experimental validation fills the research gap in the field of AM’s role in epilepsy-related neuroinflammation and provides new therapeutic insights for the intervention of CNS inflammation in pediatric epilepsy.

  Further molecular docking analysis indicated that among the multiple active components of AM, ATR-III exhibits high binding affinity and stability with epilepsy-related targets, demonstrating its potential as a key active component. Previous studies have confirmed that ATR-III can regulate the inflammatory response and function of immune cells by inhibiting the secretion of IL-6 and the activation of related signaling pathways21. Additionally, ATR-III regulates the Bax/Bcl-2 signaling pathway and activates caspase-3 protein expression in HCT-116 colorectal cancer cells, exerting significant antitumor effects22.

  In terms of neuroprotection, ATR-III significantly protects against glutamate-induced neuronal apoptosis, which is closely related to the inhibition of caspase-3 signaling pathway activation23. Similarly, in lung cancer A549 cells, ATR-III can activate caspase-3 through a mitochondria-mediated death pathway, triggering apoptosis, further revealing its close connection with the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway24. These studies collectively suggest that ATR-III may possess multitarget regulatory capabilities, especially playing a central role in anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic processes.

  Furthermore, this study experimentally validated the core role of the NF-κB signaling pathway in the anti-inflammatory mechanism of ATR-III. CCK-8 assays showed that LPS treatment significantly decreased cell survival rates, while ATR-III improved cell survival rates in a dose-dependent manner, with the effect of the high-dose group being close to that of the NF-κB inhibitor group, suggesting that the anti-inflammatory effect of ATR-III is closely related to the inhibition of the NF-κB signaling pathway. Our study found that ATR-III significantly inhibited LPS-induced phosphorylation of IκBα and p65 (Fig. 4E–G), consistent with Ji et al. (2016), who demonstrated that ATR-III blocks IκBα degradation and p65 nuclear translocation in RAW264.7 macrophages, and inhibits NF-κB transcriptional activity and reduces TNF-α, IL-6, and other inflammatory factors25. Western Blot results further validated this mechanism: ATR-III treatment significantly reduced the phosphorylation levels of p65 and IκBα, inhibited the activation of the NF-κB pathway, and decreased the expression of inflammation-related genes. QPCR assays showed that ATR-III effectively downregulated the expression of proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, especially in the high-dose group, where their levels were close to those of the normal control group. Notably, Wu further revealed that ATR-III targets the cGAS/NF-κB axis to suppress senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP)26, which, combined with our findings of upregulated PGC-1α/COX4 (Fig. 5B,C), suggests a dual mechanism of “NF-κB inhibition + mitochondrial repair” for ATR-III’s anti-inflammatory effects. Additionally, our molecular docking results showed a binding energy of -7.1 kcal/mol between ATR-III and NFKB1 (Fig. 4B), directly validating its NF-κB-targeted molecular basis. These indicate that ATR-III exerts significant anti-inflammatory effects by regulating the NF-κB signaling pathway through multiple pathways, thereby alleviating LPS-induced cell damage.

  Molecular docking results in this study further confirmed the specific binding capacity of ATR-III to key epilepsy-related targets. ATR-III showed the lowest binding energy with NFKB1 (− 7.1 kcal/mol) and BCL2 (− 6.9 kcal/mol) among the core targets (IL6, CASP3, NFKB1, BCL2, IL1B), forming stable hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions with key amino acid residues of these targets. This strong binding affinity provides a structural basis for ATR-III to regulate the NF-κB signaling pathway and inhibit neuronal apoptosis. Consistent with this, a review of atractylenolides (I, II, and III) reported that ATR-III can inhibit the activation of NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways, thereby reducing the release of proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β) in LPS-induced microglia, which are critical mediators of neuroinflammation in epilepsy3. Additionally, ATR-III was found to inhibit caspase-3 expression, which plays a key role in glutamate-induced neuronal apoptosis—a process closely related to epileptic seizures3.

  Moreover, mitochondrial dysfunction is a crucial aspect that exacerbates cell damage during inflammation, with energy metabolic disturbances and decreased mitochondrial membrane potential further leading to cell dysfunction, failure, and apoptosis. Therefore, assessing the regulatory effects of ATR-III on mitochondrial function and related genes aids in exploring its potential mechanisms in energy metabolic recovery and cell protection. This study found that ATR-III also exhibits significant effects in terms of energy metabolism and mitochondrial functional preservation. ATP production and JC-1 staining results showed that LPS induction led to impaired cellular energy metabolism and decreased mitochondrial membrane potential, while ATR-III treatment effectively restored ATP production and mitochondrial membrane potential, improving mitochondrial function. QPCR results further showed that ATR-III can upregulate the expression of mitochondrial function-related genes such as PGC-1α and COX4, promoting mitochondrial biosynthesis and functional recovery. Combined with the results of the NF-κB inhibitor group, it was further demonstrated that ATR-III alleviates inflammatory responses, preserves mitochondrial function, and restores cellular energy metabolism by inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway.

  Notably, neuroinflammation is a key pathological feature of epilepsy, and proinflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) regulated by the NF-κB pathway are significantly elevated in epileptic patients and animal models, contributing to seizure initiation and progression2. ATR-III’s ability to reduce these cytokines by targeting NF-κB may thus directly interfere with epileptogenesis. Additionally, the NF-κB pathway is involved in the activation of microglia and astrocytes in epilepsy, and ATR-III’s inhibition of this pathway may suppress glial cell overactivation, thereby reducing neuronal excitability5.

  In summary, this study systematically revealed the mechanisms by which AM and its main active component, ATR-III, regulate CNS inflammation related to pediatric epilepsy through a combination of network pharmacological analysis and experimental validation. ATR-III not only significantly reduces the expression of inflammatory cytokines and alleviates cellular inflammatory damage by inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway but also further exerts neuroprotective effects by restoring mitochondrial function and improving cellular energy metabolic disturbances. These results indicate that ATR-III has multitarget and multipathway regulatory advantages in terms of anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic, and mitochondrial functional preservation aspects, demonstrating its therapeutic potential as a key active component of AM. At the same time, strategies targeting inflammation-related targets such as the σ1 receptor have also been shown to improve epilepsy and related cognitive impairment27, further suggesting that multi-target regulation of inflammatory pathways is an effective direction for epilepsy treatment.

  The innovation of this study lies in the combination of network pharmacological and experimental validation approaches, filling the research gap in the field of AM’s role in pediatric epilepsy-related neuroinflammation and providing a theoretical and experimental basis for the further development of ATR-III as a potential anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective drug. However, further in-depth studies on the pharmacodynamics and pharmacokinetic characteristics of ATR-III in vivo are needed to promote its clinical application and translation in the treatment of pediatric epilepsy.
