# Machine learning identifies exosome related gene signatures for early prediction of non-small cell lung cancer


## Abstract
  Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) remains a major health challenge worldwide, mainly due to the lack of effective early diagnostic biomarkers. Exosome-related genes have recently emerged as potential diagnostic markers due to their roles in tumor progression and immune regulation. This study aimed to identify exosome-related gene signatures as early predictive biomarkers for NSCLC and evaluate their diagnostic and therapeutic significance. We integrated gene expression data from GEO and TCGA databases. Core ExoNSCLC-DEGs were identified using three machine learning methods to construct a NSCLC diagnostic model, and the model was validated using ROC curves, calibration curves, and DCA curves. In addition, immune infiltration analysis, drug enrichment, molecular docking analysis, and regulatory network analysis further explored the potential mechanism of action of ExoNSCLC-DEGs. qRT-PCR experiments verified the reliability of gene expression. We constructed a diagnostic model consisting of six core ExoNSCLC-DEGs (including GPM6A, HYAL1, S100A4, ROBO4, LRRK2, and HBA1). The diagnostic model showed excellent predictive performance in independent cohorts (AUC > 0.98). The calibration curve and DCA curve demonstrated the clinical applicability of the model. Immune infiltration analysis revealed the potential immune effects of some ExoNSCLC-DEGs genes, such as S100A4 and LRRK2, which may play a key role in tumor immune escape. Drug enrichment analysis predicted potential therapeutic compounds, especially sunitinib targeting LRRK2. The regulatory network further identified the key RNA-binding proteins and transcription factors that regulate these biomarkers. qRT-PCR experiments verified the reliability of the expression of ExoNSCLC-DEGs in bioinformatics analysis. The diagnostic model based on the six ExoNSCLC-DEGs has strong diagnostic performance and clinical applicability. In-depth research on ExoNSCLC-DEGs provides new insights into the pathogenesis of NSCLC and provides new directions for subsequent research.


## Introduction
  Lung cancer continues to be the principal etiology of cancer-related deaths globally, with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounting for around 85% of cases1,2. Despite advances in therapy, outcomes for advanced NSCLC are poor, largely due to late diagnosis3. Early detection significantly improves five-year survival, yet there are currently no reliable non-invasive tools for early NSCLC diagnosis. In this context, tumor-derived exosomes have emerged as promising biomarkers and mediators of disease progression.

  Exosomes are small extracellular vesicles secreted by virtually all cells, carrying DNA, RNA, proteins, and lipids reflective of their cell of origin4. They are abundant in biofluids and remarkably stable, making them attractive targets for liquid biopsy. Notably, exosomes from cancer patients differ in quantity and molecular cargo compared to healthy individuals4. Multiple studies indicate that exosomal contents harbor tumor-specific information that can aid NSCLC detection and prognostication5. For example, various exosomal proteins (e.g. CD151, tetraspanin-8) are found at higher levels in NSCLC patient serum than in healthy controls, suggesting their potential as early diagnostic markers6. Similarly, exosomal membrane proteins such as EGFR, EpCAM, and ALIX have been correlated with patient survival, underscoring their prognostic value7. Beyond proteins, exosomal RNAs (mRNAs, microRNAs, lncRNAs) actively contribute to lung cancer biology8. Tumor-derived exosomal microRNAs can promote malignant phenotypes in recipient cells; for instance, exosomal miR-21 is frequently elevated in lung cancer and drives oncogenic signaling by silencing tumor suppressors like PTEN9. Likewise, exosomes carrying specific miRNAs from NSCLC cells can downregulate key tumor suppressor genes (PTEN, PDCD4, RECK), thereby enhancing cancer cell proliferation and invasiveness ​10. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that exosomes have a critical role in the study of NSCLC, potentially acting both as a reservoir of biomarkers for early diagnosis and as promoters of tumor progression through intercellular communication.

  This study focused on and defined characteristic exosome-related genes (ExoNSCLC-DEGs) of NSCLC. An early diagnosis model of NSCLC with good predictive efficacy was constructed, providing new insights into the early non-invasive diagnosis of NSCLC.


## Materials and methods

### Data collection and preprocessing
  We acquired 5 sets of NSCLC datasets that fulfilled the requirements from The Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) as experimental cohorts, comprising datasets GSE18842, GSE19188, GSE19804, GSE75037, and GSE10351211. Furthermore, we acquired the comprehensive data for lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database to serve as a validation cohort. Included were 262 normal samples and 340 tumor samples from GEO, as well as 110 normal samples and 1043 tumor samples from TCGA. Afterwards, incomplete samples in the data were deleted and the data were normalized for the next step of analysis. Figure 1 shows the entire research process.Fig. 1Flowchart.


### Principal component analysis
  ComBat was used to correct for batch effects. PCA was used to visualize the sample distributions of the five GEO data sets before and after batch correction12. The PCA findings were illustrated utilizing the “ggplot2” package to assess the clustering of samples prior to and following rectification.


### Gene differential analysis
  The “limma” package was employed to perform differential gene analysis on the experimental group data to identify gene sets exhibiting employed between tumor and normal samples of NSCLC. The differential gene screening was annotated with |LogFC|≥ 1 and P value < 0.0513.


### Identification of exosome-related genes
  We identified and retrieved exosome-related genes from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/) with a correlation value ≥ 214. This cutoff value has been previously adopted in similar bioinformatics analyses15. Afterwards, the exosome-related gene set obtained was intersected with the NSCLC differential genes obtained in the differential analysis, and finally 65 exosome-related genes with differential expression in NSCLC were obtained. Finally, the Venn diagram was used to visualize this step.


### Analysis of pathway enrichment
  Subsequently, the “clusterProfiler” package facilitated Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) pathway enrichment analyses on the 65 identified differently expressed gene sets, employing P < 0.05 as the criterion for ascertaining highly enriched pathways16. We only visualized term-level enrichment results as bar and bubble plots generated by clusterProfiler; the KEGG pathway maps were not copied or modified.


### Screening of ExoNSCLC-DEGs
  First, univariate logistic regression analysis was performed on the 65 ExoNSCLC-DEGs to evaluate their association with NSCLC, with P < 0.05 as the criterion for significant association. Subsequently, three algorithms: Support Vector Machine (SVM), Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression, and Random Forest (RF) were used for feature selection17. Finally, the intersection of these results was taken, and the final gene set was defined as ExoNSCLC-DEGs for subsequent model construction.


### Nomogram construction and prediction efficacy evaluation
  The NSCLC diagnostic nomogram was developed with the ExoNSCLC-DEGs derived from the final screening18. Calibration curves were constructed to evaluate the alignment between anticipated probability and actual incidence rates. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was utilized to assess the clinical efficacy of the model19.


### Immune cell infiltration characteristics
  We measured the immune cell types infiltration in NSCLC using single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) with the “GSVA” program20. ssGSEA was run with method = ‘ssgsea’, kcdf = ‘Gaussian’, and abs.ranking = TRUE; enrichment scores were scaled to 0–1 across samples. This analysis used a combined immune cell marker gene set from Leukocyte signature matrix 22 (LM22) and other curated subsets. Then, we used spearman correlation analysis to investigate for a link between ExoNSCLC-DEGs and infiltrating immune cells after comparing tumor and normal tissue immune cell infiltration rates.


### Drug enrichment analysis
  The drug-gene relationship data were downloaded from Drug SIGnatures Data Base (DSigDB, https://dsigdb.tanlab.org/DSigDBv1.0)21. Using this data, we performed drug enrichment analysis on ExoNSCLC-DEGs to assess drugs that may have potential therapeutic effects on ExoNSCLC-DEGs. The screening criteria for drugs was P < 0.05.


### Molecular docking analysis
  We focused on molecular docking analysis of potential therapeutic drugs for LRRK2 and S100A4 to evaluate possible molecular effects. We acquired pertinent pharmaceuticals data from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and retrieved protein structural data from the Protein Data Bank (PDB, https://www.rcsb.org/)22,23. The CB-Dock online website (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/images/title.png) was used to analyze the molecular docking24. The server automatically preprocesses protein and ligand structures before docking (removing water molecules, adding polar hydrogens, and minimizing energy). It also ranks docked conformations based on binding affinity and visualizes the lowest-energy conformation.


### Construction of regulatory network
  The regulatory networks of transcription factors (TF) and RNA binding proteins (RBP) of ExoNSCLC-DE were constructed utilizing the TRRUST databases (https://www.grnpedia.org/trrust/) and Encyclopedia of RNA Interactomes (ENCORI, http://starbase.sysu.edu.cn/), respectively25,26. Cytoscape was used for network visualization.


### PCR experimental verification
  Three NSCLC cell lines (A549, H1299, H1975) were obtained from ATCC and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco), maintained at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. Exosomes from cell supernatants were isolated by sequential centrifugation at 300 g (10 min), 2000 g (30 min), and 10,000 g (30 min), followed by ultracentrifugation at 100,000 g (90 min, 4 °C). Total exosomal RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and RNA quality was assessed with NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). cDNA synthesis was performed using PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara) at 42 °C for 15 min. qRT-PCR was conducted using SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (Takara) on an ABI 7500 PCR system (Applied Biosystems). The cycling conditions included 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles at 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s. β-Actin served as an internal control27, and relative gene expression was calculated by the 2^-ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1. All cell lines were STR-authenticated by ATCC and confirmed mycoplasma-free prior to experiments.

  In the end, we utilized the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) website to obtain the protein expression data of select ExoNSCLC-DEGs in both lung cancer and normal cells.


### Statistical analysis
  All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.1). Batch effects were corrected using ComBat (sva package), and data were normalized using normalizeBetweenArrays (limma package). Differential expression was assessed using linear models in the limma package, adjusted using eBayes; significance thresholds were |log2FC|> 1 and FDR < 0.05 (Benjamini–Hochberg correction). GO/KEGG enrichment and GSEA were performed using the clusterProfiler and fgsea packages, with an FDR < 0.05 considered significant. Feature selection was performed using logistic regression, LASSO (glmnet package), SVM-RFE, and random forest (randomForest package). Model performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (pROC package) curves, calibration plots, and DCA curves (rms and rmda packages). Immune infiltration was assessed using ssGSEA, and associations with ExoNSCLC-DEGs were analyzed using the Spearman correlation test. Drug-gene associations were analyzed using DSigDB and the clusterProfiler package. Intergroup comparisons (e.g., boxplots) were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. All tests were two-sided, and P values  < 0.05 were considered significant unless otherwise stated.


## Results

### Data preprocessing and standardization
  In this study, we downloaded 5 datasets from the GEO database, including GSE18842, GSE19188, GSE19804, GSE75037, and GSE103512. In addition, we also downloaded LUAD and LUSC datasets from the TCGA database for validation, comprising 110 normal tissue samples and 1043 tumor tissue samples. After performing data cleaning and standardization, a total of 262 normal samples and 340 tumor samples from the GEO database were used, while the TCGA validation group included 110 normal and 1043 tumor samples. All datasets were integrated and normalized for subsequent analysis.


### Elimination of batch effects
  We performed PCA analysis on 5 datasets from the GEO database to evaluate the overall distribution of samples and resolve batch effects between datasets. Figure S1 shows that after PCA analysis, the 5 datasets were successfully merged, normal samples and tumor samples from different sources were closely aligned, and batch effects were effectively removed.


### Gene expression differential analysis
  Afterwards, we analyzed the experimental group’s gene expression data using differential expression, where the cutoff was |LogFC|≥ 1 and P < 0.05 (Fig. 2A). We found a total of 890 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in NSCLC. Figure 2B shows a heatmap of the top 30 DEGs with the most significant expression differences.Fig. 2Screening of exosome-related genes. (A) Volcano plot of gene difference analysis; (B) Heat map of the top 30 differentially expressed genes; (C) Venn diagram of intersection screening of exosome-related genes in NSCLC.


### Identification of Exosome-Associated Genes
  We searched the GeneCards database for genes related to exosomes; 813 genes were found; a correlation value ≥ 2 was used as a screening criterion (Table S2). We then intersected these exosome-related genes with the 890 DEGs identified in NSCLC tissues, resulting in a final list of 65 exosome-associated genes that were differentially expressed in NSCLC (Table S3). The Venn diagram in Fig. 2C shows our screening results.


### Enrichment study of pathways associated with exosome-related genes
  We performed KEGG and GO pathway enrichment analysis of 65 ExoNSCLC-DEGs to identify key biological functions and signaling pathways associated with NSCLC. Highly enriched pathways in GO enrichment analysis included GO:0,043,086 negative regulation of catalytic activity, GO:0,032,496 response to lipopolysaccharide, and GO:0,002,237 response to bacteria-derived molecules (Fig. 3A,B). In the KEGG enrichment analysis, pathways such as hsa04657: IL-17 signaling pathway and hsa04640: hematopoietic cell lineage were highly enriched (Fig. 3C,D). After comprehensive analysis of the entire set of enriched pathways, we observed that exosome-associated genes predominantly participate in the immune response and inflammatory mechanisms of NSCLC (Table S4, Table S5). Including: hsa04657: IL-17 signaling pathway, hsa04640: hematopoietic cell lineage, GO:0,043,086: negative regulation of catalytic activity, and GO:0,032,496: response to lipopolysaccharide, and hsa04672: Intestinal immune network for IgA production. These results provide more evidence that genes associated with exosomes may play a role in the development and incidence of NSCLC via processes involving the regulation of the immune response and inflammation.Fig. 3Partial results of GO and KEGG pathway enrichment. (A) Bar chart of GO pathway enrichment analysis results; (B) Circle chart of GO pathway enrichment analysis; (C) Bar chart of KEGG pathway enrichment analysis; (D) Bubble chart of KEGG pathway enrichment analysis.


### Screening for ExoNSCLC-DEGs
  Next, we further screened exosome-related genes to obtain exosome non-small cell lung cancer characteristic genes (ExoNSCLC-DEGs). All 65 candidate genes were found to be substantially linked with NSCLC (p < 0.05) in the univariate logistic regression analysis that was initially conducted (Table S6). Next, we used three different methods to perform feature selection on these 65 genes: LASSO regression, SVM learning, and RF analysis (Figure S2). LASSO identified 21 genes, SVM identified 27 genes, and random forest identified 18 genes. The intersection of these three groups of genes finally obtained 7 ExoNSCLC-DEGs: GPM6A, MIF, HYAL1, S100A4, ROBO4, LRRK2, and HBA1 (Fig. 4A). As shown in Fig. 4B, the 7 ExoNSCLC-DEGs were significantly differentially expressed between tumor and normal samples of NSCLC (p < 0.001). Then, we also performed correlation analysis on these 7 ExoNSCLC-DEGs (Fig. 4C), revealing complex interactions and mutual regulatory relationships between genes. Finally, Fig. 4D shows the distribution of the 7 ExoNSCLC-DEGs on chromosomes.Fig. 4Screening of ExoNSCLC-DEGs (A) Venn diagram of ExoNSCLC-DEGs screened by LASSO, SVM and RF analysis; (B) Box plot of expression differences of ExoNSCLC-DEGs; (C) Correlation between ExoNSCLC-DEGs; (D) Specific location of ExoNSCLC-DEGs in chromosomes.


### Construction, validation, and improvement of the NSCLC diagnostic model
  The 7 ExoNSCLC-DEGs were subsequently tested in the GEO experimental cohort and the TCGA validation group using ROC curve analysis to determine their clinical utility in NSCLC diagnosis. Figure 5A illustrates that, within the GEO experimental group, each of the seven genes demonstrated good predictive accuracy when evaluated independently. And the comprehensive diagnostic model showed significantly higher predictive power (Fig. 5B). When evaluated in the TCGA validation cohort (Fig. 5C,5D), most ExoNSCLC-DEGs maintained good discrimination; however, the predictive power of MIF decreased significantly in the external validation set. Given this observation, we explored whether removing MIF from the panel would improve or at least preserve the overall diagnostic performance of the remaining ExoNSCLC-DEGs.Fig. 5ROC curve of the diagnostic model constructed by ExoNSCLC-DEGs. (A) ROC curve of ExoNSCLC-DEGs in the experimental group; (B) ROC curve of the diagnostic model in the experimental group; (C) ROC curve of ExoNSCLC-DEGs in the validation group; (D) ROC curve of the diagnostic model in the validation group.

  As shown in Figure S3, excluding MIF did not impair the predictive power of other genes; on the contrary, both the single gene ROC curves in the GEO cohort (Figure S3A) and the comprehensive panel (Figure S3B) showed high AUC values, indicating that the contribution of MIF was not important for NSCLC discrimination. Similarly, multiple genes in the revised panel (Figure S3C,3D) in the TCGA validation cohort showed robust predictive performance. These findings suggest that although MIF initially emerged as a candidate biomarker in ExoNSCLC-DEG, its limited predictive power did not significantly affect the diagnostic strength conferred by the remaining six genes. Therefore, we decided to remove MIF to streamline the diagnostic model.


### Nomogram construction of the ExoNSCLC-DEGs model
  As demonstrated by Fig. 6A, we created a nomogram using the 6 ExoNSCLC-DEGs to improve the diagnostic model’s clinical utility. Afterwards, we used the calibration curve to assess the nomogram’s diagnostic reliability. Figure 6B illustrates that the calibration curve demonstrated strong concordance between the projected probability of the diagnostic model and the actual incidence probability of NSCLC. Furthermore, to assess the clinical utility of the diagnostic model, we conducted a DCA analysis. The results of the decision curve also showed that the diagnostic model constructed with the 6 ExoNSCLC-DEGs had good predictive power (Fig. 6C).

  Fig. 6Nomogram and validation of the diagnostic model. (A) Nomogram of the diagnostic model; (B) Calibration curve of the diagnostic model; (C) DCA curve of the diagnostic model.


### Immune cell infiltration and correlation analysis
  We employed ssGSEA to examine immune infiltration in NSCLC and investigate the possible immunomodulatory effects of ExoNSCLC-DEGs. We observed notable disparities in immune cell infiltration between NSCLC and normal tissues (Fig. 7A). Specifically, infiltration levels of immune cell subsets, including myeloid suppressor cells (MDSC), neutrophils, and regulatory T cells (Treg) in NSCLC, are markedly elevated compared to normal tissues, whereas dendritic cells and natural killer (NK) cell subsets were significantly diminished, while activated B cells were significantly increased in NSCLC tissues compared to normal tissues. Further correlation analysis between the expression of ExoNSCLC-DEGs and immune cell populations revealed an interaction between the two (Fig. 7B). It is worth mentioning that S100A4 shown a wide-ranging positive correlation with several types of immunosuppressive cells, such as MDSCs, macrophages, neutrophils, and regulatory T cells. This suggests that S100A4 could be crucial in facilitating tumor immune escape mechanisms.Fig. 7Results of immune infiltration analysis. (A) Box plot of immune infiltration characteristics of NSCLC; (B) Correlation analysis between ExoNSCLC-DEGs and immune cells.


### Prediction of potential therapeutic drugs
  Subsequently, we conducted drug enrichment analysis to explore the possible therapeutic targets linked to ExoNSCLC-DEGs. Figure 8A-B illustrates the emergence of various significant pharmaceutical substances, including N-nitrosodiethylamine, sunitinib, folic acid, capsaicin, miglitol, and XMD11-50, among others. The drug-gene interaction network (Fig. 8C) further elucidated these relationships, revealing specific connections between genes and candidate therapeutic drugs. For example, LRRK2 showed associations with multiple kinase inhibitors, including sunitinib and tofacitinib, S100A4 was significantly associated with metabolism-related compounds (such as MIGLITOL) and anti-inflammatory agents (such as capsaicin).Fig. 8Results of drug enrichment analysis. (A) Box plot of importance of drug enrichment analysis; (B) Bubble plot of importance of drug enrichment analysis; (C) Network diagram of correlation between ExoNSCLC-DEGs and predicted drugs.


### Molecular docking analysis of drugs
  To further investigate the interactions between the potential therapeutic drugs identified in our drug enrichment analysis and ExoNSCLC-DEGs (Table S7), we performed molecular docking studies, focusing on two key ExoNSCLC-DEGs: LRRK2 and S100A4. We found that sunitinib exhibited good binding affinity to LRRK2 with a docking score of -11.7 kcal/mol, suggesting that sunitinib may have potential therapeutic targeting of LRRK2 (Figure S4A,S4B). Docking analysis of S100A4 identified significant interactions with capsaicin (Figure S4C,S4D), folic acid (Figure S4E,S4F), and N-nitrosodiethylamine (Figure S4G,S4H). Among these compounds, capsaicin had the strongest binding to S100A4 with a docking affinity of -8.2 kcal/mol.


### RBP and TF regulatory network of ExoNSCLC-DEGs
  To further explore the potential regulatory mechanisms affecting the expression and function of ExoNSCLC-DEGs, we constructed a regulatory interaction network of RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) and transcription factors (TFs). As shown in Figure S5A, the RBP regulatory network revealed extensive interactions between ExoNSCLC-DEGs and multiple RBPs, suggesting that their expression can be complexly regulated at the posttranscriptional level, thereby affecting RNA stability and translation efficiency that are critical for tumorigenesis. In the transcription factor regulatory network (Figure S5B), specific TFs showed targeted interactions with ExoNSCLC-DEGs, indicating that transcriptional regulation is another level affecting the pathogenesis of NSCLC.


### Biological Validation
  We confirmed the expression of ExoNSCLC-DEGs in NSCLC cell lines (A549, H1299, and H1975) using qRT-PCR (Figure S6B). The findings indicated that GPM6A, HYAL1, S100A4, ROBO4, LRRK2, and HBA1 exhibited considerable differential expression in NSCLC cell lines, with the changes being statistically significant when compared to the normal control group (p < 0.001). Cell line experiments confirmed that all six core ExoNSCLC-DEGs were significantly downregulated in NSCLC cell lines compared with normal controls (p < 0.001). The results align closely with the findings of our bioinformatics research, so reinforcing the validity of our identified genes as molecular markers for the early detection of NSCLC. Figure S6A illustrates the expression of some ExoNSCLC-DEGs obtained from HPA (protein data were not available for all genes, such as ROBO4).


## Discussion
  NSCLC remains a major health problem worldwide, and the early non-invasive diagnosis of NSCLC has always been one of the research focuses of lung cancer diagnosis and treatment28. Recent studies have found that exosome-related genes abundant in body fluids may be potential biomarkers for the diagnosis of various cancers, which provides a new direction for the non-invasive diagnosis of lung cancer29. This study identified a diagnostic model consisting of a set of 6 ExoNSCLC-DEGs. The ROC curve, calibration curve, and DCA decision curve all indicated that the diagnostic model had strong diagnostic efficacy. Concurrently, we studied the regulatory network of ExoNSCLC-DEGs, projected prospective medicines, and examined the link between ExoNSCLC-DEGs and immune cell infiltration; these analyses provide new insights and useful tools for early NSCLC identification.

  Exosome-related genes in NSCLC are involved in numerous important biological processes and signaling pathways, according to KEGG and GO pathway enrichment analyses, including GO:0,043,086 negative regulation of catalytic activity, GO:0,032,496 response to lipopolysaccharide, and GO:0,002,237 response to bacteria-derived molecules. According to research, tumor cells can evade the immune system by secreting exosomes that block the host immune cells’ particular catalytic activity30. At the same time, lipopolysaccharide can also induce the release of inflammatory factors through the TLR4 pathway, promoting cell proliferation, metastasis and the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment in lung cancer31. This is likely to be the potential mechanism by which exosome-related genes affect lung cancer. KEGG enrichment analysis focused on the significant enrichment of the lhsa04657:IL-17 signaling pathway. IL-17 has been demonstrated to inhibit host anti-tumor immunity by attracting immunosuppressive cells, including MDSCs and Tregs, inside the lung cancer microenvironment, consequently facilitating tumor development, invasion, and metastasis32. In NSCLC, IL-17 is positively linked with the capacity for tumor invasion33. In addition, the enrichment of hsa04640: hematopoietic cell lineage suggests that exosome-related genes may reshape the tumor immune microenvironment by affecting the differentiation and function of bone marrow-derived cells, and current studies have pointed out that tumor-derived exosomes can guide bone marrow stem cells to differentiate into immunosuppressive cells and accelerate tumor immune escape34.

  Subsequently, we employed three feature screening techniques for identification and enhancement, ultimately identifying six major ExoNSCLC-DEGs (comprising GPM6A, HYAL1, S100A4, ROBO4, LRRK2, and HBA1). GPM6A is a membrane glycoprotein that can be used as a surface marker for diagnostic purposes35. HYAL1 is an exosomal hyaluronidase that degrades extracellular hyaluronan, remodels the matrix, and promotes polarization of immunosuppressive macrophages36. S100A4 promotes tumor progression and immunosuppression by activating STAT3 signaling37. ROBO4 maintains vascular integrity by antagonizing aberrant VEGF signaling; exosomal miRNAs targeting ROBO4 are associated with angiogenesis regulation38. LRRK2 participates in endocytic and exocytic trafficking, and its kinase activity is associated with remodeling the inflammatory microenvironment39. HBA1 has been repeatedly detected in serum and neuronal-derived exosomes, but its precise role in non-small cell lung cancer remains to be elucidated40. The ROC curves for both the experimental and validation groups indicated that the six core genes and the overall model had strong predictive capability. In addition, the Nomogram we further drew showed that the model can be directly used for the quantification and evaluation of clinical individualized diagnostic risks, while the Calibration analysis and DCA analysis further confirmed the reliability and good clinical application potential of the model. It is worth mentioning that many studies in recent years have also supported the value of exosomal nucleic acids in the early diagnosis of lung cancer. For example, a comprehensive analysis of more than 60 exosomal miRNAs showed that its AUC for diagnosing lung cancer was about 0.90, with sensitivity and specificity both above 80%41. This is comparable to the performance of our model, and the genes used in our diagnostic model are more streamlined. In addition, exosomal miRNAs have been found to regulate carcinogenic processes in NSCLC and are associated with clinical characteristics42, and exosome-mediated communication has been recognized as a key mechanism of immune regulation, metastasis, and drug resistance in NSCLC43. These reports are consistent with our observations and further highlight the dual diagnostic and immunological significance of exosome-associated genes in NSCLC.

  Analysis of immune cell infiltration demonstrated notable disparities in immune cell infiltration characteristics between NSCLC and normal tissues. Elevated infiltration of MDSCs, Tregs, macrophages, and neutrophils observed in NSCLC tissues aligns with existing evidence indicating these cells’ critical roles in establishing an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and promoting tumor immune escape44. Conversely, the reduced presence of activated NK cells and dendritic cells, known to mediate antitumor immunity, further highlights the immunocompromised state typical of NSCLC. In contrast, activated B cells were increased in NSCLC tissues. This elevation may reflect the expansion of tumor-associated B cell subsets (such as regulatory B cells), which have been reported to promote tumor progression and immune evasion rather than contribute to effective antitumor immunity45. Notably, in our ssGSEA analysis, S100A4 showed a broad positive correlation with immunosuppressive cells, suggesting a role in shaping the immune evasion microenvironment. Previous breast cancer studies have shown that S100A4 promotes metastatic growth through the RAGE pathway and is often elevated in tumors46. However, in our validation, S100A4 levels in NSCLC cell lines were lower than in normal controls. This discrepancy may be due to heterogeneity among tumor types or stages, and further validation in patient samples is necessary. Additionally, the notable correlation between LRRK2 expression and macrophage and neutrophil infiltration suggests LRRK2’s potential role in orchestrating pro-inflammatory and pro-tumorigenic environments, echoing earlier studies highlighting the immunomodulatory function of LRRK2 in various inflammatory diseases47.

  Drug enrichment analysis identified several pharmacological compounds potentially targeting ExoNSCLC-DEGs, including N-nitrosodiethylamine, sunitinib, folic acid, capsaicin, miglitol, and XMD11-50. The network analysis highlighted LRRK2’s strong connection with kinase inhibitors, notably sunitinib, aligning well with the emerging evidence of LRRK2 involvement in kinase-driven oncogenic pathways and inflammatory signaling cascades48. Molecular docking confirmed this relationship, indicating that sunitinib demonstrated the highest binding affinity with LRRK2, implying its potential as a treatment agent for NSCLC by particularly targeting LRRK2-mediated pathways. Similarly, S100A4 was closely associated with metabolic regulators and anti-inflammatory agents, particularly capsaicin, which showed a robust docking affinity. Prior research demonstrates capsaicin’s anticancer potential through modulation of inflammatory pathways and cell apoptosis, which might synergistically inhibit S100A4-mediated immunosuppression in NSCLC49. Therefore, these findings indicate novel therapeutic approaches leveraging specific ExoNSCLC-DEGs as drug targets. However, this analysis only used online docking platform predictions, and no subsequent molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed to evaluate the stability of the predicted ligand–protein interactions, and its specific docking situation still needs further experimental verification.

  Our analysis of regulatory interaction networks highlighted the significant roles of RBPs and TFs in modulating ExoNSCLC-DEGs. Notably, the RBPs IGF2BP1, IGF2BP3, and HNRNPC were identified as central regulators of HYAL1 and S100A4. These RBPs are known to influence tumorigenesis by controlling RNA stability and translation efficiency, crucial processes for tumor progression50,51. Additionally, the identified transcription factors, including STAT4, CREBBP, and RELA, are recognized for their roles in regulating genes related to immune response, inflammation, and cancer progression52,53. For example, the association between S100A4 and STAT4 underscores a potential feedback loop, reinforcing inflammatory signaling pathways known to exacerbate tumor growth and immune evasion54. Similarly, HYAL1 interactions with inflammatory TFs such as RELA further emphasize inflammation as a critical mechanism driving NSCLC pathology55.

  Ultimately, we used qRT-PCR to confirm the expression of ExoNSCLC-DEGs in NSCLC cell lines. The findings indicated that the expression of ExoNSCLC-DEGs was markedly aligned with our bioinformatics analysis conclusions, hence reinforcing the validity of our identified genes as molecular markers for the early detection of NSCLC. However, a limitation of this study is the lack of independent validation using patient-derived serum exosomes. Future studies will aim to address this gap by incorporating clinical cohorts, thereby further reinforcing the translational value and potential clinical utility of our diagnostic model.


## Conclusion
  In summary, we identified 6 exosome-related biomarkers significantly associated with NSCLC and its immune regulation: ExoNSCLC-DEGs (including: GPM6A, HYAL1, S100A4, ROBO4, LRRK2 and HBA1). The diagnostic model we constructed based on these genes showed good accuracy, stability and clinical applicability for the diagnosis of NSCLC, highlighting its potential advantage as a non-invasive diagnostic method. Furthermore, we identified the correlation between core genes and immune infiltration, as well as potential candidate therapeutic agents, offering novel insights into NSCLC treatment options. qRT-PCR method validated the expression reliability of ExoNSCLC-DEGs in the analysis. Notwithstanding the constraints of bioinformatics research, this study offers novel perspectives and insights for the future clinical translation and precision diagnosis and treatment of NSCLC.
