# Metabolomic and Single‐Cell Transcriptomic Analyses Shed Light on Secondary Metabolite Profiling and Potential Developmental Dynamics of Glandular Trichomes in Artemisia argyi


## ABSTRACT
  Plant glandular trichomes (GTs) are characterised by their ability to synthesise and store abundant secondary metabolites of significant biological importance. The medicinal plant Artemisia argyi exhibits a dense covering of GTs, yet the precise metabolic compositions and the developmental molecular dynamics of A. argyi GTs remain insufficiently characterised. Using liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) and gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS), we identified 969 differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) between GTs and non‐glandular trichomes (NGTs) of A. argyi. GTs were significantly enriched in diverse metabolites, including terpenoids, flavonoids and fatty acyls, among which sesquiterpenoids were the most abundant terpenoid subclass in DAMs. To elucidate the molecular basis of GT secondary metabolite biosynthesis and development, we generated a single‐cell transcriptomic atlas of A. argyi leaves, annotating cell populations of mesophyll cells (MCs), epidermal cells (ECs), vascular cells (VCs), stomatal guard cells (GCs) and GTs. Pseudotime trajectory analysis uncovered the continuous developmental trajectory of GTs and identified several candidate transcription factors (TFs) potentially involved in GT development. Leveraging the single‐cell atlas, we constructed cell‐type‐specific co‐expression networks for sesquiterpene biosynthesis genes. Within the GT‐specific expression module, we identified β‐caryophyllene synthase AarTPS77, while the EC‐specific AarTPS52 was found to function as β‐farnesene synthase. Furthermore, we functionally characterised AarTPS95 and AarTPS96, which catalyse the formation of germacrene A and 12 additional sesquiterpenoids. These findings provide insights into the molecular basis of trichome development and secondary metabolite accumulation in A. argyi, laying the foundation for improving the quality of medicinal materials.


## Introduction
  Trichomes are specialised epidermal outgrowths that cover many above‐ground plant surfaces, which can be classified into two main types based on their secretory capacity: secretory glandular trichomes (GTs) and non‐glandular trichomes (NGTs) (Yang and Ye 2013). These structures are integral to plant adaptation and survival (Huchelmann et al. 2017; Schuurink and Tissier 2020). NGTs provide physical defence for plants, while GTs secrete and accumulate a variety of secondary metabolites, including terpenoids, phenylpropanoids, flavonoids, acyl sugars, etc. (Balkunde et al. 2010; Liu et al. 2019; Majdi et al. 2011). Among these secondary metabolites, terpenoids are notable for their structural complexity and broad biological activities (Chen et al. 2003; Lange and Turner 2013). Sesquiterpenoids in particular stand out due to their important ecological roles and considerable pharmaceutical potential (Francis et al. 2004; Li et al. 2013; Rasmann et al. 2005). Indeed, GTs act as natural factories for valuable sesquiterpenoids in many species like the antimalarial drug Artemisinin ( Artemisia annua ) and some essential volatile oils (e.g.,  Humulus lupulus  and Ocinum basilicum) (Iijima et al. 2004; Soetaert et al. 2013; Wang et al. 2008).

  Artemisia argyi (Mugwort), a perennial herb widely used in traditional Chinese medicine, is especially recognised for its role in moxibustion (Liu et al. 2021). Mugwort also holds significant cultural value in East Asia, particularly during the Dragon Boat Festival, where it is traditionally used for its insect‐repellent properties and as a symbol of blessing and protection. The surface of A. argyi is covered by both GTs and NGTs, while GTs are important for synthesising and accumulating volatile terpenoids, flavonoids and other bioactive compounds (Cui et al. 2022). The application of multi‐omics technologies has significantly advanced the elucidation of biosynthetic pathways underlying specialised secondary metabolites in A. argyi. Genome sequencing of A. argyi revealed an expansion of the terpene synthase (TPS) gene family, which may contribute to the remarkable diversity of terpenoids in this species (Chen, Guo, et al. 2023; Miao et al. 2022). Transcriptomic analysis has identified a range of candidate genes associated with the biosynthesis and regulation of terpenoids in A. argyi (Lee et al. 2023; Yi et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2022). Moreover, functional identification of TPS genes has provided further mechanistic insights into the anti‐mosquito bioactivity of A. argyi (Zhi et al. 2024). Despite these advancements, the potential genes involved in both the development and bioactive compound synthesis of A. argyi GTs remain largely unexplored. Nevertheless, these studies have thus far been limited to tissue‐level resolution, leaving gaps in our understanding of the regulatory mechanisms and biosynthetic pathways at the single‐cell level. Addressing these limitations will be pivotal for unravelling the molecular underpinnings of trichome specialisation and terpenoid biosynthesis in A. argyi.

  Single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) has emerged as an effective tool for capturing gene expression profiles in individual plant cells, enabling the identification of distinct cell types and the reconstruction of cell differentiation trajectories (Gala et al. 2021; Mo and Jiao 2022; Shaw et al. 2021). Understanding cellular heterogeneity is key to revealing how developmental processes give rise to specialised metabolic functions. Integrating scRNA‐seq with other omics approaches, notably metabolomics, enables the spatiotemporal dissection of secondary metabolite biosynthesis at single‐cell resolution (Han et al. 2024; Li et al. 2023; Sun, Shen, et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2022). Given its unique capacity to resolve cell‐specific expression patterns, scRNA‐seq is particularly well‐suited for studying GTs, where developmental and metabolic processes are tightly interlinked. Although scRNA‐seq has been successfully applied to root tissues, leaf development and gland formation in model plants (Guillotin et al. 2023; Lin et al. 2023; Lopez‐Anido et al. 2021; Sun, Han, et al. 2023), its extension to non‐model species such as A. argyi remains limited. This limitation is largely due to technical hurdles such as protoplast isolation and the absence of well‐defined cell‐type markers (Seyfferth et al. 2021; Sun, Shen, et al. 2023). Effectively tackling these challenges may deepen our understanding of the development and specialised metabolism of medicinal plants.

  Here, we employed metabolomics, single‐cell transcriptomics and in vitro enzyme assays to investigate the metabolic and cellular landscape of A. argyi leaves. We isolated high‐purity GTs and analysed the metabolic profiles, confirming marked differences in metabolite composition compared with NGTs. Using scRNA‐seq, we developed a cell‐type‐specific expression atlas of A. argyi leaves. Based on this atlas, we delineated a pseudotime trajectory of GT development and identified several candidate transcription factor (TF) genes potentially involved in GT development. Notably, we established cell‐type‐specific co‐expression gene networks for sesquiterpene biosynthesis and functionally characterised TPS involved in sesquiterpene production in GTs and other cell types. Collectively, these findings advance knowledge of the metabolic and developmental characteristics of A. argyi trichomes and underscore the value of single‐cell omics in exploring specialised metabolism in medicinal plants.


## Result

### Comparative Metabolomic Profiling of GTs and NGTs in A. argyi
  The surfaces of A. argyi leaves are densely covered with both GTs and NGTs, while NGT density is higher on the abaxial surface than on the adaxial surface, making the leaves grey underneath and green on the adaxial surface (Figure 1a). Structurally, the GTs are multicellular and shield‐shaped, while the NGTs exhibit a distinctive T‐shaped structure (Figure 1b, Figure S1). GTs of A. argyi consist of 10 double‐row cells with diameters ranging from 30 to 50 μm, whereas NGTs are composed of basal, stalk and apical cells, with lengths varying from 300 to 800 μm (Figure 1b). These clear morphological differences enable precise differentiation and isolation of GTs and NGTs from A. argyi leaves. In this study, we optimised targeted mechanical isolation methods, which allowed effective recovery of GTs and NGTs with improved purity and preserved cellular integrity (Figure 1c) (Cui et al. 2022; Lee et al. 2023). The metabolite profiling of these two types of trichomes was conducted using liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) and gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) assays. A total of 870 differential accumulated metabolites (DAMs) were detected using LC–MS, with 650 showing higher relative abundance in GTs and 220 more enriched in NGTs (Figure 1d, Table S1). GC–MS assays identified 104 DAMs, 103 of which were enriched in GTs (Figure 1d, Table S1). Five DAMs were detected by both LC–MS and GC–MS platforms (Table S1). After removing these five overlaps, a total of 969 unique DAMs were identified (Figure 1e). These metabolite analyses clarified the metabolic profile of GTs in A. argyi, which differs distinctly from that of NGTs.

  Due to the well‐recognised roles of terpenoids, flavonoids and fatty acyls as key bioactive compounds in medicinal plants, we analysed their composition and relative abundance in GTs and NGTs of A. argyi (Feng et al. 2023; Huchelmann et al. 2017; Schuurink and Tissier 2020). Of the 969 DAMs, 142 (14.65%) were terpenoids, making them the most abundant compound class (Figure 1e). Sesquiterpenoids constituted the largest subgroup, accounting for 23.24% of all terpenoid DAMs (Figure 1e,f). Among other terpenoids, monoterpenoids, diterpenoids and triterpenoids accounted for 10.56%, 13.38% and 13.38% of the total terpenoid DAMs, respectively (Figure 1e). Flavonoids made up 8.57% of the total DAMs, with flavonoid glycosides (24.10%) and flavonols (16.87%) being the two most abundant subgroups within this class (Figure 1e). Similar to terpenoids, flavonoid accumulation was also higher in GTs (Figure 1h). Cudraflavone A and Chrysoeriol 7‐O‐(6″‐malonyl‐glucoside) were the most abundant flavonoid compounds in GTs and NGTs, respectively (Figure 1h, Table S1). Among the 122 identified fatty acyl compounds, the eicosanoids (34.43%) and fatty acids (26.23%) were predominantly enriched in GTs (Figure 1e,g). For instance, 3Z,6Z,9Z,12Z,15Z‐tricosapentaene was more abundant in NGTs, whereas GTs showed higher levels of dihydrozeatin‐9‐N‐glucoside‐O‐glucoside and maltitol (Figure 1g, Table S1).

  Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis of DAMs between GTs and NGTs revealed that the primary pathways include sesquiterpenoid and triterpenoid biosynthesis, ABC transporters, pentose and glucuronate interconversions and arachidonic acid metabolism (Figure S2, Table S2). The secondary metabolite pathways were also significantly enriched, including phenylalanine metabolism, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, terpenoid biosynthesis, biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, cutin, suberin and waxes (Figure S2, Table S2). This result highlights the pivotal role of A. argyi GTs in secondary metabolite accumulation. The distinct metabolic profile of GTs, particularly in terpenoid biosynthesis, establishes them as key sites for bioactive compound production. Thus, elucidating GT metabolism and development will provide critical insights into terpenoid biosynthetic pathways and their potential utilisation.


### Construction of Single‐Cell Transcriptomic Atlas for A. argyi Leaves
  To construct a comprehensive single‐cell transcriptomic atlas of A. argyi leaves, we isolated protoplasts from the young leaves of tissue‐cultured seedlings and prepared droplet‐based scRNA‐seq libraries through the 10X Genomics platform (Figure S3). Raw sequencing data obtained from two biological replicates were quantified through the Alevin pipeline (Srivastava et al. 2019). As a result, the high‐quality transcriptomic data were generated from 24 174 cells, with an average of 30 582 reads per cell and a median of 1595 expressed genes (Figure 2a, Table S3). The rigorous quality control filters were then applied, including cells with abnormal gene and UMI counts or significant contamination from mitochondrial (> 3%) or chloroplast genes (> 40%) were excluded (Figure 2b). Additionally, cells with unusually high gene expression, potentially representing doublets or multiplets, were removed from the doublet detection workflow (Figure S4). During clustering, we also accounted for the heterogeneity of the cell cycle and the potential batch effects between two independent samples to ensure robust results (Figure S5). Ultimately, a total of 22 032 high‐quality cells were retained for further analysis.

  Unsupervised clustering analysis of high‐quality cells via Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) revealed 18 distinct cell clusters (Figure 2c, Table S4). Cluster 0 comprised the largest cell number, with 2639 cells, while Cluster 17 covered the fewest, 163 cells (Figure S6). Given the lack of marker genes for cell types of A. argyi, we first identified homologues of well‐characterised marker genes from other plant species and examined their expression patterns in A. argyi, thereby compiling a reference dataset of cell‐type‐specific markers for A. argyi (Table S5). Using these markers, we preliminarily identified three main cell populations in A. argyi leaves: mesophyll cells (MCs), ECs and vascular cells (VCs) (Figure 2d). MCs constituted the majority, spanning nine clusters (Clusters 1, 3, 4, 6, 10, 11, 12, 14 and 15) (Figure 2d). The marker genes for MCs were predominantly expressed within these clusters, containing PHOTOSYSTEM I SUBUNIT O (PSAO), CHLOROPHYLL A/B BINDING PROTEIN 3 (CAB3), CARBONIC ANHYDRASE 1 (CA1), RIBULOSE BISPHOSPHATE CARBOXYLASE SMALL CHAIN 1A (RBCS1A), PHOTOSYSTEM I LIGHT HARVESTING COMPLEX GENE 1 (LHCA1) and MATURASE K (MATK) (Ascencio‐Ibáñez et al. 2008; Endo et al. 2014; Izumi et al. 2012; Sawchuk et al. 2008). Most of these genes encoded chloroplast proteins or were involved in photosynthesis (Figure 2d). Although MC marker genes displayed unique expression profiles in Clusters 6 and 11, these clusters were adjacent on the UMAP plot and were both classified as MCs, indicating a substantial heterogeneity within the MC population (Figure 2c,d). The VC population was distributed across four clusters (Clusters 0, 5, 7 and 9), with distinct spatial distributions observed on the UMAP plot (Figure 2c,d). The VC marker gene homologues, including BUSHY AND DWARF 2 (BUD2), SUGARS WILL EVENTUALLY BE EXPORTED TRANSPORTERS 12 (SWEET12) and  Arabidopsis Thaliana ARABIDOPSIS H (+)‐ATPASE (AHA3) (Chen et al. 2012; Pommerrenig et al. 2011; Xu and Liesche 2021), were primarily expressed in Clusters 0, 5, 7 and 9 (Figure 2d). Expression correlation analysis indicated a strong correlation between Clusters 5 and 7 with Cluster 9, which helped justify their classification as VCs (Figure S7). The high expression of the xylem marker gene BUD2 in Cluster 9 implied xylem cells, while Cluster 0 was predicted to be phloem cells due to the high expression of the phloem marker gene AHA3 (Figure 2d). Cluster 8 was identified as the EC population. Marker genes LIPID TRANSFER PROTEIN 1 (LTP1), 3‐KETOACYL‐COA SYNTHASE 10 (KCS10/FDH) and ECERIFERUM 3 (KCS11) were specifically expressed in this cell cluster (Figure 2d), which suggested the potential role of the EC population in the biosynthesis of plant cuticular lipids and waxes (Sun, Shen, et al. 2023; Sun, Han, et al. 2023). To clarify, the EC preliminarily identified here was defined as a broadly epidermal population. The remaining four cell clusters, Cluster 2, Cluster 14, Cluster 16 and Cluster 17 were categorised as unknown cell types (UN) due to the absence of specific marker genes (Figure 2d). Of particular concern is that GTs are epidermis‐derived structures; the presence of lipid‐ and wax‐related biosynthetic activity in ECs further supports their developmental linkage to GT formation. To confirm the accuracy of EC and MC classifications, we examined the expression patterns of the classical marker genes RBCS1A (for MCs) and FDH (for ECs). RNA in situ hybridisation on tissue sections of A. argyi leaves further validated these two cell‐type‐specific markers, confirming the fidelity of these crucial classifications (Figure 2e,f).

  To further verify the cell‐type identification from a functional perspective, all the predominantly expressed genes in each cell cluster were identified and classified into cell types for Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis (Figure 2g,h). As anticipated, most enriched GO terms corresponded to the relevant cell type identified in our study (Figure 2h, Table S6). In detail, genes specifically expressed in MCs showed enrichments in photosynthesis‐related pathways and were also associated with the synthesis and metabolism of terpenoid and flavonoids (Figure 2h). The enrichment of terpenoid and lipid metabolic pathways among EC‐specific genes highlights the metabolic specialisation of EC populations (Figure 2h). Furthermore, the genes specifically expressed in VCs were predominantly related to lignin biosynthesis and xenobiotic transport (Figure 2h). The integration of maker‐based cell population identification and functional enrichment analysis enabled accurate annotation of the cell types in A. argyi leaves.


### Subcluster Analysis Distinguished the GT Cell Type of A. argyi
  Plant GTs are epidermal‐related structures derived from the differentiation of ECs (Chalvin et al. 2020; Yang and Ye 2013). Additionally, the genes related to secondary metabolism are actively expressed in ECs (Figure 2h), which prompted us to propose that GTs were likely present within the EC population. Because of the complexity of the EC population, we re‐clustered the preliminarily identified ECs (Cluster 8) using UMAP. At a resolution of 0.4, these cells were re‐clustered into five subclusters, named EC_0 to EC_4 (Figure 3a,b). We examined the expression of homologous genes corresponding to previously reported GT marker genes across five subclusters. Bubble plots clearly revealed that MIXTA1, HOMEODOMAIN PROTEIN 1 (HD1) and AUXIN/INDOLE‐3‐ACETIC ACID 14 (IAA14) were specifically expressed in the EC_1 subcluster (Figure 3c) (Shi et al. 2018; Yan et al. 2017; Deng et al. 2012). To further validate the expression of these GT markers, we manually scraped trichomes (TR) from the petiole of A. argyi and isolated petiole tissue devoid of epidermis (PNE, completely excluding trichomes) (Figure 3d). The RT‐qPCR analysis confirmed that the GT marker genes MIXTA1, HD1 and IAA14 exhibited significantly higher expression in TR compared to PNE, supporting the annotation of EC_1 as GT (Figure 3e). The GO enrichment analysis showed that the genes preferentially expressed in the EC_1 subcluster were significantly enriched in processes such as fatty acid elongation, cuticle development, lipid transport and flavonoid biosynthesis, underscoring their active roles in secondary metabolism (Tables S7 and S8). This result likely accounts for the substantial accumulation of secondary metabolites in A. argyi GTs. Additionally, homologous genes of stomatal guard cell (GC) markers, including LIGB, GLUTATHIONE S‐TRANSFERASE THETA 1 (GSTT1), 12‐OXOPHYTODIENOATE REDUCTASE 1 (OPR1) and BAX INHIBITOR 1 (BI‐1), showed specific expression in the EC_4 subcluster, leading to its annotation as GC (Tenorio et al. 2022; Kim et al. 2021). These genes are also expressed specifically in GC in other single‐cell datasets (He et al. 2024). Meanwhile, EC_0 and the small cell clusters EC_2 and EC_3, which lacked marker gene expression, were classified as EC and UN, respectively (Figure 3c).

  The GTs definition was further proved by bulk RNA‐sequencing (RNA‐seq). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were compared among the petiole epidermis (PE), PNE and intact petiole (P) of A. argyi (Figure 3d). Mechanically peeled PE, which is rich in GTs similar to the leaf, contrasted with trichome‐lacking PNE at the transcriptomic level (Figure 3f,g). GO enrichment analysis of upregulated DEGs in PE (vs. PNE) showed significant enrichment in the fatty acid biosynthetic process and lipid transport (Figure 3h). Similarly, downregulated DEGs in PNE (vs. P) were enriched in the same pathways, suggesting that lipid metabolism is more active in tissues with abundant GTs and retained epidermis (Figure 3i). Such results of enrichment support the notion that epidermal tissues harbouring GTs are likely sites of lipid‐associated metabolic processes, possibly linked to the containing of specialised metabolites. Additionally, a group of genes with high expression levels in these two pathways was preferentially expressed in the EC_1 (Figure 3j). Furthermore, publicly available RNA‐seq data of A. argyi mixed trichomes (named TRL) and young leaves (named YL) from a previous study further supported the reliability of GT definition (Lee et al. 2023). The heatmap illustrated the expression patterns of the 10 most highly expressed genes of TRL (TRL_Top10) across different cell types and tissues (Figure 3k, Figure S8). Eight of these genes (Aarg09G021180, Aarg13G034030, Aarg06G024160, Aarg10G057440, Aarg10G047160, Aarg05G016420, Aarg0G016200 and Aarg04G009120) showed specific expression in GTs. These genes were annotated with functions related to lipid transport and flavonoid synthesis, suggesting their potential involvement in the biosynthesis and transport of secondary metabolites in GTs (Figure 3k). Seven TRL_Top10 genes were highly expressed in PE and low in PNE (Figure 3k). We selected Aarg09G021180 (LTP) and Aarg04G009120 (POD) for RT‐qPCR detection and found that their expression levels in TR were significantly higher than those in PNE (Figure 3l). Taken together, these lines of evidence confirm the existence of the GT cell type in A. argyi and suggest active metabolic processes within them, laying a foundation for in‐depth studies on GT development and secondary metabolism.


### Developmental Trajectory of A. argyi
GTs
  To elucidate the developmental trajectory of GTs in A. argyi, we performed pseudotime analysis including GTs and ECs, reconstructing the developmental progression with high resolution. This analysis positioned all cells along a dynamic pseudotime trajectory (Figure 4a). Guided by biological relevance and temporal ordering, we designated the branch predominantly comprising ECs as the pre‐branch (Figure 4a,b). The inferred trajectory unveiled a pivotal differentiation node (node 1), bifurcating cells into three distinct states (Figure 4c). State 1 primarily encompassed ECs, while State 3 was enriched with GTs, underscoring their advanced developmental stage. State 2, intriguingly, represented a heterogeneous blend of ECs and GTs, likely indicative of a transitional phase bridging these cell types (Figure 4c). We mapped the transcript accumulation profiles of GT marker genes onto cells along the pseudotime trajectory and observed that they are mainly expressed at the end of the trajectory (Figure 4d).

  Then, we assessed the gene expression pattern of the pseudotime‐dependent genes (PDGs) identified from the differentiation trajectory, which illustrated three distinct modules (Figure 4e). The results of GO enrichment analysis showed that the GO term secondary metabolic processes is shared among the three modules, suggesting that genes related to secondary metabolism exhibit diverse expression trends during GT development (Figure 4e). Module 1 contains the largest number of PDGs, whose expression levels are gradually upregulated along the pseudotime trajectory (Figure 4e). PDGs in this module were significantly enriched in biosynthesis and lipid binding terms (Figure 4e).

  Module 1 is also associated with cell growth and DNA‐binding TF activity GO terms, which is consistent with previous knowledge that the initiation and development of GTs are tightly regulated by complex transcriptional networks (Chalvin et al. 2020). This led us to investigate the expression dynamics of TF genes during the differentiation of GTs. In the three PDGs modules, we identified 106 TF genes potentially associated with GT development, which belong to multiple TF families (Figure 4f). Multiple TF genes have been previously implicated in the regulation of trichome development in other plant species. In  Arabidopsis thaliana  and  Solanum lycopersicum , the conserved transcriptional frameworks for trichome development, including MYB‐bHLH‐WD40 complexes, HD‐ZIP IV and cell‐cycle regulators, have been uncovered (Chalvin et al. 2020; Yan et al. 2018; Yang and Ye 2013; Zheng et al. 2021). Notably, AP2/ERF and WRKY genes enriched in GTs probably coordinate cuticle formation and activate secondary metabolic pathways (Feng et al. 2020; Xie et al. 2021). We examined the expression trends of the selected TF genes along the pseudotime trajectory and their expression patterns in different cell types (Figure S9). Additionally, using their protein sequences, we constructed a phylogenetic tree with TFs from the bHLH, MYB, WRKY and HD‐ZIP families that have been extensively studied for their functions (Figure S10). Finally, we screened out 12 candidate genes that may be involved in the regulation of GT development (Figure 4g). Specifically, HB‐7_1 and HB‐7_2, belonging to the HD‐ZIP family, showed significantly increased expression levels at the differentiation node (node 1) of ECs and GTs (Figure 4g). MYB111, MYB306_1 and MYB306_2 from the MYB family generally exhibited an upregulated expression trend, while the expression level of MYB62 decreased significantly before differentiation (Figure 4g). Their orthologous gene AaMIXTA1 in  A. annua  has been demonstrated to be expressed in GTs and regulate the initiation of GTs (Shi et al. 2018). MYC2_1 and MYC_2 from the bHLH family showed an increasing trend in their expression levels (Figure 4g). Their orthologous gene SlMYC1 and CmMYC2 are positive regulators of GT development (Guan et al. 2024; Xu et al. 2018). In contrast, the expression of MYC_3 showed a trend of first increasing and then decreasing (Figure 4g). However, the expression levels of WRKY family genes WRKY48, WRKY71 and WRKY75 decreased significantly with the development of GTs (Figure 4g). These findings indicate that A. argyi GT development is governed by a complex transcriptional network involving multiple conserved TF families. The identified candidate TFs represent valuable resources for understanding the molecular mechanisms of trichome cell fate and morphogenesis in non‐model medicinal plants.


### Cell‐Type‐Specific Sesquiterpene Biosynthesis of A. argyi
  Metabolome profiling showed that GTs in A. argyi are particularly rich in the diversity of sesquiterpenoids (Figure 1e,f). Some functionally significant sesquiterpenoids, such as caryophyllene and farnesene (or their derivatives), accumulate significantly in GTs (Figure 5a). This finding indicates that investigating the biosynthetic pathways of sesquiterpene in GTs is imperative. To explore the cell‐type‐specific sesquiterpene biosynthesis, we first analysed the single‐cell expression patterns of genes involved in the mevalonate (MVA) pathway. Interestingly, the majority of genes in the MVA pathway were expressed across different cell types (Figure S11). Consistent with the patterns observed in the MVA pathway, a set of key sesquiterpene biosynthesis genes from the TPS‐a subfamily also showed distinct expression profiles in different cell types (Figure 5b). To clarify the relationships between these genes and different cell populations, we built a co‐expression network of MVA pathway genes and TPS genes utilising the single‐cell atlas of A. argyi leaves (Figure S12). According to the correlation and associated cell types, we partitioned the entire network into six co‐expression modules, each containing MVA pathway and TPS genes with highly consistent expression patterns (Figure S12). In the GT‐specific modules, nine upstream pathway genes and seven TPS genes were captured (Figure 5c, Figure S12). Specifically, AACT5 and AACT7 (encoding acetyl‐CoA C‐acetyltransferase), HMGR4, HMGR9 and HMGR12 (encoding 3‐hydroxy‐3‐methylglutaryl‐CoA reductase), PMK3 (encoding phosphomevalonate kinase), IDI1 and IDI3 (encoding isopentenyl‐diphosphate delta isomerase), FPPS10 (encoding farnesyl pyrophosphate synthase) along with AarTPS4, AarTPS46, AarTPS77, AarTPS101, AarTPS106, AarTPS115 and AarTPS117, are primarily expressed in GTs (Figure 5c, Figure S12). To elucidate the roles of TPS genes in sesquiterpene accumulation within GTs, we attempted to clone them and successfully obtained the full‐length sequence of AarTPS77. Additionally, we also cloned three TPS genes (AarTPS52, AarTPS95 and AarTPS96) from other modules (Figure S12).

  Phylogenetic analysis of these TPS, alongside 19 functionally characterised TPS, resolved two distinct clades (Figure 5d, Table S9). The first clade, composed of AtTPS24, HlMTS2, AaQH1 and AaQH6, is associated with monoterpenoid biosynthesis (Figure 5d) (Chen et al. 2003; Jia et al. 1999; Lu et al. 2002; Wang et al. 2008). In contrast, the remaining 15 TPS genes formed a separate clade associated with sesquiterpene biosynthetic activity. Within this clade, AarTPS52 clustered with the β‐farnesene synthase (AaBFS) from  A. annua , suggesting its involvement in β‐farnesene biosynthesis (Figure 5d) (Salmon et al. 2015). Similarly, AarTPS77 grouped with β‐caryophyllene synthases from  A. annua  (AaQHS1) and  Tanacetum parthenium  (TpCarS), indicating its function as a β‐caryophyllene synthase (Figure 5d) (Majdi et al. 2011). Additionally, AarTPS95 and AarTPS96 clustered with germacrene A synthases from  Helianthus annuus  (HaGAS1) and A. argyi (AarTPS34), implying their roles as germacrene A synthases (Figure 5d) (Göpfert et al. 2009).

  The catalytic functions of these four candidate TPSs were substantiated through in vivo and in vitro assays. For AarTPS52, analysis of culture extracts or headspace by headspace solid phase microextraction‐gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS‐SPME‐GC/MS) confirmed the in vivo production of the acyclic sesquiterpene (E)‐β‐farnesene (5) (Figure 5e). Further assays with purified recombinant protein, obtained via Ni2+‐chelate affinity chromatography, demonstrated its ability to convert E,E‐Farnesyl diphosphate (FPP) into (E)‐β‐farnesene (5), identifying it as a β‐farnesene synthase (AarBFS) (Figure 5e). As expected, AarTPS77 was confirmed to function as a β‐caryophyllene synthase (AarBCS), producing β‐caryophyllene (2) as its primary product, along with trace amounts of α‐humulene (6) (Figure 5f). The catalytic function of AarTPS77 suggests that it makes a key contribution to the accumulation of caryophyllene compounds in GTs of A. argyi (Figure 5a). Despite producing a broad spectrum of 13 sesquiterpenes, AarTPS95 and AarTPS96 (with 89.09% amino acid identity) were found to primarily synthesise germacrene A, suggesting their classification as germacrene A synthases (Figure 5g, Figure S13). These sesquiterpenes, including β‐Elemene (a thermal breakdown product of germacrene A), were predominantly localised in GTs (Figure 5g, Table S1). Furthermore, we observed that AarTPS95 and AarTPS96 could also catalyse geranyl diphosphate (GPP) to produce three trace monoterpenoid compounds (Figure S14). The unexpected ability of AarTPS95 and AarTPS96 to catalyse monoterpenoid production broadens the metabolic versatility of these enzymes.

  GT development is frequently tightly coupled with the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites. To further predict potential TFs which are critical in both GT formation and terpenoid production, we used the PlantCare database to conduct in silico analyses of the 2000 bp promoter sequences upstream of the transcription start site (TSS) of the AarTPS52, AarTPS77, AarTPS95 and AarTPS96 genes, aiming to identify putative cis‐regulatory elements. Finally, 51 types of cis‐regulatory elements were identified, including several hormone‐responsive elements and stress‐responsive elements (Figure S15). Notably, AarTPS95 and AarTPS77 contained a large number of MYB and MYC binding elements, suggesting that these TPSs may be regulated by one or more types of TFs. However, the specific regulatory mechanisms require further investigation. Collectively, our results highlight the specialised pathways for sesquiterpene production in different cell types of A. argyi. The findings present a comprehensive framework for understanding the specialised metabolism in A. argyi.


## Discussion
  Mugwort (A. argyi) is a widely used medicinal plant in East Asia, valued for therapeutic efficacy and traditional use in moxibustion (Hu et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2021). This study presents a comprehensive metabolomic analysis of A. argyi trichomes, revealing marked metabolic differences between GTs and NGTs. Importantly, we also applied single‐cell transcriptomic analysis for the first time in A. argyi to investigate tissue differentiation and secondary metabolite biosynthesis (Figure 6). The results highlight potential cell‐type‐specific sesquiterpene biosynthesis and identify candidate genes that may be involved in GT development. Our findings provide a valuable basis for targeted genetic engineering strategies aimed at enhancing the yield of bioactive compounds in medicinal plants.

  Significant differences in secondary metabolite accumulation were observed between A. argyi GTs and NGTs, as demonstrated by the classification of DAMs and KEGG enrichment analysis (Figure 1, Figure S2). The remarkable chemical diversity and high abundance of flavonoids, terpenoids and fatty acyls in GTs highlight their essential function in specialised metabolite accumulation in A. argyi (Figure 1). In contrast, the relatively low accumulation of DAMs in NGTs suggests a primarily structural or protective role, in line with the traditionally recognised functions of these two trichome types (Figure 1). Collectively, these results extend our knowledge of GT and NGT functional divergence in A. argyi, highlighting their distinct metabolic capacities in addition to their morphological differences.

  ScRNA‐seq provides a powerful and high‐resolution approach for investigating the molecular mechanisms of specialised organ development and secondary metabolism in plants (Bawa et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2019). However, the application of scRNA‐seq in A. argyi remains challenging due to protoplast isolation difficulties, largely caused by the thick cuticle and waxy trichome surfaces (Cui et al. 2022). To facilitate protoplast isolation, we optimised enzymatic digestion using leaf tissues from tissue‐cultured A. argyi seedlings (Figure S3). Another challenge was the lack of established cell‐type markers, which hindered the cell‐type annotation. To overcome this limitation, we employed a homologue‐based marker strategy, allowing the identification of five major cell populations: MCs, VCs, ECs, GCs and GTs (Figures 2 and 3). This strategy was widely used in single‐cell studies of non‐model plant species (Kang et al. 2022; Sun, Shen, et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2022). Moreover, the MCs and ECs classifications were further confirmed by RNA in situ hybridisation (Figure 2e,f). The annotation of GT, the key cell population in this study, was supported by RT‐qPCR and bulk‐RNA data (Figure 3c–l). Due to current technical limitations, another intractable issue is the absence of NGT populations in the single‐cell atlas of A. argyi leaves. Mature NGTs can reach a size of 300 μm and are irregular elongated cells, making them difficult to pass through cell strainers and be captured by the 10X Genomics platform (Figure 1b, Figure S1). Thus, NGTs are not included in the protoplast suspension. Moreover, regrettably, marker genes for A. argyi unmature NGTs have not been effectively developed, and we did not observe specific expression of NGT population markers, which further increases the difficulty in identifying NGTs. Therefore, we cautiously speculate that NGTs may not be included in the current version of the atlas. This issue is prevalent in studies that use plant protoplasts as the target for capture, and it is even more challenging particularly for non‐model species. For instance, in single‐cell transcriptome studies of  C. roseus , due to the size and irregular shape of laticifer cells (LC), they were not included in the pseudotime trajectory (Sun, Shen, et al. 2023). In subsequent studies, the adoption of a single‐nucleus sequencing approach may address the issue of difficulty in capturing irregular or large cells.

  Plant development is a continuous process governed by complex transcriptional programmes. Based on the single‐cell transcriptome atlas of A. argyi leaves, we reconstructed a pseudotime trajectory that traced the differentiation of GTs (Figure 4a). This analysis revealed dynamic transcriptional changes along the trajectory and identified TF genes whose expression progressively changed during GT maturation, indicating their potential involvement in trichome development (Figure 4e–g). Many of these candidate TFs belong to gene families previously implicated in trichome development, including bHLH, R2R3‐MYB, WRKY and HD‐ZIP IV (Schuurink and Tissier 2020; Xu et al. 2018; Yan et al. 2018; Zheng et al. 2021). Other TF genes, such as those from the AP2/ERF and known for their roles in organogenesis and stress responses, exhibited progressively altered expression patterns or were enriched in GTs (Feng et al. 2020). We investigated three MYC genes from the perspectives of expression patterns across different cell types, expression trends during GT development and phylogenetics (Figure 4g, Figure S10). A particularly notable discovery is that AarMYC2_1 and AarMYC2_2 are highly likely to act as key genes involved in regulating GT development in A. argyi, and they are probably positive regulators, though this conclusion awaits further experimental validation. As for MYB TF genes, the expression levels of MYB306_1 and MYB306_2 are gradually upregulated with the development of GTs. RT‐qPCR demonstrated that MYB306_2 is highly expressed in the TR sample (Figure S16). Phylogenetically, they are closely related to their orthologous gene AaMIXTA1 (Figure S10). Thus, we speculate that they may also be involved in regulating GT development in A. argyi. More importantly, our results demonstrate that scRNA‐seq technology is a feasible and effective approach for studying the development of plant GTs.

  Terpenoid biosynthesis in plants relies on the MVA and 2‐C‐methyl‐D‐erythritol 4‐phosphate (MEP) pathways, which are generally active across diverse cell types (Vranová et al. 2013). A similar pattern has also been observed in  Gossypium hirsutum , where MVA pathway activity is confined to specialised cell types (Lin et al. 2023). Based on these results, we speculate that there are independent sesquiterpene biosynthetic pathways in the GTs of A. argyi. Nevertheless, we constructed co‐expression networks of sesquiterpene biosynthesis genes specific to different cell types and identified the β‐caryophyllene synthase gene AarTPS77 in the GT‐specific module (Figure 5c, Figure S12). The RT‐qPCR also supported the high expression of this gene in GTs (Figure S16). Derivatives of β‐caryophyllene, the main product of AarTPS77, showed significant accumulation in GTs compared with NGTs (Figure 5a,f). These results collectively reflect the cross‐validation of multi‐omics datasets, reinforcing the reliability of our conclusions. The functional characterisation of AarTPS77 has deepened our understanding of the biosynthesis of specialised secondary metabolites in GTs. The major catalytic product of epidermis‐specific AarTPS52 also showed significant enrichment in GTs, and the possibility of a product transport mechanism cannot be ruled out (Figure 5a,e). Unexpectedly, despite being tandemly duplicated genes, AarTPS95 and TPS96 exhibit distinct expression patterns, and they are also capable of producing monoterpenes, revealing catalytic promiscuity that may underlie the chemical diversity found in A. argyi GTs (Figure 5b, Figure S14). Our results provide insights into the spatial distribution of biosynthetic enzymes involved in the biosynthesis of sesquiterpene, confirming their GT‐specific production in A. argyi leaves. These findings highlight the utility of single‐cell transcriptomics in uncovering spatial regulatory mechanisms of secondary metabolism in non‐model medicinal plants. The identification of cell‐type‐specific metabolic genes and multifunctional TPS enzymes provides both conceptual insight and practical targets for future metabolic engineering aimed at enhancing terpenoid yields.


## Methods and Materials

### Plant Materials
  Leaves of A. argyi were collected from 3‐month‐old field‐grown plants at the IMPLAD botanical garden for GTs and NGTs isolation. To obtain pure and abundant protoplasts, tissue‐cultured seedlings were grown on 1/2 Murashige and Skoog (1/2 MS) medium, and young leaves with specific dimensions of 1–1.5 cm in length and 1 cm in width were subjected to enzymatic digestion and protoplast isolation. The young leaf samples were further used for RNA in situ hybridisation and bulk RNA‐seq. These materials collectively captured a range of developmental stages, supporting comprehensive downstream analysis.


### Isolation and Metabolomic Analysis of GTs and NGTs
  GTs and NGTs from A. argyi leaves were isolated using an optimised protocol involving mechanical disruption, filtration and sucrose gradient centrifugation to achieve high purity. Both GTs and NGTs were immediately flash‐frozen for subsequent metabolomic analysis. Metabolite profiling was conducted using LC–MS and GC–MS platforms. Data were processed and annotated using multiple reference databases, which are mentioned in Methods S1. Statistical analyses, including principal component analysis (PCA), partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS‐DA), and orthogonal PLS‐DA (OPLS‐DA), were performed to evaluate differences in metabolite profiles between GTs and NGTs. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was used to interpret the biological relevance of DAMs. Detailed methodologies are described in Methods S1.


### Protoplast Isolation and scRNA‐Seq Library Construction
  Young leaves from healthy A. argyi tissue‐cultured seedlings were harvested. After removing the main veins, the remaining tissue was finely sliced into strips and incubated in enzymatic digestion solution (0.6 M mannitol, 20 mM MES, 1.5% w/v cellulase R‐10, 0.5% w/v macerozyme R‐10, pH 5.8) at 25°C with gentle shaking (40 rpm) for 2 h. The suspension was filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer. Residual tissue was rinsed with elution buffer (0.6 M mannitol, 20 mM MES, pH 5.8) and passed through another 70 μm cell strainer. The filtrate was centrifuged at 100 × g for 10 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was resuspended in elution buffer, filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer, and centrifuged again at 100 × g for 10 min to obtain a purified protoplast suspension.

  Single‐cell RNA‐sequencing libraries from two biological replicates were prepared using the Chromium Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kits v2 (10X Genomics, Pleasanton, CA, USA), with the protoplast suspension adjusted to ~1000 cells/μL. Library preparation followed the protocol from the manufacturer, including the incorporation of P5 and P7 primers for Illumina bridge amplification. Libraries were quantified using a standard curve (KAPA Biosystems, Woburn, MA, USA) and assessed for quality with an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent Technologies, Loveland, CO, USA). Sequencing was performed on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform using a paired‐end configuration of 26 bp (read 1) × 98 bp (read 2).


### scRNA‐Seq Data Processing
  Raw single‐cell sequencing data in FASTQ format were obtained from the sequencing service provider. Data quality was assessed with FastQC (v0.11.9, https://github.com/s‐andrews/FastQC), visualised using MultiQC (v1.14, https://github.com/MultiQC). Adapter trimming and removal of low‐quality reads were performed using Trim Galore (v0.6.10, https://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore). Cell Ranger (v7.1.0) was used to generate initial quality metrics for the single‐cell dataset. Reads were aligned to the reference A. argyi genome (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gwh/Assembly/30841/show) using the Alevin pipeline (Srivastava et al. 2019). Genome indexing was completed with Salmon (v1.9.0) using the ‐‐keepDuplicates parameter to retain redundant transcripts. The expression matrix was generated via the Alevin tool and imported into R for further processing using the Seurat package (v4.3.0) (Hao et al. 2021).

  Cells were filtered by specific quality thresholds: those with fewer than 800 or more than 6000 expressed genes, UMI counts under 800 or above 30,000, mitochondrial gene expression over 3%, or chloroplast gene expression over 40%. Doublets, which are cells potentially captured together, were identified and removed with DoubletFinder (v2.0.3) (McGinnis et al. 2019). Cell‐cycle scores were calculated using the CellCycleScoring function in Seurat, and cell‐cycle effects were regressed out prior to clustering. Data normalisation was performed using Seurat's SCTransform function, and the top 3000 highly variable genes were selected for downstream analysis. Harmony (v0.1) (https://github.com/immunogenomics/harmony) was used to correct batch effects between samples. Dimensionality reduction was carried out using UMAP (parameters: n.neighbors = 20 L, min.dist = 0.15), and cell clustering was completed using the first 50 principal components with FindNeighbors and FindClusters (resolution = 0.55).

  Cell‐type annotation was performed using two complementary strategies: (1) identifying homologues of known marker genes highly expressed in certain cell types and (2) GO enrichment analysis of cluster‐specific genes to infer cell identity. Known plant cell‐type markers from NCBI and the PlantCellMarker database were used, with homologues of A. argyi genes identified via local BLAST (v2.13.0) for cluster annotation (Jin et al. 2022). Marker gene expression was visualised using Seurat. Subclustering was performed with Seurat's FindSubClusters function, while differential expression analysis across clusters was conducted using FindAllMarkers. Identified DEGs were then used in GO enrichment analysis with TBtools (v2.096) (Chen, Wu, et al. 2023).


### Pseudotime Trajectory Analysis
  Pseudotime trajectory analysis was conducted using Monocle2 (v2.8.0) (Trapnell et al. 2014). Branch‐specific DEGs were identified using the BEAM function with a q‐value threshold of < 1e‐4. Genes showing significant expression dynamics along the pseudotime axis were identified using the differentialGeneTest function, applying a q‐value cutoff of < 0.01. Then, gene expression patterns across pseudotime and developmental branches were visualised using Monocle's plot_genes_branched_heatmap and plot_pseudotime_heatmap functions with default parameters.


### RNA In Situ Hybridisation
  RNA in situ hybridisation was conducted based on a previous study with minor modifications (Sun, Shen, et al. 2023). Young leaves of A. argyi were collected, fixed in FAA solution, dehydrated and embedded in paraffin. Tissue sections (10 μm) were prepared using a Leica sliding microtome (RM2016, Leica Microsystem, Wetzlar, Germany). Sense and antisense RNA probes were synthesised in vitro using the DIG RNA Labeling Kit (Roche, Mannheim, Germany) with SP6 and T7 RNA polymerases. Paraffin sections were dewaxed, rehydrated and hybridised overnight with the DIG‐labelled RNA probe. Hybridisation signals were detected using anti‐digoxigenin antibody conjugated to alkaline phosphatase and visualised with NBT/BCIP chromogenic substrate. Sense probes served as negative controls to confirm signal specificity. Images were acquired using a bright‐field microscope.


### Bulk RNA‐Seq and Data Analysis
  Total RNA was extracted using the RNAprep Pure Plant Plus Kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions, with three biological replicates per sample. RNA‐seq libraries were constructed and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform. Raw reads were aligned to the reference A. argyi genome using Hisat2 (v2.2.1) (Kim et al. 2019). Read counts were generated using Subread (v2.0.6) (Liao et al. 2014), and gene expression levels were calculated as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). Additionally, publicly available transcriptomic data for young leaves (YL) and trichomes (TRL) of A. argyi were retrieved from the NCBI database (Accession: PRJNA945494). Sequencing adapters were trimmed using Trim Galore (https://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore), and the processed reads were aligned to the reference genome.


### Phylogenetic Tree Construction
  Eighteen functionally characterised TPS protein sequences were retrieved from the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org) (see Table S9 for details). These sequences, together with the previously reported sesquiterpene synthase AarTPS34 from A. argyi and the amino acid sequences of TPS candidates cloned in this study, were used to construct a phylogenetic tree. Multiple sequence alignment was conducted using MUSCLE (v5.1) (Edgar 2022), and a maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree was generated with IQ‐TREE (v2.4.3) (Nguyen et al. 2015) using 1000 bootstrap replicates. The final tree was visualised and edited using the Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) online tool (https://itol.embl.de). For TF genes, phylogenetic trees were constructed using the same methods described above.


### Enzyme Activity Assay of TPSs
  For in vitro enzyme activity assays, full‐length TPS gene sequences were cloned into the pET28a(+) expression vector containing a His‐tag and transformed into  E. coli  BL21 (DE3) cells. Protein expression was induced with 0.5 mM isopropyl β‐D‐thiogalactoside (IPTG) at 16°C overnight. Recombinant His‐tagged TPS proteins were purified using the M5 His‐Tagged Protein Purification Kit (Mei5 Biotech, Beijing, China) with an elution buffer containing 500 mM imidazole. Protein concentrations were determined with a BCA Assay Kit (Mei5 Biotech, Beijing, China). Enzyme reactions were assayed in 1 mL of buffer (30 mM HEPES, 5 mM DTT, 25 mM MgCl₂) containing 10 μg of purified protein and 20 μM of FPP or GPP (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The reaction mixtures were incubated at 30°C for 1 h, followed by 45°C for 30 min to collect volatilised‐terpene components using 75 μm DVB/CAR/PDMS fibre (Supelco Inc., Bellefonte, PA, USA). The volatiles were then analysed by GC–MS (7890B‐5977A, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Identification of volatile terpene compounds was carried out using authentic standards or by spectral matching with the NIST20 mass spectral database.
