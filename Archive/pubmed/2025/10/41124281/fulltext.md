# Anti‐Inflammatory, Antioxidant, Molecular Docking and Pharmacokinetics Properties of Cycloeucalenone Isolated From Musa × paradisiaca L. Fruit Peels


## ABSTRACT
  This study isolated a compound with anti‐inflammatory properties from Musa × paradisiaca fruit peels. The methanol extract was partitioned, and the hexane fraction with the best bioactivity was subjected to column chromatography before characterization via Fourier transform infrared (FTIR), UV–visible (UV–VIS) and NMR analysis. The in vivo anti‐inflammatory properties of the methanol extract and hexane fraction were investigated using the formalin‐induced oedema model in rats. The compound's antioxidant, red blood cell (RBC) membrane stabilization and phospholipase‐A2 (PL‐A2) inhibitory effects were evaluated in vitro. The UV–VIS spectrum at 222 nm absorbance and FTIR absorption at 2922, 2855, 1707, 1643, 1453 and 1375 cm−1 with proton and carbon‐13 spectra suggest the compound is a steroid. The in vivo anti‐inflammatory assay showed significant oedema reduction of the hexane fraction at 200 mg/kg. The compound also displayed Fe3+ reduction and scavenged free radical molecules while showing PL‐A2 inhibition and the ability to limit RBC haemolysis. The compound also presented stronger affinities for PL‐A2 (−7.6 kcal/mol) and NF‐κB (−6.0 kcal/mol) than indomethacin and ibuprofen drugs, with a favourable pharmacokinetic model. Though M. × paradisiaca fruit peels are often discarded as waste, our study highlights a sustainable approach to the reutilization of this plant material as a source of a lead molecule for inflammatory disease management.


## Introduction
  Plants and their extracts have been employed for medicinal purposes since ancient times, primarily due to their chemical composition. Phytochemicals are naturally occurring organic compounds in plants that show promise as agents for treating various diseases with beneficial effects [1]. According to Chavda et al. [2], inflammation has been described as the leading cause of nearly all diseases. This condition has been implicated in the development of diseases, such as chronic asthma, rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, psoriasis and multiple sclerosis [3, 4]. Among the contributing factors to inflammation development is oxidative stress, which arises due to an imbalance between the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the body's ability to counteract or detoxify them. However, when ROS production exceeds the capacity of the cells’ antioxidant systems, oxidative stress occurs, leading to potential damage to lipids, proteins and DNA macromolecules [5]. Clinically approved drugs, such as diclofenac, ibuprofen and aspirin, are commercially available for managing inflammation‐related diseases. Despite the effectiveness of these drugs, their use in therapeutic medicine has been associated with several unwanted side effects. This situation has necessitated the exploration of medicinal plants as an alternative therapeutic regime. Consequently, several plant species, such as Curcuma longa L., Zingiber officinale Rosc., Rosmarinus officinalis L. and Borago officinalis L., commonly consumed, have been discovered to have substantial anti‐inflammatory therapeutic effects credited to the intrinsic phytochemicals [6, 7].

  Musa × paradisiaca is an herbaceous perennial plant of the Musaceae family which originates as a hybrid between Musa acuminata Colla and Musa balbisiana Colla [8]. Although the plant is commonly known as plantain or cooking banana, in Nigeria, the Yoruba, Igbo and Hausa populations in the country refer to it as Ogede‐Agbagba, Jioko or Agada, respectively (https://dibanduherbals.com/musa‐paradisiaca/). M. × paradisiaca is distributed in the tropical and subtropical regions of Southeast Asia (Malaysia, Indonesia), Central/South America (Colombia, Brazil) and Africa (Nigeria, Uganda) [8]. The plant produces huge, brightly coloured paddle‐shaped leaves that grow to about 8 m in length. It bears yellow flowers (1–2 m long) with reddish‐purple bracts. According to the information on the Indianagriexam online database (https://indianagriexam.com/courses/jrf‐horticulture‐2/lesson/cultivation‐of‐banana‐musa‐paradisiaca‐l/), once planted around the June–July period of a particular year, the plant produces flowers around the same time the following year, before it finally matures after 12–15 months from the planting date. Moreover, the plant has elongated and curved green fruits about 15–30 cm long, which often turn yellow when ripe [9]. At the end of the fruiting stage, the plant pseudo‐stem dies, and new shoots develop to form new pseudo‐stems.

  Different parts of M. × paradisiaca are used in traditional medicine to treat and manage various diseases, including epilepsy, leprosy, ulcers, infections, diabetes, swelling and diarrhoea, as reported by Odion et al. [10]. In a previous report by Galani [11], M. × paradisiaca contains several bioactive constituents such as campesterol, stigmasterol, anthocyanins, phenolic acids, glycosides and polyphenolic compounds. Moreover, significant antimicrobial, anti‐ulcer, antioxidant, antidiabetic, anticancer, antidiarrheal, hepatoprotective and wound‐healing pharmacological activities of various parts of the plant have been reported by some studies [12, 13]. Though fruits are the major edible part of the plant, the fruit peels, though often discarded, have been shown to possess important pharmacological properties. In a study by Nnebechukwu et al. [14], the M. × paradisiaca peel ethanol extract showed antifungal activities. Despite these and other biological activities attributed to the M. × paradisiaca fruit peel, there is a need to identify the possible specific compounds that could be responsible for the bioactivity of this plant fruit peel. Hence, we reported for the first time the isolation and pharmacological analysis of the anti‐inflammatory compound from M. × paradisiaca fruit peel using in vitro, in vivo and in silico study models.


## Materials and Methods

### Preparation and Extraction of Plant Material
  Fresh unripe M. × paradisiaca fruits (Figure 1) were purchased from local farmers at Eke Agbani market in Nkanu West of Enugu State, Nigeria, on February 12 2024. The plant material was identified and authenticated by Dr. Uzor G., a botanist in charge of the herbarium unit of the Department of Pharmacognosy, Enugu State University of Science and Technology (ESUT), Agbani, where the voucher specimen (Esut/pcg/0107) was deposited. The fruits were washed with clean tap water before removing the peels and cutting them into tiny pieces. After shade‐drying until constant weight, the dried peels were pulverized into coarse powder. Weighed quantity of the powdered plant material of 500 g was macerated (cold) in 3.5 L of methanol for 72 h. After passing the extracted solution through a cheesecloth, the clear solution obtained was filtered again with cotton wool plugs placed in a funnel. Subsequently, the filtrate was concentrated using a rotary evaporator under reduced pressure before the resulting samples were exposed to open air in the laboratory for several days to allow complete evaporation of the remnant solvent.


### Purification and Isolation of the Compound From M. × Paradisiaca Fruit Peel n‐Hexane Fraction
  The methanol extract (16 g) obtained after extraction was made soluble with 100 mL of 5% methanol–water before sequential partitioning with n‐hexane (10 × 100 mL), ethyl acetate (4 × 100 mL) and butanol (2 × 100 mL). The hexane fraction had the highest yield among the recovered dried fraction samples and was subjected to further purification. The 5 g of the n‐hexane fraction sample was dissolved in 5 mL of n‐hexane, mixed with 10 g of silica gel and dried on paper towels. After packing the dried mixture into a glass column (3 cm by 30 cm), more 200–400 mesh‐sized silica gel powder was added. The column was eluted with a solvent mixture of 500 mL n‐hexane:ethyl acetate ratio (9:1, 7:3, 5:5, 3:7, 1:9) to afford 76 sub‐fractions of 5 mL each. The TLC of the 76 sub‐fractions indicated similar R f, which were combined to afford F1–4, F5–26 and F27–76. The sub‐fractions F5–26 showed prominent bands and were made on a precoated Prep‐TLC plate and developed with a solvent ratio of 9:1 (n‐hexane:ethyl acetate). The bands were recovered by scraping from the TLC plate and then transferred to a beaker containing 100 mL hexane:ethyl acetate solvent (1:1). After 10 min, the mixture was filtered, and the recovered filtrate was evaporated to give an isolated white compound (22 mg). The schematic diagram of the isolation process is shown in Figure 2.


### UV–Visible (UV–VIS) Spectroscopic Analysis of the Isolated Compound
  UV–VIS spectroscopic analysis of the hexane fraction 1 (HF1) isolate was conducted at room temperature in a UV–VIS spectrophotometer (Perkin Elmer, USA Model: Lambda 950) with a slit width of 2 and 10 mm cells. The HF1 was dissolved in chloroform before centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min and filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper. The sample was diluted to 1:10 with the same solvent and scanned under UV–VIS light in the wavelength ranging from 200 to 800 nm.


### Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) Spectroscopic Analysis of the Isolated Compound
  FTIR was used to identify the characteristic functional groups in the isolated compound. A small quantity of HF1 was mixed with dry potassium bromide (KBr). The mixture was thoroughly mixed in a mortar and pressed at a pressure of 6 bars within 2 min to form a KBr thin disc. Then the disc was placed in a sample cup of a diffuse reflectance accessory. The IR spectrum was obtained using Bruker, Germany, Vertex 70 infrared spectrometer. The sample was scanned from 4000 to 400 cm−1.


### NMR Analysis of the Isolated Compound
  The NMR analysis of the isolated compound was recorded on a Bruker MW 500 spectrometer at 500 and 125.62 MHz for proton and carbon‐13 NMR, respectively. The sample was dissolved in deuterated chloroform and tested in a standard 5 mm diameter NMR tube at room temperature. Chemical shifts (δ) were reported in parts per million (ppm) relative to the internal standard tetramethylsilane (TMS, δ = 0).


### In Vivo Formalin‐Induced Chronic Inflammation Model
  Albino rats (25 in number) weighing between 140 and 208 g for the in vivo anti‐inflammatory experiment were sourced from the animal house of the Department of Pharmacology, ESUT, Agbani, Nigeria. The handling of the animals and treatment administration were according to the guidelines of the ESUT animal research ethics committees, with Approval no. ACEEE‐1870. After 2 weeks of acclimatization, the rats were divided randomly into separate cages of five rats each in five groups. The paw volume of each animal was measured before the treatment to establish their basal volume. Group 1 (negative control) received 1 mL of distilled water orally, whereas those in Groups 2 and 3 obtained 200 and 400 mg/kg of fruit peel methanol crude extract via the same route of administration. Animals in Group 4 were given a similar volume of 200 mg/kg hexane fraction, and those in Group 5 (positive control) received 400 mg/kg ibuprofen. Induction was conducted on Day 1 after the beginning of the treatment by injecting the rats’ sub‐plantar tissue with 50 µL saline‐buffered formalin according to the Kumar and Jain [15] protocol with modification. After a second induction on Day 4, paw volume was measured daily as the treatment continued till the 11th day. The percentage inhibition of the oedema was calculated using the following expression:  Percentage inhibition of inflammation%=MCV−MTVMCV×100 where MCV is the mean control volume; MTV is the mean treated volume.


### In Vitro Membrane Stabilization Model
  Blood sample (3 mL) was obtained from the rats in the control group via cardiac puncture into ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) bottles. The blood sample was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min before washing three times with an equal volume of normal saline. The blood volume was measured and reconstituted as a 40% (v/v) suspension with normal saline. The isolated compound and indomethacin to be used were dissolved in distilled water, which served as the hypotonic solution. An aliquot (1 mL) of varying concentrations of the isolated compound (0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8 and 1.0 mg/mL) was added into each of a set of five test tubes. Five other tubes containing 1 mL of 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8 and 1.0 mg/mL indomethacin, respectively. The contents of the respective tubes were made up to 4.9 mL with the vehicle (distilled water). Two control tubes were used for this test. One control tube contains 4.9 mL of the vehicle, whereas another contains 4.9 mL of normal saline (isotonic solution). The red blood cells’ (RBCs) suspension (0.1 mL) was added to each tube, and after gentle mixing, the mixtures were incubated for 1 h at room temperature (37°C). After incubation, the reaction mixture for each tube was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min, and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 418 nm using a spectrophotometer. The tests were carried out in triplicate. Reaction media containing 1 mL of varying concentrations of isolated compound and indomethacin, made up to 5.0 mL with normal saline, without HRBCs suspension, were used as the respective blank for each test. The blank for the control tubes contains normal saline, also without RBC suspension. The percentage inhibition of haemolysis was calculated using the relation below:  %Inhibition of haemolysis=1−OD2−OD1/OD3−OD1×100 where OD1 is the absorbance of control I (isotonic solution), OD2 is the absorbance of test sample and OD3 is the absorbance of control II (hypotonic solution).


### In Vitro Phospholipase‐A2 (PL‐A2) Model
  Fresh rat blood samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant (plasma) was discarded. The red cells were washed three times with an equal volume of normal saline, measured and reconstituted as a 40% (v/v) suspension with phosphate‐buffered saline. This served as the substrate for PL‐A2. A preparation of the fungal enzyme was made using an Aspergillus niger strain culture. A nutrient broth was used to cultivate A. niger for 72 h at room temperature. The culture was put to the test with 3 mL of phosphate‐buffered saline and centrifuged for 10 min at 3000 rpm. The pellet was made up of fungal cells, and the supernatant was used to make a crude enzyme preparation. Then 0.2 mL of RBC, 0.2 mL of CaCl2, 0.2 mL of crude enzyme preparation and various concentrations of normal saline and the isolated compound (0.2–1.0 mg/mL) were incubated at 37°C for 1 h. CaCl2 (2 mM) (0.2 mL), animal RBC (0.2 mL), 0.2 mL of the crude enzyme preparation and varying concentrations of normal saline, the isolated compound and the reference drug were incubated in test tubes for 1 h. The control contained the RBC suspension, CaCl2 and free enzyme. The blanks were treated with 0.2 mL of boiled enzyme separately. The incubation reaction mixtures were centrifuged at a speed of 3000 rpm for 10 min. Samples of the supernatant (1.5 mL) were diluted with 10 mL of normal saline, and the absorbance of the solutions was read at 418 nm. Indomethacin, a known inhibitor of PL‐A2, was used as the reference drug. The percentage maximum enzyme activity and percentage inhibition were calculated using the following relation:  %Maximumenzymeactivity=(ODoftest/ODofcontrol)×100   %Inhibition=100−%Maximumenzymeactivity


### In Vitro Free Radical Scavenging Model
  One millilitre of 0.135 mM DPPH in methanol was prepared and mixed with 1.0 mL of various concentrations (0.2–1.0 mg/mL) of the isolated compound or vitamin C, used as the standard. The reaction mixture was vortexed thoroughly and left in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. The absorbance of the mixture was measured spectrophotometrically at 517 nm. The ability of the plant extract to scavenge DPPH radical was calculated using the following equation:  DPPH radical scavenging activity%=Abs control−Abs sample/Abs control×100

  Abs control is the absorbance of DPPH radical + methanol; Abs sample is the absorbance of DPPH radical + sample extract or standard (vitamin C).


### In Vitro Ferric Reduction Model
  The ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) analysis was determined using the potassium ferricyanide–ferric chloride method. The isolated compound (100 µL aliquots) was added to 2.5 mL of phosphate buffer (0.2 M, pH 6.6) and 2.5 mL of potassium ferricyanide (1%). After 20 min of incubation at 50°C of the mixtures, 2.5 mL of trichloroacetic acid (10%) was added. From the mixture, 2.5 mL was taken and again mixed with 2.5 mL of water and 0.5 mL of 1% FeCl3. The absorbance of the mixture was measured at 593 nm after 30 min of allowing the solution to stand; each experiment was carried out in triplicate. The standard used was Vitamin C, and the FRAP was calculated using the following formula:  FRAP activity=absorbance of sample/slope of the ascorbic acidstandard curve×dilution factor.


### Cycloeucalenone Pharmacological Property Prediction
  The computational prediction of the pharmacological descriptors of the cycloeucalenone isolated compound vis‐à‐vis its distribution, excretion, absorption and metabolism was determined using the SwissADME computational model described by Daina et al. [16]. Before running the virtual simulation, the canonical SMILES formular of the compound was initially retrieved from the PubChem database at https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/21594790#section=SMILES. The chemical formular was then imported to the SwissADME website to further determine the compound's pharmacokinetic properties, lipophilicity, number of rotatable bonds and topological polar surface area (TPSA). Moreover, the possible capacity of the compound to pass through the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and inhibit p‐glycoprotein and various key cytochrome enzymes vital to drug metabolism was also determined. The medicinal properties of the compound, measured by its bioavailability score, leadlikeness violations and synthetic accessibility, were also calculated.


### Cycloeucalenone Molecular Docking With the Inflammatory Protein
  This analysis was carried out to investigate the binding affinity and molecular interaction of key proteins linked with the progression of inflammatory diseases. The selected proteins include PL‐A2, tumour necrosis factor‐α converting enzyme (TACE) and nuclear factor kappa‐light‐chain‐enhancer of activated B cells (NF‐κB). The crystallographic structures of the proteins with identities 5Y5E, 3LGP and 8TQD, respectively, were obtained from the Protein Data Bank. The binding pocket of each protein was determined using the CASTp website (http://sts.bioe.uic.edu/castp/calculation.html) before uploading into PyRx virtual screening software, before conversion to macromolecules with the AutoDock tool of the programme. Cycloeucalenone with standard drugs, including ibuprofen and indomethacin, was initially retrieved from the PubChem website before it was imported into the PyRx application using the Open Babel tab. After energy minimization, the compounds were made ligands via conversion into a pdbqt file format. Docking was performed using AutoDock Vina [17] in a grid box centred around the active site of the proteins. The 2D images of the most stable protein–ligand complex scores with the lowest binding energy output were viewed with the BIOVIA Discovery Studio.


### Statistical Analysis
  Experimental data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. After data processing with Microsoft Excel software, statistical analysis to compare between treatment groups at each concentration was conducted using GraphPad Prism (V6) Student t‐test. The significance of the results obtained was determined at a 95% confidence level.


## Results and Discussions

### Phytoconstituents Present in the Extract and Fractions of the M. × paradisiaca Fruit Peel
  Plants produce a myriad of innate chemical molecules broadly classified as primary or secondary metabolites that assist them in performing various functions. Some findings indicate that phytosterol improves plants’ adaptation to environmental stress by modulating their membrane fluidity. Other phytoconstituents known collectively as tannins have also been described to protect plants against animal herbivory [18, 19]. The results of the phytochemical screening in this study revealed that the crude methanol extract and the various fractions of the M. × paradisiaca fruit peel contained rich bioactive metabolites (Table 1). Although terpenoids, steroids, flavonoids and tannins were detected in the crude extract and the ethyl acetate fraction, the hexane fraction only showed the presence of terpenoids and steroids. Furthermore, results from phytochemical analysis suggest the presence of flavonoids, tannins and terpenoids in the more polar butanol fraction (Table 1). Though Uzairu and Kano [20] reported the presence of alkaloids, in line with our findings, these secondary metabolites and saponins were not detected in the fruit peel material in other similar previous studies [21, 22]. The presence of these classes of bioactive compounds could be responsible for the antimicrobial, anticancer and anti‐ulcer properties associated with the fruit peel, thus validating its folkloric use in traditional medicine [14, 23, 24, 25, 26].


### Elucidation of the Chemical Structure of the Isolated Compound
  The results obtained in Table 2 and Figures 3 and 4 showed the outcome of the analysis to identify the white crystalline lipophilic compound recovered for the isolation procedure. The UV–VIS spectrophotometer data in Figure 4A showed that the compound gave a maximum absorbance in the wavelength region of 222 nm. The FTIR spectrum as presented in Figure 4B further displayed key functional groups such as alkane (C─H) stretches at 2922 cm−1, carbonyl (C═O) stretches at 1707 cm−1, and double bond (C═C) stretches at 1643 cm−1, which are hallmarks of steroidal compounds. The 1H NMR and 13C NMR of the isolated compound in Table 2 indicated the presence of 6 methyl protons, 12 methylene protons, 6 methine protons and 6 quaternary carbons. Prominent signals are observed at 213.8 and 156.89 ppm for carbonyl and alkene functionality at carbon positions 3 and 24, respectively. Moreover, the chemical shift at 4.70 ppm corresponds to the olefinic proton at carbon position 28. When this spectral data is compared with that obtained from Akacha and Ndyakalah's [27] previous investigation, the compound isolated from the M. × paradisiaca unripe fruit peel is cycloeucalenone (Figure 3). Interestingly, this compound, a steroidal triterpenoid with a cycloartane skeletal backbone, has been identified in some other plant species [28, 29, 30, 31].


### Pharmacological Assay

#### Anti‐Inflammatory Potential of the Isolated Compound
  Under physiological conditions, inflammatory processes are vital for the repair of damaged cells or tissue in the body [32]. However, excessive release of biochemical signalling molecules under chronic inflammation has been linked to the onset of many diseases, including cancer, Type 2 diabetes and Alzheimer's disease [2, 33, 34]. The result of the in vivo chronic inflammation experiment carried out in this study showed elevation of formalin‐induced oedema on Day 1 (Figure 5). On the administration of different concentrations of treatment samples, the oedema decreased drastically on Day 4. Upon the second induction, this inflammatory index further increased. However, on Day 11, there were significant oedema reductions in all treated experimental groups compared to the negative control. Towards the last 2 days of this intervention, there were no significant differences in the tested parameter for the hexane fraction (200 mg/kg) experimental group and the positive control (Ibuprofen 400 mg/kg). These findings showed that the bioactive metabolites in the M. × paradisiaca fruit peel possess anti‐inflammatory properties. More importantly, the compound isolated in the hexane fraction might be linked with this superior anti‐inflammatory activity, compared with the standard therapeutic drug and other crude samples.

  Further in vitro studies were conducted to confirm the anti‐inflammatory activities of the compound isolated from the hexane fraction. The result of this experiment, as presented in Figure 6A, indicates that the isolated compound significantly (p < 0.05) inhibited the lysis of the animal erythrocyte membrane in a concentration‐dependent manner. The isolated compound had 40% and 68% inhibition at 0.2 and 1.0 mg/mL, respective concentrations, with a 0.36 mg/mL overall IC50 value as compared to 0.28 mg/mL of the positive standard (indomethacin). The activity of the fruit peel compound on PL‐A2, as displayed in Figure 6B, also revealed a dose‐dependent inhibitory effect. The isolated compound inhibited PL‐A2 activity by 33% at 0.2 mg/mL and 74% at 1.0 mg/mL. Studies have shown that PL‐A2 released from vascular endothelium during tissue damage mediates the release of arachidonic acid, a signalling molecule that attracts the recruitment of immune cells [35]. Phospholipids are major constituents of the RBC membrane. Because PL‐A2 hydrolyses the ester bond at Position 2 of phospholipids, increased activities of this enzyme have been associated with elevated RBC haemolysis in sickle cell patients [36]. The observed significant reduction in oedema in the hexane fraction treatment group (Figure 5) may be linked to the ability of the cycloeucalenone in the fraction to suppress RBC haemolysis and enhance PL‐A2 inhibition in the experimental group animals. In support of our findings, other works have reported the anti‐inflammatory activity of different Musa species plant parts in various inflammatory models [37]. In the study by Batista et al. [38], where trinitrobenzenesulfonic acid given via the intracolonic route was employed to induce intestinal inflammation in male rats, the administration of Musa spp. AAA (dwarf Cavendish banana) supplemented food diet (10%) for 28 days significantly lowered levels of pro‐inflammatory biomarkers responsible for colonic damage in experimental animals. In another in vivo investigation by Apriasari et al. [39], topical administration of gels made from M. acuminata stem extract decreased the expression levels of tumour necrotic factor‐alpha (TNF‐α) and NF‐κB in mucosal injury tissue in all treatment groups (25%, 37.5% and 50%) when compared with the untreated wounded control.


#### Antioxidant Potential of Isolated Compound
  Oxidative stress arises due to disproportion imbalance between pro‐oxidants and antioxidants, which can produce excessive ROS in the body. These ROS damage essential biomolecules, such as lipids, proteins and DNA, thus disrupting the body's homeostasis and exposing it to various disease conditions [40]. With oxidative stress linked with the development of certain inflammatory disorders [41], antioxidants capable of quenching the imbalance mediated by free radical species may help stall the progression of the latter physiological condition. Our study indicated that the isolated compound had Fe3+ reducing property with the capacity to donate hydrogen atoms to stable DPPH free radicals (Figure 7). The compound DPPH radical scavenging activity in Figure 7A ranged from 2% to 24% at 0.3125 to 2.5 mg/mL concentrations when compared with ascorbic acid, which had about 64% inhibition at 2.5 mg/mL. Though the IC50 of the compound in Figure 7B was >3.50 mg/mL, its reducing power at 0.3125–2.5 mg/mL was about 25%–51%. The presence of this compound and others could be associated with the antioxidant activities documented in other Musa species using DPPH and ABTS in vitro models [11, 42]. Aside from the electron available from the pi‐bond between C24 and C28 of the cycloeucalenone (Figure 3), the oxygen bonded to C3 also has additional free electrons. Interestingly, Wojtunik‐Kulesza et al. [43] have linked the ability to donate these electrons to unstable free radicals to the antioxidant capacity of some terpene molecules. Indeed, this structural–activity relationship could explain the antioxidant properties of the isolated compound as observed in this study.


### Computational Studies

#### Molecular Docking Analysis of Isolated Compound With Inflammatory Protein Targets

##### Molecular Docking Computational Model
  In addition to in vitro and in vivo experimental approaches, analysis via in silico methodology has emerged as a widely used tool in drug discovery. One such computational method is molecular docking, which is commonly employed to predict how small molecules synthesized chemically or derived from plants interact with protein crystallographic structures [44, 45]. Information obtained from this computer‐aided investigative model has assisted researchers in identifying potential druggable compounds, thus saving cost and time that could have been spent on the other experimental models mentioned previously. Though the fruit peel isolated compound in Table 3 showed a lesser binding affinity (−6.0 kcal/mol) for TACE than ibuprofen (−8.1 kcal/mol) and indomethacin (−7.7 kcal/mol), it formed stronger binding energies with NF‐κB and PL‐A2. The 2D images in Figure 8 revealed that cycloeucalenone interacted mainly via van der Waals forces in addition to hydrogen bonds, carbon–hydrogen bonds and pi–pi stacking. Besides the amino acid involved in van der Waals forces formation, the compound interacted with CYS 25 of PL‐A2 (Figure 8A), THR106 and TYR20 of NF‐κB (Figure 8D), as well as LEU133 and TYR135 of TACE. TACE converts TNF‐α from an inactive to an active form that could interact with its downstream receptors. When TNF‐α binds to its receptors, this triggers the release of the NF‐κB inhibitor. Then, the NF‐κB in the cytosol enters the nucleus and elevates the gene expression of other signalling molecules such as cytokines, further enhancing the inflammatory process by increasing neutrophil and macrophage infiltration [46]. Consequently, TACE and NF‐κB inhibitors could play therapeutic roles by lowering circulating TNF‐α levels and other acute inflammation mediators. In this current investigation, the inhibition of the PL‐A2 and the anti‐inflammatory effect of the hexane fraction in the oedema animal model may also be linked to the modulatory effect of the cycloeucalenone in the plant sample on these other docked inflammatory biomarkers.


#### Pharmacological Properties
  The efficacy of a drug molecule is determined by its adsorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion in the body. The SwissADME website (http://www.swissadme.ch/) possesses a computational tool that could predict the pharmacokinetics and physicochemical properties of chemical molecules in relation to their medicinal chemistry properties and druglikeness. Information about cycloeucalenone in Table 4 revealed that the compound, which cannot permeate the BBB, had no inhibitory effect on cytochrome (CYP) enzymes key to drug metabolism, despite having low gastrointestinal absorption. Some reports show that CYP3A4 and CYP2D6 enzyme involvement are linked to the metabolism of about 50% of all drugs detoxified in the liver [47]. The fact that the data from this study showed that cycloeucalenone from M. × paradisiaca peel does not inhibit these enzymes further supports the safe medicinal application of fruit peel in other disease management [48]. With 0.55 and 6.18 bioavailability and synthetic accessibility scores, respectively, the result suggests that the compound did not violate the Veber rule despite having a single violation of the Egan and Lipinski rules. These latter rules, also referred to as the Lipinski rule of five, indicate that a druggable compound must have ≤10 H‐bond acceptors, ≤5 H‐bond donors, Log P < 5, and molecular weight <500 Da and have been described as being the best criteria for screening for chemicals with potential application as a therapeutic drug [49].


## Conclusion
  Given that people only consume M. × paradisiaca fruit and discard the peel as waste, our study highlights a sustainable approach to the reutilization of the waste while opening new avenues for developing plant‐based antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory therapies that could potentially replace or complement synthetic drugs currently used to manage oxidative stress and inflammatory disorders. Furthermore, our work revealed that the isolated cycloeucalenone inhibits inflammatory mediators such as PL‐A2 (IC50 = 0.42 mg/mL) and showed reasonable antioxidant activities that could help mitigate ROS reactions leading to inflammatory disorders. The molecular docking result also supported the observed activity, as the cyloeucalenone had strong binding affinity (−7.6 kcal/mol) and a good pharmacokinetic profile, reflecting its suitability as a druggable compound. This is the first report on anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant activities of cycloeucalenone. However, we recommend formulating and developing cyloeucalenone as a potential agent for managing anti‐inflammation and its related diseases.
