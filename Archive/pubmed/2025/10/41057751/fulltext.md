# Cell-free DNA–based inference of the activities of 370 + transcription factors mirrors their activities in tumors


## Background
  Cell-free DNA (cfDNA) coverage patterns are emerging as potential low-invasive approaches to determine transcription factor (TF) activation status in tumors; however, the accuracy of this approach has been confirmed for only a few TFs. Here, using paired tumor/blood samples collected from two xenograft tumor models of human liver cancer (HepG2, gain-of-function mutation in CTNNB1; HuH7, wild type), we compared the accuracy of inferences of TF activity made using tumor-derived ATAC-seq data and plasma-derived cfDNA whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data.


## Results
  ATAC-seq and cfDNA-WGS data from the HepG2 model both showed higher activation of two downstream target TFs of CTNNB1 in the Wnt signaling pathway compared with the HuH7 model. Expanding the study to 377 TFs, each with 10,000 TF-binding sites, revealed significant concordance between the data sets for both models (Spearman’s ρ: HepG2, − 0.90; HuH7, − 0.85). To elucidate potential factors that may influence the accuracy of cfDNA-based inferences of TF activity in the clinic, we prepared simulation samples by mixing tumor-derived cfDNA with healthy-donor cfDNA; we found that a minimum of 5 × sequencing coverage and a tumor fraction of at least 3% were required to obtain accurate inferences of tumor type–specific TF activity.


## Conclusions
  cfDNA-based TF activity inference is applicable to more than 370 TFs and can be used to accurately estimate tumor-specific TF activities. We believe that this study supports a fundamental principle that will be useful for future investigations of the potential of cfDNA analysis.

## Background
  Transcription—conversion of genetic information from DNA to RNA—involves RNA polymerases and transcription factors (TFs). General TFs are required for transcription initiation by RNA polymerases; they bind to the initiation sites in gene promoter regions [1]. Specific TFs regulate transcription under certain conditions; they bind to regulatory DNA sequences known as enhancers or silencers [2, 3]. In the oncology field, more than 20% of driver or tumor suppressor genes encode TFs [4]. For instance, one of the most frequently mutated tumor suppressor genes, TP53, encodes a protein that acts as a TF [5]. In addition to genetic alterations in TFs themselves, driver gene mutations can activate downstream TFs, promoting cancer growth and survival. For example, the TCF/LEF family TFs can be activated by gain-of-function mutations in beta-catenin 1 (CTNNB1), leading to increased cell proliferation and tumorigenesis [6]. Thus, TFs play important roles in cancer biology, and understanding their activation status in tumor cells remains important for anti-cancer drug discovery and development [7].

  The Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq), a technique to assess genome-wide chromatin accessibility, has been widely applied to the study of the gene regulatory landscape, including to the identification of the open chromatin regions where TFs bind and gene transcription begins [8, 9]. The ATAC-seq assay uses a mutant hyperactive Tn5 transposase to probe for open chromatin regions, and once an open region is found, Tn5 cleaves the double-stranded DNA and inserts sequencing adaptors. The tagged DNA fragments can then be purified, sequenced, and counted to reveal how open the chromatin is across the genome [8]. Recently, The Cancer Genome Atlas program (TCGA) has generated ATAC-seq data for 23 different tumor types and identified cancer- and tissue-specific elements enriched with TF-binding motifs [10]. These data suggest the potential use of chromatin accessibility profiling for tumor-subtype classification.

  Cell-free DNA (cfDNA) are DNA fragments that are released from dead cells into body fluids such as the blood. In healthy individuals, cfDNA in the plasma has been shown to originate mainly from hematopoietic cells owing to their high turnover rate [11]. However, in cancer patients, the plasma also contains cfDNA derived from dead tumor cells, known as circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) [12]. Repeated ctDNA measurement and analysis from the same individual can reveal longitudinal tumor mutational status changes during tumor development and treatment without the need for multiple invasive tumor biopsies [13]. Thus, ctDNA mutation analysis has emerged as an important tool for exploratory biomarker research and biomarker-guided treatment selection [14–16].

  Recent studies have also illustrated the potential of using what are known as “cfDNA coverage patterns” to elucidate the open/closed status of chromatin [17, 18]. Nucleosomes and TFs protect the cfDNA from digestion by DNases in blood, and sequencing of the protected fragments can generate a coverage pattern that reflects the nucleosome and TF positions. In contrast, open chromatin regions are not protected from digestion, resulting in loss of sequencing coverage. Consequently, cfDNA coverage pattern analysis near transcription factor binding sites (TFBS) and transcription start sites (TSS) has been reported to enable inference of the TF activity and gene expression, respectively, in tumors [18, 19]. Furthermore, several studies have used cfDNA to show that tumor subtype–specific differences in TF activity exist [20, 21]. For example, using cfDNA whole-genome sequencing (WGS), it has been shown that metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer and neuroendocrine prostate cancer exhibit different androgen receptor (AR) TFBS coverage, which matches the biological differences observed in these cancer subtypes [21]. Also, a longitudinal analysis using cfDNA collected from a metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer patient treated with AR inhibitor showed a gradual decrease in the activity of AR during the course of treatment, but an increase in prostate-specific antigen, a tumor marker of prostate cancer [21]. These results indicate that cfDNA could potentially be used to monitor the change in tumor dependency on AR.

  Given the low-invasive nature of ctDNA analysis compared with traditional biopsy-based approaches, the implications for clinical research and precision medicine are profound. However, it remains unclear whether the activities of TFs other than AR in other tumors can also be inferred from cfDNA-WGS data. Also, further evaluation of this technology’s potential for inferring the TF activity from cfDNA-WGS data is needed. For example, it has been reported that the number of TFBSs available for a TF, the average sequencing depth, and the tumor fraction (amount of ctDNA as a fraction of the total circulating cfDNA) all are capable of affecting the accuracy of cancer-subtype classification using cfDNA [19], implying that these factors also likely affect the accuracy of inferences of TF activities. Therefore, when considering the application of cfDNA approaches in the clinic, it will be necessary to evaluate how these factors affect the accuracy of inferences of TF activity. In this study, we explored the extent to which TF activation status inferred from plasma-derived cfDNA-WGS data reflects that inferred from tumor-derived ATAC-seq data, as well as the effects of the aforementioned factors on the accuracy of such inferences.


## Results

### Overall study design
  TCGA has estimated that mutations in CTNNB1 occur in 27% of hepatocellular carcinomas [22]. These mutations mostly induce aberrant expression of downstream target genes via activation of TCF/LEF family TFs [6]. Based on these reports, we used two human liver cancer cell lines: HepG2 cells, which harbor a gain-of-function mutation in CTNNB1, and HuH7 cells, which harbor wild-type CTNNB1 [23]. Consistent with their mutation status, HepG2 cells are reported to have higher TCF/LEF TF activation than do HuH7 cells in vitro [23].

  The present study was split into two steps (Fig. 1). In step 1, mouse xenograft models were established using the two cell lines. Once the tumors had reached a sufficient size, samples of tumor and plasma were collected from the mice; the tumor samples were subjected to ATAC-seq and RNA-seq, and the plasma samples were pooled and subjected to cfDNA-WGS. Pure human reads were then extracted from the sequencing data. In step 2, using the human read data, we compared inferences of TF activation status made using the different data sets. First, we compared inferences of the activation status of two TCF/LEF family TFs made using the plasma-derived cfDNA-WGS and tumor-derived ATAC-seq data (Step 2.1). Then, we expanded the analysis to target 377 TFs, each with over 10,000 TFBSs (Step 2.2). Finally, we evaluated the impacts of three factors that could potentially affect the accuracy of cfDNA-based inference of TF activation status in the clinic: cfDNA sequencing depth, the number of TFBS for each TF, and tumor fraction (Step 2.3).Fig. 1Overall study design. ### Inference of the activation status of two downstream target TFs of the Wnt signaling pathway
  First, we tested whether TCF/LEF family TF activity is higher in HepG2 cells than in HuH7 cells in vivo, as it is in vitro [23]. Multiple studies have indicated that among the TCF/LEF family members TCF7 and TCF7L2 show higher expression than LEF1 and TCFL1 and play greater roles in Wnt signaling activation in HepG2 cells than in Huh7 cells [23, 24]. In line with this, TCF7 and TCF7L2 were found to be expressed at higher levels than LEF1 and TCF7L1 in both xenograft models (Fig. 2A). Based on this finding, we decided to use TCF7 and TCF7L2 as representative target TFs activated by Wnt signaling in both models. The ATAC-seq data were processed, and the data quality was confirmed by examining the insert size distribution, which exhibited nucleosome phasing with a periodicity of approximately 167 bp (Fig. 2B). The quality was also confirmed by determining Spearman’s correlation coefficients of coverages between samples, mapping rates, total number of peaks, and fraction of reads in peaks (FRiP) scores (Fig. S1). As expected from the CTNNB1 mutation status of the two tumors and the results of previous in vitro studies [23, 24], we confirmed that TCF7 and TCF7L2 showed greater activation in HepG2 tumor than in HuH7 tumor in vivo (Fig. 2C). Consistent with the ATAC-seq data, the expression of AXIN2, a downstream target gene of the Wnt signal [25], and the WNT gene signature score, computed from the expression of 13 other downstream target genes in the Wnt signaling, were higher in HepG2 tumor than in HuH7 tumor (Fig. 2D) [26].   Next, to evaluate the potential of inferring the activation status of TCF7 and TCF7L2 from cfDNA sequencing data, we pooled the plasma from each xenograft mouse model, extracted the cfDNA from the pooled samples, and performed WGS. From the cfDNA-WGS data, we subtracted mouse sequenced reads and confirmed that the human fraction was 50% and 30% for the HepG2 and HuH7 model, respectively, and the mean coverage of the remaining human-derived reads was 11.8 × and 6.1 ×, respectively. Previous studies of TF activity inference conducted at 0.1–12 × WGS indicated that our sequencing depths were sufficient for the following analyses [19, 20, 27]. The human reads had a shorter median fragment length than did the mouse reads in both xenograft models (Fig. 3A), which is consistent with a previous study indicating that the fragments with mutations derived from human tumors are shorter than the fragments without mutations in cancer patients [28].Fig. 3Activation status of TCF7 and TCF7L2 inferred from cfDNA-WGS data.   After extracting the human reads, we computed the activation status of TCF7 and TCF7L2 from the cfDNA-WGS data by using the Griffin framework [19]. Griffin calculates the cfDNA coverage at known TFBSs, corrects for GC-bias, smooths the coverage profiles, and extracts three features (central coverage, mean coverage, and amplitude) that reflect TF activity; in the present study, we used the “central coverage” feature as an indicator of TF activity. To confirm the applicability of using the Griffin framework and the central coverage feature to analyze our present data, we inferred the activation status of one positive (CTCF, ubiquitous transcription repressor) and one negative (LYL1, blood-specific TF) control TF [19, 29]. As expected, CTCF, but not LYL1, showed decreased central coverage in both cfDNA samples (Fig. 3B), confirming that the Griffin framework was applicable to our data set. When the two target TFs (TCF7 and TCF7L2) were examined, both showed lower central coverage in HepG2 than in HuH7 tumor, indicating that these TFs were more active in the former than in the latter (Fig. 3B).


### Comparative analysis of the activation status of 377 TFs
  Next, we repeated our previous analysis but this time expanded the scope to target a much larger number of TFs. Following the previously reported methods used to develop the Griffin framework [19], we selected 377 TFs each with more than 10,000 TFBSs from the Gene Transcription Regulation Database (GTRD) and calculated the associated central coverage values for the TFBSs from the cfDNA-WGS data. At the same time, we calculated normalized ATAC-seq signals for the same TFBSs to eliminate GC-bias arising from any differences in the genomic regions of interest. Plots of the central coverage values against the GC-bias normalized ATAC-seq signals revealed strong correlations between the two approaches in the inferences of TF activation status for both models (Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients: HepG2, − 0.90; HuH7, − 0.85) (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, we evaluated additional statistics generated by Griffin [19]; while cfDNA mean coverage showed a similar level of association with normalized ATAC-seq signals, cfDNA amplitude did not (Fig. S2). These results suggest that the mean coverage may also be useful for TF activity inference. A previous comparative analysis between cfDNA WGS and ATAC-seq data in blood cells reported a correlation coefficient of − 0.41 [30]; we attribute this much lower association to a combination of factors, including the use of unpaired samples of cfDNA WGS and ATAC-seq samples, their simulation of ATAC-seq data using weighted mean of neutrophils and PBMC ATAC-seq data, and differences in analytical methods. In contrast, by using paired samples, we revealed that tumor chromatin status is strongly reflected in the cfDNA. Based on the strong concordance found between the inferences made with the cfDNA-WGS and ATAC-seq data, we hypothesized that ctDNA would also reflect differences in tumor-specific TF activity. To examine this, we ranked the activities of the 377 TFs and examined the placements of TCF7 and TCF7L2 in the two xenograft models. Both TCF7 and TCF7L2 showed higher rankings in cfDNA derived from HepG2 tumor compared to those from HuH7 tumor (Fig. 4B). This trend was also reflected in average rankings calculated from the ATAC-seq data: TCF7 ranked 38.5 in HepG2 and 88.25 in HuH7, while TCF7L2 ranked 176.5 in HepG2 and 191.75 in HuH7 (Fig. 4C). ### cfDNA TFBS coverage reflects tumor-specific differences of TF activity
  We further examined our hypothesis that cfDNA reflects tumor-specific differences of TF activity by first trying to identify which of the 377 TFs had the largest variations in activity between the two tumor models by using the ATAC-seq data. However, global chromatin accessibility determined by ATAC-seq is affected by technical and biological effects [31], meaning we observed differences in global chromatin accessibility between the ATAC-seq data for the two models (Fig. S3A). To handle this difference in global chromatin accessibility, we used the chromVAR package in R, which annotates TFs to peaks and calculates signal deviations while considering global chromatin accessibility. Using chromVAR to analyze our ATAC-seq data, we extracted the 100 TFs with the largest variations in activity across all tumor samples. Then, through unsupervised hierarchical clustering based on the bias-corrected deviations, we obtained two distinct TF profiles (Fig. 5A). These profiles possibly reflect differences between the models, which may allow us to identify tumor-specific TFs. Actually, TCF7 and TCF7L2 were both included in the top 100 differential TFs and both showed greater activity in HepG2 tumor than in HuH7 tumor. In contrast, many FOX family TFs showed higher activity in HuH7 tumor than in HepG2 tumor. Given that some FOX family members are reported to be liver-enriched TFs and dysregulation of these can contribute to the pathogenesis of hepatocellular carcinoma by affecting the expression of various tumor-related genes, these TFs may also affect the tumor biology of HuH7 [32].Fig. 5cfDNA-WGS central coverage at TFBSs reflects tumor-specific TF activity. A Heatmap showing the activities of the top 100 TFs with the largest variations in activity across all tumor samples in the ATAC-seq data. The visualization was constructed using unsupervised clustering of the deviation scores in chromVAR in R. The legend to the right indicates the deviation scores (B) Differential activities of tumor-specific TFs inferred from cfDNA-WGS data. A total of 377 TFs were classified into the indicated subgroups based on their degree of deviation shown in (A). In each subgroup, z-score–transformed center coverages of the selected TFs were compared between the HepG2 and HuH7 tumors. The plots for TCF7 and TCF7L2 are labeled. Nominal p-values were calculated using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test

  To evaluate whether cfDNA preserves some information on the differential activities of the identified tumor-specific TFs, we first classified the TFs with more than 10,000 TFBSs based on their activity determined from the ATAC-seq data into three subgroups: “HepG2 HIGH” (higher activity in HepG2 tumor), “HuH7 HIGH” (higher activity in HuH7 tumor), and “Others”. Then, given the variability of global chromatin accessibility in the ATAC-seq data, we compared the central coverage values of the xenograft models for each of the three subgroups. The “Others” subgroup showed a significant difference in central coverage between the tumors, while the “HuH7 HIGH” subgroups showed no significant difference, which was not the expected outcome (Fig. S3B). To address this in the following comparison, we normalized the central coverage values to z-scores. After the normalization, the significant difference in the “Others” subgroup was not observed, and significant differences were revealed between the “HepG2 HIGH” and “HuH7 HIGH” subgroups (Fig. 5B). Even after normalization, the lower central coverage of the TCF7 and TCF7L2 TFBSs persisted in HepG2 tumor (Fig. 5B). Based on the similar association between ATAC-seq signal and mean coverage (Fig. S2A), we also evaluated z-scores derived from cfDNA mean coverage. However, significant differences in “HepG2 HIGH” and “HuH7 HIGH” were not observed, supporting the use of central coverage for more accurate TF activity inference (Fig. S4).


### Factors that influence the accuracy of inferences of TF activation status
  To examine the correlation between the inferences of activation status made with the ATAC-seq and cfDNA-WGS data under conditions similar to those found in the clinic [19], we evaluated the effects of three potential key factors: the number of TFBSs per TF, sequencing depth, and the ratio of tumor-derived cfDNA.

  To explore the potential of expanding the present approach to target TFs with fewer TFBSs, we evaluated the effect of the number of TFBS on inference accuracy. Again, following the methods used to develop the Griffin framework [19], sets of TFs with 10,000 (BS10k; 377 TFs), 5000 (BS5k; 446 TFs), and 1000 (BS1k; 566 TFs) TFBSs were created. When the correlations between the inferences of activity made with the ATAC-seq and cfDNA-WGS data were compared, an inverse correlation that decreased in strength with decreasing number of TFBSs was observed for both tumors (Fig. 6A). These data suggested that the number of TFBSs affect the correlation strength.Fig. 6Impact of TFBS number and sequencing depth on inferences of TF activity. For each TFBS set, correlation coefficients were calculated between the inferences made with the ATAC-seq and cfDNA-WGS data by using Spearman’s rank correlation test.   Usually, a sequencing depth of 0.1–2 × is required for the detection of cancer by cfDNA analysis [33–35]. However, because the TF activity is inferred against more focused regions of DNA (i.e., TFBSs) than in cancer detection, a greater sequencing depth may be necessary. Therefore, we next evaluated the effects of sequencing depth using the three TFBS sets with different numbers of TFBSs and an average sequencing depth of 0.1–5 × generated by downsampling the cfDNA-WGS data. With increasing sequencing depth, the inverse correlation became stronger in each TFBS set. The correlation appeared to plateau at around 1 × depth for both tumors in both 10 k and 5 k TFBS sets. In contrast, for the 1 k TFBS set, the correlation did not plateau at until 2 × in HepG2 and continued to increase even at 5 × in HuH7 (Fig. 6B). These results suggest the sequencing depth required for accurate TF activity inference may vary depending on the number of available TFBS.

  In the previous experiment, the strongest correlation coefficient using tumor-derived reads was obtained by using the BS10k set and a sequencing depth of at least 1 × (Fig. 6B). However, in clinical samples, the ratio of tumor-derived cfDNA to the total cfDNA, called the tumor fraction, varies depending on factors such as cancer type and tumor burden, and so the tumor fraction may affect the inference of TF activity [36, 37]. To evaluate the effect of tumor fraction, we obtained WGS data from pooled healthy donor–derived cfDNA, in which we confirmed blood-specific LYL1 binding (Fig. S5A), and generated simulation data by mixing healthy and tumor model cfDNA data to mimic various tumor fractions ranging from 0 to 100%. The central coverage of the TCF7 TFBSs increased with decreasing tumor fraction (Fig. 7A). We then evaluated whether the correlation between the inferences of TF activity made with the ATAC-seq and cfDNA-WGS data was affected by the tumor fraction. The inverse correlation became much stronger with increasing tumor fraction at both 1 × and 5 × sequencing depths (Fig. 7B).   To determine the optimal tumor fraction and sequencing depth for observing tumor-specific differences of TF activity, we evaluated all combinations of tumor fraction and sequencing depth (Table S1, Fig. S5B). At 5 × sequencing depth across all tumor fractions, significant or trending differences in the z-score–adjusted central coverage values were observed between the HepG2 and HuH7 tumors for the TFs in the “HepG2_HIGH” and “HuH7_HIGH” subgroups (p < 0.05, p < 0.1) (Table S1, Fig. 7C). In contrast, at 1 × sequencing depth, no significant differences were observed in either subgroup when the tumor fraction was 3% or less (Table S1, Fig. 7D). Effect sizes of “HuH7_HIGH” subgroup at 5 × and 1 × sequencing depth were comparable to those in the “raw” (before downsampling dataset) even at 100% tumor fractions. However, this trend was not observed in the “HepG2-HIGH” subgroup (Fig. S5B). These results suggest that highly activated TFs can be detected under lower sequencing conditions, whereas TFs with lower activation status require deeper sequencing and higher tumor fraction for accurate detection. Furthermore, considering a recent large cohort study of over 23,000 patients and multiple cancer types that reported a median tumor fraction of 2.2% [37], our results suggest that at least 5 × sequencing depth is required for accurate inference and detection of TFs with fewer TFBSs.

  To further evaluate the potential influence of using the same healthy donor-derived cfDNA sample across models, we compared our in-house healthy cfDNA with a publicly available dataset (SRR2129993) and observed a strong correlation (Spearman’s rho = 0.96; Fig. S6A). However, the correlation was slightly weaker than that observed between liver cancer model-derived cfDNA (Spearman’s rho = 0.98; Fig. S6B), suggesting that the background signal differences originating from healthy donor cfDNA may affect TF activity inferences. Therefore, we conducted additional analyses by generating mixed cfDNA samples, combining tumor-derived cfDNA with either public or in-house healthy cfDNA. These analyses revealed that significant differences in highly activated TF subgroup “HuH7_HIGH” could be detected from 3% tumor fraction when comparing HepG2 + public healthy cfDNA vs HuH7 + in-house healthy cfDNA at 5 × depth condition. In contrast, differences in moderately activated “HepG2_HIGH” subgroup were not detected until 50% tumor fraction (Table S3). However, when comparing another pair (HepG2 + in-house healthy cfDNA vs HuH7 + public healthy cfDNA), no significant differences were observed across most conditions. While these analyses were affected by several factors such as difference in sequencing assay method used for healthy donor-derived cfDNA samples and donor backgrounds, these results support that at least 5 × sequencing depth is important for accurate TF activity inferences.


### Possible reasons for inconsistent inferences of TF activity between the ATAC-seq and cfDNA-WGS data
  Although 5 × sequencing depth was found to be required for accurate inferences, some inconsistencies in the inferences between the data sets were observed even under that condition (Fig. 7C). To explore the reasons for these inconsistencies, we labeled TFs with the same activation status in both data sets as “MATCH” and those with a different activation status as “DIF”. Five TFs (BHLHE40, CEBPG, TP53, TP73, and ZNF384) were classified as DIF across the five tumor fraction conditions (Fig. 8A). Even in the case of some TFs with only minimal expression levels, these TFs sometimes showed pseudo activity because of motif homology such as that among TF family members [38]. We evaluated the expression levels of these TFs and found that TP73 showed minimal expression in both tumors and comparable central coverage (Figs. 8B and C). Therefore, TP73 was excluded from further evaluation, and the remaining four TFs were labeled as “DIF_ALL TFs”. Conversely, 42.6% of the tumor-specific TFs, including TCF7 and TCF7L2, were labeled as MATCH across all tumor fraction conditions. Data are the density of GC-content in each TFBS of MATCH_ALL TFs and each of the DIF_ALL TFs

  First, we characterized the TFs and found three of the DIF_ALL TFs (BHLHE40, CEBPG, ZNF384) had motifs with GC-bias (Fig. S7A). When we evaluated the differences in GC-content of the TFBSs between each DIF_ALL TF and MATCH_ALL TF, three of the four DIF_ALL TFs (not TP53) showed a GC-biased content (Fig. 8D). Consistent with this, the coverage profiles of BHLHE40, CEBPG, and ZNF384 TFBS were different in high or low GC-content between tumors (Fig. S7B). Thus, differences in the TFBS motifs for these TFs may underly the observed inconsistencies between tumor and cfDNA.

  HuH7 cells are reported to harbor a mutation in the DNA-binding domain of TP53 and to have lower TP53 TF activity than HepG2 cells, which have wild-type TP53 [39]. Indeed, our ATAC-seq data showed that HepG2 had higher TP53 activity than HuH7 tumor (Fig. S7C). When we compared the coverage profile between the ATAC-seq and cfDNA data, we observed an increase in coverage at the center of the TFBSs only in the HepG2 cfDNA data (Figs. 8C and S7C). This characteristic coverage profile at the center of the TFBSs is known to be caused by the protection of DNA by TF binding [10]. On the other hand, this increase was not observed in the ATAC-seq profile targeting TFBS 10k (Fig. S7C). Therefore, this is considered to be a phenomenon specific to the degradation of open chromatin regions in cfDNA, which is different from the ATAC reaction. Also, in the ATAC-seq data, the signal intensities for the TP53 motif–based TFBSs (“HOMER motif”) were lower than those using TP53 BS10k set (Fig. S7C). Based on the different signal profiles, we compared the target genomic regions and found a low concordance of target regions between BS10k and the HOMER motif (Fig. S7D). Moreover, no increase in cfDNA central coverage was observed at the center of TP53 motif–based TFBSs (Fig. S7E). These results suggest that the target TFBSs influence the signal intensity in both ATAC-seq and cfDNA-WGS analyses. Although our data set did not allow us to determine which genomic region is more appropriate for inferences of TF activity, the findings suggest that selection of the target TFBSs will be needed to maximize inference accuracy. Finally, our findings imply that development of additional bias correction methods may be required for accurate TF activity inference from cfDNA-WGS data, instead of using a simple z-score transformation of central coverage.


## Discussion
  Here, we used tumor models derived from two human liver cancer cell lines, which have been extensively evaluated in preclinical research [40, 41], to obtain several valuable insights into the inference of TF activity using cfDNA-WGS data. By using paired RNA-seq and ATAC-seq data, we were able to exclude TFs with low expression in the tumor tissues. In addition, comparing the inferences made using the cfDNA-WGS and ATAC-seq data, we were able to evaluate the accuracy of inferences made using cfDNA-WGS data. However, in the clinical setting, collecting paired tumor and cfDNA samples at the same timepoint and conducting multi-omics analyses is often challenging. This makes it difficult to identify TFs whose activity is incorrectly estimated, such as TP73 (Fig. 8). Recent studies have demonstrated that evaluation of cfDNA coverage at TSSs can be used to infer whether the genes are expressed or not in tumor tissues [18, 42]. This suggests that combining approaches with different underlying rationales to take simultaneous advantage of multiple characteristics of cfDNA might enable more precise inferences of TF activity, even in cases where tumor samples are unavailable. However, such inference of expressed genes using cfDNA coverage at TSSs is reported to require a much greater sequencing depth compared with inferences of TF activity [18, 42]. For accurate TF inference, knowledge-based selection of candidate TFs reported to be expressed in each cancer type [10, 43], or combination with cfDNA-based TSS analysis, might be appropriate depending on the situation.

  In the present study, we were limited to qualitative inference of tumor-specific TF activity due to a limited sample size. The small amount of blood available from each mouse had to be pooled to perform WGS, which is the reason why replicates could not be created for each model. However, given that the activity of individual TFs is known to vary among tumors and patients, an approach that affords quantitative inferences of TF activity would be more useful for understanding tumor biology and for future drug development [10, 43]. Conducting such quantitative analyses would require a much larger sample size and further refinement of the analysis framework based on advances in our understanding of cfDNA biology. To this end, however, our present findings highlight that future approaches will at least need to account both for potential differences in global chromatin accessibility between cfDNA samples and for the effects of the GC-content of TFBSs, as well as provide a means of selecting the most appropriate TFBSs for each TF.

  Regarding the need to account for differences in global chromatin accessibility between cfDNA samples, the present limited sample size prevented us from implementing appropriate corrections, so we instead relied on simple z-score normalization. Additional studies with larger sample sizes are needed to incorporate these corrections into the cfDNA approach and clarify their impacts. Given that chromVAR, which we used in the present ATAC-seq analysis, is able to effectively correct for global chromatin accessibility and GC-bias by using deviations across multiple samples [31], such an approach may also work for cfDNA-WGS data.

  Regarding the need to account for the effects of the GC-content of TFBSs, the Griffin framework, which we used in the present cfDNA-based analysis, corrects for GC-bias based on fragment length but not for the GC-content of each TFBS [19]. Our findings (Fig. 8D) highlight the need to consider GC-bias in TFBSs, indicating that it will also be necessary to correct for the GC-content in TFBSs for more accurate TF activity inference. Furthermore, the discordance of TP53 activity between the ATAC-seq and cfDNA-WGS analyses seems to have been caused by differences in the target TFBSs between the assays (Figs. 8C and S4C–E). Considering the studies to date that have reported that TFBSs differ among tissue- and cancer-types, it is possible that appropriate TFBS selection depending on the sample and cancer type is important for accurate TF activity inference [44, 45]. Recently, the use of a deep neural network model trained on chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) peaks to identify TFBSs in cfDNA has been reported [46]. Although experimental validation was lacking, the study results suggest that this type of approach may enable more accurate TF activity inference. Furthermore, TF binding sometimes protects at the center of a TFBS and has the potential to increase TFBS coverage, as shown in the TP53 peak profile obtained from HepG2 cfDNA (Fig. 8C). Additional evaluations are required to determine whether central coverage alone can be used as an indicator of TF binding or whether it should be used in conjunction with one or more other indicators, considering the strong association between mean coverage of cfDNA and tumor ATAC-seq data (Fig. S2A). For more detailed evaluations, paired tumor/cfDNA analyses would be required, and if the TFs to be targeted are already known, the combination of ChIP-seq and cfDNA-WGS may be a useful approach.

  In the present study, we mainly used a set of TFs each with over 10k TFBSs, but many TFs with fewer than 1k TFBSs are also known [27]. For a deeper understanding of tumor biology and drug discovery, expanding the present approach to these TFs with fewer TFBSs will be important.

  The primary objective of the present study was to evaluate the concordance of inferences of TF activity made by using cfDNA-WGS and ATAC-seq data, and to do this we used samples from untreated xenograft models. However, in the clinic, longitudinal cfDNA analyses to evaluate changes in TF activity under treatment pressure would likely provide important information for treatment selection, as was reported from a study in which the subjects were treated with AR inhibitor [21]. Because the present study focused on preclinical models and in silico validation, further validation using clinical samples would be necessary.


## Conclusions
  Here, we have demonstrated that plasma cfDNA reflects tumor TF activation status for more than 370 TFs in two mouse xenograft models of human liver cancer. These findings support the potential use of cfDNA to infer tumor-specific TF activity. Furthermore, we found that the accuracy of cfDNA-based inferences of TF activity was affected by sequencing depth and tumor fraction, with our data showing that a sequencing depth of at least 5 × was necessary for accurate TF activity inference, especially at low tumor fraction (3%). Further examination also revealed some possible causes of inconsistencies observed between inferences of TF activity made using the cfDNA-WGS and tumor ATAC-seq data. Given the low-invasive nature of cfDNA-based tumor profiling, it has the potential to have a marked impact on the oncology field. We expect that our present observations will be useful for the development of improved cfDNA-based approaches for the inference of TF activity.


## Methods

### Cell culture
  Two human liver cancer cell lines, HepG2 and HuH7, were obtained from the Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources (Osaka, Japan) in 2017 and 2013, respectively. In 2022, both cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat analysis and confirmed to be mycoplasma-free. Both cell lines were incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2 in media containing 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin; HepG2 cells were cultured in Eagle’s Minimum Essential Medium (Fujifilm Wako, Osaka, Japan) and HuH7 cells in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (Fujifilm Wako).


### Liver cancer xenograft models
  Animal experiments were performed in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Eisai Co., Ltd. (Ibaraki, Japan). Liver cancer cells suspended in Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution were mixed 1:1 with Matrigel (Corning, New York, USA) and then inoculated subcutaneously into the right flank of male BALB/c nude mice (The Jackson Laboratory Japan, Kanagawa, Japan). When the tumors had reached 750–2200 mm3, plasma and tumor samples were collected under isoflurane anesthesia and stored at − 80 °C until use in the following experiments.


### ATAC-seq using nuclei isolated from frozen xenograft tumor tissue
  Library preparation for ATAC-seq analysis of nuclei isolated from snap-frozen tumor samples was conducted in line with a previously reported protocol with some modifications [47]. All of the steps were performed at 4°C. Briefly, a sample of frozen tissue (20–50 mg) was placed into a pre-chilled 2-mL Dounce homogenizer containing 1–2 mL of homogenization buffer (5 mM CaCl2, 3 mM Mg(OAc)2, 10 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.8, 320 mM sucrose, 0.1 mM EDTA, 0.1% IGEPAL® CA-630, 16.6 μM Phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, 166 μM β-mercaptoethanol). The tissue was homogenized first by using the loose pestle and then by using the tight pestle; after each of the two homogenization steps, connective tissue and residual debris were removed by passing the homogenate through a 70-μm nylon mesh filter and retaining the filtrate, which was centrifuged at 4 °C, 350 rcf for 5 min. The resulting pellet was resuspended in the homogenization buffer, and nuclei were collected by iodixanol density gradient centrifugation at 4 °C, 3000 rcf for 20 min. The gradient consisted of layers of 25%, 29%, and 35% iodixanol solution; the 25% layer was a 1:1 mixture of the nuclear suspension and 50% iodixanol solution. The interface between the 29% and 35% solutions was collected and nuclei were stained with trypan blue and manually counted. To prepare the library for ATAC-seq, 50,000–100,000 nuclei and an ATAC-Seq kit (ActiveMotif, California, USA) were used. The library was prepared in duplicate after tagmentation using the nuclei from two independent tumor samples. Paired-end sequencing of 2 × 76 bp was conducted using an Illumina NextSeq 550 system (San Diego, CA, USA).


### Peak calling and inference of TF activity using the ATAC-seq data
  Genome assemblies GRCh38 and GRCm38 (Illumina iGenomes) were used for read classification and alignment. In short, the fastq files were trimmed to remove Illumina Nextera adapter sequences and checked for overall sequence data quality using fastp (0.23.2) [48]. The human-derived sequence reads were extracted using xengsort (1.5.0) [49]. Bowtie2 (2.4.4) [50] was used to align them to the GRCh38 genome assembly using the “–very-sensitive -X 2000” option. MarkDuplicates (Picard) (2.18.29) was then used to mark duplicates with the “VALIDATION_STRINGENCY = LENIENT REMOVE_DUPLICATES = false” option. Uniquely mapped and non-duplicated reads were isolated using Samtools (1.12) with the “-F 1804” option [51]. To account for the Tn5 adapter, alignmentSieve from the deepTools (3.5.3) suite [52] was used to shift the positions of all mapped reads in the BAM files. After converting the BAM files into BED files, peaks were called by macs2 (2.2.9.1) [53] with the “macs2 –nomodel –keep-dup all –call-summits –extsize 150 –shift −75 -f BED -g 2.9e + 9” option. The size of the called summit peaks was standardized using bedtools (2.30.0) slop with the “-b 250” option. Any regions overlapping with the ENCODE blacklist were excluded [54]. The resulting peaks and the shifted BAM files containing only human autosomes were used in the subsequent analyses. To compare TF activity, merged peaks were annotated by TF motif using HOMER (4.11) annotatePeaks.pl with the “-center” option [55]. bigWig files generated from the BAM files were processed using computeMatrix from the deepTools suite, and the generated matrix files were used to plot signals around the center of the TFBSs. The quality of the ATAC-seq data was validated as follows. The output of CollectMultipleMetrics (Picard) was used to calculate the insert size and Log10-transformed fragments per million (fpm). Correlation plots between samples were created using the bigWig files by processing with plotCorrelation from the deepTools suite with the “–corMethod spearman –skipZeros –whatToPlot scatterplot” option. The output of Bowtie2 was used for the calculation of mapping rate. FRiP scores and the number of called peaks were determined using the BAM files and narrow-peak files contained in the macs2 results.


### Tumor whole-transcriptome sequencing
  RNA was extracted from snap-frozen xenografted tumors by using a Maxwell RSC simplyRNA Tissue Kit (Promega, Wisconsin, USA). Library preparation for sequencing of the isolated RNA was conducted using a SureSelect Strand-Specific RNA Library Prep System (Agilent Technologies Inc., California, USA). Pair-end sequencing of 2 × 151 bp was conducted with an Illumina NextSeq 550 system. Fastq files were processed to remove adapters using fastp, and human reads were extracted using xengsort. The fastq files for each sample were entered into FASTQC (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/) for quality assessments. Sequencing data were processed by using Trimmomatic (0.36) to remove bases with low-quality scores. Filtered reads were mapped to the human genome (GRCh38 assembly) by using STAR (2.5.0c). Transcripts per million were then quantified with RSEM (1.2.31). Wnt signal gene signature score was calculated as the average log2(TPM + 0.01) value for each gene [26].


### cfDNA-WGS
  EDTA plasma collected from 9–11 mice harboring a HepG2- or HuH7-derived xenograft tumor was pooled. cfDNA was exacted from pooled plasma using a QIAamp MinElute ccfDNA Midi Kit and the QIAcube platform (both from Qiagen K.K., Tokyo, Japan). After extraction, DNA fragment size and percentage of cfDNA were determined by electrophoresis by using the Cell-free DNA ScreenTape assay on a TapeStation 4200 fragment analyzer (Agilent Technologies Inc.). cfDNA concentration was determined by using a Qubit HS-dsDNA Assay Kit and Qubit Flex Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). The presence of cfDNA in each sample was checked by confirming the presence of a single peak at around 150 bp on the electropherogram (Fig. S8A-C). The library for the WGS was prepared by Azenta Life Sciences (Tokyo, Japan) by using a VAHTS Universal Pro DNA Library Prep Kit and Illumina ND608-02 system (Vazyme Biotech, Jiangsu, PRC). Pair-end sequencing of 2 × 150 bp was conducted using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system at Azenta Life Sciences.


### Control cfDNA library preparation
  Human Cell-Free DNA Control Z2121001 was purchased from BioChain Institute Inc. (California, USA). Double-stranded DNA libraries were generated from 50 ng of control cfDNA using a KAPA HyperPrep kit and a KAPA Unique Dual-Indexed Adapter Kit (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Pair-end sequencing of 2 × 151 bp was conducted using an Illumina Novaseq 6000 system.


### Pre-processing for cfDNA-WGS
  Following the recommendations of the Griffin framework [19], WGS pre-processing was performed according to GATK best practices [19, 56–58]. cfDNA WGS derived from tumors and healthy individuals, as well as cfDNA WGS obtained from public databases, were all processed using the same pipeline.　First, adapter trimming and data quality were checked using fastp. Sequence reads were then classified using xengsort. Human-derived reads were aligned to the GRCh38 assembly using BWA MEM (0.7.17-r1188) [59], alignment files were marked using MarkDuplicates (Picard), and reads with quality scores below 30 were removed. Quality control of BAM files was performed using Qualimap (2.2.2) [60] and Samtools. Reads classified as mouse-derived were processed similarly. Finally, base quality score recalibration was performed using GATK (4.1.8.1) BaseRecalibrator and ApplyBQSR with the “–bqsr-baq-gap-open-penalty 30.0” option. For known single nucleotide polymorphisms in the analysis, All_20180418.vcf.gz and Mills_and_1000G_gold_standard.indels.hg38.vcf.gz obtained from the NCBI and Cloud Life Sciences public data sets, respectively, were used. The resulting BAM files were used in all downstream analyses. Insert size distributions were visualized based on the output of CollectMultipleMetrics (Picard).


### Data collection using simulated tumor fractions
  Tumor fractions were mimicked by mixing pure tumor–derived cfDNA data with healthy donor–derived cfDNA data. DownsampleSam (Picard) and Samtools merge were used to downsample and merge to create BAM files of pseudo-tumor fractions of 0%, 1%, 3%, 10%, and 50% at 1 × and 5 × depth for each tumor. To control the variation in the extracted number of reads, DownsampleSam (Picard) was used with the "ACCURACY = 0.00001 STRATEGY = Chained” option.


### TFBS selection and TF activity inference from cfDNA-WGS data
  Following a previous report [19], TFBS sets (BS1k, BS5k, BS10k) were created with 1,000, 5,000, and 10,000 sites per TF, respectively, from the GTRD (version 19.10, downloaded from https://gtrd.biouml.org/downloads/19.10/chip-seq/Homo%20sapiens_meta_clusters.interval.gz). The ATAC-seq peak annotated with the TP53 motif in HOMER annotatePeaks.pl was used as the TP53 TFBS set. Griffin (0.2.0) was used to calculate coverage profiles in 15-bp bins for each TF using the created TFBS sets with default parameters [19]. cfDNA TFBS coverage profiles were constructed using the normalized coverage per 15 bases.


### Correlation analysis of TF activities determined by cfDNA-WGS or tumor ATAC-seq
  ATAC-seq TFBS coverage was calculated for the same regions as cfDNA central coverage using mosdepth (0.3.3). To eliminate the dependence of read counts on GC-content, a smoothing spline was fitted to calculate predicted values based on GC-content, and raw read counts were divided by those values. The log2-transformed median was calculated from the corrected values for each TF. These were then treated as ATAC-normalized signals for each TF. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient test was performed between the ATAC-normalized signal and the cfDNA central coverage. TFs were listed in order of signal intensity for ATAC-normalized signals and cfDNA central coverage, respectively, in descending order for ATAC-seq and in ascending order for cfDNA WGS. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient tests were performed between the ATAC-normalized signal and central coverage in each TFBS set and pseudo-tumor fraction combination.


### Comparison of tumor-specific TF activity
  Using chromVAR [31], the top 100 differentially active TFs between samples were extracted. For peak annotation, the JASPAR2022 motif database was used. Based on the calculated deviation scores, chromVAR was used to perform unsupervised hierarchical clustering. For each tumor, the top 50 TFs with the highest deviation scores were extracted. Restricting to only the TFs with more than 10,000 TFBSs, subgroups were defined as “HepG2_HIGH” (29/377 TFs), “HuH7_HIGH (18/377)”, and “Other” (330/377). Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were performed on cfDNA central coverages, on z-score–transformed cfDNA central coverages between HepG2 and HuH7 in each TF subgroup, and between tumors on the median of ATAC-normalized signals for each TF.


### Classification of TFs based on ATAC-seq and cfDNA coverage match
  To examine inconsistencies in the inferred TF activities, TFs were classified based on the ATAC-seq TF subgroups and z-score–transformed cfDNA coverages. If cfDNA accurately reflects the open-chromatin region of the tumor, the cfDNA coverage in HepG2 should be decreased in the HepG2_HIGH TFs. Therefore, HepG2_HIGH TFs that showed increased cfDNA coverage (increased z-score) in HepG2 were defined as “DIF” TFs, while those that showed decreased cfDNA coverage were defined as “MATCH” TFs. The same classification method was applied to HuH7. This classification was performed for each pseudo–tumor-fraction and was visualized. TFs classified as MATCH or DIF in all pseudo–tumor-fractions were defined as MATCH_ALL and DIF_ALL, respectively. The motif profiles in DIF_ALL were visualized using getMatrixSet in the TFBStools package [61], JASPAR2022 in the JASPAR2022 package [62], and ggseqlogo in the ggseqlogo package [63].


### GC-content and coverage in TFBS
  The GC-content of each TFBS within the 10 k TFBS set was calculated using bedtools nuc. The distribution of GC-content was visualized for both the entire MATCH_ALL and for each TF within the DIF_ALL. Also, the relationship between the GC-content of TFBS and the log2-transformed ATAC-seq TFBS coverage was visualized. To illustrate the trend of coverage changes relative to GC-content in each tumor, splines were fitted using geom_smooth(method = 'gam') in the ggplot2 package [64].


### Genomic overlap analysis of TP53 binding sites
  To analyze the overlaps of TP53 binding sites, genomic regions were compared between ATAC-seq peaks and the BS10k TFBS set. For ATAC-seq peaks, those annotated with the HOMER TP53 motif were targeted. For cfDNA, the BS10k TFBS set for TP53 was used. After standardizing the width of each region to 1000 base pairs, the genomic regions were consolidated using the reduce function in the GenomicRanges package [65]. To identify overlapping regions between these data sets, we employed the findOverlapsOfPeaks function and visualized the results.


### Statistical analyses and visualizations
  Statistical tests were conducted using R (4.4.0). Z-score transformation was performed using the scale function. Smoothing splines were calculated using the gam function in the mgcv package [66]. All Spearman’s correlation coefficients and p-values were calculated using the cor function. Wilcoxon signed-rank test and the effect size calculation were performed using the wilcox_test function and wilcox_effsize function in the rstatix package [67], respectively. If not stated, plots were created using ggplot2 [64]. All boxplots were centered at the median and display interquartile ranges and minima and maxima extending to 1.5 × the interquartile range.
