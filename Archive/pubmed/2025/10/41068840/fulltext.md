# Machine learning on transcription factor expression profiles for precision breast cancer therapy


## Background
  Although breast cancer is a significant heterogeneous disease with an increasing global prevalence, precise prognostic evaluation is a vital aspect of designing personalized therapy strategies and upholding patients’ survival rates. With the incorporation of artificial intelligence technology, in particular, machine learning, cancer prognosis and prediction have significantly been redefined.


## Methods
  In this study, we adopted a ten-fold cross-validation method to construct a Machine Learning-Derived Transcription Factor Signature (MDTS) across 108 algorithmic combinations. The optimal model was selected based on the highest average C-index across ten cohorts. We integrated single-cell data with multi-omics analysis to comprehensively assess the robustness of the MDTS model at both molecular and genomic levels. The MDTS demonstrated superior predictive power, outperforming 103 existing signatures and accurately predicting breast cancer outcomes across 10 independent cohorts.


## Results
  Our findings revealed that patients with low MDTS scores are more likely to benefit from immunotherapy, while the PAC-1 drug was identified as the most targeted agents to the chemotherapy with high MDTS score.


## Conclusions
  These insights will open the door to delivering cutting-edge MDTS strategies to customizing breast cancer therapies.

## Introduction
  Breast cancer is a heterogeneous disease with multiple prognoses, making accurate prognostic evaluation crucial for formulating individualized treatment plans and improving patient survival rates [1]. Traditionally, prognostic factors have been based on tumor characteristics and disease stage at diagnosis [2]. However, recent advances have shown that transcription factors (TFs) play a central role in cancer progression and prognosis, significantly impacting patient outcomes [3].

  Transcription factors regulate gene expression and are vital in various cancer-related processes, including tumor initiation, progression, metastasis, and resistance to therapy [4]. High expression of certain TFs, such as Ets-1, has been linked to poor prognosis in breast cancer, particularly in relation to relapse-free survival [5]. Ets-1, for example, promotes angiogenesis and extracellular matrix remodeling, both of which are associated with aggressive cancer phenotypes [6].

  Moreover, recent studies have leveraged machine learning to identify TF-related prognostic models, significantly enhancing our ability to predict outcomes in breast cancer patients [7]. For instance, models based on the HOX family of TFs have demonstrated the ability to stratify patients into different risk categories, offering a more personalized approach to treatment [8]. Another study identified a TF prognostic index incorporating TFs such as NR3C2, ZNF652, EGR3, and ARNT2, which independently predicted the progression-free interval in breast cancer patients [9].

  Despite significant advancements in breast cancer prognosis using TF-related models, challenges remain in fully understanding the molecular mechanisms that underlie these associations [10]. This study aims to further elucidate the role of transcription factors in breast cancer prognosis by developing a predictive model using machine learning algorithms. The model’s effectiveness will be evaluated across various cohorts, assessing its ability to predict prognosis, immune status, response to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), and chemotherapy, ultimately identifying potential therapeutic targets.


## Materials and methods

### Data collection
  We retrospectively collected data from 16 independent breast cancer cohorts using resources such as The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), and MetaGxData [14]. Only samples with comprehensive survival data were included in the analysis. In total, more than 9,000 patients across these cohorts were enrolled for prognostic evaluation. The cohorts included TCGA-BRCA, GSE202203, GSE96058, GSE20685, GSE86166, GSE131769, GSE58812, GSE11121, GSE20711, GSE48391, GSE21653, GSE20685, GSE96058, GSE6532, PNC and TRANSBIG (Table S1). A total of 2932 BRCA-specific TF genes were collected from the ImmReg database, which specifically annotates TFs based on breast cancer-related regulatory activities [11].


## Machine learning derived signature
  We integrated ten computational tools, including Random Survival Forest (RSF), LASSO, Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), Survival-SVM, SuperPC, Ridge Regression, plsRcox, CoxBoost, Stepwise Cox, and Elastic Net (Enet). Among these, RSF, LASSO, CoxBoost, and Stepwise Cox were selected for their capabilities in dimensionality reduction and variable selection. These tools were combined into 108 configurations to develop a predictive signature. Performance was evaluated across all cohorts, including both the TCGA training and validation datasets, and the average concordance index (C-index) was calculated to determine the most consistent prognostic model.

  To further refine our model and ensure that it included only the most predictive genes, we employed an exhaustive search method. This approach assessed all possible combinations of selected genes to identify the subset that provided optimal model performance based on predefined criteria. Consequently, the number of genes was reduced, focusing on those with the highest prognostic value.

  Finally, a risk score was calculated for each patient based on the expression levels of the selected genes, weighted by their respective regression coefficients. This signature was validated across multiple independent cohorts to ensure its reliability in predicting breast cancer outcomes.


## Genomic alteration analysis
  Genetic variations between high and low MDTS (Algorithm-Inferred Tumor Subtypes) groups were analyzed by examining mutation levels and Copy Number Alterations (CNA) using TCGA-BRCA data. These genomic alterations are critical for understanding cancer development, tumor behavior, and identifying potential therapeutic targets.

  The Tumor Mutation Burden (TMB) for both high and low MDTS breast cancer patients was calculated from raw mutation data. TMB, which quantifies the number of mutations within a tumor genome, is often associated with immunotherapy responses, as tumors with high TMB may produce neoantigens that elicit an immune response.

  We visualized the most frequently mutated genes (mutation rate > 5%) using the maftools package, providing a comprehensive landscape of common genetic alterations. Additionally, patient-specific mutational signatures were identified using the deconstructSigs package [12]. These signatures reflect DNA damage and repair processes in cancer cells, shedding light on the underlying mechanisms of mutagenesis. Four prominent mutational signatures (SBS2, SBS13, SBS7B, SBS7D) were highlighted within the TCGA-BRCA dataset, indicating specific patterns of genomic instability in breast cancer.

  Furthermore, five common regions of amplification and deletion were identified, crucial for understanding breast cancer’s genomic landscape. Amplifications and deletions often activate oncogenes or result in the loss of tumor suppressor genes. Particular attention was given to four key genes located in chromosomal regions 8q24.21 and 12p13.1, which are known to harbor essential oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes implicated in breast cancer pathogenesis.


## Single-cell data processing
  For single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis, we processed data from GSE161529 using the Seurat (v4.0) package [13]. scRNA-seq allows for detailed examination of cellular diversity within tumors, which is crucial for understanding the complex biology of cancer.

  Genes with no detectable expression were excluded, focusing on those with non-zero expression levels. The ‘SCTransform’ function in Seurat was used to normalize the expression matrix, correcting for technical variations. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) were employed to reduce data dimensionality. PCA retained most of the variability while simplifying the data, and UMAP provided a two-dimensional visualization that captured the local structure of the data.

  Cellular groupings were identified using Seurat’s “FindNeighbors” and “FindClusters” functions, which create a shared nearest neighbor graph and identify clusters of similar cells. To further enhance dataset accuracy, the DoubletFinder package was used to eliminate potential doublets, which are artificial multiplets that can occur during sequencing [14]. Strict quality control measures were applied, excluding cells with mitochondrial gene content over 15% or fewer than 500 genes, as high mitochondrial content can indicate cell stress or death, and low gene counts may reflect poor-quality cells. These steps resulted in a dataset of 23,146 cells for subsequent analysis.

  Cell types were identified by manually annotating them based on the presence of known marker genes, allowing for precise classification of the various cell populations within the breast cancer samples.


## Inference of Regulons and their activity
  We adapted the Single-Cell Regulatory Network Inference (SCENIC) methodology to construct gene regulatory networks (GRNs) from scRNA-seq data [15]. SCENIC employs a three-step process to infer regulons and their activity: (1) Co-expression modules between TFs and their potential target genes were identified, clustering genes with correlated expression patterns indicative of potential regulatory relationships. (2) Direct target genes for each co-expression module were determined by assessing the enrichment of TF motifs in the promoters of the co-expressed genes. Only those target genes with significantly enriched motifs were selected, refining the co-expression modules into regulons, each consisting of a TF and its direct targets. (3) The regulatory activity of each regulon was quantified by computing the Regulatory Activity Score (RAS) for each cell, measuring the area under the recovery curve to reflect the activity level of the regulon within individual cells.

  To address the scalability challenges of traditional SCENIC protocols for large datasets, we partitioned the data into metacells—aggregated groups of similar cells—before applying SCENIC to these gene expression profiles [16]. This modification enhances both scalability and robustness, improving data quality and reducing computational demands. This advancement significantly improves the application of SCENIC to scRNA-seq data analysis, making it more practical for extensive datasets.


### Regulon clustering
  A comprehensive computational strategy was employed to delineate the regulatory crosstalk between TFs and their corresponding target genes, with a focus on clustering TFs. The methodology involved several key steps: (1) TF-target interaction pairs were filtered to isolate those surpassing a predefined significance threshold, ensuring that the analysis prioritized the most relevant regulatory interactions. (2) Pivotal regulatory TFs were identified by calculating the extent of their target gene regulation, highlighting these TFs as hub genes within the regulatory network. (3) An undirected graph model was constructed to represent the intricate web of TF-target interactions, with spatial arrangement refined using a force-directed algorithm to intuitively portray the network’s architecture. (4) The Leiden algorithm was applied for community detection, revealing the modular configuration of transcription factors based on their regulatory interconnections.

  By integrating these steps, we provided a detailed view of the regulatory networks, highlighting the complex interactions between TFs and their target genes, and identifying key regulatory clusters for further investigation.


## Cell-cell communication analysis
  Cell-cell communication was analyzed using the “CellChat” R package, based on UMI count matrices for each group [17]. The CellChatDB.human database was referenced for ligand-receptor interactions, and the analysis followed the package’s default settings. CellChat objects from each group were merged using the “mergeCellChat” function to evaluate and compare the counts and intensities of interactions. Variations in interaction number and intensity among cell types across groups were visualized with ‘netVisual_diffInteraction’. Signaling pathway changes were identified using ‘rankNet’, and signaling gene expression distribution was illustrated using ‘netVisual_bubble’ and ‘netVisual_aggregate’.

  Additionally, the NicheNet package was employed to analyze intercellular communication from the perspective of ligand activity and the expression patterns of specific downstream targets regulated by these key ligands [18]. This approach provided a detailed understanding of the signaling processes underlying interactions between different cell types, leveraging ligand-target relationships to infer communication pathways within the cellular microenvironment.


## Evaluation of the tumor microenvironment and immunotherapy response
  To thoroughly assess immune cell infiltration levels, several algorithms were employed to analyze the presence of infiltrated immune cells in patients categorized by the MDTS [19]. Each algorithm offers unique insights into the composition and abundance of various immune cell types within the tumor microenvironment (TME). In addition, the ESTIMATE and TIDE indices were evaluated to accurately portray the immune landscape and architecture within the TME [20, 21].

  Immune checkpoints were quantified as indicators of the immune state, offering preliminary predictions of patient responsiveness to ICI therapy. Analyzing these checkpoints provides valuable information about the immune environment within tumors, crucial for predicting the efficacy of immunotherapy treatments.

  This comprehensive approach to evaluating the immune profile within the TME is essential for advancing personalized medicine and optimizing treatment strategies for breast cancer patients, ensuring that therapeutic interventions are tailored to the individual immune landscape of each patient.


### Determination of therapeutic targets and drugs for high MDTS patients
  To identify therapeutic targets and drugs for high MDTS patients, duplicate compounds were filtered out from the Drug Repurposing Hub, resulting in 6125 compounds. Therapeutic targets associated with breast cancer outcomes were identified using Spearman correlation analysis, focusing on the relationship between MDTS and gene expression levels. Genes with a correlation coefficient > 0.15 and P-value < 0.05 were selected, while genes with a correlation coefficient < −0.30 and P-value < 0.05 were linked to poor prognosis. The significance of these genes was further assessed by exploring the relationship between CERES scores from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) and risk scores specifically for breast cells, identifying genes essential for cancer cell survival as potential therapeutic targets [19].

  To refine drug responsiveness predictions, data from the Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (CTRP) and the PRISM project were used. These resources provide extensive drug screening and molecular data across various cancer cell lines. Differential expression analysis was conducted between bulk samples and cell lines, and the pRRophetic package was used to implement a ridge regression model for predicting drug response. This model, trained with expression data and drug response metrics from solid Cancer Cell Lines (CCLs), showed excellent predictive accuracy, validated through 10-fold cross-validation [20].

  Additionally, to identify promising therapeutic drugs for breast cancer, Connectivity Map (CMap) analysis was performed. This involved comparing gene expression profiles across different risk subgroups and submitting the top 300 genes (150 up-regulated and 150 down-regulated) to the CMap website. A negative CMap score indicated higher therapeutic potential against breast cancer, suggesting an inverse relationship between the CMap score and a compound’s effectiveness.

  This comprehensive approach identified viable therapeutic targets and drugs for high MDTS patients, enhancing personalized treatment strategies in breast cancer.


### Patient stratification
  RNA from breast cancer specimens was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). Complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis used GoScript reverse transcriptase, and qRT-PCR was performed with Master Mix (Promega) per manufacturer’s guidelines. Data were acquired using the CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (BioRad).

  Gene expression quantification was conducted using the 2−ΔΔCq method, with GAPDH as the normalization control. Patients were then stratified based on their gene expression profiles using a predefined formula derived from the MDTS. This stratification enabled the identification of patients with different risk profiles, facilitating the development of tailored therapeutic strategies.


### Immunohistochemistry experiment
  Tissue samples were collected from 30 breast cancer patients undergoing surgery at Guizhou Provincial People’s Hospital. These samples were subjected to Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining following established protocols, with diagnoses independently confirmed by two pathologists.

  For the immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis, procedures for paraffin-embedded samples were followed as outlined in previous studies [22, 23]. Standardized protocols and scoring systems were used to assess protein expression levels. Two pathologists independently evaluated the IHC results, ensuring consistency with methodologies from prior research [23].


## Results

### Construction of a machine learning signature
  We collected TF genes from the ImmReg database and conducted an in-depth biological study. Using a ten-fold cross-validation method, we constructed a machine learning-derived TF signature (MDTS) with 108 algorithm combinations. Each model was evaluated by calculating the average C-index of each algorithm in the training cohort and eight external cohorts. The RSF algorithm, which achieved the highest average C-index of 0.668, was selected as the final model (Fig. 1A). We built and tested the model using RSF with 1000 times (Fig. 1B). The point with the lowest error rate was chosen, and the corresponding gene was identified. We assessed the prognostic value of these TF genes through univariate Cox regression and calculated the Hazard Ratio (HR) for these genes across the nine cohorts (Fig. 1C).

  Fig. 1Construction of a machine learning signature. (A) Average C-index for each machine learning algorithm combination in the TCGA-BRCA training cohort and 8 validation cohorts. (B) Plots of the error rate for 1000 cross-validations. (C) Key TF genes associated with breast cancer prognosis. (D) Final selection of six TFs based on an exhaustive search, with patient risk scores calculated according to the expression levels of these genes and their regression coefficients

  An exhaustive search was then conducted to identify the most predictive subset of these genes. Exhaustive search involves evaluating all possible combinations of features to find the subset that offers the best predictive performance, ultimately selecting six TF genes. Each patient’s risk score was subsequently calculated based on the expression levels of these TFs, weighted by their regression coefficients (Fig. 1D). Survival analysis from the nine cohorts indicated that our model effectively distinguished patients with high and low MDTS, suggesting that MDTS is a valuable tool for predicting the survival of breast cancer patients (Figure S1).


### Evaluation of MDTS with clinical characters and published signatures
  Univariate and multivariate Cox analyses indicated that MDTS is an independent risk factor when compared with other clinical indicators (Figure S2A). A clinical nomogram incorporating MDTS, stage, and age was developed to estimate the 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival (OS) probabilities for breast cancer patients (Figure S2B). The calibration curves for the prognostic nomogram closely matched the expected and observed survival rates for the entire cohort, underscoring its superior performance (Figure S2C-E). Furthermore, the area under the curve (AUC) for MDTS was higher than that for other clinical variables in the ROC curve, indicating enhanced predictive power (Figure S2F).

  To evaluate the stability of MDTS, 103 published breast cancer signatures were manually collected and assessed across 10 independent cohorts. The results revealed that only MDTS was statistically significant in all 10 cohorts (Fig. 2A). The predictive ability of each model was evaluated by comparing their average C-index across different datasets. The model consistently ranked highly in all cohorts, placing first in 4 cohorts, second in 2 cohorts, fourth in 1 cohort, sixth in 2 cohorts, and seventh in 1 cohort, demonstrating the robustness of MDTS (Fig. 2B).

### Genetic alteration landscape of MDTS
  To account for genomic heterogeneity of MDTS, we further analyzed gene mutations and copy number changes in both groups (Fig. 3A). Combined with TCGA database of 10 classic signaling pathways of cancer, we observed that the classic tumor-suppressor genes, such as TP53, NOV, SAV1, MOB1A/B, CRB1/2, LRP5/6 and GSK3B, might play in the high MDTS groups, the opposite is true for RPS6KA3, RAC1 and IGF1R (Fig. 3A, B). We further compared TMB between high MDTS and low MDTS groups. The results showed that patients with high MDTS had a higher TMB compared to patients with low MDTS (Fig. 3A, C). Moreover, we delved deeper into the CNA scenery of the two groups. Compared to the low MDTS group, the high MDTS group were significantly more amplificated or deleted in the chromosome arm levels, Like the amplification of 6p23, 8q24.21, 10p15.1, 17q12, 20q13.2, and the deletion of 9p21.3, 9p23, 11p15.5, 16q24.3, 22q12.32 (Fig. 3A, D). Taking 6p23 and 9p23 as examples, the high MDTS group showed significant gene amplification on chromosome 6p23 (GFOD1, CD83, NOL7, SIRT5) and significant gene deletion on chromosome 9p23 (PTPRD, NFIB, MPDZ, TYRP1) at the gene level (Fig. 3A). In conclusion, high TMB, high frequency of gene mutation, and deletion and amplification of genes on chromosome arms may be one of the reasons for poor prognosis.

### Analyzing the biological mechanisms of MDTS using single-cell sequencing
  We selected 14 patients (5 normal tissue and 9 breast cancer tumor tissue) for further evaluation of MDTS (Figure S3A-B), dividing the cells into 19 clusters and 8 cell types (Fig. 4A-B). The number of 8 types of cells was counted, and the percentage of their cell types in the body of these 14 patients was analyzed (Figure S3C-D). The next step is to look at the representative markers of each of the eight cell types and the actual distribution of these markers in the cell (Fig. 4C, S3E). Single-cell sequencing revealed differences in the transcriptome of each cell type in tumor and normal tissue. The results indicate that plasma cells, macrophages, B cells, T cells, fibroblasts and epithelial cells are notably enriched in tumor tissues, while other cells are highly represented in normal tissues (Fig. 4D).

(F) Grouping of cells based on epithelial cell peaks. (G) CopyKat algorithm analyzed the distribution of diploid and aneuploid cells.   MDTS were included in the single cell analysis to obtain a specific cell distribution map (Fig. 4E), and all cells were categorized into high and low MDTS groups according to the peak MDTS score of epithelial cells (Fig. 4F). The potential pathways of MDTS were enriched and visualized by differential expression analysis and GSEA (Figure S3F, G). Take the epithelial cells for example, high MDTS cell was notably enriched in cadherin binding involved in cell-cell adhesion, GTP binding, proton transmembrane transporter activity. While the low MDTS cell was predominantly associated with electron transfer activity (Figure S3G). Additionally, we performed single cell CNA analysis using the CopyKAT package, which discriminates malignant from normal cells. Cells with obvious CNA in aneuploid tumors were successfully captured (Fig. 4G). Finally, the risk score was performed according to the model established by MDTS, and the result showed that the level of polyploid epithelial cells in the tumor cells in this model was more than that of diploid epithelial cells (Fig. 4H).


### Analyzing specific regulatory factors driving MDTS and cell recognition
  To fully construct the transcription factor regulatory network, we used SCENIC pipeline to calculate the regulatory activity score (RAS) of transcription factors in all single cells, which we then submitted to build regulatory maps for eight cell types (Fig. 5A, B). We observed that the overall differentiation trajectory of the eight cell types revealed by the regulator was consistent with that revealed by the single-cell transcriptome. We then performed PCA and variance analyses for different cell types, where PCA1 revealed specific transcription factors for cell type formation, while PCA2 was associated with MDTS specific transcription factors (Fig. 5C, D).

  We next identified 10 key transcription factors recognized by each cell and scored the specificity of each regulator according to Jensen-Shannon divergence. From these 8 cell types, the regulatory factors with high RSS scores were selected for matrix analysis, and it was found that FOXA1, XBP1 and CREB3 were the most specific regulators related to epithelial cells (Fig. 5E, F). The specific regulators most associated with the other seven cell types were also analyzed (Figure S4A).

  Transcriptional activations in organisms that are cooperative among transcription factors are crucial for understanding transcriptional regulation mechanisms. To understand how transcription factors work together to regulate specific biological functions in the MDTS model, we compared RAS scores for each regulatory pair in the map to characterize the combined pattern of MDTS, according to the Leiden algorithm. The cluster analysis results showed that a total of 11 transcription factor clusters were obtained (Fig. 5G; Figure S4B), where the contribution rate of class C and class D to the development of MDTS is relatively high, so we separately show the transcription factors of class C and Class D (Fig. 5H; Figure S4B). The strength for both outgoing and incoming signals elevated dramatically in endothelial cells, epithelial cells, fibroblasts and plasma cells, validating their key roles in the pathological remodeling of high MDTS (Fig. 6B). Notably, epithelial cells were enhanced with incoming signals from other cells, e.g., endothelial cells. Moreover, T cell were less communicated with other cells.

### Identifying anti-cancer agents for high MDTS patients
  In this study, we devised a targeted approach for breast cancer patients with high MDTS levels. Spearman correlation analysis showed a positive correlation between MDTS and the abundance of seven potential targets (SQLE, COX5B, DHCR7, NDUFA6, NDUFB9, CALR, P4HB), and there was a significant negative correlation with their CERES scores (Fig. 9A). It is suggested that these seven genes can be used as potential therapeutic targets for high MDTS patients. These seven genes are closely related to multiple pathways of drug action, and further analysis of these potential drug targets based on drug sensitivity ratios found that the vast majority of these seven genes have high drug sensitivity (Fig. 9B). So, they are considered as key therapeutic targets for breast cancer patients with high MDTS.

(B) Network analysis highlights the intricate connections between these seven therapeutic targets and their associated drug action pathways. (C-D) Box plots compare the AUC values of identified compounds, sourced from the CTRP (C) and PRISM (D) datasets, between low and high MDTS patient groups. (E) A summary table outlines the multi-perspective analysis of the nine candidate compounds, detailing their clinical status, experimental evidence, mRNA expression levels, and CMap scores

  Subsequently, we obtained 3 compounds (panobinostat, CR-1-31B, ouabain) from the CTPR dataset and 4 compounds (romidepsin, diphenyleneiodonium, PAC-1, ingenol-mebutate) from the PRISM dataset. It appears that high MDTS populations were more sensitivity to these seven chemotherapy drugs, since their AUC value was lower (Fig. 9C, D). Based on the CMap analysis, the clinical status, experimental evidence, mRNA expression level and CMap score of each compound were evaluated in detail (Fig. 9E). Ultimately, PAC-1 was identified as the most suitable therapeutic drugs for patients with high-MDTS, based on their CMap score (−85.39).


## Discussion
  Breast cancer remains the most frequently diagnosed cancer and the leading cause of cancer-related deaths among women worldwide [24]. In the United States, it is estimated that approximately 276,480 women will be diagnosed with breast cancer in 2020, with 42,170 succumbing to the disease [25]. The intricate molecular mechanisms underlying breast cancer progression, and the associated chemotherapy resistance highlight the ongoing challenge of developing effective targeted therapies. Consequently, the establishment of accurate prognostic models for breast cancer is crucial to improving patient outcomes.

  TFs are pivotal in regulating cellular processes, including responses to extracellular and intracellular signals, and are closely associated with cancer development [26]. TFs exert their effects by binding to the promoter regions of genes, modulating the expression of specific target genes. For instance, the transcription factor YY1 has been shown to upregulate SNHG17 expression by binding to its promoter, thereby promoting glioma development [27]. Given their central role in oncogenesis, TFs represent promising therapeutic targets for cancer intervention. In our study, a predictive model based on TF genes was constructed to assess breast cancer prognosis. The model was evaluated for the prognostic significance, ultimately identifying six genes (TBX2, WWOX, PLXNB2, ZNF394, NAB2, LEF1) for inclusion in the MDTS model. The risk scores derived from MDTS were used to categorize patients into low MDTS) and high MDTS subgroups.

  TBX2, a member of the T-box family, plays a crucial role in embryonic development and has been implicated in the regulation of the cell cycle and cancer progression [28]. Overexpression of TBX2 has been Linked to chromosomal amplification at 17q22-q24, which is associated with breast cancer cell proliferation [29]. The pro-apoptotic WWOX gene, on the other hand, acts as a tumor suppressor, with its phosphorylation at Tyr33 (pY33-WWOX) during early cancer stages inhibiting tumor initiation [30, 31]. Similarly, PLXNB2, a receptor involved in cell migration and angiogenesis, is highly expressed in various malignancies, including breast cancer, and correlates with poor prognosis [32, 33]. ZNF394 functions as a transcriptional repressor and has been identified as a positive prognostic factor in lung squamous cell carcinoma [34]. Meanwhile, NAB2, a nuclear polyadenylated RNA-binding protein, plays a role in mRNP biogenesis, though its specific contributions to cancer development remain unclear [35]. LEF1, another transcription factor, has been implicated in cancer progression, with studies showing that its silencing can inhibit proliferation and induce apoptosis in colorectal cancer cells [36].

  While previous studies have primarily focused on TF diagnostic models based on immune-related genes or algorithms, our approach used machine learning algorithms to identify TF genes associated with breast cancer prognosis and construct predictive models. The RSF learning method, combined with univariate Cox regression, was employed to evaluate the prognostic value of TF genes, leading to the construction of the MDTS prediction model. Unsupervised clustering divided patients into two distinct subgroups (high MDTS and low MDTS), with subsequent analysis revealing differences in immune infiltration, immunotherapy response, prognosis, and chemotherapy outcomes.

  The TME consists of a complex network of tumor cells, stromal cells, immune cells, and cytokines that can influence tumor progression. Among the components of the TME, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are key players in promoting tumor invasion and metastasis [37]. TAMs, particularly those with an M2-like phenotype, contribute to immunosuppression and are associated with poor prognosis in breast cancer [38, 39].

  Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is another critical process in breast cancer metastasis, characterized by the loss of epithelial characteristics and the acquisition of a mesenchymal phenotype, which enhances cell motility and invasiveness [40]. The differential expression of genes involved in EMT suggests potential pathways and signaling molecules that could be targeted for therapeutic intervention, though further research is needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

  Our study also identified genetic alterations, including gene deletions, amplifications, mutations, and CNA. TMB has been proposed as a predictive marker for response to ICIs, with studies showing that tumors with high TMB are more responsive to such therapies [41]. Our analysis revealed that TMB was higher in the high MDTS group, suggesting that these patients might benefit more from immunotherapy. The TIDE algorithm was used to predict responses to ICIs, and our findings indicate that patients in the low-risk group may have a better response to anti-PD1 and anti-PDL1 therapies [42, 43].


## Conclusions
  In summary, this study developed a novel prognostic model by integrating TF gene profiles and utilizing the multiple machine learning algorithms. Six key TF genes were identified as significant predictors of patient outcomes. The MDTS model effectively stratified patients into high and low subgroups, revealing distinct differences in immune infiltration, treatment strategy, and overall prognosis. Notably, our findings indicate that low MDTS patients, characterized by a higher immune infiltration and lower TMB, may respond better to immune checkpoint inhibitors, whereas the high MDTS group, with a lower immune response and higher TMB, may benefit more from chemotherapy. This stratification provides a potential avenue for personalized treatment strategies, optimizing therapeutic outcomes based on the specific molecular and immune profiles of the patients.

  The significance of this study lies in its innovative approach to combining machine learning with TF gene profiling to enhance the predictive accuracy of breast cancer prognosis. By providing insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying tumor progression and identifying potential therapeutic targets, this research contributes to the advancement of precision oncology. Future studies should further explore the clinical application of the MDTS model in larger and more diverse patient cohorts, as well as investigate the therapeutic potential of the identified targets to improve treatment outcomes for breast cancer patients.
