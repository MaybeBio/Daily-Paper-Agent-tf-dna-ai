# Dorsal determinant Hwa stabilizes β-catenin through direct inhibition of GSK3


## Abstract
  In this study, Ji et al. show that the transmembrane protein HWA stabilizes β-catenin and promotes AXIN degradation through a mechanism dependent on GSK3-mediated phosphorylation and kinase inhibition. This mechanism is similar to that of LRP6's regulation of WNT signaling, reflecting a convergence of molecular mechanisms regulating β-catenin signaling under different biological contexts.


## Results

### Tankyrase is not essential for Hwa-induced β-catenin signaling
  Tankyrase-mediated AXIN degradation has been implicated in Hwa-induced β-catenin stabilization (Yan et al. 2018). To investigate the precise mechanism by which Hwa regulates tankyrase-mediated AXIN degradation, we stably overexpressed Hwa in HEK293 cells harboring the SuperTOP-Flash (STF) TCF reporter. Consistent with the previous findings (Yan et al. 2018), overexpression of Hwa strongly increased STF reporter activity (Fig. 1A). Intriguingly, treatment of TNKS656, a tankyrase inhibitor (Shultz et al. 2013), had only a moderate effect on Hwa-induced STF activity, though TNKS656 completely inhibited WNT3a conditioned medium (CM)-induced STF activity (Fig. 1A). Consistent with STF reporter data, TNKS656 inhibited WNT3a-induced β-catenin stabilization but had minimal effects on Hwa-induced β-catenin stabilization (Fig. 1B). The protein expression of AXIN1 was robustly enhanced by TNKS656 (Fig. 1B), confirming the tankyrase inhibitory activity of TNKS656. These findings suggest that tankyrase is not essential for Hwa-mediated β-catenin stabilization.


### The PPNSP motif is essential for the β-catenin-stabilizing activity of Hwa
  If Hwa activates β-catenin signaling through promoting tankyrase-dependent AXIN degradation, TNKS656 is expected to abolish Hwa-induced β-catenin signaling. Our finding that TNKS656 has limited effects on Hwa-dependent β-catenin stabilization suggests that the underlying mechanism of Hwa-induced β-catenin stabilization remains largely unknown. It has been shown previously that the conserved PPNSP and RRSST motifs are essential for the in vivo activity of Hwa (Yan et al. 2018). To examine the role of the PPNSP motif and the RRSST motif, we generated HEK293 cells stably expressing Hwa deletion mutants and assessed the activity of these mutants in the STF reporter assay (Fig. 2A; Supplemental Fig. S1A). The Hwa mutant lacking the extracellular domain (Hwa ΔECD) showed slightly higher activity than the full-length Hwa protein. Hwa ΔPPNSP completely lost STF stimulatory activity. Conversely, Hwa ΔRRSST decreased STF stimulatory activity by 50% (Fig. 2A). These results suggest that the PPNSP motif is essential for Hwa-mediated β-catenin signaling in cell culture, while the RRSST motif also plays a role. Next, we performed an alanine scanning experiment to identify key residues of the PPNSP motif required for Hwa activity. Alanine substitution of each of the core five amino acid residues significantly reduced the STF stimulatory activity of Hwa (Fig. 2B). The S168T mutation does not affect the activity of Hwa in the STF assay (Fig. 2B). Substitution of residues outside the core PPNSP motif to alanine had weak effects on Hwa activity (Fig. 2B). Finally, we examined the effect of overexpression of Hwa mutants on cytoplasmic β-catenin and AXIN1 (Fig. 2C). The changes in cytoplasmic β-catenin protein levels corresponded well with STF activity, supporting the importance of the core PPNSP motif. Notably, overexpression of Hwa did not have a strong effect on AXIN1 expression in cells stably expressing Hwa (Fig. 2C). We observed that Hwa R160A and R172A mutants induced stronger STF reporter activity and β-catenin stabilization. Although R172A exhibited slightly higher expression levels than wild-type Hwa, R160A did not. The molecular basis underlying the elevated signaling activity of R160A and R172A is unclear.


### The PPNSP motif of Hwa protein is phosphorylated at Ser168 by GSK3
  The intracellular domain of WNT coreceptor LRP6 contains five conserved PPPSP motifs (motif A to motif E), which are phosphorylated by GSK3 and are essential for the function of LRP6 in WNT signaling (Tamai et al. 2004; Zeng et al. 2005, 2008; MacDonald et al. 2008). Fusion of a single PPPSP motif of LRP6 to truncated LDLR (LDLRΔN) is sufficient to activate β-catenin signaling upon overexpression (MacDonald et al. 2008). Interestingly, the PPNSP motif of Hwa protein closely resembles the PPPSP motif of LRP6. Given this similarity, we investigated whether the PPNSP motif (ERVNTVPPNSPVLRVT) of Hwa can activate the STF reporter in LDLRΔN-based constructs. Our results showed that the PPNSP motif of Hwa activated the STF reporter to the same extent as PPPSP motif A of LRP6 in the LDLRΔN background (Fig. 2D; Supplemental Fig. S1B), suggesting functional equivalence between these motifs. We also confirmed that alanine substitution of the five core residues of the PPNSP motif completely abolished Hwa activity in the LDLRΔN background. The S168T substitution of the PPNSP motif did not affect its ability to stimulate the STF reporter. These findings suggest that the PPNSP motif is sufficient to activate β-catenin signaling in the LDLRΔN background and that the conserved core residues in the motif are required for this activity.

  Given the considerable sequence and functional similarities between the PPNSP motif of Hwa and the PPPSP motif of LRP6, we hypothesized that Ser168 within the PPNSP motif may undergo phosphorylation. To test this, we developed a Hwa p-S168 antibody that specifically recognizes Hwa only when it is phosphorylated at Ser168. This antibody recognized ectopically expressed Hwa but not the Hwa S168A mutant (Fig. 3A). Treatment of immunoprecipitated Hwa-HA with λ protein phosphatase (λ-PPase) completely abolished the binding of the Hwa p-S168 antibody (Fig. 3B), indicating that S168 of Hwa is phosphorylated in cells. Next, we sought to identify the kinase responsible for phosphorylating Ser168 of the PPNSP motif of Hwa. Given that GSK3 is known to phosphorylate PPPSP motifs of LRP6 (Zeng et al. 2005, 2008), we hypothesized that GSK3 may also phosphorylate the PPNSP motif of Hwa. We treated immunoprecipitated Hwa-HA with λ protein phosphatase and subjected the dephosphorylated Hwa to an in vitro GSK3β kinase assay. As shown in Figure 3C, Hwa is phosphorylated by recombinant GSK3β at Ser168 in the presence of ATP. In addition, treatment of cells with GSK3 inhibitor BIO or CHIR 99021 reduced the phosphorylation of Ser168 of ectopically expressed Hwa (Fig. 3D,E). Together, these results suggest that the PPNSP motif of Hwa is phosphorylated by GSK3 and that this phosphorylation is critical for Hwa-induced β-catenin signaling.


### Inhibition of GSK3 activity by the phospho-PPNSP motif of Hwa
  It has been shown that phosphorylated PPPSP motifs of LRP6 inhibit GSK3 by engaging the kinase as a pseudosubstrate (Mi et al. 2006; Piao et al. 2008; Wu et al. 2009; Stamos et al. 2014). To determine whether the phosphorylated PPNSP motif of Hwa could similarly inhibit GSK3, we synthesized the phos-Hwa 161–174 peptide of Hwa and the phos-LRP6 motif A peptide and tested these peptides in GSK3β activity assays. In the Tau phosphorylation assay, the phos-Hwa 161–174 peptide, but not the Hwa 161–174 S168A peptide, strongly inhibited GSK3-induced Tau phosphorylation (Fig. 4A). In fact, the phos-Hwa peptide has much stronger inhibitory activity toward GSK3β than the phos-LRP6 motif A peptide. Next, we tested the phos-Hwa and phos-LRP6 motif A peptides in the GSK3β ADP-Glo assay. As shown in Figure 4B, the phos-Hwa 161–174 peptide, but not the Hwa 161–174 S168A peptide, potently inhibited GSK3β, and this activity is significantly stronger than that of the phos-LRP6 motif A peptide.

  Our finding that the PPNSP motif of Hwa has stronger GSK3 inhibitory activity than the PPPSP motif of LRP6 suggests an important role of Asn167 of the Hwa PPNSP motif in GSK3 inhibition. To investigate this, we mutated Asn167 of full-length Hwa to all 19 possible amino acid residues and tested the effects of these mutations in a Hwa-induced STF reporter assay. As seen in Figure 4C, N167P and N167C mutants retained ∼20% of the activity of wild-type Hwa, whereas all other substitutions abolished the activity of Hwa in the STF reporter assay. Consistent with STF reporter assay, the phos-Hwa N167A peptide lost GSK3 inhibitory activity, wherease the phos-Hwa N167P peptide retained GSK3 inhibitory activity, though this activity is weaker than that of the wild-type phos-Hwa peptide (Fig. 4D). These results suggest that Asn167 of the PPNSP motif is essential for the β-catenin stabilizing and GSK3 inhibitory activities of Hwa, with only Pro and Cys being partially tolerated at this position.


### Molecular dynamics simulations
  Next, we conducted molecular dynamics simulations to understand the binding between the PPNSP motif and GSK3. Our findings revealed that without phosphorylation of the serine residue, the PPNSP peptide was quickly released from GSK3's active site (Fig. 5A,B). Furthermore, the peptides pSPPNpSP, SPPNpSP, APNpSP, PANpSP, PPNpSP, PPPpSP, and PPApSP remained bound during 500 nsec simulations, with the lowest-binding free energies observed for the PPPpSP and PPNpSP peptides (Fig. 5C,D), which is consistent with our cellular data. We further estimated the binding stability contributed by asparagine residue using the Rosetta Flex ddG mutagenesis protocol. The PPNpSP motif exhibited the highest binding affinity, whereas the mutation of asparagine to proline or cysteine demonstrated affinity closer to the wild type (ddG < 0.5 kJ/mol) compared with other mutations, except for leucine (Fig. 5E). We also compared the PPNpSP and PPPpSP peptides for their binding to GSK3. We performed an additional 1 µsec dynamics simulation for both peptides, and both remained bound to the active site throughout the simulation, with no dissociation observed. From the RMSD analysis results, the PPPpSP peptide exhibits higher backbone rigidity compared with the PPNpSP peptide (Fig. 6A). However, the PPNpSP peptide maintains a higher number of hydrogen bonds (Fig. 6B,C) through its asparagine residue (Fig. 6D) during the simulation, which may explain its higher binding stability. Taken together, these findings highlight the importance of asparagine and phospho-serine residues of the PPNpSP motif on binding affinity and stability in the active site of GSK3.


### Mapping the region of AXIN responsible for Hwa binding
  We demonstrated that phosphorylated Hwa inhibits GSK3 activity. Given the important role of GSK3 in multiple biological processes, it is plausible that Hwa interacts with AXIN to confer signaling specificity by specifically inhibiting AXIN-associated GSK3. Indeed, previous studies have reported an interaction between Hwa and AXIN (Yan et al. 2018), though the region of AXIN responsible for this interaction remains undefined. To delineate the Hwa-binding region within AXIN, we conducted coimmunoprecipitation assays by coexpressing Hwa-HA and Axin1-FLAG truncation mutants (Fig. 7A,B). Our results show that full-length Axin1 (residues 1–832), the DIX domain deletion mutant (1–756), and the C-terminal fragment (502–832) all associated with Hwa, whereas the C-terminal truncation mutant (1–501) did not. These results suggest that the region between the β-catenin binding domain and the DIX domain of AXIN mediates the interaction with Hwa.


### Oligomerization of Hwa enhances its activity in β-catenin stabilization
  Although overexpression of Hwa induces β-catenin signaling, it remains unclear how Hwa is activated during the physiological setting to stabilize β-catenin. Given that oligomerization is a common mechanism for activation of membrane receptors, we hypothesized that oligomerization of Hwa might promote its activity in β-catenin signaling. To test this hypothesis, we generated a Hwa chimeric protein containing an N-terminal myristoylation signal (myr), two copies of the dimerization domain (DmrB), and the intracellular domain of Hwa. HEK293 cells stably expressing wild-type Hwa and a Myr-2xDmrB-Hwa fusion were treated with the synthetic B/B homodimerizer to induce oligomerization of Myr-2xDmrB-Hwa fusion. As seen in Figure 7C, wild-type Hwa and Myr-2xDmrB-Hwa fusion stabilized β-catenin to the same degree, and the B/B homodimerizer drastically increased the β-catenin-stabilizing activity of the Myr-2xDmrB-Hwa fusion protein. These results suggest that oligomerization of Hwa strongly increases its activity in β-catenin stabilization.


### Potential interaction between Hwa and Dishevelled
  Having established that artificial oligomerization of Hwa strongly increased its signaling activity, we wondered whether endogenous factors might promote oligomerization and downstream signaling of Hwa. DVL represents an intriguing candidate that might promote the oligomerization of Hwa. In WNT/β-catenin signaling, oligomerization of DVL enhances its local concentrations at the plasma membrane, facilitates AXIN recruitment to the WNT receptor complex, and promotes the assembly of a larger and more stable receptor signaling complex (Gammons and Bienz 2018). Notably, directional translocation of DVL clusters along microtubules during cortical rotations, and its subsequent enrichments on the prospective dorsal side have been documented in Xenopus embryos (Miller et al. 1999). To assess whether Hwa might interact with DVL, we fused the intracellular domain of Hwa to the C terminus of GFP (GFP-Hwa ICD) and coexpressed it with either an empty vector or DVL2-HA in U2OS cells, followed by immunofluorescence analysis. The GFP-Hwa ICD expressed alone localized predominantly to the nucleus (Fig. 7D). In contrast, coexpression with DVL2 resulted in colocalization of the GFP-Hwa ICD with DVL2 in cytoplasmic puncta (Fig. 7D). As a control, DVL2 coexpression did not change the localization of GFP (Supplemental Fig. S2A). The C terminus of Hwa contains a type II PDZ-binding motif (Φ-x-Φ), which could potentially interact with the PDZ domain of DVL. However, deletion of this motif of Hwa did not impair DVL2-mediated relocalization of the GFP-Hwa ICD (Supplemental Fig. S2A). Consistent with the localization data, GFP-Hwa ICD ΔPDZ interacted with DVL2 in a coimmunoprecipitation assay (Supplemental Fig. S2B). Together, these results suggest that Hwa might interact with DVL and that this interaction does not require the PDZ binding motif of Hwa.

  To determine whether DVL is required for Hwa-induced β-catenin signaling, we ectopically expressed wild-type and mutant forms of DVL2 in HEK293 DVL1/2/3 triple-knockout cells (DVL TKO) cells (Jiang et al. 2015) and assessed β-catenin stabilization in response to Wnt3a or Hwa overexpression. Consistent with previous findings (Gammons et al. 2016), wild-type DVL2 and the DVL2 ΔPDZ mutant restored Wnt3a-induced β-catenin stabilization, whereas the DVL2 polymerization-deficient M2M4 mutant and DVL2 ΔDEP mutant failed to do so (Supplemental Fig. S2C). In contrast, expression of wild-type or mutant DVL2 did not show a clear effect on Hwa-induced β-catenin stabilization (Supplemental Fig. S2D). These results suggest that DVL is not essential for Hwa-induced β-catenin signaling under overexpression conditions.


### Hwa-induced AXIN1 degradation is SIAH1-dependent and tankyrase-independent
  It has been proposed that Hwa induces β-catenin stabilization through promoting tankyrase-mediated AXIN degradation (Yan et al. 2018). However, we did not observe strong changes in AXIN1 levels in HEK293 cells stably expressing Hwa (Fig. 2D). Interestingly, although stable expression of Hwa or Myr-2xDmrB-Hwa fusion did not reduce AXIN1 expression, treatment with the B/B homodimerizer clearly reduced protein levels of AXIN1 in cells stably expressing Myr-2xDmrB-Hwa fusion (Fig. 7C). These results suggest that the discrepancy between our findings and published results is likely due to the extent of Hwa overexpression. Therefore, we switched to a transient transfection system to achieve higher levels of Hwa overexpression. Indeed, transient transfection of a construct coding wild-type Hwa, but not the Hwa S168A mutant, reduced protein levels of AXIN1 (Fig. 7E), highlighting the importance of Ser168 of the PPNSP motif for facilitating Hwa-mediated AXIN1 degradation.

  We have previously identified two distinct mechanisms mediating AXIN degradation. AXIN is PARylated by tankyrase, and PARylated AXIN is recognized by RNF146 and degraded by the ubiquitin–proteasome system (Huang et al. 2009; Callow et al. 2011; Zhang et al. 2011). SIAH1 mediates WNT-induced degradation of AXIN (Ji et al. 2017). Mechanistically, SIAH1 binds to the GSK3-binding domain of AXIN and GSK3 and SIAH1 compete for AXIN binding. WNT-induced LRP6 phosphorylation inhibits AXIN-associated GSK3, which decreases the association between AXIN and GSK3, allowing SIAH1 to bind and degrade free AXIN. We next tested which of these two mechanisms mediates Hwa-induced AXIN degradation. Using the transient transfection system, we showed that the TNKS inhibitor TNKS656 did not prevent Hwa-induced AXIN1 degradation (Fig. 7F). In contrast, depletion of SIAH1 blocked Hwa-induced AXIN1 degradation (Fig. 7G; Supplemental Fig. S3), suggesting that SIAH1 mediates Hwa-induced AXIN1 degradation. Based on our data, we propose a model of Hwa-induced β-catenin stabilization (Fig. 7H). Accumulation of Hwa at the plasma membrane of the prospective dorsal blastomeres leads to its activation, which might involve oligomerization. Once activated, Hwa binds to AXIN and is phosphorylated by AXIN-associated GSK3 at the PPNSP motif. The phosphorylated PPNSP motif then inhibits AXIN-associated GSK3, leading to the stabilization of β-catenin. Inhibition of GSK3 also allows SIAH1 to bind to free AXIN to promote its degradation.


## Discussion
  Hwa has recently been identified as a dorsal determinant responsible for β-catenin activation in dorsal organizer formation. In this study, we have uncovered the molecular mechanism by which Hwa activates β-catenin signaling. We have shown that Hwa activates β-catenin signaling through a mechanism analogous to that of LRP6. The highly conserved PPNSP motif in the intracellular domain of Hwa is phosphorylated by GSK3, which in turn inhibits GSK3. Inhibition of GSK3 by Hwa induces β-catenin stabilization and SIAH1-dependent AXIN degradation. Oligomerization significantly enhances the β-catenin stabilization activity and AXIN degradation activity of Hwa. Our study highlights a striking example of molecular convergence as two distinct membrane proteins activate β-catenin signaling under different biological contexts through very similar molecular mechanisms.

  Although Hwa and LRP6 activate β-catenin signaling through similar mechanisms, the upstream regulation of Hwa and LRP6 in β-catenin signaling differs. The short extracellular domain of Hwa is not required for the function of Hwa in vivo, suggesting that Hwa does not have an extracellular ligand. In contrast, binding of WNT to the large extracellular domain of LRP6 is essential for LRP6-mediated β-catenin signaling. It is unclear how Hwa is regulated in vivo. It is possible that the accumulation of Hwa to the plasma membrane of prospective dorsal blastomeres is sufficient to stabilize β-catenin. Alternatively, a more sophisticated mechanism might promote the activity of Hwa in β-catenin signaling. Oligomerization of LRP6 is critical for its activity in WNT signaling. Artificial dimerization of ectopically expressed LRP6 significantly increases its activity (Cong et al. 2004). Furthermore, dimerization of endogenous LRP6 using the LRP6 antibody potentiates WNT-induced β-catenin signaling (Ettenberg et al. 2010; Gong et al. 2010). WNT simultaneously binds to FZD and LRP6, increases the binding between FZD and DVL, and recruits DVL polymers to the WNT receptor complex, driving the oligomerization of LRP6 and formation of the LRP6 signalosome (Bilić et al. 2007). Through DVL–AXIN interaction, membrane-localized DVL brings the AXIN complex to the LRP6 signalosome, where LRP6 is phosphorylated by AXIN-associated GSK3 (Zeng et al. 2005, 2008; Schwarz-Romond et al. 2007; Gammons and Bienz 2018). Our finding that artificial oligomerization of Hwa markedly increased its signaling activity raises the possibility that a specific physiological mechanism may promote Hwa oligomerization in vivo. Through binding to both FZD and AXIN, oligomerization of DVL promotes the recruitment of AXIN to the WNT receptor complex and the assembly of the larger and more stable WNT receptor complex. In addition, microtubule-dependent translocation of DVL clusters to the future dorsal side of the Xenopus embryo has been reported (Miller et al. 1999; Weaver and Kimelman 2004). Therefore, DVL represents an interesting candidate that might promote Hwa oligomerization in vivo. Consistent with this hypothesis, we detected a potential interaction between Hwa and DVL, as the GFP-Hwa ICD colocalized and coimmunoprecipitated with coexpressed DVL2. However, we were unable to demonstrate a requirement for DVL in Hwa-induced β-catenin stabilization. Previous studies have shown that overexpression of LRP6 can activate β-catenin signaling in Drosophila S2 cells in a DVL-independent manner (Cong et al. 2004), though endogenous LRP6-mediated β-catenin signaling is clearly DVL-dependent. It is therefore possible that strong overexpression of Hwa in cultured cells bypasses the requirement of DVL. On the other hand, early dorsal fate specification appeared to be unaffected in maternal and zygotic DVL mutants in zebrafish (Xing et al. 2018), which does not seem to support an essential role for DVL in maternal β-catenin signaling. Thus, the potential role of DVL in Hwa-mediated maternal β-catenin signaling remains unclear and warrants further investigation. Beyond oligomerization, other mechanisms might regulate the activity of Hwa. It has been shown that ZNRF3 is essential for restricting the spatial activity of Hwa in Xenopus, and inhibition of ZNRF3 induces accumulation of Hwa (Zhu et al. 2021). Additionally, Hwa-induced β-catenin signaling requires lysosomal acidification and MVB-forming machinery in Xenopus (Tejeda-Muñoz and De Robertis 2022).

  Our data suggest that phosphorylated Hwa activates β-catenin signaling by inhibiting GSK3. To achieve pathway specificity without broadly disrupting other GSK3-dependent processes, phosphorylated Hwa must selectively inhibit the pool of GSK3 associated with AXIN. This specificity is likely mediated through the interaction between Hwa and AXIN. Unlike LRP6, which binds a region N-terminal to the GSK3 binding domain (Gammons et al. 2025), Hwa binds to the region located between the β-catenin binding domain and the DIX domain. This region of AXIN has been implicated in the binding of other AXIN-associated proteins such as PP2A (Hsu et al. 1999), MKK4 (Luo et al. 2003), Arcadia (Liu et al. 2006), and HIPK2 (Rui et al. 2004). Further biochemical and biophysical studies will be necessary to characterize the molecular details of the Hwa–AXIN interaction and to explore potential regulatory mechanisms governing this association.

  The five PPPSP motifs in the intracellular domain of LRP6 show clear cooperativity (MacDonald et al. 2008; Wolf et al. 2008). Deletion of one PPPSP motif compromises the activity of LRP6, whereas deletion of two PPPSP motifs mostly abolishes the activity of LRP6. Therefore, multiplicity and cooperativity of the PPPSP motifs amplify LRP6-mediated WNT signaling. It is interesting how Hwa can efficiently activate downstream signaling with a single PPNSP motif. Our research has shown that the phos-PPNSP Hwa peptide has significantly higher GSK3 inhibitory activity compared with the phos-PPPSP LRP6 motif A peptide or the phos-PPPSP Hwa mutant peptide (Fig. 4A,B,D). Note that motif A is one of the most potent PPPSP motifs of LRP6 in a GSK3 inhibition assay (Wu et al. 2009). Consistent with GSK3 inhibition data, mutation of Asn167 of the PPNSP motif to Pro strongly decreased the activity of Hwa in β-catenin signaling (Fig. 4C). Therefore, the PPNSP motif has stronger GSK3 inhibitory activity and β-catenin signaling activity than the PPPSP motif, which might explain why a single PPNSP motif is sufficient for the activity of Hwa. Our work suggests that phosphorylation of Ser168 of the PPNSP motif of Hwa is mediated by GSK3. Similar to the mechanism proposed for LRP6 (Zeng et al. 2008), Hwa likely binds to AXIN and is phosphorylated by AXIN-associated GSK3, which in turn inhibits this population of GSK3. The PPPSP motif of LRP6 can be phosphorylated by kinases other than GSK3; for example, phosphorylation of the PPPSP motif by the Cyclin Y/CDK14 complex has been shown to promote mitotic WNT signaling (Davidson et al. 2009; Niehrs and Acebron 2012). Similarly, the PPNSP motif of Hwa might also be phosphorylated by kinases other than GSK3 (Li et al. 2024), which then inhibits AXIN-associated GSK3.

  The structural basis of GSK3 inhibition by the phosphorylated PPPSP motif of LRP6 has been determined (Stamos et al. 2014). A loop of GSK3 undergoes a significant conformation change and clamps the bound phosphorylated LRP6 PPPSP motif. The phosphorylated residue of the PPPSP peptide binds to the predicted P+4 binding pocket of primed GSK3 substrate binding sites (Dajani et al. 2001; Frame et al. 2001; ter Haar et al. 2001) and blocks the binding of GSK3 to its other substrates. Our molecular dynamics simulation reveals that the unphosphorylated PPNSP peptide is released from the active site of GSK3 immediately when the simulation starts (Fig. 5A), consistent with the notion that GSK3 prefers binding to “primed” substrates. In our simulation, the PPNpSP peptide has lower free binding energy to GSK3 than the PPPpSP peptide (Fig. 5D,E), consistent with stronger GSK3 inhibitory activity of the phosphorylated PPNSP peptide (Fig. 4A,B,D). The asparagine residue of the PPNSP motif plays a pivotal role in stabilizing the interaction between Hwa and GSK3, potentially via the establishment of hydrogen bonds (Fig. 6D). The strong binding of the phosphorylated PPNSP motif of Hwa to GSK3, facilitated by the hydrogen bonding capacity of the asparagine residue, blocks the entry of the next substrate, thereby exerting an inhibitory effect on GSK3 activity.

  It has been proposed that tankyrase-dependent degradation of AXIN mediates Hwa-induced β-catenin stabilization (Yan et al. 2018). We have found that stable expression of Hwa stabilized β-catenin without significantly affecting AXIN1 protein levels (Fig. 2C), suggesting that AXIN degradation is not essential for Hwa-induced β-catenin stabilization. Interestingly, oligomerization of stably expressed Hwa or strong overexpression of Hwa through transient transfection clearly decreased AXIN1 expression (Fig. 7C,E), and this activity is mediated by SIAH1 (Fig. 7G). It is well known that WNT induces AXIN degradation (Willert et al. 1999; Yamamoto et al. 1999). We have previously demonstrated that SIAH1 serves as the E3 responsible for WNT-induced AXIN degradation (Ji et al. 2017). Mechanistically, SIAH1 binds to a VxP motif in the GSK3-binding domain of AXIN, and SIAH1 and GSK3 compete for AXIN binding. WNT induces phosphorylation of LRP6, and phosphorylated LRP6 inhibits GSK3, promoting dissociation of AXIN and GSK3 and allowing SIAH1 to engage free AXIN for degradation. Hwa likely induces AXIN degradation through a similar mechanism. The finding that Hwa-induced AXIN degradation is mediated by SIAH1 indirectly supports our conclusion that Hwa stabilizes β-catenin through the inhibition of GSK3. WNT-induced AXIN degradation amplifies WNT signaling, as SIAH1 knockout blocks AXIN degradation but only partially reduces WNT signaling (Ji et al. 2017). Similarly, Hwa-induced AXIN degradation is likely not essential for Hwa-induced β-catenin stabilization and only amplifies the activity of Hwa. During manuscript preparation, Li et al. (2024) reported a critical role for phosphorylation of Ser168 in regulating Hwa function both in vitro and in vivo, consistent with our findings. However, unlike our study, their work suggests that Ser168 phosphorylation enhances the interaction between Hwa and tankyrase (Li et al. 2024). Given that tankyrase is not essential for Hwa-induced β-catenin signaling and AXIN degradation, it is more likely that phosphorylated Hwa induces β-catenin signaling through inhibiting AXIN-associated GSK3.

  Our study uncovers a compelling example of evolutionary convergence, demonstrating that both Hwa and LRP5/6 activate β-catenin signaling through remarkably similar mechanisms. This observation raises the intriguing possibility that other proteins harboring GSK3 inhibitory motifs may also trigger WNT-independent β-catenin signaling in specific species or in distinct biological contexts. We were unable to identify any membrane proteins containing the PPNSP motif within the human genome. Whether such proteins exist in other organisms remains an open question warranting further investigation.


## Materials and methods

### Cell culture, constructs, and transfection
  HEK293T and U2OS cells were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). HEK293 TREX cells were purchased from Invitrogen. HEK293T and HEK293 TREX cells were grown in DMEM (Invitrogen) with 10% FBS (Seradigm) and penicillin/streptomycin (Invitrogen). All cultures were grown at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator and tested free of mycoplasma contamination. C-terminal HA-tagged Hwa and Hwa mutants were cloned into pcDNA4TO. N-terminal VSV-G-tagged LDLRΔN constructs were generated as previously described (MacDonald et al. 2008) by replacing the last 11 amino acids of LDLRΔN with the Hwa PPNSP motif (residues 161–174) and LRP6 PPPSP motif A (residues 1483–1497). C-terminal FLAG-tagged mouse Axin1 constructs were cloned into pCMV-3Tag8. DVL2 constructs were obtained from Gammons and Bienz and recloned into a lentiviral vector with the PGK promoter. All constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing. Plasmid transfection was done by FuGene6 (Promega) according to the manufacturer's instructions. siRNA transfection was done by Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's instructions. siRNA duplex against SIAH1 was obtained from Qiagen and has been validated previously (Ji et al. 2017). The target sequence of SIAH1 siRNA was 5′-ACCTAAAACTCTTTCGGTA-3′. pGL2 siRNA was used as a neutral control. HEK293 TRX STF cells stably expressing Hwa were generated by transfecting cells with Hwa-HA pcDNA4O. Pools of stably transfected cells were treated with doxycycline to induce Hwa expression. The iDimerize inducible homodimer system was purchased from Takara Bio. TNKS656 was synthesized by Novartis (Shultz et al. 2013).


### Immunoblotting, cytosolic fractionation, and immunoprecipitation
  For immunoblotting, total cell lysates were prepared by direct lysis of cells with NP40 lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl at pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1% nonidet P-40, 10% glycerol, 1 mM EDTA, protease inhibitors, phosphatase inhibitors) followed by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C. Equal amounts of proteins were resolved by SDS-PAGE, transferred to nitrocellulose membrane, and probed with the indicated antibodies. For immunoprecipitation experiments, cleared cell lysates were incubated with HA tag antibody for 2 h at 4°C and then incubated with Protein A/G-conjugated magnetic beads (Thermo) overnight. The beads were washed five times with lysis buffer, and bound proteins were eluted in SDS sample buffer for immunoblotting analysis. For cytosolic fractionation, the cells were harvested in cold PBS, and the cell pellet was resuspended in hypertonic buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl at pH 7.5, 10 mM KCl) followed by four rounds of freezing and thawing. Cleared supernatants were collected after full-speed centrifugation.

  The antibodies used were as follows: tubulin (MilliporeSigma), ACTIN (Cell Signaling Technology), active β-catenin (Cell Signaling Technology), HA (Roche), FLAG (Sigma), AXIN1 (Cell Signaling Technology), DVL2 (Cell Signaling Technology), Tau (R&D Systems), phos-Tau (Cell Signaling Technology), GFP (Clontech), and VSV-G (Abcam). Hwa phos-S168 antibody was produced by New England Peptide. Briefly, two rabbits were immunized with Hwa phos-S168 peptide (Ac-CTVPPNpSPVLRV-amide) for three rounds. Serum was positively selected with phospho-Hwa peptide and then subjected to multiple rounds of negative selection with nonphospho-Hwa peptide.


### In vitro Hwa phosphorylation assay
  HEK293 cells stably expressing Hwa-HA were lysed in NP40 lysis buffer. Cleared cell lysates were incubated with anti-HA magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After extensive washing, immunoprecipitated Hwa was dephosphorylated by λ protein phosphatase (New England Biolabs) following the manufacturer's instructions. To test phosphorylation of Hwa by GSK3β, dephosphorylated Hwa was incubated with recombinant active GSK3β (R&D Systems) in kinase assay buffer (25 mM HEPES at pH 7.5, 20 mM β-glycerophosphate, 25 mM MgCl2, 2 mM DTT, 10 mM ATP) for 2 h at 37°C. Reaction products were subjected to immunoblotting using Hwa pS168 antibody.


### In vitro GSK3β kinase assay using Tau as a substrate
  To measure the inhibitory activity of the phos-Hwa PPNSP motif and phos-LRP6 motif A in a GSK3β kinase assay, the following peptides were ordered from New England Peptide: Phos-Hwa 161–174 [VNTVPPN(pS)PVLRVT], Hwa161–174 S168A (VNTVPPNAPVLRVT), Phos-Hwa161–174 N167A [VNTVPPA(pS)PVLRVT], Phos-Hwa161–174 N167P [VNTVPPP(pS)VLRVT], Phos-LRP6 motif A: [LNPPP(pS)PA(pT)ERSH], and LRP6 motif A S1490A (LNPPPAPAAERSH).

  GSK3β kinase activity assay with Tau as a substrate was performed as described previously (Wu et al. 2009). Tau phosphorylation reaction was performed in kinase buffer (25 mM HEPES at pH 7.5, 20 mM β-glycerophosphate, 25 mM MgCl2, 2 mM DTT, 10 mM ATP) containing 0.43 µM active GSK3β (R&D Systems), 0.8 µM Tau protein (R&D Systems), and serially diluted Hwa or LRP6 peptides for 1 h at 37°C. Phosphorylation products were analyzed by immunoblotting with phospho-Tau antibody.


### In vitro GSK3β ADP-Glo assay
  ADP-Glo kinase assay was performed using the GSK3β kinase enzyme system (Promega V9371) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, 1 ng of GSK3β enzyme, 50 µM GSK3 substrate (YRRAAVPPSPSLSRHSSPHQpSEDEEE, derived from human muscle glycogen synthase 1), and 40 µM ATP were incubated in varying concentrations of peptide inhibitors in 1× kinase buffer in a total volume of 10 µL for 1 h at room temperature. Five microliters of ADP-Glo reagent was added into kinase reactions for 40 min, followed by incubation with 10 µL of kinase detection reagent for 30 min at room temperature. Kinase activity was measured using a luminescence plate reader (PerkinElmer EnVision).


### STF reporter assay
  HEK293 TREX STF cells stably expressing Hwa and its mutants were plated in 96 well plates in doxycycline-containing medium and subjected to the indicated treatments. The STF reporter activity was examined by Bright-Glo assay (Promega). LDLRΔN constructs containing the PPNSP motif of Hwa or the PPPSP motif of LRP6 were transiently transfected into HEK293T cells together with the STF reporter, and STF reporter activity was measured by Bright-Glo assay.


### Immunofluorescence assay
  U2OS cells were plated in collagen I-coated 8 chamber slides (ibidi) and transfected the next day. Forty-eight hours after transfection, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min, and blocked with 2% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. The cells were incubated with Alexa fluor 647-conjugated HA antibody (Thermo Fisher) at 1:200 dilution for 3 h at room temperature. The cells were washed three times with PBS, and images were acquired using the Echo microscope system.


### Complex modeling of GSK3 and inhibitory peptides
  The complex structure of GSK3 bound to PPNpSPV peptide was modeled based on the crystal structure of the GSK3/LRP6 e-motif complex (PDB ID: 4nm7) using the SWISS-MODEL server. The mutations of the PPNpSPV peptide, including PPPpSPV, PPApSPV, PANpSPV, SPPNpSPV, pSPPNpSPV, APNpSPV, and PPNSPV, were generated using the PyMol mutagenesis wizard tool. All of these complexes were protonated and prepared using the Schrodinger Maestro protein preparation wizard, and peptides were capped by ACE at the N terminus and by NME at the C terminus.


### Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation
  The models of prepared GSK3 and peptide complexes were solvated in a periodic TIP3P cubic water box with 0.15 m NaCl and 15 Å buffer between the protein and box edge. The equilibration steps were performed in the 2021-2 version of GROMACS with charmm36m force field. The constructed systems were first energy-minimized for 50,000 steps using the steepest descent method and then heated to 310 K with a constant box volume (NVT). Restraints were applied to Cα atoms of the protein with a force constant of 10 kJ·mol−1·nm−2 for 1 nsec. Subsequently, the heated systems were coupled accordingly using isotropic Berendsen control with a time constant of 2 psec for 1 nsec of pressure regulation (NPT). A nonbonded interaction cutoff of 12 Å was used. Long-range electrostatics were treated with the particle mesh Ewald (PME) method. Covalent bonds involving hydrogen were constrained with the LINCS algorithm. Finally, the complexes of GSK3/PPApSPV, GSK3/PANpSPV, GSK3/APNpSPV, GSK3/PPNSPV, GSK3/SPPNpSPV, and GSK3/pSPPNpSPV were conducted with 500 nsec MD simulations without any restraints applied in a 2 fsec time step with a V-rescale thermostat and a Parrinello–Rahman barostat. For GSK3/PPNpSPV and GSK3/PPPpSPV complexes, the simulations were conducted in the Xiaogan 3D Scientific Computing Center with the time step of 2.5 fsec and a Nose–Hoover thermostat (NVT) for a 1 µsec production run. The MD simulations for all systems were carried out at 1 atm pressure and 310 K constant temperature. Simulation trajectories were exported at 1 frame/1 nsec and analyzed using GROMACS, and complex visualization was performed using visual molecular dynamics (VMD) software and PyMol.


### Binding energy calculations by molecular mechanics Poisson–Boltzmann surface area (MMPBSA) analysis
  After performing simulations, binding energy calculations were conducted by molecular mechanics Poisson–Boltzmann surface area (MMPBSA) analysis with the help of the g_mmpbsa tool. The binding free energy was calculated as follows: ΔGbind = Gcomplex − (Gprotein + Gpeptide), where Gcomplex, Gprotein, and Gpeptide are the Gibbs free energy of the protein–peptide, the protein, and the peptide in the solvent, respectively. Moreover, Gx = EMM + (TS) + Gsolvation, where x strands for complex, protein, or peptide; EMM represents both bonded and nonbonded interaction energy in vacuum; Gsolvation represents electrostatic and nonelectrostatic contributions to solvation energy; and TS represents temperature and entropy. All the energy parameters were calculated by MMPBSA to obtain the total binding free energy.


### In silico mutagenesis and predicting changes in binding free energies upon mutation
  The GSK3/PPNpSPV complex was relaxed through ROSETTA scripting interfaces, optimizing both torsion and Cartesian space to align with the Rosetta beta_nov16 energy function. Subsequently, the Flex-ddg protocol was applied to the mutant of N on the PPNpSPV peptide. This involved five iterations of minimization and five backrub strides of 10 backrub trials, using the beta_nov16 scoring function. A total of 128 structures were generated in parallel for each mutant to calculate the binding energies and quantify the impact of N3 mutations.


### Quantification and statistical analysis
  Data analysis and statistical tests for qPCR data were performed using GraphPad Prism, and statistical significance was determined with one-way ANOVA followed by Bartlett's test (P < 0.001 [***]).
