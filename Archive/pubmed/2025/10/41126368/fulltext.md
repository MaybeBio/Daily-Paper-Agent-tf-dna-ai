# Bruceine D inhibits hepatocellular carcinoma by regulating the STAT3 signaling pathway through HSP70


## Background
  Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with limited effective therapies and poor prognosis. Although sorafenib and lenvatinib are approved for advanced HCC, their clinical efficacy is often compromised by drug resistance and adverse effects. Bruceine D (BD) has shown antitumor potential in several cancers, but its mechanisms in HCC remain poorly defined.


## Methods
  The effects of BD on HCC cell proliferation, apoptosis, and migration were assessed by MTT, colony formation, EdU incorporation, flow cytometry, wound healing, and transwell assays. Differentially expressed genes were identified by RNA sequencing and validated by RT-qPCR, with particular focus on Hsp70 family members. Protein expression and interactions were analyzed using Western blotting, co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP), and Cellular Thermal Shift Assay (CETSA). Molecular docking was performed to predict BD binding sites on Hsp70. The antitumor efficacy and safety of BD were further evaluated in BALB/c nude mouse xenograft models.


## Results
  BD significantly inhibited HCC cell growth, induced apoptosis, and suppressed migration in a dose-dependent manner in vitro study. Transcriptomic profiling revealed downregulation of Hsp70 family members (HspA1A, HspA1B, HspA8), confirmed by RT-qPCR and Western blotting. Molecular docking suggested hydrogen bond interactions of BD with multiple residues in the Hsp70 domain. Co-IP assays demonstrated that Hsp70 binds STAT3, and BD disrupted this interaction, resulting in reduced STAT3 phosphorylation and suppression of downstream effectors (MCL-1, survivin). CETSA proved BD protected HSP70 at various temperature gradients in HCC cells. In vivo, BD markedly reduced tumor volume without affecting body weight or causing histopathological abnormalities in major organs.


## Conclusions
  This study demonstrates that BD exerts potent antitumor effects against HCC by inhibiting proliferation, inducing apoptosis, and suppressing migration, primarily through disruption of the Hsp70/STAT3 signaling axis. With its favorable safety profile, BD represents a promising candidate for further development as a therapeutic agent in HCC.

## Introduction
  Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common primary liver malignancy worldwide [1]. The global incidence of HCC has been increasing, accompanied by high mortality and poor survival rates [2]. Surgery resection remains the primary treatment for HCC; however, the majority of HCC patients present at an advanced stage and are ineligible for surgery [3]. Sorafenib and lenvatinib are the first-line therapy for advanced HCC [4, 5], providing a modest survival benefit of approximately 2.8 months compared with placebo [6]. However, the frequent development of resistance significantly limits its clinical effectiveness [7]. This resistance arises through multiple mechanisms, among which the overexpression of biomarkers such as heat shock protein 70 (Hsp70) in HCC tissues has been prominently implicated [8, 9].

  Heat shock protein 70 (Hsp70) plays a crucial role in the folding and refolding of numerous intracellular proteins, thereby maintaining their activity, transport, and stability [10–13]. Beyond its physiological functions, the Hsp70 family has been linked to tumor growth [13], metastasis [14], and resistance to apoptosis in various malignancies. For example, in colorectal cancer, HspA8 (Heat Shock Protein Family A (Hsp70) Member 8) promotes cell proliferation via activation the Ras pathway [15]. In HCC, Hsp70 has been shown to enhance tumor progression [16] and also been identified as a potential biomarker for HCC diagnosis [17]. Mechanistically, HSP70 positively regulates TGF-α-induced migration of HCC cells through activation of the AKT signaling pathway [18]. Collectively, these findings highlight Hsp70 family as not only a key player in cancer biology but serve as a promising therapeutic target. Signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) has recently emerged as a critical oncogenic driver and therapeutic target in cancer [19]. It regulates genes involved in proliferation, migration, and survival, supporting tumor progression [20]. Notably, STAT3 can directly regulate the HSP70 promoter, increasing its expression and subsequently enhancing the upregulation of anti-apoptotic proteins [21]. Therefore, the development of novel agents with improved efficacy and safety is essential to enhance survival outcomes and quality of life in patients with HCC [22].

  Bruceine D (BD) is a natural bioactive compounds from Brucea javanica, the traditional Chinese medicine which known as Ya-dan-zi [23]. Currently, Brucea javanica oil soft capsule is being evaluated in clinical trials as an anticancer therapy, primarily to treat lung cancer [24–27]. Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that the antitumor efficacy of BD in multiple malignancies, including lung cancer [22], HCC [28, 29], breast cancer [30, 31], gastric cancer [32], and pancreatic adenocarcinoma [33]. In HCC specifically, previous studies have shown that BD inhibits tumor growth via modulation of the β-catenin/JAK1 signaling pathway [29] through regulation of microRNA-95 [34]. These findings highlight the diverse molecular targets of BD however, the precise mechanisms underlying its antitumor in HCC activity remain incompletely understood.

  Although recent studies suggest that BD can suppress STAT3 signaling [29, 35, 36], its interaction with molecular chaperones such as Hsp70 and the relevance of this axis in HCC progression have not yet been investigated. Given the central role of STAT3 in oncogenesis and the emerging evidence of crosstalk between STAT3 and other tumor-related pathways, elucidating the Hsp70–STAT3 regulatory mechanism may offer novel insights into BD’s anticancer properties for HCC.

  In this study, we aimed to elucidate the potential mechanism underlying the antitumor efficacy of BD in targeting the Hsp70/STAT3 signaling pathway in HCC. Our findings demonstrate that BD suppresses HCC cell proliferation, colony formation, and migration, while promoting HCC cell apoptosis both in vitro and in vivo, through inhibition of the Hsp70/STAT3 signaling pathway. These results highlight BD as a promising candidate for targetingHsp70 in the clinical therapy of HCC.


## Materials and methods

### Cell and cell culture
  Human HCC cells (SMMC-7721, SNU-739, and Hep3B) were provided from The Cell Resource Center of the Shanghai Institute of Biological Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences. SNU-739 cells were grown in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)—1640 medium (Gibco) with 10% fetal bovine serum. DMEM medium with 10% fetal bovine serum was used to cultivate SMMC-7721 cells. Hep3B cells were grown in MEM with 15% fetal bovine serum. All cells were grown in incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2 atmosphere.


### Compound source and preparation
  Bruceine D (BD) was purchased from Baoji City Chenguang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Baoji, China). According to the manufacturer’s certificate of analysis, the structure and purity of BD were confirmed by 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR), 13C NMR, mass spectrometry (MS), and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). The reported purity was ≥ 98%, meeting the requirements for both in vitro and in vivo pharmacological studies.

  For in vitro experiments, BD was dissolved in 100% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. D12345) and further diluted in culture medium to achieve the indicated working concentrations. The final DMSO concentration in all treatments did not exceed 0.1% (v/v) to minimize solvent toxicity.

  For in vivo studies, BD was freshly prepared by dissolving in DMSO and diluted with sterile 0.9% saline containing 5% Tween 80 prior to intraperitoneal injection. All preparations were made under sterile conditions and used immediately after preparation to ensure stability.


### Patient tissues
  Eleven pairs of human HCC tissues and corresponding adjacent non-tumor tissues were collected from patients at the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University, with approval from the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (2020023) and informed patient consent. Fresh tissue samples were immediately snap-frozen and stored at − 80 °C until further use.


### Reagents and antibodies
  The concentration of BD was 20 mM solution with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). DMSO was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Co. Propidium iodide and annexin V-fluorescein isothiocyanate apoptosis detection kit I obtained from BD Pharmingen (Franklin Lake, New Jersey, USA). HSP70 siRNA and negative control siRNA were obtained from Beijing Tsingke Biotech (BeiJing, China) and transfected into cells using invitrogen siRNA Transfection Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, USA). Acrylamide (30%), tetramethylethylenediamine, Coomassie brilliant blue, glycine, sodium dodecyl sulfate, protein markers, and skim milk powder were purchased from Bio-Rad Laboratories.

  The following antibodies were utilized in this study: P-STAT3 (Abcam, ab76315), STAT3 (Cell Signaling Technology, #12640S), Hsp70 (Abcam, ab5442), MCL-1 (Cell Signaling Technology, #4572S), survivin (Cell Signaling Technology, #2808S), E-cadherin (Cell Signaling Technology, #3195S), N-cadherin (Cell Signaling Technology, #13116), PARP-1 (Cell Signaling Technology, #9548S), Bcl-2 (Cell Signaling Technology, #15071), BAX (Abcam,E63), and GAPDH (Cell Signaling Technology, #5174S).


### MTT cell viability assay
  SMMC-7721, SNU-739, and Hep3B cells (4 × 103 cells/well) were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated overnight to allow cell attachment. Cells were then treated with Bruceine D (BD) at various concentrations for 48 h. The concentrations used in the dose–response experiments were as follows: 0 μM, 0.001 μM, 0.005 μM, 0.010 μM, 0.050 μM, 0.1 μM, 0.25 μM, 0.5 μM, 1 μM, 2.5 μM, 5 μM, 10 μM, 20 μM, 40 μM, 80 μM, 160 μM, and 320 μM.

  After treatment, 25 μL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL in PBS) was added to each well and incubated for an additional 4 h at 37 °C. The resulting formazan crystals were dissolved in 150 μL of DMSO. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader. Cell viability was expressed as a percentage relative to untreated control wells.


### Colony forming assay
  Human HCC cells (1 × 103 cells/well) were seeded into 6-well plate and cultured overnight at 37 °C in an incubator with atmosphere containing 5% carbon dioxide. Cells were treated with BD at varying concentrations (1, 2, and 4 μM), with medium refreshed every 2 days. After 1–2 weeks, colonies were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature and then stained with crystal violet for 20 min.


### Apoptosis assay
  HCC cells were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured overnight. Cells were treated with BD at varying concentrations (1, 2, and 4 μM) for 48 h. Following treatment, cells were stained with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled annexin V and propidium iodide (PI) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Apoptotic cells were analyzed using NovoCyte flow cytometer (ACEA Bioscience es Inc.), and data were processed with NovoExpress™ software.


### Migration assays
  HCC cells were seeded into the upper chambers of a transwell insert at a density of 1 × 10^4 cells/well in 200 µL of culture medium, with 600 µL of medium added to the lower chamber. After cell attachment, the medium in the upper chamber was replaced with serum-free medium containing varying concentrations of BD, while the lower chamber was filled with medium supplemented with serum. Following 24 h of incubation, migrated cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet. Images were captured under a microscope, and migrated cells were quantified using ImageJ software.


### RNA sequencing and data analysis
  RNA quantity and quality were assessed using a NanoPhotometer® spectrophotometer (IMPLEN, CA, USA) and the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit on the Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). RNA-seq libraries were constructed from 1 μg of total RNA using the NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and index codes were added to attribute sequences to each sample. Clustering of the index-coded samples was performed on a cBot Cluster Generation System using the TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v3-cBot-HS (Illumina). The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform to generate 150 bp paired end reads.

  FeatureCounts v1.5.0-p3 was used to quantify the read counts mapped to each gene. Differential expression analysis between two conditions/groups (two biological replicates per condition) was performed using the DESeq2 R package (v1.20.0). Resulting P-values were adjusted with the Benjamini–Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Genes with an adjusted P-value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change|> 1 were considered differentially expressed. Enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes in KEGG pathways was performed using the clusterProfiler R package [37].


### siRNA
  The HSP70 siRNA sequences were as follows: sense GCUAGACAUUAAGUCCAAA and antisense UUUGGACUUAAUGUCUAGC. HSP70 siRNA and negative control siRNA were obtained from Beijing Tsingke Biotech (BeiJing, China) and transfected into cells using invitrogen siRNA Transfection Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, USA). Briefly, SMMC-7721 cells were divided into three groups (medium, Si-HSP70, and negative control) and plated in 6-well plates. The cells were then transfected with 100 nM siRNA as indicated. Finally, the cells were examined for HSP70 protein levels after 48 h.


### Western blot analysis
  Cells were collected after 24 h of drug treatment and lysed on ice for 10 min using lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Total protein was extracted, separated by 10% SDS-PAGE, and transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% skimmed milk for 90 min at room temperature, followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After three washes with PBS, the membrane was incubated with the appropriate secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection kit (Pierce Biotechnology, USA).


### Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)
  Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay was conducted using a Shanghai Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China), following the manufacturer’s manual. One hundred lg purified anti-flag antibodies were first coupled with resin. Protein samples (1000 lg) were exposed to the antibody-coupled resin for 2 h. Following mixing and washing, 50 lL elution buffer was added into the protein-antibody complexes. Protein samples were subjected to immunoblotting using anti-STAT3, anti-HSP70 1:1000, CellSignaling Technology).


### Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)
  HCC cells (2 × 106) were cultured into a 100 mm culture dish overnight. After treating the cells with 16 μM BD for 24 h, they were lysed using RIPA lysis buffer, and then the total protein was collected. It was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The protein was divided equally into five portions, and each portion was heated at 37 °C, 40 °C, 44 °C, 48 °C, and 52 °C, for 5 min, respectively. Then they were placed at room temp for 3 min and finally transferred onto ice for later use. 50 μL of the supernatant was transferred to a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube and 50 μL of 5 × loading buffer was added, mixed well, and heated in a metal bath at 100 °C for 10 min. Then the sample collection was completed. The protein level was determined by Western blotting.


### Molecular docking analysis
  The crystal structure of the HSP70 protein was predicted using AlphaFold2 and subsequently refined using the Protein Preparation Wizard in Schrödinger software. The refinement steps included correction of bond orders, assignment of hydrogen atoms, optimization of hydrogen-bonding networks, energy minimization, and removal of water molecules.

  The 2D structure of the compound BD was imported as an SDF file and processed using Schrödinger’s LigPrep module to generate 3D conformations and appropriate chiral states. The potential binding site on HSP70 was predicted to use the SiteMap module. Based on the predicted site, the Receptor Grid Generation module was used to define the docking grid box encompassing the active site.

  Docking of BD into the HSP70 active site was performed using the Glide XP (extra precision) docking mode. Docking scores, which reflect predicted binding affinities (lower scores indicating stronger binding), were used to evaluate docking poses.

  To further assess the binding interaction, MM-GBSA calculations were conducted using the Prime module in Schrödinger to estimate the binding free energy (ΔG_bind) between BD and HSP70. Lower ΔG_bind values indicate greater thermodynamic stability and stronger binding affinity of the compound to the target protein.


### In vivo tumor xenograft model
  All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the institutional guidelines for laboratory animal care and were approved by the Committee of Wenzhou Medical University Animal Policy and Welfare Committee. Forty immunodeficient BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old) were obtained from the SPF Animal Laboratory. After subcutaneous implantation of SMMC-7721 cells, tumors reached approximately 5 × 5 mm within 10–15 days. Mice were then randomly assigned to four groups (n = 10 per group), with no significant differences in initial body weight or tumor volume between groups. The treatment groups received intraperitoneal injections of BD at doses of 1 mg/kg (low dose) or 2.5 mg/kg (high dose), while the positive control group received sorafenib (2.5 mg/kg). Injections were administered once every 2 days for a total of 14 days. Tumor length (L) and width (W) were measured before each injection, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula V = 0.5 × L × W2. On day 14, the nude mice were sacrificed, and the heart, liver, kidneys, and lungs were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. The tumors were dissected, photographed, weighed, and preserved at − 80 °C. Tumor tissues were homogenized, and protein levels were measured by Western blot analysis. At the end of treatment, all animals were sacrificed. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the institutional guidelines and approved by Wenzhou University’s Animal Policy and Welfare Committee.


### Statistical analysis
  All experiments were independently repeated at least three times, except for the animal model experiment. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software Inc.). Comparisons between two groups were conducted using student t-tests, while multiple group’s comparisons were analyzed by one-way ANOVA. Statistical significance was defined as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001.

### BD inhibits the Hsp70/STAT3 pathway
  In order to determine that BD inhibits the development process of hepatocellular carcinoma through the Hsp70/STAT3 signaling pathway. ### BD protected HSP70 at various temperature gradients
  To further ascertain the binding affinity of BD to the HSP70 protein in HCC cells, we used the cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA), which predicts the enhancement of the target protein’s thermal stability upon ligand binding, thereby averting degradation at specific temperatures. Western blot analysis indicated that the expression of the HSP70 protein in HCC cells treated with DMSO was significantly reduced within the temperature range of 37–52 °C, whereas its expression remained unchanged in cells treated with BD (Fig. 5d).


### BD inhibits the growth of HCC cells in vivo
  To validate the antitumor activity of BD in vivo, HCC xenograft models were established by subcutaneous injection of SMMC-7721 cells into BALB/c nude mice. Mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 10 per group): vehicle control, positive control (sorafenib, 2.5 mg/kg), low-dose BD (1 mg/kg), and high-dose BD (2.5 mg/kg). Treatments were administered intraperitoneally every other day for 14 days, and tumor volume and body weight were monitored throughout the study.

  As shown in Fig. 7a, b, BD treatment significantly reduced tumor growth compared with the control group, in a dose-dependent manner. Throughout the 14-day treatment period, no significant body weight loss was observed in any of the four groups, including the BD low-dose (1 mg/kg) and high-dose (2.5 mg/kg) groups (Fig. 7c). Although the DL group showed a slightly higher average weight than other groups, this variation was attributed to random differences at the time of group allocation. The overall trend in body weight remained stable across all groups, suggesting that BD did not induce observable systemic toxicity under the dosing conditions tested. This finding aligns with common preclinical practices where body weight is used as a general indicator of animal well-being and drug tolerability.

  Western blot analysis of tumor tissues revealed that BD treatment markedly downregulated Hsp70 and phosphorylated STAT3 (p-STAT3) levels, consistent with in vitro findings and supporting the mechanism of Hsp70/STAT3 pathway inhibition (Fig. 7d). Furthermore, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of lung, kidney, liver, and heart tissues showed no histopathological abnormalities following BD treatment, further supporting its in vivo safety (Fig. 7e). ## Discussion
  Natural products derived from traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) have shown promise as anticancer agents, with compounds such as bufalin [34], berberine [35], and Radix Codonopsis polysaccharides exhibiting antitumor effects in HCC. Bruceine D (BD), a quassinoid isolated from the seeds of Brucea javanica, has recently attracted increasing attention in treatment of HCC [38, 39]. Previous studies reported that BD disrupts the ICAT/β-catenin interaction, thereby modulating glucose metabolism in hepatoma cells [13], and suppresses STAT3 signaling, which is frequently upregulated in HCC tissues [36].

  In this study, we demonstrated that BD exerts potent antitumor activity against HCC both in vitro and in vivo. BD significantly reduced HCC cell proliferation by inducing apoptosis and suppressing migratory capacity. Notably, compared with sorafenib, BD displayed lower systemic toxicity while showing a stronger inhibitory effect on cell migration, underscoring its therapeutic potential. Consistent with in vitro findings, BD suppressed tumor growth in xenograft models without affecting body weight or causing pathological injury to major organs, further highlighting its safety profile.

  Given prior evidence that BD inhibits STAT3 signaling [29] and that exosomal Hsp70 promotes STAT3 phosphorylation in renal carcinoma by enhancing the suppressive activity of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) [30], we hypothesized a functional interplay between Hsp70 and STAT3 in HCC. Our Co-IP experiments confirmed that Hsp70 binds directly to STAT3, and this interaction was markedly reduced by BD treatment. Consistently, BD inhibited STAT3 phosphorylation in a time- and dose-dependent manner, with subsequent downregulation of STAT3 downstream targets, including MCL-1 and survivin. Together, these findings suggest that BD exerts its inhibitory effects on HCC progression by binding to Hsp70, thereby disrupting the Hsp70/STAT3 interaction and attenuating STAT3-driven oncogenic signaling.

  The JAK/STAT3 pathway is widely recognized as a central driver of oncogenesis. It is activated by diverse cytokines, hormones, and growth factors, leading to STAT3 nuclear translocation and transcriptional regulation of genes that promote tumor proliferation, migration, and survival [28, 37–39]. Our study not only confirmed the previously reported inhibition of STAT3 signaling by BD [29] but also provided novel mechanistic insights by identifying Hsp70 as a mediator of this regulation. Nevertheless, the precise binding sites between BD and Hsp70, and whether BD interferes with other chaperone-mediated signaling pathways, remain to be clarified in future studies.

  At the mechanistic level, RNA-seq analysis revealed that BD treatment downregulated several Hsp70 family members, including HspA1A, HspA1B, and HspA8. Hsp70 is a key molecular chaperone involved in protein folding, stability, and cancer progression. Previous studies have shown that Hsp70 overexpression promotes tumor survival and resistance to apoptosis [40]. Consistent with these reports, we found that Hsp70 protein expression was elevated in HCC tissues compared with adjacent normal tissues. Importantly, BD treatment suppressed Hsp70 expression and disrupted its interaction with STAT3, a critical transcription factor implicated in tumor proliferation, invasion, and immune evasion. By attenuating STAT3 phosphorylation and reducing downstream effectors such as MCL-1 and survivin, BD effectively inhibited a major oncogenic signaling pathway in HCC.

  The identification of the Hsp70/STAT3 axis as a direct target of BD is of particular relevance. Exosomal Hsp70 has been shown to activate STAT3 signaling and enhance the immunosuppressive activity of myeloid-derived suppressor cells in renal carcinoma, highlighting the broader oncogenic role of this pathway [41]. Although recent studies have shown that BD can suppress STAT3 signaling, this has not previously been demonstrated in HCC [35]. In HCC, BD has been reported to inhibit other oncogenic pathways (e.g., β-catenin/JAK1, HIF-1α). However, the roles of STAT3 and Hsp70 have not been investigated [28]. In this study, we provide the first evidence that BD directly blocks the Hsp70/STAT3 axis in HCC. Our findings indicate that BD may counteract Hsp70-mediated STAT3 activation, thereby exerting potent antitumor effects and potentially alleviating tumor-induced immunosuppression. Furthermore, molecular docking analysis revealed that BD binds to multiple residues of Hsp70 via hydrogen bonding, offering a structural basis for its inhibitory activity.

  Taken together, this study extends previous findings on the anticancer potential of BD by uncovering a novel mechanism involving inhibition of the Hsp70/STAT3 pathway. Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although in vivo xenograft models confirmed BD’s efficacy and safety, orthotopic or patient-derived xenograft models would better mimic the clinical tumor microenvironment. Second, while we identified Hsp70 as a critical target, BD may exert additional effects on other oncogenic pathways that require further exploration. Finally, studies should evaluate the pharmacokinetics, bioavailability, and immune-related effects of BD to better assess its therapeutic potential.


## Conclusion
  In summary, this study demonstrated that BD suppresses HCC progression by downregulating Hsp70 and inhibiting STAT3 signaling, leading to reduced proliferation, increased apoptosis, and diminished migration of HCC cells. Combined with its activities and favorable safety profile in vivo, BD emerged as a promising natural compound for further development as a therapeutic candidate in HCC management.
