# Integrative Analysis of Mitochondrial‐Related Genes Reveals Diagnostic Biomarkers and Therapeutic Targets in Acute Pancreatitis


## ABSTRACT
  Mitochondrial dysfunction is increasingly recognised as a critical contributor to acinar cell injury and systemic inflammation in acute pancreatitis (AP). However, comprehensive screening of mitochondrial‐related genes (MRGs) and their mechanistic roles in AP progression remains limited. We integrated transcriptomic data with MRGs from the MitoCarta database. A total of 34 differentially expressed MRGs were identified, enabling classification of AP samples into three molecular subtypes with distinct immune cell infiltration patterns and clinical severity. Three hub genes were consistently identified by three machine learning algorithms: LASSO, SVM‐RFE, and RF. qRT‐PCR validation in cellular models confirmed consistent expression trends. Multi‐level functional annotation was conducted through GSVA, CIBERSORT, transcription factor prediction, subcellular localisation and single‐cell analyses. Talniflumate and ABT‐737 were predicted as potential therapeutic agents using the CMap and validated through molecular docking and 100‐ns molecular dynamics simulations. This study establishes a mitochondria‐related diagnostic model for AP and identifies candidate therapeutic agents, offering novel insights into the molecular pathogenesis and targeted intervention of AP.


## Introduction
  Acute pancreatitis (AP) is a common acute gastrointestinal disorder characterised by sudden onset of abdominal pain, elevated serum pancreatic enzymes, and local or systemic inflammatory responses [1]. Clinically, AP manifests with diverse severity, ranging from mild self‐limiting forms to moderate or severe cases (MSAP/SAP), the latter of which may progress to multiple organ dysfunction syndrome with significantly increased mortality [2]. The global incidence of AP has been rising steadily, making it a leading cause of hospitalisation and digestive disease–related mortality [3]. According to the Global Burden of Disease study, over 2.7 million new AP cases were reported worldwide in 2021—a nearly 60% increase since 1990—with over 120,000 associated deaths [4]. Despite advancements in intensive care and supportive therapy, early molecular diagnostics and effective targeted treatments for AP remain lacking.

  Mitochondria, as essential organelles regulating cellular energy metabolism, redox balance, calcium signalling and apoptosis, play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of AP [5]. Sustained intracellular calcium overload in pancreatic acinar cells triggers the opening of mitochondrial permeability transition pores, leading to loss of membrane potential, impaired oxidative phosphorylation, ATP depletion, and excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation—events that initiate early cell injury and inflammation cascades [6]. Moreover, impaired mitophagy, disrupted mitochondrial protein import and electron transport chain dysfunction have also been implicated in AP progression [7]. Nevertheless, systematic analyses of mitochondria‐related gene (MRG) expression patterns in AP and their functional roles in immunometabolic dysregulation remain largely unexplored.

  The emergence of high‐throughput sequencing technologies and the growing availability of omics databases have enabled machine learning–based bioinformatic approaches to gain traction in disease research [8]. Algorithms such as LASSO, RF and SVM‐RFE are widely applied to identify diagnostic biomarkers from large transcriptomic datasets [9]. In parallel, the Connectivity Map (CMap) platform facilitates drug discovery through transcriptomic signature matching, while molecular docking evaluates drug–target binding affinity [10]. To address the limitations of static docking approaches, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations have been increasingly adopted to assess the structural stability of drug–target complexes in a dynamic biological context. In this study, we integrated machine learning, molecular docking, and MD to systematically analyse AP transcriptome data in conjunction with MRGs, providing a theoretical framework and novel strategies for the early diagnosis and mitochondria‐targeted therapy of AP.


## Methods

### Data Acquisition and Preprocessing
  Transcriptomic data associated with AP (GSE194331 dataset) were retrieved from the GEO database, which contains peripheral blood expression profiles from 87 AP patients of varying severity and 32 healthy controls [11]. To focus on mitochondrial function, we utilised the MitoCarta 3.0 database, from which 2030 MRGs were extracted to serve as a reference set for downstream filtering and analysis [12, 13].


### Identification of Differentially Expressed MRGs
  Differential gene expression analysis between AP and healthy samples was performed using the limma package in R [14]. Genes with an adjusted p value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| > 1 were considered differentially expressed (DEGs). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted to explore enriched biological processes and pathways in AP samples relative to controls [15]. MRGs were then intersected with the DEGs to identify mitochondria‐related DEGs (MRGs‐DEGs), which were retained for subsequent functional analysis and model construction.


### Functional Enrichment Analysis
  Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks for MRGs‐DEGs were constructed using the STRING database (https://cn.string‐db.org/) [16]. Gene–gene interaction networks were further analysed via the GeneMANIA platform (https://genemania.org/) to predict functional associations [17]. Functional enrichment and pathway analysis were conducted using the Metascape database (https://metascape.org/).


### Feature Gene Selection and Diagnostic Model Construction
  To identify robust diagnostic biomarkers, we applied three commonly used machine learning algorithms. First, LASSO regression was performed using the glmnet package in R. Ten‐fold cross‐validation was used to determine the optimal regularisation parameter (λ), and genes with nonzero coefficients were retained as candidate features [18]. Second, RF analysis was carried out using the randomForest package (ntree = 500). Gene importance was ranked based on the mean decrease in Gini index, and those with importance scores > 3 were considered significant [19]. Third, SVM‐RFE was implemented with the e1071 package. This algorithm iteratively removed the least important features to identify the subset of genes that minimised classification error [20]. Genes consistently selected by all three algorithms were defined as core genes. These core genes were subsequently used to construct the diagnostic model. Model performance was assessed on the dataset used for feature selection by plotting receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and calculating the area under the curve (AUC) using the pROC package.


### Cell Culture and Quantitative Real‐Time PCR Analysis
  Ten percent foetal bovine serum was added to DMEM to culture the mouse pancreatic acinar cell line 266‐6 (ATCC: CRL‐2151) and cell line RAW264.7 (ATCC: TIB‐71). In accordance with pertinent research, the cells were exposed to 250 μM Sodium taurocholate for 24 h in order to create the AP model [21]. Total RNA was extracted from cells using the Trizol reagent, and cDNA was produced using a reverse transcription kit. The target genes' expression levels were then assessed using a fluorescent dye‐based assay based on SYBRGreen I. Target gene expression levels between the two groups were compared using the t‐test, while RNA expression levels were evaluated and quantified using the ΔΔCt.


### Pathway Enrichment and Immune Infiltration Analysis
  To assess the biological relevance of key genes, Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) was used to evaluate the activity of metabolic and inflammation‐related pathways across different AP subtypes [22]. In parallel, the CIBERSORT algorithm was applied to estimate the relative abundances of 22 immune cell populations in peripheral blood samples [23]. Correlations between model genes and immune cell subsets were analysed to investigate their potential roles in immunometabolic dysregulation during AP.


### Transcription Factor Prediction and Subcellular Localisation
  To explore upstream regulatory mechanisms, transcription factor (TF) prediction was performed via the TFTF platform [24], integrating data from four major TF databases: hTFtarget [25], ENCODE [26], GTRD [27], and ChIP‐Atlas [28]. Subcellular localisation and single‐cell expression profiles of model genes were retrieved from the HPA database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/) [29, 30].


### Candidate Drug Prediction and Molecular Simulation
  To identify compounds with therapeutic potential for AP, the DEGs were submitted to the CMap platform (https://clue.io/), which matches gene expression signatures with drug perturbagens to predict candidate therapeutics [31]. Molecular docking was subsequently conducted to evaluate interactions between the identified compounds and target proteins. Protein 3D structures were obtained from the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/) [32] or predicted using the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/) [33]. Docking was performed using the CB‐Dock2 platform (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb‐dock2/index.php) [34]. MD simulations were carried out using GROMACS 2021.3 and the CHARMM36 force field [35]. Key parameters such as root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg), and hydrogen bond counts were analysed to assess the biophysical stability and reliability of drug–target binding.


## Results

### Identification of Mitochondria‐Related DEGs in AP
  The overall study design is outlined in Figure 1. First, the differential expression analysis between AP patients and healthy controls was conducted to identify candidate pathogenic genes. A total of 778 DEGs were identified between AP and control groups (Figure 2A). Then, GSEA revealed significant enrichment of pathways related to neutrophil extracellular trap formation, oxidative phosphorylation, and chemical carcinogenesis–reactive oxygen species in the AP group, whereas pathways such as antigen processing and presentation, Th1/Th2 cell differentiation, and T cell receptor signalling were downregulated (Supporting Information S1: Figure S1). Given the significant enrichment of related pathways such as oxidative phosphorylation and ROS in AP, we acquired 2030 MRGs from the MitoCarta 3.0 database. By intersecting these DEGs with MRGs, 34 MRGs‐DEGs were obtained (Figure 2B). PPI analysis of these genes using STRING indicated extensive interconnectivity (Figure 2C). GeneMANIA analysis further demonstrated functional clustering, with enrichment in pathways such as ATP synthesis coupled electron transport, respirasome and mitochondrial ATP synthesis coupled electron transport (Figure 2D). Metascape analysis indicated involvement in biological processes like mitochondrion organisation and aerobic electron transport chain (Figure 2E).


### Mitochondria‐Based Molecular Subtypes of AP
  Subsequently, we performed consensus clustering of AP samples based on the expression patterns of 34 MRGs‐DEGs and identified three distinct molecular subtypes (Figure 3A). These subtypes exhibited markedly different transcriptional signatures, further validated by PCA (Figure 3B,C). GSVA revealed functional heterogeneity among the subtypes: subtype C1 was enriched for immune‐related pathways, including antigen presentation, T cell activation, and antiviral responses, consistent with an ‘immune‐inflammatory activated’ phenotype; subtype C2 showed upregulation of endoplasmic reticulum structure, lipid metabolism, and vesicle transport pathways; while subtype C3 demonstrated increased activity in epigenetic regulation, amino acid metabolism and RNA degradation pathways (Supporting Information S1: Figure S2A–F). These functional profiles aligned with distinct immune infiltration patterns: C2 exhibited elevated levels of neutrophils and M0 macrophages, suggestive of a more pro‐inflammatory state; whereas differences between C1 and C3 were mainly observed in CD8+ T cells, naiive CD4+ T cells, and resting memory CD4+ T cells, reflecting divergent adaptive immune responses (Figure 3D,E). Notably, Sankey diagram analysis revealed clear subtype–severity correlations: C2, with the most pronounced inflammatory response and metabolic dysregulation, had the highest proportion of MSAP and SAP cases (Figure 3F). Collectively, these findings highlight substantial immune‐metabolic heterogeneity among molecular subtypes of AP and demonstrate a strong concordance between molecular function and clinical severity.


### Construction of Diagnostic Model Using Machine Learning
  To identify robust diagnostic biomarkers, three machine learning algorithms—LASSO, RF and SVM‐RFE—were used for feature selection among MRGs‐DEGs. LASSO regression selected 9 nonzero coefficient genes (Figure 4A), SVM‐RFE identified 13 optimal features (Figure 4B) and RF selected 4 genes with importance scores > 3 (Figure 4C,D). Three overlapping genes—MT‐ND5, MTX1 and NDUFA1—were identified from the intersection of all three algorithms (Figure 5A). Expression analysis showed that MT‐ND5 was significantly downregulated in AP, while MTX1 and NDUFA1 were upregulated (Figure 5B). ROC analysis indicated good predictive performance for each gene (Figure 5C), and the combined three‐gene model achieved an AUC of 0.992 (Figure 5D). In vitro validation using the cellular model demonstrated significantly increased IL‐6 and IL‐1β expression, confirming successful AP induction (Figure 5E,F). The expression trends of MT‐ND5, MTX1 and NDUFA1 were consistent with bioinformatic predictions (Figure 5E,F). Moreover, subgroup analysis showed no significant difference in MT‐ND5 expression between MAP and NMAP (MSAP + SAP), whereas MTX1 and NDUFA1 were significantly elevated in NMAP, suggesting their potential as biomarkers for disease severity (Figure 5G–I).


### Functional Enrichment Analysis and Immune Correlation Analysis of Model Genes
  In AP, MT‐ND5 was significantly downregulated, whereas MTX1 and NDUFA1 were consistently upregulated, indicating a divergent role of mitochondrial genes in disease progression. GSVA showed that MT‐ND5 expression was negatively associated with pathways involved in T cell receptor signalling, antigen processing and presentation, dendritic cell survival and immune‐mediated rejection, suggesting that MT‐ND5 downregulation may relieve constraints on immune activation and contribute to excessive inflammation. In contrast, MTX1 and NDUFA1 were positively associated with key adaptive immune responses, including lymphocyte co‐stimulation, B and T cell proliferation, and intestinal IgA immune network, indicating enhanced immune cell activation and mucosal immunity. All three genes showed convergence in nucleic acid metabolism pathways, such as rRNA 5′‐end processing and XMP metabolic process, highlighting a shared role in ribosomal biogenesis and purine metabolism. Additionally, MTX1 and NDUFA1 were linked to DNA repair mechanisms, Wnt signalling, and circadian rhythm regulation, reflecting a potential involvement in stress response and transcriptional reprogramming. Collectively, these results suggest that mitochondrial gene dysregulation may promote AP progression by coordinately enhancing immune activation and disturbing metabolic and genomic homoeostasis (Supporting Information S1: Figure S3A–F). Further immune infiltration analysis revealed that MT‐ND5 was negatively correlated with neutrophil abundance but positively correlated with resting NK cells (Figure 6A–C), suggesting a potential protective immune phenotype. In contrast, MTX1 and NDUFA1 showed positive correlations with neutrophils and negative associations with resting memory CD4+ T cells (Figure 6D–I), indicating a shift towards a more pro‐inflammatory and activated immune microenvironment in AP.


### TF Prediction, Immunofluorescence and Single‐Cell Atlas Analysis
  To further elucidate the regulatory mechanisms and cellular context of MT‐ND5, MTX1 and NDUFA1 in AP, we performed integrated transcription factor prediction and spatial expression analyses. Integrated TF prediction from four publicly available databases identified 13 candidate TFs potentially regulating MT‐ND5, MTX1 and NDUFA1 (Figure 7A–D). Notably, due to limitations in database coverage, only two of the databases included TF prediction data for MT‐ND5, whereas NDUFA1 was represented in three databases. Immunofluorescence staining data from the HPA showed mitochondrial and cytoplasmic localisation of MTX1 and NDUFA1 in epithelial cells, while MT‐ND5 lacked available immunofluorescence data in the HPA (Figure 7E,F). Single‐cell transcriptomic analysis of the pancreas further revealed cell type‐specific distribution: MT‐ND5 was primarily expressed in pancreatic β cells and T cells (Figure 7G), MTX1 was enriched in B cells (Figure 7H) and NDUFA1 exhibited strong expression in pancreatic β cells (Figure 7I).


### Candidate Drug Screening and Molecular Simulation
  To identify potential therapeutic agents, DEGs were submitted to the CMap database. The top 10 candidate compounds with negative enrichment scores were selected (Table S1). We then performed molecular docking of the two model genes upregulated at AP with each of the predicted drugs. Molecular docking using CB‐Dock2 showed that talniflumate–MTX1 and ABT‐737–NDUFA1 complexes had binding energies < −7 kcal/mol, indicating favourable interactions (Table S1). Docking models revealed two hydrogen bonds between talniflumate and MTX1 (Figure 8A) and one between ABT‐737 and NDUFA1 (Figure 8B).

  To assess the stability of these complexes, 100‐ns MD simulations were conducted. RMSD plots showed rapid equilibration and structural stability (Figure 8C,D). RMSF plots indicated minor fluctuations except for loop regions (< 0.5 nm) (Figure 8E,F). SASA and Rg analyses supported a compact and stable complex conformation (Figure 8G–J). Hydrogen bond dynamics showed 2–7 bonds for MTX1–talniflumate and 2–6 for NDUFA1–ABT‐737, indicating stable interactions (Figure 8K,L). Furthermore, free energy landscape (FEL) maps based on RMSD and Rg revealed distinct low‐energy basins, reflecting thermodynamic stability of the docked complexes (Figure 8M,N). PCA covariance matrices showed coordinated motion among flexible loops and surface residues, and FELs based on PC1 and PC2 confirmed multiple stable conformational states for MTX1–talniflumate, whereas NDUFA1–ABT‐737 exhibited a single dominant energy well, suggesting tighter binding specificity (Figure 8O–R).


## Discussion
  AP is a common exocrine pancreatic inflammatory disease characterised by severe abdominal pain and the potential for multi‐organ dysfunction [36]. In recent years, the incidence of AP has been on the rise, yet its molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood [37]. Mitochondria, as the central organelles for cellular metabolic regulation, play a pivotal role not only in energy production and oxidative stress regulation but also in initiating apoptosis and inflammatory cascades when dysfunctional [38]. Increasing evidence suggests that aberrant mitochondrial metabolism is closely linked to various inflammatory disorders, and may also contribute critically to the pathogenesis of AP [39, 40].

  In this study, we integrated transcriptomic datasets with a mitochondrial gene set, and identified MRGs‐DEGs in AP for the first time. The MRGs‐DEGs identified were primarily enriched in biological processes such as aerobic electron transport chain, ATP synthesis coupled electron transport, and mitochondrial membrane potential regulation—all consistent with prior studies highlighting mitochondrial energy dysfunction and oxidative stress in AP [41]. These abnormalities can result in impaired calcium homoeostasis in acinar cells, excessive ROS accumulation, and increased mitochondrial membrane permeability, ultimately leading to apoptosis and necrosis—key early events in inflammatory amplification and organ damage during AP progression [42].

  By performing consensus clustering based on the 34 MRGs‐DEGs, we classified AP patients into three molecular subtypes with distinct immune‐metabolic profiles. The C2 subtype, characterised by high neutrophil and M0 macrophage infiltration and enriched in ER stress and lipid metabolism pathways, was significantly associated with SAP. This suggests a potential pathogenic axis involving mitochondrial–ER stress–immune infiltration. In contrast, the C3 subtype, enriched in RNA metabolism and epigenetic regulatory pathways, displayed an immunosuppressive phenotype and was largely composed of MAP cases, indicating a relatively quiescent immune‐metabolic state. This molecular classification provides a theoretical foundation for stratified diagnosis and personalised interventions in AP.

  In this study, we identified MT‐ND5, MTX1, and NDUFA1 as core diagnostic genes for AP and investigated their potential biological roles. Although our results were derived from machine learning and transcriptomic profiling, the integration of GSVA and immune infiltration analyses offered mechanistic clues linking mitochondrial dysfunction to immune dysregulation. MT‐ND5, a key complex I subunit, is essential for oxidative phosphorylation, and its downregulation is known to impair electron transport and increase ROS production—mechanisms previously implicated in Parkinson's disease and defective mitophagy [43, 44]. In our AP model, MT‐ND5 was significantly reduced, suggesting that mitochondrial metabolic impairment may be an early hallmark of disease. Mitochondrial ROS and oxidised mtDNA released from injured acinar cells can activate NF‐κB, NLRP3 and cGAS–STING signalling, thereby inducing IL‐1β/IL‐18 and chemokines that recruit neutrophils and monocytes [6, 45, 46]. Consistent with this biology, our enrichment and infiltration analyses showed that reduced MT‐ND5 expression correlated with heightened immune activation and a neutrophil‐dominant inflammatory phenotype. MTX1, a mitochondrial outer membrane translocator, has been implicated in protein import and apoptosis regulation [47]. Beyond these roles, MTX1 interacts with BAK to control outer‐membrane permeabilisation, which permits mtDNA escape via VDAC/mPTP pores. This escape strongly engages TLR9 and cGAS–STING pathways, amplifying cytokine and chemokine programs. Our pathway analyses were in line with this mechanism, showing enrichment in adaptive immune activation and transcriptional reprogramming, supporting a role for MTX1 in pro‐inflammatory infiltration. NDUFA1, another complex I subunit, is typically downregulated under metabolic stress, whereas its overexpression can restore mitochondrial function [48] and has been proposed as a biomarker in Alzheimer's disease [49]. In our AP model, NDUFA1 was upregulated, which may reflect compensatory remodelling. However, disproportionate increases of specific subunits can destabilise respiratory supercomplexes, increase electron leak, and generate excessive ROS. Such mito‐ROS amplifies NF‐κB and NLRP3 signalling and promotes chemokine‐driven recruitment of neutrophils and macrophages [50, 51, 52]. This mechanism explains the positive association between NDUFA1 expression and pro‐inflammatory infiltration in our GSVA analysis, beyond a purely compensatory interpretation. In summary, we propose a model in which MTX1‐mediated membrane remodelling and NDUFA1‐driven redox stress, coupled with MT‐ND5 downregulation, converge to promote cytokine and chemokine gradients, thereby reprogramming immune activation in AP.

  In terms of therapeutic implications, talniflumate and ABT‐737 emerged as promising mitochondrial‐targeting agents. Talniflumate, a cyclooxygenase inhibitor, exhibits anti‐inflammatory properties. Talniflumate originally developed as a calcium‐activated chloride channel inhibitor for cystic fibrosis and COPD, exhibits potent anti‐inflammatory and mucus‐regulating properties [53]. Recent studies suggest its protective roles in pancreatic cancer and Parkinson's disease by modulating the mucus barrier and suppressing NLRP3 inflammasome activation, enhancing chemotherapeutic efficacy and mitigating neuronal damage [54, 55]. ABT‐737, a BH3 mimetic and classic inhibitor of Bcl‐2 family proteins, induces mitochondrial‐mediated apoptosis by binding Bcl‐2, Bcl‐xL and Bcl‐w [56, 57]. It has demonstrated potent anticancer activity in small‐cell lung cancer [58], leukaemia [59], and multiple myeloma [60], and more recently, its senolytic properties have attracted attention in ageing and combination therapies using nanoparticle delivery systems [61]. Our molecular docking and 100‐ns MD simulations confirmed stable binding between these compounds and MTX1 or NDUFA1, with favourable RMSD and hydrogen bond profiles supporting reliable interactions. Furthermore, to explore potential subtype‐specific implications, we examined the functional characteristics of AP subtypes. Talniflumate primarily targets inflammatory pathways enriched in C1 and C2, suggesting potential therapeutic effects in these subtypes. ABT‐737 targets anti‐apoptotic proteins, and while direct evidence is limited, the high metabolic and biosynthetic activity of C3 may suggest potential relevance for this compound. Although these interpretations are exploratory, they provide a framework for future experimental studies evaluating subtype‐specific drug sensitivities.

  Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. First, the data were primarily derived from public databases, and the sample size and heterogeneity may limit the generalisability of the findings. Because of the limited availability of additional public datasets, our study lacks external validation of the model. Future work should involve multicenter clinical cohorts to perform independent validation. In addition, the three mitochondrial‐associated molecular subtypes identified in this study are exploratory in nature, and the clinical relevance and drug sensitivity of these subtypes remain to be validated by future research. Finally, although our study highlights MRGs as potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets in AP, it does not fully establish mitochondrial dysfunction as a unifying pathogenic axis. Future experimental studies are needed to validate the functional roles of these biomarkers in mitochondrial regulation and AP progression.


## Conclusion
  In summary, this study systematically integrated transcriptomic data with MRGs in AP, identifying three molecular subtypes of AP. Furthermore, three core diagnostic genes were identified through multi‐algorithm machine learning and used to construct a high‐performance predictive model. Finally, molecular docking and simulation studies suggested that talniflumate and ABT‐737 may serve as potential mitochondrial‐targeted therapeutic agents. This work not only advances our understanding of AP molecular mechanisms but also provides novel targets and a theoretical framework for mitochondrial‐based therapeutic strategies in the future.
