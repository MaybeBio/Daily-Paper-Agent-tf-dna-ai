# Deep learning based semi-automated model can predict lineage in patients with pituitary neuroendocrine tumors


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40478-025-02104-x.


## Introduction
  Pituitary neuroendocrine tumors (PitNETs), commonly referred to as pituitary adenomas, originate from the hormone-secreting cells within the anterior pituitary gland. Recent research indicates an increasing incidence of PitNET diagnoses [1]. Data from radiological assessments and autopsy studies suggest that PitNETs may have a prevalence rate ranging between 17% and 22% in the general population [2, 3].

  Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining maintains its status as the gold standard for histopathological examination, playing a pivotal role in the morphological diagnosis of PitNETs. The World Health Organization (WHO) has recently endorsed the use of transcription factor immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining in the lineage subtyping of PitNETs (e.g., SF1, TPIT, or PIT1 lineage) [4]. This novel subtyping schema is instrumental for risk stratification, as it has been observed that TPIT lineage or some PIT-1 lineage tumors exhibit a more aggressive clinical course compared to their SF-1 lineage counterparts in patients with nonfunctional PitNETs.

  However, despite these advances, the widespread implementation of immunohistochemical classification poses two critical challenges: first, the absence of standardized thresholds for antibody interpretation [5, 6]; and secondly, limited accessibility to these reagents in resource-constrained settings [7].

  The ability to predict lineages directly from H&E-stained histology slides could address these limitations by circumventing the need for ancillary immunohistochemical assays while reducing manual interpretive demands. Recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) have demonstrated remarkable efficacy in diagnostic tasks, heralding a new era in medical diagnostics. The notion of completely substituting immunohistochemical staining with AI proposes a significant reduction in workload; however, this strategy would only be feasible if the AI’s diagnostic performance aligns closely with that of the established gold standard. Consequently, we explored the potential of a semi-automated system to predict lineages directly from H&E-stained histology slides (Fig. 1). This system, which integrates deep learning with human decision-making processes, could substantially alleviate workload while concurrently preserving diagnostic accuracy.

## Method

### Study design and participants
  This study was designed as a multicenter observational investigation. The participant pool consisted of individuals diagnosed with PitNETs who had undergone surgical resection, with the availability of H&E-stained slides and an adequate tumor sample for analysis.

  This multicenter study incorporated four independent cohorts derived from five institutions. The primary cohort (training cohort), comprising 1,328 surgically treated patients from Huashan Hospital (2018–2021), was utilized for model development and internal validation to establish baseline performance metrics. A second cohort, who underwent surgery 2022 and after, served prospective validation. The third cohort specifically assessed model generalizability in functional PitNETs diagnosis, while the fourth (external validation cohort) aggregated data from four geographically distinct centers to evaluate cross-institutional robustness. Notably, heterogeneity in histopathological protocols – including sample processing, staining, and slide digitization – was observed across external validation sites.

  This study was approved by the institutional review board at Huashan Hospital and received ethics approval from each participating center, with informed consent waived by the respective ethics committees in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study adheres to STROBE Checklist, detailed in Supplementary Table 1. An illustrative flowchart detailing the study design is presented in Fig. 1 to clarify the methodology.


### Sample Preparation
  At each participating center, slide preparation involved cutting tissue sections with a thickness of 3 μm from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded blocks. These sections were then stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E), transcription factors (SF-1, TPIT and PIT-1), and pituitary hormones (prolactin, growth hormone, follicle stimulating hormone, luteinizing hormone, adrenocorticotropic hormone, and thyroid-stimulating hormone). Expert pathologists reviewed the stained slides at each center to verify tumor tissue presence. Whole-slide images (WSIs) were captured at 20X or 40X magnification, utilizing either a Panoramic 250 FLASH/MIDI Scanner (3D HISTECH, Budapest, Hungary) or a Motic VM1000 Scanner (Xiamen, China).

  IHC was evaluated by two experienced pathologists. A positive result was defined by the nuclear expression of transcription factors (PIT-1, SF-1, TPIT), while a negative result was characterized by the absence of nuclear expression of these transcription factors (PIT-1, SF-1, TPIT). Typically, a tumor was categorized into a specific lineage based on the positive staining of a transcription factor within its tissue. Tumors that did not show positivity for any transcription factor, or those that displayed positivity for multiple transcription factors, were excluded from the training dataset.


### WSI preprocessing and model training
  Initially, we employed an Otsu automatic thresholding segmentation algorithm to differentiate the foreground (tissue sections) from the background (white areas) in WSIs [8]. At a magnification scale of 20X, patches of size 256 × 256 pixels were extracted using a sliding window technique.

  We then utilized the K-means clustering algorithm to cluster all patches from each WSI, and selected representative patches from each cluster through a central sampling method. This approach effectively addresses the challenge of redundant information arising from the high volume of similar patches. The predetermined number of clusters, C, dictates the extraction of C patches to represent the entirety of the WSI.

  For feature extraction, a CtransPath encoder is employed, combining a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) with a multi-scale Swin Transformer architecture, and pre-trained on a vast dataset of unlabeled pathological images [9]. This encoder translates each 256 × 256 × 3 dimensional patch into a 768-dimensional feature vector, facilitating the extraction of pertinent features into a lower-dimensional space, thereby significantly reducing computational demands.

  After feature extraction, each WSI image is transformed into a C×768-dimensional feature matrix. As shown in Fig. 1, to aggregate local patch-level features into a global representation, a feature fusion module based on an attention mechanism is employed. This module first adaptively computes an attention score for each local image patch, reflecting its contribution to the global classification task. Subsequently, a weighted aggregation of the local features, guided by these scores, yields a 768-dimensional global feature vector. This global representation is then fed into a fully connected layer for final classification. Ultimately, the model generates two outputs for each slide: a predicted lineage classification and a confidence index. The lineage classification identifies the tumor’s transcription factor lineage, while the confidence index (ranging from 0 to 1) quantifies the model’s certainty in its prediction, with values closer to 1 indicating higher confidence.


### Training process
  The training cohort was divided into training, validation, and test sets in a 4:1:1 ratio and implemented a 5-fold cross-validation on the train and validation set. The model is optimized using the Adam optimizer, with a learning rate set at 1e-4. The number of clustering classes C is established at 100. To mitigate the risk of overfitting, the model employs dropout, set at a rate of 0.5, along with an early stopping technique. The model is developed in Python and utilizes the PyTorch framework. The computational experiments, encompassing both training and inference phases, are conducted on a workstation equipped with an Intel Xeon Silver 4114 CPU and an NVIDIA GeForce TITAN V GPU.


### Interpretation
  Within each lineage classification, we extracted 200 patches with the highest attention scores from the testing cohort. We used regionprop function in the Matlab to extract cellular features and intercellular features from each patch, and compared among the different classifications. These features represent the size of the cells, their regularity, the density of the cells, the distance between cells, and whether the arrangement is orderly.

  For each WSI, we utilized a color map to visually represent attention scores: patches with higher scores appeared in red, denoting more significant contributions to the final prediction, while those with lower scores were shown in blue, indicating lesser contributions. Finally, merging these patches resulted in an interpretable heatmap, visually demonstrating the model’s attention-based predictions. The interpretable heatmap were then visually compared to the true immunochemical staining of the transcription factors.

### The accuracy of transcription factor immunostaining
  We initially assessed the diagnostic concordance of transcription factor status determined via immunohistochemistry (IHC) and manual histopathological assessment in an independent cohort with 2,786 patients. Among 1,182 patients diagnosed with SF-1 lineage tumors, 68 exhibited either negative or ambiguous IHC staining for SF-1, 6 exhibited false positive staining for TPIT or PIT-1. Among 704 patients diagnosed with TPIT lineage tumors, 6 were found to have negative staining for TPIT, 5 were found to have false positive staining for SF-1 or PIT-1. Furthermore, among 900 patients diagnosed as having PIT-1 lineage tumors, 32 showed negative or ambiguous staining for PIT-1, 12 showed false positive staining for SF-1 or TPIT. Overall, the accuracy of utilizing transcription factor IHC staining to subtype PitNETs was determined to be 95.4% and the corresponding area under the curve (AUC) was 0.985 (95% CI, 0.984–0.986).


### Fully automated approach shows suboptimal performance
  Then, we developed and internally tested a fully automated model using digitally scanned H&E-stained whole slide images in 1,328 patients. Within the test dataset in this cohort, the model achieved an accuracy of 92.5% in determining SF1 lineage, 93.7% for TPIT lineage, and 95.2% for PIT1 lineage. However, it demonstrated a 5.9% decrease in accuracy and a 0.011 reduction in the AUC (p < 0.001), compared to diagnoses based on IHC and manual review (Fig. 2). Similarly, in the prospective cohort of 268 patients, employing the fully automated model resulted in a 16.1% decrease in accuracy and a 0.040 reduction in the AUC (p < 0.001, Fig. 2).

### Semi-automated approach selects patients for manual review
  We then developed a semi-automated approach, which serves as an intermediary between the previously described fully manual review processes and fully automated model. This semi-automated method involves establishing a confidence threshold; samples below this threshold are directed towards IHC staining and manual evaluation, while those exceeding it undergo automated subtyping. This approach yielded a notable increase in accuracy, alongside a diminished but still significant reduction in workload, particularly when a higher confidence cutoff was employed (Fig. 2B). Setting the confidence threshold above 0.8 allows the model’s accuracy to align closely with that achieved through fully manual reviewing (p = 0.856). 78.0% of the slides were eligible for automated subtyping, leaving the remaining 22.0% for further IHC staining and manual evaluation.

  In the prospective cohort, the implementation of the semi-automated approach resulted in a 68.9% reduction in the workload of IHC staining. This significant decrease in manual labor was accompanied by 95.9% in overall accuracy (Fig. 3), which was statistically comparable (p = 0.610) to that achieved through fully IHC staining and manual evaluation by pathologists.

  The semi-automated approach achieved an accuracy of 99.0% when validated within 256 patients diagnosed with functional PitNETs, wherein 74.4% of the slides were suitable for automated subtyping, leaving the remaining 25.6% of slides to undergo further IHC staining. In the external cohort comprising 206 patients from four external centers, the implementation of the semi-automated approach with a confidence cut-off of 0.8 yielded an accuracy of 86.0% (Fig. 3). To enhance accuracy further, the confidence cut-off could be raised to 0.9. Under this scenario, although the workload reduction was only 39.3%, the accuracy significantly improved to 95.1%. Importantly, the performance of the semi-automated approach under this condition was statistically comparable (p = 0.293) to that obtained through fully IHC staining and manual evaluation.


### Estimated cost for the semi-automated model
  The clinical implementation entails initially performing a hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) scan and inputting the digitized images into the model. In instances where the model yields a high-confidence result, the output can be directly utilized. Conversely, in cases of low-confidence results, manual confirmatory IHC staining is required (Fig. 4).

We further enhanced our analysis by generating a virtual immunohistochemical (IHC) staining image using the color map. Additionally, we randomly selected 50 cases from each lineage and requested two pathologists to evaluate the resemblance between the virtual IHC and the real IHC using a three-point scale (3: totally similar; 2: partially similar; 1: not similar). ## Discussion
  In this study, we introduced an AI-based tool designed for clinical application, specifically for predicting lineage from routine hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stained slides in patients with Pituitary Neuroendocrine Tumors (PitNETs). This model underwent rigorous validation using prospective and independent external datasets, demonstrating its ability to accurately classify over 96% of patients.

  Although substituting transcription factor staining for hormone staining in PitNETs reduces the incidence of false-negative and false-positive results, challenges related to diagnostic pitfalls and interobserver reproducibility persist. The literature indicates that false-negatives and false-positives remain, with reported sensitivity and specificity rates of approximately 96% and 99%, respectively [10]. Furthermore, while no studies have specifically reported interobserver agreement for transcription factor staining, a concordance correlation coefficient ranging from 0.963 to 0.997 has been observed for interobserver agreement when utilizing somatostatin receptor subtype 2a (SSTR-2a) immunostaining in patients with PitNETs [11].

  Leveraging the advanced AI model, we created a clinical decision support system designed to optimize performance while significantly reducing workload. This system fills the gap between fully automated and entirely manual review processes by implementing a semi-automated, triage-driven approach. Literature also supports the effectiveness of such semi-automated or triage-driven approaches in improving diagnostic accuracy [12–14]. This approach could lead to a significant reduction in pathologists’ workload, potentially by more than 50%. Consequently, pathologists can dedicate more attention and resources to cases that are equivocal or necessitate in-depth analysis.

  In clinical settings, the model has the potential to serve as an effective pre-screening tool, capable of excluding at least one-third of patients from the need for transcription factor immunostaining. By adjusting the confidence cutoffs, the model can be finely tuned to produce satisfactory outcomes tailored to specific clinical needs. With immunohistochemistry turnaround times averaging around seven days in various clinical environments, deploying this model for pre-screening—capable of delivering results in just a few hours—presents a tangible opportunity for substantial time savings for both pathologists and patients. Moreover, it offers the possibility to optimize costs of transcription factor testing within pathology laboratories. This is particularly beneficial for institutions where transcription factor immunostaining facilities are not readily available.

  To address the overfitting risk, we implemented a series of methodological approaches. Initially, by incorporating data from multiple centers and accommodating three different scanner types, our dataset comprehensively represents the major sources of heterogeneity that could impact model training. Additionally, the integration of clustering methods during training effectively minimized feature redundancy, enhancing the robustness of the model [15]. The Swin Transformer architecture can dynamically adjust the range of attention, aiding the model in better understanding image content, especially in interpreting complex scenes and capturing details [16]. Furthermore, we employed color-jittering and attention mechanisms, innovative techniques aimed at reinforcing the causal relationships between histopathological features and transcription factor lineages [9, 17]. These approaches address the potential issue of pseudocorrelation—a common challenge in conventional AI models—by ensuring that the model’s inferences are grounded in genuine pathological evidence rather than coincidental patterns within the training data.

  Our study introduces a novel implementation within the emerging domain of virtual immunostaining. Prior methodologies, particularly those leveraging generative adversarial networks (GANs), have demonstrated transformative potential in digital pathology by computationally generating immunostaining patterns from unstained or H&E-stained tissue sections. These approaches utilize adversarial training between a generator and discriminator to synthesize pixel-level representations of molecular biomarkers, bypassing traditional physical staining protocols [18–20]. However, critical limitations persist, including restricted generalizability across heterogeneous tissue types, staining protocols, and imaging systems, which can diminish accuracy in external validation cohorts. In contrast, our methodology demonstrates superior generalizability in external datasets and provides an alternative to GAN-driven approaches through its inherently explainable framework.

  Our study highlights several limitations. First, a critical limitation relates to the confidence index that influences workload reduction, the determinants of which remain incompletely characterized. A plausible hypothesis posits that H&E slide quality—including factors such as tissue adequacy (challenging in smaller tumors), section thickness, and staining homogeneity—may significantly influence this metric. In future analyses, we plan to investigate the biological characteristics of patients classified with high confidence versus those with low confidence, thereby evaluating whether such disparities reflect genuine biological ambiguity.

  Second, our predictive framework focused exclusively on three transcription factors. Transcription factor represents an imperfect surrogate marker and we plan to explore the integration of biological or molecular anchors in future research to enhance the model’s accuracy and robustness. Thus, the model is not designed to refine the current WHO classification framework, and any such enhancements warrant additional biological research. Moreover, delineating tumor lineage represents only the initial step in the PitNET classification process, comprehensive pathological evaluation of PitNETs may still necessitate integrating hormonal markers (ACTH, FSH, LH, PRL, GH, TSH, GATA3, Ki-67, and p53) alongside histomorphological analysis by expert pathologists.

  Third, the reliance on H&E-stained slides—requiring time-intensive processing—limits real-time applicability; future studies should explore predictive models using intraoperative frozen sections or Raman spectroscopy.

  Finally, although our AI system exhibits promise for web-based deployment, it remains investigational and has not yet received clinical certification. This is particularly relevant for cases involving ambiguous or dual-lineage presentations. In future research, we plan to investigate the selection of ambiguous cases to develop a specialized model or to separate the two lineages for independent training. However, such cases are rare and require extended periods for data collection. Prospective randomized controlled studies are needed to further validate the model, ensuring its analytical rigor and clinical safety in real-world applications.

  In conclusion, incorporating the heuristics of pathologist decision-making into a semi-automated approach presents a compelling alternative to fully automated classification models. This model underwent rigorous validation using prospective and independent external datasets, demonstrating its ability to accurately subtyping patients.
