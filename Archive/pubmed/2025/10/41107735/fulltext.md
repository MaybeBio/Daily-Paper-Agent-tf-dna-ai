# Black pepper knowledge base (BlackPepKB): a centralized web resource for functional genomics of black pepper (Piper nigrum L.)


## Background
  Black pepper (Piper nigrum L.) is a highly valued spice crop with significant economic, medicinal, and cultural importance. While genomic and transcriptomic data for black pepper have rapidly accumulated in recent years, there is currently no dedicated genomic database to serve as a centralized web resource for the research and breeding community. Furthermore, despite the availability of a reference genome, the functional annotation of its proteome and a comprehensive repertoire of its transcription factors (TFs) remain incomplete.


## Results
  We identified a total of 2,503 TF-encoding genes in black pepper (PnTFs), classified into 62 TF families. Notably, 471 intronless PnTF genes were identified, with more than 50% of genes in families such as Dof, ERF, GeBP, GRAS, NF-YB, RAV, TCP, Trihelix, and ZF-HD, suggesting their potential roles in regulating stress responses. Protein-protein interaction network analysis identified 171 hub PnTFs that may serve as key regulators in diverse biological processes. In addition, Gene Ontology-based functional annotation of 63,463 proteins in black pepper revealed that 35,670 (56.2%) were annotated with at least one GO term. To support continued research and data exploration, we developed Black Pepper Knowledge Base (BlackPepKB), a user-friendly, integrative genomic platform for black pepper. BlackPepKB hosts genomic, transcriptomic, and functional annotation data, and incorporates interactive tools to enhance data accessibility. Key features include an electronic Fluorescent Pictograph (eFP) browser for visualizing gene expression patterns across tissues and developmental stages, JBrowse2 for genome navigation, BLAST for sequence similarity searches, and GeneViz for exon-intron gene structure visualization. Additionally, PepperExp and PepperClust tools enable heatmap generation and hierarchical clustering of gene expression data, while GO-Pep facilitates the retrieval of GO terms. The platform also offers user-friendly interfaces for querying single nucleotide polymorphisms and exploring TF families in black pepper.


## Conclusion
  BlackPepKB serves as the first dedicated genomic database for black pepper, providing a comprehensive and accessible platform for functional genomics research. This work lays a solid foundation for advancing genetic research and molecular breeding in black pepper. BlackPepKB is publicly accessible at: https://black-pepper-genomic-resource.vercel.app/.

## Background
  Black pepper (Piper nigrum L.), commonly referred to as the ‘King of Spices,’ is among the most widely traded and extensively used spices globally. This flowering, woody perennial climber belongs to the family Piperaceae, the largest family within the order Piperales [1–3]. For generations, black pepper has been valued not only for enhancing the flavor and aroma of food but also for its use as a preservative and for its medicinal properties [4–6].

  In recent decades, advances in genome sequencing have contributed immensely to the growing genomic resources available for economically important non-model crops. The draft genome of black pepper (2n = 52), comprising 761.22 Mb of sequences assembled into 26 pseudo-chromosomes [7], serves as a valuable resource for identifying key genes and regulatory pathways involved in various biological processes. Additionally, several transcriptome datasets are now available, providing valuable insights into gene expression across different developmental stages and tissues of black pepper, further enriching the genomic resources for this species [8–11]. Moreover, there is a continuous accumulation of molecular marker information, particularly simple sequence repeats (SSRs) [12] and single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) [13, 14], enhancing the potential for studies on genetic diversity and population structure analysis and crop improvement in black pepper. This growing wealth of genomic and transcriptomic data facilitates precision breeding strategies aimed at improving yield, enhancing medicinal properties, and strengthening resistance to biotic and abiotic stresses, ultimately contributing to the sustainable production of this economically important spice crop.

  With the accumulation of omics datasets, species-specific databases have been developed by various research communities for both model and non-model crops, including rice [15], maize [16], soybean [17], wheat [18], and sorghum [19]. These databases, equipped with diverse interfaces and tools, serve as centralized repositories for genomic and transcriptomic data, facilitating research and breeding efforts. However, only a limited number of web-based platforms are available for black pepper to assist researchers in the visualization of genomic and transcriptomic data. For instance, Piperpep [20] is a database that hosts experimentally generated peptides from the black pepper leaves, containing information on 1,001 peptides, including 36 antimicrobial peptides. Additionally, Negi and coworkers in 2021 developed a database to catalog a set of differentially expressed genes, transcription factors (TFs), pathways, protein domains, and families, as well as putative markers (SSRs, SNPs, and indels) associated with drought stress in black pepper [21]. Another valuable web resource reported is the Black Pepper Polymorphic Microsatellite Database (BlackP2MSATdb), which provides a platform for visualizing genomic and polymorphic SSRs of black pepper [12]. Despite the availability of these resources, none of the existing databases provides an interactive platform for visualizing gene expression data across different tissues and developmental stages. An electronic Fluorescent Pictograph (eFP) browser [22–24] would address this gap by enabling intuitive visualization of spatial and temporal gene expression patterns, aiding in functional genomics studies.

  The black pepper genome is predicted to contain 63,466 gene models; however, functional annotation of its proteome remains largely unexplored. Additionally, research on the identification and characterization of TFs in this crop is limited. Recently, transcriptome analysis of black pepper leaves under drought conditions has identified the expression of 2,110 TFs [21]. Furthermore, genome-wide analysis has reported 5,082 TFs belonging to 75 gene families [7], yet detailed functional characterization of these TFs is not reported. More recently, 160 MYB-R2R3 TFs [25], 34 SBP-box gene family members [26], and 46 Dof (DNA binding with one finger) gene family members [27] have been identified in black pepper. Despite these efforts, a comprehensive catalog of black pepper TFs is still unavailable for researchers. Thus, establishing a curated database of black pepper, along with functional annotations and expression profiles, would greatly enhance genomic research and facilitate crop improvement strategies. Therefore, the present study aimed to (i) predict and annotate the full complement of TF encoding genes in black pepper and investigate their functional relationship with the target genes, (ii) perform functional annotations of genes based on their Gene Ontology (GO) terms, and (iii) develop a web resource along with user friendly tools to display gene structures, functional annotations, expression data, and SNP information along with an integrated eFP browser for visualizing gene expression patterns in black pepper.


## Methods

### Identification and classification of TF repertoire in black pepper
  The black pepper genome assemblies and corresponding annotations were downloaded from http://cotton.hzau.edu.cn/EN/Download.htm [28]. To identify the TF-encoding genes within the P. nigrum reference genome [7], profile Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) representing 62 TF families [29–31], including DNA-binding domains (DBDs), auxiliary domains, and forbidden domains, were retrieved from Pfam (now InterPro [32]). The details of the protein domains used in this analysis are provided in Additional file 1.

  The black pepper proteome dataset [28] was then scanned using the hmmscan program from the HMMER package [33, 34], applying an E-value of 1e-10. Based on established TF family assignment rules [35], the identified genes were assigned to corresponding TF families. These putative TFs are hereafter referred to as PnTFs. To validate the presence of conserved domains in predicted TFs, multiple domain annotation tools were employed, including the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Batch Conserved Domain (CD)-Search tool [36, 37], InterProScan tool [38, 39], the PlantTFcat server [40, 41], and the iTAK program [42, 43].


### Structural and functional annotation of PnTFs
  The molecular weight (MW), aromaticity, instability index, and isoelectric point (pI) of PnTFs were calculated using the Bio.SeqUtils.ProtParam module of Biopython in Python [44]. Additionally, the length of each sequence was determined using the built-in len() function. The subcellular localizations of the PnTFs were predicted using the Bologna Unified Subcellular Component Annotator (BUSCA) web server [45, 46].

  To investigate the expression patterns of TFs, transcript levels of PnTF-encoding genes were analysed across various black pepper tissues, including berries (20 days after pollination (DAP), 40 DAP, 2 months after pollination (MAP), 4 MAP, 6 MAP and 8 MAP), stem, root, leaf, and flower; expression data were obtained from Hu et al. [7]. In addition, to explore the biological processes associated with the identified PnTFs, functional enrichment analysis was performed using the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING) database (version 11.5) [47, 48], applying a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of 0.05.


### Identification of CIS-acting regulatory elements in promoter regions of PnTF-encoding genes
  To identify cis-acting regulatory elements (CREs) in the promoter regions of PnTF-encoding genes, 1000 bp sequences upstream of the transcription start site of each gene were extracted from the black pepper genome sequence using the SeqIO module of Biopython in Python [44]. A curated list of 132 CREs associated with abiotic and biotic stress responses, growth and development, light-responsiveness, and phytohormone-responsiveness was retrieved from the plant cis-acting regulatory DNA elements (PLACE) database (version 30.0) [49, 50]. These CREs (listed in Additional file 2) were then used as query motifs to scan the extracted promoter sequences using the fuzznuc program from the EMBOSS package [51, 52].


### Analysis of protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks
  Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed for PnTFs belonging to different TF families along with their 50 first-shell interacting proteins, using the STRING database. Only interactions with at least medium confidence (interaction score ≥ 0.400) and a FDR stringency of 0.05 were considered for further analysis. PPI networks corresponding to each TF family were constructed using the NetworkX package in Python [53]. Hub nodes within each network were identified based on their centrality, calculated using the Maximum Clique Centrality (MCC) algorithm [54]. The top 10% of nodes with the highest MCC scores were identified as hub nodes. The resulting PPI networks were visualized using Cytoscape software (version 3.9.1) [55], employing the prefuse force-directed layout.


### Gene ontology (GO) annotation
  Protein sequences containing more than 10 amino acids were annotated using the BLAST2GO pipeline [56]. Briefly, BLASTP searches were performed against the NCBI non-redundant (nr) protein database, using Arabidopsis thaliana as the reference. The resulting BLAST hits were mapped to GO terms by matching sequence identifiers against the Gene Ontology Annotation (GOA) database [57, 58]. GO terms were assigned based on sequence similarity, GO hierarchy, and annotation quality, using the following default thresholds: an E-value cutoff of 1e-6, a hit filter of 500, a GO weight of 5, and an annotation score cutoff of 55 [59]. GO term annotations were classified into three main categories: biological processes (BP), molecular functions (MF), and cellular components (CC).


### Database architecture and content
  The BlackPepKB database was designed using MySQL 8.0.35, a robust and scalable relational database management system, structured based on an entity-relationship model (Additional file 3). The database supports the efficient exploration of publicly available genomic datasets for black pepper, including genomic assemblies retrieved from the Group of Cotton Genetic Improvement (GCGI) repository [28], normalized transcriptomic profiles obtained from the MagnoliidsGDB database [60, 61], and SNP data [13, 14]. In addition, the database integrates GO annotations and TF family information to facilitate functional and regulatory analyses. The full-stack platform was built using Next.js version 14.1.0, Node.js version 20.18.0, and npm version 10.8.2.

  Several interactive tools were integrated into the BlackPepKB platform to enhance its functionality. Pepper-eFP, built using the R Shiny framework, enables visualization of gene expression across tissues in absolute, relative, and comparative modes. PepperExp, another R Shiny-based tool, facilitates visualization of gene expression data with interactive user interface elements and dynamic plotting. A customized BLAST interface implemented using BLAST+ v2.16.0 [62] allows users to perform sequence similarity searches against the black pepper genome, coding sequences, and protein datasets via a user-friendly web interface. JBrowse2 [63] was integrated using React and Next.js to provide intuitive genome browsing, with tracks for sequences, annotated gene features, and variants. GeneViz, developed with React and D3.js, provides graphical representations of exon-intron gene structures for a given list of gene identifiers (gene IDs). PepperClust, built using Shiny and the heatmaply package, supports hierarchical clustering of expression data, with visualization options including both heatmaps and dendrograms. The GO-Pep tool, built in React, enables the retrieval and visualization of GO annotations for user-input genes, including hyperlinks to AmiGO. The source code for all tools is available at: 10.5281/zenodo.15844965.


## Results

### Collection of TFs in the black pepper genome
  A total of 2,503 PnTFs belonging to 62 families were identified, with the number of PnTFs per TF family ranging from 1 to 217 (Fig. 1). Notably, several TF families, including ERF (Ethylene Responsive Factor), bHLH (basic Helix-Loop-Helix), MYB (myeloblastosis), NAC [NAM (no apical meristem), ATAF1-2 (Arabidopsis thaliana activating factor), and CUC2 (cup-shaped cotyledon)], and WRKY, represent more than 5% of the total PnTF landscape. Conversely, families such as HB-PHD (Homeobox-Plant Homeodomain), LFY (Leafy), and NZZ-SPL (Nozzle-Sporocyteless), each constitute less than 0.05% of the total PnTFs.

  Comparative analysis of TF families in P. nigrum and four other species, including A. thaliana, Oryza sativa, Glycine max, and Zea mays, revealed that the bHLH, bZIP (basic Leucine Zipper), ERF, MYB, and NAC families each contained more than 100 members in all examined species (Table 1). The ERF family had the highest number of members (217) in P. nigrum, followed by bHLH (208) and MYB (203). Additionally, the TAZ family was notably more prevalent in P. nigrum, with 27 genes identified, compared to other species (Table 1). TF families HB-PHD, HRT-like (Hairy-Related TFs–like), LFY, NF-X1 (Nuclear TF, X-box binding 1), NZZ-SPL, S1Fa-like (Site 1 binding factor a–like), SAP (Stress-Associated Proteins), STAT (Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription), VOZ (Vascular plant One Zinc-finger proteins), and Whirly were represented by fewer than five members per family (Table 1). Notably, members of the NZZ-SPL family were observed only in P. nigrum and A. thaliana.

  PnTFs were generally distributed randomly across chromosomes, with some exceptions (Fig. 2). TF families with fewer than five members were predominantly localized to one to three chromosomes. The characteristics of PnTFs, such as their protein lengths, MWs, aromaticity, instability indices, and pI, provide valuable insights into their functional diversity, stability, and roles in regulating gene expression and responding to environmental stimuli in black pepper. The lengths of the PnTFs varied widely among different families, ranging from 68 to 1,866 amino acids. The average MW of the PnTFs was 45.29 kDa, with a range spanning from 7.07 kDa to 211.45 kDa, which is typical for plant proteins [64]. The aromaticity values of the PnTFs ranged from 0.01 to 0.17, with an average of 0.07. The instability index varied between 14.82 and 91.25, averaging 53.43. Additionally, the pI ranged from 4.30 to 11.90, with an average value of 7.57. The physicochemical properties of PnTFs are detailed in Additional file 4.

  The number of introns in genes plays a significant role in regulating gene expression in plants. Notably, this analysis identified a considerable number of intronless PnTF-encoding genes (Additional file 6). TF families with 50% or more intronless PnTFs include Dof, ERF, GeBP, GRAS, NF-YB, RAV, TCP, Trihelix, and ZF-HD.

  In silico analysis of the expression profiles of PnTF-encoding genes across various tissues, including berries, stem, root, leaf, and flower tissues, revealed distinct expression patterns among these genes (Additional file 7). The top ten highly expressed TF-encoding genes in each tissue type are summarized in Table 2. Notably, several bZIP (Pn10.265, Pn5.2068), ERF (Pn4.253), GRAS (Pn37.143), HD-ZIP (Pn5.1004), MIKC-MADS (Pn6.1125, Pn8.973), and NAC (Pn12.1527, Pn21.290) transcription factors were highly expressed during black pepper berry development. In flower tissues, MYB TFs such as Pn14.1852, Pn1.180, Pn5.3516, Pn1.90, Pn14.1695, and Pn9.2199 were highly expressed. Conversely, MYB TFs Pn6.2118, Pn13.1009, and Pn9.2199 showed higher expression in root tissues. Additionally, several ERF TFs, including Pn6.1753, Pn8.941, Pn4.253, and Pn1.1393, were highly expressed in root tissues.

  GO enrichment analysis was conducted using the STRING database to explore the biological functions of the predicted PnTFs. Generally, the majority of TF families had the highest percentage of PnTF genes involved in the regulation of transcription, with enrichment for GO terms such as ‘regulation of transcription, DNA-templated (GO:0006355)’, ‘positive regulation of transcription, DNA-templated (GO:0045893)’, and ‘negative regulation of transcription, DNA-templated (GO:0045892)’. ### Gene ontology-based proteome annotation of black pepper
  Of the 63,463 predicted protein sequences in black pepper, 35,670 (56.2%) were annotated with at least one GO term, while the remaining 27,792 sequences (43.8%) remained unannotated (Additional file 9). Among the annotated proteins, 30,762 proteins were classified under the BP category, 30,792 under MF, and 29,884 under CC. The top 10 most frequent GO terms in each category, based on counts, are presented in Fig. 5. Notably, more than 1,000 genes were associated with stress-related biological processes, including response to water deprivation (1,490), response to abscisic acid (1,435), response to salt stress (1,424), defense response to bacterium (1,241), defense response to fungus (1,200), response to cold (1,173), and response to wounding (1,071).

### Functionalities of BlackPepKB
  BlackPepKB is a comprehensive, integrated knowledge base designed to support black pepper research by hosting a diverse collection of biological data and interactive tools. The homepage (Fig. 6a) includes a search module that enables users to retrieve gene-specific information by entering a gene ID. Upon submission, a dynamically generated summary page displays relevant gene data in a structured, intuitive layout. This summary page is organized into four main sections, accessible through a tabbed navigation system. By default, the Gene Details tab is displayed, providing information such as chromosome number, TF family, and genomic coordinates. The Coding Sequence (CDS) tab provides the complete nucleotide sequence of the gene, while the Protein Sequence tab displays the translated amino acid sequence. Functional annotations are accessible through the Gene Ontology tab, which presents associated GO terms in a sortable table. Additionally, the Tools and Databases section (Fig. 6b) provides quick access to specialized utilities in BlackPepKB, facilitating seamless navigation and exploration of relevant resources.

  The BlackPepKB platform currently contains information on 63,451 genes, 62 TF families, 273,018 GO annotations, and 432,418 SNPs. To support in-depth data exploration, BlackPepKB integrates several interactive tools, including Pepper-eFP Browser for visualizing tissue-specific gene expression patterns, JBrowse2 for interactive genome browsing, BLAST for sequence alignment and similarity searches, GO-Pep for exploring GO-based functional annotations, GeneViz for viewing gene structures, PepperExp for exploring gene expression heatmaps, and PepperClust for hierarchical clustering of gene expression data. #### Pepper-eFP browser
  This comprises a four-step workflow: selecting the data source, configuring the visualization mode, adjusting expression thresholds, and rendering the output. Users can choose from three data categories: development, biotic stress-responsive, and abiotic stress-responsive, based on the biological context of interest. The browser supports three distinct visualization modes tailored for different analytical purposes: (i) Absolute Mode displays raw expression levels of a gene across tissues, using a color gradient ranging from yellow (low expression) to red (high expression). For example, Fig. 7a illustrates gene Pn17.207 in “Absolute” mode, displaying strong expression in berries after 6 months of pollination, while leaf and flower tissues show minimal activity; (ii) Relative Mode normalizes gene expression values against a user-selected control tissue. Red indicates higher expression relative to the control, while yellow-to-blue represents lower expression. As shown in Fig. 7b, gene Pn17.207 demonstrates increased expression in roots, stems, and berries (40 DAP, 2 to 8 MAP) compared to the flower (used as the control). An optional grey masking feature conceals tissues with low expression to enhance visual clarity (Fig. 7c); (iii) Compare Mode allows users to assess expression differences between two genes across tissues. A yellow-to-orange gradient highlights tissues where the first gene (primary gene) is more expressed, while green-grey to blue indicates dominance of the second gene (secondary gene). Figure 7d illustrates this mode by comparing Pn3.482 and Pn1.1112, revealing distinct tissue-specific expression dynamics.

#### Genome browser
  To facilitate genomic data exploration in black pepper, we have integrated a genome browser within BlackPepKB using JBrowse2. Currently, the draft reference genome and corresponding gene models have been imported into the browser. Users can navigate the genome using two primary navigation modes: (i) “Show all regions of assembly”, which displays a comprehensive overview of the genome, and (ii) a search function that enables targeted queries by sequences, features, or genomic coordinates. The browser interface features three synchronized tracks to support multi-scale visualization: (i) reference sequence track enables scalable browsing from chromosome-scale view down to the single-nucleotide level, displaying underlying DNA sequence; (ii) annotation track displays detailed gene model information such as genomic coordinates, feature types, lengths, unique identifiers, source data, and the complete coding sequence (FASTA format); (iii) Variant track, highlights the locations of SNPs and indels relative to the reference sequence. This multi-track layout enables the simultaneous visualization of sequence data, gene structures, and genetic variants, offering researchers a comprehensive and integrated view of genomic features. Additionally, interactive exploration of genomic features is supported through pop-up windows that display detailed biological information, such as variant annotations and gene model metadata, when users click on elements within the tracks.


#### BLAST
  To facilitate sequence similarity searches, a BLAST search tool was integrated into the BlackPepKB platform. The interface built with React supports both nucleotide (BLASTn) and protein (BLASTp) searches. Users can perform searches against three databases: P. nigrum genome (Piper nigrum_genome_db), coding sequences (Piper nigrum_cds_db), and protein sequences (Piper nigrum_prot_db). The BLAST interface features an intuitive layout that accepts input via direct FASTA sequence or sequence file upload (up to 2 Mb). The system incorporates automatic sequence type detection and cross-validates it with the selected BLAST algorithm, helping to prevent submission errors, for example, avoiding the use of a protein sequence with BLASTn. To further streamline the process, context-aware database filtering ensures that only compatible databases are displayed based on the chosen program type. Additionally, an expandable settings panel offers customized options such as E-value thresholds and the maximum number of alignments, supporting more refined and tailored searches. BLAST results are presented in two integrated formats: (i) a summary view that provides key alignment statistics, including E-values, scores, and identity percentages for each hit, allowing users to quickly assess the relevance of the matches; (ii) a detailed pairwise alignment view that visually delineates mismatches and gaps for improved interpretability. In cases where no significant hits are returned, the system proactively suggests parameter adjustments, guiding users toward more effective query configurations.


#### GO-Pep
  This web-based tool was developed to facilitate the efficient retrieval and exploration of GO annotations for black pepper genes. The tool supports the input of up to five gene IDs per query, retrieving their associated GO terms, systematically organized into three major GO domains: BP, MF, and CC. The user interface features a dynamic input panel, enabling users to add or remove gene IDs with ease. A “Try Example” option is provided to autofill the input field with sample gene IDs for demonstration purposes. The tool also incorporates built-in input validation, preventing the submission of empty or invalid queries by providing immediate feedback.

  Upon submission, GO-Pep retrieves relevant annotations from a MySQL database and presents the retrieved annotations in a structured and intuitive layout. Each queried gene is displayed as an individual entry, with its associated GO terms grouped by category. Each GO term is hyperlinked to its corresponding entry in the AmiGO database, facilitating quick access to detailed term definitions and related functional information. GO-Pep also incorporates robust error-handling capabilities. Users receive alerts if no annotations are found for a queried gene, if invalid gene IDs are submitted, or if a search is attempted without input. Additionally, the interface supports seamless toggling between the query and results views, while retaining input data to facilitate iterative searches and enhance overall workflow efficiency.


#### GeneViz
  A web-based application designed to facilitate the graphical visualization of exon-intron structures in black pepper genes. It accepts up to five gene IDs as input and generates a graphical representation of gene structures, facilitating the comparison of gene architecture across multiple entries. The user interface includes a text input field that accommodates gene IDs separated by either commas or line breaks. A “Try an Example” option auto-fills the input field with sample gene IDs to demonstrate functionality. To ensure robustness and user-friendly interaction, the system performs real-time validation to reject empty or improperly formatted submissions with appropriate error messages. Upon query submission, GeneViz accesses a MySQL database containing genomic boundary coordinates for both coding (exonic) and non-coding (intronic) regions. The resulting visualization panel displays each queried gene’s structure along with its gene ID, and exon and intron counts. Elements are proportionally scaled based on their genomic positions, offering an accurate, at-a-glance representation of gene organization. In addition, the tool provides clear feedback when invalid IDs are submitted, when the input field is left empty, or when no structural information is available for a queried gene, helping users troubleshoot and refine their input.


#### PepperExp
  This web-based application was designed to facilitate the interactive exploration of gene expression patterns across different tissues of black pepper using heatmap-based visualizations. Integrating gene expression data from multiple independent transcriptomic studies, the tool offers a unified platform for examining gene expression patterns in tissues such as fruit, leaf, root, and flower. The user interface allows multiple gene IDs, which can be entered as comma-separated or line-delimited inputs. In addition, it includes features such as preloaded example datasets, tissue-specific filters, and customizable colour scale settings, allowing users to tailor the visualization to their analytical needs. Input validation is also implemented to ensure proper formatting and to prevent the submission of empty fields or invalid gene IDs. Upon submission, PepperEXP retrieves expression values (in FPKM) from pre-loaded expression matrices, applies any selected tissue filters, and generates interactive heatmaps using the plotly.js library. Expression levels are visualized using a color gradient, with interactive elements such as hover tooltips, zoom, and pan functionalities to facilitate the exploration of detailed expression values and patterns. Additionally, a metadata table provides experimental details, including the sequencing platform, study accession numbers, and sample descriptions. The table supports pagination and a search bar to facilitate easy navigation and retrieval of relevant sample information.


#### PepperClust
  A web-based tool designed for interactive exploration of gene expression patterns through cluster analysis. Its user interface is organized into three main sections: the gene input panel, visualization controls, and a results display area. Users can either input genes or load a predefined example dataset containing seven black pepper genes. The visualization settings allow colour scheme customization and choose between two display modes: a combined heatmap with hierarchical clustering (dendrogram), or a simplified dendrogram-only view for easier cluster interpretation. In the default mode, genes are arranged as rows and samples as columns, with expression levels represented by a color gradient. An integrated dendrogram illustrates the hierarchical clustering relationships among genes based on their expression profiles. Alternatively, the dendrogram-only mode excludes the heatmap to provide a clearer view of the clustering structure. Interactive features such as zooming, panning, and hover tooltips enhance usability, allowing users to examine detailed expression data. A supplementary metadata table below the visualizations provides additional experimental details, including the sequencing platform, study accession numbers, and experimental descriptions.


#### TF family exploration module
  This module provides comprehensive access to TF families identified in the black pepper genome. The user interface supports two primary modes of gene family browsing; users can either view the 62 TF families simultaneously or explore specific TF families using an alphabetical filter. Upon selecting a TF family, the interface displays a detailed table listing all associated member genes. For each gene, the table includes essential information such as gene ID, chromosomal location, and genomic coordinates. A built-in search function enables chromosome-based filtering to streamline navigation. Additionally, clicking on a gene ID within the table opens a corresponding gene structure visualization.


#### SNP marker exploration module
  The SNP marker database developed in this study serves as an integrated platform for exploring genetic variants in black pepper. It provides researchers with access to SNP data generated from recent genomic studies, facilitating applications in genetic diversity analysis, marker-assisted selection, and genetic mapping. The interface guides users through a structured three-step workflow. The first step is the study selection interface, which displays the available SNP datasets along with their publication details. By clicking the “View SNPs” button, users are directed to the search interface of the selected dataset. The search interface provides a suite of tools for targeted SNP exploration. Users can refine their search by selecting a specific chromosome and defining a genomic interval using start and end coordinates. The system enhances usability by automatically suggesting valid coordinate ranges based on the selected chromosome, minimizing input errors. Additionally, a genome-wide SNP density heatmap is also provided, offering a visual summary of SNP distribution across all chromosomes, enabling quick identification of polymorphism-rich regions. Search results are presented in two complementary formats to facilitate data interpretation: (i) a tabular view listing details for each SNP within the defined region, including the chromosome number, genomic position, reference allele, and alternative allele; (ii) an interactive genomic visualization above the table, illustrating the spatial distribution of SNPs across the selected region. Users can hover over data points to view additional metadata or click to refine search parameters.

  In addition to the core tools and databases, the platform also includes a dedicated gallery for visual exploration of black pepper images and a user-friendly contact page to facilitate communication with platform administrators.


## Discussion

### Transcription factor repertoire of black pepper
  We conducted a comprehensive computational analysis of the black pepper genome to identify TFs based on HMM profiles of DBD families. While a previous study by Negi and coworkers in 2021 [21] reported 2,110 TFs across 58 families, our analysis identified 2,503 putative TFs classified into 62 distinct families. The TF families LIM, PLATZ, TAZ, and TUB represent novel additions to the known TF repertoire in black pepper. Additionally, we identified three more SBP genes than the 34 reported by Li et al. [26], and 43 additional MYB genes compared to the recent analysis by Fan et al. [25]. However, the number of TFs identified in the present study was considerably lower than that reported by Hu et al. [7], who reported 5,082 TFs from 75 gene families. This discrepancy can be primarily attributed to methodological differences in TF annotation. In our analysis, TFs were identified using TF family assignment rules employed in PlantTFDB, applying a strict definition limited to sequence-specific DNA-binding proteins that regulate transcription. Transcriptional regulators (TRs) and chromatin modifiers (CRs) were deliberately excluded. By contrast, Hu et al. [7] employed the PlantTFcat tool, which classifies proteins into 108 TF/TR/CR families based on conserved InterProScan domain patterns [41]. Since the domain identification method in PlantTFcat differs fundamentally from the family assignment rules used in PlantTFDB, the results are not directly comparable [43]. It was also noted that PlantTFcat does not encompass several TF families included in our study (e.g., DBB, FAR1, LSD, NF-X1, STAT), which further contributes to discrepancies between the two datasets. While the differences with previous studies are noted, the TF catalog reported here represents a comprehensive and valuable genomic resource for the black pepper research community, establishing a foundation for future investigations into the transcriptional regulatory networks governing development, secondary metabolism, and stress responses. Nevertheless, we recognize that researchers may opt to generate TF datasets tailored to their own genome assemblies and analysis pipelines, as demonstrated in the present study.

  In addition to TF annotation, our study also exhibited a broader GO coverage compared to previous black pepper genome analyses. While Hu et al. [7] reported 21,184 proteins associated with 2,071 GO terms, we identified 35,670 proteins with GO assignments. This increase can be attributed to differences in annotation methodology. Specifically, Hu et al. [7] relied on BLASTP searches against the UniProtKB Viridiplantae database and the NCBI nr protein database using a stringent E-value cutoff (1e-10), followed by functional enrichment with InterProScan. By contrast, our pipeline employed BLASTP searches against the NCBI nr protein database with A. thaliana as the reference, and GO terms were assigned through mapping to the GOA database with default parameters (E-value ≤ 1e-6, hit filter of 500, GO weight of 5, annotation score cutoff of 55). The inclusion of a larger number of high-confidence hits, combined with hierarchical GO mapping, allowed us to annotate a greater proportion of the black pepper proteome.

  Moreover, GO enrichment analysis of PnTFs provided important preliminary insights into the diverse functional roles of TFs in black pepper and suggested potential regulatory mechanisms influencing development and reproduction. For example, B3 TFs were enriched in GO terms associated with organ and reproductive structure development, while Dof TFs were predominantly linked to post-embryonic development and shoot system formation. Similarly, HD-ZIP TFs were associated with structural morphogenesis, including processes such as trichome branching, and C2H2 TFs were implicated in pollen sperm cell differentiation. However, to validate these predicted functions and elucidate the regulatory networks governed by these TFs in black pepper, further experimental studies are essential. In addition, focusing future research on unannotated PnTFs, by combining high-throughput genomic approaches with targeted functional analyses, will significantly advance our understanding of these key regulatory genes and their roles in plant growth, development, and adaptation.

  Significant progress has been made over the past decade in understanding the role of plant TFs as key regulators of biotic and abiotic stress responses in various crops [65–71]. A recent study by Bai et al. [54] reported that genes lacking introns or containing few introns, particularly in families such as AP2, EF-hand_7, bZIP, FAD_binding_4, STE_STE11, CAMK_CAMKL-CHK1, and C2, are likely to play significant roles in drought and salt stress responses in Arabidopsis and rice. Interestingly, many TF families identified in black pepper also contain intronless genes, suggesting that some PnTFs may be rapidly inducible under stress conditions.

  The global understanding of the PPI landscape of TFs and their roles in transcriptional regulation remains limited in plants [71]. To address this gap, we investigated the PPI networks of TF families in black pepper to identify hub TFs that may serve as central coordinators of transcriptional networks. Notably, several hub TFs were identified across key families, including bZIP, GRAS, MYB, MYB-related, NAC, TALE, and WRKY, suggesting their potential involvement in a wide range of biological processes.


### BlackPepKB: a comprehensive resource for black pepper genomics
  To enhance data accessibility and support hypothesis-driven research in black pepper, we developed BlackPepKB, an integrated genomic knowledge base. The platform enables users to explore TF families, GO-based functional annotations, gene expression profiles, and SNP data via an intuitive web interface. In addition, BlackPepKB integrates interactive tools to support functional genomics analyses, including the Pepper-eFP Browser for tissue-specific expression visualization, JBrowse2 for genome navigation, and BLAST for sequence similarity searches. Additional tools such as GO-Pep, GeneViz, PepperExp, and PepperClust allow users to retrieve GO annotations, visualize exon–intron gene structures, analyze expression heatmaps, and perform hierarchical clustering of gene expression profiles. By centralizing diverse datasets and visualization tools, BlackPepKB serves as a comprehensive resource to accelerate functional genomics research and molecular breeding in black pepper. Future expansions will incorporate additional omics data and comparative genomics tools to further enhance its utility.


### Conclusion and future direction
  This study presents the first comprehensive genome-wide identification and classification of TFs in black pepper, together with functional annotation of its proteome and the development of BlackPepKB, an integrated genomic knowledge base. BlackPepKB fills a critical gap in black pepper research by providing a centralized platform for exploring genomic, transcriptomic, and functional data. To ensure its continued relevance and utility, we are committed to the long-term maintenance and regular updating of the database. As sequencing technologies evolve and new high-throughput datasets emerge, future updates will incorporate additional genomic assemblies, expression profiles, and functional annotations. User feedback will be actively encouraged to guide future developments and enhance functionality. Collectively, this resource is expected to facilitate advanced studies in functional genomics, supporting the broader goal of improving productivity and stress resilience in black pepper.
