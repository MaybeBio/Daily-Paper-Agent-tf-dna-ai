## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific content, methodology, data interpretation, and claims as presented in the provided manuscript
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors report that sugar beet class 1 phytoglobin BvPgb 1.2 binds GAPDH and DNA in vitro with high affinity, as measured by isothermal spectral shift assays, and that a C86A mutation modulates these affinities. Docking studies are used to support the plausibility of these interactions.
- **Visible evidence base** Abstract, Introduction, Results (Sections 2.1–2.4), Discussion, Materials and Methods (Sections 4.1–4.5), Conclusions, Acknowledgments
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Figures 1–12 and Tables 1–3 are referenced but not provided. Raw SpS data, protein purification validation data, and docking output files are not available for independent verification.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** The manuscript presents a potentially interesting set of observations regarding the in vitro binding of a plant phytoglobin to GAPDH and DNA. However, the evidence as presented is incomplete and several claims exceed what can be supported from the described experiments. The absence of the actual figures and tables, which are central to the results, severely limits the ability to evaluate the technical quality of the measurements and the validity of the reported dissociation constants. The docking analyses are described in reasonable detail but their interpretation as evidence for biologically relevant interactions is overstated. The manuscript would benefit from a more cautious framing of the results and a clearer distinction between in vitro observations and speculative in vivo implications.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers studying plant globins, particularly phytoglobin function under stress conditions, may find the reported interactions of interest. The potential link between GAPDH and phytoglobin could also attract those working on moonlighting proteins and metabolic stress responses in plants. The DNA-binding observations may be of interest to those studying globin evolution and nucleic acid interactions, though the physiological relevance remains unclear.
- **Major strengths** The study addresses a relatively underexplored area, namely the interaction of plant phytoglobins with non-gas ligands such as proteins and DNA. The use of a relatively modern biophysical technique (isothermal spectral shift) is appropriate for measuring binding affinities. The inclusion of a cysteine-to-alanine mutant provides a useful comparative element. The docking analyses are described with sufficient methodological detail to be reproducible.
- **Major Concerns** The most significant concern is that the core experimental data, namely the figures and tables, are not available in the provided material. This makes it impossible to assess the quality of the binding curves, the appropriateness of the fits, or the statistical significance of the differences between the reported KD values. The claim of "strong" or "remarkably strong" affinity is not well contextualized against known protein-DNA or protein-protein interaction affinities. The use of human GAPDH rather than a plant GAPC in the binding experiments is a notable limitation that is only partially addressed. The physiological relevance of the observed interactions is largely speculative, and the discussion extends considerably beyond what the data can support.
- **Minor Comments** The manuscript would benefit from a more careful distinction between the "open" and "closed" conformations of the phytoglobin and how these relate to the biphasic binding curves. The statement regarding the percentage of the protein surface buried in the complex is difficult to interpret without the structural context. The discussion of heme trafficking and plastid localization is interesting but highly speculative. The Materials and Methods section is generally adequate but lacks some details on the SpS data analysis, such as the number of replicates and the fitting model used.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** The lack of access to the primary data (figures and tables) is a critical failing in the context of this review. The use of a heterologous GAPDH (human) for binding studies without a corresponding plant GAPC control weakens the claim of biological relevance. The interpretation of the docking results as evidence for a "biologically relevant complex" is not fully justified given the limitations of docking methodologies.
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** Originality: The work is moderately original, exploring interactions not previously reported for this class of protein. Scientific importance: The potential implications for plant stress responses are interesting, but the current evidence is largely in vitro and speculative, limiting the immediate importance. Interdisciplinary readership: The topic may appeal to plant biologists, biochemists, and possibly structural biologists, but the narrow focus and lack of in vivo validation may limit broader interest. Technical soundness: The technical approach is sound in principle, but the inability to review the primary data prevents a full assessment. Readability for nonspecialists: The manuscript is generally well-written and accessible, though some sections, particularly the docking analysis, may be dense for a general audience.
- **Recommendation posture** Currently not established from the provided evidence. The manuscript presents interesting preliminary observations, but the core data are not available for review, and the claims of biological relevance are not sufficiently supported. The authors should be encouraged to resubmit with the full data and a more measured interpretation.

### Major Concerns

- **Concern ID** R1-M1
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** Yes
- **Axis** Evidence integrity
- **Claim pointer** The manuscript reports specific dissociation constants (KD) for BvPgb 1.2 binding to GAPDH and DNA, with values such as 260 ± 50 nM for the rWT-GAPDH interaction and 100 ± 10 pM for the closed conformation DNA binding.
- **Evidence pointer** Section 2.1, Figures 1 and 2, Table 1 (location not provided)
- **Concern** The primary experimental data supporting the central claims of this manuscript, namely the SpS binding curves and the derived KD values, are presented in figures and tables that were not included in the material provided for review. Without access to these data, it is impossible to evaluate the quality of the measurements, the appropriateness of the curve fitting, the reproducibility of the results, or the statistical significance of the differences between the reported KD values for the rWT and C86A variants. The reported error margins are also difficult to assess without seeing the underlying data and the number of replicates.
- **Why it matters** The entire premise of the manuscript rests on the accuracy and reliability of these binding measurements. If the data are not available for scrutiny, the core findings cannot be verified, and the conclusions drawn from them are unsupported.
- **Resolution test** The authors must provide the full set of binding curves, the fitted parameters, the number of experimental replicates, and the statistical analysis for all reported KD values. The figures and tables must be made available for a complete assessment.

- **Concern ID** R1-M2
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** Yes
- **Axis** Experimental design
- **Claim pointer** The manuscript claims that the binding affinity between BvPgb 1.2 and GAPDH is biologically relevant, and that the high structural similarity between the putative B. vulgaris GAPC and human GAPDH justifies the use of the human protein in the experiments.
- **Evidence pointer** Sections 2.1, 2.4, 3, and 4.2 (location not provided)
- **Concern** The binding experiments were performed using human GAPDH, not a plant GAPC. While the authors argue that the structural similarity between the two proteins is high, this does not guarantee identical binding behavior with BvPgb 1.2. The use of a heterologous protein introduces a significant uncertainty regarding the physiological relevance of the measured interaction. The authors should have included a plant GAPC in their binding assays as a direct comparison, or at least acknowledged this as a major limitation and refrained from making strong claims about in planta relevance.
- **Why it matters** The claim that BvPgb 1.2 interacts with GAPDH in a biologically meaningful way is central to the manuscript's significance. If the interaction is only observed with a non-plant protein, the relevance to plant biology is questionable.
- **Resolution test** The authors should either repeat the binding experiments with a plant GAPC (e.g., from B. vulgaris or A. thaliana) or substantially temper their claims about the biological relevance of the interaction, clearly stating that the observed binding is with a heterologous protein and that its in planta significance is unknown.

- **Concern ID** R1-M3
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** No
- **Axis** Interpretation
- **Claim pointer** The manuscript states that the docking studies "reinforced" the binding findings and revealed "electrostatically driven interactions" and a "substantial buried surface area indicative of a stable, biologically relevant complex."
- **Evidence pointer** Sections 2.2, 2.3, 3, Figures 3–10, Tables 2 and 3 (location not provided)
- **Concern** The docking results are presented as supporting evidence for the biological relevance of the interactions. However, docking studies are predictive tools and do not provide direct evidence of binding in a biological context. The interpretation of a "biologically relevant complex" based on a buried surface area and a favorable HADDOCK score is an overstatement. Docking scores and buried surface areas are not definitive indicators of biological relevance, and the authors have not validated these predictions with any functional assays or mutagenesis studies beyond the C86A variant.
- **Why it matters** Overinterpreting computational predictions as evidence for biological relevance can mislead readers and overstate the significance of the findings. The docking data should be presented as hypothesis-generating, not as confirmation of biological function.
- **Resolution test** The authors should rephrase their interpretation of the docking results, clearly stating that they provide a plausible structural model that requires experimental validation. They should avoid using terms like "biologically relevant" based solely on computational predictions.

- **Concern ID** R1-M4
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** No
- **Axis** Contextualization
- **Claim pointer** The manuscript describes the measured KD values as "strong" and "remarkably strong" affinities for the interactions with GAPDH and DNA.
- **Evidence pointer** Sections 2.1 and 3, Table 1 (location not provided)
- **Concern** The terms "strong" and "remarkably strong" are used without proper context. The manuscript does not compare the measured KD values to those of other known protein-protein or protein-DNA interactions to justify these descriptors. A KD of 260 nM for a protein-protein interaction is not universally considered "strong," and a KD of 100 pM for a protein-DNA interaction, while high affinity, needs to be discussed in the context of other DNA-binding proteins. Without this context, the claims of "strong" affinity are subjective and potentially misleading.
- **Why it matters** The perceived strength of the interaction is a key part of the manuscript's narrative. If the affinities are not exceptional compared to other known interactions, the significance of the findings is diminished.
- **Resolution test** The authors should provide a comparative discussion of their measured KD values against those of other relevant protein-protein and protein-DNA interactions to support their characterization of the affinities as "strong."

### Minor Comments

- **Concern ID** R1-m1
- **Severity** Minor
- **Axis** Clarity
- **Affected element** Section 2.1, description of biphasic binding
- **Evidence pointer** Section 2.1, Figure 2 (location not provided)
- **Issue** The description of the biphasic binding behavior is somewhat brief. It is not entirely clear how the two distinct KD values are extracted from the binding curves and how the "open" and "closed" conformations are assigned to these two phases.
- **Required correction** The authors should provide a more detailed explanation of the biphasic binding model, including how the two KD values are derived and how the conformational states are assigned.

- **Concern ID** R1-m2
- **Severity** Minor
- **Axis** Completeness
- **Affected element** Section 4.2, SpS assay details
- **Evidence pointer** Section 4.2 (location not provided)
- **Issue** The Materials and Methods section for the SpS assay is brief. It does not specify the number of replicates performed for each binding measurement, the exact fitting model used, or how the error margins for the KD values were calculated beyond a general reference.
- **Required correction** The authors should include more detail on the experimental replicates, the data fitting procedure, and the statistical methods used to determine the reported uncertainties.

- **Concern ID** R1-m3
- **Severity** Minor
- **Axis** Interpretation
- **Affected element** Section 3, Discussion of C86A mutation effects
- **Evidence pointer** Section 3 (location not provided)
- **Issue** The discussion of the C86A mutation's differential effect on DNA binding (increasing affinity in the open form, decreasing in the closed form) is interesting but speculative. The authors do not provide a structural or mechanistic explanation for this differential effect.
- **Required correction** The authors should either provide a more detailed structural rationale for the observed differential effects or explicitly state that the mechanism is unknown and requires further investigation.

- **Concern ID** R1-m4
- **Severity** Minor
- **Axis** Clarity
- **Affected element** Section 2.4, Structural similarity analysis
- **Evidence pointer** Section 2.4, Figure 11 (location not provided)
- **Issue** The section on structural similarity between the predicted B. vulgaris GAPC and human GAPDH is presented as a justification for using the human protein. However, the functional implications of this similarity are not discussed in detail.
- **Required correction** The authors should briefly discuss whether the high structural similarity is likely to translate to similar binding interfaces and affinities, or if there are known functional differences between the plant and human enzymes that could affect the interaction with BvPgb 1.2.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that the BvPgb 1.2-GAPDH interaction is "biologically relevant" is unsupported by the provided evidence, as the experiments were performed with a heterologous human protein and no in vivo or functional validation is presented.
- The claim of "remarkably strong" DNA-binding affinity is not contextualized against other known protein-DNA interactions and is therefore difficult to evaluate.
- The interpretation of docking results as evidence for a "stable, biologically relevant complex" is an overstatement of what computational predictions can demonstrate.
- The physiological implications discussed in the manuscript, including roles in stress response, heme trafficking, and transcriptional regulation, are speculative and not directly supported by the experimental data presented.
- The statement that the closed protein species can account for 70% of the total population in class 1 rice phytoglobins is presented as a general characteristic, but its relevance to the sugar beet protein studied here is not established.
