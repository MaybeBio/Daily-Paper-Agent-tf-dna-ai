# Integrated bulk and single-cell transcriptomics identify RELB, S100A9, and SOCS1 as key autophagy-endoplasmic reticulum stress genes linking T2DM with MAFLD


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21641-y.


## Introduction
  The coexistence of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) results in a disorder characterized by hepatic fat accumulation closely linked to metabolic dysfunctions, including insulin resistance, obesity, and hyperlipidemia. This condition poses a significant global health burden, currently affecting approximately 463 million adults worldwide, with projections suggesting this number could rise to 700 million by 20451. The global prevalence of MAFLD in adults is particularly striking, ranging between 6.3% and 45%, with variations depending on the study and region. Formerly known as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), this condition is a form of metabolic stress-induced liver injury strongly associated with insulin resistance and genetic predisposition. NAFLD is the most prevalent chronic liver disease, affecting about 25% of the global population2. NAFLD, particularly its histological phenotype non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), can progress to advanced liver diseases, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)3. Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is linked to an increased risk of cardiovascular events, chronic kidney disease, intra- and extrahepatic malignancies, as well as liver failure and HCC4. Extensive epidemiological evidence indicates that MASLD is a multisystem disorder, not only associated with hepatic complications but also closely tied to cardiometabolic comorbidities and an elevated risk of extrahepatic cancers5.

  Furthermore, growing evidence suggests that autophagy is a key regulator of the two major pathological features of T2DM—impaired insulin secretion and reduced insulin sensitivity. Endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress also plays an active role in autophagy-related regulation in T2DM. Although the molecular mechanisms remain unclear, current research hypothesizes that autophagy, inflammation, and ER stress play crucial roles in T2DM and are closely interconnected6. In patients with T2DM, insufficient insulin secretion disrupts lipid metabolism, leading to hepatic steatosis. Fatty liver disease causes hepatocyte damage and inflammation, reduces hepatic insulin sensitivity, and exacerbates insulin resistance—the fundamental driver of diabetes progression. This vicious cycle not only worsens the patient’s condition but may also trigger multi-organ dysfunction, imposing substantial economic and psychological burdens on patients, families, and society. For T2DM patients with coexisting metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease, urgent intervention is critical. Current treatment strategies for MAFLD include lifestyle modifications, pharmacological therapies, and surgical interventions. However, therapeutic outcomes are often suboptimal, and many patients progress to advanced liver disease7. Therefore, identifying preventive and diagnostic biomarkers will establish a new theoretical foundation for early diagnosis and intervention.

  Currently, the interrelationship between autophagy, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and MAFLD has garnered significant attention. Research by Xuanchun Wang’s team at Fudan University suggests that EMC10 regulates hepatic endoplasmic reticulum stress and steatosis in an isoform-specific manner. Notably, mEMC10 directly inhibits hepatic endoplasmic reticulum stress, positioning EMC10 as a potential therapeutic target for MAFLD8. Concurrently, it has been demonstrated7 that ameliorating endoplasmic reticulum stress in pancreatic islet β-cells leads to complete remission of NAFLD in mice, offering a novel perspective for its treatment. Defective crosstalk between endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress resolution and autophagy sustains a pathological loop in T2DM-associated MAFLD. Mechanistically, obesity impairs the ER stress-responsive XBP1-TFEB transcriptional axis—a key hub coupling proteostasis with autophagic flux—leading to disrupted autophagy and exacerbated insulin resistance. Concurrently, tissue-specific autophagy defects (e.g., in hepatic immune cells and adipocytes) further amplify inflammation through NF-κB-dependent pathways, driving hepatic steatosis and systemic metabolic dysfunction9–11. A close and intricate relationship exists between autophagy, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and MAFLD. Autophagy, regarded as a cellular self-protection mechanism, plays a crucial role in maintaining homeostasis within pancreatic β-cells and hepatocytes, improving insulin resistance and mitigating hepatic steatosis. Conversely, endoplasmic reticulum stress may exacerbate MAFLD by fostering insulin resistance and hepatic steatosis, among other mechanisms. Additionally, a reciprocal regulatory relationship exists between autophagy and endoplasmic reticulum stress, collectively influencing cell fate and disease progression.

  The roles of autophagy and endoplasmic reticulum stress-related genes in the context of T2DM with MAFLD remain underexplored. This study aims to investigate the interplay between autophagy-related genes and endoplasmic reticulum stress genes in T2DM with MAFLD. We utilized T2DM with MAFLD-related datasets from the GEO for differential expression analysis to identify DEGs. We then employed PPI networks, machine learning techniques, WGCNA, ROC analysis, and enrichment analysis to identify key biological markers and their associated pathways, thereby elucidating their underlying biological mechanisms. In a mouse model of T2DM with MAFLD, we verified the expression levels of predicted genes, identifying CDKN1A, RELB, S100A9, and SOCS1 as potentially significant candidate molecules. Through single-cell analysis, we elucidated the potential molecular mechanisms of these four genes and explored their interrelationships within relevant cell populations, as well as potential differentiation and developmental trajectories. Specific process: (Supplementary Fig. 1). This study enhances our understanding of the pathogenesis underlying T2DM with MAFLD while identifying diagnostic factors associated with autophagy and endoplasmic reticulum stress. These factors are crucial for improving early identification, precise diagnosis, personalized treatment, and effective disease monitoring and management. The identification and application of these diagnostic factors may yield new opportunities and challenges in the prevention and treatment of T2DM with MAFLD.


## Materials and methods

### Differentially expressed genes (DEGs)
  Three datasets: GSE15653, GSE89632, and GSE24807 were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/U) using the GEOquery package (version 2.70.0)12 (see Table 1 for details). In the GSE89632 dataset, MAFLD samples were stratified and labeled as GSE89632_SS and GSE89632_NASH. The datasets GSE15653, GSE89632_SS, and GSE89632_NASH were normalized using the normalize between arrays function from the limma package13. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on the normalized datasets. The three datasets were analyzed using the limma package, yielding DEGs based on the criteria: |log2FC| > 0.5 and P < 0.0514–20. Box plots, PCA plots, volcano plots, and heat maps were generated using the “ggplot2” and “ComplexHeatmap” (version 2.13.1) packages21.

### Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network
  The intersection of the three DEG sets was visualized using a Venn diagram to identify co-expressed differentially expressed genes (co-DEGs). To further explore the potential interactions among these genes, we utilized the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) for analysis22. The interaction node data from STRING were then imported into Cytoscape for protein interaction network analysis23. Concurrently, the MCC, EPC, MNC, and DMNC algorithms in the CytoHubba plug-in were employed to identify the top 20 genes occupying key positions in the PPI network24 and to construct the hub gene network. The results of the four algorithms were then intersected to filter the most significant hub genes in the PPI network, and the intersection was visualized using a Venn diagram. The chromosomal localization of the hub genes was analyzed using the “circlize” package. Finally, Spearman’s correlation was employed to assess the relationships between these genes, with a correlation heatmap visualized using the “corrplot” package.


### Machine learning
  The GSE15653 dataset was independently screened using LASSO, random forest (RF), and support vector machine (SVM) methods. For diagnostic LASSO coefficient screening, the cleaned data were analyzed using the glmnet package (version 4.1.7) to obtain variable lambda values, likelihood values, L1 regularization values, and classification error rates, which were subsequently visualized25. The random forest algorithm, a supervised machine learning technique, ranks the importance of features in the model (Decision Tree: 100, Seed No.: 2024), allowing for the identification of genes associated with T2DM and MAFLD based on reductions in the mean Gini index26,27. Additionally, we employed a recursive feature elimination (RFE) strategy. RFE involves the iterative elimination of features that do not significantly alter accuracy within a trained model28. In this study, an SVM-based learner from the caret package [6.0–94] was combined with the RFE strategy (http://topepo.github.io/caret/data-splitting.html), implemented through three iterations of 5-fold cross-validation.


### Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)
  Weighted gene co-expression network analysis was conducted on the raw gene expression data using the WGCNA package29. Initially, we filtered out the top 25% of genes based on expression variance and identified potential outlier samples through clustering analysis. The pickSoftThreshold function was subsequently employed to identify the optimal soft threshold. Following threshold determination, the cutreeDynamic function was utilized to identify dynamic modules with a preset minimum gene requirement for each module30. Initial modules were identified using a dynamic tree cutting method, and similar modules were merged based on the clustering relationships of their module eigengenes (ME). The TOMsimilarity function was then employed to compute the topological overlap matrix between genes. Finally, we calculated Pearson correlation coefficients between module genes and trait data, assessing significance using the corPvalueStudent function to identify genes significantly associated with MAFLD.


### Acquisition of genes associated with autophagy and endoplasmic reticulum stress phenotypes
  To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying T2DM with MAFLD, we systematically extracted data from the following biological databases using strictly defined criteria: GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/), Protein-coding genes were retrieved using the keyword “Autophagy”, “Endoplasmic Reticulum Stress” with all associated genes retained without application of relevance score thresholds. PubMedGene database31 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gene/), Genes were identified through the structured query: “Autophagy“[All Fields] AND “Homo sapiens“[porgn] AND “genetype protein coding“[Properties], “Endoplasmic Reticulum Stress“[All Fields] AND “Homo sapiens“[porgn] AND “genetype protein coding“[Properties]ensuring exclusive selection of human protein-coding entries. GSEA Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB; https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/), All genes within autophagy-related gene sets, Endoplasmic Reticulum Stress-related gene sets were included in full without curation. Specialized Autophagy Databases, All genes cataloged in dedicated autophagy resources (e.g., http://www.autophagy.lu/; http://hamdb.scbdd.com/home/index/) were incorporated. We then performed an intersection of genes identified by the three machine learning algorithms, module genes significantly associated with MAFLD from the WGCNA, and both autophagy- and endoplasmic reticulum stress-related genes. This yielded diagnostic biomarkers pertinent to T2DM with MAFLD, visualized using UpSet plots.


### Analysis of expression differences
  To explore the expression differences of the diagnostic biomarkers between the disease and control groups, the data were first subjected to normality and variance chi-square tests. For data approximating normal distribution (P > 0.05), we utilized the t-test for group comparisons. Subsequently, further variance tests for the observed variables were conducted. If variances were equal (P > 0.05), an independent samples t-test was employed to quantify gene expression differences. Conversely, when variances were statistically unequal (P < 0.05), we opted for the more robust Welch’s t-test to ensure result reliability. Finally, we employed the “ggplot2” software package to generate combined dot and violin plots.


### ROC analysis
  ROC curve analyses were performed on the training set (GSE15653) and external validation set (GSE24807) using the pROC software package32. This determined the sensitivity and specificity of the genes, predicted ROC-related data at their respective cut-off values, and assessed diagnostic accuracy, quantified as the area under the ROC curve (AUC). Genes with AUC > 0.7 were identified as diagnostic in the training set, while those with AUC > 0.6 were noted as diagnostic in the validation set. Both results were visualized using ggplot2.


### Enrichment analysis
  We utilized the DAVID online database (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) for GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of the biomarkers33–35. We employed the “org.Hs.eg.db” package to convert the input gene list into IDs and the “clusterProfiler” package for enrichment analysis36. Enrichment significance was assessed by calculating the z-score for each entry using the “GOplot” package37. Results were deemed significant when P < 0.05 and FDR < 0.238. GSEA was performed, with results screened by the following criteria: normalized enrichment score |NES| > 1, FDR < 0.25, p.adjust < 0.05. Subsequently, a network-based analysis was performed using the EnrichmentMap plugin (v3.5.0)39 in Cytoscape to visually depict functional crosstalk and topological centrality among key enriched pathways identified by GSEA.


### Evaluation method of immune cell infiltration
  To investigate the infiltration of immune cells in the liver tissue of T2DM with MAFLD, we employed the Cell-type Identification By Estimating Relative Subsets of RNA Transcripts (CIBERSORT) method. Immune cell deconvolution was performed using CIBERSORT (v1.06) with the LM22 signature matrix. Although initially developed for blood-derived transcriptomes40, its application to solid tissues including liver has been established in multiple prior studies18,41,42, confirming effectiveness in estimating immune cell compositions from bulk liver tissue RNA-seq data. Enrichment scores for each immune cell type in each sample were calculated using the signature matrix of 22 immune cells available on the CIBERSORTx website (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/). We utilized the dist function to calculate inter-sample distances and the hclust function to construct a clustering model. Statistical analyses determined the percentage of each immune cell subpopulation in each sample. Correlations between DE-FRGs and immune cells were analyzed using Spearman statistics. The analysis results were visualized as violin plots, lollipop plots, correlation network heatmaps, and scatter plots using the ggplot2 package.


### Single-cell data preprocessing and clustering annotation
  High-throughput sequencing expression profiles (GSE136103) were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/)43. We screened fifteen liver tissue samples, including those from 4 MAFLD patients and 11 healthy individuals. Analyses were conducted using the Seurat software package44. We first calculated the proportions of mitochondrial, ribosomal, and erythrocyte genes separately using the PercentageFeatureSet function. Low-quality cells and genes were filtered based on the condition that a cell expressed more than 500 genes and a gene was expressed in over 3 cells. Cells where mitochondrial gene expression was less than 25%, ribosomal gene expression exceeded 3%, and hemoglobin gene expression was below 1% were filtered out. The data were then normalized using the NormalizeData function, and 2000 highly variable genes were identified using the FindVariableFeatures function45. The data were scaled using the ScaleData function. PCA was performed on the normalized data using high-variance genes as input features via the RunPCA function. Batch effect correction for single-cell RNA sequencing data was executed using the Harmony algorithm from the Harmony package (version 1.2.0)46, selecting the top 20 dimensions. Non-linear dimensionality reduction of batch-corrected single-cell RNA sequencing data was performed using t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE)47. Intercellular relationships were analyzed using the Neighbor Graph Construction method on batch-corrected single-cell RNA sequencing data. Clustering was conducted using the FindClusters function with a resolution set to 0.6. Known genealogical marker profiles were utilized alongside the CellMarker website (http://xteam.xbio.top/CellMarker/) from the human hepatocyte atlas for manual annotation48.


### Cell-cell communication
  To identify potential interactions of MPs with other cell populations, cell-communication analysis was performed using the ‘CellChat’ package (version 1.6.1)49. Firstly, subpopulations of MPs were extracted and the seurat V5 standard data processing procedure was performed on the MPs subpopulations. The MPs subpopulations were reclustered, the t-SNE visualization results of the MAFLD group and the healthy control group were compared, and the cell group with the largest difference in cell distribution was selected and named ‘MAFLD_MPs’, and the remaining cell groups were named ‘Other_MPs‘. A random seed (seed = 123456) was set, 2000 cells were randomly selected to merge with the MPs subpopulation, and the disease samples among them were selected to create CellChat objects. Subsequently, we used the CellChatDB database (http://www.cellchat.org/) for ‘human’ related data, and selected a subset of ‘secretory signaling’ to analyze related ligand-receptor interactions. Overexpressed ligand-receptor pairs in CellChat objects were identified using the identifyOverExpressedGenes and identifyOverExpressedInteractions functions, and these ligand-receptor pairs were mapped to protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks using the CellChat package. network. Excluding communication between cell populations involving less than ten cells, the probability of intercellular communication was calculated using the computeCommunProb function. Finally, the probability of communication for a specific signaling pathway was further refined using the computeCommunProbPathway function. Network centrality analysis was performed using the netAnalysis_computeCentrality function50.


### Pseudo-temporal analysis
  In this study, unsupervised pseudo-Temporal analysis was performed using the ‘Monocle’ software package (version 2.32.0)51. First, a cell data set (CDS) was constructed using the newCellDataSet function, which contained expression matrices, phenotypic data and feature data, and a negative binomial distribution was specified. Next, the scale factors between cells and the dispersion of gene expression were corrected by the estimateSizeFactors and estimateDispersions functions. Subsequently, the DDRTree method was used for dimensionality reduction and the plot_cell_trajectory function was used to construct the developmental trajectory of the cells52. Using ‘mean_expression > = 1 & dispersion_empirical > = 3 * dispersion_fit’ as the criterion, we filtered out the highly variable genes associated with pseudotimes, and analyzed these genes in pseudotimes using the differentialGeneTest function. to analyze the expression changes of these genes in pseudotime. Finally, the clustered heatmap of the screened genes was visualized using the plot_pseudotime_heatmap function. For gene expression patterns at specific branching points, the BEAM method was used for branching analysis53 and the corresponding branching gene heatmaps were generated using the plot_genes_branched_heatmap function.


### Preparation of T2DM combined with MAFLD rat model
  Twenty healthy clean-grade male Sprague–Dawley rats, aged 6–8 weeks, weighing 180–220 g, were provided by Wuhan Yunkron Technology Company. The animal production and use license number is DCXR(E)2018-0021. SYXK (E) 2013-0069, respectively. The experimental protocol was approved by the Animal Experiment Ethics Committee of Xinjiang Medical University (approval number: IACUC20181020-01).All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. All experimental animals were operated under anesthesia, and every effort was made to minimize their pain, suffering and death. All our experiments were conducted strictly in line with ARRIVE guidelines. After 1 week of normal feeding, high-fat and high-sugar diets and regular diets were prepared according to the formula. Ten rats were fed with high-fat and high-sugar diet, high-fat and high-sugar diet feed formula: 10% lard, 20% sucrose, 2% cholesterol, 60% ordinary feed, 8% egg yolk powder(Jiangsu Xietong Pharmaceutical Bio-engineering Co., Ltd.) and ten rats in normal control group were fed with normal diet. At the end of the 12th week, 10 rats in the model group were given intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (STZ) (30 mg/kg) overnight fasting54. Ten control rats fed a regular diet were injected with the corresponding volume of citrate buffer. After the STZ injecting, the random blood glucose levels of the 2 group rats were measured several times in the following 2 weeks (the 2nd, 4th, 6th, 8th, 10th, 12th and 14th days), and random blood glucose ≥ 16.8 mmol/Lwere considered a successful T2DM rat model55. At the 14th week, 4 rats in each group were randomly selected to complete liver ultrasound, and fatty liver results were formed to determine the success of modeling. Finally, the rats were divided into 3 groups: (1) T2DM + MAFLD group (n = 10); (2) normal control group (n = 10); After 15 weeks. In this experiment, the anesthetic drug used was isoflurane. The anesthesia process involved placing the rats in an induction chamber, activating the isoflurane vaporizer, and setting the concentration to 5% until the rats lost consciousness. Subsequently, the euthanasia was performed using a pharmacological method. The euthanasia drug was a pentobarbital solution, administered at a lethal dose of 150 mg/kg via a tail vein injection. The syringe needle was carefully inserted into the tail vein, and the pentobarbital solution was slowly injected, ensuring that there was no leakage during the administration process. Following the injection, we monitored the respiration and heartbeat of the rats to confirm the complete cessation of vital signs. After confirming death, proceed with tissue collection, rinsed with normal saline at 4 °C, weighed and placed in preservation solution and stored in a refrigerator at − 80 °C.


### Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) staining
  Morphological and pathological observation of the liver: After being fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, the liver tissue undergoes routine paraffin embedding, sectioning, and is stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE)staining.


### Oil red O staining
  Fresh frozen liver sections were fixed in 4% neutral-buffered paraformaldehyde for 15 min. After washing with distilled water, sections were dehydrated in 60% isopropanol for 1 min. Staining was performed using freshly prepared Oil Red O working solution for 10 min with gentle agitation in the dark. Sections were differentiated in 60% isopropanol with < 5-second dips until background clearance was achieved under microscopic monitoring. Counterstaining was conducted with Mayer’s hematoxylin for 2 min followed by blueing in 1% lithium carbonate solution for 1 min. Sections were mounted with aqueous mounting medium (glycerol gelatin) and imaged within 2 h using light microscopy. Finally, the image is collected under a microscope.


### Measurement of serum biochemical parameters
  Blood samples were collected from each rat and centrifuged at 4◦C and 3500 rpm/min for 15 min to isolate serum. Commercially available kits were used: High-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) test kit (Catalog No.: A112-1-1), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) test kit (Catalog No.: A113-1-1), total cholesterol (T-CHO) test kit (Catalog No.: A111-1-1), triglyceride (TG) test kit (Catalog No.: A110-1-1), aspartate aminotransferase (AST/GOT) test kit (Catalog No.: C010-2-1), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT/GPT) test kit (Catalog No.: C009-2-1). The levels of glucose (GLU), HDL, LDL, total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), AST, and ALT in the rat serum were measured according to the kit instructions.

### Immunohistochemical staining
  Liver tissue specimens were fixed for 48 h and paraffin-embedded. The paraffin-embedded tissue sections were serial sectioned at 3 μm, dewaxed and hydrated. 3% H 2O2 was incubated at room temperature for 10 min to remove endogenous peroxidase. After 10 min of citrate antigen repair, primary antibodies RELB (1:50), S100A9(1:300), SOCS1 (1:300), were added dropwise and incubated at 37℃ for 25 min, and then freshly prepared DAB color development solution was added dropwise. The film was stained with hematoxylin and sealed with neutral gum. At the same time, PBS was used as negative control instead of primary antibodies. The immunohistochemical pathology pictures were analyzed using ImageScope software as follows: three different 400x fields of view were intercepted from each section, enter the analysis module of lmagescope software, and set all the dark brown color on the tissue sections as strong positive, brownish yellow as moderate positive, light yellow as weak positive, and blue nuclei as negative. Each tissue point was then identified and analyzed to find out the area (in pixels) of strong positive, moderate positive, weak positive and negative, the percentage of positivity and finally the H-score rating.


### Statistical analyses
  Data analysis utilized SPSS 26.0 statistical software. Normally distributed measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared between two groups using independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed measurement data were presented using the median and interquartile range, and comparisons between groups were conducted using the Mann-Whitney test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

### WGCNA
  We conducted WGCNA on the GSE89632 dataset. Hierarchical clustering based on sample similarity was visualized as a dendrogram (Fig. 4A). The distance metrics indicated sample similarity, leading to the removal of outliers (GSM2385767 and GSM2385782) through a pruning operation. The selected representative subset was subsequently used to visualize clustering differences between the Normal and MAFLD groups via a heatmap (Fig. 4B). The top 25% of genes exhibiting the greatest variability were selected for WGCNA based on their standard deviation rankings. Scale-free fit indices and average connectivity were evaluated across various soft threshold powers, guided by the scale-free R² value. A soft threshold power of β = 8 and a scale-free R² of 0.8 were selected (Fig. 4C). A gene clustering tree with module identification illustrated the hierarchical clusteringresults and categorized genes into diverse modules, merging similar modules to minimize redundancy (Fig. 4D). Ultimately, eight modules were identified, Among them, the correlation and P-value for the MEbrown and MEyellow modules are: -0.89, P = 3e-22; 0.78, P = 8e-13. (Fig. 4E). Using “autophagy” and “endoplasmic reticulum stress” as search terms restricted to the species Homo, we retrieved corresponding genes from relevant websites (Sect. 2.5). A total of 8,299 autophagy-related genes and 6,443 endoplasmic reticulum stress-related genes were obtained (Supplementary File 4). Five diagnostic biomarkers were identified through the intersection of genes from the MEbrown and MEyellow modules, along with the results from three machine learning algorithms, and autophagy- and endoplasmic reticulum stress-related genes: CDKN1A, TLR2, S100A9, RELB, and SOCS1. ### Expression of four diagnostic biomarkers and screening identification
  The GSE15653 dataset served as a training set to assess the expression of the four diagnostic biomarkers. Results indicated that CDKN1A, RELB, S100A9, and SOCS1 were significantly elevated in the T2DM group relative to the Control group (Fig. 5A, B and C, and Fig. 5D). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were utilized to evaluate the diagnostic performance of CDKN1A, RELB, S100A9, and SOCS1. In the GSE15653 training set, the AUCs for CDKN1A and S100A9 exceeded 0.8, CI:0.622-1.000; 0.628-1.000 (Fig. 5E and G), while those for RELB and SOCS1 surpassed 0.9, CI:0.756-1.000; 0.756-1.000 (Fig. 5F and H), indicating high predictive accuracy. We then utilized an independent dataset, GSE24807, for external validation, revealing that the AUCs for CDKN1A, RELB, S100A9, and SOCS1 were all above 0.6, confirming their significant diagnostic value and consistency with prior predictions (Fig. 5I, J and K, and Fig. 5L). Ultimately, we identified these four genes as diagnostic biomarkers linked to autophagy and endoplasmic reticulum stress in T2DM with MAFLD. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that CDKN1A and SOCS1 were co-enriched in the JAK-STAT signaling pathway; RELB and CDKN1A were co-enriched in Human T-cell leukemia virus 1 infection; and S100A9 was primarily enriched in the IL-17 signaling pathway (Fig. 6A and B; Table 3). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) indicated that CDKN1A and SOCS1 were co-enriched in interleukin 4 and interleukin 13 signaling, signaling by interleukins, and adipogenesis (Fig. 7A and B). CDKN1A and S100A9 were co-enriched in vitamin D receptor pathway (Fig. 7D). RELB and SOCS1 were co-enriched in IL12 2pathway (Fig. 7E), while S100A9 and SOCS1 were co-enriched in toll like receptor tlr1 tlr2 cascade and toll like receptor cascades (Fig. 7F and G). RELB showed enrichment in MAPK signaling pathway, tnfrelated weak inducer of apoptosis tweak signaling pathway, and nongenomic actions of 125 dihydroxyvitamin D3 (Fig. 7H, I and J). S100A9 exhibited primary enrichment in IL1 and megakaryocytes in obesity and neutrophil degranulation (Fig. 7K and L). SOCS1 was primarily enriched in JAK-STAT signaling pathway, adipogenesis, and leptininsulin signaling overlap (Fig. 7M, C and N). Topological centrality analysis revealed that several pathways—including signaling by interleukins, JAK-STAT signaling pathway, toll like receptor tlr1 tlr2 cascade, toll like receptor cascades, and MAPK signaling pathway—occupied core positions within the pathway interaction network (Supplementary Fig. 2). This spatial configuration further substantiates their pivotal roles in critical biological functions. Critical to this investigation is the mechanistic interaction of these biomarkers with autophagy and endoplasmic reticulum stress pathways. SOCS1 is enriched in the JAK-STAT signaling pathway and regulates autophagy initiation through cytokine feedback regulation. Autophagy defects further promote SOCS1 expression56. Meanwhile, previous studies demonstrate that RELB inhibition attenuates increased proinflammatory cytokine expression, while high RELB expression is closely associated with cellular autophagy activation57. Our KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that RELB and SOCS1 are co-enriched in the IL12 2pathway, suggesting that RELB may mediate cellular autophagy through other immune-related pathways (e.g., IL12 2pathway). S100A9 silencing reduces cellular mTOR-ER stress signal transduction, thereby affecting endoplasmic reticulum stress58. In our analysis, S100A9 is enriched in the IL-17 signaling pathway, toll like receptor tlr1 tlr2 cascade, and toll like receptor cascades, indicating close associations with inflammation and liver injury59. S100A9 may play important roles in endoplasmic reticulum stress, inflammation, and liver injury. CDKN1A is enriched in multiple inflammatory pathways and lipid metabolism pathways. Previous studies report that CDKN1A is a hub gene related to ferroptosis and autophagy, playing crucial roles in the pathogenesis of ischemic cardiomyopathy and serving as a predictive therapeutic target. Consistent with our findings, core autophagy-related gene lists containing CDKN1A are also enriched in the JAK-STAT signaling pathway60. In this study, we additionally found its enrichment in two inflammation-related pathways (interleukin 4 and interleukin 13 signaling and signaling by interleukins ) and one lipid metabolism pathway (adipogenesis). This suggests that in T2DM with MAFLD, besides the previously studied JAK-STAT signaling pathway, involvement of inflammatory pathways may be important influencing factors in disease progression. Overall, these findings position these four biomarkers as crucial regulators of cellular autophagy and endoplasmic reticulum stress in T2DM-MAFLD pathogenesis. We specifically focused on these four genes for subsequent analysis. Furthermore, existing studies indicate that the pathways enriched in our analysis show significant associations with autophagy and endoplasmic reticulum stress, participating in T2DM with MAFLD progression. Overactivation of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway inhibits intracellular autophagy processes, thereby exacerbating cellular damage61,62. Conversely, inhibiting JAK-STAT signaling pathway activity has been demonstrated to regulate ER stress, mitophagy, and autophagy63. Additionally, IL4 and IL17 participate in the IL-17-STAT3-HIF-1α axis involved in autophagy deficiency64, while IL17 also accelerates endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) through caspase-12-dependent pathways and induces apoptosis65. Complex mechanistic relationships exist between autophagy and endoplasmic reticulum stress phenotypes. The interplay between autophagy and endoplasmic reticulum stress remains incompletely understood. It is generally accepted that endoplasmic reticulum stress and the UPR response activate cellular autophagy and trigger downstream series of biochemical reactions. However, some studies indicate that endoplasmic reticulum-specific autophagy (ER-phagy) leads to endoplasmic reticulum stress and unfolded protein response (UPR)66. Based on existing literature, we speculate that enriched pathways like JAK-STAT signaling pathway, interleukin 4 and interleukin 13 signaling, and IL-17 signaling pathway show significant associations with both autophagy and endoplasmic reticulum stress. However, enrichment of the adipogenesis pathway suggests adipogenesis as a critical link. Adipocyte differentiation correlates with abnormal autophagy. Diabetes and obesity can activate abnormally high autophagy states in cells67–69, and such high autophagy states promote adipocyte differentiation70. Previous research indicates that inhibiting autophagy under high autophagy conditions suppresses adipogenesis. This complex process also involves phenotypes like inflammation and oxidative stress. Existing studies reveal that mitophagy-mediated adipose inflammation can induce hepatic insulin resistance11. In T2DM patients with MAFLD, such induced mechanisms may further exacerbate T2DM insulin resistance progression. We speculate that autophagy, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and adipogenesis play important roles in T2DM with MAFLD disease progression. Specific mechanisms require further investigation.

## Discussion
  The global prevalence of MAFLD is rapidly increasing, particularly among patients with T2DM. MAFLD is characterized by hepatic fat accumulation, which may progress to NASH, which can further progress to cirrhosis and ultimately lead to primary hepatocellular carcinoma, posing significant health risks. This condition is intricately linked to metabolic syndrome components and poses substantial health risks, including cardiovascular disease and liver-related complications71,72. The bidirectional relationship between MAFLD and T2DM emphasizes the urgent need for effective diagnostic and therapeutic strategies to manage these interconnected diseases, as patients with MAFLD are at an increased risk for diabetes progression and associated complications73,74.

  In this study, through bulk-RNA bioinformatic analysis, we revealed four key biomarkers associated with type 2 diabetes (T2DM) comorbid with MAFLD: CDKN1A, RELB, S100A9, and SOCS1. Through enrichment analysis, we elucidated that the pathogenesis of T2DM comorbid with MAFLD may be related to multiple pathways, including JAK-STAT signaling, IL-17 signaling, toll like receptor cascades, and MAPK signaling pathways. We discovered that the key genes RELB, S100A9, and SOCS1 are closely associated with immune infiltration. Leveraging single-cell sequencing technology, we overcame the limitations of traditional bulk RNA-seq in resolving hepatocyte heterogeneity in MAFLD, specifically focusing on the role of macrophages in T2DM comorbid with MAFLD and their connection with the key biomarkers.

  Concurrent with these findings, we established an animal model of T2DM with MAFLD and conducted experiments to validate our results. The results of HE staining, Oil Red O Staining and Measurement of serum biochemical parameters results indicate the successful establishment of the T2DM with MAFLD disease model. Compared with the control group, the hepatocytes in the MAFLD group are filled with lipid vacuoles, pushing the nuclei to one side. Both of RT-PCR and immunohistochemistry results indicated significant differences in the expression of RELB, S100A9, and SOCS1 between liver tissues of rats in the T2DM with MAFLD group and the control group. Notably, the mRNA expression and antigen-antibody complexes of RELB, S100A9, and SOCS1 was significantly elevated in the T2DM with MAFLD group. The bioinformatic analysis of S100A9, SOCS1, and RELB aligned with findings from the animal model validation tests.

  RELB, also known as I-REL or v-rel avian reticuloendotheliosis viral oncogene homolog B, is a member of the Rel family of transcription factors. This family also comprises RelA-p65, c-Rel, NF-kB1 (p100/52), and NF-kB2 (p105/50). Proteins encoded by RELB play important physiological and pathological roles in organisms. RELB has been shown to be significantly elevated in samples of aqueous humor from diabetic retinopathy75, and it is a key element of the atypical pathway; known to prevent apoptosis in inflammation, RELB has been associated with a variety of diseases, including diabetes mellitus, uveal melanoma, hypoxic injury to fetal human lung microvascular endothelial cells, and multiple myeloma. In the real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR experiment, RELB mRNA expression levels in the T2DM with MAFLD group were significantly elevated compared to the control group. Similarly, immunohistochemistry revealed significantly higher expression of RELB immune complexes in hepatocytes of the T2DM with MAFLD group relative to the control group. These findings further suggest that RELB correlates with type 2 diabetes mellitus in conjunction with MAFLD. Furthermore, we observed that RELB was enriched in the KEGG MAPK signaling pathway within the disease model of type 2 diabetes mellitus with MAFLD, highlighting the prevalent roles of both NF-kB and RELB, members of the NF-kB/Rel family of transcription factors, in regulating critical biological processes, including stroke76. Some studies have pointed out that inflammation can continuously activate the NF-κB pathway to promote the development and progression of hepatocellular carcinoma. However, the absence of NF-κB signaling in differentiated hepatocytes can also significantly aggravate the occurrence of liver cancer77. This means that while NF-κB exerts beneficial effects, it may also have a negative impact on the survival of hepatocytes. The activation of NF-κB in KupHer cells and hepatic stellate cells has been confirmed to exacerbate the inflammatory response and fibrosis during chronic inflammatory liver injury78.Studies have shown that NF-κB not only promotes chronic inflammation caused by metabolic diseases but also exacerbates HCC resulting from NASH79–81.Some studies have revealed that NF-κB has dual functions in stromal cells and immune cells: in addition to activating KupHer cells and hematopoietic stem cells, NF-κB can also activate important immune functions, which may either inhibit or promote the occurrence and development of cancer82.

  S100A9 is a prominent pro-inflammatory protein secreted by neutrophils, exhibiting high expression levels in the visceral adipose tissue of obese patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus83. S100A9 has been shown to stimulate leukocyte recruitment and induce cytokine secretion84. Elevated serum levels of S100A9 have been reported in patients with NASH; however, its role in the progression of hepatic steatosis to NASH remains poorly understood85. Inhibition of S100A9 has also significantly reduced adipose tissue breakdown and liver injury in NASH-affected mice86.

  S100A9 is mainly released by activated granulocytes and is associated with various types of information-related pathways84. In our current study, we found that S100A9 was primarily enriched in the IL-17 signaling pathway. Recent multi-omics analysis of lung injury confirmed that S100A9 may regulate immune processes, inflammatory responses, oxidoreductase activity, and hydrolase activity, thereby influencing cellular metabolic pathways, protein digestion and absorption, neutrophil extracellular trap activity, and protein synthesis. Notably, analysis of the IL-17 signaling pathway showed that only IL-17Ra was significantly upregulated in S100A9 knockout mice compared to controls, while IL-17Rc, IL-17Rd, and IL-17 were significantly downregulated. Knockdown of S100A9 inhibits the IL-17-NFκB-caspase-3 signaling pathway, possibly by suppressing IL-17 signaling in epithelial cells87. During NAFLD progression, S100A9 further promotes hepatic inflammation and lipolysis88.Studies have found that in the process of inflammation related to liver cancer, S100A8 and S100A9 gene expression induced by reactive oxygen species (ROS) and protect cells from apoptosis, promote the tumor progression89.

  Neutrophils, as the main source of IL-17B in the tumor microenvironment, exhibit dual characteristics of promoting tumor (N2) and anti-tumor (N1) in liver cancer. Its anti-tumor effect is achieved by directly activating the immune system. Research results have shown that IL-17B inhibits the proliferation of HCC cells through an AkT-dependent but non-NF -κ B-dependent mechanism90.Thy1 + cells are cells that express the Thy1 molecule (CD90) on their surface. Some studies have pointed out that Thy1 + cell transplantation promotes the proliferation of endogenous hepatic progenitor cells through the IL17RB signaling pathway, thereby effectively enhancing the liver’s regenerative capacity91.Studies have shown that IL-17E derived from non-cancer stem cells (cellCSC) binds to IL-17RB on CSCs and mediates signal transduction through the JAK/Stat3 and NF-κB pathways to achieve the interaction between CSCs and non-Cscs. Therefore, the IL-17E/IL-17RB signaling pathway can serve as a potential target for the treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma92.

  SOCS (1–7) is a family of proteins that play crucial roles in immune cells and metabolic organs like the liver, adipose tissue, and skeletal muscle, mediating inflammatory responses. A study reported that a peptide mimicking the endogenous regulatory protein SOCS1 ameliorated renal inflammation and fibrosis in diabetic nephropathy by attenuating overactive JAK/STAT signaling, as evidenced by improvements in proteinuria and renal function. Furthermore, SOCS1 expression correlates with the course of diabetes mellitus, validating the JAK/STAT/SOCS axis’ critical role in pathogenesis93. It has been suggested that elevated SOCS1 levels may elucidate the epidemiological link between Alzheimer’s disease and type 2 diabetes mellitus. SOCS transcription is induced by cytokine receptor signaling pathways mediated by the JAK/STAT pathway; several SOCS proteins, including CIS, SOCS1, and SOCS3, have been identified within promoters94. This aligns with our bioinformatic analysis results, which indicate that SOCS1 is primarily enriched in the JAK-STAT signaling pathway. The signaling pathways enriched with identified biomarkers, such as JAK-STAT and MAPK, highlight the intricate relationships between immune responses and metabolic regulation in MAFLD and T2DM. The involvement of SOCS1 and RELB within the JAK-STAT pathway, coupled with S100A9’s association with the TLR signaling cascade, suggests these pathways may be crucial in mediating inflammation and metabolic dysregulation95.

  Understanding these pathways can illuminate the pathophysiological processes underlying MAFLD and T2DM, revealing potential intervention points for therapeutic strategies. Furthermore, our investigation of these pathways in the context of immune cell infiltration—evidenced by increased levels of M2 macrophages and altered T cell populations—underscores the immune microenvironment’s role in disease progression96.

  MAFLD is immunologically significant, highlighting significant alterations in immune cell infiltration, particularly the increase of M2 macrophages and the decrease of B cells. This suggests that MAFLD tends to shift towards an immunosuppressive microenvironment, thereby promoting the progression of liver disease. The positive correlation between M1 macrophage infiltration and RELB and SOCS1 expression further indicates that these immune cells may drive the inflammatory process in MAFLD. Future studies should explore the mechanistic basis of these immune interactions, as targeting specific immune cell populations may improve the disease prognosis in patients with type 2 diabetes and MAFLD. Understanding the interplay between immune cells and metabolic pathways could facilitate the discovery of novel biomarkers reflecting disease activity and therapeutic response2.

  Based on single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data, we identified and annotated 10 distinct cell types. Single-cell differential analysis revealed significant expression differences for RELB and S100A9 in the T-cell population, and for SOCS1 in macrophage and dendritic cell populations, between the MAFLD group and the control group. It is noteworthy that these expression trends were consistent with those observed in bulk RNA samples. Further cellular communication analyses indicated that macrophage-derived cells act as signal receivers, potentially communicating via pathways including CXC chemokine, Galectin, IL-16, macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF), and TNF signaling. We found that the result of macrophage-derived cells serving as key signal receivers is consistent with and further reinforces the central role of hepatic macrophages in NAFLD pathogenesis97,98. Significant heterogeneity within the macrophage population includes different subpopulations, particularly monocyte-derived macrophages. Critically, the entry of bone marrow-derived monocytes into the liver and their differentiation into macrophages is a key driver of hepatic inflammation and NAFLD progression, which has been emphasized in previous studies99,100.

  The macrophage-associated communication pathways identified in this study (e.g., IL-16, MIF, TNF) play important roles in inflammatory diseases. For instance, enhancement of the IL-16-CD4 signaling pathway has been reported to exacerbate inflammatory pain101, while inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α and chemokines like CXCL8 are associated with the progression of malignancies102. Furthermore, systemic migration inhibitory factor (MIF) levels in infection-induced inflammation are significantly higher than in non-infectious inflammation. Therefore, we propose that macrophages drive the progression of MAFLD by regulating cell migration, inflammation, immune responses, proliferation, apoptosis, and T-cell recruitment, thereby modulating immune function and tolerance. To investigate the dynamic changes of the macrophage lineage in MAFLD, we performed pseudotime analysis on MPs. Crucially, pathway enrichment analysis revealed the IL-17 signaling pathway as a key regulator of MPs’ developmental trajectory and branching. While the role of IL-17 in autoimmunity (via phagocyte-activated T cells ) and barrier defense is well-known, this study is the first to demonstrate its critical regulation of MPs within MAFLD. This suggests a potential novel, cell-intrinsic regulatory mechanism for IL-17 in MAFLD pathogenesis, which may be both distinct from and synergistic with its known functions.


### Limitations and future perspectives
  This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, despite the validation of candidate genes in animal models, further in vitro and in vivo studies are necessary to clarify their specific molecular mechanisms in the pathogenesis of T2DM coupled with MAFLD. Second, although the predictive accuracy of our model was validated in various external datasets, its validity still needs to be tested in larger and more diverse populations to ensure generalizability. Third, and most notably, our single-cell sequencing analysis was limited by a modest sample size (4 MAFLD patient samples and 11 controls), which may affect statistical power and potentially introduce bias into network inference and pathway enrichment results, particularly when deciphering intercellular communication networks among subpopulations.

  To mitigate this constraint, we implemented a multi-tiered methodological design:

  During data preprocessing, we first performed dual-threshold cell filtering (> 500 genes per cell and > 3 cells per gene). We then applied additional quality control based on gene expression percentages (mitochondrial genes < 25%, ribosomal genes > 3%, and hemoglobin genes < 1%). Finally, batch effects were corrected using the Harmony algorithm. In cell-cell communication analysis, we employed three optimization strategies: (1) excluding interactions involving cell populations with fewer than ten cells; (2) performing 2000-cell Bootstrap sampling verification; and (3) focusing on the ‘MAFLD_MPs’ subpopulation exhibiting the most significant distributional divergence. We also cross-referenced marker annotations with the CellMarker database and human hepatocyte atlas. These systematic measures enhance analytical robustness for limited-sample data, but due to the heterogeneity in MAFLD progression and comorbidities, results should be extrapolated to broader populations with caution.

  Finally, in the immune infiltration analysis, although our CIBERSORT investigation provides preliminary insights into the hepatic immune microenvironment, this methodology carries inherent constraints regarding tissue specificity and cellular resolution. These limitations may affect the precision of quantitative assessments for certain immune subpopulations. Particular caution should be exercised when interpreting identification and proportion estimation of low-abundance cell populations. While these technical considerations may not have a significant impact on the basic validity of our core conclusions, they highlight specific avenues for future verification.

  To directly address the limitations discussed above, our future research will follow a stepwise and pragmatic path:

  Functional Validation: We will prioritize in vitro functional experiments (e.g., knockdown of S100A9 and SOCS1 in hepatocyte cell lines) to establish their causal roles in regulating autophagy-ER stress pathways. Analytical Validation: We will re-analyze our scRNA-seq data with emerging algorithms designed for small sample sizes to test the robustness of the inferred cell-cell communication networks. Independent Cohort Validation: We will seek to leverage existing public transcriptomic datasets to further corroborate the expression and clinical relevance of our identified gene signatures. Methodological Advancement: Ultimately, prospective collaboration to access tissue samples for validation via integrated single-cell and spatial transcriptomic approaches remains a strategic priority to overcome the technical limitations of deconvolution algorithms.

  In conclusion, this study identifies and preliminarily validates potential biomarkers associated with T2DM with MAFLD, leveraging a comprehensive bioinformatics approach. The identification of key genes and pathways provides a foundation for further research into the molecular mechanisms underlying this complex disease. Our findings support the utility of integrating diverse data sources to advance our understanding of disease etiology and explore targeted therapeutic interventions. Given the rising burden of MAFLD, this study highlights potential biomarkers and the value of innovative strategies for early diagnosis and personalized treatment. Future research efforts will focus on the validation framework outlined above to ultimately improve patient outcomes.
