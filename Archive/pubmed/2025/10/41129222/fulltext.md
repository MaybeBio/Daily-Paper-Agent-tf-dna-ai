# Genetic regulation of the estrogen receptor and inherited predisposition to breast cancer


## Significance
  For many families severely affected with breast cancer, no inherited causal allele has been detected. To understand the genetics underlying breast cancer in these families, we evaluated rare variants near the estrogen receptor gene ESR1 that were coinherited with breast cancer in one or more families, and for which the variant and reference alleles differed in their effect on transcription by ESR1 promoters. Nine such variants were identified, with the breast-cancer-linked allele of each variant yielding higher promoter activity. At the site with the most striking effect, the breast-cancer-linked allele was associated with increased binding by transcription factor AP2-gamma TFAP2C. These results suggest that rare ESR1 regulatory alleles may contribute to inherited predisposition to breast cancer.


## Results

### Simulations of Co-Occurrence of Common SNPs with Breast Cancer in Families.
  From whole genome sequence (wgs) of all participating affected relatives of 136 extended families severely affected with breast and/or ovarian cancer, but with no damaging mutation or structural variant identified by BROCA gene panel (12, 13) or by whole exome sequencing, we extracted genotypes at each of the 79 highest-confidence SNPs reported by meta-analyses of GWAS of breast cancer (5, 6) (SI Appendix, Table S1) We then developed simulations to evaluate, for each of these 79 SNPs, whether co-occurrence with breast cancer in the severely affected families was more frequent than expected by chance. Specifically, for each GWAS SNP, we selected randomly from the genome at least 1,000 “control SNPs” of the same minor allele frequency as the GWAS SNP (excluding any SNP in linkage disequilibrium with one already selected), then for each GWAS SNP and control SNP, counted the number of families in which all cases of breast cancer were either heterozygous or homozygous for the risk-associated (i.e., minor or major) allele of the SNP. Because allele frequencies of most GWAS SNPs differ substantially by continental ancestry, only the 136 families of European ancestry were included in the simulations; 10 additional families of other ancestries were included in follow up studies of rare variants (SI Appendix, Table S1). For each of the 79 risk SNPs, a Z-score was calculated based on the number of families in which all breast cancer cases carried the risk allele of the GWAS SNP compared to the distribution of numbers of families in which all breast cancer cases carried the corresponding allele of each control SNP (Fig. 1A).

  After correction for multiple comparisons (14), the one significant Z-score (Z = 3.81, P = 6.9E-05, two-tailed) was for rs2046210 at chr6:151,627,231 (hg38). The GWAS risk-associated allele of rs2046210 was heterozygous or homozygous in all subjects with breast cancer in 62 of the 136 families, 3.81 SD more than the mean for >1,000 comparison SNPs of the same allele frequency genotypes in the same families (Fig. 1B and SI Appendix, Table S1). rs2046210 is located in the topologically associated domain (TAD) of ESR1, the estrogen receptor alpha locus (15).


### Candidate Regulatory Variants Near ESR1.
  To identify alleles that might influence expression of ESR1, we screened genomic sequence of the ESR1 TAD of the 146 families of the cohort for variants fulfilling three criteria: i) minor allele frequency <0.10 in all populations; ii) located in sites predicted by ENCODE (16) to have regulatory potential; and iii) cosegregating with breast cancer in one or more family. ENCODE sites of regulatory potential included predicted promoter and enhancer regions; and sites of DNase hypersensitivity, enhancer-enriched histone modification, or transcription factor binding in human mammary epithelial cells (HMECs). Eleven variants met these criteria, mapping across 480 kb of the ESR1 locus, each coinherited with breast cancer in one or a few families (Fig. 1 C and D and SI Appendix, Table S2).


### Effects of Candidate Regulatory Variants on ESR1 Promoters.
  For a variant to influence predisposition to breast cancer, it is necessary both that the genomic region harboring the variant have a regulatory effect on ESR1 transcription and that the effect differ for the variant vs. the reference allele. To test the potential of the genomic region harboring each candidate variant to regulate ESR1 promoter activity, we carried out luciferase reporter assays in estrogen-receptor-positive (ER+) MCF7 breast cancer cells. Reporter constructs of ~1 kb were created containing the reference or variant allele and an ESR1 promoter driving luciferase expression (SI Appendix, Table S3). Of the multiple ESR1 promoters, we evaluated promoter ESR1.1 (P1) at chr6:152,806,704-151,807,873 and promoter ESR1.5 (P5) at chr6:151,804,561-151,805,708 [Fig. 1C; promoter notation from the Eukaryotic Promoter Database (17)], because both were active in MCF7 cells. Transfection of each of these promoter regions into MCF7 cells indicated that compared to empty vector, promoter P1 increased luciferase reporter activity 2.1-fold (95%CI [1.60, 3.01]) and promoter P5 increased luciferase reporter activity 1.7-fold (95%CI [1.42, 2.15]) (Fig. 2 A and B). Promoters P1 and P5 were included in subsequent tests of candidate enhancer sequences, with results normalized to luciferase activity of 1.0 for each promoter in each experiment. Results of all tested combinations of candidate enhancer and promoter sequences are shown in Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Table S4.

  At the original GWAS SNP rs2046210 (R15 in Fig. 1B and SI Appendix, Table S2), three common variants (R15–R16–R17) were in almost complete linkage disequilibrium, forming three haplotypes spanning 1,216 bp. Compared to activity of the promoter alone, presence of the 1.4 kb genomic segment including these three SNPs repressed activity of the ESR1 promoters. Presence of this 1.4 kb segment decreased the activity of the P1 promoter by approximately 10% (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Table S4) and decreased the activity of the P5 promoter by more than 60% (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Table S4). However, effects were indistinguishable for all naturally occurring combinations of variant and reference alleles of the R15–R16–R17 haplotype (Fig. 2 A and B, compare dark green vs. light green bars). At each of the 11 sites, the presence of a genomic segment including the reference allele, compared to empty vector, altered activity of one or both ESR1 promoters. At seven sites (R23, R06, R24, R18, R19, R10, and R25), the segments repressed promoter activity; at four sites (R03, R21, R12, and R27) the segments enhanced promoter activity (Fig. 2 C and D).

  The critical test was comparison of effects on promoter activity of reference vs. variant alleles at each site. The reporter assays indicated that variant alleles at R03, R12, and R27 increased enhancer activity; that variant alleles at R23, R06, R21, R18, R19, and R25 decreased repressor activity; and that variant alleles at R24 and R10 had no significant effect on promoter activity (Fig. 2 C and D). Overall, therefore, relative to reference alleles, variant alleles at nine sites led to increased reporter activity from one or both ESR1 promoters. Two variants were located in promoters, so were also tested as alternate promoter sequences. At R21 in the P1 promoter, the variant allele increased P1 promoter activity (Fig. 2E). At R18 in the P5 promoter, the variant allele increased P5 promoter activity (Fig. 2F). All differences in regulatory effects were subtle, but highly reproducible, ranging from 1.1- to 1.5-fold (Fig. 2G). Details of all comparisons are found in SI Appendix, Table S4.


### Transcription Factor Binding to Variant vs. Reference Alleles.
  For site R03, which has the largest difference between reference and variant alleles in effects on promoter activity, we explored allele-specific effects on transcription factor binding using electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSAs) with nuclear extract from estrogen receptor positive (ER+) MCF7 breast cancer cells and from ER+ T47D cells, and DNA probes including the R03 reference or variant allele.

  Transcription factors for these assays were selected from the JASPAR CORE collection of experimentally defined transcription-factor binding sites (18). Of the four transcription factors in the JASPAR CORE collection with predicted binding sites including R03, those most likely to differ in binding to reference vs. variant R03 alleles were TFAP2A and TFAP2C, transcription factors AP2-alpha and AP2-gamma (SI Appendix, Table S5). TFAP2A and TFAP2C are known to regulate transcription of ESR1 and both are expressed in MCF7 and T47D cells (19, 20, 21).

  A shifted band in an EMSA indicates protein binding to the DNA sequence within the labeled probe. The shifted band will disappear from the EMSA if excess amounts of added unlabeled probe successfully compete with the labeled probe for protein binding. We found that the shifted band observed with either the R03 reference probe (Fig. 3A, lane 1) or the R03 variant probe (Fig. 3A, lane 7) was outcompeted by excess unlabeled reference probe (Fig. 3A, lanes 2 and 8) or excess unlabeled variant probe (Fig. 3A, lanes 3 and 9). Similar loss of the shifted bands in the presence of excess unlabeled probe containing an AP-2 binding site supports the binding of TFAP2A or TFAP2C at the R03 site (Fig. 3A, lanes 4 and 10). Inclusion of antibody to TFAP2C in the EMSA resulted in a further shift of most of the band, suggesting TFAP2C might be the principal TFAP2 protein binding the R03 site (Fig. 3A, lanes 6 and 12 vs. lanes 1 and 7). A small amount of the shifted band was further shifted by the TFAP2A antibody, suggesting that TFAP2A also binds at the R03 site (Fig. 3A, lanes 5 and 11 vs. lanes 1 and 7). The R03 variant allele also leads to increased promoter activity in T47D cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). EMSA results were parallel for MCF7 and T47D cells. Using T47D nuclear extract, similar amounts of the shifted bands were further shifted by the TFAP2A and TFAP2C antibodies, indicating that TFAP2A and TFAP2C also bind the R03 site in T47D cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B).

  Protein binding affinities of reference vs. variant R03 alleles can be compared by titrating the amount of unlabeled competitor in the EMSA. Unlabeled competitor sequences including either the reference or variant R03 allele effectively competed for protein binding to the R03 reference allele, at the highest concentration (Fig. 3B, lanes 4 and 7 vs. lane 1), while the R03 variant allele was a slightly more effective competitor at the lowest concentration (Fig. 3B, lane 5 vs. 2). In contrast, an unlabeled competitor containing the R03 reference allele competed much less effectively for protein binding to the R03 variant allele than a competitor containing the R03 variant allele (Fig. 3B lanes 9 to 11 vs. 12 to 14); P = 0.0015 for differences in competitive success of reference vs. variant allele (Fig. 3C). Similar results were seen with T47D nuclear extract (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). Taken together, the EMSA results suggest that the variant R03 allele has higher affinity than does the reference R03 allele for binding by TFAP2C, and that TFAP2C is the best candidate for MCF7 and T47D nuclear protein binding to the R03 region. The consensus AP-2 binding site is a close match to R03 reference sequence and an even closer match to R03 variant sequence (Fig. 3D).


## Discussion
  Our results indicate that multiple rare alleles regulating expression of estrogen receptor promoters cosegregate with breast cancer in families severely affected with the disease. These results were obtained by considering common GWAS SNPs as equivalent to linkage markers in families, identifying one GWAS SNP that co-occurred with breast cancer in families more frequently than expected by chance, then experimentally evaluating variants in regulatory regions linked to this SNP for allele-specific effects. This logic and the resulting observations have several implications for the relationship of the estrogen receptor gene to inherited predisposition to breast cancer, and more generally for identification of noncoding regulatory variation in inherited predisposition to cancer.

  Statistical associations of breast cancer with common noncoding variants near ESR1 have been identified in multiple GWAS (5, 6, 8, 9, 22–24), frequently accompanied by functional analyses (8, 9, 22, 25). Assays used to test for allele-specific effects on ESR1 transcription or translation have included reporter assays, EMSA, 3C assays to detect allele-specific chromatin looping, immunohistochemistry of pathology specimens of patients of known genotype, analysis of RNA from pathology specimens, and PRIME editing of targeted variants into ER+ cell lines. Collectively, these results indicate a great deal of regulatory variation at ESR1. Results of the published assays are generally concordant, with discrepancies likely due to differences in exact boundaries of tested regions, differences in sensitivity of the luciferase used in reporter assays, presence of additional undetected regulatory sites in close proximity to a targeted site, differences in sample preparation (for IHC and other methods relying on pathology specimens), and/or differences in ESR1 endogenous expression in host cell lines. We anticipate that all these approaches will continue to be informative for detecting allele-specific differences in regulatory sites. In this project, we introduced an additional feature, cosegregation of variants with breast cancer in families, which offers an independent test of the hypothesis of allele-specific effects.

  It is biologically highly plausible that inherited variation in estrogen receptor expression would influence breast cancer risk. Exposure to estrogen is a major risk factor for breast cancer (8), promoting carcinogenesis both through the genotoxic effects of estrogen metabolites and through the accumulation of mutations arising through replicative errors during estrogen receptor-mediated proliferation (26, 27) Consistent with this biology, all 30 breast cancers with known estrogen receptor status were ER positive (Fig. 1D). Persistent expression of the estrogen receptor characterizes two-thirds of breast cancers and is associated with prognosis and treatment response (8, 28, 29). Progression of breast cancer from hormone dependence to independence is due primarily to altered expression of the estrogen receptor, with somatic mutations in the ligand-binding domain of ESR1 leading to hormone-resistant breast cancer (30). [Inherited damaging missense and nonsense mutations of ESR1 have not been implicated in breast cancer (31, 32), but rather lead to estrogen resistance in multiple organ systems, usually inherited as a recessive condition and characterized by elevated estradiol and gonadotropic hormones and delayed puberty (33, 34).]

  All allele-specific effects in our families were in the direction of increased promoter activity for the breast-cancer-linked variant compared to the reference allele. At the one site we tested in detail, allelic differences in transcription factor binding explained this observation. Specifically, in MCF7 and T47D ER+ transformed breast cancer cell lines, the private variant allele at R03 altered a binding site motif of the AP-2 transcription factor family and increased the binding affinity of transcription factor TFAP2C for ESR1. This observation is consistent with the known role of TFAP2C in the regulation of ESR1 expression. TFAP2C is known to bind multiple sites in the ESR1 gene (35–37). TFAP2C knockdown reduced the expression of ESR1 and ESR1 target genes, blunted the mitogenic effect of estrogen, and reduced growth of MCF7 cells in a tumor xenograft model (38). Knockdown of TFAP2A had none of these effects, although ChIP-seq showed similar patterns of binding for TFAP2A and TFAP2C (20, 35). Our finding of lower TFAP2A binding activity with MCF7 vs. T47D nuclear extract is consistent with lower levels of TFAP2A in MCF7 cells than in T47D cells (19, 35).

  Our data suggest that the rare allele at R03 increases the binding affinity of TFAP2C for the enhancer at this site, increasing enhancer activity and leading to increased ESR1 expression. Additionally, TFAP2C is itself an estrogen-inducible gene and a direct target of the estrogen receptor (39). Thus, TFAP2C and ESR1 are mutually dependent on each other for expression. Increased expression of ESR1 may generate positive feedback, with increased TFAP2C expression leading to additional amplification of ESR1 expression.

  More noncoding regulatory variation has been reported at ESR1 than at any tumor suppressor gene. Genomic loss of part or all the BRCA1 promoter has been observed in several families, and a BRCA1 promoter point mutation leading to methylation-associated silencing has also been reported (40). Inherited mutations in the TERT promoter predispose to melanoma (41, 42) and duplications including an enhancer element upstream of the GREM1 gene predispose to colorectal cancer and polyposis (43, 44). Regulatory variation at ESR1 differs from that at tumor suppressor genes in that effects of regulatory variants on ESR1 are much milder, but the number of such variants is much larger.

  An extension of these observations will be to test whether regulatory variation at ESR1 may modify the effects of BRCA1, BRCA2, and other high penetrance breast and ovarian cancer genes. ESR1 has not been among the loci identified by GWAS screens for modifiers of BRCA1 or BRCA2, (45) but given that multiple different, individually rare alleles influence ESR1 expression, modifier effects may best be detected by evaluating together only those alleles found to have similar effects on ESR1 promoters.

  From the perspective of human genetics, the design of this project may prove useful in identifying noncoding regulatory variants influencing other conditions. The design took advantage of the vast amount of existing data on GWAS “risk SNPs,” without succumbing to false positive GWAS reports due to inadequate matching of ancestries of cases vs. controls (46). The design was not case–control, but instead was closely related to classical linkage analysis; that is, testing which of the high-confidence GWAS risk alleles co-occurred with breast cancer within families more often than expected by chance. The one cosegregating SNP, rs2046210, served as an informative linkage marker, in disequilibrium with multiple different rare alleles of functional effect, each coinherited with breast cancer in one or a few families. We suggest that for other complex conditions, testing risk alleles of reportedly high-confidence GWAS SNPs for coinheritance with the phenotype within families might similarly reveal nearby alleles of demonstrable functional effect. If haplotypes can be built from high-confidence GWAS SNPs and common SNPs in tight disequilibrium with them, then co-occurrence of haplotype with phenotype in a family can be interpreted as very likely reflecting coinheritance. With informative haplotypes for testing linkage, it is possible to search for a genomic region that may harbor alleles of functional effect. For severely affected families for whom whole genome sequence is available for multiple affected individuals in each family, the simulations and hypothesis testing are straightforward.

  Limitations of this project were consequences of the complexity of estrogen receptor biology. We evaluated activity from two of the multiple promoters of ESR1; evaluated functional effects only in breast cancer cell lines (MCF7 and T47D), not in normal breast epithelium; and included 146 of the many hundreds of as-yet-unsolved severely affected families in our cohort. Other ESR1 promoters and other cell lines and other families would very likely reveal additional regulatory effects. We anticipate that extending this study in each of these directions will yield additional meaningful genetic variation in the ESR1 region.


## Methods

### Research Subjects.
  Participants in this project are long-term members of our studies of inherited breast cancer, approved by the human subjects’ division of the University of Washington (STUDY00001583). All participants provided written informed consent. Ascertainment of the families has been previously described (47).


### Genomics.
  For whole genome sequencing, high molecular weight DNA was sonicated and constructed into ~500 bp paired end libraries with the PCR-free Illumina TruSeq Nano protocol. Sequencing was performed on NovaSeq 6000 instruments generating ~100 Gb (>330 M 150 bp paired reads) per participant. Reads were aligned to the human genome by BWA-mem v.0.7.17 (48) and aligned reads are streamed to SAMBLASTER v0.1.24 (49) to improve sorting and to extract split reads and discordant reads (50). Subsequent processing was carried out with SAMtools v1.10 (51) and Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) v4.1.4 (52), including sorting and merging of BAM files, removal of duplicate reads, realigning indels and recalibrating base quality scores. To detect SNPs and indels, we integrated calls from GATK HaplotypeCaller (54) and from FreeBayes v1.3 (53). Genomic regions of regulatory potential were based on annotations of regions with high levels of DNaseI hypersensitivity, H3K4me1 and H3K27ac signals, and binding by transcription factors in cultured HMECs by ENCODE (54).


### Statistics.
  Differences in allelic effects on promoter activity were compared by two-tail t tests, corrected for multiple comparisons by the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (13). Differences in effectiveness of competitor to block binding of variant and reference probe for TFAP2C were compared by analysis of covariance.


### Cell Lines.
  MCF7 (HTB-22) and T47D (HTB-133) cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection and cultured under standard conditions in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 2 mM GlutaMAX, 1 mM sodium pyruvate and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cell lines were tested for mycoplasma and authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) marker typing.


### Plasmid Construction.
  ESR1 promoters were located using the Eukaryotic Promoter database (https://epd.expasy.org/epd/). At the ESR1 locus, boundaries of regulatory regions were chosen by identifying the presence of DNase I hypersensitivity, transcription factor binding sites, and chromatin state segmentation annotated by ENCODE. Promoters ESR1.1 (Chr6:151,806,695–151,807,873) and ESR1.5 (Chr6:151,806,695–151,807,873) were amplified by PCR from cell line NA12878 and regulatory regions were amplified from patient DNA or synthesized commercially with and without patient-specific variants (Genscript). PCR products were cloned into pCR2.1-TOPO (ThermoFisher) and sequences verified. If no other patient-specific variants were present in the regulatory region, reference and variant alleles were isolated. If other variants were present in the regulatory region, the variant haplotype was isolated and Q5 mutagenesis (New England Biolabs) was used to create the wild-type allele while retaining the rest of the patient haplotype.

  The ESR1 promoter and regulatory regions were then cloned into reporter plasmid pNL1.2, containing NanoLuc luciferase fused to a PEST destabilization sequence (NlucP) (Promega). AscI, SbfI, and NheI restriction sites were added to pNL1.2 between the BamHI and SalI sites downstream of the luciferase gene to aid in cloning. The promoter was cloned into the KpnI and XhoI sites of the modified pNL1.2 vector, upstream of the NlucP gene, using Gibson Assembly (New England Biolabs) (55). The reference and variant alleles of each regulatory region were cloned into the NheI and SalI sites of the modified Promoter-pNL1.2 construct, downstream of the NlucP gene, using Gibson Assembly or commercial cloning (Genscript). Correct insertion of all elements was verified by restriction digest and sequencing.


### Luciferase Reporter Assays.
  Equimolar amounts of modified pNL1.2 NlucP construct and 25 ng firefly luciferase vector pGL4.54 (Promega) were transfected in quadruplicate into MCF7 breast cell lines using Lipofectamine 3000 (ThermoFisher). Cells were plated in 96-well plates and transfected with a total of 100 ng of DNA using pUC19 as a carrier plasmid. Luciferase activity was assessed 24 h after transfection using the Nano-Glo Dual-Luciferase Assay System (Promega). NlucP luciferase activity was normalized to firefly luciferase activity. Regulatory region activity was normalized to the activity of a construct containing only the promoter. Each transfection was repeated at least eight times on at least 3 different days.


### EMSAs.
  Gel shift assays were performed using MCF7 and T47D nuclear extracts and IRDye 700-labeled oligonucleotide duplexes containing R03 reference (5′-CCAGGGCTTTGGCCTCAGGAGGGAAGCCTTATCTGTAGC-3′) and variant (5′-CCAGGGCTTTGGCCTCAGGGGGGAAGCCTTATCTGTAGC-3′) alleles. Nuclear extracts were prepared using the NE-PER Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction kit (ThermoFisher) according to manufacturer instructions. The AP-2 competitor sequence was 5″-CAGAAGAGTGCCTGCGGCCAGTGGCCACCA-3′. Probe and competitor duplexes were annealed by mixing complementary oligonucleotides (IDT), heating 3 min at 95 °C and cooling gradually to room temperature. Binding reactions were performed with the Odyssey EMSA kit (LI-COR Biosciences) in binding buffer (10 mM Tris, 50 mM KCl, 1 mM DTT; pH 7.5), 2.5 mM DTT, 0.25% Tween-20, 50 ng/μL Poly(dI·dC), and 2.5% glycerol with 5 μg nuclear extract and 2.5 nM labeled probe, and incubated for 20 min at room temperature. Competitor duplexes at 10-, 30-, 100-, or 200-fold molar excess over the labeled probe were preincubated with the nuclear extract and reaction mix for 10 min at room temperature before the addition of the labeled probes and subsequently incubated for 20 min at room temperature. Reactions including antibodies (1 μL) to TFAP2A or TFAP2C (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-12726X and sc-12762X) were similar except for a 30 min preincubation after antibody addition. Reactions were separated on 6% DNA retardation gels (ThermoFisher) in 0.5× TBE for 65 min at 100 V. Gels were visualized on an Odyssey scanner (LI-COR Biosciences). Bound probe was quantified using Image Studio Lite software (LI-COR Biosciences) by measuring the signal intensity within equal size boxes at the center of each lane.
