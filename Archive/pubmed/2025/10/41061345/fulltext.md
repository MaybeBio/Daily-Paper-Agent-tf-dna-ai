# RSC4All as a machine learning nomogram to predict RSClin™ results in HR+/HER2- node-negative early breast cancer


## Background
  RSClin™ combines clinicopathological (CP) and genomic data to refine predictions of distant recurrence (DR) risk and chemotherapy (CT) benefit in node-negative (N0) hormone receptor (HR)-positive/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-negative early breast cancer (eBC). Available in the United States, RSClin™ is inaccessible in Europe due to regulatory constraints. We developed and validated RSC4All, a machine learning (ML) nomogram replicating RSClin™ predictions and evaluating clinical impact by comparing DR risk and CT benefit estimates across recurrence score (RS), RSClin™, and RSC4All.


## Materials and methods
  We retrospectively identified 290 HR-positive/HER2-negative eBC with both 21-gene RS and RSClin™ estimation at three European centers (development cohort). ML models were trained on 70% and internally validated on 30% of this cohort to reproduce RSClin™ estimates for DR risk and CT benefit. External validation used an independent cohort of 513 patients. Performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic area under the curve (ROC AUC) (classification) and R2 (regression).

  Clinical agreement between RSC4All and RSClin™ was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa and McNemar’s test, including replication of RS to RSClin™ reclassification patterns. RS and RSClin™ classifications were compared in the pooled population and CP predictors of reclassification were explored.


## Results
  ML classification models achieved excellent performance with ROC AUC 0.99 for both DR risk and CT benefit, while regression model had R2 = 0.82 and 0.77, respectively. In external validation, RSC4All maintained high accuracy (ROC AUC = 0.99; R2 = 0.82 and 0.76) with an almost perfect clinical agreement with RSClin™ (Cohen's kappa = 0.87 and 0.84) and replicated RS to RSClin™ reclassification direction in 94.1% (DR) and 93.0% (CT) of cases. In the pooled cohort, RSClin™ reclassified 22.2% of DR risk (balanced up/downgrades) and 12.2% for CT benefit (mostly upgrades), with high grade, high Ki-67, low progesterone receptor, and larger tumor size predicting upward reclassification. A web-based tool was developed for public access (https://rsc4all.streamlit.app).


## Conclusions
  RSC4All accurately reproduces RSClin™ predictions and reclassification patterns, providing a freely accessible alternative where RSClin™ is unavailable. By integrating CP and genomic data, it refines risk stratifications and supports adjuvant decisions in N0 HR-positive/HER2-negative eBC. External validation in an independent cohort strengthens its generalizability and clinical applicability.


## Introduction
  Breast cancer is the most prevalent malignancy among women globally,1 with hormone receptor (HR)-positive, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-negative breast cancer representing the majority of cases, accounting for >70% of all diagnoses.2 For these patients, endocrine therapy (ET) is the cornerstone of adjuvant treatment, and chemotherapy (CT) is typically reserved for those at higher risk of relapse, identified through clinicopathological features such as tumor size, lymph node involvement, histological grade, and proliferation markers like Ki-67.3 However, relying solely on clinicopathological factors may lead to both overtreatment and undertreatment,4 highlighting the need for more precise prognostic tools. The 21-gene recurrence score (RS) assay (Oncotype DX) provides an individualized assessment of the risk of distant recurrence (DR) and CT benefit, delivering an RS ranging from 0 to 100. This score categorizes patients into low-, intermediate-, or high-risk groups, and its prognostic value was validated in the TAILORx trial,5 which demonstrated that for patients with an intermediate-risk RS, ET alone was as effective as chemoendocrine therapy, a key finding that led to its integration into major oncology guidelines.3,6

  Subsequent research from the TAILORx trial underscored the importance of combining genomic data with the canonical clinicopathological factors to refine risk predictions. This led to the development of the RS-pathology-clinical tool, which integrates the RS with tumor size, grade, and patient age, offering a more comprehensive and accurate prediction of DR risk.7,8 Building on this approach, the RSClin™ tool was introduced in 2021 by Sparano et al. as an innovative prognostic model that combines genomic and clinicopathological data to assess the likelihood of DR and the potential CT benefit in HR-positive/HER2-negative early breast cancer patients.9,10 This integrated methodology represents a significant advancement in personalized treatment strategies, allowing for a more tailored approach to adjuvant therapy in selected patient populations.

  Unfortunately, RSClin™ remains inaccessible in many countries worldwide, including Europe, mainly due to regulatory limitations. This highlights the need for alternative, easily accessible tools that can replicate the prognostic and predictive accuracy of RSClin™. To address this gap, the primary objective of this study is to develop and validate a freely accessible machine learning nomogram (RSC4All) able to predict RSClin™ results, including DR risk and CT benefit estimates by combining genomic data with clinicopathological factors in a cohort of patients with HR-positive/HER2-negative node-negative early breast cancer.11 The tool is trained and internally validated on a European dataset and further evaluated in an independent real-world cohort to explore its generalizability.

  Additionally, we report the comparison of RS and RSClin™ in predicting recurrence risk within this large real-world cohort. Thus, the secondary objective of this study is to determine the potential clinical impact of RSClin™ and the derivative machine learning nomogram (RSC4All) in supporting the adjuvant decision-making process in patients with HR-positive/HER2-negative node-negative early breast cancer.


## Materials and methods

### Study design
  This is a retrospective, observational, multicentric study that aimed to develop and validate a machine learning nomogram (RSC4All) to predict RSClin™ estimates for DR risk and CT benefit. We identified consecutive patients diagnosed with HR-positive/HER2-negative, node-negative, early breast cancer who underwent tumor analysis with the 21-gene RS assay at Humanitas Research Hospital (ICH, Milan, Italy), ASST Papa Giovanni XXIII (PG23, Bergamo, Italy), and Institute Jules Bordet (IJB, Brussels, Belgium) from December 2020 to December 2022.

  Eligibility was based on the following inclusion criteria: histological diagnosis of invasive early breast cancer; HR-positive/HER2-negative disease; available information on clinicopathological characteristics and genomic data derived from the 21-gene RS assay and RSClin™ (RS value, DR risk, and CT benefit estimates). Patients with node positivity were excluded from this analysis.

  The research proposal was approved by the local ethical committee (independent ethical committee IRCCS Istituto Clinico Humanitas, protocol number ONC/OSS-11/2023) and conducted according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.


### Data extraction and study procedures
  Clinicopathological and treatment data were retrieved from the electronic health records of the three participating centers, including variables such as age, menopausal status, tumor size (T classification),12 lymph node status (N classification), estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PgR), HER2 status, Ki-67, grading, histotype, and lymphovascular invasion (LVI).

  For each patient, genomic information included the 21-gene RS value and the corresponding RSClin™ estimates for DR risk and CT benefit.

  In line with the TAILORx study, RS values were categorized as low (0-10), intermediate (11-25), and high (>25).5 DR risk was classified as low (<10%) or high (≥10%), while CT benefit was categorized as ‘no benefit’ for probabilities <3% and as ‘benefit’ for probabilities of ≥3%, according to literature.13 Descriptive statistics were used to analyze clinicopathological and genomic data.


### Development of machine learning models and web tool
  A total of 271 out of 290 patients with complete information were included in the model development phase. The dataset was split into a training set (70%, 189 patients) and a validation set (30%, 82 patients) to ensure robust evaluation. A correlation-based feature selection approach was applied to identify variable predictive of the outcomes. Features with an absolute Pearson correlation >0.1 with the target variable were selected for model training. Linear and logistic regression models were chosen for their simplicity, interpretability, and computational efficiency. The machine learning nomogram was developed and validated using both classification and regression models to predict DR risk and CT benefit. This process led to the creation of four main models: two classification models to assess categories of low versus high DR risk (dr_clf) and CT benefit yes versus no (ct_clf), and two regression models for quantifying the exact DR risk (dr_reg) and CT benefit (ct_reg) estimates.

  Model performance was evaluated using several metrics, with the most relevant being R2 for regression tasks, and area under the receiver operating characteristic -curve (ROC AUC) and F1 score for classification tasks. Values of R2 and ROC AUC >0.7 were considered indicative of good predictive accuracy.

  To provide easy and free access to the final model, a web-based application was developed using Streamlit (https://streamlit.io). Users can input clinical and genomic data and receive both numerical estimates and binary classifications for DR risk and CT benefit.


### External validation
  External validation used an independent, real-world cohort of 513 HR-positive/HER2-negative, node-negative early breast cancer, diagnosed between 2020 and 2024, at three Italian centers: Policlinico Federico II (NA, Napoli), Humanitas Istituto Clinico Catanese (CT, Catania), and ASST Papa Giovanni XXIII (PG23, Bergamo). All had available Oncotype DX testing and RSClin™ estimates for DR risk and CT benefit. RSC4All was applied prospectively without retraining or adjustment.

  Validation followed a two-step approach. Firstly, predictive accuracy was assessed via R2 for continuous and ROC AUC plus F1 score for categorical outputs. Secondly, clinical agreement between RSC4All and RSClin™ categorical results was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa and McNemar’s test.

  We also compared reclassification direction between RSC4All and RSClin™ relative to RS, quantifying agreement as the proportion of matching cases and assessing concordance with Cohen’s kappa.


### Overall cohort analysis: RS and RSClin™ comparison
  To assess the clinical impact of RSClin™, we pooled the development and external cohorts, to increase power and reduce selection bias. Agreement and reclassification patterns for DR risk and CT benefit were evaluated using Cohen’s kappa and McNemar’s test.

  Clinicopathological factors associated with reclassification were explored via multivariable logistic regression.


## Results

### Patients’ characteristics

#### Development cohort
  The study included a total of 290 consecutive patients with HR-positive/HER2-negative node-negative early breast cancer who underwent 21-gene RS assay according to guidelines in three European cancer centers: ICH (n = 100), IJB (n = 57), and PG23 (n = 133). All patients were pN0 at final pathology and underwent surgery followed by RS assessment in the post-operative setting.

  The median age was 61 years (interquartile range 25-87 years), with 68% of patients postmenopausal. The most common tumor histotype was non-special type (83%), followed by lobular carcinoma (11%) and other histologies (5%). Most patients had pT1c (58%) or pT2 (29%) tumors, and intermediate grade (G2) was the most frequent (66%). LVI, either focal or extensive, was present in 24% of cases and HER2-low tumors (referred to as tumors with HER2 immunohistochemistry 1+ or 2+ with in situ hybridization-negative) accounted for 51% of the cohort.

  Regarding RS distribution, 13% of patients had a low RS (0-10), 60% intermediate (11-25), and 27% high (>25). High RS values were more frequently associated with high-grade tumors, low PgR expression, and larger tumor size (>20 mm). Overall, 28% of patients received adjuvant CT, mostly anthracycline- or taxane-based regimes, while ET was primarily with aromatase inhibitors (81%). #### External validation cohort
  The external validation cohort included 513 patients with HR-positive/HER2-negative, node-negative early breast cancer from NA, CT, and PG23, all of whom underwent 21-gene RS testing with available RSClin™ estimates for both DR risk and CT benefit. Detailed clinicopathological characteristics for this cohort are presented in Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105830.


### Machine learning models
  Four machine learning models were developed to reproduce RSClin™ predictions: two multivariate linear regression models for continuous estimation of DR risk (dr_reg) and CT benefit (ct_reg), and two logistic regression models for categorical classification of DR risk (low versus high; dr_clf) and CT benefit (no versus yes; ct_clf).

  In internal validation, the regression models achieved strong predictive accuracy, with R2 values of 0.82 for DR risk and 0.77 for CT benefit. The classification models demonstrated excellent discriminative ability, with ROC AUC values of 0.99 for both DR risk and CT benefit, and F1 scores of 0.94 and 0.96, respectively, indicating a strong balance between precision and recall.

  Full model metrics and the selected features for each model are detailed in Supplementary Tables S2-S4, respectively, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105830. ### Web tool
  To facilitate easy access to the developed machine learning models, a web application, RSC4All, was developed using Streamlit. The application is available at https://rsc4all.streamlit.app.

  The web application enables users to insert patients’ data, which the models process to generate predictions for DR risk, including exact values and risk group classifications, as well as CT benefit estimates.


### External validation and clinical agreement with RSClin™
  In the external validation cohort of 513 patients, RSC4All predictions were compared directly with RSClin™ for both DR risk and CT benefit. The models maintained high performance in this independent dataset, closely mirroring the results obtained in the development phase. For continuous predictions, the R2 was 0.82 for DR risk and 0.76 for CT benefit. For categorical predictions, the ROC AUC remained at 0.99 for both DR risk and CT benefit, with F1 scores of 0.96 and 0.95, respectively, confirming excellent classification accuracy (Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105830).

  From a clinical perspective, agreement between RSC4All and RSClin™ was almost perfect for both endpoints. For CT benefit, identical classification was observed in 92.99% of patients (Cohen’s kappa = 0.847), with McNemar’s test (P = 0.1336) showing no systematic difference in classification. For DR risk, concordance reached 94.1% (Cohen’s kappa = 0.870), with McNemar’s test (P = 0.0446) suggesting a slight, non-clinically relevant tendency of RSC4All to classify more patients as low risk compared with RSClin™. Sankey diagrams (Supplementary Figures S3 and S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105830) illustrate these minimal shifts.

  For DR risk, RS classified 61.6% of patients as low risk and 38.4% as high risk, while RSClin™ classified 64.7% and 35.3%, respectively. Concordance was 77.8% (Cohen’s kappa = 0.524, moderate agreement), with no significant directional bias (McNemar’s P = 0.0812). ## Discussion
  The advent of genomic tests, such as the 21-gene RS assay, has profoundly transformed adjuvant treatment decision making for patients with HR-positive/HER2-negative early breast cancer. However, solely depending on genomic data without integrating clinicopathological features might not offer a comprehensive perspective on prognosis and prediction. Modern tools like the RSClin™ have bridged this gap by integrating clinicopathological factors, such as age, tumor size, grade, and, in the recent RSClinN+ version,14 nodal status with genomic data, offering a more personalized and precise approach.

  Despite its potential, RSClin™ is currently available only in the United States and remains unavailable in other regions, including the European Union due to regulatory limitations.

  This study addressed this gap, by developing and validating RSC4All, the first machine learning-based nomogram, which accurately predicts RSClin™ estimates with respect to both DR risk and CT benefit.

  When tested on the study population, RSC4All closely replicated RSClin™ results, accurately predicting both DR risk and CT benefit using regression and classification approaches. The regression analysis provided precise estimates for DR risk and CT benefit, while the classification approach identified dichotomous categories. The models demonstrated excellent performance, achieving ROC AUC scores of 0.99 for classification tasks and significant R2 values of 0.82 and 0.77 for regression analyses. This aligns with the primary objective of the study, offering a user-friendly and cost-effective alternative to assist clinicians in the adjuvant decision-making process when the RSClin™ original test is inaccessible.

  A major strength of this work is the inclusion of a large, independent, multi-institutional external validation cohort (n = 513). Applied without retraining, RSC4All maintained the same high technical accuracy (R2 = 0.82 for DR risk, 0.76 for CT benefit; ROC AUC = 0.99 for both) and showed almost perfect clinical agreement with RSClin™. This robust validation strongly supports the generalizability, reproducibility, and clinical reliability of the tool.

  The secondary objective of the study was to determine the clinical impact of RSClin™ and the derivative RSC4All in comparison to RS alone. To evaluate the added value of RSClin™, we carried out a direct comparison with RS in the pooled dataset (n = 784), including both the development and external validation cohorts. This combined analysis maximized statistical power and provided more generalizable estimates across diverse clinical settings.

  For DR risk, RSClin™ tended to reclassify 22.2% of patients, with shifts occurring in both directions (9.6% from low to high risk and 12.6% from high to low). This bidirectional movement highlights RSClin™’s ability to refine recurrence risk stratification by integrating of clinicopathological factors. Tumor grade, Ki-67, PgR, and tumor size emerged as the main drivers of this reclassification.

  For CT benefit, the effect was predominantly unidirectional: RSClin™ identified 36.5% of patients as likely to benefit from CT, compared with 24.7% with RS, with almost all reclassifications representing upgrades. These upgrades were largely driven by high tumor grade, larger tumor size, and low PgR expression, features reflecting more aggressive biology despite a moderate genomic score. Clinically, this is important because RSClin™ captured ∼12% more patients who might benefit from CT, particularly those with intermediate RS but adverse clinicopathological factors.

  Interestingly, RSClin™ simultaneously led to a downgrade in DR risk for some patients while increasing CT benefit indications for others. This apparent divergence underscores the distinct nature of prognostic and predictive estimates: a lower absolute recurrence risk does not necessarily mean a patient derives no benefit from CT. RSClin™ refines risk stratification by integrating clinical features that influence both tumor biology (prognosis) and treatment responsiveness (predictive value). For example, a patient with an intermediate genomic score but high Ki-67 and large tumor size may have a lower absolute risk of recurrence but still derive significant relative benefit from CT.

  Taken together, these findings underscore the potential dual impact of RSClin™: it mitigates overtreatment by reclassifying patients with an overestimated genomic risk into a lower-risk category, while expanding CT recommendations for patients whose clinical characteristics suggest they may benefit from treatment. Even though RSClin™ is not universally endorsed in international guidelines, all major guidelines recommend incorporating clinicopathological features into genomic-based risk stratification, which further supports the clinical rationale for tools like RSC4All.15 In this context, it is worth noting that, in our external validation cohort, all cases resulting in significant changes to DR risk and CT benefit categories with RSClin™ were accurately captured by RSC4All, further validating the accuracy of the machine learning model in predicting the original test results. As the treatment landscape evolves, the recent approval of cyclin-dependent kinase 4/6 (CDK4/6) inhibitors (e.g. ribociclib) in node-negative HR-positive/HER2-negative early breast cancer patients adds a new dimension to risk stratification. While genomic tests were not designed to predict CDK4/6 inhibitor benefit, artificial intelligence-driven tools that integrate prognostic and predictive factors with clinicopathological characteristics may help refine risk estimates and guide treatment escalation decisions. With an increasing number of HR-positive/HER2-negative early breast cancer patients becoming eligible for adjuvant ribociclib, incorporating such tools, like RSC4All, could optimize patient selection. Further studies are warranted to explore the interplay between RSClin™, CDK4/6 inhibitors, and CT indications.

  Despite rigorous methodology, some limitations should be acknowledged: (i) RSC4All relies on four independent machine learning models (two regression and two classification) that do not interact. As a result, in borderline cases, the continuous CT benefit estimate may occasionally diverge from the binary classification. Since categorical models demonstrated higher accuracy in validation, the binary output should guide clinical interpretation. (ii) Variability in laboratory measurements, particularly in Ki-67 assessments and ER/PgR classifications, adds another layer of complexity. Differences in protocols and techniques across laboratories may influence the accuracy of the machine learning models, highlighting the need for standardized measurement practices. (iii) Survival analysis was not included, as the study focused on reproducing RSClin™ outputs. However, survival endpoints would provide valuable insights into the real-world implications of reclassifying DR risk or CT benefit categories. Assessing survival outcomes, especially in discordant cases, is a key area for future research. Long-term follow-up will be crucial to determine whether reclassification translates into meaningful clinical benefit. (iv) While the external validation strongly supports generalizability, retraining on even larger datasets may improve calibration, particularly for outliers and borderline cases.

  Despite the aforementioned challenges, our study has several strengths: (i) This study is the first to specifically focus on RSClin™ prediction, addressing a significant gap in its application outside the United States, particularly in regions like Europe, where RSClin™ is limited. (ii) The inclusion of a large, independent external validation cohort provides robust evidence of the tool’s generalizability and clinical reliability. (iii) The inclusion of patients from two different countries enhances the diversity of the dataset, making the findings more representative of varied breast cancer demographics and clinical practices. (iv) Beyond the primary goal of RSClin™ prognostications, the true achievement lies in the development of the machine learning-based nomogram RSC4All that combines genomic data with clinicopathological features in order to enhance diagnostic accuracy and refine treatment strategies. (v) By employing both regression and classification models, the study offers a comprehensive approach to evaluating DR risk and CT benefit. This dual strategy ensures that both continuous risk predictions and categorical classifications are addressed, offering flexibility in clinical application. Combining statistical performance metrics with clinical agreement analyses confirms that RSC4All not only reproduces RSClin™’s numerical predictions with high accuracy but also mirrors its clinical decision patterns.


### Conclusion
  This study successfully developed and externally validated machine learning-based nomogram, RSC4All, able to accurately predict RSClin™ outcomes, complemented by the creation of a publicly accessible web tool. This accessible, user-friendly, and cost-free tool offers a practical alternative for the treatment decision-making process in patients with node-negative HR-positive/HER2-negative early breast cancer, particularly in settings where RSClin™ is unavailable. By integrating clinicopathological features with genomic data, the tool enhances risk assessment and supports more personalized adjuvant therapy recommendations.

  The observed differences between RS, RSClin™, and the derivative RSC4All highlight the importance of integrating clinical factors into genomic assessments.

  Future research with larger, more diverse datasets and extended follow-up will be essential to validate the clinical utility of RSC4All in real-world practice and to evaluate its impact on long-term patient outcomes.
