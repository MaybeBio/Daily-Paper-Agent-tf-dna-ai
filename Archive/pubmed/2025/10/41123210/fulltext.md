# Structural insights into γH2Ax containing nucleosomes


## Abstract
  The phosphorylation of the histone variant H2AX on the nucleosome, yielding γH2AX, acts as a 'master control switch', signaling the recruitment of DNA repair factors at DNA double-stranded break sites. This phosphorylation is recognized by BRCA1 carboxy-terminal (BRCT) domains of specific repair proteins. Using cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM), we provide structural insights into diverse mononucleosome architectures and inter-nucleosomal interactions in the presence of H2AX, mimicking nucleosomes during DNA repair. We resolved three distinct stacked structures where the nucleosomal dyad axes and disk planes align parallel. The inter-nucleosomal interactions involve unique contacts mediated by the H4 N-terminal tail, exposed H2B elements, and DNA. Geometric analysis of stacking constraints, including published structures, reveals a tight distribution of rotational parameters around 0o, with the greatest variability in the translational parameter 'slide'. Our studies indicate that phosphorylation-dependent binding of BRCT domains with γH2AX nucleosomes disrupts stacking. However, no clear densities for BRCT proteins were observed, indicative of dynamic interactions. Molecular simulations replicate the stability of BRCT binding to γH2AX but do not indicate stable docked conformations of BRCT to nucleosome. We propose that BRCT recognition of γH2AX nucleosomes could contribute to chromatin decondensation during DNA damage signaling, exposing the nucleosomal acidic patch for repair factor recognition.


## Introduction
  The nucleosome core particle (NCP), the fundamental building block of chromatin, is essential for efficient DNA packaging in the nucleus. This nucleoprotein complex, consisting of double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) wrapped in a left-handed manner around eight core histones, achieves a remarkable five-fold reduction in DNA length within its structure [1]. The compaction facilitates the astounding 10000-fold reduction in DNA length required to organize DNA into the condensed state of mammalian chromosomes [2]. Most chromatin exists as condensed heterochromatin, primarily located in telomeric regions, pericentric regions, and other transcriptionally repressed regions. Pioneering studies, using crystallography, cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM), and cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET), have attempted to explore the three-dimensional organization of chromatin compaction using in vitro reconstituted polynucleosomes [3–8], native chromatin fibers extracted from cells [5, 9–12], and liquid–liquid phase separation droplets [12–14]. These studies along with others have elucidated that nucleosomes packing does not follow a uniform model, instead involves a diverse array of structures and conformations.

  Chromatin is a dynamic structure that undergoes constant remodeling [15–19]. The precise assembly and disassembly of chromatin, i.e. transition from heterochromatin to euchromatin, is tightly regulated to facilitate essential cellular processes, such as gene expression, DNA replication, and DNA repair. Histone variants and histone post-translational modifications (PTMs) play a critical role in regulating chromatin dynamics during specific cellular processes [20, 21]. Histone PTMs involve the addition of small chemical groups, such as phosphate, acetyl, and methyl groups, to the tail of specific histone variants [22–24]. These modifications influence chromatin dynamics by altering histone–DNA or histone–histone interactions [21]. Once installed on the specific histone residues, PTMs require recognition by specific histone PTM-binding domains (readers) or removal by specific eraser enzymes. In this study, we investigate the effects of a specific histone variant–PTM pair on nucleosome dynamics, the phosphorylation of H2AX variant, which is integral to DNA damage signaling.

  DNA damage is an inevitable consequence of the genome’s constant exposure to environmental and endogenous genotoxic agents, resulting in thousands of lesions per cell each day. DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) are the most severe threat to genome stability and are repaired through homologous recombination (HR) and nonhomologous end joining (NHEJ). Immediately after DSB induction, the H2AX variant that comprises 10%–15% of total cellular H2A, is phosphorylated at Ser139 within its unique C-terminal tail, generating γH2AX foci [25–27]. This phosphorylation acts as an epigenetic mark that flags megabase regions around an individual break and is an essential and efficient coordinator of DSB recognition and DNA repair [28]. The H2AX C-terminus has the consensus Ser-Gln-Glu (SQE) motif which is a common recognition site for phosphorylation by the phosphatidylinositol-3-OH-kinase-like family of protein kinases (PIKKs) such as ATM (ataxia telangiectasia mutated) [29], ATR (ATM and Rad3-related) [29–31], or DNA-dependent protein kinase (DNA-PK) [28, 32]. The γH2AX foci specifically attracts repair factors in a coordinated fashion. BRCA1 carboxy-terminal (BRCT) repeat-containing proteins are the primary readers of γH2AX (Supplementary Fig. S1) [33]. Two BRCT domain containing proteins, Microcephalin protein 1 (MCPH1) and the mediator of DNA damage checkpoint protein 1 (MDC1), are known to localize at the DNA lesion [34–38]. This localization occurs by virtue of the interaction of the BRCT domain with the γH2AX containing nucleosome at the DSB. This interaction is critical for the amplification of the DNA damage response and recruitment of downstream repair factors [26].

  While both MDC1 and MCPH1 have been shown to specifically recognize γH2AX, they have subtle differences in their histone binding specificity which may influence their temporal recognition of chromatin during DNA damage signaling. Unlike MDC1, MCPH1 can recognize the basal phosphorylation of the C-terminal Tyr142 in γH2AX [38–41]. Tyr142 is rapidly dephosphorylated after DSB formation allowing MDC1 binding. Crystal structures of MDC1 BRCT bound to γH2AX C-terminal tail peptide (sequence: ApSQEY) [33, 35, 36, 42] indicate that phosphorylation of Ser139, as well as the side chain and main chain COO− group of Tyr142 are critical for binding. MCPH1 BRCT, on the other hand, can recognize both monophosphorylated (Ser139ph) and di-phosphorylated (Ser139ph and Tyr142ph) forms of γH2AX [38]. While these structural studies offered detailed insights into the interactions between the γH2AX peptide and BRCT domains, their influence on nucleosome dynamics remained unexamined.

  In this study, we investigated how the phosphorylation on the γH2AX containing human nucleosome core particle (γH2AX-NCP) influences nucleosome dynamics and its recognition by BRCT domains. We developed a time efficient, one-step purification method to produce human histone octamers, coupled with a one-pot native chemical ligation (NCL) strategy to produce γH2AX containing octamers. The H2Ax-NCPs and γH2AX-NCPs assembled using 145 bp Widom 601 DNA (W601) [43] was used for cryo-EM studies. This work reveals that both phosphorylated and nonphosphorylated H2AX nucleosomes stack in a manner that aligns their dyad axes, with interactions resembling those observed in certain nucleosome stack structures within the structure database. Our findings demonstrate that these stacking interactions are mediated by the H4 tail, residues of H2B, and DNA, resulting in the formation of three distinct stacked states. By linking high-resolution structural insights from cryo-EM to previously identified configurations in the nucleosome structure database, this study highlights conserved principles of nucleosome stacking and underscores the critical roles of histone and DNA interactions in chromatin organization. Our biochemical analyses confirm that phosphorylation of Ser139 is a necessary requirement for γH2AX recognition by BRCT domains; however, these highly dynamic interactions appear to disrupt nucleosomal stacking to expose the H2AX–H2B face of the nucleosome. This exposure of H2AX–H2B face is necessary for the recruitment of the ubiquitination machinery which is followed by the recognition of γH2AX recognition by BRCT domains and is critical for the recruitment of DNA repair factors [44, 45].


## Materials and methods

### Cloning of histones and overexpression
  Human histone (H2AX, H2B type 1-J, H3.1, and H4) genes were amplified by Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) from the plasmids originally obtained from the RIKEN laboratory. A hexahistidine tag followed by a Tobacco etch virus (TEV) protease recognition site was added to the N-termini of both H2AX(1–134) and H3.1(1–136). The DNA sequence pertaining to Mxe intein-chitin-binding domain (CBD) was PCR amplified from pTXB1 (New England Biolabs) and ligated in frame at the C-terminus of H2AX(1–134). The PCR amplified products pertaining to the histone pairs, H2AX(1–134–intein–CBD)–H2B[1–126] and H3.1(1–136)–H4(1–103), were clone into duet vectors. The clones are available at addgene (Plasmid# 197272, Plasmid# 197273, and Plasmid# 240442). The resulting plasmids encoding histone pairs was transformed into BL21(DE3)pLysS cells and plated on to an LB agar plate containing ampicillin (50 mg/ml), streptomycin (50 mg/ml), and chloramphenicol (25 mg/ml). The plate was incubated overnight (∼18 h) at 37°C. For a 2 l preparation, a starter culture was prepared where one colony was inoculated into 100 ml media containing the above antibiotics. After an overnight growth at 37°C, the starter culture was added to the autoclave sterilized 2 l media and was shaken at 160 rpm. When the OD600 reaches ∼0.6–0.8, histone co-expression was induced by adding 1 mM isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG). The culture was grown for 6 h at 30°C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4500 rpm for 20 min at 4°C. Cell pellets were processed immediately or stored at −80°C for future purification.


### Two-step purification of histones
  A nickel affinity chromatography step followed by a size-exclusion chromatography was used to produced native chemically ligated γH2AX containing octamer. Cell pellets were resuspended in 100 ml of lysis buffer [20 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.0, 2.0 M sodium chloride (NaCl), 1 mM phenylmethanesulfonylfluoride]. Resuspended cells were lysed by EmulsiFlex-C3 high pressure homogenizer (Avestin) and clarified by centrifugation at 12 000 rpm at 4°C for 1 h. The clarified supernatant was incubated with Ni-NTA agarose beads (Qiagen) for 1 h and was applied on a gravity flow column to remove the bacterial protein contaminants. The beads were washed with lysis buffer. γH2AX was synthesized using a ligation-desulfurization strategy [46] (Supplementary Fig. S3), where the Cα-carboxy thioester of H2AX(1–134) was first prepared by thiolysis of H2AX(1–134–intein–CBD) fusion protein with MESNa (sodium 2-mercaptoethanesulfonate) via intein-mediated protein splicing. 25 mM MESNa was added to the beads in the gravity column and incubated for overnight at room temperature. The flow through containing intein–CBD was discarded. The octamer containing MES (2-mercaptoethanesulfonate) thioester of H2AX(1–134) was eluted using imidazole gradient of 250 mM, 500 mM, and 1 M. The MES thioester of H2AX(1–134) containing octamer was then ligated with the synthetic C-terminal octapeptide containing the phosphorylated-serine residue, NH2−CTQApSQEY−COOH, to form the full-length γH2AX containing an Ala–Cys mutation at position 135, H2AX (Ala135Cys and Ser139ph). The ligation was initiated by adding 300 mM TCEP (Tris(2chloroethyl phosphate)) as final concentration to the octamer–peptide mixture. Desulfurization was then conducted on the purified H2A.X–A135C–pS139 at 37°C for 32 h in the presence of 20 mM TCEP, pH 7.5 and glutathione using 10 mM of the radical initiator, 2,2′-azobis[2-(2-imidazolin-2-yl)propane]dihydrochloride (VA-044) (Wako chemicals). The final product, i.e. γH2AX containing octamer, was purified by size-exclusion chromatography. Samples from each step were analyzed by 16% SDS–PAGE. Western blot was performed using rabbit monoclonal anti-H2AX antibody (ab124781) and anti-gamma H2AX (Ser139ph) antibody (ab81299) to confirm the γH2AX formation.


### TEV digestion
  TEV digestion was carried out by adding purified TEV in 25:1 mass ratio and incubating the samples at 4°C for overnight. The digestion was confirmed by SDS–PAGE. TEV-digested histones were then concentrated up to 4 mg/ml with ultrafiltration using Amicon YM50 membrane (MWCO 50 kDa) at 4°C (Millipore). The concentrated sample was then injected onto a Superdex 200 10/300 GL column. The histone octamer peak was eluted at an elution volume of 12.5 ml. The peak fractions were pooled and concentrated up to 2 mg/ml, aliquoted, and flash-frozen in the presence of 50% glycerol for long-term storage.


### W601-145 bp DNA preparation
  The large-scale DNA purification was performed as described [47]. Briefly, the 145 bp W601 DNA fragment was released from the pUC57 plasmid containing six inserts of the W601-145 bp sequence, by EcoRV followed by PEG precipitation. The purity of the W601 DNA as analyzed at each step using 1.5% agarose gel. After a final size-exclusion chromatography step the W601-145 bp DNA was concentrated to 1 mg/ml for nucleosome assembly.


### Nucleosome reconstitution
  Nucleosome reconstitution was done essentially as described [48]. The histone octamer and DNA were mixed in the molar ratio of octamer:DNA at 1.1:1.0 and dialyzed sequentially against buffers (10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.0 and 0.5 TCEP) containing 1 M, 800 mM, 50 mM, and 150 mM NaCl, each for at least one hour. The last dialysis was usually done overnight.


### BRCT purification
  Hexahistidine tagged constructs of hMCPH1 BRCT (residues: 639–835; referred to as MCPH1) and hMDC1 BRCT (residues: 1891–2089; referred to MDC1) were cloned into pET15b (ampicillin resistance) and pET47b (kanamycin resistance) vectors, respectively, and expressed in Rosetta™ 2(DE3) cell lines (Novagen). The His-tag based purification protocol for BRCT has been published elsewhere [41].


### Fluroscence polarizarion
  The purified his-tagged BRCTs of hMDC1 and hMCPH1 were tested for their binding affinities with fluorescein-labeled γH2AX mimicking peptide (NH2-KKATQApSQEY-COOH). A concentration of 50 nM of labeled peptide was used in a reaction volume of 20 μl. Fluorescein fluorescence was excited at a wavelength of 485 nm, and the emission was measured at 538 nm on a PerkinElmer Envision plate reader. The change in polarization was graphed as a function of the log of the protein concentration, and the dissociation constant (KD) was obtained from the resulting sigmoidal curve.


### Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA)
  The γH2AX containing nucleosome reconstitution and BRCTs’ interaction with these nucleosomes were verified on 5% native polyacrylamide gels run in 1× TBE (Tris-borate-EDTA) at 130 V for 2 h in the cold room. To validate BRCT–nucleosome interaction, 0.1 μM of nucleosome was incubated with increasing concentrations of BRCT in EMSA buffer (20 mM Hepes pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl2, and 1 mM TCEP). The gel was stained with SYBR Safe DNA gel stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to visualize DNA bands. Unmodified nucleosome was used as control.


### Microscale thermophoresis (MST)
  For the MST experiments, a concentration series of γH2AX containing nucleosome was prepared using a 1:1 serial dilution in buffer supplemented with 20 mM Hepes (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT (Dithiothreitol), and 0.05% Tween 20. The range of nucleosome concentration were from 3 μM to a final 0.1 nM, over 16 serial diluted Monolith NT.115 premium capillaries (NanoTemper) with 10 μl samples. The BRCT was labeled using his-tag labeling kit RED-tris-NTA second generation. The interaction for MST experiments was initiated by the addition of 10 μl of 20 nM His tag-labeled BRCT. The measurements were performed on a Monolith NT.115 (NanoTemper) using standard capillaries at 25°C with 80% MST power, high LED power. Data were analyzed by MO.Control and MO.Affinity software (NanoTemper). The experiments were performed in triplicate.


### Cryo-EM grid preparation and data processing
  H2AX-NCP/γH2AX-NCP samples with or without BRCTs (3 μl) in the cryo-EM buffer (20 mM Hepes pH 7.5, 50 mM NaCl, and 1 mM MgCl2) at a concentration of 5 μM were applied on 120 s glow discharged (Solarus) Quantifoil R2/2 holey carbon grids (300 copper mesh) and flash frozen in liquid ethane on an FEI Vitrobot, using a 0–5 s blot time and blotting force of 5 at 4°C and 100% humidity. CryoEM data were collected using a Titan Krios G2 (Thermo Fisher) operated at 300 keV, with a Selectris energy filter (Thermo Fisher) equipped with a Falcon 4 direct detector (Thermo Fisher). Movies were acquired in energy filtered mode with an energy slit of 10 kV. Details of data collection parameters are provided in Tables 1 and 2. The zero-tilt collection was collected with EPU (Thermo Scientific), and the tilted collection was performed using Serial EM automated acquisition software. The samples for γH2AX nucleosome–BRCT complexes were crosslinked with 0.05% glutaraldehyde and incubated on ice for 10 min before EM grid preparation. Clear densities for nucleosomes were observed in the complex samples; however, BRCTs’ densities were undetectable, even with cross-linking.

  Cryosparc version 4.2.1 was used for the cryo-EM data processing [49]. Several iterations of 2D and 3D classifications were done to remove junk particles and obtain clean 3D reconstructions (Supplementary Fig. S4). For the W601-145 bp DNA and the core histones, the cryo-EM structure of the Xenopus canonical nucleosome (PDB: 7OHC) [50] was used. The individual residues were altered to correspond to the sequences of human H2A, H2B, and H4 and performed a rigid body fit using ChimeraX [51]. We built the nucleosome model into the map using iterative rounds of model building and local refinement in Coot [52] and global real space refinement in Phenix [53] leading to the final models (Tables 1 and 2). Our canonical γH2AX nucleosome structure was used to build the stack nucleosome model. The N-terminus of H4 linker conformation adjustment was aided by ISOLDE [54]. Graphics were generated in ChimeraX and PyMol [55]. Local resolution was plotted with Resmap [56].


### Reference frame-based parameters for describing NCP and NCP–NCP stacks
  To elucidate the geometrical parameters associated with NCP–NCP stacking, the bottom NCP, referred to as NCP1, was used as the reference (see Supplementary Section A.3.1, Supplementary Fig. S11). The parameters of the second NCP, NCP2, were then calculated relative to NCP1. The geometric parameters defining NCP–NCP stacking include translational parameters: shift (along the X-axis), slide (along the Y-axis), and rise (along the Z-axis); angular parameters: tilt (rotation along the X-axis), roll (rotation along the Y-axis), twist (rotation along the Z-axis), as well as the interplanar angle and dihedral angle. Due to inconsistencies in residue numbering and chain naming, each PDB file was analyzed individually using Python. PyMOL [55] was employed to visualize the axes and planes, enabling the definition of NCP reference frames.


### Equilibrium molecular dynamics simulations
  The BRCTs bound to γH2AX 5-mer peptide (PDB: 2AZM and 3SZM) were used as control systems to assess BRCT stability, named MCPH1-Ser139phH2AX and MDC1-Ser139phH2AX. To test the effect of phosphorylation loss, Ser139ph was mutated to Ser139 using PyMol prior to simulation yielding MCPH1-Ser139H2AX and MDC1-Ser139H2AX, which confirmed H2AX peptide eviction from the BRCT in the absence of phosphorylation. For nucleosome–BRCT simulations, we used our cryo-EM γH2AX-NCP canonical structure with extended histone tails modeled using AlphaFold [57] and added using Coot. These tails were exposed into the solvent prior to simulation.

  Our cryo-EM structures of both H2AX-NCP and γH2AX-NCP revealed clear density for the C-terminal region of H2AX up to Pro118. However, the remaining 24 residues at the C-terminal end of γH2AX lack distinct electron density, indicating they are disordered. This observation aligns with our analysis of all unbound mononucleosome structures available in the PDB as of September 2024. Due to the limited information on the precise orientation of the γH2AX C-terminus, we modeled these 24 residues extending perpendicular to the nucleosome disc. We ensured that the γH2AX C-terminus was positioned away from the DNA gyre to avoid any potential conformation bias during simulation that could result from charge-based interactions between the histone tail and the W601 DNA. A 32 bp dsDNA extension was added to the entry and exit site of the nucleosome. Simulations were performed using CUDA-enabled PMEMD engine of the Graphics Processing Unit (GPU) accelerated Assisted Model Building with Energy Refinement (AMBER) software suite (v20) [58] (see Supplementary Section  A.4).


## Results

### Simplified co-expression and assembly of unmodified and phosphorylated nucleosome core particles
  Different methods of histone purification have been established to date including the refolding method [47, 59], histone chaperone mediated co-expression [60], cell-free co-expression [61], and polycistronic expression [62]. In order to study histone PTMs two methods have been largely used, an Escherichia coli cell-free protein synthesis system [63, 64] and native chemical ligation of single histones purified using the denaturation method [46]. We used the Duet vector system to co-express four histones in E. coli. The octamer is purified using one step Ni-NTA affinity chromatography, followed by size exclusion to obtain monodispersed complexes. These octamers are assembled with W601 DNA to form H2AX-NCPs (Supplementary Fig. S2). We developed a modified NCL method, which does not require denaturation of histones thereby avoiding protein loss due to precipitation during refolding. After Ni-NTA affinity purification of the octamer lacking the C-terminal eight residues of H2AX (ATQASQEY), the γH2AX peptide is attached to the C-terminus of H2AX using the NCL method (Supplementary Fig. S3A). The presence of Ser139ph was confirmed by western blot using γH2AX specific antibody (Supplementary Fig. S3B). The phosphorylated octamer was further purified to homogeneity using size exclusion chromatography and assembled with W601 DNA to form γH2AX-NCP.


### Cryo-EM reveals DNA flexibility in H2AX containing mononucleosomes
  The H2AX variant differs from H2A primarily in the C-terminus in that it has variances in residues from 122 to 130, followed by an additional 13 residues, including the Ser139 critical for phosphorylation (Supplementary Fig. S1A). We used cryo-EM to glean insight into the structures of H2AX-NCP and γH2AX-NCP (Supplementary Fig. S4). Both H2AX-NCP and γH2AX-NCP samples demonstrated preferred orientation on the EM grids (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. S5).

  Two distinct classes of monomers were reconstructed from the H2AX-NCP samples: Class 1: the canonical class at 3.2 Å and Class 2: the unwrapped class at 3.4 Å (Fig. 1B; Supplementary Figs S7 and S8; Table 1; Supplementary Movies S1 and S2). In the mononucleosome reconstructions from the γH2AX-NCP samples, DNA dynamics were observed similar to the unwrapped class resulting in the absence of DNA at one or both entry–exit sites. Based on the observed DNA density, the mononucleosomes from the γH2AX-NCP samples were classified into three types which we have named Class 1: canonical (3.4 Å resolution), Class 2: half-wrapped (3.2 Å resolution), and Class 3: extended (3.6 Å resolution) (Fig. 1B; Supplementary Figs S5, S7, and S8; Supplementary Movies S3 and S4). Except for the H2AX-NCP class 2 (unwrapped class) where parts of H2A and H2B are missing, all other classes contained two copies each of the four core histones (H3, H4, H2A, and H2B), wrapped by the left-handed super helical W601–145 bp dsDNA. The local resolution for the histone core of the canonical structure is higher than the DNA indicating flexibility in γH2AX-NCP in contrast to H2AX-NCP (Supplementary Fig. S7). A slight increase in the NCP diameter was observed in our H2AX-NCP Class 1 and γH2AX-NCP Class 1 structures compared to the previously published crystal structures with H2AX/ γH2AX variants (PDB: 6K1J/6K1I) and other mononucleosome structures from the RCSB repository (see Supplementary Section A.1; Supplementary Fig. S9). Overall, these canonical structures closely resemble each other and align well with the published H2AX-NCP and γH2AX-NCP crystal structures (Supplementary Section A.1; Supplementary Figs S9 and S19). In the canonical classes (Class 1) of H2AX-NCP/γH2AX-NCP, models were built into density for residues Arg12–Lys119 (H2AX/γH2AX), Ser33/Arg34–Ala125 (H2B), Pro39–Glu134 (H3.1), and Asp25–Gly102 (H4) (Supplementary Fig. S9).

  In the H2AX-NCP unwrapped class, one turn of dsDNA (superhelical locations: SHL −7 to SHL +1.5) is observed which is held in place by the H3.1–H4 tetramer (Fig. 1B). The residue ranges for the two protomers were Pro39–Glu134/Leu61–Glu134 (for H3.1) and Asp25–Gly102/Arg24–Phe101 (for H4). Clear density for the H2AX–H2B handshake motif formed by α1–L1–α2–L2–α3 (from H2AX) and α2–L2–α3 (from H2B) is observed on one face of the structure from the unwrapped class (Fig. 1B). The residues Ser57 and Arg87 from H2B form hydrogen bonding interactions with the DNA backbone. On the other face of the structure, a density reminiscent of the handshake motif is observed encompassing α1–L1–α2 (from H2AX) and α2–L2–α3 (from H2B). This portion of the H2AX–H2B handshake motif is held in place primarily due to main chain hydrogen bonding interaction between Arg43/Ile90, Arg43/Ser88, and Gly45/Ile90 (H2AX/H2B). Further H2B interacts with the H4 via hydrogen bonding and salt bridge interactions between side chains of residues: Glu77/Arg93 and Arg100/Thr72 (H2B/H4). No interaction between H2B and the dsDNA is observed on this face.

  The histone core is best resolved in the H2AX-NCP canonical class and γH2AX-NCP half-wrapped class (Fig. 1B). In the half-wrapped class, clear density corresponding to the His40–Arg43 is observed that holds the DNA end (70 position) in place on one end, while the densities for the base pairs (positions: 70 to 56) are missing on the other end (Fig. 1B). In the γH2AX-NCP extended class, the dsDNA on one end is pulled outward and is disordered leading to missing densities for the base pair stretch (positions: 50–70) (Fig. 1B). In this region, the densities for the C-terminal of γH2AX is missing after Leu108 in contrast to the canonical γH2AX-NCP, and the densities for the N-terminal residues of H3.1 are missing prior to Val46. Thus, in the γH2AX-NCP extended class, the dsDNA is extended outwards denoted by partial densities prior to SHL −4.5 (Fig. 1B). Although the density for the DNA base pairing is poor after SHL +5, a comparison with the canonical structure indicates that there is no distortion in DNA winding path at this end.


### Analyzing the preferred orientation of NCPs on EM grids reveals unique NCP–NCP packing arrangements
  Our cryo-EM grids revealed arrays of stacked nucleosomes in both H2AX-NCP and γH2AX-NCP samples, potentially representing a 10-nm chromatin fiber-like arrangement (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. S6). For nomenclature purposes, we named the classes based on the position of the top H2AX/γH2AX-NCP (NCP2) relative to the bottom reference H2AX/γH2AX-NCP (NCP1) (Fig. 1A). For the H2AX-NCP dataset, three distinct classes of stacking were reconstructed: left-offset, parallel, and right-offset. In each case, the stacked nucleosomes interact in a symmetric manner, about a two-fold axis parallel to the individual nucleosomal dyad axis. For the γH2AX-NCP dataset, a single class corresponding to the parallel stack was reconstructed. Our canonical H2AX-NCP/γH2AX-NCP models fit well into the EM densities of each NCP within the stack. The maps and models revealed that the two nucleosomes were stacked with their DNA entry–exit site oriented in the same direction (Fig. 1A). Local resolution estimations were performed on 3.6 Å maps for the left-offset stack and 3.9 Å maps for the parallel stack (Table 1). In the H2AX-NCP left offset and parallel classes, strong EM densities corresponding to NCP–NCP interaction interface were not observed (see Supplementary Section A.2; Fig. 1A; Supplementary Figs S7, S8 and S10). The DNA gyres in H2AX-NCP1 are well-defined in contrast to those in H2AX-NCP2. The less distinct DNA structure in H2AX-NCP2 may result from averaging NCP particles with slight variations in their orientation relative to one another, leading to a lower-resolution 3D volume. Unique interfaces were observed in the right-offset H2AX-NCPs and the parallel stack of γH2AX-NCPs, both reconstructed at a nominal resolution of 3.7 Å (Supplementary Figs S7 and S8; Tables 1 and 2).

  Our refined model reveals that H2AX-NCP1 in the right-offset stack interacts with H2AX-NCP2 at three distinct sites: (i) while continuous density for the H4 N-terminus of H2AX-NCP1 is observed up to Val22, the side chain of Arg24 forms a hydrogen bond with the DNA backbone at DA-60, positioned at SHL-6 of H2AX-NCP2. (ii) Clear EM density defines an interface buried between H2B αC region of H2AX-NCP1 (residues Lys109, His110, Ser113, and Lys117) and the H2B L1 region of H2AX-NCP2 (residues Gln48, His50, Pro51, and Asp52). (iii) Similar EM density is observed between H2B L1 region of H2AX-NCP1 (residues: Gln48–Asp52) and the H2B αC region of H2AX-NCP2 (residues: Lys109, His110, Ser113, and Lys117) (Fig. 2A). In the parallel stack of γH2AX-NCP (Fig. 2B), continuous main chain density for the H4 N-terminus is observed in γH2AX-NCP1, projecting upward to connect with H2B L1 region of γH2AX-NCP2. Specifically, the Arg20 side chain of H4 in γH2AX-NCP1 forms hydrogen bond and salt bridge with Asp49 side chain on H2B L1 region of γH2AX-NCP2. A symmetric interaction occurs where the N-terminus of the H4 tail in γH2AX-NCP2 project downward to contact the H2B L1 region of γH2AX-NCP1. These two modes of interaction, observed in right-offset (H2AX-NCP) and parallel (γH2AX-NCP) stacks, involve distinct EM densities buried at the NCP–NCP interface, which have not been previously reported (Supplementary Movies S5 and S6).


### Analysis of experimental structures reveal plasticity in NCP–NCP stacking
  To gain insight into the structural restrictions associated with internucleosomal packing, we analyzed a comprehensive dataset of 666 NCP structures, available in the Protein Data Bank as of September 2024. After a selection process using RMSD calculations to exclude similar structures, 30 distinct NCP–NCP stacked structures were identified from 15 structures obtained through crystallography and 14 obtained using cryo-EM (see Supplementary Section A.3.2, Supplementary Table S1). This curated list was generated to eliminate redundant identical NCP–NCP stacking poses by systematically superimposing the stacked structures. Importantly, the presence of different histone variants did not influence the overall mode of NCP–NCP stacking observed in our analysis (Supplementary Table S2). Thus, we use the term 'NCP' generically in this section to reflect the global nature of the stacking analysis.

  We developed an NCP-centered coordinate system or reference frame to describe the three-dimensional position of NCP2 relative to the reference nucleosome, NCP1 (Supplementary Fig. S11). A set of nine parameters was analyzed for each NCP–NCP stack: the distance between the centers of mass (COM) of the two NCPs (COM1 and COM2); the translational parameters (shift, slide, and rise); the rotational parameters (tilt, roll, and twist); the dihedral angle and the interplanar angle (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Fig. S12, and Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). The COM1–COM2 distance and rise exhibited a narrow distribution, ranging from 43 to 72 Å and 33 to 69 Å, respectively. Similarly, the NCP–NCP interplanar angle showed a tight distribution within 0 to 27°. The combined effects of these parameters-distances and the interplanar angles played a critical role in determining the overall spatial arrangement of the NCPs in the stack conformation.

  Previously similar analysis was performed on a data set of 147 mononucleosome crystal structures [65]. Both their analysis and ours revealed that mononucleosomes crystallizing in the P212121 space group exhibit an NCP–NCP packing model. This model can be derived using crystallographic 21-symmetry operations between neighbouring NCPs. In this arrangement, the NCP faces are packed against each other, with the dyad of adjacent NCPs oriented in opposite directions associated with large dihedrals as depicted by the PDBs in our curated list (PDB: 1KX5, 3LEL, 4X23, 7XVL, 7XX6, 8Q3M, and 8QKT; Supplementary Figs S12 and S13). However, when a linker histone is present in the crystal structure of the mononucleosome, (e.g. PDB entry: 5WCU), the dyad centers of adjacent NCPs align in the same direction after the 21-symmetry operation, contrasting with the stacking observed in the absence of linker histone. Additionally, we observed that the presence of long linker DNA, as seen in PDB structures 1ZBB, 6LA2, and 6HKT impose a structural constraint. This constraint causes the dyad centers to align in the same direction, resulting in lower dihedral values compared to the symmetry-related NCP stacks reported from crystallographic studies. Notably this alignment of dyad centers in the same direction was also observed in most of the cryo-EM structures from our curated list, including PDB entries: 6L4A, 6L49, 7PEV, 7PF0, 7PFC, 7U47, and the structures reported in this study. Interestingly, NCP stacks formed by one-start left-handed spiral DNA without nucleosomal linkers (PDB entries: 5GSE, 7V96, 7V9K, 8IHL, and 9GD0) exhibit high shift values (25–47 Å) and lower slide values (0 to −14 Å) (Supplementary Fig. S12). It is noteworthy that the negative value of slide as observed in PDB entries: 5GSE, 8IHL, and 9GD0, associated with one-start DNA lacking internucleosomal linkers, correlate with the absence of the H2A–H2B dimer from the NCP2 region that faces the stack interface. The shift values were primarily positive, ranging between 0 and 47 Å, with exception of PDB: 5WCU (−0.93 Å) and the left offset stack reported in this study (−2.45 Å). In contrast, the slide values ranged from −39 to +38 Å (Fig. 3). Negative slide values indicate that the COM2 of NCP2 is positioned to the left of the COM1 of the reference NCP, whereas positive slide values indicate that COM2 is positioned to the right of the reference. This stringent range of rise, slide, and shift values, along with inter-COM distances, interplanar angles, and dihedrals, promotes NCP−NCP stacking and is facilitated by specific histone–DNA and histone–histone interactions favored by virtue of these geometric constraints.


### Variability in NCP−NCP orientations originate from discrete interactions between H4, H2A−H2B, and the DNA
  NCP−NCP stacking prevents access to the acidic patch, regardless of the geometrical constraints involved (see Supplementary Section A.3 for detailed analysis). The NCP−NCP interfaces formed through crystallographic 21-symmetry operations are often stabilized by metal ions, including Mn2+, Mg2+, and Ca2+ (Supplementary Fig. S13). These ions are coordinated by histone residues or DNA bases, with the H4 N-terminal tail interacting with the acidic patch. Most cryo-EM structures reconstructed at low resolution (6–18 Å) do not provide sufficient detail to resolve the defined contacts at the NCP−NCP interface (Supplementary Fig. S14). An exception is the telomeric NCP stacks (PDB entries: 7V96 and 7V9K), where a higher shift combined with a negligible slide value enables the appropriate conformation for observing interfacial densities. In these stacks, adjacent histone tails, including the H3 N-terminus and H2A C-terminus, mediate the NCP1–NCP2 interaction. In contrast, the three cryo-EM stack structures reported in this study show low shift values (0–6 Å) and interplanar angles (2–10°). In our parallel stack (NCP_P), we observe clear densities for the H4 N-terminal tails from NCP1 and NCP2, which contribute to the formation of the interface by contacting H2A–H2B near the acidic patch (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table S1). Our right-offset stack (NCP_R) exhibits a larger negative slide and interplanar angle (30 Å/10°) similar to PDB: 7PFC (17 Å/1°), resulting in an extensive interaction interface. This is facilitated by symmetrical interactions of H2A–H2B dimers from NCP1 and NCP2, as well as by interactions of the H4 N-terminal tail with the DNA around superhelix position -6 (Supplementary Fig. S14). These specific interactions observed in our parallel and right-offset stacks have not been reported previously and contribute to the understanding of the diverse ways in which NCP–NCP interfaces can be stabilized. The left-offset stacking orientation in H2AX-NCP likely involves distinct inter-nucleosomal contacts, however our current density for this structure is of insufficient resolution to determine what these interactions might be.


### BRCTs interact specifically with γH2AX containing NCPs and disrupt NCP–NCP stacking
  Recombinantly purified MCPH1 and MDC1 BRCT domains were tested for their ability to interact with a Ser139ph-containing octapeptide mimicking the C-terminus of γH2AX using fluorescence polarization. Our data align with previous findings, demonstrating that MCPH1 and MDC1 interact with fluorescein-labeled γH2AX peptides [35, 36, 38] (Supplementary Fig. S15) and EMSA studies confirm the phosphorylation-dependence of these interactions (Fig. 4A). We quantified the interactions of BRCTs with γH2AX-containging nucleosomes using MST. MCPH1 and MDC1 interact specifically with γH2AX-NCPs with an affinity of 30 and 33.2 nM, respectively, but not with nonphosphorylated nucleosomes (Fig. 4B). MCPH1 and MDC1 coeluted with γH2AX-NCPs during size exclusion, suggesting the formation of stable complexes only in the presence of γH2AX-NCPs and not H2AX-NCP (Fig. 4C and D). Our in vitro studies conclusively demonstrate that the presence of Ser139ph is essential for recognition by BRCTs of MCPH1 and MDC1.

  We prepared cryo-EM samples of γH2AX-NCP in complex with BRCTs. The particle orientation of the γH2AX-NCP-BRCTs complexes on the grids were strikingly different from those on grids containing only nucleosome (H2AX-NCP/γH2AX-NCP). Grids were prepared under the same particle concentrations and solution conditions as the nucleosome-only samples, and consistent results were observed for several different preparations of either BRCT protein bound to γH2AX-NCP. In presence of BRCTs of either MCPH1 or MDC1, the γH2AX-NCP particles lost their stacked arrangement, as demonstrated by the absence of stacking in the initial rounds of 2D classifications and 3D reconstructions (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. S5). While we successfully reconstructed mononucleosomes, no density corresponding to the BRCTs was observed. For the γH2AX C-terminal tail, electron density was traceable up to Pro118, but the remaining 24 residues which contain the BRCT-binding site, showed no discernible density. It is possible that these small BRCTs (∼25 kDa), when bound to the disordered tail, remain flexible, resulting in an absence of density in our cryo-EM reconstructions.


### Simulation studies indicate that BRCT proteins interact with γH2AX-NCP in a dynamic manner
  Using molecular dynamics, we aimed to determine whether the BRCT domains can dock onto the γH2AX-NCP in specific conformations (see Supplementary Section A.4). We performed simulation of the co-crystal structures of γH2AX peptides bound to either MCPH1 or MDC1 as controls (Supplementary Fig. S1B, inset; Supplementary Fig. S16A and D).

  The BRCT domains were positioned on the γH2AX tails on both faces of the γH2AX-NCPs, ensuring that the initial conformations of the complexes of BRCT bound to γH2AX C-terminal were not identical. Previous reports demonstrated that simulating nucleosomes with 147 bp W601 dsDNA result in DNA unwrapping at the entry–exit sites [66]. To prevent this DNA breathing near the histone core during simulations, we extended the 145bp dsDNA by adding 32 bp overhang at both ends. As anticipated, visual inspection of the simulation trajectories revealed that while the DNA overhangs remained flexible, the nucleosomal core region was stable (Fig. 6, and Supplementary Figs S17 and S18). Previous studies have demonstrated that the DNA overhangs are stable throughout the simulation trajectory in presence of the linker protein H1 which coordinates the inter-nucleosomal region [67]. Each complex was run in duplicate for a total of 2 μs and remained stable throughout the simulation. In the absence of BRCTs, the C-term γH2AX tail makes contact with the DNA primarily at the SHL ± 6 and SHL ± 7 positions. This observation aligns with the previous findings for H2A and H2AZ [68]. Similarly, the disordered tails of H2B, H3, and H4 exhibit a high affinity for the W601 DNA as previously reported [66, 68, 69].

  Throughout the total of 8 μs simulation run for either γH2AX-NCP-MCPH1 or γH2AX-NCP-MDC1, the γH2AX phosphorylated tail remained bound to the BRCT; however, in control simulations with nucleosomes containing unphosphorylated H2AX, the H2AX tails drifted away from their bound conformations during the simulation as expected (Fig. 6; Supplementary Figs S16 and S18; Supplementary Movie S7). During the simulation timescale, the BRCTs on the either faces of the nucleosome discs settle on the nucleosome entry–exit region, while adopting very different orientations depending on the starting conformation (Fig. 6). Taken together, these results indicate that although the interactions of Ser139ph and Tyr142 from γH2AX with the BRCTs are specific, other “secondary interactions” between BRCTs and γH2AX-NCP occur during the microsecond timescale of the simulation. These secondary interactions were not replicated either by the two protomers present on the γH2AX-NCP or by another independent simulation. These observations suggest that the “secondary interactions” are likely nonspecific contacts between BRCTs with the γH2AX-NCPs.


## Discussion
  Gene regulation relies on tightly orchestrated association and dissociation events between NCPs, as well as their interactions with chromatin-binding factors. PTMs on histones are pivotal in regulating close range NCP–NCP interactions thereby controlling chromatin condensations and decondensation. For example, phosphorylation of H3 Thr3 by Haspin kinase, followed by H3 Ser10 phosphorylation by Aurora B kinase coupled with the deacetylation of H4 Lys16 by Hst2p, collectively promote local chromatin compaction [70–72]. Conversely, PTMs such as the acetylation on H4 Lys16 and ubiquitination of H2B Lys120 play direct roles in facilitating chromatin decondensation [73–75]. The phosphorylation of Ser139 on the H2AX variant, resulting in γH2AX formation within NCPs serve as critical signal for chromatin destabilization in response to DSBs [76].


### γH2AX can induce DNA destabilization without altering nucleosome core conformation
  Our H2AX/γH2AX-NCPs, reconstituted using a simplified co-expression method and one-pot NCL approach, reveal nine distinct structural ensembles. Our cryo-EM analysis reveals that phosphorylation at Ser139 does not induce structural alteration in the histone core or the overall architecture of γH2AX-NCP, consistent with the previous findings (PDB: 6K1I) [77]. However, we observe a notable widening of our H2AX/γH2AX-NCPs both along the symmetry axis and the plane perpendicular, compared to crystallographic models (Supplementary Section A.1; Supplementary Figs S9 and S19).

  In our H2AX-NCP (Class 2), γH2AX-NCP (Class 2), and γH2AX-NCP (Class 3), the entry–exit DNA regions exhibit significant disorder. The destabilization of DNA at the entry–exit sites is a fundamental feature that facilitate DNA accessibility, which is crucial of replication, transcription and DNA repair. The DNA destabilization and associated intrinsic structural dynamics of the NCP in particular ‘DNA breathing’, which involves partial unwrapping and rewrapping of DNA, has been well established since 1995 [18] and recently observed using AFM [78, 79] and cryo-EM [80]. The H2AX/γH2AX-NCP models with destabilized DNA is consistent with the previous biochemical reports showing the increased accessibility of DNA to micrococcal nuclease, restriction enzymes, and DNA methylases, compared to canonical H2AX-NCPs [81–83]. Similar disordered DNA ends have been observed in cryo-EM structures of NCPs containing H2A variants, such as H2A.B and H2A.Z.2.2 [84]. When incorporated into NCPs, these variants of H2A bind less DNA than their canonical counterparts, resulting in a more open nucleosome structure (Supplementary Fig. S19). The single gyre of nucleosomal DNA in the H2AX-NCP unwrapped model (Class 2) mirrors the structural signature observed in the intermediate states of RNA polymerase II elongation complex–NCP interactions (PDBs: 6IR9 and 6J4Y, Supplementary Fig. S19) [85].


### γH2AX nucleosome can maintain stacking interactions with adjacent nucleosomes
  We observed H2AX/γH2AX-NCP arrays on cryo-EM grids. While such arrays on the EM grids have been reported, their 3D structures had not been resolved until now [86, 87]. The left offset, parallel, and right offset NCP–NCP stacks presented in this study illustrate distinct interactions of NCPs within the chromatin landscape, offering insights into their local structural arrangement. Previously, cryo-EM structures of chromatin fibers (30 nm) in the presence of linker histone H1 and H5 had shown a zigzag configuration of nucleosomes [88, 89]. In this fiber, the linker histone imposes constraints on both rotation and spacing between adjacent nucleosomes promoting a strong interaction between H2B-helix α1/αC and the adjacent H2A-helix α2 at their interface. Similar interactions involving H2A and H2B histones are observed in our H2AX-NCP right offset stack. In our γH2AX-NCP parallel stack, deciphered in the absence of linker histones, clear main chain densities for the N-termini of the H4 tails. The importance of the H4 tail in stabilizing stacking interactions in physiological condition had been visualized using MD simulations [90] and cryo-EM studies on telomeric tetra-nucleosome [91]. Furthermore, simulation studies indicated that nucleosomes lacking histone tails cannot maintain stack conformation [90], whereas tailed nucleosomes form stacks with an inter-NCP distance of ∼62 Å, consistent with our cryo-EM structures. H2AX-NCP constitute ∼10% of the total H2A in human chromatin. In response to DSB, γH2AX domain can spread across megabase scale regions, leading to local enrichment of γH2AX-NCPs. This enrichment is essential for tethering broken chromosome, sequestering repair factors, and repressing transcription [92]. In such a scenario, H2AX- or γH2AX-NCPs may have an increased chance of encountering one another and transiently forming stacking interactions; however, this is expected to occur locally rather than as extended arrays, such as those observed on cryo-EM grids. Our cryo-EM structures, together with analysis of existing PDB entries, indicate that the mode of NCP-NCP stacking does not depend on the type of histone variant present, including H2AX or γH2AX (Supplementary Table 2).


### DNA damage triggers chromatin relaxation
  The role of chromatin accessibility in response to DNA damage have been extensively studied [93–96]. The intricate 3D organization of chromatin necessitates a dynamic “access-repair-restore” model for DNA repair [97–99]. This model posits that efficient repair requires coordinated chromatin reorganization to provide access to damaged sites. This process of reorganization begins rapidly upon DSB induction with local chromatin expansion [96, 100]. While yH2AX foci preferentially form in euchromatin region due to high H2AX concentration [97], repair in heterochromatin require specific mechanisms. Upon DSB induction in heterochromatin region, ATM phosphorylates the heterochromatin building factor KAP-1 [97, 101–103], leading to ATM- and KAP-1 dependent local and global chromatin relaxation [104], thus facilitating access to the damage site. Another chromatin-based event occurring within seconds to minutes of DSB formation is polyADP-ribosylation (PARylation) of nucleosomes by polyADP-ribose polymerase (PARP) which acts upstream of ATM kinase [105, 106]. PARylation is known to promote chromatin decondensation [107]. Furthermore, chromatin remodelers SMARCA class 2 and 4 (SWI/SNF-related, matrix-associated, actin-dependent regulator chromatin group A) are recruited to DSB early on in an MCPH1-dependent manner, ensuring chromatin relaxation and the propagation of ATM-induced yH2AX [108–110]. Collectively, these studies suggest that chromatin decondensation and NCP unstacking occur prior to or alongside yH2AX formation, positioning the yH2AX nucleosome for interactions with the PTM readers.

  In line with the well documented chromatin decondensation, our studies demonstrate that BRCT interactions with γH2AX-NCPs prevent stack formation on the cryo-EM grids, similar to PARylated NCPs [87]. Likewise, cryo-ET studies of native chromatin in presence of citrullination [11], and cryo-EM studies of NCP bound to the DNA repair factor, RAD51 [111] induces dissociation of NCP stacks. DSB-induced localized alterations in chromatin structure, linked to the activation of the DNA damage response kinase ATM have been previously reported [96, 112] and the phosphorylation of H2AX on serine139 is well documented [113]. In vivo imaging has shown that DNA damage induces chromatin decondensation in yeast [94], with ATP dependent chromatin remodelers playing a critical role in facilitating this decondensation before repair factors are recruited [95, 96]. This explains the disruption of yH2AX NCP stacks in presence of BRCTs.


### Recognition of phosphoserine139 by BRCT domains requires nucleosome unstacking
  Our studies reveal that the distance between the centers of γH2AX-NCPs in a stacked conformation is ∼63 Å. The BRCT domains specifically recognize and bind to phosphoserine 139 and tyrosine 142, located at the terminus of a 22-residue disordered H2AX tail. MD studies by our group and others have indicated that the H2A C-terminal tail in unbound NCPs primarily interacts at the SHL ± 7 position [69, 90]. Theoretically, this 22-residue tail can extend up to ∼77 Å, given that each amino acid spans ∼3.0–3.5 Å. In a stacked conformation, the two yH2AX tails at the NCP–NCP interface are likely subjected to geometric constraints that restrict their accessibility and interaction with BRCT domains and other DNA repair factors. Therefore, from geometrical standpoint, the unstacking of NCPs is likely required for efficient BRCT domain binding and recruitment of downstream repair factors.

  Recent studies have shown that chromatin tethering by MDC1 at the DSB site is mediated by its intrinsically disordered PST region (residues: 1120–1667, N-terminal to BRCT domain), which directly interacts with the acidic patch of yH2AX-NCP [114, 115]. Electrostatic surface analysis of BRCT domains of MCPH1 and MDC1 does not show conserved, positively charged patches that would suggest direct interaction with the negatively charged γH2AX-NCP surface (Supplementary Fig. S18C). This supports the view that BRCT domains primarily recognize the phosphoserine module rather than engaging extensively with the NCP surface.

  Upon recognition of yH2AX via its BRCT domain, MDC1 recruits the E3 ligases [116–118], which ubiquitinates H1 linker histones, further promoting chromatin decondensation [45, 119–121]. These ligases interact with the nucleosomal acidic patch [122–124], which is inaccessible in stacked nucleosomal arrays. These findings suggest that NCP unstacking appears essential for BRCT domain interactions, thereby facilitating chromatin ubiquitination.

  Taken together, our findings highlight the critical role of histone tails, often described as “fuzzy conformational ensembles” [69, 125, 126], in regulating H2AX/yH2AX-NCP condensation and decondensation, processes essential for efficient DSB repair. However, this study is limited by the use of single NCPs with minimal W601 DNA. In native chromatin, NCPs are connected by linker DNA, incorporate linker histones, and form higher-order arrays. This raises key questions: (i) Can yH2AX-NCP form stacked arrangements in the presence of linker histones within chromatin array? (ii) Does the intrinsically disordered PST region constrain the positioning of MDC1 BRCT domain when bound to yH2AX-NCPs in chromatin and are analogous disordered segments found upstream of the C-terminal BRCT domains in MCPH1? Addressing these questions will require future structural studies of chromatin fibres using cryo-EM or cryo-ET to resolve the spatial organization of nucleosomes and their associated DNA repair factors.
