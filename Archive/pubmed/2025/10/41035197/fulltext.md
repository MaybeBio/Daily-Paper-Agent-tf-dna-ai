# PTF-Vāc: An explainable and generative deep co-learning encoder–decoder system for ab initio discovery of plant transcription factor binding sites


## Abstract
  Discovery of transcription factor (TF) binding sites (TFBSs) and their motifs in plants poses significant challenges due to high cross-species variability. The interactions between TFs and their binding sites are highly specific and context dependent. Most existing TFBS-finding tools are not sufficiently accurate to discover these binding sites in plants. They fail to capture cross-species variability, interdependence between TF structures and corresponding TFBSs, and the context specificity of binding. Because they are coupled to a predefined TF-specific model/matrix, they are strongly influenced by the volume and quality of data provided to build the motifs. All these software applications make the assumption that the user input is specific to a particular TF, limiting their use for practical applications such as genomic annotation of newly sequenced species. Here, we report PTF-Vāc, an explainable deep-learning encoder–decoder generative system. PTF-Vāc is based on PTFSpot, a universal model of deep co-learning on variability in binding sites and TF structure, making it completely free from the bottlenecks described above. PTF-Vāc decouples the process of TFBS discovery from the prior step of motif finding and the requirement for TF-specific motif models. Because it is guided by a universal model for TF–DNA interactions, it can discover binding motifs irrespective of data volume and species and without reference to TF-specific models. In a comprehensive benchmarking study across an extremely high volume of experimental data, PTF-Vāc outperformed most advanced motif-finding deep-learning algorithms. PTF-Vāc thus opens a completely new chapter in ab initio TFBS discovery through generative AI.


## Introduction
  Transcription factors (TFs) regulate gene expression at the initial stage of transcription by binding specifically to short DNA sequences in promoter regions. DNA binding sites, which typically consist of approximately 5–20 base pairs (Mitchell and Tjian, 1989; Stewart et al., 2012), act as recognition sites for TFs, enabling them to control the transcriptional machinery (Davidson et al., 2003; Jones and Vandepoele, 2020). Across the genome, these short, repeating, and over-represented sequences define the binding motifs for any given TF. Thus, to decipher the control of gene expression, it is necessary to identify these binding spots or motifs. Recently developed technologies such as chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) (Ho et al., 2011), tiling arrays (ChIP-on-chip) (Waldminghaus and Skarstad, 2010), and next-generation sequencing (ChIP-seq) (Johnson et al., 2007) have enabled direct genome-wide identification of regions bound by any given TF. ChIP experiments enable motif finding for any particular TF, as the regions obtained from ChIPs are much larger than the actual TF binding sites (TFBSs), and their TF specificity works as the guide to decode such binding motifs for any given TF (Zambelli et al., 2013). However, it is not feasible to perform ChIP experiments for all TFs and for every possible condition and species, as the cost and potential search space increase exponentially and almost infinitely. Thus, computational models for TF–DNA interactions have become indispensable.

  At present, almost every TFBS-discovery problem is preceded by a motif-discovery problem, and this has many issues. In the current landscape of motif search, numerous challenges persist, reflecting the intricate nature of deciphering regulatory elements in genomic sequences. TF-specific profiles and position weight matrices (PWMs) are a classic and preferred approach whereby TF binding regions (TFBRs) are identified for possible alignments and used as the basis for developing a scoring matrix. The problem can be formalized as a combinatorial optimization process that involves finding the combination of oligos that build the highest-scoring profile by exploring the search space of all possible combinations. Nearly all combinatorial optimization techniques, such as greedy, local search, stochastic search, and genetic algorithms, have been tried and applied over the years (Bucher, 1990; Fan et al., 2008). The introduction of PWMs brought a quantitative dimension to motif representation, enabling probabilistic assessments of TF binding (Stormo et al., 1982). Subsequent milestones included unsupervised statistical learning and clustering approaches, most popularly Gibbs sampling, expectation maximization, and information-theory-based approaches (Lawrence et al., 1993). The creation of databases such as TRANSFAC and JASPAR provided the first repositories of motifs from experimental binding data (Sandelin et al., 2004; Matys et al., 2006).

  A direct approach is an exhaustive search in which all possible motif candidates are scanned, guaranteeing that the most desirable one will be identified. However, such a direct approach frequently becomes unfeasible because of the exponential nature of the problem, resulting in highly demanding processing needs (Alipanahi et al., 2015; Trabelsi et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2019). On the other hand, statistical models, including tools such as Consensus (Hertz and Stormo, 1999), MEME (Bailey et al., 2009), MEME-ChIP (Ma et al., 2014), and AlignACE (Roth et al., 1998) as prominent examples, use a probabilistic approach for motif identification in DNA sequences. MEME-ChIP integrates three different algorithms to handle shorter and sparser motifs, enabling it to outperform other traditional software tools for TFBS discovery (Ma et al., 2014; Jyoti et al., 2024). These algorithms have some drawbacks despite their effectiveness. Their sensitivity to user-defined parameters is one such drawback; poor decisions can affect the precision and effectiveness of motif recognition (Bailey et al., 2009). Furthermore, as demonstrated by exploration–exploitation trade-off difficulties, these algorithms may have trouble with local optima, producing results that are less than ideal (Ma et al., 2014; Hashim et al., 2019). An additional issue is that their solutions have limited biological interpretability (Kato and Tsunoda, 2007).

  The rise of machine-learning (ML) witnessed its application to motif discovery with tools such as SVMotif (Kon et al., 2007), which was developed to distinguish between TFBSs and non-binding sites using support vectors. Because of its adaptability, SVMotif was able to incorporate a variety of biological information and anticipate binding sites in sequences that had not yet been identified. However, the shortcomings of such ML approaches include their reliance on labeled data, sensitivity to parameters, limited biological interpretability, possible scaling problems for large genomic datasets, and inability to understand more complex and hidden features. They rely exclusively on the input data features supplied by the user and do not attempt to extract more information or hidden relationships. As a result, the success of an ML model depends heavily upon the expertise and insight of its developer. Advanced ML approaches, notably exemplified by the arrival of DeepBind in 2015 (Alipanahi et al., 2015), represented a paradigm shift toward leveraging deep learning (DL) for motif finding. DeepBind looks for more comprehensive sequence contexts owing to its emphasis on sequence information. It may need to be trained for use with data from different organisms because of its innate dependency upon species-specific training. In DL-based tools, the motif-discovery stage is closely linked to the TFBR-discovery stage. As a result, the accuracy of motif discovery is heavily influenced by the amount of noise and false identifications encountered during the earlier stages. Nonetheless, DeepBind is currently the backbone philosophy for most TFBS-discovery tools that use DL.

  Variability in motif length and composition, together with context-dependent functionality, presents challenges to the accurate identification of relevant motifs and binding sites. Addressing noisy data and distinguishing genuine motifs from false positives is essential (Ching et al., 2018). Furthermore, ab initio motif discovery remains a complex problem, requiring algorithms capable of uncovering unknown patterns without prior knowledge (Bailey and Gribskov, 1998). Amid this all, there stands a large lacuna: complete ignorance toward the stake of TF structure and its co-variability with the binding site. Very recently, our group performed a comprehensive review and assessment of these software tools, addressing their shortcomings and proposing ideas for improvement (Jyoti et al., 2024). The most common and gravest issue was their inability to address cross-species variation, rendering them useful only for model species. As highlighted in Jyoti et al. (2024), plants exhibit significant genetic variability across their genomes, including variability in repeats, promoters, and TFs. Unlike animal genomes, whose GC content and repeat types are relatively stable, plant genomes show substantial variability. This variability affects TFBSs, making them more variable in plants than in animals and causing difficulties in addressing cross-species variations in plants and providing credible results (Kielbasa et al., 2005; Shiu et al., 2005; de Mendoza et al., 2013; Lehti-Shiu et al., 2017; Bao et al., 2019; Gupta et al., 2024; Jyoti et al., 2024).

  In light of these issues, we had previously developed PTFSpot, a novel approach (Gupta et al., 2024) that learns from the interdependent variability of TF structures and binding regions to identify the most potential binding regions for any TF, completely independently of any TF- or species-specific model (Gupta et al., 2024). The universal TF–DNA interaction model of PTFSpot makes it highly capable of determining the most potential binding regions for even unseen TFs and species. However, PTFSpot proposes a region of ∼150–162 bases and mimics DNA affinity purification sequencing (DAP-seq)-like signals for most potential regions to look for the precise binding sites of any given TF. Unlike ChIP-seq, PTFSpot is not affected by limitations of experimental conditions. In turn, such proposed regions become a highly reliable guide on which the precise locations of TFBSs can be identified, and they can be used to build highly reliable motifs for any given TF, if the same structural and binding-site variation co-learning is carried forward to a DL encoder–decoder system in a generative manner. The very same approach has been implemented in the present work through the development of PTF-Vāc software. PTF-Vāc leverages the highly refined information generated by PTFSpot to translate the longer binding regions proposed by PTFSpot into the most informative sites, the potential binding sequence/motif components, using a Transformer encoder–decoder for speech translation. In addition, it applies interpretability using Gradient Weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) (Selvaraju et al., 2017) to support the proposed motifs. Unlike any existing software, PTF-Vāc is an autogenerative DL system that decodes the precise binding sites of any TF. The outcomes are groundbreaking, as PTF-Vāc consistently delivered the best test performance, whether given extensive data or just a single sequence, placing it far ahead of existing software tools designed for the same purpose. It has successfully decoupled the problem of motif finding and binding-site discovery while working on plant-specific systems.


## Results and discussion

### Deep co-learning by the Transformer–DenseNet system on words and structure ensures consistently good performance
  Plant genomes are extremely complex and highly dynamic, with greater diversity and complexity of repetitive sequences than animal genomes, which tend to have more stable repeat content and composition (Kielbasa et al., 2005; Shiu et al., 2005; de Mendoza et al., 2013; Lehti-Shiu et al., 2017; Bao et al., 2019; Gupta et al., 2024; Jyoti et al., 2024). This variability in plant genomes means that the binding regions and motifs of a given TF may differ significantly between species, as highlighted in the recent studies mentioned above. These reports have brought attention to the prevalent underperformance of the majority of existing TFBS discovery categories during TFBS annotations, which often result in a high incidence of false positives (Jyoti et al., 2024). A potential solution lies in understanding the variations in TF structures and their corresponding binding sites. These concerns were recently raised and addressed by our group through the development of PTFSpot software (Gupta et al., 2024).

  TFBR discovery is closely tied to motif discovery, relying on precise TF-specific information from binding experiments such as ChIP-seq. These methods assume that input sequences bind to a predefined TF, but they have several limitations: (1) non-specific sequences can introduce noise; (2) it is impractical to obtain TF-specific sequences experimentally owing to high costs and vast search spaces across species; and (3) performance is highly dependent on data volume, becoming weaker with fewer sequences and overly noisy with large datasets owing to the inclusion of non-binding motifs. Moreover, these approaches are ineffective for genome-scale scanning, especially in cases where no predefined guide exists, such as novel genomes. In such a scenario, the TFBR proposed by PTFSpot becomes a highly accurate guide, on which suitable further learning can be performed, and DL decoders may be used to identify precise TFBS motif candidates. In the PTF-Vāc implementation, as outlined in the methods section, the Transformer and DenseNet systems simultaneously acquired knowledge about the TFBS, considering both the sequence contexts and the protein structure of the TF. Taking these considerations into account, dataset E from PTFSpot was retrieved, focusing solely on positive instances, as the problem in the present work was not classification but text generation (binding-site generation). The associated 3D protein structure for each TF was also considered. The Transformer–DenseNet system underwent training and testing on a dataset comprising 48 000 instances of binding regions for 40 selected TFs, using a 70:30 training/testing split ratio with no overlap. The raised model achieved an accuracy of 92.76% on the test dataset.

  A very interesting question then arises as to which features made the greatest contribution to the observed performance. An ablation analysis was performed, evaluating the performance of one after another of the considered properties, both individually and in combination. A total of six distinct sequence-derived word representations were used. The results indicated a clear trend whereby accuracy increased significantly as more complex representations were introduced: dimeric representation resulted in 32.56% accuracy, trimeric representation in 46.1% accuracy, tetrameric representation in 59.51% accuracy, and pentameric representation in 76.49% accuracy. This progression continued with hexameric and heptameric representations, which resulted in accuracy values of 80.2% and 83.77%, respectively. It was evident from these findings that the contribution of higher-order words improved performance mainly because of an increase in vocabulary size due to the larger number of representational words. For instance, the pairing of pentameric and hexameric representations (totaling 311 words) resulted in a significant increase in accuracy to 89.97%. The best illustration of the additive benefits of combining representations occurred when pentameric, hexameric, and heptameric representations were incorporated, resulting in an accuracy of 92.76% with a total of 465 representative words (Supplemental Table 1, sheet 1). These results emphasize the necessity for information sharing among different representations, as this approach harnesses their unique attributes to create a better model.

  The hyperparameters of the aforementioned model were optimized to refine the model further, yielding an accuracy of 93.2% on the test dataset. An exploration into the performance numbers of encoders and decoders for the Transformer–DenseNet system revealed that the greatest improvement occurred at the sixth encoder–decoder layer, with performance stabilizing through the eighth layer before declining. As a result, the system was implemented with six encoder–decoder pairs. The final processing for text generation was handled by the decoder’s feedforward layer. The final hyperparameters of the output layer of the optimized model were: {“Activation function”: softmax, “Loss function”: cross-entropy, “Optimizer”: Adam, “learning rate”: 0.0001, “Epochs”: 50, “Batch size”: 6}. ### The identified binding sites display significant enrichment and match experimentally reported motifs
  The next step was to determine whether the binding sites identified by PTF-Vāc for any given TF were enriched in experimentally determined sites for that TF and to assess their similarity to the experimentally reported motifs. There is far less experimental information on TFs, their binding sites, and their interactions in plants than in humans. Experimentally validated motifs and matrices are available for only a few plant TFs in a very limited number of species (Jin et al., 2017). Because of these constraints, the present comparison was restricted to 30 TFs (DAP-seq) from Arabidopsis thaliana and six TFs with ChIP-seq data (ARR1, FHY3, LEC2, LHY3, and KAN1 from A. thaliana and GLYMA.08G357600 from Glycine max) that had experimentally reported motifs at JASPAR (Supplemental Table 1, sheet 2). The six TFs belonged to families that had not been used during training of the model. These reported motifs ranged from 8 to 15 bases. The complexity of plant genomes, species diversity, and the resource-intensive nature of experimental validation are just a few of the obstacles that may make data accessibility a limiting factor in the context of plant genomics. It is important to note that the experimentally retrieved motifs for these 36 TFs were not part of either the training or testing datasets.

  To ensure a uniform motif-search approach for a fair comparison, these motifs underwent scanning using a methodology similar to that used in the PTFSpot approach (Gupta et al., 2024). Subsequently, the binding sites reported by PTF-Vāc were evaluated for statistically significant enrichment in the corresponding binding data for the given TF. The identified motifs for all the studied TFs were significantly enriched (binomial test p value << 0.01) for their respective binding-region data. The motifs generated by PTF-Vāc covered at least 83.26% and as much as 100% of the binding data (Supplemental Table 1, sheet 3).

  The resemblance between experimentally reported motifs and their corresponding matching motifs from PTF-Vāc was assessed using TOMTOM, a part of the MEME suite (Bailey et al., 2009). This comparison aimed to assess the degree of similarity and consistency between motifs identified through experimental techniques and those obtained through PTF-Vāc. All motifs obtained from PTF-Vāc for the 36 TFs matched the experimentally reported motifs from JASPAR (TOMTOM p value << 0.01) (Supplemental Table 1, sheet 3). One hundred percent similarity between the experimentally reported motifs and the PTF-Vāc-generated motifs was observed for ANAC046, ANAC055, ANAC083, ANAC092, ARR1, BIM2, bZIP68, ERF13, GATA15, WRKY18, WRKY30, WRKY45, and WRKY75. The consensus motif T[TC]GCCGAC (for RAP2-10) was the least abundant among the PTF-Vāc motifs, covering 83.26% of the bound data. Similarly, the consensus motifs [AG][AG]AT[AG]CGCAA[TA], [TC]AAT[CA]ATT, [AC]GAATCT[ACGT], and [TC]CAACGC[TG] were the most abundant motifs, covering 100% of the bound data for ANAC083, ATHB6, ARR1, and WRKY30, respectively. Supplemental Table 1, sheet 4, provides a detailed comparison between motifs reported in this study and their experimentally reported counterparts. This portion of the study provided additional support for the PTF-Vāc methodology and its identified TFBS motifs.


### Explainable deep learning using Grad-CAM gives insight into properties that determine the TF–DNA interaction
  For the implementation of Grad-CAM in PTF-Vāc, we chose the normalization layer after the multi-head attention (MHA) layer as the layer of interest, as described in the methods section (Figure 1). This layer contains distribution maps for various sequence k-mers. Following the weighting method proposed by Grad-CAM (Selvaraju et al., 2017), we quantified the importance of these sequence k-mers and computed a weighted summation of all the distribution maps. This aggregated map identifies key k-mers crucial for binding activities. We extracted 5-mers, 6-mers, and 7-mers from the positive sequences. Each unique k-mer was assessed to determine whether it showed enrichment among the highest average Grad-CAM scores associated with each TF. Our results using the built models recapitulated multiple known trends. For example, the top scoring k-mers for any given TF often closely matched the reported core motif of that TF.

  Next, we determined how accurately Grad-CAM captured the contextual relationship to the binding site for a TF. We selected the 30 TFs (DAP-seq) on which enrichment benchmarking was performed in this study, as discussed above. The top-scoring k-mers for the 30 TFs often closely matched the motifs reported by the PTF-Vāc encoder–decoder (Supplemental Table 1, sheet 5). WRKY18 showed the highest agreement (84.94%) between the PTF-Vāc encoder–decoder-generated motif and the Grad-CAM highest-scoring region, whereas ANAC046 showed the lowest agreement (60.82%). Therefore, disagreement between the Grad-CAM-supported highest-scoring regions and the encoder–decoder-proposed scoring motifs ranged between ∼15% and 40%.

  It was necessary to locate these disagreeing results and check whether they agreed at lower-ranked regions proposed by the encoder–decoder. Because the encoder–decoder generates single TFBS motif candidates, whereas Grad-CAM provides scoring for every position, we looked in the Grad-CAM scoring ranks for the regions identified as TFBS motifs by the encoder–decoder. It was found that the encoder–decoder-proposed TFBS motifs ranked within 92.67% of the top 10 ranks of Grad-CAM scores (Supplemental Table 1, sheet 6). Thus, Grad-CAM interpretability emerged as a strong confidence measure and scoring for the proposed TFBS motifs. In addition, the Grad-CAM score also provides ways to look into other promising TFBS motif regions. We next compared the remaining top-importance scoring profiles that did not match motifs identified by PTF-Vāc with the experimentally reported motifs from JASPAR. Good overlap between the high-importance scoring regions and the experimentally validated motifs was observed for all the remaining cases (values ranging from 35.47% to 97.02%) (Supplemental Table 1, sheet 5).

### Comparative benchmarking and performance comparison
  To obtain a final picture of the performance improvement offered by PTF-Vāc relative to existing state-of-the-art methods, it was necessary to compare PTF-Vāc with the most advanced software tools for objective benchmarking. All existing tools identify motifs from sequences obtained in binding experiments for a specific TF or require a guide, and it is assumed that the user provides all sequences obtained from the binding data for a specific TF. If any departure from this assumption is made, all these tools fail to generate quality motifs owing to noise in their input data. As mentioned previously, all existing methods perform motif discovery as a prelude to binding-site discovery, and the aforementioned issue prevails for all of them. The same factor also limits their cross-species use in plants, which show considerable variation in binding sites and TF structures (Gupta et al., 2024; Jyoti et al., 2024). This is also the reason why almost none of the existing methods can credibly work for major jobs such as genomic annotation of TFBSs for any newly sequenced genome, as none of them can work without a predefined motif matrix or PWM, which they use for scanning. Expecting such motifs from a novel genome-wide scan itself is a point of error, considering the variability across plant species, as pointed out recently (Gupta et al., 2024; Jyoti et al., 2024). A possible solution is ab initio binding-site discovery, which PTF-Vāc has achieved using the universal binding model of PTFSpot. It has learned the relationship between variability in binding sites, TF structure, and context, making it free from any dependence on predefined motifs and matrices for any possible TF and species. Thus, PTF-Vāc, unlike existing tools, can identify binding sites ab initio for a single input sequence on the same level as it can for multiple sequences. We compared PTF-Vāc with four recently published DL-based tools, namely deepRAM (Trabelsi et al., 2019), DESSO (Yang et al., 2019), TF-MoDISco (Shrikumar et al., 2018), and SeqConv (Shen et al., 2021), for different performance parameters: (1) influence of the volume of input sequence data on binding-site discovery, (2) overlap and similarity with experimentally reported motifs, and (3) coverage of overall binding data for any given TF.


#### deepRAM and SeqConv
  deepRAM uses convolutional filters for feature extraction in motif identification, with a focus on local pattern recognition. On the other hand, SeqConv learns motifs from a deep convolutional neural network model by extracting active sequences in the rectification layer, yielding a 24-bp subsequence with the maximum convolution value for each sequence. However, the emphasis on individual filters may lead to redundant motif sequences and limited capture of distributed motif representations.


#### DESSO
  DESSO uses convolutional filters to identify low-level features, followed by max pooling and fully connected layers for high-level feature extraction. Motif detectors learn patterns from the DNA sequences and background sequences, which are selected to eliminate biases.


#### TF-MoDISco
  TF-MoDISco leverages contribution scores to assess positional importance, offering insights into the significance of individual bases. It identifies high-importance windows called “seqlets,” clustering recurring similar “seqlets” into motifs after post-processing steps. Although per-base importance scores aid in incorporating information from all network neurons, potential limitations arise when the model assigns disproportionately high importance to specific positions on the basis of prevalent motifs in the training data. This can lead to biases and potentially result in less frequent but biologically relevant motifs being overlooked.

  The four tools discussed above generate a PWM to identify motifs on the basis of learned patterns in the input data. However, when these tools encounter noisy data during the learning process, they tend to produce ineffective motifs. The reliance on PWMs for motif prediction can lead to challenges in accurately discerning meaningful motifs. This emphasizes the importance of robust algorithms and noise-resistant strategies for the motif-prediction process. To produce an unbiased performance comparison for the tested methods, we cross-referenced the identified motifs with experimentally validated motifs obtained from the JASPAR database. We used available DAP-seq data for the 30 DAP-seq TFs from A. thaliana and six ChIP-seq TFs to scan and report their TFBS motifs across these sequences (Supplemental Table 1, sheet 2). As mentioned above, the experimentally retrieved motifs for the 36 TFs were not part of the training or testing datasets used for model building and evaluation.


### Influence of input dataset size on TF binding-site discovery
  In the first phase of benchmarking, we performed an in-depth assessment of five software suites (deepRAM, DESSO, SeqConv, TF-MoDISco, and PTF-Vāc) to measure the influence of input sequence data on binding-site discovery. Input datasets with different numbers of sequences were constructed from the DAP-seq and ChIP-seq datasets for all TFs. In this way, the input ranged from one sequence to the whole dataset. For the majority of TFs, deepRAM needed at least 50 sequences for motif identification, DESSO and SeqConv required at least 100 sequences, and TF-MoDISco needed more than 200 sequences. Only PTF-Vāc discovered the binding sites irrespective of the number of input sequences. Moreover, deepRAM, DESSO, and TF-MoDISco often produced multiple motifs for a given TF as the volume of input sequences increased, whereas SeqConv and PTF-Vāc consistently proposed a single TFBS motif, irrespective of input volume. Figure 2B provides a snapshot of the performance for WRKY45 TF datasets of various sizes, clearly showcasing the ability of PTF-Vāc to discover motifs regardless of input data size, in contrast to the other software. Detailed results of this analysis are presented in Supplemental Table 1, sheet 7.


### Exploring similarity and overlap in TF binding-site discovery
  In the second phase of benchmarking, we rigorously assessed the overlap and similarity between the motifs discovered by various software tools and the experimentally reported motifs for the 30 TFs (DAP-seq data) and six TFs (ChIP-seq data) mentioned above. Using TOMTOM (Bailey et al., 2009), we evaluated the resemblance between experimentally reported motifs and their corresponding motifs discovered by each software suite. Notably, all the motifs identified by PTF-Vāc for the 36 TFs exhibited a significant match with experimentally reported motifs (TOMTOM p value << 0.01) (Supplemental Table 1, sheet 3). One hundred percent similarity was observed between PTF-Vāc motifs and experimentally reported motifs for 13 TFs. ### Evaluating binding-data coverage for each TF
  In the third phase of benchmarking, we performed an exhaustive assessment of overall experimental binding-data coverage by the TFBS motifs identified for each TF. PTF-Vāc exhibited the highest data coverage for the majority of TFs, with at least 83.26% data coverage, which rose to 100% coverage of the binding data for some TFs (ANAC083, ARR1, ATHB6, and WRKY30) (Supplemental Table 1, sheet 4). PTF-Vāc and deepRAM were the only tools that discovered TFBS motifs for all 36 TFs. DL approaches for motif finding would benefit significantly from incorporating biologically relevant information rather than focusing primarily on statistical and enrichment analyses (Jyoti et al., 2024). PTF-Vāc stands out as the leading solution for discovering relevant TFBS motifs in plants, far surpassing the second-best option, DESSO. The strong performance of DESSO can be attributed to its integration of biologically significant DNA shape features through DL, which are overlooked by the probabilistic models in other tools. Thus, even for binding-data coverage, PTF-Vāc emerged as the best software.

### The curious case of TF splice variants and their different binding preferences
  We next addressed the very interesting topic of identifying binding sites of TF splice variants, a question that has received little attention in plants. ARF8 has two splice variants in A. thaliana, but its binding motif has been defined for only one variant. None of the existing software tools has a binding model for this TF, nor is sufficient experimental binding data available with which to detect motifs using traditional approaches. This holds true for both of its splice variants. One completely misses out on the binding site detection for the second splice variant of ARF8, as there is insufficient binding data to construct its model. Because existing tools rely on traditional approaches to motif detection, they too are unable to identify the specific binding sites of splice variants, even within the same species. Given the limitations of existing software tools, we are far from understanding how the splice variants of any TF function and create a spatiotemporal regulatory system for plant growth and development. The case of ARF8 discussed here very clearly underlines this point.

  To evaluate the efficacy of PTF-Vāc in such a situation, we retrieved the experimental data from Ghelli et al. (2018), in which the two splice variants of ARF8 exhibited distinct tissue-specific functional roles. ChIP–qPCR results demonstrated that in one scenario, ARF8.2 regulated the IAA19 gene, thereby influencing the auxin pathway, whereas under different conditions (later stages of flower development), ARF8.4 regulated both IAA19 and MYB26, controlling tissue elongation and lignification. To assess the ability of PTF-Vāc to detect differences in the binding sites of the two splice variants, we first evaluated the structural similarity of the two proteins by superimposing ARF8.2 and ARF8.4, revealing a significant structural difference with an RMSD of 1.2 Å. Subsequent analysis with PTF-Vāc (Figure 5) identified the same two experimentally reported genes, IAA19 and MYB26, as targets of the ARF8 variants. The results indicated that ARF8.2 bound specifically to the promoter region of IAA19, whereas ARF8.4 bound to the promoters of both IAA19 and MYB26. The binding sites of the two splice variants also differed, substantiating the foundational assumption of the PTF-Vāc algorithm that structural changes in TFs lead to changes in their DNA binding sites. ### Genome-scale analysis of TF binding sites in the tea genome using PTF-Vāc
  To demonstrate the application of PTF-Vāc to genome-wide analyses, we used it to identify TF binding sites across the entire genome of tea (Camellia sinensis), selecting BES1 as the TF of interest. BES1 was chosen because it has been characterized by ChIP-seq in tea (NCBI project ID: PRJNA1206779), thus providing a dataset for validation, although the full range of its target genes in tea remains to be explored. PTF-Vāc analysis focused on the 2-kb upstream promoter regions of all genes in the tea genome, as these regions are critical for TF-mediated gene regulation. PTF-Vāc was run using the 3D structure of BES1 generated by AlphaFold2 and identified 1878 genes as potential targets of BES1.


#### Validation with ChIP-seq data
  To assess the accuracy of PTF-Vāc, we obtained and preprocessed ChIP-seq data for BES1. Analysis of the ChIP-seq peaks revealed that 539 genes were directly targeted by BES1. Comparing these with the PTF-Vāc identifications, we found substantial overlap: 519 of the 539 target genes identified by ChIP-seq (98%) were also identified by PTF-Vāc. This high concordance underscores the reliability of PTF-Vāc in identifying biologically relevant TF binding sites on a genome-wide scale, even for a completely unknown species and its TF.


#### Motif discovery and comparison
  We performed motif analysis of both the PTF-Vāc-derived and ChIP-seq-derived binding sites. The enriched motifs were compared using TOMTOM, yielding a very significant score (p value << 0.01), which indicated high similarity and statistical significance between the motifs identified by the two methods (Supplemental Figure 1). The motif described in the original work (Liu et al., 2025) was identified from the ChIP-seq binding data using MEME. The motif identified here by genome-wide PTF-Vāc analysis matched significantly with the previously reported motif. However, the free energy of binding, which is indicative of binding stability, was much more negative for the PTF-Vāc motif than for the MEME-derived motif. This analysis again demonstrates the ability of PTF-Vāc to perform genome-wide identification of binding sites for any TF in any species.


#### Biological relevance of BES1 target genes
  To investigate the biological significance of the 1878 BES1-targeted genes identified by PTF-Vāc, we performed functional enrichment analysis using the Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway databases. The results, visualized in Supplemental Figure 2 and Supplemental Table 3, revealed significant enrichment in stress response pathways. Specifically, GO analysis highlighted terms such as “response to drought stress” (false discovery rate = 1e−22) and “response to salt stress” (false discovery rate = 1e−16). KEGG analysis also identified pathways related to abiotic stress signaling, such as the MAPK signaling pathway, consistent with the GO findings.

  These results are consistent with the known roles of Arabidopsis BES1 homologs, such as BZR1 and BEH4, which regulate stress responses. For instance, BZR1 has been shown to enhance drought tolerance by modulating stomatal closure and stress-responsive gene expression (Cui et al., 2019), and BEH4 contributes to salt-stress tolerance by regulating ion homeostasis (Feng et al., 2023). Our findings suggest that BES1 plays a role in mediating abiotic stress responses in tea, offering novel insights into its regulatory functions in this economically important crop, made possible by a PTF-Vāc genome-wide scan. The entire genome-scale analysis of BES1 in tea is hosted on our web server platform (https://scbb.ihbt.res.in/PTF-Vac/Application/).


### Web server implementation
  The PTF-Vāc server hosts the software in a user-friendly manner for identification of TFBSs in plant DNA sequences. For TFBS identification, users are required to upload a genomic DNA sequence in FASTA format and the 3D structure of the associated TF in “.pdb” format generated by AlphaFold2. Although experimentally derived 3D structures are unavailable for most plant TFs, PTF-Vāc needs only relative structural information; precise structural information is not necessary, but a uniform source is important. PTF-Vāc, unlike existing tools, is capable of discovering binding sites irrespective of the number of input sequences, performing well even with a single input sequence. It remains immune to noise in the input data. Upon uploading the PDB file, the user can instantly visualize it on the home page. Once the user submits the data, the backend processing begins with PTFSpot, which initially identifies potential TFBRs specific to the given TF within the uploaded DNA sequences. These identified binding regions are then passed to the core functionality of PTF-Vāc to generate actual TFBS motifs. The results are presented in a comprehensive and interactive format on a dedicated results page. Users can also download the results in a tabular format, facilitating further downstream analysis and integration with other tools. The output of PTF-Vāc for a given input sequence is the most informative site—the most potential binding sequence/motif. For scenarios involving multiple DNA sequences, PTF-Vāc provides features such as a motif logo, which is a graphical representation of the identified TFBS; PWMs; and consensus representations, which can be used and compared. The results page also provides an explainability scoring plot in the form of a Grad-CAM score and highlights the generated motif regions in the sequence.

  Grad-CAM helps users understand which sequence-context features have the strongest effect on the binding status for each motif. It identifies significant k-mers that are crucial for binding activities, and the top-scoring k-mers often closely match the reported core motif of the TF. The Grad-CAM interpretability serves as a strong confidence measure for the proposed TFBS motifs, providing users with additional independent confidence and insights into other potentially important regions found to be critical for TF binding. Another important feature of PTF-Vāc is its integration with TOMTOM in the backend. This allows users to compare the PWM of the PTF-Vāc-generated motif with their own PWM of choice, enabling a comparative analysis of binding motifs across different datasets or experimental conditions. Such functionality enhances the flexibility and utility of PTF-Vāc in diverse research contexts.

  To enhance the utility of the web server for genome-scale studies, it has been integrated with preloaded annotated genomes in GFF3 format, including annotations of gene promoter regions (e.g., ±2 kb from the transcription start site [TSS]). At present, the server contains the genomes of four model species (A. thaliana, Oryza sativa, G. max, and Z. mays) for which complete genomes and corresponding AlphaFold2-derived PDB structures are available. In the future, more genomes may be integrated, depending upon their completion and on the availability/generation of corresponding PDB structures.

  The server results page also provides additional filters and scoring schemes. The implemented scoring scheme assigns a binding-site confidence score based on the Grad-CAM-based importance score profiles to identify the sequence-context features that have the strongest effect on the binding status for each motif. ### Limitations of PTF-Vāc
  (1)Requirement for 3D protein structures of TFs. The tool leverages AlphaFold2 3D structures because experimental 3D structures are unavailable for most plant TFs. A reliable 3D structure for the specific TF is a mandatory input if PTF-Vāc is to perform at its best. Generating a 3D structure using AlphaFold2 may consume some additional time.(2)Limited to plant TFs. Although PTF-Vāc is a universal model for TF–DNA interactions, its development, training, and extensive benchmarking studies have been focused exclusively on plant TFs and plant genomes. The specific challenges it addresses, such as the high cross-species variability and context dependence of TF–DNA interactions in plants, are particular to this biological domain. Therefore, the proven utility and performance of PTF-Vāc are restricted to the plant kingdom. The basic strategy of PTF-Vāc could nonetheless be extended further to the animal kingdom.(3)Condition-specific TF binding. Condition-specific TF binding refers to the phenomenon whereby TFs bind to DNA in a cell-type-, tissue-, developmental-stage-, or stimulus-dependent manner. This dynamic binding enables cells to precisely regulate gene expression in response to internal and external signals. PTF-Vāc can report the most potent binding sites for any given TF, but it does not provide information on condition-specific binding. Condition-specific binding is a highly multi-factorial, dynamic-network-based phenomenon that poses a completely different set of problems to be solved.

  In summary, cross-species variability is a pronounced feature of plant genomes, duly reflected across various components, including TFs and their binding sites. Thus, the traditional practice of using TFBS models and matrices developed for model species to identify TFBSs in other species is flawed and a major source of misinformation. Most existing tools are coupled to the process of defining a TF-specific binding matrix/motif/model using experimental binding data for a given TF in a selected species as their first step. As a result, they do not address cross-species variability and are not able to differentiate the binding preferences of TF splice variants within the same species. The present work describes an innovative approach, PTF-Vāc, which has learned the co-variabilities of TF structure and associated binding-site preferences while using the universal TF–DNA interaction model of PTFSpot. PTF-Vāc uses DL encoder–decoder generative systems to identify binding sites in any given DNA sequence in a completely ab initio manner. Its output is also supported by a DL-interpretability scheme. These features eliminate the shortcomings of existing software tools and the dependence on predefined TF-specific models and motifs. Thus, PTF-Vāc can accurately identify the binding sites of even completely novel TFs and across newly sequenced genomes, despite a total lack of TF information or any prior information. It promises to have a profound impact on plant science and regulatory research because, with PTF-Vāc, one does not need to perform binding experiments to generate binding data and identify binding motifs, then run them for whole-genome scanning. It is also no longer necessary to use TFBS models from one species to identify binding preferences in another species—a traditional but erroneous practice. Even the different binding preferences of TF splice variants within the same species can be detected accurately. Only a simple run on the PTF-Vāc server/standalone with user-provided DNA sequences and an AlphaFold2-derived 3D protein structure is needed to identify corresponding TF binding sites across a given genomic sequence.


## Methods

### Dataset retrieval
  To develop a universal and generalized model for the identification of TF binding sites, we retrieved data for A. thaliana from our previous work (Gupta et al., 2024), in which we created six different datasets using ChIP/DAP-seq. Positive instances of binding regions for any TF and the corresponding 3D TF protein structure were extracted from dataset E while maintaining the methodology introduced by PTFSpot (Gupta et al., 2024). A total of 40 TFs representing 40 different families were considered for model training. By considering this subset of TFs, we could effectively represent the entire set of 325 TFs in dataset E without imposing excessive computational demands. In this dataset, each TF represented a unique TF family, and we selected the TFs with the highest binding data within their respective families. This focused selection aimed to overcome the challenges associated with data handling and computational resource requirements. The TF structures were derived using AlphaFold2 (Read et al., 2023), as experimental 3D structures of most plant TFs are unavailable and will remain so for a long time. Also, in terms of structural variability, deriving all TF structures using a common approach serves the purpose of learning relative changes. The dataset was split in a 70:30 ratio for training and testing purposes.


### Transformer–DenseNet system
  The 3D structure of a protein plays a crucial role in determining its DNA binding sites, much like a lock and key. In our approach, structural information on TFs for each of the corresponding binding regions extracted from the DAP-seq data was integrated. A DenseNet model based on 3D protein structure was raised with a Transformer encoder, which takes sequences as input. This integration was achieved through a bimodal architecture, creating a universal model capable of identifying TF binding sites in a global and cross-species manner. Previously, we developed a 3D protein-structure-based DenseNet, which used the spatial coordinates of all the atoms in the protein structure as input (Gupta et al., 2024). In cases where the length of a protein was shorter, empty columns were padded with zero values. A window size of 300 covered the amino acid positions, each position having a vector with elements corresponding to the 24 atoms, each of which was mapped to 3D space using normalized x, y, and z coordinates. Each vector position signified a specific atom number. This DenseNet system was implemented in PyTorch.


### Constructing the DenseNet architecture
  The DenseNet model processes a 3D tensor representing protein structure 300 × 24 × 3 (300 amino acids, 24 atoms per amino acid, three x, y, z space coordinates for each atom). This architecture consists of a single convolution layer with 32 convolution filters (kernel size = 3), batch normalization, and 2D max-pooling (stride = 2) and incorporates four dense blocks and three transition layers, totaling 121 layers. Dense blocks, fostering feature reuse through dense connectivity, play a crucial role. In a dense block, each layer l receives direct input from all preceding layers, producing output through operations such as convolution and normalization. Transition layers effectively reduce the dimensions before the next dense block.

  Expressed mathematically, let Hl represent the output feature maps of layer I. The output of layer I is computed as(Equation 1)Hl=Hl−1⨁f(Hl−1,Wl)Here, ⨁ signifies concatenation, Wl denotes the layer’s weights, and f incorporates batch normalization (BN), rectified linear units (ReLU), and convolution operations. The unique dense connectivity enhances feature reuse, contributing to the model’s ability to learn complex hierarchical features effectively.

  The growth rate, denoted by the hyperparameter k in DenseNet, emerges as a pivotal determinant in the architecture’s extraordinary performance. DenseNet’s innovative design treats feature maps as a global network state, enabling impressive results even with a smaller growth rate. At each layer, k feature maps contribute to this global state, where the total number of input feature maps Fm at the lth layer is calculated as(Equation 2)Fm=kc+K∗(l−1)

  The term kc denotes the total number of channels in the input layer. To enhance computational efficiency, a 1 × 1 convolution layer precedes each 3 × 3 convolution layer. This design, known as a bottleneck layer, reduces the number of input feature maps, typically exceeding the output feature maps k, by generating 4k feature maps. This architecture’s output is intertwined through a bimodal architecture in a feedforward layer within the Transformer encoder to capture TFs’ relationships with the input data to construct a universal model for the identification of TF–DNA interactions in a global and cross-species manner. For a more in-depth understanding of the implementation of the DenseNet architecture, additional details can be found in our previous study (Gupta et al., 2024).


### Word representations of sequence data for Transformer
  Each sequence can be considered as a collection of independent words. Every occurrence of the dimer, trimer, tetramer, pentamer, hexamer, and heptamer sequences was transformed for the verbal representation of the sequences in an overlapping window. All these k-mers provide information on base stacking, shape, and regional motifs (Zhou et al., 2013; Hombach et al., 2016; Sharma et al., 2021). Transformer takes two inputs, i.e., source input and target input. The source input vector had a maximum length of 465 words and a sequence length of 159–162 bases. The target input vector, which was the motif for that particular sequence, had a maximum length of 12 words and a sequence length of 9–12 bases. Also, target sequences contain two flanking words situated adjacent to the binding sites. Distinct integers were used as a token for each unique word. The tokenized versions of the sequences were converted into numeric vectors and matrices, a process known as embedding. The Transformer encoder and decoder fed the tokenized and embedded sequences as input to train and assess the models. The TensorFlow (Keras) tokenizer class was used to implement the tokenization procedure.


### Implementation of the Transformer encoder and decoder
  The encoder–decoder architecture in Transformer is crucial for handling sequential data. The encoder processes the input sequence, extracting hierarchical features, and the decoder generates the output sequence by attending to relevant parts of the input sequence. This bidirectional flow of information ensures the model’s ability to capture dependencies and relationships within the input and generate contextually rich sequences as output. The integration of attention mechanisms in both the encoder and decoder enhances the model’s capacity to handle TF motif translation.


#### The encoder
  The encoder implements a multi-headed attention mechanism which itself comprises self-attention layers, allowing the encoder to attend to different words in the input and consider their relationships when making decisions. The self-attention layers use the dot-product, enabling the model to weigh the importance of different input elements on the basis of their relationships. This enables the Transformer to capture the input data’s long-range dependencies and contextual relationships. The sequences from the previous step’s tokenized representation were fed into the input layer of the Transformer system. Input sequences were padded to ensure a constant size of 465 words. To create an output matrix formatted as samples, sequence length, and embedding size, embedding converted each word’s token into a word vector whose length equaled the embedded size.

  The input sequence of word tokens is denoted as Z, with a length of I. The embedding layer projects these discrete input tokens into continuous vector embeddings. If v is the size of the vocabulary and d is the dimensionality of the word embeddings, the embedding layer is represented as a matrix Eembed∈Rv×d.(Equation 3)Eembed=[X1X2X3⋯Xv][W11,W12,⋯,W1dW21,W22,⋯,W2dW31,⋯Wv1,W32,⋯Wv2,⋯,⋯⋯,W3d⋯Wvd]

  Each word in matrix Eembed combines with its positional embedding P, where P shares dimension d with the word-embedding vector. The resulting matrix, Eembed, is obtained through E´embed=Eembed+P, where P is calculated using sinusoidal positional embedding equations.(Equation 4)Pi,2j=sin(i100002j/dmodel)(Equation 5)Pi,2j+1=cos(i100002j/dmodel)Here, i represents the position of the token, and dmodel is the embedding dimension. The Eembed′ matrix enters the Transformer encoder block, which processes it through an MHA layer. This module involves multiple heads, each dividing its query, key, and value parameters N ways and independently processing the splits. The output from each head produces an attention score derived from the key, query, and value computations.

  The process involved the repetition of the same steps, in which individual attention vectors were generated and then concatenated. These concatenated vectors were then forwarded to the feedforward network block within the encoder for further processing. For additional details of the encoder section and the mathematical equations governing the self-attention mechanism, refer to the PTFSpot work (Gupta et al., 2024). To mitigate overfitting, the output from the MHA layer was supplied to a dropout layer of 0.1 fraction, followed by a normalization layer. Following normalization, the output was directed to fully connected feedforward layers with 64 nodes that converge in the subsequent dropout layer of 0.1 fraction, followed once again by a normalization layer. After normalization, the output is seamlessly fused with the DenseNet output as discussed above, encapsulating vital structural information from the 3D protein structure. This amalgamated result is channeled into fully connected feedforward layers, converging within the subsequent dropout layer and followed by another normalization layer. Subsequently, this enhanced output from the encoder is integrated into the decoder block of the Transformer.


#### Decoder block of the Transformer
  The decoder block mirrors the structure of the encoder, comprising three sublayers: masked MHA, MHA, and feedforward network. Notably, there are two MHA sublayers in the decoder, one of which is masked. Each decoder is supplied with two inputs: one originating from the target sequence and the other derived from the encoder’s representation. This intricate interplay significantly enhances the decoder’s capacity to assimilate information from both its preceding counterpart and the comprehensive encoding of the input sentence by the encoder. The masked MHA mechanism in the decoder plays a pivotal role in capturing dependencies within the target sequence while preventing information leakage from future positions during training. This attention mechanism enables the decoder to attend to different parts of the input sequence, aiding in the generation of contextually rich representations for each position in the target sequence.

  The Transformer architecture implements masked MHA in the decoder through a series of mathematical operations. Consider the target input sequence as XT and its embedding as E, where E is the output embedding matrix obtained after converting the input into word embedding and adding positional encoding, similar to the encoder block before being fed into the masked MHA layer.


#### Masked multi-head attention of decoders
  For each distinctive head i, symbolizing i = 1, 2,…., h, three pivotal matrices are calculated: Qi (Query), Ki (Key), and Vi (Value). This is accomplished through matrix multiplication of E with the corresponding weight matrices Wqi, Wki, and Wvi. The formulation of attention scores Zi transpires through application of the softmax function to the scaled dot-product attention mechanism, adhering to the expression(Equation 6)Zi=softmax(QiKiTdk)

  To preserve causality during training, a masking mechanism is applied to the attention scores, specifically designed to impede the model from attending to future positions, encapsulating the essence of masked self-attention. The ensuing phase involves computation of the weighted sum, in which each head i contributes to the formation of Headi via the formula(Equation 7)Headi=ZiVi

  A concatenation of the outputs from all heads transpires, culminating in a subsequent multiplication with the output weight matrix Wo, yielding the definitive output of the masked MHA mechanism in the decoder:(Equation 8)MaskedMultiHeadE=head1head2……headWo

  This exposition summarizes the procedures involved in using masked self-attention across multiple heads within the decoder framework. In the decoding phase of a Transformer model, after the masked MHA mechanism processes the input sequence, the encoder output is incorporated as the second input for the decoder. The decoder MHA mechanism is then employed, similar to the encoder, enabling the model to selectively focus on relevant parts of the input sequence and the encoded information. The output from the decoder MHA mechanism then undergoes further refinement through the feedforward layer. In decoder processing, layer normalization and residual connection enhance information flow, stabilizing the network. Subsequently, the final output undergoes conditional probability estimation, unfolding as a crucial aspect of the decoding mechanism. Building upon the output after layer normalization, the model proceeds to compute the conditional probability distribution over the vocabulary for the subsequent token (Wu et al., 2022). This involves using the softmax function to transform raw scores into a probability distribution. The resulting distribution guides the selection of the next token in the sequence, a pivotal step in generating contextually meaningful and coherent outputs. The conditional probability P⟨yt|y1,….,yt−1,X⟩ for the next token yt is computed using the following equation:(Equation 9)P⟨yt|y1,….,yt−1,X⟩=softmax(woht)where wo is the weight matrix and ht is the hidden state at position t. This process repeats iteratively for each time step until the entire sequence is decoded. The model uses the generated tokens as input for subsequent time steps, enabling autoregressive decoding. The BLEU score serves as a crucial metric for evaluating the decoder performance of the Transformer model (Papineni et al., 2002). This score measures the similarity between the model-generated sequences and the target sequences, providing a quantitative assessment of the quality of the generated output.

  The BLEU score is calculated on the basis of the precision of 5-grams in the generated sequence compared with the reference sequence. The equation for the BLEU score is given by(Equation 10)BLEUscore=BP×exp(∑n=1Nwn×log(Pn))where BP is the brevity penalty, accounting for the length of the generated sequence relative to the reference; N is the maximum order of 5-grams considered; wn is the weight assigned to each n-gram precision; and Pn is the precision of 5-grams in the predicted sequence. This equation quantifies the precision of the model’s output against reference sequences, considering 5-gram orders and assigning appropriate weights.


#### Generating binding sites from the Transformer decoder output
  The decoder segment of the Transformer is designed to generate multiple words (5-mers, 6-mers, and 7-mers) as part of its output sequence, comprising both the motif and the flanking bases for each provided sequence. These multiple words are supposed to map to the target (the binding motif) in an overlapping manner, where each such word aggregating to a common region in overlaps adds to the confidence. The same is repeated for the 5′ and 3′ flanking regions for single 7-mer words to act as anchors for the binding region and boost the collective confidence value for the detected target region where three such different regions come in support, collectively for a single region and with multiple spoken words. The crucial step after decoding involves matching these generated words with target sequences. The goal is to identify the most suitable sequence region based on a similarity threshold. The Jaro similarity algorithm (Basak et al., 2023) was used for this task. Jaro similarity is a string matching algorithm that gives a similarity value between 0 and 1. A value of 0 indicates no match, and 1 signifies a perfect match. The words generated by the decoder are individually matched with the target sequence at every position using a sliding window, and their Jaro similarity scores are calculated. The region with the highest aggregated Jaro score with the highest aggregation of the words is identified as a potential binding-site component. The Jaro Similarity J(SiSj) between two strings Si and Sj is computed using the following equation:(Equation 11)J(SiSj)=13(m|Si|+m|Sj|+m−tm)where m is the number of matching characters, t is half the number of transpositions (characters that match but are in the wrong order), and Si and Sj are the lengths of the two strings.

  Likewise, the anchoring word or the flanking bases are mapped back to the sequence to determine its anchoring position while calculating the Jaro score. In cases where multiple positions were identified within the sequences, preference was given to the position where the flanking anchor words mapped beside the identified binding site and the maximum Jaro similarity score was calculated. This process enhances the model’s ability to generate meaningful and contextually aligned binding site sequences for TFs, showcasing the power of the encoder–decoder DL system for speech translation from a long DNA sequence to generate the sequence of a precise binding motif. The resulting final model was saved in pt format. The whole Transformer–DenseNet system was implemented in PyTorch, except for the tokenization procedure, which was implemented in the TensorFlow (Keras) tokenizer class. ### Implementation of Grad-CAM
  Significant efforts are under way to enhance the transparency and interpretability of DL. DL models perform exceptionally well, but causality is difficult to explain. Interpretability of DL models is crucial and represents the current frontier of DL research. Selvaraju et al. (2017) introduced the Grad-CAM technique, which provides a visual explanation of DL models. As DL models become increasingly complex, the need for interpretability grows. We used Grad-CAM score profiles to identify the sequence-context features that had the strongest effect on the binding status for each motif. The gradient of any target class c and the global average of the activation feature map F of the last attention layer l were computed. The class-discriminative profiles of the k-mers were then obtained using a weighted combination of these activation maps, followed by a ReLU function to ensure that only features with a positive influence were considered. The mathematical formulation and operational principles of Grad-CAM are described as follows:(Equation 12)GradCAMIc=ReLU(∑IαICFI)(Equation 13)αIC=1L∑i∑j(∂yC∂AijI)where αIc denotes the neuron importance weights, highlighting the most relevant features that contribute to the model’s accuracy; L is the sequence length in the feature map; and AijIrepresents the activation at the ijth position in the sequence.

  In the implementation of Grad-CAM in PTF-Vāc, we chose the normalization layer after the MHA as the layer of interest (Figure 1). This layer contains distribution maps for various sequence k-mers. The key reason for selecting the normalization layer over the MHA output lies in how feature importance is captured. The MHA produces attention weights, which indicate dependencies between different positions in the sequence. By contrast, the normalization layer operates after attention and feedforward transformations, refining the feature representations and ensuring that they are more stable and meaningful for gradient-based interpretability.
