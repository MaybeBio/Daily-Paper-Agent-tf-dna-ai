# Application of single-cell deep learning in elucidating the mapping relationship between visceral and body surface inflammatory patterns


## Abstract
  The subcutaneous fascia (scFascia) is a widely distributed connective tissue, yet its contribution to systemic responses during visceral inflammation remains insufficiently defined. In the rat model of Salmonella-induced dysentery, scRNA-seq of scFascia combined with deep neural network (DNN)-based nonlinear feature discovery revealed transcriptional alterations indicative of Warburg-like reprogramming in macrophages (MPs), accompanied by reduced activity of the citrate cycle. Cd34 + /Pdgfra+ telocytes (CPTCs) regulated MP differentiation and proliferation through Wnt/Fgf signaling, suggesting a pathological communication pattern between scFascia and visceral organs, termed the fascia–visceral inflammatory crosstalk pattern (FVICP). PySCENIC analysis showed increased activity of transcription factors Fosl1, Nfkb2, and Atf4, modulated by CPTC signaling to MPs, leading to downregulation of aerobic respiration and upregulation of the cell cycle, DNA replication, and transcription. Co-culture assays demonstrated that CPTCs suppressed the pro-inflammatory activity of MPs by transcriptionally downregulating TLR4 and CPT1A while upregulating ATF4, and enhanced anti-inflammatory potential via post-transcriptional MTOR upregulation. These findings highlight the role of scFascia in immunomodulation and metabolic reprogramming during visceral inflammation, emphasizing its contribution to systemic homeostasis.


## Introduction
  Inflammatory lesions release chemokines that mediate myeloid cell trafficking from the bone marrow and spleen to affected tissues through trans-endothelial recruitment [1]. However, the complex mechanisms governing immune cell migration remain poorly understood. Traditional phenotypic studies have primarily focused on localized changes within pathological tissues, often neglecting the broader implications for systemic homeostasis.

  The scFascia, a pervasively distributed loose connective tissue enriched with mesenchymal and immune cells, has been extensively studied in wound healing and angiogenesis [2], [3]. Due to its intricate vascular, neural, and cellular networks, scFascia is uniquely suited to receive and integrate signals from visceral pathologies [4], [5]. However, its function in maintaining systemic balance, particularly in inflammatory conditions, has not been fully elucidated.

  Among the diverse interstitial cell subpopulations within fascia, CPTCs have emerged as a distinctive population with notable morphological characteristics [6]. Formally defined by Popescu and Faussone-Pellegrini in 2010[7], their identification rests on three criteria: (1) immunophenotypic markers showing CD34 + /PDGFRα+ double-positive expression; (2) morphology characterized by long cell bodies with cytoplasmic projections; and (3) ultrastructural features revealing nanometer-scale thread-like cytoplasmic extensions under transmission electron microscopy. These criteria have since been validated across organs including the heart, lung, gastrointestinal tract, and urogenital system [8]. CPTCs play critical roles in tissue repair [9], intercellular communication [10], and organ homeostasis maintenance [11]. They establish extensive communication networks with surrounding cells through direct contact, extracellular vesicle release, and secretion of soluble factors.

  Salmonella enteritis, an acute gastroenteritis caused by invasive Salmonella infection, exemplifies a pathological condition involving the interplay between intestinal microbiota and cells within the digestive tract [12]. Upon pathogen invasion, intestinal macrophages undergo Warburg-like metabolic reprogramming—glycolysis under aerobic conditions—marked by reduced aerobic metabolism and disruption of the TCA cycle. At sites of dysenteric lesions, Salmonella breaches host defenses when the intestinal flora is disrupted, invades macrophages, hijacks metabolic regulatory pathways during intracellular proliferation, and redirects host metabolism toward pathogen-favorable conditions, thereby inducing Warburg-like reprogramming [13]. This mechanism requires distinction from the Warburg-like reprogramming observed in LPS-induced M1 polarization of pathogen-free macrophages [14]. Warburg reprogramming reflects a shift in cellular energy metabolism [15], commonly driven by heightened demands for DNA synthesis during cellular proliferation [16], immune evasion [17], or, in tumor cells, dedifferentiation [18]. The emergence of Warburg-like reprogramming thus signals enhanced proliferative capacity and complex immunological remodeling [12]. This phenomenon is particularly relevant in Salmonella enteritis, where acute inflammation strongly modulates immune cell proliferation and mobilization to counteract pathogenic threats [19].

  In this study, a Salmonella-induced enteritis model was established in Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats to investigate the systemic influence of visceral inflammation on scFascia. We characterized transcriptional alterations in scFascia cell populations in response to visceral disease using scRNA-seq integrated with DNN-based nonlinear feature discovery. This approach uncovered the previously unrecognized homeostatic regulatory role of scFascia and its functional significance in systemic inflammatory pathologies.


## Methods and materials

### Ethical approval
  The experimental subjects, female Sprague-Dawley rats aged eight weeks and weighing approximately 250 g, were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. Ethical clearance for all animal-related experimental protocols was secured from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanjing Agricultural University, under protocol number NJAU.No20220427087. This study strictly adhered to the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee's guiding principles and aligned with the Animal Research: Reporting In Vivo Experiments guidelines. The rodents were housed in a fully ventilated cage system, ensuring unrestricted access to food and water. Environmental conditions were stringently maintained with a stable ambient temperature of approximately 25°C and a natural light-dark cycle to simulate the animals' native habitat.


### Bacterial strains and growth conditions
  The Salmonella Typhimurium strain CVCC542 (hereafter referred to as S. Typhimurium CVCC542) was obtained from the Joint International Research Laboratory of Animal Health and Food Safety. The cultivation process was conducted in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth (Beyotime, # ST156), supplemented with 50 µg/mL kanamycin, at 37°C. Initial culturing involved overnight incubation in a 2-mL static culture at 37°C to select the motile bacteria. Subsequently, the upper 1 mL of this culture was transferred into 49 mL of preheated LB broth and subjected to shaking incubation for 5 h at the same temperature. The optical density of the S. Typhimurium culture was assessed at 600 nm using an Eppendorf BioPhotometer Plus to ascertain the bacterial concentration. An appropriate bacterial dose was then prepared in sterile 0.01 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) with a pH of approximately 7.4.


### Animal assays
  Six female Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats, aged eight weeks with an average body weight of 250 g, were subjected to a 3-day oral gavage regimen to induce Salmonella Typhimurium infection. The bacterial suspension was adjusted to a concentration of 5 × 10^8 colony-forming units per milliliter using 0.01 M PBS. To neutralize gastric acidity prior to infection, the dysentery group (DG) received an oral dose of 5 % sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO₃). Ten minutes later, 400 µL of the bacterial suspension was administered via gavage. This procedure was repeated every 24 h for three consecutive days at 2:00 PM. The healthy group (HG) consisting of an additional six SD rats, was maintained on a standard diet without intervention.

### Transmission electron microscopy
  Fascia samples were initially fixed in a 2.5 % glutaraldehyde solution for 48 h at 4°C, followed by post-fixation in 1 % osmium tetroxide for 2 h under the same temperature conditions. The specimens underwent dehydration through a graded alcohol series, comprising concentrations of 75 %, 85 %, 95 %, and 100 %, each for 10 min. Subsequently, the tissues were embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections, approximately 50 nm thick, were mounted on copper grids and subjected to staining with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. The prepared sections were then analyzed using a Hitachi HT7800 transmission electron microscope operating at an acceleration voltage of 80 kV.


### Tissue dissociation and cell purification for scRNA-seq
  Initially, the tissue was transferred from the Miltenyi storage solution into a sterile culture dish containing 10 mL of 1x Dulbecco’s Phosphate-Buffered Saline (DPBS; Thermo Fisher, # 14190144) on ice. This step was implemented to eliminate residual blood and adipose tissue, after which the tissue was finely minced on ice.

  A specialized dissociation solution was prepared, comprising collagenase type 4 (Sigma-Aldrich, # C5138), elastase (Sigma-Aldrich, # E1250), and 10 µg/mL DNase I (Thermo Fisher, # EN0523), all dissolved in PBS supplemented with 5 % Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher, # SV30087.02). The tissue was subjected to enzymatic digestion in this solution at 37°C with gentle agitation at 50 rpm for approximately 40 min. Dissociated cells were collected at 20-minute intervals to optimize cell yield and viability.

  The resultant cell suspension underwent sequential filtration through nylon cell strainers decreasing from pore sizes of 100 µm to 40 µm to remove debris. The remaining red blood cells were lysed using 1X Red Blood Cell Lysis Solution (Thermo Fisher, # 00–4333–57). The dissociated cells were washed with 1x DPBS containing 2 % FBS. Cell viability was assessed using 0.4 % Trypan blue staining and confirmed with a Countess® II Automated Cell Counter (Thermo Fisher). Subsequently, cell suspensions were processed with the Dead Cell Removal Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, # 130–090–101), ensuring that only samples with greater than 95 % viability were advanced for further analysis.

  To construct 10X libraries and 3’ sequencing, suspension cells were prepared using a water-in-oil solution. This solution was crafted by the Chromium™ Single Cell Controller (10X Genomics), enabling high-fidelity capture and sequencing of individual cells.

  10X library construction

  In this study, beads, each imbued with a unique molecular identifier (UMI) and cell barcodes, were loaded to near saturation, ensuring each cell was paired with a bead within a Gel Beads-in-emulsion (GEM). Upon exposure to a cell lysis buffer, polyadenylated RNA molecules hybridized to these beads and were subsequently collected into a single tube for reverse transcription. During cDNA synthesis, each cDNA molecule was uniquely tagged at the 5′ end, corresponding to the 3′ end of an mRNA transcript, with a UMI and a cell-specific label. The beads underwent sequential processes of second-strand cDNA synthesis, adaptor ligation, and universal amplification. Sequencing libraries were prepared from randomly fragmented whole-transcriptome amplification products to enrich the 3′ ends of transcripts associated with cell barcodes and UMIs, adhering to the standard protocol of the Chromium Single Cell 3′ v3.1 system. The libraries were precisely quantified using both a High Sensitivity DNA Chip (Agilent) via a Bioanalyzer 2100 and the Qubit High Sensitivity DNA Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific), culminating in sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using 2 × 150 bp read chemistry.


### scRNA-seq data preprocessing and quality control
  The expression matrix was generated by Cellranger 7.2.0. Single-cell RNA sequencing data from DG and HG fascial tissues were processed using customized R scripts using Seurat v4.4 within the R environment version 4.3.0. Raw 10X Genomics data were imported to create Seurat objects. Quality control was performed with sample-specific thresholds:

  1. DG samples: Cells with < 28,804 UMIs, < 6260 genes, and < 12 % mitochondrial content were retained; 2. HG samples: Cells with < 31,018 UMIs, < 7201 genes, and < 10 % mitochondrial content were retained. All samples required a minimum expression of 50 genes per cell and detection in ≥ 3 cells per gene. Automated outlier removal and pre/post-QC visualization were implemented before generating final quality-controlled Seurat objects.


### Single-cell Data Integration and Clustering
  Quality-controlled Seurat objects from DG and HG fascial tissues were integrated using the standard Seurat workflow. Datasets were merged with condition labels (HG vs DG), normalized via LogNormalize, and subjected to dimensionality reduction (PCA followed by UMAP/tSNE). Graph-based clustering was performed at multiple resolutions (0.2–1.5), with cluster-specific marker genes identified using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests.

  Pre-defined marker gene panels were applied in cell type annotation. Cell identities were annotated through a two-tiered system:1.Broad categories (celltype): Cd34 + /Pdgfra+ Telocytes(CPTCs), Myeloid cells (MCs), Fibroblasts (FCs), Endothelial cells (ECs), Mural cells (MuCs), Lymphatic cells (LPs), Hbb+ erythroid cells (ETCs);2.Fine subpopulations (celltype2): MC: Monocytes (MonCs), Macrophages (MPs), Dendritic cells (DCs), Neutrophils (NEUs), Mast cells (MAST); MuC: Pericytes (PerCs), Myofibroblasts (MyoFs)

  Differential gene expression (DGE) was analyzed using the following framework:1.Sample-level: Health vs dysentery comparisons across all cell types2.Subpopulation-level: Condition-specific differences within celltype2 categories (MonC, MP, PerC, etc.)

  Statistical filtering retained genes with |log₂FC= > 0.25 and adjusted p-value < 0.05.


### Deep learning model architecture and training strategy

#### Model architecture
  We adopted a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) architecture for single-cell expression-based nonlinear feature discovery tasks. The model structure includes:

  1. Input Layer: High-dimensional gene expression features (input dimension equals the number of genes); 2. Hidden Layers: 4–7 fully connected layers, with 64–128 nodes per layer (standard search) or 20–59 nodes (fine-tuned search); 3. Output Layer: Number of nodes equals the number of cell types, no explicit activation function (probability calculation integrated in loss function);


#### Hyperparameter optimization
  We conducted a comprehensive grid search across the following hyperparameter space:


##### Standard hyperparameter search space
  1. Learning Rate: 0.01–0.09 (step size: 0.01); 2. Hidden Layer Dimensions: 64, 128 nodes; 3. Network Depth: 4–7 hidden layers; 4. Optimizers: SGD, Adam, RMSprop; 5. Hidden Layer Activation Functions: ReLU, Sigmoid, ELU, Softmax, Hardswish; 6. Batch Size: 32 (fixed); 7. Loss Function: Cross-entropy loss (with built-in softmax computation); 8. Total Parameter Combinations: 1080 combinations.


##### Fine-tuned hyperparameter search space
  1. Learning Rate: 0.01–0.09 (step size: 0.01); 2. Hidden Layer Dimensions: 20–59 nodes (continuous integer range); 3. Network Depth: 4–7 hidden layers; 4. Optimizers: SGD, Adam, RMSprop; 5. Hidden Layer Activation Functions: ReLU, Sigmoid, ELU, Softmax, Hardswish; 6. Batch Size: 32 (fixed); 7. Loss Function: Cross-entropy loss (with built-in softmax computation); 8. Total Parameter Combinations: 21,600 combinations.


#### Overfitting prevention strategies
  To ensure model generalization capability, we implemented multiple regularization techniques:

  1. Early Stopping Mechanism: Training terminates if validation accuracy does not improve for 10 consecutive epochs; 2. Learning Rate Scheduling: StepLR scheduler with step size of 30 epochs and decay factor of 0.1; 3. Data Splitting: 90 % training and 10 % independent testing to ensure unbiased performance evaluation; 4. Cross-validation: Independent batch validation (b1 and b2) for robust performance assessment.

#### Model interpretability
  The feature importance was assessed through DeepSHAP (Deep SHapley Additive exPlanations), which calculates each gene's contribution to classification decisions. SHAP values were computed for all genes across all cells, then aggregated using mean absolute values across samples and classes to generate gene importance rankings. All genes entering downstream Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) must simultaneously satisfy two conditions: 1. SHAP score > 0 (indicating positive contribution to feature learning in the DNN model); 2. Significant in Differential Gene Expression (DGE) analysis (showing significant expression changes before and after dysentery).


### Group and pipeline design of deep learning and model interpretation
  We annotated single-cell sequencing data from scFascia, comparing DG and HG. The expression matrices for each cell population were normalized, scaled, and labeled for nonlinear feature discovery tasks. During the training phase, a primary class of MLP was defined at first, and an extensive parameter search for batch size, hidden layers, activation functions, and optimizers was conducted to identify the optimal model parameters based on validation dataset performance. These parameters were then used to generate models for subsequent interpretation and visualization. Model interpretation employed the DeepSHAP [20] method, ranking feature genes by their contribution to model accuracy. Genes with SHAP values were filtered and used to refine differentially expressed gene (DEG) lists, which were subsequently subjected to Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) to identify biologically relevant functions uncovered by deep learning.

  In cross-validation experiments using whole fascia single-cell data, biological replicates were isolated, and the expression matrices for the entire scFascia were processed identically to generate models. To assess generalization performance, models trained on one biological replicate were tested on datasets from other replicates, serving as unseen data. The results were visualized using confusion matrices to evaluate accuracy.


### Single-cell metabolic pathway activity analysis
  Single-cell metabolic inference is based on the scMetabolism package (https://github.com/wu-yc/scMetabolism), which infers cellular metabolic activity states by analyzing the expression patterns of metabolism-related genes in single-cell RNA sequencing data. This method utilizes the KEGG metabolic pathway database combined with the VISION algorithm to calculate metabolic pathway activity scores.


### PySCENIC regulon network inferring
  scFascia and colon count matrix was generated from the Seurat object after quality control as the input dataset, all procedures are guided by the GitHub project of PySCENIC (https://github.com/aertslab/pySCENIC). During the analysis pipeline, PySCENIC automatically performs motif-based pruning and implicit significance screening. All output TF regulons indicate they have passed the filtering based on default thresholds.


### Cell communication analysis
  We applied the quality-controlled Seurat objects of scFascia and colon from each group to generate Cellcall objects following the guide of this GitHub project (https://github.com/ShellyCoder/cellcall).


### CytoTRACE analysis
  To predict the relative differentiation states of macrophage populations, we employed CytoTRACE (Cellular Trajectory Reconstruction Analysis using gene Counts and Expression), a computational method that leverages the inverse relationship between the number of detectably expressed genes per cell and cellular differentiation status [21]. CytoTRACE analysis was performed using the CytoTRACE R package (version 1.0) on raw count matrices from macrophage populations.

### Cell state alterations of conventional M2 subsets in macrophages associated with visceral inflammation
  To further dissect the functional changes of scFascia macrophages in dysentery, we examined macrophage subpopulations. A subset of Gdf15 + macrophages co-expressing Cd163, a canonical M2 marker, was identified in the dysentery group. The UMAP distance between Gdf15⁺ macrophages in the DG and Cd163 + cells in the HG exceeded that between Gdf15⁺ cells and other macrophage subsets, indicating distinct phenotypic divergence. Moreover, the proportion of Gdf15 + macrophages was significantly higher in the DG than in the HG (Fig. 4C), suggesting that they represent a disease-specific macrophage state.

  Cytotrace analysis of differentiation states demonstrated that Gdf15 + macrophages in the DG had the highest differentiation scores (Fig. 5B), indicating greater functional specialization than their HG counterparts. Fxyd5 and S100a11 correlated strongly with Cytotrace order (Fig. 5D). Both genes are potential downstream responders to Fgf/Wnt signal [27], [28], [29]. Fxyd5 has been shown to regulate proliferation and differentiation through the Akt/mTOR pathway, which cross-regulates with Wnt/Fgf signaling [28]. Analysis of highly expressed genes in this subset revealed activation of pathways associated with fibroblast growth factor stimuli and Wnt-mediated signaling (Fig. 4E). Genes linked to hypoxia stress response, proliferation, and inflammation were markedly upregulated (Fig. 4F; Supplementary Table 1).

## Discussion
  In this study, Salmonella dysentery induced shifts in the expression profiles of scFascia cell types, demonstrating that visceral inflammation can significantly influence distal cell states. Within our deep-learning framework, each scFascia cell type contributed distinct functional roles. These insights were enabled by the ability of deep learning to uncover nonlinear relationships between features and labels [33]. The single-cell data encompassed complex biological processes characterized by pronounced nonlinearity that typically require advanced analytical approaches for identification [34].By mining nonlinear features in high-dimensional data, and through single-cell metabolic analysis, we identified transcriptional changes consistent with Warburg-like reprogramming in scFascia macrophages triggered by visceral inflammation—changes that were obscured by the noise of linear features in conventional analyses (Fig. 3D vs. Fig. 3E).

  Although our single-cell analysis used a limited number of biological replicates (two per group), this exploratory design aligns with hypothesis-generating investigations. We acknowledge that the small sample size may limit statistical power and the generalizability of our deep-learning models; therefore, the findings should be viewed as preliminary and in need of validation in larger cohorts. To support our computational predictions, additional validation experiments were performed as detailed below.

  Subsequent single-cell functional analyses and co-culture experiments indicated that CPTCs substantially modulate macrophage functional states via autocrine/paracrine mechanisms, promoting a shift of macrophage populations toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype:1.CPTCs and macrophages exhibited close interactions in vivo and in vitro. Cell–cell communication and transcription-factor inference analyses revealed a complex CPTC–MP network closely associated with the regulation of Warburg-like metabolic reprogramming in scFascia macrophages during visceral inflammation.2.CPTCs mitigated LPS-induced morphological alterations in macrophages by modulating the mTOR-associated regulatory network through autocrine and paracrine mechanisms, while simultaneously enhancing overall mTOR expression. By downregulating TLR4, they reduced macrophage sensitivity to LPS, thereby strengthening the anti-inflammatory capacity of macrophage populations [35].3.CPTCs partially enhanced glycolytic function in M2 macrophages through CPT1A downregulation [36], and modestly upregulated the stress-response gene ATF4 [37], consistent with glycolysis-driven accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in macrophage clusters [38]. Notably, unchanged expression of mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM) supported alignment with classical LPS-induced polarization paradigms, indicating no large-scale initiation of mitochondrial biogenesis [39].

  Prior studies identify the MTOR pathway as decisive for macrophage polarization, particularly in promoting M2-type phenotypes [35], whereas MTOR inhibition generally drives pro-inflammatory phenotypes [40]. Our findings show that CPTCs markedly increase MTOR expression in co-cultured macrophages, implicating MTOR-mediated metabolic reprogramming in the modulation of immune functions [41]. Under LPS stimulation, MTOR upregulation was further amplified in CPTC-containing microenvironments, highlighting a central role in regulating a Warburg-like metabolic phenotype (enhanced glycolysis under aerobic conditions) [42] In parallel, characteristic Wnt secretion by CPTCs is known to suppress the NF-κB pathway, providing a fundamental anti-inflammatory control [43]. Collectively, CPTCs likely potentiate basal glycolytic activity in macrophages through MTOR upregulation—rather than via direct pro-inflammatory signaling—thereby fostering metabolic plasticity and functional extension in M2 macrophages. This interpretation is consistent with documented roles of MTOR/Akt in metabolic reprogramming during M2 polarization and tissue repair [44]. Based on these findings, the potential impact of CPTCs should be considered in both tumor-associated macrophage (TAM)–driven inflammatory processes and anti-inflammatory mechanisms in skin wound healing [45]. Moreover, therapeutic strategies targeting inflammatory regulatory pathways, such as canonical Wnt signaling, should be evaluated to facilitate disease recovery [46].

  A further issue highlighted here is the objective impact of visceral inflammation on distal tissues. The sole intervention was induction of dysentery and acute inflammation via high-dose oral Salmonella. Subcutaneous fascial tissue exhibited significant biological changes attributable to systemic manifestations of acute inflammation. Comparable body surface–visceral correlations have been reported in other clinical scenarios [47]. Thus, scFascia may serve as a potential biomarker reflecting the progression of systemic inflammation. MPs and CPTCs, as predominant cytological components, were identified within a fascia–visceral inflammatory crosstalk pattern (FVICP) under dysbiosis. In this study, the FVICP is characterized by the following features:1.Systemic dissemination of visceral pathological signals: the FVICP is driven by the spread of signals from visceral organs and by extensive interactions between the circulatory system and microenvironments of remote responding tissues.2.Synchronization and mapping of immune states: immune states in certain visceral cells are mirrored in distal tissues; for example, transcriptional changes consistent with Warburg-like reprogramming were observed in scFascia macrophages during intestinal dysentery [13].3.Pathological homeostasis in distal microenvironments: remote immune microenvironments establish a corresponding pathological equilibrium in response to visceral dysregulation.

  Based on these FVICP characteristics, we hypothesize that cellular components in superficial tissues have potential diagnostic value. On the one hand, systemic inflammation is common, and similar inflammatory milieus are likely to elicit convergent fascial cellular responses. On the other hand, in cross-validation analyses of fascial cells, global fascial changes were reliably recognized by DNN models, yielding promising predictive performance. This logic parallels approaches that train machine-learning models on tumor single-cell data using well-annotated features and then leverage the generalization capacity of salient features to classify previously unseen data [48].

  While our findings support the potential of fascia-derived single-cell transcriptomics for monitoring inflammatory states, several limitations should be addressed in future work:1.Clinical translation challenges: single-cell sequencing faces barriers in accessibility, cost, and standardization for routine diagnostics; interindividual variability in fascia composition will necessitate reference ranges and workflow adaptations.2.Evidence for metabolic reprogramming: conclusions regarding distal Warburg-like reprogramming are transcriptomic and would benefit from direct metabolic-flux validation.3.Scope of the FVICP concept: the FVICP was derived from a single bacterial infection model and requires validation across diverse inflammatory contexts (viral infections, sterile inflammation, human disease) before broad generalization.4. In vitro validation constraints: THP-1–derived macrophages were used rather than primary rat macrophages, and the proposed signaling mechanisms require direct pathway validation beyond transcriptional correlations.
