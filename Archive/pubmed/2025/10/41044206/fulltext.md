# Emerging plasticizer induced lipid metabolism disorders revealed by network toxicology molecular docking and dynamics simulation


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-17931-0.


## Introduction
  In recent years, the impact of environmental pollutants on lipid metabolism has attracted widespread attention. These pollutants can interfere with lipid synthesis, storage, and oxidation through various signaling pathways, ultimately leading to metabolic disorders. Plasticizers, widely used in food packaging, medical devices, children’s toys, and construction materials, have become common environmental contaminants1. Among them, phthalate esters (PAEs) are extensively applied due to their excellent plasticizing properties2. However, studies have indicated potential health risks associated with PAEs, particularly concerning their role in lipid metabolism regulation.

  For example, diisobutyl phthalate exhibits high-affinity binding to peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) and modulates lipid homeostasis via phospholipase D and phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B signaling pathways3. Di(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (DEHP) promotes lipid synthesis through the liver X receptor/sterol regulatory element-binding protein-1c pathway while inhibiting the calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase beta/AMP-activated protein kinase pathway, thereby reducing fatty acid oxidation4. Furthermore, dibutyl phthalate (DBP) may induce inflammation by disrupting the gut-liver axis, while diethylene glycol dibenzoate exhibits a non-monotonic dose-dependent effect, potentially increasing the risk of obesity5,6.

  Due to the toxicity concerns associated with traditional plasticizers, regions such as the European Union and the United States have gradually restricted their use in food-contact materials and children’s products, driving the search for low-toxicity and high-efficiency alternatives7. Epoxidized soybean oil (ESBO) and acetyl tributyl citrate (ATBC) have been proposed as potential safer alternatives due to their perceived “green” and environmentally friendly properties8,9. ESBO demonstrates excellent thermal stability and relatively low toxicity, making it widely used in food packaging and medical devices8. Meanwhile, ATBC, known for its biocompatibility, is frequently employed in cosmetics and children’s toys9. However, with the exponential increase in their usage, the environmental release and biological exposure risks of emerging plasticizers have become increasingly evident.

  ATBC, in particular, lacks strong covalent bonding with polymeric materials, making it prone to environmental leaching and subsequent bioaccumulation. Its maximum migration level has been reported to reach 79.8 mg/kg, surpassing that of some PAEs10,11. Moreover, both ATBC and ESBO have been widely detected in aquatic environments, soil, airborne particulates, and human biological samples12–14, suggesting widespread population exposure and potential metabolic disruptions. ATBC may interfere with lipid homeostasis by regulating cholesterol metabolism and lipoprotein levels, while also affecting thyroid hormone synthesis and secretion—critical regulators of energy metabolism and lipid homeostasis15. As an epoxide, ESBO undergoes hydrolysis in vivo, generating biologically active metabolites that may modulate hepatic metabolic enzyme activity, influencing lipid transport and storage, and ultimately contributing to metabolic imbalances16. Although short-term toxicity studies suggest that ATBC and ESBO exhibit relatively low acute toxicity, their long-term effects on lipid metabolism and underlying molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood.

  Current research on plasticizer-induced lipid metabolism disorders has predominantly focused on traditional PAEs, with systematic studies on emerging alternatives still lagging. Existing evidence suggests that emerging plasticizers may disrupt metabolic homeostasis through mechanisms similar to those of PAEs17. For instance, ATBC has been proposed to interfere with estrogen, androgen, and thyroid hormone signaling pathways, thereby influencing lipid metabolism, energy balance, and cholesterol biosynthesis11,18. However, conventional toxicological approaches often fail to capture the complex, multi-target, and multi-pathway interactions that occur under real-world exposure conditions.

  Network toxicology, an emerging research paradigm, integrates bioinformatics and systems biology to holistically elucidate the potential targets and toxicological pathways of chemical exposures. This approach offers a novel perspective for systematically assessing the health risks associated with environmental pollutants. This study focuses on ATBC and ESBO, two representative emerging plasticizers, aiming to systematically predict their potential targets using network toxicology and validate their binding affinities to key lipid metabolism-related proteins through molecular docking techniques. By constructing a comprehensive “compound-target-pathway” interaction network, this study seeks to elucidate the core mechanisms by which these plasticizers interfere with lipid synthesis, degradation, and transport. The findings will provide a theoretical basis for assessing the health risks of emerging plasticizers, contribute to the development of low-toxicity alternatives, and support the formulation of targeted intervention strategies. This research holds significant scientific value for safeguarding public health and promoting the sustainable application of alternative materials.


## Materials and methods

### Construction of the target database for the emerging plasticizers ATBC and ESBO
  This study selected ATBC and ESBO as research subjects. First, the standardized chemical structure information was obtained from the PubChem database19, and the SMILES (Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System) codes were extracted to provide precise molecular representations for subsequent target predictions.

  To ensure comprehensive target identification, potential targets were systematically retrieved using the SuperPred20, TargetNet21, and SwissTargetPrediction databases22, with Homo sapiens as the target species. A cross-validation strategy was employed to improve data reliability, removing entries with missing information or incomplete structures and integrating deduplicated results. Target identification data were supplemented using the ChEMBL database, and UniProt was used to standardize target names to maintain consistency and comparability23. Through this multi-database integration and rigorous filtering process, a comprehensive target dataset for ATBC and ESBO was constructed to support subsequent analyses.


### Screening of lipid metabolism-related target networks
  To identify lipid metabolism-related targets, keyword (lipid metabolism) searches were conducted in the GeneCards and MSigDB databases. In GeneCards, genes were ranked based on relevance scores (GeneCards Inferred Functionality Scores), and the top 10% of genes with a score greater than 10 were selected to construct a lipid metabolism target gene set17. The MSigDB database was then integrated, with duplicate entries removed to ensure data completeness and accuracy (detailed database information is provided in Supplementary Table 1).

  A Venn diagram analysis was performed to identify the intersection between the ATBC/ESBO-associated targets and the lipid metabolism-related genes. The overlapping genes were considered potential key targets for lipid metabolism disorder and were used for subsequent network analysis.


### Construction of protein–protein interaction network and core target screening
  The STRING database was used to construct the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network of potential targets of ATBC and ESBO, with the species restricted to Homo sapiens. The minimum interaction score was set to medium confidence (> 0.4), and the significance threshold was set to P < 1.0E−16 to ensure network reliability24. The PPI results were imported into Cytoscape (v3.10.1, available at https://github.com/cytoscape/cytoscape/releases/3.10.1/) for visualization, and the CytoHubba plugin (v0.1, available at https://apps.cytoscape.org/apps/CytoHubba) was used to calculate multiple topological parameters, including Maximal Clique Centrality (MCC), Density of Maximum Neighborhood Component (DMNC), Maximum Neighborhood Component (MNC), Degree, Edge Percolated Component (EPC), BottleNeck, EcCentricity, Closeness, Radiality, Betweenness, Stress, and Clustering Coefficient, to identify core targets. Module clustering analysis of the PPI network was conducted using the MCODE (Molecular Complex Detection) plugin, with the following parameters: degree cutoff = 2, node score cutoff = 0.2, K-core = 2, and max depth = 100.


### Functional enrichment and disease association analysis of target proteins
  To investigate the biological functions and disease associations of ATBC- and ESBO-related target proteins, Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Disease Ontology (DO) enrichment analyses were performed. GO analysis, including biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF) categories, was conducted using protein annotations obtained from the GO database (http://geneontology.org/). KEGG pathway enrichment was carried out using pathway annotations from the KEGG database (https://www.kegg.jp/). DO enrichment analysis was performed using disease–protein annotations from the DO database (https://disease-ontology.org/).

  Functional enrichment analyses were implemented in R (version 4.3.2) using the clusterProfiler package (version 4.3.2). The statistical significance threshold was set at an adjusted p-value < 0.05, and multiple testing corrections were performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate.


### Molecular docking analysis of ATBC and ESBO with core targets
  To elucidate the interaction modes between ATBC/ESBO and core target proteins, molecular docking simulations were performed. The crystal structures of core target proteins were obtained from the RCSB PDB database and preprocessed using PyMOL (v3.1, available at https://pymol.org/) (removal of water molecules and original ligands, structural optimization). The optimized protein structures were further processed in AutoDock Tools 1.5.7, including hydrogenation, charge calculations, and nonpolar hydrogen bond optimizations.

  The molecular structures of ATBC and ESBO were downloaded from the PubChem database and converted into PDBQT format for docking calculations. AutoDock Vina was employed for docking simulations, with genetic algorithm optimization parameters25. The docking results were visualized using PyMOL to identify key binding sites and analyze molecular interactions, providing structural biology support for investigating the toxicological mechanisms of plasticizers in lipid metabolism.


### Molecular dynamics simulation of ATBC and ESBO with core targets
  Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using GROMACS 2022 (v2022, available at https://manual.gromacs.org/2022/download.html) to evaluate the dynamic stability of the protein–ligand complexes. The general AMBER force field (GAFF) was applied to the small molecule ligands, while the AMBER14SB force field was used for proteins. The TIP3P water model was employed for solvation. Each protein–ligand complex was embedded in a periodic cubic box filled with water molecules, and counterions were added to neutralize the system.

  All hydrogen-containing bonds were constrained using the LINCS algorithm, allowing a time step of 2 fs. Long-range electrostatic interactions were computed using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method with a real-space cutoff of 1.2 nm. The van der Waals interaction cutoff was set to 10 Å, updated every 10 steps. The system temperature was maintained at 298 K using the V-rescale thermostat, while pressure was controlled at 1 bar using the Berendsen barostat.

  Following energy minimization, 100 ps of equilibration was conducted under NVT and NPT ensembles, respectively. Production MD simulations were carried out for 100 ns, with snapshots saved every 10 ps. The resulting trajectories were analyzed using VMD and PyMOL for conformational and interaction dynamics.


## Results

### Chemical characteristics of emerging plasticizers and target gene identification
  The chemical properties of ATBC and ESBO were compiled, including their molecular structures and SMILES information (Supplementary Table 2). Through the integration of multiple target prediction platforms and removal of redundant entries, we identified a total of 133 unique protein targets (Fig. 1A), with ATBC linked to 247 proteins and ESBO to 232 (Supplementary Table 3). The compound-target interaction network (Fig. 1B) shows that these compounds interact with multiple biological targets, indicating potential multi-target interactions for these two specific compounds.

### Identification of lipid metabolism-related target genes
  We identified 2187 proteins associated with lipid metabolism by integrating data from GeneCards and MSigDB (Supplementary Table 4). Cross-referencing with the predicted protein targets of ATBC and ESBO yielded 113 overlapping protein targets (Fig. 1C), which were subsequently used for downstream analysis. The resulting plasticizer-lipid metabolism-target network (Fig. 1D) illustrates a strong overlap in mechanisms by which both compounds may interfere with lipid metabolism. This convergence indicates a shared toxicological signature and potential for cumulative biological effects.


### Construction of the PPI network and core target identification
  Using the STRING database, we constructed a PPI network comprising 113 target genes and 254 interaction edges. The PPI network was visualized using Cytoscape software (Fig. 2A), where gene nodes were arranged concentrically based on centrality measures to emphasize their relative importance within the network.

  Fig. 2PPI network of potential targets of lipid metabolism induced by novel plasticizers. (A) PPI network of protein targets. (B) Core targets are identified through the CytoHubba algorithm. The dots in each algorithm indicate that the gene is a core target within that algorithm, and the genes that are core targets in all algorithms are selected as the core targets. (C) PPI network of core protein targets. Yellow represents core targets, and light green represents proteins that interact with the core targets. (D) Clustering modules found through the modular clustering analysis of the PPI network.

  To further identify core genes within the PPI network, the CytoHubba plugin was employed for robust evaluation. The top 30 genes from each metric were selected, and five hub genes—EGFR (Epidermal growth factor receptor), STAT3 (Signal transducer and activator of transcription 3), TLR4 (Toll-like receptor 4), JUN, and AR (Androgen receptor)—were ultimately identified (Fig. 2B).

  Further, we constructed an interaction network for these core genes (Fig. 2C), where yellow nodes represent hub genes and light green nodes indicate their associated genes, thereby visualizing the intricate interactions among these key regulators. These genes were highly connected and are known to participate in lipid metabolism, inflammatory signaling, and endocrine regulation.

  Additionally, module clustering analysis of the PPI network was conducted using the MCODE plugin in Cytoscape to identify densely connected gene clusters that may play critical roles in plasticizer-induced lipid metabolic regulation. The analysis identified a significant gene module comprising 10 nodes and 25 edges, with an MCODE score of 5.56 (Fig. 2D). Functional enrichment analysis of the clustered genes revealed their involvement in immune response, oxidative stress, and nuclear receptor signaling—processes closely related to lipid metabolism and inflammation. This suggests that the module may represent a key functional unit contributing to the disruption of lipid homeostasis upon exposure to the emerging plasticizers ATBC and ESBO.

  The BP category primarily included lipid localization, steroid metabolic process, response to xenobiotic stimulus, lipid catabolic process, and fatty acid metabolic process, suggesting that the emerging plasticizers ATBC and ESBO may significantly impact lipid metabolism-related physiological activities. The CC category was enriched in ficolin-1-rich granule lumen, vesicle lumen, secretory granule lumen, and cytoplasmic vesicle lumen, indicating that plasticizer-related targets may function within vesicle-associated cellular environments. The MF category revealed significant enrichment in oxidoreductase activity (acting on paired donors with oxygen incorporation/reduction), nuclear receptor activity, ligand-activated transcription factor activity, and carboxylic ester hydrolase activity, suggesting that these genes may influence lipid metabolism through transcriptional regulation and enzymatic reactions.


#### KEGG pathway enrichment analysis
  KEGG pathway analysis identified 159 enriched pathways (Fig. 3C,D), with the hypoxia-inducible factor 1 (HIF-1) signaling pathway, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) signaling pathway, ErbB signaling pathway, sphingolipid signaling pathway, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF) signaling pathway being particularly significant. These pathways are closely tied to inflammation, oxidative stress, and metabolic reprogramming, providing insight into how plasticizers may dysregulate lipid metabolism through indirect signaling cascades.


#### DO disease enrichment analysis
  DO analysis revealed enrichment in 664 disease terms (Fig. 3E,F), including fatty liver disease, hepatitis, liver cirrhosis, lipid storage disorders, and certain bone neoplasms. These associations support the hypothesis that exposure to the emerging plasticizers ATBC and ESBO may increase the risk of metabolic and oncogenic diseases by disturbing lipid regulatory processes.

  Together, these enrichment analyses suggest that ATBC and ESBO affect lipid metabolism not only through direct metabolic pathways but also via broader immunological and hormonal regulatory networks.


### Pathway enrichment of core genes
  The five hub genes encoding EGFR, STAT3, TLR4, JUN, and AR were analyzed separately for KEGG pathway enrichment, resulting in 51 significantly enriched pathways (Fig. 4). Notably, the HIF-1 signaling pathway emerged again with the highest statistical significance, reinforcing its role as a central axis affected by plasticizer exposure. Pathview analysis (Fig. 5) showed that these core genes participate in inflammation, hypoxic responses, angiogenesis, and lipid regulation. These results provide a coherent mechanistic model linking plasticizer exposure to metabolic reprogramming via hypoxia-related signaling pathways.

### Molecular docking analysis of emerging plasticizers and core targets
  Molecular docking simulations were further performed to examine the possibility of direct interactions between the emerging plasticizers ATBC and ESBO and the five core target proteins. Molecular docking analysis demonstrated that both ATBC and ESBO exhibit strong binding affinities to the five core target proteins, with binding energies below − 5 kcal/mol in most cases (Fig. 6). TLR4 displayed the strongest binding with both plasticizers, followed by AR.

These interactions may alter protein conformation or activity, suggesting a potential for modulation of downstream lipid regulatory pathways. Notably, the strong binding to TLR4 and AR supports the notion that immune and endocrine disruption are key components of the plasticizer-induced metabolic effects.

  In summary, the docking results provide molecular-level evidence for the specific interaction between the emerging plasticizers ATBC and ESBO and key lipid-regulating proteins, aligning with network-level observations and reinforcing the proposed mechanistic framework of lipid metabolism disruption.


### Molecular docking validation and dynamic behavior of ATBC and ESBO with core targets
  To further validate the binding affinity of the emerging plasticizers ATBC and ESBO with core targets, we conducted 100 ns MD simulations. Multiple structural parameters were analyzed to assess the stability and interaction strength of each ATBC–protein and ESBO–protein complex.

  Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD) values (Fig. 8A) for all complexes remained within 0.2–0.4 nm throughout the simulation, indicating good overall conformational stability. Among them, the ESBO–TLR4 complex exhibited the lowest RMSD fluctuation, suggesting minimal structural deviation from the initial docked conformation and thus the highest stability. Radius of Gyration (Rg) analysis (Fig. 8B) showed that TLR4-based complexes had slightly lower Rg values than AR-based ones, indicating more compact structures upon plasticizer binding. All complexes reached a stable Rg after ~ 20 ns, suggesting no large-scale unfolding occurred. Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF) results (Fig. 8C) revealed that flexible regions were primarily located at the N- and C-termini. Binding site residues remained relatively rigid (< 0.2 nm fluctuation), especially in the ESBO–TLR4 complex, further supporting the formation of stable interactions at the binding interface. The center of mass (COM) distance between ATBC and ESBO and either the protein center or binding site (Fig. 8D) decreased rapidly during the initial phase and plateaued afterward, confirming that ATBC and ESBO remained close to their initial docking sites and did not dissociate during the simulation. Notably, ESBO in the TLR4 binding site showed the most stable COM profile. Buried solvent-accessible surface area (Buried SASA) (Fig. 8E) showed that most complexes maintained stable contact areas above 10 nm², with the ESBO–TLR4 complex reaching ~ 20 nm2, suggesting a deeply buried and strongly interacting interface. In contrast, the ATBC–AR complex exhibited the smallest buried SASA, possibly reflecting weaker or more superficial binding.

  Overall, these results demonstrate that both ATBC and ESBO stably bind their target proteins during MD simulations, with the TLR4–ESBO complex showing the most favorable stability and interaction features. These findings support the potential of these plasticizers to modulate target protein functions through stable binding.


## Discussion
  Emerging non-phthalate plasticizers, ATBC and ESBO, are increasingly used in food packaging, medical devices, and children’s products due to their relatively low toxicity9. Their widespread use has led to persistent environmental distribution and human exposure, raising concerns about potential metabolic disturbances26–28.

  Emerging evidence indicates that certain plasticizers act as endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs), interfering with hormonal signaling and metabolic processes29,30. Although ATBC and ESBO are generally considered less toxic than traditional phthalates, their effects on lipid metabolism remain poorly understood8. Disruption of lipid metabolism may increase the risk of metabolic disorders, including non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases31–33. Given their widespread use in food-contact materials, chronic dietary exposure raises public health concerns, yet current toxicological studies on their metabolic impact remain limited.

  To further elucidate the biological significance of the identified target genes, we conducted GO, KEGG, and DO enrichment analyses. GO analysis revealed that these functions are directly implicated in maintaining lipid homeostasis, thereby supporting the hypothesis that exposure to plasticizers may disturb metabolic equilibrium. TThe oxidoreductase and nuclear receptor activities observed in the enrichment analysis suggest that the identified core targets may perturb lipid metabolic processes. In addition, enrichment in cellular component terms such as vesicle lumens and granule compartments indicates potential involvement in intracellular lipid trafficking and storage, suggesting that exposure to ATBC and ESBO may alter lipid distribution and overall metabolic homeostasis.

  Among the enriched KEGG pathways, the HIF-1 signaling pathway plays a central role in regulating lipid metabolism, inflammation, and liver function34. HIF-1α can suppress PGC-1α-mediated fatty acid β-oxidation, promoting hepatic lipid accumulation35, whereas its deficiency under stress may exacerbate steatosis, highlighting a context-dependent role in lipid homeostasis. Disruption of HIF-1 signaling by ATBC and ESBO may therefore contribute to metabolic dysregulation.

  Similarly, the PD-L1/PD-1 immune checkpoint pathway may be indirectly affected through alterations in lipid metabolism. PD-L1 suppresses T-cell-mediated immune responses36, and disturbed lipid signaling can impair immune function, creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment37. These findings suggest that lipid dysregulation induced by ATBC and ESBO could facilitate immune evasion, linking chemical exposure to broader immunometabolic consequences.

  Furthermore, DO analysis revealed strong associations between the identified genes and various cancers, including breast, thyroid, uterine, and colorectal cancer38–40. Previous research has demonstrated that certain plasticizers, even at low concentrations, can stimulate proliferation in hormone-sensitive cancer cell lines41. These results raise the possibility that plasticizer-induced disruptions in lipid and hormone-related pathways may contribute to oncogenic processes.

  In addition, DO enrichment also identified links between these genes and liver-related diseases such as fatty liver, cirrhosis, and hepatitis. This aligns with prior research showing that conventional phthalates like DEHP can impair mitochondrial β-oxidation and enhance de novo lipogenesis, ultimately promoting the onset and progression of NAFLD, fibrosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma42,43.

  To further explore the mechanisms underlying ATBC- and ESBO-induced metabolic disruption, we performed targeted pathway enrichment analyses of core genes implicated in lipid metabolism.

  Based on enrichment analysis of core genes related to lipid metabolism, we hypothesize that emerging non-phthalate plasticizers, particularly ATBC and ESBO, may disrupt hepatic lipid homeostasis by activating the HIF-1 signaling pathway and perturbing the immune–endocrine interaction axis, thereby contributing to the pathogenesis of metabolic disorders such as NAFLD.

  Our pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant involvement of the HIF-1 signaling pathway, with key genes including EGFR, STAT3, and TLR4. The HIF-1 pathway, a central regulator of the cellular hypoxic response, has been widely implicated in lipid accumulation, inflammation, and metabolic dysfunction. Prior studies have demonstrated that HIF-1α activation can inhibit fatty acid β-oxidation while promoting lipogenesis. For instance, Zhao et al.44 reported that HIF-1α-induced activation of the PPARα/PPARγ/FXR/LXR signaling network contributes to DEHP-induced hepatic lipid metabolic disturbances. Although direct experimental evidence is currently lacking for ATBC or ESBO, the observed enrichment and gene profiles in our study suggest that activation of the HIF-1 pathway may underlie their potential metabolic effects.

  In parallel, the identification of genes related to immune and endocrine regulation—such as TLR4, STAT3, JUN, and AR—indicates that the emerging plasticizers ATBC and ESBO may disrupt lipid homeostasis via immune–endocrine signaling interference. TLR4, a key pattern recognition receptor in innate immunity, is known to exacerbate hepatic lipid accumulation and inflammation by activating macrophage-driven NF-κB pathways45,46. Moreover, bidirectional crosstalk has been observed between TLR4 signaling and lipid metabolism, as SREBP1-mediated lipid reprogramming can downregulate TLR4-driven inflammatory responses47,48.

  STAT3 functions at the interface of inflammation and lipid regulation, promoting adipogenesis and fatty acid synthesis, and has been shown to be activated by DEHP in adipocytes49,50. JUN, a component of the AP-1 transcription factor complex, contributes to oxidative stress and hepatic steatosis through the ROS/JNK/AP-1 pathway51,52. Additionally, the AR, a nuclear hormone receptor, plays a role in lipid metabolism, insulin sensitivity, and inflammatory regulation. While ATBC has been reported to exhibit weak androgenic activity15,53, the endocrine-disrupting potential of ESBO remains unclear. Nevertheless, EDCs are known to exert their effects through modulation of nuclear receptors, including AR, supporting a plausible mechanism for hormone-mediated lipid disruption54.

  Taken together, we propose that ATBC and ESBO may impair lipid metabolic balance through a dual mechanism involving HIF-1 pathway activation and interference with immune–endocrine regulatory axes. Such disruptions may contribute to the development or exacerbation of metabolic diseases, including NAFLD, chronic liver inflammation, and potentially hepatocellular carcinoma under prolonged exposure.

  Molecular docking analysis demonstrated that the emerging plasticizers ATBC and ESBO exhibit stable interactions with key proteins involved in lipid metabolism, including EGFR, TLR4, STAT3, JUN, and AR. These interactions involve multiple non-covalent contacts, forming well-embedded ligand–protein interfaces as further supported by molecular dynamics simulations. Such stable binding may induce conformational changes or modulate protein activity, thereby influencing downstream signaling pathways. For example, EGFR binding could affect transcriptional regulators such as PPARγ and SREBP1, altering lipid synthesis and storage. TLR4 interactions may disrupt innate immune signaling and hepatic lipid homeostasis, while binding to JUN and STAT3 could impact inflammation, apoptosis, and lipid metabolic regulation. Moreover, interactions with AR suggest potential endocrine-disrupting effects, raising concerns about hormonal interference and its downstream impact on metabolic processes. Overall, these findings provide molecular-level evidence that ATBC and ESBO could perturb lipid metabolism and related regulatory pathways.

  These findings provide molecular-level support for the hypothesis that ATBC and ESBO may contribute to lipid metabolic disorders—such as hepatic steatosis—by simultaneously activating the HIF-1 signaling pathway and disrupting immune–endocrine regulatory mechanisms. The observed multi-target binding profile aligns with a multi-pathway mode of toxicity, underscoring the systemic nature of the potential metabolic disruption.

  Nonetheless, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Firstly, the molecular docking simulations were confined to a limited set of high-priority targets and may not capture all relevant molecular interactions. Secondly, while docking scores provide insight into binding potential, they do not necessarily predict functional outcomes, such as receptor activation, inhibition, or downstream biological effects, under physiological conditions.

  Future research should incorporate both in vitro and in vivo studies to validate the computational findings and to elucidate the functional consequences of these ligand-target interactions. Investigations should include time- and dose-dependent analyses, as well as assessments of mitochondrial function, inflammatory markers, and lipid metabolic profiles. Such experimental validation will be crucial for refining toxicological risk assessments, informing environmental health regulations, and deepening our understanding of the metabolic health risks posed by emerging non-phthalate plasticizers.


## Conclusion
  This study integrates network toxicology, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulations to explore the potential molecular mechanisms by which emerging plasticizers such as ATBC and ESBO may disrupt lipid metabolism. By identifying 113 lipid-related target genes and highlighting core regulators including EGFR, STAT3, TLR4, JUN, and AR, our results suggest that these compounds exert multi-target and multi-pathway effects, primarily through immune, endocrine, and oxidative stress-related signaling.

  Pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant involvement of the HIF-1 signaling pathway and immune–endocrine interaction axes, supporting the hypothesis that these plasticizers may induce metabolic disorders such as NAFLD by disrupting lipid homeostasis. Furthermore, molecular docking and dynamics simulations confirmed specific and stable binding affinities between the compounds and core targets, indicating the potential for direct interference with key regulatory pathways at the protein level.

  Together, our findings support a systems-level hypothesis that exposure to ATBC and ESBO may contribute to the development of lipid metabolism disorders through combined effects on hypoxia signaling and immune-endocrine regulation. These insights provide a scientific basis for further experimental validation and underscore the need to reevaluate the safety of emerging plasticizer alternatives in public health risk assessment.
