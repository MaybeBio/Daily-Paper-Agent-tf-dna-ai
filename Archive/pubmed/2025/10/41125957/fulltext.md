# ResNet50 and Single-Cell Multi-Omics analysis identify key cellular and molecular features in pediatric acute lymphoblastic leukemia


## Abstract
  Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is the most common hematologic malignancy in children, posing challenges for early diagnosis, relapse prediction, and individualized treatment due to its high cellular and immune heterogeneity. This study addresses these challenges by integrating deep learning-based imaging analysis with single-cell transcriptomics and T-cell receptor sequencing (TCR-seq). A ResNet50-based deep learning model was developed to classify leukemia single-cell images, achieving an accuracy of ~ 85% and an AUC of 0.86. Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) indicated that nuclear morphology was the key feature for cell identification. Concurrently, Louvain clustering of single-cell RNA sequencing data revealed 18 distinct cellular subpopulations, with Cluster 4 exhibiting strong immune activity and pronounced TCRβ rearrangement. Functional analyses highlighted the roles of UBE2C and HMGB2 in leukemogenesis. Integration with TCR-seq further uncovered immune microenvironment alterations and potential relapse biomarkers. These results demonstrate that combining deep learning with single-cell multi-omics is a powerful strategy for elucidating disease mechanisms, improving early diagnosis, and informing personalized therapies for pediatric ALL.


## Introduction
  Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is a malignant hematologic disorder that primarily affects children and adolescents, characterized by the uncontrolled proliferation of immature lymphocytes in the bone marrow, peripheral blood, and other tissues [1]. Despite advances in treatment and improved survival rates, disease heterogeneity and post-treatment relapse remain major clinical challenges [2]. Variations in disease progression and treatment response result in some patients developing resistance to standard therapies, increasing the risk of relapse and metastasis [3]. Therefore, a deeper understanding of the cellular and molecular characteristics of ALL is crucial for developing personalized treatment strategies that enhance efficacy and minimize side effects. However, many current studies focus on single aspects of the disease, overlooking the complexity of ALL cell heterogeneity.

  The molecular features of ALL cells are key to understanding this heterogeneity [4, 5]. The rapid development of single-cell genomics has enabled detailed analysis of the genetic diversity within tumor cell populations [6–9]. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) allows precise mapping of gene expression profiles, revealing functional differences among distinct subpopulations [10–12]. Meanwhile, deep learning (DL) has gained widespread application in biomedical research, uncovering hidden patterns in complex datasets and helping identify unique features and therapeutic targets in ALL subpopulations [13–15]. However, studies combining DL with single-cell genomics remain limited, restricting comprehensive insights into ALL heterogeneity.

  DL, an advanced machine learning technique, excels in processing large-scale biomedical data [16]. By employing neural networks, DL algorithms learn complex patterns and relationships, proving highly effective in tasks like image analysis and genomic research [17–20]. Single-cell genomics enables detailed characterization of cell populations at single-cell resolution, shedding light on tumor cell heterogeneity and underlying mechanisms [6, 21]. Integrating DL with single-cell analysis enhances the understanding of complex diseases such as ALL by optimizing data processing, classification, prediction, and uncovering information that traditional methods may miss. This interdisciplinary approach opens new avenues for ALL research.

  However, several challenges hinder clinical translation. Ensuring high data quality and consistency, and extracting biologically meaningful information from large datasets, remain key obstacles. Additionally, individual patient differences require further validation to develop reliable personalized treatment strategies. The heterogeneity of clinical sample sources and experimental platforms complicates cross-study comparisons and broad clinical applications. Therefore, effectively integrating multiple technologies to explore the cellular characteristics and heterogeneity of ALL is an urgent scientific need.

  This study aimed to comprehensively characterize the cellular heterogeneity and molecular features of ALL by combining DL with single-cell genomic analysis. Using scRNA-seq data from patient samples, we analyzed gene expression profiles of distinct subpopulations and applied DL algorithms to model these data, identifying potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets. This approach enhances understanding of ALL mechanisms and provides a basis for developing personalized treatment strategies. By elucidating the diversity and dynamic changes of ALL cells at various treatment stages, this work lays a theoretical foundation for precision medicine, aiming to improve survival rates and patient quality of life. Furthermore, the technologies and analytical methods presented here are versatile and scalable, offering valuable insights for research on other hematological malignancies and cancers. Thus, this study holds significant scientific value and promises substantial clinical impact in translational medicine and personalized healthcare.


## Materials and methods

### Gene and imaging data collection for pediatric ALL patients
  In this study, we obtained extensive imaging data for pediatric ALL patients from The Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA), specifically the C_NMC_2019 Dataset (ALL Challenge dataset of ISBI 2019) [22]. This dataset includes labeled cell images—normal and ALL cancer cells—used to train machine learning models to differentiate normal from malignant cells. The images are segmented from microscope slides that reflect real-world scenarios, despite some staining noise and lighting variations, which were largely corrected using an internal color normalization method developed by Mourya and colleagues.

  Additionally, single-cell and gene expression data were obtained from pediatric ALL patient samples described in a study by Mark Gower et al. [23]. The samples, including newly diagnosed T-lineage ALL patients, were preserved for multi-component analyses and underwent T-cell receptor sequencing (TCR-seq) to characterize cellular heterogeneity and define T-cell subpopulations and their functions.

  The scRNA-seq data were also derived from the study by Mark Gower et al., generated using the 10x Genomics platform and prepared with Chromium Single Cell 3’ Reagent Kits v3. Each cell produced an average of 25,000–35,000 reads, with sequencing quality meeting standard analytical benchmarks. This scRNA-seq dataset comprised cellular data from eight newly diagnosed T-lineage ALL patients, which did not overlap with the 47 patients whose imaging data were sourced from TCIA. Thus, the imaging and transcriptomic datasets originated from two independent but phenotypically similar pediatric ALL cohorts. These complementary datasets were used respectively for model training and validation of biological mechanisms, enhancing the robustness and generalizability of the cross-platform integrative analysis.

### ResNet-Based Single-Cell recognition model for pediatric ALL
  During image preprocessing, a dual-filtering strategy was applied to ensure high-quality input data. First, edge clarity was assessed using Sobel operators to calculate gradient intensity, and images with an average edge strength greater than 10 (gradient magnitude per pixel) were retained to ensure sharpness. Second, grayscale distribution was evaluated using Shannon entropy, with a threshold of > 4.5 to exclude underexposed or poorly stained samples. Images failing to meet either criterion were discarded prior to model training. This preprocessing workflow, adapted from Mourya et al.’s normalization protocol, achieved a > 95% retention rate of acceptable images during pilot testing.

  A classic ResNet50 DL model (Fig. 2) was employed to classify single-cell images from pediatric ALL patients. The dataset included images of both leukemia and normal cells obtained from stained blood smear slides captured under a microscope, in RGB format with a resolution of approximately 300 × 300 pixels. To simulate real-world diagnostic conditions, the dataset maintained a balanced proportion of leukemia and normal cells to support stable training.Fig. 2ResNet-Based Single-Cell Recognition Model for Pediatric ALL. Note: Each block in the figure represents a computational operation, primarily consisting of convolution and skip connections. The four intermediate steps are labeled as Stage 1 to Stage 2, with detailed structural parameters provided later

  The ResNet50 architecture, a 50-layer deep residual network, uses skip connections to alleviate the vanishing gradient problem, improving feature extraction and classification accuracy. Transfer learning was applied by initializing the model with weights pre-trained on the ImageNet dataset, enabling efficient feature extraction and reducing training time. The network comprises five convolutional stages, followed by a global average pooling layer and a fully connected layer for final classification. The training was optimized using stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with a cross-entropy loss function to measure prediction accuracy.

  For reproducibility and robust performance, the dataset was split into training, validation, and test sets at a 7:2:1 ratio, with balanced class representation. The model was trained for up to 50 epochs, with early stopping triggered if validation loss did not decrease for 10 consecutive epochs to prevent overfitting. The initial learning rate was set to 0.001, the batch size to 32, and L2 regularization (λ = 1e-4) was used to control overfitting.

  To address class imbalance due to the smaller number of normal cell images, two strategies were implemented: (1) data augmentation—such as rotation, flipping, and scaling—was used to oversample the minority class, and (2) class weights were applied to the loss function to reduce bias toward the majority class. These measures enhanced the model’s capacity to accurately classify both leukemia and normal cells.

  A total of 10,661 single-cell images were included in the final dataset: 7,272 from ALL patients and 3,389 from healthy controls, collected from 47 ALL patients and 26 normal children, ensuring broad sample diversity. Model performance was further validated using five-fold cross-validation, confirming its generalizability and robustness. After training and fine-tuning, the ResNet50 model demonstrated high classification accuracy for distinguishing leukemia cells from normal lymphocytes, providing a valuable tool for the diagnosis and study of pediatric ALL.


### Feature extraction of Single-Cell gene data based on Louvain clustering
  In this study, we employed the Louvain clustering algorithm to analyze single-cell gene expression data for feature extraction and cell population identification. First, a cell expression matrix was constructed using high-throughput scRNA-seq data. An adjacency graph of cells was then generated, and community detection was performed using the Louvain method to identify distinct cell populations (Fig. 3). Cell clusters were defined based on gene expression similarity, ensuring biological relevance.Fig. 3Feature Extraction of Single-Cell Gene Data Based on Louvain Clustering

  To characterize each cluster, differential expression analysis was conducted. For each gene, we calculated its statistical significance (p_val) between clusters, the log-transformed average expression difference (avg_log2FC), and the proportion of cells expressing the gene in the target cluster (pct.1) versus other clusters (pct.2). Here, pct.1 represents the fraction of cells with non-zero expression in the target cluster (e.g., Cluster 4), while pct.2 denotes the same in all other clusters. Genes with high pct.1 and low pct.2 indicate cluster-specific enrichment and are potential marker genes.

  To control for multiple testing, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method (p_val_adj). The final results table includes p_val, avg_log2FC, pct.1, pct.2, p_val_adj, cluster ID, and gene name, providing a basis for further biological interpretation of transcriptomic differences among clusters.

  Before clustering and dimensionality reduction, data quality control was performed following the standard Seurat workflow. Genes expressed in fewer than three cells (min.cells = 3) and cells expressing fewer than 200 genes (min.features = 200) were filtered out. All processing and clustering were performed using the Seurat package (v4.3.0) in R. Clusters were identified using the FindClusters function with a resolution of 0.8 to balance cluster granularity. Dimensionality reduction was performed using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) with the RunTSNE function (perplexity = 30, max iterations = 1000) for consistent visualization of clusters in low-dimensional space.

  After identifying differentially expressed genes, t-SNE plots were generated to visualize the distribution of cell clusters in high-dimensional gene expression space. As a nonlinear dimensionality reduction technique, t-SNE preserves local data structures, enabling cells in the same cluster to group together visually, which facilitates the intuitive interpretation of inter-cluster similarities and differences.

  Additionally, TCR-seq analysis was performed to assess TCRβ gene rearrangements and their frequency across clusters. Rearrangement events were extracted for each cluster to examine the immune microenvironment and clonal diversity. Cell Ranger VDJ software (v6.1.2, 10x Genomics) was used with default parameters for initial processing, which included raw read alignment, V(D)J gene annotation, CDR3 sequence extraction, and TCR rearrangement identification. Clonotypes were defined based on identical CDR3 amino acid sequences and matching V and J gene usage. Only high-frequency clonotypes (≥ 5 cells) were included in downstream analyses. Clonal expansion and immune diversity were quantified using TCRβ clonotype frequencies and diversity indices such as Shannon entropy.

  For differential gene expression analysis, we used thresholds of |log2FoldChange| >1 and adjusted p-value < 0.05, aligning with established standards in Seurat, DESeq2, and Scanpy workflows. A log2 fold change greater than 1 indicates at least a two-fold change in gene expression, supporting biological interpretability. The BH method was applied to control the false discovery rate and ensure the robustness of identified gene signatures.


### Cross-Modality integration strategy
  In this study, the integration of DL and single-cell transcriptomics was performed at the biological inference level, rather than at the model or feature level. Specifically, the ResNet50 DL model independently classified leukemic versus normal cells based on microscopy images, while scRNA-seq data were used for immune subpopulation identification, TCR lineage analysis, and transcriptomic profiling.

  Integration was achieved by correlating the image-based classification results with immunologically active clusters (e.g., Cluster 4) identified from scRNA-seq data. Comparative analyses of immune activity and TCRβ rearrangement patterns were performed to uncover functional overlaps and complementarities between imaging features and gene expression profiles. This inference-level integration strategy improves our understanding of ALL cellular heterogeneity and transformation potential without requiring direct feature-level or model-level fusion.


### Statistical analysis
  Multiple statistical approaches were employed to evaluate model performance and validate data analysis outcomes. For the DL classifier, cross-validation was used to mitigate overfitting, and standard training/validation/test splits were maintained. Loss values and validation accuracy were tracked throughout training to monitor performance. Classification performance was quantified using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves and confusion matrices, with the area under the curve (AUC) computed to measure the model’s discrimination capability at different thresholds—a higher AUC indicates better classification performance.

  To assess the consistency of the ResNet50 model, five-fold cross-validation was performed, and the average AUC was reported for each fold. In addition, a bootstrap approach (n = 1000 iterations) was applied to calculate the 95% confidence interval (CI) for the AUC, enhancing the robustness of the performance estimates.

  For differential gene expression analysis, p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the BH method to control the false discovery rate (FDR < 0.05). Confidence intervals for log2FC were estimated based on standard errors to evaluate the stability and biological relevance of effect sizes.

  Visualization of expression differences and significance values facilitated the identification of key differentially expressed genes across cell populations. For t-SNE analysis, metrics such as log2FC and adjusted p-values supported the quantitative assessment of transcriptomic heterogeneity among clusters.

  Finally, multivariate statistical analyses, including ANOVA and Cox regression, were employed to quantitatively demonstrate the model’s predictive performance and to identify gene expression signatures associated with relapse risk. These results provide a robust basis for functional validation and the development of risk-adapted treatment strategies.


## Results

### Quantitative analysis of the ResNet recognition model
  A total of 10,661 single-cell images were analyzed in this study, comprising 7,272 images from 47 pediatric ALL patients and 3,389 images from 26 healthy children, ensuring sufficient sample diversity and statistical power. The constructed ResNet-based recognition model was iteratively trained to assess its classification performance. Loss values and validation accuracy were tracked and visualized over training epochs (Fig. 4). As training progressed, the loss steadily decreased and stabilized at approximately 0.15, indicating effective error minimization. The validation accuracy converged at around 0.85, demonstrating robust generalization to unseen data.Fig. 4Visualization of the ResNet Model Training Process

  These trends confirm that the ResNet model effectively distinguishes single-cell images from ALL patients versus healthy controls, providing a reliable foundation for downstream biological inference. Notably, the clear inverse relationship between loss and accuracy during early training phases indicates efficient convergence and stable learning.

  To comprehensively evaluate classification performance, we further analyzed ROC curves and the confusion matrix (Fig. 5). Using bootstrapping (n = 1000), the model’s AUC was estimated at 0.86 (95% CI: 0.81–0.91), and the classification accuracy was 0.85 (95% CI: 0.80–0.89), supporting the model’s high discriminative power and statistical reliability.Fig. 5ROC Curve and Confusion Matrix of the ResNet Model

  The confusion matrix showed a True Positive Rate (TPR) of 0.89 for detecting ALL cells and a True Negative Rate (TNR) of 0.83 for healthy cells, indicating strong sensitivity with modest room for improvement in normal cell classification. However, the overall classification accuracy of 0.86 further confirms the model’s reliability and robustness.

  To enhance clinical interpretability, we calculated additional performance metrics. On the test set, the ResNet model achieved a precision of 0.84, a recall (sensitivity) of 0.89, an F1-score of 0.86, and a specificity of 0.83. Together, these metrics indicate that the ResNet model maintains a good balance between correctly identifying ALL cells and minimizing false positives.

  In summary, based on loss curves, ROC analysis, the confusion matrix, and derived metrics, the ResNet-based single-cell recognition model demonstrates robust, generalizable classification performance with high sensitivity and specificity. These results underscore its potential as a reliable auxiliary tool for early detection and screening of pediatric ALL in clinical settings.


### Qualitative analysis based on the ResNet recognition model
  To gain deeper insights into the decision-making process of the ResNet-based single-cell recognition model for pediatric ALL, we applied Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) to visualize the model’s focus during classification. Grad-CAM generates heatmaps highlighting the image regions that contribute most to the model’s predictions, thereby enhancing interpretability.

  Grad-CAM was applied to representative single-cell images from patient samples, and the resulting heatmaps revealed that the model consistently concentrated on nuclear enlargement and deformation—hallmark pathological features of ALL (Fig. 6). In contrast, heatmaps for normal lymphocytes showed a more uniform and diffuse focus, indicating an absence of abnormal nuclear structures and supporting the model’s ability to differentiate healthy cells from malignant ones. To objectively validate the interpretability of Grad-CAM results, we performed an overlap analysis between the heatmaps and manually annotated nuclear regions. Using image segmentation software, the nuclear boundaries were delineated, and the overlap between the top 20% of most activated pixels and the nuclear regions was quantified. The average overlap rate was 76.3%, demonstrating that the model predominantly focused on nuclei—areas closely correlated with ALL pathology. This high overlap supports the biological plausibility of the model’s predictions and confirms that its learned features align with clinically relevant morphological changes. These qualitative results highlight clear morphological differences between leukemic and normal single cells, underscoring the robustness and reliability of the recognition model. By visualizing the learned features, we gain valuable insights into how the model distinguishes pathological cells, enhancing trust in its predictions and supporting its clinical applicability for early diagnosis and intervention. Overall, the integration of DL with interpretability techniques such as Grad-CAM significantly improves diagnostic accuracy and reliability, providing a strong foundation for advancing personalized medicine in pediatric ALL.Fig. 6Grad-CAM Visualization of the ResNet Recognition Model. ### Functional analysis of key genes in pediatric ALL based on the KEGG database
  To further elucidate the biological roles of the two key regulatory genes identified in Cluster 4—ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme E2 C (UBE2C) and high mobility group box protein 2 (HMGB2)—we performed differential expression analysis. The results showed that UBE2C was significantly upregulated in ALL patient cells (Log2FC = 2.14, adjusted P = 1.2 × 10−5), while HMGB2 also exhibited high expression (Log2FC = 1.87, adjusted P = 3.6 × 10−4), suggesting their potential roles in leukemia progression.

  Based on KEGG pathway analysis, UBE2C was mainly enriched in the “Cell cycle” (hsa04110) and “Ubiquitin mediated proteolysis” (hsa04120) pathways, whereas HMGB2 was associated with pathways including “Systemic lupus erythematosus” (hsa05322) and “Ribosome” (hsa03010), implicating its involvement in mitotic regulation and immune-related processes (protein structures shown in Fig. 12).Fig. 12Heatmap of Log2 FC Expression of the Top Five Genes across Different Clusters. Note: (A) represents the protein structure of UBE2C; (B) represents the protein structure of the HMGB2 gene

  UBE2C encodes an E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme that plays a vital role in regulating the cell cycle by promoting the G2/M transition, accelerating proliferation, and suppressing apoptosis—likely through activation of APC/C-related pathways. Overexpression of UBE2C drives leukemic cell expansion and has been linked to increased proliferation, apoptosis resistance, and poor prognosis in ALL. As a therapeutic target, UBE2C shows promise for drug development. Small-molecule inhibitors designed to downregulate UBE2C may suppress leukemic cell proliferation and induce apoptosis. Additionally, combining UBE2C inhibitors with other treatments, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors or standard chemotherapeutics, could enhance therapeutic efficacy and help overcome drug resistance.

  However, several challenges exist in the development of UBE2C-targeted therapies. First, UBE2C’s dual role in both normal and tumor cells makes selective inhibition difficult and may impair normal cell function. Second, achieving effective intracellular delivery of therapeutics to reach cytoplasmic targets remains a major obstacle. Lastly, further research is required to assess drug safety and side effect management to ensure clinical feasibility and tolerability. Despite these hurdles, UBE2C remains an attractive candidate for targeted therapy in pediatric ALL.

  HMGB2 encodes a high mobility group protein that regulates chromatin structure, gene transcription, and cell proliferation. Its overexpression in ALL is strongly linked to leukemogenesis, likely through modulation of oncogenes and activation of signaling pathways such as Wnt/β-catenin, which promote stemness and therapy resistance. HMGB2 plays a role in maintaining and transforming leukemia stem cells, influencing tumor-initiating capacity and disease relapse. Therapeutic inhibition of HMGB2—using small molecules, siRNA, or monoclonal antibodies—could reduce its expression, disrupt its oncogenic functions, and improve recognition of tumor cells by the immune system. Combining HMGB2 inhibition with immunotherapies may further enhance treatment responses. However, similar to UBE2C, HMGB2’s essential roles in normal cellular processes necessitate careful evaluation of potential side effects and toxicity to ensure that therapeutic strategies minimize disruption of physiological functions. In conclusion, functional analysis and pathway enrichment highlight UBE2C and HMGB2 as critical drivers of leukemogenesis and potential therapeutic targets in pediatric ALL. While both offer promising avenues for precision therapy, overcoming challenges related to selectivity, delivery, and safety will be crucial for their successful translation into clinical treatments.


## Discussion
  ALL is the most common malignant tumor in children, accounting for the majority of pediatric leukemia cases [24–26]. Despite significant advances in chemotherapy regimens that have greatly improved overall cure rates, a subset of patients continues to experience poor treatment responses, high relapse rates, and long-term impacts on quality of life [27, 28]. Consequently, precise disease stratification, reliable prediction of chemotherapy efficacy, and accurate relapse risk assessment remain critical challenges in the clinical management of pediatric ALL. Addressing these challenges is essential for optimizing treatment outcomes, minimizing relapse, and improving long-term survival. This study focused on cellular heterogeneity, transformation potential, and individualized treatment prediction in pediatric ALL by integrating multimodal data with DL techniques. By combining single-cell transcriptomic profiling with advanced imaging analysis and DL-based classification, this research aimed to develop more precise and personalized diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Although treatment advances have reduced mortality in pediatric ALL, significant obstacles persist, including variable prognosis, inconsistent chemotherapy responses, and relapse risk. Therefore, enhancing diagnostic accuracy and refining personalized treatment predictions hold substantial clinical value for improving therapeutic efficacy, reducing recurrence, and increasing patient survival.

  The multifactorial relapse risk prediction model developed in this study integrates transcriptomic features (e.g., expression levels of UBE2C and HMGB2), immune microenvironment parameters (e.g., the ratio of cytotoxic T cells to regulatory T cells), TCR clonal diversity metrics (e.g., Shannon index), and image-based morphological features derived from Grad-CAM heatmaps. Compared to traditional clinical scoring systems—such as minimal residual disease (MRD) monitoring or leukocyte count-based stratification—this integrated model captures disease heterogeneity from both molecular and phenotypic dimensions. As a result, it demonstrates higher predictive accuracy and enables earlier detection of relapse risk, providing substantial complementary value and offering the potential to serve as a robust alternative in clinical decision-making.

  A key innovation of this project lies in its multimodal data integration strategy. Conventional ALL diagnosis and prognosis often depend on isolated data sources, such as clinical examination, routine laboratory parameters, or basic imaging findings. However, with the rapid growth of big data and artificial intelligence technologies, reliance on single-source data is increasingly inadequate to address the complex clinical challenges of ALL management. By simultaneously integrating genomic, imaging, and clinical data, this study provides a comprehensive, multi-dimensional view of disease behavior. This fusion approach significantly enhances the detection of cellular heterogeneity and uncovers distinct molecular mechanisms and phenotypic features across patients, supporting more precise and individualized treatment strategies. In particular, the integration of genomic and image-derived features allows deeper insight into the behavior of leukemic cells within different tissue microenvironments. This enriched information provides a more nuanced basis for predicting chemotherapy response and relapse risk, facilitating timely intervention and optimization of treatment plans.

  Furthermore, the application of DL in this study significantly enhances the accuracy and efficiency of disease prediction. Recent advances in DL have driven major breakthroughs in medical imaging and genomic data mining, demonstrating considerable promise for the early diagnosis and prognostic evaluation of complex diseases such as pediatric ALL [29]. By leveraging deep neural network models that integrate large-scale clinical and transcriptomic data, this study enables automated learning and optimization of disease-specific patterns and features. Consequently, the model achieves high accuracy in predicting chemotherapy response (AUC = 0.87) and relapse risk, supporting precise patient stratification and timely clinical interventions that minimize overtreatment and diagnostic delays.

  In addition to robust prediction capabilities, this project provides valuable insights into the cellular heterogeneity of pediatric ALL—an intrinsic feature that critically influences clinical presentation, treatment response, and prognosis [30]. Through comprehensive multidimensional data analysis, we systematically characterized gene expression patterns across ALL cell populations and their associations with clinical features. This revealed distinct chemotherapy sensitivities among cellular subgroups and identified subpopulations with higher relapse risk. Such findings lay the groundwork for novel biomarkers to guide clinical decision-making, enabling more targeted treatment strategies and moving beyond generalized therapeutic approaches, thereby improving treatment outcomes and patients’ quality of life.

  Importantly, the multifactorial relapse risk prediction model developed in this study demonstrates strong potential for clinical application. Relapse remains one of the most formidable challenges in ALL treatment, with current clinical assessments often falling short of accurately identifying high-risk patients, thus missing opportunities for early intervention. By integrating clinical parameters, genetic features, and image-derived morphological information, this model provides a robust tool for identifying patients at increased relapse risk. This enables clinicians to implement timely, risk-adapted interventions, ultimately aiming to reduce relapse rates and enhance cure rates.

  Although this study did not directly perform functional intervention experiments, the multi-omics characterization of Cluster 4 underscores its therapeutic relevance. This cluster showed high expression of key genes such as UBE2C and HMGB2, both implicated in cell cycle control, DNA repair, and immune tolerance, as confirmed by differential expression analyses. TCR lineage profiling revealed significant T-cell clonal expansion (clonality score = 0.46), indicative of an active antigen-driven immune response. Furthermore, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis highlighted activation of pathways, including p53 signaling, cell cycle regulation, and IL-17 signaling, suggesting that this cluster is involved in both proliferative and immune-regulatory processes. Collectively, these molecular and functional features support the hypothesis that Cluster 4 plays a central role in immune modulation within the ALL microenvironment and may serve as a biologically plausible target for future therapeutic development.

  Despite the progress made in enhancing precision diagnosis and individualized treatment prediction for ALL, this study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the data used were primarily derived from a single medical center with a relatively modest sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the predictive models to broader and more diverse patient populations. Second, although the multimodal integration approach offers a more comprehensive perspective, technical challenges remain in data standardization, feature extraction, and the optimization of information fusion. These factors may influence the stability and interpretability of the predictive results. Furthermore, while DL significantly improves predictive accuracy, its “black-box” nature reduces model transparency, which can hinder its clinical acceptance. Future work should focus on refining model architectures and integrating explainable AI (XAI) techniques to improve interpretability and foster greater trust in clinical practice.

  Looking ahead, emerging machine learning approaches such as graph neural networks (GNNs) and self-supervised learning (SSL) hold promise for addressing current limitations and further enhancing model performance. GNNs are well-suited for modeling non-Euclidean spatial relationships, such as cell-cell interactions within the tissue microenvironment, which could improve the resolution at which spatial heterogeneity is captured. In this context, GNNs could be used to build patient similarity graphs based on multi-omics features or to model immune cell interaction networks derived from scRNA-seq and TCR-seq data. Meanwhile, SSL could uncover latent structures in unlabeled single-cell imaging or transcriptomic datasets, reducing reliance on extensive manual annotations and improving generalizability, especially in small pediatric cohorts. Together, these cutting-edge methods may enhance model robustness and adaptability in diverse clinical scenarios.

  To validate the real-world utility of our model, we plan to collaborate with additional clinical centers to conduct prospective studies involving larger and more diverse patient cohorts. In parallel, experimental methods such as CRISPR screening and advanced single-cell sequencing will be employed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underpinning ALL progression and treatment resistance, providing new avenues for precision therapeutic development.

  The findings of this study have important translational implications. On one hand, the DL-based imaging recognition model could facilitate early screening and rapid diagnosis, particularly in resource-limited settings where rapid diagnostic throughput is critical. On the other hand, the identification of immune subpopulations and specific TCR rearrangements at single-cell resolution may aid in the early detection of high-risk patients and inform personalized immunotherapy strategies. Clinically, the model could serve as an auxiliary diagnostic tool at initial presentation, especially for pediatric patients with atypical hematological profiles but suspicious imaging features. Additionally, following induction therapy, the model could assist in re-assessing MRD risk by detecting subtle morphological changes, thereby guiding timely adjustments to treatment regimens. Finally, key genes identified in this study—UBE2C and HMGB2—were consistently overexpressed across leukemic cell clusters. UBE2C is strongly associated with cell cycle progression, while HMGB2 plays critical roles in chromatin dynamics and transcriptional regulation. Both are enriched in pathways related to mitosis and DNA repair, implicating them in leukemic cell proliferation and aggressiveness. These characteristics highlight their potential as therapeutic targets, and their functional annotations provide a basis for the development of novel targeted or combinatorial treatment strategies for pediatric ALL.
