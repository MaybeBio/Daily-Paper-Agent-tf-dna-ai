# Analysis of the interaction of TATA-box binding protein 1 and 2 from Taenia solium to TATA-box: structural factors related to selectivity and affinity


## ABSTRACT
  TATA-box binding protein (TBP) is a core subunit of the transcription factor TFIID and plays a pivotal role in recognizing the TATA-box in protein-coding genes, facilitating the assembly of the transcription preinitiation complex. In Taenia solium, only one TBP isoform (TsTBP1) has been previously reported. Here, we identify and characterize a second isoform, TsTBP2, using a combination of molecular biology and bioinformatics approaches. TsTBP2 shares 42% primary sequence identity with TsTBP1 and exhibits distinct expression patterns between cysticerci and adult stages. To investigate the molecular determinants of DNA recognition, selectivity, and binding affinity, we performed molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations for both TsTBPs with various TATA-box sequences. Our results reveal that TsTBP1 exhibits higher affinity for T. solium TATA-box sequences compared to the consensus AdML TATA-box (TATAAAAG), largely due to the specific interaction of critical phenylalanine residues with the DNA minor groove, which induces DNA bending and stabilizes the TBP-DNA complex. Furthermore, analysis of the Buckle parameter indicates that these Phe residues are the principal contributors to DNA distortion. To our knowledge, this study represents the first analysis of TBP selectivity and affinity in cestodes, providing insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying transcriptional regulation in T. solium.


## Introduction
  Taenia solium is a cestode that infects both humans and pigs. The adult stage resides in the human small intestine, where it can grow up and produce thousands of eggs per day. When ingested, these eggs develop into cysticerci in various tissues of pigs and humans, causing cysticercosis, whose most severe manifestation in humans is the neurocysticercosis [1,2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) has identified T. solium as a significant public health problem in many countries with poor sanitation and hygiene, particularly in Latin America, Africa, and Asia [3]. The global disease burden of T. solium is considered high, with millions of people affected worldwide. Control and prevention strategies include improving sanitation, ensuring proper cooking of pork, and implementing mass drug administration programs [4].

  Transcription is a fundamental process in the expression of protein-coding genes, whereby the genetic information encoded in DNA is transcribed into RNA molecules. In eukaryotic cells, transcription occurs in the nucleus and is carried out by the three RNA polymerases (Pol I, II, and III). It is tightly regulated to ensure appropriate gene expression across different cell types and under various physiological conditions [5].

  The transcription preinitiation complex (PIC) is an assembly of proteins that forms at the promoter region of a gene before transcription initiation in eukaryotic cells. The formation of the PIC marks the beginning of transcription and represents a highly coordinated and dynamic process involving multiple protein factors. Pol II cannot initiate transcription on its own; it requires the assistance of transcription factors and co-activators to recognize specific DNA sequences in the gene’s promoter region. A key component of the PIC is the TATA-binding protein (TBP), a subunit of the TFIID complex. In TATA-containing genes, TBP specifically recognizes and binds to the TATA-box sequence, a conserved DNA element located within the core promoter [6].

  The TATA box is a DNA sequence element commonly found upstream of the transcription start site (TSS) in eukaryotic genes. It is typically located between 25 and 35 base pairs upstream of the TSS, although its exact position can vary depending on the gene and organism. A consensus sequence of TATAWAR characterizes the TATA box, although variations of this sequence can also be recognized by TBP [7].

  Once TBP is bound to the TATA box, it facilitates the recruitment of additional transcription factors and co-activators, leading to the complete assembly of the PIC. Other factors, such as TFIIA, TFIIB, TFIIF, and TFIIH, join the complex, stabilizing the PIC and positioning Pol II at the TSS. As the PIC is assembled and Pol II is correctly positioned, the DNA strands in the promoter region are unwound, enabling Pol II to access the template strand and initiate RNA synthesis. This transition marks the shift from the PIC to the active initiation complex, at which point the process of transcription begins [8].

  The assembly of the transcription PIC is a highly regulated and precise process, ensuring that transcription occurs only when required and in response to specific cellular signals. Dysregulation of PIC formation can significantly impact gene expression, development, and various cellular processes, contributing to numerous diseases and disorders [9–11]. Consequently, components of the PIC represent attractive drug targets for the development of antimicrobial, antiparasitic, and antitumor agents [12,13].

  In this study, we isolate and characterize the gene and cDNA encoding TsTBP2, and employ homology modeling, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulations to gain insights into the recognition and selectivity of the T. solium TATA box by TsTBP1 and TsTBP2.


## Materials and methods

### Ethics statement
  The institutional ethics committee approved the animal protocol for antibody production in rabbits (permission no. 2022–435). The study was conducted in strict accordance with the Official Mexican Standard for the Production, Care, and Use of Laboratory Animals (NOM-062-ZOO-1999) and the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the U.S National Institutes of Health.


### Tstbp2 gene isolation and analysis
  Preparation of T. solium genomic DNA and screening of 100,000 clones from a λZAPII genomic DNA library of T. solium cysticerci were carried out as previously described [14], using a full-length cDNA probe encoding TsTBP2. The clones obtained were amplified using a primer from the λZAPII vector, purified, and cloned into the pCRII vector (Invitrogen, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s directions. Plasmids were sequenced on an automated DNA sequencer ABI Prism model 373 (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA). Nucleotide sequences and multiple alignments were analyzed using PCGENE and Clustal Omega. The resulting sequence was validated against the T. solium Genome Project database [15]. Core promoter element analysis was carried out using the AliBaba2.1 server (http://gene-regulation.com/pub/programs/alibaba2/).


### Plasmid constructions and luciferase reporter assay
  The core promoter region of Tstbp2 (containing TATA-box, Inr and DPE) was cloned into a pGL4.10 vector (Promega, USA) using the NheI and HindIII restriction sites. Additionally, three mutated versions of the core promoter were generated (DPE-less, TATA-less and TATA-Inr-DPE-less) and cloned into pGL4.10. To evaluate luciferase activity, HEK-293 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% (vol/vol) FBS, penicillin (100 U/mL), and streptomycin (100 μg/mL) at 37°C in a 5% (vol/vol) CO₂ incubator. Cells (180,000 per well) were seeded in 12-well plates in DMEM without phenol red, supplemented with 5% (vol/vol) charcoal/dextran-treated FBS, 24 h prior to transfection. For the assays, 500 ng of each luciferase reporter construct was co-transfected with 10 ng of the pRL-TK Renilla expression vector (Promega) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). Twenty-four hours post-transfection, cells were lysed, and luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega). Luminometric readings were obtained with a Veritas microplate luminometer (Turner Biosystems, USA). Statistical analysis was performed using two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc test in Prism v10.0 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA).


### cDNA amplification and relative expression of Tstbp1 and Tstbp2
  Tstbp1 and Tstbp2 cDNA were amplified by PCR using Crystal Taq Master (2X) (Jena Bioscience, Jena, Germany) using the primers TsTBP1-X1 (5'-ATGCAGCCAACCCCCATCAATCAG-3') and TsTBP1-X2 (5'-TTAGCCAGTAAGTGCTGG-3') to amplify Tstbp1, and TsTBP2-A (5'-ATGGATAGCGCTGGTCCAAT-3') and TsTBP2-X2 (5'-TCAAGCCATCTTCCTGAAGTTC-3') for Tstbp2. The PCR cycling conditions were as follows: denaturation step of 94°C for 5 min; 30 cycles of 94°C for 1 min, 60°C for 30s, and 72°C for 1 min; followed by a final extension step at 72°C for 15 min. PCR products were visualized by agarose gel electrophoresis.

  For qPCR, 3 µg of total RNA from larval and adult stages of T. solium were reverse transcribed to cDNA using SMART Scribe Reverse Transcriptase (Takara Bio, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For each reaction, 100 ng of cDNA was used in a final volume of 25 µL, in triplicate. The primers TsTBP1-F1 (5'-CTTCTCCTCCGGCAAAATGG-3') and TsTBP1-R1 (5'-GATATGGAACCGCACGTCAC-3') were used to amplify Tstbp1, and TsTBP2-F (5'-CGAGAACCGCGAGTAACTTG-3') and TsTBP2-R (5'-CAAGGCGAATGGGGAACTTC-3') for Tstbp2. Amplification of T. solium Cu/ZnSOD was performed using primers TsSOD-6 (5'-AAGCACGGCTTTCACGTCC-3') and TsSOD-2 (5'-ACGACCCCCAGCGTTGCC-3'). Reactions were run with PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix in a StepOne Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA). Cycling conditions were: denaturation step of 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s, 60°C for 1 min, and 72°C for 30s. Tstbp1 and Tstbp2 mRNA levels were normalized to TsCu/ZnSOD expression, and relative mRNA amounts were calculated using the comparative Ct method [16]. Statistical analysis was performed using two-way ANOVA in Prism v10.0 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA).


### Antibody production and protein identification
  The peptides pTsTBP1-N (MQPTPINQLVSVVGSYAAPSSTQAHSRPPYTPNTPG) and pTsTBP2-N (MDSAGPNSSSKAVDSGPLLVHKFVP) were synthesized for antibody production in rabbits as previously described [17]. Briefly, two 12-week-old New Zealand rabbits were immunized subcutaneously with 50 μg of peptide plus 25 μg of Quil-A adjuvant (InvivoGen, CA, USA) in 0.5 mL of sterile saline solution, four times at two-week intervals. Rabbits were anesthetized with ketamine (50 mg/kg) plus xylazine (5 mg/kg) via intramuscular injection according to international anesthesia guidelines. Blood was collected by cardiac puncture 60 days post-immunization, and sera were obtained by centrifugation at 5000 g for 15 min at 4°C.

  For Western blotting, 5 μg of T. solium nuclear extract per lane was separated and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were incubated with rabbit anti-pTsTBP1-N or anti-pTsTBP2-N antibodies, or with normal rabbit IgG, at a 1:100 dilution. After washing with PBS containing 0.3% Tween, membranes were incubated with peroxidase-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG. Bound antibodies were visualized using 3,3'-diaminobenzidine and 1% H₂O₂.


### Homology modeling
  As no experimental structures are available for TsTBP1 or TsTBP2, their three-dimensional structures were generated using the homology modeling module implemented in Yasara Structure v19.1.27 (YASARA GmbH, Vienna, Austria) [18] with the PDB entries 4B0A, 1NVP, and 1QNA as templates. Model quality was assessed using SAVES (https://saves.mbi.ucla.edu/), yielding ERRAT overall quality factors of 98.3 for TsTBP1 and 95.5 for TsTBP2 [16], and PROCHECK G-values of 0.10 for TsTBP1 and 0.24 for TsTBP2 [16,19], indicating that the models were of sufficient quality for further simulations.


### Molecular docking and molecular dynamics on DNA-TsTBP complexes
  The initial DNA-TsTBP complexes were generated using HDOCK Web Server (http://hdock.phys.hust.edu.cn/) [20], which supports both protein – protein and nucleic acid – protein docking. HDOCK employs a hybrid docking strategy incorporating experimental binding-site information and small-angle X-ray scattering data. The TATA-box DNA sequences docked to the homology models of TsTBP1 and TsTBP2 are listed in Supplementary Table 1. The predicted poses with the most negative docking scores (indicating better binding) were selected for molecular dynamics simulations. Simulations were performed in Yasara Structure v.19.1.27 (Yasara GmB, Vienna, Austria) [18] using the suite’s built-in macros. Each complex was placed in an 85 Å × 85 Å × 85 Å water box with periodic boundary conditions. TIP3 water molecules were added to achieve a density of 0.997 g/cm3. The temperature and pH were set to 298 K and 7.4, respectively. Sodium (Na+) and chloride (Cl−) ions were added for charge neutralization. The particle mesh Ewald (PME) method was used with an 8 Å cutoff, and the AMBER14 force field was applied. Simulations used a 2.5 fs time step, recording snapshots every 200 ps for a total simulation time of 100 ns. Root mean square deviation (RMSD) values over time were calculated, and ligand binding energies (LBEs) were estimated using MM-PBSA. Residues interacting with DNA were analyzed using PDIViz v1.2.4a (http://melolab.org/mbl/pdiviz) [21].


### DNA parameters analysis
  To better characterize DNA conformational changes, x3DNA (http://web.x3dna.org/) [22] was used to analyze single-base-pair parameters. The w3DNA server provides tools for nucleic acid structure analysis, including the spatial arrangement of base pairs described by six rigid-body parameters: three translational (Shear, Stretch, and Stagger) and three rotational (Buckle, Propeller, and Opening) [23,24].


## Results

### Genomic analysis of Tstbp2 and protein product
  We isolated and analyzed the cDNA and genomic sequence encoding Tstbp2 (GenBank ID: PX206242), both of which were identical to the sequence reported in the T. solium genome project (TSM_contig_00490:671–1,828, PRJNA170813). Analysis of the core and proximal promoter regions of the Tstbp2 gene (Figure 1) revealed putative binding sites for transcription factors such as AP-2, NF-1, HNF, YY1, and TBP. A TSS was identified, containing an A+1 (GCACTTA). Moreover, a TATA-box (TATAAAAA) was located at − 29 bp, and a downstream promoter element (DPE; TCGTA) was observed at + 28 bp. The coding sequence is composed of five exons (exon 1: 125 bp; exon 2: 244 bp; exon 3: 105 bp; exon 4: 95 bp; exon 5: 81 bp) separated by four introns (intron I: 29 bp; intron II: 45 bp; intron III: 101 bp; intron IV: 334 bp). The intron – exon junctions display the canonical NGT – AGN donor – acceptor sites required for splicing. Additionally, a noncanonical polyadenylation signal (AAUACA) was identified in the 3' UTR. The Tstbp2 gene produces a cDNA containing a 648-bp coding sequence and encodes a protein of 215 amino acid residues (TsTBP2) with a theoretical molecular weight of 24.15 kDa and an isoelectric point (pI) of 9.7. In comparison, TsTBP1 consists of 238 amino acids, has a theoretical molecular weight of 26.69 kDa, and a pI of 10.6. Both proteins are highly similar in size and intron – exon organization (Supplementary Table 2). TsTBP2 shows high sequence identity (Figure 2) with Echinococcus granulosus TBP2 (EgTBP2, 97% identity, XP_024347065.1) and Echinococcus multilocularis TBP2 (EmTBP2, 96% identity, CDS36966.1), but low identity with Homo sapiens TBP like 2 (HsTBPL2, 40% identity, NP_950248.2). ### Core promoter functionality in the Tstbp2 gene
  To understand the functional relevance of the elements identified in the Tstbp2 core promoter, we performed luciferase reporter assays using pGL4.10 constructs containing the wild-type promoter or three mutated versions (DPE-less, TATA-less, and TATA – Inr – DPE-less).

  The relative luciferase activities obtained were as follows: Tstbp2 wild type, 137.5 ± 5.2 AU (arbitrary units); Tstbp2 DPE-less, 111.1 ± 7.3 AU; Tstbp2 TATA-less, 119.3 ± 8.5 AU; Tstbp2 TATA – Inr – DPE-less, 26.4 ± 1.9 AU; and empty pGL4.10, 11.4 ± 2.7 AU (Figure 3(A)). We observed a decrease in luciferase activity of approximately 14% when the TATA-box was mutated and 19% when the DPE was mutated. Notably, simultaneous mutation of the TATA-box, Inr, and DPE resulted in an 81% reduction in luciferase activity. Statistical analysis revealed significant differences between the wild-type Tstbp2 construct and all other constructs, except between the DPE-less and TATA-less mutants, for which no significant difference was detected. ### TsTBP1 and TsTBP2 cDNA amplification, mRNA relative expression, and protein detection
  PCR amplification of TsTBP1 and TsTBP2 yielded products of 717 bp and 648 bp, respectively (Figure 3(B)). Additionally, qPCR was performed using cDNA from T. solium cysticerci and adults to determine the mRNA expression levels of Tstbp1 and Tstbp2. For Tstbp1, the relative expression was 1.0 ± 0.11 AU in the larval stage and 2.65 ± 0.12 AU in the adult stage. For Tstbp2, expression was 0.65 ± 0.02 AU in the larval stage and 0.17 ± 0.01 AU in the adult stage (Figure 3(C)). Moreover, TsTBP1 and TsTBP2 were detected in nuclear extracts from the larval stage of T. solium by Western blot, using antibodies generated in rabbits against peptides containing the N-terminal domain of each TBP. A band of approximately 26 kDa was observed for TsTBP1 and a band of approximately 24 kDa for TsTBP2 (Figure 3(D)).


### TsTBP1 and TsTBP2 protein comparison and homology model
  Although the primary sequences of TsTBP1 and TsTBP2 share only 42% identic residues (Figure 4A), their 3D homology-modeled structures reveal a high degree of structural similarity. TsTBP1 consists of an N-terminal domain of 48 amino acids (residues 1–48) without a defined secondary structure, and a C-terminal domain of 190 amino acids (residues 49–238). The C-terminal domain contains four α-helices (α1: residues 66–72; α2: residues 113–130; α3: residues 156–162; α4: residues 204–220) and nine β-sheets (β1: residues 50–59; β2: residues 86–90; β3: residues 95–99; β4: residues 104–108; β5: residues 143–149; β6: residues 167–170; β7: residues 177–180; β8: residues 187–190; β9: residues 195–199). Figure 4.Structural analysis of TsTBP1 and TsTBP2. (A) sequence alignment of TsTBP1 and TsTBP2. Conserved residues are highlighted in gray. Secondary structure elements are represented above and below each sequence. Numbers on the right indicate amino acid positions. (B) 3D homology models of TsTBP1 and TsTBP2 shown in ribbon representation, displaying the classical saddle shape (top) and the protein surface colored by electrostatic potential in the DNA-binding domain (bottom; blue: positive, red: negative).

  Similarly, TsTBP2 comprises an N-terminal domain of 38 amino acids (residues 1–38) without a defined secondary structure and a C-terminal domain of 177 amino acids (residues 39–215). The C-terminal domain contains four α-helices (α1: residues 56–62; α2: residues 103–120; α3: residues 146–152; α4: residues 194–210) and nine β-sheets (β1: residues 40–49; β2: residues 76–80; β3: residues 85–89; β4: residues 94–100; β5: residues 133–139; β6: residues 157–160; β7: residues 164–171; β8: residues 176–180; β9: residues 185–191).

  The 3D homology models of TsTBP1 and TsTBP2 exhibit the classical saddle-shaped conformation characteristic of the TBP family. Both display a concave DNA-binding surface with a predominantly positive electrostatic potential, as well as regions involved in interactions with TFIIA and TFIIB (Figure 4(B)).


### Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation
  The HDOCK web server was used to perform molecular docking analysis of TsTBP1 and TsTBP2 with four TATA-box-containing promoter sequences from pAT5, Ts2CysPrx, AdML and AdML mut (Supplementary Table 1). This analysis allowed the identification of the amino acids interacting with each TBP – DNA complex. Additionally, in silico molecular dynamics simulations were performed to corroborate the molecular docking results.

  As expected, the residues interacting with the phosphate backbone were mainly basic residues forming electrostatic interactions, particularly along the four base pairs constituting the TATA-box. Conversely, the residues interacting with the minor groove were primarily hydrophobic, except at the center of the TBP, where Asn and Thr residues formed hydrogen bonds with the nitrogen and oxygen atoms of the central bases within the TATA-box element (Supplementary Table 3). We also observed the presence of the four Phe residues required for DNA unwinding, a pattern previously described for human TBP [25].

  To further evaluate the stability of these interactions, we monitored the RMSD over the simulation time (Figure 5). After an initial relaxation period of ~ 2 ns for most complexes, RMSD values showed only slight variations, indicating that the predicted structures had reached equilibrium and could be used for further analysis. The only exception was the TsTBP2–Ts2CysPrx complex, which exhibited a significant RMSD fluctuation at ~ 35 ns before reaching equilibrium. Figure 5.Root-mean-square deviations (RMSD) of native and mutated TsTBP proteins obtained from molecular dynamics simulations.

  We also analyzed the root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF). The results showed a decrease in RMSF values when both TsTBP1 and TsTBP2 were bound to a TATA-box (Supplementary Figure S1A). Notably, the molecular dynamics simulations confirmed that the amino acids interacting with the four TATA-box sequences in both TsTBPs were the same as those identified in the docking analysis. Supplementary Figure S1B shows a bottom view of the TsTBP – TATA-box complex.

  Given that Phe residues are important for DNA binding and are located within the DNA-binding domain of TBPs from various species, we generated 3D models in which these Phe residues were substituted with Ala. The resulting mutant proteins were designated TsTBP1mut (Phe83Ala, Phe100Ala, Phe174Ala, Phe191Ala) and TsTBP2mut (Phe73Ala, Phe90Ala, Phe164Ala, Phe181Ala). These models were used to evaluate DNA-binding affinity in comparison with the native TsTBPs.

  For both TsTBP mutants, RMSD profiles were similar to those of the native TsTBPs, suggesting that the structures had reached equilibrium and could be used for affinity analysis (Figure 5). Interestingly, molecular docking analyses revealed reduced affinity for the TATA-box in both HDOCK and MM-PBSA evaluations (Table 1). In HDOCK, for the pAT5, Ts2CysPrx, and AdML TATA-box sequences, we observed a decrease of approximately 100 kcal/mol in binding scores. However, for the mutated AdML TATA-box, the affinity was comparable to that of the native TsTBPs. Likewise, MM-PBSA results also indicated reduced binding affinity for the TATA-box in the mutant proteins, although the overall binding profile trends remained consistent with those of the native TsTBPs.


### The buckle parameter is responsible for TATA-binding and selectivity
  Analysis performed using the x3DNA web server identified the Buckle parameter as the principal factor in recognizing the TATA-box. This parameter measures the rotation of the T-A-T-A base pairs present in each TATA-box probe tested. Results showed that the Buckle parameter is higher in both T. solium TATA boxes (above 36°) compared to the AdML TATA box (below 30°) or its mutated version (below 20°) for both TsTBPs (Figure 6). Figure 6.Buckle parameter analysis of TsTBPs and TATA-box sequences. Values (in degrees) along the TATA-box for TsTBP1, TsTBP2, TsTBP1mut, and TsTBP2mut complexes are shown.

  To further confirm the role of topological changes induced by Phe residues, molecular docking and molecular dynamics experiments were repeated using TsTBP1mut and TsTBP2mut. The results demonstrated that the TsTBPmut–DNA complexes exhibited lower ligand-binding energies and reduced topological changes (buckle values of ~ 8°), highlighting the critical role of these four residues in DNA binding (Figure 6).


## Discussion
  TBP is essential for initiating transcription by RNA polymerases (I, II, III) in eukaryotic genes with promoters with or without a TATA box [26].

  In this study, we identify a gene that encodes a TBP2 that contain Inr, DPE and TATA-box motifs and describe the functionality of them using a luciferase activity assay. The promoter region harbors multiple transcription factor binding sites, including AP-2, NF-1, HNF, and YY1, which has also been identified in other T. solium genes [27–30]. Interestingly, the classic TATA-box, Inr, and DPE motifs were identified in the core promoter and located at the correct positions for functionality. Notably, the Tstbp1 gene is TATA-less but contains Inr and DPE motifs [31], whereas Tstbp2 contains TATA-box, Inr, and DPE motifs, suggesting differences in the regulatory mechanisms controlling the expression of these two transcription factors in T. solium. Luciferase activity assays using the Tstbp2 core promoter demonstrated a comparable reduction in transcriptional activity when either the TATA-box or the DPE motif was mutated, relative to the wild-type promoter. Interestingly, there was no statistically significant difference in luciferase activity between the Tstbp2 DPE-less and TATA-less constructs, suggesting that the contribution of each of these elements is equivalent when assessed separately. In contrast, the TATA – Inr – DPE-less construct exhibited a dramatic decrease in activity compared to the wild type, highlighting the important contribution of the Inr motif to Tstbp2 expression. However, the interaction of all three elements appears to be more critical than the contribution of each individual motif alone as observed previously [32,33].

  The Tstbp2 gene has a genomic architecture similar to Tstbp1, comprising 5 exons and 4 introns. Comparatively, TsTBP1 and TsTBP2 are encoded by different genes, producing cDNAs of 717 bp and 648 bp, and encoding proteins of 26.69 kDa and 24.15 kDa, respectively. However, their relative mRNA expression differs between the adult and larval stages: Tstbp1 is more expressed in adults, whereas Tstbp2 is more expressed in cysticerci. This suggests differences in expression regulation and distinct biological roles for these transcription factors in T. solium as observed previously in mouse [34], zebrafish [35] or Xenopus [36].

  The predicted homology models of TsTBP1 and TsTBP2 display the classical ribbon shape highly conserved among TBPs, consisting of a concave anti-parallel β-sheet domain enriched in positive residues that are critical for interaction with the DNA minor groove [37]. The structures of the TsTBP-DNA complexes predicted using HDOCK were consistent with other TBP-DNA complexes available in the Protein Data Bank, where TBP binds and bends the TATA-box element. Previously, we demonstrated that TsTBP1 has high affinity for TATA-box DNA sequences from T. solium, but low affinity for the AdML TATA-box sequence and very low affinity for the TGTG-mutated sequence [30]. Similar findings have been reported for the TBP of Entamoeba histolytica with mutated TATA-box sequences [38], and in mouse embryonic stem cells when testing mouse TBP, TRF2, TRF3, or yeast TBP with TATA-containing, TATA-like, or TATA-less promoters [39]. In our molecular docking models, both TsTBPs exhibited higher affinity for pAT5 and Ts2CysPrx TATA-boxes (Table 1), consistent with EMSA observations for TsTBP1 using T. solium nuclear extracts [30]. The use of molecular docking and molecular dynamic simulations is becoming a standard in computational studies since it gives valuable and more reliable information about the complexes between a ligand and its target or in the analysis of macro biomolecular interactions, including potential conformational changes that could affect the binding and have an impact in the activation or inactivation of the complex [40–42]. In this context, combining molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations allows for more accurate predictions of molecular affinity by incorporating the effects of explicit solvent molecules and conformational changes [43]. This strategy has been successfully applied to explain ligand selectivity and the consequences of conformational changes in protein activation or inhibition [44–47]. The predicted binding energies for these complexes correlated with experimental results obtained with TsTBP1-DNA complexes, supporting the predictive value of the docking – molecular dynamics approach. Interestingly, TsTBP1 displayed higher affinity for both T. solium sequences than TsTBP2, suggesting a primary role in the transcriptional initiation of housekeeping and developmental genes, respectively.

  RMSF measures the average deviation of an atom or residue from its reference position over time. Higher RMSF values indicate regions with greater conformational flexibility, which may correspond to important ligand-binding or protein – protein interaction sites [48,49]. In unbound TBPs, the regions with higher RMSF values participate in DNA binding. As expected, RMSF values decreased upon DNA binding. Notably, in bound TBP structures, the regions with the highest RMSF values correspond to loops known to participate in TFIIA binding during PIC assembly [50].

  The principal structural features of TBP-DNA complexes observed in docking studies were preserved in the predicted complexes. TBP-induced DNA bending is believed to contribute to complex stabilization and transcription initiation, facilitating the recruitment of RNA polymerase and other proteins [51,52] and consequently affecting transcriptional activity [53]. Along with electrostatic interactions between Lys and Arg residues and the phosphate backbone, the intercalation of Phe residues into the minor groove plays a crucial role in generating DNA bending [54].

  These Phe residues are located in the base pairs adjacent to the TATA-box. The mode of interaction of these Phe residues with neighboring bases, and the DNA bending they induce, explain the differences in affinity observed in both in silico and in vitro experiments.

  To further investigate this role, we replaced the four Phe residues inserted into the minor groove with Ala residues (TsTBPmut) and analyzed the effect on ligand binding energy. As expected, TsTBPmut-DNA complexes displayed lower binding energies, indicating reduced stability. Remarkably, the differences reached approximately 100 kcal/mol, underscoring the critical importance of Phe residues in complex stability.

  The Buckle parameter describes the rotation of a base pair about the X-axis (in degrees); thus, Phe insertion increases deformation between these base pairs. The most pronounced variations were observed in the Buckle parameter of bases adjacent to the TATA-box element due to Phe insertion (Phe83, Phe100, Phe174, and Phe191 for TsTBP1; Phe73, Phe90, Phe164, and Phe181 for TsTBP2). In T. solium sequences, the Buckle parameter exceeded 36°, compared with below 30° in AdML sequences and below 20° in mutated sequences. This suggests that Phe insertion in TsTBPs induces greater distortion of bases adjacent to the TATA-box, enhancing DNA binding efficiency, as reflected in MM-PBSA binding energy calculations. Analyses of TBPmut-DNA complexes support this conclusion: the methyl group of Ala cannot induce the same topological changes as the aromatic ring of Phe. For example, the buckle values for base pairs neighboring the TATA sequence were approximately − 25° in complexes with TsTBP1 and TsTBP2 but changed to − 8° in TsTBP1mut and TsTBP2mut complexes, which also showed lower binding energies. Structural and biochemical studies have demonstrated that these Phe residues intercalate into the minor groove of the TATA-box, inducing a sharp bend in the DNA helix that facilitates the formation of the preinitiation complex [55,56]. Molecular dynamics and computational analyses further support that the aromatic side chains of Phe contribute significantly to complex stability through van der Waals and π-stacking interactions with base pairs, enhancing TBP’s binding affinity and selectivity for TATA-containing promoters [54].

  Interestingly, TsTBP selectivity for T. solium sequences appears to be only partially explained by differences in bases immediately adjacent to the TATA-box, as these are similar in the four sequences analyzed. This suggests a critical role for bases located beyond three base pairs from the TATA-box element, an observation also reported for human TBP [57].

  TsTBP1 and TsTBP2 are isoforms of TBP, which play essential roles in transcriptional regulation in eukaryotic cells. While encoded by separate genes and sharing high sequence similarity, they exhibit distinct tissue-specific expression patterns and functions. Understanding the specific roles of TBP1 and TBP2 in gene regulation is critical for deciphering the complex mechanisms of transcriptional control and for exploring their potential implications in development, disease, and other biological processes [46]. Therefore, the design of drugs targeting components of transcription initiation complexes involving TBP must take these differences into account.
