# CX26 promotes pancreatic cancer progression by competitively inhibiting interaction of c-Myc with PSMD2 and enhancing c-Myc stability


## Background
  Pancreatic cancer (PC) is highly aggressive and fatal and has dismal prognostic outcomes, primarily due to its late-stage diagnosis and limited effective treatments. The molecular mechanisms triggering PC progression are largely unclear. This study investigated how CX26 contributes to pancreatic cancer development and its potential as a therapeutic target.


## Methods
  CX26 expression in PC and its clinical significance were examined through bioinformatics analysis. The biological functions of CX26 in PC cells were explored via colony formation, CCK-8, and proteomic analysis. To explore the mechanisms by which CX26 regulates c-Myc stability, co-immunoprecipitation, immunofluorescence, and molecular docking were utilized to investigate the interactions among CX26, PSMD2, and c-Myc. Additionally, subcutaneous xenografts in nude mice were applied to assess the impact of CX26 on PC progression in vivo.


## Results
  Bioinformatics analysis indicated the upregulation of CX26 in PC tissues. In addition, CX26 expression was closely related to dismal prognostic outcomes. Further study revealed that CX26 promotes PC progression in vitro and in vivo. Proteomic analysis identified c-Myc as the key downstream target of CX26. Mechanistically, CX26 indirectly regulated c-Myc stability via PSMD2 rather than directly interacting with c-Myc. Moreover, CX26 competes with c-Myc to bind to the Armadillo-like helical domain of PSMD2, thereby preventing c-Myc proteasomal degradation.


## Conclusions
  This study suggests that CX26 facilitates PC progression by stabilizing c-Myc through competitively inhibiting the binding of PSMD2 to c-Myc. Given the challenges in directly inhibiting c-Myc, this novel regulatory mechanism provides valuable insights into PC biology and reveals that targeting CX26 is a possible way to promote c-Myc degradation and suppress PC growth.

## Introduction
  Although pancreatic cancer (PC), as a highly malignant neoplasm of the digestive tract, ranks twelfth in global cancer incidence, it is the sixth leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, accounting for nearly 5% of all cancer-associated deaths [1]. By 2040, PC is predicted to become the second major reason for cancer-related mortality in the USA, surpassed only by lung cancer [2]. Surgical resection is the most effective treatment; nevertheless, only 15–20% of patients are eligible for this approach, as the majority are diagnosed at an advanced stage, rendering tumors inoperable [3, 4]. Consequently, the prognosis for PC remains poor, with a five-year survival rate of only 8–10% [3, 4]. Although molecular targeted therapies have shown promise in improving treatment outcomes for various malignancies, the key biomarkers and molecular mechanisms driving PC progression remain inadequately understood [5, 6]. Therefore, further understanding the associated mechanisms is crucial for improving PC patient prognosis and formulating more efficient anti-PC treatment strategies.

  c-Myc, a member of the Myc family of transcription factors, has an essential effect on modulating events including cell proliferation and metabolism during the cell cycle [7]. It exerts a critical effect on driving the occurrence of many cancers, including PC [8–11]. However, the inherent instability of c-Myc presents a significant challenge in its therapeutic targeting [12], necessitating a deeper understanding of the mechanisms that regulate its stability. Consequently, identifying the regulatory pathways that contribute to c-Myc stabilization is essential, as it may provide novel insights into PC progression and potential therapeutic interventions.

  Connexins are known for their effects on intercellular communication and cellular homeostasis. However, the understanding of connexins has evolved over time, especially in cancer [13]. Traditionally, connexins are considered to be tumor suppressors of various tumors [14–17]. This prevailing view, however, has been increasingly questioned by recent findings, which suggest that connexins may also exhibit protumorigenic functions [18–21]. Connexin 26 (CX26), a member of the connexin family, has been shown to contribute to tumorigenesis and metastasis in multiple cancers [22–26]. However, to date, its effect on PC occurrence and progression remains largely unexplored.

  According to our findings, CX26 has an important effect on PC proliferation through enhancing c-Myc stability. From a mechanistic perspective, we showed that CX26 interacted with PSMD2, a central unit of the 19S regulatory particle, and prevented c-Myc from binding to PSMD2, which could therefore protect c-Myc from proteasomal degradation. Notably, CX26 competes with c-Myc for binding to the Armadillo-like helical domain of PSMD2, and this interaction effectively inhibits c-Myc degradation. Consequently, elevated CX26 expression in PC cells enhances c-Myc stability, thereby facilitating cell proliferation and cancer progression. This newly identified mechanism offers valuable insights into the regulatory pathways that sustain c-Myc activity in PC and underscores CX26 as a promising target for preventing c-Myc degradation.


## Materials and methods

### Reagents and antibodies
  The following antibodies were obtained from Proteintech (Wuhan, China): anti-PSMD2 (147481-AP), anti-ubiquitin (10201-2-AP), anti-Flag (66008-4-Ig), anti-His (66005-1-Ig), anti-HA (51-064-2-AP), anti-c-Myc (10828-1-AP), anti-GAPDH (10494-1-AP), anti-β-actin (20536-1-AP), and anti-Ki67(84192-4-RR). The anti-CX26 antibody was purchased from Invitrogen (Grand Island, NY, USA). Cycloheximide (CHX), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), MG132, and chloroquine (CQ) were purchased from Med Chem Express (MCE). Unless otherwise noted, the remaining materials and reagents were purchased from New Cell & Molecular Biotech Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China).


### Data analysis of public databases
  In this study, PC RNA sequencing data were acquired from several public databases, namely, the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo), the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) (http://gdc.cancer.gov/), and ArrayExpress (http://www.ebi.ac.uk/arrayexpress/) databases. Specifically, data from the GEO dataset GSE183795, which includes microarray gene expression profiles from 139 pancreatic tumor samples, 102 adjacent non-tumor tissues, and 3 normal pancreas samples from donors, were used. The mRNA expression profiling was performed using Affymetrix GeneChip Human 1.0 ST arrays at the microarray core facility of the National Cancer Institute (NCI), Frederick, MD, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The dataset, publicly available since December 1, 2022, was processed using Robust Multiarray Averaging (RMA) normalization, and batch effects were removed using the Empirical Bayes method implemented in the ComBat suite [27]. GSE183795 data were analyzed for differential expression using the Limma package. CX26 differential expression between PC and normal tissue groups in the TCGA database was examined with GEPIA2 (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/) [28].


### Clinical samples
  Twelve PC and twelve matched non-cancerous samples were acquired from the Department of Hepatobiliary Surgery, Zhujiang Hospital, Southern Medical University. All samples were gathered post-surgery and immediately preserved in liquid nitrogen. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. In addition, this study was conducted following the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki. The Ethics Committee of Zhujiang Hospital approved the study protocol (Approval No. 2025-KY-021).


### Cell culture
  AsPC-1, BxPC-3, hTERT-HPNE, PANC-1, MIA PaCa-2, SW1990, and HEK-293 T cells were originally obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). PANC-1, MIA PaCa-2, SW1990, and HEK-293T cells were kept in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) added with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Pricella, China). hTERT-HPNE, BxPC-3, and AsPC-1 cells were kept in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640 medium (Gibco, USA) which was added with 10% FBS. All the cells were subject to incubation at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Additional file 1: Table S1 provides complete cell line metadata.


### Western blotting analysis
  RIPA lysis buffer which contained a protease inhibitor cocktail for preventing protein degradation was used to extract total cellular or tissue proteins. To analyzing the protein content in accordance with specific protocols, a BCA protein assay kit (EpiZyme, China) was adopted. Protein aliquots (20–50 µg) were separated through SDS-PAGE (10% or 12% polyacrylamide gels) under reducing conditions before being transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA) with a wet transfer system (Bio-Rad, USA). With the membranes being blocked with 5% defatted dry milk (Fdbio, China) in Tris-buffered saline which contained 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) at room temperature for 60 min, they were further exposed to incubation overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting the target proteins (diluted in TBST with 5% BSA). After being rinsed three times with TBST, the membranes were exposed to incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-rabbit or anti-mouse IgG, 1:10,000 dilution) for 60 min at room temperature. The protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system and quantified using ImageJ software. GAPDH or β-actin was used as an internal control to normalize protein expression levels.


### RNA isolation and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)
  To isolate total cellular RNA following specific protocols, TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) was used. RNA content and quality were evaluated with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Subsequently, RNA (1 µg) was reverse transcribed into cDNA with an Evo M-MLV RT Mix Kit with gDNA Clean for qPCR Ver. 2 (Accurate Biology, China) following specific protocols. Then, qRT-PCR was carried out with a SYBR Green Pro Taq HS qRT-PCR Kit (Accurate Biology, China) using the QuantStudio 6-Flex system (Applied Biosystems, USA). The 20 µL reaction system was prepared, consisting of SYBR Green Pro Taq HS qPCR Master Mix (10 µL), diverse primers (0.5 µL, 10 µM), a cDNA template (2 µL), and RNase-free water (7 µL). The PCR conditions were presented as following: 30 s at 95 °C; 5 s at 95 °C; 30 s at 60 °C; and 30 s at 72 °C for a total of 40 cycles. Amplification specificity was confirmed through melting curve analysis. The comparative Ct approach (ΔΔCt) was employed for computing relative gene levels, with β-actin being the endogenous reference. Additional file 1: Table S2 lists the primer sequences.


### Generation of stable cell lines
  Plasmids and short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting CX26 and PSMD2 were acquired from GenePharma (Suzhou, China). Additional file 1: Table S3 presents the used shRNA sequences. The cells were transfected with GenePharma’s lentiviral vectors carrying plasmids for the overexpression of c-Myc, CX26, and PSMD2 or with shRNAs targeting CX26 and PSMD2 for gene knockdown to produce stable cells. After transfection, stable clones were selected and validated via qRT-PCR and Western blotting assays to confirm successful target gene overexpression or silencing.


### Colony formation assay
  The cells (500–1000/well) were inoculated into 6-well plates for 7–14 days of culture, with the medium changed at 2–3-day intervals. Following incubation, phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was added for cell washing before 30 min of fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature and an additional 20 min of staining with 1.0% crystal violet. ImageJ was used to perform colony counting.


### Cell counting kit‑8 (CCK‑8) assay
  This study analyzed cell proliferation with a CCK-8 assay in accordance with the recommended protocols. After the cells (5 × 103/well) were seeded in 96-well plates, they were allowed to adhere for a certain period. Then, the cells were subject to incubation with 10 µL of CCK-8 solution for 1–4 h at 37 °C. A microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, USA) was used to measure the absorbance at 450 nm.


### Label‑free proteomic profiling analysis
  To identify differentially expressed proteins in the CX26 knockdown group (BxPC-3 cells), label-free quantitative proteomic profiling was conducted by Shanghai Genechem Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).


### Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)
  Protein–protein interactions were investigated through Co-IP with a Pierce® Co-Immunoprecipitation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) in accordance with specific protocols. Specifically, cell lysis was completed in the prechilled IP Lysis Buffer provided in the kit, supplemented with protease inhibitors, followed by 5 min of incubation at 4 °C. Subsequently, the sample was centrifuged (13,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C) to clear the lysates. The appropriate primary antibody (2–4 µg) was first covalently coupled to the enhanced AminoLink® coupling resin following specific instructions. After antibody coupling, the resin-bound immunocomplexes were subjected to 2 h of incubation with 500–1,000 µg of cleared cell lysate at 4 °C under mild rotation to allow protein–protein interactions. Immunocomplexes were captured using resin and rinsed three times with cold wash buffer offered by the kit to remove nonspecifically bound proteins. The immunoprecipitates were eluted through 5 min of boiling in SDS-PAGE sample buffer. Afterwards, these samples were subject to SDS-PAGE and Western blotting. Next, the target proteins were detected using specific secondary antibodies prior to visualization via chemiluminescence.


### Immunofluorescence (IF) assay
  Following 20 min of fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-100, the cells were blocked for 60 min with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA), subsequently incubated with the indicated primary antibody at 4 °C overnight and probed with the secondary antibody in the dark for 2 h. Eventually, the cells were subjected to 5–10 min of staining with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), and then fluorescence observation was performed via a microscope.


### Immunohistochemistry (IHC)
  Prior to being embedded in paraffin, the tissues were immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. Next, a microtome was used to cut the tissues into 4–5 μm sections and they were mounted onto poly-L-lysine-coated slides. These slides were then deparaffinized with xylene and rehydrated with an ethanol gradient (100%, 95%, 70%). Afterwards, the sections were heated for 20 min in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for antigen retrieval. Moreover, they were incubated for 10 min with 3% hydrogen peroxide to block endogenous peroxidase activity. Following 60 min of blocking with 5% BSA at room temperature, the sections were subjected to overnight incubation with the primary antibody in blocking buffer at 4 °C, then 1 h of incubation with the biotinylated secondary antibody at room temperature, and later incubation with streptavidin-HRP (horseradish peroxidase) for 30 min. By applying 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) as the substrate, immunoreactivity was visualized. Subsequently, the slides were counterstained with hematoxylin for 1–2 min, dehydrated, and mounted with a coverslip. The stained sections were analyzed under a light microscope.


### Molecular docking
  The protein structures of PSMD2 (ID: Q13200), c-Myc (ID: P01106) and CX26 (ID: P29033) were obtained from the AlphaFold protein structure database (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/). HawkDOCK SERVER (http://cadd.zju.edu.cn/hawkdock/) was employed for molecular docking [29]. Prior to docking, protein preparation was carried out using PyMOL 2.4, including hydrogen atom addition as well as water molecules and unnecessary ligand removal. The docking results were evaluated according to the docking score, with the top 10 docking positions being output. Thereafter, the model that had the greatest score was chosen as the optimal docking model. Finally, the docking results were visualized with PyMOL 2.4 software.


### Animal experiments
  Six-week-old BALB/c nude mice were acquired from BesTest Bio-Tech Co., Ltd. (Zhuhai, China). The animal procedures (Approval No. LAEC-2023-240) were approved by the Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee of Zhujiang Hospital, Southern Medical University. The mice were given a subcutaneous injection of 3 × 106 BxPC-3 cells. Tumor growth was observed weekly through the determination of length (L) and width (W) via a digital Vernier caliper. In addition, the formula (L × W2)/2 was used to determine the tumor volume. Four weeks following injection, the mice were euthanized, and the tumors were excised, weighed, and analyzed via hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and IHC.


### Statistical analysis
  The statistical analysis was carried out using GraphPad Prism 8.0.1 and R (version 4.4.1). Survival was compared by Kaplan–Meier curve analysis between groups, with risk factors being analyzed by log-rank tests. Correlations among variables were assessed by calculating Pearson correlation coefficients. The results are indicated as the means ± standard deviations (SDs). Two-group comparisons were performed with Student’s t test, whereas comparisons among multiple groups were conducted via one-way ANOVA. P < 0.05 indicated statistical significance (*, **, ***, and **** denoted p < 0.05, < 0.01, < 0.001, and < 0.0001, respectively, whereas “ns” denotes P > 0.05).


## Results

### CX26 upregulation is associated with a dismal prognostic outcome in PC patients
  To explore the impact of CX26 on PC development, we first studied RNA sequencing data from 179 primary PCs and 171 healthy pancreatic samples in the TCGA and Genotype-Tissue Expression (GETx) databases [30]. Based on the results, CX26 expression showed obvious upregulation in PC tissues relative to normal pancreatic tissues (Fig. 1A). We then performed Kaplan–Meier survival analysis on TCGA datasets, which revealed that patients with upregulated CX26 expression had shorter overall survival (OS) (Fig. 1B) and disease-free survival (DFS) (Fig. 1C) times. Furthermore, the mRNA expression level of CX26 in PC samples was also examined in the GSE183795 dataset, which confirmed that CX26 expression was increased in PC tissues (Fig. 1D). Additionally, CX26 mRNA upregulation was closely related to shorter OS in the GSE183795 dataset (Fig. 1E) and to shorter OS and DFS in the E-MTAB-6134 dataset (Fig. 1F and G).   To further verify the above observations, CX26 mRNA and protein expression in PC cells (BxPC-3, PANC-1, SW1990, AsPC-1, and MIA PaCa-2) and normal pancreatic ductal epithelial cells (hTERT-HPNE) were analyzed. Compared with hTERT-HPNE normal cells, PC cells presented increased CX26 expression (Fig. 1H and I). Subsequently, the CX26 protein levels in 12 PC samples and matched non-carcinoma samples collected in our laboratory were analyzed. Western blot analysis revealed that the average CX26 protein level in PC samples markedly increased relative to that in non-carcinoma samples (Fig. 1J and K). These findings demonstrate that CX26 is frequently upregulated in clinical PC samples and cells and is positively related to the malignancy of PC.


### CX26 promotes PC cell proliferation in vitro
  To analyze the oncogenic effect of CX26 on PC, we knocked down CX26 in BxPC-3 and PANC-1 cells, which exhibit higher endogenous CX26 expression. Collectively, these findings indicate that CX26 regulates PC cell proliferation primarily through c-Myc.


### CX26 stabilizes c-Myc
  Notably, qRT-PCR analysis revealed that CX26 did not significantly affect c-Myc transcription (Fig. 4A and B). Thus, CX26 likely upregulates c-Myc expression independently of transcription, potentially through regulating c-Myc protein stability. For further investigation, we examined how CX26 influences c-Myc protein stability. As expected, CX26 overexpression increased c-Myc protein stability in AsPC-1 cells (Fig. 4C). In contrast, CX26 knockdown in BxPC-3 cells had the opposite effect (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, CX26 knockdown-induced c-Myc downregulation was reversed after treatment with MG-132, a proteasome inhibitor, but not with CQ, a lysosomal inhibitor (Fig. 4E and F). Additionally, CX26 overexpression increased c-Myc ubiquitination in PC cells (Fig. 4G and H). ### CX26 binds to PSMD2 to inhibit the proteasome-dependent degradation of c-Myc in PC cells
  CX26 has recently been reported to help maintain the stability of the NANOG protein via interactions with both focal adhesion kinase and NANOG [24]. Given the established role of ubiquitination in regulating c-Myc stability, we hypothesized that CX26 influences c-Myc ubiquitination-mediated degradation, potentially via direct interaction with c-Myc. In order to investigate the mechanism through which CX26 regulates c-Myc stability, co-IP assays were performed on CX26 and c-Myc. Unexpectedly, c-Myc did not coprecipitate with CX26 (Additional file 1: Fig. S2A), suggesting that CX26 may regulate c-Myc stability indirectly in PC cells.

  To further investigate this possibility, we focused on known CX26-interacting partners that also interact with c-Myc. The HitPredict database (https://www.hitpredict.org/) identified six overlapping proteins with high confidence, including TFR1, ANTR1, PSMD2, LMNA, CAV1, and ATG5. Among these, PSMD2 emerged as a candidate, because PSMD2 serves as a key component of the 19S regulatory lid and involved in ubiquitin-mediated proteasome pathway [31]. Thus, we hypothesized that PSMD2 serves as an intermediary factor linking CX26 to the ubiquitination-mediated degradation of c-Myc. Co-IP assays confirmed the interaction between both endogenous and ectopically expressed CX26 and PSMD2 (Fig. 5A; Additional file 1: Fig. S2B). Furthermore, IF staining with validated antibodies verified the intracellular colocalization of CX26 and PSMD2 in BxPC-3 and AsPC-1 cells (Additional file 1: Fig. S2D). Additionally, Co-IP assays demonstrated that PSMD2 interacts with c-Myc both endogenously and ectopically (Fig. 5B; Additional file 1: Fig. S2C), consistent with IF staining data showing intracellular colocalization of these proteins (Additional file 1: Fig. S2E). Since PSMD2 is a key component of the 19S regulatory lid and the proteasome mediates c-Myc degradation, the PSMD2-c-Myc interaction suggests a role for PSMD2 in regulating c-Myc stability. To further investigate this mechanism, PSMD2 was ectopically expressed in AsPC-1 cells and silenced in BxPC-3 cells. Notably, PSMD2 modulated c-Myc protein levels without affecting its mRNA expression (Additional file 1: Fig. S3A–D). Furthermore, PSMD2 reduced c-Myc protein stability (Additional file 1: Fig. S3E and F), an effect that was counteracted by MG132 (a proteasome inhibitor) but not by CQ (a lysosome inhibitor) (Additional file 1: Fig. S3G and H). Knockdown of PSMD2 elevated the ubiquitination level of c-Myc and prevented its proteasome-mediated degradation (Additional file 1: Fig. S3L and M), since PSMD2 is a ubiquitin receptor recognizing and directing substrates to the 20S core to conduct further processing.   Next, we silenced PSMD2 in PANC-1 cells with CX26 shRNA. As expected, CX26 knockdown significantly increased c-Myc ubiquitination and reduced c-Myc levels and stability, effects that were completely reversed by PSMD2 knockdown (Fig. 5A to F). Moreover, PSMD2 silencing restored cell proliferation and tumor growth, which had been impaired by CX26 knockdown in PC cells (Fig. 5G–O; Additional file 1: Fig. S4A), as well as the shortened survival time caused by CX26 knockdown (Additional file 1: Fig. S4B). Collectively, these findings suggest that CX26 interacts with PSMD2 to prevent c-Myc degradation, thereby enhancing PC cell proliferation.


### CX26 competes with c-Myc for interaction with the Armadillo-like helical domain of PSMD2
  In order to detect the PSMD2 domain responsible for binding to CX26 and c-Myc, molecular docking was conducted, which identified the Armadillo-like helical domain (amino acids 355 to 853) as exerting a pivotal effect. According to molecular docking, multiple PSMD2 sites (GLU-319, GLN-387, ASP-388, THR-392, ASN-396, ASP-423, LEU-426, GLU-458, CYS-459, and ASP-496) were related to CX26 interactions (Fig. 6A), whereas additional PSMD2 sites (GLU-313, ASN-327, SER-328, LEU-331, GLU-336, GLY-386, ASP-393, ASP-423, GLY-424, and GLU-458) may interact with c-Myc (Fig. 6B).   To validate the results of the molecular docking analysis, we generated a variety of Flag-tagged vectors expressing full-length PSMD2 and various truncation mutants with different domains (Fig. 6C). These vectors, along with His-tagged CX26 or HA-tagged c-Myc, were cotransfected into HEK-293T cells, followed by Co-IP assays. Consistent with the docking results, only the PSMD2 truncation mutants that contained the Armadillo-like helical domain were capable of binding CX26 in HEK-293T cells (Fig. 6D). Notably, c-Myc also coprecipitated with both full-length PSMD2 and the corresponding truncation mutants that contained the Armadillo-like domain in HEK-293 T cells (Fig. 6E). Consequently, CX26 and c-Myc interact with the Armadillo-like helical domain in PSMD2. Therefore, CX26 likely competes with c-Myc for binding to PSMD2.As hypothesized, c-Myc silencing promoted the binding of PSMD2 to CX26 in BxPC-3 cells (Fig. 6F). Additionally, CX26 overexpression reduced the binding of PSMD2 to c-Myc in AsPC-1 cells in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 6G). Therefore, CX26 competes with c-Myc for interaction with the Armadillo-like helical domain in PSMD2 in PC cells (Fig. 6H).


## Discussion
  Despite limited progress in the clinical management of PC during the past few decades, large-scale genomic sequencing efforts have deepened our understanding of PC biology, contributing to the advent of more breakthrough targeted therapies in clinical practice. In our study, CX26 expression was markedly elevated in PC, which correlates with dismal survival according to several public databases. Furthermore, we confirmed that CX26 overexpression promotes PC growth in vivo and in vitro, suggesting that CX26 is a potential therapeutic target for PC. The major underlying mechanism is that CX26 enhances c-Myc stability through competitive inhibition of the interaction between c-Myc and PSMD2. Finally, molecular docking studies revealed that the Armadillo-like helical domain of PSMD2 is associated with interactions with both CX26 and c-Myc.

  The involvement of CX26 in tumorigenesis is controversial. CX26 overexpression predicts better prognosis and serves as a factor independently predicting the survival of patients with colorectal cancer who are undergoing curative resection [32]. Similarly, CX26 overexpression is associated with reduced aggressiveness in intestinal-type gastric cancers [33]. However, CX26 upregulation is correlated with the dismal survival of patients suffering from lung squamous cell carcinoma [34], esophageal squamous cell carcinoma [25], and papillary and follicular thyroid cancers [26]. CX26 also interacts with focal adhesion kinase and NANOG, thus triggering self-renewal in triple-negative breast cancer [24]. In addition, CX26 promotes acquired gefitinib resistance in NSCLC cells through reciprocal positive regulation between CX26 and the PI3K/Akt pathway [23]. Overall, CX26 may play a dual role (either oncogenic or tumor-suppressive) depending on the cancer type.

  Concerning the role of CX26 in PC, previous studies have showed its upregulation in human PC [35]. In our study, we also found that CX26 was significantly overexpressed in PC. Furthermore, our findings demonstrated that CX26 expression was correlated with poor prognosis and that CX26 enhanced PC cell proliferation primarily through c-Myc. These results demonstrate that CX26 may act as an oncogene, contributing to the initiation and progression of PC.

  c-Myc is a transcription factor which is constitutively expressed in a dysregulated manner in more than 70% of human cancers [36]. In PC, c-Myc functions downstream of KRAS, a key oncogenic driver of both tumor initiation and progression, with overexpression observed in 43.5% of primary PCs [37]. KRAS activates multiple signaling pathways promoting cell proliferation, including the PI3K/AKT/mTOR [38], WNT/β-catenin [39], and Raf/MEK/ERK pathways [40], ultimately resulting in increased c-Myc expression and enhanced tumor cell growth. Notably, c-Myc also modulates mTOR activity by counteracting the inhibitory effects of the LKB1/AMPK/TSC2 complex, further promoting tumor progression [41]. However, directly targeting c-Myc with a potent inhibitor is challenging considering its structural complexity [42], and inhibiting c-Myc may lead to significant adverse effects on normal tissues [43]. Interestingly, our study suggests an alternative strategy: CX26 enhances c-Myc protein stability, thereby promoting PC growth. This finding presents a potential approach for indirectly targeting c-Myc and disrupting its role in proliferative signaling pathways. Given that c-Myc acts as a common downstream mediator in multiple oncogenic pathways, targeting its stability may yield more favorable therapeutic outcomes than targeting each pathway individually.

  To elucidate the role of CX26 in the regulation of c-Myc protein stability, PSMD2, a key subunit of the 19S regulatory particle, was identified as a CX26-interacting protein. Although CX26 does not directly interact with c-Myc, PSMD2 serves as a shared binding partner for both CX26 and c-Myc. PSMD2 is well known for its role in recognizing ubiquitinated substrates and facilitating their translocation into the catalytic 20S core for degradation [44–46]. On the basis of these findings, we hypothesize that the interaction between PSMD2 and c-Myc may modulate c-Myc degradation. Consistent with this hypothesis, silencing PSMD2 slowed c-Myc degradation and elevated the protein level, while overexpressing PSMD2 reduced c-Myc stability in PC cells. Interestingly, according to a molecular docking study, the Armadillo-like helical domain of PSMD2 acts as a shared interaction domain for both CX26 and c-Myc. Furthermore, CX26 knockdown enhanced the binding between PSMD2 and c-Myc, whereas CX26 overexpression reduced the binding of c-Myc to PSMD2. These findings indicate that CX26 makes interactions with PSMD2 to prevent the binding of PSMD2 to c-Myc, thereby inhibiting its degradation through the ubiquitin-mediated proteasome system and stabilizing c-Myc in PC cells.

  Despite the promising findings presented in this study, there are several limitations that need to be considered. First, the in vitro and in vivo experiments were conducted on specific pancreatic cancer cell lines and a mouse model, which may not fully represent the complexity and heterogeneity of pancreatic cancer in humans. The effect of CX26 on other molecular pathways beyond c-Myc was not explored in detail, and further research is needed to identify additional targets and potential off-target effects. Additionally, while our molecular docking and co-immunoprecipitation assays suggest a competitive interaction between CX26 and c-Myc for PSMD2 binding, the exact structural and functional implications of this interaction require further validation using more advanced techniques such as cryo-EM or X-ray crystallography. Furthermore, the study did not assess the long-term effects of CX26 manipulation in cancer progression, particularly regarding potential resistance mechanisms or compensatory pathways that could mitigate the therapeutic impact of CX26-targeted therapies. Finally, the lack of a comprehensive screening of CX26 inhibitors limits our ability to directly translate the findings into potential therapeutic applications, as the identification of specific inhibitors is critical for advancing clinical studies. These limitations should be addressed in future studies to provide a more comprehensive understanding of CX26’s role in pancreatic cancer progression.

  These findings indicate that CX26 promotes PC development by increasing c-Myc stability. Then, PSMD2 is identified as a shared binding protein for CX26 and c-Myc. Furthermore, CX26 suppresses the interaction between PSMD2 and c-Myc and c-Myc subsequent degradation within the proteasome, despite its inability to bind to c-Myc. Therefore, a new interaction model among PSMD2, CX26, and c-Myc, which is a candidate mechanism for modulating c-Myc stability, is revealed and provides a potential way to treat PC.
