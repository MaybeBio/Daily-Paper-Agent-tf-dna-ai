# Chromatin binding regulates phase behavior and morphology of condensates formed by prion-like domains


## Abstract
  Many transcription factors (TFs) contain intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) and are thought to form biomolecular condensates in the nucleus. These proteins can be conceptualized as block co-polymers, with the IDRs driving both homotypic and heterotypic protein-protein interactions and the DNA-binding domain (DBD) mediating heterotypic interactions with chromatin. While in vitro studies have predominantly reported micron-scale, spherical condensates in the absence of chromatin, TF condensates in live cells exhibit strikingly different behavior—adopting diverse, nanoscale, often aspherical morphologies and displaying sub-diffusive dynamics. Here, we show that this distinct phase behavior can arise from TF-chromatin interactions, using engineered fusion proteins with tunable IDR-DBD architectures. Specifically, we fused the prion-like domain (PLD) of the SS18 subunit from the mammalian SWI/SNF complex—a domain known to drive homotypic phase separation—to the DBD of the pioneer factor FOXA1. While SS18PLD on overexpression forms large, spherical condensates in cells, its fusion with FOXA1DBD leads to condensates that re-localize to chromatin, adopt aspherical morphologies, and exhibit chromatin-wetting behavior. Disruption of DBD-chromatin binding shifts condensate morphology toward a mixed or spherical state, implicating chromatin affinity as a key regulator of condensate coarsening and spatial organization. Coarse-grained simulations recapitulate these observations, revealing a finely balanced interplay between PLD-PLD and DBD-DNA interactions that collectively determine condensate dynamics and structure. Together, our findings demonstrate that chromatin binding is a critical modulator of transcriptional condensate behavior in vivo.


## Introduction
  The nucleus serves as the central hub for gene expression, orchestrating the activity of various transcriptional proteins to facilitate RNA production. It contains spatially separated subcompartments that carry out distinct functions, which are thought to form through biomolecular condensation (Sabari, Dall’Agnese, and Young 2020; Pei et al. 2024; Meldi and Brickner 2011; Strom and Brangwynne 2019). In the past decade, phase-separation of proteins and nucleic acids has emerged as the underlying thermodynamic driving process for forming biomolecular condensates such as the transcriptional hubs at super-enhancer sites and the nucleolus among others (Hnisz et al. 2017; Mitrea et al. 2016; Feric et al. 2016). Recent advancements highlight the importance of multivalent interactions, sometimes facilitated by intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) within transcriptional proteins, in driving the formation of multi-component transcriptional condensates (Boija et al. 2018; Sabari et al. 2018; Ferrie et al. 2022; Hnisz et al. 2017; Már, Nitsenko, and Heidarsson 2023). In the crowded cellular milieu, these dynamic condensates are often spatially restricted to specific nuclear regions, influenced by the mechanical properties of surrounding chromatin, and exhibit atypical characteristics of a phase-separated system, such as aspherical morphology, sub-diffusive dynamics, and limited coarsening (Chen et al. 2023; Ji et al. 2024; Lee, Strom, and Brangwynne 2022; Lee, Wingreen, and Brangwynne 2021). These observations have raised important questions regarding whether these assemblies are formed by canonical liquid-liquid phase separation and what are the factors that dictate the size, shape, and localization of these condensates (Erdel and Rippe 2018; McSwiggen et al. 2019; Rippe 2022).

  In addition to the IDRs, transcription factors possess a DNA binding domain (DBD) that facilitates interactions with the chromatin. This interaction may position chromatin as a critical scaffold for the nucleation of DNA binding protein condensates in the cell nucleus. The characteristics of the resulting condensates can therefore be impacted by factors such as the number of available binding sites, DNA binding affinity, and local chromatin density. Studies on the KLF4 transcription factor and RNA polymerase II revealed that DNA promotes surface condensation at protein concentrations below their bulk threshold concentration for phase separation, known as saturation concentration (Morin et al. 2022; Pancholi et al. 2021). Chromatin interactions have also been shown to be crucial for the nucleation of glucocorticoid receptor condensates and FET-family oncofusion condensation at specific genomic sites (Stortz et al. 2017, 2020; Zuo et al. 2021). In other systems, contrasting observations were noted where loss of chromatin interactions was shown to enhance nuclear phase separation. For example, in the case of the NUP oncofusion, NUP98-HOXA9, the deletion of the DNA-binding domain of HOXA9 resulted in the formation of a fewer number of large spherical condensates whereas the wildtype protein formed numerous nanoscopic puncta in the nucleus (Chandra et al. 2022). Similarly, inhibiting the bromodomain-mediated BRD4 binding to acetylated chromatin using either a small molecule called JQ1 or truncating the bromodomains, leads to redistribution of BRD4 throughout the nucleoplasm and the formation of a few large foci (Strom et al. 2024). This phenomenon was also observed with the protein ENL, where mutations in the YEATS domain, which lead to a loss of binding to acetylated histones enhance its ability to form mesoscale condensates (Song et al. 2022). These observations collectively suggest a delicate interplay between protein-protein self-interactions driving homotypic phase separation versus protein-chromatin interactions, and the availability and density of binding sites on chromatin, that dictate the morphological diversity of nuclear condensates in cells. They also highlight the necessity of systematically mapping macromolecular condensation in live cells to capture their full range of behavior that may not be available from in vitro studies.

  In this study, we employ live cell microscopy and molecular simulations to investigate how binding to chromatin influences the size and morphology of protein condensates. We focus on the intrinsically disordered prion-like domain (PLD) of the SS18 protein, a subunit of the mammalian SWI/SNF (mSWI/SNF) complex, and engineered de novo fusion proteins with tunable DNA binding ability. SS18PLD readily undergoes phase separation with a saturation concentration of ~ 2 μM in vitro, forming large (~ 2 μm or larger) spherical condensates in the regions of low chromatin density (Davis et al. 2024). Fusion of SS18PLD with the structured Forkhead (FKH) DNA-binding domain of the pioneer factor FOXA1 leads to condensates re-localizing on chromatin. Employing live cell imaging, we observe that the size and morphology of the SS18PLD-FKH fusion protein condensates are significantly altered due to condensate-chromatin interactions. Utilizing selected mutants of FKH domain, we confirm that changes in condensate properties are driven by the FKH domain’s chromatin binding affinity. We further employ sequence-resolved coarse-grained simulations and uncover a delicate interplay between PLD-PLD and FKH-DNA interactions that governs nuclear condensate morphology. In the absence of FKH domain, homotypic PLD interactions dominate over protein-DNA interactions, leading to the formation of spherical droplets. In contrast, FKH-DNA interactions dominate in SS18PLD-FKH fusion, resulting in the formation of aspherical condensates on chromatin that display strongly sub-diffusive behavior. Simulations using mesoscale resolution coarse-grained models reveal that the condensates formed by SS18PLD-FKH fusion extensively wet the chromatin chains.


## Results and Discussion

### Fusion to a DNA-binding domain alters the size and morphology of condensates formed by SS18PLD in live cells.
  Transcription factors (TFs) typically exhibit diverse condensate morphologies within the nucleus ranging from nanoscale clusters to mesoscale condensates (Figure S1). To capture the range of such behavior, we transiently transfected HEK293T cells with eight GFP-labeled protein constructs representing either full-length proteins or their selected domains (Figure 1A; Figure S1). While expression of the GFP tag alone shows a very diffused distribution (Figure S1), we see that FUS-DDIT3, an oncogenic fusion TF containing a prion-like domain fused to a transcription factor from CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein (C/EBP) family (Davis et al. 2021; Owen et al. 2021), forms large, spherical condensates. In contrast, EWS-FLI1, another oncogenic fusion TF with a domain architecture similar to FUS-DDIT3, assembles into small cluster-like condensates resembling those formed by the RNA binding protein FUS (Figure S1A). Meanwhile, FOXA1, a pioneer TF, forms dynamic biomolecular condensates (Ji et al. 2024) that appear to be extensively wetting the chromatin with a few large foci. Despite the rich phase behavior of RNA and DNA-binding proteins that form condensates in the cell nucleus, the underlying molecular mechanism governing the size and morphology of these chromatin-bound condensates is unclear. Each transcription factor in Figure S1 possesses unique characteristics, such as differences in homotypic protein-protein interactions largely driven by the IDRs, as well as protein-chromatin interactions with varied binding affinity and binding site specificity.

  These properties can collectively influence the formation of diffraction limited clusters (EWS-FLI1), large spherical condensates (FUS-DDIT3), or condensates wetting chromatin and appearing as protein clouds (FOXA1). To systematically address the role of chromatin binding on condensate morphology, we turned our attention to SS18, a subunit of the mammalian SWI/SNF complex. SS18 full-length (SS18FL) protein forms condensates and its phase separation was shown to regulate pluripotent-somatic transition (Kuang et al. 2021). The phase separation of SS18FL is driven by the intrinsically disordered prion-like domain (PLD: 67-418 aa). PLDs are IDRs that show low sequence complexity with a high enrichment of S, Q, Y, and G and are often depleted of charged residues (Alberti et al. 2009; Couthouis et al. 2011; Lancaster et al. 2014). PLDs typically are inert to nucleic acid binding but exhibit a strong propensity for homotypic condensation (Kaur et al. 2021). The condensates of SS18FL are small and irregular both at low and high overexpression levels. The prion-like domains of SS18 (SS18PLD), however, forms micron-sized condensates that have low asphericity and increase in size with protein expression levels. This behavior is also observed for other IDR systems that do not interact with the chromatin (Shin et al. 2018) (Figure S2). Time-lapse imaging of GFP-tagged SS18PLD in HEK293T cells (Supplementary movie 1; Figure S3A) revealed that the mean condensate area increases linearly with the protein concentration (Figure S3B), while condensate numbers initially grow steadily before plateauing at a later stage (Figure S3C). Throughout this process, condensates retain their sphericity (Figure S3D) and undergo coalescence (Figure S3E), which are expected behavior of phase separated bodies with terminally viscous behavior. The transient expression of SS18PLD in HEK293T cells can result in concentrations up to ~20 μM (Figure S4) and consequently the non-physiological dominance of homotypic interactions. However, this simplified system provides an appropriate model for uncovering mechanistic insights on how condensate properties can be influenced by interactions with chromatin. To test the effect of chromatin binding on the condensation behavior of SS18PLD, we created a chimeric construct by fusing the SS18PLD with the Forkhead (FKH) DNA binding domain of FOXA1 referred to as SS18PLD-FKH (Figure 1A and 1B). The FKH domain of FOXA1 can tightly bind to various chromatin sites either through specific or non-specific interactions (Cirillo and Zaret 2007; Sekiya et al. 2009). We observe that upon being expressed in HEK293T cells, unlike SS18PLD, SS18PLD-FKH lacks the ability to form spherical condensates and instead forms irregular blob-like structures (Figure 1A–B). SS18PLD-FKH condensate morphologies appear cloud-like, similar to that of FOXA1 assemblies (Figure 1A & S1A). We hypothesize that this can be a direct manifestation of the chromatin-wetting ability of SS18PLD-FKH condensates due to the high affinity multi-site chromatin binding through the FKH domain (Figure 1C). If true, then chromatin wettability of condensates should sensitively depend on the chromatin binding strength at specific as well as non-specific DNA sites by the FKH domain (Cirillo and Zaret 2007; Sekiya et al. 2009). Previous studies have established four point mutations in the FKH domain (FKHN216A, H220A, R262A, R265A), known as the NHRR mutations, which disrupt the chromatin binding of FOXA1 (Sekiya et al. 2009). Therefore, to test our hypothesis that reducing the affinity of FKH domain for chromatin may reduce the chromatin wettability of the SS18PLD-FKH condensates, we transfected cells with a variant of SS18PLD-FKH carrying the NHRR mutations (SS18PLD-FKHNHRR). Qualitatively, we observed that SS18PLD-FKHNHRR formed small spherical condensates in the cell nucleus and displayed substantially less wetting behavior on chromatin (Figure 1A). This data suggests that protein condensates can wet chromatin through favorable interactions encoded in the canonical DNA binding domains (DBDs) (Figure 1C).

  To quantify the physical features of the condensates formed by SS18PLD, SS18PLD-FKH, and SS18PLD-FKHNHRR, we performed shape and size analysis using a custom-built image analysis toolbox that aided user bias-free foci segmentation (see Methods). Cells for quantitative analysis were selected where the mean nuclear intensity of the population for each variant is not statistically different from each other, thereby allowing us to compare condensate properties at similar overexpression levels (Figure S5). Eccentricity (e) measurements revealed that SS18PLD-FKH (e = 0.67 ± 0.21) condensates are predominantly aspherical, which is a signature of chromatin wetting. In contrast, SS18PLD (e = 0.4 ± 0.2) forms dominantly spherical condensates (Figure 1D). We next quantified the size and number of condensates for each protein. The plots of mean condensate area and condensate number per unit area of nucleus (Figure 1E & F) reveal that SS18PLD condensates were larger in size (0.3 ± 0.17 μm2) and fewer (0.23 ± 0.09 condensates/ μm2) in number. In contrast, SS18PLD-FKH condensates are smaller in size (0.19 ± 0.08 μm2) and more in number (0.45 ± 0.16 condensates/μm2). This is likely due to chromatin interactions through the FKH domain negatively regulating the coarsening of SS18PLD-FKH condensates while the abundance of binding sites disperses the protein molecules. Interestingly, SS18PLD-FKHNHRR forms smaller (0.12 ± 0.03 μm2) condensates that are higher (0.6 ± 0.2 condensates/ μm2) in number with a lower asphericity (e = 0.63 ± 0.21) as compared to SS18PLD-FKH. These observations can be attributed to the residual DNA binding affinity of NHRR mutant contributed by SW residues in the wing 1 domain of FKH (Cirillo and Zaret 2007; Sekiya et al. 2009), which may allow the SS18PLD-FKHNHRR condensates to be pinned at chromatin sites while simultaneously restricting their ability to undergo coarsening, resulting in higher number of smaller condensates. Additionally, the reduced DNA binding affinity leads to weakened chromatin wetting behavior resulting in condensates with lower eccentricity as compared to SS18PLD-FKH condensates. However, we cannot rule out other possible mechanisms such as RNA binding (Oksuz et al. 2023) and/or microphase separation (Shinn et al. 2025) that may underlie the observed differences in the size and morphology of the nuclear condensates formed by SS18PLD-FKH and SS18PLD-FKHNHRR. Next, we plotted the product of the mean condensate area and the condensate number per unit area which revealed no significant differences between the means of the three constructs (Figure 1G). This observation suggests that condensation propensity of SS18PLD, SS18PLD-FKH, and SS18PLD-FKHNHRR is likely similar, but condensate morphologies and coarsening dynamics are significantly influenced by the presence and characteristics of DNA/chromatin-interaction domains.

  The CMV promoter used in our constructs is a strong promoter in HEK293T cells (Qin et al. 2010) which leads to greater than physiological protein concentrations (0.5–20 μM, Figure S4). Although we have not examined the phase behavior of these proteins at endogenous levels, we sought to investigate how varying expression levels influence condensate morphology in our overexpression-based cellular assay. We analyzed cells expressing either SS18PLD or SS18PLD-FKH with roughly threefold difference in expression levels. Utilizing fluorescence intensity as a proxy for concentration, we categorized cells as low (0.045–0.137 a.u.) or high (0.16–0.49 a.u.) expression. While condensate counts remained relatively unchanged for both SS18PLD and SS18PLD-FKH between either category (Figure S6A), the mean condensate area increased more significantly for SS18PLD (2.5-fold) compared to SS18PLD-FKH (1.9-fold) (Figure S6B). This suggests that the coarsening of SS18PLD-FKH is slower than the SS18PLD condensates, likely due to the spatial restrictions imposed by the FKH-chromatin interactions. This interpretation is further supported by trends in mean condensate eccentricity as a function of protein expression level: SS18PLD condensates become more spherical, while morphologies of SS18PLD-FKH become more irregular due to chromatin wetting effects (Figure S6C).


### SS18PLD-FKH condensates binding to chromatin leads to spatially restricted sub-diffusive behavior
  To directly test whether the FKH-fusion constructs colocalize with chromatin, we co-expressed GFP-tagged versions SS18PLD, FKH, SS18PLD-FKH, and SS18PLD-FKHNHRR, or GFP alone, with mGarnet-tagged core histone protein H2B (Figure 2A). Utilizing a previously published protocol that probe chromatin binding of proteins in live cells via Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) (Manders et al. 1992; Raccaud et al. 2019), we quantified the colocalization of the protein of interest with H2B (chromatin marker) within the nucleus of each cell (Figure 2B). We find that the SS18PLD condensates primarily localize to regions devoid of H2B, resulting in a low PCC (0.05 ± 0.24). This is consistent with a previous report suggesting condensates formed by FUS prion-like domain tend to form and grow in low density chromatin regions (Shin et al. 2018). However, fusion to the FKH domain, SS18PLD-FKH (0.62 ± 0.09) significantly increased colocalization with H2B, with PCC values similar to the FKH domain alone (0.62 ± 0.09) (Figure 2B). In contrast, SS18PLD-FKHNHRR (PCC = 0.16 ± 0.26) showed diminished colocalization to the chromatin. The colocalization of these constructs were compared to GFP (−0.108 ± 0.2) which does not interact with chromatin (Figure 2B).

  We reasoned that if chromatin binding regulates condensates wetting the chromatin, there should be a correlation between the chromatin binding strength and the condensate morphology, with aspherical morphologies being more pronounced for proteins with higher affinity for chromatin and vice versa. To test this idea, we next employed fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) in live cells. Previous studies have established FRAP as a tool to measure the relative strengths of chromatin binding by transcription factors. Using quantitative FRAP measurements, these studies showed that TFs with stronger chromatin binding exhibit slower nuclear mobility and longer dwell times on chromatin (Sekiya et al. 2009; Phair and Misteli 2001). Following a similar approach, we measured the half-time for maximum fluorescence recovery (τ1/2) and the fraction of immobile molecules of SS18PLD alone and the fusion proteins (Figure 2C; Figure S7A). For these measurements, the bleach area was kept constant for all samples to allow quantitative comparison (see Methods). We observed that a rapid fluorescence recovery for SS18PLD condensates (τ1/2 = 2.92 ± 1.004), which is largely excluded from the chromatin. In contrast, SS18PLD-FKH exhibited substantially slower recovery (τ1/2 = 7.88 ± 4.39), consistent with its ability to interact with chromatin. SS18PLD-FKHNHRR (τ1/2 = 3.12 ± 0.67) with weakened chromatin-binding ability, displayed a fluorescence recovery dynamics similar to SS18PLD. The FKH domain alone showed rapid recovery (τ1/2 = 0.57 ± 0.27), suggesting that the condensate formation by SS18PLD-FKH slows down macromolecular diffusion in the nucleus and increases their dwell times on specific genomic loci (Kent et al. 2020). We also estimated the immobile fractions within the time frame of our analysis (30 seconds). We observe that the SS18PLD-FKH had 40% of its molecules bound to chromatin whereas the other constructs showed less than 10% still bound to chromatin (Figure S7B). These findings support the conclusion that fusion of the FKH domain to SS18PLD directs it to chromatin leading to its restricted diffusion, in contrast, mutations within the FKH domain impair its DNA binding ability consequently increased diffusion (Figure 2D), thereby regulating the condensation landscape.


### Molecular simulations reveal mechanistic insights into the effect of chromatin binding on protein condensation.
  To gain deeper insight into how chromatin binding impacts formation and coarsening of SS18PLD-FKH condensates with DNA, we employed sequence-resolved coarse-grained simulations. Using the hydrophobicity scale (HPS) model (Cao et al. 2024; Dignon et al. 2018; Tesei and Lindorff-Larsen 2022) with an elastic network applied to the Forkhead domain and stacking interactions applied to the DNA, we first simulated a single-DNA system mixed with either SS18PLD, SS18PLD-FKH, SS18PLD-FKHNH (FKH mutation that results in the loss of sequence-specific DNA interactions but preserves sequence non-specific interactions) or SS18PLD-FKHNHRR (FKH mutation that results in the loss of both sequence-specific and non-specific DNA interactions) at moderate concentrations (ρ=1.3 mM) (Figure 3A). In this single-DNA setup, we observed that the SS18PLD promotes the formation of a PLD-rich dense phase fully separated from the DNA. In contrast, simulations with SS18PLD-FKH proteins resulted in a mixed dense phase where DNA is surrounded by FKH domains (Figure 3A). By introducing various mutants (Figures 3A–B) and modulating the salt concentration of the solution (Figures 3C; Figure S8), we effectively tuned the electrostatic component of DNA-protein interactions. For generating DNA affinity comparable to experimental observations for intracellular condensates formed by various mutants, a salt concentration of ρNaCl = 0.25 M is selected for the sequence-resolved coarse-grained simulations. At lower salt concentrations typically chosen for in vitro settings (e.g., ρNaCl = 0.17 M), DNA binding is expected to be stronger, which could result in reduced dissolution of protein-DNA condensates in Figure 3D, particularly for the NHRR mutant leading to higher colocalization and lower eccentricity. Simulation snapshots (Figure 3A) and the associated radial density plots (Figure 3B) illustrate tighter protein condensation with DNA for wild type compared to mutants and SS18PLD.

  Next, to explore how the interplay between DNA binding affinity and PLD self-interactions influences condensate morphologies, we conducted multi-DNA simulations at higher protein concentrations (ρ=1.8 mM). Introducing FKH domain mutations that weaken DNA binding, significantly impacted colocalization and cluster morphology, as shown in Figure 3D. In the absence of binding domains (SS18PLD), DNA and proteins remained well-separated, forming spherical SS18PLD condensates. However, introducing FKH domains and increasing DNA affinity led to greater degree of colocalization between the DNA and protein chains, resulting in increasingly irregular condensate shapes. DNA-protein colocalization was quantified using Pearson correlation coefficient (Figure 3E). At the same bulk density of simulations, clusters with the lower eccentricity exhibit a stronger tendency to form condensates, corresponding to larger cluster sizes. The cluster size variability was analyzed by examining the distribution of the normalized largest cluster size (Figure 3F). The trend aligns with the eccentricity observed in Figure 1D. We also performed contact map analysis of condensates composed of DNA and chimeric proteins containing PLD and DBD domains (Figure S9). This analysis provides direct insights into the relative interaction strengths of different domains within the condensates. As expected, SS18PLD exhibits stronger protein-protein interactions compared to protein-DNA interactions. Comparing SS18PLD-FKH, SS18PLD-FKHNH and SS18PLD-FKHNHRR, which include a DBD with variable DNA binding affinity, our analysis reveals the contacts between DBD and DNA are significantly stronger than other regions for SS18PLD-FKH. Mutations in the DBD (e.g. in FKHNHRR) reduce these contacts compared to the original FKH construct.

  To evaluate the impact of condensate morphologies on the diffusive dynamics of TFs within chromatin-like viscoelastic microenvironments, we conducted simulations using a mesoscale-resolution coarse-grained model. In this framework, SS18PLD is modeled as an unstructured coarse-grained polymer chain with a tendency to undergo phase separation due to weak self-attractive interactions, while the FKH domain is represented as a patchy particle containing three specific binding sites with high affinity for chromatin (Figure 4A). Chromatin itself is modeled as a dense polymer mesh with weakly attractive interactions restricted to the DNA-binding patchy sites. There are two primary scenarios that arise: i) in the absence of the FKH domain, SS18PLD phase forms a macroscopic cavity within the chromatin network (Figure 4B). This cavity does not wet the chromatin, leading to an elastic deformation energy penalty associated with network distortion (Ronceray et al. 2022; Biswas, Mukherjee, and Chakrabarti 2022). ii) In contrast, when the FKH DNA-binding domain is present, FKH–chromatin interactions arrest phase separation dynamics of SS18PLD, resulting in the formation of numerous small, spatially confined microdroplets within the chromatin network. SS18PLD-FKH droplets appear to wet the chromatin network due to favorable protein-DNA interactions at the droplet-chromatin interface. This morphological difference is illustrated in the simulation snapshots shown in Figure S10, where protein condensates either exclude or wet the chromatin surface, depending on the strength of protein–chromatin interactions. Consistent with experimental results (Figure 1), our simulations show that the FKH domain in the SS18PLD-FKH fusion protein induces extensive binding to the chromatin network (Figure 4B). This chromatin-FKH interactions leads to the formation of protein cloud-like morphologies in contrast to the spherical droplets observed in the case of SS18PLD alone. The incorporation of FKH binding sites shifts the equilibrium toward a percolated network-like state, as opposed to discrete spherical droplet morphologies (Figure 4B). This transition is quantitatively captured by the spatial distribution of protein cluster radii (Rg) and their connectivity within the chromatin network (see Methods). Notably, we observe a change in the diffusive dynamics in the phase separation process, as reflected in mean squared displacement (MSD) profiles of center of mass coordinates of transcription factors (Figure 4C). The extracted power-law exponent (α) of the MSD Δr2∼tα reveals normal diffusive dynamics at the early time of the phase separation, where α=1. However, at later times, in equilibrium, SS18PLD diffusion further transitions into an arrested dynamical regime (α≈0.25), indicating that PLD condensation inside the chromatin cavity progressively restricts its mobility. On the other hand, high DNA-binding affinity, as is the case for SS18PLD-FKH fusion protein, leads to the formation of smaller clusters with increasingly arrested dynamics, which manifests in a shift towards more extreme subdiffusive behavior (α≈0.2). Overall, the number of condensate domains in equilibrium for designed TF constructs with variable DNA binding affinity corroborates the size and morphology of condensates observed in our experiments (Figure 1). Starting from a homogeneous mixed state, the system undergoes phase separation, during which the number of clusters progressively decreases as individual domains grow and coarsen over time (Figure 4D). For SS18PLD, this coarsening process leads to the formation of fewer but larger and more spherical condensates, indicating efficient droplet growth and fusion. In contrast, the presence of the FKH domain results in a more fragmented condensate morphology, with numerous smaller clusters that persist over time, suggesting that FKH–chromatin interactions inhibit coarsening and maintain a more dispersed, microdroplet-like state. To quantify the kinetics of phase separation and coarsening, we compute the time-dependent correlation length, ξ(t), from the spatial two-point correlation function C(r,t)=⟨ϕ(x,t)ϕ(x+r,t)⟩, which quantifies how spatial fluctuations in coarse-grained order parameter field ϕ(r, t) (the PLD phase) at time t are correlated across distance r. We extract the correlation length ξ(t) as the exponential decay length of C(r,t), representing the typical size of phase-separated domains at time t. The correlation length grows with time following a power law: ξ(t)∼tβ, where the exponent β depends on the mechanism of coarsening (Bray, Derrida, and Godréche 1994). For the SS18PLD system (Figure 4E), we observe typical diffusion-limited coarsening, β≈1/3, corresponding to droplet growth and eventual coalescence into a macroscopic domain. Within our simulation timescales, for smaller densities of SS18PLD systems, ξ(t) reaches plateaus, indicating an equilibrium state. In contrast, the SS18PLD — FKH system exhibits early saturation of ξ(t), reflecting suppressed coarsening due to strong, spatially restricted interactions between the FKH domain and the DNA network. These interactions inhibit droplet coalescence and lead to the formation of multiple small, spatially pinned condensates. We also compute the surface area (S) of condensates (using alphaShape algorithm) at equilibrium (Edelsbrunner and Mücke 1994), which increases linearly with protein number density for both SS18PLD and SS18PLD-FKH. Consistent with suppressed coarsening dynamics, we observe that SS18PLD forms fewer, larger droplets, while SS18PLD -FKH yields many smaller clusters due to high affinity Forkhead-chromatin binding, resulting in a thermodynamically stable, spatially arrested microdroplet state (Figure S11).

  To further probe the role of FKH-chromatin interactions, we introduced a mutation in the SS18PLD-FKH construct (SS18PLD-FKHNH), which disrupts the sequence-specific interaction between the Forkhead domain and chromatin. As a result, the restriction imposed by chromatin binding is relieved, leading to the formation of larger condensates compared to SS18PLD-FKH (Figure S12). Equilibrium snapshots from our simulations (inset) highlight this transition, where the number of small cluster nodes decreases while Rg increases upon mutation, indicating a shift from a dense, network-like structure toward a more dispersed morphology (Figure S12). Further mutations (SS18PLD-FKHNH and SS18PLD-FKHNHRR) lead to a continued increase in Rg driving the system from a chromatin-associated network toward droplet-like condensates, which is consistent with our experimental results (Figure 1). These results indicate that the balance between self-interaction among PLDs and heterotypic chromatin-binding interactions play a key role in determining condensate morphology. When self-attractive interactions dominate, condensates take nearly spherical shapes in the absence of chromatin binding. Conversely, when strong FKH-DNA interactions are introduced, condensates adopt network-like morphologies with extensive chromatin-TF association and higher cluster size variability. At higher protein concentrations, increasing DNA-binding affinity significantly enhances DNA-protein colocalization, leading to a shift in condensate morphology toward cloud-like, network-enriched structures. These findings from CG model provide a quantitative framework for our experimental observations (Figures 1–2) and underscore the critical role of interaction strengths and chromatin accessibility in shaping condensate phase behavior. This could have functional implications in transcriptional regulation by modulating the spatial organization and mobility of transcription factors within chromatin environments.


## Conclusion
  Recent studies have highlighted the role of phase separation of coactivators, transcription factors and chromatin remodelers in the context of transcription regulation and disease processes (Boija et al. 2018; Sabari et al. 2018; Davis, Moosa, and Banerjee 2022; Pei et al. 2024; Patil et al. 2023; Shirnekhi, Chandra, and Kriwacki 2023; Davis et al. 2021). In many instances, however, transcription factors within cells seldom form the characteristic large spherical droplet morphologies expected based on the in vitro assays. Instead, their morphologies resemble microphase-separated domains, suggesting that the nuclear microenvironment and transcriptional activity play a critical role in condensate formation and regulation (Chen et al. 2023; Ji et al. 2024; Schede et al. 2023; Hilbert et al. 2021).

  In this study, we elucidated the effect of chromatin binding on condensate morphology. We show that despite the presence of a strong driver of intracellular phase separation via a prion-like domain (PLD), engineered PLD-containing fusion proteins with a strong DNA-binding domain form aspherical and spatially restricted condensates that appear as “protein clouds”. Through experiments and simulations, we confirm that this behavior is correlated with their ability to wet the chromatin network through the DNA-binding domain (Figure 1A). This finding raises important questions about the role of chromatin in shaping the phase behavior of DNA- and chromatin-binding proteins in vivo.

  Sequence-specific interactions have been shown to drive the formation of transcription factor condensates at specific chromatin-binding sites (Shrinivas et al. 2019; Zuo et al. 2021; Morin et al. 2022; Strom et al. 2024). In our system, SS18PLD forms large spherical condensates while the SS18PLD-FKH fusion protein forms many small bright foci in the live cell nucleus that remain tethered to chromatin. These loci likely represent condensate nucleation sites due to sequence-specific and non-specific chromatin interactions. SS18PLD-FKH condensates display low diffusion rates in FRAP assays compared to SS18PLD or the FKH domain alone (Figures 2C and S7) and high colocalization with histone H2B, a chromatin marker (Figure 2A–B). These observations support the view that chromatin tethering inhibits bulk phase separation of the SS18PLD-FKH protein. Moreover, the FKH domain can also non-specifically target the SS18PLD to chromatin, resulting in surface condensation of the fusion protein that mirrors observations made for KLF4 in vitro (Morin et al. 2022) and RNA polymerase II in cells (Pancholi et al. 2021). Notably, the oncofusion transcription factor FUS-DDIT3 formed large, spherical condensates in HEK293T cells upon overexpression, despite containing a DNA-binding domain (DBD), in contrast to EWS-FLI1 a TF with similar domain architecture, which formed small, irregular puncta under similar conditions (Figure S1). However, in its native context within an MLS cell line, FUS-DDIT3 forms small, irregular puncta (Owen et al. 2021). Investigation of FUS-DDIT3 condensates across low and high expressions showed that similar to SS18PLD, FUS-DDIT3 condensates increased significantly in size with higher expression while retaining their spherical morphology (Figure S13). This expression-dependent growth, coupled with low asphericity, suggests that FUS-DDIT3 condensates formed via protein over-expression may not engage with chromatin in HEK293T cells, likely due to inaccessibility of the specific chromatin-binding sites or cofactors required for native interactions. These observations collectively suggest microphase-like morphologies of chromatin-associated factors in cells are likely surface condensation or adsorption on the chromatin surface that limits growth but can retain liquid-like characteristics.

  Overall, using experiments and simulations, this work establishes a broadly applicable physical framework for understanding how the domain architecture of TFs and their chromatin interactions dictate condensate morphology, dynamics, and spatial regulation in the nuclear environment. While our overexpression-based approach enabled detailed characterization of these relationships, the resulting supraphysiological protein concentrations represent a key limitation. Future studies can explore the dynamics, morphology, and material properties of nuclear condensates under endogenous protein concentrations in native cell lines, and the role of non-equilibrium processes including active transcription (Hilbert et al. 2021; Schede et al. 2023).


## Methods

### Cell culture:
  The HEK293T cells were cultured at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. To initiate transfection, 20,000 cells were seeded in 8 well-chambered slides. After 24 h, Lipofectamine 2000 reagent was used to transfect the cells with 0.5 μg plasmid, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The cells were imaged after 16-18h of transfection. Colocalization experiments were carried out with 0.5μg plasmid for each construct. Table S1 provides a complete list of proteins used in this study and their amino acid sequences.


### Fluorescence imaging:
  Cells were incubated in Fluorobrite media supplemented with 10%(v/v) FBS and Hoechst33342 dye to stain the nucleus. Imaging was carried out using either the Andor Dragonfly Spinning Disk microscope (63X objective), Leica Stellaris (63X objective),or Q2 (ISS Inc., 63X objective) laser scanning confocal microscopes, with the cells being maintained at 37 °C on a temperature and CO2-controlled stage. The plasmid constructs with mEGFP (Addgene: #54759) and H2B- mGarnet (Addgene Plasmid #104311) were imaged using 488 nm laser and 594 nm laser respectively, while Hoechst imaging was done using the 405 nm laser. Time-lapse imaging for SS18PLD condensates were performed 2 hours after transfection using Q2 (ISS Inc., 63X objective) laser scanning confocal microscope for a duration of 6 hours.

  To determine the nuclear protein concentrations in overexpressed system, we performed mEGFP (GFP henceforth) titration assay. The mean fluorescence intensity (a.u.) (y-axis) was measured using purified GFP protein across different concentration range (0.75-27 μM) (x axis) under the same image acquisition settings used for cells. Linear regression analysis revealed the following relationship where x represent GFP concentration (in μM) and represents mean fluorescence intensity: y = 0.0013 + 0.0105.


### Image analysis:
  For the quantification of biomolecular condensates in our study, we employed a custom in-house image analysis platform developed in Python. Raw fluorescence images were first pre-processed to enhance signal quality. Pre-processing included noise suppression via wavelet decomposition and Gaussian filtering, as well as feature enhancement through Laplacian of Gaussian filtering and morphological operations, including top-hat filtering. These steps served to minimize noise and artifacts while enhancing the fidelity of objects present in the images. Background removal was then performed before applying a multi-tiered segmentation strategy and a multi-faceted object filter to eliminate false positives. An integrated human-in-the-loop verification step further refined segmentation by allowing manual exclusion of spurious signals and artifacts. For colocalization analysis, image-analysis platform performed pixel-based correlation analyses between multiple fluorescence channels. Specifically, Pearson’s correlation coefficient was calculated for each channel pair to quantify the degree of spatial overlap and intensity correlation.

  For analysis of time-lapse images of SS18PLD, the condensates in a frame were segmented and analyzed using CellProfiler (Stirling et al. 2021). Once segmented, each condensate was characterized by a range of metrics, including area, eccentricity, and integrated fluorescence intensity. These measurements provide detailed insights into condensate size, shape, and protein concentration, forming the basis for downstream statistical analyses.


### FRAP (Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching) measurements:
  FRAP experiments were performed using Leica Stellaris laser scanning confocal microscope. A circular region of 0.3 microns radii was photobleached using three lasers set at 100% laser intensity for 3 seconds followed by a post-bleaching imaging scan for 30 seconds. The GFP intensity values from the bleached region of interest were then corrected for photofading by normalizing them to intensities from an unbleached reference condensate. The FRAP recovery curves were plotted using OriginPro2024 and fitted using the equation I(t)=a1−exp−tτ as previously reported (Alshareedah, Kaur, and Banerjee 2021).


### Sequence resolved simulations of protein-DNA condensates:

We only apply the ENM to residue pairs with an r0 below 0.9 nm cutoff. The predetermined configuration and boundary of the folded domain are generated by the alphaFold2 colab.

  We performed our coarse-grained (CG) simulations using OpenMM (v7.7). Simulations were conducted in a cubic box with a side length of 40 nm. For single-DNA simulations, we set the temperature T = 360K, protein bead concentration ρ = 0.6M, and ionic strength I = 170 — 330 mM (same as the NaCl salt concentration). The DNA molecule was 100 base pairs long, and approximately 60 proteins were included, with the exact number depending on their sequence lengths. For multi-DNA simulations, we used T = 340K, ρ = 0.8M, and I = 250mM. Each DNA molecule was 40 base pairs long, with 24 DNA molecules included in the system. Around 80 proteins were added, with the exact number again dependent on sequence length. After energy minimization, all systems were simulated in the NVT ensemble with a timestep of 0.01 ps. Each simulation was run for 5 × 107 steps. We applied the DBSCAN clustering algorithm to group protein data points. Each point represents the center of approximately 50 CG residues, with slight variations depending on protein length. The minimum number of points required to define a core point was set to 7, and the neighborhood distance parameter (ε) was set to 3.5 nm.


#### Contact Map analysis
  We used MDAnalysis python package to compute contact maps for protein–protein and protein–DNA interactions (Jin et al. 2025). For each frame of the trajectory, contacts were calculated using each protein residue as a reference. The analysis was averaged over 100 frames sampled every 200 ns after equilibration. A cutoff distance of d = 1.5 nm, determined from the radial distribution function (Figure 3B), was used to define contacts. The contact frequency relative to a protein chain (bottom panel in Figure S9) was obtained by averaging each column of the contact map.


### Mesoscale simulations of transcriptional condensates in chromatin network:
  We conducted mesoscale coarse-grained (CG) molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to explore the condensate droplets and chromatin network structures for chimeric protein SS18PLD-FKH and SS18PLD. First, we simulated conditions replicating the experiment for SS18PLD to capture spherical condensate. Next, we employ a spherical head domain, modeled as an independent rigid body with three patches on its surface mimicking DNA binding sites interacting with chromatin sites. One end of the protein chain is attached to the head segment. The condensate-promoting proteins are represented by chains of 100 CG beads, which interact via an attractive LJ potential with an energy depth of ε. Additionally, we incorporate long chromatin chains 3000 CG beads with specific binding sites for the fork-head. Molecular dynamics simulations were performed using the LAMMPS package. The equations of motion were integrated within the NVT ensemble for chromatin and protein chains and NVT/rigid for the head domain, employing a Langevin thermostat with a time step of 0.005 τ, where τ=mσ2/ε represents the derived unit of time, and denotes m the mass of a bead and σ denotes the bead diameter and establishes the length scale for LJ simulations. The Langevin thermostat ran 107 steps and was configured with a friction factor of 0.1 σ. DNA binding sites formed bonds with specific sites of the chromatin if the radius is within the cutoff distance rb<1.3σ. Initially, systems were homogeneously distributed at high temperatures. Non-bonded interactions were incorporated using the attractive Lennard-Jones (LJ) potential for condensate-promoting protein domains. For other non-bonded interactions, a truncated LJ (WCA) repulsive interaction was implemented. To mimic the introduction of mutant sites, we switched off the bond formation of specific sites, transitioning the system from a cloud network to completely spherical droplets. There are 20 chromatin CG chains, and 200 protein chains initially positioned randomly within a periodic cubic simulation box with edge lengths of Lx=Ly=Lz=100. The chains of biomolecule beads are connected by anharmonic FENE potential UFENE(r)=−12kR02ln1−r2/R02 , where represents the distance between adjacent beads, k=20ϵ/σ2 is the spring constant, and R0=1.5 is the maximum extensibility. Bond angles between consecutive beads were constrained by an angular harmonic potential, Uθ=Kθθ−θ02, where Kθ denotes the angular potential constant and θ0=150∘ is the equilibrium angle.
