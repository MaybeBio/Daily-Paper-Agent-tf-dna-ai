# Positional Isomers of Indolyl-benzodiazepines Display Dissimilar Binding and Recruitment of BET Transcriptional Regulators to Targeted Genomic loci


## Abstract
  BET proteins contain two tandem bromodomains (BD1 and BD2) that bind histone acetyl-lysine residues that can be targeted with small molecule inhibitors such as IBET-762, which bears a triazolo benzodiazepine core. Here, we report the consequences of substituting the pendant chlorobenzene moiety of IBET-762. Substitution with larger ring structures diminishes bromodomain binding, and even subtle changes on the phenyl ring significantly impact affinity. Structural analysis, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulations of four indolyl-benzodiazepine derivatives indicate that ligand selectivity arises from interaction with His433 in BD2. Their ability to engage BET proteins in cells was tested by incorporating them into heterobifunctional synthetic genome readers and regulators (SynGRs). The relative activity of each SynGR corresponded to the affinity of the tethered BET ligand for the BD2 domain. The development and structure-activity relationship analysis of these BET ligands provides a blueprint for the construction of increasingly selective BET inhibitors and proximity-inducing molecules.


## Introduction:
  The human bromodomain and extra-terminal (BET) family of proteins have emerged as highly validated therapeutic targets in a number of cancers [1] and cardiovascular ailments.[2] This family of four proteins, BRD2, BRD3, BRD4, and BRDT, performs essential roles in the regulation of gene transcription by binding to histones and other client proteins at targeted genomic sites and recruiting transcriptional machinery to those sites. Binding occurs via acetylated lysine residues in histones docking into a defined pocket within each of the tandem bromodomains (BD1 and BD2) of BET proteins [3–6]. Given the mechanism of BET function, small molecules that inhibit BD1 and BD2 from binding to acetylated lysines on histones are particularly attractive as potential therapeutic agents. In fact, several BET inhibitors based on a 1,4-benzodiazepine scaffold have been tested for their potential as therapeutic agents against various cancers [7], including IBET-762[8,9], JQ1 [10–12] GSK2820151 [13] and OTXO15 [14]. These inhibitory BET ligands bind to the acetyl lysine-binding pocket with high affinity and the high-resolution structures of IBET-762 bound to BRD2-BD2 and BRD4-BD1 revealed that the triazole ring of the benzodiazepine scaffold forms a hydrogen bond with a conserved asparagine in the acetyl lysine-binding pocket (Asn140 in BRD4-BD1 or Asn429 in BRD2-BD2) (Figure 1A) [9].

  Most of the benzodiazepine-based BET inhibitors lack selectivity amongst family members and between individual bromodomains of each BET protein. This lack of selectivity prevents a more nuanced understanding and modulation of the biological functions of individual BET proteins and the distinctive roles of each of their tandem bromodomains. This shortcoming has driven the search for domain-specific ligands, with some success [15–18]. In one elegant example, replacement of the chlorobenzene ring of IBET-762 with an indole ring resulted in the development of TCAC-28 [19], which shows significantly increased selectivity for BD2 over BD1 [20,21] The affinity of TCAC-28 towards BD2 is largely due to a pi-stacking interaction between the histidine side chain (His433) in the BC loop of BD2 and the indole ring of TCAC-28. In place of His433, BD1 contains Asp160, and this residue cannot engage in the same pi-stacking interaction with the ligand in its closed conformation, thus potentially explaining the selectivity of TCAC-28 for BD2 [19,20].

  Here we set out to examine the impact of modifying the solvent-accessible chlorobenzene ring of IBET-762 on binding to BET proteins (Figure 1A). We first replaced chlorine on the phenyl ring with various substituents. Next, we replaced the entire chlorobenzene ring with successively larger fused ring systems, such as indoles, anthracenes and phenanthrenes and measured their affinity for the BD2 domain of BRD2 (Table 1). Amongst this series of analogs, a set of positional isomers of indolyl-benzodiazepines displayed varying binding affinities and varying degrees of domain-selectivity in vitro. Therefore, we tested the ability of these positional isomers to elicit biological activity in two distinct cell types derived from Friedreich’s ataxia (FRDA) patients.

  The four positional isomers of indolyl-benzodiazepine were used to generate hetero-bifunctional SynGRs that function by engaging BET proteins at targeted genomic loci and regulating proximal gene expression (Figure 1B) [22]. SynTEF1, the prototype SynGR, has JQ1 as the BET ligand and an imidazole/pyrrole/b-alanine containing polyamide (PA1) as a GAA repeat-targeting module [23,24,25]. In FRDA patients, the GAA repeat expansions (> 300) attenuate transcription of FXN, an essential gene. The resulting decrease in the levels of frataxin protein [26, 27] leads to incurable neurodegeneration and early mortality [28–29]. Many strategies to overcome FXN transcriptional attenuation and thereby remedy the disease have been explored, but none have led to effective therapies [30–33]. In this context, SynGRs provide a novel therapeutic modality that restores FXN expression to the levels observed in healthy cells [22, 34].

  More proximally, functional testing of BET ligands in FRDA cells is enabled by incorporation into a heterobifunctional SynGR because when tethered to the GAA-targeting PA1, BD2-selective ligands license FXN expression whereas BD1-selective BET ligands do not [34]. Building on that observation, we examine the extent to which benzodiazepine ligands function as SynGRs in lymphoblasts (GM15850 suspension cells) and fibroblasts (GM04078 adherent cells) derived from two different FRDA patients. To identify potentially active analogs, we first characterized binding of our benzodiazepine derivatives to the BD2 domain of BRD2. Next, we determined the co-crystal structures of a set of indolyl-benzodiazepine ligands bound to BRD2-BD2. Finally, we conjugated the four indolyl-benzodiazepine ligands to PA1 to create a novel set of IND-SynGRs and studied their effects on FXN expression in the two FRDA cell lines described above. Consistent with their differing affinity and selectivity for the BD2 domain of BET proteins, IND-SynGRs demonstrate substantial variability in their ability to license transcription of FXN in these-patient derived cells. These results demonstrate that the bioactivity of these newly designed BET ligands can be inferred from their affinity to the BD2 domain of BET proteins and add to the rapidly growing repertoire of proximity-based chemical effectors of complex biological processes.


### Synthesis of IBET-762 analogs
  The synthesis of IBET-762 ester analogs was initiated from commercially available isatoic anhydride 1 [17,18]. The condensation of isatoic anhydride 1 with L-aspartic acid dimethyl ester in refluxing pyridine for 24 hours afforded the bicyclic di-amide 2 in a moderate yield of 30% (Scheme 1). Selective mono-thionation of compound 2 was achieved by using 0.9 equivalent of Lawesson’s reagent in refluxing pyridine for 6 h, giving the desired thioamide 3 in a moderate yield of 45%. Triazole cyclization was carried out in a single step by treating compound 3 with 2.5 equivalents of acethydrazide at reflux for 1 day, leading to the formation of product 4 in quantitative yield. This triazole derivative 4 was subsequently converted to the corresponding imidoyl chloride 5 in the presence of POCl3 and DMF at room temperature over 20 hours, with a yield of 50%. Imidoyl chloride 5 is unstable for long-term storage, tending to decomposition under flash chromatography; hence, the crude product was used in the next step without further purification.

  Suzuki−Miyaura cross-coupling of crude imidoyl chloride 5 with electronically and structurally diverse set of aryl and indole boronic acids afforded the IBET-762 analogs (6a-6f, 6h-6l) (Scheme 1) in moderate yield using Pd(Ph3P)4 in a dimethoxyethane/water mixture at 85 °C for 2 hours. For compound 6g, N-phenyl sulfonamide indole boronic acid was used for Suzuki-Miyauru cross coupling and selective N-phenyl sulfonamide deprotection was conducted using TBAF to afford the desired compound.

  In preparation for using these BET ligands for the construction of next generation SynGRs, we obtained the benzodiazepine acid analogs (7g-j) from hydrolysis of the benzodiazepine esters (6g-j) in the presence of NaOH/MeOH (Scheme 1).


### SPR-based analysis of BET binding
  We determined binding affinities of the newly synthesized IBET-762 analogs by surface plasmon resonance (SPR) (Table 1). Recombinant BD2 domain of BRD2 was purified and conjugated to the SPR chip and the affinity for 12 different analogs and 3 reference compounds was measured as described in the methods section. While we used BRD2-BD2 as the first filter to prioritize analogs for subsequent analysis, we also examined the ability to bind full length purified BRD2 and BRD4 proteins in parallel assays (Table S2). With two exceptions, the results with full length proteins show that most analogs do not distinguish between BRD2 and BRD4. Interestingly, three ligands display higher affinity for BRD4 over BRD2, with 6d, GSK778 and ABBV744 displaying ~2.6-fold, ~2.2-fold, and ~5.6-fold selectivity, respectively Table S2). The basis of this selectivity remains to be resolved.

  The results from BRD2-BD2 binding revealed that even modest modifications to the phenyl ring significantly reduced affinity to this domain (Table 1). Substitution of the chlorobenzene ring with increasingly larger fused ring systems revealed that certain positional isomers of indoles were tolerated whereas three ring systems were not (Table 1). Consistent with limited steric compatibility, analog 4IND (TCAC28), displayed affinities that match the reference molecules such as JQ1 that bind BD2 with a KD of ~70nM.

  Based on these results, we focused on the four indole benzodiazepine ester derivatives and more rigorously tested their affinity for each of the two tandem bromodomains (BD1 and BD2) of BRD2 (Table 1, Figure S1–2). Placing the indole group in the 3 position (3IND) was particularly detrimental to binding to both bromodomains. 5IND bound with 10-fold higher affinity than 3IND, though it displayed no selectivity for either bromodomain. Interestingly, 6IND and 4IND displayed a graded increase in selectivity for BD2, ~2-fold and ~8-fold respectively. Overall, in this series 5IND and 6IND bound reasonably well to BD1, whereas 4IND and 6IND bound with higher affinity to BD2. This range of affinities and graded selectivity between the two bromodomains motivated a more nuanced investigation of their structure-activity relationships.


### Structural studies of BRD2-BD2 binding by indolyl-benzodiazepine analogs
  To rationalize compound binding to BRD2-BD2, we co-crystallized human wild-type BRD2-BD2 with one weak affinity compound (3IND) and two relatively selective compounds (4IND and 6IND), and determined their structures to 3.2 Å, 2.34 Å, and 1.99 Å resolution, respectively (Figure 2A–C, S3A, S5 and S6; Table S1). Analysis of these structures revealed that all three compounds (3IND, 4IND and 6IND) bind to the canonical H4K12ac (H4 tail acetylated at Lys12) recognition pocket that is surrounded in BD2 by residues located near the BC loop linking helices B and C (Figure 2A–C and S3A–D).35 Interactions with the features of the scaffold that are shared between all three compounds (3IND, 4IND and 6IND) are similar. For instance, the triazole moiety is buried inside the pocket and forms hydrogen bonds with the conserved Asn429 residue, reminiscent of the interaction between the acetylated lysine and asparagine observed in the H4K12ac peptide-bound BRD2-BD2 complex (e.g., PDB 2E3K) [35,36]. The benzodiazepine moiety is bound within a hydrophobic pocket composed of conserved residues (Pro371, Phe372, Val376, and Leu383). The BD2-selective ligands (4IND and 6IND) make water-mediated interactions with Tyr386 (Figure S6). The orientation of His433 and Asp434 changes with the positioning of the indole ring in the 3IND ligand (Figure 2A).

  To gain insights into BD1/BD2 selectivity, we compared our ligand-bound BRD2-BD2 structures with a BRD2-BD1 structure bound to IBET-762/Molebresib (PDB 2YEK bound to ligand EAM; Figure 2D–F) [37,38]. The comparison suggests that many interactions are maintained across benzodiazepine ligands. The role of how two non-conserved residues (His433 and Val435 in BD2 vs. Asp160 and Ile162 in BD1) confer domain selectivity remains unclear. On one hand, His433 provides an opportunity for an indole-containing ligand to form a π-stacking interaction which would provide an affinity boost over a phenyl-containing ligand (Figure 2A–C, S3A–D). On the other hand, we observed that His433 adopts two conformations: one that π-stacks with indole ligands and one that moves away from them (Figure 2A–C, S3A–D). Although the shift in indole orientation from 4IND to 6IND suggests less favorable π-stacking with 6IND, the affinity decrease is only modest (KD of 61 nM vs 93 nM, respectively). These affinities are 2–3-fold lower than EAM-bound tandem BRD2 bromodomains (BD1/2; KD of 33 nM) [37]. The affinity drop is much more pronounced with 3IND (BD1/2; KD of 2100–2300 nM), likely due to less favorable π-stacking. We also noticed many changes in water networks around non-conserved residues in response to ligand binding which may account for the differences in affinity and selectivity [39] (Figure 2D–F, S3D). While the positioning of the indole nitrogen in 3IND changes the interactions with the water network, we cannot assess those differences given the resolution of this structure. The maintained interactions across benzodiazepine ligands and distinct water networks around non-conserved residues are consistent when compared to the structure of BRD2-BD1 with another benzodiazepine-based ligand, WSH (PDB 2YDW bound to ligand WSH; Figure S3E–F). The affinities of WSH for BD1 and BD2 are similar (KD of 46 nM for BD1 vs 53 nM for BD2). We also considered the published structure of a 4IND-bound BRD2-BD2 mutant (W370F; PDB 5DFD) [20] that led to subtle differences in the orientation of 4IND (Figure S4A–C). Collectively our structural findings indicate that the non-conserved residues (His433 and Val435) on BD2 and the surrounding water-networks contribute to the selectivity of these benzodiazepine ligands.

### Synthesis and functional gene regulation by IND-SynGRs
  To test the ability of the IND series of BET ligands to functionally engage BET proteins in living cells, we generated bifunctional SynGRs. We first synthesized the FXN-targeting polyamide PA1 by solid phase synthesis using Boc-beta-alanine PAM resin following the established protocol.40 Stepwise yields were not measured. After completion of its synthesis, the polyamide was detached from the resin support by aminolysis with 3,3’-diamino-N-methyldipropylamine at 55 °C for 12 h (Scheme 2). The crude polyamide was purified by Prep-HPLC, yielding a white solid with 30–36% yield. We then attached the Fmoc-NH PEG-6 COOH to polyamide PA1 using standard coupling reagents, HATU, DMF and DIPEA followed by FMOC deprotection in the presence of 10% piperidine in DMF to obtain PA1-PEG6-NH2 in quantitative yield. Finally, the benzodiazepine indole acid derivatives were activated with HATU in DMF and DIPEA and coupled with PA1-PEG6-NH2 to afford IND-SynGRs with 60–70% yields: 3IND-SynGR, 4IND-SynGR, 5IND-SynGR, and 6IND-SynGR (Scheme 2).

  Next, we tested the ability of these IND-SynGRs to license transcription across disease-causing repressive GAA repeats within the FXN gene in GM04078 fibroblast cells and in GM15850 lymphoblast cells derived from two different FRDA patients (Figure 4A–C and S8). In agreement with their affinity for BD2 in SPR assays, 4IND-SynGR, 5IND-SynGR, and 6IND-SynGR restored FXN expression, while 3IND-SynGR showed minimal activation even after prolonged incubation (Figure 4B). Consistent with markedly lower affinity for BD2, prolonged treatment with higher concentrations of 3IND-SynGR (5 μM) did result in modest activation of FXN transcription (Figure 4C). More specifically, even at 24 and 48 hours, the relative activity of each SynGR at the FXN locus agreed well with the SPR-based affinity measurements of the positional isomers for BD2 (Table 1); however, any selectivity for BD2 over BD1 seems to play an inconsequential role in SynGR gene regulatory activity. This observation is consistent with previous results, wherein SynGRs bearing BET ligands that favor BD1 are less effective than those that engage BD2 with higher affinity [34]. The observed functional trends were not limited to the GM04078 fibroblast cells that carry ~420 GAA repeats in both FXN alleles. Similar activity profiles were observed in GM15850 lymphoblasts that bear ~650 and ~1030 GAA repeats on the two alleles (Figure S8). In lymphoblast cells, the activities of 4IND-SynGR, 5IND-SynGR and 6IND-SynGR are each minimal at 24 h (1 μM) post treatment but increase to a meaningful level by 48 h (Figure 4).


## Discussion:
  This study contributes to the broader field of structure-activity relationships in drug design, offering actionable insights for the development of selective ligands targeting specific domains of BET proteins and potentially other related proteins for therapeutic applications in the context of chromatin regulation and gene expression.

  The varying affinities observed among the positional isomers of the indole benzodiazepine analogs for the bromodomains of BRD2 underscore the importance of the position of the indole group in modulating their interactions. While the intricate network of interactions seen in the crystal structures of BRD2-BD2 in complex with 4IND and 6IND further highlight the complexity of ligand recognition. Analysis of these crystal structures shows that both 4IND and 6IND bind to the canonical H4K12ac recognition pocket, a crucial region for protein-protein interactions involved in chromatin regulation, suggesting that binding of these ligands to BRD2 may compete with its normal interactions with acetylated histone peptides. Interestingly, the crystallographic data also revealed the dynamic nature of ligand binding, suggesting a degree of flexibility that could impact ligand interactions and binding affinity.

  Implementing the methodology of unbiased classical MD simulations, we delineated the behavioral propensity of indolyl-benzodiazepine isomers and their dynamicity at the site of the human BRD2-BD2 and BRD2-BD1 on proportionate outlook. In our study, we uncovered the superiority of 4IND associated longer time of residence over 3IND and 6IND via the mediation of the deeper side contact resolution, together with favorable protein-ligand conformational flexibility, and complementary pocket-shape plasticity. Positionally exclusive and empirically tested isomers of indolyl-benzodiazepine behaved discretely with a notable difference in interaction sustainability. Overall, 4IND exhibited the most promising stable trajectory profile through managing interactions at the deeper located residues (Phe372, Cys425, Tyr428, Asn429) as well as opening (Val376, Leu381, His433, Val435) of the BD2 cavity. Furthermore, the pivotality of contact formation of indole ring with His433 accompanied by other proximal hydrophobic amino acids facilitated the conformational stability of 4IND at the site of the BD2 domain of BRD2 that was found obligatory and possibly rationalized its specificity towards the BD1 domain. Longer simulation times and enhanced sampling techniques could potentially yield even more comprehensive insights into the ligand behavior. Moving forward, these insights may guide the design and optimization of future BET bromodomain-selective ligands with improved binding and selectivity characteristics.

  The ability of indole benzodiazepine-based SynGRs to modulate transcription within a disease-relevant gene locus underscores their broader potential as tools for therapeutic intervention. In particular, the correlation between ligand affinity and functional activity provides a valuable framework for future ligand optimization and the design of more potent and selective SynGRs or other proximity-inducing chimeric molecules. It is worth noting that, as long as the molecules bind BD2 with high affinity, the selectivity (or lack thereof) of these SynGRs for BD2 over BD1 has minimal impact on their gene regulatory activity in a cellular context. While selectivity remains an essential consideration in ligand design more generally, these results suggest that other factors, potentially involving distinct protein-protein interactions and chromatin dynamics, could contribute to the observed transcriptional outcomes in this case. Further investigation into the mechanistic basis of SynGR-mediated gene expression holds promise for advancing our understanding of chromatin biology and treating diseases caused by genetic lesions or epigenetic dysfunction. More broadly, the distinctive binding and selectivity profiles of the BD2 ligands disclosed here can be leveraged to develop a calibrated set of “warheads” for a growing repertoire of proximity-based chemical probes and therapeutic agents.


## Conclusion:
  In summary, our study reveals the contribution of the chlorobenzene moiety of IBET-762 in modulating the binding affinity and selectivity of BET inhibitors towards bromodomains. Modest changes to the phenyl ring significantly reduced binding to isolated bromodomains as well as the full length BRD2 and BRD4 BET proteins. Interestingly, positional isomers of indoles conferred dramatically different binding and selectivity profiles for the two bromodomains. Furthermore, the incorporation of these indolyl-benzodiazepine BET ligands into SynGRs enabled us to evaluate their cellular activity, reinforcing the direct correlation between bromodomain affinity, BD2 selectivity and transcriptional activation in the context of Friedreich’s ataxia patient-derived cells. These findings provide new insights for the design of next-generation BET inhibitors and catalytic proximity-inducing molecules, offering a promising path for further refinement and therapeutic development in targeting BET proteins.

### General methods:
  Reagents and solvents were purchased from standard suppliers and used without further purification. All Reactions were carried out using conventional glassware. All reagents and solvents were used as received unless otherwise stated. Reagents were of 95% purity or greater, and solvents were HPLC grade unless otherwise stated. Any anhydrous solvents used were purchased as “anhydrous” grade and used without further drying. Silica gel chromatography purification was performed using prepacked silica gel cartridges (Biotage). Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were determined with a Bruker-500 spectrometer (500 MHz). Chemical shifts are reported in parts per million (ppm, δ units). The following NMR abbreviations are used: s = singlet, d = doublet, dd = double doublet, t = triplet, q = quartet, m = multiplet, br s = broad singlet peak. High-resolution mass spectrometric analysis was performed on a Xevo G2-S Tof instrument (Waters). The microflex LT/SH (Bruker) was used for MALDI-TOF analysis. Chemical purities of the SynGRs were analyzed by high performance liquid chromatography on an C18 column using Acetonitrile (0.1% TFA) /H2O (0.1% TFA) as the mobile phase with a flow rate 1mL/min. All the SynGrs showed purity ≥ 95%.

## Methods

### Expression and purification of 6xHis-tagged BRD2-BD1 and BRD2-BD2
  The 6xHis-tagged constructs of the first (6HIS-BD1) and second (6HIS-BD2) bromodomains of BRD2, used for the SPR studies, were purified essentially as described previously [41], with some modifications. The BRD2-BD1/pET28a(+) clone was transformed into BL21(DE3)pLysS chemically competent cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA) following the manufacturer’s protocol, and maintained on LB agar plates supplemented with 50 μg/mL of kanamycin (LB/kan). The BRD2-BD2/pET28a(+) clone was transformed into BL21Star(DE3) chemically competent cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) following the manufacturer’s protocol, and maintained on Luria-Bertani media (LB) agar plates supplemented with 50 μg/mL of kanamycin (LB/kan). After confirmation of 6HIS-BD1 and 6HIS-BD2 expression, several transformants were maintained as glycerol stocks and stored at −80°C until further use. For large (4 L) expression preparations of 6HIS-BD1 and 6HIS-BD2, a 2 mL LB/kan pre-culture was seeded from a glycerol stock and grown at 37°C on an orbital shaker set to 175 rpm for 6–8 hours. The pre-culture was used to seed a 150 mL LB/kan culture, which was grown overnight at 37°C on an orbital shaker set to 175 rpm. The next day, four 1 L LB/kan (BD1) or Terrific Broth (supplemented with glycerol), TB/kan (BD2), cultures were seeded from the overnight culture and grown 37°C on an orbital shaker set to 175 rpm until the OD600 reached 1.6–1.9 (BD1) or 0.6–0.7 (BD2). The temperature was reduced to 18°C and the cultures continued to grow for 1.5–2 hours. IPTG was added to 0.1 mM to induce expression, and the cultures were grown overnight 18°C on an orbital shaker set to 175 rpm. The induced cultures were harvested by centrifugation at 4°C. The cell pellets were flash frozen in LN2 and stored at −80°C until further use. 6HIS-BD1 and 6HIS-BD2 were purified separately using nearly identical protocols, as follows. All chromatography purifications were performed at 4°C. Induced pellets were quick-thawed and transferred to ice immediately. The pellets were re-suspended in binding buffer (BB) composed of 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.5 at RT), 500 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole and 5% glycerol, supplemented with 1 mM TCEP and one tab (per pre-lysate) of Roche Complete, EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA). Cells were placed on ice and lysed by sonication (repeated twice). Nucleic acids were precipitated by the addition of 0.15% (v/v) of polyethylenimine (PEI) solution, pH 7.9 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO), stirred and incubated on ice for 10 minutes. Lysates were centrifuged at 4°C to remove cell debris and other precipitates. The supernatants were decanted carefully and kept on ice. The lysate supernatants were applied to BB-equilibrated HisPur Cobalt resin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) and stirred gently for 1 hour at 4°C. The slurries were transferred to gravity columns and the flowthroughs were collected by gravity flow. The resin beds were washed with six bed volumes of BB, then washed with six bed volumes of wash 2 buffer (BB with 30 mM imidazole). 6HIS-BD1 and 6HIS-BD2 were eluted from the HisPur Cobalt columns by sequential elutions by applying two column volumes of elution buffers with increasing concentrations of imidazole (BB with 50 mM, 100 mM, 150 mM and 250 mM imidazole). Eluates were collected by gravity flow. HisPur Cobalt purifications were checked by SDS-PAGE, and elution fractions with the purest 6HIS-BD1 or 6HIS-BD2 were combined and concentrated using an Amicon Ultra, 10kDa MWCO filtration device (MilliporeSigma), at 4°C. The HisPur Cobalt-purified and concentrated 6HIS-BD1 and 6HIS-BD2 were polished by size-exclusion chromatography (SEC). Concentrates were applied to a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300GL column, equilibrated with buffer composed of 25 mM HEPES, pH 7.5 at RT, and 200 mM NaCl, using an AKTA pure protein purification system (column and AKTA from Cytiva, Marlborough, MA). 6HIS-BD1 and 6HIS-BD2 were eluted with the above buffer, and the purest fractions (checked by SDS-PAGE) were collected and combined. The N-terminal hexa-histidine tag was cleaved by thrombin digestion overnight at 4°C in 20 mM TRIS-HCl pH 8.0, 200 mM NaCl and 1 mM TCEP [tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine hydrochloride] (Sigma-Aldrich). The undigested protein (BD2) was separated by size-exclusion chromatography (Superdex 200 Increase 10/300GL column). The final concentrations of purified 6HIS-BD1 and 6HIS-BD2 were measured by BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.). Purified proteins were aliquoted, flash frozen in LN2 and stored at −80°C until further use.


### Surface plasmon resonance
  SPR binding measurements were performed on a Biacore S200 (Cytiva). BRD2-BD1 and BRD2-BD2, diluted to 200 nM in immobilization/running buffer (10 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 50 μM EDTA, 0.005% Tween 20, 6% DMSO), were immobilized on NiHC 200M sensor chips (Xantec) at a density of 1500 RU. Analytes 3IND, 4IND, 5IND, 6IND were flowed over the surface by injecting each analyte at concentrations ranging from 10,000 – 0.3 nM at a flow rate of 60 μL/min. During the association phase, complexes were allowed to form over 60 seconds until steady-state equilibrium was reached. Upon reaching steady-state, complexes were allowed to dissociate over 60 seconds. Steady-state measurements were fitted to the one-site specific binding equation Y = Bmax*X/(Kd+X) where Y is the specific binding and Bmax represents maximal binding, using the GraphPad Prism software. All measurements were run in triplicate.


### Co-crystallization of BRD2-BD2 complexes
  Purified BRD2-BD2 was concentrated to 12 mg/ml and incubated with 2 molar equivalents of 3IND, 4IND or 6IND compound on ice for 30 min. Complexes were screened for crystallization using commercial sparse matrix (Qiagen) in 96-well hanging drop plates with drops consisting of 150 nl reservoir solution and 150 nl protein solution by a Mosquito robot (TTP Labtech) at 18°C. Rectangular shaped crystals formed for BRD2-BD2–3IND and BRD2-BD2–4IND complexes and grew over 4–6 days in 0.2 M sodium fluoride or 0.2 M ammonium tartrate dibasic pH 7.0, and 20% (w/v) PEG3350. Larger crystals were grown in 24-well VDX hanging drop plate under the same condition with (w/v) 20–23% PEG3350. Crystals were cryoprotected with the reservoir solution supplemented with 10% (v/v) glycerol and flash frozen in liquid nitrogen. Tetragonal prism crystals for BRD2-BD2–6IND formed with a well solution of 0.1 M TRIS-HCl pH 8.5 and 25% (v/v) PEG 550 MME. The best crystals appeared within 2 days in 0.1 M TRIS-HCl pH 8.5 and 28% (v/v) PEG 550 MME and were cryoprotected using well solution with 10% (v/v) PEG 400 prior to flash freezing in liquid nitrogen.


### X-ray data collection and structure determination
  Diffraction data were collected 100 K on beam line SER-CAT 22ID at the Advanced Photon Source, Argonne National Laboratory, Chicago. X-ray beam of wavelength 1.0 Å with an exposure time of 0.1 seconds was used for each frame. The diffraction data were integrated and reduced using HKL-2000 suite.42,45 The reduced data were scaled using scalepack [42]. The crystals of BRD2-BD2–3IND and BRD2-BD2–4IND belong to the primitive orthorhombic space group P212121 with cell dimensions (3IND: a = 62.18, b = 99.87, c = 120.15 Å; 4IND: a = 65.44, b = 99.55, c = 119.24 Å), and the crystals of BRD2-BD2–6IND belong to the primitive tetragonal space group P41212 with cell dimensions a = 117.80, b = 117.80, c = 42.56 Å. The BRD2-BD2 complexes were solved by the molecular replacement method using 6WWB as the initial model by Phaser-MR [43]. The difference Fourier map (Fo-Fc) clearly revealed the electron density for the compounds (3IND, 4IND and 6IND). Model building was carried out using COOT [44]. The final models were refined using PHENIX [45]. Data processing and refinement statistics are shown in Table S1.


### Molecular modelling
  Molecular modeling work was performed in MOE (MOE Release 2020.09: Chemical Computing Group ULC, Montreal, Canada, 2020) and the Schrodinger Maestro molecular modeling package (Schrödinger Release 2019–4: LigPrep, Schrödinger, LLC, New York, NY, 2019). Internally solved crystal structure of BRD2-BD2 bound to 4IND (PDB Id: 8UGU) and 6IND (PDB Id: 8UGV) were used as the receptor-ligand structure to perform MD simulation. Specifically, 4IND bound BRD2-BD2 structure was prepared using the Protein Preparation Wizard [46] with this tool, hydrogen-bond optimization and restrained minimization of the complex (converge heavy atoms to RMSD 0.3 Å) were carried out. Therefore, maestro engine was utilized to dock 3IND at the BD2 domain of BRD2. Besides, 4IND was docked using similar methodology utilizing the crystal structure of BRD2-BD1 protein complex with IBET [37] using Schrodinger Glide Extra Precision method [48]. The resulting structures were relaxed with a 500 ns MD simulation at 300K and 1.01325 bar pressure using a procedure adapted from Nishiguchi et al [42] with TIP3P water model and an orthorhombic boundary box with a 15Å buffer, the system was neutralized by adding Na+ ions where 4 Å radius around the ligand was particularly excluded. OPLS3e forcefield was used to simulate the system in GPU-accelerated Desmond (Schrödinger Release 2019–4: Desmond Molecular Dynamics System, D. E. Shaw Research, New York, NY, 2019. Maestro-Desmond Interoperability Tools, Schrödinger, New York, NY, 2019). NPT ensemble was used with timestep of 2 fs and Coulombic short range cutoff radius was 9Å. Finally, a recording interval of 500 ps resulted in 1000 frames for a 500 ns simulation.


### Activation of frataxin expression by polyamide compounds in cells
  Compounds were tested for activation of Frataxin expression in GM04078 cells. Cells were grown in MEM supplemented with nonessential amino acids and 15% (v/v) Fetal Bovine Serum. For compound testing cells were plated in six well dishes at a density of 200,000 cells per well and incubated overnight at 37C, 5% CO2 in a tissue culture incubator. Compounds were then added to the cells to the desired final concentration and incubated at 37C for 24, 48 or72 hours before collecting RNA samples.

  Total RNA was extracted from the cells utilizing either a Nucleospin RNA plus kit (Takara/Macherey-Nagel #740984) or a Nucleospin 96 RNA kit (Takara/Macherey-Nagel #740466). For RNA purification, medium was first removed from the wells, then 350 ul of LBP (or RA1) buffer (supplied with the kit used) was added to each well. Cell lysis was ensured by gently swirling the six well dishes clockwise and counterclockwise for about 15 seconds each direction. All RNA samples were processed further according to the instructions supplied with the respective kit. RNA concentrations and purity were determined using a Nanodrop.


### qRT-PCR analysis
  Frataxin expression levels were analyzed by qRT-PCR with one primer pair specific for Frataxin and one primer pair specific for GAPDH [19, 30]. Five hundred nanograms of RNA from each sample were reverse transcribed using LunaScript® RT Master mix (New England BioLabs) in a final volume of 20 μl. Following reverse transcription, each cDNA sample was diluted by addition of 50 μl of RNAse free water and 2.5 μl of each diluted sample was then used per qPCR reaction. To each reaction 2.5 μl of each primer pair (200 nM final concentration) and 5 μl 2x Luna® Universal qPCR Master mix (New England BioLabs) were added to a final volume of 10 μl. Q-PCR was performed on a QuantStudio™ 6 instrument (Applied Biosystems) for 40 cycles in a 384 well format and included a melting curve analysis. Ct (Cq) values were determined with the Software package “Design and Analysis 2.5.1” which was supplied with the instrument, thresholds were only adjusted as necessary. Relative mRNA expression levels were then calculated by the comparative CT method. The expression levels of GAPDH were used for normalization of the mRNA expression levels of the Frataxin gene.
