# Machine Learning‐Based Predictive Modeling Maximizes the Efficacy of mTOR/p53 Co‐Targeting Therapy Against AML


## ABSTRACT
  Although mTOR signaling plays a key role in acute myeloid leukemia (AML), mTOR inhibitors have shown limited efficacy against AML in clinical trials. In this study, we found that the anti‐leukemic effect of mTOR inhibition was mediated in part through the TP53 pathway. mTOR inhibition by rapamycin and TP53 activation by DS‐5272 collaboratively induced the downregulation of MYC and MCL1 partly through miR‐34a, thereby inducing cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in AML cells. Joint non‐negative matrix factorization (JNMF) and statistical regression analysis using public AML databases revealed that monocytic AMLs with distinctive gene expression profiles were highly sensitive to mTOR inhibition, leading to the generation of an 11‐gene score (Rapa‐11) to predict the rapamycin sensitivity of each monocytic AML. Consistent with our in silico prediction, mouse AML cells expressing MLL‐AF9, the monocytic AML with a low Rapa‐11 score, were highly sensitive to rapamycin, whereas those expressing RUNX1‐ETO or SETBP1/ASXL1 mutations were not. Co‐treatment with rapamycin and DS‐5272 had a dramatic in vivo effect on MLL‐AF9‐driven AML, curing 85% of the leukemic mice. Thus, machine learning‐based predictive approaches identified monocytic AML with wild‐type TP53 and low Rapa‐11 score as a rapamycin‐sensitive AML subtype and an ideal target for mTOR/p53 co‐targeting therapy.


## Introduction
  Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a heterogeneous group of diseases characterized by the uncontrolled proliferation of myeloid progenitor cells in the bone marrow [1, 2]. In the 1970s, the French‐American‐British (FAB) cooperative group developed a classification system based solely on the morphologic features of each AML in the 1970s [3]. As genetic changes in AML became apparent, the FAB classification was replaced by the World Health Organization (WHO) classification with genetic information [4]. Interestingly, recent studies have shown the importance of the cellular origin of AML, mainly reflected by its morphology, in determining the response to certain drugs such as venetoclax [5, 6]. As a result, morphology‐based classification is receiving renewed attention for the development of personalized therapies for individual AML patients [7].

  The mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) is an atypical serine/threonine kinase that is present in two distinct complexes, mTORC1 and mTORC2 [8]. mTORC1 is primarily involved in promoting cell growth and proliferation by stimulating protein synthesis and inhibiting autophagy. mTORC2 is involved in regulating the actin cytoskeleton, cell survival, and metabolism. The mTOR pathway is often hyperactivated, leading to uncontrolled cell growth and resistance to apoptosis in various types of cancer including AML. Previous studies have demonstrated the critical role of mTOR signaling in the exacerbation and acquisition of therapeutic resistance in AML, making it an important target for therapeutic intervention [9, 10, 11]. Several mTOR inhibitors, such as rapamycin (sirolimus) and its analogs (rapalogs), have been investigated for their therapeutic potential in AML. Preclinical studies have shown that mTOR inhibitors effectively reduce AML cell growth and improve survival in mouse models [12, 13, 14]. However, AML patients appear to differ in their susceptibility to mTOR inhibitors, reflecting their biological heterogeneity. Therefore, it is important to identify predictive biomarkers to select AMLs that are truly dependent on mTOR signaling. The development of combination therapies with mTOR inhibitors is also an important challenge in improving therapeutic efficacy.

  In recent years, comprehensive genomic and pharmacological databases have become available, advancing predictive personalized research in oncology [15, 16]. The Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) provides mutation, copy number alterations, mRNA expression profiles, and drug response data for more than 1100 cancer cell lines [17]. The Cancer Dependency Map Portal (DepMap) includes data from CRISPR knockout and shRNA knockdown screens to show genomic dependencies in each cancer cell line (https://depmap.org/portal/). The BeatAML database is another useful database containing genetic and transcriptomic information on primary AML samples with associated clinical metadata and drug response annotations [18]. These large databases and various machine learning methods have enabled drug response modeling based on the molecular profiles of cancer.

  In this study, we applied machine learning‐based approaches to predict drug sensitivity in AML and found that AMLs with genetic (wild‐type TP53), morphological (monocytic), and specific transcriptomic features were highly sensitive to rapamycin. We also showed that mTOR/TP53 co‐targeting therapy has a dramatic in vivo effect in a mouse monocytic AML model transformed with the leukemogenic fusion gene MLL‐AF9; whereas the combination showed only a modest effect in other AML models. These results highlight the importance of patient stratification to maximize the effects of rapamycin‐based therapies in AML.


## Materials and Methods

### Cell Culture
  Human leukemia cell lines were cultured in RMPI‐1640 medium (FUJIFILM Wako), 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Biosera), and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Mouse MLL‐AF9 cells were cultured in RPMI‐1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS (Biosera), 10 ng/mL mouse SCF, 10 ng/mL mouse IL‐3, 10 ng/mL mouse IL‐6, 10 ng/mL mouse GM‐CSF, and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Information on the cell lines and culture conditions is provided in Appendix S1.


### Animal Experiments
  Male C57BL/6 mice were obtained from the Sankyo Labo Service Corporation (Japan). NSG mice were purchased from Charles River Laboratories Japan. All animal studies were approved by the Animal Care Committee of the Institute of Medical Science at the University of Tokyo (approval number: PA21‐67 and PA22‐23). Details are provided in Appendix S1.


### Cell Viability Assay, Apoptosis and Cell Cycle Analysis, and Western Blotting
  Cells were treated with DS‐5272 and rapamycin, both individually and in combination. Cell viability was determined using the Cell Counting Kit‐8 (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) and the combination index (CI) was determined using ComboSyn software (ComboSyn Inc., Paramus, NJ, USA) [19]. Apoptosis was assessed using Annexin V‐APC (Biolegend, Cat#640920) and DAPI (Biolegend, Cat#422801). Cell cycle was assessed by DAPI staining after fixation and permeabilization with 99.5% (v/v) cold ethanol. For Western blotting, whole cell lysates were separated by SDS‐PAGE and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Immobilon‐P; Millipore). The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in TBS containing 0.05% Tween 20 (TBST) for 30 min and then subjected to immunoblotting with the indicated antibodies. Signals were detected using the ECL system, and images were captured using an ImageQuant LAS 4000 Luminescent Image Analyzer (GE). The antibodies used are provided in Table S1 and details are provided in Appendix S1.


### RNA‐Seq
  MOLM13 cells were treated with rapamycin, DS‐5272, or both. After 24 h of treatment, RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen), and cDNA was synthesized by random hexamer‐primed reverse transcription. Libraries were sequenced using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 in the paired‐end mode. Differential expression was analyzed using a linear model with limma (v3.54.2). Pathway analysis was performed using gene ontology (GO) enrichment and gene set enrichment analyses (GSEA) (https://www.gsea‐msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp) [20]. TPM values are provided in Table S2 and details are provided in Appendix S1. RNA‐seq data are available in NCBI under BioProject ID PRJNA1265436.


### miR‐34a Inhibition
  MOLM‐13 cells were transfected with non‐targeting (NT) control or hsa‐miR‐34a‐5p inhibitor via electroporation using the Amaxa 4D‐Nucleofector electroporation system (Lonza Bioscience, Basel, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Sequences of NT and hsa‐miR‐34a‐5p inhibitor were provided in the Supplemental Methods. Details of guidance and machine settings are provided in Appendix S1.


### Transplantation Assay and Treatments
  Murine MLL‐AF9, cSAM, and RUNX1‐ETO cells were generated as previously described [21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. These mouse leukemia cells and MOLM13 were transplanted intravenously (1.0 × 106 cells/mouse) into unirradiated C57BL/6 or NSG mice. For drug studies, 100 mg/kg/day DS‐5272 (Daiichi Sankyo) was administered orally to mice three times a week for 2 weeks, starting 3 days after transplantation. 4 mg/kg rapamycin (LC Laboratories, R‐5000) was administered intraperitoneally to mice three times a week for 2 weeks, starting 3 days after transplantation. Cell information is provided in Appendix S1.


### Machine Learning‐Based Analysis
  Joint non‐negative matrix factorization (JNMF) [26] was applied to CCLE and CTRP datasets, both available through the DepMap portal. These datasets include gene expression profiles from 31 human AML cell lines (CCLE) and drug sensitivities (CTRP). To correlate gene expression profiles with rapamycin sensitivity in human monocytic AMLs, we developed predictive models using linear and random forest regression algorithms, incorporating gene sets associated with rapamycin sensitivity. The performance of each model was assessed by calculating the coefficient of determination (R 2). For detailed procedures, please refer to the Supplemental Methods. The Papa‐11 score can be calculated using the code available in the GitHub repository by executing it on Google Colaboratory (Li, 2025, GitHub. Available at: https://github.com/Jingmei123/Rapa‐11). Details on data resources and preprocessing, the JNMF calculation formula, bootstrap resampling validation, and the construction of the rapamycin sensitivity predictive system are provided in Appendix S1.


### Statistics
  Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or standard error of the mean (SEM), as indicated. p values were calculated by one‐way ANOVA with Dunnett's multiple comparison test and multiple paired t‐tests. Significance between the survival cohorts was assessed using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and log‐rank test. p values < 0.05 were considered significant. All calculations were performed using GraphPad Prism software. The sample size “n” indicates the biological replicates.

  Detailed information can be found in Appendix S1.


## Results

### Co‐Targeting mTOR and TP53 Shows Synergistic Anti‐Leukemic Effects in AML
  We first investigated genetic mutations in AML that are highly associated with sensitivity to the mTOR inhibitor rapamycin using the BeatAML and DepMap datasets. AML cells with deleterious TP53 mutations were unresponsive, whereas those with functional TP53 tended to be sensitive to rapamycin (Figure 1A,B). In addition, we found a positive correlation between rapamycin sensitivity and MDM2 (a negative regulator of TP53) dependency in AML cells (Figure 1C). These results suggest that the anti‐leukemic effect of rapamycin is mediated, in part, through the TP53 pathway.

  Next, we assessed the combined effect of rapamycin and a TP53 activating drug, DS‐5272 [27, 28], in human AML cell lines with wild‐type TP53: MOLM13, MV4‐11, and OCI‐AML3. We designed a two‐drug combination at constant ratios based on the IC50 values of each drug. The combination of rapamycin and DS‐5272 exhibited significantly higher cytotoxicity than either drug alone, particularly in MOLM13 and OCI‐AML3 cells (Figure 1D). We then evaluated the effects of rapamycin and DS‐5272 on the cell cycle and apoptosis of AML cells. Treatment with either DS‐5272 or rapamycin alone induced apoptosis and cell cycle arrest in MOLM13, MV4‐11, and OCI‐AML3 cells, and co‐treatment further augmented these effects (Figure 2A,B, Figures S1 and S2). Thus, co‐targeting mTOR and TP53 showed potent and synergistic anti‐leukemic effects on TP53‐intact AML cells.


### Co‐Targeting of mTOR and TP53 Induces Downregulation of MYC and MCL1
  Next, we evaluated the molecular changes induced by mTOR inhibition and TP53 activation in the MOLM13 cells. RNA‐seq analysis revealed the activation of the TP53 pathway and inhibition of the mTORC1 pathway in cells treated with DS‐5272 or rapamycin, respectively. Interestingly, co‐treatment with rapamycin and DS‐5272 induced the downregulation of MYC target genes (Figure 3A, Figure S3A,B, Table S2). Consistent with these findings, we observed reduced phosphorylation of S6 (a marker of mTORC1 activation) in rapamycin‐treated cells, upregulation of TP53 and its target BBC3 (also called PUMA) in DS‐5272‐treated cells, and downregulation of MYC at the protein level in rapamycin/DS‐5272 co‐treated cells. In addition, rapamycin/DS‐5272 co‐treatment induced downregulation of the anti‐apoptotic family member MCL1, whereas the expression of another member, BCL2, remained unchanged (Figure 3B). As there was no change in the mRNA expression of MYC and MCL1 (Figure S4A), the combined treatment appears to inhibit protein translation of MYC and MCL1. We also found that miR‐34a was upregulated in DS‐5272‐treated cells, which could contribute to the reduced translation of MYC and MCL1 [29, 30] (Figure 3C). Furthermore, miR‐34a inhibition attenuated apoptosis and MYC/MCL1 downregulation induced by rapamycin/DS‐5272 co‐treatment (Figure 3D,E, Figure S4B), demonstrating the critical role of miR‐34a in mediating the effect of co‐targeting therapy. Thus, co‐targeting mTOR and TP53 inhibits the growth of AML cells by inducing cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, in part through downregulation of MYC/MCL1 and upregulation of miR‐34a.


### Monocytic AMLs Are Sensitive to Rapamycin
  To further identify AML subtypes with high sensitivity to rapamycin, we applied our newly developed pipeline integrating joint non‐negative matrix factorization (JNMF) [26] to three datasets of CCLE and CTRP, which included 31 AML cell lines, 19,160 gene expression, and pharmacological sensitivities to seven drugs commonly used in the treatment of blood cancers: azacitidine, cytarabine, venetoclax, panobinostat, imatinib, bortezomib, and rapamycin. Using these datasets compiled into two input matrices X 1 and X 2, our JNMF algorithm factorized the input matrices to matrices W, H 1, and H 2 by reducing the dimensionality into k dimensions (k = 60), which correspond to k clusters of relevant cell lines, genes, and drugs. Subsequently, among the k clusters, the significant feature clusters (z‐score > 1.96) were considered as common pattern modules (CPMs) (Figure 4A). Interestingly, four CPMs (CPM‐4, 14, 16, and 40) were associated with high sensitivity to rapamycin (Figure 4B, Figure S5) and were enriched in monocytic AML cell lines classified as French‐American‐British (FAB)‐M5 [31] (Figure 4C). These findings suggest a strong association between the rapamycin sensitivity and monocytic AML subtype. To validate this, we compared the drug sensitivity to rapamycin across different AML subtypes. Consistent with our prediction, monocytic AML cells (FAB‐M5) were most sensitive to rapamycin in the CCLE dataset (Figure 4D). Moreover, primary AML samples derived from patients with monocytic AML (FAB‐M4/M5) showed greater sensitivity to rapamycin than the other AML subtypes in the BeatAML dataset (Figure 4E). In contrast, CPMs associated with venetoclax sensitivity (CPM‐1, 4, 39, 56) were not associated with monocytic AML, except for CPM‐4, and cells derived from monocytic AML patients were relatively resistant to venetoclax, in agreement with a previous report [5] (Figure S6). Together, these results indicate that monocytic AML cells are particularly sensitive to mTOR inhibition by rapamycin.


### Development of a Scoring System to Predict Rapamycin Sensitivity in Monocytic AML
  Next, we used statistical regression algorithms to correlate gene expression with rapamycin sensitivity in human monocytic AML cells. First, we selected candidate genes, Gene Sets (1), (2), and (3), based on the JNMF results and correlation (see Methods) (Figure S7A,B). Second, we randomly divided the 14 monocytic AML cell lines in the CCLE dataset and 80 monocytic AML patient samples in the BeatAML dataset into a training (90%) and a test (10%) set. Third, we applied two statistical regression algorithms: linear regression and random forest regression, to infer the dependency of gene expression on rapamycin sensitivity in the training set and generated formulas to predict rapamycin sensitivity for all possible gene combinations (Figure 5A). We then calculated the best R 2 value of each gene set and regression model using the test set and found that the weighted sum of the expression of 11 genes from gene set (1) analyzed with the random forest regressor showed the highest R 2 value (R 2 = 0.694) for predicting rapamycin sensitivity (Figure 5B). Low Rapa‐11 score was strongly associated with high rapamycin sensitivity in monocytic AML. To confirm the robustness of our model, we performed bootstrap resampling validation (1000 iterations, 80% sampling ratio). The Rapa‐11 scoring system achieved an average R 2 of 0.860 ± 0.018 and an average mean squared error (MSE) of 0.011 ± 0.002, demonstrating consistently high predictive performance in predicting rapamycin sensitivity. Thus, machine learning‐based modeling provides an 11‐gene signature (Rapa‐11 score) that can predict the sensitivity of individual monocytic AMLs to rapamycin.

  Next, we evaluated the relationship between the Rapa‐11 score and genetic abnormalities in monocytic AML using the BeatAML dataset. Consistent with earlier findings, monocytic AMLs harboring TP53 mutations tended to exhibit high Rapa‐11 scores, indicative of rapamycin resistance. However, statistical significance was not achieved; likely due to the limited sample size. Additionally, monocytic AMLs with DNMT3A or IDH1 mutations demonstrated significantly higher Rapa‐11 scores compared to those without these mutations, suggesting their potential association with resistance to rapamycin treatment (Figure S8). Notably, our analysis also revealed substantial variability in Rapa‐11 scores among cells carrying mutations in the same gene, indicating that the Rapa‐11 score is not solely determined by the presence or absence of any single driver mutation. Instead, it appears to represent a composite signature that reflects the transcriptional landscape associated with rapamycin sensitivity.


### Remarkable Effect of TP53/mTOR Co‐Targeting Therapy in MLL‐Rearranged AML
  Finally, we investigated the combined effect of rapamycin and DS‐5272 using three mouse AML models with wild‐type Trp53 driven by MLL‐AF9 (one of the most common MLL rearrangements in AML), SETBP1/ASXL1 mutations, or RUNX1‐ETO9a. MLL‐AF9‐expressing cells [21] are mouse monocytic AML cells with a low Rapa‐11 score, indicating their sensitivity to rapamycin‐based therapies. Similarly, human monocytic AML cells with MLL rearrangements also tended to exhibit low Rapa‐11 scores (Figure S8), suggesting that MLL‐rearranged AMLs may be sensitive to rapamycin. AML cells expressing SETBP1 and ASXL1 mutations (which we termed cSAM cells [22, 23, 24]) are also monocytic AML, but have a relatively high Rapa‐11 score (Figure 6A). AML cells expressing RUNX1‐ETO9a [32], a shorter isoform of RUNX1‐ETO, are not monocytic but are more primitive AML cells [25]. Consistent with our previous reports [28, 33], activation of p53 with DS‐5272 inhibited the growth of all these mouse AML cells in vivo. However, rapamycin treatment, either alone or co‐treated with DS‐5272, showed no survival benefit in the cSAM and RUNX1‐ETO9a AML models (Figure 6B,C), confirming the limited efficacy of rapamycin on the growth of non‐monocytic AML and monocytic AML with a high Rapa‐11 score. In contrast, rapamycin alone modestly suppressed the development of MLL‐AF9‐driven AML and co‐treatment with rapamycin and DS‐5272 showed a dramatic in vivo effect on MLL‐AF9 cells, curing 85% of leukemic mice (Figure 6D). The collaborative antileukemia effect of DS‐5272 and rapamycin was further validated in a cell line‐derived xenograft (CDX) model using the monocytic AML cell line MOLM13 (Figure S9). Furthermore, co‐treatment with DS‐5272 and rapamycin resulted in the downregulation of MYC and MCL1 (Figure S10A) and synergistically inhibited the growth of mouse MLL‐AF9 cells in vitro (Figure S10B). Thus, mTOR/TP53 co‐targeting therapy exhibits a significant therapeutic effect on monocytic AMLs with wild‐type TP53 and low Rapa‐11 scores.


## Discussion
  Despite the well‐established role of mTOR signaling in AML, pharmacological targeting of mTOR has shown limited clinical benefits in AML patients [9, 10]. Since mTOR inhibition can also suppress anti‐tumor immunity, it is critical to identify AML subtypes that are genuinely dependent on mTOR signaling. Using machine learning‐based approaches, we found that monocytic AMLs with wild‐type TP53 and specific gene expression profiles (low Rapa‐11 score) were highly susceptible to rapamycin‐based therapies.

  mTOR and TP53 pathways are critical regulators of cell growth, proliferation, and survival. They are interconnected in multiple ways to form a complex network that ensures a balanced response to cellular stress, growth signals, and nutrient availability [34]. TP53 acts as a sensor of cellular stress and inhibits mTORC1, whereas mTORC1 drives cell growth and counterbalances the growth‐suppressive effects of TP53. TP53 dysfunction and mTOR pathway hyperactivation are hallmarks of human cancers including AML. Our study provides a rationale for co‐targeting TP53 and mTOR to treat certain AML subtypes, as suggested in a previous report [35]. We also found that mTOR inhibition and TP53 activation cooperatively induced the downregulation of MYC and MCL1 at the protein level. Previous studies have shown that both MYC and MCL1 mRNAs are under tight translational control, which is highly dependent on the activity of mTORC1 [36, 37, 38]. Our data and previous reports [29, 30] also suggest that TP53 activation inhibits the translation of MYC and MCL1 through upregulation of miR‐34a, which binds to the 3′ untranslated region of MYC and MCL1 mRNAs to inhibit their translation. Given the critical roles of MYC and MCL1 in cell growth and apoptosis, respectively, the reduction of MYC and MCL1 protein levels is likely to be associated with the potent anti‐leukemic activity of the co‐targeting of mTOR and TP53.

  In addition to genetic alterations, cellular origins and epigenetic changes can influence the drug response of individual cancer cells. In this study, we applied the novel JNMF‐based algorithm [26], which incorporates the multivariate interaction of gene expression levels and drug response as continuous variables, to identify core cell lines associated with the response to each drug. This approach identified monocytic AML, which is relatively resistant to current standard treatments [24], as a rapamycin‐sensitive AML subtype. Since monocytic AML was shown to be preferentially dependent on MCL1 for energy metabolism and survival [5], rapamycin‐mediated MCL1 downregulation likely contributes to the potent growth inhibitory effect of rapamycin against monocytic AML. Furthermore, we developed a machine learning‐based framework to predict rapamycin response based on the gene expression data of each monocytic AML, generating an optimal 11‐gene signature (Rapa‐11 score) that inversely correlates with rapamycin sensitivity. As a proof of concept, we showed that an MLL‐AF9‐driven mouse monocytic AML model with wild‐type Trp53 and a low Rapa‐11 score was highly sensitive to rapamycin/DS‐5272 co‐treatment in vivo. The strong, even curative effect of TP53/mTOR co‐targeting therapy suggests the promise of this treatment strategy for certain types of AML. In contrast, a non‐monocytic AML model driven by RUNX1‐ETO and a monocytic but high Rapa‐11 AML model driven by SETBP1 and ASXL1 mutations did not respond well to rapamycin, reflecting the heterogeneous response of AML cells to mTOR inhibition. These results underscore the importance of patient selection to maximize the efficacy of mTOR inhibitors in the treatment of AML.

  Among the 11 genes used in the Rapa‐11 scoring system, HGF is most closely related to the mTOR signaling pathway. HGF activates the MET receptor tyrosine kinase, which in turn can activate downstream signaling pathways including the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. CCNA1, a key regulator of the cell cycle, is influenced by mTOR signaling, which can modulate its expression and activity. Both mTOR signaling and CCNA1 are implicated in cell cycle dysregulation in cancer cells. Although the remaining genes are not directly associated with the mTOR pathway (Figure S11), they may still influence mTOR signaling through their roles in various processes, such as cell cycle and inflammation (TPSAB1, SLPI), immune response (LY86, MPEG1, NCF2), and metabolism or extracellular matrix regulation (PKIB, METTL7B, NID1, DPEP1). A limitation of our scoring system is the relatively small amount of rapamycin response data used for training and the lack of data with recorded clinical drug responses. Therefore, the Rapa‐11 scoring system should be further improved with additional data from various experimental and clinical studies in the future.

  In summary, our findings indicate that monocytic AMLs with wild‐type TP53 and specific expression patterns of 11 genes are promising candidates for rapamycin‐based therapies. We also demonstrated that co‐targeting mTOR and TP53 provides curative treatment in a murine monocytic AML model with MLL rearrangement. Furthermore, our results highlight the importance of drug‐transcriptomic approaches for the selection of cancer treatments. This strategy can be applied to a wide range of tumors and therapies, enabling the development of personalized treatments for individual patients.
