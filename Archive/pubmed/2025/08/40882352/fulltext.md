# Functional and clinical insights into nuclear receptor variants for advancing precision diagnostics in male infertility


## Background
  Nuclear receptors, including steroidogenic factor 1 (NR5A1/SF1) and the androgen receptor (AR), are transcription factors regulating physiological processes, e.g., reproduction. Pathogenic variants in these receptors are associated with a broad spectrum of phenotypes, ranging from differences in sexual development to isolated male infertility. However, standardised methods to classify variants of uncertain significance (VUS) in these genes are lacking, complicating diagnosis and individualised treatment.


## Methods
  We queried rare NR5A1 and AR variants in exome/genome sequencing data of 2127 infertile men. Pathogenicity assessment included thorough clinical phenotyping, familial segregation, in silico pathogenicity prediction combining traditional and machine-learning tools, and functional evaluation of the variants using in vitro assays.


## Findings
  We identified a total of seven heterozygous NR5A1 variants in 10 infertile men and 22 hemizygous AR variants in 31 infertile men with severe oligo-/azoospermia. Of these, three SF1 and seven AR variants displayed significantly reduced transcriptional activity. This study led to the reclassification of one NR5A1 variant and ten AR variants, including three AR variants that were reclassified from VUS to (likely) pathogenic. Combined phenotype, in silico, and in vitro data led to 60% of all variants (17 out of 29) being classified as (likely) pathogenic per ACMG guidelines, providing insights into the phenotypic features and spermatogenic impairment in affected men.


## Interpretation
  This study highlights the importance of combining clinical and experimental data for the assessment of VUS in nuclear receptors to reliably classify pathogenicity and to improve patient diagnosis and care.

## Introduction
  Infertility is an increasing public health problem, affecting around 17% of men worldwide.1 Although a large proportion of cases is likely to have a genetic component, genetic diagnosis for male infertility lags behind other common diseases.2 NR5A1 (nuclear receptor subfamily 5 group A member 1, MIM 184757, encoding the steroidogenic factor 1, SF1) and AR (androgen receptor, MIM 313700) encode two members of the nuclear receptor superfamily with important roles in sexual development and male reproductive function.3,4 Both AR and SF1 are crucial in the development of primary and secondary male sexual characteristics and the initiation and maintenance of spermatogenesis,5 and variants in the two genes have been associated with male infertility.4,6 However, there is lack of comprehensive and functional analyses of NR5A1 and AR variants, particularly regarding missense variants classified as variants of uncertain significance (VUS). This highlights the need for studies involving large, clinically well-characterised cohorts of men with severe spermatogenic failure and the importance of in vitro assessment to aid in the classification of such variants.

  SF1 is an orphan transcription factor that has also been shown to bind phospholipids albeit with low affinity and plays a crucial role in gonadal differentiation and function. It regulates crucial genes for steroid hormone production, affecting the development of sexual characteristics, and maintaining reproductive function.7 Variants in NR5A1 are linked to a range of conditions, including differences in sexual development (DSD) and adrenal insufficiency.8 AR is the quintessential receptor for male sexual steroids, androgens (e.g., testosterone), and functions as a ligand-inducible transcription factor, regulating spermatogenesis and fertility.9 In males, androgens play a crucial role in foetal sexual differentiation, pubertal maturation, and the maintenance of sexual characteristics throughout life.10 Pathogenic AR variants have been associated with a broad spectrum of phenotypes, including Kennedy's disease, cancer development, and varying degrees of androgen insensitivity syndrome (AIS; CAIS-complete, PAIS-partial, and MAIS-mild AIS).11, 12, 13

  Although a high load of variants in NR5A1 and AR have been reported in connection with male infertility,3,14,15 it is challenging to distinguish benign from clinically relevant/pathogenic. This is a common difficulty especially for missense variants, where functional evidence is generally lacking and classification relies primarily on in silico predictions. In fact, overall 75% of all missense variants are classified as VUS according to the ACMG-AMP (American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Association for Molecular Pathology) guidelines.16 In the case of NR5A1 and AR, despite various prior investigations, only a limited number of variants per gene have undergone functional investigation specifically in the context of isolated male infertility.3,4,6,11,12,17, 18, 19, 20

  Our study addresses these gaps by employing a multi-layered approach to investigate identified male infertility-associated missense variants in NR5A1 and AR. By analysing a large cohort of 2127 men with severe spermatogenic failure and combining in silico predictions with established in vitro functional assays, we determine the functional relevance of these variants. By demonstrating the impaired transcriptional activity of specific variants on target promoters, we establish a direct link to spermatogenic failure and infertility. This study highlights the importance of comprehensive phenotypic description of infertile men to improve variant classification. A close collaboration between research, genetic diagnostics, and clinics is essential to ensure accurate variant classification and optimal patient care. ### Study population
  At the time of query, the Male Reproductive Genomics (MERGE) study contained data from 2411 individuals (2351 exome and 60 genome sequencing datasets). The cohort is composed mostly of men recruited in Germany (97.6%); 86.6% self-reported their country of origin, with 58.9% also originating in Germany. The majority were men diagnosed with severe spermatogenic failure according to semen analysis following standard procedures (N = 2127).22 Of these, 1448 displayed azoospermia (no sperm in the ejaculate) and 454 cryptozoospermia (sperm only detected after centrifugation), while 158 exhibited extreme and 67 severe oligozoospermia (<2 and 2–10 million [Mill.] total sperm count, respectively), categorised following the recently established Human Phenotype Ontology for male infertility (HP: 0000027, 0030974, 0034815, 0034818).23 Most men were recruited at the Centre of Reproductive Medicine and Andrology (CeRA), University Hospital of Münster, where comprehensive clinical diagnostics including anamnesis, physical examination, testicular ultrasound, semen, and hormonal (testosterone [T], luteinising hormone [LH], follicle-stimulating hormone [FSH]) analyses were performed. Additionally, the androgen sensitivity index (ASI) was calculated as the product of T and LH levels; the upper threshold used was 200 IU × nmol/L2. The remaining MERGE cohort is composed of men with normal sperm counts (≥39 Mill.; N = 175), who served as controls, and participants presenting milder reductions in sperm parameters and recruited family members (N = 109). Most participants in both the control and infertile group originated in Germany and there were statistically more individuals self-reporting as German in the control group than in the infertile group (72.8% vs 57.8% respectively; Fisher's exact test p < 0.001). However, data on reported origin was sparse in the infertile group, with 11% of having “other or unknown” origin, despite 97.6% being recruited in Germany and, therefore, it cannot be concluded that the two groups are, in fact, dissimilar. The average age of the individuals was similar in both groups (34.4 years for both the control and infertile groups; p = 0.98; Supplementary Fig. S2). If possible, family history was assessed and DNA collected from relatives for segregation analyses.


### Genetic analysis
  Sequencing was performed as described previously (see Supplementary Materials and methods).14 We queried the exome/genome data for variants in NR5A1 (NM_004959.5) and AR (NM_000044.6) and filtered for loss-of-function and missense variants with a minor allele frequency [MAF] <0.01 in the gnomAD v2.1.1 (Genome Aggregation Database (RRID:SCR_014964) and UKBiobank (RRID:SCR_012815) databases. The UKBiobank data were obtained through an open repository https://app.genebass.org (Accession IDs 26041 and 48511). The MAF values were pre-calculated during the data processing phase directly from the UKBiobank dataset. Variants with a read depth <30 were confirmed by Sanger sequencing following local standard procedures. All variants, including the final assessment, were submitted to ClinVar (RRID:SCR_006169; accession no. SUB14298103). To exclude other causes of male infertility, all men with hetero-/hemizygous variants in NR5A1 or AR were also screened for variants in genes previously associated with spermatogenic failure (Supplementary Table S1a and b).24,25


### In silico pathogenicity and protein structural dynamics analysis
  The potential impact of the variants on protein function was predicted with Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD; RRID:SCR_014989) v1.6, PolyPhen v2.2.3 (RRID:SCR_13189), Sorting Intolerant From Tolerant (SIFT v6.2.1; RRID:SCR_012813), and Mutation Taster 202126 (RRID:SCR_010777) as well as the evolutionary model of variant effect (EVE)27 and AlphaMissense.28 Additional analyses on protein stability and structural dynamics were performed with DynaMut229 (RRID: SCR_021849) using the X-ray crystal structures of human SF1 associated with PIP3 (PDB no. 4QJR) and human AR bound to a ligand (PDB no. 1E3G). In the absence of crystal structures for the N-terminal regions of SF1 and AR, changes in protein stability (ΔΔG) were calculated using predicted structures from AlphaFold2 (RRID:SCR_021502; SF1: AF-Q13285-F1 and AR: AF-P10275-F1).30 Mapping of variants were achieved using UCSF ChimeraX v1.16 (RRID:SCR_015872).


### Site-directed mutagenesis
  The pcDNA3.1 (+) expression vectors (RRID: Addgene_52535) containing the wild type NR5A1 (NM_004959; NR5A1-WT) and AR (NM_000044; pcDNA3.1 (+)AR-WT) coding sequences were commercially purchased (GenScript Biotech Corp., USA). Individual missense variants were introduced using the QuikChange II XL Site-Directed Mutagenesis kit (RRID:SCR_020426), per the manufacturer's protocol (Agilent Technologies Inc., USA; primers listed in Supplementary Table S2). The coding sequences of all NR5A1 and AR mutants were confirmed by Sanger sequencing.


### Dual-luciferase reporter assays
  Dual-luciferase reporter assays were performed as previously described.24 The target promoters where chosen based on previous studies.4,6,31 Briefly, HEK293T cells (DSMZ—German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures GmbH, Germany, RRID:CVCL_0063) were cultured in a T75 flask in Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (Sigma–Aldrich, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution (Cat. #15140122, Gibco, USA) at 37 °C, 95% humidity, and 5% CO2 atmosphere. Cells were tested regularly for mycoplasma contamination using PCR. Cells were seeded in 24-well tissue culture plates for 3 h prior to transfection via X-tremeGENE™ 9 DNA Transfection Reagent (Cat. # 06365787001, Merck KGaA, Germany).

  To functionally assess the effect of individual variants on NR5A1, pcDNA3.1 (+) NR5A1-WT, NR5A1-mutant, and empty vector as negative control (NC) (250 ng/well) were co-transfected into HEK293T cells with the firefly luciferase reporter vector pGL4.10 [luc2] (RRID: Addgene_E6651) containing the CYP11A1 promoter (250 ng/well), a known target of SF1 and 40 ng/well pGL4.74 TK Rluc (containing a constitutively active TK promoter controlling Renilla luciferase expression).

  To functionally assess the effect of individual variants in AR, pcDNA3.1(+)AR-WT, AR-mutant, and NC expression vectors (250 ng/well) were co-transfected into HEK293T cells with the reporter vector pGL3 with the insertion of an androgen responsive element (ARE) in the luc+ promoter (250 ng/well) and 40 ng/well pGL4.74 TK Rluc. At 3 h post-transfection, the medium was changed to supplemented DMEM as described above. For the AR assay, cells were treated with 10 nM dihydrotestosterone (DHT) (Cat. #R2625, Sigma–Aldrich) diluted in DMSO 24 h post-transfection and harvested 24 h post-treatment. Firefly and Renilla luciferase expression were assessed using a Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega Corporation, USA) in white Nunclon 96-well-plate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using a Tecan Infinite Lumi microplate reader (Tecan Group AG, Switzerland).


### Clinical classifications of NR5A1 and AR variants as per ACMG-AMP guidelines
  The phenotypic assessment, in silico, and in vitro data were combined to classify all variants according to the ACMG-AMP guidelines,16 adapted by recent recommendations of the ClinGen Sequence Variant Interpretation Working Group.32 The details on scoring criteria as strong, supporting, or moderate are outlined as follows:

  The PS3 criterion was applied depending on the strength of functional data available to support the damaging defect of the variant. PS3 was downgraded to moderate (two independent assays or one assay with very strong experimental data published elsewhere) or supporting (only one assay demonstrates a functional effect).33 PS4 was applied when a rare variant was found in at least one additional unrelated case from the literature, disease specific database or an in-house database showing a similar phenotype. PS4 was downgraded to moderate if the same variant was observed in 2 independent cases and to supporting if it was previously identified in only one unrelated affected individual.32

  The PM1 criterion was considered if the variant occurs in a functional protein domain based on Metadome34 and additionally the affected amino acid residue is within a region, intolerant for variations (protein tolerance landscape Metadome). It was downgraded to supporting if the amino acid residue is within an intolerant protein region, but for this region no specific protein function has been described yet. PM2 was applied to variants with a MAF <0.001 (gnomAD database v2.1.1) for X-linked or dominant genes. PM5 was upgraded to strong if more than one different amino acid substitution in addition to the observed amino acid substitution were described in the literature or databases. Accordingly, it was downgraded to supporting if only one additional substitution for the respective amino acid residue has been observed.

  PP3 was applied for missense variants with a CADD score ≥20. PP4 was applied if the phenotype of the patient (testicular histology and/or hormone parameters) were highly specific for the gene. Based on the strength of clinical evidence PP4 was upgraded to moderate (i.e., in case of genital malformations allowing the diagnosis of PAIS) or strong (i.e., in case of multiple symptoms allowing the clinical diagnosis of PAIS or CAIS).

  BS2 was applied if the variant was additionally found in a healthy individual from an in-house control cohort. BP4 was applied for variants with a CADD score ≤10. BP5 was applied if in the respective proband a further (likely) pathogenic variant was observed. BS4 was applied if segregation analysis of (un)affected family members were not in line with the inheritance mode.

  Variants of uncertain significance (VUS) were further sub-classified into a temperature scale.35 Briefly, the sum of assigned criteria (supporting: 1, moderate: 2, strong: 4) determined whether the VUS was considered as cold (1 point), cool (2 points), tepid (3 points), warm (4 points), or hot (5 points). The posterior probability for likelihood of pathogenicity (Post-P value) was calculated using a Bayesian approach with the classifications including pathogenic (≥0.99), likely pathogenic (0.9–0.99), likely benign (≤0.1).36


### Statistical analysis
  Data from luciferase assays were standardised for Renilla activity, as previously described.24 Results are shown as median and quartiles (Q1: 25% and Q3: 75%) of five independent experiments, each performed in triplicate. Due to the sample size and distribution (evaluated by visual inspection of the skew), data were analysed using the nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by post-hoc pairwise comparisons using the two-stage linear step-up procedure of Benjamini, Krieger, and Yekutieli. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v9.3 (GraphPad Software Inc., USA; RRID:SCR_002798). Statistical significance was defined as a q-value <0.05, which measures significance in terms of the false discovery rate.37

  Six out of 10 infertile men with NR5A1 missense variants had reduced combined testicular volumes (<24 mL; Table 1), and 63.4% (26/41) of infertile men with NR5A1 or AR variants had elevated mean serum FSH levels (>7 U/L). The NR5A1 variants predominantly affected highly conserved amino acids in the encoded proteins (Supplementary Figs. S4 and S5), and none of these variants were present in the MERGE control group (Table 2). Among the seven NR5A1 variants detected, three were previously reported,14 while three of the remaining four had a MAF reported in either the gnomAD and/or UKBiobank datasets (Table 1). The variant p.(Ser303Asn), in individual M1959, was not present in gnomAD, UKBiobank, or any previous study. Individual M1959 exhibited strongly reduced bi-testicular volume, reduced testosterone, and elevated FSH (Table 1). Histological analysis showed tubular shadows in 36% and 34% of tubules in the left and 34% in the right testis, with spermatocytes as the most advanced germ cell type in only 3% and 5% of tubules, respectively, indicating severe spermatogenic failure (Table 1). The observed phenotype corresponds to a mild or partial form of gonadal dysgenesis, characterised by reduced testicular volume, hypergonadotropic hypogonadism (elevated FSH, LH, and low testosterone), and impaired spermatogenesis. The p.(Ser303Asn) variant is located within a region of SF1 that is highly intolerant to variation (Supplementary Fig. S3a) and affects a conserved amino acid (Supplementary Fig. S4). Additionally, this individual carries a balanced chromosomal translocation 46, XY, t (11; 22) (q23; q11.21). ### In silico characterisation of pathogenicity of the NR5A1 and AR missense variants
  We found that one out of seven NR5A1 heterozygous missense variants, c.908G>A p.(Ser303Asn), had a high CADD score (>20) and was predicted deleterious by all the in silico tools used (Table 2). Similarly, for AR, one of the 22 hemizygous missense variants, c.2567G>A p.(Arg856His), had a high CADD score (>20) and was predicted as deleterious by all the in silico tools (Table 2). This variant p. (Arg856His) was located in ligand binding domain (LBD) of the AR, which is highly intolerant to alterations (Supplementary Fig. S3b) and, fittingly, most damaging variants were localised within this domain. Additionally, we also assessed pathogenicity using EVE and AlphaMissense (Table 2). EVE predicted two SF1, p.(Ser303Asn) and p.(Ala351Val), and one AR variant, p.(Arg856His), as pathogenic. AlphaMissense predicted two NR5A1 and seven AR variants to be likely pathogenic. To further investigate the potential impact of the identified NR5A1 and AR variants on protein stability and dynamics, we utilised DynaMut2. For SF1, we found that all variants except p.(Gln460Pro) have a destabilising effect with negative value of ΔΔG (Table 2). For AR, 13 out of 22 variants had predicted destabilising effects with variable ΔΔG (Table 2).

### Clinical classification of SF1 and AR missense variants
  Following in silico and in vitro analyses, we classified the variants per the scaled points-based system of the ACMG-AMP guidelines.35 As outlined in Table 2, we could reclassify eleven variants, four of which were upgraded from likely pathogenic to pathogenic and three from VUS to (likely) pathogenic. Overall, 17 out of 29 (58.6%) NR5A1/AR variants were classified as (likely) pathogenic, with the luciferase assay playing a key role in the final classification of nine of these variants (Table 2 and Supplementary Table S3a–c).


## Discussion
  The link between NR5A1 and AR variants and spermatogenic failure leading to male infertility is partially established, but the connection remains uncertain due to the lack of formal, functional validation. Previous studies mainly focused on severe DSD phenotypes or variant identification and in silico prediction, often neglecting functional relevance and correlation with clinical characteristics.3,4,6,17 Similarly, our earlier work classified NR5A1 and AR variants without conducting functional evaluations, leaving a substantial number of missense variants as VUS, which hampers molecular diagnosis and evidence-based individual care.42 In this study, we strengthen the diagnostic setup by using a set of comprehensive and stringent analyses. By integrating functional validation, we were able to reclassify NR5A1 and AR variants as (likely) pathogenic, elucidating the causality and clinical significance of seven variants associated with severe oligo-/azoospermia. A summary of variant (re)classification is displayed in Supplementary Fig. S6. Furthermore, it is clear that affected men, especially those with mild AIS, may remain unidentified in routine evaluations of infertile couples. Identifying these individuals is vital, as it allows for potential causal treatment with LH/hCG and/or testosterone and improvement of their overall health, as these men show for instance an increased risk for osteoporosis.43,44

  In this study, 71% (5 out of 7) of the missense variants were located within SF1's LBD (Supplementary Fig. S3a). In the literature, SF1 variants affecting the LBD and the hinge region have been mostly associated with phenotypes such as mild testicular dysgenesis and non-syndromic infertility, while variants in the NTD were more frequently linked to severe phenotypes such as complete XY sex reversal.6,45,46 However, several studies have also indicated that phenotypes based solely on genotypes are difficult to predict for NR5A1/SF1 variants, which might result in a wide spectrum of clinical presentations.47 Fitting with the literature, the location of the variants we identified did not directly correlate with either the severity of the phenotype or the transcriptional activity in in vitro assays. Namely, four out of seven (57%) NR5A1 variants impaired SF1's transcriptional activity, including p.(Asp238Asn), as shown previously4,6 and three out of five variants located at the LBD led to impaired SF1 transcriptional activity (Fig. 2a and c). Although we cannot fully exclude a synergistic effect of the chromosomal translocation on the phenotype of individual M1959, this is rather unlikely due to the paternal transmission of reciprocal translocations in general and also in the case of this specific, rather frequent translocation t (11; 22) (q23; q11.21).48,49

  We identified 22 rare hemizygous missense variants for AR, 55% (12 LBD variants out of 22) of them located in the coding region of the N-terminal domain (NTD) (Supplementary Fig. S3b). The NTD is the least conserved among the AR domains and variants located therein usually show unchanged transcriptional activity in vitro (Fig. 2b). However, two of the NTD-specific missense variants identified in this study, c.1244A>G p.(His415Arg) and c.1301C>T p.(Ser434Phe), exhibited a significantly reduced ability to stimulate an ARE-containing promoter. Both variants affect highly conserved amino acids within transcription activation unit 5 (TAU-5), located on AR's activation function 1 (AF-1) (Fig. 2d). The TAU-5 refers to a specific region that is sufficient for constitutive transcriptional activity.50 The p.(Ser434Phe) variant shows a high destabilising effect (−1.01 kcal/mol) on the protein's structural dynamics (Table 2), suggesting that it could lead to the disruption of the AF-1, which predominantly regulates AR's transcriptional activity.45 In AR's DBD, p.(Leu575Val) led to a reduced transcriptional activity (Fig. 2b). This substitution is situated within the second zinc finger motif, which is part of the DBD, potentially disrupting functions such as DNA binding, nuclear export, or the recruitment of co-regulators, impacting AR transactivation.46

  We identified seven missense variants affecting AR's C-terminal LBD, including three previously unidentified variants (Table 1), all leading to a significantly reduced ability to stimulate an ARE-containing promoter (Fig. 2b). The pathogenicity of p.(Cys687Arg) is illustrated by subject M2217's PAIS phenotype (Fig. 1a). p.(Cys687Arg) is localised close to the ligand-binding pocket surface (Fig. 2d) and the ‘signature sequence’ at the highly conserved loop of the nuclear receptors, involved in coactivator recruitment. Individuals M1760 (p.(Asp829Val) (Fig. 2b)) and M3495 (p.(Arg856His) (Fig. 2c)) show signs of androgen insensitivity, suggesting that the observed loss of transcriptional activity could be due to the LBD's reduced ligand specificity, potentially influencing the ligand-binding pocket dimensions. In contrast, apart from infertility, there were no clinical signs of partial/mild androgen insensitivity in three men presenting AR variants that led to a reduced transcriptional activity, namely p.(His415Arg), p.(Leu575Val), and p.(Arg787Gln) (Fig. 2b).

  The interpretation of variants in NR5A1 and AR identified in infertile men is exceptionally challenging, not only because these are typically missense variants, but also because segregation analyses are notoriously difficult to perform in this still taboo disease. Our comprehensive clinical, in silico, and in vitro workup highlights the effectiveness of such an integrated approach. In addition, in our multi-layered approach, we also evaluate the performance of advanced machine learning-based in silico prediction models against established metrics. Among 20 variants exceeding the widely used CADD score threshold (>20), 16 (80%) were ultimately classified as (likely) pathogenic by ACMG-AMP (Table 2). In contrast, EVE accurately predicted only three, while AlphaMissense identified nine as (likely) pathogenic (Table 2). Although AlphaMissense outperformed EVE, it still missed eight (likely) pathogenic variants. These limitations are not unique to AlphaMissense but reflect broader challenges in in silico predictions. While models like AlphaMissense and EVE rely primarily on evolutionary conservation, their inability to account for complex biological contexts — such as protein–protein interactions, post-translational modifications, or cellular dynamics — can result in false negatives.51 For instance, discrepancies between in silico predictions and in vitro functional results for SF1 variants such as p.(Asp238Asn) and p.(Ala360Val) highlights these constraints. In contrast, CADD demonstrated superior performance, accurately classifying a greater number of pathogenic variants in SF1 and AR. Its strength lies in integrating diverse genomic features, including conservation metrics, gene expression data, and protein domain annotations, enabling a more comprehensive assessment of variant pathogenicity.52 This broader scope enhances its sensitivity to rare but functionally significant variants, particularly in transcription factors like SF1 and AR, which harbour many pathogenic variants in conserved functional domains, such as the AR's LBD.

  This suggests that, while AlphaMissense displays some effectiveness in predicting pathogenicity, it has limitations in accurately identifying all pathogenic variants within the analysed dataset. Although AlphaMissense, a deep learning model, and EVE, which estimates pathogenicity from evolutionary constraints, provide valuable insights, both showed limited sensitivity in this context.53,54 Therefore, while CADD appears more suitable for SF1 and AR variant classification, it is crucial to emphasise that no single in silico tool should be used in isolation.55 These tools, often trained on known variants, may miss subtle functional effects, leading to false negatives in pathogenicity predictions, which might be detected by in vitro functional assays. Furthermore, some variants impact protein function in ways not captured by sequence-based models. They may disrupt protein–protein interactions or affect non-conserved yet functionally important regions. Cellular context, co-factors, and other molecular influences are absent from computational predictions, highlighting the need to complement in silico analysis with experimental validation and/or develop big data models taking complex cellular and tissue-specific interactions into account.56,57

  Although in vitro luciferase-based promoter-reporter assays provide valuable insights into the functional consequences of NR5A1 and AR variants, they also have inherent limitations that must be acknowledged. The variability in transcriptional activity across different cell types, the artificial nature of overexpression systems, and the lack of broader physiological context can lead to discrepancies between in vitro results and clinical phenotypes. For instance, previous studies on NR5A1 variants in DSD cohorts have shown that nearly half of the tested variants exhibited functional outcomes inconsistent with patient presentations, raising concerns about false-negative and -positive results.58 Given these inconsistencies, functional assays should be interpreted cautiously in isolation and should always be complemented by other approaches, such as in silico modelling and protein interaction studies, to enhance variant classification. Given these challenges, integrating multiple assessment strategies, which might include protein-DNA interaction studies, chromatin immunoprecipitation, and patient-derived cellular models, is crucial for a comprehensive pathogenicity evaluation.58, 59, 60 Therefore, while in vitro assays remain an important tool, they should not be used by themselves for variant reclassification, and future studies incorporating patient-derived gonadal cells or transgenic animal models may further strengthen the clinical relevance of these findings.

  While the selection of patients in this study allowed for a focused investigation into the genetic basis of severe male infertility, it may not fully represent all men presenting milder forms of infertility or those from more diverse populations. The cohorts consisted primarily of individuals of European descent, which could limit the generalisability of our findings to other ethnic or geographic groups. Ethnicity information was in fact not available for this cohort and self-reported country of origin was used as a proxy for a portion of the cohort participants where this information had been recorded. Other information, such as educational level or lifestyle factors were not available. Moreover, selection bias is possible, as individuals seeking clinical care at a specialised andrology/fertility centre may differ from those with undiagnosed or untreated infertility. To mitigate this, we included a control group of men with normal sperm counts from the MERGE cohort (n = 175) to strengthen our comparative analyses.

  Our study provides critical insights into the functional and clinical significance of NR5A1 and AR variants and into their pathogenicity in the context of male infertility, however further validation in independent and ethnically diverse cohorts is essential to confirm the universality of our findings. Male infertility is a genetically heterogeneous condition, and variant effects may be influenced by population-specific genetic backgrounds.15,23 Additionally, integrating multi-omics approaches, such as transcriptomics and proteomics, could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying these variants.38 Future studies should also evaluate the impact of these variants in longitudinal clinical settings to assess their role in disease progression and treatment response. Lastly, if feasible, segregation analysis of NR5A1 and AR variants represents a valuable tool especially to rule out pathogenicity although variable expressivity and reduced penetrance cannot be completely excluded. In conclusion, our study highlights the need for a thorough phenotypic description of infertile men. The classification of missense variants in NR5A1 and AR remains difficult in cases of isolated male infertility and caution should be warranted when interpreting in silico predictions. Our multi-layered approach proves advantageous to scrutinise NR5A1 and AR missense variants associated with male infertility and beyond. In addition, close contact between the research/genetic diagnostic settings and clinicians, with precise anamnesis and physical examination, remains crucial for variant classification and personalised care of men with NR5A1 and AR variants in general and should be complemented by experimental evaluation of variants.
