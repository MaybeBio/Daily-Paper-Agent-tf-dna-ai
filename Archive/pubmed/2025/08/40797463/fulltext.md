# Systematic analysis of doxorubicin-induced myocardial injury mechanisms using network toxicology and molecular docking strategy


## Abstract
  To systematically investigate the molecular mechanisms of doxorubicin (DOX)-induced myocardial injury through network toxicology, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulations, aiming to identify critical molecular targets for reducing DOX’s cardiotoxicity. Multiple databases were systematically mined to identify DOX-related targets. A protein-protein interaction network was constructed using STRING database and analyzed via Cytoscape. Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed using WebGestalt. Molecular docking simulations evaluated binding interactions between DOX and identified hub proteins, followed by 100 ns molecular dynamics simulations to assess complex stability. Results: Network analysis identified 5 critical hub genes (AKT1, TP53, EGFR, HIF1A, and BCL2) among 47 overlapping targets between DOX activity and myocardial injury pathways. Functional enrichment demonstrated significant involvement in cellular responses to oxidative stress, reactive oxygen species (ROS) metabolism, and membrane-associated processes. Molecular docking revealed strong binding interactions with energies from −5.2 to −7.8 kcal/mol. Molecular dynamics simulations confirmed varying complex stability, with EGFR showing superior stability (root mean square deviation [RMSD] = 0.1–0.4 nm), AKT1 and BCL2 displaying moderate fluctuations (~0.6 nm), and HIF1A and TP53 exhibiting greater conformational variability (0.6–0.7 nm). This integrated computational analysis provides insights into DOX-induced myocardial injury mechanisms. The identification of key targets and their differential binding stability with DOX establishes a foundation for developing targeted strategies to minimize cardiotoxicity while preserving therapeutic efficacy.


## 1. Introduction
  Doxorubicin (DOX) is widely recognized as a potent antineoplastic agent with a broad spectrum of activity against various cancers, including acute lymphocytic and granulocytic leukemia, breast cancer, bronchogenic carcinoma, and malignant lymphomas.[1] Its primary mechanism of action involves DNA intercalation, which obstructs DNA replication and transcription, thereby exerting its anticancer effects.[2] Despite its efficacy, the clinical use of DOX is significantly hampered by its cardiotoxic effects, collectively known as DOX-induced cardiotoxicity (DIC). DIC is a major concern for both active cancer patients and survivors, as it can lead to severe myocardial injury and potentially progress to congestive heart failure.[3,4] The risk of developing heart failure increases with higher cumulative doses of DOX, particularly exceeding 400 mg/m2.[3] Observational studies highlight that the risk of cardiotoxicity escalates sharply, reaching up to 26% when the cumulative dose surpasses 550 mg/m2.[3] Furthermore, the manifestation of anthracycline-related myocardial damage is most pronounced within the 1st year of treatment, showing a strong correlation with the dosage and the left ventricular ejection fraction measured at the end of therapy.[5] Despite some advancements in drug delivery, such as liposomal formulations aimed at reducing cardiotoxicity, these methods have not fully addressed the fundamental issues underlying myocardial injury.[6] The pathogenesis of DOX-induced cardiac damage involves a complex interplay of apoptosis, metabolic disturbances, inflammatory responses, and oxidative stress, which are still not completely understood.[7–11] This highlights the urgent need for a systematic exploration of these mechanisms to devise effective cardioprotective strategies that preserve DOX’s anticancer efficacy.

  Emerging as a novel extension of network pharmacology, network toxicology offers a promising paradigm for elucidating complex biological interactions and identifying novel targets.[12] This approach integrates extensive databases to construct intricate networks that reveal interactions between toxic targets, disease-relevant targets, and biological processes (BPs).[13] Such a methodology facilitates the discovery of previously unknown toxic-target-disease interactions and novel mechanisms of toxicity. However, the application of network toxicology, combined with molecular docking techniques to specifically explore the mechanisms underlying DOX-induced myocardial injury, remains largely unexplored.

  In this comprehensive study, we aim to systematically identify and analyze candidate targets involved in DOX-induced myocardial injury using a multi-pronged approach. By constructing a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network, we pinpointed key hub genes and utilized gene ontology (GO) analysis to delineate essential BPs, cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF) involved. Further, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis helped uncover pivotal signaling pathways implicated in DOX-induced cardiac dysfunction. We also conducted molecular docking studies to assess the binding affinity between DOX and identified molecular targets. This research not only establishes a strategic and efficient methodology for evaluating chemotherapy-induced organ toxicity but also provides crucial insights that could aid in developing strategies to mitigate DOX-associated cardiotoxicity, thereby enhancing therapeutic outcomes for cancer patients.


## 2. Methods
  Neither informed consent nor approval of the ethics committee is required for this study type, because no human or animal subjects or materials were use.


### 2.1. Prediction of potential targets of toxicity of DOX to myocardial injury
  The molecular structure and simplified molecular input line entry system (SMILES) notation of DOX were obtained from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). To identify potential molecular targets of DOX, we conducted comprehensive target prediction analyses using multiple databases. First, putative DOX targets were predicted using the ChEMBL database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/chembl/) and SwissTargetPrediction (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch), with species filtered to “Homo sapiens.” Additional DOX targets were retrieved from DrugBank (https://www.drugbank.com/) and the drug gene interaction database (DGIdb, https://dgidb.org). After removing redundant entries, target protein names were standardized according to UniProt nomenclature (https://www.uniprot.org/).

  To identify therapeutic targets specifically associated with myocardial injury, we queried 3 complementary databases: GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org), Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM, http://www.omim.org/), and the therapeutic target database (TTD, https://db.idrblab.net/ttd/). Through intersection analysis of DOX targets and myocardial injury-associated genes, we identified potential mechanistic targets involved in DOX-induced cardiac toxicity.


### 2.2. Construction of PPI network and identification of hub genes
  To elucidate the molecular interaction landscape, we constructed a PPI network using the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/). The network was generated using the identified candidate targets, with a minimum required interaction confidence score threshold of 0.4 to ensure reliability while maintaining comprehensiveness. The protein interaction data were exported as tab-separated values format for subsequent analysis.

  The exported STRING interaction data were then imported into Cytoscape software (version 3.7.1) to visualize and analyze the network architecture of DOX-induced cardiac toxicity. Hub genes, representing the most functionally significant nodes within the network, were identified using the CytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape. The prioritization of hub genes was performed using 2 complementary approaches: the maximal clique centrality algorithm, which evaluates the topological importance of nodes, and degree centrality, which quantifies the number of direct interactions for each node in the network.


### 2.3. Functional annotation and pathway enrichment analysis of potential targets
  To systematically characterize the biological functions and molecular pathways associated with DOX-induced cardiac toxicity, we performed comprehensive enrichment analyses using WebGestalt (WEB-based Gene SeT AnaLysis Toolkit, http://www.webgestalt.org/option.php). GO enrichment analysis was conducted across 3 domains: BP, cellular component (CC), and (MF). Additionally, we performed KEGG pathway enrichment analysis to elucidate the signaling cascades and metabolic networks involved.

  The over-representation analysis (ORA) method was employed to identify significantly enriched terms and pathways. Statistical significance was determined using a false discovery rate-adjusted P-value threshold of .05. This rigorous statistical approach helped identify the most relevant BPs and pathways potentially mediating DOX-induced myocardial injury.


### 2.4. Molecular docking analysis
  To investigate potential molecular interactions, we performed in silico docking studies using the CB-Dock2 platform (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/php/index.php), an advanced blind docking tool that integrates cavity detection and AutoDock Vina algorithms.[14] The 3-dimensional structure of DOX was retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) and preprocessed by adding hydrogen atoms followed by energy minimization. Target protein structures were obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB) (https://www.rcsb.org/) and prepared by removing crystallographic water molecules and adding polar hydrogen atoms.

  The CB-Dock2 platform employed both template-independent and template-based blind docking approaches to determine optimal binding sites. For each target protein, the platform first searched for template ligands with high topology similarity (FP2 ≥ 0.4) in its protein-ligand complexes database. For targets with available homologous templates (sequence identity > 40% and pocket RMSD ≤ 4Å), template-based cavity detection was utilized for grid box determination. For targets without suitable templates, the platform automatically identified potential binding cavities through its curvature-based algorithm and generated cavity-specific grid boxes centered on these detected sites. The docking simulations were performed using AutoDock Vina (version 1.2.0) with an exhaustiveness parameter set to 8 to ensure adequate conformational sampling. For each target protein, the platform generated 9 distinct DOX conformations, which were ranked based on their predicted binding free energies (ΔG, kcal/mol), with lower values indicating more favorable interactions. The optimal binding modes were selected based on the lowest binding free energy conformations. “Total Score” was used to verify the combining affinity between them. The comprehensive workflow of our study is illustrated in Figure 1.


### 2.5. Molecular dynamics simulation
  To validate the docking results and evaluate the stability of DOX-protein interactions in a dynamic environment, we performed 100 ns molecular dynamics simulations for each protein-ligand complex using GROMACS 2022.3. The Amber99sb-ildn force field was applied for proteins, while DOX parameters were generated using general amber force field via AmberTools22. Restrained electrostatic potential (RESP) charges for DOX were calculated using Gaussian 16W. Systems were solvated in a cubic box with 3-Point Transferable Intermolecular Potential (TIP3P) water molecules extending 1.0 nm from the solute surface. System neutrality was achieved by adding appropriate Na+ and Cl− ions. Energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent method, followed by a conjugate gradient method to relieve unfavorable contacts. The systems were then equilibrated in 2 phases: 100 ps constant number of particles, volume, and temperature (NVT) equilibration at 300K using the V-rescale thermostat, followed by 100 ps constant number of particles, pressure, and temperature (NPT) equilibration at 1 bar pressure using the Parrinello-Rahman barostat. Production MD simulations were conducted for 100 ns with a 2 fs time step. The linear constraint solver (LINCS) algorithm was used to constrain all bonds, while the particle mesh Ewald method was employed for long-range electrostatic interactions. Temperature and pressure were maintained at 300K and 1 bar, respectively. Trajectories were saved every 10 ps for analysis.


## 3. Results

### 3.1. Identification of molecular targets in DOX-induced myocardial injury
  Parallel analysis of DOX-specific molecular interactions across ChEMBL, SwissTargetPrediction, DrugBank, and DGIdb revealed 378 putative drug targets (Fig. 2A). Through systematic database mining, we identified 531 established targets associated with myocardial injury from multiple authoritative databases including GeneCards, OMIM, and TTD (Fig. 2B).

  Integration of these datasets through Venn analysis identified 47 targets at the intersection of DOX activity and myocardial injury pathways (Fig. 2C). To elucidate the functional relationships among these targets, we constructed a PPI network using the STRING database platform (Fig. 3). This network visualization revealed key molecular nodes and interaction patterns potentially mediating DOX-induced cardiac dysfunction.


### 3.2. Identification of key molecular mediators
  Network analysis was performed by importing potential target proteins into Cytoscape to generate a comprehensive interaction network representing DOX-induced myocardial injury pathways (Fig. 4A). Application of the maximal clique centrality algorithm enabled identification of critical hub genes based on their network connectivity and functional significance. This analysis highlighted 5 prominent molecular mediators: AKT1, TP53, EGFR, HIF1a, and BCL2 as central regulators in the molecular cascade of DOX-mediated cardiac injury (Fig. 4B).


### 3.3. Functional enrichment analysis
  GO and KEGG pathway analyses were performed to elucidate the biological implications of the identified targets. The top 10 enriched BP included cellular response to chemical stress, gland development, response to oxidative stress, cellular response to oxidative stress, response to reactive oxygen species (ROS), regulation of ROS metabolic processes, response to radiation, positive regulation of ROS metabolic processes, response to xenobiotic stimulus, and response to UV radiation (Fig. 5A and B). CC analysis revealed significant enrichment in vesicle lumen, Bcl2 family protein complex, membrane rafts, membrane microdomains, secretory granule lumen, cytoplasmic vesicle lumen, platelet alpha granule, neuronal dense core vesicle, pore complex, and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase complex (Fig. 5B). The predominant MF comprised phosphatase binding, ubiquitin protein ligase binding, ubiquitin-like protein ligase binding, 1-phosphatidylinositol-3-kinase activity, protein serine/threonine/tyrosine kinase activity, transcription coregulator binding, apolipoprotein binding, protein serine/threonine kinase activity, MAP kinase kinase activity, and protein-folding chaperone binding (Fig. 5B). In KEGG pathway analysis, the top enriched pathway included apoptosis, cellular senescence, efferocytosis, HIF-1 signaling pathway, and lipid and atherosclerosis (Fig. 5C). Additionally, KEGG pathway analysis further revealed distinct functional clusters. Disease-associated pathways encompassed multiple conditions, such as cardiovascular disorders, infectious diseases, and metabolic dysfunctions. Signal transduction mechanisms were highlighted by the enrichment of multiple cascades, including MAPK signaling, FoxO signaling, and PI3K-Akt pathways, among others. Cell death-related processes were prominently represented by pathways governing efferocytosis, apoptosis, and autophagy. Metabolic alterations were evident in various pathways, such as fatty acid degradation, linoleic acid metabolism, and oxidative phosphorylation. The analysis also identified significant involvement of organismal systems, particularly adrenergic signaling in cardiomyocytes, Toll-like receptor signaling, natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity, and complement cascade activation (Fig. 5D).


### 3.4. Molecular docking analysis of DOX with core target proteins
  The binding characteristics between DOX and the identified hub proteins (AKT1, TP53, EGFR, HIF1A, and BCL2) were examined using the CB-Dock2 platform. Molecular docking simulations were performed with DOX as the ligand and the 5 core targets as receptors. The analysis revealed that hydrogen bonding was the predominant interaction mode between DOX and the target proteins (Fig. 6). Binding affinity was evaluated using Vina scores, where lower docking energy values indicate stronger protein-ligand interactions. According to established molecular docking principles, binding free energies below −5.0 kcal/mol suggest strong binding activity, while values below −7.0 kcal/mol indicate very strong binding activity.[15] The docking results (Table 1) demonstrated that all 5 proteins exhibited binding free energies below −5.0 kcal/mol with DOX, with multiple hydrogen bond formations observed at the interaction interfaces. Among the analyzed proteins, AKT1 displayed the strongest binding affinity with DOX, while BCL2 showed the lowest binding energy. These findings suggest favorable and stable binding interactions between DOX and the binding pockets of all 5 core target proteins, supporting their potential roles as direct molecular targets in DOX-induced cardiac injury.


### 3.5. Molecular dynamics simulation results
  The 100 ns molecular dynamics simulations of DOX-protein complexes elucidated critical interaction characteristics among the 5 key hub proteins. RMSD analysis demonstrated equilibration of all complexes after approximately 20 ns, with varying degrees of conformational stability: EGFR (Fig. 7A) exhibited superior stability (0.1–0.4 nm), AKT1 and BCL2 (Figs. 8A and 9A) displayed moderate fluctuations (≈0.6 nm), while HIF1A and TP53 (Figs. 10A and 11A) manifested greater conformational variability (0.6–0.7 nm). Root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) profiles (Figs. 7B–11B) revealed protein-specific patterns of local flexibility, including significant fluctuations in HIF1A residues 120–200, pronounced mobility in TP53 near residues 120 and 190, and notable flexibility in AKT1 residues 150–200. Importantly, residues directly involved in DOX coordination consistently exhibited attenuated fluctuations, indicating ligand-induced local stabilization. Radius of gyration analyses (Figs. 7C–11C) confirmed maintenance of characteristic protein compactness throughout the simulations, with HIF1A demonstrating the most compact conformation (Rg ≈ 1.3–1.4 nm), followed by TP53 and BCL2 (Rg ≈ 1.6 nm), while EGFR and AKT1 displayed larger radii (Rg ≈ 2.0–2.1 nm). Solvent accessible surface area analyses (Figs. 7D– 11D) revealed differential solvent exposure patterns, with AKT1 exhibiting maximal surface exposure (190–200 nm2) and HIF1A minimal (65–68 nm2).


## 4. Discussion
  Through comprehensive network pharmacological analysis utilizing multiple databases, we identified 47 potential targets associated with DOX-induced myocardial injury. Analysis via STRING and Cytoscape platforms revealed 5 crucial targets: AKT1, TP53, EGFR, HIF1A, and BCL2, which emerged as central nodes in DOX-induced cardiac pathology.

  AKT1, a serine/threonine-specific protein kinase, orchestrates diverse biological functions, including glucose metabolism, apoptosis, cellular proliferation, transcription, and migration.[16] Its aberrant expression across various cardiac pathologies has established it as a promising therapeutic target.[17] In acute settings, AKT1 deficiency enhances cardiomyocyte injury and apoptosis following ischemic/reperfusion injury, while chronic AKT1 modulation can improve cardiac function and ventricular remodeling postmyocardial infarction.[18] AKT1 inhibition regulates cardiomyocyte survival through the GSK-3β/β-catenin pathway and alleviates cardiac injury by promoting autophagy via AKT1-RPS6KB1 signaling.[19] Notably, Taniyama and colleagues demonstrated that myocardial Akt activation counteracts DOX-induced cardiac dysfunction,[20] while compounds such as adiponectin and HDL exhibit cardioprotective effects through Akt-dependent mechanisms.[21,22]

  TP53, the most frequently mutated gene in human cancers,[23] governs critical cellular processes including cell cycle regulation, DNA repair, and apoptosis.[24,25] In cardiac tissue, TP53 functions as a key regulator of the cardiac transcriptome, with elevated levels correlating with cardiac hypertrophy and remodeling.[26,27] Research has established a direct relationship between TP53 levels and cellular differentiation, particularly in cellular reprogramming, where TP53 mutant fibroblasts show enhanced conversion to pluripotent cells.[28,29] Studies have shown that DOX administration increases p53 levels in cardiomyocytes, mitochondria, and nuclei,[30] while p53 inhibition significantly attenuates DOX-induced cardiac injury.[31] Various therapeutic agents, including curcumin, demonstrate cardioprotective efficacy through p53 modulation.[32,33]

  EGFR, beyond its established role in cancer biology,[34] has emerged as a significant cardioprotective mediator.[35] Recent investigations highlight EGFR’s crucial role in cardiomyocyte survival and stress adaptation,[36] particularly through PI3K/Akt pathway activation.[37] Studies demonstrate that while EGFR inhibition exacerbates myocardial dysfunction and increases susceptibility to ischemia-reperfusion injury,[38] its activation confers substantial cardioprotection by reducing apoptosis and promoting cell survival.[21,39]

  HIF1A, a master regulator of oxygen homeostasis,[40] modulates over 600 target genes in response to oxygen availability.[41] It plays crucial roles in multiple organ injuries, including liver and lung.[42,43] In cardiomyocytes, HIF1A enhances glycolytic enzyme expression, optimizing ATP generation under anaerobic conditions.[44] Based on studies implicating HIF1A stabilization in tissue adaptation and protection during hypoxia or inflammation,[45–47] significant research efforts have focused on this pathway as a therapeutic target. In DIC, elevated HIF1A levels represent a compensatory response,[48] with its upregulation suppressing oxidative stress and cellular death pathways.[49]

  The BCL2 family, central to apoptosis regulation,[50] exhibits complex interactions with mitochondrial function.[51–54] These proteins can directly influence cellular energy metabolism through cyclooxygenase regulation of the respiratory chain and modulation of voltage-dependent anion channels.[51,54] DOX treatment significantly alters the balance between pro-apoptotic and antiapoptotic proteins.[55] Various therapeutic agents, including Rhein,[56] Shenmai,[57] and curcumin,[58] demonstrate efficacy through BCL2 modulation.

  GO enrichment analysis revealed that DOX-induced cardiac injury primarily involves cellular responses to chemical and oxidative stress, along with ubiquitin-mediated processes. Current evidence confirms that DIC operates through oxidative stress mechanisms, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and cellular death.[59] DOX enhances proteasome-mediated degradation of key regulatory proteins in cardiomyocytes through increased expression of ubiquitin E3 ligases[60,61] and enhanced proteasome proteolytic activities.[62,63] KEGG pathway analysis highlighted the involvement of MAPK, FoxO, and PI3K-Akt signaling cascades in metabolic regulation, apoptosis, and oxidative stress responses.[64,65] These pathways form an interconnected network mediating DOX’s cardiotoxic effects.

  Our findings highlight the overlapping roles of AKT1, HIF1A, TP53, EGFR, and BCL2 (particularly AKT1, HIF1A, and BCL2) in balancing tissue damage and repair during DOX-induced myocardial injury. AKT1 stabilizes HIF1A via the PI3K/AKT pathway, promoting cellular adaptation under oxidative stress and hypoxia.[66,67] HIF1A regulates BCL2 expression, balancing pro- and antiapoptotic signals to maintain mitochondrial integrity.[68] Meanwhile, AKT1 enhances BCL2-mediated cell survival by inhibiting pro-apoptotic proteins like BAX, preventing apoptosis and facilitating autophagy.[69] These pathways collectively influence 2 outcomes: apoptosis leading to cardiomyocyte death, and cellular adaptation promoting tissue repair and survival. The potential interactions among these key targets are illustrated in the schematic figure (Fig. S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/P633), highlighting their dual roles in DIC and underscoring their promise as therapeutic targets.

  Network pharmacology approaches have been increasingly applied to study DIC and potential protective strategies.[70–72] Recently, Liu et al[72] employed network pharmacology combined with RNA-seq analysis to investigate Andrographolide’s protective effects against DOX-induced cardiac damage. In comparing our approach with such studies, our work differs from Chen et al in several key aspects. While both studies employed network pharmacology to investigate DIC, Chen et al focused specifically on Andrographolide’s protective mechanism, identifying Tap1, Timp1 and NLRP3 inflammasome inhibition as key factors. In contrast, our approach broadly characterized the intrinsic molecular mechanisms of DOX cardiotoxicity, identifying 5 central hub proteins (AKT1, TP53, EGFR, HIF1A, and BCL2) as critical nodes and exploring their dual roles in both injury and repair processes. Our molecular docking analyses further provided structural insights into DOX-target interactions. These different but complementary approaches enhance our understanding of DIC – while Chen et al identified specific protective mechanisms of a single compound, our study provides a comprehensive framework of the underlying pathological processes that could guide various therapeutic strategies.

  The molecular mechanisms elucidated in this study provide valuable insights for managing DIC. The identified hub proteins represent potential targets for cardioprotective interventions. AKT1 pathway activators may offer protection when administered with DOX, while careful EGFR modulation could mitigate cardiac damage. Our findings also inform biomarker development for risk stratification. Molecular signatures involving these targets could enable personalized monitoring strategies. The dual role of HIF1A in both damage and repair pathways highlights the importance of context-dependent interventions, while the BCL2-centered mechanisms provide rationale for exploring antiapoptotic strategies during critical vulnerability windows. Combining these targeted approaches with conventional cardioprotective measures could establish more effective protocols for preventing DOX-induced cardiac dysfunction while maintaining optimal cancer treatment efficacy.

  While our network toxicology and molecular docking analyses provide valuable mechanistic insights into DIC, experimental validation through in vivo and in vitro studies remains necessary. Future research should focus on confirming these findings through cellular and animal models to develop more effective therapeutic strategies against DOX-induced cardiac injury.


## 5. Conclusion
  This study provides comprehensive insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying DOX-induced myocardial injury through integrated network toxicology and molecular docking analyses. We identified 5 critical hub genes (AKT1, TP53, EGFR, HIF1A, and BCL2) along with 42 additional targets that potentially mediate DOX-induced myocardial injury. Through systematic analysis, we characterized key BPs, CCs, and MF associated with DOX-induced myocardial injury. Pathway enrichment analysis highlighted crucial signaling cascades, including apoptosis, cellular senescence, efferocytosis, HIF-1 signaling, and lipid metabolism pathways. Molecular docking simulations demonstrated stable binding interactions between DOX and the identified hub proteins, supporting their roles as direct molecular targets.

  This systematic investigation advances our understanding of DOX-induced myocardial injury mechanisms and provides a foundation for therapeutic intervention strategies. Our approach demonstrates the utility of integrating network toxicology with molecular docking for investigating chemotherapeutic agent toxicity. Future research should focus on experimental validation through large-scale prospective studies, along with preclinical studies using animal and cell-based models to validate the identified targets and pathways.
