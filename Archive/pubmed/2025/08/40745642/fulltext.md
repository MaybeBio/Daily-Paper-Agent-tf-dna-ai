# A lipid metabolism-related gene signature predicts prognosis after tamoxifen treatment in ER + breast cancer and reflects tumor microenvironment heterogeneity through single-cell analysis


## Background
  Although the clinical outcome of ER + breast cancer patients receiving tamoxifen after surgery is favorable, a proportion of patients experience recurrence or death due to disease progression.


## Methods
  In this study, by integrating lipid metabolism gene expression and machine learning data, a prognostic model based on gene expression was developed using the TCGA-ER + BRCA dataset (N = 183) and validated with the GSE17705 (N = 298), GSE22219 (N = 134), GSE42568 (N = 70), and GSE58644 (N = 147) datasets. Patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups based on the median risk score of the signature. Comparative analyses of survival, genomic features, immune infiltration, and drug sensitivity were performed between these groups.


## Results
  Patients in the high-risk group had worse survival outcomes than those in the low-risk group. The five-year overall survival AUC of the model was 0.858, indicating good performance. High-risk patients were characterized by USH2A and KMT2C mutations, genomic amplification, and enriched JAK-STAT pathway and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathways. Resting CD4 + memory T cells, activated mast cells, and myeloid dendritic cells were significantly enriched in the low-risk group, while M0 macrophages were enriched in the high-risk group. Single-cell sequencing analyses also revealed that the model was significantly associated with macrophages and the percentage of proliferating myeloid cells. The signature was also associated with sensitivity to multiple drugs. Cell–cell interaction difference analyses suggested that cancer-related signaling pathways, especially the SIRPα/CD47/IL6 pathway, were decreased in high-risk patients, but these samples exhibited increased SPP1 interactions.


## Conclusion
  The signature captures lipid metabolic reprogramming and immunosuppression, providing a biomarker for prognosis and precision therapy in tamoxifen-treated ER + breast cancer.

## Introduction
  Breast cancer is the most common cancer diagnosis and a leading cause of cancer death among women worldwide, with 287,850 new cases and 61,360 deaths reported in the United States in 2022, accounting for 31% of cancers and 21% of cancer-related deaths in women[1]. Among breast cancer patients, ER + is the most prevalent subtype and is associated with a favorable five-year survival rate. Endocrine therapy, particularly tamoxifen, has been a cornerstone treatment for decades, significantly reducing local recurrence and distant metastasis in breast cancer patients[2].A recent Phase III trial (TAM-01) reported that after long-term follow-up (median follow-up: 9.7 years), recurrence occurred in 5.0% of patients receiving tamoxifen 5 mg/day compared to 8.2% in the placebo group, a statistically significant difference[3]. However, a proportion of patients experience relapse due to endocrine resistance[4].

  Significant efforts have been devoted to predicting tamoxifen resistance and identifying novel prognostic biomarkers in recent years. Using bioinformatics tools, Qiaoyu et al. reported that elevated PITX1 expression correlated with worse survival in patients with breast cancer[5]. Similarly, TRAF4 gene expression was associated with survival outcomes in patients with tamoxifen-treated breast cancer; knocking down TRAF4 expression significantly reduced the proliferation of tamoxifen-resistant cell lines[6]. Another study revealed increased HIF1A expression in tamoxifen-treated cells, correlating with a negative prognosis in contralateral breast cancer[7]. Additionally, high GCN5 expression was associated with tamoxifen resistance via its effect on AIB1 expression and correlated with shorter survival in ER + breast cancer patients. A randomized clinical trial revealed that FOXA1 was a valuable biomarker for ER +/HER2- breast cancer, with a median follow-up of 33 years[8]. However, most of these studies were not clinically recommended[9]. The reasons are complex. First, as single biomarkers, ER/PR/HER2 + status provides a robust and powerful tool for predicting survival and guiding clinical practice, but the use of other biomarkers lacks further validation[10]. Second, due to complex genetic evolution, the component of cancer is complex. Researchers even suspect that the multiple subtypes of breast cancer may exist within the same tumor[11]. Thus, multiple genes or multiple ‘omic’-based signatures or models were highlighted. For example, in breast cancer patients, after comprehensive analyses of a large cohort study, the ‘Mammaprint’ was proven to be a robust signature for clinical recommendation[12–14]. Additionally, in breast cancer, a clinical trial (WSG-ADAPT-HR +/HER2-) with 2,290 registered patients was shown to be effective at guiding systemic treatment via the RS/ET response[15]. These studies highlighted the clinical utility of multigene-based models.

  Lipid metabolism, including fatty acid metabolism, plays a critical role in breast cancer biology. Fatty acid β-oxidation has been reported to regulate self-renewal, treatment resistance, and metastasis in breast cancer[16, 17]. Aberrant fatty acid metabolism is a frequently reported pathway associated with endocrine therapy, particularly tamoxifen, in ER + breast cancer. Both fatty acid synthase (FASN) and targeting c-Jun induce alterations in fatty acid oxidation, thereby modulating the sensitivity of ER + breast cancer cells to tamoxifen; the former confers resistance[[18], while the latter enhances sensitivity[19]. Additionally, Xiao et al. reported that free fatty acid receptor 4 (FFAR4) expression correlates with endocrine sensitivity in patients with breast cancer[20].

  Therefore, we developed a lipid metabolic signature for ER + breast cancer patients receiving tamoxifen treatment. The model was validated in separate datasets. We identified clinical, genomic, biological, and microenvironmental features associated with the signature and evaluated potential drug sensitivities. The model reflects distinct disease states in ER + breast cancer patients and represents a valuable prognostic biomarker.


## Results

### Signature construction in TGCA-ER + Tamoxifen-BRCA dataset
  A prognostic model was constructed to predict the clinical outcome of ER + breast cancer patients after postoperative tamoxifen treatment, using the TCGA-BRCA dataset. Lipid-related gene (LRG) data were curated (1026 genes). Univariate Cox regression was performed in the TCGA-BRCA cohort (N = 183; ER + breast cancer patients with documented tamoxifen treatment) to evaluate the correlation between the expression of these genes and overall survival. Genes significantly associated with survival (p < 0.05) were considered candidate genes, yielding 23 candidates. An optimized gene panel was selected via exhaustive enumeration, after comparing the performance of 145,475 potential combinations. The risk score was calculated using a linear model:

  Risk Score = (0.7671 * PDGFB expression) + (−1.0696 * PPT1 expression) + (0.3116 * HADHA expression) + (−0.417 * AKR1C3 expression) + (−0.6313 * KIT expression) + (0.5884 * CYP26A1 expression). Patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups based on the median risk score. Overall survival was significantly shorter in high-risk patients compared to low-risk patients (Fig. 1a). All observed deaths occurred in the high-risk group. High-risk patients exhibited higher PDGFB, AKR1C3, and CYP26A1 expression levels and lower PPT1, HADHA, and KIT expression levels (Fig. 1b). Progression-free survival showed a similar association with risk group as overall survival (Fig. 1c). The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) for five-year survival prediction was calculated for the model and standard clinical indicators (Fig. 1d). The model achieved an AUC of 0.858, calculated using the linear model and visualized with the R package'pROC'. Compared to other signatures (EndoPredict, gene70, GENIUS, ROR-S, and TAMR13), our signature's three-year survival AUC (0.767) ranked second only to EndoPredict (Fig. S1). Taken together, these results demonstrate that the lipid metabolic signature is an effective prognostic model for ER + breast cancer patients treated with tamoxifen.Fig. 1Model construction based on the TCGA-ER + BRCA training cohort. According to the constructed signature, high-risk patients had prolonged overall survival (A), high mortality and a specific gene expression pattern (B). The progression-free survival pattern was also similar (C). The five-year survival area under the receiving operating characteristic (AUROC) curve was 0.858 (D)


### Signature validation
  The predictive value of the model was assayed in the TCGA dataset, but the signature itself was constructed using the clinical and gene expression information from the TCGA dataset. In other words, good performance may result from overfitting. Therefore, completely independent datasets from the GEO repository, including GSE17705 (N = 298), GSE22219 (N = 134), GSE42568 (N = 70), and GSE58644 (N = 147), were used for external validation. The enrolled samples were all ER + breast cancer patients who underwent tamoxifen treatment. The scores of the samples in the datasets were calculated exactly as in the training set. In all datasets, the patients were identified as high-risk or low-risk based on the median score values in the corresponding dataset. Survival differences between the high/low-risk groups were compared in all the completely independent datasets. In line with the survival pattern observed in the TCGA dataset, high-risk patients were characterized by relapse, high mRNA levels of oncogenes, and low levels of tumor suppressor genes in the GSE17705 (Fig. 2a), GSE22219 (Fig. 2b), GSE42568 (Fig. 2c) and GSE58644 (Fig. 2d) datasets. The signature can discriminate between poor and favorable outcomes in ER-positive, tamoxifen-treated breast cancer patients using data generated from different platforms (the training dataset is from RNA-seq and validation datasets are from microarrays), indicating that the signature is robust and reproducible across platforms and centers.Fig. 2Signature verification. The performance of the signature was verified in four complete cohorts, GSE17705 (A), GSE22219 (B), GSE42568 (C) and GSE58644 (D), which were evaluated with microarray platforms. Similar survival and transcriptome patterns of candidate genes were detected in these datasets


### Clinical associations
  Correlations between the signature score and clinical indicators were analyzed. The signature was significantly associated with age but was independent of pathological stage (Fig. 3a). Cox multivariate regression showed that the signature score was an independent indicator of survival (Fig. 3b) but not pathological stage (not shown). Considering the complex interaction between PD-1/PD-L1 and lipid metabolism (where the signature candidate genes originated) [21–23], the signature and PD-1/PD-L1 gene expression level was also analyzed. As a result, PD-1/PD-L1 expression was significantly associated with the signature (p < 0.01, R = −0.19), while the PD-1 gene expression level was not significantly associated with the signature (Fig. 3c). A nomogram considering the signature, age, stage and sex was constructed, and the calibration curve is also shown (Fig. 3d-e). According to the nomogram, the signature was among the most important indicators for five-year survival prediction. The nomogram demonstrated excellent calibration accuracy (MAE = 0.024) and stability across the current dataset (n = 59), reliably predicting individualized outcome probabilities (Fig. 3e). In brief, compared to clinical indicators, the signature is a valuable and independent biomarker for determining the prognosis of patients with ER + breast cancer.Fig. 3Signature and clinical indicators. The signature was significantly correlated with PD-L1 expression (p = 0.00093, R = −0.19) but not with PD-1 (C). ### Genetic correlations
  We next investigated the correlation between the signature and genetic alterations, including mutations, copy number variations and DNA methylation using the TCGA-BRCA dataset[24], and the TCGA pan-cancer study. Differences in mutations of frequently mutated genes between high-risk and low-risk patients were identified. Mutations in USH2A and KMT2C were detected in high-risk samples but not in low-risk samples (p = 9.1e-15 and p = 5.4e-9, respectively; Fig. 4a). Genes with differential copy numbers were also identified. As shown in Fig. 4b, high-risk samples were characterized by genomic amplification, while low-risk samples had deletions. Aberrant DNA methylation sites were also identified; these sites were enriched at KRBA2 (Fig. 4c). Taken together, these results suggest that the signature reflects genetic variation in ER + breast cancer, which may contribute to differences in carcinogenesis mechanisms.Fig. 4Genomic and expression signatures. The mutation frequencies of low-risk (A, left) and high-risk (A, right) patients were compared. Higher KMT2C and USH2A mutation were detected in high-risk patients. Compared to low-risk samples, high-risk samples had more amplification regions across chromosomes (B). Differential CpG site-associated sites are also shown (C). The GO and KEGG pathways associated with the signature were identified, and the BP, MF and CC categories are shown (D). The gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed pathways that are important for carcinogenesis and development (E), including metabolic pathways (F) and other cancer related pathways (G)


### Pathway associations
  We next investigated the correlation between the signature and canonical pathways using Gene Ontology (GO) analysis. For GO analyses, differentially expressed genes were identified between the signature-defined groups, and these genes were subjected to GO enrichment analysis. These genes were enriched for cancer-related biological processes (Fig. 4d), including cell–cell adhesion. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was carried out using high-/low-risk samples (Fig. 4e); both metabolic pathways and other cancer-related pathways were significantly enriched in high-risk samples. The former consisted of elevation in tyrosine and glycosphingolipid metabolism and decrease in pentose/glucuronate interconversions (Fig. 4f). The latter involved cell adhesion, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, and the JAK-STAT signaling pathway (Fig. 4g). In summary, these results suggest that the signature reflects various aspects of cancer development among patients with ER + breast cancer.


### Immune infiltration and drug sensitivity
  Previous results suggest that the signature may reflect carcinogenesis and development, which prompted us to explore the association between the signature and immune infiltration. The infiltration abundance in the TCGA dataset was estimated with algorithms, and immune cells that were differentially infiltrated were identified (Fig. 5a). Correlations between gene expression, risk score, and infiltration abundance were also analyzed. The risk score was significantly associated with multiple immune cell types, while individual genes showed mostly no association with risk score, indicating the advantage of the model over single genes (Fig. 5b). Among the differential immune cells, resting CD4 + memory T cells, activated mast cells, and myeloid dendritic cells were significantly enriched in the low-risk group, whereas M0 macrophages were enriched in the high-risk group (Fig. 5c), consistent with previous reports.Fig. 5Infiltration and drug response related to the signature. Immune infiltration was estimated in the TCGA dataset, and differentially infiltrated immune cells were identified (A), while immune infiltration was mostly correlated with KIT (cKit, B). The detailed immune cell proportions are shown (C). Compounds with differential IC50 values between groups (D). The correlation contributed by PDGFB in the signature (E)

  The signature reflects different clinical states of ER + breast cancer patients and prompted us to investigate whether it could predict sensitivity to different drugs. Using gene expression values from the TCGA dataset, the IC50 values of each sample were calculated using the ‘OncoPredict’ algorithm. As shown in Fig. 5d, most drugs showed greater efficacy in low-risk samples, while high-risk samples demonstrated higher sensitivity to Sepantronium bromide. Associations between candidate genes, the model, and IC50 values were evaluated. As a result, most candidate genes showed no significant association with survival, whereas the candidate gene-based model was significantly associated, suggesting effective integration of complementary information (Fig. 5e). These results suggest the signature may serve as a predictive biomarker for guiding ER + breast cancer therapy.


### Tumor microenvironment and the model
  To better understand the impact of the candidate genes, risk score, and tumor microenvironment, the single-cell sequencing dataset GSE176078 and paired bulk RNA-seq dataset were used for analyses. In this study, only ER + samples were used, and other subtypes were excluded. The candidate genes were most highly expressed in different cells rather than in immune cells (Fig. 6a). For example, KIT was expressed specifically in luminal progenitors, while PPT1 was highly expressed in macrophages but was also expressed in other cell types. The score for each sample was evaluated based on bulk RNA-seq data. These patients were subsequently divided into low- and high-risk groups according to the median risk score (Fig. 6b). Although almost all samples (both high- and low-risk) contained all cell types (Fig. 6c), the proportions of cell types, especially immune cells, were different (Fig. 6d). Associations between the risk score and cell subtypes were analyzed, revealing significant associations with cycling myeloid cells and macrophages (Fig. 6e). ### Cell–cell interactions and the model
  The aforementioned results suggest that the model reflects differences in immune cell abundance, prompting us to investigate whether it also indicates differences in cell‒cell interactions. Bulk RNA-seq data were downloaded for each single-cell sequenced ER + sample. Risk values were calculated, and samples were divided into low- and high-risk groups. Differences in cell‒cell interactions between groups were evaluated. Although nearly all interactions among cell types occurred in both groups (Fig. 7a), their intensities differed (Fig. 7b). The inferred interaction number in high-risk samples was slightly greater than in low-risk samples (ratio: 92.0%), while interaction strength was significantly lower (ratio: 37.0%), indicating less frequent cellular interactions in high-risk samples. Among cell types, interactions of PVL subtypes were strengthened in high-risk samples (Fig. 7c), while endothelial RGS5 and myoepithelial interactions were decreased. Differences in information flow between groups for each pathway were compared. As shown in Fig. 7d-e, SPP1, CEACAM, IFN-II, CD6, IL2 and other pathways were specifically detected in high-risk patients, while CDH1, SIRP, WNT, CD2, IL6 and others were observed only in low-risk patients. Notably, SPP1 interactions between luminal progenitors/macrophages and other cell types were detected in high-risk patients (Fig. 7f, top panel). Similarly, IFN-II interactions with NK cells and EPCAM interactions with cancer cells were specifically identified in high-risk samples. Moreover, SIRP pathway interactions between cycling cancer cells and IL16/CD23 pathway interactions between B cells and DC/macrophage/mature luminal cells were identified exclusively in the low-risk group (Fig. 7f, bottom panel). In summary, these results suggest diminished cellular communication in high-risk samples, particularly affecting pathways involved in adaptive immunity.Fig. 7Cell–cell interaction and the model. ## Materials and methods

### Data collection and signature construction
  This study did not collect new samples from patients or animals. For ethical consent, please see the statements in the original publications. The raw gene expression data for the GEO datasets were downloaded from the GEO database using accession numbers GSE17705[25], GSE22219[26], GSE42568[27] and GSE58644[28]. After background correction and normalization, log2-transformed gene expression signals were obtained. Probes were matched to genes based on the annotation file provided by each platform. Probes matching multiple genes were discarded. For genes with multiple probes, the probe with the highest mean signal value was retained, and others were discarded. For the TCGA dataset, log2-transformed RSEM + 1 values were retrieved from the UCSC Xena[29] database, along with mutation, DNA methylation, and copy number variation information. Only ER + samples with tamoxifen treatment records were included, resulting in 183 enrolled samples.

  The signature was constructed using the following steps. First, lipid metabolism genes were retrieved from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB)[30]using the GOBP_CELLULAR_LIPID_METABOLIC_PROCESS term, yielding 1026 genes. Second, Cox univariate regression evaluated the correlation between gene expression abundance and overall survival, identifying 23 candidate genes with p < 0.05. Third, to identify the optimal combination, we employed an exhaustive enumeration method to evaluate all possible gene combinations (pairs, triplets, quadruplets, quintuplets, and sextuplets) derived from the 23 candidate genes pre-selected through preliminary analysis. Specifically, we systematically tested 145,475 combinations (calculated as C(23,2) + C(23,3) + C(23,4) + C(23,5) + C(23,6)), constructed Cox regression models (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} 				\usepackage{amsmath} 				\usepackage{wasysym}  				\usepackage{amsfonts}  				\usepackage{amssymb}  				\usepackage{amsbsy} 				\usepackage{mathrsfs} 				\usepackage{upgreek} 				\setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} 				\begin{document}$${\sum }_{k}^{n}{{b}_{k}x}_{k}$$\end{document}∑knbkxk, where bk is the coefficient and xk is the processed gene abundance (log2(RSEM + 1))) for each combination, calculated risk scores, stratified patients into high- and low-risk groups, and selected the 6-gene combination with the smallest log-rank p-value for survival difference. The combination with the smallest p value was identified as the optimized panel.


### Performance validation
  After pre-processing the gene expression microarray data, risk scores for the validation datasets were calculated using the same coefficients as the training dataset. In each validation dataset, the median risk score was used as the cutoff to divide samples equally into high-risk and low-risk groups. Survival differences between groups were evaluated using the log-rank test. Survival patterns were visualized with Kaplan–Meier plots. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) for other signatures (EP, GENE70, GENIUS, RS, and TAMR13) was evaluated using the R package'genefu'[31].


### Genomic signature identification
  Processed multi-omics data, including DNA methylation array, copy number array, and genetic mutation information, were downloaded from the cBioPortal database [24]. Mutations were statistically analyzed on a gene-by-gene basis in low- and high-risk groups. Fisher’s exact test was used to evaluate differences in mutation rates between groups (p < 0.05). The mutation landscape of frequently mutated genes was visualized with the R package'GenVisR'[32]. The Wilcoxon rank sum test was used to evaluate differentially methylated CpG sites and differential copy number variations between groups; genes or CpG sites with p < 0.05 were considered significantly different.


### Gene Ontology (GO) and gene set enrichment analyses
  The R package “limma”[33] was used to identify DEGs based on samples from low- and high-risk groups, and genes with a |log₂-fold change|> 1 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.01 were retained. Enriched GO terms were identified with the R package"clusterProfiler"[34] and visualized across molecular function, biological process, and cellular component categories. GSEA was implemented with the R package"GSEAbase"by ranking all genes in the TCGA dataset according to log2 fold change.


### Immune infiltration and drug sensitivity prediction
  Immune cell abundances were evaluated using TIMER 2.0[35] algorithm and other algorithms, including XCELL[36] and CIBERSORT[37], with a web server (http://timer.cistrome.org/) using gene expression matrices (log₂-transformed TPM format) from the TCGA website. Immune abundances in low-risk and high-risk samples were compared using Student's t test, with p < 0.01 considered statistically significant. Results were visualized with the R packages"pheatmap"and"vioplot".

  The IC50 values for pharmaceutical compounds in TCGA samples were estimated using transcriptome data and the R package"oncopredict"[38], employing the package's built-in training dataset. IC50 values were compared between groups using Student's t test, with compounds showing p < 0.05 considered significantly different.


### Single-cell data analysis
  The single-cell sequencing data were downloaded from the GEO database (accession number: GSE176078). Annotation information was downloaded with the processed count matrix. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data were analyzed using the Seurat package [39] in R. Raw count matrices were imported; cells with fewer than 200 expressed genes or mitochondrial gene content exceeding 10% were excluded. Data were normalized using the LogNormalize method with a scale factor of 10,000. Highly variable genes were identified for subsequent analysis. Dimensionality reduction was performed via principal component analysis (PCA), followed by uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) for cell cluster visualization. Cell types were annotated using canonical marker genes.

  To explore cell–cell communication dynamics between high- and low-risk groups (stratified by median risk score), the scRNA-seq dataset was partitioned. Cell–cell communication networks were inferred using'CellChat'[40], employing the default human ligand-receptor interaction database. Signaling pathway activity was quantified, and differential interactions between groups were identified by permutation testing (1,000 iterations). Networks were merged to compare communication strength; significant pathways were visualized via heatmaps or circle plots.

  Cell type proportions between risk groups were calculated and compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Spearman's rank correlation assessed associations between risk scores and cell type abundances. Statistical analyses used R (v4.2.1), with visualizations created in ggplot2 (v3.4.0) and ComplexHeatmap (v2.14.0).


### Statistics
  All statistical analyses were implemented using R (v4.3.0). The ROC curve was plotted with the R package'pROC'. For comparisons between two groups, the appropriate statistical test was selected based on the variable type and variance assumptions. For continuous variables, Student's t-test was performed when homogeneity of variance was assumed, while the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (a non-parametric alternative) was used when this assumption was violated. For categorical variables, Fisher's exact test was employed. In this study, statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.


## Discussion
  In this study, we developed a novel signature using lipid-related genes in ER-positive, tamoxifen-treated TCGA-BRCA patients and validated its performance across four independent datasets from different centers and platforms. The signature was associated with clinical, genomic, transcriptomic, and microenvironmental heterogeneity, including cell proportion and cell–cell interaction heterogeneity. The AUROC for five-year overall survival reached 0.858. Compared with other previously reported signatures, our signature performed more favorably. For example, the five-year overall survival AUROC of Hao et al.'s model was 0.788, using a 10-gene expression signature with their published formula[41]. Another study developed an angiogenesis signature for breast cancer, achieving an AUROC of 0.785[42], although it was not limited to ER-positive, tamoxifen-treated BRCA. Similarly, a model developed by Andrew and colleagues reached an AUROC of 0.74 for distant metastasis in ER + breast cancer[43]. Kang et al.'s signature achieved 0.69 for five-year recurrence-free survival in ER-positive, tamoxifen-treated BRCA[44]. Compared to other survival endpoints, overall survival is more objective, reliable, and directly reflects patient survival benefit, serving as the gold standard for therapeutic efficacy evaluation and recognized by global regulatory agencies as the most authoritative clinical endpoint. However, comparing AUROCs across different endpoints and datasets cannot objectively reflect signature performance. Our model's AUROC of 0.858 demonstrates favorable performance.

  We noted the five-year overall survival AUROC for age was 0.886, slightly higher than our signature. Since the mean diagnosis age for BRCA is 50–60 years and ER + breast cancer survival is typically longer than most cancers, we suspect comorbidities and non-disease-specific mortality contribute to overall survival outcomes in addition to age-related disease progression.

  Among the candidate genes, PDGFB was reported to be associated with tamoxifen sensitivity in ER + breast cancer and to affect cell growth via the cell cycle[45]. Similarly, aldo–keto reductase 1C3 (AKR1C3) was shown to correlate with chemotherapy sensitivity in breast cancer patients[46]. Despite limited reports regarding HADHA, it was associated with breast cancer carcinogenesis[47]. As a well-known oncogene, KIT has been linked to an aggressive breast cancer phenotype [48]. Palmitoyl-protein thioesterase 1 (PPT1) has not been previously reported in breast cancer. Collectively, these findings support the value of these genes as prognostic and predictive biomarkers for ER + breast cancer.

  Among the differentially mutated genes, KMT2C and USH2A were detected exclusively in high-risk patients. Although USH2A's function in breast cancer remains unreported, its mutation correlates with immunotherapy response in lung cancer[49]. KMT2C, a key regulator of ER activity[50], shows significant associations with breast cancer survival and serves as a chemotherapy efficacy biomarker[51]. Our lipid metabolism-related signature also significantly associated with these mutations. This association may be explained by two mechanisms: 1) KMT2C and USH2A mutations may modulate lipid metabolism processes in ER + breast cancer, subsequently regulating biological processes including drug resistance; or 2) Since tamoxifen regulates various lipid pathways[44, 52], our signature reflects treatment consequences while KMT2C/USH2A mutations potentially regulate tamoxifen effectiveness (e.g., via ER expression modulation). These hypotheses require further investigation.

  It is noted that immune cell infiltration differed significantly between high- and low-risk samples. Activated mast cells and myeloid dendritic cells were enriched in low-risk samples, which are reported activators of immune response in most situations [53, 54]. Previous studies demonstrate that tamoxifen influences the immune infiltration profile of breast cancer[55]. Additionally, despite remarkably lower cell–cell interaction strength in high-risk samples, the SPP1 signaling pathway was specifically detected between macrophages and other cell types in single-cell sequencing data. Recent research indicates CXCL9:SPP1 macrophage polarity modulates cancer spatial organization and impacts progression[56]. Thus, the signature may also reflect tamoxifen's impact on immune infiltration in ER-positive breast cancer and predict prognosis.

  Collectively, these findings demonstrate the lipid metabolism-related gene signature exhibits significant associations with mutational landscape, clinical prognosticators, immune microenvironment dynamics, and therapeutic susceptibility profiles. These parameters represent critical determinants of breast cancer progression and treatment outcomes, serving as both drivers and downstream manifestations of lipid metabolic reprogramming evidenced by prior mechanistic studies. The multi-gene prognostic model comprehensively encapsulates the molecular complexity of ER-positive, tamoxifen-treated breast cancer, accounting for its robust predictive performance and cross-institutional reproducibility across diverse platforms.

  Our study has limitations. First, its retrospective design with insufficient clinical information (especially treatment details) may introduce dataset enrollment bias. Second, platform-generated gene expression heterogeneity hinders optimal cutoff determination despite supporting model robustness. Third, immune infiltration and drug sensitivity derived from TCGA transcriptome data lack experimental validation, making results less persuasive. Further validation is needed before clinical implementation. Finally, while the enumeration-based signature finds globally optimized solutions with few features, it becomes computationally infeasible for larger signatures (e.g., Mammaprint). As discussed, additional genes enhance robustness but could limit performance. This approach may also risk overfitting. Future work will explore other machine learning algorithms.


## Conclusions
  The signature we developed is robust and reproducible in predicting the survival of ER + breast cancer and reflected biological heterogeneity of breast cancer from different biological aspects.
