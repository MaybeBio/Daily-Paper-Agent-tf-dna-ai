# Hyperoside mitigates amphotericin B-induced nephrotoxicity in HK-2 cells via bioenergetic and oxidative stress modulation


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40360-025-00985-1.


## Introduction
  Amphotericin-B (Amp-B) deoxycholate (PubChem CID (Chemical Identifier): 23668620), also known as fungizone (FZ), remains a cornerstone in the management of severe fungal infections, particularly fungemia and candidemia, due to its broad-spectrum efficacy and cost-effectiveness [1]. While newer liposomal and lipid-associated Amp-B formulations offer reduced nephrotoxicity, their high cost limits widespread clinical adoption, making FZ the preferred option in many settings [2]. As a result, FZ continues to be frequently prescribed, especially for high-risk patients with invasive fungal infections, including those caused by Aspergillus and Candida species, as well as in cases of severe respiratory infections (e.g., COVID-19, influenza) and abdominal sepsis following peritonitis [3].

  To investigate the mechanisms of FZ-induced nephrotoxicity, we selected the human kidney 2 (HK-2) cell line as our in vitro model. HK-2 cells are immortalized human proximal tubular epithelial cells that retain key transport, metabolic, and injury-response characteristics of primary proximal tubule cells [4]. Their use is well established in nephrotoxicity research, as they reliably recapitulate the physiological and toxicological responses of human renal proximal tubules to nephrotoxic agents. Importantly, HK-2 cells are highly sensitive to oxidative, metabolic, and apoptotic insults, making them an ideal surrogate for studying proximal tubular damage in vitro and for screening nephroprotective interventions [5].

  Nephrotoxicity remains a significant limitation of FZ therapy, with up to 28% of patients developing nephrotoxic effects during treatment [6]. Serum creatinine elevations are observed in nearly 80% of patients, and 15% may require renal replacement therapy due to acute kidney injury [7]. The high vulnerability of renal tubular cells is attributed to their exposure to concentrated toxins following glomerular filtration and water reabsorption [8].

  FZ-induced nephrotoxicity involves significant hemodynamic alterations. The drug promotes afferent arteriole vasoconstriction, leading to reduced renal blood flow and glomerular filtration rate (GFR). This ischemic environment is further exacerbated by the release of vasoconstrictive and inflammatory mediators, resulting in compromised oxygen and nutrient delivery to renal tissues. These hemodynamic changes set the stage for subsequent cellular injury and sensitize the kidney to additional insults [9–11].

  Oxidative stress represents a central mechanism in FZ-induced renal injury. FZ increases the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) within renal tubular cells, overwhelming endogenous antioxidant defenses such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT). The resulting oxidative imbalance leads to lipid peroxidation, protein oxidation, and DNA damage. Notably, mitochondrial dysfunction, triggered by ROS, further amplifies oxidative injury by impairing ATP production and disrupting mitochondrial membrane potential.

  At the molecular level, FZ disrupts cell membranes by binding to ergosterol, a mechanism that, while effective against fungi, also damages mammalian cell membranes [12]. This disruption activates intrinsic apoptotic pathways, characterized by mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization, cytochrome c release, and caspase activation. Additionally, FZ-induced oxidative stress can trigger both intrinsic and extrinsic apoptosis, as well as upregulate pro-apoptotic proteins (e.g., Bax) and downregulate anti-apoptotic proteins (e.g., Bcl-2) [13].

  These mechanisms are not isolated. Hemodynamic compromise reduces renal perfusion, which exacerbates oxidative stress by limiting oxygen delivery and impairing mitochondrial function. In turn, oxidative damage and mitochondrial dysfunction potentiate apoptotic signaling, leading to tubular cell death and loss of renal function [14]. Thus, FZ-induced nephrotoxicity results from a complex interplay between hemodynamic, oxidative, and molecular/apoptotic pathways.

  Given the pivotal role of oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction in the pathogenesis of FZ-induced nephrotoxicity, there is a compelling need for interventions that can effectively target these injury mechanisms. Hyperoside (HP; PubChem CID: 5281643), a naturally occurring flavonoid glycoside abundant in various plant families [15], has emerged as a particularly promising candidate in this context. HP is well-documented to possess potent antioxidant and cytoprotective properties, including: (a) direct scavenging of ROS and enhancement of endogenous antioxidant enzyme activities (such as SOD and CAT), thereby restoring redox balance and protecting cellular structures from oxidative injury [16–18]; (b) activation of the Nrf2/ARE signaling pathway, which upregulates the transcription of key antioxidant genes and strengthens cellular defense against oxidative insults [16, 18]; (c) inhibition of apoptosis by modulating the expression of pro- and anti-apoptotic proteins, as well as interfering with caspase activation, thus preventing oxidative stress-induced cell death [18–20]; (d) preservation of mitochondrial integrity/function, which is essential for maintaining ATP production and preventing further ROS generation [20, 21]. These multifaceted mechanisms make hyperoside especially suitable for targeting the oxidative, mitochondrial, and apoptotic pathways implicated in FZ-induced nephrotoxicity. By intervening at multiple levels of the injury cascade, Hyperoside holds significant potential as a nephroprotective agent capable of mitigating the specific cellular damage caused by Amphotericin B [22].

  Notably, HP’s renoprotective properties have been validated in various experimental models [23–26]. Given these properties, the present study aims to determine whether Hyperoside can protect HK-2 cells from Amphotericin B-induced nephrotoxicity by targeting oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and apoptosis. We specifically ask whether Hyperoside mitigates FZ-induced cytotoxicity/genotoxicity, restores mitochondrial and antioxidant function, and modulates key molecular injury pathways. We hypothesize that Hyperoside co-treatment will significantly attenuate FZ-induced cellular injury in HK-2 cells by restoring bioenergetics and antioxidant defenses and inhibiting apoptotic signaling.


## Materials and methods

### Molecular docking
  Molecular docking was performed to predict the expected inhibitory binding affinity and interaction profiles of FZ and HP with target proteins implicated in mitochondrial bioenergetics, oxidative stress, and apoptosis based on the available literature data. The three-dimensional structures of ligands were retrieved from the PubChem database and energy-minimized prior to docking [27]. Target protein structures were obtained from the “Research Collaboratory for Structural Bioinformatics (RCSB)” Protein Data Bank (PDB) and prepared by removing water molecules and heteroatoms [28]. Docking simulations were conducted using the SwissDock web server (http://www.swissdock.ch), which employs the EADock DSS engine for accurate prediction of ligand-protein interactions [29]. The most favorable binding poses were selected based on FullFitness scores and estimated binding free energy (ΔG) [30]. Post-docking analysis, including identification of hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic contacts, was performed using the UCSF Chimera software for three-dimensional visualization and interaction mapping [31].


### In vitro assays

#### Chemicals
  The current study chemicals, including FZ, were obtained from Bristol-Myers Squibb Canada Inc in lyophilized powder form. It was used at concentrations of 30 and 60 µM, selected based on previous studies demonstrating dose-dependent nephrotoxicity in HK-2 cells within this range [32–35]. HP was purchased from Sigma (St. Louis, MO, USA) and was applied at 20 and 40 µM, doses established through preliminary cytotoxicity assays and consistent with concentrations reported to exert antioxidant and cytoprotective effects in renal cell models [36]. The same batch of HK-2 cells (within 5 passes), same reagent lots, and consistent cell culture, treatment, and test techniques ensured experiment consistency. The vehicle control group (negative control) in all in vitro tests consisted of HK-2 cells treated with the same DMSO concentration to dissolve FZ and HP. The final DMSO concentration in all treatment and control groups was maintained at ≤ 0.1% (v/v) to ensure no cytotoxic effect on HK-2 cells.


#### Cell culture
  HK-2 cells (CRL-2190, American Type Culture Collection [ATCC], Manassas, VA, USA), an immortalized human renal proximal tubular epithelial cell line, were cultured in a 1:1 mixture of Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, Gibco, Prat de Llobregat, Barcelona, Spain) and Nutrient Mixture F-12 (HAM) (F-12, Gibco). The culture medium was supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin), and 1% L-glutamine (2 mM), if not already included in the DMEM/F-12 formulation. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ and passaged at 70–80% confluence using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA [37]. All reagents were obtained from Gibco unless otherwise specified. Cells were routinely monitored for mycoplasma contamination and only passages 3–8 were used for experiments to ensure reproducibility and minimize drift.


#### Cytotoxicity assessment using LDH and MTT assays
  The HP impact on the HK-2 cell’s viability and cells’ membrane integrity was assessed by widely used MTT and LDH assays. In 96-well plates, two times as many cells as each well were seeded with HK-2 cells, which were then cultured to 90% confluence. After that, cells were treated to various HP doses (0.1, 1, 10, 100, and 1000 µM) for 24 h. The LDH test employed wells containing 2% Triton X-100 as a positive control to finish cell disintegration. The MTT test was performed using the methodology of E Elmorsy, LM Elzalabany, HM Elsheikha and PA Smith [38]. LDH was carried out under the manufacturer’s guidelines (Clontech, Mountain View, California, USA). The MTT and LDH absorbance levels were estimated using the plate reader “TopCount” (Perkin Elmer, Ueberlingen, Germany), using reading points of 590 nm and 490 nm, respectively, each time the point’s assessment was carried out in triplicate.


#### Genotoxic effect of FZ and HP on HK-2 cells
  After being seeded, cells were treated with FZ (30 or 60 µM) over 24 h, either in the presence or absence of HP (20 and 40 µM). Subsequently, the alkaline comet assay was done following M De Boeck, N Touil, G De Visscher, PA Vande and M Kirsch-Volders [39]. Briefly, the cells were removed and submerged in ice-cold PBS, and finally, 30% ethanol was added. Slides were plated following the addition of agarose to the infranatant. After the samples were lysed with an alkaline lysis solution (pH = 10), they were electrophoresed at 300 mA for 30 min. The slides were stained with PI (1 mg/ml) for five minutes after being cleaned in neutralizing solution (0.4 mM Tris-HCl) for three to five minutes. The cells were imaged using fluorescence microscopy at 546 nm excitation and 640 nm emission. The photos were analyzed using Comet score IV software (Perspective Instruments, UK).


#### Bioenergetics mitochondrial assays
  Hk-2 cells were seeded and treated with FZ (30 or 60 µM) with or without HP (20 and 40 µM) for 24 h to assess bioenergetic alterations. Following treatment, a mitochondrial-enriched fraction was isolated by differential centrifugation. Briefly, cells were harvested, washed with cold PBS, and homogenized in mitochondrial isolation buffer (250 mM sucrose, 10 mM HEPES, 1 mM EDTA, pH 7.4) [40]. The homogenate was centrifuged at 700 × g for 10 min to remove nuclei and debris, and the supernatant was further centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C to isolate the mitochondrial pellet. The mitochondrial pellet was resuspended in isolation buffer and used immediately for enzyme activity assays [41].

  • The effect of FZ and HP on HK-2 cells’ mitochondrial complexes I (MCI) and complexes III (MCIII) activities

  Mitochondrial complex I (MCI) activity was measured spectrophotometrically by monitoring NADH oxidation at 340 nm in the presence of 0.13 mM NADH, 0.065 mM ubiquinone, 25 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.2), 5 mM MgCl₂, and 2 mM KCN. Rotenone (1 µM) was used as a specific inhibitor to confirm assay specificity, following the approach described by Spinazzi et al. [41]

  Mitochondrial complex III (MCIII) activity was assessed in whole-cell lysates by measuring cytochrome c reduction at 550 nm using 0.05 mM decyl-ubiquinol, 25 µM cytochrome c (oxidized), 35 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.2), 5 mM MgCl₂, and 2 mM KCN. Antimycin A (1 µM) served as the MCIII inhibitor to validate reaction specificity, as described by Ma et al. [42, 43]. For reliable results, experiments were redone with a minimum of five samples per treatment, and controls were measured. The absorbance was measured using a spectrophotometer (Beckman Coulter, DU 800) with temperature control. The activity of both complexes was reported as nmol min-1 mg-1 of total protein.

  • The effect of FZ and HP on HK-2 cells’ intracellular ATP levels

  Using the commercial kit and instructions that were provided, ATP was computed (Abcam, ab83355, Cambridge, UK). The light was produced by the reaction of ATP with more D-luciferin and luciferase. The luminescence signal was measured using a “TopCount” luminometer (Perkin Elmer, Ueberlingen, Germany). All well values for the samples and controls were subtracted from blank readings. For every sample, ATP was adjusted to the total amount of tissue protein. The evaluation was done three times for every concentration used in the experiment.

  • The effect of FZ and HP on the HK-2 cells’ mitochondrial membrane potential (MPP)

  HK-2 cells were prepared and handled similarly to the preceding section for the ATP experiment. Using the supplier’s recommended Mito Tracker Green (MG) staining solution (final concentration 50 nM), the HP influence on the treated HK-2 cells MMP was evaluated at the time point using an MG probe (Molecular Probes, Eugene, OR) [44]. Lastly, fluorescence was measured at 490/515 nm excitation/emission filters using a Top Count microplate reader (Perkin Elmer, Ueberlingen, Germany). The uncoupler carbonyl cyanide-4-(trifluoromethoxy) phenylhydrazone was used as a positive control for mitochondrial depolarization. The evaluation was done three times for every concentration used in the experiment.


#### The effect of FZ and HP on mitophagy in the treated HK-2 cells
  The effect of FZ and HP on the mitophagy of the treated renal cells was assessed by studying the expression of PINK1 and PARKIN proteins using a commercial ELISA kit following the manufacturers (Bioscience and Abcam, respectively) using cell lysate. After adding the stop solution, absorbance was read at 450 OD by a Dyne MRX microplate reader. Levels were estimated by substituting OD450 estimated values into the prepared standard curve data.


#### The effect of FZ and HP on oxidative stress biomarkers
  For oxidative stress assays, cells were seeded and treated with FZ (30 or 60 µM) in the presence or absence of HP (20 and 40 µM) for 24 h.

  • The effect of FZ and HP on reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in HK-2 cells.

  At the time point, the intracellular ROS levels were measured using the 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFDA) assay (25 µM in Hank’s balanced salt solution) following E Elmorsy, LM Elzalabany, HM Elsheikha and PA Smith [38] protocol. At the end of treatment, cells were washed twice with PBS and incubated with 25 µM DCFDA for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Subsequently, cells were washed to remove excess dye, and fluorescence was measured using a microplate reader with excitation at 485 nm and emission at 535 nm. For each assay, a positive control (cells treated with 100 µM hydrogen peroxide for 30 min) was included to confirm ROS assay responsiveness. Cell-free blank wells were included, and their fluorescence values were subtracted from sample readings to correct for background. Results were expressed as a percentage of the fluorescence intensity relative to the untreated control group (set as 100%). All measurements were performed in triplicate.

  • The effect of FZ and HP on HK-2 cells’ lipid peroxidation level

  Thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS), a well-known biomarker for oxidative stress, were measured using a commercial kit (Abcam) based on the quantification of malondialdehyde (MDA) as a valid biomarker for lipid peroxidation following the supplied company protocol. At the time point, the cells were homogenized, sonicated, and centrifuged at 13,000 xg. The supernatant was then gathered for the assay. Finally, absorbance was read at 532 nm by a “TopCount” plate reader (Perkin Elmer). Triplicates were used for each experiment.

  • The effect of FZ and HP on HK-2 cells’ antioxidant enzyme activities

  Following R Singh, B Wiseman, T Deemagarn, V Jha, J Switala and PC Loewen [45], catalase (CAT) activity was measured calorimetrically. After harvesting cells, the amount of protein in each sample was calculated. Tissue homogenate (0.1 ml) was mixed with 0.01 M pH 7 phosphate buffer (1 ml) and 2 M H2O2 tissue (0.4 ml) for the reaction. Dichromate-acetic acid reagent (2 ml) was added to the initial solution to halt the reaction. The absorbance point of CAT was measured at 620 nm and represented in units of µmoles H2O2 ingested/min/mg protein. The superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was determined using a colorimetric commercial assay kit (Abcam, Cambridge, UK). Centrifugation (14,000 xg for five minutes at + 4 °C) was carried out after cell harvesting. After that, the supernatants were collected and refrigerated until analysis. The supernatant contains every single SOD cell, mitochondrial and cytosolic. At 440 nm, the SOD absorbance rate was monitored with a “TopCount” plate reader.


#### The effect of FZ and HP on HK-2 cells’ apoptotic pathways
  Cells were exposed to FZ (30 or 60 µM) with or without HP (30 or 60 µM) for 24 h. Then, using BD Apo-Alert caspase fluorescence assay kits (Clontech Laboratories, CA, USA), caspase-3, -8, and − 9 activities in the sample were assessed following the manufacturer’s instructions. Ultimately, excitation/emission fluorescence measurements were made at 400/505 nm (caspase-3 and − 8) and 380/460 nm (caspase-9).


#### Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)
  As previously mentioned [46], total RNA was isolated from HK-2 cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and quantified spectrophotometrically (NanoDrop 2000, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with purity confirmed by A260/A280 ratios of 1.8-2.0. RNA integrity was verified via agarose gel electrophoresis (1.5%) to ensure intact 18 S/28S rRNA bands. Reverse transcription was performed using the Accurate Biotechnology cDNA Synthesis Kit (Hunan, China) with 1 µg RNA, oligo(dT) primers, and RNase inhibitor, following the manufacturer’s protocols. Reactions included a no-template control (NTC), and genomic DNA contamination was ruled out by amplification of non-reverse-transcribed RNA.

  Primer pairs (Table S1) were designed using NCBI Primer-BLAST, with amplicon specificity confirmed by melt curve analysis (single peak) and agarose gel electrophoresis. Primer efficiencies (90–110%) and linearity (R² >0.99) were validated using 10-fold cDNA serial dilutions. qPCR reactions (20 µL) contained 2X SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems), 200 nM primers, and 50 ng cDNA. Amplification was performed in triplicate on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) under the following conditions: 95 °C for 10 min (initial denaturation), 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. Data were normalized to the geometric mean of two stable reference genes (GAPDH and ACTB) selected via NormFinder analysis. Relative quantification used the 2−ΔΔCt method, with results expressed as fold changes relative to untreated controls [47]. All procedures adhered to “MIQE guidelines (Minimum Information for Publication of Quantitative Real-Time PCR Experiments)”, ensuring transparency and reproducibility [48].


### Statistical analysis
  The statistical software utilized for the study’s analyses was PRISM 5 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, California, USA). The EC50 non-linear MTT and LDH tests were estimated using non-linear curve fitting statistics. The impact of applied exposure periods and HP concentrations on the assay’s results was assessed using two-way ANOVA. When comparing the data from the various groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) tests with Tukey’s multiple comparisons post-test, unpaired student t-tests, or Mann-Whitney tests were employed. P < 0.05 was used to indicate statistical significance. Multivariable analysis was conducted by SRplot (science and research website plot), a free available website (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/en).


## Results

### Molecular Docking
  The inhibitory binding affinities and molecular interactions of FZ and HP were assessed in relation to specific target proteins associated with oxidative stress and apoptosis based on the available data concerning their biological activities (Table 1). FZ demonstrated moderate binding affinity for MCI, MCIII, CAT, and SOD, with binding energies between − 5.16 and − 6.22 kcal/mol. The strongest binding affinity was recorded with CAT (–6.22 kcal/mol), which involved the formation of six hydrogen bonds and four hydrophobic interactions with critical residues, including Arg66, Arg68, Gln331, and His364, suggesting a stable and potentially inhibitory interaction. The interaction with MCIII (–6.18 kcal/mol) included one hydrogen bond and six hydrophobic contacts, particularly with residues Val14, Ile45, and Ala194, indicating favorable binding within a hydrophobic pocket. The binding of MCI was marginally weaker at − 5.16 kcal/mol, characterized by limited hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions with Asp150 and Tyr174.

### The effect of FZ and HP on the HK-2 cells’ viability
  The cytotoxic effect of HP was investigated using a wide range of concentrations of 0.1–1000 µM after 24-hour exposure via MTT and LDH assays. Data revealed that HP was significantly cytotoxic to the cells. Cytotoxicity was correlated to drug concentrations. MTT was more sensitive than LDH assay to the cells’ viability. At 1µM concentration, HP decreased the cell’s viability to 79.2 ± 1.9 and 84.3 ± 2.3% of the control cells’ viability in MTT and LDH, respectively. EC50 estimated by MTT assay was 57.2 µM (95% confidence interval 52.3–63.2 µM) 24-hour postexposure, while LDH estimated EC50 was 97.4 µM (95% confidence interval 93.3-105.1 µM) for the same exposure duration (Fig. 3A). HP was shown to significantly improve the HK-2 cell viability at concentrations 20.40 and 80 µM with no significant effect between the effect of HP at 40 and 80 µM (Fig. 3B). Interestingly, co-treatment with HP at concentrations 20 and 40 µM had significantly alleviated FZ (30 and 60 µM) induced cytotoxicity to variable extents (Fig. 3C).

### The effect of FZ and HP on the HK-2 cells’ genetic materials
  The alkaline comet assay was used to evaluate the effect of FZ and HP on the genetic material of the tested cells. Co-treatment with HP at concentrations of 20 and 40 µM had significantly alleviated FZ (60 µM) induced genotoxicity to variable extents based on the tested HP concentration.


### The effect of FZ and HP on the HK-2 cells’ bioenergetics
  The concentration-dependent reduction in cellular ATP generation (Fig. 4A), MMP (Fig. 4B), MCI activities (Fig. 4C), and MCIII activities (Fig. 4D) were observed in HK-2 cells exposed to FZ at concentrations of 30 and 60 µM. Moreover, it was discovered that administering HP at 20 and 40 µM could successfully offset FZ’s inhibitory impact on the bioenergetic tests (Fig. 2A and D). According to the data, FZ (60 µM) decreased the levels and activities of ATP, MMP, MCI, and MCIII in the cells to 52.7 ± 3.2, 54.3 ± 2.9, 47.3 ± 3.3, and 75.2 ± 3.5%, respectively, of the nontreated control cell levels. However, co-treatment with HP (40 µM) mitigated the effects of FZ (60 µM), and the ATP cellular level, MMP, MCI, and MCIII activities were raised to 82.1 ± 3.3, 78.1 ± 2.9, 81.2 ± 3.1, and 89.7 ± 2.9, respectively, of the control nontreated cells.

### HP alleviates FZ-induced oxidative damage in HK-2 cells
  Oxidative stress tests revealed that both metals significantly raised ROS (Fig. 6A) and TBARS (Fig. 6B) levels while also variably reducing the activity of the antioxidant enzymes CAT (Fig. 6C) and SOD (Fig. 6D) at both tested dosages. The influence of FZ on oxidative stress indicators was successfully minimized by co-treatment with HP at 20 and 40 µM doses (Fig. 6A and D). At a dose of 60 µM, FZ increased ROS and TBARS by approximately 11.5 and 5.2 times, respectively, but decreased SOD and CAT activity to 54.2 ± 3.5% and 77 ± 4.1% of the untreated cells. However, co-treatment with HP at 40 µM concentration reduced the rise in ROS and TBARS to 5.3- and 2.2-fold, respectively, and alleviated the decrease in CAT and SOD activities to 84.21 ± 3.9% and 92.7 ± 3.6% of the nontreated cells. In addition, FZ decreased the expression of the Nrf2 gene (Fig. 4E) and HO-1 (Fig. 6F) to different extents in a concentration-dependent manner. HP counteracted the oxidative stress biomarkers’ impact on the FZ at 20 and 40 µM (Fig. 6E and F).

### HP counteracted the effect of FZ on the apoptosis pathways of the human HK-2 cells
  By using caspases-3 and − 8/-9 as indicators for the common and intrinsic/extrinsic apoptotic routes, respectively, it was possible to determine the impact of drugs on the apoptotic pathways of human HK-2 cells. At 30 and 60 µM, FZ was shown to increase caspases-3 (Fig. 7A) considerably and − 8 (Fig. 7B), but only at the higher concentration (60 µM) did caspase-9 increase (Fig. 7C). In a concentration-dependent manner, FZ also markedly raised the Bax/Bcl2 ratio in the treated cells relative to the untreated cells (Fig. 7D). Fascinatingly, at concentrations of 20 and 40 µM, HP was found to partially, but not completely, mitigate the effects of the FZ on apoptotic pathways and the Bax/Bcl2 ratio (Fig. 7A and D).

### Multivariable analysis
  Principal component analysis (PCA) and hierarchical clustering were employed to demonstrate the overall effects of FZ and HP co-treatments on cellular and molecular endpoints. PCA plots (Fig. 8A and D) demonstrated clear separation among treatment groups along the first principal component (PC1), accounting for the majority of variance across datasets (71.4–89.2%). The FZ30 and FZ60 groups demonstrated clear clustering separate from the control group, with further divergence noted when co-treated with HP at doses of 10 mg/kg and 20 mg/kg, indicating a dose-dependent change in biological response. FZ60 + HP20 demonstrated the most pronounced deviation, suggesting cumulative or synergistic effects.

  The hierarchical heatmap (Fig. 8E) revealed notable differences in the expression profiles of oxidative stress, mitochondrial function, and apoptotic markers. The Control and FZ-alone groups demonstrated clear clustering differences compared to the co-treatment groups. FZ60 + HP10 and FZ60 + HP20 exhibited significant alterations, as demonstrated by a distinct blue gradient reflecting the downregulation of several parameters, including ROS, TBARS, and apoptotic markers (Caspases, Bax/Bcl2). Mitochondrial markers (ND1, ND5, ATP6/8), antioxidant regulators (CAT, HO-1, Nrf2), and viability indicators (MTT, MMP) showed relative upregulation in the FZ + HP-treated groups. The results demonstrate that co-treatment, particularly at higher doses, markedly improves mitochondrial integrity and reduces oxidative and apoptotic stress, thus reinforcing the effectiveness of combinatory approaches for neuroprotection.

## Discussion
  The current work aims to assess the protective impact of HP against FZ-induced cytotoxicity in HK-2 cells by examining the many intracellular processes that underline cytotoxicity. The source of HK-2 cells is a normal adult male human kidney. The cell line has been used in studies on toxicity. Instead of using the primary human cell line, which is challenging to obtain, an immortalized cell line was used to provide a single homogenous cell population of phenotypically similar cells for data reproducibility. Human cell lines were used to prevent interspecies effects on the robustness of the results.

  Because tubular cells are exposed to higher amounts of FZ (up to 100 µM) following water reabsorption, they were used as the model organisms for this investigation [49]. Furthermore, tubular cells’ bioenergetics are crucial to their ability to act biologically via active transport mechanisms, unlike glomerular cells, which have more mitochondria than glomerular cells [50]. Throughout the experiment’s duration, the study examined the effects of a broad range of FZ concentrations, from 0.1 to 1000 µM, or therapeutic, high therapeutic, and toxic concentrations [51–53]. While the effect of HP was checked using concentrations of 20–80 µM, which was effective in other cell lines studies [23, 26].

  The molecular docking results revealed that FZ exhibited moderate to strong binding affinities with MCI, MCIII, and CAT, aligning with its known antioxidant potential. In particular, its interaction with CAT (–6.22 kcal/mol) suggests a plausible mechanism for enzymatic modulation through stable hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions. However, the unusually high positive binding energy observed with SOD (+ 10.76 kcal/mol), despite numerous hydrogen bonds, reflects a computational artifact or conformational incompatibility, indicating that docking alone may not reliably predict functional outcomes in such cases. Similarly, HP showed weak or unfavorable docking scores with Caspase-3 and Bax, which contrasts with its previously reported pro-apoptotic activities in various models. These discrepancies may stem from several factors, including limitations in the docking algorithm, neglect of protein flexibility, solvation effects, and absence of dynamic biological conditions such as cellular uptake, metabolism, and compound stability. Moreover, binding affinity does not always equate to biological efficacy, as downstream signaling, off-target interactions, and pharmacokinetics play critical roles in modulating in vivo activity. Therefore, while docking provides preliminary insights into potential target interactions, experimental validation remains essential for accurate interpretation of pharmacological effects [54, 55].

  According to the current in-vitro investigation, FZ was cytotoxic to the treated cells. This result is consistent with previously published results that demonstrated the cytotoxicity of FZ to human cells, other cell lines, and the same culture of renal cells. According to C Leon, R Taylor, KH Bartlett and KM Wasan [32], FZ was cytotoxic to human PCT (HK-2) at 11 and 54 µM concentrations. It has been observed that even at 0.5 µM 48 h after exposure, FZ is cytotoxic to human monocytes. However, the human kidney (293T) cells employed in the same investigation and treated to the exact dosage simultaneously found this dose nontoxic [56]. Also, FZ was shown to be genotoxic to the HK-2 cells, with induction of oxidative damage and mitochondrial bioenergetic disruption in the treated cells. HP significantly alleviated FZ-induced cytotoxicity, genotoxicity, oxidative damage, mitochondrial disruption, and apoptosis in the treated cells. Using the “STITCH platform (http://stitch.embl.de/)” (last accessed 27 September 2024) [57], the authors sought to identify critical molecular partners and interacting proteins that could significantly mediate Amp-B ‘s cellular effects. These potential cellular players may mediate Amp-B ‘s toxic effects (Fig. 9A). MTT data showed that HP did not cause cytotoxicity at the tested concentrations and even increased cell viability, which was not the study’s aim. Thus, their intrinsic or proliferative bioactivity was not evaluated without FZ. The observed increase in cell viability shows HP may promote cell survival or proliferation. This study did not include cell proliferation assays, cell cycle analysis, or signaling pathways, which are needed to confirm these results. Mitophagy is the evolutionarily conserved pathway by which the cells get rid of their damaged, non-energizing mitochondria via lysosomal breakdown. Parkin and PINK1 proteins have been shown to play a significant role in activating mitophagy pathways [58]. Mitophagy eliminates damaged mitochondria, which can lead to increased release of ROS, activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, as well as triggering apoptotic pathways [59–62]. The current data revealed that FZ significantly decreased protein levels and coding gene expression. HP significantly counteracted this action. The current work did not evaluate downstream functional endpoints of mitophagy, such as mitochondrial clearance, lysosomal engagement, or mitochondrial shape and abundance, using transmission electron microscopy, mtDNA copy number, or mitotracker-based imaging. These parameters are recommended to be considered for future research. FZ was shown to induce oxidative damage in the treated human kidney cells. HP was shown to counteract FZ-induced oxidative stress in the treated cells significantly. Numerous diseases in people and experimental animal models have been linked to oxidative damage as the underlying mechanism [63, 64]. According to Chen et al. study [65], HP shields HK-2 cells against oxalic acid-induced oxidative damage. Also, it was shown that HP counteracted H2O2-induced oxidative damage and apoptosis in osteoblastic MC3T3-E1 cells [66]. Numerous mechanisms have been suggested as underlying mechanisms of HP antioxidant effects. JY Park, X Han, MJ Piao, MC Oh, PM Fernando, KA Kang, YS Ryu, U Jung, IG Kim and JW Hyun [16] claim HP induces heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) to protect cells from oxidative stress. According to HP’s utilization of phosphoinositide 3-kinase/AKT signaling pathways, human primary melanocytes are protected against oxidative damage by B Yang, Q Yang, X Yang, HB Yan and QP Lu [67]. The present study’s findings are in line with those of a prior investigation that demonstrated the flavonoid HP induces cytoprotection against oxidative stress by triggering the Keap1-Nrf2-ARE signaling pathway and elevating the expression of antioxidant enzymes, including CAT, heme HO-1, and SOD. In addition, it was suggested that in vitro human renal tubular cells and in vivo mice models of diabetic nephropathy, HP activates the extracellularly regulated protein kinases 1/2/mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling pathway. This may have a therapeutic benefit in managing diabetic kidney disease and lessen the pyroptosis in renal tubular epithelial cells caused by proteinuria [68]. Additionally, HP treatment significantly reduced the amount of ROS produced and reduced the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. It suppressed the NLRP3 inflammasome, mediated by ROS/MAPK/NF-κB signaling in another experimental model for cadmium-induced kidney injury [69]. According to the current investigation, HP considerably reduced the genotoxicity that FZ caused in the treated cells. In prior work, HP was demonstrated to be an antigenotoxic agent in human lymphocytes exposed to hydrogen peroxide and mitomycin C (MMC). It was shown that HP significantly reduced the chromosomal abnormalities, sister chromatid exchanges, and micronucleus frequencies caused by MMC and H2O2 (apart from MMC treatment). Comparing HP to the positive control, the mitotic index rose against both mutagenic agents during the 24-hour treatment period [70]. This may be directly related to HP protection against oxidative stress as oxidative stress was shown to be a primary trigger for genotoxicity with a protective effect of antioxidants to inhibit toxicant-induced genetic toxicity [71, 72]. The current data revealed that FZ significantly inhibited mitochondrial bioenergetics, while HP significantly counteracted this FZ adverse effect. This aligns with the previously published data. In HT22 neural cells, HP was demonstrated to lessen Aβ-induced mitochondrial dysfunction. Additionally, a previous study showed that in 661W photoreceptor cells, HP attenuates oxidative stress-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and cell death [73]. Combining these results, it is plausible that HP partially protects photoreceptors by reducing mitochondrial damage brought on by oxidative stress. Additionally, it was demonstrated that HP inhibits ischemia/reperfusion-induced oxidative stress and cell apoptosis by controlling the OMA1-OPA1 signaling pathway in an experimental model for ischemic acute kidney injury using both in vitro HK-2 cell line systems and in vivo Male C57BL/6 mice [74]. In addition, HP was reported to inhibit Amyloid β-protein (Aβ) 25–35-induced cytotoxicity and apoptosis in the primary cortical neuronal cell line by reversing Aβ-induced mitochondrial dysfunction, with counteracting the effect on MMP decrease, ROS production, and mitochondrial release of cytochrome c [75]. We examined apoptosis pathways and associated genes in our experiment to ascertain the apoptosis rates amongst the tested treated cell groups. The apoptosis rate increased considerably compared to when FZ was introduced in the control group. Apoptosis rates were considerably lower in the co-cultured group compared to the HP group. A family of related proteins that evolved through evolution is called Bcl-2. The mitochondrial apoptosis process depends on the Bcl-2 protein family. Both pro- and anti-apoptotic members of the Bcl-2 family existed in a balance in the living cells [76]. Apoptosis and caspase-3 activation were the outcomes of cytochrome c release and increased mitochondrial permeability caused by the reduction of Bcl-2/Bax. Bax, caspase-3, -8, and − 9 dramatically increased [61]. In this study, FZ therapy caused the activation of caspases with an increased Bax/BCL2 ratio, suggesting that FZ may raise apoptotic rates. Furthermore, following FZ treatment, Bcl-2 dramatically dropped compared to the negative control. Bcl-2 expression was considerably elevated in co-cultures with HP, suggesting that HP could shield the renal cells from FZ-induced apoptosis. During apoptosis, caspase-3 is cleaved and activated. The anti-apoptotic impact of HP on oxidative stress-induced apoptosis was demonstrated by the considerable inhibition of caspase-3 expression that FZ elicited in HK-2 cells. One of the proposed partners is the phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase-related kinase 1 (PEPKR1), which was shown to play a role in the control of gluconeogenesis in the liver cells, which couples the Krebs cycle with glycolysis [77]. Also, data revealed an interaction between HP and protein phosphatase 2A-2 (PP2A-2), which plays a role in the development, cell mobility, proliferation, apoptosis, cytoskeletal dynamics, cell cycle control, and the regulation of several cell signaling pathways [78]. The interaction with these molecular players, among others, supports the findings identified in the present study.   Interestingly, the current study evaluated the protective effect of HP against four underlying mechanisms suggested in FZ-induced cytotoxicity in HK-2 cells, including genotoxicity, mitochondrial disruption, and oxidative stress (Fig. 10). The four mechanisms overlap highly with each other. Oxidative stress and free radicals are expected to be the leading causes of DNA damage and genotoxicity [71, 72]. In parallel, free radicals can cause damage to the different cellular structures, leading to lipid peroxidation with damage to the mitochondria and DNA [63, 64]. On the other hand, mitochondrial affection with altered electron transport chain disruption are the primary sites for leakage of electrons, especially with inhibited MCI and MCIII, with the formation of ROS as superoxide anions, which then react with nitric oxide to produce the highly reactive peroxynitrites which were shown to play a significant role in oxidative damage and genotoxicity [59]. In addition, mitochondrial disruption causes leakage of cytochrome c to the cytoplasm and activation of the apoptotic pathways with induction of apoptosis [61]. In addition, oxidative stress was proven to activate the apoptosis pathways, which was applied as an underlying mechanism in cancer oxidation therapy [79].

  The current study evaluated the protective effect of HP-induced toxicity in HK-2 cell lines via different assays, supporting the conclusion that HP is cytoprotective for the human kidney cell line. However, the study has its limitations as being in vitro study, which may not resemble the normal in-vivo micro-environment interactions among the pathways. Secondly, not all pathways were investigated, which can be further assessed in subsequent studies. The study’s immortalized cell line may go through multiple in vitro cycles of dedifferentiation, which could affect the original tissues’ requirements and how they react to toxins. However, the study was based on passage 3 of the cell line, and the secondary cell line was used to get enough cells for the different assays with reproducible data. In addition, human cells were preferred to animal-isolated primary cell lines to overcome the interspecies difference effect of the assays and outcomes. Finally, further studies are recommended using in-vivo models and checking for further subcellular pathways to support the study findings. Long-term animal exposure preclinical investigations to assess systemic toxicity, histopathological alterations, and biochemical markers. These drugs’ safety window may be further clarified by dose-escalation studies and non-renal cell line testing. Moreover, future studies could benefit from employing advanced experimental systems such as kidney organoids or co-culture models that better recapitulate the renal microenvironment, allowing for more physiologically relevant assessments of nephrotoxicity and nephroprotection.

  Molecular docking represents a theoretical prediction; however, it does not account for changing protein conformations or solvent effects. Thus, biophysical approaches like SPR or ITC should be used to validate the experiment. In a more realistic biological environment, MD simulations are needed to study ligand-protein complex conformational stability and interaction dynamics. In silico data is not the exclusive basis of this work.


## Conclusions
  In conclusion, this study demonstrates that Hyperoside (HP) significantly mitigates Amphotericin B (FZ)-induced nephrotoxicity in HK-2 cells through multiple mechanisms, including attenuation of oxidative stress, preservation of mitochondrial function, reduction of genotoxicity, and suppression of apoptosis. These findings highlight the potential of HP as a promising nephroprotective agent against FZ-induced renal injury.

  Future studies should aim to validate these protective effects in vivo and explore the pharmacokinetics, bioavailability, and safety profile of HP in preclinical models. Additionally, mechanistic studies using organoid or co-culture systems may provide deeper insights into HP’s mode of action in a more physiologically relevant context. Ultimately, HP may be considered as a potential adjuvant therapy to reduce Amphotericin B-associated nephrotoxicity in clinical settings, pending further translational and clinical investigations.
