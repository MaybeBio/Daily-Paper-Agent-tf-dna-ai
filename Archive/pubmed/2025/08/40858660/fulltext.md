# Capilliposide A attenuates diabetic nephropathy via modulation of NF-κB/TLR4 and apoptotic pathways


## Abstract
  This study aimed to identify therapeutic targets and signaling pathways of Capilliposide A (LC-A) for diabetic nephropathy (DN) through integrated network pharmacology and experimental validation. Using a DN mouse model induced by high-fat diet (HFD) and streptozotocin (STZ), we investigated the molecular mechanisms of LC-A. The PubChem database provided the two-dimensional structure of LC-A, with subsequent identification of LC-A/DN-associated targets through intersection analysis. Core targets were determined via protein–protein interaction (PPI) network construction, followed by gene ontology enrichment and pathway analyses. Molecular docking simulations evaluated LC-A's binding affinity to DN-related targets. C57BL/6 mice receiving HFD and STZ intraperitoneal injections were treated daily with LC-A or metformin (MET) via oral gavage for 8 weeks. Weekly monitoring included body weight and blood glucose measurements. Post-treatment assessments encompassed serum lipid profiles, renal function biomarkers, inflammatory cytokines, oxidative stress markers, and renal histopathology. Western blotting, immunohistochemistry, and immunofluorescence were performed to analyze inflammatory pathway proteins and apoptosis-related factors. Network pharmacology identified TLR4, NF-κB1, STAT3, EGFR, mTOR, and MAPK as core targets involved in inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis regulation. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed critical inflammatory pathways including IL-17, TNF, and Th17 cell differentiation. Molecular docking confirmed stable binding of LC-A to TLR4, NF-κB1, Bax, and Bcl-2. In vivo experiments demonstrated that LC-A significantly reduced renal injury markers (KI, SCR, BUN), lipid profiles (TG, TC), and oxidative stress (MDA), while enhancing antioxidant enzymes (SOD, GPX, CAT) in DN mice (P < 0.05). LC-A effectively suppressed serum inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, MCP-1, iNOS) through NF-κB pathway modulation and apoptosis regulation (P < 0.05). Histopathological analysis revealed attenuated renal cortical vacuolization, tubular swelling, glomerular mesangial expansion, sclerosis, and inflammatory infiltration. These findings suggest LC-A as a promising natural nephroprotective agent for DN prevention and treatment.


## Introduction
  Diabetes mellitus (DM), a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by persistent hyperglycemia, induces systemic damage to vital organs including the heart1, vasculature2, eyes3, and kidneys4 through prolonged glycemic exposure. DN, a microvascular complication of DM, represents the predominant etiology of end-stage renal disease (ESRD)5. The escalating global prevalence of DN has imposed substantial socioeconomic burdens, with mortality rates demonstrating an upward trajectory in recent epidemiological studies. Current projections estimate that 783.2 million individuals will be diagnosed with diabetes by 20456, of whom 25–40% with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) and 5–40% with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) are expected to develop DN7. China harbors the world’s largest diabetic population (> 100 million), with 21.3% progressing to DN8, paralleling the 30% incidence observed in the United States9. Notably, 15% of untreated diabetic patients progress to ESRD within 20 years post-diagnosis10. The incomplete elucidation of DN pathogenesis underscores the imperative for mechanistic investigations to inform therapeutic development.

  Emerging evidence implicates inflammatory cascades as central mediators of DN progression11, particularly through dysregulation of innate immunity12. Toll-like receptors (TLRs), evolutionarily conserved pattern recognition receptors, orchestrate pro-inflammatory signaling via microbial pathogen detection. Intriguingly, TLRs also respond to endogenous ligands under sterile conditions, driving non-infectious inflammation13. Among TLR subtypes, TLR4 emerges as a pivotal regulator of inflammatory pathways14. Hyperglycemia and angiotensin II synergistically upregulate renal TLR4 expression15, subsequently activating nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signaling and enhancing secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, MCP-1)16. These findings posit immune-mediated inflammation as a therapeutic target for DN intervention.

  Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) demonstrates distinct pharmacodynamic advantages in nephropathy management, validated through centuries of clinical practice17. Lysimachia capillipes Hemsl., indigenous to southern China, has been traditionally employed in treating rheumatism, gynecological disorders, and respiratory conditions using its whole-plant preparations18. Phytochemical analyses identify saponins and flavonoids as its principal bioactive constituents19,20, with the triterpenoid saponin Capilliposide A (LC-A, Fig. 1) exhibiting remarkable anti-inflammatory efficacy21. Alkaline hydrolysis yields LC-A as the dominant sapogenin22, demonstrating therapeutic potential in acute kidney injury23, ulcerative colitis24, and macular degeneration25, primarily through modulation of oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptotic pathways23. Despite these advances, LC-A's pharmacological role in DN remains unexplored. To address this gap, we integrate network pharmacology leveraging multi-omics datasets with molecular docking simulations and in vivo validation, establishing a systems-level framework for deciphering LC-A's anti-DN mechanisms and advancing precision therapeutics.Fig. 1The structure of LC-A 2D (left) and 3D (right).


## Materials and methods

The adsorbed LC-B and LC-C were hydrolysed into LC-A by adding NaOH solution. The hydrolysis conditions were also optimized by response surface methodology (RSM). LC-A adsorbed in the resin was purified by simple ethanol elution and then characterized. #### Screening of LC-A and DN-related targets
  The SMILES, InChI, and SDF structures of LC-A were obtained from PubChem. The structural data were analyzed using SwissADME to assess drug-likeness, and LC-A meeting activity thresholds was selected as the active component. Target prediction was performed via SuperPred, PharmMapper, SwissTargetPrediction, and TargetNet. Identified targets were standardized and deduplicated using UniProt to finalize LC-A’s potential targets. DN-related targets were retrieved from GeneCards, CTD, and OpenTargets using“diabetic nephropathy”as the keyword. Duplicates were removed, and a Venn diagram (R v4.2.1; VennDiagram package v1.7.3) was generated to identify overlapping targets between LC-A and DN. Common targets were defined as LC-A’s potential therapeutic targets for DN. A LC-A-Target-DN interaction network was constructed in Cytoscape (v3.9.1), where nodes represent LC-A or targets and edges denote interactions. Topological properties, including node degree values, were analyzed using the Network Analyzer plugin26.


#### Construction of Protein–Protein Interaction (PPI) network
  A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using the STRING database. Overlapping targets of LC-A and DN were analyzed in STRING with species restricted to Homo sapiens and a confidence threshold ≥ 0.4. Interaction data were exported as TSV/PNG files and imported into Cytoscape. Isolated nodes were removed, and network topology (degree, closeness centrality [CC], betweenness centrality [BC]) was calculated. Core targets were identified using the cytohubba plugin’s Maximal Clique Centrality (MCC) algorithm26.


#### Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses
  Potential therapeutic targets of LC-A for DN were analyzed via Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment27 using the R package clusterProfiler (v4.6.2). GO terms and KEGG pathways with P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The top 20 GO terms (biological processes, molecular functions, cellular components) and top 30 KEGG pathways were visualized as bar plots and bubble plots, respectively26.


#### Molecular docking
  The core proteins in the PPI network can be downloaded from the protein structure database. PyMol software was used to remove water molecules and ligands. AutoDock Vina software was used for hydrogenation and save the molecular structures as PDBQT files. AutoDock Vina was also used to dock the receptors with small molecule ligand Capilliposide A, and the structure with the lowest binding energy was selected as the optimal structure. The docking results were visualized with PyMol software.


### Animal
  Male C57BL/6 J mice, aged 6 to 8 weeks and weighing 15–20 g, were obtained from the Institute of Animal Experimental Ethics, Institute of Basic Medicine and Oncology, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility at 25 ± 1 °C with a 12-h light/dark cycle and provided standard food and water ad libitum. After a one-week acclimatization period, mice were fed either a normal diet (NCD, 10% energy from fat) or a high-fat diet (HFD, 60% energy from fat, 20% energy from protein; 20% energy from carbohydrate; Jiangsu Xietong Pharmaceutical Bio-engineering Co., Ltd., China) for four weeks. Diabetes was induced by a single intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (STZ, 100 mg/kg body weight, dissolved in 0.1 M citrate buffer) one month after initiating the high-fat diet, based on previous research. Random blood glucose (RBG) levels were measured 72 h post-injection, and mice with RBG levels ≥ 16.7 mmol/L were classified as diabetic28. After an additional four weeks of regular feeding, the eight-week treatment period commenced. Diabetic mice were randomly divided into four groups and administered metformin (MET, 100 mg/kg/day), low-dose LC-A (LC-A-L, 60 mg/kg/day), or high-dose LC-A (LC-A-H, 120 mg/kg/day). During treatment, 0.5% Carboxymethyl Cellulose Sodium (CMC-Na) solution was diluted to the appropriate concentration and used immediately, while the model group received the same volume of 0.5% CMC-Na. At the end of the experiment, blood and kidney samples were collected for analysis. ### Calculation of blood glucose, body weight and kidney index
  After the final administration, mice were fasted for 12 h and weighed. Euthanasia was performed, and kidneys were excised and rinsed with normal saline. Excess surface moisture was removed using filter paper, and kidney weights were recorded. The renal organ coefficient was calculated as: renal index = weight of the left kidney (mg)/body weight (g). Random blood glucose levels were measured weekly using a portable blood glucose meter (Roche ACCU-CHEK, USA), and body weights were recorded weekly28.


### SCR and BUN testing
  Blood was collected from the unilateral eyeball of the mouse and allowed to clot at room temperature for 2 h. Serum was obtained by centrifugation (1000 g, 10 min, 4 °C). Serum levels of SCR and BUN were measured according to the kit instructions. Hemolyzed samples were excluded from the study.


### Inflammatory factors testing
  To detect the levels of IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, MCP-1 and iNOS in serum according to the instructions of the kit29.


### SOD, GPX, CAT and MDA testing
  The kidney was immediately isolated and stored at −80 °C. For analysis, it was minced and homogenized in nine volumes of precooled saline. After centrifugation (1000 g, 4 °C, 10 min), the supernatant was collected and stored at −20 °C. The levels of SOD, GPX, CAT, and MDA in the kidney tissue were measured according to the kit instructions30.


### Histological examination
  Kidney tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 µm thickness. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE; Solarbio Sci&Tech Co., G1120, China), Masson trichrome (Servicebio, G1006, China), and periodic acid-Schiff (PAS; Servicebio, G1008, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Images were captured using a KFBIO digital pathology scanner. All evaluations were performed in a blinded manner by a trained and blinded experimenter. Abnormalities were graded by a semiquantitative score from 0 to 4 + : 0, no abnormalities; 1 +, changes affecting less than 25% of the sample; 2 +, changes affecting 25–50%; 3 +, changes affecting 50–75%; 4 +, changes affecting more than 75%23.


### Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis
  Kidney tissue was fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C for 2 h, followed by dehydration, embedding, and sectioning. For immunohistochemistry, sections were dewaxed, and antigen retrieval was performed in citric acid buffer (pH 6.0)31. The Polymer-HRP Anti-Rabbit Secondary Antibody Kit (Aifang Biology, China) protocol was followed. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies against TLR4, Bax, and Bcl2 (1:200 dilution). Images were captured using a KFBIO digital pathology scanner.


### Immunofluorescence (IF) analysis
  Paraffin-embedded renal tissue Sects. (4 μm) were dewaxed, hydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. Sections were blocked with 3% H2O2 and 3% BSA. Multiple immunofluorescence staining for Bax and Bcl2 was performed using the Double-Label Trichromatic Multiple Fluorescence Staining Kit (Enhanced; Aifang Biology, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions32. Images were acquired using a KFBIO digital pathology scanner.


### WB
  Frozen kidney tissue was minced, homogenized, and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 5 min at 4 °C to collect the supernatant. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit. A 10% separation gel was prepared, and proteins were separated by electrophoresis, followed by transfer to a PVDF membrane. The membrane was blocked at 37 °C for 90 min and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. After three 10-min washes with TBST, the membrane was incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit or goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibodies (1:5000) at 37 °C for 1 h31. β-Actin antibody (1:2000) was used as a loading control. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) and analyzed with ImageJ software.


### Statistical analysis
  Data are expressed as mean ± SD. Comparisons between groups were made by unpaired Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using GraphPad Prism 9 software. P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001).


## Results

### Construction and analysis of the LC-A-target-DN network
  The two-dimensional structure of LC-A was retrieved from PubChem, with its SMILES formula acquired. Potential targets of LC-A were predicted using TargetNet, PharmMapper, SuperPred, and SwissTargetPrediction. After deduplication, 202 targets were identified (Fig. 3.a). DN-related targets were extracted from GeneCards, Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD), and Open Targets, yielding 1,157 unique disease targets(Fig. 3.b). Intersection analysis revealed 65 overlapping targets as potential therapeutic candidates for LC-A in DN (Fig. 3.c). The LC-A-Target-DN network consists of 67 nodes (LC-A and 66 targets) and 130 edges, indicating interactions between LC-A and its targets (Fig. 3.d).   To evaluate the renoprotective effects of LC-A in diabetic kidney injury, renal histopathology was analyzed using H&E, PAS, and Masson staining (Fig. 7.i). In controls, H&E staining showed intact glomeruli with Bowman’s capsules and normal luminal dimensions, surrounded by proximal/distal convoluted tubules. ## Discussion
  DN, a microvascular complication of diabetes, can progress to ESRD if untreated33. Its progression is characterized by persistent proteinuria and a significant decline in the glomerular filtration rate (GFR)5. Without early intervention, renal function deterioration in DM becomes irreversible, often requiring dialysis or kidney transplantation34. Although novel therapies effectively manage blood pressure and glycemia, reducing proteinuria and GFR decline, DN remains the leading cause of ESRD worldwide35. Currently, understanding the pathogenesis of DN and developing personalized treatment strategies are key to mitigating DN risks. Natural anti-inflammatory compounds offer a safe and promising approach for managing inflammatory conditions36. Previous studies have shown that LC-A exhibits anti-inflammatory, anti-tumor, and other pharmacological properties23,37,38. However, its potential to inhibit renal inflammation in diabetes remains unverified. To address this, we investigated the anti-inflammatory effects of LC-A in a DN model.

  DN is accompanied by significant renal inflammation. To explore the mechanisms underlying LC-A’s anti-inflammatory action, we examined its impact on the NF-κB pathway, a key regulator of inflammation and a therapeutic target for various inflammatory diseases39. Analysis of inflammation-related proteins in mouse kidneys revealed that LC-A modulated the expression of NF-κB p65, IκBα, Iκκα, Iκκβ, and IL-6, thereby suppressing renal inflammation and providing renal protection. Previous studies have identified NF-κB as a key transcription factor in the renal inflammatory response of progressive DN patients40. Under hyperglycemic conditions, NF-κB activation promotes the synthesis of adhesion molecules, chemokines, and inflammatory cytokines. This activation is triggered by the degradation of inhibitory IκB proteins, leading to NF-κB release, phosphorylation, and subsequent target gene tranSCription through nuclear translocation and DNA binding41. Our findings suggest that LC-A’s inhibition of inflammatory mediators and cytokines is mediated, at least in part, by suppressing the NF-κB signaling pathway. TLR4 plays a critical role in the adaptive immune response42, and its activation triggers inflammation-related signaling cascades, leading to the transcription of pro-inflammatory factors and chemokines that exacerbate renal dysfunction in acute and chronic kidney diseases36. Upon activation, TLR4 signals through the adaptor molecule MyD88, activating NF-κB and upregulating pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines15, which promote local inflammation and leukocyte accumulation. These mediators are implicated in the pathophysiology of DN. Recent studies have shown that TLR4 deficiency reduces the pro-inflammatory state in STZ-induced diabetic wild-type mice, thereby protecting renal function43. Thus, TLR4 is likely involved in the pathogenesis of DN. In this study, immunohistochemical analysis revealed elevated TLR4 expression on renal tubular cell membranes in the DM group, and Western blot confirmed a significant increase in TLR4 protein levels. LC-A treatment significantly reduced TLR4 expression in renal tissue. Using a T2DM mice model, we demonstrated that LC-A reduced inflammatory cell infiltration, decreased inflammatory cytokine production, and inhibited the NF-κB/TLR4 pathway. These findings suggest that LC-A is a promising therapeutic candidate for DN and other inflammatory disorders. Additionally, evidence links basement membrane thickening, mesangial cell proliferation, and abnormal endothelial permeability to IL-6 expression44,45. In our study, IL-6 expression was significantly higher in the DN group compared to controls, and LC-A markedly downregulated IL-6 protein levels. In summary, LC-A alleviates renal inflammation in DN by mitigating the inflammatory response.

  The Bcl-2 protein family includes anti-apoptotic members (e.g., Bcl-2, Bcl-xL, and p-Bad) and pro-apoptotic members (e.g., Bad and Bax), which are key regulators of mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis46. The Bcl-2/Bax ratio is a critical determinant of apoptotic resistance47. Caspases, a family of cysteine proteases, regulate apoptosis upon activation. Caspase-3, a downstream effector and prominent caspase family member, activates Bax, leading to cytochrome c release and caspase-9 activation48. The interaction among caspase-9, caspase-3, caspase-6, and caspase-7 activates these caspases, ultimately inducing apoptosis49. Vimentin, a major cytoskeletal protein expressed in mesenchymal and some ectodermal cells, is a marker of epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT)50. It plays roles in maintaining cell shape, nuclear positioning, cell adhesion, migration, and signal transduction51. Previous studies have demonstrated a strong association between vimentin and apoptosis induced by various factors52. In the early stages of apoptosis, vimentin is fragmented into varying sizes, with these cleavage products serving as markers of caspase-mediated apoptosis. Structural disruption of vimentin generates N-terminal peptides that further enhance caspase-induced vimentin cleavage, amplifying the apoptotic signal53. Our findings show that LC-A protects against STZ-induced renal apoptosis by downregulating the expression of Bax/Bcl-2, Bad, vimentin, caspase-3, and cleaved-caspase-3.

  Oxidative stress, driven by inflammatory cells stimulating free radical production, is a key factor in vascular complications of metabolic disorders, including diabetes54. In hyperglycemic conditions, increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reduced endogenous antioxidant capacity are the primary drivers of elevated oxidative stress in diabetic nephropathy55,56. SOD, GPX, CAT, and MDA are key indicators of oxidative stress. SOD, a critical antioxidant metalloenzyme, indirectly reflects the body’s ability to scavenge oxygen free radicals57. GPX, a key peroxidase, converts peroxides into harmless hydroxyl compounds, protecting against oxidative damage58. CAT reduces oxidative stress by catalytically decomposing H2O259, thereby lowering hydroxyl radical levels. MDA content reflects the extent of lipid peroxidation and oxidative stress in hyperglycemic conditions60. Previous results showed elevated MDA activity and reduced SOD, GPX, and CAT levels in DN mice61,62. ROS is a driver of oxidative stress, and oxidative stress markers are evidence of the molecular damage it causes. LC-A significantly inhibited the lipid peroxidation marker MDA and restored the activities of antioxidant enzymes SOD, CAT, and GPX. The specific mechanism of occurrence is that ROS promote NF-κB nuclear translocation through activation of Iκκβ phosphorylation, which drives the release of pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF-α, IL-6, etc. LC-A blocks this positive feedback loop by inhibiting the source of ROS. In addition, immunoblotting showed that LC-A inhibited the pro-apoptotic protein Bax and up-regulated the anti-apoptotic Bcl-2. ROS induced mitochondrial membrane permeability transition, leading to the release of cytochrome C and activation of the caspase cascade reaction, which was blocked by LC-A through the scavenging of ROS. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that “NF-κB signaling pathway” and “apoptosis-multi-species regulation” were the most notable inhibited pathways, and STRING database constructed the key protein interactions network, which showed that NOX4, NF-κB, TLR4 and CAS3 were the core nodes. In conclusion, LC-A exerts an anti-DN effect by blocking ROS and inhibiting the NF-κB pathway, thereby reducing the production of inflammatory cytokines and the focal death and apoptosis cascades.

  Our study still has deficiencies: (1) serum cytokines may not reflect renal inflammation, and spatial transcriptomics for mapping local inflammation in the future. (2) Rodent DN models cannot fully reproduce human disease, and human kidney organ tissue and clinical biopsy analysis should be performed in the future. (3) The current study lacks pharmacokinetic data to directly quantify systemic exposure to intact LC-A. Plasma Cmax and AUC of intact LC-A and major metabolites should be measured in future work, and metabolomics should guide dosing for pharmacokinetic optimization. (4) This study used an advanced DN model and future studies are needed to validate the metabolic activity of LC-A in early diabetes models. (5) There is a lack of long-term toxicity data under Good Laboratory Practice standards, and future studies are needed to conduct pharmacotoxicology studies.


## Conclusion
  This study reveals the multi-target and multi-pathway mechanisms underlying LC-A in the treatment of DN (Fig. 12).
