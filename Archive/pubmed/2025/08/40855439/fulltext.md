# Senescent fibroblasts secrete CTHRC1 to promote cancer stemness in hepatocellular carcinoma


## Background
  Cellular senescence plays a significant role in tumorigenesis and tumor progression. Substantial evidence indicates that senescence occurs in cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), the predominant stromal component within the tumor microenvironment (TME), which profoundly impacts tumor biology. However, despite growing evidence of stromal cell involvement in cancer progression, the specific mechanisms and clinical implications of senescent CAFs (SCAFs) in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) have not been fully elucidated.


## Methods
  The senescence signature was utilized to evaluate the senescence status of cell types within the TME of HCC using the GSE149614 dataset. The CytoTRACE and cell-cell communication analysis were used to find the correlation between cancer stemness and SCAFs. A risk prediction model associated with SCAFs was constructed to investigate potential mechanisms by which SCAFs promote tumor progression. Single-cell RNA sequencing data was used to identify senescent CAF-related genes. Gene expression and clinical data for HCC were obtained from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), International Cancer Genome Consortium (ICGC), and National Omics Data Encyclopedia (NODE) databases. Using four machine-learning algorithms, crucial genes were identified to develop a CAF-senescence-related risk model, predicting prognosis, cancer stemness, immune infiltration, tumor mutation burden, and therapeutic responses in HCC patients. Next, we explored the role of Collagen Triple Helix Repeat Containing-1 (CTHRC1) in cancer stemness using both in vitro and in vivo experiments. Through various functional experiments, we elucidated the downstream signaling pathways of CTHRC1. Additionally, chromatin immunoprecipitation experiments were used to verify that key transcription factors bind to the CTHRC1 promoter region.


## Results
  CAFs exhibited high senescence status and a strong correlation with cancer stemness in HCC. A novel CAF-senescence-score (CSscore) prognostic model was established for HCC based on 10 genes: CTHRC1, SERPINE1, RNF11, ENG, MARCKSL1, ASAP1, FHL3, LAMB1, CD151, and OLFML2B. The survival prediction performance was validated on TCGA, ICGC, and NODE cohorts. Immune analysis revealed that the CSscore was positively correlated with immunosuppressive immune cell populations, including M2 macrophages and regulatory T cells. Conversely, a negative correlation was observed between the CSscore and anti-tumor immune cells such as CD8 + T cells, dendritic cells, and B cells HCC patients with a low CSscore had a lower tumor mutation burden and showed improved responsiveness to immunotherapy and transarterial chemoembolization. In vitro experiments and bioinformatics analysis further revealed that CTHRC1 was significantly elevated in SCAFs promoted cancer stemness and metastasis via the SRY-box transcription factor 4 (SOX4)-CTHRC1-Notch1 axis in HCC.


## Conclusion
  Our study revealed that SCAFs were strongly correlated with cancer stemness in HCC. A novel machine learning model based on senescent CAF-related genes was constructed to accurately predict prognosis in HCC patients. Furthermore, CTHRC1 was identified as a novel prognostic and therapeutic biomarker to predict poor prognosis in HCC and promote cancer stemness and metastasis through the Notch signaling pathway, with its expression being transcriptionally regulated by SOX4.

## Introduction
  Liver cancer is the third most common cause of cancer-related death globally [1], with hepatocellular carcinoma accounting for 90% of primary liver cancers [2], and its early diagnosis and effective treatment remain major health challenges worldwide [3]. Many studies suggest that liver cancer stem cells (CSCs) are key drivers of HCC progression due to their tumorigenic, self-renewal, metastatic, and chemotherapy-resistant properties [4]. Further research is crucial to understand the molecular mechanisms underlying cancer stemness in HCC and to develop targeted therapies that could effectively eradicate these aggressive cancer cells and enhance patient outcomes.

  Cellular senescence has been acknowledged as a ‘hallmark of cancer’, and plays a crucial role in modulating tumor biology [5]. This process denotes an irreversible state of cell-cycle arrest induced by stressors such as telomere shortening, oncogene activation, oxidative stress, and DNA damage [6, 7]. Senescent cells undergo numerous phenotypic alterations, including metabolic reprogramming, morphological changes, and chromatin reorganization. Additionally, they acquire a proinflammatory phenotype known as the senescence-associated secretory phenotypes (SASPs), characterized by the release of diverse bioactive molecules into the extracellular milieu [8, 9]. Emerging evidence suggests that cellular senescence within the tumor microenvironment plays a critical role in tumor progression [10, 11]. Therefore, eliminating or targeting tumor-promoting senescent cells represents a promising strategy for cancer treatment.

  The TME is a critical determinant of tumorigenesis and cancer progression in HCC. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), prevalent stromal cells in the TME, are a primary source of the extracellular matrix (ECM) that forms the desmoplastic stroma, significantly impacting tumor progression and metastasis [12]. Approximately 80% of HCC cases are associated with hepatic fibrosis or cirrhosis [13], indicating the critical role of CAF accumulation and fibrotic changes within the TME in HCC development and progression. Recent research indicates that cellular senescence in stromal cells, especially CAFs, significantly influences tumor initiation and progression [14]. Senescent CAFs (SCAFs) enhance cancer stemness by suppressing the AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) pathway in esophageal cancer [15] and facilitate tumor growth in breast cancer by supplying essential metabolites, such as aspartate and proline [16]. Significantly, SCAFs have been widely reported to contribute to therapy resistance by secreting cytokines and ECM components, which promote cancer recurrence and metastasis [17, 18]. Given the ubiquitous presence of CAFs throughout the TME, elucidating the distinct functional contributions of SCAFs to HCC biology represents a critical imperative for comprehensively understanding tumor progression mechanisms and identifying novel therapeutic intervention strategies.

  In this study, we systematically characterized senescence profiles across cellular populations within the complex TME of HCC. Notably, CAFs showed the highest senescence scores in HCC. Senescence-associated CAF genes were identified and 10 significant genes with significant prognostic relevance for HCC patients were selected using four machine-learning algorithms. These 10 genes were used to establish a novel prognostic model, which was comprehensively analyzed for its relationships with prognosis, cancer stemness, immune infiltration, tumor mutation burden, and therapeutic response in HCC. This study demonstrated that SCAF-derived Collagen Triple Helix Repeat Containing-1 (CTHRC1) promotes cancer stemness and metastasis both in vitro and in vivo, supporting the role of the CTHRC1-Notch1 axis in HCC stromal-tumor interactions. Collectively, our findings highlight the prognostic significance and oncogenic mechanisms of SCAFs, providing novel insights into how senescent CAFs facilitate HCC progression and identifying potential therapeutic targets.


## Materials and methods

### Data collection and processing
  The study utilized sequencing data sourced from public databases. Data from the TCGA-LIHC project, including transcriptomic counts, Transcripts per Million (TPM), masked somatic mutations, copy number variations, and clinicopathological details, were acquired from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). The validation dataset was sourced from the Liver Cancer-RIKEN, JP Project, part of the International Cancer Genome Consortium (ICGC LIRI-JP). The OEP000321 CHCC cohort, sourced from the National Omics Data Encyclopedia (NODE) repository (https://www.biosino.org/node/), served as another validation dataset. Transcriptomics datasets (GSE104580, GSE91061, and GSE144269) and the scRNA-seq dataset (GSE149614) were sourced from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), and the IMvigor210 dataset was accessed from http://researchpub.gene.com/IMvigor210CoreBiologies. Additional RNA-sequencing data were obtained from HCCDB v2.0 (http://lifeome.net/database/hccdb2). Proteomics data for liver cancer were acquired from the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) database (https://pdc.cancer.gov/pdc/). Microarray data underwent normalization, background correction, and log2 transformation using the “affy” R package [19]. For prognosis modeling, only samples with an overall survival (OS) exceeding 30 days were kept excluding cases with non-neoplastic causes of death.

  The “Seurat V5” R package [20] was used to analyze the scRNA-seq dataset. Initial quality control criteria included selecting genes expressed in a minimum of five cells and cells expressing more than 300 genes. The percentage of ribosomal RNA and mitochondrial transcripts was calculated for each cell, with those containing greater than 15% mitochondrial gene content excluded from further analysis. Following these quality control procedures, approximately 33,202 high-quality cells were retained for subsequent investigations.


### Cell-cell communication analysis
  Cell-cell interaction networks within the TME were quantitatively characterized using the “CellChat” R package [21]. Default parameters recommended by the developers were implemented to calculate both the frequency and strength of intercellular communications across all identified cell populations.


### Identification of senescent CAF-related genes
  In the TCGA-LIHC dataset, we employed the “DESeq2” R package [22] to perform differential expression analysis between tumor and normal tissues (|logFC| >0.5 and p < 0.05), identifying differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Distinct cell populations in the scRNA-seq dataset were annotated using canonical cell markers. For the analysis of upregulated genes in CAFs, we implemented the ‘FindAllMarkers’ function in the “Seurat” R package. CAF-upregulated genes were identified using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with a significance level of p < 0.05, while other parameters remained at their default values. The senescence status of individual cells was quantified using the ‘AddModuleScore’ function with the FRIDMAN_SENESCENCE_UP signature from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) [23, 24]. Cellular senescence status was validated using senescence signatures from the CellAge database [25]. CAFs were categorized into high- and low-senescence subpopulations by comparing their senescence scores to the median value. Subsequently, we performed differential expression analysis between these senescence subpopulations using the ‘FindMarkers’ function in Seurat. SCAF-upregulated genes were identified using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with a significance threshold of p < 0.05, while other parameters remained at their default values. Through this analysis, 127 genes were identified and used to construct a gene set named Senescent CAF-Related Genes (SCRGs). The prognostic significance of the identified SCRGs was initially assessed through univariate Cox regression analysis. We employed a multi-algorithmic strategy integrating Random Forest (RF), eXtreme Gradient Boosting survival (XGBoost), Gradient Boosting Machines (GBM), and Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression to robustly identify key genes associated with SCAFs.

  Patients were stratified into two groups according to the median risk score, with those having higher scores categorized as the high-risk group. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed using the “survminer” R package to evaluate survival disparities between risk groups. The prognostic model’s sensitivity and specificity were evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and their corresponding area under the curve (AUC) values. Prognostic nomograms were developed utilizing univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses. A comprehensive nomogram was constructed using the “rms” R package [26] to visualize the CSscore’s relative contribution to the model and its impact on survival prediction. The model’s predictive performance was evaluated through calibration curves and decision curve analysis (DCA) [27]. An independent external dataset was utilized to demonstrate the robustness of the CSscore.


### Functional annotation and enrichment analysis
  Functional and pathway enrichment analysis of candidate genes were performed by the “ClusterProfiler” package [28] and the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) software [29], utilizing gene sets retrieved from the Gene Ontology (GO), the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and the MSigDB database.


### Immune infiltration analysis
  Our investigation into immune cell infiltration patterns and their association with the CSscore in HCC samples applied several methods, including single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA), Tumor Immune Estimation Resource (TIMER), Cell-type Identification by Estimating Relative Subpopulations of RNA Transcripts Absolute (CIBERSORT-ABS), Quantitative Analysis of Immune Signatures in Tumors (QUANTISEQ), Microenvironment Cell Populations-counter (MCP-counter), xCELL, Estimation of Proportion of Immune and Cancer cells (EPIC), and Tracking Tumor Immunophenotype (TIP). Additionally, we utilized the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) web tool to evaluate the association between SCAFs and several immune features [30]. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was employed to quantify the relationship between individual immune cell populations and the CSscore. The TIP algorithm was applied to assess immune activity across the seven-step Cancer-Immunity Cycle [31].


### Evaluation of association between signature and tumor mutational burden, efficacy of transarterial chemoembolization and immunotherapy
  Tumor mutational burden (TMB) was calculated for each HCC specimen utilizing the “maftools” R package [32]. The nearest template prediction (NTP) algorithm was implemented to compare expression profiles of risk-stratified groups to predict responsiveness to transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) therapy [33]. Given the observed differential immune infiltration profiles between risk stratification groups, immunotherapeutic response prediction was performed using computational approaches. To predict immunotherapy response, we employed two independent cohorts: the IMvigor210 cohort [34], which includes advanced urothelial carcinoma patients treated with anti-PD-L1 therapy, and the GSE91061 cohort [35], comprising melanoma patients who received anti-PD-1 therapy.


### Human HCC samples
  Human primary liver cancer tissue samples were collected from patients undergoing liver resection at the Hepatic Surgery Center of Tongji Hospital with informed consent. Immunohistochemical (IHC) analyses were conducted using tissue microarrays containing 54 formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded samples. Additionally, two specimens of hepatic hemangioma were acquired for comparative analyses. All human tissue specimens were procured and utilized in accordance with protocols that obtained ethical clearance from the Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital, Affiliated with Huazhong University of Science and Technology (HUST) in Wuhan, China (IRB ID: TJ-IRB202503091).


### Isolation and purification of CAFs and normal fibroblasts
  Primary CAFs were isolated from clinical HCC specimens, while NFs were obtained from liver tissues adjacent to hepatic hemangioma. Fresh liver tissue samples were meticulously washed with Dulbecco’s Hank’s balanced salt solution, necrotic tissues were excised, and samples were precisely dissected into approximately 2 mm fragments. These fragments were placed in culture vessels with DMEM (GIBCO) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) to promote cell attachment. After approximately 14 days, fibroblasts migrated from tissue fragments. Fibroblast populations with 95% purity were achieved after 2–3 passages and characterized via immunofluorescence analysis using established markers α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and fibroblast activation protein (FAP).


### Cellular senescence induction
  We followed modified protocols of cellular senescence induction according to previously published protocols [36–38]. CAFs were initially seeded at a density of 5 × 105 cells per 10-cm culture and permitted to adhere for 24 h. For hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)-induced senescence, cells were treated with 400 µM H2O2 for 2 h on days 1, 4, and 7, followed by three PBS washes and fresh culture medium replacement. For chemotherapy-induced senescence, cells with 200 nM cisplatin (Selleck, cat. no. S1166) for 3 days. Cells were subcultured at a 1:2 ratio when cell density exceeded 90%.


### Preparation of conditioned medium
  Cells were plated in 6-well culture plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well. After a 24-hour attachment period, cells were washed thrice with PBS, and 2 mL of serum-free DMEM was added to each well. Following a 24-hour incubation at 37 °C, the conditioned medium (CM) was collected and passed through 0.22 μm membrane filters to eliminate cellular debris before being preserved at −80 °C for subsequent experiments.


### Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
  CM supernatants from CAFs, SCAFs, CTHRC1-depleted SCAFs, and CTHRC1-overexpressing CAFs were collected and centrifuged at 1,500 rpm for 10 min. CTHRC1 secretion levels were then measured using an ELISA kit (SEN690Hu, Cloud-Clone Corp.) following the manufacturer’s instructions.


### Cell culture and treatment
  The SNU-398 cell line was sourced from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) and cultured in DMEM (GIBCO) supplemented with 10% FBS. The MHCC-97 H hepatocellular carcinoma and HEK-293T human embryonic kidney cell lines were obtained from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Cell Bank in Shanghai, China, and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. CAFs and NFs were maintained in DMEM (GIBCO) supplemented with 10% FBS (GIBCO). Cells were consistently maintained at 37 °C in a humidified environment with 5% CO2. However, it is important to note that all cell lines tested positive for Mycoplasma contamination using the MycoBlue Mycoplasma Detector (Vazyme). The recombinant human CTHRC1 protein (rhCTHRC1) was purchased from MCE (HY-P70092). Prior to experimental manipulation, MHCC-97 H and SNU-398 HCC cell lines were cultured in fresh DMEM with 10% FBS for 24 h before CM treatment. Subsequently, cells were treated with a 1:1 mixture of fresh medium and CAF-derived CM or fresh DMEM supplemented with rhCTHRC1 (20 ng/ml) for 48 h during the experimental procedures. To comprehensively evaluate cellular responses, various functional assays were conducted, such as colony formation, cell viability (CCK-8), 5-Ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU), migration and invasion (Transwell) assays, sphere formation, along with western blot analysis.


### Immunohistochemistry analysis
  Immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis was performed to evaluate protein expression levels of CTHRC1 (1:1000, Proteintech, 16534-1-AP) and Notch1 (1:200, Proteintech, 20687-1-AP) in HCC tissue specimens. A quantitative assessment was conducted using the ImageJ IHC profiler [39]. Staining intensity and distribution parameters were integrated for evaluation. Distribution (n%) was determined by calculating the percentage of immunopositive regions relative to the total tissue area examined. The final expression score was calculated by multiplying the intensity with distribution percentage. Scores > median(scores) were categorized as high expression, while scores ≤ median(scores) were considered low expression in the univariate and multivariate regression analyses. Three independent pathologists, blinded to patient clinical information, evaluated the results to ensure assessment objectivity.


### SA-β-gal staining
  Cellular senescence was assessed with the Senescence-Associated β-Galactosidase (SA-β-gal) Staining Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Cells underwent three PBS washing and then were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature (RT) and subsequently subjected to overnight incubation with β-galactosidase staining solution at 37 °C in a CO2-free setting. Stained cells were visualized and documented using light microscopy.


### Immunofluorescence analysis
  Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, washed thrice with PBS, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min, followed by another round of PBS washing and blocking with 5% normal fetal sheep serum for 1 h. NFs and CAFs were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting α-SMA (1:200, Boster, BM0002) and FAP (1:200, ABclonal, A6349). CAFs and SCAFs were co-incubated with primary antibodies against p21(1:200, Santa Cruz, sc-1661), CTHRC1 (1:200, Proteintech, 16534-1-AP) or SOX4 (1:300, zenbio, 680786). For SNU-398 and MHCC-97 H HCC cell lines, anti-Notch1 antibody (1:200, Proteintech, 20687-1-AP) was applied. After primary antibody incubation, cells were treated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h, shielded from light. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Cells were visualized and photographed using fluorescence microscopy.


### mIHC staining and analysis
  A total of eighteen HCC tissue samples were processed for mIHC at Wuhan Human Genetic Resource Bank. Tissue sections underwent deparaffinization, endogenous peroxidase blocking, and heat-induced antigen retrieval. Sequential mIHC staining was performed using primary antibodies against α-SMA (1:1000, Boster, BM0002), p21 (1:1000, Santa Cruz, sc-1661), and CTHRC1 (1:1000, Proteintech, 16534-1-AP) with the Opal Polaris 3-Color IHC Detection Kit (NEL871001KT, AKOYA) following iterative cycles of staining, imaging, and antibody stripping. Fluorescence imaging was acquired using the PhenoImager HT System with inForm 2.6 software. Quantitative analysis employed nuclear segmentation algorithms based on DAPI staining to identify individual cells and extract spatial coordinates and expression levels for each marker.


### Western blot analysis
  HCC tissue samples and cultured cells were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails. Following centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, supernatants were collected. Protein concentration was assessed via BCA assay. Protein extracts were resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk for 1 h at RT. Following blocking, membranes were incubated with specified primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The primary antibodies used were α-Tubulin (1:20,000, Proteintech, 66031-1-Ig), p16 (1:800, Santa Cruz, sc-1661), p21 (1:800, Santa Cruz, sc-6246), p53 (1:5,000, Proteintech, 10442-1-AP), α-SMA (1:2,000, BOSTER, BM002), FAP (1:1,000, ABclonal, A23789), CTHRC1 (1:1,000, Proteintech, 16534-1-AP), NANOG (1:1,000, Proteintech, 14259-1-AP), OCT4 (1:1,000, Proteintech, 11263-1-AP), SOX2 (1:1,000, Cell Signaling Technology, 3579), CD44 (1:20,000, Proteintech, 15675-1-AP), Vimentin (1:4,000, Proteintech, 60330-1-Ig), N-cadherin (1:3,000, Proteintech, 22018-1-AP), Snail1 (1:1,000, ABclonal, A5243), Notch1 (1:1,000, Proteintech, 20687-1-AP), Cleaved Notch1 (1:1,000, Cell Signaling Technology, 4147), HES1 (1:1,000, Cell Signaling Technology, 11988), HEY1 (1:1,000, ABclonal, A16110), and SOX4 (1:1000, zenbio, 680786). Membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies following TBST washing. After further TBST washing, the bands were visualized with SuperKine™ West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate (Abbkine) and imaged using a Tanon system (Shanghai, China).


### qRT-PCR analysis
  Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), with cDNA synthesized via HiScript® III Reverse Transcriptase (Vazyme, Jiangsu, China, R302-01) following manufacturer’s protocol. Quantitative PCR was performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q711-02). Gene expression was quantified through the comparative 2^(−ΔΔCt) method, where Ct indicates the cycle threshold. Primer sequences are presented in Additional file: Table S1.


### Sphere formation assay
  HCC cells were cultured in ultra-low attached 24-well plates (Cornig, NY, USA) and maintained in stem cell culture medium (DMEM/F12 medium with 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 1 × B27 supplement (cat# 17504-044; GIBCO), 20 ng/mL EGF (cat# PHG0311; GIBCO), 10 ng/mL bFGF (cat# PHG0266; GIBCO), and 1% methyl cellulose (cat# M0262; Sigma-Aldrich)). Medium was replenished every 4 days. Tumor spheres were counted and imaged via microscopy.


### CCK-8 cytotoxic assay
  Cell sensitivity to Sorafenib was assessed using the CCK-8 cytotoxic assay (catalog #40203ES92; Yeasen, Shanghai, China). Specifically, cells were plated at 1,000 cells per well in 96-well plates. Upon cell attachment, cells were exposed to Sorafenib (Sigma-Aldrich) at different concentrations (0, 5, 10, 15, and 20 µM). Following incubation for 24 h at 37 °C, CCK-8 reagent was added to each well. Viability was assessed after 2 h by measuring absorbance at 450 nm with a Thermo Fisher microplate reader (Shanghai, China).


### Colony formation assay
  HCC cells were seeded at a low density of 1,000 cells per well in 6-well plates. After 10–14 days of incubation, cell colonies were fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 20 min to ensure their integrity. Subsequently, the colonies were stained with crystal violet (catalog #C0121; Beyotime, China). Following three times of PBS washes and air drying, colonies were documented and quantified.


### Transwell migration and invasion assays
  Transwell chambers were utilized for migration and invasion assays. For the migration assays, serum-free cell suspensions were introduced into the upper chambers (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA), with 700 µL of DMEM containing 15% FBS in the lower chambers serving as a chemoattractant. In the invasion assays, chambers were pre-coated with Matrigel (1:7 dilution; BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). Membranes were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 to ensure their integrity. Subsequently, the membranes were stained with crystal violet (catalog #C0121; Beyotime, China). After PBS washing and drying, cells from three randomly selected fields were counted, with each experiment conducted in triplicate.


### EdU proliferation assay
  Cell proliferation was evaluated using the BeyoClick™ EdU Cell Proliferation Kit with Alexa Fluor 594 (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). After PBS washing, cells were incubated with EdU solution for 2 h. Cell nuclei were subsequently stained with DAPI. After additional PBS washing, cells were visualized and documented using fluorescence microscopy.


### Lentivirus packing and transduction assay
  HCC cells (MHCC-97 H and SNU-398) were transduced with Notch1-shRNA and Notch1 viral particles from Genechem Corporation (Shanghai, China) following manufacturer’s protocols. SOX4 plasmids were purchased from MiaoLing Biology Sharing Platform (Wuhan, China). A CTHRC1-specific shRNA sequence or a SOX4- specific shRNA sequence was synthesized and inserted into the pLKO.1 vector using primers detailed in Table S2. The CTHRC1 coding sequence was PCR-amplified from cDNA and cloned into the pHAGE-CMV expression vector for overexpression using primers detailed in Table S3. Lentivirus production adhered to Addgene’s recommended protocol. Packaging plasmids pMD2.G and psPAX2 (Addgene #12259 and #12260) were transfected into HEK-293T cells using polyethylenimine (PEI) in DMEM for 72 h. Viruses were filtered by a 0.45-µm membrane and stored at −80 °C. Target cells (CAFs or HCC cells) were infected with lentivirus in media supplemented with 10 µg/mL polybrene (Sigma H9268) for 24 h. Puromycin resistance selection was applied 48 h post-infection to establish stable cell lines.


### Chromatin Immunoprecipitation
  A chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay kit (BIOLOGY, BOLG2309) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min. GlycineBuffer was added to a 100 µl for 5 min to terminate the crosslinking. LLysisBuffer by the addition of 200 µl (30 min, 4 °C) with frequent mixing. The SONICS VCX500 system (SONICS, USA) was used to generate DNA fragment of 100–500 bp in length. After centrifugation, the supernatant was incubated with antibodies against SOX4 (66008-4-Ig, Proteintech) or isotype control antibody (30000-0-AP, Proteintech). Primer sequences are presented in Additional file: Table S4.


### Animal experiments
  Six-week-old male BALB/c nude mice were sourced from GemPharmatech company and kept in a pathogen-free barrier environment. Five animals were randomly assigned to each group for the experiments and the cell number was estimated based on the orthotopic liver xenograft assays in previous literature [40]. In orthotopic xenograft experiments, cells were slowly injected into the left liver lobe of nude mice (SNU-398: CAFs/SCAFs = 5 × 105: 5 × 105 cells/mice). Mice were humanely euthanized approximately 4 weeks after implantation ([2024] IACUC Number: 4468).


### Statistical methods
  Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.3 and GraphPad Prism version 10.2.0 software. All experiments were independently replicated at least three times to ensure data reproducibility. Appropriate statistical tests were selected based on data distribution characteristics. For approximately normally distributed data, Student’s t-test and one-way ANOVA were used. For data not following a normal distribution, statistical significance was determined using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Survival analysis was performed using the log-rank test. Results with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.


## Results

### Senescent CAFs are prevalent in the HCC tumor microenvironment and associated with cancer stemness
  We examined cellular senescence within the HCC microenvironment by analyzing a published scRNA-seq dataset of 10 tumor samples [41]. After quality control, t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) was applied to perform clustering analysis on 33,202 cells. The t-SNE plot showed a uniform distribution of cells across samples, confirming effective batch effect removal (Fig. 1A). Cells were annotated using expression levels of established marker genes, identifying six distinct cell types: B/plasma, T/NK, myeloid, CAFs, endothelial, and epithelial cells (Fig. 1B, C). To comprehensively assess the senescence profile across diverse cell populations within the TME, we first examined the expression patterns of classical senescence markers p16 (CDKN2A), p21 (CDKN1A), and p53 (TP53) across different cell types (Figure S1A). We observed that p21 and p53 showed the highest expression in endothelial cells, while p16 was predominantly expressed in epithelial cells. To further validate cellular senescence states, we employed the FRIDMAN_SENESCENCE_UP signature (Table S5) to calculate comprehensive senescence scores for each cell type. Notably, this analysis revealed that CAFs exhibited the highest senescence scores, which contrasted with the individual marker expression patterns observed above (Fig. 1D). To confirm this finding, we validated our results using additional established cellular senescence signatures from the CellAge database [25], which consistently identified CAFs as having the highest senescence scores across independent gene sets (Figure S1B). This inherent complexity demonstrates that cellular senescence cannot be reliably defined by any single biomarker, regardless of how “classical” it may be considered. Instead, comprehensive assessment incorporating multiple molecular and functional characteristics provides a more accurate representation of the senescent state [42, 43]. Given the well-documented limitations of individual senescence markers and the superior accuracy of comprehensive gene signature approaches, consistent with recent studies that have employed similar gene signature-based methods for senescence assessment [44–46], we utilized the gene signature-based scoring method to evaluate senescence states across different cell populations in subsequent analyses.

  Considering the significant influence of CAFs on the TME and their prevalence in HCC stroma, we concentrated on CAF senescence for further investigation. To validate the presence of senescent CAFs (SCAFs) in the HCC tumor microenvironment, we performed immunofluorescence staining to identify SCAFs (α-SMA+p21+cells). The results showed the presence of SCAFs within the tumor microenvironment, validating our scRNA-seq findings (Fig. 1E). Senescent cells contribute to tumor progression by secreting the SASPs [47]. To investigate cellular interactions mediated by SCAFs secretory phenotypes within the TME, we stratified CAFs into high senescent CAFs (HS-CAFs) and low senescent CAFs (LS-CAFs) based on the median CAF senescence score. Cell-cell communication analysis revealed that HS-CAFs showed a higher level of communication compared to LS-CAFs (Figure S1C, D). We also observed higher SASP-mediated signaling pathways in HS-CAFs than in LS-CAFs (Figure S1E, F). These findings suggest that SASPs from SCAFs potentially mediate immunomodulation and angiogenesis in the TME, consistent with previous studies [48–50]. SCAFs have been widely reported to promote cancer stemness and metastasis via secretory factors [15, 16, 51, 52]. Analysis of relative proportions of HS-CAFs and LS-CAFs across the 10 HCC samples revealed distinct groups, identifying tumor patients with LS-CAFs (03, 09, 10) and tumor patients with HS-CAFs (01, 02, 07) (Fig. 1F). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) indicated that epithelial cells from tumor samples with HS-CAFs were more enriched in “Stemness cell up”, “Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)”, and “Multicancer invasiveness signature” pathways (Fig. 1G). To further explore the correlation between SCAFs and stemness of tumor cells, we divided tumor cells into Epi1 and Epi2 subclusters (Figure S2A–C). The stemness degree of these subgroups was assessed using CytoTRACE [53]. Among them, Epi2 exhibited significantly higher stemness scores than Epi1 (Fig. 1H). The stemness-related genes in HCC, including SOX2, ALDH1A1, CD44 and MYC, were highly expressed in Epi2, while Epi1 exhibited notable upregulation of genes associated with differentiation, including ALB, GPC3, APOE, and TF. (Figure S2D). Using GSEA, stemeness-related pathway including “Epithelial-Mesenchymal Transition”, “Liver Cancer Malignancy”, “Transformed Stem Cell Spheres”, and “Fetal Liver Hepatoblasts” were enriched in Epi2 (Figure S2E). Moreover, fetal liver hepatoblasts-like properties have been reported to represent tumor-initiating cells with transcriptome-wide expressional changes [54]. We investigated the cell-cell communication patterns between epithelial cell subclusters and CAF subclusters in HCC patients. Cell-cell communication analysis revealed differential interaction patterns, with HS-CAFs showing significantly stronger communication with Epi2 than with Epi1 (Fig. 1I, Figure S2F). We performed DEG analysis to identify key molecular signatures associated with SCAFs and validate their correlation with cancer stemness in the scRNA-seq dataset. Genes with elevated expression in CAFs relative to other cell types were identified as CAF-upregulated genes (Table S6). Genes exhibiting higher expression in HS-CAFs compared to LS-CAFs were identified as SCAF-upregulated genes (Table S7). Furthermore, we discovered differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between tumor and normal tissues in the TCGA-LIHC dataset (with|logFC| greater than 0.5 and p-value less than 0.05). By intersecting these gene sets, 127 genes were identified and used to create a gene set called Senescent CAF-Related Genes (SCRGs), which represents SCAFs characteristics in HCC at both bulk and single-cell transcriptome levels (Figure S2G, Table S8). To validate the correlation of between SCAFs and Epi2, we evaluated the patient-level abundance of SCAFs and Epi2 in bulk RNA data, where SCAFs were represented by SCRGs and Epi2 was represented by its specific marker genes. The results revealed a significantly positive correlation between SCAFs and Epi2 abundance at the patient level in both the CHCC dataset (R = 0.86, p < 2.2e-16) and GSE144269 dataset (R = 0.42, p = 3.7e-07) (Figure S2H).To explore the role of SCAFs in HCC progression, we used single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) to calculate the SCAF scores and divided patients into High-SCAFs and Low-SCAFs groups. Then, we performed GSEA and found that “stem cell up”, “metastasis”, “stem cell proliferation”, and “regulation of stem cell differentiation” signaling pathways were more enriched in the High-SCAFs group (Fig. 1J). In summary, these findings suggest that SCAFs have a strong correlation with cancer stemness and may promote the development of HCC.


### SCAFs promote cancer stemness of HCC in vitro and in vivo
  Previous research reports have verified that SCAFs promote cancer stemness in both esophageal cancer [15] and breast cancer [16]. We sought to investigate whether SCAFs promote cancer stemness and metastasis in HCC. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that HCC patients with metastatic disease exhibited a significantly higher proportion of SCAFs compared to patients with non-metastatic tumors (Figs. 2A, S3A). Western blot analysis showed that metastatic patients showed increased expression of p21, as well as elevated levels of cancer stemness markers (CD44, Nanog, Sox2, and Oct4) and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)-associated proteins (N-cadherin, Vimentin, and Snail1) relative to non-metastatic patients (Figure S3B). To elucidate the role of SCAFs in HCC progression, we extracted CAFs from human HCC tissues and NFs from liver tissues near hepatic hemangioma. Western blot and immunofluorescence analyses revealed elevated expression levels of FAP and α-SMA in CAFs relative to NFs (Figure S3C, D). Previous studies have demonstrated that elevated levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), particularly H₂O₂, are observed during HCC progression [55]. This elevation damages telomeres, causing attrition and uncapping, thereby inducing cellular senescence [56]. Subsequently, CAF senescence was induced using H2O2 to trigger oxidative stress, which mediated cellular senescence as previously described [57]. H2O2-induced SCAFs exhibited heightened SA-β-Gal activity and upregulated expression of senescence markers p16, p21, and p53 at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 2B–D). Additionally, these SCAFs exhibited upregulation of SASP-related genes encoding cytokines, chemokines, and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) (Fig. 2E), which are established components of the SASPs [58]. Tumor cells treated with senescent cancer-associated fibroblast-conditioned media (SCAF-CM) showed markedly enhanced proliferative capabilities compared to those treated with the control group (CAF-CM), validated by EdU and colony formation assays (Figs. 2F and S3E). Moreover, transwell assays demonstrated that SCAF-CM treatment significantly enhanced tumor cell migration and invasion capacities (Fig. 2G). Furthermore, treatment of tumor cells with SCAF-CM resulted in significantly enhanced self-renewal capacity and resistance to sorafenib (Fig. 2H, I). Western blot analysis showed that tumor cells exposed to SCAF-CM increased the expression of genes associated with cancer stemness and EMT-related genes (Fig. 2J). Additionally, various anticancer therapies, including conventional chemotherapy and targeted radiation approaches, are well-documented mechanisms for inducing cellular senescence [17, 18]. Therefore, considering the direct therapeutic effects of anticancer therapies on the tumor, we selected cisplatin, a widely established chemotherapeutic agent for hepatocellular carcinoma, to develop another senescence model [38, 59]. Cisplatin-induced SCAFs exhibited heightened SA-β-Gal activity and upregulated expression of senescence markers p16, p21, and p53 at both protein levels and mRNA (Figure S4A–C). Colony formation assay, EdU proliferation assay, transwell assays, sphere formation assay, and CCK-8 cytotoxicity assay collectively demonstrated that tumor cells exposed to SCAF-CM and SCAF-CM (Cisplatin) exhibited enhanced capacities for proliferation, migration, invasion, self-renewal, and resistance to sorafenib (Figure S4D–H). Western blot analysis showed that tumor cells exposed to SCAF-CM and SCAF-CM (Cisplatin) increased the expression of genes associated with cancer stemness and EMT-related genes (Figure S4I). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that SCAFs promote the abilities of proliferation, migration, invasion, self-renewal, and sorafenib resistance in HCC cells.

  To validate these findings in vivo, we constructed a mouse orthotopic liver xenograft model. Mice were randomly allocated to two groups: the SNU-398 plus CAFs group and the SNU-398 plus SCAFs group. Four weeks after inoculation, tumors in the SCAF group showed significantly higher liver weight than those in the CAF group (Fig. 2K). Importantly, we found an increased number of mice with lung metastases in the SCAF group (Fig. 2L). IHC staining revealed an increased proportion of Ki67+, CD44+, and Vimentin+ cells in the SCAF group (Figure S5A). Western blot analysis confirmed upregulation of senescence-related, stemness-related, and EMT-related genes in tumor tissues from the SCAF group compared to the control group (Figure S5B). Collectively, these findings suggest that SCAFs promote HCC stemness and metastasis in vivo.


### Development of the CAF senescence score-based predictive model
  To refine the SCAFs signature for prognosis prediction, we conducted univariate Cox regression analysis, revealing fifty genes significantly correlated with HCC patient survival outcomes (Figure S6A). Next, we used four machine-learning algorithms to select candidate prognostic genes, each employing different selection criteria and methodologies. LASSO identified 15 genes (Fig. 3A, Figure S6B), XGBoost yielded 28 genes (Fig. 3A, Figure S6D), RF retained all 50 genes (Fig. 3A, Figure S6C), and GBM selected 28 genes (Fig. 3A, Figure S6E). The intersection of these four algorithm results identified 10 crucial genes (Fig. 3B), which were employed to develop the CAF senescence score (CSscore) via Cox proportional hazards regression in the TCGA-LIHC training set. Individual patient CSscores were calculated based on Cox coefficients and log2(TPM + 1) transformed expression values of these 10 genes. Patients were stratified into high-CSscore (H-CSscore) and low-CSscore (L-CSscore) groups using the median value across three cohorts: TCGA-LIHC and two independent validation cohorts (ICGC-LIRI and CHCC) (Fig. 3C). Kaplan-Meier analysis indicated that higher CSscores were significantly associated with worse OS in all three cohorts (Fig. 3D). Patients with H-CSscore consistently exhibited worse outcomes in disease-specific survival (DSS), disease-free interval (DFI), progression-free interval (PFI), and relapse-free survival (RFS) compared to those with L-CSscore (Figure S7A–D). The prognostic value of the CSscore was verified by ROC curve analysis, with AUC values of 0.79, 0.73, and 0.76 for 1-, 2-, and 3-year OS in TCGA-LIHC; 0.77, 0.73, and 0.73 for 1-, 2- and 3-year OS in ICGC-LIRI; and 0.75, 0.73, and 0.69 for 1-, 2-, and 3-year OS in CHCC, respectively (Fig. 3E). Moreover, restricted mean survival time (RMST) analysis confirmed that L-CSscore groups exhibited superior longevity and reduced fatality compared to H-CSscore groups, with significant differences at 7 years in TCGA-LIHC, 5 years in ICGC-LIRI, and 3 years in CHCC (Fig. 3F).

### Establishment and validation of the CSscore combined with clinical characteristic
  We evaluated the prognostic value of clinicopathologic factors in HCC by employing the concordance index (C-index). Specifically, in the TCGA-LIHC dataset, the CSscore independently predicted OS, DSS, DFI, and PFI (Fig. 4A, Figure S8A–C), while it also served as an independent predictor of OS in the ICGC-LIRI dataset (Fig. 4B), as well as OS and RFS in the CHCC dataset (Fig. 4C, Figure S8D), demonstrated by both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses. Notably, this prognostic model proved more effective than traditional clinicopathologic parameters such as age, gender, clinical stage, T stage, and histological grade. Integrating the CSscore with clinical parameters significantly improved predictive capability compared to individual factors (Fig. 4D). To maximize clinical utility, we developed nomograms based on the CSscore and clinical characteristics for all three cohorts (Figure S8E–G). Decision curve analysis confirmed substantial clinical net benefit with the help of the CSscore (Fig. 4E). Calibration plots confirmed the CSscore’s strong predictive accuracy across different risk levels (Fig. 4F). Furthermore, elevated CSscores correlated significantly with advanced grades, higher clinical stages, and T stages in the TCGA-LIHC cohort (Figure S8H–J), higher TNM stages in the ICGC-LIRI cohort (Figure S8K), and higher BCLC stages and TNM stages in the CHCC cohort (Figure S8L, M). Patient stratification based on clinical features across all three cohorts further supported these findings (Fig. 4G). Collectively, these findings suggest that the CSscore is a robust and reliable prognostic biomarker, where elevated values correlate with worse prognosis in HCC patients.

### Potential biological mechanisms and immune infiltration related to the CSscore
  We sought to elucidate the biological mechanisms underlying the CSscore by performing functional enrichment analysis of KEGG and GO pathways. The comprehensive analysis revealed that the CSscore was enriched in genes associated with the cell cycle, cell adhesion, ECM-related processes, and intercellular interactions (Fig. 5A). Subsequently, Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) of Hallmark gene sets indicated that the H-CSscore group exhibited stronger activity in PI3K-AKT-mTOR, TGF-β, Notch, and p53 signaling pathways (Fig. 5B). GSEA further validated that pathways involved in tumor proliferation, recurrence, poor survival, stemness, EMT, TP53, TNF, and TGF-β signaling were significantly enriched in the H-CSscore group (Fig. 5C). To further substantiate the underlying biological connections, we evaluated two previously established stemness indexes, including the ssGSEA-based stemness index [60] and the mRNAsi [61]. Notably, results revealed that the CSscore was positively correlated with both the ssGSEA-based stemness index (R = 0.62, p < 0.001) and the mRNAsi (R = 0.22, p < 0.001) (Fig. 5D).

  We employed multiple immune infiltration algorithms to assess the influence of SCAFs on the TME in HCC. Immune infiltration analysis revealed that the CSscore was positively correlated with immunosuppressive immune cells, including M2 macrophages, regulatory T cells (Tregs), and neutrophils, while showing negative associations with anti-tumor immune cells such as CD8 + T cells, dendritic cells (DCs), and B cells (Fig. 5E). The ssGSEA identified enrichment of activated CD8 + T cells, type 1 T helper cells, natural killer T cells, and effector memory CD8 + T cells in the L-CSscore group. Conversely, activated CD4 + T cells, central memory CD4 + T cells, and type 2 T helper cells were enriched in the H-CSscore group (Fig. 5F), which were previously reported to be associated with worse outcomes in HCC [62]. To further validate the relationship between CSscore and immune cell infiltration patterns, we performed an independent bulk deconvolution analysis using the TCGA cohort. This validation analysis consistently demonstrated that samples with higher SCAFs abundance exhibited increased infiltration of immunosuppressive cell populations, including Tregs, M2 macrophages, and neutrophils (Figure S9A), with no significant differential infiltration of CD8 + T cells observed (Figure S9B). Additionally, samples with elevated SCAFs abundance displayed significantly higher immune dysfunction and immune exclusion scores compared to those with lower SCAFs abundance (Figure S9B). Therefore, the anti-tumor immunity may be impaired by both the immunosuppressive microenvironment and the poorly-infiltrated, dysfunctional CD8 + T cells [63]. These findings suggest that SCAFs are associated with an immunosuppressive microenvironment that facilitates HCC progression.


### The CSscore as a predictor of therapeutic responses in HCC
  Intra-tumor heterogeneity (ITH), characterized by accumulated genetic mutations [64], represents a crucial genomic feature linked to malignancy progression and therapy resistance [65]. The MATH algorithm was applied to evaluate intratumor heterogeneity (ITH) in the TCGA-LIHC dataset, revealing higher MATH scores in the H-CSscore group (Fig. 6A). Further analysis revealed that patients with high MATH scores experienced significantly worse OS compared to those with low scores (Fig. 6B). Combining ITH with the CSscore, patients in the H-MATH + H-CSscore group showed significantly worse prognosis than those in the L-MATH + H-CSscore group (Fig. 6C). Mutational profiling identified 20 significant gene alterations across both groups with distinct distribution patterns (Fig. 6D). To explore correlations with between the CSscore and immunotherapy response, we implemented the TIP analytical framework to evaluate anti-tumor immunity [31]. This revealed significant inverse relationships between the CAF senescence score and critical immune processes including tumor infiltration by immune cells (step 5), T-cell recognition of cancer cells (step 6), and cancer cell elimination (step 7) (Fig. 6E, Figure S10A), indicating that elevated CSscores are associated with compromised anti-tumor immunity. Subsequently, the TIDE algorithm revealed distinctly higher immunotherapy resistance scores in the H-CSscore group, suggesting poorer immunotherapeutic responses compared to patients in the L-CSscore group (Fig. 6F). To further validate the predictive value of CSscores for immunotherapy response, we analyzed the IMvigor210 cohort [34], which showed decreased survival time and diminished response to anti-PD-1 immunotherapy in patients with elevated CSscores (Fig. 6G–I). Similar results were observed in melanoma immunotherapy datasets [35] (Fig. 6J–L). These results indicate that patients with lower CSscores might benefit more from immunotherapy. We examined the correlation between our prognostic signature and treatment outcomes in patients undergoing TACE therapy, the standard treatment for intermediate-stage HCC [66]. NTP analysis comparing our defined risk groups with TACE responders in the GSE104580 dataset indicated that patients with lower CSscores might benefit more from TACE therapy (Fig. 6M, N).

### CTHRC1 is upregulated in SCAFs within the HCC tumor microenvironment
  We further identified the most important key gene among the CSscore genes. We measured expression of the 10 crucial genes in SCAFs. SERPINE1, CTHRC1, and MARCKSL1 showed significant upregulation of more than 1.5-fold in SCAFs when compared to CAFs (Fig. 7A). Violin plot analysis showed that MARCKSL1 was widely expressed in both CAFs and endothelial cells, SERPINE1 and CTHRC1 displayed more specific expression in CAFs (Fig. 7B). Next, we assessed the correlation between senescence scores and expression levels of each candidate gene. CTHRC1 exhibited the strongest correlation with senescence scores among the three genes studied, with significant positive correlation in both the TCGA-LIHC (R = 0.50, p < 2.2e-16) (Fig. 7C) and scRNA-seq (R = 0.3, p < 2.2e-16) (Fig. 7D) datasets. Based on these findings, CTHRC1 was selected as the primary target for deeper exploration of senescence-related mechanisms in HCC. CTHRC1 is implicated in pro-migratory pathways, arterial tissue-repair processes, inhibition of collagen deposition, and regulation of multiple signaling cascades [67]. We next aimed to investigate the expression levels of CTHRC1 in SCAFs. Elisa showed that SCAFs secreted the greater amount of CTHRC1 (Fig. 7E). Immunofluorescence analyses showed higher abundances of p21 and CTHRC1 in SCAFs than in CAFs (Fig. 7F). The protein levels of CTHRC1 were measured individually in CAFs, SCAFs, and HCC tissues. Western blot analysis indicated that CTHRC1 expression was significantly elevated in SCAFs (Fig. 7G). Additionally, mIHC analysis showed that CTHRC1 expression was higher in SCAFs than in CAFs (Fig. 7H). Collectively, these findings indicate that SCAFs exhibit elevated CTHRC1 expression within the HCC tumor microenvironment.

### SCAF-derived CTHRC1 promotes cancer stemness in vitro and in vivo
  Previous research has demonstrated that SCAFs can promote cancer stemness [15, 16]. To investigate whether SCAF-derived CTHRC1 plays a pivotal role in the process of SCAFs promoting HCC stemness, GSEA functional enrichment analysis of CTHRC1 was first performed, and the results revealed significant enrichment of stemness and EMT pathways (Figure S11A). We used lentiviral transduction to knock down CTHRC1 (shCTHRC1) expression in SCAFs, verifying the efficiency with qRT-PCR, western blot, and elisa analyses (Figure S11B–D). EdU proliferation assay and colony formation assay demonstrated reduced proliferation capacity in tumor cells exposed to CM from shCTHRC1 SCAFs (Figure S11H, J). Moreover, the results of the transwell assays showed that tumor cells exposed to shCTHRC1 SCAF-CM exhibited reduced migration and invasion capacities (Fig. 8A). Similarly, these cells exposed to shCTHRC1 SCAF-CM showed significantly diminished self-renewal capacity and sorafenib resistance (Fig. 8B, C). Western blot analysis showed a downregulation of cancer stemness-related and EMT-related genes in tumor cells exposed to shCTHRC1 SCAF-CM compared to the control group (Fig. 8G). Also, CTHRC1 was upregulated in CAFs via lentiviral transduction (OE-CTHRC1), with subsequent verification of overexpression efficiency through both qRT-PCR, western blot, and elisa analyses (Figure S11E–G). EdU proliferation assay, colony formation assay, transwell assays, sphere formation assay, and CCK-8 cytotoxicity assay collectively showed that tumor cells exposed to CM from OE-CTHRC1 CAFs exhibited enhanced capacities of proliferation, migration, invasion, self-renewal, and resistance to sorafenib (Fig. 8D–F, S11I, and S11K). Western blot analysis showed a upregulation of cancer stemness-related and EMT-related genes in tumor cells exposed to OE-CTHRC1 CAF-CM (Fig. 8H). To further investigate whether CTHRC1 directly affects HCC stemness, MHCC-97 H and SNU-398 cells were cultured in different media: DMEM, SCAF-CM, shControl SCAF-CM, shCTHRC1 SCAF-CM, and DMEM supplemented with recombinant human CTHRC1 protein (rhCTHRC1, 20 ng/ml). Subsequently, a series of assays were performed to evaluate the stem-like properties of HCC cells. Colony formation assay, EdU proliferation assay, transwell assays, sphere formation assay, and CCK-8 cytotoxicity assay collectively demonstrated that tumor cells exposed to SCAF-CM, shControl SCAF-CM, or rhCTHRC1 exhibited enhanced capacities for proliferation, migration, invasion, self-renewal, and resistance to sorafenib (Figure S12A–E). Western blot analysis showed a upregulation of cancer stemness-related and EMT-related genes in tumor cells exposed to SCAF-CM, shControl SCAF-CM, or rhCTHRC1 (Figure S12F). These findings suggest that SCAF-derived CTHRC1 promotes tumor cell malignant phenotypes, including proliferation, migration, invasion, self-renewal, and sorafenib resistance.

### SCAFs promote cancer stemness via the SOX4-CTHRC1-Notch1 axis
  Next, we aimed to elucidate the mechanism through which SCAF-derived CTHRC1 promotes cancer stemness and metastasis in HCC. Previous studies have shown that the Notch1 signaling pathway is essential for enhancing cancer stemness in HCC [68, 69]. To investigate potential correlation between CTHRC1 and the Notch pathway, we performed GSEA analysis which revealed significant enrichment of Notch signaling in patients with high CTHRC1 expression from the TCGA-LIHC dataset (Fig. 9A). Using ssGSEA algorithm, we found that the expression of CTHRC1 positively correlates with the Notch pathway enrichment scores (Figure S14A). Correlation analysis confirmed positive association between the expression of CTHRC1 and key Notch pathway components, including Notch1, Hes1, and Hey1 (Figure S14B). Moreover, immunofluorescence staining showed reduced Notch1/Notch1 intracellular domain (NICD) expression in tumor cells exposed to shCTHRC1 SCAF-CM (Figure S14C). Conversely, tumor cells exposed to OE-CTHRC1 CAF-CM showed elevated Notch1/NICD expression levels (Figure S14D). Western blot analysis demonstrated that shCTHRC1 SCAF-CM reduced the expression of Notch1, NICD, Hes1, and Hey1 in tumor cells (Fig. 9B), while OE-CTHRC1 CAF-CM increased the expression levels of these Notch pathway components (Fig. 9C). Importantly, we also verified that CTHRC1 knockdown reduced Notch1, NICD, Hes1, and Hey1 expression in mouse tumor tissues (Fig. 9D).

  To elucidate the role of Notch1 signaling in CTHRC1-driven tumor progression, we generated stable tumor cell lines with Notch1 overexpression (OE-Notch1) or knockdown (shNotch1) via lentiviral transduction. qRT-PCR (Figure S15A, B) and western blot (Figure S15C, D) analyses confirmed the efficiency of Notch1 overexpression and knockdown in MHCC-97 H and SNU-398 cell lines. In tumor cells exposed to shCTHRC1 SCAF-CM, OE-Notch1 partially restored proliferation (Figure S15E and S15H), migration, invasion (Fig. 9E, H, I), and self-renewal (Fig. 9F, J) capabilities. Western blot analysis showed that OE-Notch1 also restored stemness-related and EMT-related genes expression in tumor cells (Figure S15L). Conversely, in tumor cells exposed to OE-CTHRC1 CAF-CM, sh-Notch1 suppressed proliferation (Figure S15F and S15I), migration, invasion (Figure S15G and S15J-K), and self-renewal (Fig. 9G, K) capabilities. Furthermore, shNotch1 reduced stemness-related and EMT-related genes expression in tumor cells (Figure S15M). These findings suggest that SCAF-derived CTHRC1 promotes HCC stemness and metastasis through the Notch1 signaling pathway.

  We then focused on the mechanism underlying the upregulation of CTHRC1 in SCAFs and collected all transcription factors (TFs) in SCAFs. By predicting the transcription factors related to CTHRC1 using the FIMO_JASPAR, GTRD, and HumanTFDB databases and determining their intersection (Fig. 9L), we identified sixteen transcription factors (SP1, E2F1, PBX3, E2F6, ZBTB7A, CTCF, TCF12, MYC, ZNF148, KLF4, CTCFL, SOX2, RELA, CEBPA, ASCL1, and SOX4). We then analyzed the correlation between these sixteen transcription factors, the CSscore, and the expression of CTHRC1. Correlation analysis revealed the expression of SOX4 was significantly positively correlated with the CSscore and expression of CTHRC1 (Fig. 9M). In a previous study, SOX4 was shown to activate CTHRC1 transcriptional activity in lung adenocarcinoma [70]. However, it remains unclear whether SOX4 can activate the transcriptional activity of CTHRC1 in SCAFs within HCC. To address this, we first validate whether the expression of SOX4 was upregulated in SCAFs. Immunofluorescence analyses showed a higher abundance of p21 and SOX4 in SCAFs than CAFs (Fig. 9N). Additionally, increased mRNA and protein levels of SOX4 were detected in SCAFs, respectively (Fig. 9O, P). To further validated that SOX4 regulates the expression of CTHRC1, SCAFs were used to establish cells lines SCAF-shCtrl, SCAF-shSOX4 via lentiviral transduction, with knockdown efficiency verified by qRT-PCR and western blot (Fig. 9Q, R). Conversely, SOX4 upregulated in CAFs via lentiviral transduction, with overexpression efficiency verified by qRT-PCR and western blot (Fig. 9S, T). We found the mRNA and protein levels of CTHRC1 followed the changes in SOX4 expression (Fig. 9Q–T). Subsequently, we analyzed the transcription effect of SOX4 on CTHRC1 gene using ChIP and found that SOX4 could bind to the CTHRC1 gene promoter in CAFs (Fig. 9U). Collectively, these findings suggest the expression of CTHRC1 in CAFs is induced by SOX4.


### Increased CTHRC1 expression is correlated with the Notch1 pathway activation and increased aggressive clinicopathologic features in HCC
  Expression levels of CTHRC1 were analyzed across 16 HCC cohorts from the HCCDB database [71] to determine whether its expression is higher in HCC compared to adjacent liver tissues. Expression was classified as upregulated or downregulated based on thresholds at 1.5. Results revealed that CTHRC1 was predominantly upregulated in HCC compared to adjacent liver tissues in 51% of cases, while downregulation of CTHRC1 was observed in 6.1% of patients (Fig. 10A). Additionally, high protein expression of CTHRC1 in HCC was observed in the CPTAC dataset (Fig. 10B). CTHRC1 mRNA and protein levels were markedly higher in tumor tissues than in adjacent non-tumor tissues in qRT-PCR and western blot assays (Fig. 10C, D). We evaluated the relationship between CTHRC1 and Notch1/NICD expression through IHC staining on a tissue microarray (Fig. 10E). The correlation analysis demonstrated a positive association between Notch1/NICD and CTHRC1 expression (R = 0.57, p = 7.1e-06) (Fig. 10F), suggesting that CTHRC1 may enhance the activation of the Notch signaling pathway in HCC progression. We further analyzed the clinical significance of CTHRC1 expression in HCC. Analysis of the UALCAN dataset revealed that elevated CTHRC1 expression is significantly associated with more advanced tumor stage and higher histological grade, suggesting its potential role in tumor progression [72] (Fig. 10G). Table 1 demonstrated that elevated CTHRC1 expression significantly correlates with poor tumor differentiation and advanced TNM stage. Subsequent Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed markedly reduced OS in patients exhibiting high CTHRC1 expression compared to those with low CTHRC1 expression (p < 0.001) (Fig. 10H). The Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) [73] database confirmed that higher CTHRC1 expression is associated with shorter OS in HCC patients (Fig. 10I). Cox regression analyses identified CTHRC1 expression as an independent prognostic factor (Table 2). Collectively, these findings suggest that CTHRC1 is upregulated in tumor tissues and correlates with Notch pathway activation and worse clinical outcomes in HCC patients (Table 3).

## Discussion
  In this study, we comprehensively elucidated the impact of CAF senescence in HCC and revealed a novel mechanism by which SCAFs promote cancer stemness and metastasis. We developed the CSscore prognostic model, an independent predictor of clinical outcomes for HCC patients. Further analysis identified robust associations between the CSscore and multiple cancer characteristics, including cancer stemness properties, clinicopathological features, immune cell infiltration patterns, and therapeutic response. Here, we demonstrated the presence of a critical regulatory axis within the TME, where SOX4 upregulates CTHRC1 in SCAFs and subsequently activates the Notch1 signaling pathway to promote cancer stemness and metastasis, both of which ultimately drive the malignant progression of HCC.

  Cell senescence was previously regarded as a mechanism that inhibits the development and progression of tumors [74]. However, emerging evidence suggests that senescent cells promote tumorigenesis through SASPs [5]. Understanding these complex roles requires accurate identification and characterization of senescent cells within the tumor microenvironment. Senescence manifests in diverse stromal cell populations, with particular prevalence observed in CAFs [14]. Our findings underscore the complexity of defining cellular senescence in heterogeneous tumor environments. The differential expression patterns of classical senescence markers across cell types support the use of comprehensive gene signature approaches over individual marker analysis for robust senescence characterization [42, 43]. This methodological consideration has important implications for cancer-associated senescence research, as different cell types may undergo distinct senescence programs within the tumor microenvironment. Applying this signature-based approach to our dataset, we identified significantly elevated senescence levels in CAFs compared to other cell populations. While the precise molecular triggers initiating CAF senescence in HCC remain incompletely characterized, numerous potential mechanisms have been proposed. Previous research has identified various inducers of CAF senescence in different cancer types, including Stenotrophomonas maltophilia from tumor microbiota, which activates the TLR-4-mediated NF-κB signaling pathway in HCC [75], hypoxia triggering nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 activation in esophageal cancer [15], and tumor-derived soluble factors such as the chemokine C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 1 in ovarian carcinoma [76] and inflammatory mediator interleukin-1α in colorectal adenocarcinoma [18]. Additionally, Various anticancer therapies, including conventional chemotherapy regimens and targeted radiation approaches, can effectively induce senescence phenotypes within the CAF population [17, 18].

  CSCs possess the unique capacity to sustain and propagate tumor growth even when present in minimal quantities, serving as a pivotal driving force underlying tumor recurrence and distant metastasis [4]. CAFs play a critical role in establishing and maintaining an optimal microenvironmental niche for the survival and function of CSCs. Notably, the accumulation of SCAFs can promote cancer stemness in esophageal cancer [15] and breast cancer [16]. Consistently, our study revealed that SCAFs promote cancer stemness and metastasis in HCC. The functional enrichment analysis revealed that cancer cells of samples with high abundance of HS-CAFs were more enriched in stemness-related pathways. Additionally, cell-cell communication analysis showed that SCAFs had strong functional connections with epithelial cell subclusters that had high stemness scores. We validated that SCAFs significantly enhance cancer stemness properties in HCC through in vitro and in vivo experiments. Our investigation led to the development of a CSscore signature derived from senescent CAF-associated gene expression patterns, highlighting its substantial prognostic utility and therapeutic predictive value for stratifying HCC patients. We discovered that CTHRC1 secreted by SCAFs significantly enhanced cancer stemness properties in HCC, thereby providing novel mechanistic insights into the pivotal role of SCAFs in driving cancer progression and malignant evolution. Cellular senescence of CAFs manifests across numerous solid tumor types beyond HCC. In esophageal cancer, SCAFs promote cancer stemness phenotypes and resistance to conventional chemotherapeutic agents through paracrine signaling mediated by insulin-like growth factor 1 secretion [15]. In colorectal cancer, chemotherapy-induced SCAFs promote tumor progression and drug resistance through elevated TGF-β production [17]. Previous studies have shown that TGF-β functions as an upstream regulator of SOX4, can promote its expression [77]. The upregulation of SOX4 has been implicated in tumor progression and is associated with poor prognosis across various cancers [78]. In gastric cancer, SOX4 enhances Wnt family member 2(WNT2) expression, thereby promoting cancer stemness and chemotherapy resistance, ultimately correlating with a worse prognosis [79]. Similarly, in non-small cell lung cancer, SOX4 overexpression in CAFs facilitates tumor cell invasion and metastasis [80]. Consistent with these findings, our study revealed that SOX4 is highly expressed in SCAFs and serves as a key transcription factor for CTHRC1. Specifically, SOX4 upregulates CTHRC1 expression, which in turn activates the Notch1 signaling pathway, ultimately promoting cancer stemness and metastasis in HCC. These results suggest that gene expression profiles derived from SCAFs may serve as valuable predictive biomarkers for patient prognosis and therapeutic responsiveness across diverse solid malignancies. Future investigations should focus on establishing a comprehensive pan-cancer senescent CAF molecular signature to systematically evaluate its broader prognostic significance and clinical utility in predicting responses to various treatment modalities.

  The CSscore comprised 10 senescent CAF-related genes: SERPINE1, CTHRC1, RNF11, ENG, MARCKSL1, ASAP1, FHL3, LAMB1, CD151, and OLFML2B. Among them, CTHRC1 was identified as a key extracellular factor that was highly upregulated in SCAFs. Its increased expression showed a strong correlation with senescence scores, as measured by ssGSEA in bulk RNA-sequencing data and AddModuleScore in scRNA-seq. Furthermore, CTHRC1 demonstrated relatively specific expression in CAFs. SCAFs have been characterized as a distinct myofibroblastic CAF subpopulation that accumulates within tumors and promotes their development [16, 48]. Another study showed that CTHRC1 induces pancreatic stellate cells to differentiate into myofibroblastic CAFs in pancreatic cancer [81]. Based on these findings, CTHRC1 was selected for further exploration in vitro and in vivo experiments. Previous research suggested CTHRC1 correlates with stemness signatures based on bioinformatics analyses in lung adenocarcinoma [82], and demonstrated that CTHRC1 overexpression enhances tumor metastasis in a tail vein injection assay using nude mice in HCC [83]. Our investigation elucidated the crucial functional role of CTHRC1 in promoting cancer stemness properties and facilitating metastatic dissemination specifically within the context of SCAFs. CTHRC1 knockdown in SCAFs reduced HCC cell proliferation, migration, invasion, stemness, and sorafenib resistance. In an orthotopic xenograft mouse model, we confirmed that SCAF-derived CTHRC1 promoted cancer stemness and metastasis. Existing literature has primarily established CTHRC1’s involvement in fibrosis and tumor metastasis [84]. Our study further unveiled its distinct origin from SCAFs and demonstrates its functional role in promoting HCC progression. Specifically, we identified CTHRC1 as highly expressed in SCAFs, which activates the Notch1 signaling pathway to enhance HCC stemness and metastasis. These findings provide novel mechanistic insights into SCAF-mediated tumor progression and malignant pathways.

  CTHRC1 has been previously established as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker across diverse pathological conditions [67]. In our investigation, we demonstrated that CTHRC1 exhibits significantly elevated expression patterns in HCC tissue specimens and maintains robust association with worse clinical outcomes. Moreover, CTHRC1 expression levels are tightly associated with Notch1/NICD, consistent with Notch’s established role in maintaining cancer stemness and promoting HCC progression. These findings substantiate the pivotal role of CTHRC1 in HCC pathogenesis and underscore its significant potential as a clinically valuable prognostic biomarker for risk stratification of HCC patients.

  In conclusion, our study developed the CSscore as a robust prognostic signature that effectively predicts clinical outcomes and therapeutic responses in HCC patients. Through functional experiments, we confirmed that SCAFs promoted HCC stemness and metastasis. We also demonstrated that SCAF-derived CTHRC1 promotes HCC stemness and metastasis through activation of the Notch1 signaling pathway. These findings enhance our understanding of SCAFs in HCC progression while identifying CTHRC1 as a promising therapeutic target for stromal-directed intervention in HCC. However, our investigation had several notable limitations. First, our study was constrained by sample size limitations, including a relatively small clinical cohort and limited single-cell RNA sequencing samples, which may affect the statistical power and reproducibility of our findings. Second, our tumor samples were collected from a single center, potentially limiting the generalizability of our results to broader populations with different genetic backgrounds. Third, while we identified CTHRC1 as a SCAF-derived factor promoting HCC progression through Notch1 signaling, we did not experimentally validate the specific effects of SCAFs on immune cell populations within the tumor microenvironment, which represents an important area for future investigation. Fourth, other mechanistic pathways beyond Notch1 signaling may exist and warrant further exploration. Finally, the predictive value of CTHRC1 as a biomarker requires validation in diverse patient cohorts with varied environmental exposures and genetic profiles. Future studies with larger sample sizes, multi-center validation, and comprehensive immune profiling will be essential to address these limitations and further strengthen our understanding of SCAFs in HCC progression.


## Conclusion
  In this study, a prognostic model was developed using the CAF senescence-related genes that effectively predict outcomes in HCC patients. Our findings demonstrated that the CSscore model serves as an independent factor for predicting OS. Through various functional experiments, we elucidated the downstream signaling pathway of CTHRC1 as well as the upstream transcription factors regulating its expression. In summary, the upregulation of CTHRC1 in SCAFs was attributed to enhanced transcriptional activation by SOX4, subsequently activating the Notch1 signaling pathway to promote cancer stemness and metastasis in HCC.
