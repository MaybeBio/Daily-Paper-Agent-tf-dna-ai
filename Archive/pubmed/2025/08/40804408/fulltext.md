# Structural and functional roles of conserved residues of human papillomavirus (HPV) E2 protein and biological consequences


## Background
  Human papillomavirus (HPV) is a prevalent viral pathogen that causes a variety of malignancies, including cervical cancer, one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths among women worldwide. The HPV E2 protein is a central regulator of viral replication and oncogene expression, making it a critical determinant of HPV-associated malignancies. While its core functions are conserved, variations within the E2 protein are thought to contribute to the differential oncogenic potential among HPV types, though the structural basis for this remains incompletely understood. Previous research from our laboratory suggests that mutations within a 12–base pair segment of the long control region that encompasses the E2 binding sites may influence the oncogenic potential of certain HPV strains.


## Methods
  Computational methods, including multiple sequence alignment, phylogenetic analysis, and protein structural modeling were employed to identify conserved regions and correlate these with potential cancer-associated mutations in the coding region.


## Results
  Structural modeling using AlphaFold3 and visualization in PyMOL revealed that conserved E2 residues cluster near the DNA-binding surface in the C-terminal domain and at critical interaction sites in the N-terminal transactivation domain, important for E1 DNA helicase binding and potentially other host factor interactions. Notably, species-specific adaptations, including the T309P substitution in the HPV52 subfamily B2, which may induce structural changes in the DNA-binding domain, and variations in the 12-base pair spacer, could modulate oncogene expression.


## Conclusions
  Collectively, these findings refine our understanding of E2’s essential role in viral pathogenesis and highlight promising targets for therapeutic intervention in high-risk HPV strains.

## Background
  Human papillomavirus (HPV) is among the most prevalent viral pathogens worldwide, implicated in cervical cancer and other malignancies, including anal, penile, and certain head and neck cancers [1–4]. HPVs are small, non-enveloped viruses with a circular double-stranded DNA genome of approximately 8 kilobases [5]. Their genome is typically partitioned into three regions: [1] early genes (E1, E2, E6, E7, etc.) [2], late genes (L1 and L2, which code for structural proteins), and [3] a non-coding regulatory segment known as the long control region (LCR) [5]. Among the viral early proteins, E2 is particularly important due to its role in regulating transcription of viral oncogenes (e.g., E6 and E7) and maintaining viral genome copy number, partly through its essential function in initiating DNA replication via the recruitment of the E1 DNA helicase [6–8]. E2 binds as a dimer to palindromic DNA targets (E2 binding sites, or E2BS) within the LCR, typically characterized by a consensus motif 5′-ACCgNNNNcGGT-3′, in which the central four nucleotides may vary [7–9]. These base-pair variations can influence differential DNA bending, methylation patterns, and E2 binding affinity factors that collectively govern viral replication and oncogene expression [10–12].

  The E2 protein is split into two domains: the N-terminal transactivation domain and the C-terminal DNA-binding domain, both of which are connected by a flexible linker region. The N-terminal domain (residues 1-205 in HPV18 E2) generally folds into a conserved “cashew-shaped” architecture, which can be described with subregions such as N1, Fulcrum, and N2 [7]. For HPV18 E2, our AlphaFold structure prediction indicates that this N-terminal domain contains 6 α-helices and 8 β-strands. The C-terminal domain consists of 2 α-helices and 4 β-strands, culminating in a total of 8 α-helices and 12 β-strands across the entire protein according to AlphaFold structure predictions [13]. This classification of secondary structures will be used throughout this study.

  Recent data from our lab indicate that even single-nucleotide changes within the 12-bp E2BS sites of the LCR, encompassing the E2BS motif, can alter E2’s regulatory functions and correlate with high or low-risk classification (HR/LR) [14, 15]. Previously, an extensive survey of E2 variation across six papillomavirus genera (Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Delta, Lambda, Xi) included both human and non-human papillomaviruses, providing a broad evolutionary context [16]. However, the present study narrows its scope to human papillomaviruses alone, as well as using more recent sequence data focusing on three major genera: Alpha (e.g., HPV16, HPV18, often linked to mucosal cancers), Beta, and Gamma (both predominantly cutaneous) [17–19].

  To delineate the molecular mechanisms by which HPV achieves differential oncogenic risk, this study was designed as an analytical funnel. We first performed a broad survey across the human Alpha, Beta, and Gamma genera, as they represent the major human-associated papillomaviruses with distinct tissue tropisms and a wide spectrum of oncogenic potential, making them ideal for identifying universally conserved functional residues. We then narrowed our focus to the Alpha-genus, which contains the most clinically significant high-risk (e.g., HPV16, 18) and low-risk (e.g., HPV6) types, providing the ideal context for studying the molecular determinants of oncogenicity. Finally, we zoomed in on specific structural case studies, including HPV18 as a well-studied high-risk reference, HPV6 as a non-oncogenic comparator, and HPV52 to investigate a clinically relevant, cancer-associated variant (T309P). Through this multi-scale approach, we aimed to connect broad evolutionary patterns to specific, therapeutically relevant molecular mechanisms.


## Methods

### Sequence data collection
  Complete E2 protein sequences for 215 human Alpha, Beta, and Gamma papillomaviruses were retrieved from the Papillomavirus Episteme (http://pave.niaid.nih.gov/#home, (PaVE) database [20]. Only strains classified as HPV genotypes were included to ensure both diversity and a strict focus on human hosts.


### Multiple sequence alignment (Clustal Omega)
  All retrieved E2 sequences underwent multiple sequence alignments (MSAs) using Clustal Omega with default parameters [21, 22]. MSAs were first generated for each individual clade (Alpha, Beta, and Gamma) and later created for a combined Alpha-Beta-Gamma (ABG) alignment. Each alignment was visually inspected to ensure minimized gap regions, particularly within the DNA-binding domain. Additionally, as Rogers et al. concluded, the flexible linker region within the protein contains significant alignment gaps [16]. Therefore, domain-only alignments were also produced and examined.


### Sequence visualization (Jalview)
  All MSAs were imported into Jalview to calculate residue-by-residue conservation scores and highlight fully conserved or physicochemically invariant positions [23–25]. Key conserved residues were classified according to their presence in the N-terminal transactivation domain or the C-terminal DNA-binding domain (DBD) as characterized by UniProt [26].


### Structural modeling and analysis
  E2 protein sequences for selected representative HPV types from the Alpha, Beta, and Gamma genera were annotated to visualize the conserved domain architecture of E2 proteins across genera. We then obtained structures for HPV18/52 from the Protein Data Bank (PDB), where available or predicted using AlphaFold3 [13]. The resulting coordinate files and analysis outputs, including the mapping of invariant amino acids, were visualized using The PyMOL Molecular Graphics System [27]. Further structural analyses of the static, AlphaFold-predicted models, including the corroboration of backbone dihedral angles and hydrogen bond patterns, were performed using GROMACS version 2025.1 [28]. Specifically, hydrogen bond analysis utilized the gmx hbond tool within GROMACS [29]. Secondary structure assignments were made using the Dictionary of Protein Secondary Structure (DSSP) algorithm [30], as implemented in the gmx dssp tool of GROMACS [31]. Schematic illustrations, including the viral genome maps (Fig. 1) and the phylogenetic tree visualization (Fig. 2), were created using BioRender (BioRender.com). Analysis of protein-DNA interactions, including the generation of residue-nucleotide contact maps (Fig. 5) and DNA helical parameter plots (Fig. 6), was performed using the DNAproDB web server. The analyses were based on existing crystal structures from the Protein Data Bank, specifically PDB ID: 1JJ4 for the HPV18 E2-DNA complex and PDB ID: 2AYB for the HPV6 E2-DNA complex.

### Sequence variation at the HPV52 E2 T309 homologous locus and structural modeling of the T309P variant
  Further analysis of the comprehensive MSA of 215 human HPV E2 protein sequences was utilized to understand the prevalence and potential significance of substitutions at the position homologous to Threonine 309 (T309) of the reference HPV52 E2 protein. This analysis revealed that Threonine at this position is relatively uncommon, present in only ~ 2.3% of the analyzed types. Proline, the residue resulting from the T309P substitution (genomic A3667C) found in the HPV52 B2 sublineage [38], was also infrequent, occurring in ~ 1.9% of types at this homologous site. Notably, our MSA identified Proline as the natively encoded residue at this position in the E2 proteins of high-risk HPV33 and HPV58 [39–41]. Conversely, Lysine (K) was the most prevalent amino acid at this homologous locus, found in ~ 60.9% of the analyzed E2 sequences, including those from other major high-risk types such as HPV35, HPV16, and HPV18.

  To investigate the structural impact of the T309P substitution which has been associated with increased oncogenic risk [42, 43], we modeled the C-terminal DNA-binding domains (DBD) of both wild-type (WT T309) and variant (P309) HPV52 E2 using AlphaFold3. The generated models exhibited good overall fold confidence, with high local confidence (pLDDT > 85) for the region encompassing residue 309 in both structures. Analysis of our AlphaFold model for the WT E2 indicated that T309 is located within a region displaying non-canonical helical features. This was evidenced by unusual backbone dihedral angles (Φ ≈ -82.3°, Ψ ≈ -6.0°), consistent with PyMOL and GROMACS analyses, and a disrupted α-helical hydrogen-bonding pattern, leading to a GROMACS classification of T309 as part of an ‘Extended Strand’. In the model of the T309P variant, the substitution introduced Proline with its characteristic backbone dihedral angles (Φ ≈ -65.1°, Ψ ≈ -27.5°), again corroborated by PyMOL and GROMACS. This substitution is predicted to result in several notable structural changes: a kink in the polypeptide backbone (Fig. 7), the expected loss of N-H hydrogen bond donor capability at position 309 due to the nature of proline, and a shift in local secondary structure as determined by DSSP analysis. Specifically, DSSP classified P309 as transitioning from an ‘Extended Strand’ (like T309 in WT) to a ‘β-Bridge’, while the adjacent HIS310 transitioned from ‘Extended Strand’ to ‘Coil’. These observations from our structural modeling indicate distinct conformational differences between the WT T309 and the P309 variant E2 DBDs.

## Discussion
  The striking spectrum of clinical outcomes from Human Papillomavirus (HPV) infection, which ranges from benign epithelial proliferations to malignancies, is fundamentally tied to the regulation of the viral life cycle [44, 45]. In this context, the E2 protein functions as a master regulator, governing critical viral processes including the initiation of DNA replication and the modulation of viral gene expression, particularly that of the E6 and E7 oncogenes [46, 47]. Because E2 is a principal determinant of these varied pathogenic potentials [1–3, 48], understanding the molecular determinants within the protein that are conserved for essential functions, versus those that have diverged to contribute to type-specific adaptations and differential oncogenicity, is of paramount importance.

  PyMOL analysis of AlphaFold-predicted E2 structures showed that highly conserved residues are clustered around the DNA-binding interface and the N-terminal region’s host interaction surface. This structural clustering suggests that minor mutations in these areas can significantly impact E2’s regulatory capability [7, 49]. This concept is reinforced by foundational work from the Gaston lab, which structurally compared the E2 DBDs of oncogenic HPV16 and non-oncogenic HPV6. Their findings revealed that while the core DNA recognition principles are conserved, subtle differences in local DNA conformation and protein-DNA contacts can exist, providing a direct precedent for how type-specific variations may influence function [50]. This principle is further exemplified by mechanistic work from the Morgan lab, which demonstrated that E2’s ability to ensure viral plasmid segregation during mitosis is critically dependent on its interaction with the host protein TopBP1, an interaction regulated by the phosphorylation of the conserved N-terminal residue Serine 23 [51], while other foundational work has shown that acetylation of a key conserved lysine is essential for viral replication [52].

  The structural basis for these critical E2 functions is clearly illustrated by the crystal structures of E2 in complex with its key binding partners. A key structural snapshot of the N-terminal domain of HPV18 E2 bound to its DNA helicase partner E1 is captured in the 1TUE crystal structure (35). This assembly provides a window into how E2’s transactivation domain (residues ∼1–200) mediates its “matchmaker” role during viral replication. In particular, the N1 region (residues 1–91) of E2 folds into three α-helices, which interface with helices α2 and α3 of the E1 helicase domain, forming an extensive protein–protein contact surface. Our conservation data indicate that E24 and E43, both fully or nearly invariant across human HPVs, help stabilize this interface by creating salt bridges with E1 (Arg447/622 and Arg454, respectively).

  Structurally, these contacts not only tether E2 to E1 but also shield E1’s oligomerization surface, supporting a modern view that E2 must dissociate before E1 can assemble into its hexameric helicase assembly [6, 35]. In line with current molecular virology models, small substitutions at these N‑terminal positions like Glu→Ala or Trp→Phe could undermine E2–E1 affinity and reduce viral replication efficiency. Although further biochemical validation is needed, such mutations might also modulate the specificity of E2 for particular E1 variants, opening new avenues for investigating how HPV adapts to different tissue niches or host factors. Overall, 1TUE crystal structure reveals that the N‑terminal E2 domain is not merely a passive “activation” module but is functionally central in orchestrating E1 recruitment and regulating helicase assembly, a concept that is echoed in our MSA results showing high conservation in these E2–E1 contact residues.

  In the crystal structure of high-risk HPV18 (PDB:1JJ4) [36], each E2 monomer positions its DNA-recognition helix along the major groove of the palindromic E2 binding site (E2BS). The crystal lattice captures hallmark interactions involving C301, N297, K300, Y304, R305 which together form charged or hydrogen-bonding contacts with bases and phosphates in the consensus 5’-ACCg(N)4cGGT-3’ motif. Although HPV6 is classified as a low-risk α HPV subtype, the overall DNA–protein architecture mirrors that of HPV18 (PDB:2AYB) [37]. The C-terminal region again folds into an antiparallel β-barrel that anchors helices contacting successive major grooves. Notable differences lie in the loop regions bridging β-strands, particularly residues near Cys295 and Cys298, which can form inter- or intramolecular disulfide bonds under oxidizing conditions. Despite these structural variations, the core DNA-contacting network involving N294, K297, C298, Y301, R302 remains conserved, consistent with our MSA results showing minimal divergence in key DNA-binding determinants. Notably, our conservation analysis shows these residues or their chemical equivalents remain invariant across most α and β HPV lineages, reinforcing their indispensable function in DNA major groove recognition.

  Overlaying the protein–DNA interfaces in 1JJ4 and 2AYB crystal structures clarified how the β-strands and recognition helices of E2 adopt an induced-fit mechanism, bending the DNA and narrowing the minor groove in a sequence-dependent manner [37, 53]. Our data shows that high conservation scores cluster in the hydrophobic core of the C-terminal β-barrel (e.g., Trp320 in HPV18 E2), ensuring stable dimerization. Charged and polar residues along the rim of the major groove contact site (Like Arg305 in HPV18 E2 or the homologous Arg in HPV6 E2), mediating base-specific and backbone phosphate contacts. Such structural invariance, as seen in both HPV18 and HPV6 E2 proteins, complements our MSA findings: these amino acids remain highly conserved across human HPVs, underscoring their fundamental importance in viral replication and genome maintenance, consistent with earlier detailed analyses of E2-DNA binding properties [50].

  Beyond the conservation of specific residues, our alignments also reveal a strong conservation of charge at key functional surfaces, which provides further insight into structural constraints. In the C-terminal DNA-binding domain, the cluster of highly conserved basic residues (Lysine and Arginine) on the recognition helices is critical. This positive charge is essential for electrostatic interactions with the negatively charged phosphate backbone of the E2BS DNA, a mechanism known to be crucial for the stability of the protein-DNA complex and for modulating the energy required for DNA bending. Conversely, in the N-terminal transactivation domain, it is the conservation of negatively charged acidic regions that is critical for function. Foundational work on BPV-1 E2 identified specific acidic motifs that are indispensable for transcriptional activation, likely by mediating interactions with host co-activator proteins [54]. Thus, the conservation of distinct charge characteristics in different E2 domains reflects their specialized roles in either direct DNA engagement or the recruitment of the host’s transcriptional machinery.

  Both crystal structures in 1JJ4 and 2AYB emphasize that while all E2 proteins can bend DNA toward the protein surface, different HPVs exhibit distinct preferences for A/T-rich or flexible “pre-bent” sequences [7]. Our alignments suggest that α HPVs harbor strategically positioned basic residues (such as arginines and lysines) that likely contribute significantly to E2-DNA complex stability through strong electrostatic interactions, primarily with the DNA phosphate backbone (as exemplified by contacts seen in structures like HPV18 E2-DNA [36]. This structure–function relationship echoes our MSA-based predictions, where species-specific clustering emerges despite the overarching conservation of the canonical DNA-binding fold.

  Finally, examining the crystal structures in 1JJ4 and 2AYB with our conservation analysis highlighted how subtle sequence changes in the DNA spacer region or in the E2 DBD can tip the balance of oncogenic risk [14–16, 55, 56]. Notably, minor groove widths can be crucial for E2 binding [12], with the presence of proline (T309P in HPV52 B2) near the DNA recognition helix possibly reshaping or destabilizing the interface. This change likely leads to weakening of the E2-DNA interaction. To add, spacer variation (e.g., central AT tracts) can exploit the conserved Arg/Lys or Tyr/Phe residues to alter binding affinity.

  Our comparative analysis reinforces that various regions of the HPV E2 protein are conserved across human-specific Alpha genera in both its N-terminal transactivation domain and C-terminal DNA-binding domain while Beta and Gamma genera exhibit sequence variation likely due to ecological niches. Alpha sequences demonstrated invariance in the N-terminus, possibly correlating with their capacity to efficiently bind or recruit a diverse range of host co-factors essential for their life cycle [57, 58]. Across all comparisons, the DBD’s robust conservation emphasizes the indispensability of precise and specific DNA–protein interactions in regulating viral oncogenes [56]. Notably, the strong conservation of DNA-contacting residues corroborates previous observations that E2’s structural integrity is key for accurate recognition of palindromic LCR sites. Minimal changes to the E2BS spacer region can dramatically alter E2 binding [53, 59].

  Despite universally conserved features crucial for E2’s fundamental roles, HPV types exhibit distinct strategies for genome regulation. This includes variations in E2 binding site (E2BS) characteristics, where Alpha HPVs often favor A/T-rich spacers promoting a “pre-bent” DNA conformation, contrasting with Beta HPVs’ reportedly reduced sensitivity to spacer GC content [7, 16, 60]. Beyond cis-regulatory elements, our study explored single-residue alterations within the E2 protein itself, focusing on the T309P substitution (genomic A3667C) in HPV52 E2. HPV52 is recognized as a prevalent oncogenic genotype, especially in East Asian populations [39]. This variant is characteristic of the HPV52 B2 sublineage [38], which has been associated with heightened pathogenic potential in certain populations [42, 43].

  Our structural modeling revealed that the T309P substitution could induce significant conformational changes within the HPV52 E2 DBD, including a pronounced polypeptide kink and alterations to local secondary structure, when compared to the wild-type T309. These observed structural modifications strongly suggest that the T309P mutation alters the E2 DNA-binding region, potentially impacting its DNA binding affinity and subsequent regulatory functions. The significance of a Proline at this specific locus (position 309 in HPV52 E2 or its homolog) is further illuminated by our comprehensive MSA findings. While Proline is relatively uncommon overall (~ 1.9%) at this homologous site across the 215 human HPV E2 sequences analyzed, our alignment identified Proline as the natively encoded residue in the E2 proteins of other recognized high-risk types, namely HPV33 and HPV58 [40, 41]. This identification of native Proline in these high-risk types is noteworthy, as reviews primarily focused on cataloging published E2 DBD mutations may not always emphasize such native residue characteristics unless previously linked to a distinct functional variant. The inherent presence of Proline at this locus in HPV33 and HPV58 E2 suggests that the structural features predicted for the HPV52 T309P variant, such as the induced kink, might represent shared structural motifs impacting E2 function in these oncogenic types.

  Conversely, our comprehensive alignment shows that the most prevalent amino acid at this homologous position is Lysine (K), found in ~ 60.9% of the E2 sequences analyzed. Our MSA confirms this Lysine is present in other major high-risk types such as HPV35, HPV16, and HPV18. Such single-residue distinctions, a variant Proline in HPV52 B2, native Proline in HPV33/58, or the common Lysine in HPV16/18/35 and many others, at this or other critical E2 DBD sites, can potentially tilt the balance between viral integration and normal replication by altering E2’s regulatory functions. The broader functional impact of E2 DBD variations is underscored by findings in other papillomaviruses; for instance, an A338V mutation in HPV-2 E2 reportedly increased DNA binding and altered transcriptional regulation [61]. This highlights that while a proline at the residue 309-equivalent position might represent one type-specific adaptation influencing oncogenic pathways in certain HPVs, other high-risk types like HPV16, HPV18, and HPV35 appear to utilize altered origin binding due to binding site alterations for their oncogenic activity [15].

  This predicted structural alteration in HPV52 B2 variant and its potential impact on DNA binding affinity and regulatory function align with previous foundational work exploring the role of conserved residues within the E2 DNA-binding domain. For example, Grossel et al. demonstrated that a highly conserved cysteine (C340) in the DNA-contact helix of bovine papillomavirus type 1 (BPV-1) E2 was essential for transcriptional activation but, surprisingly, not for viral DNA replication. Despite being fully competent for DNA binding, dimerization, and nuclear localization, these BPV-1 E2 mutants were unable to activate E2-dependent promoters, leading the authors to propose that the residue mediates a critical interaction with a cellular transcription factor [62]. In a compelling contrast, recent in vivo work by Gonzalez et al. on murine papillomavirus 1 (MmuPV1) revealed that the analogous cysteine was absolutely required for viral replication and pathogenesis, highlighting that the functional role of this conserved residue can diverge significantly across different papillomavirus types [63]. Analogously, the pronounced kink and altered local secondary structure we observe with the HPV52 T309P substitution could similarly disrupt specific protein-protein interactions essential for E2’s full regulatory capability, potentially explaining its link to oncogenicity even if core DNA recognition is maintained. Our findings on E2 protein variation complement those related to changes in cis-acting elements, such as the 12-bp LCR region highlighted in our lab’s previous work [14, 15]. offering a more integrated mechanistic basis for how minor sequence modifications in either the E2BS or the E2 protein itself might drive oncogenic progression in high-risk HPV types [56].

  Our in-silico findings, which predict that the T309P variant could alter E2 regulatory function, gain clinical significance from studies associating the HPV52 B2 sublineage, defined by this mutation, with heightened pathogenic potential. While the T309P variant itself is not yet cataloged in major somatic cancer databases, its presence as a lineage marker in a higher-risk sub-type suggests it could contribute to the oncogenic phenotype. This underscores a central paradox in HPV biology: while functional E2 represses the E6 and E7 oncogenes, the loss of E2 function is a key event in carcinogenesis. This loss often occurs when the viral genome integrates into host DNA, disrupting the E2 gene and leading to oncogene overexpression and viral genome instability [64].

  This dual role of E2 has significant therapeutic implications, particularly in HPV-positive head and neck squamous cell carcinomas (HNSCCs), where E2 expression is often retained and serves as a favorable prognostic marker [65]. In these tumors, E2 is not the target to eliminate but rather a potential therapeutic tool. Strategies aimed at restoring or enhancing E2’s tumor-suppressive functions [66], for example by delivering E2 mimetics or small molecules that stabilize its repressive conformation, could selectively inhibit the growth of E2-positive cancer cells. The conserved structural pockets and functional surfaces identified in our analysis could serve as rational starting points for the design of such novel therapeutics.


## Conclusions
  In this study, we synthesized broad and genera-specific MSAs of human HPV E2 proteins, identifying core residues that remain strongly conserved in both the N-terminal transactivation domain and the C-terminal DNA-binding domain. By mapping these residues onto AlphaFold models, we visualized clusters critical for DNA recognition and viral transcriptional regulation.

  Our analyses highlight how small modifications can lead to potential cancer-driving phenotypes. This includes the T309P substitution in the E2 coding region of HPV52 sublineage B2, which, as our modeling suggests, likely induces conformational changes within the DNA-binding domain that may impact its regulatory functions. Furthermore, established variations in E2 binding site spacer regions are also known to affect E2 activity and viral pathogenesis. These findings on E2 conservation, the structural and functional impact of specific E2 mutations, and E2BS variations illuminate HPV’s evolutionary trajectory, wherein certain clade-specific adaptations can finely tune the virus’s replicative and oncogenic capacities. Given E2’s essential function in viral genome maintenance, these conserved regions represent attractive therapeutic targets. Future work should clarify the precise biochemical effects of LCR and/or E2 mutations on oncogene control, particularly in high-risk Alpha HPVs that drive the most severe clinical outcomes.
