# A novel small molecule NJH-13 induces pyroptosis via the Ca2+ driven AKT-FOXO1-GSDME signaling pathway in NSCLC by targeting TRPV5


## Graphical abstract
  Graphical abstract


## Introduction
  Lung cancer, particularly non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC; approximately 85 % of all cases), is known for its high incidence and high mortality rate [1], [2]. Despite advances in tumor biology and refined treatment strategies, significant challenges persist [3], [4]. Late-stage NSCLC patients have a poor prognosis due to high rates of disease relapse. Immunotherapies, including immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), have improved overall survival. However, additional agents, particularly those able to overcome chemoresistance, are needed to enhance clinical outcomes.

  Pyroptosis is a type of lytic programmed cell death (PCD) accompanied by membrane pore formation and cytokine release [5]. Manipulating pyroptosis in cancer cells is being explored as a novel therapeutic strategy for cancer. The gasdermin protein family (GSDMs), a group of pore-forming proteins, are the executioners of pyroptosis. All GSDMs except DFNB59 possess an N-terminal (NT) effector domain and a C-terminal (CT) inhibitory domain [6], [7]. The NT domains can form transmembrane pores by binding to the acidic phospholipids of membranes and completing oligomerization [6], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12]. Cleavage and activation of GSDMs can be mediated by inflammatory caspases, resulting in pore formation, proinflammatory cytokines release, cellular contents leakage, and ultimately lytic cell death [13], [14], [15], [16]. Pyroptosis have been reported to exert its tumor-suppressive function and evokes anti-tumor immunity. However, the expression of most GSDMs, including GSDME, is often suppressed in cancers. Ectopic expression of GSDME in tumors can inhibit tumor growth by promoting pyroptosis [17]. Thus, GSDME, a tumor suppressor gene, is a promising target for cancer therapy.

  Calcium ions serve as a crucial second messenger regulating virtually every aspect of cellular processes [18]. Calcium plays a key role in multiple cellular decisions, including life and death. Intracellular Ca2+ overload and continuous cellular stress induce numerous types of cellular damage or even death. Hence, calcium ion homeostasis modulation is important for cellular survival and bioenergetics. Emerging evidence has revealed that calcium signaling is related to pyroptosis. Studies have demonstrated that calcium is involved in NLRP3 inflammasome activation, which is critical for classical pyroptosis [5], [11], [19], [20], [21], [22]. Recent studies have shown that Ca2+ overload contributes to GSDME mediated pyroptosis [23], [24], [25]. Further investigation is necessary to clarify the complex role of calcium signaling in modulating pyroptosis.

  There is a reciprocal interaction between calcium signaling and reactive oxygen species (ROS) signaling. ROS are a family of oxygen free radicals [26]. ROS formation is induced by a variety of mechanisms, with mitochondria serving as a primary source via the respiratory chain and NADPH oxidase (NOX) [27]. Under normal physiological conditions, mitochondria are capable of maintaining ROS homeostasis to preserve cellular metabolic homeostasis [28], [29]. Excessive calcium accumulation in mitochondria results in the opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP), releasing ROS and apoptotic factors into the cytoplasm [30]. ROS can further target calcium channels, leading to increased ER calcium release of calcium and, in turn, increased ROS levels [30], [31]. In addition, ROS play an important role in pyroptosis. Previous studies have shown that elevated ROS can induce the activation of caspases, resulting in the cleavage of GSDMs, which triggers pyroptosis [5], [32], [33], [34], [35], [36], [37], [38]. Both calcium and ROS act as signaling molecules that are integral to cellular homeostasis and are considered important mediators of cell death.

  Natural products are rich resources for drug discovery. The aim of this study was to screen for pyroptosis inducers from a library of 1043 medicinal plant-derived and natural-like heterocyclic compounds. NJH-13, an N-containing heterocycle, was identified to induce necrotic cell death in NSCLC cells. Its selectivity towards lung cancer cell lines was confirmed through toxicity assays against the normal human lung epithelial cell line BEAS-2B, and the NSCLC cell lines H1975 and H1299. Knockdown of GSDME rescued the cell death induced by NJH-13, supporting the hypothesis that pyroptotic cell death induced by NJH-13 was executed by GSDME. DARTS assays identified TRPV5 as a potential target of NJH-13. TRPV5 is a calcium-selective TRP channel critical to calcium homeostasis. Treatment with NJH-13 led to immediate increases in intracellular calcium levels, which were reversed by calcium chelators, indicating a role for calcium in mediating NJH-13-induced cell death. Calcium accumulation leads to the accumulation of ROS in both the mitochondria and the cytoplasm, which subsequently contributes to pyroptotic cell death. RNA-seq analysis indicated that the AKT-FOXO1 signaling pathway was involved in the induction of pyroptosis. ChIP and dual luciferase assays revealed that FOXO1 bound directly to GSDME promoter regions, thus initiating its transcriptional activity, promoting GSDME-mediated pyroptosis. Results from this study suggested that TRPV5 is a promising therapeutic target and that NJH-13 is a potential pyroptosis inducer for treating NSCLC patients.


## Materials and methods

### Cell culture and reagents
  H1975, H1299, and BEAS-2B cells were kindly provided by Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences. H1975 and H1299 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin solution (P/S), and BEAS-2B cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10 % FBS and 1 % P/S. All the cells were incubated at 37℃ with 5 % CO2. Other detailed information about the antibodies and reagents used in this study was provided in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2.


### Synthesis of NJH-13
  

  Introduce 2,4,5,6-tetrafluoroisophthalonitrile 1a (1.0 mmol) and methyl 2-(pyridin-2-yl) acetate 2a (1.1 mmol) into a 25 mL round-bottom flask containing 5 mL of water. Subsequently, add triethylamine (1.5 mmol) to the mixture. Reflux the mixture for approximately 9 h, monitoring the progress via TLC until the substrate 2,4,5,6-tetrafluoroisophthalonitrile 1a is completely consumed. Once the reaction is complete, allow the mixture to cool to room temperature. Filter the mixture using a suction funnel, wash with a small amount of ethyl acetate, or recrystallize it using ethyl acetate, and dry under an infrared lamp to obtain a red or yellow solid. This procedure yielded a series of compounds designated as NJH-13, achieving an overall yield of 87 %.

  Red solid; Mp: 143.4–144.4 °C; IR (KBr): 3424, 2955, 2235, 1718, 1653, 1631, 1605, 1516, 1402, 1137, 789 cm−1; 1H NMR (600 MHz, CDCl3) δ: 9.24 (s, 1H, NH), 9.04 (d, J = 7.4 Hz, 1H, ArH), 7.61 (d, J = 9.3 Hz, 1H, ArH), 7.34–7.31 (m, 1H, ArH), 6.72 (t, J = 6.7 Hz, 1H, ArH), 3.84 (s, 3H, CH3); 13C NMR (150 MHz, CDCl3) δ: 167.2, 159.4 (d, J1 = 261.0 Hz), 150.4, 149.8 (d, J1 = 255.0 Hz), 142.6, 139.1, 134.8, 129.5, 128.8 (d, J3 = 12.0 Hz), 121.4, 113.5, 108.6, 104.2, 94.3, 86.6 (d, J2 = 22.5 Hz), 52.8; 19F NMR (547 MHz, CDCl3) δ: −112.2 (d, J = 12.0 Hz, 1F), −126.6 (d, J = 24.0 Hz, 1F), −142.3--142.4 (m, 1F); HRMS (TOF ES+): m/z calcd for C16H9F3N3O2 [(M + H)+], 332.0641; found, 332.0638.


### Hoechst 33342/propidium iodide (PI) stanning assay and live-cell imaging
  Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 4 × 105 cells/well and treated with NJH-13, NJH-13 combined with z-VAD (pan-caspases inhibitor), necrostatin-1 (RIPK1 inhibitor), NAC (ROS scavenger), or SC79 (AKT activator). All the Cells were stained with Hoechst 33342 (5 µg/mL) and PI (2 µg/mL) to distinguish lytic cells from apoptotic cells. Images were captured by fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany) with 10× magnification. The percentage of PI positive cells was calculated via ImageJ software.

  The dynamic changes in the morphology of H1975 and H1299 cells were recorded in real-time via IncuCyte S3. Briefly, cells were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with NJH-13 and other reagents. Live cell images were captured at 0.5 h intervals for 24 h. For Annexin V and PI staining, cells were stained according to the manufacturer’s instructions.


### Drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS) assay
  The cells were lysed with NP-40 lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail. The lysates were incubated with NJH-13 or DMSO for 1 h. After incubation, the lysates were divided into 20 µL aliquots and treated with pronase E for 20 min. The samples were analyzed via a western blot assay and silver staining.


### Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)
  CETSA was performed as described previously [39]. The cells were collected after NJH-13 treatment and resuspended in PBS containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail. The supernatants were divided into several tubes and heated at the designated temperature ranging from 25 to 55℃. The proteins were detected via western blot assay.


### Molecular docking
  Molecular docking studies were analyzed with the AutoDock Vina program as described previously [40]. Briefly, Chewdraw was used to draw the 3D structure of NJH-13, and the published structure of TRPV5 (PDB: 5oeo) was downloaded from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/). The format of NJH-13 and TRPV5 are converted to PDBQT format using MGLTools (1.5.7). The molecular docking of NJH-13 and TRPV5 was carried out by Autodock Vina. The best matching posture was selected according to the docking score, and Visualized by Pymol.


### Western blot assay
  Cells were lysed with RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with a protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail. The proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. The membrane was blocked with 5 % fat-free milk for 1 h at room temperature, and then incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4℃. Then, the membrane was incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h. The bands on the membrane were visualized with ECL and captured with a ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA).


### shRNA infection, siRNA and plasmid transfection
  The cells were infected with shRNAs (shcon, shGSDME)-expressing lentivirus and selected with puromycin for stable cell lines. Constitutively active AKT (mutations with T308D and S473D; AKTDD) was transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The siRNAs targeting FOXO1 and TRPV5 were transfected with Lipofectamine RNAiMax (Invitrogen, USA). Sequences of shRNAs and siRNAs are listed in Supplementary Table S3.


### RNA extraction and RT–qPCR assay
  The cells were seeded in 12-well plates and treated with NJH-13 for 3 h. Total RNA was extracted with RNA-easy TM isolation reagent (Vazyme, China) and reversed transcripted. The qPCR analysis was performed with a NovoStart® SYBR qPCR SuperMix Plus kit (Novoprotein, China). The relative mRNA levels of target genes were analyzed via the 2-ΔΔCT method [41]. The primers are listed in the Supplementary Table S3.


### Xenograft tumor models
  The BALB/c nude mice (purchased from Jiangsu Jicui Yaokang Biotech) were randomized into 2 groups. All animals were housed with free access to food and water. H1975 cells (4 × 106) were injected subcutaneously into the flank of the mice. After the xenografts were established, the mice were intraperitoneally administered with DMSO or NJH-13 (10 mg/kg) for three times [42]. The body weights and the tumor volumes were monitored every two days. The volume = 1/2 × length × width2. The tumor and organ tissues were collected on day 38. Immunohistochemistry (IHC), hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, and western blot assays were used to analyze the effect of NJH-13 on the xenograft tumors.


### Ethics statement
  All animal procedures were conducted according to the ethical policies and procedures approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Kunming Institute of Botany, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Approval no. Kib202107001).


### Statistical analysis
  All the experiments were repeated in triplicates. All results are presented as Mean ± SD. Student’s t-test (two-sided) or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for statistical analysis. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001 were considered as significant different. p ≥ 0.05 was considered not significant (ns).


## Results

### NJH-13 induced pyroptotic cell death in lung cancer cells
  To identify potential pyroptosis inducers from medicinal plant-derived and natural-like heterocyclic compound library containing 1043 compounds, MTT assays and live cell imaging were carried out. NJH-13 (Fig. 1A) was identified with typical morphological changes associated with pyroptotic cell death. NJH-13 inhibited cell viability in H1975 (IC50 = 3.72 ± 0.06 µM) and H1299 (IC50 = 6.23 ± 0.19 µM) cells while its IC50 value in normal human bronchial epithelial cell line BEAS-2B (IC50 = 20.95 ± 0.61 µM) was much greater (Fig. 1B). NJH-13 preferentially targeted lung cancer cells. The morphological changes observed in cancer cells treated with NJH-13 included cytoplasmic swelling with large bubbles, membrane rupture and lytic cell death (Fig. 1C), suggesting that NJH-13 induced necrotic cell death. To further substantiate the type of cell death mediated by NJH-13, H1975 and H1299 cells were stained with Hoechst 33342/PI or Annexin V/PI. The enhanced fluorescence intensity of PI indicated that NJH-13-induced cell death was accompanied with the loss of membrane integrity (Figs. 1D, E and S1A, B). Furthermore, the LDH detection assay revealed that NJH-13 facilitated LDH release (Figs. 1F, G and S1C). However, no pyroptotic morphology (Fig. 1C) or LDH release was detected in BEAS-2B cells treated with the same dose of NJH-13 (Fig. 1H). In addition, the ELISA results indicated that NJH-13 triggered the release of IL-18 and IL-1β (Figs. 1I, J and S1D, E), which are two proinflammatory cytokines normally detected in cells undergoing pyroptosis. Pyroptosis is executed by GSDM family members after their cleavage into active N terminal GSDMs by corresponding caspases (such as caspase-1, -4, -5, and -11) [12], [22], [43]. As shown in Fig. 1K, NJH-13 increased the protein levels of cleaved caspase-3 and N-GSDME, which could be reversed by the pan-caspase inhibitor z-VAD-FMK (z-VAD, Fig. 1K). The cell death induced by NJH-13 was partially rescued by z-VAD but not by the necroptosis inhibitor necrostatin-1 (necro-1) (Figs. 1L–N and S1F–J). GSDMD and GSDME are well-studied pyroptosis executioners. NJH-13 induced the cleavage of caspase 3 and GSDME in a dose-, and time-dependent manner (Figs. 1O–R and S1K, L). No significant alteration in the protein levels of caspase-1 and GSDMD was detected in H1975 cells (Fig. S1M and N). The GSDMD inhibitor JC2-11 and NLRP3 inhibitor CY-09 did not rescue the cell death induced by NJH-13 in H1975 cells (Fig. S1O and P). Knockdown of GSDME in H1975 and H1299 cells rescued the pyroptotic cells induced by NJH-13 (Figs. 1S–U and S2A–G), suggesting that GSDME is the executioner of NJH-13 triggered pyroptosis. ### Identification of TRPV5 as a putative cellular target of NJH-13
  To reveal the cellular target of NJH-13 in inducing pyroptosis in NSCLC cell lines, DARTS was performed. The silver staining results and the western blot results indicated that transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily V member 5 (TRPV5) exhibited a higher stability upon NJH-13 incubation (Fig. 2A–C), suggesting that TRPV5 is a potential target protein for NJH-13. In addition, the results of the cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) confirmed a higher thermal stability of TRPV5 in NJH-13-treated cells. As demonstrated in Fig. 2D, NJH-13 increased the thermal stability of TRPV5 within the temperature range of 37–53 °C. The binding pocket of NJH-13 with TRPV5 was further explored by molecular docking assay. The endogenous ligand phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PI(4,5)P2) has been reported to be an agonist of TRPV5 [44]. Our in silico results showed that NJH-13 occupied a binding pocket in TRPV5 similar to that as PI(4,5)P2, with a binding affinity of –7.8 kcal/mol for NJH-13 and a binding affinity of –6.7 kcal/mol for PI(4,5)P2 (Fig. 2E and F). TRPV5 was involved in stabilizing the binding of NJH-13 through hydrogen bonds, similar to the way of the endogenous ligand PI(4,5)P2. To investigate whether TRPV5 mediates NJH-13-induced pyroptosis, TRPV5 was knocked down using small interfering RNA (siRNA). Knockdown of TRPV5 resulted in a reduction in cell viability, indicating that TRPV5 contributed to pyroptotic cell death induced by NJH-13 (Fig. 2G–I). Pharmacological inhibition of TRPV5 with econazole or gentamicin rescued NJH-13-induced cell death (Fig. S3A–E). The inhibition of AKT, activation of FOXO1 and caspase-3-GSDME was reversed by econazole and gentamicin as well (Fig. S3F–I). Thus, both genetic ablation or pharmacological inhibition of TRPV5 rescued the pyroptosis induced by NJH-13. Moreover, TRPV5 is correlated with poorer prognosis in lung cancer patients (https://www.kmplot.com) [45]. In the TRPV5 high expression cohort, patients showed a median survival of 56.5 months compared to an 80.0 months median survival in the TRPV5 low expression cohort (Fig. 2J). ### NJH-13-induced pyroptosis via ROS accumulation induced by Ca2+ overload
  TRPV5 is a calcium-selective TRP channel, which mediates Ca2+ influx into cells. The intracellular Ca2+ levels were significantly increased after the cells were treated with NJH-13 (Figs. 3A and S4A). The Ca2+ chelator EGTA effectively dampened the increase in Ca2+ and rescued the pyroptosis induced by NJH-13 (Figs. 3B–G and S4B–I). Similarly, the intracellular Ca2+ chelator BAPTA-AM attenuated pyroptotic cell death induced by NJH-13 (Figs. 3E–G and S4C–I). Thus, Ca2+ overload is a critical triggering event for NJH-13-induced pyroptosis.Fig. 3NJH-13 triggered pyroptosis via Ca2+ and ROS overload. A. Inhibitory effects of EGTA (200 µM) on Ca2+ levels in H1975 treated with NJH-13. D-F. The Ca2+ chelators EGTA (200 µM) and BAPTA-AM (10 µM) significantly rescued the cell death induced by NJH-13. G.   Ca2+ and ROS are irreplaceable signaling molecules that coordinate physiological and pathophysiological processes [31]. Studies have shown that increases in intracellular Ca2+ and ROS induce caspase-3 activation, which may cause pyroptosis [16], [32]. There is a mutual interplay between calcium signaling and ROS signaling. The role of ROS was further investigated. Chelation of ROS with NAC significantly decreased cell death induced by NJH-13 (Figs. 3H–K and S5A–D). The protein levels of cleaved caspase-3 and N-GSDME also decreased when cells were treated with NJH-13 and NAC (Fig. 1K). In addition, intracellular ROS levels were dramatically inhibited by EGTA (Figs. 3L, M and S5E), indicating that the increase in calcium preceded the ROS accumulation when the cells were treated with NJH-13.

  Mitochondria are critical organelles for the regulation of ROS homeostasis in cells. High levels of calcium can stimulate respiratory chain activity, leading to mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) accumulation [28]. In recent studies, mtROS have also been reported to contribute to pyroptosis [33], [34], [46], [47], [48]. To investigate whether Ca2+ overload led to mtROS accumulation in NJH-13-induced pyroptosis, mtROS levels were detected with MitoSOX. NJH-13 promoted mtROS accumulation, which was effectively prevented by incubation with EGTA and NAC (Fig. 3N, Fig. S5F). Mitochondrial potential sensor JC-1 analysis showed that NJH-13 induced mitochondrial dysfunction and decreased the mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) (Fig. S5G). These results indicated that NJH-13 increased the Ca2+ levels by activating TRPV5, leading to increased Ca2+ to stimulate mtROS generation, resulting in mitochondrial dysfunction.


### NJH-13 induced pyroptosis via the AKT/FOXO1 signaling pathway
  To further investigate the molecular mechanism underlying how NJH-13 regulates pyroptosis, RNA-seq analysis was carried out. A total of 4511 mRNAs were upregulated and 3981 mRNAs were downregulated between NJH-13- and DMSO-treated cells (Fig. 4A). Notably, insulin resistance and the phosphatidylinositol signaling system play a role in NJH-13-induced pyroptosis (Fig. 4B and Table S4). Protein kinase B (PKB or AKT) plays a vital role in cell survival, growth and death, including ferroptosis and pyroptosis [49], [50], [51]. NJH-13 treatment attenuated the phosphorylation of AKT (Fig. 4C). To explore whether NJH-13 regulated pyroptosis through the AKT signaling pathway, the AKT activator SC79 was used together with NJH-13 in H1975. SC79 rescued pyroptotic cell death induced by NJH-13, as evidenced by decreased cleaved GSDME, less membrane rupture and less IL-18 release (Figs. 4D–I and S6A, B). Consistently, cells transfected with constitutively active AKTDD (AKTT308D, S473D), also reduced the cell death upon NJH-13 treatment (Fig. 4J–N). Moreover, treatment of cells with uprosertib, an AKT inhibitor, increased the cytotoxicity of NJH-13 (Fig. S6C and D).   Forkhead box O1 (FOXO1), an important PI3K-AKT downstream effector, is a transcription factor that mediates many cellular processes, including oxidative stress, apoptosis and DNA repair, and appears to be a therapeutic target in lung cancer [52]. Phosphorylation of FOXO1 by AKT retains FOXO1 in cytoplasm and inhibits the functions of FOXO1. MAFF, the downstream gene of FOXO1, was upregulated upon NJH-13 treatment (Fig. 4A). Besides, FOXO1 has been reported to transactivate GSDMD [53]. To further investigate the role of the FOXO1 axis in NJH-13-triggered pyroptosis, the mRNA expression levels of FOXO1, MAFF, GSDMD and GSDME were checked. As shown in Fig. 5A, NJH-13 upregulated MAFF and GSDMD at the transcriptional level. Interestingly, the mRNA levels of GSDME were also upregulated. NJH-13 attenuated the phosphorylation of FOXO1 (Fig. 5B and C). The phosphorylation state of FOXO1 and the increased levels of N-GSMDE were reversed by z-VAD, SC79, EGTA, and NAC in H1975 cells (Fig. 5C). The knockdown of FOXO1 decreased the mRNA levels of GSDME and partially rescued NJH-13-mediated pyroptosis (Figs. 5D–I and S6E). To investigate whether FOXO1 can directly transactivate GSDME, a ChIP assay was carried out. Using JASPAR database (https://jaspar.genereg.net), the potential FOXO1 binding elements in the promoter region of GSDME were predicted (Fig. 5J). ChIP analysis revealed that endogenous FOXO1 occupied the +543 ∼ +703 bp promoter region of the GSDME gene (Fig. 5K–M). A dual luciferase assay confirmed the transactivation of GSDME by NJH-13-activated FOXO1 (Fig. 5N). ### NJH-13 retarded tumor growth in vivo
  The in vivo antitumor effects of NJH-13 was evaluated in H1975 xenograft tumors. Compared with the control group, only three doses of NJH-13 (10 mg/kg) effectively suppressed tumor growth, with respect to both tumor weight and tumor volume (Figs. 6A–E and S6F). The levels of Ki-67 were also decreased in NJH-13-treated tumors (Fig. 6F). The upregulated levels of cleaved caspase-3 and N-GSDME, and the downregulated levels of p-AKT and p-FOXO1 were observed in NJH-13-treated tumors (Figs. 6G, H and S6G). NJH-13 did not exhibit obvious toxicity to major organs, and didn’t change the body weights of mice (Figs. 6I, J and S6H). ## Discussion
  Here, we have shown that NJH-13 induces GSDME-mediated pyroptosis by targeting TRPV5 in NSCLC. Knockdown of TRPV5 largely rescued cells treated with NJH-13. Increased calcium levels induced the generation of ROS, leading to oxidative stress, including mitochondrial ROS accumulation. Further RNA-seq analysis revealed that the PI3K-AKT signaling was downregulated. NJH-13 decreased the levels of p-AKT. AKT can phosphorylate FOXO1, leading to its cytoplasmic retention and inhibition of FOXO1-mediated transcription. A decreased level of p-FOXO1 was observed, resulting in the activation of FOXO1 downstream genes. Notably, FOXO1 directly interacted with the GSDME promoter, enhancing the transcription of GSDME. Pharmacological and genetic activation of AKT or inhibition of FOXO1 partially rescued NJH-13-induced pyroptotic cell death, supporting the critical role of the AKT-FOXO1 signaling in NJH-13-induced pyroptosis. NJH-13 suppressed tumor growth in vivo with only three doses of NJH-13, suggesting its potential for development as a pyroptosis inducer in cancer treatment.

  Transient receptor potential vanilloid (TRPV) channels play critical roles in pathophysiological processes, such as the immunological response and cancer progression, by regulating the calcium homeostasis [[54], [55]]. TRPV5 is highly selective Ca2+ channels [54], [56]. Typically, TRPV5 can be activated by the endogenous modulators PI(4,5)P2 to regulate intracellular calcium homeostasis. Molecular docking experiment revealed that NJH-13 occupied the similar binding pocket as PI(4,5)P2. Increased Ca2+ levels were detected upon NJH-13 treatment, and pyroptotic cell death was rescued by the Ca2+ chelators EGTA and BAPTA-AM, highlighting the critical role of calcium signaling in NJH-13-induced cell death. TRP channels have been implicated in tumor suppression in cancers. For instance, TRPV1 agonists, endovanilloids, and the synthetic vanilloid arvanil, induce cell death in high-grade astrocytomas, while the activation of TRPV4 by cannabidiol (CBD) initiates lethal mitophagy in glioma [57], [58]. In this study, we showed that TRPV5 activation facilitated calcium flux induced by NJH-13. Moreover, TRPV5 levels are also correlated with poor survival in lung cancer patients. The induction of pyroptosis via targeting TRPV5 may reduce the chemoresistance in cancer therapy. These findings indicated that TRPV5 is a potential target and biomarker in the context of lung cancer, especially NSCLC.

  ROS and Ca2+ are reciprocally regulated. As indispensable second messengers within cells, the intracellular levels of Ca2+ and ROS is pivotal in regulating programmed cell death, including pyroptosis [5], [23], [36], [59]. In this study, NJH-13-induced pyroptosis was accompanied with the accumulation of ROS and Ca2+. The ROS scavenger or Ca2+ chelators NAC, EGTA and BAPTA-AM effectively reversed the NJH-13-induced cell death. Mitochondria are key organelles for ROS homeostasis in cells. It has been reported that Ca2+ affects the production of mtROS by electron transport chain (ETC), NOX1, NOX2 and NOX5 [31]. In this study, the results revealed that mtROS accumulated and that the mitochondrial membrane potential decreased with NJH-13 treatment. It could further increase calcium efflux from the cytosol, creating a positive feedback loop that exacerbates oxidative stress and imbalance in calcium homeostasis.

  AKT signaling promotes cell survival under many physiological and pathological conditions. KEGG analysis revealed decreased gene enrichment in PI3K-AKT-related signaling system. AKT is involved in GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis by activating the NLRP3 inflammasome complex [51], [53]. However, few studies have reported the function of AKT in GSDME-dependent pyroptosis. The results from our current study showed that NJH-13 inhibited AKT activation. The AKT activator SC79 and the ectopic expression of constitutively active AKT (AKTDD) partially rescued the cell death induced by NJH-13, indicating that AKT is a critical regulator of NJH-13-induced pyroptosis. In addition, the relationships between AKT and TRPVs are still being uncovered. TRPVs exert dual roles in regulating the AKT signaling pathway [58], [60]. Calcium flux induced by TRPV4 activation triggers lethal mitophagy in glioma via the ATF4-DDIT3-TRIB3-AKT1-MTOR axis. AKT-MTOR was inhibited upon TRPV4 activation [58]. Few studies have reported the role of TRPV5 in the regulation of AKT. Loss of Trpv5/6 in ionocytes, a type of Ca2+ transporting epithelial cells in zebrafish, resulted in increased levels of p-Akt under normal extracellular [Ca2+] [61]. In this study, we showed that activation of TRPV5 by NJH-13 led to decreased p-AKT levels, whereas TRPV5 antagonists or the Ca2+ chelators reversed the inhibition of AKT signaling pathway.

  GSDMs are a protein family that mediates pyroptosis by forming pores in membranes. Individual GSDMs are selectively expressed [11]. GSDMD, the key executioner in disease associated inflammation, is the most extensively studied GSDM and is found in the gastrointestinal epithelia and immune cells. The canonical pyroptosis pathway is activated by inflammasome assembly, activation of caspase-1 and subsequent cleavage of GSDMD [7]. This process protects host cells from specific pathogens and GSDMD has been implicated in many inflammatory diseases [62]. GSDME, the most ancient gene in the GSDM family, is distributed in various tissues and serves as a potential tumor suppressor. GSDME is mutated in many cancers and these mutations are associated with impaired pore-forming ability, consistent with its tumor suppressive role [17]. Chemotherapy drugs activate the caspase-3/GSDME signaling pathway, facilitating the switch between apoptosis and pyroptosis [63]. Pyroptosis could be triggered with high levels of GSDME. GSDME is low expressed in tumor cells while it is high expressed in normal tissue. The regulation of GSDME expression, including transcriptional regulatory mechanisms, is of great significance. Transactivation of GSDME facilitates the switch from apoptosis to pyroptosis, making cancer cells more vulnerable to chemotherapeutic drugs and overcoming drug resistance. Previous reports have shown that the transcription factor STAT3 and specificity protein 1 (Sp1) can transcriptionally enhance GSDME expression for pyroptosis [64], [65]. Our current study identified FOXO1, an AKT downstream gene, as a transcriptional activator of GSDME upon NJH-13 treatment. The FOXO1 binding site is located between 543 bp and 703 bp downstream of the GSDME gene. ChIP and dual luciferase assays verified this discovery. FOXO1 upregulated GSDME expression to ensure that pyroptosis occurred during NJH-13 treatment. The caspase-1/GSDMD signaling pathway was also checked in H1975 cells and no obvious alterations were detected upon NJH-13 treatment. In addition, the GSDMD inhibitor JC2-11 and the NLRP3 inhibitor CY-09 did not rescue the cell death induced by NJH-13 in H1975 cells. Upon NJH-13 treatment, the mRNA levels of GSDME increased. Knockdown of GSDME or inhibition of caspase-3 rescued the NJH-13-induced cell death. Moreover, NJH-13 treatment also triggered the release of LDH, IL-18, and IL-1β. All these data suggested that GSDME, rather than GSDMD, is the major executioner of NJH-13-induced pyroptotic cell death in H1975 cells. However, GSDME also serves as a double-edged sword. Excess pyroptotic cancer cells or normal cells cause tissue damage and severe excessive cytokine release [66], [67]. Further investigation is still in need to elucidate the transcriptional regulation of GSDME thoroughly and provide methods to avoid typical tissue damage caused by pyroptosis inducers without reducing their therapeutic effects.


## Conclusions
  Collectively, we identified NJH-13 as a promising candidate for inducing pyroptosis as a therapeutic strategy against NSCLC. NJH-13 triggered GSDME-mediated pyroptosis through targeting TRPV5. Mechanistically, NJH-13 perturbed calcium homeostasis, leading to ROS-mediated pyroptosis via the AKT/FOXO1/caspase-3/GSDME signaling pathway. FOXO1 directly occupied the promoter region of GSDME and upregulated GSDME to promote pyroptosis. Our findings highlight TRPV5 as a potential therapeutic candidate for inducing pyroptosis and FOXO1 as a critical regulatory mechanism for GSDME expression, which sheds light on lung cancer treatment.
