# Ref-1 is overexpressed in neovascular eye disease and targetable with a novel inhibitor


## Abstract
  Reduction-oxidation factor-1 or apurinic/apyrimidinic endonuclease 1 (Ref-1/APE1) is a crucial redox-sensitive activator of transcription factors such as NF-κB, HIF-1α, STAT-3 and others. It could contribute to key features of ocular neovascularization including inflammation and angiogenesis; these underlie diseases like neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD). We previously revealed a role for Ref-1 in the growth of ocular endothelial cells and in choroidal neovascularization (CNV). Here, we set out to further explore Ref-1 in neovascular eye disease. Ref-1 was highly expressed in human nAMD, murine laser-induced CNV and Vldlr−/− mouse subretinal neovascularization (SRN). Ref-1’s interaction with a redox-specific small molecule inhibitor, APX2009, was shown by NMR and docking. This compound blocks crucial angiogenic features in multiple endothelial cell types. APX2009 also ameliorated murine laser-induced choroidal neovascularization (L-CNV) when delivered intravitreally. Moreover, systemic APX2009 reduced murine SRN and downregulated the expression of Ref-1 redox regulated HIF-1α target carbonic anhydrase 9 (CA9) in the Vldlr−/− mouse model. Our data validate the redox function of Ref-1 as a critical regulator of ocular angiogenesis, indicating that inhibition of Ref-1 holds therapeutic potential for treating nAMD.


## Introduction
  Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a neurodegenerative, complex cause of irreversible loss of central vision in individuals aged 60 years and above. AMD has a global impact, affecting approximately 200 million people, the number of which is anticipated to grow to 288 million by 2040 [1]. The dry form of AMD is characterized by the deposition of lipids, exogenous proteins, complement and other cellular components to form drusen [2,3] and deterioration of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and photoreceptor inner and outer segments [4]. Growth factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) are released in response to the drusen deposits, which can accelerate vascular permeability of choroidal endothelial cells, resulting in the “wet” or neovascular (n) form of AMD with new blood vessel growth (neovascularization) and accompanying fluid leakage [5]. Macular neovascularization (MNV) in nAMD is characterized by invasion of proliferative vasculature arising either from the choroid that intrudes into the outer retina (CNV/Types I and II MNV) or from the deep plexus of the inner retina (retinal angiomatous proliferation [RAP] seen in about 10-15% of nAMD patients) that invades subretinally to form subretinal neovascularization (SRN), also known as Type III MNV [6,7]. CNV and SRN are largely driven by inflammation and hypoxia, respectively.

  Presently, intravitreal (IVT) injections of anti-VEGF drugs (such as ranibizumab, aflibercept, brolucizumab, bevacizumab, and faricimab [blocking both VEGF and angiopoietin-2]) remain the effective, first line treatment for nAMD, though they have significant challenges including tachyphylaxis, refractory patients and ocular and systemic side effects [8]. Moreover, in a survey study, 80% of retinal vascular disease patients who receive IVT injections reported a preference to receive topical or oral therapies if/when they were available [9]. Therefore, identification and understanding of new molecular targets and novel therapeutics are essential to overcome the shortcomings of current treatments.

  In that quest, we and others identified a bifunctional protein that holds two vital biological functions. This protein was originally identified as a DNA base excision repair protein (apurinic/apyrimidinic endonuclease or APE1). Subsequently, a protein was identified that had redox signaling activity regulating a number of redox sensitive transcription factors (TFs) such as HIF-1α, NF-κB and others; it was named reduction-oxidation effector factor 1 or Ref-1. Additional studies determined that Ref-1 and APE1 were the same protein with two activities. This protein has been identified as a potential target for nAMD and other ocular neovascular diseases including diabetic retinopathy (DR), diabetic macular edema (DME), and retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) [10-12]. While APE1 functions to regulate the base excision repair of DNA by its endonuclease activity, Ref-1 redox activity regulates TFs by chemically reducing disulfide bonds, which enhances the binding of these TFs to DNA and triggers the transcriptional activation of various genes key for ocular neovascular diseases, including mediators of inflammation, angiogenesis, oxidative stress, and cellular survival [13]. Blocking the redox activity of Ref-1 is appealing as by modulating transcription it can regulate multiple underlying mediators of ocular angiogenesis and inflammation, and such blockade may be more favorable over the present therapies that target solely VEGF (± angiopoietin) signaling.

  Small molecule inhibitors that target only the redox function of Ref-1 have shown significant potential in ocular disease. For instance, the Ref-1 redox inhibitor, (2E)-2-[(4,5-dimethoxy-2-methyl-3,6-dioxo-1,4-cyclohexadien-1-yl)methylene]-undecanoic acid, a dimethoxybenzoquinone referred to here as APX3330/E3330, enhances the oxidation of functional disulfide residues of Ref-1 and hampers the transcriptional activation of TFs without interrupting the DNA repair function of APE1 [14-21]. Antiangiogenic potential of APX3330 has been shown in vitro in human retinal endothelial cells (HRECs) and a macaque choroidal endothelial-like cell line (Rf/6a), where it decreased canonical angiogenic features including cell proliferation, tubule formation and migration in a concentration-dependent manner [22]. Additionally, APX3330 has shown promise in reducing neovascularization in vivo in widely-used mouse models of laser-induced CNV (L-CNV) [22] and in the Vldlr−/− mouse model of SRN [23] upon intraperitoneal (i.p.) and IVT delivery, respectively. Plus, oral gavage delivery of APX3330 demonstrated 50% reduction of lesion volume of L-CNV [13], which demonstrates APX3330’s oral bioavailability for eye diseases. Based on these findings and successful Phase 1 trials in cancer [24], APX3330 has recently completed a Phase 2b clinical trial for DR and DME as a first in class oral drug, demonstrating required safety and efficacy (NCT04692688) [25,26]; a subsequent Phase 3 registration trial is under consideration. While APX3330 is a potent clinical candidate for some of the ocular neovascular indications, exploration of a second-generation Ref-1 inhibitor APX2009 is appealing due to its reduced lipophilicity than APX3330, which could benefit in increasing the efficacy and potential bioavailability. APX2009 demonstrated more effective redox activity than APX3330 in a redox electrophoretic mobility shift assay as well as in HIF-1α and NF-κB reporter assays [27]. Further, APX2009 reduced in vitro angiogenesis and lesion size in vivo in the L-CNV model when delivered via i.p. [22].

  Considering the strong prior antiangiogenic evidence of APX2009 and increased Ref-1 redox inhibition over APX3330 as well as the necessity of novel therapeutics to bypass the limitations of current nAMD therapies, the current study extensively validates the potential of APX2009 to inhibit Ref-1 in ocular angiogenesis in both culture and in mouse models of nAMD. Here, we characterize the expression of Ref-1 in human nAMD and in murine L-CNV and Vldlr−/− models and for the first time present detailed molecular interaction of APX2009 with Ref-1 by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and drug-target binding by molecular docking analysis. The antiangiogenic nature of APX2009 was assessed in both ocular (transformed human choroidal endothelial cell line; iCECs) and non-ocular endothelial cells (brain microvascular endothelial cells; BMECs and human umbilical vein endothelial cells; HUVECs). Plus, potential neurotoxicity of APX2009 was evaluated in human pluripotent stem cell-derived retinal ganglion cells (hPSC-RGCs). In summary, we demonstrate a novel, specific APX2009-Ref-1 on-target interaction, lack of neurotoxicity and show that local and systemic delivery of APX2009 diminished murine neovascularization in models driven by inflammation and hypoxia, respectively.


## Material and Methods

### Animals
  All animal studies were performed after the approval of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, Indiana University School of Medicine (IUSM). The Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology guidelines for the “Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Visual Research” and “Animal Research: Reporting of In vivo Experiments” (ARRIVE) were followed. Wild-type C57BL/6J mice (6-8 weeks-old female) and B6;129S7-Vldlrtm1Her/J (Vldlr−/−; very-low density lipoprotein knockout) or wild-type male and female age-matched C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Jackson laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA). Genotypically validated Vldlr−/− strains were inbred for continual research use. The mice were housed under standard conditions at the Laboratory Animal Resource Center (LARC) facility, IUSM.


### Laser-induced choroidal neovascularization (L-CNV) in mice for expression studies
  L-CNV was performed in mouse eyes as described earlier [28]. Briefly, wild-type C57BL/6J mice (female, 7 weeks old) were anesthetized with intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of a mixture of ketamine hydrochloride (90 mg/kg) and xylazine (5 mg/kg). The pupils of the eyes were dilated using tropicamide (1%; Henry Schein Inc., Melville, NY, USA) and phenylephrine (2.5%; Akorn, Lake Forest, IL, USA) ophthalmic solutions following the application of hypromellose ophthalmic demulcent solution (Gonak; Akorn). The eyes were then challenged with 270 mW powered laser induction using a Micron IV laser injector (Phoenix-Micron, Bend, OR, USA) for a duration of 70 ms with 532 nm wavelength and 50 μm spot size (4 lesions/eye and positions were either 12, 3, 6 and 9 o’clock or 1, 4, 7, 10 o’clock in order to avoid hitting major blood vessels). To assess the temporal expression of Ref-1 during CNV progression, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and eyes enucleated at days 1, 3, 5, 7, 10, and 14 after laser challenge. The untouched control mice were euthanized at the end of the experimental period. All the enucleated eyes were fixed immediately in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) in PBS overnight at 4°C and processed for immunostaining.


### Subretinal neovascularization (SRN) in Vldlr−/− mice
  Vldlr−/− mice (at days 14, 21, 28 and at months 2, 3 and 6) and age-matched C57BL/6J control mice were anesthetized by i.p. injections of ketamine hydrochloride and xylazine and the pupils of the eyes were dilated as described in the previous section. Following the application of hypromellose lubricant to the mouse eyes, retinal optical coherence tomography (OCT) and fundus imaging were performed to assess the development of SRN, with the mouse placed on a heated stage of the Micron IV intraocular imaging system (Phoenix-Micron). To examine the lesion leakage of SRN, fluorescein angiography (FA) was performed by i.p. injection (5 mL/kg) of 2.5% fluorescein sodium in PBS (Fisher Scientific, Pittsburgh, PA, USA). The Micron IV system was used for this imaging modality using the GFP filter set and the instrument’s three-chip charge-coupled device [CCD] camera with 2 μm pixel resolution and 3 μm retinal imaging resolution. After imaging was complete, mice received i.p. atipamezole (1 mg/kg) anesthesia reversal. Two hours before euthanasia, mice received i.p. injection of 60 mg/kg pimonidazole (Hypoxyprobe, Burlington, MA). Vldlr−/− and age-matched control mice were euthanized at the above-mentioned time-points and the eyes were enucleated and fixed in 4% PFA in PBS overnight before immunostaining.


### Flat-mount staining
  Mouse eyes were fixed in 4% PFA (for the time-course Ref-1 expression in L-CNV and time-course Ref-1 expression in the eyes of Vldlr−/− mice) for 1 hour and then were dissected to separate choroid and retina tissues for preparing flat-mounts as detailed before [28] with slight changes. Briefly, the choroid and retina (fixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4°C) were washed with PBS twice for 10 minutes each to remove PFA. The tissues were permeabilized in a blocking buffer that contained 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Fisher) and 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 2 hours (the retinal tissues underwent additional permeabilization with 1% SDS for 3 minutes and two times wash with PBS for 15 min each before blocking). The tissues were then incubated with primary antibodies for 48-72 hours at 4°C. Primary antibodies/lectins and dilutions used were mouse anti-Ref-1/APE (13B8E5C2) antibody (1:150; # NB100-116, Novus Biologicals, Centennial, CO, USA) and Griffonia simplicifolia isolectin B4 (GS-IB4), biotin-XX conjugate (1:250; #I21414, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) diluted in 0.5% BSA, 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS. Appropriate mouse IgG control (#sc-2025, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX) was used as a control for mouse Ref-1 in L-CNV, Vldlr−/− and age-matched mice tissues. Following three 20 min PBS washes, the tissues were incubated overnight at 4°C with respective secondary antibodies/detection reagents (1:400 DyLight 488-conjugated streptavidin, #21832; Invitrogen, Rockford, IL; 1:150 anti-mouse IgG AlexaFluor 647, #A31571, Invitrogen and 1:100 Hypoxyprobe [used only for Vldlr−/− and age-matched mice tissues], #HP1-200Kit, Hypoxyprobe). The tissues were then washed three times with PBS for 20 min each and these immunostained tissues were flat-mounted and cover slipped on glass slides using Fluoromount-G (#0100-01; Southern Biotechnology, Birmingham, AL, USA). The flat-mounts were safeguarded from light and stored at 4°C until imaging, and they were imaged with a 5x (whole flatmounts) and 20x objective (individual lesions) on a confocal microscope (LSM700; Zeiss, Thornwood, NY, USA) using Z-stack optical sectioning (5 stacks/lesion or location).

  As necessary in Vldlr−/− and age-matched mice eye tissues, mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of Ref-1 in choroidal flat-mounts was quantified as described earlier [29]. Briefly, the image acquisition laser settings that include gain, intensity, pinhole, and digital offset were the same for all the channels used to image these samples on the confocal microscope. Using ImageJ software, a region of interest (ROI) was selected using a freehand selection tool and the mean staining intensity of the ROI was calculated by dividing the mean intensity by the total number of pixels. The final MFI was derived by subtracting the MFI of the ROI from the MFI of background in the same image. To minimize investigator bias, a masked investigator performed this quantification analysis.


### Immunostaining
  Paraffin sections of deidentified human donor eyes of nAMD patients and healthy controls were obtained from the National Disease Research Interchange (NDRI, PA, USA). Sections from different eyes were used to achieve two or three independent replicates and the ages of nAMD and healthy control eye samples were matched to the highest degree possible. The NDRI acquires donor materials upon informed consent following the principles of the WMA Declaration of Helsinki and the Department of Health and Human Services Belmont Report. The Indiana University Institutional Review Board reviewed and designated the deidentified, decedent human eyes as “Not Human Subjects Research”. Briefly, the human eye tissue sections were deparaffinized using xylene and ethanol gradients, rehydrated with PBS, and underwent heat-induced antigen retrieval in boiling citrate buffer (1x, pH 6.0, Thermo Scientific). The sections were permeabilized with PBST (PBS+0.025% Tween-20) and blocked with 10% normal donkey serum prepared in 1% BSA in PBS for 2 hours at room temperature. The sections were incubated at 4°C overnight with primary antibodies prepared in blocking solution including mouse anti-Ref-1 (1:150)/rabbit anti-Ref-1 (1:150; #NB100-101, Novus Biologicals), and cell-type specific antibodies/lectins rabbit anti-RPE65 (1:500; #ab231782, Abcam, Waltham, MA, USA), mouse anti-rhodopsin (1:300; #ab3267, Abcam), peanut agglutinin, biotin conjugate (1:250; #L6135, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), mouse anti-calbindin (1:250; #ab82812, Abcam), rabbit anti-vimentin (1:200; #PLA0199, Sigma-Aldrich) and rabbit anti-RBPMS (1:200; #ab152101, Abcam), and washed with PBST three times for 10 minutes each. They were then incubated with AlexaFluor 555 goat anti-rabbit (#A21428; Invitrogen) and/or AlexaFluor 488 goat anti-mouse (#A11001; Invitrogen), or DyLight 488-conjugated streptavidin (#21832; Invitrogen) prepared in blocking solution for an hour in a dark humidified chamber at room temperature. The sections were washed three times with PBST 10 min for each wash. Finally, the sections were dehydrated using an ethanol gradient and xylene and mounted using Vectashield hardset antifade mounting medium coupled with the nuclear stain, DAPI (#H-1500-10; Vector laboratories, Inc; Newark, CA, USA). These immunostained sections were imaged using the LSM700 confocal microscope with a 20x objective. The MFI of Ref-1 on these sections was measured using the aforementioned method (section 2.4).

  For mouse L-CNV, dissected retina and choroid from the mouse eyes collected at different days (section 2.2) following L-CNV induction and the eyes of untouched control mice were fixed in 4% PFA prepared in PBS for overnight at 4°C. The tissues were processed through sucrose gradient solutions and embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound and stored at −80°C until sectioning. The tissues were then cryosectioned to 5 μm thickness and immunostained following the similar immunostaining protocol detailed above without employing deparaffinization, rehydration and antigen unmasking steps. Instead, the cryosections were warmed on a slide warmer at 40°C for ~1 hour and washed with PBS followed by a blocking step. Primary antibodies and their dilutions were: mouse anti-Ref-1/mouse IgG control (1:150) and GS-IB4 (1:250), and detection reagents included Alexafluor 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse antibody (1:150) and DyLight 488-conjugated streptavidin (1:400). Washing, counterstaining, and imaging were done as above.


### Immunoblotting
  Eyes from Vldlr−/− or age-matched C57BL/6J mice were enucleated and immediately dissected to isolate the retina and choroid and snap frozen. Tissue lysates were prepared by lysing the tissues in RIPA buffer (#R0278; Sigma-Aldrich) with protease inhibitor cocktail (#04693159001 and #04906837001; Roche, Basel, Switzerland) on ice for 20 minutes followed by homogenization and centrifugation at 12,000xg for 15 min at 4°C. The supernatants were recovered from each tissue type and pooled from three tissues in each sample. Total protein concentration was determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay kit. Equal amounts of total protein (20 μg) from each sample were resolved by 10% (w/v) SDS-PAGE and then transferred onto PVDF membranes (Cytiva Amersham, Marlborough, MA, USA). These membranes were blocked with 5% BSA (#BP9703-100, Fisher) in Tris buffered saline (TBS) for 1 hour. The membranes were then incubated with mouse antibody against Ref-1 (1:1000) overnight at 4°C. As loading controls, the membranes were incubated either with mouse anti-β-actin (1:1000; #A4700, AC-40, Sigma-Aldrich) or mouse anti-α-tubulin (1:1000; #T9026, DM1A, Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 hour at room temperature. The blots were washed three times with TBST for 5 min each, then incubated with a secondary goat-anti-mouse H&L IgG antibody (1:10,000; #610-1302, Rockland, Victoria, BC, Canada) for an hour at room temperature. Following 3x5 minute washes with TBST, signals were detected with Amersham ECL immunoblotting detection reagent (#RPN2236, Amersham, UK) on a c600 imaging system (Azure Biosystems, Dublin, CA, USA). The bands were quantified by densitometry using ImageJ software (version 1.53e).


### NMR studies of Ref-1 interactions with APX2009

#### Protein expression and purification
  For NMR studies, we expressed human 15N-APE1/Ref-1 (39-318/C138A) as a His-SUMO-fusion in BL21/Rosetta Escherichia coli. 15N was introduced using 15N-ammonium chloride (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Inc. Tewksbury, MA) as the sole nitrogen source in 2 L minimal media cultures [30,31]. Cell cultures were induced with 0.2 mM IPTG at an OD of 0.6 and grown for 4 hours at 37 °C. They were then pelleted and stored at −80°C prior to purification.

  Lysis buffer, 50 mM sodium phosphate, 0.3 M sodium chloride, 20 mM imidazole pH 7.8, was used to resuspend cells prior to lysis using a microfluidizer M110L. The cell lysate was then centrifuged at 35,000 rpm for 30 min. Following ultracentrifugation, the supernatant was filtered and loaded on two 0.5 mL Ni-NTA (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA) columns (one column/L growth) run at 4°C. After loading the supernatant, the columns were washed with 10 column volumes of buffer. On-column cleavage to remove the His-SUMO tag resulted from incubation with Ulp1 protease (10 μg/column) in 8 mL per column overnight. Isotopically labeled APE1/Ref-1 was collected as the flowthrough the next day. 15N-APE1/Ref-1 was assessed by SDS-PAGE and by QToF intact mass spectrometric analysis to confirm purity and mass.


#### NMR spectroscopy
  15N-APE1/Ref-1 at a concentration of 80 μM was dialyzed against 20 mM sodium phosphate (pH 6.5), 0.1 M sodium chloride in 90%/10% H2O/D2O and placed in an NMR tube. NMR spectra, 2D 1H/15N heteronuclear single-quantum coherence (HSQC) spectra, were acquired at 25°C on a 600 MHz Bruker AVANCE spectrometer equipped with a 5-mm triple-resonance cryoprobe (1H, 13C, and 15N) and z-axis pulsed gradient. NMR data were processed with TOPSPIN and analyzed with CCPN NMR [32]. The inhibitor APX2009 (a generous gift from Apexian Pharmaceuticals) [27] was dissolved in acetonitrile-D3. For the inhibitor experiments, concentrations of acetonitrile-D3 were maximally 2% (v/v). The chemical shift perturbations (CSP) (Δδ) were calculated using the following equation, which reflects the total weighted change in 1H and 15N chemical shift for a given peak in the 2D spectra: Δδ = [(ΔδH)2 + (0.1ΔδN)2]1/2.


### Molecular Docking
  Structural modeling of APX2009 bound to APE1/Ref-1 was performed with rigid-receptor docking using AutoDock 4.2 and AutoDock Vina [33,34]. Prior to docking, a crystal structure of human APE1/Ref-1 (PDBID: 4QHD) [35] was prepared with the MolProbity webserver [36,37] to check for residue flips and with PROPKA [38] to determine appropriate protonation states for titratable residues at pH = 7.0. APX2009 was constructed using UCSF Chimera [39]. Receptor and ligand PDB coordinates were then formatted and parameterized for docking with the AutoDock Tools program, and docking grids were created with AutoGrid [33,34,40,41]. Docking then commenced with default parameters in both programs, except that an exhaustiveness setting was increased to 50 and 100, for AutoDock 4.2 and AutoDock Vina respectively, to account for the many degrees of freedom that could be sampled in APX2009. Clustering of the AutoDock 4.2 results with AutoDock Tools and manual inspection of the top 10 rank-ordered docking poses from both programs was performed to identify the lowest energy consensus structures for APX2009 bound to APE1/Ref-1 [42].


### Cell Culture
  HRECs (#ACBRI 181; Cell Systems, Kirkland, WA, USA), BMECs (#ACBRI 376; Cell Systems), and HUVECs (#C0035C; Thermo Fisher Scientific) were cultured in endothelial basal medium (EBM-2; #CC-3156) mixed with the components of an endothelial cell growth medium (EGM-2) bullet kit (#CC-4176; Lonza, Walkersville, MD, USA) under standard conditions. iCEC2 cells (transformed human CECs) were obtained as a gift from Dr. Robert Mullins laboratory at the University of Iowa and cultured in endothelial growth medium (#CCM027; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) under the incubation at 33°C since these cells possess a temperature-sensitive hypomorphic T-antigen and actively proliferate at 33°C [43]. The functional assays were performed when the cells were cultured at 37°C as proliferation of these cells becomes slower under standard incubation conditions.


#### Endothelial cell proliferation assay
  The proliferation of endothelial cells was assayed as previously described [44,28]. Briefly, the cells (2.5 x 103 in 100 μL growth media) were seeded in black 96-well plates with a clear bottom and allowed to grow for a period of 24 hours, followed by the addition of 1 μL APX2009 (0.1 nM to 100 μM) or DMSO (vehicle, at 1% final concentration to the cells in the growth medium) and incubation for 44 hours at 37°C. Then the cells were incubated with the addition of 11.1 μL of alamarBlue reagent (#BUF012B, BioRad, Hercules, CA, USA) for 4 hours and the fluorescence was measured at 560 nm and 590 nm wavelengths of excitation and emission, respectively in a Synergy H1 plate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA). The growth inhibitory (GI50) concentrations of APX2009 in each endothelial cell type were calculated using GraphPad Prism 9.0.


#### Endothelial cell cycle analysis by flow cytometry
  The cell cycle progression of iCECs, BMECs and HUVECs was assessed as detailed before [28]. In brief, the cells (2 x 106) were cultured in 6-well plates. When the cells were 70% confluent, they were treated with APX2009 (0.1 μM, 1 μM and 10 μM) or DMSO at 1% final concentration to the cells in the medium for 24 hours and washed twice with PBS and fixed in 70% ethanol under vortex agitation. The fixed cells proceeded to flow cytometry analysis after careful washing twice with PBS. The cell pellets were resuspended with a mixture of propidium iodide (PI; 20 μg/mL) staining solution prepared in PBS, 100 μg/mL of RNAse A and Triton X-100 (0.1%) and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. The cells were then analyzed using an Attune NxT flow cytometer (Thermo Fisher). Area histograms and cell cycle profiles were generated using Modfit software (v. 5.0, Verity Software House, Topsham, ME, USA). Modfit data analysis also included pulse shape assessment to exclude any cell debris, cell aggregates and doublets from the whole cell population assessed.


#### Endothelial cell migration assay
  The ability of endothelial cell (iCECs, BMECs and HUVECs) migration was examined as described earlier [45]. Concisely, the cells were cultured in 96-well plates at 37°C until they were confluent. A 700-800 μm wide scratch was made down the center of each well without damaging the cells using a mechanical scratch wound maker (Incucyte, Sartorius, Ann Arbor, Michigan, USA). The cells were washed with PBS and treated with APX2009 (1 μM, 3 μM, and 10 μM) or DMSO (1% final concentration to the cells in medium) in fresh complete media. The cells were then incubated in an Incucyte incubator (37°C) equipped with a live imaging system (Essen Bioscience, Ann Arbor, MI, USA). The ability of these endothelial cells to migrate across the scratch made was monitored and the live-cell images were captured for 8 hours or 24 hours. The number of migrated cells were manually counted and normalized to DMSO controls.


#### Endothelial cell tubule formation assay
  The tubule formation ability of iCECs, BMECs and HUVECs over a layer of Matrigel was analyzed as detailed earlier [45,28]. In brief, the wells of black 96-well plates with a clear bottom were layered with Matrigel (50 μL volume/well) without introducing any bubbles and the layer was allowed to solidify for 20 min at 37 °C. The cells (1.5 x 104) were seeded in the wells over the Matrigel layer in complete media that contained APX2009 (1 μM, 3 μM, and 10 μM) or DMSO (1% final concentration to the cells in complete medium). The cells were incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2, and they were observed for tube formation every 2 hours until 8 hours. Using a brightfield microscope (4x objective), the cells in each well were imaged and the measurement of tubule length was assessed using AngiogenesisAnalyzer Plugin in ImageJ software (v. 1.8.0; http://image.bio.methods.free.fr/ImageJ/?Angiogenesis-Analyzer-for-ImageJ.html) and the tubule lengths were normalized to DMSO controls.


### hPSC maintenance and RGC differentiation
  Human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs) were maintained and expanded as previously described [46,47], using the H7-BRN3b:tdTomato:Thy1.2 cell line [48,49]. Briefly, hPSC colonies were grown on Geltrex (Thermo Fisher)-coated 6 well plates in mTeSR1, with fresh media exchanged daily. At approximately 75% confluency, hPSC colonies were enzymatically lifted using dispase (2 mg/mL) and split at a ratio of 1:6 onto fresh Geltrex-coated 6-well plates. Subsequently, hPSCs were initially differentiated into retinal organoids for the subsequent derivation of RGCs using previously established protocols, with minor modifications [46,47,50]. Briefly, enzymatically passaged hPSC colonies were grown in suspension to generate embryoid bodies (EBs). Within 3 days of differentiation, EBs were slowly transitioned from mTesR1 medium into neural induction medium (DMEM/F12 with 1% N2 supplement, MEM nonessential amino acids, and 2 μg/mL heparin). At day 6, EBs were fed with fresh NIM media and supplemented with bone morphogenetic protein 4 (BMP4, R&D Systems, 50 ng/mL). At day 8, EBs were induced to adhere to a 6 well plate in the presence of 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). At day 9 and 12, half-media changes with NIM were performed, with a full media change at day 15. On day 16, presumptive retinal organoids were mechanically lifted and placed in suspension culture in retinal differentiation medium (RDM), with media changes every other day. After 30 days of differentiation, 1% FBS and Glutamax (Invitrogen) were added to maintain retinal organization. At 45 days, organoids were enzymatically dissociated with Accumax (Life Technologies) for 20 min at 37°C and RGCs were purified by magnetic activated cell sorting against the Thy1.2 cell surface antigen (Miltenyi, Gaithersburg, MD, USA) [49,51]. Purified RGCs were then plated onto laminin-coated 24 well plates at a concentration of 10,000 cells per well. Following differentiation and purification, RGCs were allowed to mature for an additional 1.5 weeks, at which point RGCs were incubated with either (i) APX2009 at three different concentrations (0.1 μM, 0.3 μM and 1 μM), (ii) DMSO as a vehicle control, (iii) staurosporine (SP) at 5 μM as a positive control or (iv) left without any treatment (untreated cells). Images of BRN3b:tdTomato expressing RGCs were taken every day for 7 days using a Nikon TS2R-FL microscope with a Nikon DS-Ri2 camera, and culture medium with freshly added APX2009, DMSO or SP was changed halfway through. Fluorescence intensity was determined using ImageJ. Data represent all analyzed images for a total of four independent experiments.


### Ocular pharmacokinetics (PK) of APX2009 in mice
  APX2009 was quantified by the Indiana University Simon Comprehensive Cancer Center (IUSCCC) Clinical Pharmacology Analytical Core via LC-MS/MS. In vivo, 7-8 weeks old wild-type C57BL/6J mice (male and female) eyes received IVT injections of APX2009 (10 μM) and the eyes were enucleated at regular time intervals of 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 24 h (n = 6; 3 male+3 female mice per time-point) and snap-frozen immediately. At the time of analysis, the eyes were homogenized in 0.5 mL PBS and temazepam added as internal standard. Samples (0.4 mL) underwent extraction with ethyl acetate, the supernatant was removed, evaporated to dryness, and reconstituted with 50 μL acetonitrile. 5 μL was injected onto a Sciex 6500 QTRAP via an Intersil ODS-2 150 x 4.6 mm 5 μm column at flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. Initial mobile phase conditions were 50:50 0.2% formic acid, 50% acetonitrile, increasing to 90% acetonitrile over 10 min. The lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) was 0.004 ng/g. All 24 hour timepoint samples were below the LLOQ and excluded from analysis. Ocular PK was determined for the mean concentration-time profiles by sex using standard noncompartmental pharmacokinetic analysis in Phoenix 64 (8.1.1.279, Pharsight). Elimination rate constant (ke) was estimated as the slope of the terminal elimination phase. The area under the concentration-time curve from 0 to infinity (AUCinf) was determined as the sum of the linear trapezoidal, linear interpolation method to estimate to the last observed value (6 hours, AUClast) and the last observed concentration divided by ke.


### L-CNV, intravitreal (IVT) injection and in vivo ocular imaging in mice
  L-CNV was induced in the right eyes of experimental mice as detailed above in section 2.2. The antiangiogenic potential was assessed by a single IVT injection of APX2009 in mice eyes. The injections were performed immediately after the laser challenge by locally anesthetizing the eye with 0.5% tetracaine hydrochloride ophthalmic solution (Oceanside Pharmaceuticals, Mission Viejo, CA, USA). Using a 30-G needle, a small incision was made approximately 4 mm below the iris to get access into the vitreous chamber. The treatments including APX2009 (25 μM and 50 μM final estimated intravitreal concentration), vehicle (a mixture of propylene glycol, Kolliphor HS15 and Tween 80 [PKT]), and R&D Systems mouse anti-VEGF164 antibody (5 ng/eye; standard of care positive control) were injected IVT in 0.5 μL in the mice eyes (via the incision made earlier) using a high precision sterile Hamilton syringe with 33-G Hamilton needle without damaging the lens and the retina at an angle of about 45-60° towards the plane of injection. Before removing the needle from the eye, the needle remained in the vitreous for about 30-60 seconds to avoid reflux. After the injections, triple antibiotic ointment (Vetropolycin; Dechra Pharmaceuticals, Fort Worth, TX, USA) was applied to the eyes to prevent infection. Seven days post laser challenge and treatment, OCT (horizontal and vertical images of each L-CNV lesion), fundus, and FA imaging were performed in the mice eyes as detailed earlier (section 2.3) using the Micron IV intraocular imaging system. The lesion size was analyzed using ImageJ on the horizontal and vertical OCT images as defined before [52,53]. The lesion leakage observed with fluorescein was graded on a scale from 0-3 depending on the lesion intensity (0: no leakage, 1: mild leakage, 2: moderate leakage and 3: intense leakage) assessed by the majority of three masked investigators [54]. Abnormal lesions that did not abide by standard inclusion criteria were excluded from the study [55,53]. The mouse eyes were harvested after imaging and they were fixed in 4% PFA and processed for immunostaining.


#### Ex vivo choroidal and retinal immunofluorescence
  Flat-mounts of choroid and retina were prepared as detailed above (section 2.4). The markers and the dilutions used to stain choroids were biotin GS-IB4 (1:250) plus DyLight 488-conjugated streptavidin (1:400) and rhodamine-conjugated Ricinus communis agglutinin I (1:250; RCA-I; #RL-1082-1; Vector Laboratories). The primary antibodies/lectins and their dilutions used to prepare the retinal flat-mounts were rabbit anti-IBA1 (1:200; # 019-19741, Wako, Richmond, VA, USA), rat anti-CD11b (1:100; # 550282, BD Biosciences Pharmingen, San Diego, USA) and GS-IB4 (1:250) and detection reagents and their dilutions were Alexa Fluor conjugated 555 goat anti-rabbit (1:400), Alexa Fluor conjugated 647 goat anti-rat (1:500), and DyLight 488-conjugated streptavidin (1:400). The Z-stack optical sectioning (5 stacks/choroidal lesion or lesion area noted in the retina) images were captured using a 20x objective on the LSM700 confocal microscope. Using ImageJ software, the measurement of CNV lesion volumes from both RCA-I and GS-IB4 staining and the count of IBA1 and CD-11b positive microglia and macrophages in the retina were assessed from these confocal images. CNV lesion volumes were calculated from the sum of labelled areas of targeted markers in each optical section multiplied by the distance amongst the sections [22]. Standard exclusion criteria for abnormal lesions were followed [56,55,53].


### SRN in Vldlr−/− mice and i.p. injections
  The antiangiogenic potential of APX2009 was also investigated in the Vldlr−/− mice with SRN. The treatments including APX2009 (12.5 mg/kg and 25 mg/kg) and vehicle (PKT) were administered to these mice i.p. (b.i.d.) for 7 days from postnatal day 21 (P21). On the last day of treatment (P27), the eyes of the mice underwent OCT, fundus and FA imaging as explained above in section 2.3. Afterward, the mice were euthanized and their eyes were enucleated and fixed in 4% PFA for overnight at 4 °C. The fixed tissues were immunostained and the flat-mounts were prepared following the protocol detailed in section 2.4. The reagents and their dilutions used were rabbit anti-carbonic anhydrase 9 primary antibody (CA9 at 1:200 dilution; #NB100-417; Novus Biologicals, Centennial, CO, USA) and GS-IB4 (1:250). The detection reagents and their dilutions were Alexa Fluor conjugated 555 goat anti-rabbit (1:400) and DyLight 488-conjugated streptavidin (1:400). The flat-mounts were mounted with Vectashield hardset antifade mounting medium with DAPI. Using a 20x objective on the LSM700 confocal microscope, these flat-mounts were imaged to acquire Z-stack optical sectioning (5 stacks/lesion) images. The MFI of CA9 on these images was quantified by the method mentioned above (section 2.4). The number of lesions in the FA images was counted manually and compared across treatments by a masked investigator. The percentage quantification of SRN area was performed using Adobe Photoshop.


### Statistical analysis
  GraphPad Prism, versions 9 or 10, was used to perform all statistical and graphical analyses. Data are presented as mean ± SEM with n specified and defined in figure legends. Student’s t-test with Welch’s correction was employed to compare two means. One-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s post-hoc or Kruskal-Wallis tests was used to compare three or more independent treatment groups. To analyze two independent variables among three or more treatment groups, two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc tests was performed. Simple linear regression analysis was done to estimate the relationship among two variables in the pharmacokinetic data. Statistical significance was considered when two-sided p values were <0.05.


## Results

### Ref-1 is highly expressed in human nAMD
  To investigate the relevance of Ref-1 in nAMD, we first explored the expression of Ref-1 in retina and choroid sections from postmortem eyes of human nAMD patients vs. non-AMD/healthy control subjects. The RPE-choroid of nAMD sections showed predominantly higher expression of Ref-1 in the RPE in comparison with healthy control eyes, and nAMD retina sections displayed increased Ref-1 expression in the inner nuclear layer (INL), outer nuclear layer (ONL), and ganglion cell layer (GCL; only in some patient samples) with moderately increased and higher expression (noted only in some patient samples) in photoreceptor inner and outer segments compared with non-AMD control eyes (Fig. 1 a, b). Regardless of varied distinctive nAMD phenotypes (such as disrupted retinal nuclear layers and loss of photoreceptor inner and outer segments) among the samples studied, the mean difference of Ref-1 MFI in the RPE, INL and ONL were higher in the nAMD eyes than in the healthy control eyes, while in some nAMD eyes, Ref-1 MFI was also higher in the GCL in contrast to the healthy control subjects (Supp. Fig. 1).


### Ref- 1 expression associates with multiple cell types in human nAMD
  Considering the observed increase in Ref-1 expression in the RPE and different layers of the retina in nAMD patient samples, we sought to validate which specific type of cells in these retinal layers specifically express Ref-1 in human nAMD. This was studied by probing nAMD and non-AMD control sections using various cell-specific markers alongside Ref-1, including RPE65 (RPE), rhodopsin (rod photoreceptors), peanut agglutinin (PNA; cone photoreceptors), calbindin (horizontal cells), vimentin (Müller glia), and RNA-binding protein with multiple splicing (RBPMS; retinal ganglion cells/RGCs). Intriguingly, Ref-1 was expressed in cells with markers of RPE, rod and cone photoreceptors, horizontal cells, Müller glia and RGCs in diseased eyes (Fig. 2); the robust Ref-1 expression and its association with these multiple cell types of the retina and RPE of nAMD vs. control samples suggest that Ref-1 perhaps has functional implications in multiple nAMD pathological mechanisms.


### Ref-1 is overexpressed in mouse L-CNV and SRN
  We next questioned whether Ref-1 is analogously upregulated in experimental murine CNV. This was assessed using the widely used L-CNV mouse model, in which Bruch’s membrane of mouse eyes was damaged with laser pulses and the eyes enucleated at different time points during the progression of L-CNV. In contrast with untouched control eyes, the L-CNV eyes showed overexpression of Ref-1 in and around the L-CNV lesions and that colocalized with vasculature as labeled by GS-IB4 (Fig. 3a; Supp. Fig. 2). In addition, the cryosections prepared from these experimental eyes also demonstrated overexpression of Ref-1 in the lesion area of the RPE-choroid and its partial association with GS-IB4 in the L-CNV eyes vs. untouched control eyes (Fig. 3c). The retinal sections of the L-CNV eyes displayed markedly increased Ref-1 expression in the GCL, INL, ONL and photoreceptor layers (similar to human nAMD), and GS-IB4-positive blood vessels partially colocalized with Ref-1 (Fig. 3b).

  Since a subset of nAMD patients develop SRN, we used a well-established Vldlr−/− model to validate expression of Ref-1 over the course of SRN development in the mouse eyes as this model spontaneously develops SRN arising from the inner retina from P15 until at least 6 months of age [57]. The lesion formation and severity were assessed from P14 to 6 months at various time points (days 14, 21, 28 and months 2, 3, and 6). Compared to C57BL/6J controls, Vldlr−/− mice developed prominent neovascular phenotypes from P21 to 6 months with more severe lesions developed at 2, 3, and 6 months as seen in OCT, fundus, and FA images (Supp. Fig. 3). To investigate Ref-1 expression, pooled choroids from these mice collected at different times were immunoblotted, revealing significantly upregulated Ref-1 expression in Vldlr−/− mouse eyes at P21 compared to C57BL/6J control eyes (P21). This higher Ref-1 expression at P21 is intriguing, suggesting it associates with the initiation of SRN. This upregulation was significant in comparison with that noted in Vldlr−/− mouse eyes at P14 and P28 (Fig. 4a, c). Ex vivo, the Ref-1 expression was seen on the choroidal flat-mounts (as subretinal lesions culminate their development in the choroid due to retinal choroidal anastomosis). The MFI of Ref-1 expression was notably higher at all time points except P14 in Vldlr−/− mouse eye lesions vs. their corresponding control mouse eyes. Unsurprisingly, direct assessment of Ref-1 in these lesions by immunostaining revealed a greater degree of upregulation than that seen in the whole retinal lysates by immunoblot. Partial co-localization of Ref-1 with hypoxyprobe (a marker of hypoxia) and GS-IB4 (a marker of vasculature) on these lesions further validates that Ref-1 may play a pivotal role in hypoxia-mediated angiogenesis in SRN (Fig. 4b, d). The retinal Ref-1 expression and its association with hypoxic and vasculature labeling were also investigated. Although Ref-1 expression in the whole retina was also high, as noted in choroids, the incomplete lesion structures (because of their late developmental stages in the choroid) limit MFI quantification in these tissues (Supp. Fig. 4, 5).


### APX2009 interacts directly with Ref-1
  Given the robust expression of Ref-1 seen in ocular neovascularization, we sought a drug lead that could inhibit the redox function of this protein target and focused on APX2009 to study its structural interaction with Ref-1. Interactions of APX2009 with Ref-1 were assessed using 1H-15N HSQC spectra collected for a 1, 2, 4, 6, 10, and 12-fold molar excess of APX2009 over Ref-1. Ref-1 residues involved in interactions with APX2009 were determined through assessment of chemical shift perturbations (CSPs). Shown in Fig. 5a are CSPs for a 10-fold molar excess mapped on the structure of Ref-1. CSPs above a threshold of 0.02 indicating a significant interaction of APX2009 with Ref-1 include the following residues: Asp70, Val131, Gln137, Ser164, Val172, and Asn212 (Fig. 5a-e). Of these, 137 and 164 are surface residues that define a small pocket on Ref-1 on the opposite face of the protein from the DNA-binding site. In contrast, residues 70 and 212 reside within the endonuclease active site. The remaining residues with CSPs above 0.02, Val131 and Val172, are located within the interior of the protein. This analysis suggests that APX2009 binds directly to Ref-1.

  To create a structural model of APX2009 bound to APE1, molecular docking was performed with AutoDock 4.2 and AutoDock Vina, two related but different docking programs that use different conformational search algorithms and scoring functions [33,34,40]. These studies focused on the small pocket defined by Gln137 and Ser164, since this site has previously been shown to bind small molecules [58]. Two consensus poses were identified which support the WaterLOGSY characterization of APX2009 binding [27]. Both poses are predicted to have similar binding affinities, within 0.6 kcal/mol of each other (which is within the predictive accuracy of rigid receptor docking) and either may be equally likely to occur. As shown in Fig. 5f,g, APX2009 can adopt a “ring-in” or “ring-out” pose. When the 1,4-naphthoquinone core of APX2009 binds into the pocket, it sits atop Ile91 and adjacent to Phe162, forming favorable hydrophobic and π-π interactions, respectively. The rest of APX2009 protrudes outward from the pocket, and due to the greater flexibility of this part of the molecule, can interact with several residues surrounding the pocket or with water. In the “ring-out” conformation, one amide-ethyl and the n-propyl group bury into the pocket to form favorable hydrophobic interactions with Ile91 and Leu62. In this pose, the 1,4-naphthoquinone core sticks out into solution, but a carbonyl group can hydrogen bond with Ser164, and the methoxy group can serve as an acceptor for a hydrogen bond with Gln137.


### Ref-1 inhibitor APX2009 inhibits proliferation in various endothelial cells
  Given the pronounced binding of the naphthoquinone core (which accounts for redox inhibitory potential) of APX2009 with Ref-1, we investigated the antiangiogenic potential of APX2009 in vitro. We previously showed antiangiogenic effects of APX2009 in HRECs and Rf/6a, a macaque cell line with similarity to choroidal endothelial cells [22]. Here, we assessed antiangiogenic effects of APX2009 in human choroidal endothelial cells (iCECs) and for comparison non-ocular BMECs and HUVECs. APX2009 dose-dependently reduced proliferation of iCECs, BMECs and HUVECs in a low-micromolar range (GI50 values: 3.02 μM in iCECs, 2.88 μM in BMECs and 2.70 μM in HUVECs) relative to DMSO treatment (Fig. 6 a-c). This antiproliferative potential was further confirmed by APX2009-enhanced arrest of cell cycle progression in these cells. APX2009 blocked these endothelial cells at G1/S phase. This G1/S phase arrest led to an accompanying cell population increase in the G0/G1 phase on escalating doses of APX2009 without triggering apoptosis, which proposes a dose-dependent arrest of cell cycle transition to S-phase from G0/G1 phase (Fig. 6d-l).


### Ref-1 inhibitor APX2009 exhibits antiangiogenic properties in various endothelial cells
  In addition to proliferation, angiogenesis encompasses other key events in endothelial cells, including degradation of the extracellular matrix (ECM), migration and tubule formation. To evaluate the effect of APX2009 on endothelial cell migration, we employed a widely used scratch-wound assay. Interestingly, Ref-1 inhibition by APX2009 effectively reduced migration of iCECs, BMECs and HUVECs in a dose dependent manner (Fig. 7a-f). Further, the ability of APX2009 to hamper iCECs, BMECs and HUVECs to organize and form tubule structures was assessed. Consistent with the effects on proliferation and migration of these endothelial cells, APX2009 significantly obstructed the ability of iCECs, BMECs and HUVECs to form tubules (Fig. 7g-l). Together these data indicate that inhibiting Ref-1 via APX2009 has the potential to halt key events of angiogenesis in vitro.


### APX2009 is non-toxic to hPSC-RGCs
  We assessed the toxicity of APX2009 in cultures of hPSC-derived RGCs. The mature hPSC-RGCs treated with APX2009 up to 1 μM showed no decreased cell numbers from day 1 through day 7 (Fig. 8a). In contrast to APX2009 treated or untreated cells, SP (an effective inducer of apoptosis)-treated hPSC-RGCs exhibited a substantial toxicity, the results of which are represented by the fluorescence intensity ofBRN3b:tdTomato expressing RGCs (Fig. 8b-h). These data suggest that APX2009 is safe and does not cause toxic effects to the RGCs, although higher doses did reveal some antiproliferative effects (Supp. Fig. 6).


### Intravitreal APX2009 is antiangiogenic in murine L-CNV
  Existing anti-VEGF drugs are delivered via IVT injections in patients with ocular neovascularization. Although the systemic delivery of APX2009 demonstrated promising antiangiogenic effects in mouse L-CNV [22], the potency of APX2009 to treat CNV via IVT delivery (comparable to the standard of care) has not been tested. Before testing the potential of IVT APX2009 in murine L-CNV, the ocular PK of this drug was assessed post IVT administration in wild-type C57BL/6J mice. The maximum concentration (Cmax) was 13.5 ng/g with a half-life of 1.3 hour and AUC0-last (h*ng/g) of 18.6 (Table 1).

  Given detectable IVT APX2009 concentrations noted in the PK evaluation (Fig. 9a), the efficacy of IVT APX2009 was validated in mouse L-CNV. The OCT images of the mouse eyes that received APX2009 at low (25 μM final IVT concentration) and higher (50 μM) doses reveal a significant reduction in the L-CNV lesion volume similar to mouse anti-VEGF treatment in comparison to the vehicle (PKT)-treated eyes (Fig. 9b,c). The eyes that received anti-VEGF and low and high doses of APX2009 demonstrated few or no grade 3/intense lesions in a fluorescein leakage assessment, whereas PKT-treated mouse eyes presented lesions of grades 1, 2, and 3 (Fig. 9b,d,e). Ex vivo, in contrast to the lesions of eyes that had vehicle treatment, the lesions of the mouse eyes that had anti-VEGF and APX2009 treatments showed markedly reduced lesion volume, and the effects seen with APX2009 were analogous to anti-VEGF treatment (Fig. 9b,f,g).


### Intravitreal APX2009 reduces inflammatory cells in murine L-CNV
  Further we asked whether the CNV induced in RPE-Bruch’s membrane-choroid complex has any inflammatory impact in the overlying retina. Thus, the retinal flat-mounts were prepared to label innate inflammatory cells such as microglia (IBA1) and macrophages (CD-11b) and the number of IBA1 and CD-11b positive cells were counted on the deepest retinal layer of Z-stack images. Similar to anti-VEGF control, APX2009-mediated Ref-1 inhibition significantly diminished the microglial and macrophage cell population compared with the eyes that had received PKT (Fig. 10a-c).


### APX2009 regulates hypoxia-driven angiogenesis in murine SRN
  To complement the inflammation-driven CNV evident in the L-CNV model, we tested the antiangiogenic potential of APX2009 in the Vldlr−/− SRN mouse model. Owing to the continuous development of SRN in this genetic model, the mice were i.p. treated for 7 days from P14 to P21 with either APX2009 (12.5 mg/kg and 25 mg/kg) or vehicle (PKT). PKT-treated mouse eyes had neovascular lesions and notable subretinal alterations in the fundus and OCT images (Fig. 11a), respectively. However, the eyes of the mice that received APX2009 displayed diminished neovascular lesions in the fundus and substantially alleviated subretinal alterations by OCT. Additionally, as seen in FA images, APX2009 treatment markedly lowered the number of neovascular lesions at the lower dose in comparison to vehicle treatment (Fig. 11d). The reduction at the higher dose was not significant, perhaps due to the varying number of spontaneous neovascular lesions in this mouse model. Finally, ex vivo quantification of neovascular area in the entire retina confirmed a significant reduction with APX2009 treatment (Fig. 11c,f).

  To determine if the antiangiogenic effect is mediated via the regulation of hypoxic signaling, the expression of CA9 was investigated ex vivo in the choroidal flat-mounts co-stained with GS-IB4 and DAPI. The redox activity of Ref-1 regulates HIF-1α which upon binding to hypoxia response elements directly upregulates various proangiogenic genes including VEGFA and CA9 [59]. While GS-IB4 uniformly stained the neovascular lesions in the choroid, strikingly decreased CA9 expression was observed in the lesions from eyes treated with both lower and higher doses of APX2009 (Fig. 11b, e) and CA9 also associated with GS-IB4 and DAPI, which together suggests that Ref-1 regulates hypoxia-driven angiogenesis.


## Discussion
  Understanding molecular mechanisms of potential cellular angiogenic targets and identifying novel therapeutics for nAMD treatment is crucial due to the heterogeneity of the disease and lack of universal responsiveness to standard-of-care nAMD therapy. Here, we provide evidence that Ref-1 is overexpressed in human nAMD and murine L-CNV and SRN, associating with ocular angiogenesis driven by inflammation and hypoxia. We also show that a Ref-1 specific small molecule inhibitor, APX2009, offers therapeutic potential via inhibiting the redox function of Ref-1 in models of angiogenesis in vitro and in vivo. Previously, we identified the antiangiogenic efficacy of APX2009 in HRECs and Rf/6a cells in addition to its systemic therapeutic potential in the L-CNV mouse model [22]. The current study identifies the cellular level involvement of Ref-1 associated with ocular neovessel genesis.

  We have assessed Ref-1 expression in the retina and the RPE-choroid for the first time in human nAMD, murine L-CNV, and Vldlr−/− SRN models [60,61]. Aging contributes to prolonged inflammation, which plays a central role in early to late stages of human AMD and is responsible for the deterioration of RPE and photoreceptors [62,63]. Ref-1’s redox-mediated activation of TFs like NF-κB, STAT3, activator protein 1 (AP-1) and early growth response 1 (EGR-1) has direct influence in the immune system via regulating proinflammatory genes such as TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-8 [17,64,65]. In this study, the higher expression of Ref-1 seen in the RPE-choroid of human nAMD and murine L-CNV eyes suggests that Ref-1 may have an association with CNV pathology. Given the involvement of Ref-1 regulated inflammatory mechanisms in multiple cancers, inflammatory bowel disease, and other indications [11], increased Ref-1 in CNV could be a cause or consequence of inflammation, acting directly within inflammatory cells or in other retinal cells producing proinflammatory signals. The deeper understanding of this hypothesis at the molecular level will be an exciting next step.

  In both human nAMD and murine L-CNV, Ref-1 is expressed in multiple layers of the retina and in the choroid, with higher expression in disease, perhaps including the activation of endothelial or microglial cells during neovascularization in nAMD. The overexpression of Ref-1 could potentially be linked to the characteristic increase of activated microglial cells as seen in early and late AMD [66,67], especially given Ref-1’s known effects on promoting NF-κB signaling. We acknowledge though the variability between eyes; notably the AMD sections used to label the RGCs appear to have higher cell number compared to healthy control sections as the AMD samples were obtained from a macular region. The colocalization of Ref-1 with choroidal vasculature in murine L-CNV suggests that Ref-1 signaling has implications for proangiogenic events influenced by excessive production of VEGF and subsequent activation of endothelial cells. The detection of Ref-1 in different cell types of the retina and RPE-choroid in human nAMD indicates that Ref-1 may have functional implication in the regulation of key transcription factors in these cell types; a DNA-repair related role also cannot be ruled out.

  Vldlr−/− mice have extensively been used as a model of human RAP/Type III MNV. In this genetic model, the deficit of the Vldlr gene promotes the crucial angiogenic events in RECs (although the exact mechanism is not known yet) to develop SRN [68]. However, the modulation of hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) under hypoxic cellular environments leads to the upregulation of VEGF-A, which promotes angiogenesis in type III MNV [69]. Ref-1 mediated regulation of hypoxia signaling has previously been reported in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) cells [70]. In our study, increased Ref-1 protein expression in the choroidal and retinal lesions of Vldlr−/− mouse eyes observed over a period of 6 months from the onset of lesion development plus Ref-1’s association with markers of hypoxia (CA9) and vasculature (IB4) strongly indicate the potential of Ref-1 in influencing hypoxia-driven SRN. Ref-1 expression beyond the neovascular area in this model further suggests expression in invading inflammatory cells.

  Considering the contribution of the redox potential of Ref-1 to ocular angiogenesis, we chose to inhibit the redox function of this protein target by using a highly specific small molecule inhibitor. While our first-generation Ref-1 inhibitor APX3330 has completed a Phase 2b clinical trial for DR and DME, we were interested to explore the therapeutic potential of one of our second-generation Ref-1 inhibitors, APX2009, that possess improved redox inhibiting potential, reduced lipophilic nature, and a better efficacy profile. We assessed the interaction of APX2009 with Ref-1 via 1H-15N HSQC NMR. Chemical shift perturbations (CSPs) for Q137 and S164 measured by 1H-15N HSQC NMR suggest that APX2009 binds to a small pocket remote from the endonuclease active site of Ref-1. Molecular modeling of APX2009-Ref-1 interactions are consistent with ligand-based WaterLOGSY measurements and interaction with the small pocket defined by Q137 and S164. CSPs for D70 and N212 might indicate interactions of APX2009 with the endonuclease site or might result from conformational changes in Ref-1 transmitted from the interaction with the remote binding pocket. Binding of other small molecules to the small/remote pocket distant from the active site has been previously reported [58]. In contrast, APX3330 was previously reported to bind to the endonuclease active site pocket (when present at high concentrations) [71] and more recently to the small distant pocket [72]. Thus, the interactions of APX2009 with Ref-1 are consistent with those of APX3330. Neither APX3330 nor APX2009 inhibit the endonuclease activity of this protein [27]. Therefore, APX2009 may mediate its effects through binding to Q137 and S164 that define the distant small pocket. Two binding modes were identified through docking, where the 1,4-naphthoquinone core of APX2009 binds either into or out of the small pocket. In a redox electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA), APX3330 blocked the redox function of Ref-1 while replacement of the benzoquinone core in the analog RN7-58 by a dimethoxynaphthalene core (also with a shorter sidechain) abrogated APX3330’s inhibitory activity [73]. Together, these data are consistent with direct interaction of APX2009 with Ref-1, although further studies will be required to detail the mechanism by which APX2009 inhibits redox activity via interacting with the small pocket.

  Having validated the interaction of APX2009 with the target protein Ref-1, we wanted to further assess the effect of this Ref-1 inhibitor on endothelial cells. Angiogenesis entails the intricate nexus between vascular endothelial cells, various growth factors and cell signaling molecules, which results in the stimulation of vascular endothelial cells, endothelial cell proliferation, cell cycle progression, migration, and tubule formation [74-76]. Ref-1 has been shown to regulate these properties in HRECs [22]. Here, we show that Ref-1 mediates angiogenic features and that the redox inhibitor APX2009 effectively blocks Ref-1 mediated angiogenesis and cell cycle progression (seen via blockade of G1/S-phase transition) involving ocular (human choroidal origin) and non-ocular (human brain and umbilical vein) endothelial cells in a dose-dependent manner. This implies that APX2009’s redox inhibitory potential is antiangiogenic not only in the ocular context but also possibly in other systems. Importantly, APX2009 does not have toxicity towards hPSC-RGCs upon treatment at low micromolar doses although this toxicity study was conducted within a limited scope. Further studies should explore whether APX2009 may offer neuroprotection to the RGCs, given the neuroprotective effects of APX2009 in primary cultures of sensory neurons (dorsal root ganglia) challenged with cisplatin [77] and of APX3330 in the enteric nervous system of a murine model of chronic intestinal inflammation [78].

  Our earlier study showed that i.p. APX2009 with repeated twice daily doses for 14 days is effective in treating murine L-CNV [22]. While we used the same murine L-CNV model which is relevant to studying CNV of human AMD, here we tested the previously unexplored but clinically relevant antiangiogenic potential of a single IVT injection of APX2009 dosed at low micromolar levels . Although one dose of IVT APX2009 in the mouse eyes had a modest half-life, IVT APX2009 in L-CNV mouse eyes had substantial effect nearly similar to the standard of care anti-VEGF treatment in blocking ocular angiogenesis as observed via reduction in lesion volume and lessened lesion leakage. Moreover, APX2009 did not cause any obvious signs of ocular toxicity as noticed in vivo by fundus, OCT, and FA imaging. Given the short half-life, we surmise that APX2009 could potentially inhibit the initial inflammatory reaction resulting from the laser challenge and thus the sequential proangiogenic events likely instigated in CNV. To explore this hypothesis, we asked whether APX2009 diminished innate immune cells of the retina overlying CNV, microglia and macrophages, as these cells can critically regulate vascular formation and their dysregulation upon stress promotes neovascularization [79,80]. These innate immune cells are recruited to the site of inflammation and/or injury in the retina, accumulating and provoking the inflammatory response by releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines in a VEGF-A dependent process [81]. A prior study has shown the anti-inflammatory potential of APX3330 in a mouse model of inflammatory bowel disease [78]. Moreover, our earlier study has displayed the anti-inflammatory activity of APX2009 via reducing nuclear translocation of NF-κB’s subunit p65 and the gene expression of NF-κB’s downstream targets in HRECs [22], which supports the notion that Ref-1 inhibition through APX2009 is capable of exhibiting anti-inflammatory effects in the context of ocular neovascularization. In this study, like anti-VEGF therapy, APX2009 diminished microglia and macrophage populations in the layers of the retina adjacent to the laser challenge. Therefore, the inhibition of Ref-1 can potentially regulate proinflammatory and proangiogenic events in ocular neovascularization. The combination effects of APX2009 and anti-VEGF need to be evaluated to precisely assess sustained effects in blocking ocular angiogenesis.

  To complement our findings in murine CNV, we pursued the potential of APX2009 for the first time in the Vldlr−/− mouse model of SRN. Under low-oxygen cellular environments, the redox activity of Ref-1 influences the function of HIF-1α which dimerizes with its co-subunit HIF-1β to form the HIF-1 complex. The association of this HIF-1 (translocated to the nucleus) with hypoxia response elements in promoters upregulates proangiogenic VEGF-A, CA9, angiopoietin-2 and others [82,59]. In our investigation, Ref-1 inhibition via the systemic treatment with APX2009 in SRN considerably reduced the number of neovascular lesions in Vldlr−/− mouse eyes as seen in the fundus, OCT, and FA images. Moreover, Ref-1 inhibition significantly diminished neovascular area in SRN. This antiangiogenic effect of APX2009 is intriguing as this corroborates the previous finding in which APX3330 impeded SRN in Vldlr−/− mice [23]. APX2009 significantly decreased the expression of CA9, a marker of hypoxia and a known Ref-1 responsive gene downstream of HIF in these lesions. Additionally, CA9 expression associates with a marker of vasculature implying that APX2009 regulates hypoxia-driven angiogenesis in SRN.

  In summary, we demonstrated that the redox function of Ref-1 is required for ocular neovascularization and that Ref-1 inhibition has promise as an ocular antiangiogenic therapy. We showed the structural interaction and target engagement of Ref-1 with a potent redox inhibitor, APX2009, and a conformational change of Ref-1 upon binding to APX2009. This Ref-1 inhibitor displayed antiangiogenic effects in vitro in cultured endothelial cells without toxicity to human RGCs, and in vivo in murine models of CNV (L-CNV) and SRN (Vldlr−/−). Taken together, this study validates that Ref-1 inhibition through APX2009 has therapeutic potential for treating nAMD and potentially other ocular neovascular indications. Additional investigation of therapeutic prospects may enable clinical testing of APX2009 alone or in concert with existing anti-VEGF biologics. A direct comparison of the efficacy of APX2009 vs. APX3330 will also be important. One limitation to be acknowledged in this study is short intraocular half-life of IVT APX2009; however, short exposure could be enough to block multiple downstream signaling pathways through Ref-1 redox inhibition; the oxidized TFs may remain oxidized for an extended period of time. Nonetheless, different formulations or delivery routes will be important to explore. Further, a preclinical synergy testing of IVT APX2009 and an anti-VEGF drug will be valuable to determine amplified combinatorial effects.
