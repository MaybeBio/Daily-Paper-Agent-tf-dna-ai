## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text including abstract, introduction, materials and methods, results and discussion, conclusion, and supplementary figure legends as provided.
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific content, experimental design, data interpretation, and internal consistency of the presented claims. No assessment of editorial fit or journal-specific policy.
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors propose that four superrepressor mutants of the GntR/FadR family regulator DgoR from *E. coli* (A97V, S171L, T180I, M188I) exhibit compromised response to D-galactonate through distinct mechanisms, including loss of effector binding (T180I) or altered allosteric communication (A97V, S171L, M188I). They further propose a model of allosteric communication based on molecular dynamics simulations and validate predictions with experimental data.
- **Visible evidence base** In vivo growth assays, fluorescence reporter assays, western blotting, electrophoretic mobility shift assays, microscale thermophoresis, limited proteolysis, size exclusion chromatography, circular dichroism thermal denaturation, and molecular dynamics simulations (400 ns, 20 total simulations).
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Supplementary figures and tables (S1–S10) referenced but not provided. Detailed simulation analysis (DCC maps, PCA, dynamical network analysis) only partially described in main text. Raw data for binding curves, simulation trajectories, and statistical analyses not available for full assessment.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript presents a comprehensive genetic, biochemical, and computational analysis of allosteric regulation in a GntR/FadR family transcriptional regulator. The combination of in vivo and in vitro approaches with molecular dynamics simulations is commendable and provides a plausible model for allosteric communication in DgoR. However, several concerns regarding the interpretation of simulation data, the quantitative rigor of binding measurements, and the strength of the predictive validation limit the current impact of the work. The manuscript is technically sound in its experimental execution but would benefit from additional controls and more direct evidence linking the proposed allosteric pathways to the observed functional outcomes.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers studying transcriptional regulation in bacteria, particularly those focused on GntR/FadR family regulators, allosteric mechanisms in multidomain proteins, and the application of molecular dynamics simulations to understand protein function. The work may also interest synthetic biologists seeking to engineer allosteric regulators for metabolic pathway control.
- **Major strengths** 1) The use of naturally occurring superrepressor alleles provides a physiologically relevant approach to probe allostery. 2) The combination of multiple experimental techniques (genetics, biochemistry, biophysics) with computational analysis is rigorous. 3) The identification of distinct mechanistic classes among the mutants (loss of binding vs. altered allostery) is a valuable contribution. 4) The predictive validation of the allosteric model with new mutants (F142A, E151A) strengthens the proposed framework.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **Concern ID** R1-M1
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Technical soundness of simulation methodology
  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that MD simulations reveal "unique dynamics disrupting the intrinsic allosteric communication pathways" for each mutant and that these findings "showed strong concordance with experimental data."
  - **Evidence pointer** Results and discussion, section "Molecular simulations of WT DgoR suggest underlying allosteric communication" and "Mutants reveal altered conformational dynamics and allosteric communication compared to WT DgoR"; Figure 8; Supplementary Figures S5–S9
  - **Concern** The simulation methodology has several limitations that are not adequately addressed. First, the DgoR structure is a homology model, not an experimentally determined full-length structure. The accuracy of the modeled dimer interface and the relative orientation of the N-terminal and C-terminal domains are critical for the allosteric conclusions but are not validated. Second, the simulations are only 400 ns in length, which may be insufficient to capture the conformational transitions relevant to allostery, particularly for a large dimeric protein–DNA complex. Third, the authors state that the ED-bound state is "transient" and "biologically plausible" but do not provide experimental evidence for its existence. The comparison of mutant dynamics to WT in this potentially non-physiological state could lead to misleading conclusions. Fourth, the dynamical network analysis and suboptimal path calculations are sensitive to the choice of parameters (e.g., community detection algorithm, edge definitions), and no sensitivity analysis is provided.
  - **Why it matters** The central claim of the manuscript is that the mutants disrupt specific allosteric communication pathways. If the simulation methodology is not robust, the proposed mechanism is not established. The lack of experimental validation for the modeled structure and the transient ED-bound state weakens the foundation of the computational analysis.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should provide additional validation of the modeled structure (e.g., comparison to experimentally determined structures of homologous proteins, cross-linking experiments, or SAXS analysis). They should also demonstrate that the simulation results are converged (e.g., by extending simulation time or showing block-averaged analyses) and that the identified allosteric pathways are robust to parameter choices in the network analysis. Experimental evidence for the existence of the ED-bound state (e.g., via FRET or HDX-MS) would substantially strengthen the model.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M2
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Quantitative rigor of binding measurements
  - **Claim pointer** The authors report apparent KD values for DgoR–DNA interaction from EMSA (WT: 502 ± 32 nM; mutants: 298 ± 21 to 569 ± 28 nM) and for DgoR–effector interaction from MST (WT: 8.4 ± 1.3 μM; A97V: 3.3 ± 0.3 μM; S171L: 20.1 ± 0.6 μM; M188I: 11.3 ± 0.9 μM).
  - **Evidence pointer** Results and discussion, section "In vitro analyses indicate that A97V, S171L and M188I are non-/slightly responsive to D-galactonate due to altered allosteric behavior"; Figure 4 and 5; Supplementary Figure S3
  - **Concern** The KD values are reported as apparent values, but the experimental details for their determination are incomplete. For EMSA, the protein concentrations used, the fitting model (Hill equation with what Hill coefficient?), and the number of independent replicates are not stated. For MST, the authors do not report whether the binding curves reached saturation, whether the signal was specific, or how many replicates were performed. The claim that A97V has "slightly tighter affinity" (3.3 vs. 8.4 μM) is based on a 2.5-fold difference, but without error estimates from multiple independent experiments, this difference may not be statistically significant. Similarly, the claim that S171L has "slightly weaker affinity" (20.1 vs. 8.4 μM) is a 2.4-fold difference that may not be robust.
  - **Why it matters** The distinction between mutants that cannot bind the effector (T180I, R102Q) and those that bind but show altered allostery (A97V, S171L, M188I) is central to the manuscript's conclusions. If the affinity differences are not statistically robust, the classification of the mutants is called into question.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should provide complete experimental details for all binding measurements, including the number of independent replicates, the fitting procedures, and statistical tests for comparing KD values. Ideally, binding isotherms should be shown for all proteins, and the significance of affinity differences should be assessed with appropriate statistical methods.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M3
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Strength of predictive validation
  - **Claim pointer** The authors state that they "validated the allosteric communication model by testing in silico predictions with experimental data" and that F142A and E151A mutants confirm the model's predictions.
  - **Evidence pointer** Results and discussion, section "Establishing hypothesis of allosteric communication via predictive studies"; Figure 9
  - **Concern** The predictive validation is limited to two mutants (F142A and E151A) and the experimental readouts are qualitative (growth phenotype and fluorescence reporter expression). The authors do not provide quantitative measurements of DNA binding affinity or effector response for these mutants. The prediction that F142A would be defective in effector response is consistent with its proposed role in the effector binding pocket, but this is a relatively straightforward prediction. The prediction for E151A (complete loss of DNA binding) is more striking, but the mechanism by which an E151A mutation in the C-terminal domain abolishes DNA binding is not explained in detail. The authors do not test whether the E151A mutant is properly folded or whether the mutation affects protein stability.
  - **Why it matters** The predictive validation is presented as a key strength of the manuscript, but the limited scope and qualitative nature of the validation weaken this claim. A more rigorous test of the model would involve multiple predictions with quantitative readouts.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should provide quantitative binding data (e.g., EMSA or MST) for F142A and E151A mutants, including DNA binding affinity and effector response. They should also assess protein folding and stability (e.g., by circular dichroism or size exclusion chromatography) to rule out non-specific effects of the mutations.
- **Minor Comments**
  - **Concern ID** R1-m1
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Clarity of presentation
  - **Affected element** Figure 1B
  - **Evidence pointer** Results and discussion, section "In vivo analyses validate the superrepressor behavior of DgoR mutants"
  - **Issue** The location of the mutation sites in the C-terminal E-O domain is shown in Figure 1B, but the figure is not described in sufficient detail in the text. It is unclear whether the figure shows a structural model, a sequence alignment, or a schematic diagram.
  - **Required correction** The authors should clarify the nature of Figure 1B and describe the structural context of the mutated residues more explicitly.
  - **Concern ID** R1-m2
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Statistical reporting
  - **Affected element** Growth curves and fluorescence reporter assays
  - **Evidence pointer** Results and discussion, sections "In vivo analyses validate the superrepressor behavior of DgoR mutants" and "Establishing hypothesis of allosteric communication via predictive studies"; Figures 1D, 2, 3, 9B, 9C
  - **Issue** The growth curves and fluorescence reporter data are presented as representative experiments, but the number of biological replicates and the statistical analysis are not stated.
  - **Required correction** The authors should state the number of independent experiments performed and provide appropriate statistical analysis (e.g., mean ± SD or SEM with significance tests).
  - **Concern ID** R1-m3
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Completeness of methods
  - **Affected element** Molecular dynamics simulation details
  - **Evidence pointer** Materials and methods, section "Simulation system setup"
  - **Issue** The methods for MD simulations are described, but several important details are missing: the force field parameters for the ligand (D-galactonate), the method for generating the protein–DNA complex, the equilibration protocol, and the analysis tools used for DCC, PCA, and dynamical network analysis.
  - **Required correction** The authors should provide complete details of the simulation setup and analysis, including software versions and parameters, to ensure reproducibility.
  - **Concern ID** R1-m4
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Interpretation of limited proteolysis data
  - **Affected element** Figure 5B and Supplementary Figure S4
  - **Evidence pointer** Results and discussion, section "In vitro analyses indicate that A97V, S171L and M188I are non-/slightly responsive to D-galactonate due to altered allosteric behavior"
  - **Issue** The limited proteolysis data are used to infer conformational changes upon effector binding, but the interpretation is somewhat subjective. The authors state that A97V and S171L show a conformational change upon effector binding, while M188I does not, but the differences in digestion patterns are not quantified.
  - **Required correction** The authors should provide a more quantitative analysis of the proteolysis data (e.g., densitometry of specific bands) to support their conclusions.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** R1-M1 (simulation methodology and validation of the structural model) is the primary technical concern that must be addressed. The lack of convergence analysis and sensitivity analysis for the network calculations undermines the central claim of distinct allosteric pathways. R1-M2 (quantitative rigor of binding measurements) is also important, as the classification of mutants depends on the reliability of the KD values.
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** 
  - **Originality** The manuscript addresses a significant gap in understanding allosteric mechanisms in GntR/FadR family regulators, particularly for sugar acid metabolism regulators. The use of superrepressor alleles combined with MD simulations is a creative approach. However, the overall concept of allosteric regulation in this family is not entirely novel, and the specific findings, while valuable, represent incremental progress rather than a paradigm shift.
  - **Scientific importance** The work has clear importance for understanding transcriptional regulation in bacteria and the molecular basis of allostery in multidomain proteins. The identification of distinct mutant classes (binding-defective vs. allosterically altered) provides a framework for future studies. However, the broader significance beyond the DgoR system is not fully articulated.
  - **Interdisciplinary readership** The manuscript bridges molecular biology, biochemistry, and computational biology, which could appeal to a broad audience. However, the heavy reliance on simulation data without sufficient experimental validation may limit accessibility for readers without a computational background.
  - **Technical soundness** The experimental techniques are generally well-executed, but the simulation methodology has limitations (as detailed in R1-M1). The lack of quantitative rigor in some binding measurements (R1-M2) and the limited scope of the predictive validation (R1-M3) are additional concerns.
  - **Readability for nonspecialists** The manuscript is clearly written and logically organized. The figures are informative, but some (e.g., Figure 8) are dense and may be difficult for nonspecialists to interpret. The abstract effectively summarizes the key findings.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The manuscript presents a valuable contribution to understanding allosteric regulation in GntR/FadR family regulators, but the central claims regarding the molecular basis of altered allostery in the mutants require stronger computational validation and more quantitative experimental support.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that MD simulations reveal "unique dynamics disrupting the intrinsic allosteric communication pathways" for each mutant is not fully supported due to the limitations of the simulation methodology (homology model, short simulation time, lack of convergence analysis).
- The claim that the ED-bound state is "biologically plausible and relevant" is speculative, as no experimental evidence is provided for its existence.
- The classification of A97V and S171L as "slightly responsive" to D-galactonate based on KD differences that may not be statistically significant is not fully supported.
- The predictive validation of the allosteric model using F142A and E151A mutants is limited by the qualitative nature of the experimental readouts and the lack of mechanistic explanation for the E151A phenotype.
- The statement that "each mutant exhibits unique dynamics" is not quantitatively demonstrated; the differences between mutants are described qualitatively without statistical comparison.
