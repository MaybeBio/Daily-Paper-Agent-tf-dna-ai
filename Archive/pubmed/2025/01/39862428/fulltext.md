# A 2D cell segmentation protocol for monitoring multiple STAT signaling pathways by fluorescence microscopy


## Summary
  Microscopic cell segmentation typically requires complex imaging, staining, and computational steps to achieve acceptable consistency. Here, we describe a protocol for the high-fidelity segmentation of the nucleus and cytoplasm in cell culture and apply it to monitor interferon-induced signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) signaling. We provide guidelines for sample preparation, image acquisition, and segmentation. The approach performs indistinguishably from neural-network-based segmentation while requiring only conventional and cost-effective techniques. The protocol can be adapted to other signaling molecules undergoing nucleo-cytoplasmic shuttling and to high-throughput applications.

  This protocol enables simultaneous monitoring of two STAT isoforms using only conventional techniques and equipment and improves upon the assay published in Szanda et al.1


## Before you begin
  Here, we describe a method for monitoring the activity of multiple STAT proteins in fixed cells by confocal microscopy. The nucleus and a perinuclear cytoplasmic region are precisely isolated, and the nucleus-to-perinuclear cytoplasm ratios of fluorescent STAT proteins are determined as an indicator of STAT activity.1,2 Beyond STAT signaling, we successfully applied this technique for the concurrent quantification of ERK1/2 and JNK kinase activities using fluorescent translocation reporters.3 The approach is ratiometric, suitable for high-throughput assays, and retains single-cell resolution, providing insights into both population behavior and non-genetic cellular diversity.4

## Step-by-step method details

### Serum starvation
  Timing: 15 min (day 2)

  Serum starvation for 14–16 h is necessary to eliminate background activation of many signaling pathways, including the JAK/STAT cascade, induced by ligands present in FBS.10 Volumes are specified for 1 cm2 surface area 8-well plates, adjust according to plate and well dimensions.4.Serum-starve the cells for 14–16 h prior to stimulation (day 2 late afternoon).a.Gently aspirate the medium from wells.b.Add 250 μL DMEM (without FBS)/well and aspirate immediately.c.Add once more 250 μL DMEM (w/o FBS)/well and culture in CO2 incubator for 14–16 h.Note: Use sterile technique and a biosafety cabinet to carry out this step. DMEM (w/o FBS) should be 37°C and equilibrated with 5% CO2 prior to use.

## Expected outcomes

With the specified spectral settings (Figure 1), recommended excitation intensities and acquisition cycle (DAPI – (eGFP + Cell Mask DeepRed) – mScarlet), fluorescent bleed-through should be negligible. Nuclear segmentation is expected to be highly precise, even with suboptimal nuclear staining, while cell boundary identification may show some imprecision, particularly in larger cell clusters.   This approach is sensitive enough to detect the effects of 4 pM IFNβ after 60 min and 1 nM IFNβ after 10 min on STAT1 and STAT3 activity (Figures 4A and 4B, respectively). The protocol outperforms our previously published method for measuring STAT3 activity1 in terms of sensitivity (Figure 4C) and its ability to measure two different STAT isoforms simultaneously, while relying solely on commercially available materials, conventional staining, imaging, and computational methods. The approach provides results comparable to those obtained with a pre-built deep learning-based segmentation method17 (Figure 4D) while being considerably less computationally intensive.


## Limitations
  This method is not suitable for cell segmentation in 3-D cell cultures or tissues, as boundary determination is uncertain in structurally complex cellular environments. Additionally, depending on the detector’s dynamic range, the residual probe fluorescence in the cytoplasm may approach or fall below the detection limit when robust stimuli induce supraphysiological STAT translocation. In such cases, cells with very low cytoplasmic fluorescence are excluded (see Step 15a), reducing yield and potentially underestimating the biological response (agonist efficacy). (However, similar limitations also apply to western blotting.18) Caution is needed when interpreting the biological meaning of STAT translocation, as this parameter may not directly indicate extranuclear STAT activity,19 phosphorylation status or transcriptional activity, particularly in the context of mutant STAT isoforms.20 Notably, different STAT isoforms can exert synergistic effects on certain biological functions (e.g., inflammatory processes) while acting antagonistically in others, such as tumorigenesis.2,19,21,22 This complexity must also be taken into account when interpreting translocation data obtained with present protocol.


## Troubleshooting

### Problem 1 (steps 1–3)
  Low expression of STAT fluorescent probes.


### Potential solution to problem 1
  Low expression levels of STAT translocation probes may result from incorrect amounts of plasmid DNA or transfection reagent in the reaction. Determine the optimal DNA and lipofection reagent amounts in preliminary experiments. To minimize cell toxicity and improve reproducibility, use endotoxin-free plasmid preparation methods. Dilute plasmid DNA in molecular biology-grade TE (pH 8.0) and store plasmids at −20°C in small aliquots to minimize freeze-thaw cycles. Avoid small pipetting volumes (<1 μL) to prevent pipetting inaccuracies.


### Problem 2 (step 9)
  Cell loss during fixation is a common problem in plate-based assays involving multiple solution changes. The primary causes include high confluency at the time of fixation, inadequate or insufficient surface coating for cell adhesion, cold solutions, or cell adhesion being sensitive to Ca2+ and Mg2+ free conditions.


### Potential solutions to problem 2
  Minimize cell loss during fixation/staining by implementing one or more of the following measures. Maintain cell confluency between 70-80% at the time of fixation. Experiment with different surface coatings (e.g., fibronectin, collagen, laminin, polyornithine) depending on cell type. Use 20–25°C or 37°C solutions. Supplement all solutions with 1.2 mM CaCl2 and 1 mM MgCl2; add freshly and monitor for precipitation.


### Problem 3 (step 11)
  Poor nuclear and/or cytoplasmic staining.


### Potential solutions to problem 3
  The concentration of Triton X-100 in the permeabilization solution can be increased to 0.2–0.3%. Nuclear staining can be enhanced by adding 0.25 μM Hoechst 33342 to the Storage solution. Staining times can be extended to 60 min, however, avoid overly bright CellMask staining to minimize fluorescent cross-talk (see before you begin).


### Problem 4 (step 12)
  Fluorescent bleed-through of the HCS CellMask signal into other channels is unlikely with the specified microscope settings and fluorophores. However, due to the brightness of HCS CellMask dyes, batch-to-batch variability and pipetting errors may still pose a risk.


### Potential solutions to problem 4
  Prepare samples expressing the fluorescent protein probes and stain them with the nuclear dye (Hoechst 33342) but not with Cell Mask, following steps 1–11. Image these 'control samples’ using the settings in microscope settings, fine-tuning parameters for acceptable signal-to-noise and minimal photobleaching (step 12). Plate and fix naïve cells (lacking fluorescent probes and not stained with the nuclear dye) and stain them with a series HCS Cell Mask dilutions (e.g., 1:1,000 to 1:6,000,000). Image these samples using the STAT probe and nuclear channel settings determined for ‘control samples’ while optimizing far-red channel for CellMask. Choose dye concentrations and far-red channel settings that provide strong far-red signals with minimal bleed-through (see Figure 1 for spectra).


### Problem 5 (step 14)
  Inadequate segmentation of cytoplasm.


### Potential solutions to problem 5
  Effective identification of cell borders is most sensitive to: i) the quality of CellMask staining, ii) proper imaging of CellMask staining, and iii) the lower threshold setting in the Identify Secondary Object (cytoplasm) module. Based on our experience, CellMask dyes provide robust and stable staining when appropriate concentrations are determined in preliminary experiments. For better microscopic resolution of cell boundaries, consider using a smaller confocal pinhole or longer dwell times during imaging. Additionally, to increase contrast between cells, you may add 50 μg/mL wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) to the cells for 2 min at the end of the stimulation period, immediately before fixation. If WGA is conjugated with a far-red fluorophore (e.g., Alexa647, CF633, CF640R), it can be combined with HCS CellMask Deep Red staining using our protocol, typically enhancing cell boundary contrast. Finally, it is recommended to adjust the lower threshold in the Identify Secondary Object module for each set of dependent images.
