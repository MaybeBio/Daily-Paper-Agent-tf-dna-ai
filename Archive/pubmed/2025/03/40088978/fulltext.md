# Blood-Brain Barrier Crossing Biopolymer Activates Immunity in Primary Brain Lymphoma by Targeting c-Myc and α-PD-1: Artificial Intelligence Analysis


## Abstract
  Primary Central Nervous System Lymphoma is an aggressive central nervous system neoplasm with poor response to pharmacological treatment, partially due to insufficient drug delivery across blood-brain barrier. In this study, we developed a novel therapy for this lymphoma by combining a targeted nanopolymer treatment with an immune checkpoint inhibitor antibody (anti-PD-1).

  A N-(2-hydroxypropyl)methacrylamide copolymer-based nanoconjugate was designed to block tumor cell c-Myc oncogene expression by antisense oligonucleotide. Angiopep-2 peptide was conjugated to the copolymer to facilitate nanodrug crossing of the blood-brain barrier. Systemically administered polymeric nanodrug, alone or in combination with immune checkpoint inhibitor antibody anti-PD-1, was tested in syngeneic mouse model of A20 intracranial brain lymphoma. There was no significant survival difference between saline- and free anti-PD-1-treated groups. However, significant survival advantage vs. saline was observed upon treatment with nanodrug bearing Angiopep-2, H6 (6 histidines for endosome escape), and c-Myc antisense alone and especially when it was combined with anti-PD-1 antibody. Animal survival after combined treatment was also significantly increased vs. free anti-PD-1. Artificial Intelligence-assisted analysis of gene expression database after RNA-seq of tumors was used to find novel immune pathways, molecular targets and the most effective multifunctional drugs together with future drug prediction for brain lymphoma in vivo model. Spectral flow cytometry and RNA-seq analysis revealed a robust activation of tumor infiltrating T lymphocytes with enhanced interferon γ signaling and polarization to M1-type macrophages in treated tumors, which was confirmed by immunofluorescence staining. In summary, a new effective blood-brain barrier crossing nano immuno therapeutic system was developed that effectively blocked tumor c-Myc acting in combination with immune checkpoint inhibitor anti-PD-1 to treat primary brain lymphoma. The treatment improved survival of tumor-bearing animals through activation of both the adaptive and innate immune responses.


## Introduction
  Primary Central Nervous System Lymphoma (PCNSL) is a rare but very aggressive form of lymphoma with a poor clinical outcome. PCNSL is typically confined to the brain, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and eyes, without systemic spread. PCNSL represents only 4% to 6% of all extranodal lymphomas, but its incidence is increasing, particularly in the elderly1,2. Men acquire this pathology twice more often than women3,4. Approximately 95% of PCNSL are diffuse highly infiltrative large B-cell lymphomas characterized as a “whole brain disease” particularly at relapse5,6. Although the overall PCNSL incidence is rather low, this disease is devastating with only 20–25% of long-term (>5 years) survival as compared with systemic lymphoma with over 70% of long-term survival6,7. The median survival of PCNSL patients did not change for the last 40 years and is about 6–7 months7.

  Surgical removal of PCNSL generally has little or no benefit for survival. To date, there is no standard treatment for recurrent PCNSL, which was confirmed in a recent paper summarizing HOVON 105/ALLG NHL 24 phase III intergroup study5. Because nearly 90% of NHL is of B cell origin with overexpression of CD20, the anti-CD20 antibody (Rituximab, αCD20) has been a paradigm-shifting approach to treat those tumors. However, with the first therapeutic Rituximab antibody approved for oncology patients, drug resistance and tumor relapse are common upon treatment8–10.

  Upregulation of c-Myc oncogene8 is among the most common genetic alterations in PCNSL (such as mutations in TP53, CDKN2A/p16 and MYD88 as well as the rearrangement of IGH-BCL-6 genes9). C-Myc is one of the master transcription factors governing many aspects of cancer biology. C-Myc-driven tumors usually express high levels of immune checkpoint molecules including CD274/PD-L1 and CD479 (also known as the “don’t eat me signal”) that regulate anti-tumor immune response. Increased expression of these checkpoints in cancer suppresses immune response through the PD-1/PD-L1 axis and CD47-SIRPα interactions, and thereby helps tumors to evade immune surveillance10–13. Although immune checkpoint inhibitors have dramatically changed the theme of modern cancer treatment, the Non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma (NHL) tumors, especially PCNSL, generally have a poor response to these therapies14,15, partially due to the poor drug penetration across the blood-brain barrier (BBB).

  HPMA copolymers are widely used biocompatible, non-immunogenic carriers of biologically active compounds16–21. Various anticancer agents22,23, IgG fragments24, peptides25, and antisense oligonucleotides (AONs)26 have been successfully conjugated onto HPMA copolymers for treatment of various human diseases including cancer. Combination of HPMA copolymer-based chemotherapy and checkpoint inhibition using either anti-PD-L1 (αPD-L1) antibodies or multivalent HPMA copolymer-based PD-L1 peptide antagonists demonstrated enhanced therapeutic efficacy in breast cancer models compared to monotherapies27,28. Additionally, HPMA copolymers have been used in CD20 and CD38 receptor crosslinking-mediated induction of apoptosis in lymphoma animal models29–32 and cells from patients diagnosed with various subtypes of B cell malignancies33,34.

  Artificial intelligence (AI) as a tool for medical navigation is still at an early stage, but it is fast developing and is the future of precision medicin35–40. Generative AI enables the creation of Digital Twins (DTs) that are digital replicas of physical objects or systems connected by bidirectional data and information flow38,41. State-of-the-art cell DTs will focus on predicting changes of single cellular characteristics, e.g., transcriptome or cell morphology, and a multimodal DT could cover numerous cell characteristics38,41. Machine learning (ML) subfield of AI focuses on the development of models that can analyze, cluster, interpret data and make predictions based on provided input42. AI and ML are used to train models with pre-collected datasets, which can then be applied to predict outcomes for new input data. We hypothesize that the development of a model able to scale from the PCNSL “cell level to the human level” may become a breakthrough in drug development in the age of precision medicine. Our pre-collected datasets include relevant data such as nanopolymer characteristics, pharmacodynamic (PD) data, omics data, pre-clinical information, and other variables deemed relevant. AI of gene expression analysis was used here to find novel pathways and molecular targets together with future multifunctional effective drug prediction for PCNSL in vivo models. In the current study, we aimed to improve the treatment outcome of PCNSL by combining a targeted nano therapy with immune checkpoint inhibition. We designed a HPMA (P) copolymer-based nanodrug (P/AP-2/H6/c-Myc AON) with BBB crossing capacity to target c-Myc oncogene based on the hypothesis that c-Myc blockade may render PCNSL more susceptible to immunotherapy. We tested the efficacy of nano drug P/AP-2/H6/c-Myc AON alone, and in combination with αPD-1 in a preclinical PCNSL immunocompetent mouse model (Table 1). Our results demonstrated promise of this new strategy for PCNSL treatment.


## Results

### Design, Synthesis, and Characterization of HPMA copolymer-based nanoconjugates
  Brain vascular endothelial cells (ECs) enable limited transport of molecules in an active, energy-driven mode. Low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR)-related protein (LRP)-1 mediates the transport of Angiopep-2 peptide (TFFYGGSRGKRNNFKTEEY) across the BBB43–46. Natural biodegradable polymers for drug delivery through BBB using AP-2 were successfully used to treat brain tumors and neurodegenerative conditions47,48. Therefore, in this brain tumor targeting study, AP-2 was selected to be incorporated into polymer carrier to facilitate the nanoconjugate BBB crossing and to ultimately improve therapeutic efficacy. The pH-sensitive peptide H6 was chosen to assist the escape of AON-based c-Myc inhibitor from the endosomal compartment. Moreover, a monomer containing pyridyldithio (SS-Py) group, N-[2-(2-pyridyldithio)ethyl methacrylamide (PDMA), was synthesized to provide an attachment site of c-Myc AON via thiol-disulfide exchange reaction. The graphical structure of each component and rationale for PCNSL therapy design are summarized in Table 1.

  The synthetic strategy for the HPMA copolymer nanoconjugate is shown in Figure 1A. RAFT polymerization was used to synthesize the polymer precursor as previously described22,23. The step-by-step characterization as described in Experimental section indicated that the synthetic approach was feasible and successful. The Mw of the polymer precursor (P/AP-2/H6-ss-Py) was 114 kDa, Mw/Mn = 1.29. The nanoconjugate contained 23.2% wt. of AP-2, 4.8% wt. of H6, and 23.5% wt. of c-Myc AON. The in vivo pilot study confirmed that AP-2 would enhance BBB penetration when it is grafted onto a linear polymer carrier (Fig. 1B). The size of the conjugate in aqueous solution was measured on a Malvern Zetasizer Nano-zs90 (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK). The hydrodynamic size of the polymer carrier is 14.5 ± 0.15 nm; when conjugated with AON, it is 20.2 ± 0.15 nm.

  Biodistribution of different conjugates in various organs in 24 hours after systemic injection is shown on Fig. 2. To examine drug accumulation, ex vivo imaging of organs after injection of PBS (control) and conjugates P-Cy5.5 and P-AP-2-Cy5.5 was performed. Both polymers showed accumulation in draining organs (liver, kidney) with some spleen and lung signals (Fig. 2A), quantified in Fig. 2B. Because the polymers are biodegradable, this accumulation is transient. The strongest accumulation in the brain was observed with the AP-2 containing polymer when compared to the mice injected with PBS and polymer without AP-2, indicating that the conjugate with AP-2 had increased capacity to traverse the BBB (Fig. 2C).


### Therapeutic effects of HPMA copolymer – c-Myc AON nanodrug (P/AP-2/H6/c-Myc AON) treatment alone and in combination with αPD-1 antibody in a PCNSL preclinical tumor model
  We assessed the therapeutic efficacy of the HPMA copolymer – c-Myc AON nanodrug in vivo by using an A20 mouse brain lymphoma tumor model. Groups of BALB/c mice carrying intracranial A20 lymphoma were treated with: PBS (Group 1), free αPD-1 antibody (Group 2), P/AP-2/H6/c-Myc AON (Group 3), and the combination of αPD-1 with P/AP-2/H6/c-Myc AON (Group 4) (Fig. 3 A, B). The concentration of AON and αPD-1 was 5 mg/kg each, based on doses recommended for patient treatment (range 3–10 mg/kg for AONs and αPD-1) and on our published experience47,76.

  After five consecutive drug treatments, tumor-bearing mice were kept till they met the endpoint criteria. First, we verified the efficiency of c-Myc specific AONs in blocking target expression. Western blots using A20 cells exposed to the c-Myc specific AONs demonstrated a substantial inhibition of c-Myc protein expression (Fig. 3C). Similar result was obtained in treated tumors (Fig. 3D), and c-Myc inhibition was increased in the combination group. Survival analysis (Fig. 3E) showed that the checkpoint inhibitor αPD-1 antibody (Group 2, n=6) did not confer any advantage over PBS control (Group 1, n=10). However, P/AP-2/H6/c-Myc AON nanodrug alone (Group 3, n=6) conferred significant survival advantage over PBS group in mice with intracranial A20 brain lymphoma (*p<0.05). In mice with lymphoma treated by combination of αPD-1 and P/AP-2/H6/c-Myc AON nanodrug (Group 4, n=6), the survival advantage over PBS control mice was even greater (**p=0.001). Increase in survival upon combined drug treatment was also highly significant vs. free αPD-1 (***p=0.0001). These results clearly demonstrated an additive effect on lymphoma treatment when the c-Myc targeted nano therapy was combined with the checkpoint inhibitor αPD-1.


### Activation of T lymphocytes in vivo by P/AP-2/H6/c-Myc AON nanodrug alone and in combination with αPD-1 antibody by spectral flow cytometry
  The survival study using A20 mouse brain lymphoma model showed that the combination of P/AP-2/H6/c-Myc AON and αPD-1 had an additive survival benefit when compared with monotherapy groups using either αPD-1 or P/AP-2/H6/c-Myc AON nanodrug alone. To determine the effects of our drug treatment on the immune functions, we used spectral flow cytometry to examine changes in T cell populations upon treatment as the therapeutic mechanism at the cellular level. A20 lymphoma tumors were surgically removed from mouse brain after three drug treatments (Fig. 4A, B), and tumor tissues were dissociated and processed for spectral flow cytometry. We did not observe significant changes in total CD3+ T cell counts in the drug treatment groups (Fig. 3C), but there was a trend of downregulation of helper CD4+ T lymphocytes in all treatment groups vs. PBS control, especially in combination Group 4 (Fig. 3D). Intriguingly, no significant changes in FoxP3+CD4+ regulatory T cells (Tregs) were found in all treatment groups (Fig. 4E). However, both αPD-1 alone (Group 2) and the combination therapy (Group 4) significantly increased the number of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells (Fig. 4F). There was also a trend of CD8+ T cells to increase in Group 3 tumors treated by P/AP-2/H6/c-Myc AON alone, but it was not statistically significant due to sample-to-sample variability (Fig. 4F).

  We further focused on the CD8+ T cell activation by examining the frequency of CD69+ and interferon γ (IFNγ) expressing subpopulations. The results showed a significant increase in CD69+ CD8 T cell subpopulation in both monotherapy groups (Group 2 and Group 3), with a more significant increase of the activated CD8+ T cells upon combination treatment (Group 4) vs. PBS (Fig. 4G and 4H; *p<0.05 and **p<0.01). A similar significant increase in IFNγ producing (IFNγ+) activated CD8 subpopulation was observed upon c-Myc inhibition by the nanodrug (Group 3, *p<0.05) and upon combination treatment (Group 4, **p<0.01). Overall, the spectral flow cytometry data revealed that both polymer nanodrug and the combination of nanodrug with αPD-1 treatment resulted in the activation of the adaptive immune response represented by CD8+ T cell populations.


### Activation of immune function by P/AP-2/H6/c-Myc AON nanodrug alone and in combination with αPD-1 antibody confirmed by RNA-seq
  To further understand the impact of the HPMA copolymer-based nanodrug treatment on brain lymphoma, RNA-seq was performed using total RNA extracted from the A20 brain tumors after drug treatment in vivo. Gene expression profiles were analyzed in two groups where c-Myc was inhibited (Group 3 tumors treated with P/AP-2/H6/c-Myc AON drug and Group 4 tumors treated with the combination of P/AP-2/H6/c-Myc AON and αPD-1). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified by comparing the treatment groups with PBS control (Group 1). DEGs were defined as genes with at least 1.5-fold higher or lower expression than that in PBS Group 1, with p<0.0549. As shown in the volcano plots, we identified 59 DEGs in Group 3 as compared with PBS control, and 69 DEGs in Group 4 as compared with PBS group (Fig. 5A). Among those DEGs in both Group 3 and Group 4, 42 were shared between the two groups (Venn diagram in Fig. 5B), indicating that blockade of c-Myc expression by the nanodrug may be the major driver of the changes in the gene expression profile, which is largely related to immune function.

  Further analysis revealed that in both Group 3 and Group 4 approximately 50% of DEGs were members of the IFNγ signaling and response pathways (Figs. 5C,D), including multiple important regulators of immune function such as Ccl5, Gbp, Ifng, Nos2, and Tgtp34–37. Pathway analysis showed that a significant portion of the DEGs were related to the T lymphocyte chemotaxis, proliferation as well as differentiation (Figs. 5C,D). For instance, DEGs from Group 3 treatment were primarily linked to T cell proliferation and T cell chemotaxis/migration (Fig. 5C1). This can be attributed to the action of genes from the shared pool (Ccl5, Cxcl9–11, Ifng, Igf2) and genes unique to Group 3 (Tnfaip2 and Xcl1)50,51, and both Ccl5 and XCL1 have been linked to recruitment of T cells into the tumor microenvironment52. Similarly, most of the DEGs in Group 4 treatment were found in the shared T cell related gene pool (Fig. 5C2), and a fraction of genes that were unique to Group 4 treatment were related to white blood cell activity (Ccl22, Hcar2, Itgam, Klrk1, Klrk4). This may be due to the action of the nano polymer drug and αPD-1 co-injected in Group 4, which led to better survival advantage over monotherapy (Fig. 3E).

  AI-based Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) Immune Gene Enrichment helped to predict, understand and confirm the mechanisms of action and nano immunotherapy treatment direction. Many gene changes revealed by IPA were linked to IFNγ pathway (Fig. 5E1, E2) that is important for anti-tumor response and M1 MΦ activation (innate immune response). Other bioinformatic methods including PANTHER Biological Process (Fig. 5D) and ClueGO immune pathway analysis (Fig. 5F) also confirmed activation of T cell responses and IFNγ signaling. The convergence of these methods convincingly shows that activation of adaptive T cell-mediated immune response and IFNγ pathway genes were hallmarks of DEGs in treated groups.


### Activation of M1 macrophage population by P/AP-2/H6/c-Myc AON nanodrug alone and in combination with αPD-1 confirmed by immunohistochemical analysis
  RNA-seq has also identified a subset of genes (7 of the DEGs in Group 3 and Group 4) associated with intratumoral M1 macrophages (MΦ) (Fig. 6A). A M1 MΦ phenotype is generally considered proinflammatory and anti-tumorigenic. To validate RNA-seq data, immunofluorescent staining of tumor sections was performed for a common M1 MΦ marker iNOS/NOS2. Our immunostaining clearly demonstrated a substantially increased iNOS+ population in the tumor tissues after treatment with the nanodrug or its combination with αPD-1 (Fig. 6B) compared to PBS group.

  Overall, in line with our spectral flow cytometry data, the gene expression signatures in the A20 lymphoma further suggested that our nanodrug treatment alone or in combination with checkpoint inhibition significantly enhanced both the adaptive and innate immune functions inside the brain lymphoma microenvironment.


## Discussion
  The PCNSL has been viewed as one of the most common malignancies associated with acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) and its incidence is highly linked to Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection in HIV patients53. However, in the past three decades, there has seen a steady rise in PCNSL incidence in the elderly immunocompetent (HIV negative) population54. These PCNSL patients with an intact immune system would be a perfect population for immunotherapy treatment such as checkpoint inhibition if we are able to define an effective approach to render those tumors more responsive to immune checkpoint inhibitors55.

  In this study, we sought to use a different strategy and devised a HPMA copolymer-based nanodrug containing a morpholino AON specifically targeting c-Myc gene expression for PCNSL treatment. With the AP-2 peptide as the tumor BBB-penetrating moiety45,57–60, this nanoconjugate was shown to efficiently cross this biological barrier, was enriched in the brain parenchyma and exhibited anti-tumor function. Based on the promising results of the nano polymer drug on brain lymphoma treatment, we hereby propose a potential mode of action of the HPMA copolymer-based nanodrug for PCNSL treatment (Fig. 7).

  Briefly, once injected in the vein, the P/AP-2/H6/c-Myc AON nanodrug can cross BBB through AP-2 mediated transcytosis (Fig. 7, Step 1). AP-2 peptide can also function as a tumor cell targeting mechanism as various human cancers have enhanced expression of low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP-1), the receptor with high binding affinity with AP-243 (Fig. 7, Step 2). Upon endocytosis mediated by LRP-1, another peptide (hexahistidine, H6) conjugated with the polymer helps nanodrug release into the cytoplasm by permeabilization of the endosomal membrane (Fig. 7, Steps 3 & 4). The c-Myc specific AON will be released in the cytoplasm by glutathione through the cleavage of the disulfide bond that connects the AON with the polymer to block the biosynthesis of c-Myc protein (Fig. 7, Step 5), which will likely result in tumor cell apoptosis/growth arrest and down-regulation of CD47 and PD-L1 (Fig. 7, Steps 6 & 7). These series of orchestrated actions of the nanodrug will hopefully elicit a strong anti-tumor activity, and this tumor killing effect will be enhanced by checkpoint inhibitor (αPD-1) treatment to further activate adaptive and innate anti-tumor immunity (Fig. 7, Steps 8–10).

  Cancer immunotherapy has completely reshaped the cancer treatment paradigm in recent years. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (such as αCTLA-4, αPD-1 and αPD-L1) have been increasingly expanding their indications. However, very little success has been seen in the PCNSL treatment with immune checkpoint inhibitors. C-Myc is one of the most studied oncogenes and it also plays an important role in PCNSL8,61. Recent studies have shown that c-Myc is also involved in the regulation of adaptive immune function as a positive regulator of two immune checkpoints PD-L1 and CD479. Therefore, c-Myc blockade can not only slow down tumor cell proliferation but can also activate the immune system to fight cancer by down-regulating the expression of PD-L1 and CD47. Indeed, our western blot analysis demonstrated that in the nanodrug-treated PCNSL tumor tissue, with the decreased c-Myc levels, PD-L1 was also downregulated (Supplemental Fig. 1), indicating that the nanodrug might well activate the local tumor immunity in addition to blocking the expression of transcription factor c-Myc. A limitation of our study is that we did not study CD47, which is not yet considered a significant target for PCNSL therapy. However, it is expected to decrease by our therapy as previous work has shown that c-Myc inhibition decreased both PD-L1 and CD4713.

  When combined with αPD-1 antibody, an additive effect was observed between our c-Myc targeting nanodrug (that also inhibits PD-L1) and the checkpoint inhibitor in both the survival study and in the functional flow cytometry analysis (Figs. 3 and 4). These data are in line with the results of a study showing that inhibiting both PD-L1 and PD-1 using a bispecific antibody in mouse tumor models resulted in a greater anti-tumor effect than monotherapies against PD-L1 or PD-162. Intriguingly enough, our combination seems to have activated not only the adaptive immune function (T cells), but also enhanced the innate immunity, as our RNA-seq data and immunofluorescence staining demonstrated a strong signature of M1 macrophage gene and protein expression (Fig. 6). We also hypothesize that the additional suppression of c-Myc by systemic αPD-1 (Fig. 3D) may be an indirect effect of αPD-1 administration through upregulation of c-Myc inhibitory IFNγ pathway63 by M1 macrophages and Cd69+IFNγ+ CD8 cells (Figs. 4 and 6).

  Deregulated c-Myc transcription is also a hallmark of aggressive B cell non-Hodgkin lymphomas61,64 and multiple myeloma65, with an extremely poor prognosis due to enhanced chemoresistance and aggressiveness. Currently, only few agents have been utilized to interfere with c-Myc oncogenic functions in order to inhibit c-Myc-driven lymphomagenesis66,67. Increased MYC (c-Myc in human) in PCNSL was confirmed by recent CALGB 50202 study68. However, a correlation between the anti-lymphoma efficacy and immune response activation by c-Myc suppression, or c-Myc inhibition strategy for PCNSL treatment have not been previously reported. Just like all transcription factors, c-Myc is generally considered challenging to target pharmacologically69. With our HPMA copolymer-based nanodrug, we were able to efficiently block the protein synthesis of c-Myc in the primary brain lymphoma. This tumor treatment strategy can achieve even better therapeutic efficacy when combined with the immune checkpoint inhibitors. Our nano therapy is independent of CD20 inhibition and, therefore, might be used to treat Rituximab-resistant forms of lymphomas. A novel HPMA copolymer-based nanomedicine drug property70 is activating a variety of immune cells. These include cytotoxic T cells (such as anti-tumor activated CD69+ CD871), neutrophils, natural killer cells, and macrophages in the brain. As a result, IFNγ signaling72 and response pathways are activated, including multiple important regulators of immune function such as Ccl5, Gbp, Ifng, Nos2, and Tgtp, which provides robust cancer immunotherapy.

  Nevertheless, in this work, we only systemically delivered the αPD-1 therapy in its native form with limited BBB crossing capacity mainly due to the leaky nature of tumor BBB. Even though the combination of αPD-1 with P/AP-2/H6/c-Myc AON exhibited additive effects. In fact, our recent work using a different naturally occurring poly(β-malic acid) polymer-based drug delivery system to deliver checkpoint inhibitors across BBB to treat glioblastoma demonstrated excellent BBB penetrating efficiency as well as therapeutic efficacy47,73. Thus, it is reasonable to expect that a greater synergy can be obtained when we deliver both the targeted nano therapy and checkpoint inhibitor antibodies using the nano polymer system with active BBB-penetrating and tumor-specific targeting mechanisms74. We believe this strategy can be a valuable addition to the current arsenal of lymphoma therapies.


## Conclusions
  The combination therapy of BBB crossing HPMA copolymer-based c-Myc targeted nanodrug with αPD-1 checkpoint inhibitor demonstrates novel effective treatment approach in PCNSL activation of anti-tumor immune response. It could be particularly useful for therapy of Rituximab-resistant PCNSL. This nanomedicine approach fully responds to the modern requirements in clinical oncology where immunotherapy is combined with effective targeted therapy. AI-powered signaling pathway analysis predicts activation of IFNγ-mediated immune response suggesting that inhibitors of this pathway may be useful in future targeted therapy for PCNSL.

  A common challenge in AI analysis and model training is tuning hyperparameters to optimize performance39,75. Here, we introduce a new approach that combines data from network science (multifunctional drug syntheses, RNA-seq and flow cytometry analyses) as an example for future precision medicine. This strategy is on the crossroads of molecular pharmacology, synthetic chemistry, immunology, neurosurgery, neuro-oncology, and AI. Our data predict effective drugs based on AI approach, and preclinical survival of mice bearing PCNSL highlights the potential for precision cancer treatment where molecular science meets AI76.


## Materials and Methods

### Materials
  HPMA77, N-[2-(2-pyridyldithio)ethyl methacrylamide (PDMA)78, and RAFT agent, 4-cyanopentanoic acid dithiobenzoate (CTA)79 were synthesized as previously described. N-methacryloylglycylglycine-Angiopep-2 (MA-GG-AP-2; AP-2: TFFYGGSRGKRNNFKTEEY), cysteine-modified AP-2 (CGG-TFFYGGSRGKRNNFKTEEY) and N-methacryloylglycylglycylhexahistidine (MA-GG-H6) were synthesized by solid phase peptide synthesis (SPPS) methodology using Fmoc/tBu strategy on RINK amide MBHA resin. Their structure was validated by MALDI-TOF-MS and RP-HPLC (Agilent Technologies 1100 series, Santa Clara, CA). Mouse c-Myc morpholino antisense oligonucleotide (5’-CCAACGCCCAAAGGAAATCCAGCCT-3’) with 3’- pyridyldithio (SS-Py) modification was purchased from Gene Tools (Philomath, Oregon). N-(3-Aminopropyl)methacrylamide hydrochloride (APMA) was purchased from Polysciences (Warrington, PA). Cy5.5-NHS ester was purchased from Lumiprobe (Hallandale Beach, FL). The heterobifunctional linker succinimidyl-4-(N-maleimidomethyl)cyclohexane-1-carboxylate (SMCC) was purchased from Highfine Biotech (Suzhou, China). Initiators VA044 (2,2’-azobis[2-(2-imidazolin-2-yl)propane]dihydrochloride) and V65 (2,2’-azobis(2,4-dimethylvaleronitrile) were from Wako Pure Chemical Industries (Deerfield, IL). Tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine hydrochloride (TCEP) was from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA).


### Synthesis of P-AP-2-H6-c-Myc AON

#### Preparation of polymer precursor P-AP-2-H6-ss-Py
  RAFT polymerization was performed in mixed solvent of DMSO/H2O at 45°C according to the molar feed ratio: MA-GG-AP-2 (2%; 24.8 mg), PDMA (SS-Py; 4%; 5 mg), HPMA (92%; 66 mg), MA-GG-H6 (2%; 10 mg); [M]/[CTA] =1,000; [CTA]/[VA044] = 3. Monomers were added into a 2-mL ampoule with a stirring bar and then connected to a Schlenk line. After three vacuum-nitrogen cycles to remove oxygen, 0.3 mL degassed DI H2O/DMSO was added into the ampoule to dissolve the (co)monomers followed by addition of the RAFT agent CTA and initiator VA044. The ampoule was sealed, and polymerization was performed at 45°C for 20 h. The polymer into acetone. After purification (re-dissolving/re-precipitation) and end-modification with 100 folds of V-65, 62 mg polymer precursor was obtained. Yield ~60%.


#### Characterization of polymer precursor P-AP-2-H6-ss-Py
  The average molecular weight and polydispersity of P-AP-2-H6-ss-Py were determined on AKTA FPLC system equipped with Superose 6 HR10/30 column and UV detector (280 nm) (GE Healthcare, Waukesha, WI). Sodium acetate buffer (50 mM pH 6.5) containing 30% acetonitrile was used as eluent and flow rate was 0.4 mL/min. Mw and Mw/Mn were calculated using polyHPMA standards as 114 kDa, Mw/Mn=1.29.

  The amount of incorporated peptides AP-2 and H6 was quantified by amino acid analysis (AAA). 1.83 mg polymer was hydrolyzed with 0.5 mL 6 N hydrochloric acid (HCl) at 120°C for 16 h in a sealed ampule. HCl was then removed under reduced pressure, and the sample was further dried under vacuum followed by addition of 1 mL DI water. After centrifugation, the supernatant was precolumn derivatized with 4-phthaldialdehyde and analyzed by HPLC with fluorescence detection (Ex 229 nm; Em 450 nm). Free L-tyrosine (for AP-2) and L-histidine were used for calibration curves, respectively. Based on the calculation, the polymer precursor was found to contain 23.2% wt peptide AP-2 and 4.8% wt peptide H6.

  To determine the content of ss-Py, a series of 2-thiopyridone solutions (10 – 200 μM) in PBS pH 7.4 was prepared. A standard curve was obtained by measuring the absorbance of each solution at 360 nm. Then the polymer precursor was dissolved in PBS with concentration of 2 mg/10 mL. Solution of 100 μL cysteamine in methanol (1.53 mg/mL, 20 mM) was added into 1 mL polymer solution. The sample was kept at RT for 30 min and then scanned (200 – 450 nm) using a UV-visible spectrophotometer. Based on the absorbance at 360 nm of the sample and the concentration, the SS-Py content was calculated as 200 nmol/mg (5% wt).


#### Attachment of c-Myc AON to polymer precursor P-AP-2-H6-SS-Py
  c-Myc AON with 3’-pyridyldithio (SS-Py) modification was first reduced to expose the thiol group (-SH) using TCEP (20 mM, PBS pH 7.4, 37°C, 40 min). After ultracentrifugation (Mw cut-off 3 kDa, PBS pH 6.5 for 3 times), the c-Myc AON solution with thiol group was then added to the polymer precursor solution (5 mg/mL polymer in PBS pH 6.5). The reaction was allowed for 3 h at room temperature. Unconjugated c-Myc AON was removed via ultracentrifugation (Mw cut-off 30 kDa, distilled water, 4 times). After lyophilization, the c-Myc AON content was quantified using UV-vis spectrophotometry (molar extinction coefficient = 262720 at 262 nm in 0.1 N HCl) as 23.5% c-Myc AON (wt). To ensure c-Myc AON release into the cytoplasm, it is conjugated to HPMA polymer backbone via cleavable S-S linker. In vivo intracellular glutathione concentration (1–10 mM) is about 1000 times higher than that in the extracellular compartment (2–20 μM), therefore c-Myc AON will be efficiently released.


### Crossing of blood-brain barrier by nanodrugs
  A pilot study was performed in healthy BALB/c mice to ensure transcytosis capacity of HPMA copolymer nanoconjugates containing the AP-2 peptide.

  Fluorescently labeled HPMA copolymer-based carrier (P-Cy5.5) was synthesized by copolymerization of HPMA with APMA followed by conjugation of Cy5.5-NHS ester according to previously described protocol29. The number average molecular weight of the polymer was 8.72 ×104 Da (Mw/Mn = 1.14). To incorporate AP-2, the pendant amino groups of P-Cy5.5 were first converted to maleimide groups by reaction with SMCC, then cysteine-modified AP-2 was attached onto the polymer chain via thioether bond. The AP-2 content was quantified using bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay as 12 AP-2 per chain.

  The conjugates were administered intravenously to mice with 12 nmol Cy5.5 concentration equivalence. In vivo images were taken 3 h to 24 h post tail vein administration. By comparison with controls, AP-2 appears to be an efficient BBB-passing agent. Images of live mice (Fig. 1) and ex vivo images of brain and other organs (Fig. 2) were taken using IVIS live imaging system (Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA) after mice were sacrificed. The IVIS software was used to perform a semi-quantitative analysis of fluorescent signals shown as average radiant efficiency (Fig. 2).


### Mouse PCNSL model system and nanodrug treatment
  Animal studies were approved by Cedars-Sinai Medical Center Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Syngeneic PCNSL tumor model was established by using BALB/cAnNCrl mice (Charles River Laboratories) intracranially inoculated with A20 mouse brain lymphoma cells (5000/mouse). Tumor cells were implanted using the stereotactic apparatus (Stoelting, IL) as reported elsewhere80. Mice carrying the A20 brain lymphoma tumors were treated with anti-PD-1 antibody (BioXcell, NH) or nanodrug 5 days post tumor inoculation. Five doses of drugs were systemically delivered through intravenous (tail vein) injection twice a week to all groups of animals in the survival study. Then animals were followed up and their health condition was monitored daily till they reached the designated experimental endpoint.


### Survival study
  To evaluate the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of the HPMA copolymer-based nanoconjugates on brain lymphoma, we treated BALB/c mice inoculated with A20 B-cell lymphoma cells (5000 cells/mouse). Five days post tumor inoculation, mice were treated with PBS (Group 1), anti-PD-1 antibody (Group 2; 5 mg/kg of antibody), P-AP-2-H6-c-Myc AON (Group 3; 5 mg/kg of AON) and the combination of anti-PD-1 and P-AP-2-H6-c-Myc AON (Group 4; 5 mg/kg of antibody and 5 mg/kg of AON) through tail vein injection under isoflurane anesthesia as we published before81. All mice were monitored daily after drug treatment and were euthanized when they met the set experiment endpoint criteria. Deaths were recorded and Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated using Prism 10.


### Prevention of anaphylaxis and cytokine storm
  Most preclinical studies used intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of checkpoint inhibitor antibodies81 to avoid anaphylactic shock after intravenous (i.v.) injection. The i.p. therapy in mouse models increased anti-tumor CD4 and CD8 T cell effector function causing Tregs apoptosis in the tumor. However, the clinically preferred route of drug administration is i.v., in particular for BBB delivery, but it is more immunogenic. Acute adverse immune mediated side effects like anaphylaxis were developed after i.v. and could be effectively inhibited with premedication using platelet activating factor (PAF) inhibitors and antihistamine treatment82–87. To treat PCNSL with clinically relevant setting and dose of αPD-1 at 5 mg/kg, we administered NITs i.v. To reduce the immunogenicity and avoid shock, we administered PAF inhibitor CV6209 (100 mg/kg; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA) and antihistamine triprolidine (200 mg/kg, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) i.p. 30 min prior to the second and subsequent doses of drug i.v. administration for groups 2 and 4. In preliminary i.v. treatments, the nanodrugs were used with concentration of αPD-1 at 3 or 5 mg/kg, and 50% of animals died after second-third treatment from anaphylaxis, with tremendous elevation of IgE antibody and IL-4. As premedication is a standard procedure in the clinic88–90, our premedication regimen was used here as we published47,73. After premedication, no animals died when NIT was administered to mice together with the clinically used 5 mg/kg of αPD-147.


### Spectral flow cytometry analysis
  In parallel to the survival study, another cohort of animals bearing the A20 brain lymphoma tumors were sacrificed for flow cytometry study after 3 doses of various I.V. drug treatments. After dissection, tumor tissues were immediately chopped into small pieces and digested with 1.5 U/ml Liberase TL (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany) in RPMI medium at 37°C for 30 min. The dissociated cells were filtered through 70 μm cell strainers and loaded into a 96-well plate (~1 million/well) for the flow cytometry staining procedures. After blocking with rat anti-mouse CD16/32 (Mouse BD Fc Block, clone 2.4G2, BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA), the tumor cells from each tumor were split into two 96-well plates and stained with a panel of T cell antibodies (Supplemental Table 1). Flow cytometry was performed using the spectral flow cytometer SONY SA3800 (SONY Biotechnology, San Jose, CA). From each well, 100,000 events were recorded and analyzed. One-way ANONA and post-hoc two-tailed t-test were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.1.1 (San Diego, CA) to determine statistical significance between treatment groups with a cutoff p value of 0.05.


### RNA-seq analysis
  In parallel with the spectral flow cytometry assays, we split part of the A20 brain lymphoma tissues during tumor harvesting and extracted total RNA for RNA-seq analysis. Total RNA samples from Group 1 (PBS), Group 3 (P-AP-2-H6-c-Myc AON) and Group 4 (P-AP-2-H6-c-Myc AON + anti-PD-1) were extracted using the RNeasy mini kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA). Libraries for RNA-Seq were prepared with Nugen Universal Plus kit and sequencing data was generated by the NextSeq500 for a single-read 75 bp read run at the UCLA core lab (http://www.cores.ucla.edu/clinical-microarray-core/). Data quality check was carried out on Illumina SAV. Demultiplexing was performed with Illumina Bcl2fastq2 v 2.17 program. The reads were mapped to the latest UCSC transcript set using Bowtie2 version 2.1.092 and the gene expression level was estimated using RSEM v1.2.15. Among all 24,055 sequenced genes, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as genes with 1.5x fold higher (upregulated) or lower (downregulated) expression levels compared to Group 1 with a statistical difference p<0.0549.


### Western blot
  Total lysates from A20 lymphoma cell culture or fresh brain lymphoma tumor derived from A20 cells were generated with either RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) or with T-PER tissue protein extraction reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with cOmplete™ protease inhibitors and PhosStop™ phosphatase inhibitors (Roche) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Total protein concentration was determined by standard BCA method and 30 μg of total lysate was loaded onto the NuPAGE Bis-Tris 4–12% gradient Gel and run in the 1x MES running buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Proteins were then transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane, and the membrane was blocked with Odyssey blocking buffer (Li-COR, Lincoln, NE) for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies were incubated with the membrane in the Odyssey blocking buffer overnight at 4°C with shaking. Membrane was then washed 4 times by PBS-T (0.1% Tween-20) and incubated with IRdye-680 or IRdye-800 conjugated secondary antibodies (Li-COR) all at 1:40,000 dilution in the dark for 1h at room temperature. Finally, the membrane was washed 4 times by PBS-T (0.1% Tween-20), rinsed with PBS and scanned by the Odyssey CLx near-infrared imaging system (Li-COR). Primary antibodies used in this study are as follows: Rabbit anti-c-Myc (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA; Cat No. 5605S. 1:500 dilution), Mouse anti-β actin (Cell Signaling Technology; Cat. No. 3700S. 1:2000 dilution), Rabbit anti-GAPDH (Cell Signaling Technology; Cat. No. 5174S. 1:2000 dilution) and rabbit anti-PD-L1 (Biorbyt, Cambridge, UK; Cat. No. orb235069, 1:200).


### Immunofluorescence staining and image analysis
  Monoclonal antibody for iNOS/NOS2 detection was purchased from Cell Signaling Technologies (rabbit anti-mouse iNOS, Cat. No. 13120). Secondary antibody goat anti-rabbitRhodamine was from Millipore-Sigma. Cryo-sectioned brain slices with the A20 tumors were air dried then fixed with 1% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. Then tissue sections were denatured in 6 M urea solution (pH 3.5 in 0.1M glycine-HCl) for 1 h at 4°C. After PBS wash, the sections were blocked with blocking buffer (10% BSA, 2% goat serum with 0.05% Tween-20 in 1x PBS) for 1–2 h at 4°C. Primary iNOS antibody (1:100 in the blocking buffer) was incubated overnight with shaking at 4°C followed by PBS wash and incubation of secondary antibodies (1:250) for 2.5 h at room temperature. After washing, slides were mounted with mounting solution containing DAPI and imaged with the Apotome Axiocam microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).


### Data analysis and statistics
  Kaplan-Meier analysis was used for animal survival studies and log-rank test was applied to determine the statistical differences between drug treatments (p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant). Spectral flow cytometry data were recorded and analyzed using the Sony SA3800 software (SONY Biotechnology), and final results were analyzed in each group by two-tailed Student’s t-test using Prism 10.4.0 software (GraphPad) to evaluate the differences between drug treatment and PBS control group with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant).

  In RNA-seq data analysis, the reads were mapped to the latest UCSC transcript set using Bowtie2 version 2.1.0, and the gene expression levels were estimated using RSEM v1.2.15. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the trimmed means of M-values (TMM) from the edgeR program. TIMMs from edgeR were also used to construct a 3D Principal Component Analysis (PCA) plot based on gene expression profile of individual samples and were used to normalize the gene expression. ClueGO gene enrichment analysis was performed to identify the DEGs related pathways by using Cytoscape93.

  Immunofluorescence imaging data acquired with the Apotome Axiocam microscope (Zeiss, Germany) were analyzed by the Zeiss Zen Lite software, and the numbers of M1 MΦ and total cells were counted by Image J. The percentage of M1 MΦ to total number of cells in each study group was calculated and graphed by Prism9 software, with t-test used to determine statistical significance.
