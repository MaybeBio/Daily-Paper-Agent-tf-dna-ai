# Astaxanthin ameliorates benzalkonium chloride–induced dry eye disease through suppressing inflammation and oxidative stress via Keap1‐Nrf2/HO‐1 signaling pathways


## Background
  Dry eye disease (DED) predominantly results from elevated tear film osmolarity, which can not only cause ocular inconvenience but may lead to visual impairments, severely compromising patient well‐being and exerting substantial economic burdens as well. Astaxanthin (AST), a member of the xanthophylls and recognized for its robust abilities to combat inflammation and oxidation, is a common dietary supplement. Nonetheless, the precise molecular pathways through which AST influences DED are still poorly understood.


## Methods
  Therapeutic targets for AST were identified using data from the GeneCards, PharmMapper, and Swiss Target Prediction databases, and STITCH datasets. Similarly, targets for dry eye disease (DED) were delineated leveraging resources such as the Therapeutic Target Database (TTD), DisGeNET, GeneCards, and OMIM databases, and DrugBank datasets. Interactions among shared targets were charted and displayed using CytoScape 3.9.0. Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway analyses were conducted to elucidate the functions of pivotal targets within the protein–protein interaction network. Molecular interactions between AST and key targets were confirmed through molecular docking using AutoDock and PyMOL. Molecular dynamics simulations were performed using GROMACS 2022.3. Viability of human corneal epithelial cells (hCEC) was assessed across varying concentrations of AST. A mouse model of experimental DED was developed using 0.1% benzalkonium chloride (BAC), and the animals were administered 100 mg/kg/day of AST orally for 7 days. The efficacy of the treatments was assessed through a series of diagnostic tests to evaluate the condition of the ocular surface after the interventions. The levels of inflammation and oxidative stress were quantitatively assessed using methods such as reverse transcription‐polymerase chain reaction (RT‐PCR), Western blot, and immunofluorescence staining.


## Results
  Network pharmacology suggests that AST may alleviate DED by influencing oxidation–reduction signaling pathways and reducing oxidative stress provoked by BAC. In vivo experiments demonstrated an improved overall condition in AST‐administered mice in contrast to the control group. Immunofluorescence staining analyses indicated a decrease in Keap1 protein in the corneal tissues of AST‐treated mice and a significant increase in Nrf2 and HO‐1 protein. In vitro studies demonstrated that AST significantly enhanced cell viability and suppressed reactive oxygen species expression under hyperosmotic (HS) conditions, thereby protecting the human corneal epithelium.


## Conclusion
  AST is capable of shielding mice from BAC‐induced DED, decelerating the progression of DED, and mitigating oxidative stress damage under HS conditions in hCEC cells. The protective impact of AST on DED may operate through stimulating the Keap1‐Nrf2/HO‐1 signaling pathway. Our research findings indicate that AST may be a promising treatment for DED, offering new insights into DED treatment.


## INTRODUCTION
  Dry eye disease (DED), impacting as many as 30% of individuals globally, 1  is predominantly triggered by the hyperosmolarity of the tear film. 2 ,  3  This condition leads to ocular discomfort and may impair vision, significantly reducing the quality of life of patients 4 ,  5  and imposing a substantial economic cost on society. 6 ,  7  Although the complete pathogenic mechanism of DED remains unclear, inflammation exerts a pivotal part. It stems from the primary innate immune response followed by secondary adaptive responses, propelling a harmful cycle of worsening DED symptoms. This cycle encompasses inflammation, hyperosmolarity, cellular apoptosis, and degradation of the tear film, further intensifying the condition. 8  The persistent cycle of inflammation compromises the ocular surface, exacerbating symptoms, increasing clinical severity, and causing neuropathic pain. 9 ,  10  Addressing the inflammatory component is crucial, as effective and safe anti‐inflammatory treatments are vital for optimal therapeutic effects. 11  Although corticosteroids are commonly prescribed, their long‐term use is limited due to risks such as glaucoma and cataracts. Alternative treatments like non‐glucocorticoid immunomodulators, 12  including tacrolimus (or FK506) eye drops, 13  offer promise by inhibiting T‐lymphocyte activity and somewhat relieving symptoms, though they require extended periods to become effective and may suppress necessary inflammatory mediators. 3  Oxidant–antioxidant imbalance further exacerbates inflammation through pro‐inflammatory pathways. 14 ,  15 ,  16  In individuals affected by DED, diminished levels of protective antioxidants result in an accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the cornea. External factors, including environmental pollutants, UV radiation, and microbial infections, can intensify oxidative stress, aggravating the vicious cycle and worsening DED symptoms. 16 ,  17 ,  18  Research has shown that antioxidants, whether applied topically or ingested as part of the diet—including substances like lactoferrin, vitamins D and E, catechins, or specific plant extracts—can help alleviate the signs and symptoms of DED. 19 ,  20 ,  21 ,  22

  Astaxanthin (AST), a member of the xanthophylls category that includes carotenoids, is a red‐pigmented carotenoid. 23 ,  24  It was initially extracted from lobsters 24  and is predominantly obtained from Haematococcus pluvialis, the richest natural source, where its concentration can be as high as 3.0% of the dry weight. 25  AST is recognized as a potent nutritional supplement with strong anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant properties. Extensive preclinical and clinical research has highlighted its diverse biological effects, ranging from UV protection and immune modulation to cardiovascular protection, antidiabetic benefits, neuroprotection, antiaging, anticancer properties, and the prevention of cell membrane peroxidation. 26 ,  27 ,  28 ,  29  Research has shown that oral administration of astaxanthin can improve the symptoms and signs of mild to moderate DED in middle‐aged and elderly patients. 30  Additionally, astaxanthin has been found to alleviate dry eye by activating the SLC7A11/GPX4 pathway, inhibiting ferroptosis, enhancing autophagy, and reducing inflammation. 31 ,  32  However, the mechanism by which astaxanthin treats dry eye through its effects on reducing oxidative stress remains to be explored.

  Network pharmacology and bioinformatics integrate core principles and analytical methodologies from fields such as medicine, biology, and computer science, allowing for a comprehensive mapping of the complex relationships within biological networks that link drugs, targets, and diseases. This approach facilitates the exploration and prediction of drug pharmacodynamics and mechanisms of action. 33  Additionally, molecular docking is a highly effective tool for estimating the binding orientation and affinity of ligands to proteins, providing a means to confirm both the effectiveness and mode of action of drugs. 34  In this research, we employed bioinformatics to thoroughly explore the molecular mechanism of AST in the treatment of DED and conducted both in vivo and in vitro validation experiments to support the conclusions. ## MATERIALS AND METHODS

### Prediction of AST and DED targets
  Potential human targets for AST were predicted using databases such as GeneCards, PharmMapper, and Swiss Target Prediction, and STITCH datasets. These predicted targets were then standardized in the UniProt database and converted into gene names. After duplicates were removed, a comprehensive list of AST targets was compiled.

  For DED, human‐related targets were extracted from databases like TTD, DisGeNET, GeneCards, OMIM, and DrugBank. The final dry eye target list was prepared after duplicates were eliminated.


### Identification of common targets for AST and DED
  Common targets between AST and DED were identified, and a Venn diagram was drawn using the online platform. The drug‐target‐disease network was visualized using CytoScape version 3.9.0, providing a comprehensive view of the interrelations between AST and DED targets.


### Construction of protein–protein interaction network
  Protein–protein interaction (PPI) data were retrieved from the STRING database using a medium‐confidence threshold of 0.40. Using CytoScape 3.9.0, a PPI network was constructed, with node color, size, and layout dynamically representing the significance of each node. The MCODE plug‐in in CytoScape was applied to detect biological function modules within the target proteins. This analysis was carried out with a node score threshold set at 0.1 and a K‐core value of 2. Hub genes were further extracted using the cytoHubba plug‐ins.


### Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analysis
  Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted to explore the molecular function (MF), cellular component (CC), and biological process (BP) associated with the 83 identified common targets. In addition, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment was analyzed using the DAVID tool to further elucidate the functional pathways involved. The top 50 KEGG pathways were categorized into six groups by the KEGG pathway database, and irrelevant pathways were excluded. The outcomes were presented using an online visualization tool (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/).


### Molecular docking validation
  AutoDock Vina was employed to assess the binding affinities between AST and key target proteins. The process began with obtaining and preparing the three‐dimensional structures of the target proteins, followed by optimizing and minimizing the energy of the ligand structures, all using default settings. The ligands were then flexibly docked into the target proteins' binding sites, enabling the exploration of multiple binding conformations. Finally, the docking interactions were visualized for further analysis.


### Molecular dynamics simulation
  GROMACS 2022.3 was employed to carry out molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. Initial preprocessing of the small molecule was accomplished by implementing the general amber force field (GAFF force field using AmberTools22, adding hydrogen atoms with Gaussian 16 W, and computing restrained electrostatic potential (RESP) potentials. The calculated potentials were subsequently integrated into the system's topology file for MD simulations. 35 ,  36  Simulation parameters were maintained at 300 K and 1 bar, utilizing the Amber99sb‐ildn force field and the Tip3p water model as the solvent. System neutralization was achieved by adding Na+ ions. Energy minimization was conducted using the steepest descent method. This phase was followed by equilibration under number of particles volume temperature (NVT) and number of particles pressure temperature (NPT) ensembles, each over 100 000 steps with a coupling constant of 0.1 ps and for a duration of 100 ps. The main simulation ran for 5 million steps, using a step length of 2 fs, summing up to a total simulation time of 100 ns. Trajectory analysis was then conducted, which included calculating the root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), and radius of gyration for each amino acid in the trajectory. This analysis was further supplemented with molecular mechanics/generalized born surface area (MMGBSA) and a free energy topography examination.

  Binding free energy calculations were performed using the molecular mechanics Poisson–Boltzmann surface area method to evaluate the strength of intermolecular interactions within the AST–IL6 and AST–PPARG complexes. The g_mmpbsa tool in GROMACS was utilized to compute the binding affinities of the simulated receptor–ligand complexes.


### Reagents
  Benzalkonium chloride (BAC, B6295) was sourced from Sigma‐Aldrich (USA). AST (472–61‐7) was obtained from MCE (China). Minimum essential medium, fetal bovine serum, and penicillin–streptomycin solution were obtained from Gibco (USA). ROS assay kit (S0033S), SOD assay kit (S0101S), and CAT assay kit (S0051) were purchased from Beyotime (China). Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8) was also sourced from Beyotime. DNA extraction and RNA isolation were performed using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit and the RNeasy Mini Kit, both of which were provided by QIAGEN (Germany). Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (23227) from Thermo Fisher Scientific (USA). Trizol reagent for RNA isolation was procured from Invitrogen (USA), and radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer and glutaraldehyde for cell lysis and tissue fixation were obtained from Biosharp (China). Blot blocking buffer was purchased from NCM Biotech (China). A protease inhibitor cocktail was purchased from New Cell and Molecular Biotech (China). For immunodetection, antibodies against Keap1 (60027‐1‐Ig), Nrf2 (16396‐1‐AP), HO‐1 (10701‐1‐AP), matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9) (10375‐2‐AP), glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) (60004‐1‐Ig), interleukin ‐ 1 beta (IL‐1β) (26048‐1‐AP), and α Tubulin (14555‐1‐AP) were obtained from Proteintech (China).


### Animal model
  Thirty female C57BL/6N mice (6–8 weeks of age) were obtained from the Capital Medical University and accepted by the experimental animal ethics committee(AEEI‐2024‐202, July 12, 2024, Beijing, China). 37  The mice were randomly divided into three groups: phosphate buffered saline (PBS), BAC, and BAC + AST (n = 10 per group). In summary, mice in the BAC and BAC + AST groups accepted a daily topical application of 5 μL of 0.1% BAC in both eyes for seven consecutive days, whereas the PBS group received PBS in both eyes under the same conditions. In the DED + AST group, a weighed quantity of 5% AST (100 mg/kg) was dissolved in 0.5% sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC‐Na) and administered orally using plastic feeding tubes. For purity of the experiment, the PBS and BAC groups received the same volume of 0.5% CMC‐Na as the BAC + AST group.


### Corneal fluorescein staining and tear secretion measurement
  A sodium fluorescein solution was applied to the mouse's lower conjunctival sac using a micropipette. After a trio of blinks, a slit‐lamp biomicroscope enhanced by cobalt blue illumination was employed to observe the cornea, and the resultant image was both captured and analyzed. Each of the cornea's four quadrants underwent individual scoring based on the extent of fluorescein staining, evaluated through a four‐level scale: 0 for no detectable staining, 1 for superficial stippling with fewer than 30 dots, 2 for punctate marks exceeding 30 but lacking diffuse staining, 3 for extensive diffuse staining absent of any distinct plaques, and 4 for visible fluorescein plaques. These scores were cumulatively calculated to yield a comprehensive score ranging between 0 and 16. After treatment, tear production was assessed; a phenol red cotton thread was inserted into the lower fornix, one third of the distance from the lateral canthus, and kept there for 60 s. The interaction of the tears with the phenol red dye turned the thread red, the extent of which was meticulously measured to determine tear production.


### RT‐PCR
  Total RNA from mouse corneal tissues was extracted using Trizol reagent, whereas total RNA from human corneal epithelial cells (hCEC) was isolated using the RNeasy Mini Kit. The RT‐qPCR protocol, per reference, commenced with reverse transcription of 1.0 μg of RNA to complementary DNA (cDNA) using the High‐Capacity RNA‐to‐cDNA kit. 38  Subsequent qPCR analyses were performed using PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix on a 7500 Real‐Time PCR system. GAPDH served as the internal control gene, and quantitative PCR data were processed using the comparative cycle threshold (Ct) method, where target gene expression changes were calculated as 2−ΔΔCt. Details of the primers employed for qRT‐PCR are presented in Table 1.


### Hematoxylin and eosin and periodic acid–Schiff staining
  Eyeballs, complete with upper and lower eyelids, were obtained and subsequently stored in 10% neutral buffered formalin for preservation. After preservation, the samples were progressively dehydrated using a series of ethanol solutions and washed with xylene. Once prepared, the specimens were then embedded in paraffin blocks. Sagittal sections were cut from these blocks and were stained using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) reagents. The structure of the cornea and conjunctiva, alongside the goblet cell count, were meticulously examined under a BX51 microscope configured with specific optical settings.


### Western blot
  hCECs were lysed on ice with RIPA buffer containing a protease inhibitor. Protein concentrations were measured using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal protein amounts from each sample were separated via 10% sodium dodecyl‐sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE, Invitrogen) and transferred onto 0.22‐μm polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Merck Millipore, Germany). The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk for 90 min at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies specific for Keap1 (1:1500), HO‐1 (1:1500), α Tubulin (1:3000), and GAPDH (1:15000). After the tris‐buffered saline with tween 20 (TBST) wash, PVDF was incubated with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies (1:15000) for 90 min at indoor temperature. Protein bands were detected using a Bio‐Rad system, and their levels were quantified using ImageJ software, normalized to GAPDH and α Tubulin as internal controls.


### Cell viability assay
  Cell viability was evaluated using the CCK‐8 assay. hCECs were cultured for 24 h in either a 312‐mOsm/L common or a 450‐mOsm/L hyperosmotic (HS) medium with different concentrations of AST. After the addition of CCK‐8 reagent, the cells were incubated, and optical density was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.


### ROS measurement
  ROS levels were measured using a ROS assay kit. hCECs were cultured for 24 h in either a 312‐mOsm/L isotonic medium or a 450‐mOsm/L HS medium with varying concentrations of AST, and then washed with fresh culture medium. After the hCECs were washed, their ROS production was assessed using a spectrophotometer.


### Detection of superoxide dismutase(SOD) and catalase(CAT)
  The activities of SOD and CAT were measured using their respective detection kits(Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, Nanjing, China). According to the manufacturer’s instructions, the provided protocols were followed. Briefly, 1 × 106 c‐kit positive cell lysate or homogenate was added into the detection buffer, and the maximum absorption wavelength was examined at 450 and 520 nm by the colorimetric method for the detection of enzymatic activities of SOD and CAT respectively.


### Immunofluorescence staining
  Entire eyeball sections or isolated cells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. After a rinse, 0.1% Triton X‐100 was employed to permeabilize the samples at room temperature for 15 min. The sections were then treated with 10% goat serum for 1 h at the same temperature to block nonspecific binding. After this preparation, the sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies specifically targeting Keap1, Nrf2, HO‐1, MMP‐9, and IL‐1β at dilutions of 1:100, 1:100, 1:100, 1:200, and 1:100, respectively. On the following day, these were exposed to secondary antibodies from Invitrogen at a dilution of 1:500, maintained at 37°C for 1 h. DAPI (4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole) staining was performed to highlight the nuclei. Fluorescent images were obtained using a confocal laser scanning microscope. Finally, band analyses were performed using Image J.


### Statistical analysis
  The data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Group differences were evaluated using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with p‐values < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Each experiment was repeated at least thrice.


## RESULTS

### Acquisition of targets associated with AST intervention in DED
  The two‐dimensional chemical structure of AST is shown in Figure 2A. To gather AST‐related targets, four different databases were utilized. After duplicates were removed, 474 unique AST targets were identified (Figure 2B). Similarly, 1467 DED‐related targets were compiled from 5 databases by excluding duplicate entries (Figure 2C). Using the UniProt database to standardize gene symbols, 1163 targets from the GeneCards database were selected based on a relevance score exceeding 5.2. Of these, 83 common targets were identified between AST and DED. A Venn diagram, drawn using an online tool, shows this overlap (Figure 2D). To construct the drug‐target‐disease interaction network, CytoScape version 3.9.0 was employed, with common targets highlighted in red, other targets in blue, DED in green, and AST in purple (Figure 2E).


### Development and evaluation of a PPI network
  The overlapping targets were uploaded to STRING platform to form the PPI network (Figure 3A).

  CytoScape version 3.9.0 was also used to build a PPI network with 58 nodes and 289 edges (Figure 3B). The increased size and dark color of the nodes in the figure represent a more significant effect on AST. The top 20 targets ranking by degree are presented in Table 2 and shown in Figure 3C. Using MCC algorithm in cytoHubba plug‐in, the top six hub genes (TNF, IL6, STAT3, JUN, PTGS2, MYC) were determined (Figure 3D). In addition, the MCODE plug‐in was used to cluster three different function modules. The MCODE score for cluster 1 was 23.857, comprising 29 nodes and 334 edges. For cluster 2, the score was 4.0, with five nodes and eight edges, whereas cluster 3 had a score of 3.0, including three nodes and three edges (Figure 3E). The protein clusters identified were further analyzed using Gene Ontology biological processes (GO‐BP) analysis. The identified protein clusters were analyzed for GO‐BP (Figure 3F).


### Functional enrichment analysis of GO and KEGG
  The DAVID platform was employed to analyze the GO and KEGG pathway enrichment data for the 83 common targets of AST and DED. Figure 4A shows the top 20 BPs, MFs, and CCs. It revealed the main enriched BP is positive regulation of transcription from RNA polymerase II promoter, positive regulation of gene expression, and signal transduction. The main CC is cytoplasm, nucleus, and cytosol. The main MF is protein binding, identical protein binding, and DNA binding.

  Dry eye was mainly involved in FoxO signaling pathway, IL‐17 signaling pathway, and Wnt signaling pathway (Figure 4B). Besides, chemical carcinogenesis‐ROS is particularly important. Figure 4C shows that chemical carcinogenesis‐ROS is mostly related to bio‐macromolecule damage, gene mutations, altered gene expression, activation of oncogenic pathways, and inflammatory response; besides, we found that it interacts with the classic oxidative stress pathway Keap1‐Nrf2/HO‐1. Figure 4D shows the classification of the top 50 pathways enriched by KEGG. Figure 4E shows that these pathways can be categorized into four groups: cellular processes (e.g., cell growth and death, transport, and catabolism), organismal systems (e.g., the immune system, endocrine system, and development and regeneration), human diseases (encompassing cancer, infectious diseases, endocrine and metabolic disorders, cardiovascular diseases, neurodegenerative diseases, and drug resistance to antineoplastic agents), and signal transduction. Figure 4F presents a chord diagram of these three pathways. Additionally, the drug‐disease‐target‐pathway network is shown in Figure 4G. Each pathway was influenced by shared targets, suggesting that AST exerts therapeutic effects on DED through the modulation of multiple biological pathways.


### Molecular docking validation
  Molecular docking was conducted using AutoDock Vina software to verify the binding ability and modes between AST and the eight targets of dry eye. Eight targets (PTGS2, STAT3, CXCL8, JUN, MYC, TNF, PPARG, and IL6) were selected as docking receptors, with AST serving as the ligand Figure 5. Additionally, the affinity was used as a quantitative parameter to assess the binding strength between AST and its corresponding docking receptors. An affinity value below −4.25 kcal/mol indicates the existence of binding activity between the ligand and target. Affinity values below −5.0 kcal/mol suggest good binding activity, whereas values below −7.0 kcal/mol indicate strong docking interactions. 39  With the exception of JUN, all eight targets exhibit strong binding capability. Particularly, PTGS2 demonstrates the highest affinity among them (Table 3).


### MD simulation
  To validate the stability of ligand–receptor binding, MD simulations were conducted utilizing the AST–IL6 and AST–PPARG complexes.

  RMSD is a crucial metric for evaluating protein structural alterations. In this study, we determined the RMSD values for protein–small molecule interactions and constructed corresponding plots. Specifically, Figure 6A shows the RMSD curve for the AST–IL6 complex, whereas Figure 7A shows the RMSD profile for the AST–PPARG complex. The findings demonstrate that the RMSD values for both AST–IL6 and AST–PPARG complexes remain relatively stable within the 20–100 ns time frame, indicating the stability of their binding interactions.

  RMSF serves as an important measure to evaluate the dynamic properties of proteins. In this analysis, we computed the RMSF values for the interactions between proteins and small molecules and visualized them in plots. Specifically, Figure 6B shows the RMSF pattern for the AST–IL6 complex, whereas Figure 7B presents the RMSF distribution for the AST–PPARG complex. The results suggest that the RMSF values are lower in the binding regions and higher in the nonbinding regions, implying that the binding of small molecules exerts a notable influence on the protein's stability.

  The radius of gyration (Rg) is an indicator of the compactness of a system and the extent of constraints, often used to evaluate protein folding. A higher Rg signifies reduced stability, whereas a lower Rg suggests a more compact and stable system. Figure 6C shows the Rg plot for the AST–IL6 complex, and Figure 7C shows the Rg for the AST–PPARG complex. The results reveal that the Rg values decrease when the proteins bind to small molecules compared to their unbound state, indicating that small‐molecule binding induces a more compact protein conformation.

  Hydrogen bonding (HBond) is a key metric for evaluating interactions between proteins and small molecules. In this study, we calculated the HBond values and plotted the results. Figure 6D shows the HBond profile for the AST–IL6 complex, whereas Figure 7D shows the HBond data for the AST–PPARG complex. The maximum number of hydrogen bonds for AST–IL6 is 3, and that for AST–PPARG is 2, contributing to the overall stability of these complexes. SASA is an indicator for evaluating the surface area of proteins. In this simulation, we calculated the SASA between the protein and small molecules, and plotted the SASA chart. Figure 6F shows the SASA plot of AST–IL6. Figure 7F shows the SASA plot of AST–PPARG. The results show that before the protein binds with the small molecules, the SASA value of the protein is relatively large, whereas it decreases after binding, indicating that the binding of small molecules reduces the surface area of the protein.

  The Gibbs energy landscape (Figures 6E and 7E) represents the energy distribution and interactions between molecules within a given system, providing valuable insights into molecular interactions, conformational states, and overall stability. This analysis helps elucidate critical features, contributing to an in‐depth understanding of the system's behavior at a molecular level. Peaks or low‐energy regions in the free energy landscape typically correspond to energetically stable conformations. Principal component analysis was conducted on RMSD and gyration radius data, yielding two principal components: PC1 and PC2. These components were then utilized to generate the Gibbs energy landscape. In the Gibbs energy landscape of both the AST–IL6 and AST–PPARG complexes, there exists a region of lower energy. This indicates that both complexes have stable binding states.

  Table 4 presents the results of calculated binding free energy. In this study, we computed the binding free energies of AST–IL6 and AST–PPARG complexes. The results reveal that AST–PPARG exhibits significantly negative values across multiple energy terms, including VDWAALS (−2156.25 ± 17.42 kcal/mol), EEL (−20350.27 ± 61.21 kcal/mol), and ∆TOTAL (−6061.30 ± 106.91 kcal/mol), whereas AST–IL6 shows corresponding values of −1345.50 ± 15.78 kcal/mol, −11868.05 ± 98.03 kcal/mol, and −4800.75 ± 127.11 kcal/mol, respectively. These findings suggest that under the simulation conditions, AST–PPARG may demonstrate higher stability and stronger solvent interactions compared to AST–IL6.


### AST intervention in vivo

#### Evaluation of AST used on dry eye animal
  The anti‐DED effect of AST was observed by developing a C57 mouse DED model. Compared to the BAC group, the BAC + AST group exhibited a significant reduction in corneal fluorescein staining scores; however, a small region with epithelial defects still persisted at low concentrations (Figure 8A,B). For tear secretion, no statistically significant differences were observed among the three groups (Figure 8C). Besides, BAC can reduce conjunctival goblet cells, but compared to the BAC group, the BAC + AST group showed an increase in the number of conjunctival goblet cells (Figure 8D,E). Meanwhile, H&E staining showed that, compared to the PBS group, the BAC group had a significantly increased infiltration of corneal inflammatory cells, accompanied by structural damage characterized by thinning of the epithelial cell layer and thickening of the stromal layer. In contrast, the BAC + AST group exhibited significant improvement in the ocular surface compared to the BAC group (Figure 8F).

#### AST alleviates inflammation
  The expression of MMP9 and IL‐1β, which are indicative of corneal epithelial barrier function, was also examined. AST significantly suppressed MMP9 (Figure 9A) and IL‐1β (Figure 9B) expression, both induced by BAC, as detected using immunofluorescence staining in the corneal epithelium. Statistical analysis of the fluorescence intensity of MMP9 and IL‐1β (Figure 9C) revealed significant differences between the BAC group and both the PBS and BAC + AST groups. Overall, these findings suggest that AST effectively mitigates BAC‐induced corneal injury and inflammation.


#### AST ameliorated dry eye via the Keap1‐Nrf2/HO‐1 pathway
  To examine whether AST modulates the Keap1‐Nrf2/HO‐1 signaling pathway in BAC‐induced DED in vivo, a series of experiments were carried out. Keap1 acts as an inhibitor of Nrf2, whereas HO‐1 is a downstream target gene regulated by Nrf2. AST downregulated Keap1 (Figure 10A) and upregulated the expression of Nrf2 and HO‐1 (Figure 10B,C), as observed in the corneal epithelium after BAC exposure, with detection carried out using immunofluorescence staining. A significant difference in fluorescence intensity for Keap1, Nrf2, and HO‐1 (Figure 10D) was found when comparing the BAC‐treated group to the groups treated with PBS or BAC + AST. These outcomes indicate that AST modulates the Keap1‐Nrf2 /HO‐1 pathway, providing protective effects against BAC‐induced corneal damage and inflammation. Generally, AST could protect against BAC‐induced DED and oxidative stress through the Keap1‐Nrf2/HO‐1 pathway.


### AST intervention in vitro

#### Effect of AST on hCEC viability and oxidative stress
  hCECs were cultured in a HS medium for 24 h to simulate DED conditions, as described in Dai et al. 40  The results indicated a significant decrease in cell viability under HS conditions, whereas treatment with AST at concentrations of 5 or 10 μM significantly improved cell viability (Figure 11A), suggesting that AST may mitigate cell damage caused by HS. Oxidative stress marker ROS, including ROS, SOD, and CAT, were assessed, revealing that SOD and CAT levels were significantly elevated, whereas ROS levels were markedly reduced in the HS group treated with 5 μM AST compared to the HS group alone (all p < 0.05, Figure 11B‐11E).


#### Effect of AST on hCECs in Keap1‐Nrf2/HO‐1 pathway and inflammation
  The Keap1‐Nrf2/HO‐1 signaling pathway is essential for controlling cellular defense mechanisms against oxidative stress and facilitating the removal of ROS. In our study, we observed that AST treatment notably reduced the expression of Keap1 protein while increasing HO‐1 mRNA levels (Figure 12A) and protein expression (Figure 12B) in HS stress–induced hCECs. Furthermore, astaxanthin treatment significantly reduced the mRNA expression of inflammatory factors such as IL6, IL‐1β, TNFα, and MMP9 (Figure 12C).


## DISCUSSION
  The Tear Film and Ocular Surface Dry Eye Workshop (TFOS DEWS) II defines DED as a complex condition of the ocular surface marked by a disruption in tear film homeostasis, coupled with ocular symptoms. This syndrome involves multiple etiological factors, including tear film instability, tear hyperosmolarity, and ocular surface inflammation and damage, along with neurosensory issues. 9  DED is prevalent, affecting 5%–50% of adults, and places substantial burdens on both societal resources and individual well‐being. 9 ,  41  The economic impacts of DED involve direct medical costs, lost productivity, and diminished quality of life. 42 ,  43  Personal hardships are noted in areas like social interaction, workplace performance, and overall life satisfaction. 44  Long‐term research into nutritional supplements has highlighted their potential benefits in promoting ocular health. 45  Emerging evidence suggests that dietary modifications and nutritional supplementation could prevent or treat ocular surface disorders. Essential micronutrients from diet or supplements can influence the ocular surface's structural and functional health via diverse metabolic routes. 46  Recent extensive studies have investigated the multiplicative effects of nutritional supplements on DED. These studies have explored anti‐inflammatory, 47 ,  48  epitheliotrophic, 49  neuroprotective, 50  and tear quality enhancement 51  aspects, in addition to oxidative stress reduction, 52 ,  53  inhibition of corneal neovascularization, 54  and macrophage activity enhancement. 48 ,  55  Furthermore, it is increasingly common to find research on nutritional supplements targeting multiple mechanisms and outcomes in both preventing and managing DED. 56 ,  57

  AST, a vividly red carotenoid, has gained regulatory endorsement as a dietary supplement primarily due to its potent antioxidant capabilities. 58 ,  59  It exhibits a broad spectrum of health‐promoting effects that include anti‐inflammatory, antiapoptotic, antiproliferative, neuroprotective, antidiabetic, and protective properties on the skin, eyes, kidneys, and liver. 60 ,  61 ,  62  A pivotal aspect of AST's function, tied to its antioxidant attributes, is its capacity to combat inflammation triggered by ROS‐induced oxidative stress. 63  Research has consistently shown that AST can inhibit the classical NF‐κB signaling pathway in reaction to oxidative stress. This inhibition is achieved by blocking the oxidation of IκB kinase (IKK), which occurs regardless of ROS source, cell types, or organs involved. 64 ,  65 ,  66  As a result, AST curtails the NF‐κB‐mediated transcription of various pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as IL‐1β, IL6, IL‐8, iNOS, and TNFα, ultimately preventing the progression of inflammation. Moreover, AST exerts its beneficial effects through the modulation of several critical molecular pathways. These include the activation and inhibition of the PI3K/AKT pathway, 67  the JAK/STAT‐3 signaling pathway, 68  the Nrf2 transcription factor, 69  MAPKs, 70  and PPARγ. 71  Direct (HS) stress triggers the activation of the mitogen ‐ activated protein kinase (MAPK) and NF‐κB pathways in corneal epithelial cells, underscoring their importance in the innate immune response and the early development of DED. 72  The accumulation of ROS prompts an inflammatory response that aggravates cellular oxidative stress, fueling a detrimental cycle that contributes significantly to the progression of DED.

  In this investigation, initial hypotheses, grounded on network pharmacological assessments, suggested that AST could modulate the oxidative stress instigated by BAC and thereby ameliorate DED by influencing redox‐sensitive signaling pathways. To confirm this, 0.1% BAC eye drops were administered to mice, establishing a DED model. Comparative analysis revealed that the DED cohorts exhibited substantially elevated corneal fluorescein staining scores relative to controls, highlighting enhanced ocular surface damage that AST effectively mitigated. Histological evaluations using H&E along with PAS staining techniques demonstrated AST's capability to reduce corneal inflammatory infiltration, alleviate edema, and augment the density of conjunctival goblet cells, induced by BAC exposure. Further cellular studies entailed developing a HS stress–induced model in hCECs. Here, the CCK‐8 assay indicated a significant uptick in hCEC viability upon AST treatment.

  ROS, often referred to as oxidative stress, are pivotal in the development of DED. 40  The body's defense against ROS includes a robust antioxidant system, composed of both endogenous antioxidants (produced internally) and exogenous antioxidants. Endogenous antioxidants comprise enzymatic types like SOD, CAT, and GPX, and nonenzymatic types such as glutathione. These antioxidants are crucial for cellular repair and regeneration, and affect intracellular gene expression. 73  Research indicates that AST bolsters SOD activity, thus countering ROS‐induced cellular damage, 74 ,  75  a finding corroborated by our experimental results. The Keap1‐Nrf2/HO‐1 signaling pathway is crucial for maintaining redox homeostasis. 76  Under typical conditions, ROS and electrophiles trigger structural changes in Keap‐1 influencing Nrf2. When Nrf2 degradation is inhibited, it enhances the nuclear expression and initiates the antioxidant response by activating genes such as HO‐1 and GPX4. 77  Such activation is critical in preserving cellular redox homeostasis. As we know, Nrf2 can enter the nucleus and bind to the antioxidant response elements, thereby activating the expression of various antioxidant genes and directly reducing the accumulation of ROS, 78  and HO‐1 facilitates the breakdown of heme into carbon monoxide, ferrous ions, and biliverdin, all of which are essential components in the inflammatory response. 79  Additionally, it enhances the cell's tolerance to oxidative stress, protecting the cells from oxidative damage. Therefore, the downregulated expression of Nrf2 and HO‐1 indicates oxidative damage, during which the organism's capacity to counteract oxidative stress diminishes, making it difficult to mitigate the detrimental effects of excessive ROS production. 80  Existing studies link oxidative stress with the progression of DED, pinpointing proteins in the Keap1‐Nrf2/HO‐1 pathway as likely therapeutic targets. 81  Our findings include immunofluorescence data showing significant upregulation of Nrf2 protein levels in the corneas of DED mice treated with AST, as well as a downregulation of Keap1 proteins. Western blot analysis demonstrated increased HO‐1 expression and reduced Keap1 protein expression in HS stress–damaged hCECs. Similarly, RT‐PCR results revealed significant upregulation of Nrf2 and HO‐1 levels alongside reduced Keap1 mRNA expression in the same cells. Collectively, these findings suggest that AST mitigates BAC‐induced DED in mice by modulating inflammation and oxidative stress through the Keap1‐Nrf2/HO‐1 pathway.

  Our study elucidates the therapeutic role of AST in DED from an oxidative stress perspective, offering a novel approach to the use of nutritional supplements as a treatment for DED. This study, although informative, has certain limitations and identifies potential areas for refinement. One notable deficiency in the animal experiments is the absence of varied dosage groups for AST. Specifically, the study lacked a structured comparison across low, medium, and high doses of AST, which could provide deeper insights into dosage‐dependent effects and optimize therapeutic outcomes. In the cellular experiments, another gap was the failure to experimentally verify whether AST's protective impacts on hCECs under HS conditions are mediated, at least in part, via the Keap1‐Nrf2/HO‐1 pathway by specifically knocking out the Keap1 gene. Tackling these issues could significantly enhance the validity of the results and offer a more thorough understanding of the underlying mechanistic pathways. Moving forward, our research aims to explore these dimensions more thoroughly to elucidate the multifaceted mechanisms through which astaxanthin can effectively treat DED. This future work will expand on current findings and potentially unveil novel therapeutic targets or interventions.


## CONCLUSIONS
  In summary, this research demonstrates that AST alleviates the general symptoms of DED in mice, mitigating oxidative stress and inflammation on the ocular surface caused by BAC. The protective effects of AST on the ocular surface are likely mediated through the modulation of the Keap1‐Nrf2/HO‐1 signaling pathway, which in turn activates the body's intrinsic antioxidant defenses. Consequently, AST emerges as a potential therapeutic agent for treating ocular surface disorders associated with DED.
