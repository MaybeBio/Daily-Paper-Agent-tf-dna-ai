# Insights From Nonsense‐Mediated mRNA Decay for Prognosis in Homologous Recombination‐Deficient Ovarian Cancer


## ABSTRACT
  Not all ovarian cancer patients with homologous recombination deficiency, especially those with germline BRCA mutations, can benefit from platinum‐based and targeted therapy. Our study aimed to determine the value of nonsense‐mediated mRNA decay, which targeted these mutations. The retrospective analysis of 797 ovarian cancer patients was performed using two public cohorts and one in‐house cohort. We developed a prediction algorithm for nonsense‐mediated mRNA decay to discriminate between trigger and escape status, finding that escape status indicated a better prognosis. Subsequently, we analyzed differential gene expression and functional pathways between the two statuses and filtered 8 genes associated with the cell cycle. Then the optimized key gene model was built using integrated machine learning algorithms (mean AUC > 0.89), which had a higher independent prognostic value for ovarian cancer with germline BRCA variants or homologous recombination deficiency than the nonsense‐mediated mRNA decay algorithm. Furthermore, we classified patients into high‐ and low‐risk groups by the machine learning model and found that the low‐risk group had a better prognosis with higher drug response and immune levels of activated dendritic cells than the high‐risk controls. Our findings provide a perspective based on nonsense‐mediated mRNA decay and cell cycle pathways to distinguish subtypes of germline BRCA or homologous recombination deficiency.


## Introduction
  Germline homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) mutations, particularly BRCA‐mutated variants associated with hereditary breast and ovarian cancer (OV) syndromes, have been identified as a significant prognostic biomarkers, often related to increased sensitivity to platinum and polyadenosine diphosphate‐ribose polymerase inhibitor (PARPI) therapy [1, 2, 3, 4]. However, not all BRCA‐mutated OV patients benefit from improved survival, and large cohort studies have demonstrated mutational heterogeneity [5, 6]. Some BRCA variants confer a short‐term survival benefit, but this can diminish or even be reversed over time [7]. It is important to distinguish different BRCA mutation subtypes for personalized diagnosis and treatment.

  Indeed, massive truncations are associated with DNA damage repair, particularly HRD [8, 9, 10]. Approximately 80% of germline BRCA mutations generate premature termination codons (gBRCA‐PTCs) [3]. Nonsense‐mediated mRNA decay (NMD) mainly identifies PTCs and downgrades their expression to further influence gene functions and disease phenotypes [3, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. NMD‐trigger (NMD‐T) eliminates the abnormal mRNA‐encoding proteins with dominant‐negative or gain‐of‐function effects, but it can also result in haploinsufficiency [3]. Conversely, some truncated proteins retaining partial function alleviate disease occurrence by NMD‐escape (NMD‐E) [16, 17]. The two NMD statuses depended on the location of the PTCs according to common NMD rules, which meant that the alternating regions containing PTCs corresponded to different abnormalities of domains and functions [18]. But whether NMD‐T or NMD‐E affects prognosis is unclear.

  In this study, we sought to develop a model based on the NMD phenomenon to distinguish subtypes of gBRCA, even in HRD patients. By comparing differences in clinical presentation, pathway expression, drug response, and tumor immune microenvironment, we exert to find a novel prognostic biomarker for OV patients to guide personalized treatment.


## Material and Methods

### Public Ovarian Cancer Datasets
  Ovarian cancer patients' datasets with gBRCA or wild‐type BRCA1/2 were collected from the TCGA (n = 569) [19, 20, 21, 22] and NG2022 (n = 110) [23] cohorts. The clinical and RNA data of TCGA were downloaded from cBioPortal (https://www.cbioportal.org/) and TCGA (http://tcga.cancer.gov/dataportal). The clinical, mutation, RNA data, HRD status (HRD+ means detection of HRD‐related mutations or HRD characteristics) and COSMIC signature information of TCGA‐OV and NG2022 cohorts could be found in the article's corresponding supplements [22, 23, 24].


### TJMUCH Specimen Clinical Data
  We collected 118 OV patient samples with gBRCA or wild‐type BRCA1/2, which comprised a clinical cohort from the Tianjin Medical University Cancer Institute and Hospital (TJMUCH). Tumor tissue samples and peripheral blood were collected from these patients. Approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of TJMUCH (bc2022130). Informed consent was obtained from all participants. Mutations and NMD prediction details in three cohorts are available in Table S1. The expression matrix of RNAseq generated in our study has been deposited in the National Genomics Data Center (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/omix/) under accession code OMIX007959.


### TJMUCH RNA‐Sequencing (RNA‐Seq) and Analysis
  We collected 31 TJMUCH tumor tissue samples for RNA‐seq using the RNeasy kit (Qiagen, Germany). The RNA was fragmented, reverse transcribed, ligated with adaptors, and amplified by PCR. The library was then sequenced with 150 paired‐end reads on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (IIlumina, USA). The RNA‐seq reads were aligned to the human genome (GRCh37) using Hisat2. The reads that mapped to each gene were quantified using Featurecount (version 2.0.1). Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM) values for each gene were calculated based on gene length and mapped read counts. Then the FPKM values were Log2‐transformed for downstream analysis. Differential analysis was performed using EdgeR (version 3.34.1).


### TJMUCH Targeted DNA Sequencing of Peripheral Blood Samples
  The DNA sequencing panel included 19 OV susceptibility genes (i.e., ATM, BARD1, BRCA1, BRCA2, BRIP1, CDH1, CHEK2, EPCAM, MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, NBN, PALB2, PMS2, PTEN, RAD51C, RAD51D, STK11, and TP53). We used the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) to extract genomic DNA. Sequence libraries were constructed during shearing, end repair, A‐base addition, and ligation of barcoded sequence adaptors. The prepared libraries were sequenced using the MiSeq sequencer as 150‐bp paired‐end reads. Sequence reads were aligned with the human genome (hg19) with BWA‐MEM (v 0.7.12). The GATK (Genome Analysis Toolkit 4.0) software was used for variant identification and annotation.


### Identify Variant Pathogenicity
  Following the guidelines of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG), BRCA1/2 mutations were evaluated using GnomAD (http://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/), 1 K Genomes Project (http://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/), Clinvar (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/), BRCAexchange (https://BRCAexchange.org/), and BIC (https://research.nhgri.nih.gov/bic/).


### The Nonsense‐Mediated Decay (NMD) Prediction Algorithm
  To detect the NMD status of gBRCA‐PTCs, we developed an algorithm for predicting the propensity of BRCA‐PTC to trigger NMD. In brief, whether gBRCA‐PTCs were NMD‐T or NMD‐E depended on their position in the DNA coding sequence (CDS). First, there were three classic NMD‐E regions of gBRCA‐PTCs: the last exon, 55 nt to the last exon–exon junction (EEJ) in the penultimate exon, and the CDS start to 150 nt. In contrast, there were two NMD‐T regions of gBRCA‐PTCs: BRCA1/2 short exons (≤ 400 nt) or long exons (> 400 nt) within the next EEJ to 55 nt. When a PTC appeared in other regions of long exons, the BRCA1/2 NMD score of the NMDetectiveA tool [12] for the classical transcripts (BRCA1: NM_007294, BRCA2: NM_000059, Table S2) and their mRNA level were considered in our BRCA‐NMD algorithm. The gBRCA‐PTCs with the following conditions were determined to be NMD‐E: [1] the upper quartile (Q75) of the mRNA level of the BRCA1(BRCA2)‐mutated, [2] between the lower and upper quartile (Q25 ~ Q75) level with an NMD score ≤ 0.39, and [3] between the median and upper quartile (Q50 ~ Q75) level with 0.40 ≤ NMD score ≤ 0.54. Other conditions were considered to be NMD‐T. If the transcriptome data were unavailable, PTCs with an NMD score ≤ 0.52 were NMD‐E and ≥ 0.53 were NMD‐T (Figure S1). Theoretically, a higher NMD score of the NMDetectiveA tool and a lower mRNA level suggested an increased possibility of triggering NMD. All mutation information was checked with Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) software.


### Clinical Manifestation Relevance Analysis
  Differences in overall survival (OS), disease‐free survival (DFS), and progression‐free survival (PFS) were analyzed by the Kaplan–Meier test. Chi‐square tests were performed to identify the following clinical indicators: age, tumor history, tumor family history, histological typing, stage, distant metastasis, platinum resistance, recurrence, HRD status, and smoking history. Unavailable information was excluded from the chi‐square tests, and the data are denoted as “Unknown”


### Key Gene Selection and Pathway Enrichment Analysis
  We selected differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between NMD‐T, NMD‐E, and WT groups by Wilcoxon test in three cohorts (p < 0.05). Univariate Cox regression analysis further filtered the key genes as prognostic indicators in TCGA and NG2022 cohorts (for OS, DFS and PFS: p < 0.05). Cox regression, hazard ratio (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), and co‐expression analysis were realized with R packages.

  To identify pathways in which key genes associated with NMD were involved, we performed Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Reactome enrichment analyses by the clusterProfiler R package. All patients were scored to determine the differential pathways (p < 0.05) by gene set variation analysis (GSVA). The relative GSVA scores were equal to: mean (trial) – mean (control).


### Development of NMD‐Linked Gene Prognostic Index Machine Learning Model
  The potential prognostic index models comprised the key genes based on 14 machine learning algorithms, including multivariate Cox, Lasso, CTree, CForest, CoxBoost, xgBoost, generalized boosted regression modeling (GBM), random survival forest (RSF), obliqueRSF, partial least squares regression for Cox (plsRcox), stepwise Cox, Ridge, elastic net (Enet), and supervised principal components (SuperPC). The training sets were the TCGA cohort, and the NG2022 cohort was used to validate. We calculated the area under the curve (AUC) value and concordance index (C‐index) for all potential models, and ranked them by average AUC and C‐index from both cohorts. Through comprehensive comparison, the best performing model was selected as the NMD‐linked gene prognostic index model for further analysis. The curves of time‐dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC), calibration, and decision curve analysis (DCA) were plotted with the R packages.


### Hematoxylin–Eosin (HE) and Immunohistochemistry Staining (IHC)
  Pathological paraffin sections from 34 OV patients were divided into NMD‐T (n = 6), NMD‐E (n = 9), and WT (n = 19) groups and stained using HE and IHC according to standard protocols. The slices were incubated with antibodies including anti‐SMG1, anti‐UPF1, and anti‐BLM (Merck, USA). All sections were photographed at representative areas using both 200× and 400× magnifications (BX61, Olympus, Japan). To evaluate target protein expression, we used three staining intensity levels: low (score: 0), median (score: 1), and high (score: 2), then calculated the mean score of five random visual fields per section.


### Drug Sensitivity Predictions
  To predict the drug responses of these patients, gene expression data from the three cohorts were fitted to the half‐maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) and the gene expression of cancers from the Broad Institute Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) by the oncoPredict R package [25]. Lower imputed drug response scores indicate higher sensitivity.


### Immune Microenvironment Analysis
  The immune infiltration assessment based on gene expression in three cohorts was conducted by the immunedeconv R package, which predicted common immune cell fractions.


### Statistical Analysis
  Statistical analysis and visualization were realized with the R 4.2.3 software and Prism 9.5 software using the recommended methods of the software instructions. Differences between groups were calculated by the Wilcoxon test (two groups) and the Kruskal–Wallis test (multiple groups). Spearman's correlation was used for correlation analysis. A two‐tailed test was used, and p values below 0.05 were considered significant (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001).


## Results

### Study Population in Three Cohorts
  Our study included 797 OV patients, including 157 gBRCA‐mutant and 640 BRCA wild‐type cases from three cohorts (TCGA‐OV: n = 569, NG2022: n = 110, and Tianjin Medical University Cancer Institute and Hospital (TJMUCH): n = 118). To explore the impact of NMD, all gBRCA‐PTCs were divided into the NMD‐T and NMD‐E groups. Based on the key factors, we further categorized patients with gBRCA‐PTCs or HRD variants (HRD+: n = 249, HRD‐: n = 548) into high‐ and low‐risk groups to analyze multidimensional differences. The flowchart of subject selection was shown in Figure 1. Patients with mutation details can be found in Table S1.


### Algorithm to Predict NMD Status in Ovarian Cancer With gBRCA‐PTCs
  In the process of building the NMD algorithm (Figure 2A and S1), the TCGA‐OV cohort was used as a training set, while the NG2022 and TJMUCH cohorts were used as validation sets. In the training set, we divided their positions into three classes: (A) three classic NMD‐E regions, (B) long exons, and (C) other regions. Significant disparities in both the BRCA1/2 mRNA levels and NMD scores were detected in the three classes (Figure 2B,C & Table S2). The NMD scores of region A were ≤ 0.39 and those of region C were ≥ 0.55 (Figure 2B). Consistently, their mRNA levels in region A were higher than those in region C (Figure 2C). However, the conditions of region B were disputed. Therefore, we predicted the NMD status of gBRCA‐PTCs in region B by our NMD algorithm. Scatterplots of BRCA expression in the TCGA cohort were created using mRNA values for mutated BRCA1 (Figure 2D) and BRCA2 (Figure 2E). The small sizes of the NG2022 and TJMUCH cohorts precluded meaningful differences between the BRCA1 and BRCA2 groups. These cohorts were combined into a validation set, assessing mRNA levels as percentiles from NG2022 and TJMUCH data. Boxplots showed lower BRCA1 expression in the NMD‐T group compared to the NMD‐E group (Figure 2F). BRCA2 expression did not differ significantly between groups (Figure 2G), likely due to limited data. But analysis of TCGA‐OV reverse phase protein array data indicated significantly lower BRCA2 protein levels in the NMD‐T group compared to the NMD‐E group (NMD‐T vs. NMD‐E: p = 0.023).

  Additionally, we evaluated the protein levels of NMD factors SMG1 and UPF1, which were significantly higher in the NMD‐T group, with no differences between the NMD‐E and WT groups (Figure S2A–C). We then analyzed mRNA expression of SMG and UPF family members across the TCGA and NG2022 cohorts, categorizing values into high, medium, and low levels. The results are shown in stacked diagrams (Figure S2D–I). The most notable difference for SMG1 was observed in the NG2022 cohort, with high expression levels of 56.3%, 23.1%, and 29.3% for the NMD‐T, NMD‐E, and WT groups, respectively.


### The NMD‐T and NMD‐E Groups Had Distinctive Survival Characteristics and Clinical‐Pathological Features
  To assess the impact of gBRCA‐PTCs with different NMD subtypes on clinical outcomes, we compared clinical manifestations between NMD‐T and NMD‐E groups. Survival analysis indicated that gBRCA‐PTC‐bearing OV patients showed significantly longer OS than WT subjects in the TCGA‐OV (Figure 3A) and NG2022 (Figure 3B). However, significantly longer DFS was only observed in the TCGA‐OV group (Figure 3C), not in the NG2022 (Figure 3D). The chi‐square tests of clinical indicators showed that patients with gBRCA‐PTCs were younger, more platinum‐sensitive, and exhibited higher rates of HRD compared to the WT group. Furthermore, a higher percentage of patients with gBRCA‐PTCs had a cancer history or a family cancer history (Table S3).

  Ovarian cancer patients with gBRCA‐PTCs were further divided into NMD‐T and NMD‐E groups using our BRCA‐NMD algorithm. Median OS, DFS, and PFS in the NMD‐E group were longer than in the other groups (Figure 3E–H & Table S3). Moreover, the NMD‐T group had higher percentages of disease progression than those of the NMD‐E group in the NG2022 cohort (Figure 3I) and more distant metastasis events than those of the NMD‐E group in the TJMUCH cohort (Figure 3J). In addition, we found more advanced International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) stages in the NMD‐T group in the NG2022 cohort (Figure 3K) and TJMUCH cohort (Figure 3L) than in the NMD‐E group.


### Differences in Gene Expression and Pathways Between NMD‐T and NMD‐E Groups
  To explore the factors contributing to differences in survival and clinical manifestation, we analyzed gene transcriptome and hazard ratio (HR) of OS, DFS, and PFS between three groups, and 8 differential genes had been found: BIRC5, BLM, CDC7, GTSE1, LMNB1, MTHFD1, NCAPH, and RAD54L. The NMD‐E group exhibited the highest expression levels of these genes in the three groups (Figure 4A). We performed IHC to compare the protein levels of BLM as a representative. The results indicated that the NMD‐E group had significantly higher BLM levels than the NMD‐T and WT counterparts (Figure S3A,B). Thereafter, the samples were categorized into high‐ and low‐BLM groups based on median mRNA levels, and the NMD‐T group had a lower percentage of high‐BLM cases than the other two groups (Figure S3C–E).

  Moreover, univariate Cox regression analysis showed that NMD status and mRNA levels of these genes were prognostic indicators (Figure 4B, OS and DFS/PFS: p < 0.05). Compared with high‐level or NMD‐E counterparts, low‐level or NMD‐T patients had higher hazard ratios in the TCGA‐OV and NG2022 cohorts. However, significant differences in NMD status were only observed for OS in TCGA and DFS in NG2022. These differential genes linked to NMD may have greater prognostic value than NMD status alone. Furthermore, co‐expression of seven genes, excluding MTHFD1, was identified (Figure 4C–E). Pathways associated with these eight genes were selected from GO, KEGG, and Reactome for GSVA analysis (Table S4) and drug response predicted by OncoPredict. The heatmap illustrated relationships between key pathways, drug response, and NMD status (Figure 4F). NMD‐E status was associated with upregulated pathways related to cell cycle checkpoint, replication fork, and DNA replication compared to NMD‐T counterparts. Additionally, AZD7762, a cell cycle checkpoint kinase inhibitor, showed higher sensitivity in the NMD‐E group, consistent with the upregulation of the positive regulation of the cell checkpoint pathway.


### Construction and Validation of the NMD‐Linked Gene Prognostic Index Model Based on Machine Learning Algorithms
  Given that NMD prediction required PTC positions which were not usually available, we constructed a novel prognostic index model of 8 NMD‐linked genes based on the integrative machine learning algorithms for OV with gBRCA‐PTCs. We then calculated the 5‐year AUC values and C‐index of 36 models in TCGA and NG2022 cohorts, and found that the random survival forest (RSF) model had the best performance with the highest average AUC value (0.904) and second highest C‐index (0.786) (Figure 5A). ROC, calibration, and DCA curves also showed that the RSF model was stable and accurate in predicting 1‐, 3‐, and 5‐year OS (Figure 5B–G). As a basis for further analysis, we considered the RSF model to be the optimal model. Thereafter, we computed risk scores for all patients with gBRCA‐PTCs using our model. The risk scores in the TJMUCH cohort without survival data was calculated for GSVA analysis by Enet (alpha = 0.8). The comparison suggested that the NMD‐T group has a higher average risk score than the NMD‐E group (for OS: Figure 5H,I).


### Difference in Mutation Signatures, Enrichment Pathways, Drug Response, and Immune Levels in Risk Groups With gBRCA‐PTCs
  We divided gBRCA‐PTC patients into high‐ and low‐risk groups by median risk indexes. Median OS, DFS, and PFS in the low‐risk group were markedly longer than those in other groups (Figure 6A–D). Regarding COSMIC signatures, BRCA‐related Sig 3 levels in both risk groups were markedly higher than the WT group (Figure 6E,F). Furthermore, GSVA analysis of pathways associated with NMD‐linked genes revealed that the low‐risk group exhibited significantly upregulated pathways compared to high‐risk controls, including double‐strand break repair via break‐induced replication, DNA unwinding, cell cycle checkpoint, and DNA recombination (Figure 6G & Table S4). Interestingly, the low‐risk group in the TCGA cohort had fewer platinum resistance cases (Figure 6H) and was predicted to be more sensitive to cisplatin (Figure 6J,K & Table S5). Our cellular drug sensitivity test confirmed this result (Doc S1: Supporting Information S2). In the NG2022 cohort, smoking status also differed between the three groups (Figure 6I). Additionally, the low‐risk group enriched higher levels of activated dendritic cells and M1 macrophages than the other groups (Figure 6L–O & Table S6).


### NMD‐Linked Gene Prognostic Index Model Identified Differences in Functional Pathways, Drug Response, and Immune Infiltration in HRD Patients
  To better target the HRD population, we trained our NMD‐linked gene prognostic index RSF model to optimize its parameters. We then divided HRD patients into high‐ and low‐risk groups by median risk indexes. ROC, calibration, and DCA curves demonstrated that this model exhibited strong stability and accuracy in predicting 1‐, 3‐, and 5‐year OS for HRD patients (Figure 7A–F). The survival of low‐risk patients was significantly better than that of other groups (Figure 7G–J). As expected, GSVA analysis revealed that pathways associated with NMD‐linked genes showed similar results to the gBRCA‐PTC groups, indicating significantly upregulated cell cycle and DNA repair pathways in the low‐risk group compared to the high‐risk controls. These pathways included double‐strand break repair via break‐induced replication, cell cycle checkpoint, chromosome segregation, replication fork, and DNA recombination (Figure 7K & Table S4, for TJMUCH cohort by Enet). Furthermore, the low‐risk group had a lower percentage of platinum resistance cases (Figure 7L) and higher sensitivity to cisplatin, gemcitabine, and irinotecan than the high‐risk controls (Figure 7M,N & Table S5). Additionally, the low‐risk group displayed elevated levels of activated dendritic cells compared to other groups (Figure 7O,P and Table S6).


## Discussion
  In the present study (Figure 8), we focused on NMD status in relation to gBRCA‐PTCs. We collected data on 797 OV patients from three cohorts and constructed an algorithm to predict whether gBRCA‐PTCs induced NMD [12]. In clinicopathological features, as previously reported [1, 26], we found that gBRCA‐PTCs were correlated with an earlier age at diagnosis, family history of tumor, platinum sensitivity, HRD, and longer survival. When we divided PTC cases into NMD‐T and NMD‐E groups, we found that the NMD‐E group showed better phenotypes than NMD‐T controls, including longer survival, fewer malignant progression events, and a lower percentage of advanced staging, despite similar platinum sensitivity and HRD. NMD‐T is indeed a prognostic factor, but there are few reports that it directly leads to shorter survival and worse prognosis in OV. The differences may be influenced by therapeutic interventions, but the extent of this impact remains unclear due to missing treatment data.

  By univariate Cox analysis, we found the eight differential NMD‐linked genes that might hold the key to the prognostic differences seen in different NMD statuses. Not only were they prognostic, but there was also co‐expression. Pathway enrichment suggested that these genes were primarily associated with cell cycle checkpoints, replication forks, and DNA replication. Surprisingly, despite similar HRD scores between NMD‐T and NMD‐E groups, the latter, with higher mRNA levels of these genes, had lower hazard ratios, and the upregulation of the cell cycle checkpoint pathway coincides with increased sensitivity to a cell cycle checkpoint inhibitor (AZD7762). Furthermore, we analyzed drug sensitivity of 6 OV cell lines based on CCLE and GDSC and found that the NMD‐E group exhibited sensitivity to several drugs, including AZD7762 (Table S7 and Figure S4). This suggests HRD status alone may incompletely reflect the comprehensive status of DNA damage repair, and alternative double‐strand break repair mechanisms beyond the homologous recombination pathway, such as non‐homologous end joining (NHEJ) and microhomology‐mediated end joining (MMEJ), may contribute to these observed variations. In addition, individuals with impaired HR and NHEJ (due to BRCA1/2 mutations or drug interventions) exhibit increased reliance on MMEJ to preserve genome stability [27]. Notably, the eight genes were more likely to represent key contributors to phenotypic distinctions beyond HRD. For example, BLM, as a member of the RecQ helicase family, played an important role in replication fork stability by unwinding structures, modulating DNA replication and repair [28]. And BLM overexpression was a biomarker for CHK1 inhibitor response in BRCA‐mutant OV [29].

  Considering accuracy, practicality, and dimension reduction, we constructed an optimal prognostic index model of 8 NMD‐linked genes by the RSF machine learning algorithm. As expected, the NMD‐E group with a longer survival has a lower risk score than the NMD‐T group. Compared with the NMD grouping method, it was refreshing that the model had better performance and ease of use in predicting survival and a high independent prognostic value for OV with gBRCA‐PTCs or HRD variants (all 5‐year AUC values > 0.8 in training and validation cohorts). According to the comprehensive studies, 21.8% of OV patients carry gBRCA pathogenic variants (compared to only 5.7% in breast cancer) [30], and the HRD frequency is approximately 50% of high‐grade serous OV [19]. In our cohorts, the number of HRD cases is approximately twice that of gBRCA‐PTC counterparts. Therefore, our model has a broad application potential in prognosis.

  To explore the relevant biological processes and internal mechanisms leading to differential prognosis, all patients were then divided into low‐and high‐risk groups. On the one hand, many cell cycle pathways were upregulated in the low‐risk group compared to the high‐risk controls, including break‐induced replication (BIR), DNA unwinding and recombination, and cell cycle checkpoints. They are induced by transcription‐replication conflicts, suggesting imprecise DNA synthesis, hypermutagenesis, and chromosome rearrangements [31]. Indeed, the frequency of frameshifts is extremely high during BIR [32]. In addition, gBRCA or HRD mutations also enrich for a large number of these variants. Mutations escaping from NMD retain more aberrant products that may stimulate DNA repair mechanisms but increase genome instability. In particular, it is likely that more activated dendritic cells in the low‐risk group will make a contribution. Thus, our model consisting of eight key genes, such as BLM, RAD54L, and CDC7, required for DNA replication and repair, not only BIR, links NMD status to cell cycle processes [33].

  The low‐risk group with higher NMD‐linked gene expression may benefit from the genomic instability with increased chemosensitivity. In our subsequent experiment, we assessed the cell viability and predicted risks of BRCA1‐PTC OVCAR3 before and after carboplatin treatment. The results showed that the latter with increased IC50 had higher risk scores (Figure S5). Bugreev proposed that high BLM expression can exacerbate DNA damage by disrupting the Rad51‐ssDNA filament, D‐loops, and double Holliday junctions to stimulate DNA repair synthesis in D‐loops [28]. Besides, BLM overexpression led to a dominant anti‐homologous recombination function that caused genomic instability [34] and reduced chemoresistance to platinum, temozolomide, and PARP inhibitors in ovarian, breast, and glioma cancer [34, 35]. Cell replication and repair pathways can also destabilize genomes and may lead to sensitivity to cisplatin, gemcitabine, irinotecan, and other drugs that are commonly used in combination chemotherapy for OV.

  In this study, an NMD prediction algorithm and an optimized 8 NMD‐linked gene prognostic index model were generated by machine learning algorithms in OV. Our results provide a new perspective based on NMD status and cell cycle pathways to discriminate subtypes of gBRCA or HRD patients. Notably, given the high mutation frequency of BRCA1/2 within DDR genes and their significant clinical implications, the majority of mutation information identified in both public and our internal cohorts predominantly pertains to BRCA‐mutant. Therefore, our study focused on the impact of NMD on BRCA‐mutated variants. The lack of available information limits the ability to thoroughly investigate the NMD mechanisms of DDR genes, underscoring the need for continued collection of clinical data from patients with DDR mutations.

  Furthermore, the NMD rules we have referenced pertain only to the criteria of exon junction complex‐dependent NMD, which recognizes mutant variants with PTC for quality control. However, the NMD mechanisms are diverse and complex, with approximately 25% of the variance in NMD efficiency remaining unexplained [18]. Notably, the influence of NMD factors on tumor prognosis varies, and the expression levels of individual NMD factors may not accurately reflect NMD efficiency [36, 37]. Except for NMD, translation‐dependent mRNA decay pathways for quality control include non‐stop decay (NSD) and no‐go decay (NGD). Competing with NMD, Staufen‐mediated mRNA decay (SMD) is activated by Staufen proteins binding double‐stranded RNA [38]. Similarly, the RNA‐binding protein Regnase‐1, which has RNase activity, interacts with UPF1 to induce specific mRNA degradation [39]. Notably, most RNA degradation pathways primarily target defective or specific transcripts, with defects from mutations often being unpredictable. Consequently, accurately assessing their in vivo activity poses a significant challenge [40]. This underscores the need to include well‐characterized mRNA degradation markers for a comprehensive assessment of mRNA decay in cancer development and progression [41].
