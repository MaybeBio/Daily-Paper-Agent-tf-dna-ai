# Transducin-like enhancer of split 3 protects against lipopolysaccharide-induced inflammation through DEAD-box helicase 5–activating transcription factor 1–protein phosphatase 2 regulatory subunit 5A signaling

  Graphical abstract


## Introduction
  Sepsis is an infection-related systemic inflammatory response syndrome caused by the excessive activation of immune cells and overproduction of inflammatory cytokines, which eventually leads to multiple organs failure or destruction [1]. Despite advancements in medical research and the timely use of antibiotics, fluid resuscitation, and multi-organ support therapy reducing sepsis mortality rates, a Global Burden of Disease report indicated that there were still 48.9 million sepsis cases globally, with a mortality rate of 22.5 %, contributing to nearly 20 % of worldwide deaths [2]. Therefore, the number of deaths from sepsis remain high as there are currently no specific approved therapies[2], [3]. The association between COVID-19 infection and septic shock has further emphasized the urgent need for novel therapeutic targets beyond traditional hormonal therapies [4], [5]. The absence of specifically approved therapies that target the fundamental pathological mechanisms currently presents a significant challenge in developing effective treatment strategies.

  Mammalian transducin-like enhancers of split-family proteins (TLEs), homologous to Drosophila Groucho, consist of seven family members. Among them, TLE1-4 encodes full-length TLE proteins with unique and multiple functions, TLE5 and TLE6 are short-type proteins that lack several functional domains, and TLE7 is expressed at a minimal level, and its function remains unclear [6]. In contrast to transcription factors (TFs), members of the TLE family do not bind directly to DNA, however, they can act as co-regulators to positively or negatively control target gene expression through various epigenetic mechanisms [7]. Generally, through interaction with various TFs or cofactors, TLEs play critical roles in animal development, tissue homeostasis, cell differentiation, immune regulation, and tumorigenesis [6], [8], [9], [10]. In the immune system, TLE1 and TLE4 suppress proinflammatory responses in macrophages by modulating hairy and enhancer of split 1 [11], [12]. Moreover, TLE1 and TLE3 play key roles in monocyte and myeloid cell differentiation [11], [13]. TLE1, TLE3, and TLE4 critically regulate T cell development, differentiation homeostasis, lineage specification, and plasticity by integrating multiple transcription factors [14], [15]. TLE3 and TLE4 are also involved in modulating memory B cells and early B cells development, respectively [16], [17]. These studies have revealed the diverse and essential functions of TLEs in immune regulation; however, the functions and molecular mechanisms of TLE3 in macrophages and innate inflammatory diseases remain unknown.

  The DEAD-box helicase family has highly conserved Asp-Glu-Ala-Asp (DEAD) domains, and forms RNA and RNA-protein complexes in an ATP-dependent manner [17]. DEAD-box helicase 5 (DDX5) was found to affect many aspects of RNA processing, such as RNA splicing, stability, and biogenesis [18], [19]. Emerging studies have also reported that DDX5 can serve as transcription coactivator and/or corepressor to regulate gene transcription [20], [21], [22]. DDX5 plays essential roles in cellular differentiation, embryonic development, metabolism, metastasis, viral infection, and bacterial infection by modulating various signaling pathways [23], [24], [25].

  This study aimed to improve comprehension of the mechanisms whereby TLE3 regulates the inflammatory response. To achieve this, we used lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-challenged macrophages and mouse models [26], and found that macrophage exposure to LPS elevated TLE3 expression and TLE3 overexpression in macrophages mitigated LPS-induced inflammatory factor expression, lung and liver injury, and septic death in mice. Additionally, TLE3 competed with activating transcription factor 1 (ATF1) to interact with DDX5 and promotes its cytoplasmic retention, thereby suppressing the transcription of the DDX5/ATF1 target gene Ppp2r5a. Finally, we found that TLE3-DDX5-ATF1-PPP2R5A signaling suppressed inflammation by dephosphorylating various mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) by modulating the activity of the protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A) complex.


## Results

### TLE3 is upregulated in survivors of sepsis, LPS-induced septic mouse models, and inflammatory macrophages
  We analyzed the data from a previous study by Covarrubias et al. that, following LPS treatment, used an NF-κB activity-driven green fluorescent protein (GFP) fluorescence reporter and genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 library in mouse bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs)[27], The single-guide RNAs of TLEs—including TLE2, TLE3, TLE4, and TLE6 (but not TLE1)—were enriched in cells with low GFP fluorescence, compared to the enrichment ratio of the well-known NF-κB activator myeloid differentiation primary response 88 (Fig. 1A), indicating their potential ability to negatively regulate NF-κB activity. The GDS4971 dataset was used to evaluate the expression of Tle genes in a cohort of patients with sepsis. Among the TLE family members, only the mRNA of Tle3 showed a significant increase in peripheral blood samples obtained from survivors of sepsis, whereas it decreased in non-survivors (Fig. 1B), suggesting that TLE3 is involved in sepsis pathophysiology. In addition, we studied human monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs) isolated from the circulating monocytes of healthy volunteers (Fig. 1C). In human MDMs ex vivo, we found that Tle3 mRNA was significantly upregulated in response to LPS stimulation (Fig. 1D). The transcriptional levels of the LPS-induced cytokine Il6 negatively correlated with both basal Tle3 mRNA levels pre-LPS stimulation (Fig. 1E, left) and stimulated Tle3 mRNA levels post-LPS treatment (Fig. 1E, right) in human MDMs from the recruited participants.   To further investigate these findings, we examined the mRNA levels of Tle3 in vivo and observed that the gene was significantly upregulated in the lungs, livers, and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of mice treated with LPS (Fig. 1F). Similar results were obtained in primary mouse BMDMs, where the Tle3 mRNA level increased in responding to dose- and time-dependent LPS stimulations, which peaked at a concentration of 1 μg/mL and after 24 h, respectively (Fig. 1G-H). At the protein level, immunoblotting assays revealed that TLE3 was upregulated and the levels of phosphorylated NF-κB p65 in BMDMs treated with LPS increased (Fig. 1I). Immunofluorescence assays consistently revealed increased TLE3 staining in CD68-positive macrophages in the lungs of LPS-challenged mice, compared to that in their control-group littermates (Fig. 1J). We also observed that LPS treatment enhanced TLE3 staining in BMDMs (Fig. 1K). Moreover, increased TLE3 fluorescence was observed to accumulate in the cytoplasm of macrophages, but not in the nucleus (Fig. 1K). To confirm this finding, the cytoplasmic and nuclear proteins of BMDMs were separated, and we confirmed that LPS-induced TLE3 was enriched in the cytoplasmic fraction (Fig. 1L). Altogether, we found that TLE3 expression was elevated in survivors of sepsis, experimental LPS-challenged human MDMs, murine BMDMs, and mouse models of sepsis.

  Upon activation of toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) by LPS, TNF receptor associated factor 6 is recruited and assists TLR4 in mediating the activation of NF-κB and MAPKs signalling [28]. We found that the NF-κB inhibitor Bay-11–7082, c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) inhibitor SP600125, and p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (p38 MAPK) inhibitor SB203580 all effectively blocked the LPS-induced increase of Tle3 mRNA in BMDMs. However, the extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) inhibitor U0126 did not (Fig. S1A). Subsequently, we investigated whether NF-κB [29], interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3)[30], and other TFs downstream of the JNK and p38 signaling pathways, including cAMP-response element binding protein (CREB)[31] and activator protein-1 (AP-1)[32], were involved in LPS-induced Tle3 upregulation. Compared with the immunoglobulin G (IgG; negative control), CREB, NF-κB p65, and AP-1 were found to be enriched on Tle3 promoter in basal condition (Fig. S1B). LPS stimulation significantly upregulated the enrichment of NF-κB p65 and AP-1 on Tle3 promoter, while inhibiting CREB enrichment (Fig. S1B). These data indicate that activated MAPKs, NF-κB, and AP-1 are responsible for the LPS-induced upregulation of TLE3 in macrophages.


### TLE3 protects against LPS-induced acute multi-organ injury and systemic inflammation in vivo
  To determine the role of TLE3 in LPS-mediated innate inflammatory responses, the recombinant adenovirus encoding wild-type (WT) human TLE3 (Ad-TLE3) group was administered via the tail vein to mice; resulting in TLE3 systemic overexpression in various tissues, particularly the liver, lungs, and heart, compared with the empty vector recombinant adenovirus (Ad-NC) group (Fig. S2A). In response to the lethal LPS challenge [33], systemic TLE3 overexpression decreased and delayed the onset of death (Fig. S2B). Moreover, TLE3 overexpression resulted in a significant reduction in the plasma concentrations of the proinflammatory cytokines interleukin (IL)1β, IL6, and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα) following LPS administration [34] (Fig. S2C), indicating TLE3-induced downregulation of endotoxemia-induced inflammatory. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and immunohistological staining of lung sections revealed that TLE3 overexpression decreased the lung injury score and F4/80-positive inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. S2D). In lung tissues, transcriptional levels of LPS-induced cytokines—including Il1β, Il6, and Tnfα—were suppressed by Ad-TLE3 transfection (Fig. S2E). Compared to the Ad-NC group, in the Ad-TLE3 group, immunoblotting assays showed that TLE3 overexpression significantly suppressed LPS-induced phosphorylation of p65, phosphorylation and degradation of inhibitor of NF-κB alpha (IκBα) in lung tissues (Fig. S2F). The liver plays a critical role in the maintenance of metabolic and immune homeostasis, and liver injury is strongly associated with poor outcomes in patients with sepsis [35]. Similarly, LPS-induced liver injury and hepatic inflammatory signaling were also alleviated by Ad-TLE3 transfection (Fig. S2G-I). We did not observe any TLE3 overexpression-induced biological effects in the absence of LPS stimulation, suggesting that TLE3 function depends on cellular context. These results reveal that TLE3 overexpression prevents LPS-induced NF-κB activation, overproduction of inflammatory cytokines, and organ injury.

  To confirm the significance of TLE3, we used a recombinant adenovirus that encoded a short hairpin RNA against mouse TLE3 (Ad-shTLE3). Compared to the control shRNA recombinant adenovirus (Ad-shNC), substantial knockdown of TLE3 was achieved in fat, spleen, lung, and liver tissues by systemic Ad-shTLE3 transfection (Fig. S3A). Consistent with the decline in TLE3 expression in non-survivors of sepsis (Fig. 1B), we observed the deleterious effect of TLE3 knockdown on LPS-induced mortality (Fig. S3B). Serum IL1β, IL6, and TNFα levels were further increased in Ad-shTLE3 transfected mice following LPS treatment (Fig. S3C). Accordingly, Ad-shTLE3 transfection exacerbated LPS-induced inflammation in both lung and liver tissues, including elevated inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. S3D and S3G), Il1β, Il6, Tnfα mRNA (Fig. S3E and S3H), and NF-κB signal transduction (Fig. S3F and S3I).

Following LPS challenge [33], LV-TLE3 to WT mice showed improved survival and deferred onset of death compared to the LV-NC to WT mice (Fig. 2E). H&E and immunohistological staining of lung sections revealed that the transplantation of LV-TLE3 transfected BMDMs decreased the lung injury score and F4/80-positive inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 2G). Using flow cytometry, we observed that the LPS-induced increase in macrophages was significantly reversed by TLE3 overexpression (Fig. 2H), indicating mitigated systemic inflammation and macrophage infiltration. ## TLE3 modulates LPS-induced inflammation in BMDMs in vitro
  To confirm the function of TLE3 in LPS-induced immune responses in macrophages in vitro, TLE3 expression in BMDMs was induced or silenced using an adenovirus. Consistent with our previous in vivo findings, we observed that elevating or silencing TLE3 expression did not alter the basal levels of inflammatory factors transcripts—including Il1β, Il6, and Tnfα (Fig. 4A and S5A). In LPS-activated macrophages after both 4 and 20 h, Ad-TLE3 transfected BMDMs exhibited downregulation of Il1β, Il6, and Tnfα mRNAs, while TLE3 silencing increased them (Fig. 4A and S5A). The production of endotoxin-TLR-mediated inflammatory factors mainly depends on the activation and transduction of NF-κB and MAPKs. Exogenous TLE3 reduced the LPS-induced phosphorylation levels of NF-κB p65, IκBα, JNK, and p38, with no effect on ERK (Fig. 4B). In the control group, the phosphorylation levels of NF-κB p65, JNK, ERK, and p38 peaked at 0.5 and 2 h post-LPS treatment, and then dropped rapidly to near-baseline levels at 10 h (Fig. 4C). Conversely, TLE3 overexpression weakened NF-κB and MAPKs activation and accelerated its gradual attenuation, except for ERK (Fig. 4C). Our immunofluorescence assay demonstrated that TLE3 inhibited LPS-induced NF-κB p65 nuclear translocation in BMDMs (Fig. 4D). We used human MDMs from 15 donors, and compared the levels of Il1β, Il6, and Tnfα upon LPS stimulation, with or without Ad-TLE3 transfection (Fig. 4E). Ad-TLE3-transfected human MDMs showed significant downregulation of LPS-induced Il1β, Il6, and Tnfα mRNA (Fig. 4F). We indicated that activated NF-κB, JNK and p38 signals are responsible for the LPS induced upregulation of TLE3 in macrophages (Fig. S1). Regarding TLE3 inhibited these inflammatory signals strongly, indeed, we observed that TLE3 overexpression mediated decrease in Il-6, accompanied with decrease in endogenous mouse Tle3 (Fig. S5B).   Numerous studies have reported a central role for pyroptosis in sepsis development [37]. Pyroptosis is classified mechanistically into two types: the cysteinyl aspartate specific proteinase (caspase) 1-mediated classical pyroptotic pathway and caspase-4/11-mediated non-classical pyroptotic pathway [38]. Results in vitro (Fig. S6A) and in vivo (Fig. S6B), consistently revealed that TLE3 overexpression significantly decreased LPS-induced NOD-like receptor thermal protein domain associated protein 3, Cleaved caspase1, Cleaved IL1β, and Cleaved gasdermin D levels, but did not impact the levels of Cleaved caspase11. Thus, TLE3 can protect against LPS-induced canonical inflammasomes and pyroptosis but does not influence the caspase-4/11-mediated non-classical pyroptotic pathway.

  In contrast to LPS-induced M1 macrophages, which elicits inflammation to promote host defense and clear pathogens, IL4 facilitates the differentiation of M2 macrophages, which resolve inflammation and repair tissues [39], [40]. We observed that 50 ng/mL IL4 was sufficient to fully inhibit Tle3 mRNA, and increasing its concentration to 1,000 ng/mL did not induce a further decrease in Tle3 (Fig. S7A). Additionally, Tle3 mRNA levels were decreased in response to time-dependent IL4 stimulation, peaking at 12 h before being restored to baseline levels at 48 h (Fig. S7B). We then explored whether TLE3 regulates the expression of the IL4-induced classical M2-associated genes. In macrophages, forced expression of TLE3 led to a more rapid and significant increase in Arg1, Fizz1, Cd206, and Ym1 mRNA levels post-IL4 treatment, and TLE3 silencing mimicked the effects of TLE3 overexpression (Fig. S7C-D). These data indicate that in addition to its involvement in regulating LPS-induced inflammatory response, TLE3 also promotes IL4-induced macrophage M2 polarization.


## TLE3 inhibits LPS-induced inflammation by interacting with DDX5 and promoting its cytoplasmic retention
  To identify the molecular mechanisms of action of TLE3 following LPS challenge, an immunoprecipitation-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) assay was used to identify the proteins that interact with TLE3 in primary BMDMs. Accordingly, we found that GO-MF was significantly enriched in the “transcription coregulator activity” pathway among TLE3-interacting proteins, including DDX5, TLE4, TLE5, TRIM21, and TLE3 itself (Fig. 5A-B). RNA helicase DDX5, an important transcriptional and RNA metabolism regulator, along with its family members, play a pivotal role in viral and bacterial innate immunity [23], [41]. Therefore, we hypothesized that TLE3 cooperates with DDX5 to regulate LPS-induced inflammation. We then used AlphaFold (Hinxton, Cambridgeshire, UK) to generate the predicted structures of DDX5 and TLE3. The slate DDX5 protein, cyan TLE3 protein, and their binding sites are shown as the corresponding-colored stick structures. Using protein–protein docking analysis, we predicted that there are multiple groups of residues that form hydrogen bonds between DDX5 and TLE3, including Ser435 of DDX5 and Ser387 of TLE3. Therefore, the binding score of DDX5:TLE3 was –640, suggesting a strong interaction (Fig. 5C). Co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays showed that TLE3 interacted with DDX5 in HEK293 cells and BMDMs (Fig. S8A-B). As TLE3 was enriched in the cytoplasmic fraction (Fig. 1K-L), the immunofluorescence assays of BMDMs revealed colocalization of TLE3 and DDX5 staining, mainly in the cytoplasm, following LPS treatment, compared to the signal in the nucleus (Fig. 5D). The co-IP assays further confirmed that TLE3 interacted with DDX5 in cytoplasmic extracts (Fig. 5E). Moreover, we conducted bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays [42], which showed that co-transfection of TLE3-VC155 and DDX5-VN173 constructs in RAW264.7 cells resulted in a time-dependent formation of cytoplasmic TLE3-DDX5 complexes post-LPS stimulation, with peak interaction intensity at 2 h (Fig. 5F-G). This temporal resolution indicates that TLE3 orchestrates cytoplasmic DDX5 during LPS stimulation in macrophages. Notably, DDX5 performs nucleocytoplasmic shuttling and participates in the regulation of RNA processes in the cytoplasm and transcriptional activity in the nucleus [43], [44]. To determine the functional consequence of this interaction, we examined its effect on DDX5 subcellular distribution.   To clarify the critical role of the TLE3:DDX5 interaction in LPS-induced inflammatory responses, DDX5 expression was induced or silenced using adenoviruses in BMDMs. DDX5 knockdown promoted the TLE3-mediated downregulation of Il1β, Il6, and Tnfα mRNA levels (Fig. S8D). Conversely, compared to the Ad-TLE3 group, the BMDMs transfected with Ad-TLE3 and Ad-DDX5 groups, showed increased LPS-induced inflammatory responses—including elevated Il1β, Il6, and Tnfα mRNA levels (Fig. 5J)—as well as NF-κB and MAPK signal activation (Fig. 5K-L) and NF-κB p65 nuclear translocation (Fig. 5M). Therefore, the protective role of TLE3 in LPS-induced acute inflammation was negated by DDX5 overexpression and facilitated by DDX5 knockdown. Thus, we hypothesized that the binding of TLE3 to DDX5 promotes the cytoplasmic retention of DDX5, subsequently alleviating the proinflammatory effect of nuclear DDX5 through LPS-related inflammation.


## DDX5-PPP2R5A signaling contributes to TLE3-mediated negative regulation of LPS-induced inflammation
  Nuclear DDX5 participates in transcriptional regulation [45]. To further understand the underlying mechanism of the TLE3:DDX5 interaction-modulated LPS-induced inflammation, we performed cleavage under targets and tagmentation sequences (CUT&Tag seq) to survey the genome-wide DDX5-binding sites in primary BMDMs. Furthermore, we observed a strong enrichment of DDX5 surrounding the transcription start sites (TSS) of known genes with vehicle + Ad-DDX5, LPS + Ad-DDX5, or LPS + Ad-DDX5 + Ad-TLE3 (Fig. S9A). A heat map of DDX5 enrichment signals in genes revealed that LPS treatment significantly increased DDX5 binding to clusters of target gene promoters, whereas TLE3 overexpression reversed this effect (Fig. 6A). To explore DDX5-mediated downstream signaling, the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathways of genes with significant changes in DDX5-binding signals were analyzed. This analysis revealed that genes with DDX5-enriched signals belonging to the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT) signaling pathway, including Mapk1, Ppp2r5a, and Ywhag, were upregulated by LPS and downregulated by TLE3 overexpression (Fig. 6B). Considering the significant function of PI3K/ AKT signaling in the regulation of macrophages both in chronic low-grade systemic inflammation and LPS-induced acute inflammation [46], [47], we measured its mRNA levels in Ad-NC- or Ad-TLE3-transfected BMDMs following vehicle or LPS treatment. Ppp2r5a mRNA expression was substantially induced by LPS stimulation and restored by TLE3 overexpression (Fig. 6C). No changes were observed in Mapk1 or Ywhag (Fig. 6C). ## TLE3 competes with ATF1 for its interaction with DDX5 and decreases the transcription of the ATF1 target gene Ppp2r5a

Furthermore, TF motif enrichment analysis of genes with LPS-induced DDX5 enrichment was performed using the gProfiler online tool, and the top seven predicted TFs were assessed using a co-IP-IB assay. Myc-tagged DDX5 interacted with all top seven predicted TFs in both HEK293 and RAW264.7 cells (a mouse macrophage cell line), including ZF5, E2F1, E2F4, E2F7, ATF1, CREB1, and FOXO4 (Fig. 7A-B). In addition, the forced overexpression of flag-tagged TLE3 suppressed DDX5 binding to ATF1 in LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells (Fig. 7B), though it was not affected in HEK293 cells (Fig. 7A). The different results observed with HEK293 and LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells indicated that TLE3 has a cell-context-dependent role. By predicting the binding structures of DDX5 and ATF1, we found that multiple residue groups, including Lys391 of DDX5 and Cys228 of ATF1, formed hydrogen bonds (Fig. S9B). Likewise, using co-IP-IB assays, we found that TLE3 overexpression blocked nuclear interactions between DDX5 and ATF1 in RAW264.7 cells (Fig. 7C) and lung tissues (Fig. 7D) with LPS treatment. Transcription factor ATF1 was identified as a downstream effector molecule of TLE3-DDX5 signaling. TLE3 overexpression significantly suppressed LPS-induced DDX5 peaks on the Ppp2r5a promoter in a previous DDX5 CUT&Tag-sequence assay (Fig. 7E). Therefore, it was hypothesized that ATF1 cooperates with the transcriptional co-activator DDX5 to directly regulate Ppp2r5a transcription. Additionally, we further confirmed that the human (−305 to −278 bp) and mouse Ppp2r5a promoters (−324 to −396 bp) were highly homologous and contained a conserved ATF1 binding domain using the Eukaryotic Promoter Database (EPD) online tool (Fig. 7F). Compared with the IgG, we found that LPS stimulation upregulated the ATF1 enrichment on the Ppp2r5a promoter; the enrichment potential was further increased by forced DDX5 overexpression, and was decreased by TLE3 overexpression (Fig. 7G).

## Discussion
  Our study focused on the impact of TLE3 on LPS-induced sepsis. First, we analyzed the expression of TLEs in the prior clinical transcriptome profiles of whole blood from controls, sepsis survivors, and sepsis non-survivors [51], and observed that TLE3 had the most significant change among the TLE family members, with an increase in sepsis survivors and decreased in non-survivors (Fig. 1B). We then carried out a study on human MDMs; these ex vivo macrophages facilitated our understanding of the heterogeneity of TLE3 transcriptional dynamics pre- and post-exposure to LPS across different individuals, as well as their relationship with the inflammatory response. Overall, we hypothesized that TLE3 was induced in response to septic challenge, and loss-of-TLE3-function may contribute to poor clinical outcomes in patients with sepsis.

  Previously, Zhang et al. observed that TLE3 and TLE4 levels increased significantly in an NF-κB and JNK pathway-dependent manner in LPS-stimulated BMDMs [52]. We observed that both mRNA and protein levels were upregulated during sepsis or in LPS −treated macrophages (Fig. 1F-I), indicating that TLE3 was induced by inflammatory activation and negatively regulated feedback. In contrast, alternatively activated macrophages (M2) that differentiate upon IL4 stimulation exhibited a dramatic decrease in Tle3 mRNA expression (Fig. S7A-B). Therefore, we hypothesis that TLE3 expression is regulated in a macrophage polarization-dependent manner. The direct elements regulating TLE3 expression, potentially involving LPS-related NF-κB (Fig. 1I) and IL4-related signal transducer and activator of transcription 6 [53], remain unclear, although we were able to identify LPS-induced TLE3 as a ‘‘brake’’ on the innate inflammatory response.

  In different cellular contexts, various epigenetic mechanisms may contribute to TLE-mediated transcriptional regulation [54], including recruitment by TFs, interaction with histone deacetylases, and competition with coactivators, which do not depend on their recruitment to target promoters [55], [56], [57]. In this study, we demonstrated that TLE3 negatively regulates inflammatory responses through competitive binding. Considering that complex posttranslational modifications (PTMs) control the molecular properties of TLE3, including its binding activity and subcellular localization [58], we examined TLE3 acetylation, phosphorylation, and ubiquitylation of TLE3. However, no significant changes were observed post-LPS treatment (Fig. S10A), and the reasons underlying LPS-induced TLE3 cytoplasmic accumulation warrant further exploration.

  In our investigation of TLE3-DDX5 interactions in the context of inflammation, TRIM21 was also identified as a TLE3 interactant through our IP-LC-MS/MS assay. However, we prioritized DDX5 over TRIM21 due to its well-established role in transcriptional regulation. Although E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIM21 has been implicated in innate immunity [59], [60], [61], we hypothesized that TRIM21 regulates TLE3 protein stability, rather than acting as a downstream factor—a relationship that requires further investigation to fully elucidate. Xu et al. reported that cytoplasmic DDX5 inhibited LPS-induced inflammation by promoting TLR2/4 mRNAs degradation in an RNA process-dependent manner [23]. These findings support the multiple roles of DDX RNA helicase members (including DDX5) in both positive and negative regulation of innate immune and inflammatory responses in different cellular contexts [62], [63]. Consistent with the findings of our current study, we propose that the conflicting functions of DDX5 in positively and negatively regulating LPS-induced inflammation can be explained by its subcellular functional compartmentalization; specifically, cytoplasmic DDX5 inhibits inflammatory response, whereas nuclear DDX5 promotes it. Furthermore, TLE3 is an attractive target for modulating DDX5 subcellular localization and function rather than controlling its expression. DDX5 interacts with p50 and activated NF-κB in glioma [64]; consistent with these results, we found that TLE3 overexpression also inhibited the interactions between DDX5 and NF-κB p50 by decreasing nuclear DDX5 (Fig. S10B), which could also contribute to the anti-inflammation effect of TLE3 in LPS-stimulated macrophages. Overall, TLE3 and TLE3-DDX5 signaling appear are important therapeutic targets for inflammatory diseases.

  PP2A, an abundant enzyme in eukaryotic cells, accounts for 0.2–1 % of total cellular protein content, is responsible for approximately 50 % of cellular serine/threonine protein phosphatase activity, which profoundly influences all aspects of cell biology [65], [66], [67]. PP2A regulates LPS-TLR4 signaling cascades at serial points from several levels, including the MAPK and NF-κB pathways, through its serine-threonine phosphatase activity [68]. However, considering the broad functions of PP2A in cell biology, its indiscriminate activation to suppress the inflammatory response is unadvisable. Herein, we identified an attractive target, PPP2R5A, which inhibits PP2A activity and modulates inflammatory signaling. As a regulatory subunit, PPP2R5A has the potential to modulate the catalytic activity, substrate specificity, and subcellular localization of the PP2A complex [68], [69]. We observed that the TLE3-DDX5-ATF1 axis downregulated the transcription of Ppp2r5a and decreased the phosphorylation level of JNK, p38, and NF-κB p65, but had no effect on the phosphorylation of TAK1 or ERK (Fig. 4B-C, Fig. S10C). These findings implicate PPP2R5A in modulating the substrate selectivity of PP2A. In addition, compared with the increase in PPP2R5A, no significant changes were observed in the scaffolding subunit PPP2R1A or the catalytic subunit PPP2CA (Fig. S10D). Besides PP2A, the catalytic subunit of PP1, PP3 and PP5 were not changed as well (Fig. S10D). The underlying mechanism requires further study, including the resolution of the structural features of PP2A complexes containing PPP2R5A.

  LPS-TLR4 also activates TRIF-IRF3-mediated type I IFN responses [70]; therefore, we evaluated the expression of total and phosphorylated IRF3 and its target genes. Firstly, without LPS stimulation, we found that TLE3 overexpression did not affect the basal expression of IRF3 downstream transcripts, including Ifnβ, Isg15, Il12, and Cxcl10. (Fig. S11A, right, Fig. S11B, right). Both in vitro and in vivo, TLE3 significantly inhibited LPS-induced IRF3 signaling and IRF3 target genes in BMDMs and lungs, respectively (Fig. S11A, left, Fig. S11B, left, Fig. S11C-D). Since PP2A is also an in vivo phosphatase of IRF3 [71], these findings expand the effect of the TLE3-DDX5-ATF1-PPP2R5A axis, which may potentially dephosphorylate IRF3 to prevent inflammation.

  While this study provides novel insights into TLE3-mediated inflammatory regulation, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our experimental model utilized LPS-induced endotoxemia to mimic sepsis-like inflammation, which, while well-validated, does not fully recapitulate the polymicrobial complexity of clinical sepsis. Future studies employing cecal ligation and puncture (CLP, a model of polymicrobial sepsis) could strengthen translational relevance. Second, macrophages from healthy donors were used; however, investigating TLE3 dynamics in septic patients' immune cells may yield additional clinically actionable insights. Third, although the PP2A inhibitor LB100 was selected to investigate its therapeutic potential, its off-target effects on other phosphatases (e.g., PP5) necessitate caution in interpreting pharmacological outcomes [72]. Finally, TLE3 is implicated in multiple pathways (e.g., Wnt signaling), raising the possibility of unintended effects [73]. These limitations highlight the need for further mechanistic and translational studies to fully elucidate TLE3′s multifaceted roles in inflammatory pathogenesis.


## Conclusion
  In summary, we have discovered that LPS-induced TLE3 is a key negative regulator of the innate LPS-TLR4 signaling pathway. By binding directly to cytoplasmic DDX5, TLE3 promotes its cytoplasmic retention and suppresses its cooperation with the transcription factor ATF1, subsequently suppressing the nuclear ATF1/DDX5 transcriptional activity on the target gene Ppp2r5a in macrophages. Decreased PPP2R5A levels upregulated PP2A phosphatase activity and dephosphorylated MAPKs (JNK and p38) and NF-κB subunits, preventing hyper-inflammation and related injury (Fig. 8). Thus, macrophage TLE3 plays a pivotal role in modulating inflammatory signals, and that the development of medicines targeting TLE3 or related DDX5-ATF1-PPP2R5A signaling should be considered in the future for the treatment of sepsis and other related inflammatory diseases.Fig. 8Schematic of the molecular mechanisms of TLE3 in inflammatory responses. Upon LPS challenge, TLE3 binds directly to cytoplasmic DDX5, then promotes the cytoplasmic retention of DDX5 and inhibits nuclear ATF1/DDX5 transcriptional activity on target gene Ppp2r5a in macrophages. Decreased PPP2R5A upregulates PP2A phosphatases activity, dephosphorylates MAPKs (JNK, p38) and NF-κB subunits, thus alleviating inflammatory responses after TLR4 signaling activation and balancing macrophage polarization.


## Materials and methods
  Human peripheral blood

  All donors provided written informed consent before participating. Then the peripheral blood mononuclear cells were isolated and cultured for more research.


## Animals
  In our animal facility, male C57BL/6 mice between the ages of 8 and 12 weeks were bred and kept in standard living circumstances. Mice received tail vein injections of 5 × 109 pfu/mouse of either Ad-NC, Ad-shNC, Ad-TLE3, or Ad-shTLE3 three days before the endotoxemia model was established. Mice were injected intraperitoneally with either 15 mg/kg [34] or 25 mg/kg [33] of LPS (L2630, Sigma) in order to create the LPS-induced endotoxemia model. Comparable amounts of saline were given to the control animals. Up to 72 h, their survival was observed and documented. After 24 h, every mouse was put to death for histological analysis. The experiments were performed with biological replicates (n = 6 per group) and that all key findings were independently repeated at least twice to ensure reproducibility.

## Primary human monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs) culture
  Peripheral blood mononuclear cells were isolated by diluting the sample in PBS and transferring it to the Ficoll layer (Solarbio, P8680). The tubes were centrifuged at 1000 g for 30 min at room temperature with slow acceleration and braking. After centrifugation, the top layer was removed and the layer of peripheral blood mononuclear cells was collected in a new 50 ml Falcon tube. Cells were washed twice with PBS prior to counting. Monocytes were subsequently extracted using the Pan Monocyte Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec catalogue no. 130–096-537) according to the manufacturer's directions. Monocytes were cultured in X-VIVO15 serum-free medium (Lonza) and differentiated into macrophages using 20 ng/mL M−CSF (MCE, HY-P7085A).


## Bone marrow-transplantation (BMT)
  Eight-week-old recipient mice were given two 5.5 Gy radiation doses spaced four hours apart, for a total of 11 Gy [74]. They also were given drinking water laced with antibiotics (6000 U/ml polymyxin B sulfate and 100 µg/ml neomycin) one week prior to and four weeks following the transplant procedure. Eight-week-old C57BL/6J mice were used to harvest BMDMs from their tibiae and femurs. The cells were then infected with lentiviruses (LV-NC, LV-shNC, LV-TLE3, or LV-shTLE3)[75]. Next, 5 × 107 of these transfected BMDMs were implanted into each recipient mouse individually through the tail vein [74]. In order to restore hematopoietic cells for additional model establishment, transplanted mice were given a six-week recuperation period, as previously mentioned.


## Plasmids constructs, transfections and reporter assay
  The entire human TLE3 coding region was cloned in pCMV-Flag, pCMV-GFP-Flag and pCMV-HA plasmids. The entire human DDX5 coding region was cloned in pCMV-Myc plasmid. The entire human ATF1 coding region was cloned in pCMV-Flag plasmid. The plasmids were validated by gene sequencing (Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China). RAW 264.7 cells and HEK293 cells were transfected in presence of Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA). cDNA encoding full-length human TLE3, or DDX5 were cloned and inserted into the pBiFC-VN155 or pBiFC-VC173 vector. The Ppp2r5a promoter was PCR amplified from mouse genomic DNA and cloned into the pGL3-Basic vector using the One Step Cloning Kit (C112-02, Vazyme) for the reporter assay. RAW264.7 cells were cotransfected with the internal control plasmid pRL-TK (Renilla luciferase reporter plasmid, Promega) and followed by the LPS stimulation. Cells were harvested and lysed following the manufacturer's protocol, and luciferase activity was measured using the Dual Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (DL101-01, Vazyme).


## Virus preparation
  Replication-defective recombinant adenovirus carrying the entire coding sequence of TLE3 (Ad-TLE3) was constructed. As a negative control, an adenovirus only expressing green fluorescent protein (GFP) was employed (Ad-NC). Three siRNAs targeting mouse TLE3 were synthesized, and the most effective one was chosen to create shRNA (CACCGCACAAGCAGACAGAGATT GCCGAAGCAATCTCTGTCTGCTTGTGC). This shRNA was then incorporated into adenoviral vectors to develop an adenovirus expressing shRNA against TLE3 (Ad-shTLE3). The negative control adenovirus was designed to express Ad-shNC, a non-targeting shRNA serving as a universal control. Viruses were supplied by OBiO Technology Ltd., Shanghai, China.


## Chromatin immunoprecipitation assay
  The chromatin immunoprecipitation test (ChIP) was carried out using a commercially available kit following the manufacturer's instructions. BMDMs were cross-linked, quenched, lysed, and sonicated before being centrifuged and treated with particular antibodies for 12 h. Protein A/G Sepharose was then added for an additional 2 h on a rotator at 4 °C. The beads were washed and then eluted. The supernatant underwent reverse-crosslinking overnight at 65 °C, followed by a 4-hour digestion at 55 °C. Following ChIP, the input DNA was purified with qPCR.


## Mass spectrometry analysis
  For LC-MS/MS analysis of TLE3-associated proteins, the lysis of BMDMs was obtained and then subjected to incubation with TLE3 antibody or control IgG, along with protein A/G-magnetic beads at 4 °C overnight. After washing with Tris-buffered saline, the immunoprecipitates were subjected to LC-MS/MS analysis. SpecAlly Life Technology Co., Ltd., located in Wuhan, China, conducted the mass spectrometry analyses. The mass spectrometry data from this study have been deposited to Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Table 2.


## Protein-Protein docking
  The predicted structures of DDX5, TLE3 and ATF1 were generated by Alphafold. The AutoDockTools-1.5.7 was employed to create the protein, by manually removing water molecules and adding polar hydrogens to ensure accurate docking results. Docking Web Server (GRAMM) was used for protein–protein docking. The protein–protein complex was manually refined by eliminating water molecules and incorporating polar hydrogen using AutoDockTools-1.5.7. Finally, the protein–protein interactions were predicted and a corresponding interaction figure was generated by PyMOL.


## CUT & Tag-seq
  Magnetic beads coupled to concanavalin A were employed to bind the cells, and digitonin was used to permeabilize the cell membrane. The transposon, in conjunction with protein A/G and aided by the DDX5 antibody and its secondary antibody, accurately targeted and cleaved the DNA sequence near the DDX5 protein. Transposon cleavage adds adaptor sequences to both ends of cleaved fragments. Following PCR amplification, the sequence may be immediately utilized for high-throughput sequencing. The procedure followed the instruction manual's sequence: cell collection, primary and secondary antibody binding, hyperactive protein A/G-transposon binding, fragmentation, DNA extraction, library amplification, PCR product purification, library quality control, and sequencing. Frasergen (Wuhan, China) conducted the sequencing and data analysis.


## Immunohistochemistry staining
  Sections were incubated with 0.5 % H2O2 in PBS for 30 min at room temperature, followed by blocking with 5 % BSA and 0.5 % Triton X-100 for another 30 min. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. With Histostain TM-SP kits, the immunoreaction was visualized by diaminobenzidine (brown color). Next, the areas were enclosed with a glass lid utilizing mounting fluid and the images were examined using a microscope.


## Flow cytometry
  Mouse lung samples were dissected, minced, and placed in tubes with digestion buffer (1 mg/ml Type I collagenase, DNase I) in RPMI1640 medium. The cells were washed with 5 % FBS in PBS, centrifuged at 500 g for 5 min, resuspended in the same solution, filtered through a 45-μm strainer, and centrifuged again and resuspended in FACS buffer (2 % FBS in PBS). Cells were then incubated for 30 min with the primary antibodies. The primary antibodies used were: 7-AAD Staining Solution (559925, BD Biosciences), Rat Anti-CD45 Antibody, APC-Cy7 Conjugated (557659, BD Biosciences), Rat Anti-CD11b Antibody, PE Conjugated (557397, BD Biosciences), Rat Anti- F4/80 Antibody, BV421 Conjugated (565411, BD Biosciences), Rat Anti-CD86 Antibody, PE-Cy7 Conjugated (560582, BD Biosciences), Rat Anti-CD206 Antibody, FITC Conjugated (141703, Biolegend), Rat Anti-GFP Antibody (46–6498-80, Thermo Fisher). Subsequently, cells were washed with PBS and centrifuged at 500 g for 5 min, and resuspended in FACS buffer. Flow Cytometry was conducted utilizing FACS Calibur system (BD Life Sciences, UK). Data were analyzed utilizing FlowJo software (BD, USA). The gating strategies have been deposited to Supplementary Fig. 2.


## Bifluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay
  For the TLE3 and DDX5 BiFC assays, RAW264.7 cells were cotransfected with human TLE3-pBiFC-VC155 and DDX5-pBiFC-VN173 plasmids. The cells were maintained in DMEM containing 10 % FBS at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5 % CO2 for 48 h. Then the cells were given vehicle or LPS treatment. DAPI was used to visualize nuclei. An LSM800 fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany) was used to examine cell fluorescence.


### Statistical analysis
  GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA) was used for statistical analyses. Image J software was used to quantify the band intensity in IB images. The results were presented as the means ± standard error of at least three different experiments. The Student's t-test assessed the statistical significance of differences between the two groups. Significance was assessed for various groups using the one-way ANOVA and the Bonferroni test. It was deemed statistically significant when p < 0.05. Animal survival was analyzed using Kaplan-Meier survival curves, with log-rank tests applied for curve comparison. Whenever feasible, blinding and randomization techniques were applied. Sizes of the animal cohorts were established using comparable earlier research.
