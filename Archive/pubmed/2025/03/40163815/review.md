## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text including abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion, and conclusion. No figures, tables, or supplementary files were provided for review.
- **Assessment boundary** The review is limited to the textual content of the manuscript as provided. Claims regarding figures, supplementary tables, and supplementary text are assessed based on their description in the main text only.
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors present an updated compendium of human nuclear transcription factors (TFs) with putative occupancy over the mitochondrial genome (chrM), based on analysis of 6,153 ChIP-seq datasets from the ENCODE Project (covering 942 proteins, 763 sequence-specific TFs). They identify 50 TFs and two other chromatin proteins with evidence for chrM occupancy, with bZIP family TFs most prominent. They report variable reproducibility across antibodies and protocols, and use BPNet deep learning models to predict chrM occupancy profiles. They discuss evidential criteria and conclude that direct functional validation is needed.
- **Visible evidence base** The manuscript text includes descriptions of methods (ChIP-seq alignment, BPNet model training), results for individual TF families (bZIP, bHLH, RFX, Forkhead, C2H2 zinc finger, THAP, Rel, nuclear receptors, Runt, HSF, Homeodomain), and discussion of limitations. No actual data, figures, or supplementary materials were available for inspection.
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** All figures (Fig 1-15), all supplementary figures (S1-S56), supplementary text (S1 Text), and any tables or data files referenced in the text were not provided. This substantially limits the ability to verify the central claims of the manuscript.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript provides a useful and timely update to the authors' previous work on nuclear TF occupancy over the mitochondrial genome, leveraging the substantially expanded ENCODE dataset collection. The systematic approach to cataloging putative chrM-binding TFs, combined with BPNet predictions, represents a valuable resource for the field. However, the central claims rest entirely on data that were not available for review, and the manuscript itself acknowledges substantial reproducibility issues across experiments. The lack of any experimental validation, combined with the acknowledged possibility of artifacts, means the evidence for most of the 50 claimed TFs remains suggestive rather than conclusive. The manuscript is well-written and the discussion of evidential criteria is thoughtful, but the conclusions are necessarily provisional given the data presented.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers studying mitochondrial biology, particularly mitochondrial transcription and its regulation, would be the primary audience. Additionally, those interested in the growing phenomenon of nuclear transcription factors "moonlighting" in mitochondria, and computational biologists working on ChIP-seq analysis and deep learning models of TF occupancy, would find this resource valuable. The manuscript also has relevance for the ENCODE consortium community and those interested in the reliability and reproducibility of ChIP-seq data across antibodies and protocols.
- **Major strengths** The manuscript addresses an important and unresolved question about whether nuclear TFs genuinely occupy the mitochondrial genome. The use of a greatly expanded dataset (6,153 experiments) compared to the previous analysis is a significant improvement. The incorporation of BPNet deep learning models as a complementary predictive approach is methodologically sound and adds value beyond simple peak calling. The authors are appropriately cautious in their interpretation, explicitly discussing the limitations of ChIP-seq for mitochondrial DNA and the lack of reproducibility across experiments. The discussion of evidential standards for establishing chrM occupancy is thoughtful and will be useful for the field.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **Concern ID** R1-M1
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Evidence quality and reproducibility
  - **Claim pointer** The manuscript claims to identify 50 nuclear TFs and two other proteins with evidence for chrM occupancy, and specifically highlights bZIP TFs as most likely to play a role in mitochondria.
  - **Evidence pointer** Results sections for each TF family; Figures 2-14; Supplementary Figures S2-S56
  - **Concern** The central claim of the manuscript, that 50 TFs show evidence of chrM occupancy, cannot be adequately assessed without access to the actual data. More critically, the manuscript itself documents extensive non-reproducibility: for many TFs, peaks observed in one experiment are not replicated in others using different antibodies, different cell lines, or even the same antibody. For example, ATF3 peaks are seen in K562 but not in other cell lines; FOSL1, FOSL2, and NFE2L1 peaks are not replicated beyond a single dataset; and JUND shows discordant results even within the same cell line. The authors acknowledge this but still include these TFs in their compendium. The evidential bar for inclusion appears inconsistent: some TFs with single-experiment support are included, while others with similar support are described as "weak." Without a clear, pre-specified criterion for what constitutes "evidence for chrM occupancy," the list of 50 TFs is difficult to interpret.
  - **Why it matters** The primary output of this manuscript is a compendium of TFs claimed to occupy chrM. If the inclusion criteria are not transparent and consistently applied, the compendium may mislead researchers who use it as a reference. The reproducibility issues documented here suggest that many of the 50 TFs may be false positives, and the manuscript does not provide a clear framework for distinguishing robust from spurious signals.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should provide a clear, quantitative definition of what constitutes "evidence for chrM occupancy" (e.g., peak strength thresholds, replication requirements, BPNet support criteria). They should then apply this definition consistently to all TFs and clearly label each TF as "strong," "moderate," or "weak" evidence based on pre-specified criteria. Ideally, they should provide a table listing all 50 TFs with the specific experiments supporting each, and the reproducibility status across experiments.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M2
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Technical validity of BPNet predictions over chrM
  - **Claim pointer** The authors use BPNet models trained on nuclear ChIP-seq data to predict chrM occupancy profiles, and use agreement between predictions and observed data as supporting evidence for genuine occupancy.
  - **Evidence pointer** Methods section "BPNet model training and predictions"; Results sections referencing BPNet predictions for individual TFs
  - **Concern** The manuscript does not adequately address whether BPNet models trained on nuclear genomic sequences can be meaningfully applied to the mitochondrial genome. The mitochondrial genome has a very different sequence composition, organization, and chromatin context compared to the nuclear genome. The authors note that in some cases BPNet predictions do not match observed data (e.g., for BHLHE40, MITF, HSF1, E2F1), but they do not systematically evaluate the overall concordance rate between BPNet predictions and experimental observations across all TFs. Without a baseline expectation of what BPNet would predict over chrM by chance, the significance of any individual prediction is unclear. Additionally, the manuscript does not specify how many TFs had usable BPNet models, and for how many the predictions were considered "supportive."
  - **Why it matters** The use of BPNet as corroborating evidence is a key methodological innovation of this manuscript. If the models are not appropriately calibrated for chrM, their predictions could be misleading. A systematic evaluation of BPNet performance over chrM is necessary to establish its validity as a supporting tool.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should provide a systematic evaluation of BPNet predictions over chrM, including: (1) the number of TFs for which models were successfully trained, (2) the overall concordance rate between BPNet predictions and observed ChIP-seq peaks, (3) a comparison of BPNet predictions over chrM to predictions over negative control sequences (e.g., shuffled or GC-matched sequences), and (4) a discussion of whether the BPNet models, trained on nuclear data, are expected to generalize to the mitochondrial genome.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M3
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Interpretation of the D-loop enrichment artifact
  - **Claim pointer** The authors state that D-loop enrichment in ChIP-seq datasets is "almost certainly an experimental artifact" and exclude it from consideration as a genuine occupancy site.
  - **Evidence pointer** Introduction; Results section "Evaluating the evidence for mitochondrial occupancy of nuclear TFs in the expanded ENCODE ChIP-seq collection"
  - **Concern** The authors dismiss D-loop enrichment as an artifact based on the lack of expected strand asymmetry and its presence in nearly all datasets. However, the D-loop is the primary regulatory region of the mitochondrial genome and the site where transcription and replication initiate. It is plausible that some TFs genuinely bind there, and the lack of strand asymmetry may reflect a different mode of binding or a technical limitation of ChIP-seq in this region. The authors do not provide any direct evidence that the D-loop signal is artifactual beyond its ubiquity and lack of asymmetry. This is a significant interpretive claim that affects the overall conclusions.
  - **Why it matters** If the D-loop enrichment is dismissed as an artifact without rigorous justification, the manuscript may overlook genuine TF binding at the most biologically important region of the mitochondrial genome. This could lead to an incomplete or biased compendium.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should provide additional evidence to support the artifact interpretation, such as: (1) a comparison of D-loop enrichment in input/control samples, (2) an analysis of whether the D-loop signal correlates with mitochondrial DNA copy number or cell type, or (3) a discussion of alternative explanations for the lack of strand asymmetry. Alternatively, they should acknowledge the possibility of genuine D-loop binding and discuss how it could be tested experimentally.
- **Minor Comments**
  - **Concern ID** R1-m1
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Clarity of methods
  - **Affected element** ChIP-seq alignment strategy
  - **Evidence pointer** Methods section "ChIP-seq data processing"
  - **Issue** The authors state that reads were aligned against an index containing only the mitochondrial genome, with settings "-v 2 -k 2 -m 1". This means reads mapping to multiple locations were discarded. However, the manuscript does not discuss how this affects the analysis, particularly given the presence of nuclear mitochondrial DNA segments (NUMTs) which could confound alignment.
  - **Required correction** The authors should briefly discuss the potential impact of NUMTs on their alignment strategy and explain why aligning only to the mitochondrial genome is sufficient to avoid NUMT-derived false positives.
  - **Concern ID** R1-m2
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Reproducibility of methods
  - **Affected element** Manual inspection of Circos plots
  - **Evidence pointer** Methods section "Screening for TF occupancy over the mitochondrial genome"
  - **Issue** The screening process relies on manual examination of Circos plots to identify likely occupancy events and exclude artifacts. This is inherently subjective and not reproducible without clear criteria.
  - **Required Correction** The authors should provide a more objective, quantitative screening procedure, or at minimum describe the specific features used to distinguish genuine occupancy from artifacts in the Circos plots.
  - **Concern ID** R1-m3
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Completeness of results
  - **Affected element** List of TFs with evidence
  - **Evidence pointer** Results sections for each TF family
  - **Issue** The manuscript describes individual TFs in the text but does not provide a consolidated table listing all 50 TFs, their evidence strength, and the specific experiments supporting each. This makes it difficult for readers to quickly assess the full compendium.
  - **Required Correction** A supplementary table should be provided listing all 50 TFs, the cell lines and antibodies/tags used, the number of supporting experiments, and a classification of evidence strength.
  - **Concern ID** R1-m4
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Discussion of limitations
  - **Affected element** Generalizability of findings
  - **Evidence pointer** Discussion section
  - **Issue** The manuscript focuses exclusively on human data from ENCODE. The authors do not discuss whether their findings might extend to other species, or whether the lack of evidence for chrM occupancy in mouse (from their previous work) might reflect species-specific differences or technical limitations.
  - **Required Correction** A brief discussion of the species-specificity of the findings and the potential for cross-species comparisons would strengthen the manuscript.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** The primary technical failing is the lack of a clear, quantitative, and consistently applied criterion for defining "evidence for chrM occupancy." The manuscript relies on manual inspection of Circos plots and subjective interpretation of BPNet concordance. Additionally, the BPNet models are applied to the mitochondrial genome without adequate validation of their suitability for this context. The manuscript also does not provide a systematic assessment of the false positive rate, either through negative controls or through comparison with known non-binders. These issues must be addressed before the compendium can be considered reliable.
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** Originality: The manuscript is a valuable update to previous work, but the core question (do nuclear TFs occupy chrM?) is not new. The use of BPNet is a novel methodological addition. Scientific importance: The question of nuclear TF moonlighting in mitochondria is of significant interest, and a reliable compendium would be a useful resource. However, the provisional nature of the findings limits their immediate impact. Interdisciplinary readership: The manuscript will appeal to mitochondrial biologists, transcription factor researchers, and computational biologists, but the lack of experimental validation may limit broader interest. Technical soundness: The computational methods are generally sound, but the lack of validation for BPNet over chrM and the subjective screening criteria are concerns. Readability for nonspecialists: The manuscript is clearly written and accessible, with appropriate context provided for the biological questions.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The manuscript addresses an important question and provides a useful resource, but the central claims require clearer evidence criteria and validation of the computational approach before they can be fully accepted.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that 50 TFs show "evidence for chrM occupancy" is not fully supported without a clear, quantitative inclusion criterion and without access to the underlying data.
- The claim that D-loop enrichment is "almost certainly an experimental artifact" is not adequately supported by direct evidence.
- The claim that bZIP TFs are "most likely to be playing a role in mitochondria" is speculative and not directly tested.
- The claim that the compendium is "the most comprehensive catalog of human TFs potentially occupying mitochondrial DNA compiled so far" is plausible but cannot be verified without comparison to other resources.
- The BPNet predictions over chrM are presented as corroborating evidence, but the validity of applying models trained on nuclear data to the mitochondrial genome is not established.
