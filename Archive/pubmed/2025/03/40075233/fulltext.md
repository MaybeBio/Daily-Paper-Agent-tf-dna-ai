# Identification of bioactive compounds and molecular targets of Fuke Huahuang formulation to treat vaginitis


## Background
  Fuke Huahuang formulation (FHF) is widely used in the treatment of vaginitis, with clinical evidence indicating its promising anti‐inflammatory properties.


## Methods
  We explored the bioactive components and potential mechanisms of FHF for treating vaginitis, and reveal its pharmacological activities against vaginitis.


## Results
  A total of 12 anti‐inflammatory components in FHF and 584 pharmacological targets were identified. Furthermore, 1427 vaginitis‐associated targets were identified, and 184 intersection targets between FHF and vaginitis were constructed for network analysis. Gene Ontology and pathway analysis revealed that the therapeutical targets of FHF against vaginitis are involved in modulating inflammatory stress, enhancing immunoregulation, reconstructing the microenvironment, and suppressing cell damage. Molecular docking analysis further suggested the possible direct binding of the bioactive compounds of FHF (fumarine) to the core targets, including AKT Serine/Threonine Kinase 1 (AKT1), Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 3 (STAT3), and nuclear factor‐kappaB (NF‐κB). Experimental validation found that FHF‐treated vaginitis rats exhibited reduced intracellular AKT1, STAT3, and NF‐κB protein expressions.


## Conclusion
  Overall, we identified the bioactive compounds and pharmacological mechanisms of FHF against vaginitis, thus offering a theoretical fundament for exploring FHF for treating vaginitis in the future.


## INTRODUCTION
  Vaginitis is one of the genitourinary syndromes in women of reproductive age caused by infection, inflammation, or an imbalance in the vaginal flora. 1  The most common diagnoses are bacterial vaginosis, Candida vulvovaginitis (vulvovaginal candidiasis [VVC]), Trichomonas vaginalis, or a combination of these pathogens. 2  Bacterial vaginitis can disrupt reproductive function before pregnancy as tubal infertility may occur after inflammatory infiltration. 3  A retrospective study showed that 75% of women will experience VVC at least once in a year, whereas 5% suffer from recurrent VVC infections experiencing four or more times a year. 4  Women with VVC experience obvious symptoms, such as vaginal itching, burning, and abnormal discharge (often described as thick and white and resembling cottage cheese). 5  Current treatment for vaginitis generally involves antibiotics, which are effective in the short term but are associated with high recurrence rates. 6  Therefore, there is an urgent need to identify new natural products for treating vaginitis.

  According to traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) theory, vaginitis is considered a form of damp toxin in women, and its common symptoms are classified as “liver‐kidney Yin deficiency,” “liver‐meridian heat,” and “damp invasion of lower energizer.” TCM has a long historical record, characterized by low drug resistance and side effects in the management of vaginitis. Based on syndrome differentiation, TCM treatment has provided conclusive evidence supporting the efficacy of herbal therapy. The common TCM prescriptions, including Yi Huang decoction, Longdan Xiegan decoction, and Zhibai Dihaung decoction, are reported to be used for invigorating the spleen, clearing dampness and heat, and nourishing the kidney. 7  Fuke Huahuang formulation (FHF) is an ethnodrug that consists of the natural extracts of Phellodendron amurense, Gentianella acuta, Gardenia jasminoides Ellis, Lonicera confusa, Rhizoma phei, and Houttuynia cordata. Pharmacological studies of Chinese medicine suggest that the molecular mechanisms underlying the effects of TCM ingredients in treating vaginitis include antibacterial activity, estrogen‐like effects, and immune enhancement. 8  However, there are no study that have reported the potential therapeutic effectiveness of FHF, which are needed to explore the bioactive compounds and mechanisms of FHF for treating vaginitis.

  The potential efficacy of FHF, especially complex prescriptions, frequently contains multiple ingredients for targeting numerous targets and pathways. It is emerging that network pharmacology, a methodology of integrating pharmacology, bioinformatics, and computer technology, has been used to understand the detailed networks consisting of multiple ingredients, targets, and signaling pathways involved in TCM against disorders. 9  Our previous reports have suggested that network pharmacology strategy can be used to identify the core targets and enriched pathways in natural compounds from TCMs for treating human diseases. 10 ,  11  This study aimed to employ a network pharmacology analysis to delineate the pharmacological mechanisms of FHF actions for treating vaginitis and the bioactive compounds of FHF. Furthermore, computational molecular docking analysis was applied to characterize the spatial interactions between the bioactive compounds of FHF and its identified target proteins against vaginitis. Furthermore, a pharmacological experiment was conducted in VVC rat model to assess the pharmacological activities of FHF, and immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis was used to validate the spatial docking findings at protein levels.


## METHODS

### Identification of bioactive compounds of FHF and its associated targets
  The representative medicinal herbs in FHF (P. amurense, G. acuta, G. jasminoides Ellis, L. confusa, R. phei, and H. cordata) were determined via the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology database (https://tcmspw.com/tcmsp.php), in which the bioactive compounds were screened and identified using the criteria, including oral bioavailability 30.0% or more and a drug‐like index ≥0.18. The natural compounds were identified based on the molecular structure and formula using PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). Then the potential targets of these compounds were screened and obtained using online databases, including Swiss Target Prediction (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch) and SuperPred (https://prediction.charite.de/index.php); then correction analysis was performed using the reviewed (Swiss‐Prot) Human dataset from UniprotKB (http://www.uniprot.org/) for target rectification and standardization.


### Identification of vaginitis‐associated genes
  We used the keyword “vaginitis” to identify the vaginitis‐associated genes using online databases DisGeNET (http://www.disgenet.org) and GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/). Then the vaginitis‐associated genes were overlapped with the identified FHF‐associated genes to obtain the targets of FHF against vaginitis.


### Protein–protein interaction of FHF targets against vaginitis
  The protein–protein interaction (PPI) assessment was conducted on the intersection targets of FHF against vaginitis using the String online platform (https://string‐db.org/). And the species was set as Homo sapiens, in which the confidence was set as ≥0.9, to create a PPI network that integrated both physical interactions and functional associations. Cytoscape, version 3.9.1, was employed for topological analysis to further narrow down the core targets; the topological parameters “betweenness centrality” and “degree” were determined to correlate with the significance of the potential targets of FHF against vaginitis.


### Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analysis
  To determine the anti‐vaginitis mechanisms of FHF against vaginitis, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis were performed using the Database for Annotation Visualization and Integrated Discovery (http://www.david.niaid.nih.gov). GO‐based test provided an in‐depth analysis of the functional sets, including molecular functions (MF), biological processes (BP), and cellular components (CC). The KEGG annotations integrated all genetic functions from core targets and characterized them in signaling pathways. We characterized the reported data in the bubble charts (p < 0.05) to identify the MFs and mechanisms of the core targets.


### Molecular docking analysis
  Molecular docking analysis was used to assess the possible binding between the bioactive ingredients of FHF and the identified core targets. The molecular structures of core proteins, including AKT1 (4GV1), STAT3 (5AX3), and nuclear factor‐kappaB (NF‐κB) (4ND5), were downloaded from the Protein Data Bank dataset (https://www.rcsb.org/). The AutoDock, version 1.5.6, was used to conduct binding analysis between the bioactive compounds, including kaempferol and quercetin, and the core target proteins. Then the binding energy was determined, and the binding energy <−5 was considered as positive binding.


### Animal experiments
  The experiments were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of the Guangxi Medical University; 7‐week‐old Sprague–Dawley female rats were purchased from the Animal Experimental Center of Guangxi Medical University. All rats were acclimatized for ~7 days and randomly divided into four groups, with three mice in each group, namely the control (Ctrl) group, saline treatment against candidal vaginitis (VVC) group, 15 mg/mL clotrimazole (CTZ) treatment against VVC (VVC + CTZ) group, and 0.09 g/mL FHF treatment against VVC (VVC + FHF) group. After adaptive feeding for a week, estradiol benzoate (2 mL/4 mg) was used to induce estrus in each rat thrice, whereas the Ctrl group was injected with sesame oil. Candida albicans (2.5 × 108 CFU/mL) was used to develop the VVC animal model. Vaginal tissues were collected daily to observe exfoliated fungal spores or mycelia using smear microscopy, serving as the standard for a successful VVC animal model.


### Hematoxylin and eosin staining and IHC analysis
  The vaginal tissues were fixed in formalin solution, embedded in paraffin, and sliced into thin sections. Histological sections were used for hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining. The sections were dewaxed, rehydrated, subjected to antigen retrieval, and then incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide to block endogenous peroxidase activity for IHC. After preincubation with 3% bovine serum albumin, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight and incubated with secondary antibodies. Nuclear counterstaining was performed using hematoxylin. Modified histologic quantitative data of IHC staining were analyzed using ImageJ software.


### Statistical analysis
  All results were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was determined using Student's t‐test or one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using SPSS software. Statistical significance was set at a p value < 0.05.


## RESULTS

### Potential bioactive components and targets of FHF
  The bioactive components in FHF‐based herbs were identified through an online database. A total of 38 biocomponents in P. amurense, 10 biocomponents in G. acuta, 15 biocomponents in G. jasminoides Ellis, 2 biocomponents in L. confusa, 16 biocomponents in R. phei, and 7 biocomponents in H. cordata were identified. Based on the inflammatory characteristics of vaginitis, there were 12 bioactive components that could effectively exert anti‐vaginitis effects (Table 1). And 584 FHF‐associated genes and 1641 vaginitis‐associated genes were obtained. Then, we overlapped the FHF‐ and vaginitis‐associated genes, and we found 184 targets of FHF against vaginitis (Figure 1); then we constructed a PPI network; Cytoscape analysis (version 3.9.1) was performed to screen and identify the core targets of FHF against vaginitis (Figure 1). We found 13 pharmacological targets, namely GAPDH, AKT1, TP53, TNF, ALB, STAT3, EGFR, ESR1, CASP3, MMP9, BCL2, NFKB1, and HIF1A (Figure 2).


### Therapeutical functions of FHF target genes
  Enrichment analysis was used to reveal the pharmacological functions and mechanisms of FHF against vaginitis. GO analysis highlighted the BPs related to the negative regulation of apoptotic process, positive regulation of pri‐miRNA transcription from RNA polymerase II promoter, positive regulation of transcription, DNA‐templated neuron apoptotic process, positive regulation of nitric‐oxide synthase activity, negative regulation of gene expression, positive regulation of transcription from RNA polymerase II promoter, negative regulation of production of miRNAs involved in gene silencing by miRNA, positive regulation of peptidyl‐serine phosphorylation, and digestive tract morphogenesis (Figure 3A). The results of CCs show that the target genes were mainly enriched in the macromolecular complex, cytoplasm, nucleus, transcription factor complex, chromatin, nucleoplasm, cytosol, membrane raft, nuclear membrane, and euchromatin (Figure 3B). Moreover, MFs were enriched in identical protein binding, enzyme binding, nitric‐oxide synthase regulator activity, protease binding, transcriptional activator activity, RNA polymerase II transcription regulatory region sequence‐specific binding, 14–3‐3 protein binding, protein kinase binding, transcription factor activity, sequence‐specific DNA binding, transcription coactivator binding, and transcription regulatory region sequence‐specific DNA binding (Figure 3C). Furthermore, the top‐ranked signaling pathways in the KEGG analysis were proteoglycans in cancer, hepatitis B, pathways in cancer, Hypoxia Inducible Factor 1 (HIF‐1) signaling pathway, lipid and atherosclerosis, hepatitis C, prostate cancer, endocrine resistance, Epstein–Barr virus infection, and advanced glycation end products‐receptor for advanced glycation end products (AGE‐RAGE) signaling pathway in diabetic complications (Figure 4A). And the functional roles and pathways controlled by the FHF targets against vaginitis were analyzed (Figure 4B).


### Binding of FHF to the target proteins
  Molecular docking analysis showed that the core proteins, including AKT1 (4GV1), STAT3 (5AX3), and NF‐κB (4ND5), exhibited the highest binding affinity with fumarine. In the OXZ protein (4GV1) with root mean square error (RMSD) = 2.61 Å, the free docking energy of the primary ligand 0XZ with the 4GV1 protein was −9.4 kcal/mol. Fumarine formed a hydrogen bond with the amino acid residue of ASP‐292, PHE‐161, and THR‐160, and the free docking energy was −5.8 kcal/mol with OXZ (Figure 5A). In the 5ID protein (5AX3) with RMSD = 2.75 Å, the free docking energy of the primary ligand 0XZ with the 4GV1 protein was −6.7 kcal/mol. Fumarine formed a hydrogen bond with the amino acid residue of GLU‐24, and the free docking energy was −8.1 kcal/mol (Figure 5B). In the AGS protein (4ND5) with RMSD = 2.43 Å, the free docking energy of the primary ligand AGS with the 4ND5 protein was −8.9 kcal/mol. Fumarine formed a hydrogen bond with amino acid residues of LEU‐472, and ASP‐519, and the free docking energy was −7.5 kcal/mol with AGS (Figure 5C).


### Preliminary animal data and validation results
  The rats in the VVC group exhibited severe inflammation, including congestion, redness, and swelling in the reproductive tissue (vagina, cervix, “Y”‐type uterus, and ovaries), accompanied by purulent vaginal secretions (Figures 6A and S1). Compared with the VVC group, the VVC + CTZ and the VC + FHF groups exhibited a protective effect on inflammatory response (Figures 6A and S1). HE staining revealed no necrosis of the vaginal mucosal epithelial cells in the Ctrl group, whereas varying degrees of necrosis, exfoliation, and ulceration, accompanied by inflammatory cell infiltration, hyperemia, edema, tissue ulceration, congested and edematous lamina propria, as well as notable neutrophil, lymphocyte, and monocytic cell infiltration and fibrocyte proliferation, were observed in the VVC group (Figure 6B). Treatment with CTZ and FHF significantly improved the continuity of the vaginal epithelium, minimized superficial erosion, and reduced inflammatory cell infiltration (Figure 6B). Moreover, fungal spores derived from vaginal tissues were significantly increased in the VVC group compared to the Ctrl group (Figure 6C,D, p < 0.01). Notably, the VVC + CTZ and the VVC + FHF groups exhibited a significant decrease in the number of fungal spores compared to the VVC group (Figure 6C,D, p < 0.01).

  To further validate the molecular docking results, immunohistochemistry analysis was conducted to validate the molecular docking determination of target proteins. The data revealed that FHF and CTZ treatment in VVC rats resulted in a significant reduction in the expression levels of AKT1, STAT3 and NF‐κB, compared to the VVC group (Figure 7A‐C, p < 0.01). Specifically, the VVC group showed elevated AKT1 and NF‐κB protein expression compared to control rats (Figure 7A,C, p < 0.01). These findings demonstrate that FHF significantly mitigate inflammation, tissue damage, and fungal proliferation in the VVC rat model, highlighting their potential therapeutic efficacy for treating vaginitis


## DISCUSSION
  FHF is a traditional prescription used to treat vaginitis, and this formulation comprises P. amurense, G. acuta, G. jasminoides Ellis, L. confusa, R. phei, and H. cordata, with beneficial functions such as anti‐inflammatory, antibacterial, immunity‐enhancing, and blood circulation–promoting effects. As a hospital prescription, FHF can be administered for managing some gynecological disorders, including increased leucorrhea, vaginal itching, and inflammation. However, there is no report to study the pharmacological target or molecular mechanism in FHF against vaginitis. Using bioinformatic analysis, we first identified the bioactive compounds in FHF, namely fumarine, cavidine, phellamurin, quercetin, kaempferol, isovitexin, and eupatin. These beneficial compounds from FHF were used to assess the correlative genes associated with vaginitis. Therefore, 184 intersection targets were obtained and identified using the PPI network analysis. Further topological analysis identified 13 core targets, namely GAPDH, AKT1, TP53, TNF, ALB, STAT3, EGFR, ESR1, CASP3, MMP9, BCL2, NFKB1, and HIF1A. These core target genes can perform the key roles in inflammatory stress, immune dysfunction, microenvironment dysregulation, and cell lesion/death. They can predict that these conditions may be regulated or improved by FHF for treating vaginitis. GO enrichment analysis indicated that the key functions were enriched in the biological processes, including cell apoptotic process, miRNA molecular regulation, and transcription factor activity. The molecular pathways mediated by FHF for treating vaginitis could be through immunoregulation, cellular activity improvement, and microenvironment regulation. The HIF‐1 molecular pathway and AGE‐RAGE molecular pathway in vascular cell injuries were also enriched in the analysis, and they played key roles in inflammatory stress, reactive oxygen species generation, and cell proliferation. 12 ,  13  The negative correlation of miRNAs and vaginal microbiota is reported, 14  and the abnormal community of vaginal microbiota can be attributed to the progression of vaginitis. 15

  The component analysis of the medicinal herbs in FHF showed that kaempferol and quercetin exhibited anti‐inflammatory effects against vaginal inflammation. Kaempferol, a promising flavonoid, naturally occurs in ethnic herbs, and it was found to exert effective anti‐inflammatory pharmacological activities. 16  A study reported that a nutrimental polyphenol found in TCMs has different beneficial effects, including anti‐inflammatory and antiviral activities, immunoregulation, and hypolipemic action. 17  For further molecular docking analysis between kaempferol and quercetin and target core proteins, six proteins and the docked locations were identified, including fumarine‐AKT1 (4GV1), fumarine‐STAT3 (5AX3), and fumarine‐NF‐κB (4ND5). AKT1, a protein kinase, modulates the key cellular events, including metabolism migration, proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis. 18  AKT1 is involved in cell survival migration, cell metabolism, and genic expression. It is reported that AKT1 is required for acute inflammation through cytokine infiltration. 19  STAT3, a transcriptional regulator, helps control immunity and inflammation. If there is dysfunction with STAT3 dysfunctions, some clinical diseases, including autoimmunity or immunodeficiency, may develop. 20  STAT3 plays important roles in anti‐inflammation and in meliorating tissue lesion. 21  NF‐κB, a transcription factor, mediates various aspects of innate and adaptive immune actions and may function as a crucial mediator of inflammatory stress. 22  Additionally, the level of pro‐inflammatory NF‐κB activation increases in vaginal candidiasis in vivo. 23  Collectively, current bioinformatics findings were initially verified using in silico data of fumarine and target proteins, suggesting the pharmacological effectiveness of FHF against vaginitis.

  Bioinformatics analyses are limited by available data, which may lead to the identification of targets with experimental validation. We first developed the VVC rat model successfully for pharmacological assessment. And we found that FHF treatment in VVC rats resulted in effective pharmacological activities, characterized by reduced inflammatory impairment. Additionally, IHC stains showed that VVC rats exhibited significantly increased NF‐κB expressions, indicating potential inflammatory stress. Interestingly, FHF‐treated VVC rats exhibited decreased expressions of AKT1, STAT3, and NF‐κB, implying the anti‐inflammatory effect and improving the intracellular microenvironment for FHF treatment against VVC. Notably, bioinformatics analyses can suggest potential therapeutic targets, although bioinformatics models do not typically account for the heterogeneity of patient responses to treatment. Additionally, the interaction of FHF with standard treatments for vaginitis, such as antibiotics and antifungals, could introduce further complexity, as natural compounds may alter the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of these conventional drugs. Overall, this study provides valuable insights into the potential mechanisms through which FHF may treat vaginitis. Future research should focus on the development of manufactured and standardized drugs, as well as conduct rigorous clinical trials to confirm the clinical efficacy and safety of FHF in treating vaginitis.


## CONCLUSION
  Our results reveal the detailed mechanisms of FHF against vaginitis using network pharmacology and molecular docking analysis. Kaempferol and quercetin are the promising bioactive components of FHF action, and AKT1, STAT3, and NF‐κB were identified as the core pharmacological targets of FHF against vaginitis. Enrichment findings reveal the mechanisms of FHF through regulating inflammatory reaction, enhancing immunoregulation, reconstructing the microenvironment, and reducing cell impairment. These preclinical findings offer promising evidences for future clinical use of FHF for treating vaginitis after clinical and experimental validation.
