# Bioinformatics insights into mitochondrial and immune gene regulation in Alzheimer's disease


## Background
  There is growing evidence that the pathogenesis of Alzheimer's disease is closely linked to the resident innate immune cells of the central nervous system, including microglia and astrocytes. Mitochondrial dysfunction in microglia has also been reported to play an essential role in the pathogenesis of AD and other neurological diseases. Therefore, finding the mitochondrial and immune-related gene (MIRG) signatures in AD can be significant in diagnosing and treating AD.


## Methods
  In this study, the intersection of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from the GSE109887 cohort, immune-related genes (IRGs) obtained from WGCNA analysis, and mitochondria-related genes (MRGs) was taken to identify mitochondria–immune-related genes (MIRGs). Then, using machine learning algorithms, biomarkers with good diagnostic value were selected, and a nomogram was constructed. Subsequently, we further analyzed the signaling pathways and potential biological mechanisms of the biomarkers through gene set enrichment analysis, prediction of transcription factors (TFs), miRNAs, and drug prediction.


## Results
  Using machine learning algorithms, five biomarkers (TSPO, HIGD1A, NDUFAB1, NT5DC3, and MRPS30) were successfully identified, and a nomogram model with strong diagnostic ability and accuracy (AUC > 0.9) was constructed. In addition, single-gene enrichment analysis revealed that NDUFAB1 was significantly enriched in pathways associated with diseases, such as Alzheimer's and Parkinson's, providing valuable insights for future clinical research on Alzheimer's in the context of mitochondrial–immune interactions. Interestingly, brain tissue pathology showed neuronal atrophy and demyelination in AD mice, along with a reduction in Nissl bodies. Furthermore, the escape latency of AD mice was significantly longer than that of the control group. After platform removal, there was a notable increase in the path complexity and time required to reach the target quadrant, suggesting a reduction in spatial memory capacity in AD mice. Moreover, qRT-PCR validation confirmed that the mRNA expression of the five biomarkers was consistent with bioinformatics results. In AD mice, TSPO expression was increased, while HIGD1A, NDUFAB1, NT5DC3, and MRPS30 expressions were decreased. However, peripheral blood samples did not show expression of HIGD1A or MRPS30. These findings provide new insights for research on Alzheimer's disease in the context of mitochondrial–immune interactions, further exploring the pathogenesis of Alzheimer's disease and offering new perspectives for the clinical development of novel drugs.


## Conclusions
  Five mitochondrial and immune biomarkers, i.e., TSPO, HIGD1A, NDUFAB1, NT5DC3, and MRPS30, with diagnostic value in Alzheimer's disease, were screened by machine-learning algorithmic models, which will be a guide for future clinical research of Alzheimer's disease in the mitochondria–immunity-related direction.

## Introduction
  Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a predominant neurodegenerative disorder characterized by progressive cognitive dysfunction and behavioral impairment [1, 2]. It accounts for 60 to 80 percent of all patients with dementia [3–5]. The incidence of the disease increases with age and is most common in people over the age of 60 years [6]. The parthenogenesis that is currently recognized includes the accumulation of amyloid β (Aβ) plaques and hyperphosphorylated Tau tangles, severe synaptic and neuronal loss, reactive astrogliosis and microgliosis, brain atrophy and enlarged ventricles [7, 8]. However, the correlation between the degree of Aβ deposition and AD progression is not high [9], and most drugs targeting Aβ have failed in clinical trials [10]. This suggests that other mechanisms play a key role in AD development, and therefore, it is essential to explore new targets for intervention to improve the therapeutic effect of AD.

  Growing evidence suggests that the pathogenesis of Alzheimer's disease is closely linked to the neuroinflammatory process [11, 12]. Due to the protection of the blood–brain barrier (BBB), the peripheral immune cells are not able to enter the brain [13, 14]. Inflammatory responses within the central nervous system (CNS) are mainly caused by the release of a range of inflammatory mediators from microglia and astrocytes activation induced by injury [15, 16]. Under normal conditions, microglia are phagocytic and remove damaged neurons, thereby encouraging foreign molecules or pathogens to repair tissue at the site of invasion, where cellular immunity is initiated [12, 17, 18]. Astrocytes clear cerebrospinal fluid debris and exert neuroprotective effects [19]. In AD development, microglia are recruited to the injury lesion, and astrocyte expression increases; structural changes occur, resulting in astrocyte hyperplasia [20, 21]. Activated microglia release pro-inflammatory cytokines and neurotoxic factors, leading to phosphatase inactivation, kinase activation, and hyperphosphorylation of TAU proteins, which directly or indirectly lead to neuronal cell death and dysfunction and actively contribute to the neuroinflammatory process in the brain process [22, 23]. In addition, monocytes, macrophages, neutrophils, and T cells from the peripheral blood were found to be involved in the pathogenesis of AD due to the BBB breakdown [24].

  Mitochondria derive energy from glycolysis, respiration, and fatty acid oxidation and use oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) to provide the major biological requirement of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) [25, 26]. Mitochondria maintain the energy metabolism of the organism, regulate Ca2 + channels, and regulate oxidative stress (OS) and apoptosis [20, 27]. Meanwhile, mitochondria can maintain their own dynamic balance through division, fusion, transport, and autophagy [28, 29]. Mitochondria are the cell's primary energy providers, and neurons are particularly vulnerable to mitochondrial damage due to their high metabolic demands [30, 31]. More recent research indicated that mitochondria dysfunction occurs in the early stage of AD development, which induces the pathology, including ROS production, neuroinflammatory, Aβ accumulation, and hyperphosphorylation of TAU protein [20, 32–34]. Therefore, finding the mitochondrial and immune-related gene (MIRG) signatures in AD can be significant in the early diagnosis and treatment of AD.

  This study aims to search for mitochondrial and immune-related genes in AD and further investigate the mechanism of action of these genes in AD. We first analyzed the differential expressed genes in cohorts from GSE109887 downloaded from the GEO data set. Then, the abundance of 28 immune cell infiltrates was calculated using the ssGSEA algorithm; the WGCNA was performed using differential immune cells as traits to screen the immune-related genes (IRG) that were associated with immune cells. Mitochondria-related genes (MRG) were obtained in the MItoCarta 3.0 database, and MIRGs were obtained by the intersection of DEGs, IRGs, and MRGs. Machine learning algorithms further screened the key genes in MIRGs, and a nomogram was constructed. Then, their regulatory mechanism was predicted.


## Materials and methods

### Data acquisition and processing
  The mRNA expression matrix of AD patients and non-diseased control was downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). GSE109887 [35] was used as a training set to perform the subsequent analysis, which included 46 middle temporal gyrus tissues from AD patients and 32 middle temporal gyrus tissues from non-diseased control (Supplementary Table 1, retrieval data: Jun. 14, 2023). GPL10904 Illumina HumanHT-12 V4.0 expression– beadchip (gene symbol) platform was used to perform a gene chip sequencing. GSE5281 [36] was used as a validation set to validate the expression and diagnostic value of screened gene signatures, which included which including 16 middle temporal gyrus tissues from AD patients and 12 middle temporal gyrus tissues from non-diseased control (Supplementary Table 2, retrieval data: Jun. 14, 2023). GPL570 [HG-U133_Plus_2] Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 Array platform was used to perform a gene chip sequencing. The probe IDs in the gene expression matrix were converted to gene symbols by combining them with the corresponding annotation files of the sequencing platform.

  1136 mitochondrial-related genes (MRGs) were obtained from the MItoCarta 3.0 database (https://www.broadinstitute.org/mitocarta/mitocarta30-inventory-mammalian-mitochondrial-proteins-and-pathways) according to the previous reference [37] (Supplementary Table 3).


### Differential expression gene analysis
  The GSE109887 gene expression matrix and sample grouping information were obtained from the GEO database. It was also worth noting that these data had been well standardized with the help of the quantile–quantile normalization function in R software (4.2.2). The differentially expressed genes were identified from AD patients and non-diseased control in GSE109887 using the limma R package (3.42.2) [38]. The corresponding P value and Log2 Fold Change (FC) values were obtained, and the P value was further corrected to obtain the adjusted P Value (P adj.) by multiple test calibration using the Benjamini & Hochberg method. The gene was considered a differential expressed gene when |log2FC|> 0.5 and P adj. < 0.05. The heatmap was constructed using the ComplxHeatmap package (2.14.0) [39].


### Immune cell infiltration analysis
  To evaluate the immune infiltration in AD samples compared to control samples in GSE1109887, the expression matrix was downloaded and analyzed using the single sample gene enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) algorithm via the GSVA R package (1.42.0) [40]. The abundance of 28 immune cells was calculated and tested via the Wilcox test via the ggplot2 R package (3.3.2) [41]. The cells were considered differentially infiltrated between the control and AD groups when the P value < 0.05. The correlation between the biomarkers and the differential immune cells was calculated using the Spearman test, and the P value and correlation coefficient r were displayed with a heatmap constructed by the ggplot2 R package.


### Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)
  WGCNA was performed to obtain genes highly related to immune cell infiltration. WGCNA R package (1.70.3) [42] was used to perform the analysis. Samples in GSE1109887 were hierarchically clustered with Euclidean distance, and immune cell infiltration was induced as a trait. The optimal soft threshold power β was calculated to convert the correlation matrix into an adjacency matrix according to the co-expression similarity, and the adjacency matrix was transformed into a topological overlap matrix (TOM). The TOM-based dissimilarity (1-TOM) was used to categorize genes with similar expression patterns via hierarchical clustering and the dynamic tree-cut function. Modules associated with at least 7 differential immune cells were defined as key modules.


### Gene ontology (GO) annotation and Kyoto Encyclopedia of gene genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis
  To predict the biological function of intersected genes, the GO terms, including biological process (BP), cellular components (CC), molecular function (MF), and KEGG pathways, were analyzed using the clusterProfiler R package (3.14.3) [43]. The TOP10 terms ranked by P value and related genes were visualized using the ggplot2 R package.


### Robust prediction of hub genes with a variety of machine learning algorithms
  The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression analysis was performed using the glmnet R package (4.0–2) with the parameters family = "binomial" and type.measure = "class," tenfold cross-validation was used to calculate the error rates for different features, and the minimal penalty term (λ) was selected. The corresponding gene signatures were chosen based on the minimal classification error. Using the e1071 R package (1.7–9) [43] to apply the Support Vector Machine (SVM) algorithm for ranking intersected genes, the Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) method is employed to calculate the importance ranking. Unimportant genes are removed, and the remaining genes are re-ranked for importance. This process provides each iteration's importance, error rate, and accuracy. Cross-validation is set with 5 folds, and model accuracy is computed for different feature combinations. The best combination is selected when the highest accuracy is achieved, and the corresponding genes are chosen as gene labels. The random Forest (RF) algorithm was performed using the randomForest R package (3.36.0) [44]. The Mean Decrease Accuracy and Mean Decrease Gini of each gene were obtained, and the Top 10 genes ranked by Mean Decrease Accuracy were selected as gene signatures. The biomarkers were selected as the intersection of the gene signature obtained from the above algorithms.


### Construction of ROC curves and nomogram
  The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve of each biomarker was created by the pROC R package (0.7.7) [45], and to evaluate the diagnosis value of the biomarker, the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated. The expression of biomarkers was compared using the Wilcox test and plotted by the ggplot2 R package. To calculate the expression correlation of biomarkers, the expression of biomarkers in GSE109887 was obtained, and the expression correlation was calculated by Spearman correlation; the P value and correlation coefficient were plotted using the ggplot2 R package. To construct the nomogram, the risk score of each biomarker related to the risk of AD was obtained according to the expression of the biomarker, and the nomogram was constructed by the RMS R package (6.2–0). The calibration curve and Decision curve analysis (DCA) curve were constructed to evaluate the precision and clinical benefit of the nomogram.


### Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of biomarkers
  To further investigate the signaling pathways and potential biological mechanisms for biomarkers, GSEA proceeded with reference to KEGG (c2.cp. Kegg.v7.5.1. symbols.gmt, retrieval data: Jun. 14, 2023) gene sets in MsigDB database (http://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb) via clusterProfiler R package. The Spearman correlation was used to calculate the correlation coefficient of biomarkers to all genes. The samples were classified as high- and low-expression groups in the light of correlation coefficients (NOM P < 0.05, |NES|> 1).


### Regulation network construction
  The binding of transcription factors (TF) and miRNAs to biomarkers were predicted via the NetworkAnalyst platform (https://www.networkanalyst.ca) based on JASPER and mirTarbase. The drugs targeted to biomarkers were predicted by the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD, http://ctdbase.org/). The network was visualized by Cytoscape (3.6.1) [45].


### Clinical sample collection
  The peripheral blood samples of 30 AD patients, along with 30 healthy controls, were collected from The Second People's Hospital of Kunming from 2023 to 2024. The patients were diagnosed according to the diagnostic criteria of Alzheimer’s disease [45–47]. The inclusion criteria were set as follows: (1) clinically diagnosed as mild or medium AD patients; (2) a Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score < 20; (3) age ranged in 60–90 years; and (4) stable administration of anti-dementia or mood-stabilizing medication. The exclusion criteria were set as follows: (1) patients with psychiatric diseases; (2) patients with other brain diseases; ((3) patients with recent cognitive training; (4) patients with cardiac, pulmonary, and renal dysfunction; and (5) patients with malignant. All patients and/or their family members were informed of the usage of the sample and signed the informed consent form. The human protocols were implemented strictly on the basis of the Ethical Guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. ### Animal model and Morris water maze detection
  APPswe/PS1Δ9 transgenic mice [48] at 10 months and wild-type C57BL/6J mice at 10 months were used for validation. All the mice were housed under SPF conditions at consistent temperatures and with free access to food and water. All the procedures were undergone according to ARRIVE guidelines and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Yunnan Labreal Biotechnology Co, Ltd (No, PZ20240207). Morris water maze (MWM) was used to test the spatial learning ability of mice as previously described [49–51]. MWM pool (120 cm diameter and 60 cm height) was added with a 30 cm depth of water and divided into four quadrants. In the training trials, mice were placed into water at the starting position located in one of the quadrants, and mice were allowed to find the hidden platform for 60 s and remain on the platform for 5 s. After a training trial for 5 days, the platform was removed on the sixth day, and escape latency, time spent in the target quadrant, and the number of platform crossings were recorded and analyzed by the analysis system AnyMaze (Stoelting, IL., USA). The mice were euthanized through intravenous injection of 1% Sodium pentobarbital (150 mg/kg) followed by cervical dislocation. The brain tissues were collected for subsequent experiments.


### HE staining and Nissl staining
  Biran tissues were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and washed with distilled water. For HE staining, the tissues were stained with hematoxylin (G1004, servicebio, China) and Eosin staining (G1001, servicebio, China), respectively, per the manufacturer’s guideline. For Nissl staining, tissues were stained with 0.06% Tar violet stain solution for 60 min at 56 ℃. After dehydration, transparency, and sealing, images were acquired by laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSM710, Carl Zeiss, Germany) and analyzed using ImageJ.


### mRNA expression detection
  The cortex tissues were extracted and grounded in liquid nitrogen, and total RNA from cortex tissues and peripheral blood was extracted by TRIZOL reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 15,596–018, MA, USA). The total RNA concentration was determined by an ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MI, USA). In addition, the cDNA was synthesized by riboSCRIPTTM mRNA/lncRNA qRT-PCR Starter Kit (Ribobio, C11030-1, China). PCR amplification was performed by Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q712-03, TaKaRa Bio, Japan), and qPCR analysis was performed. mRNA level was detected using GAPDH as the internal reference gene. ### Statistical analysis
  R software conducted the two-tailed statistical analysis and visualization for bioinformatic analysis (4.2.2) unless specific. The experimental data was analyzed by GraphPad Prism 10. Shapiro–Wilk test was used to test the distribution of data, and normally distributed data was expressed as mean ± standard deviation. An unpaired parametric t test was used to compare the data between two groups; statistical significance was considered when P < 0.05 in all analyses unless expressly stated.

### The screening of gene signatures via machine learning algorithms
  To further determine the gene signatures in 26 MIRGs, LASSO logistic regression was first constructed based on the expression of MIRGs in each sample and grouping information in GSE109887. The lowest misclassification error was reached at the minimal penalty term lambda equaled to 0.01226492, and 11 gene signatures were selected, including GLS, HIGD1A, MRPS30, NDUFAB1, NT5DC3, OPA1, OXR1, PREPL, SLC25A12, SUCLA2, TSPO (Fig. 5A, B, Supplementary Table 10). Then, SVM was used to determine the gene signatures. ## Discussion
  There is no effective treatment to halt the development of AD now may indicate that other biochemical and morphological features present early in the course of AD, such as altered phospholipid metabolism, elevated circulating cholesterol levels, abnormal calcium regulation, decreased brain glucose levels, mitochondrial dysfunction should not be overlooked [52]. Mitochondrial damage is not only associated with Aβ production and abnormal deposition but also exacerbates Aβ neurotoxicity, leading to and accelerating the onset and progression of AD [53]. There is growing evidence that the pathogenesis of Alzheimer's disease is closely linked to the resident innate immune cells of the central nervous system, including microglia and astrocytes [54, 55]. Mitochondrial dysfunction in microglia has also been reported to play an essential role in the pathogenesis of AD and other neurological diseases [31, 56]. Therefore, in this manuscript, we downloaded GSE109887 and GSE5281, 776 differential expression genes between AD and control samples from GSE109887. Then, the ssGSEA algorithm was used to analyze differentially enriched immune cells. 14 immune cells were found, including CD56dim natural killer cell, Central memory CD8 T cell, Effector memory CD8 T cell, Immature B cell, Mast cell, MDSC, Memory B cell, Natural killer cell, Natural killer T cell, Neutrophil, Plasmacytoid dendritic cell, Regulatory T cell, T follicular helper cell, Type 1 T helper cell. Apart from microglia and astrocytes, other immune cells in peripheral blood were also found to exert an effect on AD pathological development. The blood–brain barrier (BBB) is a highly specialized endothelial membrane that separates components of the circulating blood from neurons in concert with pericytes, astrocytes, and microglia [24]. The tight junction could disrupted due to aberrant angiogenesis, vessel regression, brain hypoperfusion, and inflammatory responses [57] and induce progressive synaptic and neuronal dysfunction and loss in disorders, such as Alzheimer's disease [58]. Agatha et al. proved that P2X7 receptors may induce BBB disruption and chemotaxis of peripheral immune cells to the CNS, resulting in brain parenchyma infiltration [59]. Xu et al. also found five immune cell subsets: CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T cells, B cells, and natural killer cells in peripheral blood from AD patients [60]. Brian-resident CD8 + T cells were found to restrict β-amyloid deposition and cognitive decline through CXCL16–CXCR6 [61]. B cells were also found to contribute to the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer's disease and Parkinson's disease [62]. Xiong et al. found a significant decrease in B cells in AD patients. B cell depletion in the early stage of AD markedly accelerated and aggravated cognitive dysfunction and augmented the Aβ burden in AD mice [63]. Neutrophils were recently found to induce vascular oxidative stress via neutrophil adhesion and neutrophil-induced myeloperoxidase increase and, therefore, aggravated cognitive dysfunction [64]. Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) are extracellularly releasing reticular complexes of double-stranded DNA (dsDNA), histones, and granulins from neutrophils [65]. Under physiological conditions, NETs degrade cytokines and chemokines through protease to exert anti-inflammatory effects. Still, excessive amounts of NET release (NETosis) were found to irritate the neuroinflammation and disrupt the BBB [66]. These researches evident that immune cells play a significant role in AD pathogenesis.

  We, therefore, performed WGCNA analysis of 14 differential immune cells as traits to obtain the highly related genes with immune cells, and 17,263 hub genes were screened. Meanwhile, 1136 mitochondria-related genes were obtained in the MItoCarta 3.0 database, and 26 differentially expressed mitochondrial and immune-related genes were obtained. By analyzing their biological function through GO and KEGG enrichment, the MIRGs were enriched in the terms related to mitochondrial functions, including mitochondrial transmembrane transport, regulation of autophagy of mitochondrion, positive regulation of mitophagy, the terms related to immune responses, such as neutrophil extracellular trap formation, regulation of necrotic cell death, positive regulation of macroautophagy, selective autophagy, and the terms related to neuro diseases including Parkinson disease, Alzheimer disease, and pathways of neurodegeneration-multiple diseases. Mitochondrial dysfunction is not only associated with the occurrence of energy metabolic abnormalities in neuronal cells. Still, it is also closely related to abnormalities in Reactive oxygen species (ROS) homeostasis, Ca2+ imbalance, abnormalities in apoptosis, mitochondrial morphology, alterations in mitochondrial DNA, and autophagy [67, 68]. Nadia et al. found that lower mitochondrial genome abundance induced impacting neuronal and glial cell function by reducing mitochondrial bioenergetics [69]. The mitochondrial cascade hypothesis [70] argued that mitochondria are a novel and critical target site for Aβ to exert its toxic effects. Alzheimer's amyloid precursor protein 695 (APP) is the source of the toxic Aβ, and it was found in contact with mitochondrial translocase proteins and caused mitochondrial dysfunction and impaired energy metabolism [71]. Alexander et al. found that Aβ 25–35 aggregates affected the ion transport and Ca2 + -dependent swelling in mitochondria [72]. Organelle-specific autophagy is one of the significant autophagy subtypes, selectively targeting different organelles for quality control [73]. Mitophagy diminished neuronal cell apoptosis and neuroinflammation, culminating with rescued synaptic loss and cognitive decline [74]. Kaempferol and Rhapontigenin, two mitophagy inducers, were found to increase the survival and functionality of glutamatergic and cholinergic neurons and abrogated amyloid-β and tau pathologies [75]. Our results and existing research showed that mitochondrial dysfunction is an irreplaceable part of AD pathological development, especially by mitochondrial transmembrane transport and mitophagy.

  To further select the biomarkers, three machine learning algorithms, including LASSO, SVM, and Random Forest, were performed in GSE109887, and 5 biomarkers, i.e., TSPO, HIGD1A, NDUFAB1, NT5DC3, and MRPS30 were obtained by taking the intersection of the results of the three machine learning methods mentioned above. ROC curves showed a strong diagnostic ability to differentiate between disease and control samples, and a nomogram, which was verified by the calibration curves, ROC curves, and decision curves (DCA), was constructed in accordance with the risk score calculated by the expression of the biomarkers. Dissipation of the mitochondrial transmembrane potential leads to permeation of the inner mitochondrial membrane through the opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP), a phenomenon known as the mitochondrial permeability transition, which can cause irreversible structural damage to the mitochondrial membrane [76, 77]. Translocator protein (TSPO) is a significant component of the mPTP and is located in the outer mitochondrial membrane [78]. TSPO has been considered the PET imaging biomarker of reference to measure increased neuroinflammation, which is principally detected in activated microglia, endothelial cells, and activated astrocytes [79]. Consistent with our result, TSPO was found to be more expressed in AD patients and mice brain tissues [80], but its mechanism in AD development and pathological change was not precise. Cytochrome c oxidase (CcO) is the only enzyme that generates a proton gradient for ATP production during mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation with oxygen [80]. Hypoxia-inducible gene, domain member 1A (HIGD1A), is a mitochondrial inner membrane protein acting as a subunit of CcO that catalyzes the reduction of oxygen to water [81]. Hiroki et al. found that overexpression of HIGD1A attenuated hypoxia-induced γ-secretase activation on the mitochondrial membrane and the accumulation of intracellular amyloid β [82]. The mitochondrial respiratory chain is located in the inner mitochondrial membrane and comprises five complexes [83]. Complex I is the first enzyme complex in the respiratory chain, consisting primarily of flavoproteins and iron–sulfur proteins, and is the main entrance to the respiratory chain, which accepts electrons delivered by reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) and transfers them to ubiquinone [84]. NADH: ubiquinone oxidoreductase subunit AB1 (NDUFAB1) is the itochondrial acyl carrier protein (ACP) essential for cell viability [85]. It participates in lipoic acid synthesis in the type II fatty acid biosynthetic pathway (FAS II) and acts as an accessory subunit of complex I [85]. NT5DC3 is an evolutionarily conserved 5’-nucleotide enzyme that belongs to the 5’-nucleotidase domain-containing (NT5DC) family [86]. It is reported to exert catalyzing intracellular nucleotide hydrolysis and contains a haloacid dehalogenase motif [87]. Mitochondrial ribosomal protein S30 (MRPS30) encodes a member of the mitochondrial ribosomal large subunits [88]. MRPS30 genomic region was found to be related to postmenopausal breast cancer risk [89, 90]. Few reports showed the role of NT5DC3 and MRP30 in the AD process, but according to our GSEA enrichment, they may participate in the dysregulation of neuronal Ca2+ homeostasis. In addition, Acrolein, the drug predicted targeted to NT5DC3, was found to alter the pattern of the Ca2 + response to acetylcholine (ACh) but not resting cytosolic Ca2 + concentration [91]. Indomethacin, the drug predicted targeted to MRP30 was found influent extracellular homeostasis [92, 93]. However, this hypothesis remains as evidence. The correlation between biomarkers and differential immune cells was calculated using the Spearman method. The results showed that the strongest positive correlation between TSPO and Natural killer T (NKT) cells was significant. The strongest negative correlation was significant between MRPS30 and CD56dim natural killer cells (CD56dim NK). Jeff et al. found four subsets of NK cells in AD, including CD56dim effector NK cells [94]. NK cells and NKT cells are both significant immune cells in defense against pathogens and transformed cells derived from a common lymphoid progenitor in the bone marrow [95]. CD56dim NK cells showed a higher abundance of CD16, killer-cell immunoglobulin-like receptors, granzymes, and perforin, and, therefore, showed a higher cytotoxic [96]. Weng et al. also found correlations of NK cells between aging and AD by exploring different levels of AD and aging from population cohorts, single-cell data, and GWAS cohorts [97]. However, how NKT cells and CD56dim NK cells participate in AD pathological development remains a more detailed research.

  In this study, we successfully identified biomarkers associated with mitochondrial and immune gene regulation and elucidated their essential roles in the pathogenesis of Alzheimer's disease (AD). Correlation analysis between the biomarkers and differential immune cells revealed the association between these biomarkers and immune cells. At the same time, the drug prediction results demonstrated the potential for biomarker-based drug development. These findings provide substantial evidence for the study of therapeutic AD and its molecular mechanism of action and lay a solid foundation for future research. However, this study also has some limitations. The study's reliance on database data may introduce bias, and while progress has been made in the mechanism study, it remains insufficient. For instance, the interaction network between biomarkers and its detailed regulation mechanism in the cell is not yet fully understood. In addition, drugs derived from the prediction of biomarkers have not yet been validated in clinical trials, and their clinical application value needs to be further evaluated. Subsequent studies will prioritize experimental validation of functional biomarkers, including the design of additional cellular and animal experiments and further exploration of their mechanisms of action to validate the biological functions of biomarkers. In addition, molecular docking will be employed to simulate the binding between biomarkers and related drugs, observing their binding poses and obtaining binding sites. This will facilitate further exploration of the interaction mechanism between biomarkers and drug molecules and the validation of research results through clinical trials. The ultimate objective is to promote the development of AD therapy.

  In conclusion, five mitochondrial and immune biomarkers, i.e., TSPO, HIGD1A, NDUFAB1, NT5DC3, and MRPS30, with diagnostic value in Alzheimer's disease, were screened by machine-learning algorithmic models, which will be a guide for future clinical research of Alzheimer's disease in the mitochondria-immunity-related direction.
