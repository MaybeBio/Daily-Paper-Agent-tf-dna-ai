# Characterization of the Swi6/HP1 binding motif in its partner protein reveals the basis for the functional divergence of the HP1 family proteins in fission yeast


## Abstract
  The heterochromatin protein 1 (HP1) family recognizes lysine 9‐methylated histone H3 (H3K9me) and recruits other transacting factors to establish higher order chromatin structures. In the fission yeast Schizosaccharomyces pombe (S. pombe), two HP1 family proteins, Swi6 and Chp2, play distinct roles in recruiting transacting factors: Swi6 primarily recruits Epe1, a Jumonji C domain‐containing protein involved in histone H3K9 demethylation, whereas Chp2 recruits Mit1, a component of the Snf2/Hdac Repressive Complex. However, detailed mechanisms of how multiple HP1 family proteins and their respective interactors work cooperatively or exclusively to form higher order chromatin structures remain elusive. In this study, we investigated the interactions between Swi6 and Epe1. We found that Swi6 interacts with Epe1 through its chromoshadow domain, and identified a unique motif, named the FVI motif, in Epe1 involved in this interaction through detailed mapping of the region. Enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) tethering assays showed that the FVI motif is sufficient to recruit ectopically expressed EGFP to heterochromatic regions, and mutational analyses revealed that conserved hydrophobic residues in this motif are essential for proper targeting. Structural simulations further supported the importance of these residues in Swi6 binding. Interestingly, Mit1 containing the Epe1 FVI motif was recruited to the heterochromatic regions by Swi6 but not by Chp2. Cells expressing mutant Mit1 maintained heterochromatic silencing even in chp2∆ cells, suggesting that Chp2 is not required for heterochromatin formation when Mit1 is recruited by Swi6. These findings highlight distinct HP1‐binding motifs in interactors, contributing to functional divergence among HP1 family proteins.


## INTRODUCTION
  In eukaryotic cells, higher order chromatin structures, known as heterochromatin, play important roles in various chromosomal processes, including chromosome segregation, genome stability, and gene silencing. 1 ,  2 ,  3  Histones in heterochromatic regions are generally hypoacetylated, and histone H3 lysine 9 methylation (H3K9me)—catalyzed by the evolutionarily conserved SUV39 family of histone methyltransferases—is a hallmark of heterochromatin. 4  The heterochromatin protein 1 (HP1) family recognizes the H3K9me and recruits various transacting factors to form higher order chromatin structures. 5 ,  6 ,  7 ,  8

  HP1 family proteins contain two evolutionarily conserved domains connected by an unstructured hinge region 9 ,  10 : the N‐terminal chromodomain (CD), 11 ,  12 ,  13  which reads and binds to the H3K9me, 14 ,  15 ,  16 ,  17 ,  18  and the C‐terminal chromoshadow domain (CSD), 19  which forms dimers and facilitates recruitment of other transacting factors involved in heterochromatin assembly. 20 ,  21 ,  22  Although the well‐established role of the HP1 family proteins is to form repressive heterochromatin structures, these proteins also facilitate transcriptional activation. 23 ,  24 ,  25

  Multiple HP1 isoforms exist across organisms, including mammals (HP1α, HP1β, and HP1γ), 26  Xenopus laevis (HP1α and HP1γ), 27  Drosophila melanogaster (HP1a, HP1b, HP1c, Rhino, and HP1E), 28 ,  29  Caenorhabditis elegans (HPL‐1 and HPL‐2), 30  and fission yeast species (Swi6 and Chp2). 31 ,  32 ,  33 ,  34 ,  35  However, the mechanisms by which these isoforms function and whether they act cooperatively or differentially in the heterochromatin assembly is not fully understood. In addition to the distinct physical properties of each isoform, HP1 proteins also act as adaptors to recruit other transacting factors to the H3K9me‐enriched chromatin, 36  adding another layer of complexity to their cooperative or differential functions.

  Many of the HP1 interactors contain a pentapeptide motif called PxVxL, originally identified in fruit fly HP1a through phage display. 37  Structural analyses have revealed that PxVxL‐containing peptide interacts with a hydrophobic pocket formed by β‐sheets of CSD dimers, with a central valine surrounded by bulky hydrophobic residues (−2/+2 positions) being a common feature of these motifs. 21 ,  22  Although the PxVxL motif is common across HP1‐interacting proteins in mammals and flies, studies suggest that CSDs in different organisms have altered specificities for interacting proteins. 22  A detailed analysis of the interaction between Drosophila HP1a and heterochromatin protein 2 (HP2) identified a unique LCVKI motif in HP2 that binds to the HP1a‐CSD. 38  Additionally, different combinations of CSD dimers may have unique specificities, as the three mammalian HP1 isoforms can form different heterodimers.

  In the fission yeast, Schizosaccharomyces pombe, the heterochromatin structure is essential for the organization of functional chromosomal domains such as centromeres, the mating‐type region, and telomeres. 36 ,  39 ,  40  In S. pombe, the H3K9me mark, established by the SUV39 family methyltransferase Clr4, serves as a binding site for two HP1 family proteins, Swi6 and Chp2, which recruit other transacting factors to form higher order chromatin structures. 16 ,  31 ,  32 ,  33 ,  41 ,  42  Swi6 primarily recruits, Epe1, a Jumonji C (JmjC) domain‐containing protein involved in H3K9me demethylation, to the heterochromatic regions. 43 ,  44  Deletion of Epe1 impairs heterochromatic silencing and extends silent chromatin into adjacent euchromatin. 45  In contrast, overexpression of Epe1 disrupts heterochromatin and suppresses position‐effect variegation at heterochromatic regions. 46  Thus, Epe1 is thought to stabilize the heterochromatin boundaries. Chp2, another HP1 family protein in S. pombe, specifically recruits Mit1, a nucleosome‐remodeling component of the Snf2/Hdac Repressive Complex (SHREC). 35 ,  47 ,  48 ,  49  A detailed structural study revealed that the Chp2‐CSD dimer interacts with the N‐terminus of Mit1, which contains a unique CkIvV motif and CD‐like (CDL) domain (Figure S1A). 50  An amino acid substitution within this motif (I11R) or deletion of the CDL domain disrupts the interaction between Chp2 and Mit1, impairing heterochromatic silencing. 50  Although the two HP1 proteins in S. pombe play distinct roles in recruiting transacting factors, the molecular mechanisms by which they recruit specific interactors remain unclear.

  In this study, we focused on the interaction between Swi6 and Epe1. We demonstrated that Swi6 interacts with Epe1 via a unique motif, the FVI motif, and that this motif is sufficient to recruit ectopically expressed enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) to heterochromatic regions. Amino acid substitution analyses revealed that conserved hydrophobic residues in this motif are essential for the interaction. S. pombe cells expressing mutant Mit1 containing the Epe1 FVI motif maintained proper heterochromatic silencing even in chp2∆ cells, suggesting that Chp2 is not required for heterochromatin formation when Mit1 is recruited by Swi6. These results provide mechanistic insights into the division of labor among the HP1 family proteins.


## MATERIALS AND METHODS

### Strains and plasmids
  The S. pombe strains used in this study are listed in Table S1. The media and genetic techniques followed were previously described. 51  Gene deletion and epitope tagging were performed using a PCR‐based module method. 52  Strains expressing mutant Mit1 (Mit1I11R and Mit1Epe1‐FVI) were generated as previously described 41 ,  53  using the rpl42  P56Q  mutant and an rpl42 + cassette to introduce each mutant allele at the endogenous locus. To create the EGFP fused with nuclear localization signal (NLS‐EGFP) targeting vector, a double‐stranded oligonucleotide encoding the nucleoplasmin bipartite basic NLS (KRPAAIKKAGQAKKKK) 54  was inserted into the 5′‐end of the EGFP gene in the plasmid pREP4K‐EGFPc (pREP4K‐NLS‐EGFP). 53  The coding sequence for the Epe1‐FVI motif, with or without the neighboring region, was PCR‐amplified and introduced into the 3′‐end of pREP4K‐NLS‐EGFP for use in the NLS‐EGFP targeting assay. Site‐directed mutagenesis was used to introduce each amino acid substitution in the resulting plasmids, as described previously. 55  To produce 6His‐tagged recombinant proteins, the coding sequence for Swi6‐CSD (256–328 amino acids) was PCR‐amplified from S. pombe genomic DNA and cloned into the pCold I‐HRV‐3C vector, a derivative of pCold I (TaKaRa) containing an HRV‐3C cleavage site.


### Antibodies
  The following antibodies were used: anti‐Swi6, 35  anti‐Chp2, 35  anti‐Myc (M192‐3 and M192‐7; MBL), anti‐HA (120 138 119 001; Roche), anti‐FLAG (A8592; Sigma), and anti‐tubulin (T5168; Sigma).


### Fluorescence microscopy
  Cells were grown in Edinburgh minimal medium (EMM) at 30°C or incubated overnight at 25°C in a pombe minimal glutamate medium plate containing the antibiotic G418. The cells were then suspended in EMM and mounted on glass slides for microscopic observation. Live‐cell imaging was performed as previously described. 56  Images were acquired using a DeltaVision Personal microscope system (Cytiva) by collecting 10 optical sections along the z‐axis at 0.3 μm intervals. All the images were analyzed using SoftWoRx software (Cytiva).


### YTH assays
  YTH assays were performed using the Matchmaker Yeast Two‐Hybrid System (Clontech). Coding sequences for swi6 + and epe1 +, along with sequences for deletion or amino acid substitution mutants of each gene, were cloned into YTH vectors (pGADT7 and pGBKT7). These plasmids were then introduced into two hybrid strains (AH109 and Y187) to detect protein–protein interactions.


### Immunoblotting
  Harvested cells (approximately 2 × 107) were washed once with ice‐cold water, resuspended in 75 μL of alkaline 2‐ME solution (1.85 N NaOH and 1.07 M 2‐mercaptoethanol), and incubated on ice for 5 min. The cell suspension was mixed with 75 μL of 50% trichloroacetic acid and further incubated on ice for 5 min. Cellular proteins were precipitated by centrifugation at 21880 × g for 10 min, and the protein pellets obtained were resuspended in sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) sample buffer, followed by boiling at 95°C for 5 min. Proteins were separated using SDS‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) and analyzed by western blotting.


### Immunoprecipitation
  Cells were grown at 30°C in yeast extract with adenine (YEA) medium until the cell density reached 1 × 107 cells/mL. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, washed once with ice‐cold STOP buffer (150 mM NaCl, 10 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid [EDTA], 1 mM NaN3, and 50 mM NaF), and transferred to a 2 mL tube. Cells were lysed with Nonidet P‐40 (NP‐40) lysis buffer (50 mM HEPES/NaOH [pH 7.9], 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% NP‐40, and 0.1 mM Na3VO4) containing protease inhibitors (Complete™; Roche) and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) and 1 mM dithiothreitol and disrupted with a Multibeads Shocker (Yasui Kikai). Cell lysates were clarified by centrifugation for 20 min at 4°C, and incubated with antibodies for 2 h at 4°C. After incubation, antibody‐bound proteins were adsorbed onto 25 μL Protein A‐conjugated magnetic beads (Dynabeads; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 1 h at 4°C. The beads were washed twice with NP‐40 lysis buffer and resuspended in NP‐40 lysis buffer and SDS sample buffer mixture, followed by boiling at 95°C for 5 min. Proteins were separated by SDS‐PAGE and analyzed by western blotting.


### Production of recombinant protein
  Recombinant 6His‐tagged Swi6‐CSD proteins used in isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) experiments were expressed in Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) and purified using TALON metal affinity resin (Clontech) according to the instructions provided by the manufacturer. Purified proteins were treated with HRV‐3C (1 U/20 μg substrate) to cleave the 6His‐tag. The resulting proteins were further purified by gel filtration chromatography.


### ITC
  ITC experiments were performed using a MicroCal PEAQ‐ITC calorimeter (Malvern Panalytical) at 293 K. All the proteins were dissolved in ITC buffer (10 mM HEPES [pH 7.5], 150 mM NaCl, and 0.25 mM TCEP). Typical titrations comprised injecting 2 μL aliquots of ligand (Epe1‐FVI‐L: QSNFIGVVISTNHNIIKKCQESQIQTDK; FVI‐S: SNFIGVVISTNH) into the protein sample (Swi6‐CSD) at 3‐min intervals, ensuring that the titration peak returned to baseline. The data were analyzed with Microcal PEAQ‐ITC analysis software using a one‐site model. For each interaction, at least three independent titration experiments were performed to show the averaged dissociation constants with mean standard deviation.


### Silencing assays
  Silencing assays were performed using unsaturated cultures grown in yeast extract with adenine (YEA) medium. Cells carrying a silencing marker (otr1R::ade6 + or mat3M::ura4 +) were grown, collected by centrifugation, and resuspended in water. Serial dilutions (10‐fold) were prepared and spotted onto YE medium (for the strains carrying otr1R::ade6 +) or nonselective medium (YEA), minimal medium lacking uracil (‐Ura), or minimal medium containing 5‐fluoroorotic acid (5‐FOA) (for the strains carrying mat3M::ura4 +). Plates were then incubated at 30°C for 2–5 days.


### ChIP and quantitative PCR
  For all ChIP experiments, cells were grown at 30°C in YEA medium until the cell density reached 1 × 107 cells/mL. The cells were then incubated for 1 h at 18°C and fixed with 1% formaldehyde for 30 min at 18°C. The fixed cells were harvested by centrifugation, washed twice with ice‐cold phosphate‐buffered saline, and transferred to a 1.5 mL tube. Antibodies were preincubated with 20 μL Protein A‐conjugated magnetic beads (Dynabeads; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were lysed using ChIP lysis buffer (50 mM HEPES‐KOH [pH 7.5], 140 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton‐X, and 0.5% sodium deoxycholate) containing protease inhibitors (Complete™; Roche) and 1 mM PMSF, and disrupted using a Multibeads shocker (Yasui Kikai). Chromatin was sheared by sonication using a Bioruptor (Cosmobio) and incubated with antibody‐bound magnetic beads overnight at 4°C. The beads were subsequently washed twice with ChIP lysis buffer, twice with ChIP lysis buffer containing 0.5 M NaCl, twice with wash buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl [pH 8.0], 250 mM LiCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP‐40, and 0.1% sodium deoxycholate), and twice with TE buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl [pH 8.0] and 1 mM EDTA). The precipitated chromatin was incubated with RNase A, eluted from the beads by incubation with TES buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl [pH 8.0], 10 mM EDTA, and 1% SDS), and reverse cross‐linked by incubation at 65°C overnight. DNA samples were purified using NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Clean‐up (Macherey‐Nagel). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using the Luna qPCR master mix (NEB) and a real‐time PCR machine (StepOnePlus™; ABI). The primers used for qPCR are listed in Table S2.


### RT‐qPCR analysis
  Total RNA was extracted from cells as described previously. 34  RNA samples from each strain were preincubated with RNase‐free DNase I (0.4 U/μg RNA; TaKaRa) to remove any contaminated genomic DNA. cDNA was synthesized using the Luna One Step RT‐qPCR Kit (NEB) and a real‐time PCR machine. The primers used in RT‐qPCR are listed in Table S2.


## RESULTS

### Dimerized Swi6‐CSD is required for interaction with Epe1
  Previous studies have shown that Epe1‐GFP colocalizes with Swi6 and that its heterochromatic localization is disrupted in swi6 mutant cells. 43 ,  44  To investigate the molecular details of the Swi6‐Epe1 interaction, we first verified previous observations by generating S. pombe cells expressing Epe1 fused to EGFP at its C‐terminus (Epe1‐EGFP) and Swi6 fused to Kusabira orange at its N‐terminus (KOR‐Swi6). 57  As expected, Epe1‐EGFP formed multiple nuclear foci, which overlapped with the KOR‐Swi6 signals (Figure S1B). These Epe1‐EGFP foci disappeared in swi6∆ cells, confirming that Epe1 localizes to the heterochromatic loci in a Swi6‐dependent manner.

  Next, we performed a yeast two‐hybrid (YTH) assay to identify the domain(s) of Swi6 responsible for its interaction with Epe1 (Figures S1C,D and S2A). Consistent with previous findings, 45  full‐length Swi6 was associated with Epe1 in the YTH assay (Figure S1D). This assay also revealed that Swi6‐CSD alone was capable of associating with Epe1, whereas Swi6 lacking CSD (ΔCSD) or mutant Swi6 (Swi6L315E) defective in CSD dimer formation 35  failed to associate with Epe1 (Figure S1D). To confirm the importance of the dimerized configuration of Swi6‐CSD in Epe1 recruitment, we examined Epe1‐EGFP localization in cells expressing mutant Swi6L315E (Figure S1E). 35  Consistent with the YTH assay (Figure S1D), nuclear Epe1‐EGFP foci detected in wild‐type (swi6 +) cells disappeared in Swi6L315E‐expressing cells (Figure S1E). These results suggest that Swi6‐CSD is necessary and sufficient for the interaction of Swi6 with Epe1 and that the dimerized configuration of Swi6‐CSD is critical for this interaction. The importance of the Swi6‐CSD and its dimerized configuration for the interaction between Swi6 and Epe1 was also confirmed in a previous study by in vitro binding assays using recombinant proteins. 58


### Epe1 interacts with Swi6‐CSD via a unique FVI motif
  We examined which region(s) of Epe1 were responsible for its interaction with Swi6 using the YTH assay. A series of C‐ or N‐terminally deleted mutant Epe1 constructs were generated and tested for their ability to interact with Swi6 (Figure S3). Mutant Epe1 constructs containing residues 1–577 were able to interact with Swi6, while constructs with further C‐terminal deletion were not. Similarly, mutant Epe1 constructs containing residues 566–948 interacted with Swi6, but constructs with further N‐terminal deletion did not. These results indicate that residues 566–577 of Epe1 are critical for its interaction with Swi6 (Figure 1A). Although the residues (SNFIGVVISTNH) contain several hydrophobic amino acids, they do not fit the canonical PxVxL motif. In this study, based on the mutational analysis described below, this Epe1 sequence was referred to as the FVI motif.

  To confirm the importance of these residues in the Epe1‐Swi6 interaction, we created constructs to express a mutant Epe1 lacking residues 566–577 (Epe1∆FVI) and tested its interaction with Swi6 in the YTH assay (Figure S2B). As shown in Figure 1B, Epe1∆FVI failed to interact with Swi6. Next, we generated a strain expressing EGFP‐fused Epe1∆FVI (Epe1∆FVI‐EGFP) and confirmed that mutant Epe1 failed to localize to heterochromatic regions (Figure 1C), showing a pattern similar to that of Epe1‐EGFP in swi6Δ cells (Figure S1B). We also generated strains expressing FLAG‐tagged wild‐type Epe1 or mutant Epe1∆FVI and examined their interaction with Swi6 by coimmunoprecipitation assay. We found that Epe1‐FLAG was efficiently coimmunoprecipitated with Swi6, whereas the ∆FVI mutation significantly decreased the amount of immunoprecipitated Epe1 (Figure 1D). We next sought to analyze the effect of the ∆FVI mutation on the silencing function of Epe1. As observed previously, epe1Δ induced heterogeneous expression of the pericentromeric marker otr1R::ade6 + (Figure 1E), resulting in a mixture of red and white colonies on adenine‐limited medium (Figure 1F). Similarly, cells expressing Epe1∆FVI‐EGFP showed a heterogeneous pattern of otr1R::ade6 + expression (Figure 1F). Collectively, these results indicate that the FVI motif of Epe1 is required for heterochromatic localization and its function in vivo.


### Hydrophobic residues within the FVI motif are essential for targeting heterochromatic regions
  The YTH assay and mutational analysis revealed the importance of the FVI motif in its interaction with Swi6. Among the residues in this motif, F568 and V571 are strictly conserved in closely related fission yeast species, whereas V572 and I573 are also conserved but replaced by similar bulky hydrophobic residues in some fission yeast species (Figure 2A, highlighted in red). Notably, residues located adjacent to this motif (N578–K592) are conserved across fission yeast species and are predicted to form an α‐helix (Figure 2A, highlighted in blue), 59  suggesting a functional involvement of this C‐terminal region with the FVI motif.

  To investigate the role of hydrophobic residues in the FVI motif and the C‐terminal region in the interaction with Swi6, we selected three regions: FVI‐long (FVI‐L: Q565–K592), FVI‐half‐long (FVI‐HL: Q565–Q587), and FVI‐short (FVI‐S: Q565–H577). We performed a nuclear localization signal (NLS)‐EGFP tethering assay where each of these regions was expressed as fusion proteins containing NLS and EGFP (Figure 2B). Their cellular localization was analyzed by fluorescence microscopy. While control NLS‐EGFP alone localized predominantly to the nuclei with weak cytoplasmic signals, NLS‐EGFP fused to each of the three FVI peptides (L, HL, and S) localized to the nuclear foci (Figures 2C and S3A). The localization patterns of the three FVI peptides were similar to those of Epe1‐EGFP or KOR‐Swi6 (Figure S1B), although the nucleoplasmic signals were slightly higher in cells expressing FVI‐S compared to those expressing FVI‐HL or FVI‐L (Figures 2C and S4A). The nuclear foci were not detected when the fusion proteins were expressed in swi6∆ cells (Figure 2C). These results suggest that although the C‐terminal predicted α‐helix region may play a cooperative role in the interaction with Swi6, FVI‐S is sufficient for recruiting NLS‐EGFP to the heterochromatic regions.

  Using the NLS‐EGFP tethering assay, we further validated the importance of conserved residues in and around the FVI region. In the canonical PxVxL motif, a central valine and hydrophobic residues at the −2/+2 positions are crucial for interaction with the HP1‐CSD dimer. 22  Thus, we selected FVI‐S and FVI‐HL and introduced a series of amino acid substitutions, replacing bulky hydrophobic residues with aspartic acid (D), and performed the tethering assay with the resulting constructs. For FVI‐HL, V571D and I573D clearly abolished the heterochromatic localization of NLS‐EGFP, whereas F568D, I569D, and V572D increased nuclear signals but maintained some degree of heterochromatic localization (Figures 2D and S4B). In contrast, I579D or I580D in the C‐terminal region did not affect the heterochromatic localization of NLS‐EGFP (Figures 2D and S4B).

  We introduced the same set of amino acid substitutions into FVI‐S and performed the tethering assay (Figures 2E and S4C). As observed for FVI‐HL, V571D and I573D completely abolished the heterochromatic localization of NLS‐EGFP. Notably, F568D, I569D, and V572D also abolished the heterochromatic localization of NLS‐EGFP. These results suggest that the FVI motif is essential for recruiting NLS‐EGFP to the heterochromatic regions, and that the C‐terminal predicted α‐helix region plays a cooperative role in stabilizing the interaction between the FVI motif and Swi6. Among the hydrophobic residues in the FVI motif, V571 and I573 are crucial for the heterochromatic localization of NLS‐EGFP, while F568, I569, and V572 also play critical roles in its heterochromatic localization, though their effects are partially suppressed by the presence of the C‐terminal region (Figure 2F).

  To further investigate the crucial role of the Epe1 FVI motif and its C‐terminal region, we performed isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC). Although we did not detect a clear interaction between the Epe1‐FVI‐S peptide and Swi6‐CSD, the Epe1‐FVI‐L peptide was bound by Swi6‐CSD with a dissociation constant (K  D ) of 55.9 ± 42.2 nM (Figure S5). The reason for the lack of binding of Swi6‐CSD to the Epe1‐FVI‐S peptide is currently unclear; these results further support the idea that the C‐terminal predicted α‐helix region is crucial for a stable interaction between the Epe1 FVI motif and Swi6‐CSD.


### Structural simulation of interaction between Epe1 FVI motif and Swi6‐CSD dimer
  To gain further insights into the structural basis of the interaction between Epe1 and Swi6‐CSD, we predicted the structure using AlphaFold3 (Figure 3). 59  Modeling the Epe1 FVI‐S (Q565–H577) and a Swi6‐CSD dimer revealed that the five independently predicted structures aligned well, with Epe1 FVI‐S occupying the canonical peptide‐binding groove at the CSD dimerization interface (Figure 3A, symmetrical CSD dimers (A) and (B) are shown in different colors for differentiation). Compared to the interaction between the previously solved structure of the HP1‐CSD dimer and the canonical PxVxL motif, 22  V571 occupied the central position (position 0) of the symmetry axis of the CSD dimer and interacted with Y318 (A and B) and L322 (A and B) of Swi6‐CSD (Figure 3B). Similarly, I573 at position +2 was embedded in hydrophobic pockets formed by L278 (A), I279 (A), F317 (A), Y318 (A), H321 (A), L322 (B), and F324 (B) in the binding groove of Swi6‐CSD. Notably, F568 at position −3, but not I569 at position −2, was buried in hydrophobic pockets formed by L278(B), I279 (B), F317 (B), Y318 (B), H321 (B), L322 (A), and F324 (A) in the binding groove. Moreover, I569 at position −2 faced outward, opposite to the peptide‐binding groove (Figure 3B). This predicted structure suggests that the hydrophobic pockets formed by the Swi6 dimers are bicircularly symmetrical, whereas the Epe1 FVI motif occupies the binding groove asymmetrically. Furthermore, this structure explains the essential roles of V571 and I573 in the NLS‐EGFP targeting assay (Figure 2). Compared to the binding mode of the canonical PxVxL motif with HP1‐CSD, deposition of F568 at position −3 is unusual. It is likely that G570 at position −1 provides flexibility to the peptide chain, allowing F568 at position −3 to interact with the hydrophobic pocket. Although the neighboring I569 is not conserved among closely related fission yeast species, it may assist in positioning F568 within the hydrophobic pocket.

  To further investigate the role of the loosely conserved C‐terminal region, we predicted its structure using Epe1 FVI‐HL (Q565–Q587) or FVI‐L (Q565–K592) and a Swi6‐CSD dimer. The C‐terminal region of both FVI‐HL and FVI‐L was predicted to form an α‐helical structure. When FVI‐HL was used, the five independently predicted structures did not converge (Figure S6A). However, when FVI‐L was used, the five independently predicted structures converged well (Figure S6B), suggesting that the C‐terminal region helps stabilize the interaction between the FVI motif and the peptide‐binding groove. The effect of the F568D or I569D amino acid substitution may be mitigated by the interactions involving the C‐terminal region.


### Mutant Mit1 containing the Epe1 FVI motif is recruited to the heterochromatic regions independently of Chp2
  In the fission yeast S. pombe, two HP1 family proteins, Swi6 and Chp2, play distinct roles in recruiting transacting factors. Mit1, the remodeling component of the SHREC complex, is recruited to heterochromatic regions by Chp2, another HP1 protein in fission yeast. 48 ,  49  Mit1 interacts with the Chp2‐CSD dimer via its N‐terminal region, which contains a unique CkIvV motif and CDL domain (Figures 4A and S1A). 50  To clarify the biological significance of the expression of multiple HP1 proteins in S. pombe cells and their recruitment of specific interacting factors, we examined whether the Chp2‐Mit1 axis could be replaced by introducing the Swi6‐interacting motif.

  Based on the solved structure of the N‐terminus of Mit1 and the Chp2‐CSD dimer, the CkIvV motif is located adjacent to an α‐helix and a linker that connects the CDL domain, all of which contribute to the interaction with the Chp2‐CSD dimer (Figure 5A). Considering that the Epe1‐FVI motif and the adjacent α‐helix region contribute to its interaction with the Swi6‐CSD dimer, we replaced the CkIvV motif and part of the adjacent α‐helix region of Mit1 with Epe1‐FVI‐HL, creating the chimeric protein Mit1Epe1‐FVI (Figure 4A). We first confirmed that the expression level of Mit1Epe1‐FVI‐13Myc was comparable to that of Mit1‐13Myc (Figure S7A, B), then examined its effect on the silencing of mat3M::ura4 + (Figure 4B). Cells that effectively repressed mat3M::ura4 + grew well on a medium containing 5‐fluoroorotic acid (5‐FOA) but poorly on a medium lacking uracil (Figure 4C,D). The silenced status of mat3M::ura4 + was further confirmed by reverse transcription‐quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR) analysis (Figure 4E). As observed in previous studies, deletion of chp2 + alleviated mat3M::ura4 + silencing, 8 ,  41  while deletion of swi6 + had a more pronounced effect on silencing (Figure 4C,E). The I11R mutation in the Mit1 CkIvV motif, which affects the Mit1‐Chp2 interaction, resulted in a silencing defect similar to that observed in chp2∆ cells (Figure 4C,E). Notably, cells expressing Mit1Epe1‐FVI maintained silencing at levels comparable to those expressing wild‐type (WT) Mit1, suggesting that Mit1Epe1‐FVI functioned effectively at the mating type region (Figure 4C,E). Moreover, cells expressing Mit1Epe1‐FVI maintained mat3M::ura4 + silencing even in the absence of chp2 +, whereas silencing was abolished by swi6 + deletion, similar to that in cells expressing WT Mit1 (Figure 4D,E). These results suggest that Mit1Epe1‐FVI is recruited to heterochromatic regions via Swi6, independently of Chp2.

  To investigate the mechanisms underlying the recruitment of Mit1 or Mit1Epe1‐FVI to heterochromatic regions, we performed a chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay (Figure 4F). In WT cells, Mit1‐13Myc localized to the mat3M::ura4 + locus, and this localization was almost entirely absent in chp2∆ cells. Mit1‐13Myc localization was also reduced in swi6∆ cells, likely due to Chp2 mislocalization in these cells, as previously reported. 35  Mit1Epe1‐FVI‐13Myc was also localized to the mat3M::ura4 + locus, although at lower levels compared to Mit1‐13Myc. The reduced enrichment of Mit1Epe1‐FVI compared to that of WT Mit1 may reflect competition between Mit1Epe1‐FVI and other Swi6 interactors, including Epe1, as they target the same Swi6‐CSD. Consistent with the silencing assay, Mit1Epe1‐FVI‐13Myc localization was maintained and slightly increased in chp2∆ cells, likely due to increased Swi6 levels competing with Chp2 for binding to the common substrate, H3K9me, at heterochromatic regions. However, Mit1Epe1‐FVI‐13Myc localization was almost completely lost in swi6∆ cells. These results suggest that Mit1Epe1‐FVI localizes to heterochromatic regions via Swi6, independently of Chp2, and that the function of Chp2 can be compensated by artificially introducing the Mit1‐Swi6 interacting module (Figure 5).


## DISCUSSION
  In this study, we focused on the interaction mechanism between Epe1, a JmjC domain‐containing protein involved in H3K9 demethylation, and Swi6, a member of the HP1 family in fission yeast, S. pombe. Our analysis revealed the manner in which Epe1 interacts with Swi6 and provided insights into how the HP1 family proteins function with distinct interacting partners.

  Although Swi6 has been shown to interact with several proteins involved in heterochromatin assembly, 35 ,  42 ,  49 ,  60 ,  61  detailed mechanisms governing how Swi6 interacts with each partner, and how this interaction differs from that between Chp2 and Mit1 are not completely understood. In this study, we demonstrated that Epe1 localization to heterochromatic regions depends on Swi6 (Figure S1B) and that a FVI motif within Epe1 is required for this interaction (Figure 1). Recently, another group identified the same region in Epe1 as responsible for Swi6 binding by structural simulation and silencing assay. 62  In this study, we further dissected this motif using an NLS‐EGFP tethering assay, and showed that the Epe1‐FVI motif is sufficient to target NLS‐EGFP to heterochromatin regions (Figure 2C) and that evolutionarily conserved hydrophobic residues within the FVI motif are indispensable for this interaction (Figure 2D–F). Structural simulation analyses revealed that the side chains of three hydrophobic residues (F568, V571, and I573) in the FVI motif are predicted to be located within hydrophobic pockets of the Swi6‐CSD dimer, with the adjacent predicted α‐helix likely stabilizing the interaction. Finally, we showed that Mit1Epe1‐FVI was recruited to heterochromatic regions in a Swi6‐dependent manner, and that cells expressing Mit1Epe1‐FVI maintained heterochromatic gene silencing even in the absence of chp2 +. These results suggest that the artificially introduced Mit1‐Swi6 interaction bypasses in vivo functions of Chp2 (Figure 5).

  The Epe1 FVI motif, identified as essential for binding to the Swi6‐CSD dimer, deviates from the canonical PxVxL motif shared by many other HP1‐binding proteins, and differs from the CkIvV motif of Mit1, which is essential for binding to Chp2. For the canonical PxVxL motif and CkIvV motif of Mit1, hydrophobic amino acid side chains at the central (position 0) and positions −2 and + 2 were found to be buried within the hydrophobic pockets of the CSD dimer. However, in the Epe1‐FVI motif, F568 at position −3 was predicted to be buried in one of the hydrophobic pockets. Importantly, F568 at position −3 is highly conserved among related fission yeast species, whereas I569 at position −2 is replaced by Ser in these species (Figure 2A), supporting the idea that F568 at position −3, but not I569 at position −2, is recognized by the Swi6‐CSD dimer. While the predicted structure needs to be verified using X‐ray crystallography or nuclear magnetic resonance, the contribution of the position −3 residue to binding could expand the range of recognized HP1‐binding motifs.

  Previous studies showed that the CSDs of all three human HP1 isoforms interact with the PxxVxL motif of histone H3 with similar affinities; however, detailed structural analysis revealed that the proline at position −3 is not accommodated within the hydrophobic pocket formed by the CSD dimer, 63  suggesting a different binding mode from that of the Epe1‐FVI motif and the Swi6‐CSD dimer. A recent study that investigated interaction between fission yeast Swi2 (a factor involved in mating‐type switching) and Swi6 identified a region in Swi2 (V270–P277) essential for Swi6 binding. 42  Notably, the Swi6 interacting region of Swi2 contains residues FDAVVI (271–276), which is highly similar to the central region of the Epe1‐FVI motif (FIGVVI). Structural simulation analysis using the identified Swi6‐binding region in Swi2 revealed that residues F271, V274, and I276 are accommodated in the hydrophobic pockets formed by the Swi6‐CSD dimer (Figure S8) in a manner analogous to the Epe1‐FVI motif and Swi6 dimer (Figure 3). These results suggest that FxxVVI is a common motif present in Swi6 interactors in fission yeast.

  This study also highlighted the contribution of the region downstream of the Epe1‐FVI motif, predicted to form an α‐helix, in stabilizing the binding of Epe1‐FVI motif to the Swi6‐CSD dimerization groove. Although the binding modes of Epe1 and Swi6‐CSD differ from those of the Mit1 N‐terminus with the Chp2‐CSD dimer, both interactions share the feature that the region adjacent to the FVI motif plays an auxiliary role. The auxiliary role of the region adjacent to the FVI motif has also been highlighted in recent structural modeling studies. 62  The contribution of adjacent regions toward binding HP1‐CSDs may be a conserved feature in other HP1‐binding proteins.

  Despite the presence of multiple HP1 isoforms in many organisms, the mechanism by which each isoform has distinct or overlapping functions remains unclear. In this study, expression of chimeric Mit1, wherein a part of the N‐terminal region of Mit1 was replaced with the Epe1‐FVI motif and the adjacent region, bypassed the Chp2 function almost completely. These observations suggest that Chp2 may have evolved as an HP1 isoform, specifically dedicated to recruiting Mit1 and SHREC to H3K9me‐enriched heterochromatic regions. The ancestral solo HP1 might have functioned to recruit a variety of interactors via a shared CSD dimerization groove. Therefore, it is interesting to speculate on the benefits of increasing the number of HP1 isoforms, each preferentially recruiting specific factors to the heterochromatin region. If a single HP1 recruited multiple interacting factors, regulating their balance would have been critical, as heterochromatin assembly is thought to be controlled by the interplay of cooperating or counteracting enzymatic activities. Because such HP1 interactors would compete for the same binding groove on the HP1‐CSD dimer, controlling their balance can be modulated by altering their binding specificity to the CSD dimer or by increasing the cellular dosage of a specific interactor. Another way to modulate the balance and/or prioritize interactors is to increase the number of HP1 isoforms, using one or more to recruit specific interactors. Chp2 binds more tightly to H3K9me than Swi6, 35  thereby ensuring that sufficient Mit1 is recruited in preference to other Swi6 interactors. This dynamic balance between the HP1 proteins and their interactors appears relevant in this case. When Mit1Epe1‐FVI is expressed, its level at heterochromatic regions is lower than that of WT Mit1, which is recruited by Chp2, its natural partner (Figure 4F). Alternatively, the increased level of Mit1Epe1‐FVI upon Chp2 depletion may be due to the increased amount of Swi6 at the heterochromatic regions (Figure 4F). Another important relationship between HP1 and its interacting factors might be that HP1 itself regulates the quantitative balance of these interacting factors. The intracellular level of Mit1 increases with the addition of the Epe1‐FVI motif, whereas the amount of Mit1Epe1‐FVI decreases with the loss of Swi6 (Figure S7C,D). The quantitative balance between HP1 isoforms may also regulate the cellular levels of each interactor, which in turn regulates the dynamics of higher order chromatin structures. Analysis of the binding modes of HP1 isoforms and their interactors will help to further elucidate the distinct and/or overlapping functions of these isoforms.
