# Fine-Tuning of ATF4 DNA Binding Activity by a Secondary
Basic Motif Unique to the ATF-X Subfamily of bZip Transcription
Factors


## Abstract
  

  The fine-tuning of transcription factor DNA-binding activity is often governed by transient intramolecular interactions between the transactivation domain and the DNA-binding domain. An example of such interaction is found in the transcription factor ATF4, a central regulator of the Integrated Stress Response. In ATF4, dynamic coupling between the transactivation domain and the basic-leucine zipper (bZip) domain modulates the phosphorylation levels of the disordered transactivation domain by casein kinase 2. However, the structural and molecular basis of these interdomain interactions remains poorly understood. This study focuses on a secondary basic motif at the C-terminus of ATF4, which is shared exclusively with its closest paralogue, ATF5. Through a combination of solution NMR spectroscopy, fluorescence polarization assays, and long-timescale molecular simulations, we demonstrate that this secondary basic motif is the primary driver of interdomain coupling between the transactivation and bZip domains of ATF4. Moreover, this motif enhances ATF4′s DNA-binding specificity via interaction with the transactivation domain while also potentially facilitating rapid DNA scanning. Our findings reveal the pivotal role of a conserved motif in establishing disorder-mediated interactions that critically modulate ATF4′s DNA-binding activity.


## Introduction
  Activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4) is a critical regulator within the integrated stress response (ISR), mediating both pro-survival and pro-apoptotic pathways in response to various internal and external stressors.1−3 Under normal physiological conditions, ATF4 expression remains low due to its short half-life. However, during stress-induced ISR activation, ATF4 mRNA translation is selectively upregulated, while global translation is inhibited.4−6 Once ATF4 levels rise, it associates with coregulators and RNA Polymerase II to initiate transcription of genes essential for cellular stress adaptation (Figure 1A).7 Elevated ATF4 expression has been linked to multiple human cancers, where it plays a key role in promoting cell proliferation, stress adaptation, and metastasis.8,9 Furthermore, ATF4 contributes to resistance against proteasome inhibitors and chemotherapeutic agents, positioning it as a promising target for cancer therapy.10,11 Despite its recognized importance, significant gaps remain in understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying ATF4 activation, its interactions with partner proteins, and its recognition of DNA.

  Structurally, ATF4 consists of a long N-terminal intrinsically disordered transactivation domain (TAD) and a C-terminal, conditionally ordered basic-leucine zipper domain (bZip) (Figure 1B).3 Our recent findings reveal that these domains are coupled via long-range interactions involving the N-terminal region of the TAD.12 This interdomain coupling is crucial for the phosphorylation of the TAD by casein kinase 2 (CK2), which subsequently triggers the degradation of ATF4.12−14 Similar interdomain couplings are seen in transcription factors such as CREB,15,16 p53,17,18 and FOXO4,19 where they typically regulate DNA binding specificity.

  Human bZip transcription factors are classified into three major families: CREB-ATF, ATF-X, and CEBP (Figure S1A).20 Additional members, such as Jun and Fos, are closely related to the CREB-ATF and ATF-X families, respectively. Among these factors, ATF2 stands out as it lacks a direct evolutionary link to any of the three main bZip families (Figure S1A). Using sequence analysis, we identified a distinctive short C-terminal basic motif in two of the ATF-X family members, ATF4 and ATF5 (Figure S1B). The motif in ATF4 (residues 341–351: VRKARGKKRVP) shares 50% identity with the corresponding motif in ATF5 (residues 271–280: VYKARSQRTR), and both motifs are highly conserved across eukaryotes (Figure S2). A BLAST search revealed that this short C-terminal motif does not occur in any other human transcription factors. This observation raises interesting questions about the potential biological functions and significance of this motif. While such short secondary basic motif appears to be unique to ATF4 and ATF5, secondary basic domains have been reported in non-bZip transcription factors such as E2F-721 and p53.22 The C-terminal domain (CTD) of p53 has been shown to facilitate the search for cognate DNA sequences by enabling the rapid linear diffusion of p53 along genomic DNA.23−26 Additionally, the CTD of p53 undergoes numerous post-translational modifications, such as lysine acetylation, which modulates its DNA binding affinity.26 Notably, no such regulatory mechanism has been reported for members of the bZip family of transcription factors.

  In this study, we investigate the functional role of the secondary basic motif (named hereafter B2) of ATF4 using various biophysical techniques. Our solution NMR experiments reveal that long-range interdomain coupling between the TAD and bZip domains is largely driven by the B2 motif. Fluorescence polarization assays further show that the TAD enhances DNA-binding specificity by competing with nonspecific DNA sequences for access to the bZip domain. Remarkably, deletion of the B2 motif abolishes nonspecific DNA binding by the isolated bZip domain, suggesting that B2 plays a pivotal role in facilitating the rapid intersegmental search for DNA through weak, nonspecific interactions. These findings shed light on a unique feature of the ATF-X transcription factor family that contributes to the fine-tuning of transcriptional regulation.


## Results

### Secondary Basic Motif of ATF4 Mediates Interdomain
Coupling
  The 1H–15N HSQC spectrum collected for the isolated transactivation domain of ATF4 (ATF41–275, Figure 1C) displays homogeneous peak intensities. Despite the narrow 1H spectral width, characteristic of a largely disordered domain, the spectrum shows minimal overlap and good overall quality, allowing for near-complete assignment of backbone chemical shifts (Figure S3). Analysis of secondary chemical shifts and temperature coefficients indicates that the isolated TAD of ATF4 is predominantly disordered in solution, with the exception of a small helical motif at its N-terminal end.12 Comparison with the full-length ATF4 spectrum (ATF41–351) reveals that resonances corresponding to the bZip domain are NMR invisible (Figure S4), suggesting that the C-terminal domain undergoes conformational exchange on an intermediate time scale, leading to excessive line broadening. Chemical shift comparisons show minimal differences between the observable resonances of ATF4 and ATF41–275 (Figure S5A). Evidence of transient interactions between the TAD and bZip domains emerged from comparing individual amide peak intensities between ATF41–275 and ATF41–351 spectra (black, Figure 2A). When plotted against the protein sequence, this comparison revealed that peak attenuation primarily affects two regions at the N-terminus of the TAD, designated as R1 (residues 30–50) and R2 (residues 80–130) (Figure 2A).12

  The link between peak attenuation and interdomain interaction was further investigated through titration experiments. Titrating 15N-labeled ATF41–275 with unlabeled bZip domain (bZip351, Figure 1C) replicated the attenuation pattern observed in the peak intensity analysis (Figure 2B), suggesting that the two regions at the N-terminus of the TAD, R1 and R2, form transient long-range interactions with the bZip domain. Additional evidence for this intramolecular interaction between the TAD and bZip domains was provided by solvent paramagnetic relaxation enhancement (sPRE) experiments. In these experiments, individual amide peak intensities were monitored as a function of Gadodiamide (Omniscan) concentration, a soluble contrast agent that enhances the apparent transverse relaxation rates of nearby amide groups. Residue-specific attenuation coefficients were calculated as the normalized change in peak intensity as a function of Gadodiamide concentration (Figure S6A). While quantitative sPRE analysis is notoriously difficult to perform on largely disordered proteins like ATF4, this method allows for a qualitative assessment of sPRE attenuation profiles. The sPRE experiments performed on full-length ATF4 revealed that both the R1 and R2 regions of the TAD are partially protected from solvent, while the remainder of the transactivation domain shows stronger PRE attenuation (Figure S6B). Taken together, the peak intensity analysis, titration data, and sPRE experiments all indicate that the R1 and R2 regions at the N-terminus of the TAD are involved in transient long-range interactions with the bZip domain.

  As previously mentioned, no resonances from the bZip domain are visible in the 1H–15N spectrum of full-length ATF4 (Figure S4), indicating that the entire domain undergoes conformational exchange, resulting in significant line broadening.12 Consequently, it is challenging to pinpoint which specific regions of the bZip domain are involved in long-range interactions with the TAD domain. To address this limitation, we designed a construct lacking the C-terminal basic motif B2 (ATF41–341, Figure 1C) and compared its spectral properties with those of full-length ATF4 (Figures S7 and S5B) and the isolated TAD domain (Figures S8 and S5C). The amide resonances of ATF41–341 show minimal chemical shift deviations (less than 0.05 ppm) from those observed in the full-length ATF4 or ATF41–275 spectra (Figure S5B,C), suggesting that deletion of the B2 motif does not cause any major conformational rearrangement in the transactivation domain on a fast time scale. However, comparison of individual amide crosspeak intensities revealed significant differences. Notably, resonances corresponding to the R1 and R2 regions exhibit reduced line broadening in the ATF41–341 spectrum, with peak intensities comparable to those observed in the isolated TAD domain (cyan, Figure 2A). The reduced peak broadening was further confirmed by titrating 15N-labeled ATF41–275 with unlabeled bZip341 (Figure 2C). These findings suggest that the C-terminal B2 motif plays a critical role in mediating the interaction between the TAD and bZip domains of ATF4.


### Isolated bZip Domain of ATF4 Is Predominantly Disordered and
Undergoes Extensive Conformational Exchange
  Our previous work demonstrated that the transactivation domain of ATF4 is largely disordered in solution, with modest helical propensities (∼15%) localized in the R1 region.12 Disordered regions can engage in interactions through diverse mechanisms, including induced fit,27 conformational selection,28 or mixed models.29,30 To investigate the molecular basis of the long-range coupling between the TAD and bZip domains of ATF4, we expressed and purified the isolated bZip domain for detailed analysis using solution NMR. 1H–15N NMR spectra of the isolated bZip domain revealed that less than 50% of the expected cross-peaks were detectable, both in the absence and presence of dsDNA containing the cognate CRE motif (Figure 3A). This observation suggests that, in both conditions, residues of the bZip domain undergo significant conformational exchange on the intermediate time scale leading to extensive line broadening. Partial backbone assignments indicated that the observable resonances correspond primarily to residues in the DB region and in the linker connecting the DB and LZ regions (highlighted in gray in Figure 3A, upper panel). Secondary 13C chemical shifts revealed minimal deviations from random coil values, with the notable exception of Q297 which shows significant negative secondary 13Cβ chemical shift (Figure S9). Collectively, these results confirm that the DNA binding domain remains predominantly disordered in the absence of DNA. Attempts to fully assign the bZip domain in the presence of DNA using triple resonance experiments were unsuccessful, due to sample instability and persistent line broadening.

  Analysis of the bZip domain’s conformational dynamics in the absence of DNA revealed that it samples a broad range of dynamical states. For example, three distinct amide resonances of comparable intensity were assigned to the C-terminal glycine residue, G346 (Figure 3B, black spectrum). ZZ-exchange experiments further provided evidence of slow exchange processes, as minor peaks corresponding to the three sets of G346 amide resonances emerged (Figure 3B, blue spectrum). This suggests that G346, and likely a significant portion of the B2 motif, participates in an exchange between at least three conformational states. Overall, these results indicate that the bZip domain is predominantly disordered in the absence of DNA and experiences conformational exchange over a broad time scale, spanning from intermediate (μs) to slow (μs-ms) relative to NMR detection.


### All-Atom MD Simulations of the Isolated bZip
Domain
  To complement the NMR study, we conducted a series of independent all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of the isolated bZip domain. MD simulations is a powerful technique that provides complementary, atomistic resolution, insight on short time scales (ns-μs). The simulations were performed here with the force field AMBER99SB-disp, specifically parametrized for describing the behavior of partially disordered proteins in solution.31 Starting from a fully helical conformation extracted from the ATF4-c/EBP heterodimer (PDB ID 1CI6), four independent all-atom MD simulations of the bZip domain were carried out, resulting in a total production time of 8 μs. Principal component analysis (PCA) in the ϕ, φ dihedral space show an apparent convergence of the simulations within 1.2 μs (Figure S10A). Secondary structure analysis using DSSP, however, indicated unique unfolding trajectories in each simulation, leading to partially disordered conformations. These conformations retain residual helical motifs, predominantly localized to either the N-terminus or C-terminus of the bZip domain (Figure S10B).

  To further explore the partially disordered conformational ensemble populated by the bZip domain, two additional simulations were performed, each initiated from a representative conformation. This extended the total simulation time to 10 μs. PCA of the ϕ, φ dihedral space revealed a complex conformational landscape with multiple populated basins (Figure S11). Cluster analysis of the most populated conformations identified a predominantly disordered structural ensemble with intermittent helical segments, mainly localized within the LZ region (Figure S11). DSSP analysis across the 10 μs of simulation confirmed that the LZ region exhibits a higher propensity for helicity than the DB region (Figure 3C). The linker region between the two domains (residues 302–305) displayed a notable helical propensity (∼60% for E304), suggesting a potential role in stabilizing or mediating folding of the DB region under specific conditions. In contrast, the C-terminal B2 motif is predicted to remain largely disordered throughout the simulations.

  Overall, the combination of NMR data and MD simulations portrays the isolated bZip domain of ATF4 as largely disordered in solution. The simulations confirm the presence of transient helical motifs, particularly in the LZ region, while the DB region and B2 motif remain predominantly disordered. Despite the disordered nature of the B2 motif, NMR data show the presence of slow exchange among at least 3 different conformations, which is not captured by the 10 μs MD simulations.


### Secondary Basic Motif B2 Modulates the DNA
Binding Activity
of ATF4
  Fluorescence polarization experiments were conducted using full-length ATF4 and a series of truncated variants to identify the molecular determinants of DNA binding affinity and specificity (Figure 4A). We measured changes in fluorescence as a function of protein concentration using 5′-HEX labeled double-stranded DNA motifs containing either the canonical CRE sequence (5′-TGACGTCA), which is the primary DNA binding target of ATF4, or a randomized sequence with the same nucleotide composition, referred to as non-CRE (5′-CAGTACTG) (Figure 4B). Comparison of results for ATF41–351 (black, Figure 4A) and the bZip351 (green, Figure 4A) reveals that the isolated bZip domain has a higher affinity for CRE dsDNA than the full-length ATF4, suggesting that the presence of the TAD negatively impacts the bZip domain’s ability to bind DNA (Table 1). However, when fluorescence polarization was performed with dsDNA containing the non-CRE sequence, a significant reduction in binding affinity was observed for the full-length ATF4 construct (gray, Figure 4A), while the isolated bZip domain displays similar binding affinities for CRE and non-CRE motifs (cyan, Figure 4A) (Table 1). These findings suggest that, similar to other bZip transcription factors like CREB, the TAD of ATF4 enhances DNA binding specificity by competing with nonspecific DNA sequences for access to the basic region of the bZip domain. Importantly, the nonspecific binding of the isolated bZip domain to noncognate DNA sequences is abolished upon deletion of the C-terminal basic motif B2 (blue and purple, Figure 4A). This indicates that the B2 motif plays a crucial role in nonspecific protein–DNA interactions.


## Discussion
  Misregulation of transcription factors is implicated in a wide range of human diseases, including cancers, autoimmune conditions, neurological disorders, diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases.32−36 Despite their critical role in disease pathways, transcription factors are often labeled as an “undruggable” class of proteins.37,38 Historically, this has been attributed to two main factors: the extensive overlap in their interaction networks—where multiple transcription factors may bind the same partners—and their intrinsically disordered regions, which challenge conventional drug discovery strategies. Recent therapeutic efforts have sought to address these issues through various approaches, such as (i) disrupting key interactions with binding partners,39 (ii) targeting structurally defined regulatory domains,40 and (iii) inhibiting transcription factor-DNA complex formation.41

  ATF4, a member of the predominantly disordered bZip family of transcription factors, serves as a valuable model for understanding how disordered regions mediate interactions, offering insights into potential new drug targeting strategies.1−3 Our previous research demonstrated that the transactivation domain (TAD) of ATF4 engages in long-range, transient interactions with its bZip domain, a coupling that modulates the accessibility of the TAD to casein kinase 2 (CK2).12 This regulatory interplay is thought to be a common feature in many transcription factors, such as CREB,15,16 p53,17,18 and FOX4,19 where long-range interactions influence the binding affinity of their DNA-binding domains for specific promoter regions. These interactions are further fine-tuned by post-translational modifications, notably phosphorylation events within the TAD.

  In this study, we aimed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms behind the transient coupling between the TAD and bZip domains in ATF4 and to assess the functional implications of these interactions. Our NMR data, including amide peak intensity analysis and titration experiments (Figure 2), reveal that a short secondary basic motif located at the C-terminus of the bZip domain, which we refer to as B2 (Figure 1B), plays a key role in mediating the interaction between the TAD and bZip domains. The highly basic composition of the B2 motif (VRKARGKKRVP) likely drives its interaction with the acidic N-terminal regions of the TAD (R1 and R2), which we have identified as the primary binding interfaces (Figures 2 and S5). To our knowledge, the presence of a secondary basic motif like B2 has not been reported in other members of the bZip family of transcription factors. Interestingly, the B2 motif appears to be unique to ATF4 and its closely related paralog, ATF5, both part of the ATF-X subfamily (Figure S1). The presence of this motif in both proteins suggests a potential sub-family-specific regulatory feature that warrants further investigation. Structural characterization of the bZip domain was challenging due to significant line broadening in the NMR spectra, indicative of conformational exchange dynamics occurring both in the absence and presence of DNA (Figure 3A). Despite these limitations, partial backbone assignments and relaxation analysis suggest that the isolated bZip domain is largely disordered in solution and exhibits conformational exchange over a wide range of time scales. Specifically, fast exchange dynamics dominate within the DNA binding region, while slower exchange is observed in the B2 motif (Figure 3B). These findings are consistent with prior studies on GCN4, the yeast homologue of ATF4, where Palmer and colleagues identified a similar gradient of exchange dynamics, with faster motion in the DNA binding region and slower motion in the leucine zipper region.42,43 All-atom MD simulations of ATF4 isolated bZip domain further support the experimental data, indicating that the DNA binding region is predominantly disordered, while the leucine zipper region retains partial helical structure. In contrast, the B2 motif is consistently disordered throughout the simulations (Figure 3C).

  Secondary basic regions have been observed in other transcription factor families. A well-known example is p53 CTD, a secondary basic domain that has been extensively characterized both structurally and functionally.22−26 The CTD plays a crucial role in facilitating p53′s rapid linear diffusion along DNA via transient, nonspecific protein–DNA interactions. This mechanism accelerates the search for the specific DNA motifs that are recognized by p53′s primary DNA-binding domain. Our fluorescence polarization and NMR data indicate a comparable function for the B2 motif in ATF4, which interacts broadly with both CRE and non-CRE dsDNA motifs (Figure 4). These findings suggest that the B2 motif in ATF4 serves two distinct functional roles. First, by mediating the long-range coupling between the TAD and bZip domains, the B2 motif enhances the binding specificity of the primary DNA-binding domain for its cognate DNA sequences. Second, by engaging in nonspecific DNA binding, the B2 motif likely facilitates rapid linear diffusion of ATF4 along DNA, akin to the mechanism described for p53. However, it remains unclear whether this involvement in nonspecific DNA binding occurs via direct interaction or through an allosteric effect involving the DB region.


## Conclusions
  This study sheds light on the complex regulatory mechanisms that govern the DNA-binding activity of ATF4, emphasizing the critical role played by the C-terminal secondary basic motif. This motif appears to be a unique evolutionary feature of the ATF-X subfamily of bZip transcription factors that contributes to the fine-tuning of transcriptional regulation.


## Materials and Methods

### Sample Preparation
  ATF4 and ATF41–275 samples were prepared as recently described.12 Genomic sequences corresponding to ATF41–341, bZip351, and bZip341 were amplified by PCR and inserted to pGEX-4T1 using EcoRI and XhoI restriction sites. TEV protease cut site was introduced between N-terminal glutathione S-transferase (GST) tag and the sequence of interest. A single point mutation (C310A) was introduced by PCR mutagenesis into bZip351 and bZip341 sequences to prevent aggregation at high sample concentration. The constructs were transformed into Escherichia coli protein expression strain BL21 (DE3) and protein expression was induced at an OD600 of 0.8 with 0.4 mM IPTG at 25 °C overnight (16 °C in the case of ATF41–341). Cells were harvested by centrifuging at 6000 rpm and resuspended in lysing buffer (50 mM CHES pH 9.0, 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM DTT, 1 mM EDTA) in the presence of PMSF, Leupeptin, DNase I, and Halt Protease Inhibitor Cocktails (Thermo Scientific). Cells were then lysed by sonicating at 4 °C. Purification was initiated by passing the supernatant through Glutathione-Sepharose beads (UBPBio). Unbound proteins and contaminated DNA/RNA were washed with 50 mM CHES pH 9.0, 500 mM NaCl, 5 mM DTT, and 1 mM EDTA buffer solution. Cleaved GST-tag and ATF4341 were separated by passing through Glutathione-Sepharose beads while cleaved GST-tag and bZip constructs (bZip351 and bZip341) were separated by gel filtration chromatography using Hi-Load 16/600 Superdex 200 pg (GE Healthcare) on AKTA pure in a 20 mM MES pH 6.0, 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA buffer solution. All purification steps were carried out at 4 °C.

  Complementary oligonucleotides containing the cognate CRE motif (5′-CACTCTGACGTCACACTC-3′) or a randomized sequence of identical composition (non-CRE: 5′-CACTCCAGTACTGCACTC–3′) with 5′-HEX labeling were purchased from Integrated DNA Technology. The lyophilized oligonucleotides were resuspended in water to a concentration of 10 μM and stored at −20 °C. To prepare the final samples, equal volumes of complementary oligonucleotides were mixed and pelleted using a vacuum concentrator. The oligonucleotides were resuspended in 20 mM HEPES pH 7.2, 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EDTA and at 95 °C for 10 min before cooling down to 25 °C over 4 h using a thermal cycler.


### Solution NMR Spectroscopy
  NMR spectra were collected at ISU Biomolecular NMR Facility on Bruker 700 and 800 MHz spectrometers, equipped with z-shielded gradient triple resonance 5 mm TCI cryoprobes. NMR samples (either 1H/15N or 1H/15N/13C labeled) were prepared in 50 mM potassium phosphate pH 6.0, 25 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EDTA, and 90% H2O/10% D2O (v/v). 2D 1H–15N TROSY-HSQC experiments for peak intensity analysis and titration experiments were recorded at 293 K with a time domain matrix consisting of 100* (t1, 15N) × 1024* (t2, 1H) complex points with acquisition time of 104 ms (t1) and 121 ms (t2) using 1.5 s interscan delay. Spectral widths for 1H and 15N dimensions were set to 15.9 and 28.2 ppm respectively, with carriers set at 4.821 ppm (1H) and 119.138 ppm (15N). For sPRE experiments, 15N ATF4 were prepared at ∼80 μM in 80 mM sodium potassium pH 6.0, 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EDTA, 10 mM DTT, 5% D2O with increase amounts of Gadodiamide (0, 200, 400, 600, 800, or 1000 μM), respectively. NMR spectra were acquired at 293 K at a 1H frequency of 700 MHz. A TROSY pulse scheme was used with 128* (t1, 15N) × 1024* (t2, 1H), 16 scans, and 1.5 s interscan delay.

  Sequential 1H/15N/13C backbone assignment of the bZip341 was achieved using conventional 3D triple resonance correlation experiments [HNCO, HNCA, HNCACB, HN(CO)CA, and HN(COCA)CB] collected at 700 MHz. Secondary 13C chemical shifts were calculated using Poulsen’s random coil database with corrections for pH, temperature, and neighboring amino acids.44,45 The weighted 1H–15N chemical shift differences between individual amide resonances were calculated as followwhere γN and γH represent the gyromagnetic ratio of 15N and 1H nuclei, respectively. Titration experiments were carried with independent samples of uniformly 15N labeled ATF41–275 at 80 μM with 1:0, 1:1, 1:2 mol equiv of unlabeled isolated bZip351 or bZip341. All spectra were processed using NMRPipe46 and displayed with SPARKY.47 The 1H–15N ZZ-exchange spectra were collected at 800 MHz using a TROSY variation of the 15N magnetization exchange pulse sequence provided by Bruker (tretexf3gpsi). The spectra were recorded at 293 K with 128* (t1, 15N) × 1024* (t2, 1H) complex points and a 1.5 s interscan delay.


### Molecular
Dynamics Simulations
  All-atom MD simulations were performed for the isolated bZip domain (residues 276–351) using the Pronto and Nova clusters at the High-Performance Computing facility at Iowa State University. These simulations were conducted with GROMACS.2022.2 using the AMBER99SP-disp force field.31 The systems were solvated in a triclinic simulation box with TIP4P water molecules. Bonds to hydrogen were constrained via the LINCS algorithm.48 Short-range electrostatic and Lennard-Jones interactions were calculated with a plain coulomb cutoff of 1.0 nm. The Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) scheme with grid spacing of 0.16 nm was utilized for long-range electrostatic interactions.49 Solvent and solute were separately coupled to a modified Berendsen thermostat (velocity rescale) with a reference temperature of 300 K and Parinello-Rahman barostat with a reference pressure of 1 bar. After energy minimization and a 1 ns equilibration, simulations were carried out using the leapfrog integration method with 2 fs timesteps. Analysis of trajectories was done using mdtraj library and in-house developed python scripts. The frequency contact maps were calculated using a 7-Å cutoff for backbone Ca–Ca distances.


### Fluorescence Polarization
  Fluorescein-labeled DNA (20 nM) was mixed with varying concentrations of protein sample in triplicate in a black 96-well plate (BRANDplates pureGrade 96-Well Microplates) and shake at RT for 30 min. The excitation and emission wavelengths were 520/25 nm and 560/15 nm, respectively. FP experiments were conducted at the W.M. Keck Metabolomics Research Laboratory at ISU using a Synergy 2 Multi-Mode Microplate Reader controlled by BioTek Gen5 software. Samples of ATF4, bZip351, and bZip341 were prepared at concentrations varying from 5 nM to 3.5 μM in 20 mM HEPES pH 7.2, 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EDTA, 5 mM DTT, containing 20 nM HEX-labeled dsDNA containing either the cognate CRE motif or a randomized sequence of identical composition (non-CRE). Five μM of isolated GST was added to each well to homogenize the viscosity of the solution. Data were fitted with SigmaPlot 14.5 using a one site saturation model (for ATF4 and bZip341) or one site saturation + nonspecific model (for bZip351).
