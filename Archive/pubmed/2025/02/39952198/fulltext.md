# ROS regulates circadian rhythms by modulating Ezh2 interactions with clock proteins


## Abstract
  Redox imbalance induced by the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) accelerates age-related processes, often accompanied by a decrease in circadian rhythm amplitude. However, the underlying mechanisms by which ROS modulate circadian rhythms remain poorly understood. In this study, we found that ROS disrupt circadian rhythms in both zebrafish, as indicated by changes in diurnal behavior and clock gene expression, and in a human cell model. Using weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) and machine learning approaches (RF, LASSO, SVM), EZH2 was identified as a key gene involved in regulating circadian rhythms under oxidative stress conditions. To further investigate the role of EZH2, we employed ezh2−/− mutants, Morpholino injection, and overexpression treatment and discovered that EZH2 is crucial in mediating the effect of ROS on circadian rhythms. Furthermore, EZH2 interacts with the CLOCK-BMAL1 complex to regulate the transcription of clock genes, as demonstrated through co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP), chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP), and dual-luciferase reporter assays. Our study revealed that ROS disrupt circadian rhythms by regulating the interaction between EZH2 and the CLOCK-BMAL1 complex, shedding light on the molecular mechanisms of circadian rhythm disruption under oxidative stress and suggesting potential targets for age-related and circadian disorders.


## Introduction
  The circadian clock is an internal time perception system that enables organisms to adapt to the day‒night cycle and maintain a stable 24-hour rhythm [[1], [2], [3]]. The central oscillator that influences the circadian clock is formed by CLOCK and BMAL, a heterodimeric transcription factor complex. This complex is a fundamental component of transcription‒translation feedback loops (TTFLs), which promote the transcription and expression of Period (PER1, PER2, and PER3) and Cryptochrome (CRY1 and CRY2) by binding to regulatory elements containing E-box sequences, thereby establishing positive feedback regulation [4,5]. The proteins encoded by the PER and CRY genes further combine to form the Per/Cry complex, which inhibits the activity of CLOCK and BMAL, potentially achieving negative feedback regulation [6]. The circadian clock allows the organism to adapt to the day‒night cycle, coordinating various physiological, behavioral, metabolic and immune functions. As aging progresses, the circadian clock undergoes amplitude weakening and phase shifting, which impairs its ability to maintain normal physiological functions.

  Environmental stressors disrupt circadian rhythms by affecting HPA axis signalling, temperature sensing, light transduction and RoRα activation to impair metabolism, cardiovascular health, sleep and aging [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. Extensive research has demonstrated the interaction between the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), the master clock, and the HPA axis [11,12]. Maternal stress during pregnancy elevates glucocorticoid levels to disrupt the synchronization between the fetal suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) and the maternal circadian system, which in turn affects offspring circadian rhythms [13]. Cortisol is widely regarded as a critical mediator of stress-induced regulation of biological rhythms, and the role of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which are closely linked to glucocorticoid levels and act as primary mediators of oxidative stress, in circadian rhythm regulation remains underexplored. Previous studies have demonstrated that ROS can influence the proliferation of mouse embryonic fibroblast (MEF) cells, induce phase shifts, and alter rhythmicity in vitro [14]. Exposure to hyperoxic conditions elevates ROS levels in mouse lung fibroblasts (MLg), leading to phase shifts in the expression of core clock genes such as Rev-erbα and Bmal1, alongside attenuated amplitudes of Per2 and Rev-erbβ [15]. Notably, critical oxidative stress (cOS) in mouse embryonic fibroblasts (NIH3T3) induced by near-lethal doses of ROS resets the circadian clock while simultaneously eliciting protective cellular survival responses [16]. Furthermore, our previous research revealed that the Clock1a protein binds to the E-box region of the promoter of the oxidative stress target gene nfe212a, establishing a molecular link between ROS and circadian regulation. Thus, oxidative stress mediated by ROS may play a pivotal role in circadian regulation [17]. Most studies to date have focused on cellular models, while the effects of ROS on in vivo behavioral rhythms and specific regulatory mechanisms, particularly in diurnal animals, remain largely unexplored.

  Enhancer of zeste homologue 2 (EZH2) is a key member of the polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2), which functions primarily by trimethylating histone H3 at lysine 27 (H3K27me3) and plays a critical role in various biological processes, such as hematopoiesis, cancer, and lipid synthesis and metabolism [[18], [19], [20], [21]]. EZH2 is associated with intracellular ROS levels, and mutual crosstalk between EZH2 and ROS forms a positive and negative feedback mechanism to help maintain the cellular redox balance. This bidirectional regulation is crucial for maintaining the dynamic balance of various biological processes [22]. Multiple studies have shown that downregulation of EZH2 gene expression in senescent cells leads to the onset of related diseases. Notably, EZH2 can be directly regulated by the circadian clock through binding to the E-box and RoRE motif [23,24]. Although EZH2 has been reported to regulate oxidative stress, the mechanisms by which ROS regulate the circadian rhythm and whether EZH2 is involved remain unclear.

  This study comprehensively elucidates the mechanisms by which ROS regulate the circadian rhythm at the organismal, cellular, and molecular levels, using zebrafish and U2OS cells as research models and in vivo behavioral monitoring to explore their role. We further validated that EZH2 is a key factor in the ROS-mediated regulation of the circadian rhythm via methods such as WGCNA, high-throughput sequencing, Co-IP, and ChIP. These findings provide new insights for future research into the treatment of diseases related to oxidative stress and circadian rhythm disorders.


## Results

### ROS induce changes in the behavioral rhythms of zebrafish
  To determine the optimal concentration of H₂O₂ for inducing oxidative stress in zebrafish, we established a concentration gradient of H₂O₂ ranging from 0.5 mM to 4 mM and assessed its physiological effects on zebrafish embryos, including survival curves, body length, spontaneous tail-beating movements, and heart rate. The results indicated that none of the concentrations affected the body length of the zebrafish larvae (Fig. 1A), but 4 mM H₂O₂ reduced the survival rate of the larvae (Fig. 1B) and decreased the spontaneous tail-beating frequency within 1 min (Fig. 1C).   Next, we evaluated the expression of the nrf2 gene, a key marker of oxidative stress, following exposure to various concentrations of H₂O₂ and treatment with DPI, an inhibitor of ROS production. The results revealed that 2 mM H₂O₂ significantly upregulated nrf2 expression, confirming its effective induction of oxidative stress, whereas DPI treatment significantly reduced nrf2 levels (Fig. 1E). Treatment with 2 mM H₂O₂ significantly increased ROS levels and expanded their distribution in zebrafish, whereas DPI treatment reduced ROS levels and confined their distribution, as observed via DCFH-DA dye (Fig. 1F–G). Overall, 2 mM H₂O₂ is the optimal concentration for inducing oxidative stress and increasing ROS levels in zebrafish without significantly impacting their growth and development.

  To determine whether ROS directly affect zebrafish behavioural rhythms, we performed behavioural analysis on zebrafish larvae treated with H₂O₂ and DPI under dark-dark (DD) and light-dark (LD) conditions. Under LD conditions, modifying ROS levels, either by upregulation or downregulation, had no significant effect on zebrafish activity during the day or night (Fig. 2A–C). We assessed the proportion of time zebrafish spent in three activity states—resting, short-distance movement, and long-distance movement. The results also revealed that ROS treatment increased the duration of long-distance movement, both during the day and at night, as determined by assessing the proportion of time zebrafish spent in the resting state, short-distance movement, and long-distance movement (Fig. 2D).   To quantitatively analyse the changes in behavioral rhythms, we performed polynomial fitting on the zebrafish diurnal behavior curves (Fig. 2F). The results revealed that elevated ROS levels significantly shortened the movement period, decreased the amplitude, and did not significantly affect the phase (Fig. 2G–J). Behavioral pattern analysis revealed that elevated ROS levels had no significant effect on the proportion of time spent resting but increased the duration of long-distance movement while reducing the time spent on short-distance movement (Fig. 2K). Conversely, these effects were reversed when the ROS levels decreased. These data suggest that changes in ROS levels significantly alter the rhythmic behavior of zebrafish.


### ROS induce dysregulation of circadian gene expression in zebrafish

JTK-Cycle analysis of gene expression rhythms revealed that elevated ROS weakened the amplitude and shortened the period of circadian genes (Fig. 3A–D), whereas decreased ROS levels typically caused the opposite effect (Fig. 3E–H). ### ROS levels affect circadian rhythms in U2OS cells
  To investigate whether the relationship between ROS and circadian rhythms is conserved in mammals, we examined the effects of H₂O₂ and DPI treatments in U2OS cells. A concentration of 200 μM H₂O₂ was used to induce oxidative stress in cells, as described in previous studies [25]. The results showed that, similar to the findings in zebrafish, H₂O₂ treatment significantly increased ROS levels in U2OS cells, as observed through DCFH-DA dye (Fig. 4A and B). Neither the 200 μM H₂O₂ nor DPI treatment had a significant effect on cell viability according to the results of the CCK-8 assay (Fig. 4C). We subsequently synchronized the circadian rhythm of U2OS cells via dexamethasone and assessed the rhythmic oscillation of core clock genes at ZT1 and ZT15 following ROS and DPI treatment. The results revealed that elevated ROS levels reduced the expression of core clock genes, whereas decreased ROS levels increased their expression (Fig. 4D). Additionally, we measured the protein levels of CLOCK and BMAL1 at these two time points and found that the changes in their protein levels were consistent with the trends in gene expression (Fig. 4E–G). Furthermore, we assessed the impact of ROS on clock gene expression in the U2OS::PER2-dLuc and U2OS::BMAL1-dLuc cell lines. The results revealed that elevated ROS levels resulted in an extended period length, reduced amplitude, and a phase shift of BMAL1 and PER2 in U2OS cells, whereas reduced ROS levels induced the opposite effects (Fig. 4H–O). These results suggest that ROS significantly regulate circadian rhythms in mammalian cells, similar to their effects in zebrafish.Fig. 4ROS levels affect circadian rhythms in U2OS cells. ### Machine learning analysis reveals EZH2 as a key gene in the oxidative stress-related rhythm
  To further investigate the mechanistic link between ROS and the circadian clock, we analysed four relevant datasets from the GEO database: GSE72991, GSE104684, GSE126057, and GSE164742. These datasets examine the effects of H₂O₂ across various human tissues and cell types. We downloaded the TPM-format data from these datasets and applied batch effect correction via the ComBat package to standardize and eliminate batch effects, ensuring the consistency and comparability of the data (Fig. 5A). After normalization, principal component analysis (PCA) revealed no clear separation between the datasets, but notable differences were observed between the experimental groups (Figs. S1A and S1B). We then performed differential expression analysis via the limma package and identified 628 differentially expressed genes, including 355 downregulated genes and 273 upregulated genes (Fig. 5B). A heatmap of these DEGs was created to visualize the relationships between the samples and genes (Fig. 5C). KEGG and GO enrichment analyses revealed significant upregulation of the p53, circadian rhythm, and MAPK signalling pathways, whereas the DNA replication and T-cell signalling pathways were notably downregulated (Fig. 5D). Additionally, biological processes related to oxidative stress and cell development were upregulated, whereas phase transcription processes linked to the cell cycle tended to be downregulated (Fig. 5E). For the GSEA, we used the human circadian rhythm-related gene set extracted from the Misdb database as the background gene set. (B–C) Volcano plots and heatmaps displaying the distribution of upregulated and downregulated genes in the dataset. (D–E) KEGG and GO enrichment analysis results of differentially expressed genes (DEGs). (F) GSEA enrichment analysis using the Misdb circadian gene set as the background.   To further explore the mechanisms through which ROS regulate circadian rhythms, we performed weighted gene coexpression network analysis (WGCNA) and sample clustering, ultimately retaining 35 samples for further analysis (Fig. S2A). Using the "WGCNA" package, we constructed a gene coexpression network and assessed the network topology at different soft threshold values. The best power value (β) was found to be 5 (Fig. S2B), whereas at a threshold of 30, the connectivity approached zero (Fig. S2C). Therefore, we selected a soft threshold of 5 for subsequent analysis. On the basis of these two phenotypes, we divided the genes into 18 modules, with the black module showing the strongest correlation with H₂O₂ treatment (Fig. 5G). We then intersected the genes in the ME black module with the DEGs, yielding 291 overlapping genes (Fig. 5H), which were used as the background for further machine learning analysis. We employed three machine learning methods—Random Forest, Lasso, and Support Vector Machine—splitting the dataset into training and testing sets and assessing model performance via ROC curve analysis (Fig. 5I). Additionally, we compared the residual cumulative distributions of the models, and the results demonstrated that all three models displayed good predictive performance (Fig. 5J). Finally, we extracted the gene importance scores from all three models, revealing that these genes are likely closely involved in the molecular mechanisms of H₂O₂ treatment, with the EZH2 gene showing high stability and importance across all the models (Fig. 5K). We further examined the expression levels of EZH2 in both the Ctrl and H₂O₂-treated groups and found that EZH2 expression was significantly lower in the H₂O₂-treated group than in the control group (Fig. 5L). Next, we performed RT‒qPCR to assess EZH2 expression at different time points in U2OS cells treated with H₂O₂ and DPI. H₂O₂ treatment caused rhythmic downregulation of EZH2 expression, whereas DPI treatment led to its rhythmic upregulation (Fig. S3A). These findings suggest that EZH2 is likely involved in the molecular mechanisms regulated by H₂O₂ and that its expression patterns play a role in modulating the circadian rhythm.


### Zebrafish transcriptome validation of machine learning findings on the ROS-regulated circadian rhythm
  To further validate the machine learning findings in zebrafish, we performed transcriptome sequencing in zebrafish after ROS treatment. We compared the expression patterns of the zebrafish sequencing data with those from the GEO datasets by obtaining homologous gene sets for both humans and zebrafish from the NCBI database. We then randomly divided the Ctrl and H₂O₂ treatment groups from the GEO datasets into two subgroups, calculated the mean TPM values for each subgroup, and determined the Pearson correlation coefficients between these groups and the homologous genes in the zebrafish data. The results revealed a strong correlation between the GEO and zebrafish datasets within the same groups, indicating similar expression patterns (Fig. 6A). Next, we performed differential expression analysis on the zebrafish data via the DESeq2 package and identified 665 differentially expressed genes, including 292 downregulated and 373 upregulated genes (Fig. 6B). KEGG and GO enrichment analyses revealed that ROS regulate pathways such as circadian entrainment, TNF, and MAPK pathways and significantly alter biological processes related to the oxidative stress response and cellular reactions to oxidative stress (Fig. 6C and D). To investigate changes in circadian rhythm, we performed GSEA enrichment analysis using the zebrafish circadian rhythm gene set from the Misdb database as the background. The results revealed upregulation of the circadian rhythm pathway and downregulation of the biological process of positive regulation of the circadian rhythm, which was consistent with the GEO dataset findings (Fig. 6E). The expression heatmaps of circadian rhythm-related genes revealed that Ezh2 tended to be downregulated in the zebrafish transcriptome (Fig. 6F). RT‒qPCR analysis of the ezh2 gene in zebrafish samples confirmed that under both DD and LD conditions, the H₂O₂ treatment group displayed rhythmic downregulation of ezh2, whereas DPI treatment had the opposite effect (Fig. 6G‒H). In conclusion, the transcriptomic data revealed a similar expression pattern between H₂O₂-treated zebrafish and the GEO dataset. ### Overexpression of EZH2 reversed the effect of ROS on the circadian clock

Through luciferase reporter assays, we monitored bioluminescence levels in the U2OS::BMAL1-dLuc and U2OS::PER2-dLuc cell lines. The overexpression of EZH2 significantly increased the rhythmic expression of the BMAL1 and PER2 genes, whereas H₂O₂ treatment attenuated the effects of EZH2 overexpression by shortening the period, increasing the amplitude, and causing phase shifts (Fig. 9G–N). ### ROS weaken the ability of EZH2 binding to CLOCK-BMAL proteins to promote clock gene expression

## Discussion
  Oxidative stress is the result of redox imbalance and the accumulation of ROS, which are closely linked to aging and disease progression [[26], [27], [28]]. External environmental stress stimulates oxidative stress in vivo, which is interconnected with the circadian system through the hypothalamic‒pituitary‒adrenal (HPA) axis [[29], [30], [31]]. The circadian clock system in vivo controls their adaptation to environmental changes and plays a crucial role in maintaining redox balance and responding to physiological activities [32]. Compared with previous studies on the interaction between the circadian clock and oxidative regulation in mice [33], there has been limited research on the mechanism by which oxidative stress mediates EZH2 regulation of the circadian rhythm. This study elucidates the disruptive effects of oxidative stress on the zebrafish circadian rhythm and validates the regulatory role of the ezh2 gene in zebrafish behavioral rhythms. Using co-IP and ChIP, we demonstrated that the ezh2 gene interacts with clock proteins and participates in the ROS-mediated regulation of the zebrafish circadian clock.

  Our study revealed that oxidative stress leads to reduced clock gene amplitude and phase changes in zebrafish, which is consistent with previous research showing that H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress affects phase propagation in mouse MEFs, causing phase shifts [14]. High levels of H₂O₂ disrupt the autonomous coupling mechanisms between the three biological oscillators (circadian clock, cell cycle, and redox rhythm), consequently impairing the progression of the cell cycle [34]. Ageing is associated with phase shifts and amplitude reductions in circadian rhythms [[35], [36], [37]]. Our results indicated that elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels reduce the amplitude of circadian clock genes, which may be a consequence of oxidative stress-induced aging. Compared with previous studies, we not only validated the effect of ROS on the circadian rhythm at the gene level but also modulated ROS levels in vivo via various drugs and performed periodic analysis of zebrafish behavioral rhythms. Both increased and decreased ROS levels led to alterations in the amplitude, period, and phase of the circadian rhythm. Further analysis of the behavioral data revealed that under LD conditions, changes in ROS levels and downregulation of the ezh2 gene resulted in zebrafish larvae spending more time being active (increasing both long-distance and short-distance movement). Interestingly, under DD conditions, elevated ROS levels decreased the total movement distance of zebrafish larvae but increased the proportion of time spent on long-distance movement, whereas decreased ROS levels showed the opposite trend. We interpret this phenomenon as oxidative stress causing zebrafish to exhibit more aimless and disorganized movement, whereas reduced ROS leads to more purposeful and clear activity. For the first time, we reported the effects of ROS on the behavioral rhythm and behavior patterns of live animals.

  EZH2 is a histone methyltransferase that catalyzes H3K27 trimethylation and is a core subunit of the polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2) [38]. In previous studies, ezh2 was regarded primarily as a key enzyme involved in histone modification. In recent years, however, its nonmethylation role has been increasingly recognized. It participates in regulating the cytoskeleton, cell migration, and DNA damage repair and replication, as well as in modulating signalling pathways through binding to phosphorylated p38 [[39], [40], [41], [42]]. In addition, we identified evidence of its involvement in the ROS-mediated regulation of the circadian rhythm. Specifically, in the zebrafish model, H₂O₂ treatment led to downregulation of EZH2 in multiple transcripts and rhythmic downregulation of ezh2 in the zebrafish model. Previous studies have shown that elevated ROS levels in a diabetic mouse model result in the upregulation of several microRNAs that suppress EZH2 transcription, indirectly inhibiting the protective effect of the antioxidant thioredoxin (Trx), which exacerbates diabetic pathology [43]. These findings support our finding that increased ROS levels lead to oxidative stress, resulting in oxidative damage and downregulation of EZH2. Previous studies have shown that endogenous EZH2 participates in the circadian clock mechanism by methylating circadian rhythm promoters [44]. To investigate whether the downregulation of EZH2 induced by oxidative stress is linked to the circadian clock, we found that both ezh2 knockout (ezh2−/−) mutant zebrafish and zebrafish larvae in which ezh2 was knocked down by morpholino exhibited disrupted movement rhythms and circadian behavior, indicating a pattern similar to the downregulation of ezh2 induced by H₂O₂. The overexpression of EZH2 upregulated these changes and reversed the effects of H₂O₂ on circadian rhythms. Additionally, Ezh2 promotes circadian rhythms by binding to core clock proteins and regulating clock genes through the E-box. This effect is diminished upon the addition of H₂O₂, which directly demonstrates that EZH2 is involved in the H₂O₂-mediated regulation of circadian rhythms. Furthermore, previous studies have shown that this binding interaction is independent of the methylation activity of EZH2, which also validates our findings regarding the relationship between EZH2 and the circadian rhythm [45].

  In conclusion, our study demonstrated that increased ROS levels due to oxidative stress lead to disruptions in the circadian rhythm of zebrafish, accompanied by a downregulation of ezh2. EZH2 interacts with the CLOCK-BMAL protein and regulates clock genes through the E-box of per1b. The downregulation of EZH2 impairs this interaction, thereby affecting the zebrafish circadian mechanism. This binding interaction is suppressed by the oxidative stress-induced downregulation of EZH2 (Fig. 11). Through behavioral monitoring and analysis of activity patterns, we linked behavioral rhythms with the underlying circadian clock regulatory mechanism. Oxidative stress is a major hallmark of aging and is associated with a reduction in circadian rhythm amplitude. Our results also revealed that amplitude reduction is one of the consequences of oxidative stress. This study offers new insights into the regulatory mechanisms by which oxidative stress-induced aging affects the circadian clock.Fig. 11Diagram of the mechanism by which ROS regulates circadian rhythms by regulating the interaction of Ezh2 with clock proteins.Fig. 11


## Materials and methods

### Animals
  Embryos were derived from wild-type (WT) and ezh2−/− mutant strains obtained through natural mating at 28.5 °C. After collection, the embryos were transferred to E3 embryo medium and maintained at a constant temperature of 28.5 °C in Hank's solution at a pH of 6.5–7.5 [46]. Zebrafish larvae were reared under a 14-h light/10-h dark cycle (LD conditions, with light starting at 8:30 a.m. and dark at 10:30 p.m., ZT0 = light) and under constant darkness (DD conditions) until different developmental stages.


### Cell lines
  Human osteosarcoma U2OS cells were used for RT‒qPCR, Western blot (WB) analysis, and overexpression experiments. The cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5 % CO2, and only cells from passages 3–7 were used in the experiments.


### Bacterial strains
  Escherichia coli DH5α cells (Invitrogen, USA) were used for plasmid cloning experiments. Briefly, the cloned plasmids were transferred into DH5α cells and cultured overnight on LB agar plates containing ampicillin (50 μg/mL) to select for successful colonies (Servicebio, China). The positive colonies were then transferred to LB liquid media without ampicillin and cultured overnight at 37 °C to amplify the plasmid.


### Plasmid construction
  The cDNA of zebrafish ezh2 was amplified via primers ezh2-His-F and ezh2-His-R and then cloned and inserted into the pCDNA3.1/His vector containing XhoI and BamHI restriction sites. The cDNA was also amplified via primers ezh2-HA-F and ezh2-HA-R and subsequently cloned and inserted into the pCDNA3.1/HA vector containing the BamHI and XhoI restriction sites.


### Cell culture and transfection
  Mature U2OS cells were seeded in 3.5 cm culture dishes and cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10 % FBS. Once the cell density reached 90 %, the cells were passaged and transfected with 2.5 μg of plasmid DNA. Transfections were carried out via standard protocols, and experiments were performed 24 h post-transfection.


### Dual luciferase assay
  U2OS cells were plated in 3.5 cm dishes and cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10 % FBS. When the cells reached 40–50 % confluence, they were synchronized by treatment with 10 μM/L dexamethasone for 2 h. After synchronization, the culture medium was replaced with DMEM (without phenol red, supplemented with 10 % FBS), and the cells were treated with drugs. After the treatment period, dual-luciferase substrate was added, and bioluminescence was monitored in real time via the Lumicycle32 system for 5 days. The data were analysed via Lumicycle Analysis software.


### Drug treatment
  A 3 % standard hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) solution was prepared and diluted to different working concentrations from a 200 mM/L stock to induce reactive oxygen species (ROS). DPI (HY-100965, MCE) was used to inhibit ROS production. At 4 days postfertilization (dpf), the zebrafish were treated for 3 h in drug-containing EM medium. U2OS cells were treated with 10 % FBS standard culture medium. After treatment, the ROS levels were assessed via the fluorescent probe DCFH-DA. The cells and zebrafish were incubated with 10 μM/L DCFH-DA in the dark for 1 h, followed by fluorescence measurement in the green channel (488 nm) via a fluorescence microscope. The fluorescence intensity and regional distribution were quantified via ImageJ.


### Co-IP and Western blotting experiments
  U2OS cells were seeded in 10 cm culture dishes, and when they reached 90 % confluence, 15 μg of bmal1b-HA or bmal1b-His, clock1a-Flag, ezh2-His, or ezh2-HA plasmids were transfected into each dish. After 48 h, the cells were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors for co-IP. Briefly, cell lysates were incubated with protein G-agarose beads conjugated with anti-HA antibodies and washed five times, and the pellet was subsequently resuspended in SDS‒PAGE sample buffer, boiled for 5 min, and subjected to electrophoresis on a 12 % SDS‒PAGE gel. After transfer, Western blotting (WB) was performed using mouse monoclonal antibodies against His, HA, and Flag to detect protein expression in zebrafish and cells. Furthermore, zebrafish embryos and U2OS cells were treated with H₂O₂ and DPI for 3 h, and proteins were extracted via RIPA buffer. The protein extracts were subjected to SDS‒PAGE and Western blotting using rabbit antibodies against CLOCK, BMAL, and EZH2.


### Quantitative real-time PCR (RT‒qPCR) analysis
  Rhythm samples from zebrafish were collected at 5 days postfertilization (dpf), with ZT0 corresponding to 9:30 a.m. Samples of 30 zebrafish larvae were collected every 4 h until 9:30 a.m. on day 6. U2OS cells were seeded in 6 cm culture dishes and treated with various drugs and transfection reagents. Total RNA was extracted via TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer's instructions, and 1 μg of RNA was treated with DNase and reverse transcribed via a reverse transcription kit. SYBR Master Mix (Veyzame) was used for quantitative PCR. The PCR conditions were 95 °C for 10 s, followed by 60 °C for 30 s, for approximately 40 cycles. The RT‒qPCR primers used are listed in the Supplementary Table 1, with three biological replicates per time point and three technical replicates per sample. The 2-ΔΔCT method was used to calculate the mRNA levels of target genes, which were normalized to β-actin mRNA levels and expressed as relative expression values compared with those of the control group [47].


### Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay
  In accordance with the manufacturer's protocol, 200 ezh2-mRNA injection, bmal1b-mRNA injection, and wild-type zebrafish larvae from the two groups were collected. The larvae were cross-linked with 2 % formaldehyde at room temperature for 30 min, followed by the addition of a 1/10 volume of 1.12 M glycine to terminate cross-linking. The samples were then washed with PBS three times, each for 10 min. The experiment was conducted according to the Millipore ChIP detection kit protocol. Purified rabbit or mouse IgG (Invitrogen) was used as a negative control. Primers located on both sides of the E-box, D-box, and RORE regions, as well as primers targeting the gene's 5′ promoter region, which is not near these regions, were used as controls in the ChIP‒PCR. The primers used for ChIP‒qPCR are listed in Supplementary Table 1.


### Morpholino microinjection
  Ezh2 translation-blocking morpholino (ezh2-MO:5′-CCGATTTCCTCCCGGTCAATC CCAT-3′) and control morpholino (cMO: 5′-CCTCTTACCTCAGTTACAATTTATA-3′) (Gene Tools) were resuspended in water to create a 4 mM stock solution and diluted to appropriate concentrations for microinjection into zebrafish embryos at the one-to two-cell stage in a solution containing 0.2 % phenol red and 0.1 M KCl.


### Monitoring the behavior of the zebrafish
  Zebrafish rhythmic activity was monitored under both DD (constant darkness) and LD (light/dark cycle) conditions via the Viewpoint system. WT/AB zebrafish treated with different drugs or ezh2−/− mutants were placed in 48-well plates, with 16 fish per group. The activity was monitored continuously for three full cycles under both LD and DD conditions. The light conditions were as follows: continuous darkness (using infrared light throughout) or normal light (from 8:30 a.m. to 10:30 p.m.). The activity was continuously recorded via an automatic video tracking system (Viewpoint), with the data analysed via Zebralab 3.11 software. The average movement distance for each group was calculated per 10-min interval. Circadian rhythms, amplitudes, and phases were analysed via publicly available BioDare2 software. The behavioral patterns of the zebrafish were assessed on the basis of data from Zebralab, including Inaduration (resting time), Smlduration (short-distance movement duration), and Larduration (long-distance movement duration). The longest duration of movement within each minute was selected as the primary movement mode for that minute. The total number of occurrences for each movement pattern was summed for each group, and the proportion of each behavior was calculated.


### Transcriptomic analysis based on deep sequencing
  Total RNA was extracted from 4 dpf zebrafish in the H₂O₂ and WT/AB groups. Four micrograms of RNA were used to construct RNA-seq libraries. The libraries were prepared via the UltraTM RNA Library Prep Kit (NEB, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. Sample indexing and clustering were performed via the TruSeq PE Cluster Kit (Illumina, USA). After clustering, sequencing was conducted on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed via relevant bioinformatics tools.


### GEO data integration and differential analysis
  Four RNA-seq datasets (GSE72991, GSE104664, GSE126750, and GSE164742) from the GEO database were integrated. The data are expressed as TPM values and were normalized via the limma package. Batch effect correction was performed via the ComBat method. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with an adjusted p value < 0.05 and LogFC >0.54 (1.5-fold change) were selected. GO, KEGG, and Reactome functional enrichment analyses were conducted via the clusterProfiler package. GSEA was performed via the Circadian Rhythm gene set from the MSigDB database with clusterProfiler:GSEA.


### Weighted gene coexpression network analysis (WGCNA)
  A weighted gene coexpression network was constructed via the WGCNA package, with a soft-thresholding power parameter of 5. Key modules and significant genes within those modules were selected on the basis of the correlation between module eigengenes and the H₂O₂ treatment group.


### Machine learning for key gene selection
  Machine learning models were constructed via the randomForest, e1071 (support vector machine, SVM), and glmnet (LASSO) packages. The data were split into 30 % training and 70 % testing sets. Model performance was evaluated via the AUC value. Gene importance scores were used to select key genes. Visualization of the results was carried out via ggplot2 and pheatmap.


### Pearson correlation coefficient calculation
  Homologous gene data between zebrafish and humans were downloaded from the NCBI website. For different treatment groups in the GEO datasets, TPM values were averaged, and the data were randomly divided into two groups. The expression profiles of homologous genes from the GEO dataset and zebrafish transcriptome sequencing data were analysed via the Pearson correlation coefficient, which was calculated via the R package stats.


### Statistics and analysis
  The experimental data were analysed via GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. The data were analysed via t tests, one-way ANOVA, or two-way ANOVA. The rhythmicity of the data was assessed via JTK_CYCLE. Differences were considered statistically significant at ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001. Bar graphs display the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). All experiments were performed in triplicate, with the sample size (n) indicated in the figure legends.
