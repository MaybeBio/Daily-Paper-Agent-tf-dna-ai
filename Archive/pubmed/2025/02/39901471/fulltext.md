# Comparative epigenetics of domestic animals: focusing on DNA accessibility and its impact on gene regulation and traits


## Importance
  Chromatin accessibility is vital for gene regulation, determining the ability of DNA-binding proteins to access the genomic regions and drive transcriptional activity, reflecting environmental changes. Although human and murine studies have advanced the understanding of chromatin dynamics, domestic animals remain comparatively underexplored despite their importance in agriculture and veterinary medicine. Investigating the accessibility of chromatin in these species is crucial for improving traits such as productivity, disease resistance, and environmental adaptation. This review assessed chromatin accessibility research in domestic animals, highlighting its significance in understanding and improving livestock traits.


## Observations
  This review outlines chromatin accessibility research in domestic animals, focusing on critical developmental processes, tissue-specific regulation, and economically significant traits. Advances in techniques, such as Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin using sequencing, have enabled detailed mapping of regulatory elements, shedding light on epigenetic regulation of traits, such as muscle development and productivity. Comparative studies have uncovered conserved and species-specific cis-regulatory elements across multiple species. These findings offer insights into regulatory mechanisms that can enhance breeding strategies and animal management. In addition, high-throughput techniques, such as single-cell analysis and deep-learning models, have advanced the study of chromatin accessibility in lesser-studied species.


## Conclusions and Relevance
  Chromatin accessibility is crucial in gene regulation in domestic animals, influencing development, immune response, and productivity. Despite the progress, more comprehensive epigenomic datasets and cross-species analytical tools are needed to harness chromatin accessibility in domestic animal research. Understanding these mechanisms has practical applications in improving livestock traits, advancing breeding programs, and developing disease-resistant animals, highlighting the importance of integrating epigenetic and genomic tools for enhancing animal health and productivity.


## INTRODUCTION
  Genomic sequencing technologies, such as RNA-seq, have been instrumental in elucidating cellular programs by profiling gene expression. On the other hand, they often fail to capture the complexity of the epigenetic landscape, which plays a crucial role in regulating gene expression, particularly during developmental processes. Hence, the shift in research focus toward epigenetics has increased. A central component of this field is chromatin accessibility, which is a key regulator of transcriptional programs that influence chromatin structure. Chromatin accessibility reflects the degree to which the chromatin structure allows DNA-binding proteins, such as transcription factors and polymerases, to access genomic DNA. This concept is essential in gene regulation because it determines the potential of genomic regions to participate in transcriptional activity. Chromatin exists in two primary states: a condensed form (heterochromatin), generally inaccessible to regulatory proteins, and a relaxed form (euchromatin), which is accessible and conducive to gene transcription. Recent advances in the field have expanded this concept, emphasizing the interplay between DNA and proteins in chromatin regulation. Researchers now explore dimensions, such as compartment permeability, global chromatin accessibility, and localized hyper-accessible sites, to understand how the epigenome modulates gene expression [1].

  In the medical field, chromatin accessibility is integral to understanding various biological processes and disease states. Aberrant chromatin accessibility is often linked to cancer, where the improper accessibility of oncogenes or tumor suppressor genes can result in uncontrolled cell growth or the evasion of apoptosis [234]. In addition, disorders related to epigenetic modifications, such as Rett syndrome and CHARGE syndrome, are associated with mutations in chromatin remodeling genes [567]. By analyzing the chromatin accessibility profiles, researchers can identify disrupted regulatory elements in these diseases, aiding in developing targeted therapies. In regenerative medicine, manipulating chromatin states enhances the efficiency of somatic cell reprogramming and stem cell differentiation, offering potential advances in tissue repair and replacement [8].

  Advanced techniques have been developed to map the accessibility of chromatin, providing insights into the regulatory architecture of the genome. Prominent methods include DNase I Hypersensitivity Assay (DNase-seq) and Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq). DNase-seq identifies regulatory regions by targeting accessible chromatin, while ATAC-seq, developed in 2013, uses a hyperactive Tn5 transposase to insert sequencing adapters into open chromatin regions [910]. (Fig. 1A) ATAC-seq has become the preferred method for mapping chromatin accessibility because of its high resolution and suitability for small samples and single-cell analysis [111213]. Fig. 1B presents the general workflow for processing ATAC-seq data [14]. Formaldehyde-Assisted Isolation of Regulatory Elements sequencing (FAIRE-seq), Micrococcal Nuclease sequencing (MNase-seq), Chromatin Immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq), and Nucleosome Occupancy and Methylome sequencing (NOMe-seq) can be used to investigate chromatin accessibility using different principles. Table 1 lists those techniques, their principle, and applications.

  Previous reviews on chromatin accessibility have focused on technical advances and guidelines, particularly in human studies. On the other hand, no comprehensive review has summarized chromatin accessibility in animals, particularly in domestic species. Understanding chromatin accessibility in domestic animals is critical for genetic regulation, with significant implications for improving traits such as productivity, disease resistance, and environmental adaptation. Consequently, many studies have focused on exploring various biological processes through chromatin accessibility (Fig. 2). Compared to humans and mice, however, the regulatory elements in livestock species are less characterized, limiting their research potential. This review explores the applications of chromatin accessibility in domestic animals, providing insights into how these epigenetic concepts can be applied to enhance livestock traits and research.


## METHODS
  A literature search across electronic databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, and Google Scholar, was conducted to identify recent studies on chromatin accessibility in domestic animals. The search strategy used a combination of keywords such as “chromatin accessibility,” “ATAC-seq,” “domestic animal,” and name of species, along with related epigenetic and transcriptional regulation keywords. Studies included in this review met the following criteria: (1) used chromatin accessibility assays, such as ATAC-seq or DNase-seq; (2) identified specific genomic regions or transcription factors involved in chromatin regulation; (3) were published in peer-reviewed journals; (4) were published within the last 10 years; (5) were written in English. This systematic approach ensured a focused literature review of chromatin accessibility in domestic animals.


## OBSERVATIONS
  This review provides a comprehensive summary of the application of chromatin accessibility studies across various domestic animals. The key findings related to the role of chromatin accessibility in major developmental stages, cellular differentiation, and economically important traits are discussed, along with insights from comparative analyses across species. In addition, the review addresses recent technological advances in chromatin accessibility assays and proposes future research directions to explore and utilize these epigenetic mechanisms.


### Comparative epigenetics in multi-species
  Recent studies have compared multiple domestic species to uncover species-wide epigenomic patterns through chromatin accessibility analysis. The Functional Annotation of Animal Genomes (FAANG) project, launched in 2019, pioneered multi-species and multi-assay genome annotation, contributing significantly to understanding the regulatory elements in cattle, goats, pigs, and chickens. Using RNA-seq, ATAC-seq, and Hi-C sequencing on liver and T cells, this project generated comprehensive functional maps that profile chromatin accessibility, transcription, and chromatin conformation across various tissues and developmental stages. This study identified numerous potential regulatory regions and revealed complex gene expression regulatory mechanisms and evolutionary conservation of proximal and distal regulatory elements through integration with RNA-seq data [15].

  Halstead et al. [16] mapped over 300,000 active regulatory elements in pigs and cattle, highlighting the importance of annotating regulatory elements within individual species to understand genome-phenome relationships. Their findings showed that chromatin accessibility is highly tissue-specific, with thousands of bovine and swine regulatory elements enriched for tissue-specific transcription factor binding motifs. Comparative analyses further showed that the conservation of regulatory elements correlates with evolutionary distance. For example, pigs and cattle share approximately 20% of accessible sites, whereas pigs and mice share only approximately 10%. Conservation is more pronounced at the promoters than intergenic regions, highlighting the rapid evolution of enhancers and the need for detailed species-specific annotations. In addition, ruminant-specific regulatory elements were identified, particularly in liver development, metabolism, and immune processes, by the integrative analyses of gene expression, chromatin accessibility, and DNA methylation across multiple species [1718].

  Novel deep-learning models have been developed for cross-species regulatory genome prediction to address the limited availability of high-quality epigenetic data in domestic animals. Tools, such as DeepSATA, have improved the accuracy of chromatin accessibility predictions across species, including pigs, chickens, cattle, humans, and mice. These models have proven effective in analyzing genetic variants associated with economically significant traits [19]. Furthermore, deep learning has facilitated the construction of enhancer models for diseases (e.g., melanoma), enabling the prediction of mutation impacts on enhancer accessibility across multiple species [20]. Ouyang et al. [21] used the integrative approach to predict the dynamics of transcription factor binding sites clustered regions using machine learning. They provided valuable insights into the epigenetic dynamics during embryonic development and species evolution.


### Cattle
  Yuan et al. [22] generated a comprehensive catalog of 976,813 cis-regulatory elements (CREs) in bovine using ATAC-seq, organizing them into 16 components. They reported that these elements correlate with gene expression and are enriched in transcription factor binding motifs, confirming their regulatory potential. Another study explored chromatin accessibility in indicine cattle, a breed common to tropical regions, identifying critical regulatory factors such as HNF4, MEF2, and SOX in liver, muscle, and hypothalamus tissues, respectively. Another study reported the dynamic regulatory landscape across tissues, with transcription factor enrichments also observed in human cell lines, validating the tissue specificity. Understanding this dynamic nature is crucial for interpreting gene regulation in response to developmental and environmental stimuli [23].

  Further research into bovine oocytes and embryos uncovered unique chromatin accessibility patterns during preimplantation development. Chromatin accessibility was initially low in oocytes and early embryos but increased significantly during major embryonic genome activation, peaking in elongating embryos [24]. In arrested 8-cell embryos, which frequently encounter developmental blocks, changes in the MAPK signaling pathway were identified, distinguishing them from successfully developing embryos [25]. In somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT) technology, research revealed aberrant chromatin accessibility in SCNT blastocysts because of the persistence of chromatin memory from donor cells, a phenomenon also observed in mouse SCNT models. This was particularly evident in the inner cell mass, where strong enrichment of H3K4me3 was observed [2627]. The findings from histone modification and chromatin states suggest that cattle may be a more accurate model for studying human preimplantation development than rodents, given the similarities in regulatory mechanisms between cattle and humans [28].

  ATAC-seq and RNA-seq have been used to identify the key genes and transcription factors involved in muscle growth in the Tianzhu White Yak. These studies reported differences in chromatin accessibility between calves and adults, with implications for optimizing muscle growth in agriculture [29]. Research on bovine myoblasts at various time points explored the changes in chromatin during cell proliferation and differentiation, modeling fetal skeletal muscle development in vitro. Transcription factors, such as ATF3, AP-1 family members, MyoG, Myf5, HLH-1, ZBTB18, TCF4, and TEAD family proteins, were identified, with Myf5, MyoD, myogenin, and MRF4 also being observed in similar mouse models [3031]. In particular, NCAPG was identified as a key regulator of chromatin accessibility, affecting muscle-specific gene expression during myogenesis [32]. Single-cell studies of bovine skeletal muscle development further identified dynamic gene expression profiles across various life stages, highlighting key subpopulations, such as adipocytes, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, lymphocytes, and myogenic precursors, and predicted transcription factors, such as MYF5, MYOD1, FOXP3, ESRRA, and ATF4, as critical for muscle development [33].

  The epigenetic differences in the immune systems of global cattle breeds were also analyzed. Powell et al. [34] examined DNA methylation and chromatin accessibility across three cattle lineages, revealing significant epigenetic divergence between taurine and indicine cattle in immune cell types. Comparative epigenomic analysis of the NF-κB response to acute inflammation showed conserved chromatin interactions across cattle, humans, and mice, particularly in regions with high NF-κB occupancy [35]. Recent single-cell sequencing studies examined the effects of lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a key mediator of chronic inflammation, on chromatin accessibility in cattle peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), aligning with the findings in humans and identifying the key transcription factors involved in immune responses, such as NFKB1, NFKB2, and RELB in monocytes [3637]. Furthermore, studies on the Bacillus Calmette–Guérin vaccine in cattle revealed increased chromatin accessibility at the promoters of innate immunity-related genes in gamma delta T cells, linking the epigenetic changes to enhanced immune responses [38].

  Chromatin accessibility studies have also been pivotal in linking the regulatory elements to economically important traits in cattle. The dynamic changes in chromatin states induced by butyrate in rumen epithelial cells were associated with significant alterations in gene expression related to key traits [39]. Other studies identified the regulatory elements critical for chondrocyte and rumen development, which are linked to growth and productivity pathways, including those involved in cell adhesion, migration, and morphogenesis [4041]. Genome-wide studies integrating other epigenetic markers, such as histone modifications and DNA methylation, showed that the most dynamic chromatin states during weaning were associated with weak enhancers and flanking active transcription start sites (TSS) in rumen epithelial tissues [42]. Finally, chromatin accessibility in diseased cattle has been investigated to identify biomarkers and improve the health traits in cattle breeding. For example, ATAC-seq analysis of the bronchial lymph nodes from dairy calves challenged with bovine respiratory syncytial virus identified the chromatin regions linked to immune response genes. These regions were frequently located in protein-coding genes and enriched for viral and pathogen recognition pathways, highlighting the increased transcription of genes involved in the innate and antiviral immune responses [43].


### Pigs
  Chromatin accessibility in pigs has been studied extensively, revealing a broad range of regulatory elements across various tissues and developmental stages. A prominent study identified 557,273 merged peaks from 20 tissues in two female pigs, significantly expanding the repository of pig regulatory elements [44]. Another study profiled chromatin accessibility and transcriptional features in newborn pig tissues, including the heart, kidney, liver, lung, skeletal muscle, and spleen, revealing tissue-specific peaks and dynamic changes during development. They also observed non-coding tissue-specific peaks involved in physical activity and the morphogenesis of neonatal tissues and characterized stage-specific peaks during postnatal liver development [45]. The concept of “chromatin hubs,” where groups of co-accessible regulatory elements interact with common transcription factors, has been applied to pigs. For example, in myoblasts, MEIS1 and its cofactor PBX1 were crucial for chromatin remodeling at a significant fraction of sites that normally open during myoblast differentiation, acting as recruitment sites for epigenetic remodeling enzymes—a role consistent with previous studies on MEIS1/PBX1 [46].

  Pigs are valuable models for studying human physiology, meat production, and xenotransplantation, highlighting the importance of research on porcine embryonic development. Epigenetic mechanisms are vital in regulating porcine embryonic development, as observed by low-input DNase-seq (liDNase-seq) studies that identified KLF4 as a critical regulator facilitating chromatin accessibility remodeling during preimplantation development [47]. Similar to cattle, studies on SCNT in pigs showed that overexpressing BRG1, which is necessary for zygotic gene activation in mouse embryos, in donor cells enhances the developmental potential of SCNT embryos by increasing the chromatin accessibility at key loci [48]. In addition, research on porcine-induced pluripotent stem cells (piPSCs), which require exogenous pluripotency factors for maintenance, unlike human or mouse iPSCs, showed that incomplete chromatin remodeling hinders pluripotent activation and the establishment of fully germline transmission-competent iPSCs [49]. OCT4, a key pluripotency transcription factor, prevents the mesoendoderm differentiation of piPSCs by repressing EOMES, consistent with findings in human iPSCs [50].

  Chromatin accessibility studies in pigs provide insights into complex traits and conservation across species. Integrating 223 epigenomic and transcriptomic datasets across 14 tissues produced the most comprehensive catalog of pig regulatory elements thus far. This shows that porcine regulatory elements are more conserved in DNA sequence under rapid and slow evolution than those under neutral evolution across pigs, mice, and humans [51]. Another study characterized CREs across 12 tissues from four pig breeds, showing higher conservation of CREs between pig and human genomes than between human and mouse genomes [52], positioning pigs as strong models for human epigenetic studies. Research on adipose tissue identified differential DNase I hypersensitive site (DHS)-associated genes between the visceral and subcutaneous adipose depots, with Krüppel-like factor family members playing vital regulatory roles. The promoter of CRYM, which regulates insulin sensitivity in human white adipose tissue, showed strong DHS in both depots. In contrast, Fra1, a key transcription factor involved in adipose differentiation and meat quality in pigs, also affects intramuscular adipose tissue in aged skeletal muscles, similar to the findings in mice [5354].

  Pigs have also been used to model human neurodegenerative diseases. Chromatin accessibility profiling of the pig hippocampus across postnatal stages identified more than 500,000 accessible chromatin regions, uncovering key transcription factors, such as POUF3 and EGR1 in neurogenesis, and RXRA and FOXO6 in oligodendrocyte differentiation. This study also analyzed human genome-wide association data, identifying neurological disease-associated cell types, such as microglia and astrocytes in Alzheimer’s disease, aligning with previous studies [5556]. Similarly, studies on the impact of Zearalenone exposure on porcine intestinal epithelial cells revealed its estrogenic toxicity; ATHB family transcription factors emerged as key regulatory factors in response to Zearalenone exposure, as confirmed by integrated RNA-seq profiling [57].

  The epigenetic regulation of skeletal muscle development in pigs has been studied extensively. ATAC-seq and RNA-seq analyses identified the key genes and regulatory regions associated with postnatal skeletal muscle development, showing that chromatin accessibility becomes increasingly complex as muscle development progresses, with EGR1 and RHOB playing critical regulatory roles. Comparative analysis with muscular dystrophy mouse models showed that the regulatory network governing skeletal muscle development in pigs mirrors that of muscular dystrophy [585960]. Studies using Omni-ATAC-seq in pig semitendinosus muscle tissue suggested that chromatin accessibility affects the fetal size, with regions related to muscle development being open in small-sized but not large-sized fetal piglets. Enriched transcription factors in size determination, such as GMEB2, TFAP2C, HOXD12, FOXH1, and CEBPE, were identified. CEBPE is upregulated after muscle injury and associated with muscle strength in humans and mice [61]. In addition, research on pig skeletal muscle satellite cells showed that the gradual loss of proliferative and differentiative abilities during in vitro cultivation is linked to the closure of numerous open chromatin regions, with the p53-p21-RB signaling pathway playing a critical regulatory role [62].

  Regarding breeding and pork quality, chromatin accessibility studies have identified functional enhancers and transcription factors that regulate essential traits. For example, STARR-seq analysis in porcine testicular and kidney epithelial cell lines identified 2,576 functional enhancers, offering valuable insights into comparative genomics and evolutionary studies. The PPP3CA gene was linked to a previously reported quantitative trait locus associated with the loin muscle area trait and identified a distal functional enhancer affecting the QKI gene, which affects alkaline phosphatase activity [63]. Comparative studies between pig breeds, such as Meishan and Duroc, revealed transcription factors, e.g., ANXA4, CBFA2T2, and TFCP2L1, associated with fertility and endometrial development. Multi-tissue analyses of eastern and western pigs identified cis-regulatory variants linked to LYZ in the small intestine and TNNC1 in the skeletal muscle, contributing to phenotypic differences, such as gastrointestinal immunity and muscle fiber composition [6465]. Using reciprocal crosses between Duroc and Lulai pigs, multi-omics characterization across four developmental stages showed that tissue specificity in DNA methylation, chromatin accessibility, and gene expression was much stronger than the developmental stage specificity. In addition, allele-specific expression was explained mainly by allele-specific methylation and chromatin accessibility [66]. Another study profiled chromatin and transcriptome differences in liver tissue between castrated and intact pigs, identifying SP1 as a transcription factor targeting the HSD3B1 gene, which is involved in a regulatory network related to androsterone metabolism—crucial for improving pork quality [67].


### Chickens
  Studies on chromatin accessibility in chickens have provided valuable insights into tissue specificity, gene regulation, and developmental processes. A comprehensive chromatin accessibility atlas for chickens, generated using ATAC-seq across 11 tissues, identified an average of 50,796 open chromatin regions per sample, covering 20.36% of the genome. These data have been instrumental in fine-mapping functional variants in birds, showing that tissue specificity is driven mainly by differences in intergenic and intronic peaks. As in other domestic animals, functional regulation in chickens involves the recruitment of sequence-specific transcription factors and the direct regulation of adjacent functional genes [68].

  Chromatin accessibility analysis in chicken embryos revealed potential CREs and transcription factor binding sites involved in asymmetric gonadal development. The genes related to morphogenesis, meiosis, and subcellular structure formation were enriched significantly, with LHX9 emerging as a key transcription factor binding to left-biased peaks of cell cycle-related genes [69]. Interestingly, LHX9 is a hallmark of the gonadal coelomic epithelium and mesenchymal cells in humans and mice, alongside GATA4 and ARX, highlighting its critical role in gonadal development [70].

  Muscle development, essential for growth and health, has been a major focus of chromatin accessibility studies in chickens. One study identified 45 differentially expressed long non-coding RNAs associated with chromatin accessibility in myofibers, including MYH1G-AS, regulated by transcription factors SMAD3 and SP2, which influence myoblast proliferation and differentiation [71]. Another study examined breast muscle and intramuscular fat across chicken breeds, showing that chromatin loops facilitate the interaction of these distal accessible regions with myogenic genes. These interactions varied significantly between fast-growing and slow-growing chickens, affecting gene expression related to intramuscular fat deposition [72]. Early muscle fiber morphology was also investigated across breeds, identifying genes, such as GOLM1, ISLR2, and TOPAZ1, as upregulated and associated with neuroimmune functions, highlighting the interactions between muscle formation and the nervous system [73].

  Chromatin accessibility has been studied in the context of the inflammatory response in chickens. ATAC-seq showed that genes, such as TLE1 and JUN, previously noted as specific transcription factors in cattle monocyte responses to LPS, regulate the key NF-κB signaling pathways during LPS stimulation. Dietary daidzein inhibited DNA topoisomerase II, reprogramming chromatin conformation during the inflammatory response [74].

  Unique phenomena in chickens have also been explored, including species-specific regulatory dynamics during limb development and the molecular mechanisms of sex reversal following aromatase inhibitor treatment. In chicken wing buds, chromatin accessibility shows stage-specific divergence, in contrast to the synchrony observed in mouse forelimb buds, revealing species-specific regulatory dynamics during limb development [7576]. Studies on Müller glia identified conserved and species-specific gene networks that regulate glial reactivity and neurogenesis in the retina, a process that varies significantly among vertebrate species. In post-hatch chicks, Müller glia can generate a limited number of neurons after injury, but this regenerative ability decreases later in life. Comparative analyses between zebrafish, chicks, and mice suggested that epigenetic mechanisms underlie these differences in regenerative capacity [77]. Single-cell sequencing further elucidated the role of S-adenosylhomocysteine hydrolase and histone methyltransferases in controlling chromatin accessibility during the reprogramming of Müller glia into progenitor cells [78].


### Dogs
  Chromatin accessibility studies in dogs have greatly enhanced the knowledge of breed-specific traits, hereditary diseases, and potential therapeutic targets. Using ATAC-seq, researchers identified 76 candidate genes and several transcription factor binding motifs that may impede somatic cell reprogramming to iPSCs in dogs. The study compared the chromatin accessibility landscapes of canine stromal and pluripotent stem cells before and after reprogramming, showing significant differences in cell type clustering and chromatin remodeling. These findings suggest that the chromatin landscape of adult stromal cells, which reflects phenotypic differentiation and somatic cell-fate stability, may hinder the efficient reprogramming of iPSCs [79].

  The BarkBase project provides comprehensive epigenomic data, including ATAC-seq and RNA-seq, for 27 adult canine tissues and tissues from four embryonic time points. This project has expanded genomic resources and enhanced the annotation of the dog genome [80]. Son et al. [81] expanded this resource using next-generation sequencing to profile transcriptomes, histone marks, and DNA methylomes across 11 tissue types, supporting comparative studies between dogs and humans, particularly in complex diseases such as cancer. Their work defined distinct chromatin states, super-enhancer, and methylome landscapes, revealing associations with a broad range of biological functions and tissue identities. In addition, the chromatin states of dogs had more significant similarities to humans than mice. Chromatin accessibility has been linked to gene expression and age prediction in dogs, similar to human studies on age clocks and health metrics [82]. ATAC-seq measurements of chromatin accessibility can predict the chronological age in dogs, with the common features near genes implicated in cognitive and neurological impairment [83].

  In disease-related studies, chromatin accessibility has been explored in canine non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) and osteosarcoma. The inhibition of PRMT5, a driver of oncogenesis in human lymphoma, led to decreased chromatin accessibility near the MYC gene in canine NHL, suggesting a similar regulatory mechanism [84]. In addition, single-nuclei multiome sequencing of canine osteosarcoma revealed the complexity of the tumor microenvironment, identifying the fundamental cellular interactions and pathways relevant to cancer progression. In particular, the study highlighted hypoxic, necrotic, and non-proliferative osteoblast clusters alongside highly active cancer-associated fibroblasts modulating the surrounding tumor environment. These findings underscore the strong similarities in intra-tumoral heterogeneity between canine and human osteosarcoma [85].


### Horses and goats
  Building on the methodologies established by the FAANG and ENCODE projects, Peng et al. [86] reported the feasibility of using flash-frozen tissues and cryopreserved nuclei to reveal and enhance ATAC-seq data quality in horses. Their integrated annotation of the horse genome identified candidate CREs and genome-wide open chromatin regions across various tissues. They reported that promoter-TSS regions were enriched in open chromatin peaks but absent in tissue-specific peaks as in other domestic animals. In contrast, most promoter-TSS regions exhibited concordant patterns with mRNA-seq data, and a subset of genes displayed discordant patterns [87].

  Similarly, the FAANG project mapped the regulatory elements in goats, which were later used to assess Pasteurella multocida infections—a cause of hemorrhagic sepsis and pneumonia. Chen et al. [88] conducted ATAC-seq on infected goat bronchial epithelial cells, identifying open chromatin regions linked to immune response pathways. Integrating RNA-seq, they pinpointed genes, such as SETBP1, IRF5, and TNF, as essential players in the immune response to infection.


## DISCUSSION
  Chromatin accessibility is crucial in gene regulation across various biological processes in domestic animals, including development, immune response, and disease states. Among domestic animals, chromatin accessibility dynamics have been studied extensively in cattle and pigs, with research in these species outpacing that in others (Fig. 2). Initial studies focused primarily on chromatin accessibility during the critical developmental stages, such as embryogenesis, SCNT, and stem cell differentiation, emphasizing its role in cellular differentiation through gene regulation. On the other hand, chromatin accessibility also influences other processes, such as immune cell differentiation and response to treatment, highlighting its broader impact on gene expression.

  In farm animals, epigenetic regulation of economically important traits, such as muscle development and productivity, has been studied extensively. Understanding chromatin accessibility in these contexts improves breeding strategies and livestock management. Studies on disease-resistant animals have shown how epigenetic changes contribute to health and longevity, offering insights into improving animal health (Fig. 3).

  Comparative epigenetic studies, such as the FAANG project, have been instrumental in identifying novel CREs across species. These studies compare the regulatory regions to determine if they are evolutionarily conserved or species-specific, providing critical insights into traits common across species or unique to certain breeds. Such comparisons also facilitate better breeding practices.

  The advances in high-throughput techniques, including single-cell analysis and Omni-ATAC-seq, have deepened the understanding of heterogeneous cell populations, such as tumors or blood immune cells. These techniques have uncovered previously unidentified CREs that have enhanced chromatin accessibility analysis in less-studied species when combined with deep-learning models. Despite the progress, more comprehensive epigenomic datasets, and improved cross-species analytical tools are needed in domestic animal research. Leveraging epigenetic insights can lead to breakthroughs in areas where genomics has fallen short.
