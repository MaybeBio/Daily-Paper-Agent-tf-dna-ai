# Integrated analysis of single-cell and bulk transcriptomics reveals cellular subtypes and molecular features associated with osteosarcoma prognosis


## Background
  Osteosarcoma (OS) is the most common primary bone malignancy with variable molecular biology and prognosis. However, our understanding of the association between cell types and OS progression remains poor.


## Methods
  We generated a human OS cell atlas by integrating over 110,000 single cells from 17 samples. Multiple machine learning algorithms were applied to develop tumor purity prediction models based on transcriptomic profile of OS. The Scissor algorithm and gene enrichment analyses were conducted to delve into cell-intrinsic molecular characteristics linked to OS prognosis. Moreover, the study investigated the impact of ATF6α in OS aggressiveness through genetic and pharmacological loss of function analyses. Lastly, the CellChat algorithm was employed to investigate cell-cell communications.


## Results
  Utilizing the high-quality human OS cell atlas, we identified tumor purity as a prognostic indicator and developed a robust tumor purity prediction model. We respectively delineated cancer cell- and immune cell-intrinsic molecular characteristics associated with OS prognosis at single-cell resolution. Interestingly, tumor cells with activated unfolded protein response (UPR) pathway were significantly associated with disease aggressiveness. Notably, ATF6α emerged as the top-activated transcription factor for this tumor subcluster. Subsequently, we confirmed that ATF6α was markedly associated with OS progression, while both genetic and pharmacological inhibition of ATF6α impaired the survival of HOS cells. Lastly, we depicted the landscape of signal crosstalk between the UPR-related subcluster and other cell types within the tumor microenvironment.


## Conclusion
  In summary, our work provides novel insights into the molecular biology of OS, and offers valuable resource for OS biomarker discovery and treatment strategy development.

## Introduction
  Osteosarcoma (OS) is a primary bone malignancy with an incidence rate of 3.4/1,000,000 each year worldwide [1]. The 5-year survival rate is around 65–70% for localized OS, but drops to under 30% for metastatic and recurrent cases. The current treatment of newly diagnosed OS includes neoadjuvant chemotherapy, followed by surgical resection and additional cycles of postoperative therapy. Developing novel treatment strategies and identifying new drugs to prolong survival have been the major focus of OS treatment. Recent investigation of immunotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted therapy has yielded promising results [2]. Of note, the overall prognosis of OS patients is highly variable due to striking genetic complexity and interpatient heterogeneity [3]. Emerging evidence suggests that the tumor microenvironment (TME) plays a dual role in OS progression, either promoting or inhibiting tumor growth depending on the context and cell type [4]. For example, specific immune cells such as B cells, T cells, and natural killer (NK) cells exhibit antitumor activity, while others, including neutrophils, M2-like macrophages, and regulatory T cells (Tregs), contribute to tumor growth and metastasis [4]. Hence, a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms and key cellular players driving OS progression is urgently needed to facilitate the development of novel prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

  Tumor purity refers to the proportion of cancerous cells within a tumor sample relative to non-cancerous cells, such as stromal and immune cells [5]. It serves as a reflection of the TME, which plays a critical role in tumor progression and therapeutic response [5, 6]. Previous studies have identified tumor purity as a potential prognostic indicator [7–10]. In certain cancer types, such as head and neck cancer [7] and cervical cancer [8], lower tumor purity is associated with improved prognosis. Conversely, in other cancer types, such as glioma [9] and colon cancer [10], lower tumor purity is linked to unfavorable survival outcomes. With regard to OS, the association between tumor purity and prognosis has not been characterized. While immunohistochemical techniques and certain algorithms based on whole exome/genome sequencing (WES/WGS) (such as ABSOLUTE [11]) are considered standard protocol for estimating tumor purity, these approaches are constrained by tissue and sequence data availability. Nowadays, the ESTIMATE algorithm [5] has been widely used for estimating OS tumor purity [12, 13]. Unfortunately, the dependency of ESTIMATE on infiltrating stromal and immune cells resulted in obvious limitations, particularly the inability to accurately infer tumor cellularity of hematopoietic or stromal tumors like leukemia, sarcoma and OS, due to the high tumor-intrinsic expression of stromal- or immune-related genes [11]. Therefore, it is important to develop a user-friendly, robust, and OS-specific tumor purity tool.

  Perturbations in endoplasmic reticulum (ER) proteostasis triggers the adaptive unfolded protein response (UPR), which is initiated by three ER transmembrane stress sensors: inositol-requiring enzyme 1 alpha (IRE1α), protein kinase R-like ER kinase (PERK), and activating transcription factor 6 alpha (ATF6α) [14]. Recent studies have provided comprehensive insight into the role of UPR signaling in promoting various hallmarks of different cancers, while targeting the key components of the UPR signaling hub may offer novel therapeutic strategies [14–18]. Our recent work based on bulk transcriptomic data generated from a number of OS cohorts, including our Zhengzhou OS cohort, established UPR activation as a common molecular feature of OS [19]. Of the three UPR branches, the mechanistic role of ATF6α in cancer progression has been relatively less understood. An earlier proteomic study observed elevated level of UPR proteins, particularly that of ATF6α branch, in OS tissues compared to in normal tissues [20]. With the recent development of single-cell sequencing technology, the specific subpopulation of cells with high ATF6α expression remains to be identified, and whether this is of any prognostic value requires detailed investigation.

  In this study, we first assembled a scRNA-seq meta-atlas of human OS. To construct a robust tumor purity prediction mode, we developed an integrative pipeline combining bulk transcriptomic data, scRNA-seq data and multiple algorithms. Furthermore, by employing the Scissor algorithm [21] along with TME deconvolution method, we comprehensively identified the major cell types, cell states and molecular characteristics associated with OS prognosis. Specifically, a subpopulation of UPR-associated tumor cells was ubiquitous in OS tissues and strongly associated with unfavorable prognosis. By combining in-house data and experimental means, we found that ATF6α was highly expressed in OS tumor samples and was indispensable for OS cell survival. Finally, comprehensive analysis unveiled the intricate cellular crosstalk between the UPR-related subcluster and other cell types within the TME.


## Materials and methods

### Bulk transcriptomic data retrieval and processing
  To build an integrated resource of transcriptomic features of OS, we conducted a comprehensive search of literature and databases to collect published transcriptomic profile of OS. Subsequently, we curated a meta-cohort of 344 OS samples from six bulk RNA-seq datasets, the HRA003260 (SGH-OS) [22], PRJNA588395 (GSE140131) [23], PRJNA345550 (GSE87686) [24], Pediatric Cancer (PeCan) [25], TAGRET [26] and our in-house Zhengzhou OS cohort [19]. For PeCan and TAGRET OS datasets, raw count data was downloaded from corresponding official websites. For available FASTQ files of SGH-OS, PRJNA588395, PRJNA345550 and Zhengzhou OS cohorts, we employed a unified pipeline for further processing: (1) The overall quality of sequencing reads was evaluated using FastQC (v0.11.9). (2) Sequence alignments to the reference human genome (GRCh38) and count matrix generation were performed using Salmon (v1.4.0, https://github.com/COMBINE-lab/salmon). (3) Samples were adjusted for batches and normalized with the variance stabilizing transformation in the R statistical environment using DESeq2 (v1.28.1, https://github.com/thelovelab/DESeq2) pipeline. The sample and dataset characteristics are summarized in Table S1. For survival analyses, the TARGET-OS dataset was utilized as the discovery cohort, while independent cohort GSE21257-OS was used for validation. For the progression-free survival analysis using the GSE21257-OS dataset, patients who experienced an event on day 0 were excluded. All datasets with survival information included in our study consist exclusively of primary tumor samples, as documented in their respective data sources. Transcript per million (TPM) value for RNA-seq data was calculated.


### Bulk WES data analysis
  We conducted WES analysis on the available FASTQ files from the SGH-OS dataset [22], following the GATK best practices tutorial (https://gatk.broadinstitute.org/hc/en-us). Briefly, the overall quality of sequencing reads was evaluated using FastQC (v0.11.9, https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). After quality control, we aligned them to the GRCh38 reference sequence using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA, v0.7.17). PCR duplicates were removed by Sambamba (v1.0.0, https://lomereiter.github.io/sambamba/), and the BAM files were then indexed by Samtools (v1.7, https://www.htslib.org/). Base quality score recalibration was performed by the BaseRecalibrator (https://gatk.broadinstitute.org/hc/en-us/articles/360036898312-BaseRecalibrator) and ApplyBQSR (https://gatk.broadinstitute.org/hc/en-us/articles/360037055712-ApplyBQSR) tools. Somatic mutation calling was performed utilizing Mutect2 (https://gatk.broadinstitute.org/hc/en-us/articles/360037593851-Mutect2) from GATK, which was applied to the processed exome data from both tumor and matched normal samples. The variant data was annotated using Annovar with the Refseq gene model. Copy number variations (CNVs) were detected using CNVkit (v.0.9.10, https://cnvkit.readthedocs.io/en/stable/) with BAM files post-BQSR. Finally, GISTIC2 (v.2.0.23, https://github.com/broadinstitute/gistic2) was utilized to pinpoint regions of significant amplification or deletion across the samples based on CNVs results.

  Meanwhile, we included two additional WES datasets, TARGET OS and TCGA Sarcoma. The processed somatic mutations and CNV data were downloaded using R package TCGAbiolinks (v2.26, https://github.com/BioinformaticsFMRP/TCGAbiolinks). The estimation of tumor purity was conducted using paired tumor-normal exome sequencing data, employing R package ABSOLUTE (v1.0.6, https://www.genepattern.org/#gsc.tab=0) and DoAbsolute (v2.2.0, https://github.com/ShixiangWang/DoAbsolute).


### Single-cell RNA-seq dataset selection and processing
  In this study, we aggregated over 110,000 single cells from 17 samples sourced from two publicly available human scRNA-seq OS datasets, GSE152048 and GSE162454 [27, 28]. As previously described [29], to prevent any interference with the analysis, we removed potential doublets, as predicted by Scrublet in Python (v3.8.5), and low-quality cells defined as those with fewer than 250 detected genes or more than 20% mitochondrial content for each sample. After pre-annotation and merging, 114,419 high-quality cells were preserved for subsequent analyses. Next, Scanpy (v1.9.3, https://scanpy.readthedocs.io/en/stable/) was employed to conduct normalization, identification of variably expressed genes, and principal component analysis. The BBKNN integration algorithm was used to mitigate potential batch effects arising from the utilization of diverse protocols and batches, following the tutorial of Scanpy (https://scanpy.readthedocs.io/en/stable/tutorials.html). Then clustering and distribution of single cells is mapped using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) with the Leiden algorithm. Finally, three cellular compartments and seven major cell types were identified by manual annotation and combined automatic annotation method Celltypist (v1.6.0, https://www.celltypist.org/). For single-cell subclustering analysis, a similar procedure was applied. Besides, we identified cell type specific DEGs through differential expression analysis by FindAllMarker function (avg_log2FC > = 0.25, p_val_adj < 0.01). LASSO regression model was applied for feature selection to identify key markers for each cell type using OS scRNA atlas (Table S2).

  Seurat (v.4.1.0, https://satijalab.org/seurat/) was adopted for subsequent analyses and visualization. To investigate prognostic cell types and molecular characteristics at single-cell resolution, we applied Scissor analysis to associate overall survival (cox-regression model, cutoff set as 0.2) from TARGET-OS dataset with single-cell data according to demonstration notebook [21]. We subsequently calculated the fraction of Scissor + cells (positively associated with worse survival), Scissor– cells (negatively associated), and the log2 ratio between the fractions of Scissor + and Scissor– cells. Differential expression analysis was performed to compare the expression profile of Scissor + and Scissor– cells. We defined DEGs based on a cutoff of|avg_log2FC| > 0.6 and an adjusted p-value (p_val_adj) of < 0.01. Then Scissor scores were calculated using VIPER algorithm, which combined both the UP and DN Scissor-related signatures. For the Kaplan-Meier analysis, the optimal cutpoint was determined using the TARGET-OS discovery cohort by the ‘surv_cutpoint’ function in the ‘survminer’ R package (v.0.4.9), and then applied as a predefined threshold to the validation cohort GSE21257-OS.


### Identification of malignant cells
  To distinguish the malignant cells from non-malignant cells, we used interCNVpy (v0.4.3, https://github.com/icbi-lab/infercnvpy) for inferring large-scale chromosomal copy number variations. We used cnv.tl.infercnv function with parameter ‘window_size = 250’ on OS subpopulations. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and pericytes were used as the reference “normal” cells, while cancer-associated fibroblast (CAFs) as spike-in reference cells. Only malignant cells showed clear copy number aberration.


### Development and validation of tumor purity prediction model
  We designed a pipeline to construct a tumor purity prediction model for OS. Initially, we generated a single-cell atlas of OS and identified 1132 tumor-specific DEGs, along with 801 stroma-specific DEGs and 1228 immune-specific DEGs through differential expression analysis by FindAllMarker function (wilcoxon rank sum test with Bonferroni correction, avg_log2FC > = 0.25 and p_val_adj < 0.01). Next, seven classical machine learning algorithms were performed to construct a tumor purity prediction model based on the cell type specific DEGs and TARGET-OS TPM expression profile, including elastic network (Enet), least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), ridge regression, gradient boosting machine (GBM), random forest (RF), supervised principal components (SuperPC), and support vector machine (SVM). To minimize overfitting, we employed the following strategies: (a) Ensured all input gene features were Z-scaled to prevent any single feature from dominating the learning process. (b) 10-fold cross-validation was performed within the training dataset to optimize model parameters. (c) The final models were evaluated by indexes including R2, C-index, and correlation analysis in three validation cohorts (SGH-OS, scRNA-seq Psedobulk and TCGA-Sarcoma datasets). In addition, we also identified three thinned cell-intrinsic gene signatures by feature selection (Table S3). Immune score and stromal score for each sample was calculated by the single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) algorithm with thinned cell-intrinsic gene signatures.


### Deconvolution of cell-type infiltration based on single-cell expression matrix
  We used BayesPrism methods [30] to determine the relationship between cell type infiltration proportions and prognosis in bulk RNA-seq and microarray data for the 7 major cell types and 30 subdivided cell types in the OS scRNA-seq atlas. BayesPrism algorithm was performed according to official tutorial (https://github.com/Danko-Lab/BayesPrism) using R package BayesPrism (v.2.0).


### SuperCell algorithm for calculating metacells
  To succinctly describe the observed phenotypic diversity, we employed SuperCell algorithm (https://github.com/GfellerLab/SuperCell) [31] to compute metacells separately for each sample and cell type using the SCimplify function with parameter ‘gamma = 20’, ‘k.knn = 5’, ‘n.pc = 10’, ‘cell.split.condition = sample’, ‘cell.annotation = cell_type’, and ‘n.var.genes = 1000’ in SuperCell R package. The metacell approach groups similar single cells into distinct, highly detailed cell states. This ensures that any variations among cells within a metacell are more likely due to technical factors rather than biological differences [31]. By aggregating counts within each metacell, we obtained robust and comprehensive transcriptomic quantification for each cell state, effectively reducing noise and addressing the sparsity inherent in scRNA-seq data. Furthermore, metacells offer the advantage of enumerating observed cell states that are more amenable to comparative analysis.


### Cell communication analysis with metacells
  To explore cell-to-cell communication patterns, we employed the CellChat method (v1.5.0, https://github.com/sqjin/CellChat) [32]. In summary, we followed the official workflow, creating the CellChat object utilizing the OS metacell atlas using ‘createCellChat’ function. Subsequently, we applied the ‘identifyOverExpressedGenes’ and ‘identifyOverExpressedInteractions’ functions for data processing with standard parameters. We assessed potential ligand/receptor interactions across all cells, with particular focus on interactions of UPR-related cancer cells with other cell types. This analysis was conducted through functions such as ‘computeCommunProb’, ‘computeCommunProbPathway’, and ‘aggregateNet’, all using standard parameters. To further validate the results, we conducted the qPCR experiments using HOS OS cells. The primer sequences used for qPCR are provided in Table S4.


### Enrichment analysis
  As previously described [29], we conducted Gene Ontology (GO) and rank-based GSEA using the R package clusterProfiler (v4.7.1, https://github.com/YuLab-SMU/clusterProfiler). For bulk transcriptomic data, we employed the ssGSEA method with the R package GSVA (https://github.com/rcastelo/GSVA). To mitigate the impact of dropouts and technical variability inherent in scRNA-seq data, we opted for the AUCell algorithm, which allowed for enhanced discovery and characterization of distinct cell states [33].


### Immunohistochemistry
  Tissue collection and immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed as previously described [19]. After de-paraffinization, antigen retrieval was performed using a 0.01 M citric acid sodium buffer solution (pH 6.0), followed by incubation with 3% H2O2 for 10 min to quench endogenous peroxidases. The tissue sections were blocked with 5% serum/0.3% Triton X-100/PBS. Subsequently, ATF6α antibody (37149, Abcam, UK) was carried out using standard histological procedures described in the manual for the immunohistochemical kit (Anti-Rabbit Secondary Antibody, G1215-200T, Servicebio, China). Quantitative analysis of ATF6α expression in the histologic sections was performed using the IHC Image Analysis Toolbox plugin within ImageJ software (v1.51j), following the manufacturer’s instructions (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/plugins/ihc-toolbox/).


### Cell culture, siRNA and plasmid transfection, and pharmacological treatment
  OS cell line HOS (RRID: CVCL_0312) was obtained from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Culture (China). The cells were cultured in DMEM medium (C11995500BT, Gibco, USA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS500-H, Hycezmbio, China), 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 mg/ml streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. The authenticity of the cell line was confirmed by short tandem repeat profiling conducted within the last 3 years. Additionally, all experiments were carried out using mycoplasma-free cells.

  The application of ATF6α siRNA, ATF6α inhibitor Ceapin-A7, and human pCDNA3-Flag-ATF6α-EGFP (ATF6α) plasmid was described in our previous study [15]. For transfection of ATF6α siRNA, HOS cells were seeded at a concentration of 2.5 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates and were cultured to be 70–90% confluent at transfection. Lipofectamine® RNAiMAX Reagent (13778-075, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used to make transfection of siRNA into cells. Lipofectamine 3000 DNA Transfection Reagent (L30000015, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used to make transfection of plasmids into cells. For treatment of ATF6α inhibitor Ceapin-A7, HOS cells were treated with either Ceapin-A7 (10 µM) or DMSO for 12 h. If necessary, thapsigargin (Tg, 30 nM) was treated for 3 h before harvested. All operations were performed according to the manufacture’s protocol.


### CCK-8 assay
  Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay (A311-01, Vazyme, China). Briefly, HOS cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 103 cells per 100 µl of DMEM, followed by treatment and incubation for 48 h. Subsequently, 10 µl of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for 3 hours at 37 °C in a humidified incubator. The number of viable cells was determined by measuring absorbance at 450 nm using a Syngene BioTek microplate reader (USA).


### Colony formation assay
  HOS cells were trypsinized, plated on plastic dishes at a density of 5 × 103 cells per 1 ml of DMEM, and incubated for a duration of 2 weeks. Afterward, the cells were fixed using a 4% paraformaldehyde solution (G1101, Servicebio, China), followed by staining with 0.1% crystal violet (G1014, Servicebio, China). The total area covered by the colonies was captured using a camera.


### Cell proliferation assay
  HOS cells were seeded at an initial concentration of 5 × 104 cells per 1 ml in 6-cm plates. Cell concentration was counted by cell counting plate every 3 days for detecting proliferation ability.


### Statistical analysis
  All data analyses were conducted using the R platform (v.4.1.0) or Python (v.3.8.5). For comparisons between two groups, we applied either the Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables, while one-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis tests were utilized for comparing differences among three groups. Correlations between normally distributed variables were assessed using Pearson’s correlation, whereas Spearman’s correlation was employed for non-normally distributed variables. To address the issue of multiple testing, the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method was employed to estimate the false discovery rate. For survival analysis, Kaplan-Meier analysis with log-rank tests was executed using the ‘survminer’ R package. The cut-off for defining high and low groups was determined using the ‘surv_cutpoint’ function in the ‘survminer’ package [29]. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P-value < 0.05. If necessary, odds ratios (ORs), hazard ratios (HRs), and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.


## Results

### The limitations of ESTIMATE algorithm for OS tumor purity
  To explore the relationship between tumor purity and OS prognosis, we assessed the associations of OS tumor purity with clinical and molecular characteristics. OS samples from the TARGET dataset were arranged in the order of increasing tumor purity (Figure S1A, see Methods). Interestingly, OS tumor purity was significantly correlated with poor prognosis, such as relapse, metastasis, and death (Figure S1A). Furthermore, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed that the overall survival of the high tumor purity group was significantly shorter than that of the low purity group in TARGET-OS cohort (Figure S1B). These results suggest that tumor purity is a potential prognostic indicator for OS.

  The ESTIMATE algorithm is a common method for evaluating tumor purity, and its implementation with transcriptomic data is straightforward. Considering that OS is believed to originate from mesenchymal cells [34], we proceeded to assess its effectiveness for OS. As expected, we found that stromal gene signature from the ESTIMATE built-in gene set showed low effectiveness when assessed using the OS scRNA-seq atlas assembled by us (Figure S1C-F, see Methods). For example, nearly half of the stromal genes were expressed nonspecifically (Figure S1C-E). Moreover, a low correlation was observed between stromal scores inferred by ESTIMATE algorithm and stromal cellular fraction in our OS scRNA-seq atlas. Taken together, these results underline the need for developing a robust tool to evaluate tumor purity in OS.


### Construction of tumor purity prediction model based on scRNA-seq atlas and bulk RNA-seq datasets of OS
  To develop an accurate method for estimating tumor purity from OS gene expression profiles, we designed a pipeline that involved cell-intrinsic gene markers, and applied multiple machine learning models based on the TARGET-OS bulk RNA-seq dataset and the OS scRNA-seq atlas we assembled (Fig. 1A, see methods). Finally, we generated a total of 15 artificial intelligence-derived tumor purity prediction models. Taking advantage of three validation datasets, including SGH-OS, OS scRNA-seq pseudobulk and TCGA-Sarcoma, we found that most algorithms showed high Pearson correlation coefficients between predicted tumor purity scores and actual purity (Fig. 1B). Meanwhile, we compared our models with previously published four classic tumor purity methods, where our models unanimously outperformed the existing methods (Fig. 1B-C). Among them, we identified GBM-driven model as most effective (Fig. 1B-D), which we hereafter referred to as OS purity-related signature. Furthermore, multivariate Cox regression analysis showed that the GBM-based tumor purity prediction model is an independent prognostic factor for OS (Figure S2A). On top of this, we identified three thinned cell-intrinsic gene signatures with high effectiveness through feature selection (Figure S2B-D). Finally, we found that higher stromal score and immune score were both associated with improved prognosis (Fig. 1E-F).

### Identification of the major cell types associated with OS prognosis
  Next, we set out to explore the cell types associated with progression at single-cell level. First, we identified seven major cell types based on the OS scRNA-seq atlas, including OS cells (ALPL, PTH1R, RUNX2), CAFs (FAP, DCN), pericytes (RGS5, PDGFRB, NOTCH3), endothelial cells (PLVAP, VWF), T cells (CD3D, CD3E), myeloid cells (CD14, C1QC, CD163), and osteoclasts (MMP9, ACP5, CTSK) (Fig. 2A-C). The distribution of the cells from each sample in the UMAP showed that potential batches were well handled (Fig. 2B). By calculating the odds ratio (OR) index [35], we noted that T cells, osteoclasts and myeloid cells showed a strong distribution preference in the primary tumor group, whereas OS cells were enriched in both two progression groups (metastasis and relapse) (Fig. 2D). Similar analyses stratified by samples confirmed this trend (Fig. 2E). Using the BayesPrism algorithm to deconvolute the TARGET-OS bulk RNA-seq data based on seven major cell types, we found that OS cells were associated with poor prognosis, whereas myeloid cells, CAFs, osteoclasts, T cells, and pericytes were associated with better prognosis (Fig. 2F and Figure S3A). These findings were validated in GSE21257-OS microarray dataset (Fig. 2G and Figure S3B). By employing GO functional enrichment analysis, we identified both shared and distinct features of T cells, osteoclasts, and myeloid cells associated with prognosis (Figure S3C). For instance, shared molecular pathways included leukocyte migration and activation, as well as mononuclear cell migration, which is critical for immune activation (Figure S3C). On the other hand, distinct pathways were observed among these cell types: T cells exhibited enrichment in pathways such as T cell differentiation and activation, while osteoclasts were specifically associated with bone remodeling and osteoclast differentiation (Figure S3C). Myeloid cells showed unique enrichment in pathways like antigen presentation and processing (APP) (Figure S3C). These findings highlight the complex interplay between these cell types in shaping the tumor microenvironment and their prognostic implications.

### Identification of prognosis related cell types and molecular features in the TME
  Comprehending the tumor immune microenvironments is crucial for identifying factors that modulate cancer progression and for the development of immunotherapy [36]. In this regard, we divided the myeloid cells and osteoclasts into 11 subclusters based on representative markers [34, 36, 37] (Fig. 3A-B). These subclusters included monocytes, five macrophage-related subclusters (Mph_C1Q+, Mph_LYVE1, Mph_IL1B, Mph_SPP1 and Mph_IFIT3), two dendritic cell (DC)-related subclusters (DCs and pDCs), and two osteoclast-related subclusters (Osteoclasts and Osteoclast_MKI67). Subsequently, we employed the Scissor algorithm to integrate the phenotypic data from TARGET-OS dataset with transcriptomic expression profiles of myeloid cells (see Methods). Here, we identified Mph_SPP1 (represent 5.17% of total myeloid cells) might serve as the potential macrophage subcluster associated with OS progression (Fig. 3C). Interestingly, deconvolution analysis of bulk data from the TARGET and GSE21257 datasets showed that a limited correlation between SPP1-expressing macrophages and overall survival, but an association between higher SPP1-expressing macrophage abundance and poorer progression-free survival (Figure S3D). We further compared the transcriptomic profiles between Scissor + cells (unfavorable prognosis) and Scissor– cells (favorable prognosis), and revealed that up-regulated DEGs in Scissor + cells were enriched for response to hypoxia, apoptotic signaling, neutrophil and granulocyte migration, osteoclast differentiation, as well as wound healing (Fig. 3D). On the other hand, up-regulated DEGs in Scissor–cells were enriched for several immune response and activation terms, such as APP, leukocyte mediated immunity, T cell activation and leukocyte cell-cell adhesion (Fig. 3D).

  For T cells, we identified nine subclusters including CD4 + naive T cells (CD4Tn), T follicular helper or T helper 1 cells (Tfh/Th1), regulatory T cells (Treg), CD8 + tissue-resident memory T cells (CD8Trm), CD8 + effect memory T cells (CD8Tem), CD8 + exhausted T cells (CD8Tex), cycling T cells (T_MKI67), and natural killer cells (NK) (Fig. 3E-F) [34–36]. Interestingly, Scissor algorithm associated most T cell subclusters with favorable prognosis (Fig. 3G). Furthermore, GO enrichment analysis failed to identify any up-regulated terms for Scissor + cells, whereas Scissor–cells showed enrichment in numerous terms related to leukocyte recruitment and activation, as well as immune response terms, such as leukocyte migration, NK cell mediated cytotoxicity, and positive regulation of cytokine production (Fig. 3H). Together, these results unveil immune cell-intrinsic molecular characteristics linked to OS prognosis at single-cell resolution.


### Transcriptomic intra-tumor heterogeneity of OS cells
  Consistent with existing knowledge [34], mesenchymal and OS cells are the main constituents of OS TME (Fig. 2E). Clustering analysis was subsequently performed for mesenchymal and OS cells. Based on established cell markers, mesenchymal cells were divided into three subclusters, including MSCs, CAFs and pericytes (Fig. 4A-B). Meanwhile, according to the CNV calculation results (see Methods), we identified six cancer cell states and assessed their intrinsic functional characteristics (Figure S4A-D and Fig. 4C).

  C1_OS_MT + tumor cells were characterized by high expression of metallothionein (MT) related genes, such as MT1X, MTG and MT1M (Fig. 4B). These MT related genes were frequently observed to be upregulated in OS [38]. In addition, GSEA analysis of C1_OS_MT + tumor cells indicated significant enrichment for TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. 4C). C2_OS_Oxidative tumor cells expressed low levels of MT related genes but high levels of several mitochondrion-associated genes, namely CYC1, NDUFB9 and NDUFB10 (Fig. 4B). Moreover, C2_OS_Oxidative tumor cells showed the highest activity in oxidative phosphorylation pathway. On the other hand, C3_OS_UPR cells predominantly exhibited high expression of ER stress and UPR-related marker genes, such as HSPA5 (GRP78), HERPUD1, DDIT3 (CHOP), MYC and BAG3 (Fig. 4B). Besides, these cells showed significant activation of MYC target V1, WNT-β catenin pathway, and TGF-β signaling (Fig. 4C and Figure S4D). These observations were in line with the frequent activation of UPR in OS previously documented by us and others [19, 39–41]. C4_OS_qCSC expressed high levels of several quiescent cancer stem cell marker genes, including BEX2, SOX2 and PROM1 (CD133) (Fig. 4B). Interestingly, the GSEA terms enriched for this subtype were associated with epithelial-mesenchymal transition and interferon signaling (Fig. 4C). C5_OS_CSC tumor cells were distinguished by their high expression of cell cycle related genes, such as TOP2A, EZH2 and E2F1, and high pathway activity in stemness and cell cycle related signaling (Fig. 4B-C, and Figure S4D). Cell cycle analysis confirmed their proliferative nature (Figure S4E). Finally, subcluster C6_Chondroblasts expressed high level of chondroblast related markers [28], such as COL2A1, SOX9 and ACAN. These cells were also characterized by induced hypoxia and glycolysis pathways (Fig. 4B-C, and Figure S4D). Collectively, these findings unveil a notable degree of heterogeneity within OS TME.


### Dissection of progression-related cell states and molecular characteristics of OS tumor cells
  Next, we employed Scissor algorithm to illustrate the tumor cell states and molecular features associated with OS progression (Fig. 4D, see Methods). Intriguingly, we observed that C3_OS_UPR cells displayed markedly higher log2 ratio than other cell states (Fig. 4D-E, and Figure S4F). This suggested a correlation between UPR activation of this specific cell state and adverse prognosis in OS. Furthermore, we employed the VIPER algorithm [42] to integrate the Scissor + and Scissor– cell-related signatures, demonstrating their predictive value for both overall survival and progression-free survival in the TARGET discovery dataset (Fig. 4F, and Figure S4G). In the GSE21257 validation dataset, although the results did not reach statistical significance, we observed a clear trend consistent with the findings in the discovery dataset (Fig. 4G, and Figure S4H).

  To uncover the molecular features associated with OS progression, we conducted GO analysis, which indicated that the up-regulated DEGs in Scissor + cells were mainly enriched for terms related to bone differentiation and morphology, UPR signaling, as well as extracellular matrix (ECM) functions and angiogenesis (Fig. 4H-I). Meanwhile, the up-regulated DEGs in Scissor– cells were associated with oxidative phosphorylation, APP, leukocyte migration and activation, as well as interferon response-related terms (Fig. 4H-J). To our interest, a number of ATF6α target genes, including GRP78, P4HB (PDI), and HSP90B1 (GRP94) [15], exhibited markedly increased expression in Scissor + cells (Fig. 4I), suggesting a potential link between the ATF6α branch of UPR and OS progression. Finally, GSEA analysis validated the activation of UPR in Scissor + cells, and identified the presence of activated interferon alpha response pathways in Scissor– cells (Figure S4I-J). Taken together, these data not only reveal key molecular features of OS progression at single-cell level, but also indicate that activation of the UPR, particularly the ATF6α pathway, in a specific tumor cell population is strongly linked with poor prognosis of OS.


### ATF6α activation is associated with OS progression
  To compare the relationship between 30 subdivided cell types and OS prognosis, we employed BayesPrism algorithm to deconvolute the TARGET-OS and GSE21257 bulk transcriptomic data using OS scRNA-seq atlas. Remarkably, the C3_OS_UPR tumor subcluster ranked at the top in overall survival forest plot, compared to the other cell subclusters (Fig. 5A and B, and Figure S5A-S5B). In addition, we found that the C3_OS_UPR subcluster was also linked to progression-free survival of OS (Fig. 5C and Figure S5B), indicating that C3_OS_UPR population was significantly correlated with OS progression.

  In the meantime, SCENIC analysis revealed that ATF6α ranked as the top transcription factor (TF) for the C3_OS_UPR tumor subcluster (Fig. 5D). This was validated using the ATF6α branch gene signature we recently established [15] (Fig. 5E). Consistently, C3_OS_UPR cells exhibited high expression of several ATF6α branch target genes (Figure S5C), such as GRP78, HERPUD1, CREB3L1 and DNAJB1. Furthermore, by utilizing our in-house dataset, we observed significant up-regulation of ATF6α branch gene signature in OS tumor samples compared to in matched adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 5F and Figure S5D). This was mirrored by markedly stronger staining of ATF6α, in particular nuclear signal, in OS specimens than in matched adjacent tissues (Fig. 5G).

  Based on these findings, we analyzed the functional role of ATF6α in OS cells. We first transiently repressed ATF6α expression in HOS cells by two independent ATF6α siRNAs. CCK-8 assay showed that HOS cell viability was decreased upon ATF6α knockdown (Figure S5E). Furthermore, long-term culture of HOS cells led to significant reduction in the colony-forming ability and cell growth following ATF6α depletion (Fig. 5H-I). In keeping with this, pharmacological repression by the ATF6α inhibitor Ceapin-A7 [15] similarly resulted in reduced cell viability, colony formation, and growth in HOS cells (Fig. 5J-K, and Figure S5F). Collectively, these observations corroborate the pro-survival role of ATF6α in HOS cells.


### The landscape of cellular crosstalk between UPR-related tumor subcluster and various cell types within the TME
  Lastly, by utilizing the large OS RNA-seq meta-cohort assembled by us, we observed a universal presence of C3_OS_UPR cells across all OS samples (Fig. 6A). Besides, we also noted a significant correlation between the estimated tumor purity and the predicted fraction of C3_OS_UPR subcluster using deconvolution method (Fig. 6B). Given that exploring the cell‒cell interaction ligand/receptor (L/R) pairs can provide new information on the OS TME, we calculated the attraction strengths of ligand‒receptor pairs in the OS scRNA-seq atlas using CellChat with metacells (see Methods). The results implicated active communication of the C3_OS_UPR subcluster with stromal cells and CD8Tex cells (Fig. 6C). Besides, we depicted the communication signal network between C3_OS_UPR and CD8Tex cells (Fig. 6D). Furthermore, we identified the main outgoing and incoming signals of the activated signaling pathway for C3_OS_UPR cancer cells, including several cell-cell adhesion terms, ECM-receptor and secreted signaling (Fig. 6E). For example, TGF-β signaling and WNT pathway emerged as the top outgoing signals, and their activation was observed in C3_OS_UPR cells in parallel (Fig. 4C). Moreover, we identified that the L/R pairs from signaling pathways like WNT, TGF-β, NRXN, BMP, and PDGF were the most significant ligands from C3_OS_UPR cells to the other cell subclusters (Fig. 6F). Notably, qPCR experiments in HOS OS cells overexpressing ATF6α revealed increased expression of TGF-β pathway markers and ligands (SMAD2, SMAD3, SMAD7, TGFB1, TGFB2, and TGFB3), along with WNT pathway markers and ligands (AXIN2, WNT2B, and WNT10B) (Figure S5G). These findings support, at least in part, the predictions made by CellChat. In conclusion, these results provide a comprehensive map of cellular crosstalk between UPR-related tumor subcluster and relevant cell types within the TME.

## Discussion
  In this study, we first identified tumor purity as a predictive marker for the prognosis of OS patients. High tumor purity, characterized by a high proportion of cancer cells relative to immune and stromal components, is often associated with clonal expansion of cancer cells that enhance proliferation, metastasis, and therapy resistance, contributing to poorer prognosis [5–8, 43, 44]. In OS, it is commonly observed that patients with higher immune and stromal infiltration have longer overall survival [12, 13, 43, 44]. This may be attributed to the lack of immune and stromal cells fostering an immunosuppressive microenvironment and reducing the response to immunotherapy or chemotherapy, collectively leading to worse outcomes in OS [4]. Moreover, we conducted an integrative analysis of scRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq data to derive a GBM-based tumor purity prediction model for OS. Compared to existing algorithms, our model demonstrates higher accuracy, as validated across independent datasets, and broader applicability due to its integration of tumor-specific, stromal-specific, and immune-specific gene signatures. Furthermore, its ability to utilize bulk transcriptomic data makes it well-suited for clinical translation, offering potential for patient stratification and personalized treatment.

  Furthermore, scRNA-seq analysis also associated increased OS cell abundance with greater aggressiveness and poorer prognosis. Conversely, higher proportion of stromal cells, including pericytes and CAFs, as well as immune cells like T cells and myeloid cells, was associated with better prognosis. Detailed dissection of immune cell states and their molecular characteristics revealed both shared and distinctive molecular patterns between myeloid and T cells linked to OS prognosis. Notably, macrophage subpopulations with high SPP1 expression were significantly associated with poor prognosis, as previously described in other cancer types such as head and neck and colorectal cancers [36, 45]. Shared characteristics between T cells and myeloid cells linked to better prognosis included lymphocyte recruitment, migration and activation, as well as APP signaling. Meanwhile, granulocyte and neutrophil migration were unique characteristics of myeloid cells with unfavorable prognosis, implying that these cells may recruit granulocytes and neutrophils. This was in keeping with the immunosuppressive role of neutrophils recently described in other tumors [46, 47], even though their function in OS remains to be elucidated. In summary, our analyses of OS TME identified prognostically relevant cell populations and their molecular features at single-cell level.

  Intra-tumor heterogeneity is shaped by genetic, epigenetic and microenvironmental factors, and plays a critical in treatment response and prognosis [48]. In this regard, the intra-tumor heterogeneity of OS remains to be fully understood. To this end, we subdivided the tumor cells, identified six distinct OS cell states, and elucidated their intrinsic functional characteristics. For instance, we identified a ubiquitous subcluster of OS cells characterized by activated UPR, which showed a positive correlation with tumor purity. This was in line with the established activation of UPR in OS samples at the bulk tissue level [19, 39]. Meanwhile, we found that OS cells displaying activation of oxidative phosphorylation, APP, leukocyte-related pathways, as well as interferon response-related signaling were correlated with good prognosis, whereas those harboring UPR signaling, ECM functions and angiogenesis-related pathway were with poor prognosis [19, 39, 49, 50]. These findings provide novel clues to the heterogeneous outcome of OS from single-cell perspective.

  Notably, we identified C3_OS_UPR as one of the top cell types associated with unfavorable prognosis. Further analyses pinpointed ATF6α as a major TF for this tumor subcluster. Analyses of our in-house data confirmed the activated ATF6α (exemplified by IHC nuclear signal) as well as elevated expression of ATF6α canonical targets in OS tumors compared to adjacent tissues. Furthermore, genetic and chemical manipulation demonstrated a pro-survival role of ATF6α in HOS cells. But whether ATF6α drives the formation of this specific cell population is still an open question. Besides, targeting UPR pathways presents certain challenges, such as the difficulty of selectively inhibiting tumor-specific UPR activation without affecting normal cellular stress responses [14, 51]. Additionally, compensatory mechanisms within the UPR network might limit the efficacy of single-pathway inhibitors [14, 51]. Opportunities include the development of highly specific inhibitors targeting UPR activation or its downstream effectors, as well as combination strategies to disrupt UPR signaling alongside conventional therapies and immunotherapy [16, 18, 52, 53]. Lastly, we constructed the communication map between C3_OS_UPR cells and other cell types. In this network, we observed significant interaction primarily involving signaling pathways such as WNT and TGF-β signaling between C3_OS_UPR cells and other cell subclusters, suggesting that C3_OS_UPR-secreted Wnt or TGFb may modulate other tumor cells and stromal cells in a paracrine manner. Based on previous research in other cancer types, several hypotheses can be proposed regarding how UPR signaling contributes to poor prognosis in OS [14–18, 20, 51, 52]. First, UPR signaling enables tumor cells to adapt to the hostile microenvironment, such as hypoxia and nutrient deprivation, by alleviating cellular stress and maintaining proteostasis [14]. Second, UPR activation may modulate immune signaling, for example, by downregulating antigen presentation or fostering an immunosuppressive microenvironment, thereby promoting immune evasion [17, 51]. Lastly, UPR signaling has been implicated in therapy resistance, enhancing tumor cell survival and reducing the efficacy of apoptosis-inducing treatments [51]. Future studies are needed to validate these hypotheses and further explore the role of UPR signaling in OS progression and therapy resistance.

  Despite these advances, the present study has several limitations. Firstly, the bulk RNA-seq datasets available for validation were limited in sample size, potentially reducing statistical power. As a result, while clear survival trends were observed, statistical significance was not consistently achieved in some analyses, as seen in Figs. 2G and 4G, and Figure S3D, S4H. This was partially due to the overall rarity of OS. Furthermore, it is important to note that the publicly available OS single-cell datasets we enrolled in this study were exclusively composed of Asian populations, which may not fully represent the genetic diversity of this malignancy in other ethnic groups. To overcome these limitations as much as we can, we employed a combination of in-house data, experimental means, and the compilation of larger publicly available OS bulk RNA-seq datasets [19, 22–26] to create a comprehensive OS RNA-seq meta-cohort for validation. Lastly, although this study identified several gene signatures and cell types associated with OS prognosis, our attempt to construct a “super” signature for predicting OS outcomes using various machine learning models was unsuccessful (data not shown). Despite testing different parameters and configurations, these models consistently exhibited pronounced overfitting. While these negative results highlight the challenges inherent in developing a unified “super” signature, they also underscore the complexity and heterogeneity of the data. Future research will focus on refining modeling approaches and leveraging larger, more diverse datasets to overcome these limitations and improve the predictive power of combined signatures.

  By establishing tumor purity as a robust prognostic marker and identifying the cellular and molecular characteristics associated with OS progression at the single-cell level, this study underscores the critical role of the TME in clinical decision-making for OS. For instance, patients with low tumor purity (indicative of high immune or stromal infiltration) may benefit from therapies targeting immune or stromal components, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors or TME-modulating treatments [4, 54]. Conversely, patients with high tumor purity could respond better to therapies targeting cancer cell-intrinsic pathways, such as the UPR, WNT, and TGF-β signaling. In conclusion, our findings enhance the understanding of the biological heterogeneity of OS, providing valuable insights into how variations in TME composition influence clinical outcomes.
