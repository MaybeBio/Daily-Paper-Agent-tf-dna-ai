# A mechanism-informed deep neural network enables prioritization of regulators that drive cell state transitions


## Abstract
  Cells are regulated at multiple levels, from regulations of individual genes to interactions across multiple genes. Some recent neural network models can connect molecular changes to cellular phenotypes, but their design lacks modeling of regulatory mechanisms, limiting the decoding of regulations behind key cellular events, such as cell state transitions. Here, we present regX, a deep neural network incorporating both gene-level regulation and gene-gene interaction mechanisms, which enables prioritizing potential driver regulators of cell state transitions and providing mechanistic interpretations. Applied to single-cell multi-omics data on type 2 diabetes and hair follicle development, regX reliably prioritizes key transcription factors and candidate cis-regulatory elements that drive cell state transitions. Some regulators reveal potential new therapeutic targets, drug repurposing possibilities, and putative causal single nucleotide polymorphisms. This method to analyze single-cell multi-omics data demonstrates how the interpretable design of neural networks can better decode biological systems.


## Introduction
  Cells comprise multi-level regulatory processes from the epigenetic, transcriptional, and translational regulation for individual genes, to interactions and pathways across multiple genes. Developing computational methods that model the multi-level cellular system can deepen our understanding of the molecular mechanism of cellular functions, cell fate determinations, and potentialities for cellular modulations1,2.

  Existing works have demonstrated the power of biologically informed neural networks in modeling multi-level biological systems for scientific discoveries3–12. For example, DCell6 and DrugCell7 modeled the hierarchical function of cells according to gene annotations; LEMBAS10 and KPNN11 incorporated prior knowledge on signaling pathways; P-NET5 further demonstrated that biologically informed neural networks can indeed accelerate biological discoveries with translational implications. However, these works did not model the more basic regulatory processes for each gene such as chromatin remodeling and transcription factor (TF) binding, which are essential for determining cellular changes during development and disease progression. There are also some works focusing on the regulatory modeling of gene expressions13,14, but there exists a gap between gene expressions and cellular phenotypes, which impedes their further application to trait-relevant research. Therefore, a model encapsulating cross-level regulatory processes from upstream regulators to genes, functions, and phenotypes is desirable. It can uncover regulators behind key cellular events in a biologically interpretable manner. This is becoming possible thanks to the availability of multi-omics sequencing data at single-cell resolution.

  Here, we developed regX, a deep neural network model that included both gene-level regulation and gene-gene interaction mechanisms. The trained regX model can be used to prioritize potential driver regulators during cell state transitions through in-silico perturbation. The mechanism-informed design further enables regX to provide mechanistic explanations of the discoveries. We applied regX to two single-cell multi-omics datasets of cell state transitions during disease and development: type 2 diabetes (T2D) progression and hair follicle development. regX exhibited reliability and robustness in prioritizing key TFs and candidate cis-regulatory elements (cCREs) in both studies. Notably, we identified top-ranked regulators as promising new therapeutic targets or indicating drug repurposing possibilities for T2D. The top-ranked regulators also pinpointed putative causal single nucleotide polymorphisms (pcSNPs) from previous GWAS results, which showcased an extra use of regX for one of the most important tasks in the post-GWAS era. Promising biological discoveries were also observed in the hair follicle development data.


## Results

### Study overview
  We designed a mechanism-informed neural network that predicts cell states from cCREs and TFs (Fig. 1a). Target genes, proteins, or functions were embedded in the hidden layers as important routes in the regulation of cell states. To model the gene expression process, we invented a learnable transcriptional activity matrix (TAM), which captures how TFs regulate the expression of a gene by interacting with accessible chromatin regions (cCREs) around the gene. Each element in the TF-by-cCRE TAM of a gene is a multiplication of the TF expression, cCRE accessibility, and TF-cCRE interaction learned from data (“Methods”). These elements were then connected to the target gene to form a gene subnet architecture. To model gene-gene interactions, we used a graph neural network (GNN) layer to embed protein-protein interactions (PPIs) or GO-GO interactions. These two types of interactions, obtained from databases, can be used interchangeably with appropriate GNN structures. Each node in the GNN layer represents a protein or GO term in the PPI or GO graph. The GNN layer holds all subnets together by linking transcripts in the previous layer with corresponding proteins or GO functions according to prior knowledge. The philosophy behind this design is that both PPI networks and GO graphs are reasonable abstractions of the true underlying relations among genes from different angles, making them suitable intermediates for modeling cell state regulation. Representations of proteins or GO functions in the GNN layer were then concatenated and connected to the scaler output indicating cell state probabilities.   We proposed a two-step strategy to train the model from single-cell multi-omics data. First, we learned TF-cCRE interactions from data to construct the TAM for each gene. This was achieved by training a network for each gene with the same structure as the gene subnet. The objective was to predict the gene expression level from an updating TAM with learnable TF-cCRE interaction parameters. Then, we took the learned TAMs as input and trained the entire network to classify cell states (Fig. 1a) (“Methods”).

  The goal of training regX was to prioritize potential driver TFs (pdTFs) or cCREs (pdCREs) during cell state transitions and provide mechanistic explanations. We achieved this through in-silico perturbations on the trained model. By comparing the predicted cell state probability changes before and after given levels of TF or cCRE perturbations, we prioritized these regulators based on the extent of probability changes in selected samples. Benefiting from the mechanism-informed design, we can also prioritize the target genes of the top-ranked regulators by comparing the expression level changes of the target genes under the same perturbations (Fig. 1b) (“Methods”).

  We applied regX to two single-cell multi-omics datasets, T2D and hair follicle datasets, both provided with paired gene expression and chromatin accessibility data of cells under different states. The T2D dataset was sequenced by 10x multiome technology and profiled human Pancreatic beta cells from non-diabetic, pre-T2D, and T2D donors15; whereas the hair follicle dataset was sequenced by SHARE-seq technology and profiled mouse skin cells undergoing hair follicle development16. Given the abundant resources available for human data in downstream analysis, in the following paragraphs, we will mainly use the T2D dataset to explain the features and capabilities of regX in prioritizing pdTFs and pdCREs, along with their mechanistic and therapeutic significance (Fig. 1c). After that, we will use the hair follicle dataset to briefly discuss structural flexibility and versatility of regX, and highlight its advantage in quantifying the influence of regulators on cell fate determinations (Fig. 1d).


### Data-driven transcriptional regulatory modeling captures TF-cCRE interactions
  The gene subnets stand as cornerstones for the entire network. We designed the input feature matrix, TAM, for each gene subnet by modeling the gene expression process. Instead of intuitively concatenating TF expressions and cCRE accessibilities to represent the transcriptional regulatory activities, we fused information from these two groups of regulators along with their interactions that were learned from data (“Methods”). The philosophy behind this design is that TF concentrations (represented by TF expression levels), chromatin accessibilities of cCRE regions, and interaction strengths between each TF-cCRE pair are three non-isolated components in the process of TFs binding to accessible chromatin regions, thus should be modeled interactively.

  We assessed the informativeness of the designed TAM by evaluating its performance on gene expression predictions across 667 differentially expressed (DE) genes computed from the T2D dataset, with one gene per model. For each gene, we split 64% samples into the training set, 16% into the validation set, and 20% into the test set, and evaluated the model performance using the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) calculated between the true and predicted gene expression levels on test set samples. We compared the performance with models using other features. Results show that the designed TAM outperforms the concatenated TF expression and cCRE accessibility feature vector (average PCC: 0.83 vs. 0.76, p = 2e-174). It also outperforms TAMs without TF-cCRE interactions or with random interaction coefficients (average PCC: 0.83 vs. 0.74/0.75, p < 3e-207) (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 1a–c). Moreover, the learned TF-cCRE interactions are highly robust in repetitive experiments (Fig. 2b). Similar results were observed on 3000 highly variable genes (Supplementary Fig. 1d–h). These results suggest that the designed TAM is more informative than concatenating TF expressions and chromatin accessibilities, and the learned TF-cCRE interaction matrix should contain extra regulatory information.Fig. 2Evaluations of the designed TAM and learned TF-cCRE interactions.a Distribution and boxplot of Pearson correlation coefficients (PCCs) between real and predicted expression levels on 667 DE genes. ### Top-ranked TFs suggest drug repurposing possibilities and new therapeutic targets for T2D
  Beyond prioritizing potential driver regulators in an end-to-end interpretation scheme, the mechanism-informed design of regX enables us to open the “black box” between regulators and cell states and prioritize the regulatory effects of certain regulators on target genes (“Methods”). This unique feature distinguishes regX from existing methods for identifying key regulators, and facilitates investigations of drug repurposing possibilities and novel therapeutic targets in providing possible mechanistic explanations.

  Drugs that interact with the 23 prioritized pdTFs hold profound drug repurposing possibilities. Of the 35 drugs and dietary supplements that interact with these TFs, two prescription drugs (ROSIGLITAZONE and INSULIN) that interact with HNF4A have already been used to treat diabetes27 (Table 1 and Fig. 3d); two dietary supplements, MELATONIN and RESVERATROL, which interact with RORA and FOXO3, respectively, were reported to have an anti-hyperglycemia effect and may help relieve T2D symptoms28–32 (Table 1 and Fig. 3b, e). More interestingly, BROMOCRIPTINE, interacting with FOS, was originally indicated for hyperprolactinemia, acromegaly, and Parkinson’s disease27. It was later repurposed as an adjunct therapy for T2D to regulate blood sugar levels33. However, the way BROMOCRIPTINE works to treat T2D has been unclear. regX identified that the upregulated genes induced by FOS upregulation, including FOSB, MT1E, MT2A, and MT1X, were significantly enriched in metal ion-related biological processes (FDR < 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 9), which are closely related to oxidation. More specifically, genes encoding Metallothioneins (MT1E, MT2A, and MT1X) were among the top six genes that significantly upregulated following FOS upregulation (FDR < 1e-50) (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Data 1). Metallothioneins have been well-known for their antioxidant and antiapoptotic effects, and their role in T2D has gained increasing attention in recent years34–36. A previous study also reported the function of FOS and JUND in preventing cell death from oxidative stress37, consistent with regX’s interpretations (Table 1 and Fig. 3f, g). Consequently, regX offers a possible explanation of how BROMOCRIPTINE works to regulate blood sugar levels in T2D patients. It may function through interacting with the FOS gene in beta cells, thereby alleviating oxidative stress and restoring insulin production (Fig. 3j). These findings indicate considerable potential for repurposing other drugs that interact with the identified pdTFs to treat T2D and insulin-related symptoms.

  The prioritized pdTFs also suggest novel therapeutic targets for T2D. We noticed that TRPS1, ranking 9th in the in-silico downregulation experiment, is one of the few top-ranked TFs that were barely reported to be related to T2D or beta cells (Table 1), but regX predicted that TRPS1 knockout drives beta cell transitions from the decompensated to compensated and normal states (Fig. 3h). Among the top-ranked upregulated genes induced by TRPS1 knockout (Supplementary Data 2), HSP90B1 (rank: 3, FDR: 0.002) and PDIA3 (rank: 10, FDR: 0.0002) are critical for the protein folding process in endoplasmic reticulum (ER), and protect cells from ER-stress-induced apoptosis38–40; PDX1-AS1 (rank: 6, FDR: 0.0004) and ABCA1 (rank: 13, FDR: 0.0008) help promote insulin secretion41,42 (Fig. 3k). Upregulation of these genes suggests relieved ER stress and decreased apoptosis of beta cells, which slow down and potentially reverse T2D progression (Fig. 3l). However, it is worth noticing that the expression changes here seem modest compared to those induced by FOS upregulation (Fig. 3i). It might also be that in a complex system, many small changes can collaboratively lead to a large difference. While these findings are intriguing, further experimental validation is required to assess whether TRPS1 inhibitors could be viable therapeutic agents for T2D patients.


### regX prioritizes cCREs that potentially drive beta cell transitions during T2D progression
  Besides identifying pdTFs, regX is also capable of identifying pdCREs, where the chromatin accessibility alterations may drive cell state transitions. We prioritized cCREs through in-silico perturbations of the opening status of the cCRE regions, where we simulated the opening (in-silico opening) and closing (in-silico closing) of each cCRE region (“Methods”).

  We prioritized the top 5% (1259) cCREs in the in-silico opening and closing experiments, respectively, resulting in a total of 1409 non-overlapping cCREs. We define them as pdCREs. About 6.25% of these pdCREs were found to regulate more than one gene in our model, surpassing the background percentage of 2.67. Notably, we observed that this “multi-gene-regulatory” percentage increases as the number of prioritized cCREs decreases (Supplementary Fig. 10a). Specifically, as high as 30.8% of the top 0.1% cCREs and 12.0% of the top 1% cCREs were found to regulate multiple downstream genes. These results indicate that cCREs capable of simultaneously regulating multiple genes may exert greater influence on determining cell states, which is consistent with our intuitive understanding.

  We also investigated the functional annotations of pdCREs. We found that approximately 93% of these pdCREs overlap with known regulatory elements. Specifically, about 87.9% of pdCREs overlap with known enhancers or silencers, 2.8% overlap with promotors, and 2.3% overlap with CTCF binding sites (Supplementary Fig. 10b) (Methods). These results suggest that regX should indeed have captured the intrinsic gene regulation logic hidden in the data, thereby emphasizing the regulatory roles of functional genomic regions.

  Motivated by the curiosity of whether and how the regulated genes of two types of regulators, TFs and cCREs, overlap with each other, we compared the regulated genes within the 250 kb range upstream and downstream of the pdCREs with the regulated genes of pdTFs (“Methods”). We found that less than half of the 70 genes regulated by the pdCREs overlapped with the regulated genes of the pdTFs (Supplementary Fig. 10c). Moreover, of the top ten genes regulated by the most number of pdCREs, eight do not overlap with the pdTF-regulated genes. These findings suggest that the roles of TFs and cCREs in regulating cell state transitions are to some extent, complementary. They may function through targeting different downstream genes and should be irreplaceably focused on.

  We also noticed that many top-ranked pdTFs and target genes of pdCREs, for example, GLIS3, SGIP1, OPCML, etc., were mapped genes of significant T2D loci in previous GWASs. This led us to investigate whether pcSNPs of T2D can be pinpointed through these top-ranked regulators.

  rs10974438 (chr9:4291928 A > C), which is located at the 1st intron of GLIS3, is an example of pcSNPs that function via regulating the expression of pdTFs. After narrowing down 2441 T2D GWAS loci to 34 that fall into the 250 kb region upstream and downstream of the 23 pdTFs, we extracted the genotype in each sample and performed cis-eQTL analysis on the eight loci with provided genotypes. This resulted in six significant loci (FDR < 1e-12), one of which is rs10974438 (Supplementary Fig. 11a). Data shows that the alternate C allele at rs10974438 is associated with lower GLIS3 expression levels. According to the HaploReg database, rs10974439 is located at an important regulatory region functioning as both promoter and enhancer in Pancreatic islets45. The alternate C allele decreases the sequence-dependent binding affinity of many transcriptional activators, including PDX1, HOXA5, HOXB3, LHX3, etc45. In particular, PDX1 (Pancreatic And Duodenal Homeobox 1) is a master regulator of beta cell fate and positively regulates many genes essential for beta cell identity and functions46,47. This could explain how the alternate allele leads to lower GLIS3 expression in beta cells. Furthermore, GLIS3 downregulation leads to a higher rate of beta cell apoptosis and impeded insulin secretion, which accelerates beta cell dysfunction and T2D progression24,48–51. From the in-silico downregulation experiment, we also observed that GLIS3 knockout promotes beta cell transition from an enhanced functional state to a less functional states (Supplementary Fig. 8a). Taking all the data and knowledge together, we proposed a possible regulation path for the T2D risk C allele at rs10974438 (Fig. 4b): The alternate C allele reduces the sequence-dependent binding affinity of many transcriptional activators, thus lowering the expression level of GLIS3. GLIS3 downregulation speeds up beta cell dysfunction by promoting beta cell apoptosis and reducing insulin secretion, in turn, increases the risk of T2D.

  rs4655617 (chr1:67010654 A > C) is an example of pcSNPs that function via regulating the chromatin accessibility of pdCREs. rs4655617 is one of the two T2D GWAS loci that fall into pdCREs, and is also a significant chromatin accessibility quantitative trait locus (caQTL) of the pdCRE chr1:67010359-67010859 (FDR < 2.3e-5) (Supplementary Fig. 11b). The caQTL result shows that the alternate C allele at rs4655617 is associated with higher chromatin accessibilities of chr1:67010359-67010859. This region is located at the first intron of SGIP1, whose expression levels are also positively correlated with the chromatin accessibilities of this region in samples (Spearman correlation = 0.28, p = 5e-13) (Supplementary Fig. 11c). Data also suggests that the expression level of SGIP1 increases along with T2D progression (Supplementary Fig. 11d). These findings indicate a possibility that the alternate C allele at rs4655617 may promote SGIP1 transcription by increasing the chromatin accessibility of its located region. This type of regulatory role of SNPs was rarely reported in previous studies. SGIP1 functions as an endocytic protein, and is implicated in clathrin-mediated endocytosis, but its exact functions in beta cells remain poorly understood52. We deduce that SGIP1 might impede insulin endocytosis in beta cells, similar to how SGIP1α overexpression reduces transferrin and EGF endocytosis53. When exposed to the increased glucose environment, beta cells with higher SGIP1 expression levels might be less capable of compensating insulin secretion due to excessive inhibition of the insulin receptor signaling pathway, which increases the risk of T2D onset. Collectively, we proposed a possible regulation path for the pcSNP rs4655617 of T2D (Fig. 4c): The alternate C allele at rs4655617 increases the chromatin accessibility of its located region at chr1:67010359-67010859, and in turn increases the SGIP1 expression level. SGIP1 may impede insulin endocytosis followed by insulin secretion in beta cells, so that beta cells with higher SGIP1 expression levels may be less responsive to the increased glucose environment. Individuals with such beta cells may be more susceptible to T2D. The hypothesized function of SGIP1 in this novel regulation path is worthy of further mechanistic investigations.

  Broad applications of the identified regulators in the field of drug and GWAS highlight the superiority and reliability of regX in providing biologically meaningful interpretations.


### regX prioritizes TFs in cell fate determinations during hair follicle development
  We extended the application of regX to study hair follicle development. From the hair follicle dataset, we extracted the developmentally linked transit-amplifying cells (TACs), inner root sheath (IRS), hair shaft (HS) cuticle/cortex, and medulla cells (Fig. 5a). This dataset differs from the T2D dataset in several aspects: it is a mouse dataset, sequenced by a different technology, and focuses on development rather than disease. “Hair follicle development” is already a well-established GO term (GO:0001942) belonging to the biological process category. Therefore, we extracted this GO term and its descendent terms, resulting in a total of 12 terms. We further constructed a directed acyclic graph (DAG) of these 12 terms from the GO database54,55 that describes their relationships (Supplementary Fig. 12a), and extracted genes that are related to these GO terms according to the Gene Ontology Annotation (GOA) database. We adjusted the network structure to accommodate the DAG and protein(gene)-GO relationships by replacing the GCN layer with a graph attention layer (GAT layer), and adding a sparsely connected linear layer between the target gene and GO layer. Moreover, the designed TAM outperformed the concatenated feature vector in the task of gene expression prediction (p = 4e-189) (Supplementary Fig. 12c).

  We conducted two groups of experiments using different data-splitting strategies to evaluate the model performance on cell state and pseudotime prediction (“Methods”). In the first group, all cell states were seen in model training. We used the F1 score and PCC as the evaluation metric for cell state classification and pseudotime regression, respectively. We observed that the model with the GAT layer outperformed the model with two linear layers in pseudotime regressions (average PCC: 0.955 vs. 0.946, p = 0.04), and showed a performance gain, though not significant, in cell state classifications (average F1 score: 0.967 vs. 0.957, p = 0.12) (Supplementary Fig. 12d). We used PCC to evaluate the model performance on pseudotime regression. We observed that the GAT model showed better performance than the controls (p < 0.03) (Supplementary Fig. 12e).   Using the trained model, we performed in-silico upregulation and downregulation to prioritize pdTFs during hair follicle development (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 13) (“Methods”). The top-ranked pdTFs can be very well validated by previous works. For example, the top two TFs, Lef1 and Gata3, were reported to be lineage-priming TFs of the HS and IRS lineage, respectively16,56,57; Sox5 is a global transcriptional repressor16; Gli2 and Tcf12 are associated with the Sonic hedgehog (Shh) signaling pathway, which is essential for embryonic hair follicle development16,58.

  We further focused on pdTFs that determine cell fates at branch points (states 0, 2, and 3). We extracted samples belonging to the branch state of interest and examined their predicted cell state probability changes of each state after in-silico perturbation of TFs. An overall increased probability represents “transitioning to” this state. We observed that TACs under state 0 are almost determinant to transition to state 1 (IRS) if provided with Gata3 upregulation, whereas cell fate determinations for the HS lineage appear to be more complex. Specifically, TACs at state 0 tend to transition to HS cells only when Lef1 is highly upregulated, otherwise their lineage choices are obscure (Fig. 5e–g). These observations indicate that different upregulation levels of even the same TF can result in entirely different cell fates. They also highlight the advantage of regX in quantifying the influence of regulators on cell state transitions.


## Discussion
  Constructing a cross-level model that describes the multi-level regulatory processes in biological systems has been an intriguing yet challenging task. Previous methods have demonstrated incorporating prior knowledge into neural networks to link molecular variations to cellular phenotypes, but there lacks a mechanism-informed network design. With the availability of single-cell multi-omics data, we can incorporate more fundamental regulatory processes into a cross-level deep learning model, and study the molecular mechanisms of dynamic cellular processes at an unprecedented resolution.

  In this study, we developed regX, a deep neural network that models the cross-level regulatory processes within a cellular system. regX is a predictive model from TFs and cCREs to cell states. It is composed of multiple subnets, with each subnet describing the epigenetic and transcriptional regulation process of a gene. We invented a learnable TAM as the input of each subnet. It is a specially designed TF-by-cCRE feature matrix constructed from TF expressions, chromatin accessibilities of cCREs around the gene, and TF-cCRE interactions learned from data. The complex interplay among different subnets (genes) is modeled through a PPI or GO graph embedded in a GNN layer. Representations of the proteins or GO functions were then flattened and connected to the cell state output. We trained the model with single-cell multi-omics data and used an in-silico perturbation scheme to prioritize potential driver regulators that govern cell state transitions and provide further mechanistic interpretations.

  We applied regX to T2D and hair follicle datasets. The effectiveness and reliability of the designed TAM and network structure were validated under both contexts. On the T2D dataset, we prioritized pdTFs and pdCREs that drive beta cell transitions during T2D progression. We further elucidated the implications of the top-ranked regulators in identifying new therapeutic targets, guiding drug repurposing, and pinpointing pcSNPs. Specifically, we proposed TRPS1 as a potential new therapeutic target for T2D, which may be involved in modulating ER stress and beta cell apoptosis, but needs further experimental validation; we computationally reproduced the repurposing of BROMOCRIPTINE to control blood sugar levels, and provided mechanistic explanations not previously established; we also identified five novel pcSNPs of T2D mediated by two types of potential driver regulators, and proposed possible regulation paths for each pcSNP. On the hair follicle dataset, we showed the flexibility of the network structure and its applicability to diverse biological processes. We also discovered that cells treated with different upregulation levels of the same pdTF may exhibit distinct lineage choices at pivotal branch points.

  There are already some methods for identifying key TFs of a certain cell type or condition (e.g., disease)18,19,59, but our major goal is to identify potential driver TFs that direct cellular transitions from one state to another. To our knowledge, only ANANSE60 (and its single-cell version scANANSE61) and sc-compReg62 were developed for this goal. They are both based on differential GRN analysis, but beyond the gene level, there are also interactions between genes and their products that regulate cellular phenotypes. CellOracle63 and MIRA64 can be used to identify key TFs in cell fate determinations, but they can only be applied to continuous cellular trajectories. We incorporated both gene regulation and gene-gene interaction mechanisms into the neural network design, to provide a new perspective on modeling the cross-level regulatory processes and identifying potential driver TFs in both continuous and discrete cellular processes.

  We acknowledge that it is challenging to ensure the inferred regulators are “drivers” because it is hard to justify causality based only on computational analyses. It is also often true even for wet-lab experiments because of many underlying limitations in accessible samples, observations, or feasible experimental designs. Our model was designed according to biological mechanisms and enables the direct prediction of cell state transitions caused by regulator changes. We believe such a mechanism-informed predictive model could better reveal potential driver regulators compared to observational or “black-box” models, so we carefully referred to the identified regulators as “potential drivers”.

  Another important aspect is about the flexibility of the regX framework. The core of regX is the cross-level framework which can identify potential driver TFs or cCREs during cell state transitions. TAM and GNN are important components to realize this design but can be adjusted according to users’ needs. We noticed that in TF prioritization, the top-ranked TFs identified by the model with TAM and concatenated features are highly consistent (Supplementary Fig. 14a, b). This suggests that if no further interpretation regarding TF-cCRE interactions is needed, TAM may also be substituted by the concatenated features in TF prioritization. But in cCRE prioritization, TAM cannot be replaced by the concatenated feature due to the low robustness of the latter model (Supplementary Fig. 14c–f). Regarding the GNN part, users may freely choose GCN, GAT, or other forms of network structures to connect the transcript layer and cell state output. We implemented these alternative structures in our online software for more choices.

  regX is still a preliminary cross-level framework, with ongoing room for improvement. First, the designed TAM for each gene is relatively large, which may impede the network to scale up. Optimizations are required to reduce computational costs and accommodate more genes. Second, we primarily focused on the influence of a single regulator on cell state transitions, with limited explorations on the influence of regulator combinations. Developing an appropriate model interpretation scheme may help address this limitation and provide deeper insights into the synergistic or antagonistic effects of multiple regulators. In addition, the current model is tailored for gene expression and chromatin accessibility data, making it less directly applicable to other omics datasets. The flexibility of regX can be further exploited to study additional biological processes.

  In conclusion, our work integrates the multi-level regulatory processes into a neural network framework, facilitating the prioritization of potential driver regulators and offering a novel approach for mining single-cell multi-omics data. Our work explored the molecular underpinnings of cellular behaviors and disease phenotypes, and advanced our understandings on complex biological processes, such as disease progression and development.


## Methods

### T2D data preparation

#### Preprocessing
  The published T2D 10x multiome dataset was downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under accession code GSE200044. We used Seurat to preprocess the 10x multiome data65. We kept cells with 5e3 < nCount_ATAC < 9e4, 1000 < nCount_RNA < 20000, and percent.mt < 2. For joint embedding of gene expression and chromatin accessibility, we performed principle component analysis (PCA) and latent semantic indexing (LSI) for dimensionality reduction on RNA and ATAC data, respectively, and computed a weighted nearest-neighbor (WNN) graph using the FindMultiModalNeighbors function (reduction.list = list(“pca”, “lsi”), dims.list = list(1:40, 2:30)).


#### Construction of pseudo-bulk samples
  To solve the sparsity problem of single-cell sequencing data, we aggregated cells to construct pseudo-bulk samples. We adopted the same strategy as used in DIRECT-NET to summarize the gene expression and chromatin accessibility profiles of 30 nearest neighbor cells on the WNN graph, respectively66. We used the Aggregate_data function, which is implemented in the R package DIRECTNET, to aggregate cells in each sample separately, resulting in 1394 pseudo-bulk samples in total. Each sample was labeled with “normal”, “compensated” or “decompensated” as its cell state corresponding to its originated non-diabetic, pre-T2D or T2D donor.


### Hair follicle data preparation

#### Preprocessing
  We downloaded a processed version of the hair follicle SHARE-seq dataset from the Python package scglue67(http://download.gao-lab.org/GLUE/dataset/Ma-2020-RNA.h5ad for RNA data and http://download.gao-lab.org/GLUE/dataset/Ma-2020-ATAC.h5ad for ATAC data). Similar to the T2D dataset, we used Seurat for preprocessing. We kept cells with 500 < nCount_ATAC < 3e4, 100 < nCount_RNA < 8000, and percent.mt < 20, and performed two rounds of preprocessing. In the first round, we jointly embedded all cells on a WNN graph computed by the FindMultiModalNeighbors function (reduction.list = list(“pca”, “lsi”), dims.list = list(1:30, 2:30)), and clustered cells on the WNN graph using the FindClusters function (resolution=1.5). We extracted cell clusters that contain TACs, IRS, HS-cuticle/cortex, and medulla cells according to the provided annotation, and performed a second round of preprocessing. Parameters are almost the same as in the first round, except that we clustered cells on the newly computed WNN graph with resolution = 0.7, resulting in ten clusters.


#### Trajectory analysis
  To learn the differentiation path and how far a cell has progressed along the dynamic differentiation process, we performed trajectory analysis on the WNN graph using Monocle3 and obtained the predicted trajectories along with pseudotime for each cell68.


#### Cell state assignment
  To assign cell states, we manually merged the ten clusters into six states. Specifically, we merged clusters along a trajectory without branch points, assigning them altogether as a cell state. For clusters that contain branch points, we assign each one as an individual cell state.


#### Construction of pseudo-bulk samples
  Similar to the T2D dataset, we aggregated cells on the WNN graph using the Aggregate_data function, with 30 nearest neighbors aggregating into one sample. Cells were aggregated in each of the ten Seurat clusters separately, resulting in 832 samples in total. Each sample was labeled with a previously defined cell state and an averaged pseudotime of all cells aggregated in this sample.

#### Output layer
  The output layer of the network represents cell states. On the T2D data, the output layer is a three-node channel representing normal, compensated, and decompensated states, respectively. On the hair follicle data, the output layer is a seven-node channel, with the first six nodes representing the six discrete cell states, and the last node representing the continuous pseudotime.

### Feature and gene selection

#### TF selection
  We downloaded the latest version of PFM matrices of TF binding motifs from the JASPAR database72 and extracted TFs whose expressions were captured in the corresponding dataset. This resulted in 457 TFs for the human T2D dataset and 165 TFs for the mouse hair follicle dataset.


#### cCRE selection
  We selected cCREs within 250 kb upstream and downstream of the target gene, whose accessibilities were captured by the corresponding single-cell ATAC-seq data.


#### Gene selection for the T2D dataset
  We used the FindAllMarkers function (only.pos = TRUE) in Seurat to perform DE analysis for beta cells from non-diabetic, pre-T2D, and T2D donors with adjusted p-value < 0.05. We excluded DE genes that were previously selected TFs, resulting in 667 DE genes in total. These genes were later used for evaluating the performance of the designed TAM in the gene expression prediction task.

  We further ranked the DE genes according to their avg_log2FC and selected at most 100 genes in each state with the highest avg_log2FC, resulting in 297 DE genes in total. These genes were included in regX.


#### Gene selection for the hair follicle dataset
  Similar to the T2D dataset, we performed DE analysis on the ten Seurat clusters with adjusted p-value < 0.05, and removed selected TFs from the DE gene list, resulting in 736 DE genes. We used these genes in evaluating the performance of the designed TAM through gene expression prediction.

  Different from the T2D dataset, we selected genes (not restricted to DE genes) that were included in regX according to GO and GOA, as described in the “Graph neural network layers embedded with biological knowledge” section.


### Performance evaluation

#### Gene expression prediction
  In the ablation study, we evaluated the performance of gene expression prediction using the following feature matrices or vectors: the designed TAM, the designed TAM without TF-cCRE interaction strengths or with random interaction strengths, and the concatenated TF expression and chromatin accessibility vector. PCC calculated between the real and predicted gene expression on the test set was used as the evaluation metric and was calculated using the Python package sklearn. We trained each model for three times under random seeds and took the average of the three PCCs as the final performance on this gene. We used the Python package scipy to perform a paired one-tail t test in comparing the performance of gene expression predictions between different feature groups.


#### Cell state prediction on the T2D dataset
  In the ablation study, we replaced the GCN layer with two linear layers, which accommodate more parameters, and trained each of the two networks ten times under random seeds to compare their performance. The evaluation metric we used is the average F1 score, which was calculated using the Python package sklearn. We used the Python package scipy to perform an unpaired one-tail t test to calculate the comparison p-values.


#### Cell state and pseudotime prediction on the hair follicle dataset
  On the hair follicle data, we evaluated the performance of the model on cell state and pseudotime prediction with two groups of experiments.

  In the first group of experiments, we split the training, validation and test set according to cell state stratification, making sure that all cell states were seen in the training process. We repeated the training process for ten times under random seeds and calculated F1 scores for cell state classification and PCCs for pseudotime regression. In the ablation study, we replaced the GAT layer with two linear layers or with a GCN layer embedded with a PPI network and compared their performance. We also evaluated the performance of the model removing the gene subnets and directly using gene expressions as input. We used the Python package scipy to perform an unpaired one-tail t test to calculate the comparison p-values.

  In the second group of experiments, we left out samples in states 2 and 3 for validation and testing, and only used samples in other states for training, making samples under states 2 and 3 unseen to the network. We compared PCCs of the test set for pseudotime regression in models with the GAT layer, GCN layer, or replaced by two linear layers. We used the Python package scipy to perform an unpaired one-tail t test to calculate the comparison p-values.

### Benchmark of TF-cCRE connections
  We benchmarked regX with SCENIC+ and LINGER on predicting TF-cCRE connections on SNARE-seq17 (downloaded from GSE126074), PBMC 10x multiome (downloaded from the Signac tutorial: https://stuartlab.org/signac/articles/pbmc_multiomic), and simulated DPCL (downloaded from the SCope website: https://scope.aertslab.org/#/scenic-v2/scenic-v2) datasets. We evaluated the prediction results using the same ChIP-seq data as in SCENIC + and LINGER.

  Since the training of regX TAM was based on genes, for each dataset, we selected 3000 highly variable genes using Seurat, excluded genes that are TFs, with high dropout rates, or with unclear genomic annotations, and evaluated different methods on the cCREs surrounding the rest of the genes. For the SNARE-seq and PBMC 10x data, we included cCREs within 250 kb upstream and downstream of the genes; for the DPCL dataset, we changed the range to 100 kb, since the number of simulated cCREs in this dataset is larger than in the real datasets. When comparing the F1 scores, we decided the thresholds of the continuous TF-cCRE interaction scores in regX and LINGER that optimize their F1 scores. We used the Python package sklearn to calculate the F1 and precision scores.


### In-silico perturbation

#### In-silico upregulation of TFs
  For each TF in the model, we added a 0.9-quantile of its expression values across all samples to its original expression values in all samples and used the trained model to predict cell state probability changes for each sample after perturbation. In the case of hair follicle development, we added one, two, and three times 0.9-quantile of the TF’s expression values across all samples to its original expression values, representing low, moderate, and high levels of TF upregulation, respectively.


#### In-silico downregulation of TFs
  For each TF in the model, we zeroed its expression values in all samples and used the trained model to predict cell state probability changes for each sample after perturbation.


#### In-silico opening of genomic regions
  For each genomic region in the model, we set the scaled accessibility scores in all samples to one and used the trained model to predict cell state probability changes for each sample after perturbation.


#### In-silico closing of genomic regions
  For each genomic region in the model, we zeroed the scaled accessibility scores in all samples and used the trained model to predict cell state probability changes for each sample after perturbation.


### Prioritization of potential driver regulators
  We prioritized pdTFs and pdCREs separately in each dataset. For each candidate regulator (TF or cCRE) in each treatment (in-silico upregulation, downregulation, opening or closing), we calculated the total of the absolute cell state probability changes after perturbation in all samples (in the hair follicle dataset) or test set samples (in the T2D dataset), and prioritized the regulators in different treatments accordingly.

  In analyzing TFs that determine cell fates at branch points in the hair follicle case, we first chose a branch state of interest, then extracted samples that belong to this state. We calculated the total of their predicted probability changes of a target state after TF perturbations, and focused on the TFs that induce the most drastic probability increase in the target state. These TFs potentially drive cells from the branch state to the target state.


### Benchmark of potential driver TFs
  We benchmarked regX with SCENIC +, LINGER, and scANANSE on prioritizing potential driver TFs using a TF perturbation dataset23 (downloaded from GSE217215). In this dataset, human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) were treated with TF overexpressions (one TF ORF for each cell, 198 TFs in total), and were then profiled with SHARE-seq after culturing for 4 or 7 days. We used Seurat to preprocess the data, filtering cells with nCount_ATAC > 7e4, nCount_RNA > 10000, nucleosome_signal > 6, and TSS.enrichment < 1.5. After quality control, there were 69,025 cells left with paired RNA-seq and ATAC-seq profiles. These cells were aggregated into 13 major clusters in the original work. We adopted the cluster labels and treated each cluster as a cell state. We identified the ground truth TFs of each cluster following the approach described in the original work, resulting in 93 TFs in total. We then used the 93 TFs to evaluate the performance of regX on TF prioritization, as well as comparing regX with SCENIC +, LINGER, and scANANSE.

  To train regX, we first decided TFs according to the “TF selection” section. This resulted in 553 TFs. Then, we decided target genes to be included in the model. We identified markers for each cluster with adjusted p-value < 0.05 and avg_log2FC > 0.8. After excluding genes that are TFs, there were 275 genes left. This process is similar to selecting genes in the T2D dataset (See the “Gene selection for the T2D dataset” section). After that, we extracted cCREs within 250 kb upstream and downstream of the target genes.

  After training the models, we prioritized potential driver TFs in each cluster. For regX, we perturbed samples in all the other clusters in the test set except the target cluster through in-silico upregulation experiments (See “In-silico perturbation”) and prioritized TFs according to the total of the predicted probability changes. For SCENIC +, LINGER, and scANANSE, we used Regulon Specificity Scores (RSS), t test statistics, and influence scores calculated by these methods, respectively, to rank the TFs.

  To calculate the evaluation metrics, we extracted the top 10 TFs in each cluster inferred by each method. If less than 10 TFs were identified, we kept all identified TFs.


### Prioritization of the regulatory effect of a TF on target genes
  We adopted the same in-silico upregulation or downregulation strategy as used in pdTF prioritization on the trained gene-level networks from the first training step to prioritize the regulatory effect of a TF on target genes. The architectures of the gene networks were the same as the gene subnets, except that their outputs were real gene expression levels instead of hidden gene representations. For a certain TF in the in-silico upregulation experiments, we added 0.9-quantile of its expression values across all samples to the original expression values of the TF in all samples and obtained the predicted expression levels of the gene before and after in-silico perturbation. We quantified the regulatory effect of the TF on this gene by calculating the average log2 fold change of the predicted gene expression levels before and after in-silico perturbation across all samples. We could then obtain a prioritization list recording the regulatory effect of the TF on all target genes. Similar strategies were used in the in-silico downregulation experiments. We used the Python package scipy to perform paired one-tail t test in comparing the predicted expression level changes of a gene before and after perturbation and used the Python package statsmodels to calculate FDR for multiple test corrections on multiple genes.


### GO enrichment analysis
  We performed GO enrichment analysis on the top-ranked regulated genes using the R package clusterprofiler73.


### Functional annotations of pdCREs
  The promoter of each gene was defined as 100 bp upstream and downstream of the gene’s transcriptional start site (TSS). The enhancer list for homo sapiens was downloaded from the EnhancerAtlas2.074. The silencer list for homo sapiens was downloaded from the SilencerDB75. We only kept experimentally validated silencers. We also downloaded a comprehensive list of all regulatory features on the human genome from the Ensembl database76, and extracted CTCF binding sites complementing previously mentioned regulatory elements. We annotated pdCREs in the following order of priority: promoter, enhancer, silencer, and CTCF binding site.


### Comparison of the regulated genes by pdTFs and pdCREs
  We defined the regulated genes of pdCREs as genes within the 250 kb upstream and downstream range of the pdCREs. We defined the regulated genes of pdTFs as the top ten target genes with the highest absolute average log2 fold change values of each pdTF in the in-silico upregulation experiments (See the “Prioritization of the regulatory effect of a TF on target genes” section).


### Identification of pcSNPs in T2D
  We identified pcSNPs by integrating the significant SNP-trait associations summarized from previous GWASs and the genotyping data provided in the T2D study. The SNP-trait association list was downloaded from the GWAS Catalog77. After filtering, there were 2441 significant GWAS loci associated with the trait “Type 2 diabetes”. Genotyping data of the T2D dataset was downloaded from the GEO under accession number GSE170763.


#### Identification of pcSNPs mediated by pdTFs
  Firstly, we filtered SNPs from the 2441 significant T2D GWAS loci, retaining those located within 250 kb upstream and downstream of the 23 pdTFs. This filtering process resulted in 34 SNPs. Then, we used the PLINK toolkit78 to call SNPs at these 34 loci from the genotyping data, resulting in eight SNPs. Subsequently, we performed cis-eQTLs analysis for these 8 SNP-gene pairs using the R package MatrixEQTL79. We used the additive linear model and considered age, sex, and bmi as covariates. After multiple test corrections, there were six significant SNP-gene pairs remaining (FDR<1e-12).


#### Identification of pcSNPs mediated by pdCREs
  Firstly, we identified SNPs located within the 1409 pdCREs from the 2441 significant T2D GWAS loci, resulting in two SNPs. Then, we called SNPs at these two loci from the genotyping data and found only one SNP remaining. After that, we used MatrixEQTL to test whether this SNP is a caQTL of its located region. We used the additive linear model and considered age, sex, and BMI as covariates. The result was positive, with p = 2.3e-05, suggesting that this SNP is a caQTL of its located region.
