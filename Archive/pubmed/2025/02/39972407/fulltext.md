# Bioinformatics-based identification of CTSS, DOK2, and ENTPD1 as potential blood biomarkers of schizophrenia


## Background
  Although schizophrenia is a severe mental disorder that significantly impacts patients and society, there are currently no reliable blood-based biomarkers to assist in its diagnosis. The diagnosis primarily relies on clinical assessment and patient history, a method that is inherently subjective and prone to errors, potentially leading to diagnostic delays. In this study, we aim to utilize bioinformatics approaches to explore potential blood-based biomarkers for the diagnosis of schizophrenia. By employing advanced bioinformatics techniques, we hope to identify key genes and construct an effective diagnostic model, providing the clinic with a more objective and accurate diagnostic tool.


## Methods
  In this research, we employed bioinformatics techniques to identify potential blood-based biomarkers for the diagnosis of schizophrenia. Initially, we selected schizophrenia-associated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database through the datasets GSE27383, GSE38484, and GSE38481. Subsequently, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses on these DEGs to elucidate their biological functions and related pathways. Furthermore, we constructed a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network of the differentially expressed genes to identify key genes and matched them with their target microRNAs (miRNAs). In addition, we assessed the diagnostic potential of these key genes through immune infiltration analysis. The aim of this study is to reveal the roles of these hub genes in the pathogenesis of schizophrenia.


## Results
  Through bioinformatics analysis, we have identified three potential hub genes associated with the pathogenesis of schizophrenia: CTSS, DOK2, and ENTPD1. These genes are significantly correlated with the development of schizophrenia and may serve as promising diagnostic biomarkers for the condition.


## Conclusion
  In this study, we have identified three pivotal genes—CTSS, DOK2, and ENTPD1—that are intimately associated with the pathogenesis of schizophrenia. The discovery of these genes not only enhances the precision of diagnostic efforts for schizophrenia but also provides a robust scientific foundation for the development of innovative treatment approaches for schizophrenia and related disorders. The identification of these biomarkers offers a tangible basis for early, accurate diagnosis, treatment, prognostic assessment, and rehabilitation evaluation in schizophrenia, potentially improving patients’ quality of life and supporting the development of personalized therapeutics and antipsychotic medications.

## Background
  Schizophrenia (SCZ) is a severe, persistent mental illness that often initially manifests in adolescence and young adulthood and is characterized by cognitive, mood, and behavioral disturbances that frequently influence adolescents or adults. Common symptoms of schizophrenia include hallucinations, delusions, distorted thinking, emotional apathy, and social disengagement [1, 2]. The mortality rate among patients with schizophrenia is 11.2-fold higher than that of the general population overall, ≤ 9.7-fold higher than that for persons attempting specific causes, especially suicide, and a mortality rate that is higher than those of the general population for other unnatural factors, such as poisonings, pneumonitis, and other factors in the general population [3]. The global incidence of schizophrenia has increased from 13.3 million in 1990 to 20.9 million in 2016 [4]. This condition contributes to 13.4 million years of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) [4]. According to the The Global Burden of Disease Study of 2015 reported that DALYs for schizophrenia have increased by > 17% since 2005. Schizophrenia is also associated with high rates of disability, productivity loss, and high excess mortality rates due to elevated rates of suicide, substance abuse, and chronic somatic disease as well as heavy socioeconomic burdens on the public health care system [5, 6]. Only clinical observation and symptom assessment are currently available for the exclusionary diagnosis of schizophrenia [7]. However, it was discovered that gene transcripts and genetic polymorphisms markedly differ between patients with schizophrenia and healthy controls. Nevertheless, the underlying genetic and molecular mechanisms of this disorder remain to be elucidated, and reliable diagnostic markers for it are urgently needed [8].

  The GEO (Gene Expression Omnibus; http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo; accessed on September 8, 2023) database contains high-throughput gene expression data submitted by various research institutions worldwide [9]. Here, we screened the GSE27383, GSE38484, and GSE38481 datasets from GEO [10–12]. Their tissue sources were peripheral blood samples from patients with schizophrenia and healthy individuals. We then applied bioinformatics techniques to detect hub genes related to the onset and progression of schizophrenia. We found 55 DEGs (31 up-regulated genes and 24 down-regulated genes) at the intersection of the datasets and performed gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses on them. We identified the hub genes CTSS, DOK2, and ENTPD1 and determined the processes and pathways they regulate of the three hub genes in the initiation and development of schizophrenia were further investigated. Within the field of immunology, schizophrenia is characterized by distinctive features. Given the established link between schizophrenia and microglial involvement, as well as the role of DOK2 in modulating microglial function, we conducted an immunological analysis to explore the differences between individuals with schizophrenia and healthy controls. The present work aimed to explore, identify, and characterize blood markers that could serve in the timely differential diagnosis of schizophrenia.


## Methods
  In this study, we extracted datasets GSE27383, GSE38484, and GSE38481 datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/; accessed on September 8, 2023). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were then discovered by differential analysis using the “limma” package(https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/limma.html) in R (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria). Subsequently, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses on these DEGs to elucidate their biological functions and associated pathways. We constructed a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network using the STRING (https://string-db.org/cgi/input?sessionId=bRs8WRBRck9k%26input_page_show_search=on), and identificated hub genes using Cytoscape (https://cytoscape.org/download.html). Buliding on this foundation, we explored gene-miRNA interactions with NetworkAnalyst (https://www.networkanalyst.ca/), and immune infiltration was analyzed in R. Additionally, we assessed the diagnostic value of key genes through the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (Fig. 1).

### Data sources
  The gene expression datasets analyzed herein were curated from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/; accessed on September 8, 2023). Before the data were uploaded, they were processed to ensure that they were directly analyzable and that significant differences between the disease and control groups could be identified within each dataset. As the present study strictly applied bioinformatics techniques and involved no animal experiments or human clinical trials, no ethical review was necessary here. However, the clinical information was incomplete in certain datasets. Consequently, the selection criteria were limited to platform and disease type. The three datasets consulted herein were based on the following platforms: (1) GPL570 [HG-U133_Plus_2] Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 Array; (2) GPL6947 Illumina HumanHT-12 v. 3.0 expression beadchip; and (3) GPL6883 Illumina HumanRef-8 v. 3.0 expression beadchip. The GSE27383 dataset comprised peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) samples from 43 patients with schizophrenia and 29 healthy individuals. The GSE38484 dataset comprised whole blood samples from 106 patients with schizophrenia and 96 healthy individuals. The GSE38481 dataset comprised whole blood samples from 15 patients with schizophrenia and 22 healthy individuals.


### Data extraction for DEG identification
  The gene expression profiles of GSE27383, GSE38484, and GSE38481 were analyzed using the GEO2R online analytical tool (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/geo2r/). There were significant differences in gene expression between the patients with schizophrenia and those with other conditions. The differential expression screening criteria were applied to other diseases such as cancer. Screening was performed by adjusting the P-values and cutoff thresholds for schizophrenia and revealed virtually no significant differences among the DEGs. It also demonstrated that there were insufficient DEGs for the subsequent analysis. Hence, the inclusion criteria were set to|log fold change (FC)| > 0.1 and p < 0.05 to avoid omitting important DEGs [13]. The GSE27383, GSE38481, and GSE38484 datasets were screened for DEGs. Those with log FC > 0 were deemed upregulated while those with log FC < 0 were deemed downregulated. SangerBox (http://sangerbox.com/; accessed September 8, 2023) was used to construct volcano plots from the data generated by the GEO2R analysis. Venny v. 2.1.0 (http://www.liuxiaoyuyuan.cn/; accessed on September 8, 2023) was used to identify the intersections of the upregulated and downregulated DEGs.


### Functional GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses
  The Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/; accessed on September 8, 2023) was utilized to perform GO and KEGG enrichment analyses on the DEGs and the hub genes. The GO analysis examined biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF). KEGG is a comprehensive database used to explore genomes, biological pathways, diseases, chemicals, and drugs. Here, the results of the GO and KEGG analyses of the DEGs were deemed significant at p < 0.05 and counts were ≥ 3 for the DEGs and at p < 0.05 for the hub genes. Bubble charts were generated online with Microbiology Letter (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/; accessed on September 8, 2023).


### Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network and hub gene analyses
  The PPI networks were constructed using with the open-access online protein-protein interaction analysis tool STRING (https://cn.string-db.org/). The DEGs from GSE27383, GSE38484, and GSE38481 were screened and imported into the STRING database to evaluate the PPI networks. The selection criteria were: (1) minimum required interaction score = 0.15 and (2) default value = 0.4. Visual network maps of the PPI were plotted with Cytoscape (http://www.cytoscape.org/; accessed on September 8, 2023). The CytoHubba (https://apps.cytoscape.org/apps/cytohubba) and MCODE (https://apps.cytoscape.org/apps/mcode) plugins in Cytoscape were used in combination to screen for the hub genes.


### Gene-microRNA (miRNA) analysis
  NetworkAnalyst (https://www.networkanalyst.ca/; accessed on September 8, 2023) was used to identify and plot visual network graphs of the gene-miRNA interactions.


### Correlation analysis of the hub genes and immune infiltration
  The CIBERSOFT deconvolution algorithm (https://bio-protocol.org/exchange/minidetail? type=30%26id=10014632) was used to determine the relative proportions of 22 different immune cells in the peripheral blood of the patients with schizophrenia and the healthy individuals in the GSE38484 dataset. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated for the hub genes and the immune infiltrating cells and visualized with the ggplot2 package (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/ggplot2/index.html) in R.


### Diagnostic value of hub genes
  A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve plotting tool (https://www.xiantao.love/products; accessed on September 8, 2023) was used to determine the diagnostic significance of the hub genes for schizophrenia. P < 0.05 indicated that the hub gene was diagnostically relevant.

### GO and KEGG pathway analyses of DEGs
  We performed GO and KEGG pathway analyses on the 31 upregulated and 24 downregulated DEGs meeting the P < 0.05 and count ≥ 3 screening criteria. The upregulated DEGs were enriched mainly in the cytoplasm, chromatin, and chromosome telomere region associated with signal transduction as well as functions related to DNA and protein synthesis. The downregulated DEGs were enriched mainly in the cytoplasm, nucleus, and lysosomal lumen, apoptosis, the Toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling pathway, and positive regulation of the inflammatory response as well as functions related to binding ubiquitin-protein ligase and proteins. The KEGG pathway analysis revealed that the upregulated DEGs were enriched mainly in transcriptional dysregulation and natural killer (NK) cell-mediated cytotoxicity in cancer while the downregulated DEGs were enriched mainly in apoptosis and ubiquitin-mediated protein hydrolysis (Fig. 4).

The nodes represent these key proteins, while the edges signify the physical interactions or functional associations among them, potentially linking to biological pathways or signaling networks implicated in schizophrenia (Fig. 5).

### Diagnostic evaluation of hub genes
  We conducted receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses on GSE27383, GSE38484, and GSE38481 to determine the sensitivity and specificity of CTSS, DOK2, and ENTPD1 in the diagnosis of schizophrenia. The areas under the curve (AUCs) and the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each gene are shown in Fig. 12. For GSE27383, the AUCs for CTSS, DOK2, and ENTPD1 were 0.674 (95% CI: 0.550–0.797), 0.683 (95% CI: 0.557–0.810), and 0.616 (95% CI: 0.482–0.749), respectively. For GSE38484, the AUCs for CTSS, DOK2, and ENTPD1 were 0.580 (95% CI: 0.501–0.659), 0.635 (95% CI: 0.557–0.712), and 0.602 (95% CI: 0.524–0.679), respectively. For GSE38481, the AUCs for CTSS, DOK2, and ENTPD1 were 0.658 (95% CI: 0.482–0.833), 0.712 (95% CI: 0.547–0.877), and 0.718 (95% CI: 0.545–0.891), respectively. The AUC values for the combination of hub genes in GSE27383, GSE38484, and GSE38481 were 0.759 (95% CI: 0.640–0.877), 0.653 (95% CI: 0.576–0.730), and 0.791 (95% CI: 0.647–0.935), respectively. Figure 12 shows that CTSS, DOK2, and ENTPD1 could potentially diagnose schizophrenia as their AUCs were in the range of 0.60–1.00. Of these, DOK2 demonstrated the highest diagnostic capacity to differentiate between patients with schizophrenia and healthy controls. The AUC for all three hub genes combined surpassed those of the individual markers. Hence, the combination of hub genes had a higher diagnostic value than each separate index.

## Discussion
  Schizophrenia, a severe and chronic mental disorder [14], places a substantial burden on both the affected individuals and society as a whole. Timely and definitive diagnosis is crucial for the initiation of effective treatment and enhancement of patient quality of life [15]. Despite the importance of early diagnosis, no serum biomarkers have been identified for schizophrenia to date. In the present study, limma differential analyses were conducted on the blood samples of patients with schizophrenia and healthy individuals registered in three GEO databases. This analysis identified 55 commonly DEGs, with 31 being upregulated and 24 downregulated. Subsequent KEGG pathway enrichment and GO analyses were conducted to elucidate the roles of these DEGs in the pathogenesis and progression of schizophrenia. The DEGs were predominantly enriched in pathways such as “transcriptional dysregulation in cancer,” “natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity,” “apoptosis,” and “ubiquitin-mediated cytotoxicity,” indicating a link to immune-inflammatory responses. The GO terms associated with these biological processes included “signal transduction,” “positive regulation of inflammatory response,” and “apoptotic process,” all of which are recognized as key elements in the etiology of schizophrenia. Further analysis of the cellular components and molecular functions of the DEGs indicated their involvement in DNA and protein synthesis and degradation. Collectively, these findings suggest that all 55 DEGs are implicated in the etiology and progression of schizophrenia. Collectively, these findings suggest that all 55 DEGs are implicated in the etiology and progression of schizophrenia.

  We then mapped these DEGs onto a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network and identified the top 10 DEGs using CytoHubba. The intersection of these DEGs with cluster modules extracted by the Molecular Complex Detection (MCODE) algorithm revealed three hub genes: CTSS, DOK2, and ENTPD1. These genes were further subjected to gene-miRNA analysis. Utilizing the CIBERSORT algorithm, we compared the immune cell infiltration in the peripheral blood of schizophrenia patients with that of healthy individuals, finding a significant reduction in CD8 T cells in the patient group. The expression levels of CTSS and ENTPD1 were inversely correlated with CD8 T cell abundance, while DOK2 expression showed a positive correlation. ROC analysis demonstrated that the AUCs for these hub genes ranged from 0.60 to 1.00, indicating their diagnostic potential for schizophrenia. Notably, the combined application of all three hub genes yielded a higher diagnostic efficacy than any single gene. These results suggest that CTSS, DOK2, and ENTPD1 are associated with the progression of schizophrenia and may serve as potential biomarkers for the disease. This study leverages comprehensive bioinformatics analysis of data from reputable public databases to elucidate the potential involvement of CTSS, DOK2, and ENTPD1 in schizophrenia. Although our research does not include experimental validation, the reliance on extensive and high-quality public data sources lends a degree of credibility to our findings. Future studies should further validate these findings through experimental methods to enhance the biological and clinical relevance of our conclusions.

  We identified CTSS, DOK2, and ENTPD1 as potential schizophrenia biomarkers. We then investigated their roles and analyzed their related signaling pathways in the onset and progression of this condition.

  The lysosomal cysteine protease cathepsin S (CatS) is highly expressed in antigen-presenting cells (APC) such as dendritic cells (DCs), B lymphocytes, and macrophages [16]. Previous studies highlighted the involvement of microglia in the inflammatory response as well as synaptic pruning and modification [17, 18]. Microglia-mediated inflammation might be a mechanism by which the brain structure is altered in patients with schizophrenia [19, 20]. Meta-analyses confirmed that proinflammatory factors are upregulated in their peripheral sera [21, 22]. Clark et al. (2007) suggested that CatS plays a pivotal role in microglia-neuron communication and regulates an inflammatory response activation pathway [23]. When microglia are stimulated by exposure to lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and high ATP concentrations, the P2 × 7 receptors on their cell membranes are induced and promote the secretion of CatS via the p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway. CatS causes soluble fractalkines (FKN) such as CX3CL1 to be released from the dorsal horn neurons. Thence, CX3CL1 binds the CX3CR1 receptors on the microglia and, by extension, promotes the release of various proinflammatory factors. The CX3CL1-CX3CR1 axis also regulates neurogenesis, synaptic pruning, and cell apoptosis. CatS may participate in microglia activation through the CX3CL1-CX3CR1 axis and elicit inflammatory reactions that cause neuronal degeneration, synaptic dysfunction, and possibly the onset of schizophrenia (Fig. 13).

  Fig. 13Inflammatory response activation pathway involving CatS. When microglia are stimulated by high extracellular ATP and LPS concentrations①, P2 × 7 is activated and CatS is released via the p38 MAPK intracellular signaling pathway②. CatS then induce the secretion of soluble FKN by dorsal horn neurons③④ which feed signals back to the microglia via the CX3CR1 receptor⑤, upregulate the p38 MAPK pathway, and release proinflammatory factors⑥

  Neuron-microglia interactions govern brain development and homeostasis in schizophrenia [24]. As neurons communicate with microglia, the latter regulate the activity of the former and inform them of their status [25]. This bidirectional communication regulates neurogenesis, synaptogenesis, and pruning. It also controls the immune response which, in turn, may influence the normal operation of the central nervous system (CNS) [26]. Schizophrenia may eventually occur after this two-way communication becomes dysfunctional.

  DOK2 pathway I is associated with schizophrenia. Though CD200 is not expressed by microglia, it is nonetheless vital for controlling and maintaining their dormancy as it mitigates immune cell activation and the inflammatory response [27]. Myeloid cells express the CD200 cognate receptor. The CD200-CD200R axis regulates bidirectional communication between neurons and microglia and maintains CNS homeostasis [26]. CD200 and CD200R are cell surface glycoproteins with two immunoglobulin-like structural domains [28]. In the presence of abnormal DOK2, the CD200-CD200R axis balance is dysregulated and the maternal immune system may become overactive. This condition upregulates CD200 and downregulates CD200R on the microglia, thereby breaking their dormancy, causing immune and neurodevelopmental abnormalities, and potentially contributing to the pathogenesis of schizophrenia. DOK2 is associated with three immunological disorders that could adversely affect the microglia. Proinflammatory cytokine upregulation and microglia activation are related to schizophrenia. Microglia mediate the neuronal activity of proinflammatory cytokines [21]. Schizophrenia may be associated with an imbalance in proinflammatory cytokines resulting in increased Th2 and decreased Th1 cytokine release [29]. DOK2 may also be implicated in the abnormal metabolism of kynurenine (KYN) which reduces cognitive flexibility. Patients with schizophrenia exhibit KYNA upregulation as they perform various stress-induced cognitive tasks. Elevated KYNA may increase the likelihood of early task termination. High salivary KYNA may serve as a quantitative biomarker of schizophrenia [30]. KYNA upregulation is also related to oxidative stress indicated by increased levels of harmful reactive chemicals and attenuated antioxidant defense. Oxidative stress may contribute to poor prognosis in patients with schizophrenia [31]. Compared to the general population, patients with schizophrenia exhibit lower plasma antioxidant, superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GPx), and glutathione levels, more abundant reactive oxygen species (ROS), and higher levels of oxidative stress [32–35]. High free radical levels damage the neural system, disrupt oxidative stress, and trigger or exacerbate schizophrenia symptoms by decreasing free radical formation and attenuating alterations in the oxidative stress response. The administration of antioxidants may mitigate schizophrenia symptoms. DOK2 abnormalities affecting the microglia may result in neurodevelopmental defects and the mild impairment of behavioral, cognitive, and/or social functioning characteristic of schizophrenia. Microglia release cytokines and chemical signals that regulate neuronal growth and function. In response to defective DOK2, microglia produce complement which contributes to excessive pruning and the loss of dendritic spines. These responses adversely affect brain function and cause emotional instability and social impairment [36]. DOK2 influences synaptic plasticity by releasing extracellular signaling molecules that modulate the rate and extent of clearance. Abnormal promotion or inhibition of synapse function has a negative impact on learning, attention, and memory [37]. DOK2 phosphorylation in response to CD200R binding recruits and activates Ras-specific guanosine triphosphatase-activating protein (Ras GAP). It also triggers intracellular inhibitory pathways that regulate the activation of human myeloid cells [38, 39] by modulating their binding and causing defects in myelin sheath development. By this mechanism, the speed and quality of nerve impulse conduction are reduced and neurological functions are impaired.

  DOK2 pathway II is associated with schizophrenia. DOK2 controls the tyrosine kinase signaling pathway through negative feedback. The actions of DOK2 involve the Ras-RAF-MAPK pathway (which causes Ras signaling inhibitors to block MAPK activity) and the phosphatidylinositol 3-carboxykinase (PI3K)-Akt pathway (which inhibits the phosphorylation and activation of protein kinase B (Akt)) (Fig. 14). The foregoing mechanisms influence cell proliferation and migration, neuronal growth, synaptic transmission, and schizophrenia [40]. The Ras family (Ras, Rap1, and Rap2) of guanosine triphosphatases, their downstream ERK, JNK, and p38MAPK, and the PI3K signaling cascades regulate the physiology and pathophysiology of learning and memory as well as other processes in humans. Pathway hypoactivation and hyperactivation affect synaptic plasticity [41].

  Fig. 14Mechanism by which DOK2 inhibits MAPK. DOK2 recruits negative regulators of Ras signaling, inhibits Akt phosphorylation and activation, alters neuronal development and synaptic transmission, and contributes to schizophrenia pathology

  ENTPD1 encodes ectonucleoside triphosphate diphosphohydrolase-1 (ENTPD1/CD39) which collaborates with ecto-5`-nucleotidase (NT5E/CD73) to convert extracellular adenosine triphosphate (eATP) into adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) which then forms extracellular adenosine (ADO) [42–44]. ENTPD1 downregulation leads to the accumulation of eATP and disrupts the balance between it and ADO. Elevated eATP levels on myeloid cells activate P2 × 7 receptors [45–47] which are expressed in the nervous and immune systems and participate in inflammasome-mediated proinflammatory cytokine release through various signaling pathways [45]. The abnormal activation of microglia in response to the upregulation of proinflammatory cytokines may result in synaptic phagocytosis, aberrant synaptic connectivity, behavioral and cognitive deficits, and memory disorders (Fig. 15) [48–50].

  Fig. 15Mechanisms underlying the effects of ENTPD1 on behavioral and cognitive deficits. ENTPD1 encodes the enzyme extracellular nucleoside triphosphate diphosphate hydrolase 1. In association with CD73 and other molecules, ENTPD1 converts eATP to ADP and cAMP, and eATP accumulates when ENTPD1 is downregulated. The eATP activates and binds the P2 × 7 receptor on myeloid cells, thereby causing the latter to release abundant proinflammatory cytokines via inflammatory vesicles. The proinflammatory cytokines abnormally activate and alter the synaptic pruning function of microglia. The latter may erroneously clear blue inhibitory synapses, reduce synaptic connectivity, and cause behavioral and cognitive deficits

  Here, bioinformatics analysis and mechanistic studies identified and confirmed the association of CTSS, DOK2, and ENTPD1 with the pathogenesis of schizophrenia. The discoveries made herein facilitate further exploration into blood biomarkers of schizophrenia and provide insights into its etiology, diagnosis, and treatment.


## Conclusion
  In this study, we employed cutting-edge bioinformatics analysis to uncover significant associations between the hub genes CTSS, DOK2, and ENTPD1 and schizophrenia. These findings not only offer a novel perspective on the mechanisms by which these genes contribute to the onset and progression of the disease but also underscore their importance as potential biomarkers. The identification of these genes aids in early diagnosis, predicts disease progression, and treatment response, thereby facilitating personalized therapy for schizophrenia. Our work provides a crucial molecular foundation for the etiology, diagnosis, and treatment of schizophrenia and points the way for future research directions.
