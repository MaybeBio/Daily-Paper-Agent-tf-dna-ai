# Substitutions at rheostat position 52 of LacI have long-range effects on the LacI conformational landscape


## Abstract
  In proteins, amino acid changes at “rheostat” positions exhibit functional changes that vary with the substitution chosen: some substitutions enhance function, some are like wild-type, some are partially detrimental, while others abolish function. One way that substitutions might exert their complex effects is by altering protein conformational landscapes. To test this, we studied five substitutions of V52 in E. coli LacI, an experimentally-known rheostat position. For each variant, we mapped the accessible conformational landscapes by performing molecular dynamics simulations at ambient conditions and under three perturbations: increased pressure, binding to allosteric ligand “ONPF”, and ONPF plus pressure. The simulated DNA binding domain landscapes were compared to published experimentally-measured parameters, and the results suggest that complex combinations of dynamic parameters and/or additional simulations in the presence of DNA are needed to predict DNA binding specificity. For the variants regulatory domains all landscapes displayed boundaries similar to wild-type, but changes within the boundaries were unique. Of these, V52A/ONPF was striking: The regulatory domains for ONPF-bound, wild-type LacI are in an “Open” conformation and, experimentally, ONPF enhances DNA binding. Four variants responded to ONPF like wild-type, but ONPF binding to V52A shifted these domains to a “Closed” conformation that is associated with diminished DNA binding for wild-type LacI. This finding predicted that ONPF’s allosteric regulation of V52A would change from “anti-inducer” to “inducer”, which we experimentally validated in vivo and in vitro. This supports the hypothesis that substituting rheostat positions can alter function by altering the relative populations on protein conformational landscapes.


## Introduction
  During protein engineering and evolution, amino acid substitutions at individual “rheostat” positions can have varied and complex outcomes on protein function [1–4]. Indeed, rheostat positions act like volume knobs, providing opportunities to “dial” function up or down by varying the substituted amino acid. Experimental studies of rheostat positions have documented changes in a wide variety of functional parameters, including binding affinities [5,6], allosteric coupling between two separate binding events [3,5,7,8], and ligand specificity [5,9]. At some rheostat positions, amino acid substitutions lead to simultaneous, independent contributions in multiple functional parameters [1,5,7,8]. For example, mutations at V52 of the Escherichia coli lactose repressor protein (LacI) have disparate effects on allosteric response and the binding affinities for different DNA operators, as shown in Figure 1. Furthermore, the observed functional changes often do not correlate with side chain chemistries, and many rheostat positions do not directly contact ligands [1,2,4]. Finally, substitutions at rheostat positions do not appear to have large effects on the overall protein conformation [1,10–12]. Thus, the biophysical mechanism(s) by which rheostat substitutions exert their effects is intriguing. One possibility is that substitutions at rheostat positions alter various aspects of the protein dynamics [13].

  To assess the effects of substituting rheostat positions on protein dynamics, we have been focusing on LacI [13–15]. LacI has been a particularly useful model because molecular dynamics simulations can be compared to extensive experimental, mutational, and structural studies [16–22]. Functionally, LacI is a transcriptional repressor protein that is allosterically regulated by the binding of small molecules. More than 40% of the positions in E. coli LacI are rheostat positions that alter function [8]. Structurally, LacI’s minimal functional unit is a homodimer [23–25], as shown in Figure 2. The two DNA binding domains of the dimer comprise one DNA binding site (see Figure S1); where the regulatory domain of each monomer contains one allosteric site (two per dimer). Some “inducer” allosteric ligands – such as isopropyl ß-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) – diminish LacI binding to DNA [26]. Other “anti-inducer” allosteric ligands – such as orthonitrophenyl-β-D-fucoside (ONPF) – strengthen DNA binding and thereby reduce transcription [26].

  LacI’s differently liganded states have been associated with structural changes observed using x-ray crystallography [24,25,29,30], small angle x-ray scattering [31,32], nuclear magnetic resonance spectrometry [33–36], and hydrogen/deuterium exchange-mass spectrometry [37]. One prominent change involves the conformations of the regulatory domains, which can be characterized as being (i) “Open”, as observed when DNA and anti-inducer ONPF were bound (PDB 1EFA; [24]), or (ii) “Closed”, as observed for apo- (PDB 1LBI) and IPTG-bound LacI (PBDB 1LBH) [29,38,39]. Changes in conformation were also detected in high-pressure fluorescence studies of WT LacI [14,40,41]. Recently, the transitions between these experimentally observed conformations were explored with molecular dynamics simulations of wild-type LacI (WT), using pressure changes, ligand binding, and domain truncation to perturb the conformational ensemble available to the regulatory domains of WT LacI. The simulation results agreed well with extensive experimental results and prior simulations by other groups, and were used to map a conformational landscape accessed by different liganded states [14]. The results suggested that (i) the energy wells of the landscape are broad and shallow and that (ii) the stability and hydration of the “hinge helix” in the linker connecting the DNA binding domain to the regulatory domain (see Figure 2) plays a key role in these transitions [14].

  Interestingly, the LacI linker also contains a high density (9 of 12 positions) of rheostat positions [3,4]. Of these, position 52 – for which valine is the WT residue – particularly stands out for the breadth of changes associated with alternative side chain substitutions [4,5,19]. Experiments with sets of substitutions at V52 (herein referred to as V52X) reveal widespread effects on transcription repression, DNA binding affinity, specificity, and allosteric response; results for a subset of these variants are summarized in Figure 1. Indeed, position 52 displays nearly “perfect” rheostatic control of binding to and repression via the O1 DNA operator [4,5]. The functions associated with V52X variants sampled nearly the full range of accessible values for these parameters: from “better” than wild-type (V52A), to near wild-type (V52I), to a range of partially-diminished values (V52G and V52Q and others [4,5]), to completely “dead” (V52E). Structurally, the V52 side chains of each LacI monomer are the point of closest approach between the two hinge helices of a DNA-bound LacI dimer [42], as shown in Figure 2B. The V52 side chains do not directly contact DNA but are near other amino acids that do [43]. Furthermore, the alpha carbon of V52 is ~3.0 nm from the regulatory domain’s allosteric site [24].

  We previously assessed the effects V52X substitutions in dimeric LacI using an approach that coupled all-atom, explicit solvent, molecular dynamics simulations with force perturbation [13]. Those results showed that the V52X substitutions altered dynamic flexibility of the DNA-contact positions, and that the flexibility changes correlated with changes in measured binding affinities for the “O1” DNA operator [13]. However, because the V52X substitutions also changed the specificity of binding [27] to different operator sequences [5] (see Figure 1), the correlation observed between altered flexibilities and O1 binding affinities cannot provide a complete description of the biophysical relationships governing DNA binding. One possibility is that the V52X substitutions affect operator specificity by allowing LacI to sample conformations not assessed in the original flexibility study.

  Thus, we were intrigued whether the additional experimental differences among V52X variants could also be reconciled with dynamics changes. Here, to address that question, we performed dynamics simulations of LacI V52X variants with and without additional perturbations: increased pressure and binding to anti-inducer ONPF. This represents a logical extension of our previous studies of the WT protein [14]. The ensembles for each V52X variant sampled conformations very similar to those observed for WT [14]. However, within this common conformational landscape, each V52X variant showed different sensitivities to perturbation. No clear correlations were found between the conformational changes of the DNA binding domains and changes in DNA binding affinities and specificities; this suggests that additional simulations are needed and/or that altered DNA binding derives from complex combinations of effects on conformational ensembles and dynamic flexibilities to explain this functional change. Nevertheless, the simulated changes of the regulatory domains predicted that ONPF binding to the V52A variant would switch its allosteric regulation from an anti-inducing to an inducing effect, providing a means to experimentally test the simulations. Here, we also report results from in vivo and in vitro experiments that did indeed validate this prediction, thereby providing another example in which simulations of protein dynamics prove useful for understanding the complex effects that arise from substitutions at rheostat positions.


## Results and Discussion
  As described in the Introduction, various conformational changes have been experimentally associated with differently-liganded states of LacI. Using these structures as refence points, our previous simulations of WT LacI mapped the protein conformational landscape by perturbing the proteins with different ligands and pressures [14]. Increased pressure is known to shift conformational equilibria towards the state with the lowest overall volume [44,45]. Hence, one can use changes in pressure to help explore the conformational landscape available to proteins that display conformational flexibility, such as LacI [14].

  In this work, classical explicit-solvent all-atom molecular dynamics simulations were performed for the V52A, V52I, V52Q and V52G substituted LacI variants that, in experiments, exhibited a range of functional effects [4,5] (see Figure 1). We first modeled the side chain mutations onto the LacI dimer structure extracted from the DNA and ONPF-bound crystal structure (PDB 1EFA; [24]). We then used simulations under a variety of conditions to map the conformational landscapes available to each variant. For each variant, simulations started from the Open (PDB: 1EFA) conformation and were performed for 1 μs. In addition to simulations under ambient conditions (1 bar, no ONPF), simulations were performed in the presence of the anti-inducer ONPF and at an elevated pressure of 3 kbar both in the presence and absence of ONPF.

  We next used the simulations to assess any V52X effects on the overall LacI structure. For rheostat positions in other proteins, a variety of computational and experimental studies suggested that their substitutions have few, if any, effects on the protein’s overall conformation (reviewed in [1]). Consistent with this, the substitutions at LacI rheostat position 52 that were modelled in this work had minimal effects on the overall protein shape, as indicated by the length of the first principal axis (Figure S2). Since prior SAXS experiments showed that removing DNA caused an increase in the long axis of LacI [31], presumably due to unfolding of the hinge helix in which position 52 is located, we also assessed simulated changes in the radius of gyration. At 1 bar (no anti-inducer ONPF), the radius of gyration showed slight differences between WT and the V52X variants (Figure S3), with the most notable change being a reduction in the radius of gyration observed for V52Q (~0.2 nm). We conclude that global changes in the WT and mutant LacI structures were minimal during the simulation. This conclusion agrees with results from prior, separate simulations of the V52X variants that assessed the dynamic flexibilities of individual amino acid residues in the DNA binding sites [13].


### The conformations of the substituted hinge helices are stabilized by the rest of the LacI protein
  Rheostat position 52 is located in the middle of the LacI hinge helix, and hinge helix folding/unfolding has long been implicated as important for binding operator DNA [29,31,46–50]. Thus, we next investigated the effects of V52X substitutions on the secondary structure of the two hinge helices under ambient and 3 kbar pressure. To that end, we computed the root mean square deviation (RMSD) from an ideal helix for (i) each hinge helix in the simulated LacI V52X structures and (ii) from simulations of the isolated peptides in aqueous solution (Figure S4). The comparison between the helical percentages for the helices in the WT structure and the isolated aqueous peptide sequence provides information regarding the role of the local protein environment towards the stabilization or destabilization of this important region. The percentage of time that the helix remained folded (RMSD < 0.1 nm) was then determined; results are shown in Figure 3.

  Over the course of the 1 bar simulations, the isolated aqueous WT hinge helix exhibited unfolding, with only a small percentage of average helicity remaining. This was also observed for all V52X variants, although the aqueous helix with V52I remained helical nearly 50% of the time. At 3 kbar pressure, the isolated aqueous hinge helices for WT, V52G, and V52Q unfolded even more, whereas helicity increased by 25% in V52I and V52A. By comparison, the hinge helices in the intact protein structures at both pressures were stabilized for all but one subunit in the V52I mutant at 1 bar. For three variants, helicity was further enhanced at higher pressures, presumably due to changes in the local hydration of the hinge helices.

  When interpreting these results, it is important to note that, compared to NMR experimental data, the AMBER14SB force field with the TIP3P water model used here has been shown to underpredict the helical propensity of alanine and overpredict the helicity of valine, glutamine, and isoleucine [51]. Therefore, we must be careful about over-analyzing the simulated helical propensities. Nonetheless, relative to the aqueous helices, enhanced helicity was observed for 19 of the 20 simulated hinge helixes in the LacI WT and V52X homodimers (Figure 3). Thus, this is a general and reproducible feature of our simulations, and we conclude that the presence of the rest of the protein promotes helix folding. This finding is also consistent with previous experimental studies, which concluded that extrinsic factors, beyond changes in intrinsic helical propensities of the hinge helix, contribute to the altered DNA binding affinities of the V52X variants [5].


### Changes in hydration of the hinge helices
  In our previous WT simulations at 1 bar, changes in hinge helix hydration appeared to be strongly associated with the “Open” to “Closed” transition experienced by the regulatory domains early in the simulation; in contrast, hydration changes were absent for >50% of other positions in WT LacI [14]. The hinge helix hydration changes in WT arose as the starting DNA/ONPF-bound conformation of the crystal structure responded to the loss of these two ligands in the simulation. Thus, we next compared the hydration changes at the V52X substitution sites for the five simulations at 1 bar (no ONPF) before and after the Open to Closed transition experienced by WT LacI. For the V52X variants, the transition was confirmed by inspecting changes in the angle of the regulatory domains (indicated in Figure 2).

  Figure S5 illustrates the relative probability of locating a water molecule around position 52. Like WT LacI [14], all variants showed changes in hydration between the Open and Closed states. For V52A, water was essentially absent from both monomers of the Closed state. This finding is consistent with the two alanine chains of a dimer being very closely packed; position V52 is the point of closest approach between the two hinge helices in WT [29], and enhanced packing of these side chains could provide one explanation for the enhanced DNA binding affinity of V52A (see Figure 1). We also determined the number of water molecules within 0.5 nm of position 52 (Table S1). The largest change in the number of hydration waters was observed for Q52 on moving towards the Closed state. This finding aligns with the distance analysis, where the distance between hinge helices (Q52A-Q52B) increased (~2 nm) for V52Q at 1 bar without ONPF (see below).

  While a role for hydration changes in the stability of the hinge helices seems reasonable, it is less clear if (or how) these hydration changes may affect the larger conformational changes of the regulatory domains. Nevertheless, all five simulations of these homodimers showed the same trends for both of their hinge helices where the Open to Closed conformational change of the regulatory domains was accompanied by hydration changes of the hinge helices. This agrees with the experimental and computational evidence from other studies [5,29,33,37,43,52] that the hinge helix has intra-protein communication with the regulatory domain that is important for allosteric communication between the DNA binding and regulatory domains.


### Simulations of V52X variants display both short- and long-range conformational changes
  Next, we considered that the different operators for which the V52X variants showed altered specificity (Figure 1) have different spacings of their DNA half sites [5] (described in the Figure S1 legend). Each DNA half site is bound by one LacI DNA binding domain (Figure S1), and the LacI hinge helices contact a region between the DNA half-sites. In addition, many experimental and computational studies agree that the orientations of the DNA binding domains, the hinge helices, and the regulatory domains are important to allosteric communication between the DNA and allosteric binding sites [5,29,33,37,43,52]; the magnitudes of allosteric response also changed among the V52X variants [5]. Thus, we next assessed changes in the domain-domain orientations of the V52X simulations.

  To that end, we monitored the relative orientations of (i) the DNA binding domains, using the distance between Cα of V15A-V15B (subscripts indicate the two monomers of a dimer) and (ii) the hinge helices using the distance between Cα of X52A-X52B. In addition, we assessed the distances between the (iii) DNA binding and regulatory domains, using the distance between Cα V15A-N142B and V15B-N142A (the two options within the dimer are hereafter indicated as “A/B” and “B/A”), and (iv) hinge helices and regulatory domains, using the distances between Cα of X52A/B-N142B/A. For the current V52X simulations, the time histories of all distances and angles under four conditions (1 bar and 3 kbar, −/+ ONPF) are shown in the Supplemental Information (Figure S6–S11). Together, these plots show that the final ensembles of conformations populated by each simulation were stable; that is, once the initial conformational change was complete, the major characteristics of each variant’s ensemble did not vary.

  The mean distance changes, compared to the crystal structure of WT LacI, are shown for all systems in Figure 4. Please note the varying scales on the Y-axis of Figure 4. The results are summarized as radar plots in Figure 5. During the simulations, the V52X variants and WT LacI deviated from the crystal structure by varying degrees. In the absence of ONPF, large deviations were noted for the V15A-V15B and X52A-X52B distances at 1 bar, especially for V52Q. Deviations in the V15A-V15B distances likely arose from the increased mobility of the DNA binding domains in the absence of the DNA. At 3 kbar (no ONPF), the deviations from the crystal structure values were reduced in X52A-X52B (except for V52A) and X52A/B-N142B/A. The addition of ONPF also perturbs these distances at both ambient and 3 kbar.

  When the simulated structural differences were compared to measured experimental parameters for DNA binding affinity and allosteric response (Figure 1), no obvious correlations were observed. Instead, for each V52X variant, the set of simulated changes was unique from all other sets (Figure 5). Thus, associating changes in dynamics with a variant’s functional effects on DNA binding specificity (as shown in Figure 1) may require complex combinations of dynamic parameters and/or additional simulations in the presence of DNA. Alternatively, some of the differences observed among the ensembles of the DNA-binding domain and hinge helix orientations may arise from the degree of sampling in the current simulations. That is, the simulated time may not allow for a full search of conformational space for such a large protein. Fortunately, the conformational space describing the relative arrangements of the regulatory domains is more accessible during this time regime. Previous simulations showed that transitions of the regulatory domains matched very well with experimental benchmarks, and the sensitivity of the WT ensemble to perturbation suggests that the conformational ensembles are separated by low energy barriers that are readily accessible by simulation [14]. Thus, we next considered the conformations of the regulatory domains in more detail.


### Conformational changes of the WT LacI regulatory domains serve as a reference landscape
  To assess the relative positions of the regulatory domains for each simulated LacI dimer, two useful metrics were (i) the angle (θ) between the two vectors connecting the center of mass of the two N and C-subdomains of the protein (see Figure 2), and (ii) the distance (d) between the Cα atoms of the V96 residues in chain A and chain B (see Figure 2). These two metrics delineate the Open and Closed states determined from the crystal structures of differently liganded states and were used to generate a two-dimensional reaction coordinate with which to map conformational changes observed for the regulatory domains [14]. The angles and distances determined from crystal structures serve as reference points on this landscape: The coordinates of O were determined from WT LacI bound to DNA and anti-inducer ONPF [24,29]; those for O’ were from LacI bound to DNA with an empty allosteric pocket [29]; those for C were from LacI bound to inducer IPTG in the absence of DNA [29].

  Previously, we showed that simulations of WT LacI at different pressures, both with and without ONPF, populated regions near these experimentally known reference points (Figure 6) [14]. Among various controls, this prior study simulated the 18 unique WT homodimers in the crystal structure 1EFA in the presence of DNA and anti-inducer ONPF to show that conformational changes were reproducible [14]. When DNA and ONPF were removed and dynamics were simulated at 1 bar, the regulatory domains quickly transitioned from the Open towards the Closed conformation via the O’ conformation (Figures S6–S7 and Figure 6 black) [14,29]. Increased pressure (3 kbar) prevented the system from accessing the Closed conformation, with the ensemble occupying a distinct region of the landscape with d ≈ 1 nm and θ ≈ 18° (Figure 6, magenta); we speculated that this region of the landscape might be important for LacI binding to different DNA operator sequences [14]. At both pressures, binding to the anti-inducer ONPF also shifted the ensemble away from the Closed conformation toward the Open conformation (Figure 6, orange and plum), which is consistent with the experimental observation that ONPF enhances DNA binding affinity. Together, these simulations mapped out a conformational landscape for the LacI regulatory domain comprising several shallow basins separated by relatively low energy barriers.


### Simulations predict that V52X substitutions have long-range effects on the regulatory domain conformations
  As noted above, the three reference points for the LacI conformational landscape were determined from LacI bound to different allosteric effectors. Accordingly, we hypothesized that LacI ensembles that occupy regions near the Closed conformation have decreased DNA binding affinity (like IPTG-bound WT LacI), whereas ensembles that occupy regions near the Open conformation would have enhanced DNA binding (like ONPF-bound WT LacI). Using this framework, we next explored whether the altered DNA binding affinities of the V52X variants correlated with shifts in the conformational landscape of the regulatory domains.

  For 1 bar in the absence of ONPF, the time trajectories for two reference metrics are shown in Figure S6–S7 and the corresponding conformational landscapes are provided in Figure S12. Contrary to our expectation that altered DNA binding affinities would correlate with shifts on the conformational landscape, the ensembles for all variants were centered near the Closed conformation (Figure S12 and small, open circles in Figure 7). Even V52A, which has enhanced DNA binding and thus was predicted to remain closer to the O and O’ reference points, was similar to WT LacI. This reinforces our earlier conclusion that predicting the effects on DNA binding specificity may require complex combinations of dynamic parameters and/or additional simulations in the presence of DNA. Nevertheless, the concordance of these five independent simulations supports the reproducibility of the simulations.

  Next, we perturbed each variant three different ways, with (i) increased pressure, (ii) inclusion of ONPF, and (iii) pressure plus ONPF. The angle and distance metrics are shown in Figure S6–S7 and the corresponding conformational landscapes are provided in Figure S13–S15. Results for all the variants are summarized in Figure 7, with the arrows highlighting the effects of enhanced pressure (left panel) and ONPF binding (right panel). Upon perturbation, the V52X substitutions appear to exhibit long range effects on the conformational landscapes of the regulatory domains.

  With increased pressure (Figure 7, arrows on left panel; Figure S13), the conformational ensembles for most V52X variants shifted closer to the O and O’ states, albeit to varying degrees. An intriguing exception was V52G (Figure 7, cyan arrow on left panel), which shifted in the opposite direction, closer to the Closed conformation. Nonetheless, the V52G ensemble remained within the boundaries sampled by WT LacI under different perturbations, as did the other V52X variant ensembles. Again, no direct correlations could be made between the magnitude of simulated changes and the known DNA binding affinities.

  In the presence of anti-inducer ONPF, the V52X variants again showed distinct responses. At ambient pressure, the addition of ONPF had a small effect on the ensembles for WT and most variants (Figure S14 and Figure 7, small filled circles and connecting arrows on the right panel) that was in the expected direction of being closer to O’. The notable exception was V52A, which shifted in the opposite direction with the addition of ONFP (Figure 7, right panel, red arrows). Strikingly, this was similar to the conformation previously shown to be adopted by WT LacI bound to inducer IPTG [14,30,38]. As such, the V52A simulation raised the intriguing possibility that the allosteric effect of ONPF is inverted, causing induction (diminished DNA binding/repression) rather than anti-induction.

  The effects of ONPF binding to the variants were further magnified at 3 kbar (Figure S15 and Figure 7, right panel, large circles), as indicated by longer arrows than for the 1 bar simulations. Again, the direction of the V52A conformational shift was reversed relative to the other variants. In addition, ensembles for V52Q and V52I were centered in the region of the landscape that WT occupied at 3 kbar without ONPF (d ≥ 1 nm), which is the only region for which we lack an experimental benchmark. The fact that three different simulations populate this region of the conformational landscape suggests that it is readily accessible, even if it has not (yet) been captured in a resolved crystal structure.

  In summary, although the V52X simulations all started in the Open conformation and experienced similar conformational changes at 1 bar, each variant responded differently to three separate simulations with perturbations. Thus, the conformations of the regulatory domains appear to be sensitive to long-range amino acid changes in the hinge helices. This result complements those from independent simulations of the V52X variants, which showed that intra-protein couplings were also associated with hinge helix substitutions [13]. We previously concluded that the landscape around the functionally important Open and Closed conformations must be relatively flat for the WT protein [14]. This could explain why the ensemble of LacI conformations is easily perturbed by either pressure, ONPF binding, or amino acid substitutions. The new simulations of LacI mutants presented here clearly support this view. It is also possible that repeat simulations of a single LacI variant would also sample different regions of the accessible, flat landscape. Fortunately, the simulations in the presence of ONPF, particularly the results for V52A, provide an opportunity to experimentally test their validity.


### Experimental validation of ONPF anti-induction for V52A
  To test the simulation predictions that ONPF is an inducer of V52A, we next experimentally assessed the effects of this ligand on the V52X variants. First, we assessed in vivo repression of the O1 operator in the presence of varied concentrations of IPTG inducer and ONPF anti-inducer. The results are shown in Figure 8. In this assay design, we used a plasmid-based LacI system in which the O1 operator controlled expression of a YFP reporter gene. Then, we used flow cytometry to measure the YFP expression levels at different concentrations of IPTG and ONPF. As expected, IPTG induced all V52X variants and WT LacI (Figure 8, circles). Although the ONPF titration curves were limited by its solubility, data for WT and three variants showed the enhanced repression expected for this anti-inducer (Figure 8, triangles). Strikingly, V52A shows induction by both IPTG and ONPF, concordant with the simulation predictions (Figure 8, blue symbols).

  To further confirm this finding, we purified the WT and V52A proteins and monitored binding to O3 operator DNA in the absence and presence of ONPF using biolayer interferometry (BLI). Here, the O3 operator sequence, with weaker binding than O1 for WT LacI [53], was chosen so that the enhanced affinity associated with anti-inducer binding could be readily detected. For these studies, we used an experimental design in which the height of the BLI signal at equilibrium was known to reflect the fraction of DNA-bound LacI [53]. Again, WT LacI showed the expected increase in binding, whereas V52A showed diminished DNA binding in the presence of ONPF (Figure 9).


## Conclusion
  These simulations of the LacI 52X variants, along with previous independent simulations of substitutions at the same position under ambient conditions [13], support the hypothesis that changes arising from amino acid substitutions at this rheostat position propagate over long distances through the protein. Importantly, aspects of the simulations agree well with experiment: The prior simulations show a correlation between altered flexibilities of the DNA binding contact positions and DNA binding affinities [13]. In the current simulations, the conformational landscapes of the V52X variants show good agreement with experimentally known conformational changes. Strikingly, the simulations predicted that the response of the V52A mutant to the perturbations was different from other mutations, suggesting the binding of ONPF would also be different. These simulation predictions were subsequently validated by in vivo and in vitro experiments. It is also striking that a single amino acid change, far from the allosteric ligand binding site, can have such profound effects on the protein as to reverse the effect of an allosteric ligand.

  Thus, both simulations and experiments suggest that the changes alter the dynamics and the occupied regions of the landscape, without substantially changing the features of the landscape itself. This is consistent with results from studies of other proteins [10,11,43], which suggest that substitutions at rheostat positions do not cause large changes on the overall protein conformation. As such, this may be a general mechanism by which amino acid changes at rheostat positions can cause functional change.

  Nevertheless, even though a “simple” metric could predict the reversal of the allosteric effect for V52A, no clear patterns emerged between simulated structural changes and experimentally observed changes in DNA binding specificity or the magnitude of the allosteric response to IPTG. Thus, we expect that complex combinations of dynamic parameters – such as the two studies we have completed – and/or additional simulations in the presence of DNA are needed to improve predictions of altered DNA binding specificity. The dynamics and vibrations of DNA are also known to be sequence dependent [54], and complementary dynamics of the two macromolecules may make critical contributions to the affinity of a protein-DNA complex.


## Methods

### System setup
  The boundaries of the LacI domains shown in Figure 2 are as follows: The first 1–49 positions, known as DNA binding domain, contain a helix-turn-helix that binds to the major groove of the DNA and connects to the hinge helix [24]. Positions 50–58 belong to the “hinge helix” which interacts with the minor groove of the DNA [24]. In the absence of DNA, the hinge helix appears to be unstable [29,31,46–50] and simulations suggest that residues 1–61 can move freely relative to the regulatory domain (residues 62–333) [14]. Positions 45–62, which encompasses the end of the DNA binding domain and the hinge helix, has been designated as a “linker” that joins the DNA-binding to the regulatory domain. The regulatory domain consists of two subdomains, an N-subdomain (positions 62–161 and 293–320) and a C-subdomain (positions 162–289 and 321–329). Allosteric ligands bind to a pocket in between the N and C subdomains, with one binding site per monomer. Dimeric LacI lacks the C-terminal leucine heptad repeat, positions 340–357, that facilitate tetramerization of full-length LacI [23].

  For simulations, the modified crystal structure of dimeric LacI (PBD code 1EFA; [24]) with missing atoms (in residues 2, 26–37 in chain A and 2, 31, 36, 37, 44 in chain B) added via the AMBER leap module was provided to us by Paul Campitelli and S. Banu Ozkan [13]. Operator DNA was removed for all simulations, as was ONPF anti-inducer for the WT simulations in the absence of ONPF. Each monomer of this LacI dimer contained 328 amino acid positions. Since the crystal structure does not contain the first residue, only positions 2–329 were used in simulations. This starting structure was previously used in separate simulations by the Ozkan lab [13] and the Smith lab [14].

  Next, the 52nd position of LacI (valine, V) was mutated to alanine (V52A), isoleucine (V52I), glutamine (V52Q), or glycine (V52G). Since their crystal structures are not available, mutations were modeled onto the initial WT structure using the PyMOL software [28]. All systems were solvated with TIP3P water molecules in a rhombic dodecahedron simulation box for which the distance between two parallel faces was 15 nm. Counterions (6 Na+ ions in each system) were added to neutralize the charge. All systems had the same charge since the substituted amino acids are all neutral. All simulations were performed in the presence and absence of anti-inducer ONPF. Force field parameters used for ONPF can be found in our publication for WT LacI [14].

  Simulations of the isolated hinge helix peptides were conducted for the blocked (N-Acetyl and C-methylamide) nine-residue hinge helix, NRVAQQLAG. The initial helical coordinates for these simulations were obtained from residues 50–58 in the 1EFA crystal structure. Again, mutations were modeled onto the initial crystal structure using PyMOL software [28].


### Molecular dynamics simulations
  Atomistic MD simulations were performed using the AMBER14SB force field [55] and the GROMACS 2016 or 2016.4 versions [56]. All the simulations were performed in the isothermal isobaric ensemble (NpT) at 300 K temperature, and both 1 bar and 3 kbar pressure. All the protein bonds were constrained using the LINCS algorithm [57], and water with the Settle algorithm [58]. A time step of 2 fs was used to integrate the equations of motion with the Leap Frog algorithm [59]. Electrostatic interactions were determined using the particle mesh Ewald (PME) technique [60], with 0.12 nm Fourier grid spacing being used. The Verlet cut-off scheme was used with a 0.9 nm cut-off distance for van der Waals and electrostatic interactions. Periodic boundary conditions and the minimum image convention were applied to all the systems. The steepest descent algorithm was used for energy minimizations. The systems were then equilibrated up to 100 ps by gradually increasing the temperature (100, 200, and 300 K) with heavy atom position restraints (1000 kJ mol-1 nm-2) and the Berendsen T-coupling and Berendsen isotropic P-coupling approaches. The constant temperature and pressure were achieved using Parrinello-Rahman pressure coupling [61,62] and the Nose-Hoover temperature coupling [63,64], for the production simulations. All the production simulations were continued up to 1000 ns.


### Analyses of molecular dynamics trajectories
  The angle between the two vectors connecting the N-subdomain and C-subdomain of chain A and chain B was computed for each system. This calculation served as a basic metric to assess the dynamic motions of the regulatory domain in each variant. Further details on the methodology can be found in our previous WT LacI paper [14]. The measured angle in the 1EFA (Open/O) crystal structure was 23°, whereas in the 1LBH (Closed/C) structure it was 14°. Another low-resolution DNA bound structure (PDB:1LBG), which has an empty allosteric binding pocket, in a slightly different Open conformation (O’) was also used as a reference. The measured angle for 1LBG was 17°.

  The distance between the Cα atoms of residue 96 in chains A and B was also used to monitor the motions of the regulatory domains. In the 1EFA (O) crystal structure, this distance measured 0.84 nm, while in the 1LBH (C) structure it was 0.49 nm. The measured distance for 1LBG (O’) was 0.76 nm. This measurement roughly reflects the separation of the two domains. Figure 2 illustrates the representation of the two vectors used for the angle calculation and the residues involved in distance metrics. All the distance metrics, radius of gyration, and length of the principal axis were calculated using GROMACS software.

  The probability distribution of water molecules (giw) around residue 52 for the WT and variants were determined at 1 bar. The first 10 ns of the production runs were used to calculate the Open state giw of all variants, since the calculated angle (θ) was close to the Open state angle during this time. The 90–100 ns segments of the trajectory were used to calculate the Closed state giw. For WT, the 1–60 ns segment and the 100–160 ns segments were used to determine the hydration of the Open and Closed ensembles.


### In vivo experimental measures of ONPF effects on V52X repression
  To test the effects of the V52X mutations on the in vivo dose response of LacI, we used a previously described plasmid system (the pVER system from [18]; the annotated plasmid map is included with the Supplementary Material as “SI file 2.pVER plasmid map.gb”. Briefly, that plasmid system contains two expression cassettes: i) a cassette for constitutive expression of the full-length tetrameric form of LacI or the V52X mutants, and ii) an inducible cassette, regulated by the lac O1 operator to control expression of yellow fluorescence protein (YFP). For each of the V52X mutants, the appropriate coding sequence was inserted into the pVER plasmid in place of the wild type coding sequence (each coding sequence used is included with the Supplementary Material as “SI file 3.LacI V52X CDSs for in vivo assay.csv”). The plasmids were transformed into strain MG1655Δlac (MG1655 with the entire lac operon deleted [65]). For each variant, two independent transformants were chosen (i.e., two colonies were picked from agar plates after transformation), and the plasmid sequence for each was verified using whole-plasmid sequencing (Plasmidsaurus).

  A previously described automation method for reproducible growth and measurement was used to measure the in vivo dose response with flow cytometry [66]. For the dose-response measurements, the mean YFP expression level was measured at zero ligand, 5 non-zero concentrations of ONPF (3-fold dilution series spanning from 24.69 μmol/L to 2000 μmol/L), and 6 non-zero concentrations of IPTG (3-fold dilution series spanning from 8.23 μmol/L to 2000 μmol/L). For each LacI variant, two biological replicates were measured (i.e., two transformants).

  To improve the accuracy of the measurements of basal expression and ONPF response, non-fluorescent controls were also measured with each batch of flow cytometry measurements: MG1655Δlac transformed with the pAN1201 plasmid from [67]; pAN1201 was a gift from Christopher Voigt (Addgene plasmid # 74695; http://n2t.net/addgene:74695; RRID:Addgene_74695); it has the same origin of replication, kanamycin resistance marker, and LacI expression cassette as pVER, but no fluorescent protein expression cassette.

  Flow cytometry data was analyzed using the flowgatenist automated gating and data analysis procedure ([68] https://github.com/djross22/flowgatenist). For the results shown in Fig. 8, the mean YFP fluorescence is reported after calibration to molecules of equivalent fluorescein (using Rainbow Calibration Particles, Spherotech Cat. #RCP-30-20A, see [69]). For each measurement, the mean fluorescence is shown with the non-fluorescent control value subtracted.

  The fit lines shown in Figure 8 and Figure S16 were obtained by fitting the data to a Hill equation model using Bayesian parameter estimation by Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling with CmdStanPy (https://mc-stan.org/cmdstanpy/). Fits were performed using all of the replicate data for each LacI variant. MCMC sampling was run with 4 independent chains, 1,000 warmup iterations and 1,000 sampling iterations per chain, the adapt delta parameter set to 0.99, and the max treedepth parameter set to 15. To assess convergence of the MCMC sampling, the Gelman-Rubin R^ diagnostic was computed for each fit and the result was less than 1.05 for all model parameters. The error bars shown in Figure 8 and Figure S16 show the width of the posterior estimate of the error for each cytometry measurement (± one standard deviation). This error estimate includes contributions from the estimated experimental error of each replicate and the replicate-to-replicate variability. Full details of the statistical model used for the Hill equation fit is included in the Supplementary Material as “SI file 4.Stan model for Hill equation fit.txt”.


### In vitro experimental measures of ONPF effects on DNA binding by purified WT and V52A LacI
  The V52A substitution was re-generated on the pLS1 plasmid that contains the coding region for tetrameric LacI [70], using the methods previously reported [5]. The codon change was verified with Sanger sequencing (ACGT, Wheeling, IL). WT and V52A proteins were purified according to [5]. The final protein concentration for each assay was determined from its absorbance at 280 nM using an extinction coefficient of 0.597 ([mg/ml]cm)−1 [71]. For these experiments, concentrations are reported as molarity of monomeric LacI; each monomer binds one allosteric effector. WT LacI was from two independent purifications and V52A was from one protein purification.

  Biolayer interferometry (BLI) was used to monitor DNA binding, as previously described [53]. We previously found that the kinetic assays traditionally monitored with BLI had un-interpretable association and dissociation curves [53]. Thus, the experimental design used here monitored signal of each sample at equilibrium. In brief, DNA binding assays were performed in Filter Binding Buffer that consists of 10 mM Tris-Base, 150 mM KCl, 0.1 mM EDTA and 0.3 mM DTT (added fresh before use), pH 7.4. The fiber optic tip used for BLI (High precision Streptavidin (SAX) Biosensors, item # 18–5117, Sartorius, Germany) was modified with streptavidin, which bound to biotinylated DNA operator. The O3 operator was carried on a double stranded 45 base pair oligomer (Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, IA). The sequences of the lacO3 forward and reverse strands (underlined) were embedded in a 45-mer oligo as follows [72]:

  Assays were performed using a Octet RED96 biolayer interferometer (ForteBio, Menlo Park, CA), which has the capacity to simultaneously monitor eight tips. Experiments were designed so that the effects of eight different protein concentrations and ONPF could be directly compared. In our prior study, the concentration of immobilized DNA appeared to be high enough to allow tetrameric LacI to simultaneously bind two separate operators [53], which in itself enhances DNA binding [73]. We wished to avoid this “looping” so that we could better detect any ONPF-enhanced DNA binding. Thus, for the current experiments, O3 operator was loaded to a BLI wavelength shift of ~ 0.1 nm. Protein concentrations were chosen to be near the middle of a DNA binding curve (the estimated Kd for WT LacI is >10−7 M [53], so that either enhancing or diminishing effects of ONPF could be detected.

  For each assay, the BLI fiber optic tips were hydrated for 35 min in FBB buffer prior to commencing the binding experiment in 96-well, black plates (item number 655900; Greiner Bio-one). The stages of each BLI experiments included: (1) Buffer baseline (60 sec); (2) blocking nonspecific binding with 0.1 mg/ml BSA solution in FBB (300 sec); (3) washing in FBB (30 sec); (4) immobilization of biotinylated lacO3 DNA from a stock comprising 40 nM DNA (~48 sec); (5) washing in FBB (30 sec); (6) association of WT or V52A LacI with or without 1 mM ONPF (8000+ sec). Each curve was normalized to the signal after step (5), and the plateaus of association at equilibrium were assessed. These values correspond to fraction protein bound [53]. At high protein concentrations, the “equilibrium” phase started to diminish over the course of the experiment. This appears to correspond to protein aggregation, which was visually evident in some samples.
