# Mebendazole induces ZBP-1 mediated PANoptosis of acute myeloid leukemia cells by targeting TUBA1A and exerts antileukemia effect


## Graphical abstract
  Graphical abstract


## Background
  Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a highly aggressive hematological malignancy. A significant proportion of patients who initially achieve remission subsequently develop resistance to chemotherapeutics, experience relapse, and progress to refractory leukemia. Moreover, gerontal patients often face challenges in tolerating the toxic side effects associated with intensive chemotherapy, resulting in a poor 5-y ear survival rate [1], [2]. However, advancements in scientific knowledge regarding the underlying biology of AML over the past decade have led to the emergence of novel treatment options, such as targeted agents and cell therapies. These innovations have shown promise in improving outcomes for select patient populations [3], [4]. Nevertheless, the widespread adoption of these innovative treatments is hindered in economically disadvantaged regions due to their exorbitant costs. Consequently, the identification of cost-effective, potent, and low-toxicity drugs for AML treatment holds immense significance as a valuable adjunct to current clinical approaches.

  In recent years, Z-DNA-binding protein 1 (ZBP1) was first observed in bone marine-derived macrophages of mice infected with Influenza A virus (IAV) as an internal sensor that senses the virus and activates the NLRP3 inflammasome by cutting Caspase-8. At the same time, the three death modes of cell apoptosis, necroptosis and pyroptosis are induced, suggesting that there is crosstalk between these three death pathways [5]. However, it is unclear whether these three cell death pathways are activated independently or regulated by the same upstream signal. In 2019, researchers found that ZBP1-PANoptosis multiprotein complexes (called PANoptosome) are formed during IAV infection, which provide molecular scaffolders for key molecules of the above three programmed death pathways and complete complex regulation of cell death, confirming that this is a new mode of programmed cell death and named PANoptosis (Pyroptosis-Apoptosis-Necroptosis, PANoptosis) [6].

  Evidence shows that PANoptosis can occur under many conditions, such as bacterial infection, viral infection, tumor, cytokine storm and other pathological conditions [7], [8], [9], suggesting that targeting PANoptosis pathway related molecules can be a new idea for clinical treatment of inflammatory diseases [10]. In solid tumors, sulconazole induces PANoptosis of esophageal cancer cells by triggering mitochondrial oxidative stress, thus exerting anti-tumor effects [11]. Polylactic acid inhibits TNF-α-induced prostate cancer cell PANoptosis through metabolic reprogramming, resulting in tumor development [12]. In addition, studies have found that oncolytic virus triggers ZBP-1-mediated PANoptosis, which can enhance the body's anti-tumor immune response [13]. These results indicate that drugs that induce tumor cells to produce PANoptosis have powerful antitumor effects. However, no drugs have been reported to induce PANoptosis in leukemia.

  In this study, Mebendazole (MBD), an FDA-approved cost-effective anthelmintic drug, was screened from a library of small molecule drugs. MBD blocked cell cycle targeting TUBA1A induced ZBP-1 mediated PANoptosis in AML cells, and demonstrated strong anti-tumor effects in both cell line-derived xenograft and patient-derived tumor xenograft models derived from human AML cell lines and primary AML blasts, respectively. Furthermore, no hepatorenal or hematopoietic toxicity was observed in normal mice treated with the same dosage. These findings provide evidence for the potent anti-AML activity of MBD in preclinical assessment, with significant clinical implications.


## Materials and Methods

### Reagents and antibodies
  Mebendazole (T 1155) was purchased from TargetMol (Wellesley, USA), and resuspended in DMSO for in vitro experiments or in 20 % β-cyclodextrin at the expected concentration for in vivo experiments. DiscoveryProbe™ FDA-approved Drug Library was purchased from APExBIO (L1021, USA). Necrostatin-1 (T 1847), Necrosulfonamide (T 6904) and Z-VAD(OMe)-FMK (T 6013) were obtained from TargetMol. Primary antibodies for western blot used were as follows: p-MLKL (ab187091, Abcam), MLKL (#14993, CST), p-RIP3 (#93654, CST), RIP3 (#13526, CST), p-RIP1 (#65746, CST), RIP1 (#3493, CST), cleaved caspase-1 (#4199, CST), cleaved caspase-3 (#9661, CST), NLRP3 (#15101, CST), TUBA1A (ab95966, Abcam), GAPDH (AF0006, Beyotime), and β-actin (ACTB) (AF0003, Beyotime). Antibodies for flow cytometry used were as follows: human CD45-FITC (304006, Biolegend, USA), CD11b-APC (301310, Biolegend) and CD11c-FITC (337214, Biolegend).


### Cell culture
  The leukemia cell lines K562, Jurkat, HEL92.1.7, KG-1, Raji, HL60, and Molt4, and glioblastoma multiforme cells U87 MG and U118 MG were acquired from Shanghai Cellbank or ATCC by vendors and were confirmed further using short tandem repeat genotyping. K562, Jurkat, HEL92.1.7, KG-1, Raji, HL60 and Molt4 cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 medium with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) (FSP500, Excell Bio, China) and 1 × Primocin (ant-p m-1, invivoGen, French). U87 MG and U118 MG cells were grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium with 10 % FBS, and 1 × Primocin.


### Microscopy morphology
  To examine the morphology of cell death, AML cells were seeded in 48-well plates at about 70 %–80 % confluence and then treated to the indicated stimulants. After that, static bright field cell images were captured using EVOS XL Core microscope (AMEX1000, ThermoFisher).


### Flow cytometry and analysis
  For cell cycle analysis, cells were harvested and fixed in ice-cold 70 % ethanol overnight and then stained with propidium iodide/RNase Staining Buffer (550825, BD Biosciences Pharmingen, USA). For cell differentiation detection, MBD-treated THP-1 and U937 cells were centrifugatively collected, stained with CD11b-APC and CD11c-FITC, and then detected by flow cytometry. Stained cells were analyzed with CYTEK flow cytometer (DxP Athena™) and data were processed using FlowJo V10 software.


### Cell viability
  As described previously [14], the AML cells were seeded, and stimulated by relevant stimulants. When the stimulation time was reached, the ATP level in the cells were measured by CellTiter-Lumi™ Luminescent Cell Viability Assay Kit (C0065, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instruction.


### Immunoblotting
  The treated AML cells were collected by centrifugation with 300 g for 8 min, and washed twice in cold PBS and then lysed with RIPA Lysis Buffer (Beyotime). The lysates were quantified, and then the proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, immunoblotted, and visualized using Tanon 5200 Chemiluminescent Imaging System (Tanon Science & Technology).


### Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay
  The AML cells were seeded in 48-well plate, and then treated with indicated stimulants. The culture supernatant was collected and analyzed for human IL1β level by using human IL1β kit (#EH001, Excell Bio, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.


### RNA-seq
  As described previously [14], [15], [16], leukemia cells were stimulated with MBD, and then the cells were harvested and total RNA was isolated. Libraries preparation were performed with using NEBNext® UltraTM RNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina). The reads were mapped to the genome using Hisat2 v2.0.5. The DESeq2 R package was used to analyze differential expression. The Benjamini & Hochberg method was used to adjust P values.


### Mass spectrometry analysis
  MBD-treated AML cell samples were analyzed by mass spectrometry by win innovate biotech company. Specifically, cell lysates were concentrated, incubated with iodoacetamide, and after proteolytic digestion, mixed with iTRAQ reagent (AB Sciex, USA) dissolved in isopropanol, followed by termination of the reaction with MilliQ water. The samples were then loaded into nanoViper C18 (Acclaim PepMap 100, 75 μm × 2 cm) trap column, separated on the Easy nLC 1200 system (ThermoFisher, USA), and analyzed on an analytical column (Acclaim PepMap RSLC, 75 μm × 25 cm C18-2 μm 100 Å). Finally, tandem MS data were acquired on a ThermoFisher Q Exactive mass spectrometer (ThermoFisher) fitted with a Nano Flex ion source using DDA (data-dependent acquisition) mass spectrum techniques.


### Molecular docking
  To analyze the binding affinities and modes of interaction between the tubulins and drugs, the molecular structure of MBD was retrieved from PubChem Compound (https:/pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The 3D coordinates of TUBA1A (PDB ID, 3j6e), TUBA1C (PDB ID, 3e22), TUBB (PDB ID, 3j8x) and TUBG1 (PDB ID, 1z5v) were downloaded from the PDB (https://www.rcsb.org). For docking analysis, all protein and molecular files were converted into PDBQT format with all water molecules excluded and polar hydrogen atoms were added. The grid box was centered to cover the domain of each protein and to accommodate free molecular movement. Molecular docking studies were performed by Autodock Vina 1.2.2 (https://autodock.scripps.edu/).


### Lentivirus infection
  AML cells were seeded in 24-well plate at a density of 2.5 × 104 cells per 200 µL, then transfected with lentiviral vector pLV [Exp]-EGFP: T2A: Puro-EF1A > hTUBA1A [NM-001270399.1] or negative control (VectorBuilder, China) at a multiplicity of infection of 25. After 24 h, the culture medium was replaced according to the protocol provided by the manufacturer, and then EGFP-positive cells were sorted by flow cytometry. TUBA1A level in cells was detected by western blot to analyze overexpression efficiency.


### Cell line derived tumor xenograft
  As described previously [14], [15], [16], for THP-1 leukemia mice model, THP-1 cells stably expressing the firefly luciferase gene were injected into the tail vein of B-NDG mice (1 × 106 cells per mouse). Seven days after injection, the mice were randomly grouped and treated intraperitoneally with 200 μl vehicle or MBD once every two days for 3 weeks. For U937 leukemia mice model, U937 cells (6 × 105 cells per mouse) were used in B-NDG mice. Three days after injection, the mice were randomly grouped and treated with 200 μl vehicle or MBD once every two days for ten days. The statistical significance of survival rate was analyzed by log-rank test. After treatment, leukemia progression was determined by HE staining and/or flow cytometry in the spleen, liver, and bone marrow from randomly three mice per group. Furthermore, MBD effects were analyzed in BALB/c mice. Blood was gathered from the BALB/c mice and analyzed for complete blood count and blood chemistry.


### Primary AML cell assays
  Primary AML cells derived from whole peripheral blood from patients with AML were isolated as described previously [14], [15], [16]. The primary AML cells were treated with MBD or vehicle (DMSO) for 24 h, then the cells were collected to detect cell viability, and culture supernatant was analyzed for human IL1β level as described above.


### Patient derived tumor xenograft
  As described in the section of cell line derived tumor xenograft, female B-NDG mice were injected with human primary AML blasts by tail vein. When the mice developed upper abdominal enlargement, dimmed hair, or other ethically restricted conditions, the mice were sacrificed and bone marrow were taken to analyze the proportion of human CD45. If the proportion of human CD45 in the bone marrow reaches more than 90 %, it indicates that the primary AML cells have successfully expanded in mice. Human CD45 positive cells from these mice were injected into new B-DNG mice for amplification. After screening two generations of mice, a stable third generation of patient-derived xenograft model mice was obtained, which was used to analyze the anti-leukemia effect of MBD. Blood or bone marrow collections were performed to monitor leukemia onset in mice, which was assessed by staining for human CD45 expression using anti-human CD45 antibody staining. After confirmation of engraftment, the mice were randomly grouped and treated intraperitoneally with 200 μl vehicle or MBD (50 mg/kg) once every two days for about 4 weeks. The primary endpoint of this experiment was body weight losing greater than 15 % or signs/symptoms of progressing disease, including loose and dull hair, and limitation of hind limb movement. Once reach the end of the experiment, the liver, spleen, and bone marrow were gained for paraffin embedding and HE staining and/or flow cytometry to analyze the leukemia progression.


### Statistical analysis
  Data were showed as mean ± SEM. Statistical software GraphPad Prism 7.0 was used for data analysis. Comparison between only two groups was implemented by two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test; comparisons among three or even more groups were analyzed by ANOVA with post-hoc test (multiple comparisons, one-way ANOVA for one categorical independent variable, while two-way ANOVA for two categorical independent variables). Differences were identified statistically significant at P < 0.05.

  All mouse experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of China Science and Technology Industry Holding (Shenzhen) Co., Ltd (approval number 202300113). The primary leukemia cells specimens were derived from leftover diagnostic samples from patients, and this study was approved by Institutional Research Ethics Committee of Shenzhen Second People’s Hospital (approval number 2023-064-01PJ).


## Results

### The FDA-approved anthelmintic MBD exerts potent anti-AML activity
  For exploring potential therapeutic agent with strong clinical applications, we screened a comprehensive 5000 small molecule drug library by utilizing inflammatory cell death as the primary determinant (Supplemental Fig. S1A). This led to the identification of Mebendazole (MBD), an FDA-approved broad-spectrum anthelmintic drug, capable of inducing THP-1 cell death. The MBD significantly increase the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokine IL1β (Supplemental Fig. S1B), indicating its role in promoting inflammatory responses alongside cell death. Subsequently, the in vivo efficacy of MBD was evaluated using a highly invasive THP-1 leukemia cells in B-DNG (NOD-SCID Il2rg-/-) mouse model (Fig. 1A). Treatment with MBD resulting in substantial reductions in the total tumor burden by 68.23 %, 95.24 %, and 98.86 % at weeks 2, 3, and 4, respectively, compared to the control group of mice model (Fig. 1B). The treatment also significantly inhibited tumor cell invasion in multiple critical organs, including the bone marrow, liver, and spleen (Fig. 1C), highlighting the MBD potent anti-leukemic activity. Furthermore, MBD treated mice led to substantial improvement in survival rate than control mice, with extending the median survival of treated mice by 16.5 days (P = 0.0006, Log-rank test) (Fig. 1D). Additionally, the treatment also effectively prevented weight loss, highlighting its productive and supportive role in maintaining overall health during treatment (Fig. 1E).   For the assessment of potential in vivo toxicity of MBD, BALB/c mice with similar genetic backgrounds were administered with same dosage of target drug. No adverse effect on liver or kidney function and nor any hematopoietic system dysfunction were observed (Fig. 1F, Supplemental Fig. S3), confirming that MBD has a low toxicity profile in in vivo. These findings collectively validate the MBD drug strong efficacy in AML tumor cells and the observed safety profile suggested that MBD could be a promising candidate for combating AML.


### MBD induces PANoptosis in AML cells
  We previously identified MBD (Fig. 2A) as a potent inducer of inflammatory cell death (Fig. 2B, Supplemental Fig. S1C). To further elucidate the precise mechanisms behind MBD-induced cell death, we performed a detailed microscopic examination of cellular morphology. The analysis revealed that MBD triggers a multifaceted cell death process characterized by both apoptotic and necroptotic/pyroptotic. Specifically, we found that the cell morphology in death included both apoptotic bodies and necroptotic or pyroptotic membrane swelling (Fig. 2C). These findings clearly suggest that MBD triggers a combination of apoptotic and inflammatory death pathway, providing insights into its potential mechanisms of action. Complementary Western blot further analyzed the findings, showing upregulation of associated proteins with necroptosis (p-RIP1, p-RIP3, p-MLKL), apoptosis (cleaved caspase-3), and pyroptosis (NLRP3-caspase-1) (Fig. 2D). Treatment with Necrostatin-1 (Nec-1, RIP1 inhibitor) or Z-VAD (OMe)-FMK (Z-VAD, pan-caspase inhibitor) each significantly reduced MBD induced cell death in AML cells (Fig. 2E). Notably, the combination of Z-VAD with Nec-1 further enhanced the reduction in cell death (Fig. 2E), indicating a synergistic effect among the pathways. These results confirm that MBD simultaneously activates multiple cell death pathways as PANoptosis. PANoptosis is a newly defined programmed cell death mediated by ZBP-1 and including multiple forms of death [6].   RNA-seq analysis revealed that MBD significantly upregulates inflammation related factors in AML cell line, as highlighted by the red markings in Fig. 2H. KEGG pathway further identified the involvement of critical inflammatory signaling pathways, including Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, TNF signaling, and NFκB signaling pathway (Fig. 2I). Importantly, MBD specifically induced IL-1β secretion in AML cell lines, including THP-1, U937, and HL60, while this effect is not observed in any other original leukemic cell lines (Fig. 2J). Additionally, MBD-induced IL1β secretion in AML cells was almost completely abolished by Z-VAD (Fig. 2K). Collectively, these observations suggest that MBD-induced PANoptosis, which includes apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis, exhibits a preferential of myeloid cells, indicating a targeted mechanisms of action for MBD in these cell lines.


### MBD targets TUBA1A inhibition to block cell cycle and induce cell death
  In recent years, some studies have documented the potential of MBD to elicit anticancer effects through modulation of intracellular signaling pathways, for example, it has been reported that MBD induces AML cell differentiation [17]. We previously examined the morphology of cells treated with MBD and found that a subset of the surviving cells exhibited a dendritic differentiation phenotype (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, through the utilization of RNA-seq and subsequent gene ontology analysis, we determined that the induction of MBD resulted in the upregulation of genes associated with leukocyte differentiation (Supplemental Fig. S4A). The flow cytometry analysis showed that the protein level of surface CD11b and CD11c proteins on AML cells did not change after MBD treatment for 24 h (Supplemental Fig. S4B), but that was significantly upregulated after MBD treatment for 48 h (Supplemental Fig. S4C and S4D). These results suggest that MBD-induced differentiation of AML cells may be an indirect effect of MBD on the inflammatory mediators produced by AML cells in static cell culture.

  However, the precise molecular target of MBD in AML cells remain to be elucidated, which means facilitating the development of new pharmaceutifiable targets for discovery. MBD role is previously reported as anthelmintic drug, involve in disrupting microtubule polymerization in helminths, which is a highly conserved process across various species [18]. Consequently, we conducted an investigation to assess the impact of MBD on the cell cycle. As expected, cell cycle analysis revealed that MBD induced cell cycle arrest in the G2/M phase in AML cells (Fig. 3A). This finding led us to focus and investigate the tubulin family as a potential target for MBD in AML. Proteomic analysis of AML-treated cells revealed differential expression in seven tubulin subtypes, with TUBA1A and tubulin β4B (TUBB4B) showing the significant and potential reduction in expression (Fig. 3B). Importantly, analysis of the cancer genome atlas (TCGA) database demonstrated that TUBA1A, but not TUBB4B or other tubulin subtypes, was significantly overexpressed in patients with AML compared to healthy people (Fig. 3C and Supplemental Fig. S5). These data suggested that TUBA1A might be a specific target for MBD in AML cells. Molecular docking studies provided further conformational evidence that MBD had the highest binding affinity for TUBA1A protein (Fig. 3D, Supplemental Fig. S6 and Supplemental Fig. S7). Subsequent validation through Q-PCR and western blot analysis confirmed significant reduction in TUBA1A expression levels in both THP-1 and U937 cells, following MBD treatment (Fig. 3E and 3F). For further functional relevance investigation of TUBA1A on MBD induced cell death, the lentivirus was employed for the overexpression of TUBA1A protein in AML cells (Fig. 3G). Subsequent analysis of cell viability revealed that the overexpression of TUBA1A mitigated the AML cell death induced by MBD (Fig. 3H).   Given its potential as a promising anti-tumor agent characterized by low toxicity and its ability to target TUBA1A, MBD exhibits potential validity in the treatment of tumors with high levels of TUBA1A. To investigate this further, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of the TCGA database, revealing substantial up-regulation of TUBA1A expression in multiple cancers types, including glioblastoma multiform (GBM), kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP), brain lower grade glioma (LGG), liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM), testicular germ cell tumors (TGCT), and thymoma (THYM). It is noteworthy that TUBA1A expression was up-regulated in GBM and LGG is astonishing (Supplemental Fig. S8). In vitro examination of MBD cytotoxic effects was evaluated in GBM cell lines, including U87 MG and U118 MG. The results confirmed that MBD induced cell death in a dose dependent manner, accompanied by a substantial downregulation of TUBA1A expression (Supplemental Fig. S9). These findings further highlighted the MBD potential as a targeted treatment for tumors with triggering TUBA1A levels. Particularly, suggest that MBD holds promising therapeutic potential for brain tumor treatment.


### MBD against primary AML blast cells in vitro and in vivo
  Many genetic polymorphisms in tumor cell lines could lead to huge differences and disparate reactivities in primary cancer cells. For this reason, we isolated primary AML cells from patients with different subtypes of AML (the specific disease information is shown in Supplemental Table S1). The results showed that MBD treatment could reduce the cell viability of primary AML cells and promote the secretion of IL-1β, indicating that MBD had anti-AML effects on primary AML cells similar to killing AML cell lines in ex vivo experiments (Fig. 4A). In addition, the primary blasts isolated from two patients with AML (a 15-y ear-old female patient #90 diagnosed with M5 AML, and a 79-y ear-old female patient #94 diagnosed with M1 AML) were utilized to establish patient-derived xenografts (PDX) models in B-NDG mice with severe immune deficiency (Fig. 4B). The successful engraftment of tumor cells was confirmed through flow cytometry analysis (Supplemental Fig. S10). The PDX model mice were subjected to MBD treatment three times per week for four weeks. In contrast to the control group, mice exhibited dull hair and enlarged spleens, where the MBD-treated mice displayed spleens comparable to those of normal healthy mice (Fig. 4C, Supplemental Fig. S11). Flow cytometry analysis showed a significant reduction in the proportion of human CD45-positive cells in the spleen, liver, bone marrow, and peripheral blood of MBD-treated mice (Fig. 4D–H, Supplemental Fig. S12). Histological analysis through H&E staining (Fig. 4I) and immunohistochemistry of human CD45 (Fig. 4J) confirmed that MBD effectively suppressed tumor cell invasion. ## Discussion
  In recent years, remarkable progress has been made in the research and development of drugs targeting gene mutations [19]. FLT3 inhibitors [20], BCL2 inhibitors [21], [22] and IDH1/IDH2 inhibitors [23] have been successively launched, bringing new options for the treatment of some AML patients, some of which have shown good efficacy. However, with the deepening of application, the problem of primary and acquired drug resistance of these novel drugs has gradually emerged, which brings new challenges to the treatment of AML, especially relapsed/refractory AML. Therefore, screening and identification of novel AML therapeutics by novel mechanisms or targets is of great significance for avoiding drug resistance.

  With the rapid development of high-throughput sequencing technology, great progress has been made in the understanding of the pathogenesis of AML, and the era of precision treatment is being entered [24], [19]. However, a certain proportion of patients still develop relapsed/refractory AML. Considering the high cost and cycle in the process of new drug development, it is a favorable supplement to solve this contradiction to screen and identify drugs that can be treated as single agents or combined therapies (Drug Repurposing) from known clinical drugs [25], [26].

  For an extended period, “apoptosis”, a non-inflammatory form of cell death that typically lacks immunogenicity, has been the primary mechanism through which numerous chemotherapeutic drugs impact DNA synthesis or stop the cell cycle, ultimately resulting in cell death. Nevertheless, the accumulation of mutations has rendered most tumor cells resistant to apoptosis, thereby impeding successful treatment. In recent years, the exploration of cell death mechanisms has unveiled alternative forms of programmed cell death, which have emerged as a novel approach for screening anti-tumor drugs [27], [28]. Several pieces of evidence have demonstrated that small molecules capable of inducing necroptosis or pyroptosis have notable anti-tumor effects [29], [30], [31], [32], [33], [34]. More recently, an increasing body of evidence suggests that small molecules inducing PANoptosis also exhibit significant anti-tumor effects [11]. Our findings indicate that the treatment of AML cells with MBD activates pathways associated with apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis, leading to the secretion of inflammatory factors. This process, known as PANoptosis, is characteristic and contributes significantly to the anti-AML effect.

  MBD, a broad-spectrum anthelmintic drug, exhibits one of its mechanisms of activation by tightly binding to tubulin in the worm. This interaction inhibits microtubule polymerization, thereby impeding mitosis and ultimately resulting in cell death and parasite eradication [35]. Our findings demonstrate that MBD effectively halts the mitotic cycle of AML cells at the G2/M phase, thereby indicating its influence on tubulin within human AML cells. Notably, this study represents the first elucidation of MBD's activity on tubulin in tumor cells, revealing a mechanism akin to the effects observed with classical antineoplastic drugs such as paclitaxel and vinblastine. These findings suggest a novel antineoplastic activity associated with MBD. In conjunction with the combined analysis of the TCGA database, mass spectrometry and molecular docking, our findings reveal that solely the expression of TUBA1A was significantly upregulated in AML, and subsequent MBD treatment resulted in a downregulation of TUBA1A levels. The overexpression of TUBA1A mitigated the capacity of MBD to induce cell death. These outcomes imply that TUBA1A serves as a functional target of MBD in AML.


## Conclusions
  Collectively, we successfully identified MBD, a traditional anthelmintic known for its low toxicity and low cost, and induced PANoptosis in AML cells by targeted downregulation of TUBA1A, which exhibited significant anti-AML activity in vitro and in vivo. These results highlight the remarkable clinical translational potential of MBD, providing new potential medicine for AML patients or for use in combination with existing clinical drugs. In addition, TUBA1A can be used potential novel therapeutic target in tumors with abnormal TUBA1A expression.
