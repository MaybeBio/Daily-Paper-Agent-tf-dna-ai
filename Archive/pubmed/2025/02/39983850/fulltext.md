# The Janus-facedness of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor pathway Report of the 6th International AHR Meeting: Research, Prevention, Therapy


## Abstract
  The ability to sense and process environmental cues is a fundamental aspect of an organism’s biology. The evolutionary ancient transcription factor AHR (aryl hydrocarbon receptor) has evolved in animals to sense low molecular weight compounds derived from environmental exposure, dietary plants, the gut/skin microbiome, or generated endogenously from tryptophan upon ultraviolet light (UV) exposure or enzymatic catabolism. The binding of such molecules results in a cascade of events leading to the transcription of target genes. The AHR gene locus was first identified in mice in 1982. Since then, the beneficial and detrimental effects of AHR agonist-driven activation or lack thereof have been studied, particularly in relation to environmental chemical toxicity, carcinogenicity, or tissue homeostasis, e.g. barrier tissues. AHR ligands are also being considered as a potential new therapeutic class of molecules for the treatment of cancer, debilitating and chronic inflammatory diseases or metabolic disorders. A series of international meetings initiated twenty years ago have provided a comprehensive overview of AHR research. At the meeting in Düsseldorf in 2024, the identification of tailor-made ligands using modern, artificial intelligence (AI)-based approaches was a key topic of discussion, as were current attempts to resolve the dual nature of AHR activation - beneficial and harmful. While our understanding is still in its infancy, research was also presented that highlights previously unrecognized roles of the AHR in many diseases.


## Introduction
  The AHR is a cytosolic transcription factor activated by a diverse range of exogenous and endogenous small molecular weight compounds. Initially identified in the mid-1970s as a receptor protein binding aromatic pollutants, such as 2,3,7,8-tetrachlorodibenzo-p-dioxin (TCDD), the AHR plays a key role in regulating xenobiotic metabolism and mediating the toxic effects of dioxins, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), and related environmental pollutants [1, 2]. Subsequent research has revealed that AHR also interacts with a variety of endogenous, plant- and microbiota-derived compounds, and is involved in the regulation of various physiological and pathophysiological processes, including the development, maintenance and function of certain immune cell populations. Being one of the major cellular pathways translating environmental and physiological signals into biochemical responses, AHR signaling was intensively investigated in multiple in vitro and in vivo studies and in silico analyses, and its molecular details and facets are thoroughly summarized in several recent overview articles [1, 2].

  Briefly, inactive AHR is trapped in a cytosolic multiprotein complex. Upon binding of a ligand, the AHR undergoes conformational changes, sheds its co-chaperones, translocates to the nucleus and heterodimerizes with the AHR nuclear translocator (ARNT). The AHR/ARNT complex binds to so-called xenobiotic-responsive elements (XRE) in the enhancer region of genes and enhances their expression by recruiting the general transcription machinery [1, 2]. In addition to this canonical AHR signaling pathway, several non-canonical pathways and functions of AHR have been described [3, 4]. The ligand-triggered dissociation of the cytosolic multiprotein complex, for instance, may lead to a release of the soluble tyrosine kinase c-src, which can move to the inner plasma membrane and activate the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) by phosphorylation. Other examples are the interaction of AHR with transcription factors of the nuclear factor (NF)-κB and the JAK/STAT pathway, for instance in response to inflammatory or infectious cues. In addition, there is evidence that the AHR binds to and regulates function of tissue factors involved in thrombosis [5]. This brief glimpse into the multiple facets of AHR signaling already highlights how complex AHR biology can be.

  Over the last decades, the pronounced ligand-, tissue- and context-specificity of AHR was described and clearly this complicates any therapeutic use. There are still many unknowns, in particular all the ligand-related unknowns regarding intracellular stoichiometry and metabolism, AHR-binding affinity, and possible differences in changing AHR conformation based on their structure.

  Regulation of gene expression on many levels is essential for living organisms. Evolutionary, the AHR is a great example of how a given molecule can be employed for different purposes, depending on context, which was strongly highlighted by Chris Bradfield in his keynote lecture on the per-arnt-sim (PAS)-basic helix loop-helix family of proteins. For any therapeutical exploitation, it is paramount to understand such complexity, and the meeting in Düsseldorf in 2024 was a big step forward in this direction. As described in more detail below, identifying novel ligands and how they can be designed to interact with AHR, as well as new knowledge of AHŔs role in a wide range of was discussed. Older friends such as AHŔs role in immunity and cancer were in focus again, as there is still so much to learn. Let us just mention epigenetics, the microbiome influence, or checkpoint-inhibitor modulation. As always, meeting experts and exchanging unpublished data and challenging perspectives, invigorated our understanding of AHR.


## Sessions and Presentations of the Meeting

### Molecular facets of AHR signaling in physiology and toxicology: the chase for more ligands and interactors
  In figure 1 players in AHR signaling are summarized. The AHR is a protein that integrates multiple environmental signals, and interacts with or regulates numerous proteins with critical cellular functions. A conundrum remains why different ligands seem to activate AHR at different strengths. In this context, Sarka Tumova (Institute of Entomology, Biology Centre CAS, Czech Republic), adapted and used the split nanoluciferase technology (NanoBiT) to monitor the dynamics of AHR activation [6, 7]. With this novel method they detected a rapid, specific, and dose-dependent interaction between AHR and ARNT upon exposure to agonists like FICZ and indirubin in live cells. Mutations in the PAS-B domain of the receptor abolished this interaction. To assess the heterodimerization of AHR with ARNT and how AHR is promiscuously activated by different ligands, Dalei Wu from Shandong University, China, presented crystal structures of heterodimers bound to 6 different ligands; this revealed a distinctive dimerization mode unique to the basic-Helix-Loop-Helix/Per-ARNT-Sim (bHLH-PAS) protein family. Specifically, the ligand-binding PAS-B domain of AHR interacts with the PAS-B domain of ARNT forming a relatively separate structure from the PAS-A and the other domains within the dimer: it suggests that the PAS-B domain acts quite independently from the bHLH and PAS-A segments of the receptor. This is in line with its known inhibitory function in AHR activation and the unlocking functions of the ligands. High resolution structural data of the crystallized AHR was “devoutly to be wished” for decades and only recently William Bourguet and Jakub Grusczcyk from the Center of Structural Biology, Montpellier, France, succeeded determining the cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structure of AHR bound to indirubin, which revealed structural insights into its ligand-binding PAS-B domain [8]. At the Düsseldorf meeting, the same team presented a 2.75Å resolution structure of the AHR complexed with benzo[a]pyrene, highlighting a bipartite PAS-B binding pocket [9]: the primary binding site controls ligand-specificity and affinity, while a secondary site allows for binding promiscuity. Docking studies further validate this structure. Lastly, Bourguet et al. compared this structure with other AHR proteins (Drosophila melanogaster and Mus musculus) that suggested a ligand-induced loop conformational change, potentially regulating AHR function.

  This question of the modulatory role of AHR ligands (endogenous and exogenous) may also benefit from the most promising advances in AI: while technological advancements have provided comprehensive datasets, using AI for large-scale high-throughput screening to identify AHR-interacting compounds remains challenging. Lianyi Han from Fudan University, China, developed a Graph Convolutional Network (CGN) to predict the activation potential of small molecules interacting with AHR (based on luciferase reporter gene experiments). Using data from a high-throughput screening assay conducted at the Scripps Research Institute on 320,000 small molecules, the GCN model had strong prediction capabilities. F1 score, precision, and recall rates were close to 90%. Additionally, molecular docking technology predicted the binding affinity of over 7,000 active molecules, with 99% accuracy, showing strong affinity for AHR. Han et al. therefore suggested that their new GCN model can effectively identify therapeutic candidates and open new pathways in drug discovery.

  While this research clearly aims at finding new specifically tailored therapeutic agonist, it is well known that physiological AHR agonists exist.

  First, tryptophan metabolites appear to be the major source of host - and microbiome-generated AHR ligands. Indole 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO1/2) and tryptophan 2,3-dioxygenase (TDO) in the liver are the key enzymes to tryptophan catabolism eventually producing a series of metabolites, with kynurenine, kynurenic acid and xanthurenic acid being AHR agonists. The human gut microbiome can metabolize tryptophan to numerous metabolites, with at least 12 that activate the AHR [10]. Gary Perdew from the Pennsylvania State University, State College, USA quantitated tryptophan metabolites in germ-free versus conventional mice serum and levels of indole-3-lactic acid, indole-3-carboxaldehyde, kynurenine, kynurenic acid and indole-3-acetic acid were essentially the same, the latter three appear to be the key drivers of AHR activation potential [11]. This suggests that the host produces the major circulating tryptophan metabolites that would drive systemic basal AHR activation. While the TDO pathway is responsible for the production of kynurenine and kynurenic acid, the source for indole-3-acetic acid is less certain. Tyrosine amino transferase and glutamic-oxaloacetic transaminase both highly expressed in liver metabolize tryptophan to indole-3-pyruvic acid, which then undergoes further metabolism to indole-3-carboxaldehyde, indole-3-acetic acid, indole-3-aldehyde and indole-3-lactic acid. In addition, evidence was presented demonstrating that the serum levels of these metabolites are under the influence of circadian rhythm in mice and likely diet. Some of these metabolites exhibit greater human AHR activation potential versus the mouse AHR, suggesting that mouse studies may underestimate the impact of these metabolites [12]. Indole-3-pyruvic acid is unstable and may undergo a series of chemical reactions especially under oxidant stress conditions to form polycyclic compounds, such as FICZ [13]. However, whether the formation of FICZ would form the basis for systemic AHR activity remains to be established.

  In addition, research from Casey Wright (Galveston, TX, USA) suggests the importance of different ARNT isoforms regarding the regulation of AHR responses, in immune cells. His presentation thereby focused on the comparison of two ARNT isoforms in naïve T cells vs T cell malignancies. Besides this, ARNT is not the only dimerization partner of AHR. ARNT can also dimerize with Hypoxia-Inducible Factor 1 alpha (HIF1α) which thus competes for AHR binding. This role of ARNT, which is also known as HIF-1β, was highlighted by Sonja Faßbender from the IUF in Düsseldorf, Germany. It is known that both transcription factors HIF-1α and AHR act as ultraviolet (UV)B-responsive sensors in keratinocytes [14, 4], influencing cellular metabolism, immune responses, xenobiotic responses, and DNA repair. Sonja Faßbender and colleagues developed keratinocyte-specific HIF-1α/AHR double knock-out mice and CRISPR-Cas9-generated human single and double knock-out HaCaT cells. In the double knock-out mice, the skin exhibited UVB-exposure-like skin aspects, such as hyperpigmentation, mild acanthosis, and altered Langerhans cells (LC) activity, and immunosuppressive activity. The double-knockout in human keratinocytes also suggest an alteration in epidermal functions. Interestingly, preliminary results suggest that UVB-induced DNA damage was lower in the double knock-out mice, potentially due to hyperpigmentation. Interaction between AHR and hypoxia signaling pathways has functional consequences for other physiological systems, and new evidence pinpoints Obstructive Sleep Apnea and hypertension as disease states, where environmental influence may play a role, but also where AHR can be therapeutically exploited (presentation by Antonio B. Pimpao from Sofia A. Pereirás group, Universidad NOVA de Lisboa, Portugal).

  Another partner of the AHR is its transcriptional repressor, the AHR Repressor (AHRR) protein. AHRR expression is inducible by AHR signaling and limits chronic activation of the receptor [2]. Indeed, AHRR competes with AHR for binding to the cofactor ARNT, reducing the transcriptional activity of the receptor. While AHR is broadly expressed, AHRR is mainly found in immune cells of barrier organs. Heike Weighardt from the University of Bonn, Germany, presented her most recent data showing how AHRR plays a role in regulating both systemic and local inflammation. In models of DSS-induced colitis, both AHR and AHRR are protective [15], while AHR alone limits infection-related erythropoiesis and splenic remodeling in Salmonella Typhimurium infections [16]. Additionally, both AHR- and AHRR-deficient mice are protected from diet-induced obesity, highlighting their roles in metabolic regulation [17].

  This overview shows the extent to which the AHR can be considered (i) as an integrator of our exposome, capable of binding many different molecules (with for sure, a therapeutic potential) and with unknowns that will have to be resolved on the differential effects of these chemicals, and (ii) as a complex interactor with an ARNT partner whose importance has been underestimated in recent years. The presentations allow us to look forward to a future in which these questions will be answered.


### AHR in barrier organs skin and gut
  The AHR plays an important role in orchestrating and maintaining organ barrier function, such as in skin and gut. Extensive research in mice and humans has shown that AHR activation in both, immune cells and non-immune cells, influences barrier formation and epithelial homeostasis in vivo. On this background, several talks provided new knowledge. Regarding skin and atopic dermatitis, that is a non-infectious chronic inflammation, Chih Hung Lee (Kaohsiung, Taiwan) reported about the importance of AHR signaling in a standard mouse model of atopic dermatitis. When these mice lack AHR in Langerhans cells, the antigen presenting cells of the skin, these mice had increased pathology. AHR presence in langerin positive cells restrict Th2 and Th17 induced pathology in this model [18]. Tapinarof is the first AHR ligand approved for clinical use, i.e. the treatment of plaque psoriasis. Despite its clinical efficacy, there are no mechanism of action studies in living human skin. Rachael Clark (Harvard Medical School, USA) tested the effects of topical tapinarof in immunodeficient mice grafted with living human skin and injected with allogeneic PBMC, in 3D fibroblast and keratinocyte psoriasis models, and in purified human immune cells. Tapinarof reduced DAMP sensing, inflammasome activation, and cGAS/STING activation, and suppressed the activation of dendritic cells, macrophages and T cells. Immunoregulatory signaling was enhanced, and there was a reduction in type 1, type 2 and type 17 T cell mediated inflammation. Down-regulation of inflammation was associated with reduced mitochondrial reactive oxygen species production, decreased release of mitochondrial DNA and RNA, and reduced cytoplasmic mitochondrial DNA/RNA sensing. Tapinarof also blocked the entry of cells into glycolysis, reduced oxidative phosphorylation and induced a low energy state. In summary, tapinarof exerts its potent immunomodulatory effects at the level of the mitochondria by reducing inflammatory tone and inhibiting key metabolic pathways.

  The skin barrier is formed in utero, and reduced or too slow skin barrier development can result in enhanced susceptibility to injury und infection. Mercedes Gomez de Agüero (Würzburg, Germany) had previously shown in mice that the early postnatal development of the intestinal immune system depended on microbial metabolites, in particular AHR ligands, which are produced by the maternal microflora [19]. In set of sophisticated experiment, she now recolonized germ free dams with the auxotrophic Escherichia coli HA107 strain, and studied the effect on the fetuses (who remain sterile). It turned out that only the epidermis of the offspring of gestationally colonized dams showed a properly developed architecture of the stratum corneum and developing hair follicles. This need for microbe-derived signals is constricted to the development in utero. Further experiments, e.g. with radioactively labelled bacteria and epidermal organoids demonstrated that the maternal metabolites are indeed AHR agonists, which modulate the stemness of the epidermal keratinocytes, resulting in a functional skin barrier. Beyond skin and lung, the gut is our interface between the body and the environment, and by now it is well known that AHR signaling is necessary for proper gut homeostasis and the gut-associated lymphoid tissue. Interestingly, the diet and gut-residing bacteria provide the necessary levels of AHR ligands, in particular indoles present in plants (family Brassicaceae, touted as “eat your veggies”) and tryptophan metabolites from bacteria. Harry Sokol, a gastroenterology researcher from Paris, has studied this in the context of gut inflammation in humans. His group supplemented healthy subjects orally with tryptophan and analyzed AHR activation [20]. The long-term goal here was to ask if levels of AHR ligands determine gut health and if this is eventually of therapeutic values in intestinal disorders. Tryptophan supplementation was well tolerated and led to increased AHR activation in the duodenum but not “far down” in the feces itself. In addition, microbial indoles and host kynurenine, i.e., tryptophan metabolites, were detectable, which might be immunomodulatory in the host. Indeed, in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) patients the levels of tryptophan derivatives kynurenic acid as well as xanthurenic acid, improved with the disease, and low levels of those mediators correlate with a higher risk of flares of IBD [21]. Similarly, the group of Jörg Lehmann from Leipzig, Germany, identified therapeutical agonists in colitis. He strongly emphasized that there is an urgent need for pharmacologically and toxicologically well-characterized AHR ligands and new drugs need to be tested for IBD therapy. Further, the Sokol group screened human gut bacteria for production of AHR agonists and transferred those with high AHR activity into mice that had been treated with dextran sulfate sodium (DSS) in a colitis model. The transferred bacteria improved colitis in this model, but the positive effect did not work in AHR-deficient mice. When applied to mice after DSS colitis induction, the AHR ligands kynurenic acid and xanthurenic acid supported proliferation of intestinal epithelial cells, enhanced IL-22 production and increased Th17 responses. In addition to influencing tryptophan metabolism towards production of protective metabolites, Sokol et al. propose the application of the enzyme kynurenine aminotransferase, which catalyzes the transamination of kynurenine to kynurenic acid, as a protective measure. The gut is served by vasculature, e.g., to transport the nutrients out of the small intestine (blood vessels), or for enteric immune cell trafficking (lymphatic vessels). Using single cell RNA Seq Benjamin Wiggins from London, United Kingdom, dissected the molecular landscape of small intestine endothelial cells [22]. He could detect high expression of AHR in endothelial cells, which can be activated by dietary AHR ligands. This is associated with protection of the intestinal barrier under homeostatic conditions by the inhibition of proliferative and pro-inflammatory pathways. Absence of the AHR in endothelial cells led to an enhanced proinflammatory profile. In human endothelial cells, AHR signaling promoted quiescence and restrained activation by inflammatory mediators. Importantly, AHR-signaling by endothelial cells helped dampen LPS induced inflammation in mice, while AHR deficiency resulted in higher susceptibility to the enteric pathogen Yersinia pseudotuberculosis. Thus, endothelial expression of AHR is responsible for an optimal response to inflammation through the promotion of endothelial cell quiescence and vascular normalcy.


### AHR in infections and autoimmunity
  The discovery of a crucial role of AHR in immunity, in particular in Th17 and regulatory T cells, has brought AHR research from its niche in toxicology to the medical field communities. Immunity against infections is one such field. Paige Lawrence from Rochester, USA, discussed immunity to viral infections in the context of air pollutants that bind AHR. Measles, influenza, coronaviruses and others remain a major source of global morbidity and mortality, and new viral threats from zoonotic diseases emerge all the time. Dr. Lawrence presented data from mice modeling human influenza A infections, with a focus on very young mice, whose mothers were exposed to xenobiotic AHR ligands, i.e., she showed that AHR activation very early in life influences the developing immune system. When infected with influenza A virus, AHR activation of the dams altered T cell clonal expansion and differentiation of the pups. In the non-infected state, no differences in cell frequency or phenotype were detectable between pups from exposed vs. unexposed pups. Thus, AHR triggers cryptic changes in the developing mice, which become only evident upon infection. The underlying reasons are both direct activity of AHR-signaling in certain cells, as well as changes in intercellular communication, and most likely involve epigenetic changes, especially DNA-methylation, remaining even after removal of AHR ligands [23, 24]. Bacterial pathogens were studied by Pedro Moura-Alves, now in Porto, Portugal. His interest lies in how AHR functions as a pattern recognition factor to detect bacterial density (“quorum sensing”). Bacteria can sense their density, a function called quorum sensing [25], and then adapt their physiology to escape the hosts immune system, for instance by forming biofilms or virulence factors. Some quorum sensing molecules were shown to be AHR ligands [26]. The hosts thereby counteract – they detect and discriminate bacterial molecules and tailor immune responses accordingly. The Moura-Alves group discussed their diverse infection models where they found that modulation of AHR activity enables the host to gauge bacterial community densities and predict infection kinetics. Consequently, the immune system can finetune its antibacterial response. On an adverse level, AHR modulation may affect the efficacy of antibiotic therapy. At least in the context of tuberculosis, Moura-Alves presented data showing that AHR activity can result in antimicrobial resistance. AHR may bind anti-bacterial drugs, such as rifampicin and rifabutin, which are used for anti- tuberculosis therapy, impairing their activity. In addition, AHR activation induces metabolization of rifabutin and AHR inhibition restores the anti-microbial effect of rifabutin [27]. These findings underscore that knowledge about host-pathogen interactions at the molecular level must inform targeted therapeutic interventions against infectious diseases. An altogether beneficial effect of AHR signaling can be seen in the gut. Physiological AHR signaling triggered by dietary indoles or bacterial tryptophan derivatives are necessary for a healthy gut barrier and for fighting bacterial or parasitic infections. Gitta Stockinger (London, United Kingdom) and her group demonstrated how the large intestine is particularly sensitive to ligand deprivation, which results in high susceptibility towards bacterial Citrobacter rodentium infection. In the small intestine, AHR activation by dietary indoles can safeguard against eukaryotic Cryptosporidium, via support of the intraepithelial lymphocytes. This may have practical impacts for therapy. The Stockinger group showed in mice that indole supplementation of nursing mice enhances resistance to Cryptosporidium, while deprivation of AHR ligands to nursing mice increased infection susceptibility in their offspring [28]. Parasites often induce Th2 immune responses, and this was studied by Caspar Ohnmacht from Munich, Germany. Working with macrophages, he showed that the AHR regulates eicosanoid production in these cells, via transcription of PTGS1 encoding for the enzyme cyclooxygenase 1 (COX1) and ALOX5 encoding for the arachidonate 5-lipoxygenase. As a consequence, AHR-deficiency aggravates house dust mite induced allergic airway inflammation [29]. Looking at macrophages in the gut lamina propria, the group of Allison Ehrlich (Davis, CA, USA) also identified a role for AHR signaling in this gut-relevant immune cells. Her presentation concerned the autoimmune type 1 diabetes. A particular mouse strain, non-obese diabetic (NOD) mice, develops this type 1 diabetes, and AHR-deficient NOD mice produce a distinct macrophage type, whose ability to express the immunoregulatory cytokine IL-10 depends on the microbiome in the gut, at least it is rescued by bacterial cell wall lipopolysaccharide. This means that the immunoregulatory capacity of lamina propria macrophages needs two signals, one via AHR, the other via bacteria. This data collectively outlines a general new aspect of the development of an autoimmune disease, where regulatory T cells have failed [30].


### AHR ligands – The good, the bad, and the ugly
  The AHR is considered a possible therapeutic target to treat a number of chronic diseases, such as Crohn’s disease, skin inflammatory diseases, autoimmune disorders and cancer. As discussed in section 2.2 above, in 2022 the U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved tapinarof as a topical treatment for plaque psoriasis, the first approved drug that activates the AHR as the primary mechanism of action. An AHR antagonist E/Z-2-benzylidene-5,6-dimethoxy-3,3-dimethylindan-1-one (BDDI) is in use in sunscreens. Several pharmaceutical companies are interested in the use of an AHR antagonist to inhibit AHR transcriptional activity within the tumor microenvironment. In particular, the use of AHR antagonist treatment to increase efficacy of immunotherapy is being considered. A presentation by Gabriele Leder from Bayer AG Pharmaceutical, Germany, examined the use of BAY2416964 as an AHR inhibitor to counteract the immunosuppressive effects of kynurenine especially in the context of immune checkpoint programmed death (PD)1-targeted treatment [31]. Pre-clinical studies demonstrated that BAY2416964 opposes the immunosuppressive effects of monocytes, dendritic cells, and T cells. These findings add to those of others showing that the AHR tends to induce immunosuppressive cells in several cancer settings [32–36] Clinical studies with a combination of BAY2416964 and anti-PD1 are ongoing. One consideration for these trials is to develop molecular or metabolic profiles that identify subsets of patients that might benefit from AHR-targeted therapy.

  On the other hands, AHR ligands are not always beneficial, but may cause adverse health effects, especially in the context of environmental influences. Air pollution is the 4th leading risk factor mediating the global burden of diseases leading to premature deaths (www.stateofglobalair.org/resources/report/state-global-air-report-2024). Air pollution is thought to contribute to ~19% of lung cancer deaths. The major source of air pollution that associated with lung cancer appears to be particulate matter less than 2.5nm in size particulate matter (PM2.5), which are produced through the combustion of coal, tobacco, disease fuel and other organic matter. Johan Øvrevik, from the Norwegian Institute of Public Health/Oslo University, Norway, presented data examining the complex chemical composition of (PM2.5) with phenanthrene, pyrene and fluoranthene being the most abundant polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) species. In addition, significant levels of PAHs with 4 rings (e.g. chrysene), 5 rings (e.g., benzo(a)pyrene) and other mutagenic species can be found, many of which are potent AHR agonists [37]. Studies suggested that AHR activation is perhaps the most sensitive response to PM2.5 and this receptor-mediated activity occurs at orders of magnitude lower concentrations than other endpoints of air pollution exposure (e.g., oxidative stress). PM2.5 mixtures are capable of inducing both genomic and non-genomic signaling. An example of the latter is increased calcium-mediated signaling, which may be selectively activated by pyrene and phenanthrene, in contrast to mutagenic PAHs such as benzo(a)pyrene which primarily appear to induce canonical genomic AHR signaling. A presentation by Christoph Vogel from UC Davis, USA, expanded on the AHR-dependent effects of PM2.5 that mediate inflammatory signaling. In the context of PM exposure from wildfires in the US, he focused on Toll-like receptors and NF-kB pathways that are activated in part through the AHR pathway and enhanced oxidative stress [38]. Frederick Hartung from IUF Düsseldorf, Germany, discussed how PM exposure can also increase the risk of atopic dermatitis. The proposed mechanism is through redox cycling of PAHs through NADPH:quinone oxidoreductase 1, aldo-keto reductase 1C3 (AKR1C3) and carbonyl reductase (CBR3). In human keratinocytes AKR1C3 and CBR3 are upregulated by PAH exposure, which appears to involve non-canonical AHR signaling pathway involving c-Src, EGF and Nrf2. Repeated PM exposure in human skin ex vivo also upregulates these enzymes. The data presented highlighted the important role of the AHR as a regulator of oxidative and inflammatory stress responses in air pollution-exposed skin. Yet, another aspect of PAH exposure is the effect of co-exposures to other pollutants. Gaëlle Deval and colleagues (Université de Paris Cité, France) examined the impact of environmentally relevant BaP-coated CeO2 nanoparticles in the human placental barrier [39]. Confocal and transmission electron microscopy confirmed the internalization of nanoparticles into trophoblasts. Results demonstrated that the AHR was activated, while there was no apparent cellular toxicity observed. However, there were significant variations in AHR activity and in critical trophoblast functions. These results highlight the concept that CeO2 nanoparticles altered the benzo(a)pyrene metabolism and kinetics, illustrating that co-exposures are important to examine the complexities of actual environmental conditions. The effects of air pollutants that activate the AHR leading to toxicological effects continue to be a major health concern.


### AHR and cancer
  There were several oral talks and posters presenting data on the now well-established but incompletely understood role of the AHR in both carcinogenesis and cancer immunity. These studies fell into three general categories: 1) Production and role of AHR ligands in cancer, 2) AHR-mediated immune modulation in cancer, and 3) AHR control of cancer mechanisms. Presentations relating to these topics are summarized below.


#### Production and role of AHR ligands in cancer
  The symposium featured several studies demonstrating a role for tryptophan-derived AHR ligands in cancer. In a novel study, Christiane Opitzś lab from the German Cancer Reseach Center, Heidelberg, showed increased AHR expression in tryptophan-deprived human glioblastoma and breast cancer cells through activation of the mTORC1–4E-BP1 and p38 pathways. This could be viewed as a mechanism to compensate for any decrease in tryptophan-derived AHR ligands. Given previous results showing that the AHR drives production of its own ligands via tryptophan pathway enzyme (e.g., IDO, TDO) up-regulation, this may suggest that the compensation for tryptophan deprivation includes an attempt by malignant cells to express both more AHR and more AHR ligands.

  Building on a foundation of previous work from their lab [11], Dhwani Patel from Gary Perdewś lab addressed the question of the source of AHR ligands that drive constitutive AHR activity in cancer, specifically oral cancer. They found a set of six tryptophan-derived AHR agonists in the blood of human donors on a controlled diet. A pool of these six metabolites was sufficient to maintain AHR activity in two HNSCC lines and to drive colony formation. These are important results that suggest that the levels of AHR agonists in blood may be useful biomarkers for correlating circulating AHR agonist activity and HNSCC outcomes or for applying tryptophan metabolic enzyme-directed treatments. Supporting the contention that AHR ligand profiles may represent cancer biomarkers, the Opitz lab used machine learning to predict the production of AHR ligands in various cancers. They found that these profiles can be used to project cancer trajectories and that the AHR profiles align with clinical subgroups. Such results may be important not only for determining a prognosis but also for interventions targeting AHR ligand-generating enzymes.

  Tracy McGaha (Toronto, Canada) presented data showing that AHR is overexpressed in pancreatic cancer and negatively correlated with patients’ survival. The group identified a key role for gut microbiome-derived AHR ligands in immunosuppression mediated by intratumoral macrophages and T cells.


#### AHR-mediated immune modulation in cancer
  In a study on one of the most aggressive forms of cancer, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), Jason Matthews (University of Oslo) and colleagues deleted the AHR from murine pancreatic duct PDAC cells and demonstrated that, while they grow at the same rate as wildtype cells in immunocompromised NSG mice, they grow considerably more slowly in immunocompetent mice. Furthermore, AHR-KO tumors recruit more T cells and macrophages to the tumor microenvironment and those tissues infiltrating lymphocytes exhibit RNA profiles characteristic of robust innate immune responses. These studies are reminiscent of similar work in oral cancers [40].

  Expanding on previous studies in oral squamous cell carcinomas [41], David Sherr (Boston, MA, USA) and colleagues showed that AHR knockout in murine and human Non-small-cell lung carcinoma cells (NSLC) reduces PD-L1, PD-L2, and IDO expression, kynurenine production, and the expression of several NSCLC-associated genes (e.g., Thbs1, Egfr, CD109) through what appears to be an AHR control of the canonical JAK/STAT pathway. In vitro, AHR knockout in transplanted NSCLC cells significantly reduced or, in almost half of the cases, completely blocked tumor formation. Of the AHR-KO tumors that did grow, there was a significant increase in infiltration of active CD8+ T cells. Notably, AHR deletion decreased expression of several MHC-related molecules suggesting that the AHR may contribute both beneficial and harmful effects to the immune system. It was suggested that these contrary and possibly balancing immune effects may be one of the factors that limit the effectiveness of systemically applied AHR inhibitors.

  Marco Gargaro (from Francesca Fallarino’s group, Perugia, Italy) studied the effect of AHR deletion in conventional dendritic cells (DC), cells that typically express higher AHR levels than other DC subsets. AHR deletion enhanced IL12 and TNFα production and enhanced T cell activity in DC/CD8+ T cell co-cultures. In an in vivo fibrosarcoma model, conditional AHR deletion in host DCs enhanced tumor immunity and tumor rejection. In humans, higher AHR levels correlated with poorer outcomes in soft tissue sarcomas, strongly suggesting a pro-tumorigenic penchant characterized by an increase in conventional DC infiltration into the sarcoma tumor microenvironment.


#### AHR control of cancer mechanisms
  Xavier Coumoul (Paris, France) tested the hypothesis that fatty breast tissue serves as a lipophilic environmental chemical depot. Indeed, he and his colleagues demonstrated that several persistent organic pollutants, including TCDD, accumulate in breast fat. Furthermore, they showed that exposure of preadipocytes to TCDD drove breast cancer cells towards a long-lived, metastatic stem cell phenotype and the appearance of giant cancer cells associated with breast cancer metastasis as seen in an elegant zebra fish metastasis model. Yet another cancer signaling pathway impacted by the AHR was studied by Thomas Tüting from the University of Magdeburg, Germany, who presented data showing that AHR activation by FICZ, a photoproduct of tryptophan, increased migration of dedifferentiated melanoma cells. Furthermore, AHR activation in these cells contributed to resistance to front line melanoma drugs, BRAF and MEK inhibitors.

  Finally, it should be noted that the nature of AHR activity is highly ligand- and context-specific, especially in cancer [42, 43]. The finding of both pro- and anti-tumor effects of the AHR may reflect the ability of the AHR to simultaneously drive pathways that favor and inhibit cancer. If confirmed on a molecular level, this might explain the modest effects of AHR inhibitors in cancer models. That is, AHR inhibitors may block some adverse signaling pathways that lead to cancer cell “stemness” or pathways responsible leading to immune evasion, while simultaneously decreasing helpful anti-tumor components.


### Emerging frontiers
  The scope of AHR functions added to the plethora of existing knowledge and respective data, including unpublished data, was discussed in an emerging frontiers session and prompted reflections on future prospects.

  The regulation of lipid metabolism by AHR has been on the horizon for some time [44]. In a presentation by Rosanna Huchzermeier from the van der Vorst group in Aachen, Germany, it was reported that feeding a high-fat Western diet to apolipoprotein E-deficient mice on an AHR-deficient background resulted in increased blood leukocytes yet reduced atherosclerotic plaque formation. The results of RNA sequencing and phenotype analysis both indicated that AHR plays a pivotal role in lipid metabolism and hyperlipidemia, which are crucial factors in the development of cardiovascular disease. It is noteworthy that when healthy adult humans were given a strictly defined, wholesome plant-based diet versus a high-fat “Western diet” for three days, there was a significant decrease in AHR activity in blood following a plant-based diet. The study from the TAhRget consortium (“Targeting AhR-dependent Inflammation for Organ Protection”), funded by the German Government, presented by Laura de Boni, Cologne) thus emphasized the necessity for further research into the kinetics and short- versus long-term effects of triggering AHR via the diet (and, consequently, the potential impact of microbiome-derived agonist changes). Similarly, further research is required to elucidate the effects of the AHR in the context of an inflammatory background, which differs from those observed in healthy individuals. This was demonstrated once more in studies conducted on mice, in which the blood-brain barrier was examined. Kynurenines, the tryptophan metabolites generated by AHR-induced enzymes IDO1/2 or TDO, have been observed to increase in response to inflammation. Conversely, AHR deficiency has been linked to the amelioration of diseases such as experimental autoimmune encephalitis in mice. Immune cells were retained “within paravascular cuffs, likely restricting central nervous system infiltration” (talk by Timo Mikel Rhiem, University of Düsseldorf, Germany). In addition to the many contributions dealing with the screening of AHR ligands, Ouldouz Ghashghaei, University of Barcelona, Spain, presented novel multicomponent reaction-based synthesis methods for the generation of indolocarbazole-based AHR ligands.

  The upper sections of this paper present a discussion of the interference of AHR pathway signaling with that of checkpoint inhibitor treatment. Similarly, AHR inhibitors resulted in the development of severe cutaneous rashes and adverse drug reactions. This had been shown for vemurafenib, an incidental AHR antagonist [45], and further studies are currently being conducted to identify other such drugs, with a particular focus on utilizing in silico approaches to assess potential AHR binding activity. As shown by Alina Kuklinski, University of Düsseldorf, one such drug, capmatinib, which is used in the treatment of metastatic non-small cell lung cancer and is known to cause adverse skin reactions, is a strong candidate for further investigation. Further gaps in our understanding of AHR signaling were addressed through molecular studies on importins, the proteins that facilitate AHR nuclear translocation. Jakub Gruszczyk from the Bourguet group in Montpellier, France, which recently succeeded in visualizing the cryo-EM structure of AHR bound to benzo(a)pyrene [9], identified the importin α and β complexes involved in the interactions with the AHR.


## Conclusions
  The data presented at this meeting impressively demonstrated how AHR research continues to expand, particularly in the biomedical field. The regulation and function of this important transcription factor is being investigated not only in the context of dioxin toxicity, immunology and cancer research, but also in relation to chronic kidney disease, ocular toxicity, particulate air pollution-associated respiratory diseases, cardiovascular complications, sleep disorders and others. Several poster and oral presentations focused on (often AI-guided) in silico and in vitro approaches to identify, characterize and predict the binding of small molecules to the AHR in order to better understand the critical parameters and improve the design of AHR-targeting compounds. Therapeutic targeting of the AHR has reached the level of clinical research, particularly in cancer immunotherapy (AHR antagonists) and chronic inflammatory skin diseases (AHR agonists). However, what resonated most with a large number of presentations at this meeting was that the realization of the full therapeutic potential of AHR is significantly limited by the pronounced ligand specificity of the AHR response, which needs to be better understood. As well, in experimental settings, traditional dose-effect pharmacokinetic of ligands usually is not fully addressed, making comparisons between results between different experimental systems hard, especially in vivo research. Understanding the parameters that shape and determine ligand specificity and streamline dose-effects is therefore an important task for future research on this interesting transcription factor. Finally, we think that also the research of the integration of the AHR pathway with other signaling pathways will be needed to eventually understand AHR effects in a particular system. Last but not least, the incredible number of almost 200 participants from 23 countries around the world was a clear and pleasing sign of an extremely vibrant and interactive AHR community.
