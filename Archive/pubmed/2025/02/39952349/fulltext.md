# Identification and Understanding of Allostery Hotspots in Proteins: Integration of Deep Mutational Scanning and Multi-faceted Computational Analyses


## Abstract
  Motivated by recent deep mutational scanning (DMS) experiments, we have carried out a diverse set of computations to better understand the distribution and contributions of allostery hotspot residues in a transcription factor, TetR. These include extensive atomistic simulations and free energy computations for different functional states of TetR, machine learning analysis of the DMS data and a statistical thermodynamic model for the experimental induction data for the WT protein and a handful of hotspot mutants. Collectively, these computations provided insights into the structural and energetic basis of allostery in TetR, and the distinct contributions of allostery hotspots. The results highlight that the allostery function (i.e., the induction activity) of TetR can be modulated by perturbing both inter-domain coupling and intra-domain properties, such as the population of the binding-competent conformation of each domain. This mechanistic degeneracy qualitatively explains the broad distribution of allostery hotspots across the protein structure observed in the DMS experiments, and also informs the design of strategies aimed at identifying allostery hotspots. The mechanistic framework and the multi-faceted computational approaches are expected to be applicable to the analysis of other allostery systems, especially those sharing the similar two-domain structural topology, and to the design of allostery modulators.


## Introduction
  Since its discovery in the 1960’s, allostery has captivated the scientific community[1]. Numerous experimental, computational and theoretical studies have explored the mechanism, function and biomedical/bioengineering applications of allostery[2–13]. Thanks to these extensive efforts, great progress has been made concerning the understanding of allostery at both molecular and conceptual levels, which in turn has guided the development of various techniques for modulating and engineering allostery in diverse applications[14–19]. These include, for example, the design of allosteric drug[16–18, 20, 21], analysis of allosteric effects in proteins implicated in major diseases such as various Sars-CoV-2 targets[22–26], enzyme activity regulation[27–31] and engineering proteins to respond to external stimuli[15, 19, 32–34].

  To further deepen our understanding of allostery so as to better guide realistic applications, new experimental approaches that inspire the development of new mechanistic models are critical. For example, one of the major challenges is the identification of “allostery hotspots”[15, 18, 35], which are residues that dictate the degree of cooperativity between allosteric and active sites. Candidates for hotspots can be identified based on consideration of structural transitions[36], dynamics[37–39], motional coupling networks[40–42] as well as patterns in homologous sequences[5, 43]. However, since these analyses typically focus on one particular type of properties, the prediction is unlikely complete; with sequence based analysis, allostery is only one of the driving factors for fitness and therefore some of the identified residues may not be relevant to allostery. Moreover, to test predictions, with powerful but low-throughput experiments such as site-directed mutagenesis[44] and NMR chemical shift perturbations and relaxation measurements[45–47], only a limited number of sites can be studied, again preventing a comprehensive analysis of hotspot residues.

  Recent progress in the next generation sequencing technologies have enabled deep mutational scanning (DMS)[48], in which every site in the protein can be mutated to other amino acids in an exhaustive fashion. If the members of this comprehensive library can be screened for function in a high throughput fashion, DMS analysis allows an unbiased and systematic evaluation of each site’s contribution to function. In the context of allostery, this opens up the exciting opportunity to comprehensively identify allostery hotspots and understand their contributions to allosteric function.

  In the past few years, DMS has been applied to several allosteric systems[10], including transcription factors[49–51], membrane receptors and ion transporters[52–54]. These studies have provided a new function-centric angle to examine allostery and the data motivated the development of models to explain some of the fascinating observations that challenged our understanding of allosteric coupling in proteins. The system that drove our recent studies is TetR[55, 56], which is a family of bacterial transcription factors that respond to a broad set of ligands ranging from antibiotics (e.g., tetracycline) to lipids and signaling molecules. The specific TetR we studied is from E. coli and responds to tetracycline (Figure 1A). Without ligand, TetR is bound to the promoter sequence and represses expression of the downstream gene. Upon binding to tetracycline, a modest conformational change is induced in the DNA binding domain (Figure 1B, C), which leads to the detachment of TetR from the DNA, allowing gene expression. With a GFP gene incorporated downstream to the promoter sequence, activities of TetR variants can be readily characterized by monitoring GFP fluorescence in a high throughput fashion. In particular, if a mutation reduces the anti-cooperativity between ligand binding and DNA binding, TetR remains bound to the promoter, leading to the “dead” (non-inducible) phenotype (Figure 2A). If a high fraction of mutations at a site lead to the dead variant, this site is classified as an allostery hotspot.

  Motivated by observations from the DMS analysis of TetR[49], we have conducted a series of computational studies using different tools ranging from atomistic molecular dynamics (MD) simulations[49, 57, 58] to machine learning techniques[50] and statistical thermodynamic models[59]. The general goals of these studies are to answer the following questions: (1) What properties best distinguish the allostery hotspot residues? (2) What are the molecular level contributions from the hotspot residues to the allostery function? (3) What dictates the distribution of hotspot residues in the protein structure? (4) How do we systematically identify hotspot residues, by combining molecular simulations, experiments and potentially sequence information? (5) How do we evaluate the impact of hotspot mutations using computational models for guiding applications?

  In the following, we first briefly summarize the key observations from the DMS analysis, and then present the multi-faceted computational analyses that help answer the questions listed above. While different types of computational studies were conducted and published separately[49, 50, 57–59], we combine results from these studies in the discussion below to highlight how they complement each other in answering the key questions relevant to allostery hotspots. Finally, we conclude with a summary of major findings from our analyses and a discussion of future challenges and opportunities.


## Key Observations from DMS Analysis
  The DMS analysis was used to identify allostery hotspots in TetR[49] and three other homologous transcription factors[50]. The allostery hotspots were defined as sites for which a significant fraction of mutations abolished induction (“dead mutants”), and were not in direct contact with the ligand in the crystal structure; the threshold for the fraction of dead mutations was not unique and taken to be ~25%, although the precise value did not alter the qualitative trends regarding the location and distribution of hotspot residues.

  The most remarkable observation in the DMS analyses was that the allostery hotspots were distributed across the protein structure. Among the four homologs studied, the hotspots are somewhat concentrated in the C-terminal half of the protein in TetR and MphR, while they are more evenly distributed across the entire protein structure in TtgR and RolR (Fig. 2B). Importantly, the DMS results present a different picture for the locations of allostery hotspots identified based on the consideration of structural transitions. For TetR, for example, using the Ohm server[36] that analyzes propagation of structural distortion between the ligand and DNA binding sites leads to allostery hotspots localized in the DNA binding domain and the interface between the ligand-binding and DNA-binding domains (Fig. 1D), in stark contrast with the DMS results. This discrepancy suggests that the “stereochemical” type of models[1, 60, 61] abstracted by the schematic in Fig. 1C does not provide a complete description for the allostery that underlies the function of TetR, calling for the development of a different mechanistic framework.

  For TetR, in the background of five hotspot mutants with abolished induction function, additional DMS analyses were conducted to identify mutations that can restore induction[49]. As illustrated in Fig. 2C, these dead mutants could be rescued by diverse additional mutations, again highlighting a considerable level of degeneracy in the allostery network. Interestingly, different hotspot mutants could be rescued by different numbers of additional mutations, pointing to distinct degrees of rescuability of hotspot mutations. These observations also require a molecular level of explanation.


## Properties that Distinguish Allostery Hotspots: Insights from Machine Learning Analysis of DMS data and Atomistic Simulations
  To help develop algorithms for predicting allostery hotspots, we first aim to identify properties that distinguish these hotspot residues from non-hotspots. The systematic and unbiased nature of the DMS data provides an opportunity to conduct such an analysis. In addition, we have carried out extensive MD simulation for TetR to probe the properties of hotspot residues at the atomic level.

  The significant amount of the DMS data (~19×202 residues per monomer) suggests that machine learning (ML) techniques[11, 62] are poised to identify the properties unique to the hotspot residues. As shown in Fig. 3A, we start by assembling a set of features that include fundamental physicochemical properties of a site (e.g., its hydrophobicity), its local properties (e.g., solvent accessibility and local structural entropy[63]) and global properties (e.g., covariance matrix[64] with residues in the ligand-binding/DNA-binding sites). Then, a genetic algorithm-neural network (GA-NN) approach[65] was used to determine the minimal set of features that are most effective at classifying whether a site is an allostery hotspot or not[50]. The results for the four TetR homologs suggest that good classification accuracy can be achieved by using a modest (~5–6) set of features, although the best accuracy varies from ~50–60% for TtgR and RolR to ~80% for TetR and MphR. In terms of the features that consistently emerge in top-performing models, global features such as the covariance with ligand-binding/DNA-binding sites and the distances to peak positions of the centrality plots, which evaluate the level of connectivity of residues in a protein structure, dominate. Nevertheless, some local features, such as the B-factor[66], local structural entropy[63] and frustration index[67], also contribute to the classification of hotspot residues. These observations suggest that while allostery reflects long-range co-operativity in a protein, whether a site is an allostery hotspot or not may depend on its properties at both local and global scales.

  The relevance of properties at different scales is further illustrated in more detail with atomistic simulations for TetR[57], which probed the differences in a set of local and global properties between different functional states (apo, ligand-bound and DNA-bound states). The local properties include backbone and sidechain dihedral angle distributions and contact probabilities, and the global properties include covariance matrix elements with residues in the ligand-binding and DNA-binding sites, which were in turn used in suboptimal path analysis[27, 40] with the ligand-binding site and DNA-binding site as the source and drain, respectively. In Fig. 3B, the locations for the experimental hotspots that exhibit major changes of specific properties among different functional states are shown. The results clearly indicate that different hotspot residues feature distinct changes in specific properties. For example, hotspot residues feature dihedral and local contact changes among functional states are located near the domain interface, the C-terminal region and near the ligand binding site. By contrast, hotspot residues emerged in the covariance and sub-optimal path analyses distribute more broadly in the protein structure. This variation immediately suggests that the broad set of hotspot residues contribute to the allostery function of TetR in distinct ways, which we further clarify using atomistic simulations and a statistical thermodynamic model in the next section.


## Distinct Contributions from Allostery Hotspots: Atomistic Simulations and Statistical Thermodynamics Modeling
  To qualitatively rationalize the idea that hotspot residues contribute to the allostery function in distinct ways, we have developed a thermodynamic model for the TetR family of two-domain proteins. We then conducted extensive atomistic simulations for WT and hotspot mutants as well as a quantitative analysis of dose-response (induction) curves for a series of hotspot mutants to examine the contributions of hotspot residues in the framework of the thermodynamic model.

  In this thermodynamic model (Fig. 4), which is reminiscent of the classic MWC model for allostery and its extensions[2, 6, 68–70], the TetR protein is “coarse-grained” into two domains: the ligand-binding domain (LBD) and DNA-binding domain (DBD). In the low-free-energy (inactive) conformation, each domain has a low (or none) binding activity; upon activation, which is associated with a free energy cost (ϵL for LBD and ϵD for DBD), the domain is poised for binding, inducer for LBD and DNA for DBD, with an intrinsic binding free energy assumed to be insensitive to mutations. There is a free energy penalty, γ, that penalizes the two domains for adopting the same (inactive/active) conformation. In the WT, the parameters (ϵL, ϵD, γ) are such that the lowest-free energy state is the ligand-bound state, which reflects the induction activity (i.e., inducer binding displaces DNA) of TetR. Upon mutation, if either the DNA-bound or doubly-bound (i.e., with both inducer and DNA bound) state is the most stable, the induction function is abolished or compromised, leading to the “dead” variant. The key realization is that the dead variant can, in principle, arise due to two distinct “mechanisms”. If a mutation reduces γ significantly, the stability of the doubly-bound state is enhanced, which can lead to the non-inducible (dead) variant; this type of mutation likely implicates residues near the domain interface and connect the ligand and DNA binding sites. On the other hand, if a mutation either increases ϵL or decreases ϵD significantly, the DNA-bound state is better stabilized than the ligand-bound state, also leading to the dead variant; this type of mutations may involve residues coupled only to the ligand binding or DNA binding sites and therefore are distributed more broadly in the protein structure. Therefore, at a qualitative level, the thermodynamic model provides a framework that explains the broad distribution of hotspots in the protein structure as observed in the DMS experiment.

  Guided by the thermodynamic model, we carried out extensive atomistic MD simulations in different functional states for WT TetR[57] and several hotspot mutants[58] to understand the contributions of hotspot residues. We examined structural properties and free energy landscapes projected onto coordinates that represent the conformation of DNA binding and ligand binding sites. First, by comparing structural features such as contact probabilities in different functional states, we were able to identify residues that coupled conformational transitions in the ligand and DNA binding domains; mutation of these residues is expected to modify inter-domain coupling, γ. In Fig. 5A–B, we illustrate a set of polar interactions that govern the coupling between α9’ (D178’), α6 − l6 (G102, T103, R104), near the bottom of α8’ (E147’, E157’ and R158’) and α4 (R49, D53). Rearrangements of these polar interactions help propagate changes due to ligand binding to the interface between α4 and the DBDs, thus making a major contribution to the coupling between the two binding sites, which is more quantitatively illustrated with two-dimensional free energy surfaces computed using either long unbiased MD simulations[57] or the enhanced sampling approach metadynamics[58].

  We visualize the free energy surface (FES) by projecting onto the DBD distance and the l6-α8 distance, which reflects the compactness of the ligand binding site (Fig. 5C). In the WT, the FES of the two bound states are distinct and exhibit limited overlap along either collective variable, reflecting the strong anti-cooperativity between the LBDs and DBDs. This is in contrast with the FES of the hotspot mutants, for which the DBD distance distributions are much broader in the ligand bound state. For example, for G102D and G138H, two basins are clearly observed along the DBD distance coordinate, reflecting weaker inter-domain anti-cooperativity in these mutants. The trend is also reflected by the broader binding pocket distributions at the compressed DBD distance, suggesting that the ligand binding pocket becomes even more flexible when the DBDs favor DNA binding[60, 71] and thus showing even weaker inter-domain coupling in the doubly-bound basin in these mutants. Changes in inter-domain coupling are also evident in the apo FES, which is shown in Fig. 5D for several TetR variants; the specific plots are spanned by the collective DBD motion (represented by the first principal component PC1 used in Ref.57), and α7−α8′ distance (the Cα distance between Q109 and E147’) of the ligand binding pocket (Fig. 5E). The FES of the WT is much broader than those for the bound states (Fig. 5C), pointing to a conformational selection mechanism for TetR function[57, 72], and the diagonal shape of the surface is consistent with the strong anti-cooperativity expected for the LBDs and DBDs. For the two mutants involving the domain interface, G102D and R49A, the FES deviates significantly from the diagonal shape with an additional basin that features a positive PC1 value and a short α7 − α8′ distance, which corresponds to a conformational state that has low binding propensity to both the ligand and DNA. The appearance of this additional basin on the apo FES is qualitatively consistent with the expectation based on the thermodynamic model that a reduction in γ stabilizes the LE · DE configuration (Fig. 4).

  The MD simulations also revealed that in addition to inter-domain coupling, some hotspot mutations perturb intra-domain properties. This is most clearly illustrated with the ligand binding pocket in the apo state (Fig. 5F). While the WT favors the binding-competent conformation (long α7-α8’ distances), in the two mutants involving the domain interface (G102D and R49A), the ligand binding pocket is dominated by the binding-incompetent conformation (short α7-α8’ distances); this is expected to increase ϵL. The similar change is also observed for V145A, in which the mutation is close to both the domain interface and the monomer-monomer interface. In G102D and C-terminal mutants (I193A and G196D), the peak positions of the distributions favoring ligand-binding are notably shifted relative to WT, suggesting change in the binding-competent conformation and therefore variation in the intrinsic binding affinity. In other mutants such as L127D, the α7-α8’ distance distribution becomes unimodal and the peak position shifts towards a short distance, also suggesting considerable conformational rearrangements in the binding pocket and change in the ligand binding affinity.

  To further quantify the degree of perturbation in the inter-domain and intra-domain properties, we employ a quantitative biology approach to analyze the function of TetR. In particular, following the pioneering work of Phillips and co-workers[73, 74], we revised the thermodynamic model to describe the induction activity of TetR in E. coli. The key experimental observable is the induction curve (or dose-response curve), which describes the level of induction (probed by the expression of gene following the promoter sequence repressed by TetR) as a function of the inducer concentration; it is normalized against the WT activity and thus referred to as “fold change”. Following a series of justified approximations, the fold change (FC) can be expressed as,  (1) FC=1+R*e-εD1+e-εL-γcK21+e-εLcK2-1,  where R* is the rescaled transcription factor copy number in the cell[59, 74]; c is inducer concentration, and K is the dissociation constant of inducer to the repressor with an active LBD. We take R* and K to be constants not perturbed by mutations, and used Bayesian inference[75] to extract the three physical parameters using experimental induction data for a series of TetR variants. It is worth noting that, as illustrated in Fig. 6A, perturbations in the three parameters have qualitatively distinct impacts on the induction curve; these trends are indeed observed in the experimental data for the hotspot mutants (Fig. 6B), including an interesting mutant in which the cooperativity between inducer binding and DNA binding becomes positive, a phenotype of TetR mutants noted in previous studies[76]. In other words, by inspecting the changes in the experimental induction curve, one could draw qualitative conclusions regarding what physical parameter(s) are perturbed.

  At the quantitative level, the Bayesian inference was able to extract the physical parameters with generally small uncertainties, making it possible to compare different TetR variants. As shown in Fig. 6C, while γ is indeed reduced in most mutants that exhibited a reduced level of induction activity compared to the WT protein, many mutants feature a considerable amount of change in either ϵL or ϵD. Thus, the results directly support the general idea that hotspot mutations may perturb either inter-domain or intra-domain properties, or both, leading to varied levels of induction activities.


## Distribution and Evolution of Allostery Hotspots
  The thermodynamic model in Fig. 4 posits that the induction activity can be modulated by perturbing either the inter-domain coupling or intradomain properties, which is qualitatively consistent with observations from both atomistic MD simulations and the Bayesian inference analysis of experimental induction data. This parameter-activity degeneracy provides a plausible explanation for the distributed allosteric network observed in the DMS analysis[49]. By the same token, the model explains why rescue mutations were also observed to distribute broadly (Fig. 2C), as there are multiple ways to perturb the physical parameters to re-establish the ligand-bound state as the dominant population and therefore restore the induction function. Nevertheless, the idea of two limiting types of mutations is clearly abstract and realistic mutations likely implicate both effects albeit to different degrees.

  For example, we note in Figure 7A that, in the mutants analyzed herein, the intra-domain (especially ϵL) and inter-domain (γ) properties are often perturbed simultaneously, which points to the interconnectivity of allosteric networks[59]. This is consistent with the idea that when a residue is involved in the conformational rearrangements induced by both effector and operator binding, it functions as a bridge through which the two events are coupled. Within such a conceptual framework, it is unlikely to observe a mutation that changes γ alone without perturbing the intra-domain energetics.

  Such a consideration is also qualitatively consistent with observations from atomistic simulations discussed above that a single mutation may lead to notable perturbation in both types of properties. For example, G102D clearly impacts both inter-domain coupling, as reflected by the perturbed FES relative to the WT protein (Fig. 5C, D), and conformational distributions of the ligand-binding pocket (Fig. 5F). As additional nuances, the analysis of FES of multiple functional states further revealed that the inter-domain coupling is not simply a constant value but dependent on the particular conformational and binding state of the protein (Fig. 5C, D). Moreover, the apparent binding affinity can be modulated by perturbing either the activation free energy component (ϵL), which depends solely on the conformational distribution of the binding site in the apo state, or the intrinsic binding affinity associated with the binding-competent conformations (which was assumed to be a constant in the thermodynamic model but could be perturbed by mutations as observed in Fig. 5F).

  The computational results also provide hints regarding the evolution of the allostery function of TetR. On the one hand, the parameter-activity degeneracy implies a considerable degree of redundancy in the allostery network, suggesting that the activity-sequence landscape is likely rugged with many fitness peaks (Fig. 7B). This enables the TetR family of proteins to explore variations in functional characteristics, such as inducer specificity, without sacrificing the induction function significantly. Such a consideration is consistent with the relatively low level of conservation of hotspot residues in comparison to those dictate folding stability and DNA binding[49], and the chemically diverse set of inducers for the TetR family, ranging from antibiotics (e.g., tetracycline) to lipids and signaling molecules.[55, 56]. On the other hand, as noted above, the interconnected nature of allosteric networks dictates that mutation effects would take place along the diagonal of the ϵL − γ plane in Figure 7A, which modify intra- and inter-domain properties simultaneously. This feature potentially offers a functional advantage of preventing mutants from regions of low allosteric response (corresponding to upper right and lower left of Figure 7A), facilitating efficient adaptation to new effectors during evolution.


## Prediction of Allostery Hotspots: Atomistic Simulations and Machine Learning
  In applications, it is valuable to employ computations to identify allostery hotspot residues for guiding the experimental modulation of the allostery function. The algorithm designed for this purpose would rely on the mechanistic basis for these hotspots. For example, in the “traditional” stereochemical models, hotspot residues are expected to relay conformational transitions that propagate from the allosteric site to the active site; this mechanistic model inspired computational algorithms that search for hub residues that mediate conformational transitions based on either the analysis of structural distortions[36] or covariance matrix from MD simulations[40–42]. As discussed above (Fig. 1D) and in previous work[49], the overlap between residues identified with these algorithms and the DMS experiment for TetR is rather limited, suggesting that the underlying mechanistic model is incomplete and that complementary strategies are required.

  The mechanistic analyses discussed thus far highlight that hotspot residues contribute through perturbing inter-domain coupling and intra-domain properties to different degrees. Thus our strategy for hotspot search is to analyze both local and global properties, especially their variations among different functional states, as also emphasized in previous work that focused on contact probabilities[42]. The underlying hypothesis is that hotspot residues undergo changes, in either local or global properties, as the system transitions among functional states. Specifically, we systematically analyzed changes in the distributions of backbone (ϕ,ψ) and sidechain (χ1) dihedral angles and local contact probabilities between the ligand-bound and DNA-bound states. For global properties, we analyzed covariance matrix with the ligand-binding and DNA-binding residues in long (~ 50μs) MD simulations; we found that the apo state was most revealing for this purpose since it is structurally most flexible, while the bound states tend to be rigidified due to ligand/DNA binding[57]. Finally, the covariance matrix is also used in a suboptimal path analysis[27, 40] to identify hub residues that most frequently connect the ligand and DNA binding sites through motionally correlated residues.

  Representative results are shown in Fig. 8A. There are two key observations. First, these analyses point to a large number of residues, especially the dihedral analysis and covariance analysis, many of which were not identified by the DMS experiment (compare Figs. 3B and 8A). The suboptimal analysis helps “filter” the results of the covariance analysis by considering also the frequency of being along the pathway that connects the ligand and DNA binding sites. Nevertheless, this still leaves a considerable number of residues not in the list of experimental hotspots. In other words, these analyses lead to a considerable number of “false positives”. This is perhaps not surprising since the analyses are based entirely on the WT protein and do not probe explicitly the effect of the mutation of these residues on functionally relevant properties, which is the topic of the next section. Indeed, while some residues participate in allostery in the WT protein, their mutations may have a limited functional impact and therefore will not be identified as hotspot residues in experiment. For example, several charged residues at the C-terminus of α8 (E157 and R158) are observed to relay conformational changes to α4 (Fig. 5A, B), but they were not identified as hotspot residues, likely because the C-terminal region of α8 contains other charged amino acids (K155, E156, E159), and thus the roles of E157/ R158 can be taken up by these residues upon mutation.

  Second, to recapitulate the experimentally identified list of hotspots, it is required to combine these analyses; as shown in Table 1 of Ref.57 and Fig. 3B, each type of analysis identified a subset of the experimental hotspots, again highlighting the point that different hotspots contribute to allostery in distinct ways. For example, some hotspot residues were identified by the covariance analysis, but not by the suboptimal path analysis; in the framework of our thermodynamic model, the former hotspots are likely to modulate intra-domain properties while the latter hotspots are expected to govern inter-domain coupling. It is also worth noting that even by combining the four types of analyses, a number of experimental hotspots in the C-terminal region and the middle of the α8 helix were missed by the WT based analysis[57]. This again highlights the importance of explicitly studying mutants for probing allostery hotspots (vide infra).

  As a complementary data-driven approach, we explore whether ML models can be leveraged for hotspot prediction[50]. In particular, we ask to what degree a ML classification model trained for one protein (Fig. 3A) can be used to infer hotspot residues in a homologous protein. As shown in Figure 8B, direct application of a GA-NN model trained for one or more homologous systems (e.g., TetR, TtgR and RolR) to another homolog (MphR) leads to very poor classification results. However, an interesting observation is that transfer learning can be remarkably effective: with a modest re-training of the model with 10% of the mutation data for MphR, the model is almost as accurate as a model specifically trained for MphR. This observation suggests that there is indeed conserved “underlying structure” for the allostery network among the homologs that can be taken advantage of for cross-protein hotspot predictions. For this purpose, the reliability of the prediction is not highly sensitive to the quality of the structural model (8C); considering the dramatic progress in structural predictions in recent years[77], the availability of experimental structures is not expected to be a major bottleneck for hotpot predictions.


## Evaluation of Allostery Hotspot Mutants: Free Energy Simulation and Statistical Thermodynamic Modeling
  The limitations in the hotspot analysis discussed in the last section highlights the importance of explicitly probing the effect of mutation for a better understanding of the contribution of a particular site to allostery. For this purpose, it is desirable to compute properties that are directly relevant to the functional readout, rather than inferring the functional impact indirectly by structural and dynamical properties. Specifically for TetR, this pertains the ability to bind to the DNA in the presence of the inducer, which we pursue again using complementary approaches of atomistic simulations and statistical thermodynamic models.

  With atomistic simulations, we quantify the impact of mutation by performing functionally relevant free energy computations. In particular, structural studies of TetR suggested that inducer binding rigidifies the protein structure such that the DBDs remain too far apart to bind DNA[60, 71]. Thus we reasoned that the loss of the induction function in at least some hotspot mutants is due to the enhanced propensity to adopt DBD conformations favorable for DNA binding even in the inducer-bound state. Accordingly, we conducted free energy simulations using the DBD distance separation as the collective variable, and compared the free energy profiles for several hotspot mutants with that of the WT protein[58]. In particular, we selected several representative mutants that involve hotspot residues in distinct regions of the protein: domain interface (G102D and R49A), near the ligand binding site (L127D and G138D/H), and near the monomer-monomer interface, either in the middle of the α8 helix (V145A) or in the C-terminal helix (I193A and G196D).

  Representative results are shown in Fig. 9A–B. In the WT protein, the free energy profile exhibits a single minimum at long (~46 Å) DBD center-of-mass distance, which is not favorable for DNA binding. This is consistent with the induction function of the WT protein, which does not bind DNA in the inducer-bound state. With the hotspot mutants studied, the free energy profile all exhibit an additional basin at the short DBD distance (~38 Å); comparison of conformational distribution with the DNA-bound form confirmed that this basin is consistent with favorable DNA binding. Therefore, the free energy simulations explicitly showed that these hotspot mutations stabilize the doubly-bound (i.e., both inducer and DNA-bound) conformation, leading to the decrease of induction function. These results demonstrate that hotspot mutation effects can propagate from many regions in the protein to the DBDs, including the C-terminus, which is at the opposite end of the protein structure to the DBDs. This observation highlights the global nature of energetic coupling in proteins[70], and is consistent with the distributed nature of allosteric networks as observed in the DMS study[49] and our MD analysis[57].

  The stability of the basin at short DBD distance varies among the hotspot mutants. It is the highest for G102D, less so for V145A and even less for those in the C-terminal region (I193A and G196D) and mutation of a charged residue at the domain interface (R49A). This trend is similar to that in the rescuability of these mutations, which was defined as the ability of restoring the induction function with additional mutations (Fig. 2C)[49]. Therefore, the observation supports the idea that mutations that lead to a large change in the functional free energy landscape are more difficult to rescue. The fact that free energy computations can be used to rank the rescuability of hotspot mutants is of major practical interest, as successful design of allostery inhibition needs to target allostery hotspots that are particularly difficult to rescue, since otherwise allostery can be readily restored with additional mutations, leading to abolishment of inhibition.

  As an alternative approach, we also explored the use of the thermodynamic model to predict the activity of higher order mutations[59]. With the simplest model, we assume that the mutation effects on the three physical parameters (ϵL, ϵD, γ) are additive. Despite its simplicity, this type of model worked well for the LacI repressor studied by Phillips and co-workers[74] and a recent protein stability analysis[78]. However, it did not work well for TetR[59], highlighting the complexity of allosteric systems. Nevertheless, we find that the performance can be substantially improved if we assume that the changes of the three parameters are dominated by the mutation close to the relevant part of the protein structure (e.g., domain interface for γ, see Fig. 9C–D). Although this was tested only for a handful of higher order mutants, the results provide the hint that reliable prediction can be obtained with the current thermodynamic framework and a relatively simple model for epistasis.


## Concluding Discussions: Progress, Challenges and Future Prospectives
  In this article, we have discussed our recent computational studies of a transcription factor, TetR, motivated by the DMS analysis that provided a systematic and unbiased evaluation of residual contributions to the allostery function. By integrating atomistic simulations, machine learning analysis and statistical thermodynamic modeling, we have gained new insights into the structural and energetic basis of allostery in TetR, and the contributions of allostery hotspots. We have also established strategies for identifying hotspot residues and evaluating the functional impact of their mutations. The mechanistic framework and the multi-faceted computational approaches are expected to be valuable to the analysis of other allostery systems, especially those sharing the similar two-domain fold, and to the design of allostery modulators.

  Regarding the anti-cooperativity between the two binding sites, our analyses focused primarily on the thermodynamic and structural aspects. Free energy calculations indicated that the anti-cooperativity is already encoded in the apo state: although the apo protein samples a broad set of conformations, the diagonal shape of the free energy surface (Fig. 5D and Fig. 10) suggested that only combinations of specific conformations of the ligand-binding and DNA-binding sites are energetically accessible. Comparison of structural features and contact probabilities in different functional states helped identify residues that mediate the coupling between the two binding sites, and their contributions were evaluated by examining how the free energy surface was perturbed by the corresponding mutations. The computed free energy surfaces in different ligation states clearly illustrated that allostery in TetR is well described by a conformational selection model, which is consistent with NMR analysis of related systems[79]. Visualization of free energy landscape and its variation due to either mutation or other perturbations (e.g., ligand binding) is a powerful and quantitative way to analyze allostery, as echoed by studies of other systems[80, 81]. Shifts in the free energy landscape often has clear manifestation in the variation of protein dynamics, regardless of the magnitude of change in the average conformation[39, 82, 83]. For example, it was suggested that in some systems, ligand binding leads to the emergence of slow, global dynamics that propagate into changes in distal regions[8, 84]. In TetR, although we did not explicitly dissect the nature of protein motions or their time scales, computed free energy landscapes clearly indicate significant structural rigidification upon ligand or DNA binding, which is indeed expected to lead to slow and collective motions, thus supporting the previous suggestion[84].

  One major point of our analysis is that the allostery function (i.e., induction activity) of TetR can be modulated by perturbing both inter-domain coupling and intra-domain properties, such as the population of the binding-competent conformation of each domain. This mechanistic degeneracy qualitatively explains the broad distribution of allostery hotspots across the protein structure observed in the DMS experiments, and the fact that dead variants can be rescued in also a distributed fashion. This mechanistic framework also informs the design of strategies aimed at identifying allostery hotspots. On the one hand, it is important to systematically analyze both local and global properties, especially those exhibiting changes during functional state transitions. Focusing on those that dictate inter-domain coupling, for example, as often done in pathway type of analysis, albeit informative, is likely to miss a considerable set of residues important to the allostery function. On the other hand, the mechanistic degeneracy also suggests that compensatory effects are prevalent, thus it is essential to explicitly evaluate the mutation effect of hotspot candidates. To provide guidance in real-world applications such as allosteric modulator design, the goal is not merely to identify sites that exhibit some outstanding features in the WT (e.g., change in local contact probabilities among different functional states) but those whose perturbations (i.e. mutations or modulator binding) tend to have a major impact on functionally relevant properties (e.g., free energy landscape; see Fig. 10). Thus a major task is to develop effective and efficient computational strategies for the evaluation of functional activities for mutants, which might rely on either detailed simulations or parameterized surrogate models based on experimental data.

  In terms of atomistic simulations, adequate sampling and identification of collective variables for functionally relevant free energy simulations represent two particularly important challenges. Even for a relatively small protein such as TetR (a homodimer with ~ 200 residues each), conducting more than 50 μs of sampling required specialized computers (Anton 2)[57]; computed structural/dynamic properties were not identical for the two monomers after tens of μs of sampling, suggesting that convergence was still not achieved. Therefore, enhanced sampling is essential, especially for meaningful free energy simulations when the impact of mutation is on the order of a few kcal/mol or less. Along this line, innovative approaches for identifying collective variables based on, for example, machine learning[85, 86], or consideration of energy flow in biomolecules[87], are particularly relevant. Another fascinating direction is to integrate molecular simulations with modern technologies such as virtual reality[88, 89], such that human intuition and spatial awareness can be taken advantage of during the exploration of effective collective variables. More efficient ways to generate conformational ensembles[90] and assign their functional relevance[91] are also highly valuable. Finally, it is important to remember that despite significant progress[92, 93], biomolecular force fields are not yet “perfect” and it is always worthwhile verifying the robustness of computational analyses by quantitatively comparing results obtained using different force fields[94–96].

  In terms of surrogate models, such as the statistical thermodynamic model explored in our work, an interesting direction is to integrate residual interactions into the model so that higher order mutants can be effectively analyzed to guide experimental design with more efficient sampling of the sequence space[97, 98]. Such information may come from sequence analysis of residual co-evolution[99], which was found valuable in structural prediction[100, 101], enzyme design[102], and analysis of drug resistance[103] and emergence of epistasis in protein evolution[104]. On the one hand, to parameterize a reliable model, an extensive amount of experimental data for activity is essential[105]. On the other hand, analyzing large dataset in the framework of a physically motivated model not only enhances molecular level understanding[74] but also improves the data-efficiency of the learning and engineering process[106]. This is particularly relevant for the development of interpretable machine learning models for the analysis and design of allostery. In this context, recent developments in continuous evolution techniques have enabled the generation of a large number of sequences driven by fitness better connected to specific functions[107], and such data sets are particularly suited for the parametrization of models relevant to these functions.

  Finally, we note that all the analyses discussed are equilibrium in nature and focused on, for example, motional covariance and free energy landscapes of different functional states. Much insight can be expected regarding allostery hotspots from non-equilibrium analyses of conformational transitions that propagate from allosteric to active sites, as illustrated by several recent studies[26, 108–110]. While such conformational transitions had to be studied with biased molecular simulations such as targeted molecular dynamics for large systems in the past[111], methodological advances have enabled the sampling[112] and analysis[113, 114] of these events and their kinetic properties for increasingly complex systems. At the cellular level, non-equilibrium effects were also shown to be important to many processes, such as the regulation of transcription in eukaryotic cells[115], giving rise to additional level of complexity for allostery in the cellular context. Understanding the unique functional characteristics of allostery under non-equilibrium conditions[13, 116] will be an interesting area of research.
