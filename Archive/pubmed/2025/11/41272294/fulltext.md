# Identification of potential anti-biofilm agents targeting LasR in Pseudomonas aeruginosa through machine learning-driven screening, molecular docking, and dynamics simulations

## Introduction
  Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a significant health threat, causing millions of deaths annually. Over 80% of infections are linked to biofilm-forming pathogens, which contribute to AMR, chronic infections, immune responses, and external stress making treatment difficult1–3. Pseudomonas aeruginosa stands out among these pathogens as a particularly severe opportunistic pathogen that can cause a variety of infections, such as respiratory, urinary tract, soft tissue, and otitis4. P. aeruginosa’s pathogenicity is mostly due to its capacity to build biofilms, which function as a barrier against the immune system, antibiotics, and unfavorable environmental circumstances. This characteristic makes P. aeruginosa harmful, especially to immunocompromised people who are more likely to have a serious infection. Biofilms have detrimental effects on both healthcare and industry, contributing to persistent infections, higher treatment costs, and longer hospital stays in healthcare, while leading to increased maintenance expenses and product contamination in industry5. P. aeruginosa can colonize implanted medical devices and progress to a growth mode characterized as biofilm, where it creates biofilm made of proteins, DNA, and exopolysaccharides-rich extracellular matrix. The bacterial population is enclosed in this matrix, which enables it to stick to both living and non-living surfaces6,7. P.aeruginosa has developed a number of defense mechanisms against antibiotics, including a decreased outer membrane permeability, efflux pumps to remove antibiotics, and the synthesis of enzymes that render them inactive. Resistance in P. aeruginosa can arise from mutations or from the horizontal transfer of resistance genes.

  P. aeruginosa forms biofilms due to its diverse metabolic capacity, which acts as a barrier against antimicrobials and enhances resistance. However, if the biofilm structure is compromised, the bacteria’s sensitivity to antibiotics may be restored. P. aeruginosa uses a communication mechanism called quorum sensing, which is dependent on cell density, to control the production of biofilms and pathogenicity. Signaling molecules including N-acylated L-homoserine lactones (AHLs), which are detected by LuxR-like receptors, are essential to this system8, which are subsequently recognized by LuxR-like receptors. P. aeruginosa features two LuxR-like receptors: RhlR, which binds N-butanoyl homoserine lactone, and LasR, which binds N-3-oxo-dodecanoyl homoserine lactone. Each receptor generally comprises two domains: one for DNA binding and one for AHL binding. The bacterium depends on two main LuxI/R quorum-sensing systems: the LasR and Rhl systems9,10. LasR transcriptional regulator responds to the presence of N-3-oxo-dodecanoyl homoserine lactone (3OC12-HSL). The 3OC12-HSL signal influences the expression of genes via modulating the transcription of effector genes by attaching to a particular signal receptor known as LasR (encoded by LasR)11,12. LasR upon binding with 3OC12-HSL undergoes a conformational change, resulting in its dimerization initiating transcription of genes involved in virulence factor production and biofilm formation (Fig. 1). LasR also controls the expression of other quorum sensing systems, including the Rhl system.

  Targeting LasR represents a strategic approach to disrupt biofilm formation and reduce the pathogenicity of P. aeruginosa13,14. The rationale for focusing on LasR lies in its central role in controlling biofilm development and regulating the expression of multiple virulence factors. Inhibiting LasR could potentially dismantle the biofilm structure, enhance bacterial susceptibility to antibiotics, and diminish the overall virulence of P. aeruginosa. This strategy has the potential to offer a novel therapeutic avenue for combating infections caused by this opportunistic pathogen.Recent therapeutic advances against Pseudomonas aeruginosa infections involve novel strategies to combat resistance mechanisms including quorum sensing inhibition, vaccine development, and monoclonal antibodies. These novel approaches aim to combat resistance by interfering with biofilm formation and enhancing antibiotic efficacy. Specifically, disrupting quorum sensing, especially LasR signaling, offers potential for reducing biofilm formation and restoring antibiotic sensitivity15–17. Previous research has made major contributions to our understanding of anti-biofilm agents for Pseudomonas aeruginosa. For example, Nadine Khadraoui et al. discovered compounds from Myrtus communis that inhibit quorum sensing-regulated virulence factors and biofilm development, demonstrating the promise of natural products in fighting biofilm-related illnesses18. Another study focused on flavonol esters and found that they inhibited Pseudomonas aeruginosa19. In addition, research on benzoic acid derivatives has showed promising anti-biofilm action in Pseudomonas aeruginosa, demonstrating that diverse chemical architectures can efficiently disrupt biofilm development20.

  Advances in computational methods like machine learning, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics have significantly reduced the time and cost of drug discovery21–23. These methods can rapidly screen chemical libraries, predict compounds with high binding affinities, and assess the stability and dynamics of interactions in a realistic biological environment24,25. Phytochemicals are rich in diverse chemical matter and biological activities, contributing to drug discovery in infectious diseases26,27. Small molecules that disrupt bacteria’s biofilm formation can overcome antibiotic tolerance and form combination therapies with conventional antibiotics28. Biologically active phytochemicals have been extensively researched for their QS inhibition and antibiofilm effects. These chemicals can inhibit virulence factors and biofilm formation without killing bacteria, lowering the selective pressure for resistance development. Phytochemicals can block QS by interfering with signaling molecules, destroying autoinducers (AIs), and preventing QS gene expression29–31. In this study, an integrated approach was employed to identify potential anti-biofilm agents. While extensive research exists on anti-biofilm agents, this work uniquely combines machine learning-based screening, molecular docking, and dynamics simulations to specifically target the LasR quorum-sensing system in Pseudomonas aeruginosa, which may efficiently impede the production of biofilms, hence aiding in the creation of novel treatment methods to combat P. aeruginosa infections.

## Methods

### Preparing the dataset and descriptors generation
  A dataset comprising 116 molecules with reported inhibitory activity against LasR was obtained from the BindingDB database32. Additionally, 324 decoy molecules, considered to be inactive, were generated using the DUDE database33. This resulted in a total of 440 compounds, with 116 active compounds from BindingDB labeled as “1” and 324 decoys labeled as “0”. The dataset was then split into two subsets, 70% for training and 30% for test data set using Scikit-learn python library. Data preprocessing and cleaning were conducted using the Pandas library in Python. The 2D descriptors of the data set were generated using RDKit (https://www.rdkit.org/). Total 35 descriptors were generated. Descriptors with null values were discarded from the dataset only the most relevant and informative features were kept ensuring that the dataset contained high-quality features for model training and testing. The overall workflow of the present strategy is presented in Fig. 2.

### Principle component analysis (PCA)
  Using the PCA tool from Scikit-learn, principle component analysis (PCA) was carried out to decrease the number of features (dimensionality) in the dataset while keeping the majority of the variability (information). PCA is a technique that preserves most of the pertinent data while converting high-dimension data to low-dimension data. The original characteristics are changed into a new collection of variables known as primary components. Its main advantages include low sensitivity to noise, reduced capacity and memory requirements, and improved performance34. PCA was configured to extract two principal components. Once the PCA model was fitted to the data, these two components were used for further analysis.


### Machine learning methods
  Various machine learning models were developed based on the train and test data sets. The machine learning methods used to trains the models were used Random Forest, Naïve Bayes (NB), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) and Support Vector Machine (SVM). Out of these four the best model was further used to find the active compounds from a library of molecules.


### Support vector machine (SVM)
  Boser and Vapnik created SVM, machine learning technique for identifying recurring patterns in complicated datasets34. The data is represented as feature vectors shown in a space with corresponding dimensions, resulting in the formation of a hyper plane to separate data points into two distinct groups. Although SVM is often used for supervised learning with predefined categories, it may also be utilized in unsupervised learning scenarios35. In virtual screening (VS), SVM is used because it allows for precise classification of drugs as active or inactive36. SVM is often used for data categorization by creating margins between classes and attempting to increase the distance between the margin and the classes in order to decrease mistakes. SVM is useful for high-dimensional data classification and relies on kernel functions; nevertheless, its performance might suffer when the dataset contains noise or overlapping target classes37.


### K nearest neighbor model (KNN)
  Cover and Hart proposed K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), a simple, non-parametric supervised machine learning technique for pattern classification. KNN classifies query data points based on votes from the nearest k data points, with proximity determined by distance metrics such as Euclidean, Hamming, and Cosine distances to measure closeness. KNN memorizes all existing data points and classifies new instances based on their closeness to these previously recorded points38.


### Naïve Bayes (NB)
  The Naive Bayes (NB) algorithm, based on Bayes’ theorem, is a trustworthy classification approach that assumes each feature contributes equally and independently. A specific variation, Gaussian Naive Bayes (GNB), is widely used for classification problems and works under the assumption that characteristics are independent of the class label. This classification method assumes that the chance of activity is proportional to the number of active vs. inactive molecules with the same descriptor value39,40.


### Random forest (RF)
  Random Forest (RF) is an ensemble method that consists of many decision trees, much like a forest does41. It is typically applied to classification and regression problems. In RF, decision trees are trained on different subsets of the training dataset, with each tree generated by randomly selected feature subsets. Each decision tree evaluates a fresh sample’s input vector before determining the final categorization by majority vote42. RF outperforms individual decision trees, particularly on big datasets, and it also highlights the most important variables or features for categorization. The RF model and other machine learning models were developed using Python’s Scikit-learn module.


### Model evaluation
  GridSearchCV and cross-validation were employed to optimize and validate models. Evaluation of the model performance is an important phase in machine learning since it allows you to measure the accuracy and efficacy of a model. Models were evaluated using four parameters, with accuracy being among the most often used for measuring model performance. This metric shows the classifier prediction accuracy for both true positives (TP) and true negatives (TN)43. In addition, the ROC curve was created, which visually depicts the classification model’s performance over all conceivable thresholds. As a thorough assessment of the model’s performance across all thresholds, the Area under the ROC Curve (AUC-ROC) was also computed. The AUC indicates the degree or amount of separability. It describes how well the model can discriminate between positive and negative classes44.


### ML based virtual screening
  A dataset of 9000 bioactive compounds were used to select the active compounds using the optimized machine learning model. This dataset was collected from PubChem, ChEMBL, and ZINC database which provide detailed information about the small molecules, making them invaluable for drug discovery. This new dataset was preprocessed and feature extracted in the same way as the original dataset. Lipinski’s Rule of Five was used to narrow our compound selection and increase the possibility that these molecules have drug-like characteristics. This criterion is a commonly established guideline in the field of drug discovery and is used to assess a molecule’s drug-likeness. Lipinski’s Rule of Five states that a substance is more likely to be orally effective in humans if it possesses MolWT ≤ 500, LogP ≤ 5, HBD ≤ 5, HBA ≤ 10 in humans.


### Molecular docking
  The crystallographic structure of LasR was downloaded from PDB under the PDB ID 3JPU https://www.rdkit.org/. Ligands and Protein structures were prepared using AutoDock tools. To prepare the protein for docking simulations all the crystallographic waters and other molecules were removed to ensure that only the protein is present for the docking simulation prior to docking. The protein structure was prepared using AutoDock tools. Only polar hydrogens were added to the protein and charges were assigned. Grid box was centered based on the co-crystallized ligand N8 atoms x, y, z coordinates (X_center = −19.473, Y_center = − 6.344, Z_cenetr = 19.984) and grid box size was set to x = 45, y = 45 and z = 45Angstrom (Å). Finally the docking simulations were performed using AutoDock vina45 with exhaustiveness 16 and number of poses 20 for each compound. After docking the compounds were ranked on the basis of the binding energy scores. Consequently, the “scoring power,” which refers to the ability of the docking program to correctly predict binding poses, was assessed to estimate the docking accuracy. For this purpose the crystallized ligand was re-docked into the corresponding binding site of LasR.


### Molecular dynamics simulations
  Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulation is an insilico technique which is used to model the binding behavior and mechanisms of ligand-protein complexes. For this study, GROMACS 202146 was employed for simulating both the Apo forms and the complexes derived from AutoDock Vina. The CHARMM force field was used for protein topology, while ligand topology was constructed using the CGENFF server47,48. The systems were solvated in a cubic box with the TIP3 water model, positioning the protein 15 Å from each side of the box. Sodium ions (Na+) were added to neutralize the system. Following neutralization, steepest descent methods was used for energy minimization for 1000 steps. The systems were then equilibrated using NVT and NPT ensembles to stabilize temperature and pressure, respectively. Each system underwent a 100 ns simulation, and the resulting trajectories were saved for further analysis. A 100 ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulation was selected due to its established efficacy in capturing essential structural and dynamic properties for systems of comparable complexity. Prior studies have demonstrated that this simulation duration is sufficient to monitor the stabilization of ligand-protein interactions and analyze the dynamic stability of complexes. Additionally, it enables the system to equilibrate and explore relevant conformational spaces, offering strong insights into interaction dynamics without placing excessive computational demands49–51​. All calculations were carried out on an RTX 2060 GPU. Analysis of RMSD and RMSF was conducted using GROMACS utilities.


### Free energy analysis (MMPBSA)

This helps in understanding biomolecular interactions and their components. The gmx_MMPBSA module was utilized for these calculations52. This program determines the binding energy of a protein-ligand complex by evaluating various binding components, including polar, non-polar, bonded, and non-bonded interactions. ## Results

### Data set processing
  A total of 116 molecules were downloaded from the BindingDB for LasR and 324 decoys were generated using DUDE. The 116 inhibitors of LasR were labeled as active (1), while the 324 decoys were marked as inactive (0). The dataset divided into a train set (70%) and a test set (30%), containing 308 compounds in the train set and 132 in the test set including both active and inactive. In this study, four different algorithms KNN, SVM, GNB, and RF were employed to train models. A total of 35 descriptors were calculated using RDkit (Table S1). Descriptors with zero values were discarded, as not all extracted features inevitably represent the optimal properties of the molecules. To ensure the quality of the features, further optimization was conducted to eliminate redundancy.


### Principle component
  In this study, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was used to reduce the original 35 descriptors of the compounds to two principal components capturing the majority of the dataset’s variance (information). PCA excludes those features that cause sparsity in our dataset. By reducing the data to two dimensions, it becomes easier to visualize and analyze, while retaining most of the original information. The eigenvalues for the first and second components were 791.67 and 7.40, respectively, indicating how much variance each component explains. The PCA1, with a much larger eigenvalue, accounted for approximately 98% of the variance, thus encapsulating most of the significant information from the original high-dimensional data. The PCA2 added about 2% to the explained variance indicating that PCA1 captures almost all of the meaningful variation in your data, while PCA2 contributes very little. The PCA1 is heavily influenced by the MolWt feature, which is a major contributor to the variance captured by this component while PCA2, on the other hand, is more influenced by the NumValenceElectrons feature, explaining a smaller fraction of the variance. These findings show that PCA is a useful method for reducing dimensionality while preserving a significant amount of important data from the dataset. The two main components are plotted in scatter form in Fig. 2, making it easy to distinguish between the active and inactive phytochemical compounds and presents that how the compounds are distributed in the reduced dimensional space. The axes ranging in Fig. 3 from − 80 to + 80 (PC1) explains the maximum variance in the dataset which is 98%. The large range of −80 to + 80 indicates significant differences in the features contributing most to the variance among compounds. While − 4 to + 8 (PC2) explains the second-largest variance (2%) orthogonal to PC1.

### Chemical space and model evaluation
  The efficiency and accuracy of machine learning models are significantly influenced by the chemical diversity within the dataset, also known as the “chemical space.” This includes molecular descriptors and properties like molecular weight and LogP etc. A model trained on a chemically diverse dataset is more likely to generalize well to new data, making chemical diversity a crucial factor in predictive model development53.

  In this work, a thorough physicochemical distribution analysis was conducted of both the training and test sets, with a particular emphasis on essential molecular descriptors. The study revealed a broad range of chemical properties in our sample, with molecular weights ranging from 80 to 650 Daltons and LogP values ranging from − 3 to 7 (Fig. 4). This vast range reflects a diversified chemical space, which should improve the model’s capacity to generalize across different chemical entities. To categorize active inhibitors of LasR, a quorum-sensing transcriptional regulator, four different machine learning techniques were used. These methods were chosen because they are often used and successful in classification tasks, notably in cheminformatics. To further evaluate and compare the performance of these models, Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve was used (Fig. 5), a graphical tool that depicts the trade-off between sensitivity and specificity at various threshold levels. The ROC curve is a useful tool for determining how effectively a model distinguishes between a hit and a non-hit for a certain ligand and protein. The AUC (ROC) is a summary statistic that measures performance at all categorization levels. An AUC value of 1 implies a flawless model with 100% true positives and 0% false positives, whereas an AUC value of 0.5 shows a model with no discriminative capacity54. The performance of models was evaluated based on accuracy, sensitivity, MCC and specificity. Random Forest outperformed the other models with accuracy 0.98 and MCC 0.95, delivering the highest AUC values of 0.99 on the training set and 0.92 on the test set (Table 1; Fig. 5). Consequently, the Random Forest model was used to classify 9000 phytochemicals, predicting 367 as active against LasR. Table 1 shows the performance of the models on the test set.

### Molecular docking
  The 155 hits were obtained by the machine learning-based virtual screening procedure, and they were then docked into the LasR protein’s ligand-binding site. The binding pocket in LasR is positioned between the helices α3, α4, and α5 and the β-sheet core. The L3 loop, which extends from Leu-40 to Phe-51, is critical for preserving the binding pocket’s integrity. This loop efficiently encloses the ligand-binding site, keeping the ligand within the pocket and shielded from the solvent environment. This protective process serves to stabilize the ligand within the protein while also contributing to binding selectivity. At the bottom of the binding pocket, crucial amino acid residues create hydrogen bonds with the co-crystallized ligand TP4. These interactions are required for appropriate ligand binding and stability inside the pocket55. To evaluate the precision of docking procedure and parameters, ligand TP4 was removed from the active site and docked back into the binding site of LasR. The RMSD value between the highest ranked docked conformation and the co-crystallized ligand was 0.323 Å (Figure S1A), demonstrating the high accuracy of Autodock vina docking procedure. The crystallized compound forms four hydrogen bonds with the key catalytic residues of LasR, including Tyr56, Asp73, and Ser129 (Figure S1B). This confirms the reliability of the docking process and the interactions within the ligand-binding pocket. When comparing the final top compounds to the crystallized compound, all of them demonstrated stable interactions with the residues in the binding site of the LasR target. Among these compounds, PubChem 3795981 exhibited the strongest interactions. It formed five hydrogen bonds with the active site residues. Oxygen of dihydronaphthalen ring of compounds made three hydrogen bonds with side chain oxygen of residue Thr75, Thr115, and Ser129, One hydrogen bonds with the Trp60 while the benzyl ring formed face to face π- π stacking interaction with Tyr56 with binding energy score − 12.0 kcal/mol. Compound PubChem 42607867 also formed hydrogen bond interactions with the ligand binding site resiude Tyr56 and Ser129 which are crucial for binding site integrity, and established a π-stacking interaction with Tyr46. This compound also achieved a binding energy score of −12.0 kcal/mol. Compound PubChem 6971066 formed 2 hydrogen bonds with Trp60 and Ser129 with binding energy score − 11.8 kcal/mol. Figure 5 depicts the binding orientation and hydrogen bond interactions within the ligand binding site of LasR. All the compounds were well accommodated in the ligand binding site of LasR forming hydrogen bond interactions with the critical residues, demonstrating their potential as effective binders. The consistent involvement of Tyr56 and Ser129 in hydrogen bonding across different compounds highlights their critical role in ligand binding and the overall structural integrity of the active site. The binding orientations and hydrogen bond interactions of these compounds, as depicted in Fig. 6, reveal a consistent pattern of key residues being targeted by potential inhibitors. ### Molecular dynamics simulation
  The RMSD (Root Mean Square Deviation) data give information on the stability of the protein-ligand complexes over the simulation period. Figure 7A describes the RMSD of the apo LasR, reference compound TP4 complexed with LasR and the three candidate compounds complexed with LasR. The RMSD of complex 3795981 is greater, varying between 0.2 and 0.25 nm from 20ns to 50ns, indicating considerable structural alterations and instability. However, after 50 ns, the system gradually approaches equilibrium and remains stable until the completion of the 100 ns experiment, indicating the complex eventually achieves a more stable conformation. The RMSD of complexes 6971066 and 42607867 is rather steady, similar to the Apo form, oscillating between 0.1 nm and 0.15 nm. Complex 3795981 exhibited greater fluctuations compared to the reference and other compounds, indicating relatively higher instability. In contrast, complexes 6971066 and 42607867 remained relatively stable and close to the reference throughout the simulation. A replicate run for this complex 3795981 was performed. The average RMSD for both run was 1.5 nm. However the second run demonstrated. The replicate MD run demonstrated improved stability, as indicated by fewer fluctuations as compared to the first run through the first 80ns (Fig. 8). But after 80ns, the RMSD value for the second MD run suddenly began to increase towards the end rather than converging indicating that that the system may be undergoing structural changes or instability in the end of the simulations.

  The RMSF values shed light on how flexible each residue is in proteins-ligand complexes. Across the residue numbers, all complexes exhibit comparable fluctuation patterns, including the Apo form underscoring the stabilizing influence of ligand binding. Nonetheless, certain areas show more variations. Notably, the L3-loop (Leu40-Phe50), identified as a key flexible region, displayed higher fluctuations compared to other residues across all simulations (Figs. 7B and 9). This loop covers the pocket and sequesters the ligand into the interior of the polypeptide55. This elevated flexibility is particularly significant as it was observed that the L3-loop moved slightly away from the binding pocket during the simulations. This subtle conformational shift likely played a positive role by creating a more accommodating environment for ligand binding, enabling stronger and more stable interactions with the active site residues. Outside the L3 loop most of the regions of protein in all complexes remained stable within an acceptable range, indicating minimal structural disruption. Only a few other loop regions showed changes around 0.3 nm. These findings highlight the LasR binding site’s dynamic plasticity, as well as the importance of localized flexibility in ligand binding and stability. Further, the RMSF values of the active site residues (Table 3) show that complex 3795981 exhibited comparatively higher fluctuations at key residues such as Trp60 and Thr75 than the reference and other compounds. In contrast, complexes 6971066 and 42607867 displayed values close to the reference, indicating relatively stable interactions at the binding site. ## Discussion
  In this study a machine learning-driven approach combined with molecular docking and dynamics simulations was employed to identify potential anti-biofilm agents targeting the LasR receptor of P. aeruginosa. For machine learning screening a dataset of 440 compounds (116 active and 324 decoys) was prepared and cleaned for model generation and training. Various machine learning models were trained such as SVM, RF, NB, and KNN. Performance of each model was evaluated based on accuracy, specificity, MCC and AUC. The Random Forest model outperformed with an accuracy of 0.98 was used to screen a library of phytochemicals resulting in 367 active phytochemicals. After that these active phytochemicals were screened based on the Lipinski rule of five to select only the drug like compounds. In total 115 compounds screened out as drug like compounds. Molecular docking was performed to calculate the interactions and binding affinity of drug like molecules with LasR. Among them PubChem 3795981, PubChem 6971066, and PubChem 42607867 exhibit strong binding affinity and key interactions with the crucial active site residues. One hydrogen bond was common among the three compounds with Ser 129. This interaction is also observed in the native LasR-TP4 crystallized complex, underscoring its importance for ligand binding and receptor stability55. In the native LasR-Tp4 complex, the crystallized ligand forms hydrogen bonds with Ser129, Tyr56, and Asp73, which are key residues involved in stabilizing the ligand-receptor interaction. Interestingly, PubChem 42607867 not only formed a hydrogen bond with Ser129, but also with Tyr56, mimicking two of the essential interactions seen in the native ligand complex. This suggests that PubChem 3795981 and 42607867 achieved a binding mode similar to that of the natural ligand, potentially enhancing its inhibitory effect on LasR’s function (Fig. 11). Further molecular dynamics and MMPBSA results highlight the PubChem 42607867 and 3795981 compounds as potent anti-biofilm therapeutics. These compounds have strong binding affinities for their target sites, as evidenced by their free energy values. PubChem 42607867 has a binding free energy of −38.58 kcal/mol, whereas compound 3795981 follows closely behind with a value of −36.95 kcal/mol. These binding free energy values imply robust and sustained interactions with the biological target, bolstering their usefulness as potential therapeutic interventions. PubChem 42607867 is a flavonoid derived from Achyrocline flaccid belonging to flavanone subclass. Which is t is one of several secondary metabolites identified that have long been utilized in folk medicine. According to studies, substances with structural characteristics such as hydroxyl and epoxy groups have a wide range of biological functions, including free radical scavenging. Flavonoids possess potential anti-bacterial properties. Their antibacterial effect is due to their ability to break bacterial membranes, inhibit critical enzymes, and interfere with DNA or protein production. These processes combine to make flavonoids a potential family of natural chemicals in fighting bacterial infections58–60. The strong binding affinity of PubChem 42607867 and 3795981 with key LasR residues and favorable molecular dynamics performance make this excellent candidate for experimental validation as a LasR inhibitor. While molecular docking and dynamics simulations can provide insights into potential binding patterns and stability, they are not definitive indicators of biological activity. Experimental validation, such as in vitro and in vivo research, is critical for confirming the efficacy of the identified chemicals, specifically PubChem 42607867 and 379598.

## Conclusions
  This study demonstrates the potential of machine learning and computational modeling to develop anti-biofilm agents that target Pseudomonas aeruginosa’s LasR quorum-sensing receptor. Using an integrated methodology, we discovered potential phytochemical compounds with excellent binding affinity and stability for LasR inhibition. Notably, PubChem 42607867 and PubChem 3795981 emerged as top candidates, showing stable interactions and minimal structural deviations during MD simulations, indicating their potential as powerful LasR inhibitors. Comparative analyses with the reference through docking, MD simulations, and MM-PBSA further confirmed that these compounds exhibited binding stability and free energy values comparable to the reference. These findings demonstrate the effectiveness of integrating machine learning-driven screening with molecular dynamics and binding energy studies to accelerate the development of therapeutics for drug-resistant infections. Future research will concentrate on experimental validation and optimization of these drugs for therapeutic use.
