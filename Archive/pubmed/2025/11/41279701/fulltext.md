# Deep learning the dynamic regulatory sequence code of cardiac organoid differentiation


## Abstract
  Defining the temporal gene regulatory programs that drive human organogenesis is essential for understanding the origins of congenital disease. We combined a time-resolved, single-cell multi-omic atlas of human iPSC-derived cardiac organoids with deep learning models that predict chromatin accessibility from DNA sequence, enabling the discovery of the regulatory syntax underlying early heart development. This framework uncovered cell-state-specific rules of cardiogenesis, including context-dependent activities of TEAD, HAND, and TBX transcription factor families, and linked these motifs to their target genes. We identified distinct programs guiding lineage divergence, such as ventricular versus pacemaker cardiomyocytes, and validated predictions by perturbing Myocardin (MYOCD), establishing its essential role in ventricular specification. Integration of chromatin, transcriptional, and genetic data further highlighted regulatory regions and disease-associated variants that perturb differentiation state transitions, supporting evidence that suggests congenital heart disease emerges early in development. This work bridges developmental gene regulation with disease genetics, providing a foundation for mechanistic and therapeutic insights into congenital diseases.


## Introduction
  Organogenesis, the formation of functional organ systems, begins in the third week of human development and requires the coordinated differentiation of multiple cell lineages1-3. Dysregulation of the complex gene regulatory networks controlling this process can lead to congenital anomalies4-6, with structural heart defects being the most common7, affecting up to 3% of live births8. Despite being heritable, a significant proportion of congenital heart disease (CHD) cases remain genetically unsolved9-12. This failure of genetic attribution is likely because CHD arises from a complex combination of multiple coding and non-coding variants7, and its origins lie in early developmental events4,13,14, such as the critical paracrine signaling between nascent cell states15-18, which are difficult to study in vivo.

  To model the stepwise, multicellular events associated with organogenesis, human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC) differentiation towards organoid models holds substantial promise19. These in vitro systems, which contain multiple organ-relevant cell types, allow for the study of the complex cellular interactions and paracrine signaling essential for organogenesis20-22. Compared with in vivo models, which are challenging to scale and genetically manipulate, and homotypic cell models, which lack multicellular interactions, organoids provide a tractable and scalable system. They enable the dissection of the temporal cis-regulatory architecture that governs cell identity and allow the functional testing of its role in human disease through targeted genetic perturbations23,24.

  Interpreting genetic perturbations at the level of gene regulation is currently challenging with existing experimental and computational approaches due to limited tools and a lack of resolution. Standard assays for mapping chromatin accessibility can identify active regulatory regions but lack the resolution to decipher discrete, complex regulatory grammar25-28. When paired with standard next-generation sequencing methods, the specific latent combinations, affinities, and spacing of transcription factor binding sites that dictate cell-type-specific gene expression patterns cannot be defined due to their clustered organization within the genome29,30. While techniques like ChIP-seq can infer TF occupancy31-33, they are difficult to scale across all TFs and cellular contexts, and require prior knowledge of the TF of interest. However, deep learning models offer a powerful solution by effectively fusing the strengths of multiple assays34-37. They learn non-linear, salient predictive features at single-base-pair resolution using a corpus of genome or transcriptome-wide information38-41. This enables the de novo discovery of regulatory motifs and systematic interrogation of their syntax14,41-43.

  To identify complex TF motif grammar that governs the multicellular, nascent stages of human heart development and how its perturbation leads to CHD, here we integrate advances in deep learning with a high-resolution cardiac organoid single-cell multi-modal atlas containing approximately 120,000 cells across six key differentiation time points. A base-pair resolution neural network learned complex regulatory motif grammar and then defined a compendium of syntax rules that guide cell fate, including a distinction between HAND+TBX dimers and HAND+TEAD dimers. To validate the model’s predictions, we functionally tested and validated its nomination of MYOCD as a key driver of ventricular cardiomyocytes. This analysis also uncovered a non-autonomous effect that originates in cardiomyocytes but alters the ectoderm cell fate trajectory, highlighting how effective paracrine signaling is a crucial determinant of differentiation. We subsequently evaluated CHD-associated genes and variants using our in vitro and in vivo models, and observed that multiple intermediate, progenitor cell states are regulated and impacted by disease-associated gene regulatory networks. Ultimately, this work provides a powerful framework for decoding the molecular principles of early heart development and offers a valuable resource for investigating the complete genetic etiologies of congenital heart disease.


## Results

### Temporal sampling of cardioids reveals differentiation trajectories and lineage specification events
  To map the regulatory landscape of early human heart development, we differentiated human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) into cardiac organoids (Cardioids) by modulating key signaling pathways, including WNT, TGFβ, and retinoic acid44,45 (Fig. 1A, S1A). The resulting cardioids successfully recapitulated key developmental milestones, transitioning from pluripotency (Day 0; SOX2, OCT4) through mesendoderm specification (Day 1; TBXT) and into early cardiac mesoderm (Day 3.5; SRF) and ectodermal states (Day 3.5; NR2F2). By day 5 and 10, the cardioids had developed into complex, multicellular structures containing cardiomyocytes (MYL7), endothelial cells (PECAM1), and non-neural ectoderm (WNT5A), among other lineages (Fig. 1A).

  To resolve the cellular and molecular dynamics of this process, we generated a paired single-cell transcriptome and chromatin accessibility (scRNA/ATAC-seq) dataset from approximately 60,000 cells across six time points (days 0, 1, 2, 3, 5, and 10). We used Seurat46 and DecontX47,48 for preprocessing with RNA-contamination removal in the scRNA-seq data, followed by principal component analysis and UMAP embedding (Methods). Clustering analysis identified 20 distinct cell states, revealing a clear temporal progression of lineage specification (Fig. 1B-D, Fig. S1B-D). Early time points (days 0–2) were dominated by pluripotent (NANOG, POU5F1), epiblast (NANOG, TBXT) and mesendoderm (TBXT, w) populations44. These progenitors diversified at intermediate stages (days 3–5) into cardiac mesoderm (NKX2-5), non-neural ectoderm (EYA1), and hepatic progenitors (HNF4A). This transition from proliferative progenitors to differentiated cells (Fig. 1C) was further supported by EdU incorporation assays, which showed robust cell division at early stages that progressively decreased over time (Fig. S1E). By day 10, the atlas was composed primarily of diverse somatic-like cell types, including ventricular cardiomyocytes (vCMs; MYL7, TTN), pacemaker-like cardiomyocytes (pCMs; SHOX2, TBX18), endothelial cells (EC; PECAM1, CDH5), endoderm-derived hepatic-like cells (Endo; AFP, HNF4A), and non-neural ectoderm (Ecto; TFAP2A, DLX5) (Fig. S1F). The non-neural ectoderm state exhibited expression of the unique amniotic ectoderm marker GABRP, in addition to VTCN1 and TFAP2B, which supports prior studies suggesting that the epiblast-derived non-neural ectoderm gives rise to amniotic ectoderm (AE)49,50.

  Having defined the cellular landscape, we next sought to connect cell identity to its underlying regulatory architecture. Performing k-means clustering of 628,378 consensus chromatin accessibility peaks (peaks shared across cell states out of 9,643,338 total cell-state-specific peaks) revealed dynamic, cell-state-specific patterns. We parsed this data into five main categories of chromatin accessibility: (i) consistently low accessibility in all cell types (n=40,850), (ii) TSS regions (n=11,689), (iiI) regions that are either increasing (n=36,270) or (iv) decreasing during differentiation regardless of the lineage (n=173,971), and (v) cell-type-specific accessibility (n=365,598) (Fig. 1E, left, Fig. S1G). We observed a qualitative correlation between the accessibility of these cis-regulatory elements (CREs) and the expression of nearby genes (Fig. 1E, right). This regulatory linkage was evident across a core set of 1,989 lineage-defining genes and transcription factors. The patterns included CREs that were accessible only in specific cell types, as well as those that were dynamic across all lineages during differentiation. Together, these analyses confirm that our cardioid system captures the rapid, lineage-specific regulatory events of early cardiogenesis, providing a high-resolution map connecting transcription factor activity, chromatin state, and gene expression.


### A large, uncharacterized ZNF repertoire is dynamically expressed during cardiogenesis
  The Zinc Finger (ZNF) protein superfamily, the most abundant class of transcription factors in the human genome, plays diverse roles as activators, repressors, and architectural proteins51,52. To systematically investigate their functions in cardiogenesis, we identified 502 ZNF genes with cell-state-specific chromatin accessibility across 14 developmental stages (Fig.1F, Fig. S1H, Table S1). A supervised AI text-mining approach (Note S5) identified a significant knowledge gap: 85% of these dynamically regulated ZNFs are poorly characterized or lack known functions, with fewer than 10% being well understood (Fig. S1H). Structurally, many of these ZNFs contain domains consistent with transcriptional repression.

  Despite the large number of uncharacterized factors, the mapping of ZNFs to cell states was validated by demonstrating the cell-state-specific activity of well-established cardiac regulators, such as the essential cardiac progenitor factor and GATA4 cofactor ZFPM2 (also called FOG2)53 (Fig. S1K), which we assigned to early and mid-mesoderm clusters, marking its importance in very early mesoderm development. This framework enables the nomination of candidates for functional interrogation. Among these are CASZ1, a cardiac transcription factor essential for differentiation and proliferation, with mutations linked to congenital heart defects and dilated/arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy54-60, specifically assigned to the early developing vCMs (Fig. S1K). Another candidate for functional interrogation is the mid-mesoderm-expressed ZNF516, a KRAB-ZNF that suppresses EGFR signaling61. This under-characterized gene is a potential repressor that ensures cardiac lineage fidelity. These examples demonstrate the strength of our integrated approach in generating specific, data-driven hypotheses about the roles of previously uncharacterized factors in human cardiogenesis.

  Out of the 502 ZNFs, 13 possess a functionally validated repressor domain62 and exhibited cell-state-specific expression profiles (Fig. 1G, Fig. S1I-J): (i) ZNFs active during early differentiation stages (e.g., ZNF7, ZIK1), (ii) those with intermediate activity (e.g., ZNF2, ZNF82), and (iii) ZNFs active during later differentiation stages (e.g., ZNF84, ZFP30). Additionally, some ZNFs showed lineage-specific activity, such as ZNF81, mainly active in Pacemaker CMs; ZNF34, active during later stages of the Endoderm lineage and early differentiation; and ZNF2, ZNF45, ZFP82, which display lower expression in AE. Our analysis highlights these ZNFs, most of which have unknown functions (12 out of 13), as potential key regulators across different stages of differentiation.


### Cardioids recapitulate human organogenesis and cardiac lineage specification trajectories
  To demonstrate that the cardioid model broadly recapitulates early human development, we projected our scRNA-seq data onto a reference atlas of the human gastrula (Fig. 2A-B)3,46. This comparison revealed a strong alignment between our in vitro cell states and key in vivo stages of embryogenesis, including the epiblast, primitive streak, emergent mesoderm, and advanced mesoderm (Fig. S2A). Notably, the earliest time points of differentiation (days 0–3) showed the highest mapping scores to the gastrula reference, with the correlation decreasing at later stages as cells progressed toward more specified fates (Fig. 2B). These results establish that the cardioid system is an accurate model for investigating the specific and often inaccessible timeframe of human gastrulation and nascent organogenesis.

  Having confirmed the relevance of our model to early embryogenesis, we next sought to reconstruct the developmental trajectories that lead from pluripotent cells to differentiated lineages. To achieve this, we applied Waddington-OT (WOT), a computational approach that infers lineage relationships by calculating the most probable fate of each cell between successive time points63. This analysis quantitatively revealed differentiation paths into distinct lineages (Fig. 2C, S2B). For example, tracing a continuous progression from pluripotency through mesoderm (days 1-2), into pre-cardiac mesoderm (day 3), and ultimately to ventricular cardiomyocytes by day 10.

  This lineage tracing demonstrated that lineage-specific gene expression profiles necessary for proper differentiation occur as early as the first day of differentiation, with a higher tendency toward mesodermal lineages, as expected (Fig. 2D, Fig. S2C). By day 2, distinct progenitor populations were already predicted to give rise to ventricular cardiomyocytes (vCMs), AE and hepatocyte-like cells. By day 3, many of these cells show strong commitment to their terminal fate (Fig. 2E-F, Fig. S2D), expressing increasingly more progressive gene programs (Fig. S2E). For pCMs, we observed shared cell states with the vCM trajectory (Mesoderm and Pre-cardiac Mesoderm) with a day 5 trajectory divergence (Fig. S2B-C). This illustrates a later differentiation divergence point from a common unknown progenitor state shared with the vCM state. The early lineage specification observed in our model aligns with gene expression signatures observed during human gastrulation in vivo, underscoring the model's capacity to accurately recapitulate gene expression programs that govern cell fate decisions.


### Deep learning models decipher the regulatory sequence syntax of cardiac lineage commitment
  To decipher the sequence syntax that mediates regulation of chromatin accessibility by TFs defining lineage commitment, we trained ChromBPNet deep learning models to predict base-pair resolution, cell-state-specific chromatin accessibility profiles directly from DNA sequence. ChromBPNet also corrects distortions in ATAC-seq profiles caused by sequence biases of the Tn5 enzyme used in the experiment38. Model interpretation techniques can be applied to assess the predictive contribution of every nucleotide in each regulatory element to its predicted accessibility profiles, revealing TF motifs and their syntax rules which govern cell-state-specific accessibility patterns64,65. This approach overcomes the limitations of traditional motif scanning by learning the quantitative effects of de novo discovered motifs on cell-state-resolved accessibility conditioned on their local genomic sequence context.

  We first trained cell-state-resolved models for each of the clusters identified across cardioid differentiation (Fig. 3A). To assess the accuracy of our models, we computed the Pearson correlation between the observed and predicted total counts for each cell state across all peaks in that cell state. The model correlations ranged from 0.78 to 0.88, with a median of 0.85 (Fig. S3A-B). We also computed the Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD) between the observed and predicted base-resolution distribution of counts (profile shape) within each peak. The median JSD (smaller is better) across all peaks ranged from 0.47 to 0.73 across all models (Fig. S3A). As an illustrative example, we observe that our ventricular cardiomyocyte model accurately recapitulates the complex accessibility pattern at the promoter of NKX2-5, a key cardiac regulator (Fig. 3B, left).

  We next used the DeepLIFT method to interrogate each model and estimate sequence contribution scores for all peaks in each cell state64,66. We then used the TF-MODISCO motif discovery method to infer all de novo, predictive sequence features, corresponding to TF motifs, learned by each of the models65. Unifying motifs learned by all models yielded a comprehensive regulatory lexicon of 418 individual DNA sequence patterns, consisting of 172 distinct motifs from 106 TF families (MoitfCompendium67). Motif families were defined as sets of motifs having very similar core sequence preferences with variable flanking bases (Methods). The models’ cell-state specificity is evident, for instance, when inspecting the NKX2-5 promoter, at which the predictive binding sites for vCM-specific factors like MEIS/TBX heterodimers and SRF are uniquely present in vCM models but absent in models from all other lineages (Fig. 3B, right).

  We next used this unified motif lexicon to understand the dynamic activity of globally predictive motifs and their associated TFs across all cell states. As expected, analysis of cell-state-specific peaks confirmed the enrichment of known lineage-driving TFs: SMAD and TBX motifs were enriched in mesendoderm, MEF2 and NKX motifs in cardiac mesoderm, and HNF and TFAP2 motifs in endoderm and ectoderm, respectively (Fig. 3C, Fig. S3C).

  Next, we used the cell-state-resolved sequence contributions to identify dynamic patterns of combinatorial TF occupancy at predictive motif instances mapped to the unified motif lexicon in all peaks across all cell types (Methods). Peaks encoded on average eight predictive motif instances, resulting in a more precise and specific annotation of motif syntax compared to traditional motif annotation methods that identified an excessive (90 per peak on average) number of motif instances (Fig. S3D). We further quantified the total number of motif instances across all peaks in each cell state and found both shared and cell-state-specific TF motifs (Fig. 3C). Thus, our models reveal local and global occupancy patterns of TFs through predictive motifs syntax, mediating regulation of accessibility dynamics across the differentiation time course.


### Integrating dynamic motif syntax and TF expression resolves context-dependent regulation of accessibility by distinct TF family members
  The precise regulation of gene expression is governed by a complex interplay between TFs and cis-regulatory elements (CREs). A gene can be controlled by different TFs binding to multiple distinct CREs, or by different TFs acting on the same CRE in a cell-state-specific manner. The latter, which we term context-dependent cis-regulatory elements (cdCREs), are particularly challenging to decipher. Our integrative framework can resolve both types of regulatory logic. The cdCRE upstream of DHRS3, a gene involved in regulating retinoic acid levels during embryogenesis68, serves as a clear example. While this single element is accessible in multiple lineages, our model predicts that distinct TF occupancy patterns contribute to its accessibility in each context, via pleiotropic motif syntax (Fig. 3E). In vCMs, NKX2 motifs contribute to the observed accessibility. In contrast, in AE, TFAP2 motifs contribute to the observed accessibility, while TEAD motifs contribute similarly in both cell states. Examining profiles of expressed TFs from the NKX family nominates more specific TF family members likely to bind the predicted motifs. In the vCMs, NKX2-5 is the highest expressed gene in the NKX2 family, aligning with its known role as a critical regulator for proper heart development. In AE, other members of the NKX2 family are expressed (NKX2-3, NKX2-6, NKX3-1), although they are not predicted to contribute to the DHRS3 regulatory element accessibility (Fig. S4).

  We extended this analysis to examine a broader set of peaks accessible across multiple terminal lineage states (Fig. 3C, light orange fourth row). Despite exhibiting similar accessibility across these cell states, the most prevalent predicted drivers of these peaks were lineage-specific. For example, the NKX2 motif family was the most prevalent motif in these peaks in vCMs and was not a key driver in any other lineage. Similarly, TFAP2 was the largest driver in and specific to AE, POU:SOX was specific to hiPSCs and early states across all lineages, and FOX showed the highest presence in endoderm-derived cells (Fig. 3D, Fig. S3E). These motif dynamics are consistent with lineage-specific TF expression patterns (Fig. S4, Fig. S5). These results highlight the models’ ability to disentangle activity from regulatory logic, suggesting that cdCREs with similar accessibility across cell states may encode pleiotropic motif syntax reflecting context-dependent regulation by distinct TFs.

  Our framework can also resolve the ambiguity of multiple TFs from the same family binding to a common motif. The FOX family, which contains many members implicated in congenital heart disease, illustrates this challenge. While the canonical FOX motif is active in nearly all cell states, by integrating our model's FOX motif predictions (Fig. 3D) with single-cell gene expression (Fig. S5), we can nominate the specific FOX gene family members responsible for this activity in a cell-state-specific manner. For example, we find that the congenital heart disease-associated genes FOXP1 and FOXF1 are specifically expressed in vCMs, whereas other family members like FOXM1 and FOXO1 are transcriptionally inactive in the same cells (Fig. S5). These examples demonstrate our framework's ability to link transcription factor expression to motif activity in a cell-state-specific manner.

  When comparing cell-state-specific motif proportional composition, different cell states might show similar composition of attributing motifs (Fig. S3F, top). When summarizing and focusing on our annotated motifs, we are able to identify specific cell states where the motifs are predicted to contribute to the observed accessibility (Fig. S3F, bottom). This suggests that the proportional usage of the regulatory vocabulary is conserved across developmental lineages. This finding points to a fundamental syntax pattern of the genome, where different cell states draw upon a shared toolbox of TFs, but in distinct levels and proportions, to achieve specialized functions.


### in silico marginalization quantifies context-dependent motif accessibility
  A central goal in developmental genomics is to resolve how individual TFs contribute to gene regulation within complex, multi-motif enhancers and across cell states. To address this, we used an in silico marginalization strategy35,69. This technique computes an estimate of a motif’s impact on chromatin accessibility in each cell state by using the corresponding model to predict the accessibility of background genomic regions both with and without the motif present, thereby measuring the marginal effect of the motif itself. This approach measures the isolated effect of each motif, unconfounded by the effects of neighboring motifs present in accessible regions. Furthermore, this allows us to accurately predict the accessibility of rare or sparse motifs, which are often overlooked by conventional methods that rely on statistical enrichment.

  The MEF2 family provides a clear case study. While MEF2 motifs are abundant in mesodermal peaks and several gene family members (MEF2A, MEF2C, MEF2D) are expressed in both vCM and AE lineages, our analysis revealed that MEF2 motif accessibility is restricted to the vCM lineage. We predict a wave of MEF2 accessibility that begins in pre-cardiac mesoderm and peaks in vCMs, which correlates with the sequential expression of MEF2A, MEF2D, and MEF2C (Fig. 3F, upper panel). In contrast, our model does not predict MEF2 motif accessibility in the AE lineage despite detectable expression of MEF genes (Fig. 3F, lower panel). This demonstrates our approach’s ability to discriminate between TFs that are expressed and those that are functionally active in shaping the chromatin landscape.


### Modeling deciphers the context-dependent grammar of TF cooperativity
  Extending the in silico marginalization strategy beyond individual TFs, our analysis can decipher the complex “regulatory grammar” that governs TF cooperativity. The model predicts, for instance, that a TBX homodimer motif exhibits strong synergistic chromatin activity when compared to a TBX monomer, while a tandem POU:SOX motif provides no increased chromatin activity over a monomer (Fig. 3G, Fig. S3G).

  The TEAD motif provides an instructive case study in predicted context-dependent regulatory syntax. While the core TEAD motif is present across most cell states, its contribution to accessibility becomes highly specific when paired with other TFs’ motifs (Fig. 3H, Fig. S6). The model predicts that the TEAD motif’s chromatin activity is dictated by its partner motif: it cooperates with SRF motifs in cardiomyocytes, with GRHL in the ectoderm, and with HNF4 in the endoderm. The model's precision extends even to the base-pair spacing between motifs. For example, it is predicted that a HAND and TEAD motif pair has the highest chromatin activity when separated by exactly three base pairs, demonstrating a highly sensitive ability to resolve the subtle syntactical rules that define lineage-specific enhancer function (Fig. 3H). Other prominent TF families exhibit the same sequence-specific activity patterns (Fig. S6). This capacity to predict how subtle changes in motif syntax dictate cell-state-specific activity provides a powerful new lens for interpreting the non-coding genome.


### A gene-centric framework links regulatory syntax to developmental cascades
  To translate our understanding of motif syntax into gene-level regulation, we computationally linked active regulatory elements to their putative gene targets. We used the scE2G method70 to assign our 628,378 consensus peaks to genes, which resulted in 1,256,939 scE2G individual peak-gene links across all cell states (Fig. S7A). We intersected the model's motif accessibility predictions with these region-to-gene links, calculated a cumulative contribution score for each motif to each gene and ranked the potential effects of motifs on gene regulation based on this score (Methods). This analysis generates a cumulative contribution score for each motif on each gene in each identified cell state. (Fig. 4A, Fig. S7B).

  We first validated this approach by using it to reconstruct the characterized regulatory cascade of vCM specification (Fig. 4B). To visualize general regulatory contributions of motifs during this differentiation, we average our gene-by-motif contribution across cell states matrix for all lineage-defining genes (Fig. 1E). The resulting average contributions of motif by cell state matrix accurately recapitulates known sequential regulatory changes over developmental time: from pluripotency factors (POU, SOX) in hiPSCs, specification-associated TFs (TBX, EOMES, SMAD) in epiblast-like cells, to mesendoderm-associated TFs (GATA, HAND) in early progenitors, and finally to the canonical cardiomyocyte TFs (SRF, NKX2-5, and later MEF2 and NFI) that orchestrate later stage differentiation in vivo. Therefore, the predicted TF activities we observe faithfully recapitulate the known principles of vertebrate heart development.

  Expanding this analysis genome-wide and across differentiation trajectories, we generated a global map of motif-to-gene regulation across all cell states (Fig. 4C, Fig. S7B). This map captures the relative contributions to accessibility of both transcriptional activators and transcriptional repressors, such as ZEB1/2 and SNAI. These repressors are specifically expressed in the mesodermic lineage, where our models assigned their motifs significantly lower contribution scores, consistent with their role in binding and compacting chromatin. The decrease in accessibility of ZEB/SNAI motifs in the mesodermal lineage is reflected in the Endothelial-to-Mesenchymal Transition (EndMT) genes, such as CDH1, EPCAM, CRB3, and CLDN, which were all downregulated in the mesoderm. Additionally, genes known to be activated by ZEB/SANI, such as ACTA2, VIM and FN1, were upregulated (Fig. S8A).


### An integrative framework to define the regulatory programs of cardiac subtypes
  Congenital heart disease can arise from the misregulation of highly specific cell subtypes, yet resolving their distinct regulatory programs has been a major challenge. Our framework provides a powerful solution, offering the ability to define the precise regulatory elements, from active enhancers to governing transcription factors, for any given cell state. By applying this framework to dissect the syntax of ventricular (vCM) and pacemaker (pCM) cardiomyocytes, we can directly investigate the programs controlling critical disease-associated genes. To identify the key TFs that drive this bifurcation, we focused on genes in their respective progenitor states that were both differentially expressed and linked to differentially accessible peaks (Fig. 4D), then calculated the differential contributions of all TF motifs associated with this gene set (Fig. 4E).

  Our analysis revealed that pCM progenitors exhibit high predicted contribution from the GATA, PDX/HOX, and LHX/SHOX motif families. These motifs were linked to key pacemaker identity genes, including FBN1 and TWIST1, which are associated with congenital syndromes71-74, and were highly accessible at the enhancers of other pCM-specific genes like LHX2, TBX18 and the ion channel KCNJ3 (Fig. 4F, Fig. S7D, Fig. S7F). In contrast, vCM progenitors exhibited strong contributions from MEF2, NKX2-5, and SRF motifs. These TF motifs were linked to vCM-specific targets like BMP2, as well as genes like TTN, ANKRD1 and MYH6, which are linked to various cardiomyopathies, confirming that a distinct and clinically relevant regulatory syntax underlies the two progenitor states (Fig. 4G, Fig. S7E).


### Functional validation reveals MYOCD is essential for ventricular cardiomyocyte specification
  Based on our model’s prediction that the MYOCD-SRF network is a key vCM-specific driver (Fig. 4B-C), we sought to functionally understand the dysregulation mechanism upon genetic perturbation. We used a CRISPR-interference (CRISPRi) system to knock down (KD) MYOCD, a lineage-specific coactivator for the ubiquitously expressed SRF (Fig. 5A-C; Fig. S9F). The perturbation resulted in a significant reduction in canonical in vitro vCMs by day 10, did not affect overall organoid size (Fig. 5B) and led to a non-significant decrease in cardioid overall cell numbers, suggesting that the perturbation induced a switch in cell fate rather than a general defect in cell viability or proliferation (Fig. S9A).

  To identify the molecular dynamics affected by MYOCD KD, we generated single-cell multiomic data from control-matched knockdown cardioids at days 0-1, 1.5-2, 3, 5, and 10. We observed distinct alternative cell states, specifically in the vCM and AE lineages, where MYOCD KD resulted in alternative developing CM cells that did not cluster with the Control vCMs or any other cell state. In the AE lineage, we observed a slight shift in the Early AE state, resulting in a lack of the Late AE subcluster (Fig. 5D). To better identify the altered states, we projected control and knockdown cells onto a murine embryonic development atlas encompassing E8 to P0 embryos75. This revealed a qualitative divergence beginning on day 3 (Fig. 5E; Fig. S9D-E). While control mesodermal cells progressed toward a first heart field (FHF) ventricular state, MYOCD KD cells were arrested in a progenitor state most similar to the second heart field (SHF) (Fig. 5E; Fig. S9B). Additionally, projecting the control and MYOCD KD cells onto the mouse developmental atlas’s fetal time confirmed that MYOCD KD CMs exclusively mapped to the E8-E8.5 stage, whereas control CMs mapped mainly to the later developmental stages of E18.5-P0 (Fig. S9C). This confirms that MYOCD is essential for proper FHF-derived cardiomyocyte specification and that its loss causes a developmental stall, extending previous in vivo observations76.

  Differential gene expression analysis pinpointed the timing of the molecular effects induced by MYOCD KD. Through day 2, the only significantly changed gene was MYOCD itself (Fig. S9G, “mesoderm”), but by day 3, significant transcriptomic changes appeared, almost exclusively in the precardiac mesoderm (Fig. 5F, left; Fig. S9G). These changes included a significant downregulation of genes associated with heart development and similar Gene Ontology (GO) terms (Fig. 5F, right), directly linking the perturbation to the molecular programs implicated in heart disease (Fig. 5G). Intriguingly, this analysis also suggested a non-cell autonomous effect of MYOCD KD on the amniotic ectoderm (AE) lineage, which exhibited significant transcriptomic changes, suggesting a previously unappreciated signaling axis between the developing mesoderm and ectoderm.


### MYOCD loss perturbs cardiac and ectodermal regulatory programs
  To identify the core regulatory networks disrupted by MYOCD KD, we analyzed motif enrichment within chromatin regions that lost or gained accessibility upon KD (Fig. 5H). For each cell state, we performed k-means clustering (k=3) based on the log2 fold change (log2FC) between Control and MYOCD KD in that specific cell type, aiming for three major chromatin accessibility profiles: (i) decreased accessibility with knockdown, (ii) unchanged accessibility, and (iii) increased accessibility with knockdown. Then, for each motif family, we conducted Fisher’s exact test to assess motif enrichment between the k-cluster showing increased accessibility in KD and the k-cluster showing decreased accessibility. Only active peaks (peaks with at least one motif within +/− 500bp) were considered for each cluster. This analysis revealed an apparent dichotomy in the cardiomyocyte lineage. Regions that gained accessibility were enriched for general promoter GC-rich motifs, likely reflecting indirect gene regulation of the observed stalled developmental state. In contrast, chromatin regions that lost accessibility were most significantly enriched for SRF and MEF2 motifs (Fig. 5H, grey vertical bars). This result provides direct evidence that the primary autonomous effect of the perturbation is the targeted disruption of the MYOCD-SRF/MEF2 regulatory network, which is essential for cardiac development76.

  Next, we investigated the regulatory basis for the phenotype observed in the amniotic ectoderm (AE). Our analysis revealed that cardiac TFs did not directly drive the disruption in this lineage, but instead, the most significant perturbation was in the activity of the PDX/HOX transcription factor family (Fig. 5H). The indirect effect of MYOCD KD on PDX/HOX motifs suggests that defects in cardiomyocyte lineage differentiation may lead to cell non-autonomous perturbed signaling in the AE lineage. Indeed, our data indicate that cardiomyocytes ectopically express the signaling molecule BMP2, while AE cells express its receptor, BMPR1A (Fig. S9H). Given that BMP signaling is a known inducer of HOX gene expression77-79, these findings suggest a paracrine mechanism: aberrant BMP2 signaling from the perturbed mesoderm acts on the ectoderm, where it converges on the PDX/HOX regulatory network to mediate the non-autonomous effect.


### Disrupted regulatory syntax at the MYH7 locus aligns with independent experimental validation
  To understand precisely how MYOCD KD leads to silencing of its target genes, we examined the regulatory syntax at the cardiomyocyte-specific gene MYH7. Our model revealed a significant disruption of the regulatory program at the onset of gene expression. In control cells, MYH7 is driven by a coordinated activity of TFs (e.g., MEIS, MEF2, HAND and SRF). In knockdown cells, key activators like MEIS and MEF2 lose their predicted activity, though they remain expressed (Fig. 5I-J). This regulatory imbalance provides a mechanistic view of how a gene’s regulatory landscape is altered upon MYOCD KD.


### Independent experimental data validate model predictions
  To validate our model's predictions of contributive motif instances to MYOCD regulation, we compared our findings to a recent in vivo reporter assay of human heart enhancers80. Applying our scE2G analysis on the in vivo tested enhancers, one enhancer (“HT2”) was linked to the putative target gene SSH2, a phosphatase that regulates the cytoskeleton and is critical for cell migration and morphogenesis. Our model predicts that the primary downstream effect of MYOCD KD is a sharp reduction in the regulatory activity of the enhancer key cofactors, MEF2 and SRF (Fig. S10A).

  The Kosicki et al.80 study provides a direct experimental test of this prediction. They found that experimentally mutating the MEF2 or SRF motifs within the HT2 enhancer resulted in a "Full loss" or "Major loss" of reporter gene expression in the mouse heart. This provides an in vivo phenotypic readout for the molecular event our model predicts. Furthermore, their experiment showed that reducing MEF2 activity did not affect a nearby GATA motif, which mirrors our model's prediction that this GATA motif remains unperturbed following MYOCD loss (Fig. S10B). This concordance provides strong external validation that our model accurately identifies the specific TF motifs driving the downstream effects of a perturbation at disease-relevant enhancers linked to critical developmental genes.


### Noncoding variants associated with CHD genes have cell-state-specific predicted effects
  Congenital heart disease (CHD) encompasses a broad spectrum of structural malformations present at birth, most of which originate during the first four weeks of embryogenesis. Genetic etiologies involving variants that perturb these early developmental processes remain difficult to define and experimentally validate owing to the limited availability of physiologically relevant models9,81,82. This challenge is especially pronounced for noncoding variants, which are predicted to exert effects in a highly cell state–specific manner42,83,84.

  To identify cell states disrupted in CHD, we evaluated the gene expression pattern of 410 genes associated with CHD through cohort studies70. We first combined them into expression modules via k-means clustering (Fig. 6A, Fig. S11A-B) and confirmed that 88% of genes are expressed across multiple lineages and trajectories in our data, including progenitor states. This result reiterates the need to expand the cell states used for variant adjudication and functional genomics studies, moving away from employing somatic cell states that fail to reflect the cause of disease.

  To define state and/or lineage-specific gene regulatory logic for CHD genes expressed across multiple states, we next integrated cell state-specific motif hits derived from ChromBPNet models with scE2G links. This analysis identified 6,607 unique putative CREs linked to known disease genes, with a median of 12 linked peaks per gene (Fig. S11C). NOTCH1 is a prominent CHD gene associated with myriad structural defects of disparate origins85-87 and was expressed early in differentiation in all lineages (Fig. S11D). While most prominently studied in endothelial cells88, in vivo studies have suggested NOTCH1 is additionally required for second heart field development89 and neural crest-derived smooth muscle differentiation90. NOTCH1 is also required for the diversification of cells derived from the pharyngeal pouch endoderm91, whose proper differentiation to FGF-secreting cells is critical for outflow tract and aortic arch development in the heart92. Indeed, this analysis identified 33 unique peaks predicted to regulate NOTCH1 across all cell states, highlighting cell state-specific putative CREs (Fig. 6B). Given our aforementioned observations that peak level analyses obscure TF-driven regulatory logic, we next defined the cell state-specific NOTCH1 motif syntax based on ChromBPNet nominations with scE2G-linked peaks (Fig. 6C, Fig. S11E). This analysis defined discrete roles for TF families along the differentiation trajectories; a POU-SOX role transitioned to GATA/HAND role in the mesoderm and cardiac progenitors, and finally the MEIS TF family in cardiomyocytes, while nominating the FOX/TEAD/LHX families in the endoderm (Fig. 6C-D). To predict the effect of the nominated motifs, we next quantitatively evaluated the effect of mutating single base pairs that ChromBPNet identified as necessary for accessibility, which revealed cell-state-specific effects on accessibility, supporting the motif nominations (Fig. 6E). This result highlights that, unlike coding variants, which typically affect all isoforms of a gene, noncoding variants in CREs can perturb gene expression in a cell-type- or stage-specific manner. Consequently, they may give rise to phenotypes that diverge from those observed in individuals with coding variants in the same gene.

  To identify noncoding variants associated with CHD that are likely to perturb differentiation, we next quantitatively evaluated the predicted effect on chromatin accessibility of various single-nucleotide polymorphisms encoded within a noncoding region predicted to regulate TBX593. This analysis revealed a single rare variant in that region (rs377307764) that disrupts a contributive GATA motif (Fig. 6F). The presence of this variant reduced accessibility beginning at the mesendoderm stage of differentiation, prior to detectable TBX5 expression (Fig. 6G). This result highlights how integration of scMultiome data with noncoding variant interpretation tools can uncover disease-relevant genetic mechanisms.


## Discussion
  Efforts to define genotype–phenotype relationships in CHD cohorts have been hampered by the complexity of noncoding variation, which likely acts through disruption of cis-regulatory elements and their associated gene regulatory networks. While fetal atlases have mapped later-stage cardiac cell states relevant to CHD pathogenesis, they lack resolution of the early post-gastrulation developmental transitions when lineage specification and regulatory architecture are first established—stages at which noncoding perturbations are most likely to exert pathogenic effects. In this study, we generated a high-resolution, single-cell multi-omic atlas of human cardiac organoid differentiation. We then leveraged this resource to train a base-pair-resolution deep learning framework that decodes the complex cis-regulatory syntax governing cell fate. Our models uncovered a latent layer of regulation encoded by cell-state-specific combinations of transcription factor motifs, a feature obscured by traditional genomic analyses. We then employed this approach to uncover gene regulatory networks disrupted by noncoding variants discovered in patient cohorts. This work presents a comprehensive framework for linking non-coding genetic variants to their mechanistic roles in congenital diseases.

  Deep learning frameworks provide a powerful means to move beyond conventional motif scanning by inferring the regulatory grammar that orchestrates developmental programs. While single-cell chromatin accessibility assays delineate the global landscape of regulatory potential, they lack the resolution to disentangle how specific combinations of transcription factor (TF) motifs, often embedded in close proximity, synergize to drive cell state transitions. This limitation is compounded by the rapid kinetics of differentiation, wherein successive states frequently exhibit only subtle differences in chromatin accessibility despite profound shifts in lineage identity. Our framework addresses this limitation by systematically evaluating the accessibility contributions of each nucleotide. This methodological innovation has enabled us to assess the dynamic, lineage-specific activities of key regulators such as MEF2, to delineate the syntax of cell-state-specific regulation within cdCREs at loci like DHRS3, which is broadly required for retinoic acid signaling. We extend previous results nominating the TEAD family to highlight how its activity is modulated by its interacting partners, exemplified by SRF in cardiomyocytes and GRHL in ectodermal tissues, as well as by discrete spacing between motifs. By linking cis-regulatory logic to their cognate target genes, we can reconstruct gene regulatory networks that chart the developmental trajectories underlying cardiac lineage diversification. This framework captures the nuanced regulatory differences that distinguish closely related cell types, such as ventricular versus pacemaker cardiomyocytes, which arise from a recent shared progenitor pool. Importantly, the predictive capacity of this model provides a substantial advance in our ability to interpret how combinations of transcription factor motifs encode the functional instructions within the noncoding genome, thereby enabling systematic dissection of the cis-regulatory architecture that drives cell fate specification.

  While in vivo studies have highlighted essential roles of enhancers in heart development, identifying noncoding variants that disrupt enhancer activity and contribute to the causal genetic etiologies of CHD has faced many technical obstacles. Specifically, the large number of non-coding variants in the human genome makes it difficult to pinpoint the ones that are functionally significant. Furthermore, linking these variants to their specific target genes is challenging, as enhancers can regulate multiple genes over considerable genomic distances. Lastly, traditional genomic assays often lack the resolution and scale needed to address these issues, leaving the regulatory basis of many CHD cases unresolved. By employing base-pair resolution computational models to elucidate the regulatory grammar that is active during the early stages of cardiac differentiation, our research delineates lineage-specific regulatory cascades and identifies disease-associated noncoding variants that disrupt these pathways. Through the identification of variants predicted to regulate the initial phases of cardiogenesis, our study enhances the understanding of the precise developmental contexts in which noncoding variants may interfere with normal development, leading to the manifestation of CHD. Furthermore, our findings underscore the notion that noncoding variants residing within the regulatory landscape of a known disease gene may impact a limited set of cell states, differing from those caused by coding variants in the same gene. Because cis-regulatory elements and enhancers can modulate the expression of multiple genes, a single noncoding variant within these regions has the potential to perturb several targets simultaneously, thereby broadening its pathogenic impact. Moreover, previous efforts to link noncoding variants to CHD have faced several key limitations, which may explain the lack of robust genetic etiologies. First, many studies have mainly focused on cardiomyocytes, overlooking the diverse cell types involved in early heart development. Second, they have often depended solely on transcriptomic data, which does not reveal the full regulatory landscape. Finally, these analyses are usually conducted on later-stage or postnatal tissues, missing the crucial post-gastrulation period when cardiac lineages are first established. Our finding that noncoding variants in CHD patients are predicted to affect chromatin accessibility across multiple lineages during these early developmental stages directly addresses these issues. These findings highlight the urgent need for experimental models that accurately represent relevant developmental stages to properly interpret the effects of genetic variants.

  In summary, this work presents an integrated framework for transitioning from a descriptive atlas of human organogenesis to a predictive model of its underlying gene regulatory code, which can be utilized to identify the genetic etiologies of human congenital abnormalities. The power of deep learning models trained on high-resolution, rich sc-Multi-omic data lies in their potential to address a central challenge of modern genetics: interpreting the function of the vast number of non-coding variants linked to human disease. Our regulatory models can now be used as an in silico screening tool to systematically prioritize disease-associated variants that are most likely to disrupt cis-regulatory syntax and alter cell fate. This provides a direct path from correlative GWAS findings to testable hypotheses about the origins of congenital disease. Ultimately, by learning to read the grammar of the genome, we can improve our ability to decipher how its misspellings lead to disease.


## Limitations of our study
  While our cardioid model successfully recapitulates early developmental events, its present cell state composition presents limitations for modeling the later, more complex aspects of organogenesis and disease. The current system lacks key supportive cell types known to be critical in vivo, such as neural crest cells. The absence of these lineages and their associated paracrine signals likely limits the maturation potential of the cell types that are present. Therefore, while our framework is powerful for dissecting early cell fate decisions, future studies aimed at modeling complex disease phenotypes will require more comprehensive organoid systems. Incorporating additional growth factors and mechanical cues, such as sheer stress, will be a critical next step in generating a more complete model of the human heart.


## Methods

### Cell culture

#### Cardioid generation
  The cardioid generation protocol was adapted from 44,45. Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 10,000 cells per well after achieving 70% confluency and dissociating with Accutase. Cells were resuspended in mTesR+ (StemCell Technologies, 100-0276) with ROCKi (Tocris, 1254) and incubated overnight. On day 0, media was changed to FlyAB(Ins), containing RPMI (Thermo Fisher, 11875119), B-27 with insulin supplement (Thermo Fisher, 17504044), FGF2 (Fisher, 23-3FB), BMP4 (Fisher, 314-BP), LY294002 (Fisher, 1130), CHIR (Tocris, 4423), and Activin A (R&D, 338-AC) in specified concentrations. Subsequent media changes were performed as follows: on day 1, with a refresh of FlyAB(Ins); after 36-40 hours, media was changed to BWIIFRa, which includes RPMI, B-27 with insulin supplement, BMP4, FGF2, IWP2 (Tocris, 3533), and retinoic acid (Tocris, 0695). Media was refreshed every 24 hours for days 2-4 with BWIIFRa. On days 5/6, media was changed to BFI containing RPMI, B-27 with insulin supplement, BMP4, and FGF2. On day 7, the media was switched to maintenance media with RPMI and B-27 , refreshed every 48 hours.


#### Cardioid dissociation and cryopreservation
  At every time point (Days 0, 1, 2, 3, 5, 10), cardioids were dissociated into single cells with >90% live cells before freezing. TrypLE 10x Gibco™ A1217701) was warmed to 37°C, and the centrifuge was cooled to 4°C. Cardioids were washed with DPBS on a bi-directional 37 μm filter (Stemcell, 27215) and then dissociated using prewarmed TrypLE 10x in a thermomixer set at 1000 RPM at 37°C for 20 minutes with gentle pipetting every 5 minutes. When cardioids were fully dissociated, cells were centrifuged at 300g for 5 minutes at 4°C, resuspended in PBS, and viability checks were performed, aiming for >95% live cells. Cells were thoroughly resuspended in a freezing medium (Bambanker, NIPPON Genetics, BB05) and cryopreserved using a slow rate-controlled cooling process. Cryovials were then transferred to liquid nitrogen for long-term storage.


#### Cardioid Thawing
  Cryopreserved dissociated cardioids were transferred from liquid nitrogen to a bead bath until approximately half-thawed. The cell suspension was then immediately mixed with 1 mL of cold RPMI and transferred to a low-bind Eppendorf tube. The cells underwent two subsequent wash steps: they were centrifuged at 300g for 5 minutes at 4°C, and then gently resuspended in 1 mL of 1X PBS (Thermofisher, 10010023) with 0.04% BSA (Thermofisher, 15260037) using a wide-bore tip. After the final wash, the cell pellet was resuspended in an appropriate volume of 1X PBS with 0.04% BSA. To remove any remaining aggregates or debris, the final cell suspension was passed through a 70 μm cell strainer. The recovered cells were kept on ice, and their viability was assessed via trypan blue staining, with a quality control goal of greater than 95% viability.


### Immunofluorescence and microscopy
  Organoids were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and embedded in Optimal Cutting Temperature (OCT) compound (Tissue-Tek, 4583). Samples were cryosectioned at 10μm using a cryomicrotome (Thermo Scientific; Microm HM 550), and adjacent sections were collected on coated glass slides (VWR, VWRU48311-703). Sections from the central region of each organoid were selected for analysis. For immunofluorescence staining, sections were fixed again in 4% PFA for 20 minutes and washed with PBS. Samples were blocked with 5% goat serum (BioAbChem, 72-0480) in PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 (MP Biochemicals, 194854) for 1 hour at room temperature, then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies. After three PBS washes, sections were incubated with fluorescently conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 hours at room temperature and washed again. Finally, slides were mounted with DAPI Fluoromount-G (Southern Biotech, 0100-20) and imaged using a Zeiss LSM 990 confocal microscope.


### Single-cell processing

#### SHARE-seq
  SHARE-seq protocol was adopted from 94,95, and a detailed protocol is provided in Note S1.


#### Library Sequencing
  All DNA libraries were sequenced on a NextSeq 500/550 High Output Kit v2.5 (150 Cycles, 20024907), NovaSeq X Series 25B Reagent Kit (300 cycles, 20104706), NovaSeq 6000 S4 Reagent Kit v1.5 (300 cycles, 20028312) with XP workflow. Paired-end sequencing was run with a 96-99-8-96 configuration (Read1-Index1-Index2-Read2). We quantified DNA libraries using Qubit and tapestation, then prepared library pools at 4nM concentration. Sequencing was performed at the Stanford Genome Technology Center (NovaSeq6000), NovoGen (NovaSeqX) and in-house instrument (NextSeq550).


### SHARE-seq data preprocessing
  Raw sequencing data was preprocessed using a customized pipeline suited to SHARE-seq https://github.com/GreenleafLab/shareseq-pipeline (stable release v1.0.0) 40.


### Data analysis
  All analysis scripts can be found here https://github.com/GreenleafLab/Cardioid. A brief description of the analysis steps is provided in the following sections.


#### SHARE-seq data QC and filtering
  We performed per-sample QC filtering by manually inspecting and thresholding the following metrics: 1) TSS enrichment ratio and number of fragments for ATAC fragment files, 2) number of UMIs, number of genes and percentage of mitochondrial reads for RNA sparse matrices, 3) ratio of RNA UMIs vs ATAC fragments to remove cells with low quality in one modality. All sample filtering thresholds are summarized in Table S2.


#### RNA normalization, ambient RNA removal, dimensionality reduction, and clustering
  We used Seurat (v4.3.0)46 in R (v4.1.2) to process filtered RNA sparse matrices into Seurat objects. We adopted an iterative dimensionality reduction and clustering workflow to sequentially annotate cell types. For each iteration, we first performed SCTransform v2 and variable feature selection on RNA raw counts of each sample, then selected the top 3,000 consensus variable features across samples using the SelectIntegrationFeatures function from Seurat, excluding mitochondrial genes, sex chromosome genes, and cell cycle genes to minimize batch effects. We merged the raw RNA counts from per-sample objects into a single matrix, performed SCTransform v2 using consensus features, and used the DecontX function from the celda (v1.6.1) package 47 to remove ambient RNA contamination per cell. The decontaminated counts were then scaled to 10,000 UMIs per cell and log-normalized. Similar to the process mentioned above, we selected a list of the top 3,000 consensus variable features. PCA was performed on the merged object with the consensus features, followed by cell clustering using the Louvain algorithm at a resolution of 0.3 with 50 PCs and UMAP embedding. We then inspected each cluster and removed any low-quality clusters with significantly lower UMIs than other clusters. After removing cells in the low-quality clusters, we repeated the processing steps starting from RNA raw counts for each sample. This process was repeated until no more low-quality clusters were identified, which required three iterations. Cells in the final set of clusters passing this iterative QC were considered “whitelisted”. For each cell type cluster, marker genes were identified in a one-vs-all Wilcoxon rank-sum test versus all other clusters. Top marker genes were filtered to genes with a log2 fold change greater than 1.

  All final cluster annotations are included in Table S3. All cluster markers are summarized in Table S4.


#### scATAC data processing and label transfer
  We used ArchR (v1.0.2)96 to process filtered ATAC fragment files into a single ArchR project. After filtering to the final whitelisted cell barcodes from the iterative RNA processing workflow and transferring the clustering and cell type annotations, we called peaks per cluster using Macs2 (v2.2.7.1)97, merged peaks into a single reproducible peak set using ArchR’s iterative overlap strategy, and created a cell-by-peak matrix of fragment counts. We identified marker peaks per cluster using a Wilcoxon rank-sum test. For final cluster annotation, we use the well-defined RNA cluster labels and store them as metadata in the ArchR object.


#### scE2G
  We used the scE2GMultiome model to predict enhancer-gene connections in each cell type (https://github.com/EngreitzLab/scE2G/tree/v1.2)70. Briefly, scE2G first defines a set of candidate element-gene pairs where candidate elements are derived from peaks called on the pseudobulk ATAC-seq data from each cell type and are paired with genes with a transcription start site within 5 Mb. We used the model’s default TSS reference file with some manual adjustments to select the appropriate TSS for each gene. Next, it annotates each pair with seven features calculated from the multiome data, including 1) a pseudobulk Activity-By-Contact (ABC) score, where 3D contact is estimated by an inverse function of genomic distance; 2) the Kendall correlation across single cells between element accessibility and gene expression; 3) whether the gene is “ubiquitously expressed”, and 4) several other measures of genomic distance and chromatin accessibility around the element and promoter. Then, the features are integrated to assign each candidate element-gene pair a score representing the likelihood that the element regulates expression of the gene. Regulatory enhancer-gene interactions were defined as element-gene pairs with a score greater than 0.177, which is as the score yielding 70% recall when evaluating predictions in K562 cells against CRISPRi-validated enhancer-gene pairs98.


#### Azimuth
  To compare our in vitro cell states to an in vivo reference, we performed a projection analysis using Azimuth46 (v2.1.0). The decontaminated scRNA-seq counts matrix from the integrated cardioid dataset was used as the query. We ran the RunAzimuth function to map these query cells onto the human gastrula reference atlas ("hGas"). The mapping was performed using pre-computed reference data, and cell annotations were transferred at two levels: celltype.l1 (broad developmental lineages) and celltype.l2 (specific cell identities). The resulting object, containing the projected UMAP coordinates and cell predictions (predicted.celltype.l1, predicted.celltype.l2, and mapping.score), was saved for downstream analysis. The metadata, including query cluster labels and all predicted annotations and scores, are available in Table S8.


#### Optimal transport
  We applied Waddington-OT63 to model single-cell trajectories for each modality, with default pipeline settings. A cell at a time is represented by the gene-expression vector x∈RG where G is the number of genes. For each adjacent pair of time points (ti,ti+1), we first carried out a pairwise principal-component analysis, retaining the top d components shared by both time points. Coordinates in this Rd subspace served as input to an entropic, unbalanced optimal-transport problem 99. Only the row sums were constrained, allowing the column sums to vary so that proliferation or death could be accommodated. Solving this optimisation yielded a coupling matrix πti−>ti+1∈RNti×Nti+1. Matrix multiplication of the cell embedding matrix at ti with πti−>ti+1 denotes the mass transport between cells in ti to ti+1. Assuming Markovian dynamics, long-range couplings were approximated by successive matrix multiplication: γti−>tj=πti−>ti+1πti+1−>ti+2…πtj−1−>tj. For each starting cell, the probability mass in γ arriving in user-defined final clusters was summed to create an aggregate fate matrix F∈RN×K , where N is the total number of cells and K is the number of fates. The full pipeline was executed three times—once on RNA expression, once on ATAC gene scores, and once on a concatenated RNA + ATAC feature matrix. Within each modality, we trained separate models on control cells, on knockdown cells, and on the combined dataset, enabling direct comparison of transport maps and fate probabilities across perturbations.


#### ChromBPNet

##### Training
  For each cardioid cell state, ATAC-seq fragments from cells constituting that cell state were aggregated. These aggregated fragments were fed into the ENCODE peak calling pipeline to identify peaks in each cell state. The per-cell state aggregated fragments and peaks were then used to train ChromBPNet (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39829783/) models.

  The ChromBPNet models were trained using the framework provided in the ChromBPNet Python package (https://github.com/kundajelab/chrombpnet). For each cell state, 5 individual ChromBPNet models were trained by following the 5-fold cross-validation scheme used in the ChromBPNet package, in which each chromosome is present in the test set of at least one cross-validation fold. The chromosomes used per fold are shown in Table S9. All training was done using the hg38 reference genome. All of the ChromBPNet models were trained using a Tn5 bias model trained on the background regions from the fold 0 chromosomes of the Ventricular Cardiomyocytes cell state. The per-fold background regions were defined by the ChromBPNet package. A bias threshold factor of 0.6 was used.


##### Per-cell state predictions
  For every cell state, once the 5-fold models were trained, predictions for that cell state were made by averaging individual predictions from the 5-fold models. The log counts prediction for the cell state was the average of the log counts predictions of all the individual folds. The profile logits prediction for the cell state was the average of the profile logits predictions of all the individual folds. The profile logits prediction for the cell state was then softmaxed to produce the profile prediction for the cell state. The log counts prediction for the cell state was exponentiated and then multiplied by the profile prediction for the cell state to produce the counts-scaled profile prediction for the cell state. This averaging procedure was used for both the with-bias and de-biased model predictions.


##### Generating contribution scores
  For any genomic sequence and for each model, we used the DeepSHAP66 implementation of the DeepLIFT algorithm64 to compute the contribution score that each base is predicted to have towards the total chromatin accessibility over that sequence. The contribution scores in each cell state are computed as the average of the contribution scores from each of the 5-fold models. Contribution scores were calculated on the de-biased models.


##### Identifying a motif lexicon
  In each cell state, we identified the motifs of transcription factors that are active in that cell state and contribute towards chromatin accessibility predictions. To do this, for each cell state, we began by computing contribution scores for all 2114 bp sequences centered at every peak summit identified in that cell state. Then, TF-MoDISco 65 (https://github.com/kundajelab/tfmodisco) was run on the per-cell-state contribution scores to identify motifs present in that cell state. The modisco motifs command was run with 1,000,000 seqlets (the -n 1000000 option).

  Once motifs were identified in each cell state, the motifs were clustered to create a non-redundant set of motifs. The 2169 motifs identified across all 59 control and knockdown cell states were clustered using the MoitfCompendium67 (https://github.com/kundajelab/MotifCompendium) Python package to yield a set of 418 distinct motifs. Clustering was done at the 0.95 similarity threshold. The 418 distinct motifs were further classified into 172 motif groups by automated annotation (MoitfCompendium) followed by manual curation. The 172 groups were next clustered into 106 distinct motif families (Note S3).


##### Marginal footprinting
  For each motif and for each cell state, an in silico marginal footprint score was computed to quantify the effect that motif has in that cell state. First, for each model, 1000 of the training background sequences identified during ChromBPNet model training were sampled. Then, the predicted counts from the de-biased model were computed (in this case, the predicted counts were computed as the average of the predicted counts of the forward and reverse sequence). This served as a baseline for the amount of accessibility latent in these background regions. Then, for each motif, a consensus DNA sequence was determined by taking the maximum contributing base pair at every position. For each motif, that motif’s consensus sequence was then inserted into the center of the 1000 background sequences. Predictions from the de-biased models were made on these sequences with in silico motif insertions in them; the predicted counts of the forward and reverse sequences were averaged to produce the predicted counts of these sequences. The log2 fold change between the predicted counts of the motif-inserted-sequence and the predicted counts of the without-motif-sequence was then computed per sequence. This log2 fold change was then averaged across all 1000 sequences to produce an effect size for the marginal effect of the motif in that model. These effect sizes were then averaged across the five-fold models to give cell-state-specific marginal footprint scores of the motif. This process was done per motif to give marginal footprint scores for each motif in each footprint.

  These marginal footprint scores were plotted as a line plot across the differentiation trajectory to produce Figure 3F, S6 and Note S3. These marginal footprint scores were row-normalized and then plotted in a heatmap to produce Figure 3G and S6. Figure 3H shows rows of Figure 3G, which have been subset to highlight differences in the marginal footprint effect sizes of different motifs in the same family.


##### Annotating motifs in each cell state’s regulatory landscape
  We performed a comprehensive identification of motifs present in the regulatory regions of every cell state. For each cell state, we used the FiNeMo (https://github.com/kundajelab/Fi-NeMo) Python package to identify all occurrences of each motif in the non-redundant motif set within the summit-centered peak sequences of that cell state. The result was an annotation of all motif instances of every motif within the peak sequences of every cell state. The finemo call-hits command was run with default options.


##### Motif enrichment in peaks
  We computed motif enrichment within cell states across peak clusters (Figure S3C). To do this, we first began by selecting two peak clusters of interest in a cell state. Then, we counted the number of motif instances of each type of motif present in that cell state in each peak in the two peak clusters. Then, for each motif, we created a contingency table where the top left was the number of instances of that motif in all peaks in the first peak cluster, the top right was the number of motif instances of that motif in all peaks in the second peak cluster, the bottom left was the total number of other motif instances in all peaks in the first peak cluster, and the bottom right was the total number of other motif instances in all peaks in the second peak cluster. Then, an odds ratio and p-value were computed for each contingency table using Fisher’s exact test.


##### Gene-motif linking analysis
  The first step of the gene-motif linking analysis was to filter genes. First, for each gene, the maximum expression of the gene across all cell states in normalized units of logTPM was computed. Then, any gene whose maximum expression is < 1% of the maximum expression (in units of logTPM) of any gene in any cell state is removed.

  Then, the next step is to identify linked peaks for each gene. For each gene and each cell state, all scE2G links to that gene in that cell state were considered. If any of those links overlapped a peak in that cell state, that peak was added to the total set of peaks linked to that gene. This was done across all cell states to yield a comprehensive set of scE2G-linked peak regions that are linked to that gene in any cell state.

  Then, for each cell state, gene, and motif, all instances of that motif identified in that cell state that overlapped any scE2G-linked peak region were considered. For each one of those hits, the sum of the contribution scores of that motif CWM is weighed by a function of the distance of the hit from the gene TSS. The distance weighting function is e^-(distanceToTSS/5000)+1/e. The distance-weighted contribution scores of all hits of a motif are summed to compute a score for how much a motif contributes to a gene in a cell state. This is done across all motifs, genes, and cell states.


#### Consensus non-overlapping peakset
  A consensus, non-overlapping peakset was constructed by merging peaks from every cell state. For each cell state, each peak set was transformed into a list of 500 bp bins centered around each peak summit. Then, these summit-centered bin lists were combined across all control and knockdown cell states, leading to a list of 9,643,338 500 bp regions. The peak calling pipeline, which uses macs3, annotates each summit with a p-value indicating how likely that summit is really a peak summit. The combined summit-centered bin list was then sorted by macs3 p-value, with the bins with the lowest p-value (most likely to be a peak summit) at the top and bins with the highest p-value (least likely to be a peak summit) at the bottom. At this point, the consensus peakset is an empty set and contains no summit-centered bins. Then, each summit-centered bin in the sorted list was considered, and the non-overlapping peakset was built with the following rule: if the bin had any overlap with any bin already in the peakset, the bin was not retrained in the final peakset; however, if the bin did not overlap with any bin that was already in the peakset, it was added to the peakset. Through this procedure, a non-overlapping summit-centered peakset of 628,378 500 bp bins was created.


#### Peak clusters
  For each of the 628,378 peaks in the consensus peakset, its total accessibility is computed per cell state as the sum of the total number of Tn5 insertions in the peak region in that cell state. This gives a peak by cell state counts matrix. This raw counts matrix is then normalized by the read depth in each cell state so that each element in the matrix, which corresponds to a specific peak in a specific cell state is divided by the read depth in that cell state. Then, a copy of the normalized counts matrix is made which is then column-wise z-score normalized. Within each column (corresponding to a specific peak), this z-score normalization is done by first computing the column-wise mean (the mean normalized accessibility across all cell states) and then subtracting this mean from all values in the column, and then computing the column-wise standard deviation (the standard deviation of normalized accessibility across cell states) and then dividing the column values by this standard deviation. Then, the normalized counts matrix and the z-score-normalized counts matrix are concatenated along the cell state axis so that each peak is now represented by a vector of the normalized counts value across all cell states, concatenated with the z-score-normalized values across all cell states. Then, the peaks are clustered using scikt-learn’s Mini-Batch K-Means clustering with k-20 (sklearn.cluster.MiniBatchKMeans(n_clusters=20, random_state=0, batch_size=1000, n_init=50)). Three pairs of clusters with similar dynamics were merged (original clusters 6 and 16 were merged, original clusters 1 and 11 were merged, and original clusters 8 and 19 were merged). Finally, the clusters were manually re-ordered. This led to 17 peak clusters.


#### Motif changes in knockdown
  Every control cell state has a corresponding knockdown cell state. For every control/knockdown cell state pair, for each peak in the consensus peakset, we compute the log2 fold change between the normalized counts per million in the control and knockdown cell states; a positive log2 fold change indicates that the peak was more accessible in the control than the knockdown, and a negative value indicates that the peak was more accessible in the knockdown than the control. Peaks that had no counts in neither control nor knockdown were removed from this analysis for that cell state pair. Then, the remaining peaks were clustered by their log2 fold change value using k-means clustering with k=3. For each cell state pair, this separated the peaks into 3 clusters: a cluster containing peaks more accessible in control than knockdown, a cluster containing peaks more accessible in knockdown than control, and a cluster containing peaks with similar accessibility across both control and knockdown cell states.

  Then, for each motif, we created a contingency table where the top left was the number of peaks in the control-accessible peakset that contained any instance of that motif family, the top right was the number of peaks in the knockdown-accessible peakset that contained any instances of that motif family, the bottom left was the number of peaks in the control-accessible peakset that did not contain any instances of the motif family, and the bottom right was the number of peaks in the knockdown-accessible peakset that did not contain any instances of the motif family. Then, an odds ratio and p-value were computed for each contingency table using Fisher’s exact test. The odds ratio and p-value of differential motif enrichment between control and knockdown are shown for each motif family and each control/knockdown cell state pair in Figure 5H.


#### Variant Scoring.
  We made in silico variant effect predictions of noncoding variants using our ChromBPNet models. Each variant was specified by a genomic coordinate, a reference allele (which matched the hg38 reference genome), and an alternate allele. For each variant, we took the 2114 bp genomic sequence centered around the variant to be the “reference sequence”. We substituted the center reference allele for the alternate allele to create the “alternate sequence”. To predict the effect of a variant in a model, we compared the model’s prediction on the reference sequence to the prediction on the alternate sequence. All predictions were made using the de-biased models.

  When comparing the reference and alternate predictions, a variety of scores are computed to quantify the effect of the variant. These include the log2 fold change (LFC) of the total predicted coverage (total counts) over the two sequences, the absolute value of the LFC, the Jensen Shannon Distance (JSD) between the base-resolution predicted probability profiles, and the active allele quantile (AAQ), which is a measure of how the predicted counts in the reference and alternate sequences compare to the distribution of total counts in all peak regions used to train the models. For each of these variant effect scores, an empirical p-value is computed by comparing the variant effect against a null distribution generated from the scores of variants placed in dinucleotide shuffled sequences. Variant scores and p-values were computed using the variant-scorer Python package (https://github.com/kundajelab/variant-scorer). To compute cell state scores, the 5-fold model scores were averaged; all scores were averaged using an arithmetic mean, while the p-values were averaged using a geometric mean. The heatmap in Figure 6E and the line plot in Figure 6G show the predicted fold-averaged LFC across all cell states.
