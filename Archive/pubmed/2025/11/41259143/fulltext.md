# Glutathionylated DNA adducts accumulate in mitochondrial DNA and are regulated by AP endonuclease 1 and tyrosyl-DNA phosphodiesterase 1


## Significance
  Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) plays a crucial role in energy metabolism, cellular signaling, and human disease development. While it is known that mtDNA is susceptible to damage, the biological consequences of this damage remain partially understood. We report a type of mtDNA damage, glutathionylated DNA (GSH-DNA) adducts. These adducts form from endogenous abasic sites and alkylation DNA damage, catalyzed by a mtDNA packing protein (TFAM) and polyamines. Our study clarifies their accumulation, regulation, and biological effects in mitochondria. Given the emerging role of mtDNA and a commonly used oxidative DNA damage marker, 8-oxo-7,8-dihydro-2’-deoxyguanosine, in promoting inflammation, the high levels of GSH-DNA adducts in mtDNA highlight the critical need to investigate their roles in innate immune and inflammatory diseases.


## Results

### Accumulation of GSH-DNA Adducts in mtDNA.
  AP sites, formed through BER or spontaneous depurination/depyrimidination, can undergo AP-lyase reactions (26). As shown in Fig. 1A, the reaction can be catalyzed by DNA glycosylases (27), biological amines (purple arrow) (33, 34), or TFAM (green arrow) (17, 18), yielding reactive 3′-PUA residues via a common Schiff base mechanism. These 3′-PUA residues readily react with biological thiols, such as glutathione (GSH) and protein cysteine (Cys) residues, such as those in TFAM (18). Notably, GSH can compete with TFAM for 3′-PUA, suppressing the formation of TFAM DNA–protein cross-links (TFAM-DPCs) through the formation of 3′-glutathionyl-2,3-dideoxyribose (3′-GS-ddR) (18).

  To quantify GSH-DNA adduct levels in cultured human cells, we adapted our previously developed LC–MS/MS method (30) to detect GSH-DNA adducts in both nDNA and mtDNA. We used a HeLa cell line engineered for the inducible formation of mitochondrial AP sites by overexpressing a mitochondria-targeting human UNG1-Y147A variant (18, 35–37). The variant removes nucleobases at thymidine residues in addition to deoxyuridine, generating AP sites in mtDNA. Inducible overexpression of UNG1-Y147A with 0.5 μg/mL doxycycline for 24 h results in an approximately twofold increase in AP site levels in mtDNA, accompanied by a decline of mtDNA copy number to 10-20% of that of the untreated control (18). Following doxycycline treatment (or no treatment), mtDNA and nDNA were isolated, and DNA samples were derivatized with 5 mM methoxyamine (MX) to block reactive aldehyde groups (30). Enzymatic digestion yielded canonical nucleosides and the target analyte, glutathionyl-2,3-dideoxyribose-MX (GS-ddR-MX). An oligodeoxynucleotide containing a heavy isotope labeled [13C2, 15N]-GS-ddR-MX was used as an internal standard for quantification. Light and heavy analytes were enriched using HPLC and quantified by nanoLC-MS/MS. The representative selected-ion chromatograms (SICs) are shown in Fig. 1B, with the corresponding fragmentation pattern shown above the right panel.

  Quantification based on the integrated peak area ratios (light vs. heavy) revealed approximately 0.8 GS-ddR-MX per 107 nucleotides in nDNA (Fig. 1C), comparable to the reported levels in HEK293T cells (30). Strikingly, mtDNA exhibited a significantly higher GS-ddR-MX level, averaging 6 lesions per 106 nucleotides, approximately 70-fold greater than in nDNA. Upon induction of mitochondrial AP sites with doxycycline, nDNA GS-ddR-MX levels remained unchanged (0.9 lesions per 107 nucleotides), whereas mtDNA GS-ddR-MX levels significantly increased to 31 lesions per 106 nucleotides relative to the uninduced condition. The approximately fivefold increase in mtDNA GS-ddR-MX levels under doxycycline treatment mirrors the increase in mtDNA AP site levels (18, 38), indicating the consequential formation of GSH-DNA adducts from AP sites in mitochondria.


### APE1 and TDP1 Regulate GSH-DNA Adduct Levels in mtDNA.
  To assess GSH-DNA adduct formation under other genotoxic stress conditions, we treated HEK293A cells with 1 mM N-methyl-N-nitrosourea (MNU), an alkylating agent. MNU reacts with cellular DNA to form mainly N7-methyl-2’-deoxyguanosine (N7mdG) and N3-methyl-2’-deoxyadenosine (N3mdA) adducts (39), which can undergo spontaneous depurination or BER to produce AP sites, potentially leading to GSH-DNA adduct formation. We measured GS-ddR-MX levels in nDNA and mtDNA from wild-type (WT) HEK293A cells (Fig. 2A, black bars). Under the solvent vehicle control (VC) condition, HEK293A cells exhibit a significantly higher level of GS-ddR-MX in mtDNA compared to nDNA (7 lesions per 106 nucleotides in mtDNA vs. 4 lesions per 107 nucleotides, P = 0.027, a 17-fold difference). Under incubations with 1 mm MNU for 8 h, the mitochondrial GS-ddR-MX levels decreased slightly (4 lesions per 106 nucleotides), while the nuclear GS-ddR-MX level increased moderately (7 lesions per 107 nucleotides). The difference between mitochondrial and nuclear GS-ddR-MX levels was not statistically significant under MNU treatment.

  To identify factors influencing GSH-DNA adduct accumulation in mtDNA, we examined two key DNA repair enzymes that are known to process 3′ DNA lesions, i.e., APE1 and TDP1 (40). We generated HEK293A TDP1 knockout (KO) and APE1 KO cells using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene knockout (41) and confirmed KO by western blotting (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). In TDP1 KO cells, GS-ddR-MX levels followed a trend similar to the control cells, with a significant accumulation of GS-ddR-MX in mtDNA under VC (24-fold higher GS-ddR-MX in mtDNA vs. nDNA, Fig. 2A, red bars). Under MNU treatment, a significantly higher level of GS-ddR-MX in mtDNA was also observed (13-fold higher), although a moderate but significant decrease in the lesion level was observed compared to the control (by approximately 30%, Fig. 2A, red data, mtDNA NMU vs. VC). The decrease is likely due to the consumption of GSH by MNU (42). TDP1 KO resulted in a significant accumulation of GS-ddR-MX in mtDNA under VC (Fig. 2A, red bar with ####), suggesting a role of TDP1 in regulating the basal formation of GSH-DNA adducts. Similarly, under NMU treatment also resulted in a significant increase of GS-ddR-MX in mtDNA in TDP1 KO cells, albeit the extent of increase is less than that of VC.

  By comparison, APE1 KO cells showed the most significant accumulation of GS-ddR-MX in mtDNA (approximately 145-fold) compared to nDNA, with and without MNU treatment (Fig. 2A, blue bars). Notably, the decreasing trend of GS-ddR-MX in mtDNA under MNU treatment compared to VC was reversed in APE1 KO cells, demonstrating a more pronounced effect of APE1 in preventing GSH-DNA adduct accumulation under MNU treatment. Compared to WT HEK293A cells under MNU treatment, a significant accumulation of GS-ddR-MX levels in mtDNA was also observed in APE1 KO cells (Fig. 2A, blue bar with ###). Unlike TDP1 KO cells, the difference in GS-ddR-MX levels in mtDNA was not significant when comparing WT to APE1 KO cells under VC. Neither APE1 nor TDP1 KO resulted in a significant accumulation of GS-ddR-MX in nDNA, likely due to redundant repair mechanisms.

  To elucidate the biochemical basis of these observations, we performed enzyme kinetic analyses of 3′-exonuclease reactions using recombinant TDP1 and APE1 with a DNA substrate containing a site-specific 3′-GS-ddR lesion (Fig. 2B). The substrate was generated by reacting a 36-nt double-stranded (ds) DNA containing an AP site (AP-DNA) with GSH and spermine, followed by purification (hereafter referred to as AP32-GSH). TDP1 efficiently cleaved 3′-GS-ddR and yielded a 3′-phosphate-terminated DNA fragment (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 B, Left panels), consistent with its unknown 3′ lesion processing activity. Product yields increased under higher TDP1/DNA molar ratios (Fig. 2 B, Left gel panels), achieving nearly complete 3′-GS-ddR removal at a 1:1 molar ratio of TDP1/DNA within 30 min. APE1, known for its phosphodiester bond incision and exonuclease activity, also processed the 3’-GS-ddR lesion (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 B, Right panels). The multiple product bands reflect the exonuclease activity of APE1. While APE1 appeared less efficient than TDP1 under enzyme excess conditions (5:1 and 1:1 enzyme/DNA molar ratios), product yields were comparable under enzyme-limiting conditions (1:5 enzyme/DNA molar ratio).

  To quantify catalytic efficiency, we conducted steady-state kinetic experiments (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 and S4). APE1 concentration was optimized to ensure specific 3′-p-GS-ddR removal, allowing direct comparison with TDP1. Michaelis–Menten kinetics analysis (Fig. 2 C and D and Table 1) revealed that TDP1 had a slightly higher catalytic turnover (kcat, 5.0 ± 1.2 min−1) than APE1 (3.2 ± 0.4 min−1), but a significantly higher Km (360 ± 110 nM) compared to APE1 (36 ± 14 nM), suggesting a lower DNA-binding affinity for TDP1. Overall, APE1 exhibited approximately sevenfold higher catalytic efficiency (92 ± 21 μM−1min−1) than TDP1 (14 ± 2 μM−1 min−1) with the tested substrate, corroborating its dominant role in preventing mitochondrial 3′-GS-ddR accumulation under alkylation DNA damage. The catalytic efficiencies are comparable to the reported values of the exonuclease activities of the two enzymes with their canonical substrates (Table 1) (43, 44). Furthermore, our western blotting-based semiquantification demonstrates that HEK293A cells contained approximately eightfold higher APE1 than TDP1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 and SI Appendix, Table S2). Together, the mass spectrometry-based quantification, enzyme kinetic analysis, and protein quantification suggest different roles of TDP1 and APE1 in regulating the GS-ddR-MX in mtDNA. While TDP1 plays an important role in preventing GS-ddR-MX accumulation in mtDNA without genotoxic stress, APE1 plays a major role in regulating the mitochondrial GS-ddR-MX levels under alkylation DNA damage conditions, likely due to its higher protein level and overall higher catalytic efficiency of APE1 and the presence of different damaged DNA structures (see Discussion).


### TFAM and Polyamines Affect GSH-DNA Adduct Formation.
  Within mitochondria, mtDNA is compacted into nucleoid structures by TFAM, potentially restricting GSH access and thus limiting GSH-DNA adduct formation. To further understand the effect of TFAM-DNA packaging on GSH-DNA adduct formation, we performed biochemical assays using AP-DNA substrates and varying TFAM concentrations. Given the millimolar concentrations of biological polyamines (e.g., spermine and spermidine) in the mitochondrial matrix (45), and their potential association with mtDNA [inferred from bacterial nucleoid studies (46)], we also included 1.2 mM spermine into these reactions. In addition, the ability of spermine to catalyze β-elimination at AP sites (33, 34) motivated its inclusion. We designed two DNA substrates, AP32 and AP37, based on the mtDNA light-strand promoter (LSP) region (sequences shown in SI Appendix, Table S1), with AP lesions located within and outside the LSP, respectively. This design allowed us to explore the effect of TFAM-DNA interactions on GSH-DNA adduct formation, as TFAM preferentially binds to the LSP sequence (47).

  Reactions were conducted with 1.2 mM spermine and 1.0 mM GSH under varying times and TFAM concentrations. In the absence of TFAM, AP-DNA was efficiently converted to GSH-DNA fragments, confirming spermine’s catalytic activity (“no TFAM” reactions in Fig. 3 A and B). A direct comparison of reaction (AP-DNA and GSH) time courses with or without spermine demonstrates that the GSH-DNA adduct formation is extremely slow without spermine and that spermine accelerates the reaction dramatically (SI Appendix, Fig. S10). Conversely, in the presence of TFAM, TFAM-DPC formation and a concurrent decrease in GSH-DNA fragments were observed (compare 8 μM TFAM to 0 μM TFAM reactions in Fig. 3 A and B). The inhibitory effect of TFAM was evident across increasing TFAM concentrations, regardless of AP lesion position (Fig. 3 C and D, complete TFAM concentration series in SI Appendix, Figs. S7 and S8).

  Notably, distinct differences in product yields and overall AP-DNA cleavage rates were observed with AP32 and AP37 substrates. The decrease in GSH-DNA fragment yield was more pronounced with AP32 compared to AP37 under increasing TFAM concentrations (Fig. 3C). For example, at a 4:1 TFAM/DNA molar ratio, the GSH-DNA adduct yield was approximately 20% for AP32 compared to 80% for AP37. Furthermore, the AP-DNA disappearance rate (kdis) decreased with increasing TFAM concentrations for both substrates, indicating that increased TFAM-DNA packaging restricts spermine access to AP sites (Fig. 3E). Notably, different trends of kdis decrease with two substrates were observed. The decrease followed by a gradual increase in kdis with AP32 can be explained by the catalytic effect of TFAM in AP-lyase reactions at the preferred DNA-binding site (17), whereas the continuing decrease with AP37 is consistent with spermine and TFAM competing for AP-DNA substrate. Given the uneven TFAM occupancy along mtDNA (48, 49), with high and low occupancy sites (50), the gradual decrease in kdis with increasing TFAM concentration for AP32 suggests that at high TFAM occupancy sites, spermine and GSH access is limited, favoring TFAM-DPC formation. Conversely, at low TFAM occupancy sites, polyamine-mediated GSH-DNA adduct formation is favored, requiring higher TFAM concentrations to suppress adduct formation. Collectively, these data demonstrate the regulatory roles of TFAM-DNA packaging and polyamines in GSH-DNA adduct formation, particularly highlighting the catalytic effects of polyamines at low TFAM occupancy sites.


### Effects of GSH-DNA Adducts On GSH Homeostasis and Protein Expression.
  To understand the impact of GSH-DNA adduct formation on cellular GSH homeostasis, we measured GSH/GSSG ratios and total GSH levels in HEK293A cells. In WT HEK293A cells, 0.1 mM MNU treatment for 8 h did not significantly alter GSH/GSSG ratios, whereas 1 mM MNU induced a decrease (Fig. 4A, black bars), which is likely due to direct reactions between MNU and GSH (42). Similar effects were observed in APE1 KO cells (Fig. 4A, blue bars). Notably, APE1 KO cells exhibited a slight but significant increase in GSH/GSSG ratios relative to WT cells under VC treatment (Fig. 4A, blue bar with #). TDP1 KO cells showed no significant changes in GSH/GSSG ratios upon MNU treatment (Fig. 4A, red bars). We also found no significant difference in total glutathione levels (sum of GSH and GSSG) in any of the three cell lines following 1 mM MNU treatment. These results indicate that mitochondrial GSH-DNA adduct formation is not associated with a disruption of cellular GSH homeostasis under the current experimental conditions.

  Given the observed effect of MNU on GSH levels, we next examined doxycycline-inducible Tet-On HeLa and HEK293 cells, with inducible expression of mitochondria-targeting UNG1-Y147A variant to form mitochondrial AP sites. Although a significant GSH-DNA adduct accumulation in mtDNA was observed under our treatment conditions (0.5 μg/mL doxycycline for 24 h) (Fig. 1C), neither the GSH/GSSG ratio nor the total glutathione levels in HeLa cells were significantly altered (Fig. 4B and SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). Because the mitochondrial GSH pool accounts for 10-15% of total cellular GSH (51), we attempted to measure the mitochondrial GSH/GSSG ratio directly. Despite multiple careful attempts at subcellular fractionation, the luminescence-based method is not sensitive enough to detect changes in GSH/GSSG ratios in the mitochondrial lysate. As an alternative approach, we measured the protein level of SLC25A39, a GSH transporter whose increased expression can indicate a local decrease in mitochondrial GSH (52). We used the buthionine sulfoximine (BSO)-treated HEK293A APE1 KO cells as a positive control, but there was no significant difference in SLC25A39 levels in either cell line after 72 h of doxycycline induction (Fig. 4C and SI Appendix, Fig. S5C). These findings provide additional support that GSH homeostasis remains stable at both the cellular and mitochondrial levels, even when GSH-DNA adducts accumulate in the mitochondrial genome.

  To further explore the mitochondrial protein level changes under GSH-DNA adduct accumulation, we performed stable isotope labeling by amino acids in cell culture (SILAC)-based quantitative proteomic analysis using Tet-On HeLa cells. Upon isolation of mitochondria from HeLa cells, mitochondrial proteins were isolated and digested, followed by fractionation on HPLC before proteomic analysis. Compared to the untreated control, doxycycline-induced mitochondrial AP sites resulted in significant changes in the expression level of several mitochondrial proteins (Fig. 4D). A decrease in mitochondrial ribosomal proteins (MRPS35, MRPL15, MRPL37, MRPL13, and MRPL4) and complex I subunits (NDUFA12 and NDUFA13) was observed, suggesting a suppression of mitochondrial protein synthesis and energy production under these conditions. Since these proteins are encoded by nuclear genes, the decrease is unlikely due to the decline in mtDNA copy number (18).

  In addition, the levels of ARF5 (ADP ribosylation factor 5), DNM1L (dynamin-1-like protein), and PRDX6 (peroxiredoxin 6) were upregulated. ARF5 is involved in protein trafficking, and DNM1L mediates mitochondrial and peroxisomal division. PRDX6 is a key player in cellular redox homeostasis, and its increase likely represents a cellular adaptation to GSH-DNA adduct formation. We confirmed the increase in PRDX6 levels in HeLa cells via western blotting (Fig. 4 E and F). A similar increase in PRDX6 levels was also observed in both WT and APE1 KO HEK293A cells under both VC and MNU-treated conditions. This is likely due to a compensatory upregulation in the absence of APE1, since PRDX6 has been shown to interact with APE1 through immunoprecipitation, particularly with a redox-deficient APE1 variant C65S (53). In sum, our results show that mitochondrial GSH-DNA adduct accumulation does not alter cellular or mitochondrial GSH homeostasis, but is associated with changes in mitochondrial protein expression. Specifically, we observed down-regulation of mitochondrial ribosomal proteins and complex I subunits and up-regulation of PRDX6, ARF5, and DNM1L.


### Effects of GSH-DNA Adducts on TFAM-DNA Binding and Stability of TFAM-DNA Complexes.
  To investigate the impact of GSH-DNA adducts on TFAM-DNA binding, we measured the equilibrium dissociation constant (Kd) of TFAM with AP32-GSH and canonical DNA substrates using fluorescence polarization assays. Although AP32-GSH resulted in a slightly lower Kd compared to the canonical substrate, the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.075). The Hill coefficient decreased significantly from 2.5 (canonical DNA) to 2.0 (AP32-GSH), indicating slightly altered binding cooperativity of TFAM in the presence of GSH-DNA adduct (Fig. 5A). Although the change in Hill coefficient was statistically significant, a value of 2.0 falls within the reported value range of TFAM (54, 55) and is indicative of cooperative DNA binding.

  To examine the effect of accumulated GSH-DNA adduct on conformational stability of DNA and TFAM-DNA complexes, we carried out a series of MD simulations. First, we used the Dickerson-Drew dodecamer (56), a well-characterized B-DNA structure, as our model DNA substrate (Fig. 5 B–D and SI Appendix, Fig. S9 A and B). The 3′-GS-ddR lesion was modeled in the middle of the top DNA strand, substituting thymine in the unmodified substrate and introducing a nick (Fig. 5B). Analysis of the RMSD of C1’ atoms over 500 ns MD simulations revealed high stability for the unmodified DNA, with an average RMSD of 1.8 Å. In contrast, three independent MD simulations of the 3′-GS-ddR-containing DNA, initiated with different random number seeds, exhibited higher and less stable RMSD values. Notably, the RMSD of the GSH-adducted DNA tended to stabilize toward the end of the simulation (Fig. 5B). These data suggest that 3′-GS-ddR introduces increased flexibility into the DNA duplex, allowing it to adopt multiple conformations during the simulation (Fig. 5D and SI Appendix, Fig. S9A).

  In addition, we assessed the fluctuation of each DNA base pair by calculating the Rms fluctuation (RMSF) of C1’ atoms (Fig. 5C). As expected, a slight increase in RMSF was observed at the terminal positions of the dodecamer. However, the GSH-DNA substrate exhibited consistently higher RMSF throughout the entire sequence, with prominent peaks at the lesion position (T7), its complementary site (A18), and the 3’ end (G13). The peaks at T7 and A18 can be explained by the increased flexibility introduced by the 3′-GS-ddR lesion and the loss of hydrogen bonds between 3′-GS-ddR and A18. The peak at G13 reflects the inherent flexibility of the DNA terminus. Furthermore, we observed apparent conformational changes in the GSH-DNA adduct between the initial (time = 0 ns) and final (time = 500 ns) simulation states (Fig. 5D and SI Appendix, Fig. S9A). These conformational changes are partly attributed to the loss of hydrogen bond interactions involving the 3′-GS-ddR lesion (SI Appendix, Fig. S9B). Hydrogen bonding interactions between the GSH-DNA adduct and surrounding nucleobases (Fig. 5D and SI Appendix, Fig. S9A, zoomed-in views) may contribute to the observed RMSD stabilization at later simulation times.

  Furthermore, we assessed the impact of GSH-DNA adducts on the stability of TFAM-DNA complexes. We used the solved crystal structure of the TFAM-DNA complex containing the LSP sequence (PDB: 3TQ6) (57) as the initial coordinate and modeled the GSH-DNA conjugate according to the lesion position in AP32-GSH. Binding energy calculations revealed that the lesion-containing TFAM-DNA complex exhibited a 37 to 133 kcal/mol lower binding energy compared to that of a canonical DNA-containing complex (the plateau region of the curves in Fig. 5F). These observations corroborate a decreasing trend in Kd in the presence of GSH-DNA lesion in fluorescence polarization experiments. Additionally, structural analysis over the course of the 400 ns simulation revealed a notable conformational change in the GSH-DNA adduct and a potential basis for stabilization. The GSH moiety intercalates into the DNA duplex (also observed in the Dickerson-Drew dodecamer, Fig. 5D) and forms several hydrogen bonds with nearby nucleobases (yellow lines in Fig. 5E and SI Appendix, Fig. S9C). Overall, MD simulations demonstrate that an increase in the conformational flexibility of GSH-adducted DNA may enhance the binding environment for TFAM, potentially contributing to the decrease in binding energy and a slight decrease in Kd with the adducted substrate.


## Discussion
  Our study provides compelling evidence for the abundance and biological importance of GSH-DNA adducts in mtDNA. We have demonstrated that GSH-DNA adducts accumulate in mtDNA at significantly higher levels relative to nDNA in two cell lines under different DNA-damaging conditions, including induced mitochondrial AP sites and alkylation damage from MNU treatment. The accumulation is consistent with BER being the major DNA repair mechanism in mitochondria, with AP sites as a key intermediate. The formation of GSH-DNA adducts is regulated by TFAM-DNA packaging, polyamines, and repair proteins APE1 and TDP1. In addition, we found that the accumulation of these lesions is associated with the downregulation of mitochondrial ribosomal proteins and complex I subunits and the upregulation of a redox-related protein PRDX6, a vesicle trafficking related protein ARF5, and a mitochondrial fission-related protein DNM1L. Furthermore, MD simulations demonstrate that GSH-DNA adducts can also stabilize TFAM-DNA binding due to their conformational flexibility.

  Our research highlights APE1 as a critical enzyme in regulating GSH-DNA adduct accumulation, particularly under genotoxic stress. The higher abundance of APE1 and its superior binding to DNA substrates, compared to TDP1, likely contribute to this role. The promiscuous exonuclease activity of APE1 may also contribute to the degradation of DNA fragments containing these adducts. On the other hand, TDP1 may be more adept at processing GSH-DNA lesions in DNA structures other than the tested substrate. For example, TDP1 can process 3′-phosphoglycolate lesions at double-strand breaks (58), whereas APE1 has little activity on the terminal modifications at double-strand breaks (59).

  Our study also uncovers the intricate interplay between TFAM, polyamines, and GSH-DNA adduct formation. TFAM, a key mtDNA packaging and transcription factor, can inhibit GSH-DNA adduct formation by competing for AP sites. Conversely, polyamines, abundant in the mitochondrial matrix, promote GSH-DNA adduct formation, particularly in regions of low TFAM occupancy. We surmise that both TFAM-DPCs and GSH-DNA adducts may act as replication and transcription blocks, potentially marking damaged mtDNA for negative selection or further processing through repair or degradation. The increased stability of TFAM-DNA complexes in the presence of GSH-DNA adducts observed in our MD simulations supports this notion. This structural stability could shield damaged nucleoids from processes like transcription and replication.

  The observed association between GSH-DNA adduct accumulation and upregulation of PRDX6 in HeLa and HEK293A APE1 KO cells suggests a protective role for PRDX6 against mtDNA damage. PRDX6, a member of the mammalian Prdx family, detoxifies H2O2 and lipid hydroperoxides using GSH as a reductant. While PRDX6 is primarily localized in the cytoplasm, it can translocate to mitochondria under oxidative stress (60). While the upregulation of PRDX6 in HEK293A APE1 KO cells could be due to APE1 deficiency, its upregulation in HeLa cells, where APE1 and GSH homeostasis were not disrupted, argues for its link to GSH-DNA adduct formation. Clearly, the mechanistic linkage between GSH-adduct accumulation and upregulation of PRDX6 needs to be firmly established.

  The formation of GSH-DNA adducts via 3′-PUA reactions with GSH shares similarities with GSH conjugation to other DNA oxidation products, such as base propenals (31), lipid peroxidation byproducts (acrolein and 4-hydroxy-2-nonenal) (32), and the proto-oncometabolite fumarate (61). These conjugates often play a role in the detoxification of these reactive electrophiles. The conjugates or their processed derivatives have been suggested as markers for oxidative stress or as a stimulant in ROS-mediated signaling. For example, the mercapturate derivatives of base propenal-GSH conjugates have been detected in rat urine under CCl4-induced oxidative stress or treatment with the DNA-cleaving antitumor agent, bleomycin (31). Similarly, the proto-oncometabolite fumarate can conjugate with GSH to amplify ROS-dependent signaling in cultured human cancer cells (61). Compared to these examples, our study is unique in demonstrating GSH conjugation with a repair intermediate on a macromolecule, mtDNA.

  In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive analysis of GSH-DNA adducts in mtDNA, clarifying their formation, regulation, and biological implications. The level of GSH-DNA adducts in mtDNA is comparable to that of 8-oxodG, a commonly used oxidative DNA damage marker. Given the emerging role of mtDNA and 8-oxodG in innate immunity and inflammation (19–21), further research into the potential immunomodulatory effects of GSH-DNA conjugates is warranted.


## Materials and Methods

### Cell Culture.
  The doxycycline-inducible Tet-On HeLa (18) and Tet-On HEK293 cells (62) were prepared as described previously. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Gibco, 12100-046) supplemented with 10% Tet System Approved Fetal Bovine Serum (Takara Bio USA, 631106) under 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Mitochondria-specific abasic (AP) sites were produced by overexpressing UNG1 Y147A variant under doxycycline induction. Cells were treated with 0.5 μg/mL doxycycline for 24 h when cells reached 50 to 70% confluence. HEK293A TDP1 knockout (TDP1 KO) and HEK293A APE1 knockout (APE1 KO) cells were prepared as previously described (41). Cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, 12100-046) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (Fisherbrand, FB12999102) under 5% CO2 at 37 °C. DNA alkylation damage was induced using 0.1 or 1 mM MNU (MedChemExpress, HY-34758) for 8 h after cells reached 80% confluence. The VC contained 0.1% DMSO in DMEM. For GS-DNA adduct quantification by nanoLC-MS/MS, cells from four to ten 150-mm dishes were pooled to generate a single data point, corresponding to approximately 35 to 100 million cells per measurement.


### Isolation of Mitochondria From Cultured Cells.
  Mitochondria were extracted from cells using homemade buffers and components from an mtDNA isolation kit (BioVision, K280). Briefly, 1 × 108 cells were resuspended in 10 mL of 1× cytosol extraction buffer, and the suspension was incubated on ice for 10 min. Cells were homogenized using an ice-cold Dounce tissue grinder with 50 passes. The homogenate was centrifuged at 1,200 g for 5 min at 4 °C, yielding a supernatant containing mitochondria, a pellet containing nDNA and residual mitochondria. The supernatant was transferred to a new centrifuge tube, followed by centrifugation at 1,200×g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was then transferred into a new tube. The pellet was resuspended in another 5 mL of fresh 1× cytosol extraction buffer and homogenized again with 50 passes. The centrifugation, separation of the supernatant, and homogenization processes were repeated another two times. To pellet the mitochondria, the supernatant fractions from a total of three centrifugation steps were combined and spun at 16,000 g for another 30 min at 4 °C. To remove nDNA contaminants, the mitochondria pellet was treated with 200 U of turbo nuclease (Millipore Sigma, T4330) in 200 μL of reaction containing 5 mM HEPES buffer pH 7.4, 210 mM mannitol, 70 mM sucrose, 10 mM MgCl2, 5 mM CaCl2, 0.2% (w/v) BSA, 1× proteinase inhibitor cocktails (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A32955) and 20 mM ascorbate sodium for 30 min at 4 °C. The suspension was supplemented by 1 ml of buffered solution containing 5 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 210 mM mannitol, 70 mM sucrose, 10 mM MgCl2, 5 mM CaCl2, 0.2% (w/v) BSA, and 1 M KCl followed by centrifugation at 16,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C. The pellet was washed with 1 ml of the aforementioned HEPES buffer twice before further processing.


### Reactions of AP site–containing DNA with GSH, spermine, and TFAM.
  The AP site–containing DNA oligomers were prepared using deoxyuridine-containing precursor oligomers with a 5′ fluorescein (FAM) label, as described previously (17). The resulting AP-containing oligomer was annealed with an unmodified complementary oligomer to form an AP-DNA substrate. Reactions with TFAM contained 0, 2, 4, or 8 µM TFAM, 1 µM of AP32 (or AP37), 30 mM HEPES (pH = 7.4), 90 mM NaCl, and 10 mM EDTA. The reaction was quenched with 100 mM NaBH4, followed by incubation for 30 min. The sample was then mixed with 5 volumes of a stop solution containing 90% (v/v) formamide, 10% (v/v) water, 50 mM EDTA, and 0.005% (w/v) bromophenol blue, 0.005% (w/v) xylene cyanol. The reactions with GSH or spermine were performed under the same conditions with the addition of 1 mM GSH and 1.2 mM spermine. Reaction products were separated by the 8% SDS-PAGE stacking on 18% urea-TBE-PAGE containing 90 mM Tris, 90 mM boric acid, and 2 mM EDTA, running in 0.5X SDS-glycine (12.5 mM Tris, 96 mM glycine, 0.5% SDS) and 0.5X TBE (45 mM Tris, 45 mM boric acid, and 1 mM EDTA) mixed buffer at 150 V.
