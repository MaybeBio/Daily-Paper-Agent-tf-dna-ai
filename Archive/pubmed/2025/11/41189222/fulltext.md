# Identification of biomarkers associated with SUMO modification in heart failure


## Abstract
  Several studies have found that Small Ubiquitin-related Modifier (SUMO) exert therapeutic effects in heart failure (HF) by affecting different pathways, but the mechanisms of SUMO regulation in HF were unclear. The single sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) and weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) were utilized to explore SUMO modification score-related gene modules, which were crossed with differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in HF to obtain candidate genes. Next, the key genes associated with SUMOylation were identified by machine learning algorithms such as least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) and SVM-RFE. In addition, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and expression validation were used to further confirm the importance of key genes. Furthermore, biomarker-related functions and regulatory networks were explored. The Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD) database was used to predict biomarker-related diseases and potential therapeutic agents. DEGs were intersected with SUMO modification-related genes score-related genes obtained from WGCNA (WGCNA-SUMO modification-related genes) to screen 83 candidate genes. Machine learning algorithms were further utilized to finally screen 8 key genes as potential HF biomarkers. ROC curve analysis and external validation confirmed that PSME4 and CYLD exhibited significantly differential expression between HF patients and healthy controls, demonstrating excellent predictive performance for disease diagnosis. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) analysis revealed that PSME4 and CYLD may be involved in biological processes such as the cell cycle and lysosomal function. Moreover, a lncRNA-miRNA-biomarker regulatory network and a transcription factors (TFs)-biomarker-miRNA regulatory network were constructed in HF. Disease prediction analysis showed that PSME4 and CYLD might be associated with other diseases, such as drug-induced liver injury. Subsequently, the results of drug prediction showed that CR845 might target CYLD in HF. PSME4 and CYLD were identified as SUMOylation-related biomarkers in HF, offering novel mechanism insights into the pathological underpinnings of HF.


## 1. Introduction
  Heart failure (HF) is one of the leading causes of death worldwide, whose main characteristics are hypoperfusion and congestion symptoms.[1] HF usually becomes the end-stage of many heart diseases, such as coronary heart disease, myocarditis, and valvular heart disease, with complex mechanisms.[2] The latest clinical guidelines incorporate novel pharmacologic interventions to improve the treatment options, such as SGLT-2 inhibitors (SGLT-2i) and vericiguat.[3,4] However, the majority of HF patients are still faced with many real problems, like repeated hospitalizations and a significant decrease in life quality. IF we explore the new genes and potential pathogenesis associated with HF, which may provide targets for clinical diagnosis and therapy.[5]

  Small ubiquitin-like modifiers (SUMO) are widely found in eukaryotic cells.[6] SUMO modification (SUMOylation) is widely involved in life activities, as a posttranslational modifier, which plays a critical role in nuclear progression, such as gene expression, DNA damage reaction, and cell cycle.[7] SUMOylation is a key regulatory mechanism that influences many biological functions, whose disorder is associated with various diseases, such as nervous system diseases and cardiovascular diseases.[8,9] Many previous studies found that SUMOylation may have a therapeutic function in hypertension, but its regulatory pathogenesis is still unclear.[10] Therefore, investigating the mechanism of SUMOylation in HF is critical for developing novel therapeutic measures for HF.

  This study utilized HF-related transcriptome sequencing data obtained with from the GEO database. Through a series of bioinformatics methods, the key SUMO-related genes in HF were identified. In addition, the mechanisms of action of these key genes in HF were further explored through enrichment analysis and drug prediction analysis. Investigating the role of SUMOylation in HF not only offers novel insights into potential biomarkers but also established a theoretical foundation for the development of targeted therapeutic strategies.


## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1. Data source
  Transcriptome sequencing data for HF were obtained from the Gene Expression Ontology database (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Specially, the GSE59867 dataset was used as the training set, while the GSE9128 and GSE116250 datasets were used as the validation sets. The details of the dataset are shown in Table 1. Meanwhile, a set of 187 SUMO modification-related genes (SRGs) was downloaded from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, http://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/).


### 2.2. Identification of SRGs score-related genes
  The SRGs enrichment scores for each sample in the training set were calculated based on SRGs using the single sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) and tested for significance between the HF group and the control group utilizing the Wilcoxon rank sum test (P < .05). Afterwards, we searched for SRGs score-related genes by weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) (WGCNA-SRGs) in the training set. First, the median absolute deviation was calculated for each gene, and genes with median absolute deviation values in the bottom 50% were removed. Ineligible genes and samples were further filtered using the goodSamplesGenes function. Next, an optimal soft threshold was determined to ensure that the interactions between genes maximally conformed to the scale-free distribution. Genes were then clustered into distinct modules using the hybrid dynamic tree cutting algorithm, with parameters set to a minimum module size of 200 genes and deepSplit = 0.5. Finally, the correlations between the modules and the SRGs scores were assessed using Pearson correlation analysis, and the module with the greatest correlation coefficient was selected for further research.


### 2.3. Identification and functional analysis of candidate genes
  Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between HF and control samples in the training dataset were recognized using the R package limma (v3.54.0)[11] at P < .05 and |log2 fold change (FC)| > 0.5. DEGs and WGCNA-SRGs were defined as candidate genes after overlapping. Functional enrichment analysis of candidate genes was performed by ClueGO and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG). We also built a protein–protein interaction network for the candidate genes to probe their interactions at the protein level.


### 2.4. Identification and disease prediction value analysis of key genes
  We implemented the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression and support vector machine recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) algorithm to construct diagnostic models and obtain the key genes. Key genes were selected based on the area under the curve (AUC) values across both training and validation set, followed by comprehensive expression levels in the training set. Subsequently, we constructed a nomogram to visualize the best diagnostic model, with calibration curve and decision curve employed to determine its predictive accuracy and clinical applicability. Incidentally, the predictive potential of the nomogram was evaluated by the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves of the training set.


### 2.5. Intracellular localization and GSEA analysis of key genes
  The chromosomal locations of key genes were mapped using the R package RCircos (v1.2.2)[12] based on annotated genomic coordinates, while subcellular localization was analyzed via GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/). To explore the molecular mechanisms of model genes in regulating HF, GSEA was performed by downloading c2.cp.kegg.v2023.1.Hs.symbols from the MSigDB database as reference gene sets. The KEGG pathway with an adjusted P value <0.05 were considered statistically significant.


### 2.6. Construction of regulatory network
  To gain a deeper understanding of the regulatory mechanisms of biomarker expression, the R package multiMiR (v1.20.0)[13] was used to investigate the lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA networks of biomarkers through the miRDB database (https://mirdb.org/), the PicTar database (https://dorina.mdc-berlin.de/) and the starbase database (https://rnasysu.com/encori/). We also used the miRNet database (https://www.mirnet.ca/) to retrieve transcription factors (TFs) targeting biomarkers. Next, the biomarkers-associated TFs and corresponding miRNA were collected for constructing a TF-mRNA-miRNA network. Therefore, we constructed an interaction network using GeneMANIA (https://genemania.org/) to find the key genes-related genes.


### 2.7. Disease prediction and drug prediction
  To assess the potential role of key genes in other diseases, we performed disease association analysis using the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD, https://ctdbase.org/). In addition, based on the drug–gene interaction data from the Synapse database (https://synapse.zhihuiya.com/), we screened potential therapeutic agents associated with the key genes with the screening criterion of InferenceCount > 2.


### 2.8. qRT-PCR validation of the key genes
  Total RNA was extracted from HF patients and healthy controls blood samples using the RNA isolater MolPure® Blood RNA Kit (YEASEN, Cat. 19241ES50, China). Subsequently, cDNA synthesis was performed using the Hifair®Ⅲ 1st strand cDNA synthesis supermix according to the manufactures protocol. qPCR amplification was then conducted to specifically detect PSME4 and CYLD expression. The PCR conditions included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 seconds, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 20 seconds, 55 °C for 20 seconds, and 72 °C for 20 seconds. The sequences of the primers are listed in Table 1. Gene expression relative to GAPDH expression was determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method.


### 2.9. Statistical analysis
  Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (v4.2.2), and network visualizations were generated using Cytoscape (v3.9.1). For comparison of continuous variables between groups, we employed the Student t test for normally distributed data, and the Mann–Whitney U-test (Wilcoxon test) for non-normally distributed data. A two-tailed P < .05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise stated.


## 3. Results

### 3.1. Identification and functional characterization of candidate genes in HF
  To investigate the correlation of our datasets with SUMOylation, we first accessed the enrichment score between samples and SRGs in the training set. The results suggested that SRGs score was significantly different with HF samples (Fig. 1A). After determining the availability of the dataset, we explored the gene modules of coordinated expression using WGCNA. Firstly, WGCNA identified an optimal soft threshold of 11, with the blue module was maximally positively correlated with the SRGs scores among the final 11 modules obtained, of which 2202 WGCNA-SRGs (Fig. 1B–F). Subsequently, intersection analysis revealed 83 candidate genes common to both the WGCNA-SRGs and 540 DEGs from GSE9128 dataset (Fig. 2A–C). To further investigate the functions of these candidate genes, the functional enrichment analyses results suggesting that they were mainly associated with immune response, DNA replication, and Ubiquitin mediated proteolysis (Fig. 2D and E). In addition, the protein–protein interaction network composed of 83 candidate genes included 42 nodes and 38 edges, among which the gene for KMT5B was aliased to SUV420H1 and the gene for RESF1 was aliased to KIAA1551 (Fig. 2F).


### 3.2. Two genes (PMSE4 and CYLD) were identified as biomarkers
  We used the LASSO regression analysis and SVM-RFE algorithm to further select the key genes in HF (Fig. 3A–C). Through the 2 analytical methods, we identified 8 candidate genes: MRPS14, SEC23IP, PSME4, BET1, STAP1, SC5D, MGAT4A, and CYLD (Fig. 3D). Subsequent validation via ROC analysis revealed that only PSME4 and CYLD exhibited AUC values > 0.7 in both the training and validation sets (Fig. 3E). Meanwhile, a secondary validation using another external validation set GSE116250 further supported this result. In addition, expression analysis of these 2 genes confirmed consistent trends in training and validation sets, supporting their potential as reliable biomarkers (Fig. 3F). Subsequently, to better visualize the diagnostic model, a nomogram was plotted to infer the probability of HF in clinical patients, where the higher the total score, the higher the risk of HF (Fig. 4A). Further analysis of the calibration and decision curves showed that the predictions were in good agreement with the actual observations (Fig. 4B and C). The results of the ROC curve of the nomogram model also showed that it was a good predictor of disease occurrence (AUC = 0.788, Fig. 3D).


### 3.3. Characterization of the biomarkers in HF
  Further characterization of the biomarkers revealed distinct chromosome and subcellular localization. Figure 5A shows that PSME4 is located on chromosome 2, and CYLD is located on chromosome 16. Chromosomes 2 and 16 play critical roles in physiological functions and developmental processes, our results further validated that PSME4 and CYLD play important roles in HF. In addition, the subcellular localization results implied that PSME4 was mainly distributed in the cytoplasm and nucleus, while CYLD was mainly distributed in the cytoplasm and cytoskeleton (Fig. 5B), these results implied that the biomarkers may regulated the development of HF through influent functions of these locations. GSEA was applied to analyze the function of each model gene. Specifically, PSME4 and CYLD were jointly involved in several processes such as spliceosome and lysosome (Fig. 5C and D). In addition, PSME4 was related to the cell cycle and CYLD to ribosomes.


### 3.4. The regulatory networks of biomarkers
  To further investigate the potential mechanism of PSME4 and CYLD, we predicted their target TFs, miRNAs, and lncRNAs. A total of 21 miRNAs predicted by both databases predicted 4 lncRNAs, and the regulatory network indicated that CYLD could regulate AC087477.2, NEAT1, and XIST through hsa-miR-142-5P (Fig. 6A). There were 48 TFs associated with biomarkers, of which NANOG, SOX2, PRDM14, and TBX5 were co-enriched by both (Fig. 6B). The TF-biomarker-miRNA regulatory network had 70 nodes and 72 edges (Fig. 6C). Moreover, the GeneMANIA database yielded 20 genes associated with biomarkers. These genes were somewhat more closely linked to cellular responses to tumor necrosis factor, regulation of cytokine-mediated signaling pathways, and other pathways (Fig. 6D).


### 3.5. Disease prediction and drug prediction of biomarkers
  Potential therapeutic targets and associated diseases for HF were screened through the Synapse and CTD databases. Disease association analysis revealed significant correlations between PSME4 and chemical/drug-induced liver injury and prenatal exposure delayed effects diseases, while CYLD was more strongly linked to chemical/drug-induced liver injury disease (Fig. 7A and B). Subsequently, 3 approved drugs, including difelikefalin acetate (CR845), pembrolizumab, and nivolumab, as potential targeted therapeutic agents for CYLD, while PSME was nonpredicted potential targeted therapeutic agents. In detail, CR845 is a peripherally-restricted kappa opioid receptor (KOR) agonist whose primary therapeutic effects include analgesic, anti-inflammatory and antipruritic.[14] Separately, pembrolizumab[15] and nivolumab,[16] are programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) inhibitors that belong to the immune checkpoint inhibitors class of drugs. These agents are primary used to treat various malignancies, including melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer, and renal cell carcinoma.[17]


### 3.6. qPCR validation
  Previous bioinformatics analyses identified PSME4 and CYLD as biomarkers in HF. To further validate these findings, we compared their mRNA expression levels in HF patients and healthy controls. Quantitative analysis revealed that CYLD expression was significantly downregulated in the HF group compared to controls (*P < .05, Fig. 8A), In contrast, PSME4 expression showed no statistically significant difference between the 2 groups (Fig. 8B). These results corroborate the reliability of our bioinformatics approaches and underscore its potential utility in biomarker discovery for HF.


## 4. Discussion
  HF is a chronic progressive disease with high morbidity and mortality. It has become the leading cause of death globally and an increasingly serious public health problem. SUMOylation modification has been found to play a certain regulatory role in the treatment of HF.[18] In the current study, we identified 8 key genes, and ultimately selected PSME4 and CYLD as biomarkers for further analysis due to their strong predictive value. Subsequently, we investigated the biological processes and regulatory mechanisms of these 2 key genes in HF using comprehensive bioinformatics approaches.

  This study found that PSME4 and CYLD are key genes in the occurrence and development of HF, and they may become good indicators for predicting HF. PSME4, located on chromosome 2, is expressed in various tissues of the human body. PSME4 protein is mainly involved in the process of proteasome activation. PSME4, through degrading damaged proteins and interacting with other proteins, helps cells maintain balance and regulate the cell cycle.[19] Additionally, PSME4 plays a critical role in promoting the mesenchymal stem cells differentiated cardiomyocytes, demonstrating that PSME4 is a target to provide new therapeutic measures for cardiovascular diseases.[20] CYLD is a tumor suppressor located on chromosome 16, which encodes a 56 kb CYLD protein, whom a member of the deubiquitinating enzyme family.[21] CYLD removes ubiquitin molecules from proteins to regulate multiple cellular signaling pathways, thereby influencing cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, immune responses, and inflammatory processes.[22] A previous study suggested that the interaction between VPO1 (myeloperoxidase) and CYLD enhances the ubiquitination and degradation of RIPK1, thereby activating RIPK3 and MLKL in downstream.[23] This cascade response will cause the programmed necrosis of cardiomyocytes, due to CYLD becoming the potential therapeutic target for HF.

  The GSEA results found that PSME4 and CYLD jointly participate in multiple processes such as the spliceosome and lysosome. In addition, PSME4 is associated with the cell cycle, while CYLD is related to the ribosome. Small introns regulated by spliceosome activity could regulate the expression and functions of voltage-dependent ion channels in cardiomyocytes, which play a critical role in the development of HF.[24] A previous study demonstrated that the editing process plays a different role in the development of ischemic cardiomyopathy and dilated cardiomyopathy.[25] Lysosomes are integral in the removal process of mitochondria, which is critical for preserving the functions of the heart.[26] CYLD inhibits autolysosome efflux in cardiomyocytes, to involves a part mechanisms of heart pathological remodeling and heart dysfunction.[27] Despite the majority of research having focused on tumor cells of PSME4 and cell cycle regulation, accumulating evidence indicates that cell cycle dysregulation is associated with HF pathogenesis.[28,29] Clinical trial results demonstrated that PSME4 gene therapy, through regulating the cell cycle, improves the heart function of HF animal models.[30] The regenerative capacity of the heart is limited after myocardial infarction. Cell cycle regulation can improve the forced proliferation of a part of cardiomyocytes, thereby improving the heart functions of myocardial infarction mice. Xu et al[31] demonstrated that treatment of cardiomyocytes with cell cycle factors establishes the requisite energetic and biosynthetic foundation for cellular proliferation. This intervention promotes cardiomyocytes proliferation and cardiac repair, thereby exerting a positive impact on HF. The abnormality of the cell cycle pathway promotes the deterioration of HF.[32] CYLD may affect the synthesis of specific immune-related proteins or inflammatory mediators by ribosomes through the regulation of NF-κB and other related signaling pathways, thereby indirectly influencing the function or activity of ribosomes and further improving the myocardial hypertrophy response.[33] Ribosome-related signaling pathways, including the mTOR-p70S6K axis, are implicated in cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis.[34] The ribosome-associated lncRNA CARDINAL modulates stress-induced myocardial hypertrophy, with its overexpression alleviating hypertrophic responses.[35]

  The regulatory network of biomarkers indicates that CYLD can regulate 3 lncRNAs, namely AC087477.2, NEAT1 and XIST, through the microRNA hsa-miR-142-5P, suggesting their potential roles in HF pathogenesis. During the development of HF, immune imbalance and chronic inflammation are important pathophysiological mechanisms. Has-miR-142-5p may affect the production of inflammatory factors and the activity of immune cells, such as T cell and macrophages, thereby influence the progression of HF.[36,37] The NEAT1 expression is upregulated in serum exosomes of acute ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction patients, which is associated with myocardial ischemic necrosis, and may become a diagnostic biomarker for HF.[38] NEAT1 inhibited the expression of Smad7 by recruiting EZH2 to promote cardiac fibrosis and function disorder.[39] In acute myocardial infarction, lncRNA NEAT1, through the miR-450-5p/ACSL4 pathway, mediates ferroptosis, and silencing NEAT1 reduces myocardial ischemic injury.[40] A study found that the expression of lncRNA XIST was upregulated in CVB3-infected viral myocarditis (VMC) mouse models and Treg cells. XIST targets miR-195-5p, induces caspase-1 expression, promotes the secretion of inflammatory factors, and inhibits the secretion of anti-inflammatory factors, to improve pyroptosis of Treg cells.[41] In an ISO-induced hypertrophy model, XIST upregulation is associated with increased apoptosis and myocardial enzymes, whereas inhibited XIST could reverse this influence.[42] Currently, there is no detailed research on the mechanism of AC087477.2 in cardiovascular diseases. As a lncRNA AC087477.2 potentially promotes or inhibits the transcription of heart-failure-related genes at the transcriptional level. Additionally, it may function as a ceRNA and regulate the pathogenesis and progression of cardiovascular diseases by binding to miRNAs.

  TFs are proteins that can bind to specific DNA sequences to regulate gene expression. Multiple reports have been published regarding the research on the role of TFs in the pathogenesis of HF. In this study, we predicted potential TFs for PSME4 and CYLD and constructed a TF-mRNA regulatory network. Our analysis revealed 19 TFs potentially regulating PSME4 and 32 TFs potentially regulating CYLD. Among these, NANOG, SOX2, PRDM14, and TBX5 were co-enriched in these 2 biomarkers. A previous study suggests that SOX2-OT could cause the deterioration of cardiac function in the rat model.[43] Inhibiting the expression of SOX2-OT could inhibit the expression of NLRP3, ASC, caspase-1, IL-1β, and TGF-1β, and the production of reactive oxygen species, thereby reducing myocardial necrosis and fibrosis and promoting the prognosis of HF.[43,44] PRDM14 is critical in embryonic stem cell self-renewal and trophoblast stem cell formation, which may promote myocardial repair. Through the regulated Socs3-Stat3 signaling axis, PRDM14 supports stem cell renewal, demonstrating that it may become the therapeutic target for HF treatment.[45,46] TBX5 genetic mutation leads to the occurrence of multiple heart diseases, such as HF, arrhythmias, and congenital heart defects.[47] A previous study showed that the absence of TBX5 in mouse ventricles could cause faint cardiac dysfunction and arrhythmia, suggesting that TBX5 has cardioprotective effects.[48] Despite the studies are limited of NANOG in HF, its role in early embryonic development and stem cell maintenance demonstrates its potential influence for cardiac cell differentiation and regeneration.[49,50] The dysregulated expression of NANOG may affect the normal formation and differentiation of cardiomyocytes in heart development progression, thereby increasing the potential risk of HF in adulthood.[51] Regulating factors like NANOG may promote stem cells to differentiate into cardiomyocytes, thereby promoting myocardial regeneration and repair, thus having a therapeutic meaning for HF.[52] These reports collectively emphasize the critical roles of TBX5, SOX2-OT, NANOG, and PRDM14 in HF pathogenesis and highlight their potential as targets for therapeutic intervention.

  Computational drug prediction using the Synapse database identified 3 FDA-approved drugs targeting CYLD: difelikefalin acetate (CR845), pembrolizumab, and nivolumab. CR845 is a selective κ-opioid receptor (KOR) agonist. KOR agonist is particularly helpful for HF treatment.[53] Currently, the clinical applications of CR845 are limited to uremic pruritus management, but clinical evidence shows its cardioprotective potential by multiple mechanisms, such as alleviating mitochondrial dysfunction, regulating myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury, and promoting diuretic efficacy without causing electrolyte imbalance.[54,55] On the contrary, pembrolizumab and nivolumab are excluded from consideration because of their cardiotoxicity characteristics, including risks of myocarditis and pericarditis.[56,57] These evidences suggest that CR845 is an available candidate drug for HF therapy, and highlights the importance of safe assessment in drug repurposing strategies.

  This study first comprehensively identified the SUMOylation-related biomarkers (PSME4 and CYLD) in HF using a series of bioinformatics approaches, indicating their potential functions in disease mechanisms, and providing novel diagnostic and therapeutic targets. While our computational approach provides robust predictions of regulatory networks and drug interactions, these findings require experimental validation through in vitro and in vivo studies, including functional characterization of SUMOylation-mediated regulation of these biomarkers and detailed pathway analysis using proteomics and posttranslational modification approaches to fully elucidate their mechanistic contributions to HF progression. It should be clearly stated that the main limitation in our study is the excessively small sample size of the validation set (GSE9128). The relatively small sample size of this validation set may reduce the statistical power of the tests. Furthermore, small samples are more susceptible to the influence of individual heterogeneity, which could limit the generalizability of the results.[57]


## 5. Conclusion
  In conclusion, this study comprehensively investigated the role of SUMOylation-related genes in HF. We identified 2 SUMOylation-related key genes, namely PSME4 and CYLD, which are the novel biomarkers for HF. These biomarkers are associated with pivotal biological progression, such as immune reaction, protein degradation, and organelle functions. Our findings provide novel insight for realizing the relationship between SUMOylation and HF pathogenesis, which helps to further research in HF.
