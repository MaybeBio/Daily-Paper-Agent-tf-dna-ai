# Qingre Huoxue decoction and its active compound Quercetin modulate bone remodeling by inhibiting ATF4 hyperactivation to attenuate bone erosion in rheumatoid arthritis


## Background
  Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is characterized by synovial inflammation and bone erosion driven by osteoblast-osteoclast imbalance. Qingre Huoxue decoction (QRHXD), a traditional Chinese medicine, alleviates RA symptoms, yet its mechanism in mitigating bone erosion remains unclear.


## Methods
  Network pharmacology identified active compounds and targets of QRHXD. Collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) rats were treated with QRHXD or Methotrexate. Arthritis severity (HE staining), bone microstructure (X-ray micro-CT), osteoblast/osteoclast markers (RT-qPCR, TRAP staining), and ATF4 expression (Western blot, immunofluorescence staining) were assessed. Primary osteoblasts from CIA rats were treated with QRHXD or its active compound to validate ATF4 modulation.


## Results
  QRHXD reduced joint swelling, arthritis scores, and bone loss in CIA rats, restoring trabecular parameters (Tb.N, Tb.Th, BV/TV) and suppressing bone resorption markers (TRAP, CTX-1, RANKL). Moreover, the number of osteoclast was significantly reduced. Protein interaction analysis highlighted ATF4 as a core regulator of bone remodeling and potentially regulated by the active compound Quercetin in QRHXD. Both QRHXD and Quercetin inhibited ATF4 hyperactivation in CIA osteoblasts, rebalancing RUNX2 and RANKL expression, with Quercetin showing greater efficacy. Molecular docking further exhibited strong binding affinity between Quercetin and the ATF4-regulating transcription factors RAD21, CBFB and HDAC2.


## Conclusion
  QRHXD attenuates bone erosion in RA by inhibiting ATF4 hyperactivation via Quercetin, restoring osteoblast-osteoclast crosstalk.

## Introduction
  Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune polyarticular arthritis characterized by symmetrical and progressive joint swelling and invasive joint destruction, primarily affecting peripheral joints. The global incidence of RA ranges from 0.5 to 1%, with a higher prevalence among women. In addition to chronic synovial inflammation and pannus formation, bone and cartilage damage play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of RA [1]. While treatment can maintain low disease activity, achieving stringent or sustained remission and reversing bone destruction remain challenging. Therefore, it should pay close attention to preserving continuous relief and delaying bone erosion.

  Physiologically, bone remodeling maintains the dynamic equilibrium and homeostasis of bones [2]. However, bone remodeling in RA tends to be disrupted, leading to an imbalance between bone formation by osteoblasts with bone resorption by osteoclasts [3]. Consequently, three distinct bone alterations occur in RA: localized bone erosion at the interface between the pannus and bone, loss of periarticular bone in adjacent joints, and systemic osteopenia. Osteoclasts are the sole cell responsible for degrading and resorbing bone tissue. It has been identified in both RA patients and animal models of arthritis [4]. However, reducing osteoclasts does not effectively halt bone erosion in RA during treatment [5]. It may be attributed to the inhibition of osteoblast formation in RA [6]. Osteoblasts, derived from undifferentiated mesenchymal cells, not only participate in the formation of osteocytes but also play a crucial role in regulating osteoclasts [7]. For instance, osteoblasts can secrete M-CSF and RANKL to promote osteoclast formation or excrete OPG and WNT16 to inhibit osteoclast activity. In turn, osteoclasts may release TGF-β, which induces osteoblast formation [8]. Therefore, understanding bone remodeling mediated by the balance between osteoblasts and osteoclasts will yield valuable insights and opportunities for addressing bone destruction in RA.

  Qingre Huoxue decoction (QRHXD), an empirical prescription for RA treatment, is intended to clear heat, dispel dampness and invigorate blood circulation. QRHXD consists of 9 major herbal ingredients, including Jin Yin Hua (Lonicera japonica Thunb.), Tu Fu Ling (Smilax glabra Roxb.), Cang Zhu (Atractylodes lancea DC.), Huang Bai (Phellodendron chinense Schneid.), Bai Shao (Paeonia lactiflora Pall.), Dan Shen (Salvia miltiorrhiza Bge.), Qing Feng Teng (Sinomenium acutum Thunb.), Huang Qi (Astragalus mongholicus Bunge.), and E Zhu (Curcuma aeruginosa Roxb.). Previously, we conducted multi-center, double-blind, randomized controlled trials to demonstrate that QRHXD, when used in conjunction with Methotrexate (MTX) and Hydroxychloroquine, not only alleviated active RA activity but also ameliorated the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) 50/70 response rate in a safer manner [9]. This effect was observed over a period of 52 weeks, and radiological progression was delayed [10]. However, the specific molecular mechanism by which QRHXD alleviates bone erosion in RA remains unclear. Therefore, we aimed to validate the therapeutic efficacy of QRHXD in collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) rats, in conjunction with network pharmacology to analyze the key active compounds and their targets of action and to validate these effects on primary osteoblasts.


## Materials and methods

### Network pharmacology analysis
  Active compounds and their known targets of 9 major herbal ingredients in QRHXD, including Jin Yin Hua, Tu Fu Ling, Cang Zhu, Huang Bai, Bai Shao, Dan Shen, Qing Feng Teng, Huang Qi, and E Zhu, were analyzed using the Biofiducial Analysis Tool for Molecular Mechanisms of Traditional Chinese Medicine (BATMAN-TCM) database (http://bionet.ncpsb.org.cn/batman-tcm/). For these targets, disease enrichment analysis was performed using the Therapeutic Target Database (TTD) and Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) database embedded in BATMAN-TCM database, as well as biological process of Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses. Terms with the top 10 gene enrichment numbers and P < 0.05 were displayed, respectively.

  Relevant genes for osteoblast (score > 1) and osteoclast (score > 1) were obtained separately by the PathCards database (https://pathcards.genecards.org/) and analyzed by the Venn diagram interaction with targets of QRHXD. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) was analyzed for the common targets in Venn diagram using the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/). Interaction confidence was set to 0.9 (highest), and only nodes with connections were displayed, while targets associated with ossification, bone mineralization, and osteoblast differentiation were flagged.


### Drug preparation
  Extract of the herbal ingredients of QRHXD was soaked in water for 30 min, heated and decocted for another 30 min. Then, the filtrate was extracted and the filter residue was re-decocted. The second filtrate was mixed with the initial filtrate and filtered through gauze. Afterward, it was concentrated to obtain a liquid with a concentration of 1 g/mL, which was then refrigerated at 4 ℃ for further use. Additionally, the extract was freeze-dried using a freeze-dryer to obtain a lyophilized powder for in vitro research. The reprocessed preparation was standardized as QRHX formula (QRHXF) in subsequent experiments.


### CIA induction and QRHXF treatment
  Animal experiments were conducted in strict accordance with the "Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals". All studies received approval from the Ethics Committee of Guanganmen Hospital, China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences (Beijing, China) (Approval number: IACUC-GAMH-2019-001). After one week of acclimatization under standard conditions (temperature: 24 °C, humidity: 60% relative, 12-h light–dark alternation), 66 male Sprague–Dawley rats at 6 weeks of age, provided by Beijing Huafukang Biotechnology Co (No SCXK-[Jing] 2019-0008), were weighed.

  Rats were divided into 6 groups based on body weight: normal control (12 rats, 3 for osteoblast isolation), CIA model (18 rats, 9 for osteoblast isolation), low dose (8.5 g/kg) of QRHXF (9 rats), medium dose (17 g/kg) of QRHXF (9 rats), high dose (34 g/kg) of QRHXF (9 rats), and MTX (0.2 mg/kg) as positive control (9 rats). CIA rats were induced by injecting with a well-recognized emulsion (a 1:1 mixture of bovine collagen type II (CII) and incomplete Freund's adjuvant) (Chondrex, USA). In normal control group, 0.2 mL of 0.9% normal saline was injected, while all other groups received 0.2 mL of CII subcutaneously at the heel and back. Intensive immunization was performed 7 days after the initial immunization. QRHXF and MTX (Xinyi, China) were administered via gavage at the prescribed doses starting from the first day of modeling and continued for 35 days.


### Arthritis swelling evaluation
  Toe swelling: The volumetric swelling of the hind paw of rats was measured using a paw volume meter, and the mean value was obtained. Arthritis index: The degree of swelling and deformity was assessed by two independent, blinded observers, referring to a previous report [11]. For the wrist or ankle or the mid-foot and mid-forepaw: 0 points, no inflammation; 1 point, slight swelling; 2 points, moderate swelling; 3 points, severe swelling; 4 points, joint distortion with lameness. For the interphalangeal and metacarpophalangeal joints of 4 lateral digits: 0 points, no symptoms; 1 point, redness and swelling. The total score was 80, with higher scores signifying more serious arthritis. Pathological section: The serial sections of rats’ hind limbs were stained with hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining (ServiceBio, China).


### X-ray micro-computed tomography (CT) detection
  After 35 days of the initial immunization, the rats were euthanized, and their hind limbs were dissected and fixed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution. The region of interest (ROI) was determined using a microscope and then scanned by X-ray micro-CT (Skyscan1174) with a resolution of 145 μm and a view size of 1304 × 1024 pixels. Subsequently, three-dimensional (3D) images were reconstructed and analyzed. Relevant bone microstructure parameters were measured, including bone surface (BS), bone volume (BV), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), and structural model index (SMI). Additionally, the ratios of BS/BV and BV/tissue volume (TV) were calculated [12].


### TRAP staining
  Rats’ knee and ankle joints were embedded in paraffin and sliced into 4 μm sections. These sections were then stained with TRAP (Sigma, USA) and observed under a light microscope to visualize the osteoclasts. The total number of osteoclasts was determined by counting the TRAP-positive polymorphonuclear cells observed in 5 fields under a magnification of 40 × .


### Osteoblast isolation and culture
  Under sterile conditions, tibias (3 in the normal control group and 9 in the CIA model group) were dissected, and surrounding muscles, periosteum, and cartilage were meticulously removed using surgical scissors. The diaphysis of the tibia was isolated, and the marrow cavity was flushed with PBS to eliminate residual hematopoietic cells. The cortical bone was then longitudinally split and cut into 1–2 mm3 fragments. Bone fragments were subjected to 3 cycles of vortex agitation (2 min each) in PBS containing 2% penicillin/streptomycin (Beyotime, China) to remove residual soft tissue and marrow cells. The fragments were rinsed until they appeared milky white.

  Bone fragments were transferred to a 15 mL tube containing 5 mL of high-glucose DMEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, USA). Vortexing at maximum speed for 3 cycles (2 min each) was performed to dislodge osteoblasts from the bone matrix. The cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer to remove debris, followed by centrifugation (1000 × g, 10 min) to pellet cells. Pelleted cells were resuspended in osteogenic medium (DMEM + 10% FBS + 50 μg/mL ascorbic acid + 10 mM β-glycerophosphate) and seeded into 6-well plates pre-coated with 0.1% gelatin (Beyotime, China). Plates were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Medium was partially replaced (50%) every 3 days to retain autocrine growth factors.


### Cell viability assay
  Osteoblasts were seeded into 96-well plates at 5 × 103 cells/well, and 100 μL of osteogenic medium was added to culture for 12 h, and then replaced by 100 μL of QRHXF- (0.1, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 4, and 8 mg/mL) and Quercetin- (5, 10, 20, 40, 80, 160, and 320 μM) containing medium, respectively. After culture for 48 h, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent (Beyotime, China) was added to detect the optical density (OD) value of the cells at 450 nm. In addition, the cell viability curves were plotted and the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values were calculated based on the OD values corresponding to the gradient concentrations.


### Reverse-transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) assay
  Bone tissues were collected from tibias, processed into bone powder with liquid nitrogen, added with RIPA (Beyotime, China) lysate (tissue weight:lysate = 1:5), homogenized on ice for 5 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and frozen at − 20 °C for later use. Cells were collected and lysed in RIPA buffer containing broad protease inhibitor (Beyotime, China). RNA extraction was performed by adding Trizol (Invitrogen, USA), followed by centrifugation. The cDNA was synthesized by reverse transcribing the RNA using the Reverse Transcription Kit (Vazyme, China). RT-qPCR analysis was conducted using the SYBR qPCR SuperMix Plus Kit (Novoprotein, China). RT-qPCR steps: Pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 15 s. Relative quantification was performed using the 2−ΔΔCt method. ### Western blot (WB) assay
  The isolated proteins (20 µg) were separated on 12% SDS gels and electrotransferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. Then, incubation for 16 h with primary antibodies against ATF4 (1:1500, ABclonal, China) and ACTB (1:1500, ABclonal, China). The membrane was washed and incubated in horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody. Antibody-bound protein bands were assayed using a chemiluminescent luminol enhancer solution (YEASEN, China). The gel imaging system was used for imaging analysis. Gray values of protein bands were calculated by Image-Pro Plus (v6.0) software.


### Immunofluorescence (IF) staining
  Cells cultured on glass coverslips were fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min at room temperature, followed by permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 (ServiceBio, China) for 10 min. Cells were incubated with 5% BSA in PBS for 1 h to block nonspecific binding. A mixture of rabbit anti-RANKL (1:200, Abcam, USA) and mouse anti-RUNX2 (1:300, Santa Cruz, USA) antibodies in 5% BSA (ServiceBio, China) was applied to the cells and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBS, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated anti-rabbit (1:500, Invitrogen, USA) and Alexa Fluor 405-conjugated anti-mouse (1:500, Invitrogen, USA) antibodies for 1 h in the dark. 1 µg/mL of DAPI (ServiceBio, China) was applied for 10 min. Coverslips were mounted using ProLong Gold Antifade Reagent (Cell Signaling, USA) and air-dried. Fluorescence signals were captured using a widefield fluorescence microscope equipped with DAPI (Ex/Em: 358/461 nm), Cy5 (Ex/Em: 640/670 nm; for Alexa Fluor 405), and TRITC (Ex/Em: 547/572 nm; for Alexa Fluor 594) filters.


### Promoter sequence acquisition and in silico prediction of transcription factor binding sites
  The promoter region of the human ATF4 gene (NCBI Gene ID: 468) was identified and extracted from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Gene database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). The sequence corresponding to the 2 kb region immediately upstream of the annotated transcription start site (TSS) was obtained using the latest human genome reference assembly, GRCh38/hg38, for subsequent analysis.

  To predict which transcription factors regulate ATF4, the extracted promoter sequence was analyzed using a panel of bioinformatics tools. The sequence was independently submitted to the Human Transcription Factor Database (Human TFDB, http://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn/HumanTFDB), the Gene Transcription Regulation Database (GTRD, https://gtrd20-06.bioumL.org/), and hTFtarget (https://guolab.wchscu.cn/hTFtarget/#!/). These databases leverage extensive collections of Chromatin Immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) data and computational models to identify putative transcription factor binding sites (TFBS).

  A comparative analysis was performed to identify the most reliable transcription factor predictions. The lists of TFs predicted to bind the ATF4 promoter from Human TFDB, GTRD, and hTFtarget were intersected. Only those transcription factors that were common to the results of all three independent databases were retained for further investigation, ensuring a stringent and high-confidence selection of potential regulators.


### Molecular docking
  Quercetin was retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/, CAS: 117-39-5), and its 3D structure was generated and energy-minimized using Chem3D software (v20.0.0.41). Then, Quercetin was analyzed for their binding affinities and interaction patterns with the target using AutoDock Vina 1.5.7 software.

  The initial model of CBFB, HDAC2 and Rad21 complex were collected from Protein Data Bank (PDB ID:8CX0, 8BPA and 7W1M, https://www.rcsb.org/). During the docking analysis, all protein and ligand files were converted to PDBQT format, with all water molecules removed and polar hydrogen atoms added. The grid box was centered to cover each protein’s structural domain and to accommodate the free movement of the ligand. The grid box was set to 30 Å × 30 Å × 30 Å, with a grid point spacing of 0.05 nm. The molecular docking study was conducted using Autodock Vina 1.5.7 (http://autodock.scripps.edu/).


### Statistical analysis
  We used SPSS v22.6 for data analysis and Graphpad Prism v8.0 for graphing. If data conformed to normal distribution, the t-test was used for comparing two groups, while one-way ANOVA for multiple groups. If not, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or non-parametric tests were employed. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.


## Results

### Therapeutic potential of QRHXD for bone disease in RA
  In order to understand the pharmacological effects of QRHXD, we constructed a network pharmacology of it through the BATMAN-TCM database. Analysis of 9 major herbal ingredients in QRHXD revealed 287 known active compounds and their 743 targets (Supplementary materials). Figure 1E exhibited characterization information on the top 5 compounds (Resveratrol, Curcumin, Quercetin, Dioscin, and Caffeine) with the broadest pharmacological effects in QRHXD and their 10 known targets. The 743 targets of QRHXD were further analyzed for disease enrichment to reveal their therapeutic directions in human diseases. TTD and OMIM analyses revealed that these targets are mainly enriched in cancers (such as breast, colorectal, prostate, and lung cancers) and cardiovascular diseases (such as diabetes and atherosclerosis), and they are also implicated in RA and osteoporosis (Fig. 1A-B). GO and KEGG analyses revealed that they are involved in the regulation of various biological processes (such as ion binding, signal transduction, kinase, and oxidoreductase activities) and biological pathways (such as neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, PI3K-Akt, CGMP-PKG, and MAPK signaling pathways) (Fig. 1C-D). ### QRHXD ameliorated bone microstructure in CIA rats
  X-ray micro-CT scanning further revealed that the administration of QRHXF had the ability to attenuate bone destruction and alleviate osteoporosis in CIA rats. Compared to normal rats, the model rats displayed smaller joints, narrowed joint gaps in the ankle, interphalangeal, and knee, severe periarticular bone destruction, and bone loss in the tibial epiphysis. Conversely, QRHXD treatment showed a dose-dependent effect in mitigating local joint destruction, with the most pronounced and effective results observed at a dose of 34 g/kg, surpassing the effects of MTX (Fig. 3A-B). Treatment with different doses of QRHXF and MTX resulted in decreased values for BS, BS/BV, SMI, and Tb.Sp, as well as increased values for Tb.N, Tb.Th, and BV/TV (Fig. 3C). ### QRHXD remodeled osteogenic-osteoclastic environment in CIA rats

### QRHXD inhibited ATF4 hyperactivation in bone environment of ICA rats

Based on this finding, we are interested in searching for the key regulatory molecules of QRHXD that mediate the amelioration of bone erosion. ### Quercetin exerted a major anti-ATF4 hyperactivation effect in osteoblasts
  To clarify the anti-ATF4 component in QRHXD, we reviewed the results of network pharmacological analyses and found that Quercetin may be the key compound at work (Fig. 1E, Supplementary materials). This was further validated by isolating osteoblasts from tibial tissues of normal and CIA rats. Cell viability assay showed that QRHXF and Quercetin were less toxic to normal osteoblasts with IC50 values of 2.5 mg/mL and 149.6 μM, respectively (Fig. 6A-B). Based on the IC50 values, the maximum safe dose (dose at 80% cell viability) for administration of QRHXF and Quercetin was set at 2 mg/mL and 120 μM, respectively. In vitro experiments revealed that CIA rat-derived osteoblasts highly expressed ATF4 compared to normal rat-derived osteoblasts (Fig. 6C-D). Additionally, both QRHXF and Quercetin down-regulated ATF4 in CIA rat-derived osteoblasts, and the inhibitory effect of Quercetin was more effective (Fig. 6C-D).Fig. 6Effects of QRHXD and Quercetin on ATF4, RUNX2, and RANKL expression in osteoblasts.   In ATF4-mediated bone turnover homeostasis, bone formation is promoted by upregulating BALP and PINP through RUNX2 activation in osteoblasts, while bone resorption is exacerbated by stimulating RANKL secretion from osteoblasts to activate osteoclast differentiation signals TRAP and CTX-1 [13]. Our study found that ATF4 was abnormally expressed in bone environment of CIA rats, indicating an imbalance of bone turnover with predominant bone resorption. Therefore, the expression imbalance between RUNX2 and RANKL, which are key molecules in osteoblasts to regulate bone formation and bone resorption separately, may be the main cause of bone erosion. IF staining further validated our hypothesis that model osteoblasts significantly overexpressed RANKL and slightly decreased RUNX2 expression compared to normal osteoblasts (Fig. 6E). Moreover, QRHXF and Quercetin reversed this phenomenon with better inhibition of RANKL by Quercetin (Fig. 6E). These results suggest that Quercetin is the main active compound in QRHXD that exerts anti-ATF4 effect. QRHXD and Quercetin stabilize the RUNX2/RANKL expression balance by inhibiting ATF4 hyperactivation, resulting in amelioration of bone erosion in RA.


### Quercetin potentially affected ATF4 expression by regulating RAD21, CBFB or HDAC2
  Based on previous evidence demonstrating that Quercetin downregulates ATF4 transcription and protein expression, it was hypothesized that ATF4 expression is modulated by Quercetin through transcription factors regulating its promoter. Consequently, cross-database analysis of hTFtarget, GTRD and HumanTFDB identified four potential common protein targets: RAD21, CBFB, HDAC2 and BRD4 (Fig. 7A). To elucidate the interaction mechanisms, molecular docking studies were performed between Quercetin (Fig. 7B) and these targets, excluding BRD4 due to the unavailability of its structural data. The results revealed that Quercetin demonstrated the highest binding affinity for the RAD21 complex, with a calculated binding energy of − 9.1 kcal/mol (Fig. 7C). ## Discussion
  The present study provides compelling evidence that QRHXD, a traditional Chinese herbal formula, alleviates bone erosion in CIA rats by restoring osteoblast-osteoclast crosstalk through inhibition of ATF4 hyperactivation. Our findings not only validate the therapeutic potential of QRHXD in RA-associated bone destruction but also identify Quercetin as its key active compound mediating these effects. These findings are informative in the context of RA pathophysiology and bone remodeling regulation.

  The observed overexpression of ATF4 in CIA rat bones aligns with emerging evidence that sustained activation of this transcription factor disrupts bone remodeling homeostasis in chronic inflammatory conditions [14]. ATF4 regulates both osteoblast differentiation through RUNX2 activation and osteoclastogenesis via RANKL induction [15]. Our data demonstrate that CIA-induced ATF4 hyperactivation creates a pathological imbalance, with excessive RANKL-driven osteoclast activity overwhelming RUNX2-mediated bone formation. This dual regulatory role explains why previous strategies targeting osteoclasts alone showed limited efficacy in halting RA bone erosion [16, 17]. The normalization of RUNX2/RANKL expression ratios following QRHXD treatment suggests that modulating ATF4 activity could simultaneously address both aspects of bone remodeling imbalance. Notably, the association between ATF4 hyperactivation and RA severity further supports its clinical relevance as a therapeutic target [18]. Recent single-cell RNA sequencing studies have also identified ATF4 as a key driver of bone loss, linking its overexpression to impaired bone repair capacity [19].

  Network pharmacology and subsequent validation experiments identified Quercetin as QRHXD's primary bioactive compound targeting ATF4. This flavonoid has previously demonstrated anti-osteoporotic effects through Wnt/β-catenin pathway modulation, but its role in inflammatory bone erosion remains underexplored [20]. The superior ATF4 inhibitory activity of Quercetin compared to the whole QRHXD extract suggests potential synergies between Quercetin and other components in the formulation. Importantly, Quercetin's ability to reduce RANKL expression while preserving RUNX2 activity addresses a critical limitation of current RA therapies that suppress bone resorption at the expense of bone formation [21]. The dose-dependent restoration of PINP/CTX-1 ratios further confirms Quercetin's dual regulatory capacity, mirroring findings from an in vitro study using osteoblast-osteoclast co-culture systems [22]. Kim HR et al. demonstrated that Quercetin suppresses osteoclastogenesis via inhibition of IL-17 signaling, providing additional mechanistic insights relevant to RA pathology [23].

  While Quercetin emerges as a primary active compound, the formula's clinical efficacy likely stems from synergistic interactions among its components. Several QRHXD constituents including Salvianolic acid B from Dan Shen and Pachymic acid from Tu Fu Ling, have demonstrated independent anti-arthritic properties through NF-κB inhibition and oxidative stress reduction [24, 25]. This multi-target approach may explain why high-dose QRHXD outperformed MTX in improving trabecular parameters, as MTX primarily targets inflammatory pathways without direct effects on bone remodeling [26]. The observed preservation of articular architecture suggests additional chondroprotective effects potentially mediated by Bai Shao-derived Paeoniflorin, which was recently shown to inhibit inflammation of RA fibroblast-like synoviocytes by downregulating hsa_circ_009012 [27]. Furthermore, Huang Qi-derived Astragaloside IV has been reported to modulate NGF-induced osteoblast differentiation via the GSK-3β/β-catenin signaling pathway, potentially contributing to the improved BV/TV ratios observed in treated CIA rats [28].

  Additionally, X-ray micro-CT findings reveal QRHXD's ability to restore 3D bone microstructure, particularly through increased trabecular number and thickness. These structural improvements correlate with reduced SMI values, indicating a transition from rod-like to plate-like trabeculae, and decreased Tb.Sp, reflecting enhanced trabecular connectivity. Such microarchitectural preservation is clinically significant given that trabecular bone loss precedes cortical erosion in RA progression [29, 30]. The simultaneous reduction in both systemic (CTX-1) and local (TRAP+ cells) bone resorption markers suggests QRHXD acts on both circulating osteoclast precursors and mature joint-resident osteoclasts. This dual mechanism aligns with recent insights into RA bone pathology, where synovial inflammation creates a self-perpetuating cycle of osteoclast activation and osteoblast suppression [31, 32].

  Our molecular docking studies successfully identified RAD21, CBFB and HDAC2 as high-affinity binding targets for Quercetin. These findings suggest a direct molecular mechanism through which Quercetin may exert its previously observed downregulatory effect on ATF4 expression. Crucially, RAD21 (a cohesin subunit), CBFB (a core-binding factor subunit) and HDAC2 (a histone deacetylase) are not merely passive binding partners; they are pivotal regulators of chromatin architecture, gene transcription, and epigenetic modifications. The effective binding of Quercetin to these proteins implies its potential to interfere with their normal functions. Dysregulation of these specific targets is strongly implicated in the pathogenesis of several major diseases. For instance, aberrant RAD21 expression and cohesin dysfunction are frequently associated with genomic instability in various cancers [33]. CBFB, particularly through its interaction with RUNX proteins, plays critical roles in hematopoiesis, and its dysregulation, such as through chromosomal translocations (e.g., RUNX1-RUNX1T1 in AML), is a well-established driver in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [34, 35]. HDAC2 overexpression is commonly observed in cancers; silencing HDAC2 can aid in suppressing the tumor microenvironment [36]. and has also been linked to cognitive decline in neurodegenerative conditions like Alzheimer's disease by repressing synaptic plasticity-related genes [37]. Collectively, by directly binding and potentially modulating key chromatin regulators RAD21, CBFB, and HDAC2, Quercetin provides a molecular basis for inhibiting ATF4 hyperactivation.

  Our findings build upon previous clinical trials demonstrating QRHXD's safety and efficacy in RA management. The identification of ATF4 as a molecular target provides a mechanistic basis for these clinical observations, particularly the delayed radiological progression reported in long-term follow-up studies. However, several questions warrant further investigation. First, the pharmacokinetic profile of Quercetin in arthritic joints needs clarification, while our in vitro data suggest direct osteoblast modulation, systemic anti-inflammatory effects may contribute to the observed bone protection. Second, the temporal relationship between ATF4 inhibition and clinical improvement requires longitudinal analysis. Finally, comparative studies with biologic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) could establish QRHXD's position in the RA treatment algorithm, particularly for patients with rapid radiographic progression.
