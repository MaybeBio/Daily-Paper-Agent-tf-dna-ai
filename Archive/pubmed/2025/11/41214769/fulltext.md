# Lactylation modulates immune infiltration in sepsis-induced acute respiratory distress syndrome: a multi-omics and machine learning study with experimental confirmation


## Objectives
  To investigate lactylation-driven mechanisms in the pathogenesis of sepsis-induced acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).


## Methods
  Multi-cohort transcriptomic data sets (GSE10474, GSE32707, and GSE66890) were integrated with machine learning algorithms (LASSO, support vector machine, random forest) to identify differentially expressed lactylation-related genes (LRGs). Five hub genes (ALDH1A1, CALM1, CCNA2, HIST1H2BN, SH3GL1) were prioritized. Orthogonal experimental validation was performed using qRT-PCR and Western blotting. Subsequent analyses explored immune cell correlations (focusing on ALDH1A1), regulatory networks (transcription factors and miRNAs), and potential therapeutic drug candidates.


## Results
  Integration of bioinformatics analyses identified 25 differentially expressed LRGs and prioritized 5 hub genes. Experimental validation (qRT-PCR/Western blot) consistently demonstrated downregulation of all five hub gene proteins. Notably, this contradicted the bioinformatically predicted upregulation of CCNA2, HIST1H2BN, and SH3GL1, revealing a significant transcriptional–translational discordance. Further analysis revealed ALDH1A1-associated myeloid-derived suppressor cell and neutrophil correlations, a regulatory network comprising 68 transcription factors and 79 miRNAs, and 26 prioritized drug candidates.


## Conclusions
  This study establishes a protein-verified lactylation-related gene signature with diagnostic relevance for sepsis-induced ARDS. Crucially, it highlights post-transcriptional regulation, evidenced by the discordance between mRNA levels and protein expression of key hub genes, as a key mechanistic feature in the pathogenesis of this condition. The identified regulatory networks and drug candidates provide potential avenues for further research and therapeutic development.

## Introduction
  Sepsis remains a major global health concern, with an estimated incidence of 437 cases per 100,000 individuals and approximately 5.3 million deaths annually. According to the 2021 international guidelines for the management of septic shock, sepsis is defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction resulting from a dysregulated host response to infection [1]. The lungs are among the most vulnerable organs affected during sepsis, often leading to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), a severe complication with mortality rates exceeding 40% [2]. The recent COVID-19 pandemic has further highlighted the critical importance of understanding shared molecular mechanisms in lung injury, as systems biology analyses of SARS-CoV-2 infections have revealed significant inflammatory gene expression profiles overlapping with pulmonary comorbidities, such as pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), including key mediators such as S100A8 and S100A9 that drive inflammatory responses in the lung microenvironment [3]. Despite this growing recognition, the molecular mechanisms driving sepsis-induced ARDS are incompletely understood, and a critical shortage of reliable biomarkers and effective therapeutic targets persists.

  In recent years, epigenetic mechanisms have also been implicated in the pathogenesis of ARDS [4]. Among these, protein lactylation—a novel post-translational modification involving the covalent attachment of lactate to lysine residues—has emerged as a key regulator of immune and inflammatory responses [5]. Lactate accumulation, a hallmark of the Warburg effect commonly observed in inflammatory microenvironments, is notably elevated in acute lung injury models [6–8]. These findings suggest a potential mechanistic role for protein lactylation in the pathophysiology of sepsis-induced ARDS. The value of computational approaches in elucidating such complex biological processes is well-established, with mechanistic modeling frameworks providing powerful tools to analyze regulatory dynamics in pathological systems [9]. However, the specific roles of lactylation-related genes (LRGs) in this context remain largely undefined.

  The identification of disease-specific biomarkers is paramount for early diagnosis and targeted therapy. The integration of machine learning with high-throughput data has proven highly successful in extracting molecular insights across diverse diseases. This is evidenced by the use of statistical methods to define variant-level biomarkers in prostate cancer [10], the fusion of spectroscopic data with Support Vector Machine (SVM) and AdaBoost to achieve high diagnostic accuracy in recurrent miscarriage [11], and the construction of a random forest model based on ferroptosis-related genes for early myocardial infarction detection [12]. These studies collectively highlight the power of computational approaches to decode complex pathophysiology. Inspired by these advances, we hypothesize that a similar integrative strategy can be applied to dissect the role of LRGs in sepsis-induced ARDS.

  Here, we integrated gene expression profiles from sepsis-induced ARDS cohorts with lactylation-related databases, bioinformatics, and experimental validation to identify candidate LRG biomarkers. Our results suggest that an LRG-based signature holds promise for diagnosis and provides mechanistic insights into disease progression. Furthermore, immune infiltration analysis elucidated the underlying immunological basis. This study not only delineates key biological pathways but also offers a rational roadmap for future translational research, potentially contributing to improved clinical strategies for sepsis-induced ARDS.


## Materials and methods

### Data preprocessing, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identification, and acquisition of LRGs
  Figure 1 displays the overall procedure of the study. To identify DEGs associated with sepsis-induced ARDS, we obtained gene expression data sets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, specifically GSE10474, GSE32707, and GSE66890 (Supplementary Table S1). These data sets included clinically annotated samples of sepsis and sepsis-induced ARDS. Batch effects among the data sets were corrected using the ComBat algorithm from the sva R package. The effectiveness of batch correction was evaluated using principal component analysis (PCA). DEG analysis between sepsis and sepsis-induced ARDS samples was conducted using the limma R package. Genes with an absolute fold change (|log2FC|) > 1.5 and an adjusted P value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Visualization of DEGs results was performed using volcano plots and hierarchical clustering heatmaps, generated with the ggplot2 and pheatmap R packages, respectively. A total of 336 LRGs were curated from previously published studies on protein lactylation (PMID: 37242427, 35761067, 36092712).Fig. 1Workflow of the machine learning-guided identification and validation of lactylation-related hub genes in sepsis-induced ARDS


### Identification and functional enrichment of differentially expressed lactylation-related genes (DLRGs)
  To identify DLRGs, the list of DEGs was intersected with the curated LRG data set. The overlapping genes were visualized using Venn diagrams. Functional enrichment analysis of the identified DLRGs was performed using the ClusterProfiler R package to explore their biological significance in the context of sepsis-induced ARDS. This included Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment across three domains: biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF). The statistical significance of enriched terms was assessed using the hypergeometric test with false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Enrichment results with adjusted P values < 0.05 were deemed statistically significant.


### Identification of hub DLRGs based on protein–protein interaction (PPI) and machine learning algorithms
  To identify potential hub biomarkers among DLRGs, we integrated PPI network analysis with multiple machine learning approaches. The PPI network was constructed using the STRING database, with an interaction confidence score threshold set at > 0.4.

  The feature selection process employed three distinct algorithms to ensure robust identification of hub genes. Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression was implemented using the glmnet package with alpha set to 1 (L1 penalty). The optimal penalty parameter (lambda) was determined via tenfold cross-validation (nfolds = 10) minimizing binomial deviance (type.measure = "deviance"), and the lambda.min value was applied for feature selection. Support vector machine–recursive feature elimination (SVM–RFE) was executed with the caret package using a Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel, chosen for its capacity to model non-linear relationships. Recursive feature elimination was controlled with tenfold cross-validation (method = "cv"), and hyperparameters (sigma, C) were automatically tuned within each subset. For the Random Forest, an ensemble of 500 trees (ntree = 500) was initially grown. The optimal number of trees, determined by minimizing the out-of-bag (OOB) error, was used in a final model to compute variable importance for feature confirmation. Hub genes independently identified by LASSO, SVM, and random forest were intersected to determine the most reliable candidate biomarkers.


### Evaluation of immune cell infiltration and correlation analysis with hub DLRGs
  To characterize immune infiltration, we used single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) to quantify the abundance of 23 immune cell types in the integrated data set. Infiltration levels across samples were visualized using heatmaps generated with the pheatmap R package. Comparative analysis of immune cell infiltration between the sepsis and sepsis-induced ARDS groups was performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Results were presented in box plots. In addition, Spearman correlation coefficients between hub DLRGs and immune cell infiltration levels were calculated and visualized using scatter plots created with the ggplot2 package.


### GSEA of hub DLRGs
  To investigate the biological functions of each hub DLRG, we first calculated correlation coefficients between the expression of each hub gene and all other genes in the data set. The top 50 positively correlated genes for each hub gene were visualized using heatmaps. Subsequently, single-gene GSEA was conducted for each hub gene using the Reactome pathway database. Enrichment analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler R package. The top 20 significantly enriched pathways were reported for each gene. Pathways meeting the following criteria were considered significant: absolute normalized enrichment score (|NES|) > 1, adjusted P value < 0.05, and FDR q value ≤ 0.25.


### Identification of transcription factors (TFs) and miRNAs
  To explore the upstream regulatory mechanisms of the hub DLRGs, TFs and miRNAs potentially targeting these genes were predicted using the RegNetwork database (https://regnetworkweb.org/). The resulting TF-hub gene and miRNA-hub gene interactions were used to construct regulatory networks, which were visualized using Cytoscape software.


### Prediction of drug molecule interactions
  Potential drug–gene interactions for the identified hub DLRGs were predicted using the NetworkAnalyst platform (https://www.networkanalyst.ca/).


### Cell culture and in vitro modeling
  Human bronchial epithelial (HBE) cells (CRL-2741, ATCC) were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Vivacell) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Invitrogen). EA.hy926 cells (GNHu39, Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences) were maintained in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) containing 10% FBS (Vivacell) without antibiotics. All cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. To establish an in vitro model of sepsis-induced ARDS, HBE cells were stimulated with 10 μg/mL LPS (Sigma-Aldrich) for 24 h, as described previously [13]. EA.hy926 cells were treated with 10 or 20 μg/mL LPS for 24 h to induce an inflammatory phenotype, following protocols from recent studies [14].


### RNA extraction and quantitative real‑time PCR (qRT‑PCR)
  Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells using TRIzol reagent (T9424, Sigma-Aldrich) and purified via the phenol–chloroform method. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from total RNA using the Transcriptor First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (04896866001, Roche). qRT-PCR was performed using SYBR Green reagent (04887352001, Roche) on a real-time PCR system. The thermal cycling protocol consisted of an initial denaturation step at 94 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 45 °C for 30 s, elongation at 72 °C for 105 s, and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. β-actin was used as the internal reference gene. Primer sequences for target genes were as follows: ALDH1A1 (forward, 5ʹ-CTGTGTTCCAGGAGCCGAAT-3ʹ and reverse, 5ʹ-TTGACTCCATTGTCGCCAGC-3ʹ), CALM1(forward, 5ʹ-GTTAGCTCTCCTTGAGGTCTCG-3ʹ and reverse, 5ʹ-TGGTGCCATTACCATCAGC-3ʹ), CCNA2 (forward, 5ʹ-GTGATGTTGGGCAACTCTGC-3ʹ and reverse, 5ʹ-TTAAGGGGTGCAACCCGTCTC-3ʹ), SH3GL1 (forward, 5ʹ-AAGCAGTTCTACAAGGCGAGC-3ʹ and reverse, 5ʹ-GTTTCTTCAGGTGGTGCTGG-3ʹ). Due to the absence of unique priming sites, a specific primer pair for HIST1H2BN could not be designed despite repeated attempts.


### Western blot analysis
  Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Protein concentrations were determined, and 20 μg of total protein per sample was separated on 10–12% SDS–PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST (TBS with 0.1% Tween-20) for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibody incubation was performed overnight at 4 °C using the following antibodies: ALDH1A1 (1:1000, CY5141, Abways), CALM1 (1:1000, CY5234, Abways), CCNA2 (1:1000, CY5837, Abways), HIST1H2BN (1:1000, PA548515, Invitrogen™), SH3GL1 (1:2000, BD-PE1818, BrainVTA), and β-actin (1:5000, ab8226, Abcam, loading control). Following TBST washes, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000, Cell Signaling Technology) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL, Bio-Rad) and quantified using ImageLab software (Bio-Rad Laboratories).


## Results

### Data preprocessing and identification of DEGs
  Gene expression profiles from sepsis-induced ARDS patients and sepsis controls were integrated from three independent GEO data sets (GSE10474, GSE32707, and GSE66890). Batch effects were corrected using the ComBat algorithm (sva R package), with pre- and post-correction distribution visualized through boxplots (Fig. 2A, B). Differential expression analysis using the limma R package on the corrected data set identified 978 DEGs, applying stringent thresholds of |FC| > 1.5 and adjusted P < 0.05 (Benjamini–Hochberg correction). Among these, 505 genes were significantly upregulated and 473 downregulated. ## Discussion
  Bioinformatics approaches have become indispensable in modern medical research, facilitating the identification of diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets critical for disease stratification and personalized treatment strategies [15]. The power of this methodology is powerfully demonstrated in oncology, where cross-cancer analyses of biologically similar cancers—such as those sharing DNA-repair deficiencies—have successfully identified both novel biomarkers and repurposable drug candidates, transcending traditional organ-based classification [16]. Building upon this paradigm, this study applied a similar integrative bioinformatics framework to sepsis-induced ARDS, where the discovery of novel regulatory genes is essential for improving early diagnosis and prognosis. We specifically focused on the emerging role of lactate-derived histone lactylation, an epigenetic modification that, driven by the glycolytic reprogramming of inflammatory macrophages, serves as a key mechanism regulating gene expression and immune homeostasis [17–19]. Therefore, by systematically analyzing lactylation-related genes (LRGs), our work bridges a critical gap, identifying a panel of hub LRGs as promising diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets, thereby establishing a foundational roadmap for future exploration in sepsis-induced ARDS.

  To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically identify DLRGs associated with sepsis-induced ARDS using a bioinformatics-based approach. We identified 25 candidate DLRGs through transcriptomic integration and differential expression analysis. Functional enrichment analysis via GO and KEGG pathways suggested these genes are primarily associated with autophagy-related processes, implicating autophagy as a central mechanism in the pathophysiology of sepsis-induced ARDS. Using three complementary machine learning algorithms—SVM–RFE, LASSO, and RF—we ultimately prioritized five robust gene signatures: ALDH1A1, CALM1, CCNA2, HIST1H2BN, and SH3GL1.

  Among the five identified hub DLRGs, ALDH1A1 plays a crucial enzymatic role in catalyzing the oxidation of retinaldehyde to retinoic acid—an essential signaling molecule for regulating cellular growth, differentiation, and immune modulation [20]. Dysregulated expression of ALDH1A1 has been associated with metabolic imbalance and systemic inflammation, often correlating with adverse prognoses across multiple diseases [21, 22]. Intriguingly, in contrast to its upregulation in chronic inflammatory conditions, ALDH1A1 expression is markedly reduced in septic ALI, impairing immune tolerance and weakening intercellular signaling pathways. This reduction likely contributes to compromised alveolar–endothelial barrier integrity, thereby exacerbating pulmonary dysfunction in sepsis-induced ALI [23].

  CALM1, a calcium-binding EF-hand protein, orchestrates intracellular signaling events essential for proliferation, migration, and differentiation. Previous studies have established CALM1’s regulatory influence over apoptosis, autophagy, and inflammation [24]. Notably, miR-202-3p has been shown to directly target CALM1, mitigating LPS-induced pro-inflammatory signaling in murine ARDS models, thereby reinforcing its immunomodulatory role [25].

  CCNA2, encoding cyclin A2, governs cell cycle progression by activating CDK2, enabling transitions through G1/S and G2/M checkpoints [26]. Elevated CCNA2 expression has been linked to poor outcomes in sepsis, where patients with lower CCNA2 levels exhibit improved 28-day survival, highlighting its potential as a prognostic biomarker [27].

  HIST1H2BN encodes a variant of the histone H2B core protein, essential for nucleosome assembly and chromatin organization. As critical structural components of chromatin, histones govern DNA accessibility and transcriptional regulation. Among these, lysine acetylation and other PTMs play pivotal roles in modulating gene expression dynamics [28]. The involvement of histones in the pathogenesis of sepsis and ARDS has been well-documented in animal models [29]. Notably, exogenous histones can bind directly to pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells, activating inflammatory pathways and promoting cytokine release, thereby contributing to endothelial injury in sepsis-induced ARDS [30].

  SH3GL1, a membrane-binding protein involved in clathrin-mediated endocytosis, has been implicated in autophagy regulation and tumorigenesis [31]. Recent epigenetic studies have revealed that SH3GL1 is among several genes whose transcription is potentially regulated by site-specific DNA methylation. In particular, methylation sites cg07830557 and cg08418670 were identified as key epigenetic markers that distinguish ARDS patients from healthy controls [32].

  Initial bioinformatic analyses predicted that ALDH1A1 and CALM1 were downregulated, while CCNA2, HIST1H2BN, and SH3GL1 were upregulated in sepsis-induced ARDS. However, subsequent experimental validation revealed consistent downregulation of all five hub genes at the protein level using Western blotting. qRT-PCR further confirmed reduced mRNA levels of four genes, with HIST1H2BN excluded due to unsuccessful primer design. This discrepancy between transcriptomic predictions and proteomic observations highlights a critical biological reality: mRNA abundance does not necessarily correlate with protein expression, particularly in dynamic inflammatory settings such as sepsis.

  Several mechanisms may account for this divergence. Post-transcriptional regulation—including miRNA-mediated transcript degradation or translational repression—may disrupt the expected correlation between mRNA and protein levels. In addition, the highly inflammatory microenvironment of sepsis could promote rapid protein turnover through pathways, such as the ubiquitin–proteasome system. Notably, lactylation—the central focus of this study—may also modulate protein stability or functional activity, potentially competing with other post-translational modifications to influence final protein outcomes.

  Despite these discrepancies, the consistent downregulation observed at the protein level strongly supports the clinical relevance of these five hub genes. Their coordinated suppression may reflect an integrated pathogenic response indicative of metabolic–epigenetic reprogramming during sepsis-induced ARDS. These findings underscore the necessity of complementing bioinformatic predictions with proteomic validation and employing multi-omics approaches to fully capture disease mechanisms. Future studies should focus on elucidating the precise regulatory pathways governing these genes and evaluating their potential as therapeutic targets in sepsis-induced ARDS.

  Numerous studies have established the central role of the immune system in the pathogenesis of sepsis-induced ARDS [33]. Consistent with these findings, our immune infiltration analysis revealed significant differences in the abundance of multiple immune cell subsets between sepsis-induced ARDS patients and controls. Notably, substantial alterations were observed in eosinophils, MDSCs, macrophages, NK cells, neutrophils, and Tfh cells. These shifts are critically implicated in the pathogenesis of sepsis-induced ARDS. The expansion of MDSCs contributes to immunosuppression and is linked to adverse outcomes [34], while perturbations in Tfh cells suggest a dysregulated adaptive immune response [35]. Furthermore, the imbalance between pro-inflammatory and reparative macrophage subsets is a hallmark of ARDS, directly influencing alveolar inflammation and tissue repair [36]. Interestingly, the role of eosinophils is being re-evaluated, with emerging evidence suggesting they may exert both pro-inflammatory and immunomodulatory functions in the lung [37]. The concerted action of these dysregulated innate and adaptive immune cells drives a maladaptive host response, injuring the alveolar–capillary barrier and compromising repair. Consequently, targeting these specific immune networks presents a promising avenue for immunomodulatory therapy in sepsis-induced ARDS.

  Our analysis aimed to identify candidate drugs targeting the five identified hub genes. Despite this comprehensive search, no direct agents were found for HIST1H2BN, a finding attributable to the challenges of targeting core histone proteins. Nevertheless, for the remaining four genes, we successfully curated a list of 26 candidate drugs/compounds and established a structured prioritization. Among these, Disulfiram, Valproic acid, C646 were classified as high-priority candidates, as they have documented anti-inflammatory or epigenetic regulatory effects and, in several cases, prior evidence in ARDS or acute lung injury models [38–40]. Notably, some of these compounds have also been implicated in metabolic reprogramming and post-translational modifications, suggesting a potential intersection with lactylation pathways [41–43]. These features make them suitable for subsequent docking studies, in vitro validation of lactylation-related effects, and in vivo evaluation in ARDS models.

  However, this study is not without limitations. First, qRT-PCR validation of HIST1H2BN mRNA could not be performed due to the absence of specific primer binding regions. Second, while we identified a clear discordance between bioinformatics-predicted gene regulation (two downregulated, three upregulated) and experimental results demonstrating uniform protein-level downregulation across all five hub genes, the molecular basis of this discrepancy remains unresolved. Post-transcriptional and post-translational mechanisms—such as altered mRNA stability, miRNA-mediated repression, and enhanced proteasomal degradation—are likely contributors. Nonetheless, due to resource constraints, we were unable to directly investigate these regulatory layers through functional assays, including miRNA manipulation, RNA-binding protein interaction studies, or protein turnover assessments. Future work should prioritize mechanistic dissection of these regulatory hierarchies to elucidate the functional relevance of LRGs and to identify viable immunopharmacological targets in sepsis-induced ARDS.
