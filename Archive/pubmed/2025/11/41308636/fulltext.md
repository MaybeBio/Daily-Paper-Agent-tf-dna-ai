# Thermodynamic principles link in vitro transcription factor affinities to single-molecule chromatin states in cells


## SUMMARY
  The molecular details governing transcription factor (TF) binding and the formation of accessible chromatin are not yet quantitatively understood—including how sequence context modulates affinity, how TFs search DNA, the kinetics of TF occupancy, and how motif grammars coordinate binding. To resolve these questions for a human TF, erythroid Krü ppel-like factor (eKLF/KLF1), we quantitatively compare, in high throughput, in vitro TF binding rates and affinities with in vivo single-molecule TF and nucleosome occupancies and in vivo-derived deep learning models. We find that 40-fold flanking sequence effects on affinity are consistent with distal flanks tuning TF search parameters and captured by a linear energy model. Motif recognition probability, rather than time in the bound state, drives affinity changes, and in vitro and in nuclei measurements exhibit consistent, minutes-long TF residence times. Finally, in vitro biophysical parameters predict in vivo sequence preferences and single-molecule chromatin states for unseen motif grammars.


## INTRODUCTION
  Sequence-specific transcription factor (TF) binding to DNA is essential for spatiotemporal regulation of eukaryotic gene expression.1 However, the molecular details driving TF binding and the establishment of accessible chromatin are not understood from first principles—including how TFs search DNA, how local sequence context modulates affinity, the kinetics of TF binding, and how motif grammars establish TF-occupied, nucleosome-depleted regions that regulate gene expression.

  A physical model requires understanding how a TF searches for and identifies its cognate motif on accessible DNA and how binding subsequently influences nucleosome occupancy. The classical two-state model of protein-substrate binding posits that variable affinities across DNA sequences are governed by dissociation rate, while diffusion-limited association rates remain constant. This view conflicts with the speed-specificity paradox in TF search,2 neglects nonspecific diffusion along DNA,3–7 and cannot explain observed sequence-dependent association rates.8,9 Recent work with the bacterial lac repressor10 found that the probability of transitioning from a nonspecific to a specific interaction, rather than bound time, dictated affinity. Determining whether this holds for weaker, less specific eukaryotic TFs would enable a quantitative microscopic model of TF search and motif recognition in eukaryotes.

  Furthermore, measurements of TF-DNA binding kinetics in vitro and in vivo demonstrate surprising discrepancies. In live cells, reported residence times (typically measured via single-molecule fluorescence without resolving genomic localization) are seconds long and within an order of magnitude of nonspecific interactions.11–17 By contrast, in vitro-measured residence times are orders of magnitude longer than nonspecific interactions.16 Recent work identified long-duration binding events in vivo,18–21 suggesting that technical limitations may contribute to underestimating live cell residence times. Non-imaging methods quantifying TF off rates at pre-identified binding sites would provide an orthogonal approach to dissect binding kinetics in cells.

  Beyond kinetic discrepancies, in vitro-derived thermodynamic parameters poorly predict in vivo binding, with appreciable TF binding at <1% of predicted motifs.22–25 One possible explanation is that sequence context outside of the motif may impact TF search and motif recognition. Indeed, motif-flanking sequences can tune TF occupancy through a range of mechanisms—by creating a more favorable DNA shape at the motif,26–31 a funneled energy landscape through low-affinity motifs,32,33 or opportunities to directly bind flanks through weak but entropically favorable sites (i.e., repetitive elements).34–39 However, much of this work is in vitro-only, specific to bHLHs and motif-proximal bases, and focused on equilibrium, rather than kinetic, measurements.

  Finally, genomic enhancers are not just composed of one isolated motif. Rather, these elements contain many motifs,40,41 enabling potential for TF-TF cooperativity through direct physical interactions (i.e., DNA allostery)42,43 or indirect mechanisms (i.e., nucleosome competition or cofactor recruitment).44–47 Integrating in vitro measurements, thermodynamic models, and in vivo observations across motif grammars promises a mechanistic framework for linking sequence features to TF and nucleosome occupancy. To do so, we chose Krüppel-like factor 1 (KLF1/eKLF) as a model TF, a C2H2 zinc finger protein (C2H2-ZF) within the most common class of human TFs.48 KLF1’s three-finger DNA-binding domain (DBD) is highly conserved across the KLF family,49 which enacts wide-ranging developmental roles50 with KLF1 regulating erythropoiesis and the fetal-to-adult hemoglobin expression switch.51

  We measure KLF1’s binding kinetics and affinity to thousands of DNA sequences in vitro and find that the probability of recognizing the motif, rather than the time spent in the bound state, dictates affinity. Motif-flanking sequence drives ~40-fold affinity variation that is well-described by a linear energy contribution per flanking base pair (bp), with proximal effects likely driven by direct contacts from an unconventional binding mode. Distal sequence helical turns away from the motif tune affinity up to 2-fold, in a manner consistent with sequence-dependent TF search. We then deploy single-molecule footprinting (SMF)52–54 on genome-integrated constructs in an erythroleukemia cell line to quantify TF and nucleosome occupancies along individual molecules. A footprinting-based measure of KLF residence time at known motifs in nuclei agrees with in vitro measurement on a timescale of minutes. We implement thermodynamic models to explain how nucleosome competition drives nonlinear KLF occupancy across motif numbers and spacing. Lastly, our in vitro flanking sequence models predict how sequence context tunes in vivo TF binding, as observed with SMF and base-resolved deep learning models trained on chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) data.55 Together, our findings demonstrate that quantitative TF binding measurements in vitro and in vivo provide a biophysical understanding of the sequence features that establish accessible chromatin elements.


## RESULTS

### High-throughput measurement of KLF1 binding kinetics and affinities
  To measure KLF1 affinity in vitro, we designed a library of 11,812 DNA sequences containing variants within the consensus nine-bp KLF motif (CCMCRCCCN). This library spans all single, all double, and some triple mutations of a high-affinity motif (CCACGCCCA), which we define as the consensus motif, extensive programmed variation in motif-flanking sequence, and negative controls lacking the motif (STAR Methods; Figure 1A). We then performed “high-throughput sequencing”-“fluorescent ligand interaction profiling” (HiTS-FLIP)56 by sequencing the library on an Illumina MiSeq and using the resultant flow cell to quantify DNA binding of purified, fluorescently labeled KLF1 DBD (Figure 1B). We assayed binding kinetics by imaging while flowing in KLF1 to measure association rates relative to a sequence with a consensus motif and intermediate strength flank (ka/ka,ref, Figures S1A–S1D) and, post-equilibration, flowing in buffer alone to measure dissociation rates (kd) (STAR Methods; Figures 1C, 1D, and S1A). HiTS-FLIP binding energies (ΔG) linearly relate to biolayer interferometry (Octet) measurements for flank and motif mutations (Figures 1F, S1G, and S1H). The ratios of association and dissociation rates yield relative affinities (STAR Methods) consistent with dissociation constants (KD) from equilibrium measurements across KLF1 concentrations (Figures S1E and S1F). The KLF1 position weight matrix (PWM) constructed from motif mutations yields a CACCC-box, the canonical Sp/KLF family motif, with finger 1 contributing the least to overall affinity as observed with homologs KLF4 and Sp157–59 (Figure 1E).


### KLF1 affinity is driven by motif recognition probability
  We first tested whether our binding measurements aligned with a recent model10 positing a nonspecific DNA-associated TF “testing” state, from which the TF either specifically binds the motif (kon,μ) or dissociates into solution (koff,NS) (Figure 1G). If affinities for different targets vary due to altered probabilities for specifically binding the motif (ptot = kon,μ/(kon,μ+koff,NS)), then this three-state microscopic model predicts an anticorrelation between macroscopic association and dissociation rates (Figure 1H). Indeed, we observed this coupling, as previously seen for the prokaryotic lac repressor,10 for the eukaryotic TF KLF1 across motif and motif-flanking mutations in HiTS-FLIP and Octet measurements (Figures 1I, 1J, S1I, and S1J). On surface-based platforms, KLF1 can rebind in trans to another DNA molecule within the cluster before dissociating (Figure S1K). By incorporating this possibility into the microscopic model, we find that at constant DNA density, the observed association rate variability and the macroscopic rates’ anticorrelation are not artifacts of rebinding in trans (Figures S1L–S1N). Indeed, within-cluster DNA density is highly consistent between library members on HiTS-FLIP (Figure S1O) and, on Octet, is measured and controlled when loading DNA (Figure S1G). Consistent with our modeling results, a separate library of motif and flank mutations with ~20-fold lower HiTS-FLIP cluster density (Figure S1O) yields up to 2-fold faster dissociation rates, while the macroscopic rates’ anticorrelation remains (Figures S1P and S1Q).

  The three-state model predicts that if microscopic reassociation (kon,μ) is blocked during dissociation (e.g., with a binding competitor) and dissociation from the testing state (koff,NS) is fast, then variability in macroscopic dissociation rates (kd) converges on the microscopic dissociation rate (koff,μ). Indeed, dissociation with the dark competitor (high concentration of unlabeled KLF1) showed that macroscopic dissociation rates substantially decreased in variability, collapsing near the fit microscopic dissociation rate (Figures 1I and S1P). Since the dark competitor should eliminate all rebinding, including in trans, it provides an absolute upper bound on measured dissociation rates (Figure 1I). Indeed, the measured microscopic dissociation rate is consistent even at lower DNA cluster densities (Figure S1P). Together, these results suggest that motif recognition probability (the transition to specific binding, ptot), rather than time spent in the fully bound conformation (the microscopic residence time, 1/koff,μ), drives KLF1 affinity.


### KLF1 affinity is tuned by motif-flanking sequence
  Mutations to the flanking sequence (nucleotides outside of the core, 9 bp motif) substantially modulate KLF1 affinity (Figure 1H). As sequences with flank mutants but no core motif (3,295 sequences) show no measurable binding (Figures S2A and S2B), observed variability is due to altered occupancy at the motif rather than binding to additional sites in the flanks.34–39 To determine the distance at which flanking bases can affect motif binding, we assayed binding to the KLF1 motif embedded in quasi-random sequences with increasing lengths of constantsequence flanks (STAR Methods; Figure 2A). These sequences (containing the motif and proximal bases) were embedded either at a fixed position within variable background sequences or at variable positions within a fixed background sequence (STAR Methods; Figure 2A). As expected, variation in binding kinetics (i.e., ptot) is largest without constant flanking sequence and decreases as more flanking bases are held constant (Figure 2B). There is ~40-fold variability in affinity (KD) without constant flanks (Figure 2C). This variability decays to 2-fold with three flanking bases constant and remains present with 15 flanking bases constant (Figure 2C). Thus, KLF1 binding is tuned not only by immediately proximal flanks (~2 bp) but also by distal flanks.


### An extended PWM captures flanking sequence effects
  To quantify the flanking sequence determinants that tune KLF1 affinity, we fit a PWM model that includes 10 bases on each side of the core motif (Figure 2D). Both the training and validation data are well-fit (r2 = 0.92 and 0.92; Figures 2E and S2C). This extended PWM (60 parameters) performed similarly to a more complex and less interpretable machine learning model (~1.6M parameters, r2 = 0.97 and 0.95; Figures S2D and S2E), suggesting that most variability is explainable by a linear binding energy contribution from each flanking base. Motif-proximal bases contribute most to affinity, with a strong preference for guanines in the 5′ flank (adjacent to where the N-terminal KLF1 zinc finger binds) and a weaker preference against GC content in the 3′ flank (adjacent to where the C-terminal KLF1 zinc-finger binds) (Figure 2F). Consistent with this, KLF1 ChIP studies often identify longer motifs with guanines in the −1 and −2 positions.60–63

  We next explored mechanisms for how proximal flanks tune affinity. First, motif-adjacent bases could create low-affinity motifs overlapping the core motif.33 However, comparing the core motif PWM scores for sites overlapping the motif in low- and high-affinity groups showed no change in the energy landscape (Figure S2F). Second, proximal bases could alter DNA shape at the motif.26–31 Using Deep DNAshape,64 which captures the influence of seven flanking bases, to predict shape parameters (minor groove width [MGW], propeller twist, helical twist, and roll) at the motif (Figure S2G), we found a few correlations between affinity and shape parameters. However, the correlation signs are inconsistent between datasets or yield negligible changes in shape parameters (Figures S2H and S2I). These data suggest that proximal flanks do not perturb affinity through DNA shape, consistent with findings that C2H2-ZFs largely rely on base rather than shape information.65

  Lastly, proximal flanks could act through direct contacts, as base interactions adjacent to the canonical DNA triplets are known to occur with C2H2-ZFs.66 Indeed, homolog Sp1 has a unique binding mode where its first finger contacts five bases: the canonical triplet and two 5′-adjacent bases.57–59 Mutating these two 5′-adjacent bases from guanines to threonines decreases Sp1’s affinity,57 mirroring KLF1’s 5′ flank effects. Indeed, direct flank contacts are consistent with proximal flank changes contributing more to binding energy (up to 1.4 kBT) than some core motif changes (down to 0.9 kBT) (Figures 1E, 2F, and S2J). Thus, these two 5′ bases, which we have described as proximal flanking sequence, should likely be considered as part of KLF’s “core motif,” which we define as the bases with direct contacts and energetic contributions during specific protein binding. Taken together, KLF1 binding has substantial sequence context dependence that is captured by a linear energy model, with direct contacts during specific binding likely driving effects from positions −1 and −2.


### GC-rich sequence helical turns away from the motif decrease affinity
  The two 5′ motif-adjacent bases (positions −1 and −2) dominate flank-driven variability in KLF1 affinity. However, the combined energetic contribution from the remaining flanking bases is roughly equivalent to that of these two proximal bases (Figure 2G), producing a ~10-fold potential affinity change. We next dissected these distal effects that are likely not driven by direct contacts and thus outside the core motif. To do so, we measured affinity to sequences where the core motif is flanked by 5 constant bp and moved along the background sequence (Figure 3A). These data show that affinity varies across positions and therefore variable distal sequence context. Autocorrelation analysis reveals that affinity changes exhibit a periodicity of ~10.5 bp, the helical pitch of B-DNA (Figures 3B and 3C). The spectral peak amplitude at ~10.5 bp is largest with fixed flanks of 3–5 bp (sequence variation 7–9 bp away from the motif center) and decays for longer and shorter fixed flanks (Figures 3D and S3A–S3C).

  Consistent with AT enrichment in favorable distal flanks (Figure 2G), we observed a strong correlation between KD and GC content (in a 4 bp window) one helical turn away from the motif (r = 0.60; Figure 3E). This correlation extends across the flanks with a periodicity of ~10.5 bp in both the sliding and fixed position motif libraries, with highest-affinity sequences depleted in GC content helical turns from the motif (Figures 3F, 3G, S3D, and S3E). Since the KLF1 motif is GC-rich, we asked if “motiflike” flank sequences correlated with decreased affinity. Scoring flanks with the PWM (see STAR Methods) revealed strong correlations between “motif-likeness” at certain positions and KD, with the degree of correlation exhibiting the same periodic trend (Figures S3F–S3H).


### Distal flanks tuning hopping probabilities during TF search
  These periodic trends cannot be explained by allosteric interactions through DNA between two binding sites43,67 for two reasons. First, control sequences without the core motif show no measurable binding, demonstrating that KLF1 is not specifically binding the flanks (Figures S2A and S2B). Second, the correlation is opposite to that expected from allostery: motif-like sequences helical turns away decrease measured affinity. Additionally, DNA shape preferences do not explain these effects, as sequence context one helical turn away negligibly affects Deep DNAshape-predicted parameters (Figures S2G–S2I).

  Since the observed periodicity matches B-DNA’s, we wondered if TF search dynamics might explain distal sequence effects. TF search occurs through 3D diffusion, inter-segmental jumps, and rotation-coupled (sliding) and rotation-uncoupled (hopping) 1D diffusion along DNA.4,9 We hypothesized that a GC-rich or motif-like sequence could decrease the hopping probability, thereby reducing the likelihood of hopping one groove over onto the motif and, instead, increasing the likelihood of sliding and dissociating before reaching the motif. We therefore modified our microscopic model of TF binding (Figure 1G) to separate the nonspecific-to-specific transition into two parts: TF search for the motif along DNA and, once there, recognition of the motif. We incorporated a model for target search9 in which a TF combines groove-tracking sliding and hopping between major grooves (Figures 3H and S3I) and then evaluated the model using stochastic reaction diffusion simulations on discretized DNA (see STAR Methods). Here, the TF’s probability of motif recognition (ptot) is deconvolved into the probability of finding the motif during 1D diffusion on DNA (pfind, a function of sliding and hopping) and the probability of binding the motif once on top of it (pbind) (Figures 3H and S3I).

  To evaluate hopping landscape impacts on affinity, we systematically tuned the hopping probability at individual positions. The model predicts that a higher hopping probability is unfavorable adjacent to the motif and favorable at integer helical turns away (Figure 3I). This is consistent with our experimental data if lower motif-likeness (i.e., GC content) and weaker nonspecific binding increase hopping probabilities. Therefore, we used the motif-likeness to calculate a hopping probability at each position and simulated the affinity for our experimental sequences. These results recapitulate the ~10.5 bp periodicity in autocorrelation across physically reasonable parameter ranges,9 with or without groove-tracking sliding (Figures 3J, 3K, S3J, and S3K). The model reproduces the dependence of the autocorrelation power spectra on constant flank length more strongly when groove-tracking sliding is included, but a hopping-only model cannot be ruled out (Figures 3D, 3K, and S3J–S3L). This dependence can be understood intuitively: long constant flanks attenuate sequence context effects on motif search (Figure 2C), while short constant flanks allow proximal sequence effects to outweigh distal ones (Figure 2F). With evidence against DNA allostery or shape driving distal effects (Figures S2A, S2B, and S2G–S2I), flanks tuning the hopping probability in this “goldilocks zone” during target search best explains our experimental observation that sequence context perturbs the probability of transitioning from a nonspecific-to-specific interaction (ptot, Figure 2B). While KLF1’s preference for two 5′ flanking bases is likely driven by direct contacts, preference for AT-rich sequence helical turns away from the motif is consistent with sequence content tuning KLF1 hopping rates during TF search.


### Tandem in vivo SMF and in vitro kinetics of KLF1 binding
  Given the strong flanking sequence effects observed in vitro, we wondered if sequence context drives TF occupancy and chromatin architecture differences in cells. SMF provides high-resolution accessibility information47,52–54 capable of quantifying TF and nucleosome binding events on single molecules of engineered and genome-integrated sequences.47 This method entails incubating isolated nuclei with methyltransferase (here, GpC methyltransferase M.CviPI), which deposits methylation on accessible bases. However, KLF1’s observed in vitro residence time of ~1 min (Figure 1D) is shorter than the standard 7.5 min of methyltransferase exposure. While this short residence time is a technical challenge, it also presents an opportunity to measure in nuclei off rates at cognate motifs by methylating for increasing durations. As KLF1 dissociates, we expect its footprint of DNA protection to be “eroded” through the continuing action of methyltransferases methylating previously bound sites (Figure 4A).

  To measure dissociation in cells, we built a library of synthetic constructs in two sequence backgrounds containing variable numbers (0–7×) of the KLF1 consensus motif with 15 bp spacing in between motifs (Figure 4B). Each motif was flanked with GpCs for consistent proximal bases and to ensure high-quality footprinting with M.CviPI. To demonstrate KLF-specific binding, we included versions with a single motif mutation (Figure 4B) that ablates binding >400-fold in vitro (Figure 1E). As a control, we added a construct with one high-affinity CTCF motif, as CTCF has a longer residence time than other mammalian TFs in live cell measurements16,68–70 and in vitro residence times of ~30 min.71 Furthermore, since this CTCF motif is almost always bound in cells, its flanking bases are highly accessible (due to statistical positioning of nearby nucleosomes52,54,72,73), allowing a measure of the M.CviPI methylation rate at accessible bases. Lastly, we included a construct with six STAT3 motifs since STAT3 has a fast 8-s residence time in single-particle tracking experiments.74

  We integrated these constructs at the AAVS1 safe-harbor locus in an erythroleukemia cell line, K562, as KLF1 regulates erythropoiesis60 (Figure 4C). RNA-seq in K562s confirms high expression of KLF1, lower levels of a number of additional KLFs, and expression of a few related but less conserved specificity proteins (Sps) (Figure S4A). Since the DBD is highly conserved across the KLF family, with most containing identical amino acids for the predicted DNA-contacting residues,49 we refer to in vivo occupancy measurements as “KLF” binding.

  To directly compare in vivo and in vitro measurements, we first quantified KLF1 association and dissociation in vitro for these SMF-compatible constructs. Consistent with independent binding, in vitro apparent association rates (the rates of fluorescence appearance) linearly scale with motif number (Figure S4B), while the in vitro dissociation rate is constant, with a residence time of 1.4 min (Figure 4D). No binding was observed for the mutated KLF1 motif (Figure S4B). Next, we methylated nuclei from K562s with these constructs for incubations ranging from 2 min, the shortest time possible with sufficient methylation to reliably footprint (Figure S4C), to 15 min. DNA was enzymatically converted75 to preserve methylation information during targeted amplification and Illumina sequencing (Figure S4D).


### In vitro and SMF-inferred in nuclei KLF residence times agree
  In SMF data for a construct with seven consensus motifs, we observed strong motif protection and accessibility in between motifs at the 2 min methylation time, consistent with TF binding (Figures 4E and S4E). No such protection is present with seven mutated motifs (Figures 4F and S4E), indicating that measured occupancy is KLF-specific. As methylation time increases, locus accessibility increases and motif protection is lost (Figures 4E and S4E), consistent with KLFs dissociating and allowing M.CviPI to methylate the motif.

  To quantify TF and nucleosome binding, we modified a probabilistic model47 that takes in methylation signal and motif locations and assigns the most likely configuration to each molecule (Figure S4F; STAR Methods). To model the decrease in motif protection due to KLF dissociation, we wrote ordinary differential equations (ODEs) describing KLF irreversibly dissociating with rate kd and M.CviPI then irreversibly methylating the accessible motif with rate km (Figure 4G). These ODEs allow KLF dissociation before methylation of the flanks, in which case a footprint would never be detected. While KLF could reassociate before methylation, we excluded this possibility by assuming that KLF concentration drops once cells are permeabilized and resuspended. We quantified the methylation rate by fitting methylation of accessible CTCF-flanking bases with a single-rate isotherm (r2 = 0.99, km = 0.55 min−1; Figures 4H and 4I). With this methylation rate, we can quantify TF residence times longer than 30 s (Figure S4G).

  Using the measured methylation rate and assuming a constant dissociation rate across motif number (as measured in vitro), occupancy data are well-fit by the ODE model (r2 = 0.93; Figure 4J). The fit residence time is 1.8 min, within 2-fold of the in vitro measurement of 1.4 min (Figure 4K). Although live cell imaging typically reports seconds-long residence times, a method optimized for long-term dynamics measured a 1.8 min residence time for homolog Sp1,76 supporting our observation of a minutes-long in vivo KLF residence time. By contrast, CTCF does not dissociate during the methylation time course, consistent with a slow off rate68,69,71 (Figures 4K and S4C). STAT3, with an ~8 s residence time in live cell imaging,74 generates accessibility by 7.5 min but no observable footprints at 2 min (Figure S4H). These data demonstrate that for TFs with residence times faster than the assay’s sensitivity, footprints are unobservable. Together, these results show that methylation time courses provide imaging-orthogonal estimates of KLF residence time in cells at known motifs that agree with in vitro minute-long measurements.


### KLF binding exhibits cooperativity in cells but not in vitro
  Since KLF motifs are enriched for clusters within the genome (Figures S5A and S5B), we next examined how motif number influences KLF occupancy in cells. The probabilistic model calls from SMF data (Figures 5A and S4F) show that average TF occupancy is nonlinear with motif number: no binding with one or two motifs, then roughly linearly increasing from three to seven motifs (Figure 5B). Nucleosome clearance demonstrates a sigmoidal trend, with molecules fully protected up to three motifs and then completely accessible after five motifs (Figures 5C and S5C). Mutated motifs exhibit neither increased TF occupancy nor decreased nucleosome occupancy across motif numbers, consistent with KLF-specific binding (Figures 5B and 5C). This low-end thresholding in KLF occupancy (Figure 5B) occurs only when TFs compete with other DNA-binding proteins, such as nucleosomes, in cells, while in vitro binding scales linearly with motif number (Figure S4B).


### Thermodynamic model explains in vivo KLF binding across motif number
  Thermodynamic partition functions77–79 provide a framework for modeling non-equilibrium steady-state TF and nucleosome binding at the single-molecule level.47 In these models, energies are assigned to TF-DNA and nucleosome-DNA interactions, with an added nucleosome destabilization energy term when TFs recruit chromatin remodelers (Figure 5D). Energy parameters are fit by maximum likelihood estimation by enumerating all possible binding microstates, summing their energetic interactions, and computing their Boltzmann probabilities. Directly fitting such a model to the KLF dataset is intractable due to dissociation-induced uncertainties in absolute in vivo occupancies. Instead, we fit the average nucleosome occupancy distribution along each construct, a metric robust to TF dissociation (Figure S5D).

  We first fit a simple competition model involving only TF-DNA and nucleosome-DNA binding energies (Figure S5E). This model produced linear occupancy changes and failed to capture sigmoidal accessibility or low-end thresholding in TF occupancy (Figures S5F and S5G). Prior SMF studies47 attributed such nonlinearities to TF-recruited chromatin remodelers, such as BAF. Indeed, a three-parameter model47 including a nucleosome destabilization term when TFs are present (Figure 5D) can recapitulate nucleosome occupancy, observed single-molecule states, and the lack of KLF binding at low motif numbers (Figures 5B, 5C, 5E, S5F, and S5H). Since endogenous KLF contains structured and disordered domains beyond its DBD, we also tested a model wherein protein-protein interactions (PPIs) enabled KLF molecules to stabilize each other’s binding (Figure S5E). While this model recapitulates low-end thresholding, it more poorly fits KLF and nucleosome occupancies (Figures S5F and S5G). The stronger performance of the nucleosome destabilization model is supported by evidence, both in vitro and in vivo, that KLF1 interacts directly with BAF subunits80–82 and other cofactors83–85 to remodel chromatin.86

  In this model, the effective TF binding energy combines the TF-DNA binding energy with the free nuclear TF concentration. Using the derived relationship between fitted energy and TF concentration (see STAR Methods), we compared the partition-function fit energy with the in vitro extended PWM-predicted energy to estimate the concentration of free nuclear KLF molecules. We inferred ~30,000 molecules per nucleus, which is within an order of magnitude of many human TF counts87 and comparable to ~15,000 KLF1 molecules per cell measured during erythropoiesis88 (Figure S5I).


### Increased motif spacing lowers KLF occupancy in vivo, but not in vitro, due to competition with nucleosomes
  To test our thermodynamic model’s predictive power for unseen motif configurations, we designed a library with increased motif spacings from 15 bp to 25 and 40 bp for two, four, and six motifs (Figure S5J). The nucleosome destabilization model predicts that farther spacing decreases TF binding and shifts nucleosome occupancy into shallower and wider stretches of accessibility (Figures 5F, 5G, and S5K). Experimentally, increased motif spacing indeed caused these changes to both TF and nucleosome occupancy (Figures 5F–5H and S5K). Predictions are more accurate for nucleosomes than for TFs, likely because nucleosomes’ lack of dissociation enables more accurate footprinting. While PPIs between KLFs could drive motif-spacing dependence, this model failed to predict changes to nucleosome occupancies (Figure S5L).

  To validate these observations, we used base-resolved deep learning models BPNet89 and ChromBPNet55 trained on data from HEK293 cells with KLF1 overexpression to predict ChIP and assay for transposase-accessible chromatin (ATAC) signals, respectively, from DNA sequence. With increasing spacings between two KLF motifs, BPNet predicts decreasing TF occupancy, and ChromBPNet predicts decreasing accessibility (Figure 5I). Consistent with nucleosome competition, rather than PPIs driving motif-spacing dependence, these model-predicted decreases occur monotonically over length scales incompatible with interactions between KLF molecules.

  We next measured in vitro binding affinity across motif spacings, where KLF1 alone is binding DNA, and found no trend (Figures S5M and S5N). Thus, decreased KLF occupancy in cells with increased spacing is likely due to nucleosome competition (Figure 5J). In this model, less dense motifs impose a greater energetic penalty because more extensive nucleosome displacement is required to maintain full TF binding (Figure 5J). In summary, thermodynamic models explain how nucleosome competition and chromatin remodeler recruitment modulate KLF binding across motif number and spacing in cells.


### In silico experiments support relevance of sequence-context effects in vivo
  We next asked if the sequence-context effects on affinity observed in vitro are sufficient to tune KLF binding in cells. First, we tested whether in vitro binding predicts ChIP signal in cells by making HiTS-FLIP measurements for DNA sequences underlying HEK293 KLF1 ChIP peaks. In vitro association rates showed minimal correlation with ChIP signal (r = 0.1), suggesting that the multitude of additional factors, such as other TF motifs and chromatin state, substantially tune binding in cells and preclude direct prediction of KLF1 occupancy from in vitro affinity alone (Figure S6A).

  To regress out indirect effects from other motifs, we performed in silico marginalization with the KLF1 BPNet model. For each base at each position (either in the motif or flanks), we measured its impact on predicted ChIP signal. We fixed that base (keeping the rest of the motif constant) and averaged the change in predicted signal across 1,000 random sequence contexts relative to predictions where the base was not fixed. Previously, this approach extracted affinities for yeast TFs Pho4 and Cbf1 comparable to in vitro measurements.90 For KLF1, the in silico-derived PWMs are qualitatively comparable to in vitro-derived PWMs for both the core motif and flanks, including the two 5′ motif-adjacent guanines (Figures 6A and 6B). Furthermore, the BPNet PWM weights linearly relate to in vitro-derived weights (core motif r2 = 0.90, flank r2 = 0.74; Figure 6C). Similar to our in vitro findings, the BPNet-derived sequence preferences reveal a preference against GC-rich flanks one helical turn away (r = 0.6; Figures 6B and 6D) and a ~10.5 bp periodicity in the correlation between GC preference and predicted TF binding (Figures S6B and S6C). Together, these results demonstrate that in vitro sequence preferences for motifs and flanks are highly consistent with the sequence determinants of KLF1 occupancy in cells, as distilled genome-wide through deep learning models. Since in vitro experiments were performed with only KLF1’s DBD, this concordance suggests that KLF1’s sequence preferences in cells are driven by the DBD with minimal contribution from its other structured or disordered domains.


### Combined extended PWM and thermodynamic models predict in vivo flanking sequence effects
  We next asked whether sequence context could quantitatively predict in vivo molecular states by combining our extended PWM (for predicting TF energies from flanking sequence) with our thermodynamic model (for predicting binding within chromatin from TF energies). Extended PWM energies were transformed into partition-function-compatible energies, enabling prediction of single-molecule states from flanking sequence perturbations without additional fit parameters (Figure 6E). To test this combined model, we designed a 300-member SMF-compatible library in which each sequence contains four consensus motifs spaced 32 bp apart, with flanks consistent across all four sites but variable across the library (Figure 6F). Flanks span the energetic range observed in vitro and contain sufficient GpCs for footprinting. To quantify effects from sequence perturbations increasingly distant from the motif, we included library members with increasing lengths of constant flanks from two distinct sequence contexts, each paired with 15 randomized backgrounds (Figure 6F).

  We installed this library at AAVS1 in K562 cells and performed SMF. Despite all sequences containing four consensus motifs, binding varied widely as predicted by the combined models (Figure 6G). Approximately 20% of the library (38/300 members) showed evidence of non-KLF binding outside the motif (visible in bulk accessibility [Figure S6E]) and were excluded from further analysis. Across the remaining library members, single-molecule state calls reveal reproducible average TF occupancies varying from zero to three (Figure S6F). Consistent with flanking sequence perturbations driving this effect, extended PWM-predicted energies correlate with in vivo occupancy (p = −0.56; Figure S6D). To confirm that occupancy changes are specific to KLF affinity changing across sequence contexts, we repeated the experiment with transient overexpression of KLF1 (Figure S6G; STAR Methods). Indeed, occupancy increased for mid-affinity sequences at higher KLF1 concentrations, consistent with mass action (Figures S6H and S6I). These data show that motif-flanking bases can shift a KLF site’s in vivo occupancy from never bound to nearly always bound.

  In agreement with in vitro findings, in vivo KLF occupancy is largely driven by the first two flanking bases (which likely directly contact KLF1), and variability decreases with longer constant flanks (Figures 6H and S6J). In the variability that still arises from distal flanks, we investigated if it reflects the in vitro preference against higher GC content one helical turn away from the motif. Indeed, in vivo KLF occupancy decreases as distal GC content increases for sequences with fixed proximal flanks (Figures 6I and S6K). Consistent with BPNet results, this experiment shows that proximal flanks strongly tune in vivo KLF occupancy, while distal flanks (which we posit alter TF search dynamics) also tune binding, albeit more subtly.

  Beyond TF binding changes, nucleosome occupancy (a metric robust to KLF dissociation during methylation) shows a sigmoidal relationship to predicted TF energies, with molecules ranging from fully inaccessible to fully accessible (ρ = 0.53; Figure S6L). The combined models recapitulate this sigmoidal response, matching moderately well when using the TF concentration inferred earlier (Figure S5I) and well when directly fitting TF concentration in this experiment (r = 0.51 and 0.88, respectively; Figure S6L). As expected, transient KLF1 overexpression increases the fit TF concentration (Figures S6M–S6P). Collectively, these data show that in vitro affinities predict in vivo sequence preferences, sequence context substantially tunes binding in cells, and biophysical and deep learning models can quantitatively predict these effects.


## DISCUSSION
  Our study provides tandem in vitro and in vivo measurement of KLF1 equilibrium and kinetic binding and implements biophysical models to predict in vivo occupancy of unseen sequences from in vitro affinities. We found that, as in prior lac repressor studies,10 KLF1 binding strength is driven by motif recognition probability. This observation holds across these bacterial and eukaryotic TFs despite their distinct DNA recognition modes. The dimeric lac repressor’s helix-turn-helix conformationally changes and bends DNA upon specifically binding its 20–21 bp palindromic motif.91 By contrast, monomeric C2H2-ZFs like KLF1 bend DNA less, with conformational changes restricted to finger linker regions upon specifically binding shorter non-palindromic motifs.58,59 Thus, this microscopic model of DNA binding is likely relevant across human TF families even though binding mechanisms vary. Furthermore, anticorrelated relationships between macromolecular association and dissociation rates have been observed for other systems when sequence or salt conditions are perturbed.92–95 We believe our theoretical framework10—a sequential binding pathway with one or several testing states that the searching molecule must pass through before specifically binding—may be generally applicable to many kinetic systems in molecular biology. Mutations affecting earlier steps in a binding pathway primarily modulate macroscopic association rates, while mutations affecting later steps primarily modulate macroscopic dissociation rates,10 allowing this model to account for such experimental observations.96

  KLF1’s motif recognition probability depends on both proximal and distal sequences outside of the core motif. This flankdependence may explain how different KLF family members could regulate distinct targets despite highly conserved core motifs.61,97,98 Since direct contacts with finger one likely underlie proximal flank preferences, KLF1’s core motif likely should include flank positions −2 and −1. High-throughput quantitative measurement of flank effects, as performed here, may reveal overlooked core motif bases for other TFs. Extending such measurements across zinc fingers and other TF families could test the generalizability of distal flank preferences and distinguish whether GC content (i.e., DNA stiffness) or motif-likeness drives these preferences. We anticipate that in vitro single-molecule target search assays9 could resolve the molecular details underlying distal flank effects.

  Our observation of a minutes-long in nuclei residence time for KLF contradicts the model that TF-DNA interactions are highly transient in mammalian cells.16,99,100 Locus-resolved single-molecule tracking (SMT) of the yeast TF Gal4 also reports minutes-long residence times,21 interactions previously undetected by non-locus-specific SMT.101 We anticipate that methods such as these, which track TF kinetics at specific loci rather than across the nuclear milieu, will clarify TF binding timescales and their relationship to downstream processes (e.g., transcriptional bursting). We note, however, that footprinting-based measurements will only quantify an upper bound of TF residence time for TFs with fast off rates due to the time required to methylate accessible sites (Figures S4G and S4H). However, more efficient or concentrated enzymes may extend the measurable range.

  Here, we show that accurate biophysical prediction of in vivo chromatin configurations for unseen motif grammars (e.g., spacing and affinity) is possible using in vitro-derived TF binding parameters and thermodynamic assumptions about TF-chromatin interactions. Pairing in vitro and in vivo measurements demonstrates that inter-motif spacing can perturb TF binding via nucleosome competition without requiring structural or DNA allosteric interactions as in the enhanceosome model.102 Recently, we demonstrated that gene expression can be explained from in vivo TF binding and simple physical models.47 Together, these studies highlight that, with high-resolution and quantitative measurements, cellular processes can be understood from biophysical parameters and statistical mechanics models. Our models accurately predict chromatin states with few physical and interpretable free parameters, in contrast to highly parameterized “black box” deep learning models. Moreover, we do not need to invoke highly cooperative biological state changes, such as phase separation or condensation, to describe in vivo TF binding in our observed contexts. Incorporating more biophysical information into these models, such as nucleosome-DNA sequence specificity and additional TF affinities and concentrations, should further improve prediction of chromatin configurations at genomic regulatory elements.


### Limitations of the study
  Our in vitro measurements use only KLF1’s DBD due to challenges in purifying full-length proteins, while SMF experiments in cells relied on endogenous full-length KLF1, which contains effector domains and intrinsically disordered regions. These non-DBD domains could mediate PPIs and drive in vivo-specific cooperativity (e.g., with respect to motif spacing). However, including PPI energies in our partition-function model could not explain the data as well as nucleosome-eviction models. In vivo-derived deep learning models predict motif-spacing effects beyond the range of direct PPIs, supporting the nucleosome destabilization model. Still, we cannot fully exclude potential PPIs between KLF1 molecules.

  While KLF1 is the most highly expressed KLF in K562s (Figure S4A), and our conclusions are consistent under KLF1 overexpression, other KLF family members may also bind the inserted motifs in cells due to the highly conserved DBD and similar sequence preferences.

  Finally, TFs bound to nucleosomal DNA are likely invisible to accessibility-based readouts like SMF. We therefore cannot exclude the possibility that KLF1 binds directly to nucleosomal DNA. Indeed, the homolog KLF4 is considered a classical pioneer factor because it weakly binds nucleosomal DNA in vitro.103 However, compared with specific motif binding, these low-affinity nucleosome interactions, if they exist for KLF1, are unlikely to substantially affect energetics in our thermodynamic model. Future ChIP-SMF experiments (i.e., SMF readouts on pulled-down molecules) may clarify potential pioneering activity in cells.

#### Protein expression and purification

Pellets were thawed on ice and purified using Ni-NTA Fast Start Kit (Qiagen, 30600) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The kit lysis, wash and elution buffers were all supplemented with 1 mM DTT and one protease inhibitor tablet (Pierce, A32953). The pellet was resuspended in 10 mL lysis buffer and lysed on ice for 40 minutes. Lysate was spun down at 14,000g for 30 min and supernatant was applied to the Ni-NTA fast kit columns. The column was washed with 16 mL wash buffer (provided by kit, low imidazole concentration) and eluted with 4 mL elution buffer (provided by kit, high imidazole concentration). Eluted protein was then buffer exchanged into storage buffer (20 mM Hepes pH 7.3, 400 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM DTT) on 10 kDa cut-off Amicon Ultra-0.5 centrifugal filters (Millipore Sigma, UFC901008). Protein was then further purified with a HiTrap Heparin HP column (Cytiva, 17-5247-01). The column was first washed with 5 mL 20% ethanol and then 5 mL buffer B (20 mM Hepes pH 7.3, 2 M NaCl, 0.5 mM DTT) before equilibrating with 5 mL buffer A (20 mM Hepes pH 7.3, 250 mM NaCl 0.5 mM DTT). Protein was applied to the column, washed with 10 mL buffer A and eluted with increasing concentration of salt up to 2M NaCl in Buffer A. 6xHIS-TEV-HaloTag-KLF1DBD eluted around 775 mM NaCl and was >90% pure, evaluated via SDS-PAGE. Protein was then buffer exchanged into storage buffer on 10 kDa cut-off Amicon Ultra-0.5 centrifugal filters and diluted to 50% glycerol. Protein was then flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for later use.

  The protein was then labeled with HaloTag TMR ligand (Promega, G8251), and the His-tag was cleaved off using TEV protease (NEB, P8112S), in the same reaction. The protein was diluted to 5 μM in storage buffer supplemented with 10 μM HaloTag TMR ligand and 330 units/mL TEV protease. The reaction was incubated for 1 hour at 30 °C. Then, an additional 10 μM of HaloTag TMR was added, followed by incubation at room temperature for 2 hours. Free excess dye was then removed using Heparin purification with the same procedure as described above for the unlabeled protein. The protein was eluted with Buffer A with 775 mM NaCl. SDS-PAGE of the labeling reaction, a control labeling reaction without TEV, and collected fractions from the purification verified that excess dye did not elute with the protein and that there was a shift downwards in molecular weight expected from TEV removal of the His-tag. Protein was then buffer exchanged into storage buffer on 10 kDa cut-off Amicon Ultra-0.5 centrifugal filters and diluted to 50% glycerol. Absorbance was measured at 280 nM (A280, protein) and 548 nM (A548, TMR) with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. The concentration of TMR in the sample was estimated using the A548 measurement, and the concentration of protein was estimated using the A280 measurement (while correcting for TMR absorbance at A280), to quantify a labeling yield of 98%. The labeled protein (TMR - KLF1 DBD) was then flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for later use.


#### In vitro library design
  A total of 22,517 sequences were designed for in vitro experiments. The vast majority of these (including all results in Figures 1, 2, and 3) contains sequence variations in a 174 bp long variable region, varying the motif sequence, the motif position, the flanking sequence context around a motif, the number of motifs and motif spacing, and combinations thereof. Background sequences were generated randomly and any sub-sequences that resembled the KLF motif were removed. Unless stated otherwise, all HiTS-FLIP results in Figures 1, 2, and 3 used background seq3 (see Table S1), where this background was varied in a number of different ways (see below) to create varying sequence contexts. The flanking sequence context around a motif was varied both by directly mutating the flank for a constant motif position, and by changing the motif position (with varying length and identity of constant flank sequence proximal to the motif) in a constant sequence background. The 20 constant flank sequences used were generated randomly and any sub-sequences that resembled the KLF motif were removed. For the fixed motif position, flanks were mutated in a number of different ways, including randomizing the sequence 60 bp to the left and/or right of a motif (with varying length and identity of constant flank sequence proximal to the motif) and point mutating smaller regions in the 15 bp closest to the motif to the left and/or right (with varying length and identity of constant flank sequence proximal to the motif). For these flank variations with fixed motif positions, no removal of sub-sequences that resembled the KLF motif was performed. Many of these flanking sequence context variations were also included in sequences where the strong consensus motif (CCACGCCCA) had been mutated to a dead motif (TACTTAGAT) (Figures S2A and S2B, 3,295 sequences measured). To directly compare in vitro binding signals to binding observed in vivo in KLF1 chromatin immunoprecipitation experiments (Figure S6A), 200 sequences taken from KLF1 ChIP-seq peaks in HEK293 cells (ENCODE: ENCSR859BMR) were included in the library. Sequences were taken in a 173 bp window around the peak centers. Peaks were chosen to get a broad range of peak signal values, from the sub-set of peak sequences that only contained one clear KLF1 motif. KLF1 motifs were counted using PWMScan106 with a PWM model from K562 cells (ENCODE: ENCSR550HCT) with a cutoff score of 1312 for counting a sub-sequence as a KLF1 motif.

#### HiTS-FLIP measurements
  High-throughput biophysical binding measurements were performed using the sequenced MiSeq flow cell and a custom prism total internal reflection fluorescence microscope described in She et al107 and Becker et al.108 Briefly, the XY-stage, Z-stage, lasers, syringe pump, objective and camera were salvaged from an Illumina GAIIx instrument, and integrated with a fluidics adaptor that was designed to interface with Illumina MiSeq flow cells. The instrument was further equipped with a temperature control system and laser control electronics. Imaging of TMR - KLF1 DBD and TMR fiducial marker fluorescence was performed with a 532 nm laser and a 590 nm center wavelength and 104 nm guaranteed minimum 93% bandwidth bandpass emission filter (Semrock). Imaging for DNA amount quantification was performed with a 660 nm laser and a 664 nm long pass emission filter (Semrock).

  Following sequencing, MiSeq flow cells were prepared for binding experiments. First, DNA not covalently attached to the flow cell surface was removed by washing the flow cell with 100% formamide at 55 °C. To remove residual fluorescence from the sequencing, the flow cell was then washed with a cleavage buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8, 100 mM NaCl, 0.05% Tween20, 100 mM TCEP) for 15 minutes at 60 °C. Fiducial marker oligo and a primer for double stranding (Table S3) were then hybridized to the single stranded DNA on the flow cell surface in a number of steps. First, the flow cell was washed with hybridization buffer (5x SSC buffer (ThermoFisher 15557036), 5 mM EDTA, 0.01% (v/v) Tween 20) at 60 °C. Fiducial marker (500 nM) and double stranding primer (500 nM) were then introduced into the flow cell in hybridization buffer at 60 °C and incubated for 12 minutes. The temperature was then decreased to 40 °C, followed by incubation for another 12 minutes. The flow cell was then washed with annealing buffer (1x SSC, 5 mM EDTA, 0.01% (v/v) Tween 20) at 40 °C. Fiducial marker (500 nM) and double stranding primer (500 nM) were then introduced into the flow cell in annealing buffer at 40 °C and incubated for 8 minutes. Double stranding of the DNA was then performed using the DNA polymerase I Klenow fragment (NEB, M0210S). After washing the flow cell with 1 x NEBuffer 2 (NEB, M0210S) at 37 °C, the flow cell was incubated with 0.2 U/μl Klenow fragment in 1 x NEBuffer 2 fragment for 30 minutes 37 °C. Following the double stranding reaction, the flow cell was washed with hybridization buffer.

  All TMR - KLF1 DBD binding measurements were performed in an imaging buffer (10 mM Hepes, pH 7.3, 185 mM NaCl, 0.5 mg/ml BSA, 1 mM EDTA, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM ZnCl2, 0.5 mM DTT) at 22 °C. The imaging buffer was degassed using a desiccator and vacuum before each experiment. Prior to introduction of TMR - KLF1 DBD, imaging buffer was introduced into the flow cell, and fiducial markers were then imaged using a 600 ms exposure time for registration to the sequencing data.

  For kinetic experiments, four tiles of the flow cells were looped over and imaged continuously, resulting in an effective imaging period over all tiles of ~20 s. For association experiments, 50 nM TMR - KLF1 DBD was introduced into the flow cell with a flow rate of 60 μl/min during the entire experiment. After the association experiment, the dissociation experiment was performed by flowing in imaging buffer without TMR - KLF1 DBD. For the dissociation experiment with dark competitor, the imaging buffer was supplemented with 500 nM unlabeled KLF1 DBD. Aflow rate of 100 μl/min was used during the entire dissociation experiments. An exposure time of 200 ms and laser fiber input power of 145 mW were used in the first association experiment and dissociation experiment, and an exposure time of 250 ms and fiber input power of 120 mW was used during the second association experiment, and dissociation experiment with dark competitor.

  For the protein titration experiment, increasing concentrations of TMR - KFL1 DBD were introduced in the flow cell with a constant flow rate of 15 μl/min during the entire incubation time. For each concentration point, 14 tiles were imaged with an exposure time of 600 ms and laser fiber input power of 120 mW. The flow cell was incubated with 1.85, 5.56, 16.7, 50 and 150 nM TMR - KFL1 DBD for 49, 49, 29, 30 and 30 minutes, respectively.

  Cluster DNA amounts were quantified by preparing the MiSeq flow cell in the same way as for the binding experiments, but replacing the double stranding primer with a double stranding primer with the same sequence, but labeled in the 5’ end with a Alexa647 fluorophore. Imaging was performed over 10 tiles in the red laser channel with an exposure time of 600 ms and laser fiber input power of 150 mw.


#### Image processing of HiTS-FLIP measurements
  For each experiment, the sequencing data were aligned with images captured during the binding experiment. Sequenced clusters were matched to fiducial marker images via an iterative cross-correlation method, following established protocols.107,109 To account for small drifts in position during each experiment, each individual image was also aligned to the image of fiducial markers taken right before TMR - KLF1 DBD flow in. Post-alignment, fluorescence intensity for each cluster was quantified by fitting itto a 2D Gaussian.


#### Fitting of kinetic and equilibrium constants
  Following quantification of the fluorescence signal for each cluster, the fluorescence signal for each library member (at each time point or TMR-KLF1 DBD concentration) was calculated by taking a trimmed mean over the clusters’ fluorescence values, removing the 10% lowest and 10% highest clusters. A small subset of clusters with quantified fluorescence intensity identical to zero at all time points or TMR - KLF1 DBD concentrations (indicating unsuccessful alignment and/or Gaussian fitting of this cluster) were removed before taking the trimmed mean. This raw quantified fluorescence signal is plotted in Figure 1D. This fluorescence signal was then subtracted with the fluorescence signal of a negative control sequence without KLF1 motifs (Neg. control DNA in Figure 1D, seq3 in Table S1) to create a background subtracted fluorescence signal (Figure S1A, top and S1F). For dissociation experiments, a normalized fluorescence signal (Figure S1A, bottom) was also calculated, by dividing the background subtracted fluorescence signal with the background subtracted fluorescence signal at the first time point of dissociation for each library member. Dissociation rates constants (kd) for each library member were estimated by fitting of the equation  (Equation 1)f(t)=e-kdt+c,  with fit parameters kd and c, by minimizing the mean squared deviation of the model and the normalized fluorescence signal data values. Relative association rate constants (ka/ka,ref) were estimated via the initial slopes of the background subtracted association curves, right after TMR - KLF1 DBD flow in when the curves are approximately linear. The initial slopes were fit via linear regression by minimizing the mean squared error between the line and the data (Figure S1A, top). The reference sequence (Figure S1A, middle) defined to have ka/ka,ref = 1, with reported association rate constants for all other sequences being relative to this one, is a sequence with the consensus CCACGCCCA motif and an intermediately strong flank.

  As a control, association rate constants ka for single motif sequences were also fit with an alternative approach (Figure S1B) by fitting of the equation  (Equation 2)f(t)=fmax1-e-ka[TF]+kdt-t0,  with fit parameters fmax,t0,kd and ka, by minimizing the mean squared deviation of the model and the entire background subtracted association curves after TMR - KLF1 DBD addition. In this fitting, fmax was allowed to wary between the 95% percentile and the maximum of the background subtracted association curves at the last time point, and t0 was allowed to vary between −20 to 20 s from TMR - KLF1 DBD addition.

  Equilibrium constants (KD), from the TMR - KLF1 DBD titration experiment, were estimated using the Hill equation  (Equation 3)f([TF])=fmax[TF]nKDn+[TF]n,  where [TF] is the TMR - KLF1 DBD concentration, n is the Hill coefficient, and fmax is the fluorescence signal at saturating TMR - KLF1 DBD occupancy at the DNA binding site. More specifically, the Hill equation was used within an algorithm which identified the most informative concentration data points for model fitting for each library member (e.g. after the fluorescence signal can be detected but before the signal saturates). We will first focus on how KD was estimated for 0 and 1 motif sequences (all of Figures 1, 2, and 3), and then clarify how this procedure was modified for multiple motif sequences (Figure S5M). The background subtracted fluorescence signal for the different concentration points (titration curve) was used for fitting for each library member, and, unless stated otherwise, fitting was done by minimizing the mean squared error between equation (3) and the titration curve.

  First, a selection of strong binding sequences with a single consensus CCACGCCA motif, which demonstrated saturation in binding for increasing TF concentrations, were identified by selecting the sequences where the second to last concentration point had a signal that was 95% of or higher than the signal at the last concentration point. The signal at the last concentration point for these sequences was then used to create a range of fmax allowed in fitting, defined as the average of this signal ±4 standard deviations (here quantified to be 53±4×6A.U). Equation 3 was then fit to the titration curve for each library member, while using all three of fmax, n and KD as free fit parameters. A global fmax was then locked to be the mode of a histogram with 20 bins (quantified to 57 A.U.) over the individually fitted fmaxs. To obtain an initial crude estimate of the equilibrium constant KD,initial for each library member, equation (3) was fit to the titration curve while locking to the global fmax and n=1. For relatively weak binders (defined as KD,initial<75nM), since the titration curve is uninformative of fmax when the curve is far from saturing, the global fit (rather than individually fit) fmax was used in later fitting.

  KD estimates were then obtained by inputting fmax,n=1,[TF], and the experimental f([TF]) into equation (3) and solving for KD, while trying to choose a [TF] point that had substantial fluorescence but was not close to saturation. This was implemented by choosing the 2nd titration point ([TF]=5.56nM) if f/fmax>0.1, otherwise choosing the 3rd titration point if f/fmax>0.2, otherwise choosing the 4th titration point if f/fmax>0.2, and otherwise choosing the 5th titration point. To mitigate systematic bias based on what [TF] point is chosen for a given library member (which could be caused by uncertainty in [TF]), a correction factor was calculated for the 3rd - 5th titration points from library members that had informative (substantial binding but not saturated) fluorescence signals at multiple titration points. More specifically, the correction for a library member using the ith titration point was applied according to  (Equation 4)log10⁡KD(i)corrected=log10⁡KD(i)+meanlog10⁡(K‾(i-1))log10⁡(K‾(i)),  where K‾ is a set of KD values with informative fluorescence signals at both concentration i and i −1.

  Initial attempts with more simple curve fitting showed that those procedures were problematic to apply unsupervised to large amounts of titration curves, since they were prone to overfit to uninformative parts of our data, either when titration curves were very low or very high and close to saturated. The described procedure does not suffer from these limitations, and should by design produce robust estimates of KD, and for ΔΔG even if the uncertainty in [TF] is high. The excellent agreement between ΔΔG measured by kinetic and titration experiments (Figure S1E), validates that the fitting algorithm performs well on the current dataset.

  KD for multiple motif sequences (Figure S5M) were estimated in the same way as for the 0 or 1 motif sequences, with the exception of allowing fmax to vary and be fitted over an m times larger interval, where m is the number of motifs for a given sequence. At the last step of analysis, KD values for multiple motif sequences were divided by m. With this normalization performed, the occupancy of a sequence with any number of motifs (which can be greater than 1 for multiple motif sequences) will be proportional to 1/KD at low TF concentrations, which we think is consistent and logical definition of overall KD for DNA sequences with multiple motifs.

  Binding measurements to each library member were obtained over many replicate clusters (mode of the number of clusters per library member: 22, 18, and 49 for the normal kinetic experiment, kinetic experiments where dissociation was performed with dark competitor, and protein titration experiment, respectively), enabling error estimation by bootstrapping over the clusters, with standard errors estimated as the half-width of 68% confidence intervals for ka/ka,ref,kd and KD. For the fluorescence signals in Figure S1F, standard errors were estimated as the standard deviation of the bootstrapped distribution. In Figure S1E, weak binders (grey points) are defined to have KD>500nM (from the titration experiment) and/or standard error in kd>0.0025s-1. As these weak binders give noisy and uninformative kd estimates, they were omitted from the plot and analysis in Figure 1H. A few of the sequences taken from ChIP-seq peaks were outliers in quantified DNA amount compared to the main in vitro library. Therefore, only sequences with quantified DNA fluorescence between 150 and 290 A.U. were included in the ChIP-signal versus association rate comparison in (Figure S6A). Out of the total 200 ChIP-seq sequences, 33 sequences were removed by this filtering.


#### Kinetic three-state model describing TF-DNA binding
  This model for TF-DNA binding (Figure 1G) is a three-state markov chain, where the states are (1) free and dissociated TF and DNA, (2) a testing state where the TF is bound nonspecifically to DNA nearby or at the motif and (3) a specifically bound state where the TF is in the bound conformation on the motif. [TF] is the free TF concentration, kon,max[TF] is the association rate from solution to the nonspecifically DNA bound testing state, koff,NS is the dissociation rate from the testing state to solution, kon,μ is the rate of going from the testing state to the specifically bound conformation on the motif, and koff,μ is the rate of going from the specifically bound state on the motif to the testing state. With the key assumption that the average time spent in the testing state 1/koff,NS+kon,μ is very short compared to the average time spent in the free state 1/kon,max[TF] and in the specifically bound state 1/koff,μ, we have previously shown10 that the macroscopic observables ka,kd and KD relate to the microscopic parameters of the model according to  (Equation 5)ka=kon,max-kon,maxkoff,μkd,   (Equation 6)ka=kon,maxptot,   (Equation 7)kd=koff,μ1-ptot,  and  (Equation 8)KD=koff,μ1-ptotkon,maxptot,  where  (Equation 9)ptot=kon,μkon,μ+koff,NS,  is the probability to bind the motif specifically when in the testing state, before dissociating macroscopically into the DNA dissociated free state. The key assumption, that the time spent in the testing state is short, collapses the two parameters koff,NS and kon,μ into a single parameter ptot, that only influences the macroscopic observables ka, kd and KD by how many times the TF needs to try and pass through the testing state before successful macroscopic association (state (1) → (3)) or dissociation (state (3) → (1)). As stated in Marklund et al,10 we believe that this is a reasonable assumption, true for most search processes where a searcher is looking for a rare target among many false decoys. It is also straightforward to verify this assumption experimentally, by validating that the binding is specific. If the time spent in the testing state is non-negligible, the TF should bind nonspecific DNA without motifs with similar affinities compared to specific DNA with motifs. On the other hand, if the time spent in the testing state is negligible, the TF should bind non-motif DNA with much lower affinity than motif DNA. That KLF1 binds non-motif DNA with orders of magnitudes lower affinity than motif DNA (Figure S2A) thus shows that the time spent in testing state must be very short.

  For normal kinetic experiments (Figure 1I, middle) koff,u was estimated by fitting equation (5) to the association and dissociation rate constants for the motif mutation data. For the kinetic experiments where dissociation was performed with a high concentration of dark competitor (Figure 1I, middle), koff,u was estimated by taking the average ofkd for the motif mutation data. The three-state model predicts that the macroscopic dissociation rate kd should approach koff,u when blocking kon,u with dark competitor, while assuming that koff,NS is fast compared to koff,u.


#### Kinetic four-state model describing TF-DNA binding
  The four-state kinetic model of TF-DNA binding (Figure S1L) builds on the three-state model, but has an additional state that the TF needs to pass through before it reaches the bound conformation on the motif, so that the model can account for possible rebinding in trans on platforms where multiple DNA molecules are attached to a surface, like the HiTS-FLIP array. The model is a serial four-state markov chain where the states are (1) free and dissociated TF and DNA, (2) a state where the TF is near the DNA on the surface (inside a cluster on the HiTS-FLIP array) but not associated to DNA, (3) a testing state where the TF is bound nonspecifically to DNA nearby or at the motif and (4) a specifically bound state where the TF is in the bound conformation on the motif. [TF] is the free TF concentration, kon,max[TF] is the association rate from state (1) to state (2), koff,cluster is the rate for going from state (2) to state (1), [DNA] is the DNA density at the surface, kon,NS[DNA] is the rate for going from state (2) to state (3), koff,NS is the rate for going from from state (3) to state (2), kon,μ is the rate of going from state (3) to state (4), and koff,μ is the rate of going from state (4) to state (3). The mean first passage times to go from state (1) to state (4) (t1,4) and from state (4) to state (1) (t4,1) can be obtained from the general linear system of equations for serial markov chains described in Marklund et al.10 After solving for t1,4 and t4,1 while assuming that the time spent in states (2) and (3) are very short, this gives  (Equation 10)ka=1t1,4[TF]=kon,maxkon,NS[DNA]kon,μkoff,clusterkoff,NS+koff,clusterkon,μ+kon,NS[DNA]kon,μ,   (Equation 11)kd=1t4,1=koff,clusterkoff,NSkoff,μkoff,clusterkoff,NS+koff,clusterkon,μ+kon,NS[DNA]kon,μ,  and  (Equation 12)KD=kdka=koff,clusterkoff,NSkoff,μkon,maxkon,NS[DNA]kon,μ.

  These equations were used to generate the results in Figures S1M and S1N while varying the microscopic parameters of the model.


#### Derivations and equations for the target search model
  The target search model (Figures 3H and S3I) builds on the three-state model (Figure 1G), but in this model the nonspecific “testing” state is broken up into many more microscopic states that describe TF search, including TF hopping and sliding during 1D diffusion along DNA. We define kon,max times the free TF concentration as the association rate from solution to the nonspecifically DNA bound search mode, pbind as the probability to bind the motif when being in the search mode and directly on top of the motif, and koff,μ as the rate at which the TF exits the specifically bound state, to go into the search mode on top of the motif. We now define the model quantity pfind,assoc as the probability to find the motif given entering the search mode in an association process (appearing on a random position along the DNA) before dissociating from the DNA. pfind,dissoc is the probability to re-find the motif given entering the search mode in a dissociation process (appearing on top of the motif), before dissociating from the DNA. pfind,assoc and pfind,dissoc both depend on the microscopic model and parameters governing the search mode (more on this in the next section).

  Below we will derive an equation to get ΔΔG of motif binding for different flank mutations independent of pbind,koff,μ and kon,max, which are parameters that we believe do not change with flanking sequence variations. Namely, we will show that  (Equation 13)ΔG=ln⁡1-pfind,dissocpfind,assoc+constant,  when we assume that pbind, koff,μ and kon,max are constant across the considered set of sequences.


#### Proof
  We start off by observing that, as for the three-state model, assuming that the time in the search mode is very short, equation (6) and (7) for the target search model becomes  (Equation 14)ka=kon,maxptot,assoc,  and  (Equation 15)kd=koff,μ1-ptot,dissoc,  where ptot,assoc is the probability to enter the specifically bound state on the motif (rather than just finding the motif as in pfind,assoc) given entering the search mode in an attempted association process, before macroscopic dissociation, and ptot,dissoc is the probability to re-enter the specifically bound state on the motif given entering the search mode in an attempted dissociation process, before macroscopic dissociation. Dividing equation (15) with (14) gives  (Equation 16)KD=koff,μ1-ptot,dissockon,maxptot,assoc.

  We will now derive equations for how ptot,assoc and ptot,dissoc depend on pfind,assoc, pfind,dissoc, and pbind. We start by considering an attempted dissociation process (entering the search mode from the motif), in which the TF can re-enter the specifically bound confirmation on the motif by re-finding the motif and binding it (which includes scenarios in which the TF re-finds the motif, fails to bind, but re-finds the motif any number of times until the TF ultimately binds the motif successfully). The total probability of binding before macroscopic dissociation is thus  (Equation 17)ptot,dissoc=pfind,dissoocpbind+pfind,dissoocpbind[pfind,dissoc(1−pbind)]+pfind,dissocpbind[pfind,dissoc(1−pbind)]2…=∑k=0∞pfind,dissocpbind[pfind,dissoc(1−pbind)]k.

  This is a geometric series, and since 0 < pfind,dissoc < 1 and 0 <pfind,dissoc<1 and 0<pbind<1, pfind,dissoc1-pbind < 1, this series converges to  (Equation 18)ptot,dissoc=pfind,dissocpbind1-pfind,dissoc1-pbind.

  We now consider an attempted association process, entering the search mode on a random position along the DNA. The TF can find and enter the specifically bound confirmation on the motif by finding the motif and binding it (which includes scenarios in which the TF finds the motif and fails to bind it the first time, but re-finds the motif any number of times until the TF ultimately binds the motif successfully). The total probability of binding before macroscopic dissociation is thus  (Equation 19)ptot,dissoc=pfind,assocpbind+pfind,assoc1-pbindptot,dissoc.

  Inserting equation (18) into equation (19) gives us  (Equation 20)ptot,accoc=pfind,assocpbind+pfind,assoc1-pbindpfind,dissocpbind1-pfind,dissoc1-pbind.

  To relate to KD, we now insert equations (19) and (20) into equation (16), which after simplification gives  (Equation 21)KD=koff,μkon,max1-pfind,dissocpbindpfind,assoc.

  Equation 21 is a general result, true independent of the exact microscopic model and parameterization of the search mode (that govern pfind,assoc and pfind,dissoc), and true also for sequences that have different pbind,koff,μ or kon,max.

  Finally we consider a set of DNA sequences that have the same pbind, koff,μ and kon,max, but different pfind,assoc and pfind,dissoc. According to equation (21), their ΔG in kBT units can be calculated according to  (Equation 22)ΔG=ln⁡KD=ln⁡1-pfind,dissocpfind,assoc+ln⁡koff,μkon,maxpbind=ln⁡1-pfind,dissocpfind,assoc+constant,  which is the sought equation (13) (QED).


#### Simulations and parameterization of the target search model
  To obtain pfind,assoc and pfind,dissoc, the target search process of the target search model (Figures 3H and S3I) was evaluated using stochastic diffusion simulations on a discretized DNA with 1 bp steps, building on the simulations described in.9 pfind,assoc and pfind,dissoc both depend on the microscopic model parameters governing the search mode, which we define to include two forms of 1D diffusion: hopping and sliding. In our case, the microscopic parameters are: khop, the rate of hopping to place the protein in a major groove an integer of DNA periods away from the previous position, xhop, the average hop length in bp, pflip, the probability that the TF flips 180 degrees and changes from sensing the forward or reverse strand given a hop, D1helix, the rate at which the TF takes 1 bp helical diffusion step to the left or right, and koff,NS, the dissociation rate from the search mode and DNA into solution. pfind,assoc and pfind,dissoc can thus be viewed as a function of these microscopic parameters:  (Equation 23)pfind,assoc,pfind,dissoc=F‾khop(sequence),xhop,pflip,D1helix,koff,NS,  where khop(sequence) is a DNA sequence and/or position-dependent hop rate landscape (more on this below).

  At any given simulation step when the TF is nonspecifically bound to the DNA (in the search mode), the TF can take a hop of average length xhop to a nearby major groove with probability khop/pz, or take a 1 bp helical diffusion step with probability 2D1helix/pz (to the left or right with equal probability), or dissociate macroscopically from the DNA with probability koff,NS/pz, where  (Equation 24)pz=khop+2D1helix+koff,NS.

  Hop lengths were sampled from a normal distribution with average 0 and standard deviation xhop, and then rounded off to the closest 10.5 bp to place the protein in the major groove an integer of DNA periods away from the previous position. Given a hop, the TF can flip to change if it is sensing the forward or reverse strand with probability pflip. Association and dissociation processes were sampled to estimate pfind,assoc and pfind,dissoc, the probability to find the motif, i.e. be on top of the motif and sensing the forward DNA strand, before macroscopic dissociation. Association processes were initiated by sampling the starting position from a uniform distribution along the DNA, and equiprobable association to the forward or reverse strand. Dissociation processes were initiated with the TF on top of the motif on the forward strand, and the trajectory was then run until the TF found the motif again, or dissociated macroscopically.

  The default values of model parameters were taken as (khop, xhop, pflip, D1helix, koff,NS) =(10−4 s−1, 20 bp, 0.25, 3×104 bp2s−1, 2×103 s−1) when not stated otherwise. These values were chosen to be within a factor of ~2 of the values that have been determined experimentally for the lac repressor,7,9 but while modifying them slightly to reflect that HaloTag-KLF1 DBD is smaller and perhaps a slightly weaker nonspecific binder than the lac repressor dimer.

  In Figure 3I, khop was modified on the forward strand at one specific position per simulation, while keeping all other hopping rates constant. When using a sequence dependent hop rate landscape (Figures 3J, 3K, S3J, and S3K), khop was calculated from the motif-likeness score at a given 9 bp sequence position according to  (Equation 25)khop(sequence)=aeb×ΔGPWM(sequence),  where the normalization parameter a is defined as  a=k‾hopmedianeb×ΔGPWM(sequence),  and khop is the desired median khop given as an input parameter. ΔGPWM(sequence) was calculated from the motif PWM model fitted on our motif mutation data (Figure 1E), and b was set to 0.1 to give a reasonable distribution of hop probabilities (phop=khop/pz, where higher values forb will give many hop probabilities very close to 0 or 1, and lower values forb will result in all hop probabilities being very similar.

  To obtain ΔΔG for different hop rate landscapes and model parameters, the pfind,assoc and pfind,dissoc calculated by the above described simulations were inserted into equation (13).


#### PWM models
  The binding energy ΔGPWM for a sequence in our PWM models is defined as  (Equation 26)ΔGPWM=∑b,pE(b,p)×S(b,p)=ΔGreference+∑b,pΔE(b,p)×S(b,p),  where b is the current base and p is the current position in the sequence. S(b,p) is a function that outputs 1 if the current sequence has the base b at position p, and otherwise outputs 0. E(b,p) is the absolute energy contribution of each base at each sequence position, and ΔE(b,p) is the corresponding relative energy contribution compared to some reference sequence with an experimentally determined binding energy of ΔGreference. A PWM model with a total of P sequence positions has 4×P parameters represented in the 2D matrix ΔE. The parameters of ΔE were fit by minimizing the mean squared deviation between the experimental and model predicted ΔG. The reference sequence was chosen so that ΔGreference was the lowest energy in the training dataset, and the parameters in ΔE corresponding to this sequence were locked to 0. This reduces the number of free parameters to 3×P.

  The input data for the flank PWM model consisted of 4,096 DNA sequences (174 bp in length), and their corresponding in vitro measured values for ΔG = ln(KD). These sequences all contain the consensus KLF1 binding motif CCACGCCA, and has a varying flanking sequence context obtained by both moving the motif position and mutating the flanking sequence with a fixed motif position. 10% of the data was then selected at random as test data, 10% as validation data, and the remaining 80% as training data. The model in Figures 2E–2G used the 10 bp flank sequences to the left and and right of the motif (20 sequence positions), resulting in 80 parameters whereof 60 were free in the fitting of ΔE(b,p).

  The input data for training of the core motif PWM model (Figure 2E) consisted of 356 sequences and their corresponding in vitro measured values for ΔG. These sequences contained all possible single, double, and a few higher order mutations to the consensus CCACGCCA motif. All sequences had the motif in the same position, in the same strong flanking context background. This model has a total of 36 parameters, whereof 27 were free in the fitting of ΔE(b,p).


#### Genomic analysis of clustered motifs
  TF-bound motifs in the human genome were acquired from a compiled dataset that measured DNase I footprints in 243 human cell and tissue types.40 The footprints in this dataset were mapped to TFs using JASPAR PWM motifs110 and PWMScan.106 PWMScan was first run over hg38, for all non-redundant human JASPAR motifs, with a p-value cutoff of 0.001. Looping over all PWMScan outputted TF motifs and DNase I footprints, a TF was mapped to a footprint if the center of the PWMScan motif overlapped with the footprint. The number of nearby same TF motifs was then calculated with the current JASPAR PWM motif with a p-value cutoff of 0.001, in a 100 bp window centered at the motif overlapping with the footprint. For each JASPAR TF, the average number of nearby motifs (y-axis on Figure S5A) was then calculated by taking the average over all mapped footprints for this TF.

  For null hypothesis testing, the number of nearby same TF motifs was also calculated when the bases in the 100 bp window were scrambled, while excluding the bases of the motif at the center from scrambling. Scrambling was performed by assigning a new position for each base by a randomized permutation. For a given TF-mapped footprint, this scrambling and counting of nearby motifs was performed 50 times, allowing for estimation of z-scores and p-values. For each JASPAR TF, this was performed for 200 TF mapped footprints per chromosome. For each JASPAR TF, the average number of nearby motifs when scrambling (x-axis on Figure S5A) was then calculated by taking the average over all mapped footprints where scrambling was performed for this TF.

  The average number of nearby motifs when scrambling was also calculated for each of the 50 bootstrapped iterations. This enabled calculation of z-scores (y-axis, Figure S5B) with the equation  (Equation 27)z=absy-μσ,  where y is the average number of nearby motifs when not scrambling, and μ and σ are the average and standard deviation of the bootstrap samples when scrambling.


#### Deep learning model for flanks in vitro
  The training, validation and test data for the deep learning flank model consisted of the same DNA sequences and measured KD values as for the flank PWM model. The 174 bp sequences each contain the consensus KLF1 binding motif CCACGCCA. This motif was then cut out from the sequence along with the 30 closest nucleotides flanking each side of the motif. The resulting sequences were thus 69 base pairs long, centered on the binding motif. The values for KD were normalized and logarithmized for the training and validation set, respectively.

  PyTorch was used to implement the model with both the transformer and positional encoder class inheriting from torch.nn.Module. The model follows the classic transformer schema, starting off with an embedding layer, followed by a positional encoding layer. An encoder layer with 16 heads and a dropout of 0.2 makes up the transformer encoder. After this, a dropout layer is followed by a linear layer that outputs the values for ln(KD). In total, this model has 1,587,075 trainable and 8,960 untrainable parameters. The model was trained for 100 epochs, updating the trainable parameters to minimize the mean squared error between the model predicted and experimental ln(KD).

  The positional encoding class uses the positional encoding (PE) functions from111 to create the tensors for odd and even positions. pos refers to the current position of the sequence being encoded. i refers to each dimension in the assigned positional encoder vector of length d.

#### Single-molecule state calling model
  State calls were performed as in Doughty et al47 with a few modifications. Briefly, single-molecule states were assigned to each read using a maximum-likelihood approach. First, all possible single-molecule states (for a given amplicon) were enumerated (and precomputed to speed up future computations). A single-molecule state represents the occupancy status of each motif, as well as the position (start and end coordinates, snapped to the nearest GpC) of all nucleosomes covering the molecule. The full list of states was enumerated by taking the Cartesian product of the power set of the motifs (each can be bound or unbound) and all possible positions of as many nucleosomes will fit along the molecule (mandating that the nucleosomes start and end at GpCs, have at least one accessible GpC in a linker between any pair of two nucleosomes, have at least 25 bp in between bound TFs and nucleosomes, overlap the molecule with at least 30 bp, and are between 110-140 bp in length).

  For each hypothetical state (TF occupancy and nucleosome positioning), the expected methylation signal was computed by determining whether each GpC is accessible, protected by a nucleosome, or protected by a TF. These GpC occupancy states were then converted into probabilities using different methylation probabilities (unobserved) for each of the three possible occupancy states. Methylation probabilities were decreased for short methylation times. This results in a matrix of size (number of states) x (number of GpCs) representing the probability that GpC j is methylated in state i. This matrix is then converted into a matrix P representing the probability of observing a converted base after the EM-seq reactions using the methylated (pUC19) and unmethylated (lambda) conversion rates from the controls. The maximum (log-)likelihood state for each input molecule (from single-molecule methylation matrix M, which is (number of GpCs) x (number of molecules)) was computed using a Bernoulli likelihood across all GpC positions in vectorized format, resulting in a vector of the most likely state for each input molecule. The code is available at https://github.com/GreenleafLab/klf_manuscript_code/blob/main/classify_single_molecule_binding_v4.py.


#### Partition function model
  The partition function model was constructed as in Doughty et al47 with a few modifications. Briefly, the steady-state non-equilibrium thermodynamic model was constructed by deriving a function that assigns each molecule an energy. This energy function takes as input every possible single-molecule configuration from the state-calling model and counts the number of TFs (integers) and nucleosomes (including fractions of nucleosomes) occupying the enhancer. Each bound TF or nucleosome contributes a linear binding energy to the total energy of the molecule, except that nucleosomes bound to molecules with TFs also bound have their affinity reduced. Thus, three parameters ΔETF,ΔEnuc,ΔEremodel are used to assign each molecule an energy. A given set of these parameters determines an energy, and therefore a Boltzmann probability, for each molecular state. The nucleosome energy was assigned to the value fit in Doughty et al47 due to the same locus being used. In Figure 5, we then used maximum likelihood estimation to fit the two parameters ΔETF,ΔEremodel that optimize the probability of seeing all observed molecules. The Simple Competition (Figures S5E–S5G) was fit as above, although forcing the value of Eremodel to 0. The Protein-Protein Interaction (Figures S5E–S5G) was fit as above, although the three parameters fit are ΔETF, ΔEnuc, and ΔEPPI, where ΔEPPI is an energy of interaction that is then multiplied by the number of pairs of bound TFs in order to calculate the overall energy of the molecular state. In Figure S6L, the now fixed parameters ΔEnuc,ΔEremodel are used, and the TF concentration ([TF]) is either fit directly or inferred (from value in Figure S5I) to derive ΔETF from the PWM-predicted energies. The models were fit separately to each biological replicate of each experimental condition, and statistics were done on the independent fit values. Code is available at https://github.com/GreenleafLab/klf_manuscript_code/blob/main/partition_function_model_functions.py and https://github.com/GreenleafLab/amplicon-smf/blob/master/workflow/scripts/fit_partition_function_model_v3.py.

  In our implementation, ΔETF is defined by the relative probabilities of observing TF unbound versus bound DNA and is in units of kBT:  (Equation 30)ΔETF,in vivo=ln⁡1-P(TF:DNA)P(TF:DNA).

  In comparison, in vitro measured energies are defined as:  (Equation 31)ΔETF,in vitro=ln⁡KD=ln⁡[TF][DNA][TF:DNA]=ln⁡([TF])+ln⁡1-P(TF:DNA)P(TF:DNA).

  We can then (as in Figure S5I) directly relate between these in vitro and in vivo defined energies as follows:  (Equation 32)ΔETF,in vivo=ΔETF,in vitro-ln⁡([TF]).


#### Methylation and dissociation model
  The kinetic model of methylation and dissociation was defined as a system of ordinary differential equations as follows:  (Equation 33)dAdt=-kdA,   (Equation 34)dBdt=kdA-kmB,   (Equation 35)dCdt=kmB,  where the states A, B, and C refer to a TF-bound and unmethylated motif, an unbound and unmethylated motif, and an unbound and methylated motif, respectively, kd is the TF dissociation rate, and km is the methylation rate. To fit the model, km was input from a measured methylation rate (Figures 4H and 4I) and A was assumed to be zero if the motif number was less than or equal to two and to linearly increase from three to seven motif numbers. Therefore, two parameters were fit across the average occupancy methylation time dataset: the initial t=0 average occupancy for the sequence with seven motifs (A, which was then scaled per motif number as described) and the dissociation rate, kd. Fitting was performed using scipy.optimize.curve_fit() and standard deviation on the parameter was calculated from the output covariance matrix, pcov, as follows: numpy.sqrt(numpy.diag(pcov)).


#### RNA-seq data processing
  RNA-seq dataset for K562s was sourced from ENCODE: ENCSR000CPH and the transcripts per million (TPM) for each KLF or Sp protein was identified. Code is available at https://github.com/GreenleafLab/klf_manuscript_code/blob/master/RNA-seq_analysis.ipynb.


#### BPNet predictions
  BPNet predictions were performed as in Avsec et al.89 Briefly, we used a BPNet model trained on KLF1 chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) data in K562s (ENCODE: ENCSR550HCT). Each sequence from the in vitro KLF library in Figure 1A are inserted at the center of one hundred 2114 bp random backgrounds. The average ChIP profile predictions were then obtained using the trained BPNet model (see https://github.com/kundajelab/bpnet/) and fold changes in ChIP profile predictions were calculated relative to the backgrounds input without the inserted sequence of interest.


### QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS
  Statistical details can be found in the figure legends. All single-molecule footprinting experiments were done with two biological replicates (n = 2). All biolayer interferometry experiments were done with two technical replicates (n = 2). Pearson and spearman correlations were calculated with the python function stats.pearsonr or stats.spearmanr. Coefficients of determinations (r2 values) were calculated with the python function sklearn.r2_score. Significance between populations in Figure 6I were defined as follows: * for p<0.05 and ** for p < 0.01. For in vitro HiTS-FLIP experiments, binding measurements to each library member were obtained over many replicate clusters (mode of the number of clusters per library member: 22, 18, and 49 for the normal kinetic experiment, kinetic experiments where dissociation was performed with dark competitor, and protein titration experiment, respectively), enabling error estimation by bootstrapping over the clusters, with standard errors estimated as the half-width of 68% confidence intervals for ka/ka,ref,kd and KD.
