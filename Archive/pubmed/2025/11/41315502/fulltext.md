# Identification of potential molecular targets of rhaponticin in the treatment of periodontitis using bioinformatics tools


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26734-2.


## Introduction
  Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory oral disease, the sixth most common disease in the world, which eventually leads to loosening, shifting and even loss of teeth1. Globally, the prevalence of severe periodontitis is as high as 11%2. In addition, periodontitis is closely associated with many systemic diseases such as kidney disease3, diabetes mellitus4, rheumatoid arthritis5and Atherosclerotic6and can affect their prognosis.

  Today’s treatments for periodontitis lesions include basic treatment, surgical treatment and medication. Basic treatment includes oral hygiene education, supragingival scaling, subgingival scraping and root planing. Surgical treatment comprises gingival flap surgery, gingivoplasty, gingivectomy and guided tissue regeneration7. However, these surgical treatment modalities have achieved limited success in periodontal tissue regeneration. Pharmacological treatments are mainly used to locally attenuate infection and bone loss and to control their spread8, including antibiotics9 and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs10. Antibiotics such as metronidazole and azithromycin are widely used in the treatment of chronic periodontitis caused by Porphyromonas gingivalis, the main causative agent of periodontitis, because of their excellent anti-anaerobic effect11,12. However, the administration of these drugs is often accompanied by gastrointestinal discomfort and the development of drug resistance in the body. Natural medicines are widely used due to their low side effects and their good therapeutic effects on the disease13, therefore, it is important to find a natural agent that effectively targets periodontitis and promotes alveolar bone repair.

  Rha is a stilbene glycoside analog that can be found in medicinal plants of the genus Rhubarb such as Rheum officinale, Rheum undulatum, Rheum hotaoense and Rheum palmatum14. In Asian countries such as China, Korea and Japan, Rha has been widely used in the treatment of diseases characterized by poor circulation, pain and chronic inflammation15. Both it and its metabolites are rich in biological activities16,17, including anti-tumor18, anti-inflammatory19, antioxidant20and anti-osteoporotic21properties. This opens the possibility of its application in periodontitis characterized by bone defects and chronic inflammation. Hypoxia is considered to be an important influence in the normal metabolism and repair of alveolar bone, which can directly affect the vasculature to form the microbial environment at the base of the periodontal pockets and activate the HIF-1 signaling pathway, which is involved in the regulation of the development of periodontal tissue lesions in periodontitis22. Kim et al.23 demonstrated that in a cellular model of hypoxia, Rha inhibited hypoxia-induced HIF1A accumulation and expression by enhancing HIF1A degradation through increased VHL expression, suggesting that Rha is an important regulator of the HIF-1 pathway. Therefore, we speculated that Rha could treat periodontitis by regulating HIF1A.

  HIF1A, the only known hypoxia-specific transcription factor to date, is regulated by hypoxia signaling24. It activates the HIF-1 signaling pathway25under hypoxic conditions, which in turn affects the expression of biological factors in the cellular microenvironment, such as nitricoxidesynthase26, vascular endothelial growth factor(VEGF)27, pro-inflammatory cytokines28 and angiopoietins29, among others. Several studies have shown that hypoxia is closely related to the development of periodontitis, and analysis of Wistar rats subjected to chronic and sustained hypoxia for three months revealed alveolar bone loss and an increase in the width of the periodontium30. It has also been shown that the altitude of the area in which 26% to 44% of the population lives is positively correlated with the severity of periodontitis and the rate of moderate or severe alveolar bone resorption31. Oxygen tension (pO2) at the base of untreated periodontal pockets was approximately 13.3 mmHg (mean 1.8% O2 ), which coincided with a more comfortable growth of Gram-negative anaerobic bacteria, the main causative agent of periodontitis, which in turn produced more metabolites and further lowered the O2 concentration at a deeper site32. In recent years, a series of advances have also been made in the study of HIF1A and bone development and alveolar bone resorption33–35. Thus, hypoxia may be one of the important factors affecting alveolar bone metabolism. In a clinical study, it has been demonstrated that HIF1A expression levels in gingival sulcus fluid were significantly higher in periodontitis patients than in a healthy group36. The hypoxic environment promoted the expression of HIF1A in periodontal tissues and accelerated the progression of periodontitis, suggesting that HIF1A may play a key role in the pathogenesis of periodontitis37. Most previous studies on the treatment of periodontitis have focused on the progression of inflammation on periodontal tissues and how to control inflammation to repair dental tissues for alveolar bone reconstruction, but have neglected the key factor that the hypoxic environment is not favorable to alveolar bone metabolism.

  Network pharmacology is a new discipline that reveals and visualizes the potential interaction networks of Chinese medicine against multifactorial diseases and its application promotes the safety, efficacy and mechanistic studies of traditional Chinese medicines38. Molecular docking is a well-established and computer-based structural approach that is now widely used in modern drug design and exploration of binding sites for macromolecular targets39. In this study, we first used network pharmacology and molecular docking to analyze the potential target genes and pathway enrichment pathways in the bone remodeling of Rha for periodontitis and to clarify the therapeutic potential of Rha for periodontitis and its possible mechanisms. In addition, we observed whether Rha could promote the proliferation, migration and osteogenic differentiation of human hPDLSCs through in vitro experiments to verify its properties on bone remodeling. This study provides a reference for the therapeutic mechanism and clinical application of Rha for periodontitis.The technology roadmap for this study is shown in Fig. 1.

## Materials and methods

### Bioinformatics analysis

#### Prediction and screening of targets of action related to Rha treatment of periodontitis
  The SMILES numbers of Rha were obtained from the Pubchem Web site (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and the numbers were uploaded into the SwissDrugDesign (http://www.swissadme.ch/index.php), Super-PRED ( https://prediction.charite.de/) website, the 2D structure of Rha was automatically retrieved and the targets were predicted to obtain the predicted targets of Rha; the predicted targets by SwissDrugDesign (http://www.swissadme.ch/index.php) website were characterized by a Probability value > 0.1 as the screening threshold; from GeneCardsSuite (https://www.genecards.org/), DISGENET (https://disgenet.com/) and OMIM (https://omim.org/) websites, the predicted targets were obtained with the “periodontitis” as the keyword to search for periodontitis targets; GeneCardsSuite and DISGENET websites used the median of greater than (relevance) scores as the screening threshold; the Rha and periodontitis targets obtained from the databases were de-emphasized to obtain the potential targets for Rha treatment of periodontitis in the Intersections. A Wayne diagram of the intersection of the two targets was plotted using the online tool available at the BioinfoGP (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/) website.


#### Core target screening and PPI network construction
  The obtained target intersections were imported into the search box of STRING (https://cn.string-db.org/) and the organism “Homo sapiens” was selected to obtain the protein-protein interactions. The TSV file was downloaded and imported into Cytoscape software and the core targets were obtained after setting the filtering parameters of the software to generate the core target PPI network map of Rha targets for periodontitis.


#### GO enrichment analysis and KEGG pathway analysis
  The target intersections were imported into the Metascape website (https://metascape.org/) and the species was selected as “H.sapiens” and then submitted for rapid analysis and the GO enrichment analysis and KEGG enrichment analysis were selected respectively. The GO functional analysis was divided into three parts, i.e., biological process (BP), cellular component (CC) and molecular function (MF) and the enriched entries of the three parts were sorted by “Count” and the top 10 entries were selected respectively (P value < 0.05) and the GO enriched entries were generated by using Microbiotics platform. Split-plane diagram. In the KEGG enrichment analysis, the top 20 entries (with P value < 0.01) were selected in “Count” order and the KEGG-enriched Sankey diagram was generated by using the Wei Sheng Xin Online Website (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/).


#### Molecular docking
  Download the 3D structure of the target protein receptor and “Rhaponticin” from the PDB website (https://www.rcsb.org/). Remove the water molecules and ligands from the 3D structure of the target protein receptor. The 3D structure of the target protein receptor was removed from the water molecules and ligands and then opened with AutoDock software to add hydrogen, calculate the charge number, determine the nature of the rigid structure and set the torsion parameters. After opening the latest processed 3D structure file of the target protein receptor and the 3D structure file of the ligand in AutoDock software, the docking parameters and algorithms were set. The receptor 3D structure file was opened and the required energies for the docking process were sorted and displayed. When the binding energy is less than 0 kcal/mol, it is determined that the receptor and ligand have good binding. The above files were saved in pdpqt format. The pdpqt format file in which molecular docking was completed was converted to a pdb format file using OpenBalbel software. Open this pdf format file with PyMOL software and label the residues of the ligand attached to the receptor and calculate the number of hydrogen bonds and distances. Adjust the docking image to the appropriate position as a visualization of the pocket structure and save it as a pse format file.


### Cell experiments

#### Materials
  The following instruments and reagents were used in this study: CO2 incubator (Thermo Fisher, USA), Anaeropack system (Mitsubishi Gas Chemicals, Tokyo, Japan), inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan), benchtop low-temperature and high-speed centrifuge (Hunan, China), ultrasonic cell crusher (Shanghai, China), ultrapure water machine (Shenzhen, China).

  The hPDLSCs were purchased from FuHeng BioLogy Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Rhaponticin (purity ≥ 98%)was purchased from Aladdin Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Vitamin C, dexamethasone, sodium β-glycerophosphate pentahydrate, BCA Protein Assay Kit and Alizarin Red S Solution were purchased from Solarbio Biotech Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). α-MEM medium and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were purchased from CellMaxcell Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). CCK-8 detection kit, alkaline phosphatase coloring kit and EDU kit were obtained from Beyotime Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Transwell chambers were purchased from Corning (USA), HIF1A and PCNA antibodies were purchased from CST (USA), OCN antibody was purchased from Abcam (UK), CXCR4 antibody was purchased from Proteintech (USA).β-actin antibody was purchased from Boster Bioengineering Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China), IgG-HRP antibody was purchased from HUABIO Co., Ltd. (Zhejiang, China).


#### Cell culture
  The hPDLSCs were cultured using α-MEM complete medium at a concentration of 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin mixture. When the cell density reached 90%-95% of the area covering the T75 culture flask, passaging was performed for subsequent experiments. The cells used in this study were P3-P5 passaged cells. To simulate the hypoxic microenvironment within periodontitis, the oxygen concentration in the cell culture environment was adjusted to 0.1% using an anaerobic generator pocket for culturing under hypoxic conditions. The experimental groupings were: normoxic, hypoxic and hypoxic + Rha groups. The hPDLSCs were inoculated into medium-sized culture dishes at a density of 2 × 104/well. After 24 h of cell attachment, the cells were placed in a hypoxic environment for incubation.


#### Cell viability assay
  Inoculate each well of a 96-well plate with 100 µL of cell suspension (~ 4000 cells/well). Add 100 µL of sterile PBS buffer to the grouped outer well plates. After the cells were stabilized, they were treated with different concentrations of Rha (0 µM, 100 µM, 200 µM, 300 µM, 400 µM, 500 µM) for 24 h. After the drug treatment, 10µL of CCK-8 solution was added and the cells were incubated in an incubator for 2–3 h. The cell suspensions were measured at 450 nm using an enzyme marker. The absorbance value at 450 nm was measured using an enzyme meter.


#### EDU assay
  EdU incorporation assays were performed using the BeyoClickTM EdU Cell Proliferation Kit. The experiments were performed by following the kit instructions. An appropriate amount of hPDLSCs were inoculated overnight in 6-well culture plates and the cells were treated differently according to the experimental grouping. After the EdU incubation was completed, the cells were immobilized in 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by the addition of Triton X-100 and incubated at room temperature for 5–10 min. The Click reaction solution was prepared according to the instructions and after the Click reaction was completed the Hoechst 33,342 solution to stain the cell nuclei. Finally, the fluorescence images were observed under the microscope and the number of red fluorescent cells and the number of blue fluorescent cells were counted.


#### Cell scratch assay
  The appropriate amount of hPDLSCs were inoculated into a 6-well plate and when the cell density reached 90%, the tip of a pipette with a range of 1000 µL was pipetted perpendicular to the bottom of the plate with the help of a straightedge to make a “cross” line to produce cell scratches. The old medium was removed and cell debris was removed using a sterile PBS buffer. Under the inverted fluorescence microscope, different fields of view were photographed and the labeled sites were recorded. 0 h and 24 h migration were photographed and the migration area of the cells to the “cell-free zone” was calculated.


#### Transwell migration assay
  200µL of hPDLSCs were inoculated in the Transwell upper chamber by the experimental group and 600µL of DMEM medium (containing 20% fetal bovine serum) was added to the lower chamber. 500 µL of 4% paraformaldehyde was added to each well for cell fixation for 15 min and the cells were stained with 500 µL of 0.1% crystal violet for 30 min. Different fields of view were randomized and the number of cells penetrating the upper chamber to the lower chamber in each field of view was counted.


#### Alkaline phosphatase staining
  After 7 days of osteogenic induction using an osteogenic induction medium, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature, washed 2–3 times with PBS, prepared by the instructions of BCIP/NBT Alkaline Phosphatase Chromogenic Kit, stained for 5–30 min and photographed and recorded for staining under inverted microscope observation.


#### Alizarin red staining
  After the cells underwent osteogenic induction for 21 days, the old medium was removed, the cells were washed 2–3 times with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, washed twice with PBS, stained with perylene red solution for 15 min and finally rinsed 3 times with PBS and the formation of calcium nodules was observed and photographed for recording by inverted microscope.


#### Western blotting
  Cells from each experimental group were placed on ice, lysed for 15 min by adding an appropriate amount of RIPA lysate containing protease inhibitors, physically lysed using an ultrasonic cell breaker for 1 min, centrifuged and the total cellular protein lysate was collected. The protein concentration was determined by the BCA Protein Assay kit (Thermofisher, USA), followed by purification using a 10% SDS polyacrylamide gel. Then the protein-free rapid containment solution was used for containment, the primary antibody was diluted to the working solution concentration with PBST and incubated with the protein at room temperature for 2 h. The secondary antibody was diluted to the working solution concentration with PBST and the protein was incubated in a constant temperature shaker for 1 h. Finally, the grayscale values of the protein bands were calculated and analyzed by ImageJ software.


### Animal experiment

#### Construction of a periodontitis model and experimental design
  In this study, we used 6-week-old male SD rats weighing between 180 and 220 g. All rats were purchased from Henan Skibbes Bio-technology Co. They were housed in a controlled environment at a temperature of 22 ± 3 °C under a 12 h/12 h light/dark cycle with humidity between 50% and 60% while being fed with water and food. After a one-week acclimatisation period, rats were anaesthetised by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital. After anaesthesia, the maxillary first molar was ligated using a 0.2 mm orthodontic wire to establish a ligature-induced periodontitis (LIP) model. They were randomly divided into three groups: A:Control (healthy; n = 10), B: Periodontitis (n  = 10; C: Periodontitis + Rha (n = 10). Rha (20 mg/kg) gavage treatment for Periodontitis + Rha group. After four weeks, the rats were treated and relevant tissues were collected for analysis. The animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Committee for Animal Protection of Jiangxi University of Traditional Chinese Medicine and followed the national guidelines for animal protection under license No. JZLLSC20250310. All experiments were conducted in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. The report of this study was conducted according to ARRIVE guidelines.


#### Micro-CT
  The maxillae were collected post-euthanasia, fixed in formalin, and examined by microcomputed tomography (Micro-CT; Engineering Technology Research Institute Co., Ltd., Xidian University, Xi’an, Shaanxi, China), followed by histological analysis to assess key bone-related parameters including bone volume, mineral density, and alveolar bone resorption.


#### Histological staining
  Gingival and jawbone tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Solarbio, Beijing, China), dehydrated sequentially using graded ethanol, transparent in xylene, subsequently embedded in paraffin, and cut into 5 μm thick serial sections.Staining methods used for histological analysis included Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E), Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and Masson’s Trichrome Stain.

  For H&E staining, the sections were dewaxed and hydrated, then sequentially subjected to hematoxylin staining of the nucleus, hydrochloric acid alcohol differentiation, reblue treatment, and then staining of the cytoplasm with eosin, and finally the sections were sealed by ethanol dehydration and xylene transparency. After completion of staining, the tissue structure was observed under a microscope (Olympus BX53, Tokyo, Japan) to assess the epithelial thickness, connective tissue characteristics and inflammatory cell infiltration of the gingiva, as well as the morphology of the bone trabeculae and distribution of osteoblasts in the jaws.

  For immunohistochemical staining, sections were antigenically repaired and blocked with 3% H₂O₂ to seal endogenous peroxidase, followed by 5% BSA to block non-specific binding. Sections were subsequently incubated in primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. On the following day, HRP-labelled secondary antibody was added, and the nuclei were stained by DAB and re-stained with hematoxylin. Positive staining showed a yellow-brown signal.

  Masson staining was used to observe collagen fibres and bone matrix composition. After the sections were dewaxed and hydrated, the nuclei were stained sequentially with ferric hematoxylin, the cytoplasm was stained with cochineal, the phosphomolybdic acid was differentiated, and the collagen was stained with aniline blue or bright green, and then finally dehydrated, transparent and sealed. The collagen fibres were blue, the cytoplasm red and the nuclei black.


### Statistical analysis
  Web-based pharmacology data were collected and synthesized through a public data repository. All the above experimental data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). SPSS 26.0 and GraphPad Prism 8 software were used for analysis and plotting. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for multiple group comparisons. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.


## Results

### Core target screening, PPI network mapping and enrichment analysis
  A total of 1861 periodontitis-associated targets were obtained from GeneCards, DisGeNET and OMIM databases and 86 Rha-associated targets were obtained by querying SwissADME and SuperPred databases. The Rha targets were intersected with periodontitis-related targets and the intersecting targets were identified by drawing Venn diagrams through the microbiology platform (Fig. 2A). The obtained 38 intersecting genes were uploaded to the STRING database for analysis to generate the PPI network. The results of the protein interaction network graph were imported into Cytoscape 3.8.0 software and a total of 37 nodes and 158 edges of the intertarget interaction network were screened and obtained and the topological parameters of the network were analyzed, as shown in Fig. The larger the node, the higher the degree value and the higher the probability that it is a core target (Fig. 2B). As shown in Table 2, we screened the six target genes with the highest degree value, BC and CC: HIF1A, EGFR, PTGS2, HSP90AA1, TLR4 and NFKB1. The analysis of Rha target parameters for periodontitis is shown in Table 1. In this analyzed network HIF1A had a connectivity of 23, a mediocrity of 223.539 and a tightness of 0.0196. the predicted HIF1A is the most important target of Rha to modulate periodontitis. To further explore the potential mechanism of action of Rha in the treatment of periodontitis, we imported drug-disease cross-targets into the Netscape data platform to analyze the signaling pathways of Rha in regulating periodontitis-related targets. The results of the GO enrichment analysis are presented in the form of a bubble chart visualization (Fig. 2C). For the top 50 genes with significant levels of p < 0.01, we selected three entries of Biological process (BP), Molecular Function (MF) and Cellular Component (CC) and each of the top 10 enrichment items with significant level p < 0.05 were selected. 0.05, we found that Rha could regulate the progression of periodontitis by modulating biological processes such as cell migration and hypoxia response. KEGG analysis (Fig. 2D) identified the HIF-1 signaling pathway as the signaling pathway involved in the regulation of periodontitis by Rha. Based on the enrichment results and previous studies, we speculate that the HIF1A gene and its representative hypoxia signaling pathway may be a potential key mechanism by which Rha regulates the development of periodontitis and bone remodeling.

### Molecular docking
  Based on the analysis of network pharmacology data, we performed molecular docking of the treated HIF1A (PDB ID:2ILM) protein structure as a receptor and Rha as a ligand. If the binding energy is < 0 kJ-mol−1 and the molecules can bind to each other through multiple hydrogen bonds, the compound is indicated to spontaneously bind to the target protein43. The molecular docking results showed (Table 2) that the binding energy of HIF1A to Rha was − 4.46 kcal/mol. Figure 3 shows the optimal docking image of the receptor and ligand after visualization. The molecular docking results of HIF1A and Rha were visualized using PyMOL 2.3 software and it was found that they interacted with each other through multiple hydrogen bonds and Fig. 3A showed that Rha formed seven hydrogen bonds with residues ASN58, HIS313, GLN314, THR302, PRO301 and ALA300 in HIF1A. Fig. 3B shows the pocket structure of receptor-ligand docking, as shown, the compounds were able to bind well into the depths of the protein cavity and form a good fit with the protein. Molecular docking simulations showed that Rha can interact well with HIF1A.

### Effects of Rha on the proliferation and migration capacity of hPDLSCs and osteogenic differentiation capacity under hypoxic conditions
  The results of CCK-8 experiments showed (Fig. 4A) that the viability of periodontal membrane stem cells cultured under a hypoxia environment for 24 h was inhibited and significant compared with the normoxic group; the cell viability was significantly higher in the 100 µM Rha group compared with the hypoxic group and the cells were significantly inhibited from growth when the drug concentration was increased step by step. This indicated that Rha could significantly promote the proliferation of hPDLSCs in the hypoxia environment, but inhibited their growth beyond the safe concentration range. Based on the results shown, we chose 100 µM as the drug concentration for Rha to stimulate hPDLSCs in subsequent experiments. EdU can be doped into DNA strands during DNA synthesis and can fluorescently label the DNA of proliferating cells. Therefore, the proliferation of individual cells can be assessed by the EdU assay. As shown in Fig. 4B, when cells were labeled with EdU, the proliferation rate of cells treated with hypoxia was significantly lower than that of cells in the normoxic group and when hPDLSCs in a hypoxia environment were treated with Rha, there was a significant and statistically significant difference in proliferation when compared with the hypoxic group. This is consistent with the results of CCK-8 assay. The migration ability of hPDLSCs were detected using the cell scratch method and the Transwell method. The results in Fig. 4C showed that after 24 h of hypoxic culture, a significant decrease in the ability of periodontal membrane stem cells to move toward the middle scratched blank area could be observed, indicating that hypoxia inhibited the migration of periodontal membrane stem cells. When 100 µM Rha was added to the cells cultured in the hypoxic environment, the migration ability of the cells was enhanced. The results of the Transwell assay showed the same results as those of the cell scratches, which proved that Rha was able to enhance the migration ability of the hPDLSCs in the hypoxic environment (Fig. 4D). The results of ALP staining showed that the staining in the hypoxic condition was significantly lighter than that in the normoxic condition, which indicated that in the hypoxic environment, the migration capacity of the hPDLSCs was significantly reduced. Periodontal membrane stem cells had decreased osteogenic differentiation ability. When treated with Rha, the color was darker compared to that under hypoxia without the addition of the drug, indicating that Rha could reverse the hypoxia-induced impairment of the osteogenic differentiation ability of periodontal membrane stem cells and promote the osteogenic differentiation of periodontal membrane stem cells under hypoxic conditions (Fig. 4E). Consistent with the ALP staining results, the calcified nodules of hPDLSCs cultured by hypoxia were lower than those of the normoxic culture group were reversed by Rha (Fig. 4F).

### Effect of Rha on protein expression of genes related to proliferation, migration and osteogenesis in hPDLSCs
  Western blotting results showed (Fig. 5) that the protein expression of PCNA, CXCR4 and OCN, genes related to proliferation, migration and osteogenesis in hypoxia-induced cells was significantly lower than that of the normoxic group at 6 h, 12 h and 24 h, but the protein expression of HIF1A was stably higher than that of the normoxic group at 6 h, 12 h and 24 h (Fig. 5A). Under hypoxia, Rha significantly promoted the protein expression of PCNA, CXCR4 and OCN and inhibited the protein expression of HIF1A (Fig. 5B). These results indicated that Rha effectively inhibited the expression of HIF1A in a hypoxic environment and promoted the proliferation, migration and osteogenic differentiation of hPDLSCs.

### Rhaponticin attenuates damage to periodontal supporting tissues in rats with periodontitis
  The rat model of periodontitis established by ligation method was divided into three groups into rearing. The mice were divided into three groups for rearing (Fig. 6A). (Fig. 2A). Four weeks after ligation, the alveolar bone of the rats was examined by micro CT. The results showed that the measurement of CEJ-ABC in the Periodontitis group was significantly higher than that in the control group, which indicated that the periodontitis rat model was constructed successfully (Fig. 6B and C). To investigate the effect of Rhaponticin on the alveolar bone, rats with periodontitis were gavaged with Rhaponticin for four weeks. The results showed that the CEJ-ABC values of the Periodontitis + Rha group were significantly lower than those of the Periodontitis group, which indicated that Rhaponticin was effective in reducing the degree of damage to the alveolar bone (Fig. 6B and C). In addition, we analysed several important osteological indicators of alveolar bone, including BMD, BV/TV, Tb.Sp, Tb.N, (Fig. 6D–G). Analysis of the data showed that Rhaponticin significantly altered these osteological indices to be reduced or elevated in periodontitis, and to some extent showed potential for the treatment of osteoporosis.

### Rhaponticin inhibits the expression of inflammatory factors and HIF1A in the gingiva of periodontitis rats
  The expression level of HIF1A was increased in inflamed gingival tissues compared to healthy gingival tissues. However, the expression of HIF1A decreased after the intervention of Rha (Fig. 7A). H&E staining results showed the presence of a large number of inflammatory cells infiltrated in subepithelial connective tissue in gingival tissues of Periodontitis group. But in Periodontitis + Rha group showed less number of inflammatory cells present, which indicated that Rhaponticin was effective in suppressing localised gingival inflammation (Fig. 7B). Masson’s trichrome staining showed that in the Periodontitis + Rha group, the gingival tissues showed broken and disorganised collagen fibres, reduced staining intensity, and reduced collagen content in the localised areas; this was accompanied by a large number of inflammatory cells infiltrating the gingival tissues, as well as vasodilatation and oedema. However, in Periodontitis + Rha group, the distribution of collagen fibres in gingival tissues was significantly restored compared with Periodontitis group, and the arrangement tended to be regular and the inflammatory reaction was reduced (Fig. 7C), which suggests that Rhaponticin has a certain restorative effect on gingival tissues damage.

## Discussion
  Periodontitis is now a global epidemic, with more than 1 billion people worldwide suffering from severe periodontitis by 2021 and it is projected that by 2050, more than 1.5 billion people will suffer from severe periodontitis and more than 660 million people will be edentulous. In the same year, approximately 130.23 million people in the Chinese population will suffer from edentulism, accounting for 19.67% of the global edentulous population44. Therefore, public health is under serious threat and its prevention and treatment is urgent. The main goal of periodontal therapy is to control the further progression of periodontal inflammation while restoring the structure and function of damaged periodontal tissues45. Even though individuals undergo conventional periodontal treatment including tooth cleaning, root planing and standard periodontal surgery, restorative regeneration of periodontal tissue is still limited46. Stem cells have attracted much attention in regenerative medicine because of their strong regenerative capacity at the level of tissue regeneration and their immunomodulatory properties in organic inflammation47–49. hPDLSCs are a type of mesenchymal stem cells50, which can differentiate into various cell types such as osteoblasts and odontogenic osteocytes, as well as strong clonogenic ability and self-renewal potential. With the ability to differentiate into various cell types such as osteoblasts and osteoclasts, as well as strong clonogenic capacity and self-renewal potential, is considered to be a potential stem cell population for the effective repair of periodontium51. Studies have shown that enhanced chronic inflammation and localized inflammatory tissues cause oxygen depletion, which can lead to the formation of a hypoxic microenvironment in inflamed periodontal tissues52. hPDLSCs cultured under long-term hypoxic conditions exhibit abnormal morphology and organelles involved in oxygen metabolism, reduced proliferation and diminished osteogenic differentiation. Most of the current studies have addressed inflammation as a cause to investigate the pathogenesis of periodontitis and a few studies have focused on the pathogenesis of hypoxia-induced periodontitis.

  As the only transcription factor that can respond to hypoxia, HIF1A is stably expressed under hypoxic conditions and regulates its transcription by binding to target genes, playing a wide range of physiological and pathological roles in inflammatory response53, cell cycle regulation54, apoptosis55 and migration56. In this study, we screened the possible targets and enriched pathways of Rha action in periodontitis by PPI network analysis and pathway enrichment analysis, in which the core genes mainly included inflammatory factors such as HIF1A, PTGS2, etc.; angiogenic factors such as AKT1, VEGFA, etc.; and pathway factors such as EGFR, HSP90AA1, TLR4, NFKB1, etc. The screened pathways included signaling pathways such as inflammation and hypoxia, all of which are closely related to periodontitis and bone remodeling, reflecting the potential role of Rha in periodontitis treatment. In addition, the importance of both the HIF1A gene and the hypoxia signaling pathway was confirmed by the screening results of the core target genes and the KEGG pathway enrichment results. The binding strength and interaction of Rha as a ligand with the core target genes were confirmed based on the binding energy data obtained in molecular docking and 3D visual molecular modeling further confirmed the existence of a stable binding relationship with the core genes. These results emphasize the feasibility of the interaction of Rha with HIF1A and the potential mechanism in the treatment of periodontitis and the stability of the interaction provides a solid molecular basis for Rha as a potential therapeutic agent for periodontitis.

  At the cellular functional level, this study focused on the proliferation, migration and osteogenic differentiation capacity of hPDLSCs under hypoxic conditions. PCNA is a widely expressed nuclear protein that plays an important role in the cell cycle, especially in DNA replication and repair57.CXCR4 plays a key role in cell proliferation migration and angiogenesis and is one of the most important chemokine receptors58 OCN is an important marker of cellular osteogenic differentiation, which is highly expressed and stable in the process of osteogenesis, with strong specificity59. In this study, ALP and ARS staining became lighter and PCNA, CXCR4 and OCN protein expression decreased in a hypoxic environment, while HIF-1α was stably and highly expressed, which proved that hypoxia led to the weakening of human hPDLSCs’ proliferation, migratory ability and osteogenic differentiation ability. However, some studies have reported that hypoxia-induced HIF1A has complex effects on MSCs: overexpression of HIF1A promotes cell proliferation under short-term hypoxia and inhibits cell proliferation under long-term hypoxia; under hypoxia, upregulation of HIF1A protects bone marrow MSCs from H2O2 and oxygen deprivation, which inhibits oxidative stress leading to cell apoptosis and autophagy60. High expression of HIF-1α under hypoxic conditions promotes the proliferation, migration and osteogenic differentiation of MSCs61; In a mouse model of periodontitis cultured under chronic intermittent hypoxic conditions, the reduction of HIF expression may lead to a decrease in the expression of osteogenesis-related genes as well as MDM21, which in turn affects the regenerative capacity of alveolar bone in periodontitis rats, resulting in a delayed regeneration level. This phenomenon suggests that changes in HIF expression levels directly affect the regeneration efficiency of alveolar bone62. The seemingly contradictory roles in stem cells may stem from differences in multiple intervention conditions in actual studies. In vitro, cell culture provides a relatively stable microenvironment, but its properties differ significantly from those of the in vivo environment. Certain properties that are apparent in vivo can be mimicked by adjusting the microenvironment. For example, HIF-1α is highly susceptible to degradation63when cells are reintroduced from a hypoxic state to a normoxic environment and its hypoxia-induced effects can be rapidly reversed. Therefore, the adverse effects of hypoxia on cells may be related to the specific growth environment as well as the degree and duration of hypoxia.

  In previous studies, we have found that Rhaponticin can target bind HIF1A and inhibit its expression64. Based on the above background, the present study simulated a very hypoxic environment close to the base of a severe periodontitis lesion and was given a longer duration of hypoxic treatment. To further demonstrate that Rha can promote periodontal stem cell proliferation, migration ability and osteogenic differentiation ability by modulating HIF-1 A and thereby promoting periodontal stem cell proliferation, we demonstrated by CCK8 experiments that 100 µM Rha could promote human periodontal stem cell proliferation under hypoxic environment. We also confirmed that Rha significantly enhanced the migration ability as well as the osteogenic differentiation ability of human hPDLSCs by scratch assay, Transwell assay, ALP staining and ARS.WB assay confirmed that Rha decreased the protein expression of HIF-1α in hPDLSCs and promoted the protein expression of PCNA, CXCR4 and OCN. The above studies demonstrated that Rha interacted with HIF1A, inhibited the expression of HIF-1α and promoted the ability of periodontal membrane stem fine proliferation, migration and osteogenic differentiation.

  Exploring the study from histological and imaging perspectives, Rha also demonstrated a good protective effect on periodontal supporting tissues.HE staining showed that Rha significantly reduced inflammatory cell infiltration and improved gingival tissue structure. Masson staining showed that Rha retarded the degradation of periodontal collagen fibres, which helped to maintain and restore the integrity of periodontal supporting tissues. In the severe periodontitis model, alveolar bone resorption was evident. Imaging assessment showed that the CEJ-ABC distance was shortened in the Rha-treated group, and the abnormalities of bone volume ratio (BV/TV), bone mineral density (BMD), and structural indexes of bone trabeculae were significantly improved, suggesting that it played a positive role in slowing down bone resorption and promoting bone reconstruction. The histological and imaging results corroborated each other, fully demonstrating the potential efficacy of Rha on periodontal supporting tissue protection and bone recovery. Immunohistochemistry results showed that in the periodontitis group, the HIF1A protein expression level was significantly elevated, whereas the HIF1A expression was significantly reduced in the Rha intervention group, showing a trend of change consistent with the degree of inflammation and tissue destruction. This result corroborated with the Western blot results, suggesting that Rha may alleviate the chronic inflammatory response driven by hypoxia by down-regulating HIF1A expression, which in turn reduces tissue damage and promotes periodontal tissue repair.

  At the mechanistic level, studies have been conducted to reveal multiple mechanisms of action of Rha in bone diseases. In a mechanistic study of the effects of Rha on osteoclast differentiation and function, the results showed that the expression of Nox1 was inhibited, whereas the expression of antioxidant enzymes, such as SOD-2 and HO-1, was enhanced, suggesting that the antioxidant activity of Rha was manifested through the reduction of ROS production and the enhancement of ROS depletion. In addition, Rha significantly inhibited MAPKs, NF-κB and intracellular Ca2+ oscillatory pathways and these changes resulted in the inactivation of NFATc1, which led to a decrease in the downstream expression of integrin β3, c-Fos, cathepsin K and Atp6v0d2, as detected by qPCR and Western blot assays65. These results suggest that Rha can effectively inhibit RANKL-regulated osteoclast formation and bone resorption. Other animal experiments have shown that ovariectomized (OVX) rats were treated with Rha by oral tube feeding of Rha for 3 months showed enhanced expression of ACP, BGP and E2, as well as improved levels of creatinine, phosphorus, TRAP, calcium and serum inflammatory markers and markedly enhanced femoral trabecular junctions and richer bone tissues observed microscopically21. The above studies further validate the protection of Rha on bone tissue.

  Although this study systematically demonstrated the therapeutic potential of Rhaponticin for periodontitis at multiple levels, several limitations remain. Firstly, we employed the AnaeroPack™ MicroAero anaerobic pouch system to rapidly establish a 0.1% oxygen microenvironment within a closed container66. While this oxygen concentration is lower than the local oxygen tension commonly reported in periodontal pockets, previous studies have indicated that oxygen levels at the pocket base can drop as low as 5 mmHg (~ 0.66% oxygen), and even lower in severe periodontitis cases32. Therefore, we consider this setup relevant for modeling extreme hypoxia associated with severe periodontal inflammation.

  In addition, a previous study utilizing a 20 mg/kg/day dose of Rhaponticin as a pharmacological intervention did not evaluate its in vivo toxicity. In the present study, we employed the same dosage to assess the safety profile of Rhaponticin in rats. Histopathological analysis revealed no significant damage to major organs including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, suggesting that Rhaponticin is well-tolerated at this dosage and may have a favorable safety profile for the treatment of periodontitis.However, the current validation remains confined to in vitro experiments and animal models, with a lack of clinical sample analysis and long-term safety data. The dose–response relationship, optimal route of administration, and metabolic stability of Rhaponticin in humans are still unclear. Further confirmation through multicenter, large-scale, and long-term clinical trials is necessary. These studies should involve comprehensive assessments, including body weight monitoring, hematological tests, and biochemical analyses, to systematically evaluate the long-term efficacy and safety of Rhaponticin.Furthermore, although this study identified HIF1A as a key target within inflammation- and hypoxia-associated pathways, its full upstream regulatory network and downstream signaling mechanisms remain to be elucidated, necessitating further in-depth investigation.


## Conclusion
  In summary, this study integrated network pharmacology predictions with experimental validations to comprehensively demonstrate the therapeutic potential of Rhaponticin for periodontitis at multiple levels, including molecular mechanisms, cellular behaviors, and tissue repair. Our findings indicate that Rhaponticin may exert synergistic multi-target effects primarily through modulation of the HIF1A-related pathway. Both in vitro and in vivo results confirmed that Rhaponticin effectively mitigates periodontal inflammation, inhibits bone tissue degradation, and promotes alveolar bone regeneration, thereby providing a solid theoretical foundation and experimental evidence for its clinical application. Moving forward, with further optimization of dosage, thorough safety assessments, and deeper mechanistic investigations, Rhaponticin holds promise to become a natural anti-inflammatory agent with well-defined mechanisms and multi-pathway targeting, offering a novel therapeutic strategy for managing chronic oral diseases such as periodontitis.
