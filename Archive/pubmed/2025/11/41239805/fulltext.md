# An AMPK‐Centric Strategy: Foenumoside B Coordinates PINK1/Parkin‐Mediated Mitophagy With Nrf2/HO‐1 Antioxidant Signaling to Resolve Oxidative Stress in Radiation‐Induced Lung Injury


## ABSTRACT
  Radiation‐induced lung injury (RILI) is a dose‐limiting toxicity of thoracic radiotherapy driven by mitochondrial damage–mediated oxidative stress, persistent DNA damage, and senescence, which together destabilize the alveolar–interstitial niche and promote fibrosis. Here, we identify Foenumoside B (FSB), a natural saponin from Lysimachia foenum‐graecum, as a dual‐action modulator that preserves mitochondrial quality and restores systemic redox homeostasis to attenuate RILI. In a murine total lung irradiation model (20 Gy), oral FSB (10 mg/kg/day) mitigated inflammation and fibrotic remodeling, improved pulmonary mechanics and arterial blood gases, and exhibited no overt hepatorenal toxicity. Mechanistically, FSB activated PINK1/Parkin‐dependent mitophagy in alveolar epithelial cells. Pharmacologic autophagy blockade (3‐MA) or PINK1 silencing abrogated these benefits, increasing mtROS, γH2AX foci, comet tail moments, and p16/p21 expression, and reducing cell viability, thereby confirming mitophagy as indispensable for FSB's cytoprotection. Molecular docking analysis demonstrates that FSB binds to the catalytic α1 and α2 subunits of AMPK, thereby significantly increasing the p‐AMPK/AMPK ratio. Upon pharmacologic inhibition of AMPK, the FSB‐mediated improvements in mitophagy, mitochondrial function, redox homeostasis, and tissue protection are markedly attenuated, indicating that FSB's biological effects rely on AMPK activation. FSB also restored Nrf2/HO‐1 signaling and antioxidant capacity. Co‐IP/ChIP showed AMPK directly associates with Nrf2, enhances its phosphorylation and ARE binding at the HO‐1 promoter, and weakens Keap1‐mediated degradation. Overall, FSB suppresses reactive oxygen species at their mitochondrial source and, through AMPK‐driven mitophagy and Nrf2 activation, enhances endogenous antioxidant defenses, providing a translatable strategy to preserve alveolar architecture during radiotherapy.


## Introduction
  Radiotherapy remains a cornerstone in managing thoracic malignancies, including lung, breast, and esophageal cancers. However, the radiosensitivity of pulmonary tissue imposes critical constraints, with 10%–20% of patients developing radiation‐induced lung injury (RILI) of variable severity [1]. This dose‐limiting complication not only compromises oncological outcomes but also elevates mortality risks in severe cases, particularly when progressing to irreversible radiation‐induced pulmonary fibrosis [2]. Emerging evidence highlights cellular senescence as a pivotal driver of pathological remodeling in RILI [3]. Ionizing radiation induces senescence programs in alveolar type 2 epithelial cells (AEC2s) the tissue‐specific cells responsible for alveolar homeostasis thereby compromising their regenerative capacity while allowing persistent occupancy of the stem cell niche [4]. This dual dysfunction disrupts alveolar repair and perpetuates tissue destabilization. Crucially, ionizing radiation inflicts extensive DNA damage, including double‐strand breaks (DSBs) and oxidative base modifications, which activate persistent DNA damage response (DDR) pathways [5]. Persistent DNA damage foci and consequent cellular senescence jointly drive radiation‐induced injury development through sustained DDR signaling and pathological senescence‐associated secretory phenotype (SASP) amplification [6, 7]. Concurrently, senescent AEC2s orchestrate a pathological triad through persistent emission of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and SASP components, thereby activating interconnected pro‐inflammatory and pro‐fibrotic signaling cascades [3]. This self‐perpetuating oxidative stress–inflammation–fibrosis axis progressively dismantles the alveolar‐interstitial niche, mediating replacement of functional pulmonary parenchyma with aberrant extracellular matrix deposition, ultimately culminating in fibrotic remodeling of lung parenchyma.

  Mechanistically, radiation‐induced damage involves core processes such as DNA damage, mitochondrial dysfunction, and a cascade of oxidative stress responses. Ionizing radiation induces ROS generation through direct ionization where energy deposition in biomolecules causes radical formation and indirect ionization, which involves water radiolysis‐mediated free radical chain reactions. This synergistic action results in excessive ROS overproduction and accumulation while simultaneously impairing antioxidant defenses, culminating in pathological oxidative stress that propagates tissue damage. Mitochondria, as the central organelles for cellular energy metabolism and apoptosis regulation, are both the primary sites of ROS production and major targets of oxidative stress. Following radiation exposure, mitochondrial dysfunction can manifest as a loss of membrane potential, excessive generation of ROS, and the opening of the permeability transition pore, triggering either cellular senescence, apoptosis, or necrosis. To mitigate the detrimental effects of damaged mitochondria, cells activate mitophagy, a selective autophagy process that clears dysfunctional mitochondria. This process primarily relies on signaling pathways such as PINK1/Parkin and BNIP3/NIX. When mitochondrial damage occurs, PINK1 accumulates on the outer mitochondrial membrane and recruits Parkin, an E3 ubiquitin ligase, which ubiquitinates various mitochondrial surface proteins. These ubiquitinated proteins serve as signals for the autophagic machinery to engulf and degrade the damaged mitochondria. Additionally, BNIP3 and NIX, which are pro‐apoptotic factors, facilitate the recognition of damaged mitochondria by promoting their engulfment by autophagosomes. By selectively removing dysfunctional mitochondria, mitophagy plays a crucial role in preserving cellular homeostasis, maintaining energy production, and preventing excessive ROS accumulation, thereby protecting cells from radiation‐induced damage.

  AMPK signaling emerges as a central regulator of mitochondrial homeostasis and radioprotection in this pathological cascade [8]. Its organization‐protective effects demonstrate significant conservation across various organs [9, 10, 11]. Recent studies indicate that AMPK activation triggers distinct protective mechanisms in different forms of acute lung injury [12, 13]. In sepsis‐induced lung injury, AMPK activation attenuates NLRP3 inflammasome‐dependent pyroptosis by enhancing autophagic clearance [12]. In the lung ischemia–reperfusion injury model, AMPK activation reduces oxidative damage by promoting Nrf2 nuclear translocation and inhibiting ROS generation [13]. Similar protection occurs in cardiac tissue through AMPK‐mediated suppression of oxidative apoptosis [14], while in brain tissue, AMPK/Tet2 signaling maintains neurogenesis after irradiation [15]. Despite extensive reports of AMPK‐mediated cytoprotection, whether AMPK affords protection in RILI and the precise underlying mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated. As a metabolic sensor activated by radiation‐induced energy stress, AMPK activation mitigates mitochondrial dysfunction via PINK1‐Parkin‐mediated mitophagy, while concurrently suppressing ROS accumulation through Nrf2‐dependent antioxidant pathways in damaged tissue [16, 17, 18]. The potent corrective capacity of AMPK in mitigating oxidative stress underscores its significant potential in the development of therapeutic strategies for RILI. Although existing AMPK activators like metformin show preclinical efficacy [12, 19], plant‐derived compounds such as Foenumoside B (FSB) may offer superior specificity in activating the AMPK/PINK1‐Parkin axis to mitigate pulmonary senescence and fibrosis. FSB activates AMPK signaling and inhibits the production of pro‐inflammatory cytokines in adipose tissue induced by a high‐fat diet, indicating its protective role against insulin resistance [20]. Furthermore, the plant Lysimachia foenum‐graecum, from which FSB is derived, has been confirmed to possess significant antioxidant activity [21]. Therefore, investigating the efficacy of FSB in RILI represents a promising and underexplored therapeutic strategy that warrants systematic evaluation.


## Materials and Methods

### Drugs and Reagents
  All chemicals and reagents were of analytical grade. FSB, Compound C (CC), 3‐methyladenine (3‐MA) were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Fluorescent probes included MitoTracker Red CMXRos and LysoTracker Green DND‐26 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), 2′, 7′‐Dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH‐DA) and Dihydroethidium (DHE) (Sigma‐Aldrich), JC‐1 mitochondrial membrane potential assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), DAPI (Sigma‐Aldrich), and ethidium bromide for the comet assay (Sigma‐Aldrich). Molecular biology and biochemical reagents comprised TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) for RNA extraction, RIPA buffer (Beyotime; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for protein lysis, Human AMPK alpha‐1, 2 Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for ELISA, BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for protein quantification, SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for qPCR, ATP Luminescent Cell Viability Assay Kit (Yeason) for the measurement of ATP production capacity. Tissue fixation and histology reagents included 10% neutral‐buffered formalin (Solarbio, Beijing, China), Hematoxylin & Eosin staining kit (Solarbio), and Masson's trichrome staining kit (Sigma‐Aldrich). Primary antibodies used for Western blot and immunofluorescence were: anti‐Caspase‐3 (A11021), anti‐Bax (A19684), anti‐Nrf2 (A3577), anti‐HO‐1 (CST #43966), anti‐PINK1 (A7131), anti‐Parkin (CST #4211), anti‐phospho‐AMPK (Thr172; CST #2535), anti‐AMPKα (CST #2532), anti‐p16 (A23450), anti‐p21 (CST #2947), anti‐KEAP1 (CST #8047) and anti‐γH2AX (Ser139, CST #9718). All antibodies were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA) or Abclonal (Wuhan, China) and used at manufacturer‐recommended dilutions.


### Experimental Animals
  All animal care and experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the National Health Commission Key Laboratory of Radiobiology at Jilin University, and all experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Jilin University (ethics number: 2025–03–006). The mice were housed in a specific pathogen‐free environment with controlled temperature and humidity under a 12/12 h light/dark cycle, with ad libitum access to water and food. Male C57BL/6 mice (wild type, 6–8 weeks old) were purchased from Beijing SPF Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China). Mice were randomly divided into different experimental groups.

  In the total lung irradiation (TLI) group, a total dose of 20 Gy was delivered at a dose rate of 2.0 Gy/min. FSB was administered at a dosage of 10 mg/kg/day via gavage starting 1 day prior to irradiation and continuing until tissue collection at 56 days post‐irradiation, as described in previous publications [22, 23]. CC, an AMPK competitive inhibitor, was administered via intraperitoneal injection at a dose of 25 mg/kg once per week [24]. The control group received the same volume of solvent.


### Histological Analysis
  Lung tissues were collected and fixed in 10% formalin, followed by paraffin embedding. For H&E staining, sections (5 μm thick) were stained with hematoxylin and eosin following standard protocols. Masson's trichrome staining was performed to assess pulmonary fibrosis. According to previous studies, lung injury was evaluated based on two parameters: inflammatory cell infiltration and alveolar damage in lung tissues, whereas the Ashcroft scoring system was employed to assess the degree of pulmonary fibrosis by examining the extent of fibrotic changes and collagen deposition [25, 26].


### Cell Culture
  BEAS‐2B cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO₂. They were maintained in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. To simulate an in vitro model of RILI, cells were exposed to a total dose of 6 Gy radiation at a dose rate of 1.0 Gy/min. FBS was administered at a concentration of 10 μg/mL 1 h prior to radiation exposure. Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) was applied at a concentration of 200 μM for 6 h to stimulate ROS accumulation. Additionally, 3‐MA, an autophagy inhibitor, was administered at a concentration of 5 mM for 6 h to exert its inhibitory effect. Cells were transfected with si‐PINK1 at a final concentration of 50 nM using Lipofectamine 2000 in Opti‐MEM medium, following the manufacturer's protocol during a 6 h incubation period. After transfection, the medium was replaced with complete culture medium, and the cells were incubated for an additional 48 h prior to downstream analyses.


### Western Blotting
  Tissues and cells were lysed using RIPA buffer with protease inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA protein assay kit. Equal amounts of protein (30–50 μg) were subjected to SDS‐PAGE and subsequently transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. Membranes were probed with specific antibodies overnight at 4°C. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies, and protein bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent. Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ software.


### Cell Viability Assays
  Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.


### Comet Assay
  The comet assay was performed to evaluate DNA strand breaks. Isolated cells were embedded in agarose gel on slides, lysed, and subjected to electrophoresis. Slides were stained with ethidium bromide and analyzed under a fluorescence microscope, quantifying the percentage of DNA in the comet tails versus the head.


### Quantitative PCR (qPCR)
  Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol method. cDNA was synthesized and amplified using SYBR Green Master Mix. Relative mRNA expression levels were quantified using the ΔΔCt method. The primers used in the present study are listed in Table 1.


### Mitochondria and Lysosome Staining
  Cells were labeled with MitoTracker Red for mitochondria and LysoTracker Green for lysosomes following intervention. Subsequently, nuclei were stained with DAPI. Fluorescence imaging was performed using a confocal laser scanning microscope with appropriate excitation and emission wavelengths to capture mitochondrial, lysosomal, and nuclear signals.


### ROS Staining
  Tissue sections were incubated with DHE probe, while cultured cells were treated with DCFH‐DA probe, following the manufacturer's instructions to measure overall ROS levels. Fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope, and the fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.


### JC‐1 Staining
  Mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed using JC‐1 staining. Cells were incubated with JC‐1 for 30 min, and fluorescence imaging was conducted to visualize red aggregates (healthy mitochondria) and green monomers (damaged mitochondria).


### Cell Immunofluorescence Staining
  Cultured cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized using 0.1% Triton X‐100, and blocked with 5% BSA. Cells were then incubated with primary antibodies targeting specific antigens at 4°C overnight, followed by incubation with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies, and counterstained with DAPI to visualize nuclei. Fluorescence images were acquired via confocal microscopy, and subsequent analysis of fluorescence intensity was performed using ImageJ.


### Molecular Docking Simulation
  Protein structures of AMPK α1 (https://www.rcsb.org/sequence/6C9H#A) and AMPK α2 (https://www.rcsb.org/sequence/6B1U#A) were retrieved from the RCSB PDB. Docking of AMPK α1/AMPK α2 with small molecules was performed using the HDOCK server (http://hdock.phys.hust.edu.cn/). Docking results were visualized in PyMOL (version 3.1), and the interaction profiles and putative binding sites of FSB on the AMPK α1 and α2 subunits were displayed.


### Chromatin Immunoprecipitation PCR (ChIP‐PCR)
  ChIP was performed following established protocols. Briefly, irradiated alveolar epithelial cells were cross‐linked with 1% formaldehyde for 15 min, followed by chromatin fragmentation via sonication. Immunoprecipitation utilized anti‐Nrf2 antibody with isotype IgG as a negative control. DNA‐protein complexes were eluted and reverse‐crosslinked at 65°C overnight. Specific HO‐1 promoter regions containing antioxidant response elements (ARE: −3917 to −3908 bp) were amplified using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) with primers: forward: 5′‐AGAGCCTGGGGTTGCTAAGT‐3′, and reverse: 5′‐GGCC GGTCACATTTATGCTC‐3′ [27].


### Co‐Immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP)
  After BCA quantification, cell lysates were incubated with anti‐AMPK antibody conjugated to Protein A/G magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher) at 4°C for 12 h. Negative controls used species‐matched IgG. Immunoprecipitated complexes were resolved by 10% SDS‐PAGE followed by immunoblotting with anti‐Nrf2 and anti‐KEAP1 antibodies.


### Statistical Analysis
  Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Multiple group comparisons were conducted by one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Bonferroni or Tukey post hoc tests, as appropriate. Comparisons between two groups were made using two‐tailed unpaired Student's t‐tests. Statistical significance was defined as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.


## Results

### FSB Ameliorates Radiation‐Induced Lung Injury Through Attenuating Inflammation and Oxidative Stress
  Based on previously published studies and our preliminary experimental data, the dosing regimen and administration frequency of FSB were optimized to ensure maximal therapeutic efficacy while minimizing systemic burden. Comprehensive evaluation of hepatic and renal function, as well as organ histopathology, confirmed that FSB administration did not induce any overt toxicity or structural abnormalities in these tissues (Figure S1A–D). According to the workflow diagram shown in Figure 1A, we performed in vivo modeling in mice and FSB treatment in order to elucidate the potential therapeutic effect of FSB on RILI. Compared to the control group, mice in the TLI group exhibited significant body weight reduction, whereas the FSB‐treated TLI mice showed less body weight loss, indicating that FSB may alleviate the overall systemic disruption caused by total lung irradiation in mice (Figure 1B). H&E staining of mouse lung tissues was performed to evaluate histopathological changes. The results demonstrated that FSB pretreatment significantly ameliorated radiation‐induced alveolar septal edema, alveolar structural damage, and inflammatory cell infiltration in the lungs of mice (Figure 1C,D). Masson staining revealed that FSB simultaneously alleviated abnormal fibrosis and excessive ECM deposition during the repair process of lung tissue (Figure 1E,F). The elevated LDH levels induced by radiation were significantly reduced following FSB treatment. Additionally, MPO levels, which indicate neutrophil infiltration, were notably decreased in the serum of FSB‐treated mice (Figure 1G,H). Additionally, the mRNA expression levels of inflammatory factors Ccl2, IL‐6, IL‐1β, TNF‐α, as well as fibrosis‐related genes α‐Sma and Fibronectin in lung tissues of the FSB treatment group were all significantly lower than those in the TLI group (Figure 1I–N). Moreover, measurements of airway resistance and pulmonary ventilation showed that FSB markedly ameliorated irradiation‐induced pulmonary dysfunction (Figure 1O,P). Arterial blood gas analysis further indicated that the hypoxemia and carbon dioxide retention characteristic of RILI were substantially reversed by FSB (Figure 1Q,R). These findings strongly support a functional therapeutic effect of FSB on lung tissue. To determine whether FSB also confers protection against acute radiation‐induced injury, we advanced the sampling time to 14 days postirradiation (Figure 2A). At this earlier time point, FSB partially mitigated inflammatory exudation and structural damage in the lung (Figure 2B–D). Consistent with these observations, quantification of MPO and LDH levels, together with analysis of inflammatory cytokine transcripts, demonstrated that FSB effectively attenuates the inflammatory response, including neutrophil recruitment (Figure 2E–H). Taken together, these data indicate that FSB, while exhibiting no appreciable toxicity, confers protection across multiple stages of RILI.


### FSB Attenuates Radiation‐Induced Cellular Senescence in Alveolar Epithelial Cells Through p16/p21‐Dependent Pathways
  Given the protective effects of FSB on lung tissue injury and fibrosis, we selected BEAS‐2B cells as the experimental model to investigate its effects on alveolar epithelial cells and the underlying mechanisms. First, we employed the CCK‐8 assay to determine the maximal noncytotoxic concentration of FSB in alveolar epithelial cells. Our findings demonstrated that FSB concentrations exceeding 10 μM in the culture medium significantly suppressed cell viability, providing a rational basis for selecting the optimal dosing regimen for subsequent in vitro experiments (Figure 2A). Furthermore, in the context of radiation exposure, the CCK‐8 assay revealed that FSB intervention significantly restored cell viability, which had been compromised due to radiation exposure (Figure 2B). This suggests that FSB confers a fundamental protective effect on irradiated epithelial cells. Western blot results for Cleaved Caspase‐1 and Bax indicated that FSB alleviated apoptosis in BEAS‐2B cells following irradiation (Figure 2C,D). Subsequently, β‐galactosidase staining revealed that ionizing radiation induced pronounced cellular senescence, which was effectively reversed following FSB treatment (Figure 2E). As shown in Figure 2F–H, FSB reduced the protein levels of p16 and p21, further confirming its anti‐cellular senescence function. The comet assay demonstrated an increase in DNA damage in the irradiated group, which was mitigated in the FSB treatment group (Figure 2I). Fluorescence imaging and quantitative analysis showed that the number of γH2AX foci was significantly increased in the irradiated group, and this was reduced in the FSB treatment group (Figure 2J,K). We next examined the mRNA levels of the senescence‐associated secretory phenotype (SASP) markers CCL20, EGF, TGF‐β, and IL‐8, and found that FSB markedly suppressed the SASP phenotype induced by IR in epithelial cells (Figure 2L–O). These results indicate that FSB significantly alleviates epithelial cell senescence and DNA damage induced by RILI, thereby restoring lung tissue function.


### FSB Attenuates Oxidative Stress to Suppress Cellular Senescence in A Murine Model of Radiation‐Induced Lung Injury
  FSB exhibited potent antioxidant activity both in vivo and in vitro. The results indicated that the fluorescence intensity of ROS in lung tissues from the irradiated group was significantly elevated compared to the control group; however, this intensity was notably reduced in the FSB treatment group (Figure 3A,B). Furthermore, serum levels of malondialdehyde (MDA), a marker of oxidative stress, were significantly increased in the irradiated group. In contrast, the levels of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione (GSH) were significantly decreased; following FSB treatment, these indicators showed substantial improvement (Figure 3C–E). In vitro experiments corroborated these findings, revealing increased ROS fluorescence intensity in irradiated cells that was diminished following FSB treatment (Figure 3F,G). We further assessed mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) levels and found that mtROS generated as a consequence of radiation‐induced mitochondrial injury were likewise effectively scavenged by FSB (Figure 3H,I). The following experiments were conducted to verify the extent of restoration of the cellular redox system. Western blot analysis demonstrated that the expression levels of Nrf2 and HO‐1 proteins were reduced in the irradiated group, but these proteins were restored to baseline levels in the FSB treatment group (Figure 3J–L). Additionally, intracellular SOD levels were decreased in the irradiated group; however, these levels were significantly elevated after FSB treatment (Figure 3M). Moreover, the mRNA expression levels of iNOS and COX2 were significantly elevated in the irradiated group and were effectively reduced by FSB treatment (Figure 3N,O). Importantly, in cells treated with both irradiation and FSB, the expression levels of p16, p21, and γH2AX proteins increased following H₂O₂ treatment, suggesting that FSB alleviates epithelial cell senescence by clearance of ROS accumulation and resisting the redox imbalance during RILI occurrence (Figure 3P–S). The specific mechanisms underlying FSB's antioxidant response require further investigation. Based on the above results, we conclude that FSB restores cellular redox homeostasis and suppresses mitochondria‐derived ROS release following radiation‐induced mitochondrial injury. Together, these actions attenuate oxidative stress in epithelial cells, thereby directly alleviating RILI‐associated epithelial cell senescence.


### FSB Preserves Mitochondrial Homeostasis to Attenuate Radiation‐Induced Oxidative Stress and Cellular Senescence
  Mitochondrial dysfunction accompanying organ injury increases inner membrane permeability and impairs the electron transport chain, leading to enhanced peroxide production and subsequent ROS release into the cytosol—the primary source of ROS overload. Accordingly, we next investigated whether FSB can restore mitochondrial function following RILI. JC‐1 fluorescence imaging revealed that FSB treatment significantly restored mitochondrial membrane potential in irradiated cells, as evidenced by increased JC‐1 aggregates (red fluorescence) and decreased monomers (green fluorescence) compared to radiation‐only controls (Figure 4A). Assessment of the proportion of cells capable of ATP synthesis confirmed that FSB partially restores mitochondrial respiratory function suppressed by irradiation (Figure 4B). Confocal microscopy analysis showed FSB promoted the colocalization of mitochondria and lysosomes, facilitating the occurrence of mitophagy under both baseline and pathological conditions (Figure 4C). We subsequently extracted mitochondrial proteins and analyzed autophagy‐related markers, demonstrating that mitochondrial‐localized LC3B II levels exhibited no significant alterations under baseline conditions, whereas FSB treatment markedly enhanced LC3B II expression following IR exposure (Figure 4D,E). Consistent with this finding, post‐irradiation FSB administration accelerated mitochondrial p62 degradation, thereby underscoring mitophagy activation (Figure 4F). Importantly, pharmacological inhibition of autophagy with 3‐MA abolished FSB's protective effects, leading to increased mtROS accumulation (Figure 4G,H). Simultaneously, the transcription levels of the oxidative stress response genes iNOS and COX2 were significantly elevated following 3‐MA administration, providing further corroboration (Figure 4I,J). At the molecular level, Western blot analyses revealed that 3‐MA intervention significantly reversed the improvements in cellular senescence achieved by FSB treatment (Figure 4K–N). Following the inhibition of mitophagy, the nuclear accumulation of γH2AX protein was markedly increased, and comet assays further corroborated the exacerbated DNA damage (Figure 4O,P). In summary, FSB exerts its protective effects by activating mitophagy to clear damaged mitochondria and mitigate excessive ROS accumulation.


### FSB Activates PINK1/Parkin‐Mediated Mitophagy to Attenuate Radiation‐Induced Cellular Damage
  Our mechanistic investigations demonstrate that FSB exerts radioprotective effects through activation of the PINK1/Parkin mitophagy pathway. Western blot analyses revealed FSB treatment significantly restored radiation‐induced alterations in PINK1 expression and Parkin phosphorylation (Figure 5A–D), indicating activation of this quality control pathway. Functional validation through PINK1 knockdown (si‐PINK1) demonstrated the indispensable role of this pathway in mediating the protective effects of FSB against radiation‐induced cellular injury. Specifically, silencing PINK1 markedly impaired the recovery of mitochondrial membrane potential, as indicated by an altered JC‐1 monomer‐to‐aggregate ratio (Figure 5E), and diminished mitochondria–lysosome colocalization (Figure 5F). Furthermore, PINK1 depletion significantly reduced cell viability in FSB‐treated irradiated cells (Figure 5G), collectively confirming that PINK1 is critical for preserving mitochondrial integrity and cellular survival under these conditions. Importantly, PINK1 depletion abolished FSB's protective effects, leading to elevated expression of senescence markers (p16 and p21) and DNA damage indicators (γH2AX) (Figure 5H–K). These findings establish that FSB‐mediated activation of the PINK1/Parkin signaling axis promotes mitophagy, which is essential for its ability to mitigate radiation‐induced cellular damage and senescence.


### FSB Activates AMPK to Promote Mitophagy and Sustain Redox Homeostasis in Radiation‐Exposed Alveolar Epithelial Cells
  Given that AMPK plays a critical role in initiating general autophagy and activating the PINK1/Parkin pathway, and considering that FSB functions as a natural AMPK activator, we subsequently investigated whether FSB could regulate mitochondrial homeostasis and redox balance through AMPK activation, thereby deepening our understanding of its protective effects against RILI. It was found that compared to the control group, the p‐AMPK/AMPK expression ratio was increased after FSB treatment (Figure 6A,B). As shown in Figure 6C, molecular docking predicted with high confidence that FSB could bind to the catalytic α1 (HDOCK score = −286.81) and α2 (HDOCK score = −277.20) subunits of AMPK, thereby modulating AMPK functional activity. By introducing the AMPK‐specific inhibitor Compound C (CC) as a regulatory approach, we found that inhibition of AMPK activity markedly attenuated the protective effects of FSB on epithelial cells following irradiation (Figure 6D). Further assessment of mtROS levels revealed that Compound C substantially reversed the inhibitory effect of FSB on mtROS in cells treated with both irradiation and FSB (Figure 6E,F). In keeping with our hypothesis, inhibition of AMPK blocked the restoration of mitochondrial membrane potential and mitochondrial respiratory function (Figure 6G,H). The colocalization between mitochondria and lysosomes was reduced after treatment with CC (Figure 6I). The protein levels of PINK1 and p‐Parkin were also markedly elevated following CC intervention (Figure 6J–L). Next, we assessed the senescence and extent of DNA damage in alveolar epithelial cells. The comet assay demonstrated an increase in DNA damage after CC treatment (Figure 6M). Fluorescence imaging showed an increase in the number of γH2AX foci after CC treatment (Figure 6N). Accordingly, AMPK is a central target through which FSB mitigates RILI. By upregulating PINK1‐mediated mitophagy, AMPK promotes the clearance of damaged mitochondria, attenuates mtROS production, and thereby, to a considerable extent, ameliorates epithelial cell senescence.

  Given that Result 3 indicated that the redox markers Nrf2 and HO‐1 were upregulated by FSB, we hypothesize that another mechanism by which FSB ameliorates oxidative stress in epithelial cells is the restoration of the Nrf2‐centered redox pathway. Consequently, after inhibiting Nrf2's DNA‐binding ability with ML385, the ROS scavenging capacity of FSB was partially reversed (Figure 7A,B). Concurrently, we assessed downstream protein levels and found that Nrf2 inhibition led to a reduction in HO‐1 protein levels, while senescence markers p16 and p21 were significantly elevated in irradiated cells treated with FSB (Figure 7C–F). These results substantiate that Nrf2 activation modulates the FSB‐mediated correction of systemic redox dysregulation. Subsequently, we focused on determining whether Nrf2 activation is dependent on AMPK. Upon inhibition of AMPK, the total cytosolic ROS levels were markedly elevated to a certain extent (Figure 7G,H). Western blot analysis revealed that both phosphorylated Nrf2 (p‐Nrf2) and total Nrf2 protein levels were significantly decreased following AMPK suppression, and HO‐1 exhibited a similar downregulation trend (Figure 7I–L). Interestingly, RT‐qPCR results indicated that CC suppressed the transcriptional level of HO‐1, whereas Nrf2 mRNA levels remained unchanged, suggesting that the observed changes in Nrf2 protein levels arose from AMPK‐dependent post‐transcriptional regulatory mechanisms (Figure 7M,N). Published studies have demonstrated that AMPK activation enhances Nrf2 function without affecting its nuclear translocation, yet exerts a pronounced regulatory influence on its DNA‐binding capability [28]. As illustrated in Figure 7O, ChIP assay results confirmed that AMPK inhibition significantly diminished Nrf2 binding to the antioxidant response element (ARE) sites within the HO‐1 promoter region, thereby attenuating HO‐1 transcription. CO‐IP assays elucidated the mechanistic basis for this phenomenon, demonstrating that FSB‐activated AMPK associates with Nrf2 and disrupts its binding to KEAP1, the classical substrate‐recognition subunit of the E3 ubiquitin ligase complex mediating Nrf2 degradation, which consequently triggers Nrf2 activation and leads to an elevation in its overall protein abundance (Figure 7P–S). A comprehensive evaluation of this Results subsection supports the conclusion that AMPK is a critical molecular target through which FSB exerts its protective effects against RILI. FSB directly binds to AMPK, promoting its activation, which, on one hand, enhances mitophagy to eliminate damaged mitochondria, and on the other hand, activates Nrf2 to restore systemic redox homeostasis, thereby alleviating oxidative stress in epithelial cells during the onset of RILI.


### AMPK Inhibition Abrogates the Protective Effects of FSB Against RILI in Mice
  We subsequently conducted in vivo studies to delineate the pivotal role of AMPK in the FSB‐mediated amelioration of RILI. In the TLI model, H&E staining revealed exacerbated lung tissue damage after CC treatment (Figure 8A,B). CC intervention also aggravated the degree of pulmonary fibrosis in the lungs of RILI mice following FSB therapy (Figure 8C,D). Fluorescence imaging indicated enhanced ROS fluorescence intensity after CC treatment (Figure 8E,F). By measuring LDH and MPO levels in lung tissues, it was further demonstrated that CC markedly aggravated neutrophil infiltration and lung tissue injury in mice (Figure 8G,H). Lung function tests and blood gas analyses indicated that inhibition of AMPK markedly reversed the functional recovery of lung tissues in RILI mice conferred by FSB treatment (Figure 8I–L). Collectively, these in vivo findings provide compelling evidence that AMPK serves as a critical mediator in FSB‐induced protection against RILI.


## Discussion
  Radiation‐induced lung injury is a severe clinical complication characterized by destruction within the lung tissue, subsequent fibrosis, and the accompanying inflammatory response. In recent years, an increasing number of researchers have demonstrated that ROS accumulation resulting from mitochondrial damage in radiation‐induced organ injury is a key factor leading to DNA damage and cellular senescence [29, 30]. Due to their low toxicity and high biocompatibility, natural compounds have become a focal point in many organ protection studies. This study focuses on FSB, a natural AMPK agonist, and investigates the mechanisms by which AMPK activation triggers mitophagy and re‐establishes redox homeostasis, thereby attenuating excessive ROS accumulation and delaying the progression of RILI, ultimately providing a mechanistic basis for clinical translation (Figure 9).

  In the context of radiation‐induced organ damage, excessive generation of ROS serves as a pivotal mediator bridging DNA damage and cellular senescence. Ionizing radiation directly ionizes water molecules to generate hydroxyl radicals (•OH), while indirectly activating NADPH oxidase (NOX) and disrupting mitochondrial electron transport chains, collectively amplifying ROS overproduction [31]. These ROS not only induce oxidative DNA lesions such as 8‐oxoguanine and DNA double‐strand breaks (DSBs) but also impair base excision repair (BER) and homologous recombination (HR) pathways by oxidizing repair enzymes [32]. Persistent DNA damage triggers sustained activation of the ATM/ATR‐p53‐p21 axis, driving irreversible cell cycle arrest and SASP secretion [33]. Notably, mitochondrial‐derived ROS further propagate senescence signals through cGAS‐STING pathway activation, which recognizes cytosolic mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) released via permeability transition pores [34]. This ROS‐DNA damage‐senescence axis establishes a self‐reinforcing loop, as senescent cells secrete pro‐oxidant cytokines to exacerbate oxidative stress in bystander cells. Beyond their direct oxidative damage to proteins and lipids, leading to structural disruption of organelles and cellular membranes, ROS also function as signaling molecules. They directly or indirectly activate signaling pathways such as NF‐κB, AP‐1, and p38, thereby modulating the expression of senescence‐associated genes [35, 36, 37, 38]. This, in turn, promotes the formation of SASP and the release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, propagating senescence signals between cells. Increasing evidence has demonstrated that targeted alleviation of oxidative stress through the elimination of excessive ROS can effectively mitigate radiation‐induced organ damage resulting from ROS accumulation, underscoring its potential as a promising therapeutic target in clinical settings [10, 39]. The distinctive feature of FSB selected in this study lies in its ability to effectively intervene in both the sources and the abnormal accumulation of ROS. Mechanistically, FSB directly targets the primary source of ROS—damaged mitochondria—by restoring mitochondrial homeostasis and promoting the clearance of impaired organelles. In parallel, we observed and confirmed that FSB induces Nrf2 phosphorylation, thereby re‐establishing redox balance and efficiently mitigating ROS accumulation. Notably, these biological effects of FSB are critically dependent on the activation of its central molecular target, AMPK.

  AMPK, as the central sensor of cellular energy homeostasis, was initially shown to activate autophagy directly or indirectly via promoting mTOR phosphorylation [40]. However, its effects and mechanisms in regulating mitophagy remain incompletely understood. Notably, current studies demonstrate that AMPK exerts opposing roles in distinct mitophagy pathways. On one hand, in the NIX/BNIP3‐mediated, ULK1‐dependent initiation axis, AMPK directly phosphorylates ULK1 at Ser556 and Ser694 upon activation, creating binding sites for 14–3‐3 proteins; this interaction sequesters and functionally inhibits ULK1, thereby blocking assembly of the autophagy‐initiating complex and autophagosome formation. Such inhibition prevents inadvertent removal of functionally intact mitochondria under energy stress, ensuring selective clearance of only damaged organelles [41]. On the other hand, AMPK promotes the PINK1/Parkin‐dependent pathway: although ULK1 was once proposed as a Parkin kinase, AMPK still robustly enhances Parkin phosphorylation at Ser108—and thus its activation—in ULK1/2‐deficient contexts, accelerating the removal of dysfunctional mitochondria [41]. Additional reports indicate that AMPK can upregulate PINK1 protein levels to further activate the canonical mitophagy cascade [42]. In the present study, we observed that FSB both upregulated PINK1 and Parkin expression and promoted Parkin activation, leading to a pronounced enhancement of mitophagy under basal or pathological conditions. Reports on the role of AMPK in activating Nrf2 to maintain systemic redox homeostasis are well documented, yet the underlying mechanisms remain debated. In this study, we found that AMPK activation not only increased Nrf2 protein levels without altering its mRNA expression but also enhanced Nrf2 phosphorylation. These findings, to some extent, align with observations reported by Shi et al. and Heiss et al. [6, 28]. Subsequent combined Co‐IP and ChIP assays further demonstrated that AMPK directly interacts with Nrf2, exerting its kinase activity to promote Nrf2‐mediated transactivation of HO‐1, while competitively inhibiting Keap1‐mediated Nrf2 degradation. This work provides mechanistic evidence supporting the protective effects of FSB and other AMPK activators against oxidative stress–related injuries.

  Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. First, due to experimental constraints, although molecular docking yielded high‐confidence scores for the binding of FSB to AMPK, we were unable to further validate this interaction using surface plasmon resonance (SPR) or related biophysical assays. Second, because both AMPKα1 and AMPKα2 appear to be bound by FSB, we attempted to generate mice with combined deletion of both catalytic α isoforms (AMPKα double knockout, AMPKα‐TKO). However, these mice exhibited excessive sensitivity to total lung irradiation, which prevented us from administering FSB intervention to the extent achieved in wild‐type controls. We will continue to optimize experimental conditions to enable definitive conclusions in future studies.

  In summary, this study demonstrates that FSB directly activates AMPK, thereby bidirectionally integrating mitochondrial quality control with the Nrf2‐dominated antioxidant network to systematically correct radiation‐induced redox disequilibrium and cellular senescence, resulting in marked improvements in inflammation, fibrosis, and pulmonary dysfunction at both organismal and cellular levels. Specifically, AMPK‐dependent, PINK1/Parkin‐mediated mitophagy efficiently eliminates damaged mitochondria, restrains mtROS leakage, and, via the AMPK–Nrf2/HO‐1 axis, reinforces antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory transcriptional programs, collectively interrupting the self‐amplifying loop of ROS‐driven senescence. Pharmacologic AMPK blockade abolishes FSB's mitochondrial and redox protective actions in vitro and in vivo, establishing AMPK as the core molecular target mediating FSB's radioprotective efficacy. Accordingly, FSB represents a safe, plant‐derived candidate capable of repeatable AMPK activation; coordinated control of mitochondrial quality and redox homeostasis constitutes a key therapeutic strategy for precision intervention in RILI, warranting further translational studies across different pathological stages and in combination with anti‐tumor treatment settings to accelerate clinical implementation.
