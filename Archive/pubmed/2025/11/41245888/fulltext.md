# Distinct IRF6 dysfunction mechanisms in syndromic orofacial clefts: Computational evidence for allosteric versus direct disruption


## Graphical Abstract
  Graphical Abstract


## Introduction
  Orofacial clefts (OCs) represent the most common congenital anomalies affecting the craniofacial region [1], and rank as the third most frequent congenital condition among live births in Brazil [2]. OCs are typically classified into three main categories: cleft lip (CL), cleft palate (CP), and cleft lip and palate (CLP). While the majority of OC cases are non-syndromic, the Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) database currently lists over 500 syndromes associated with OCs [3].

  OC are a substantial financial burden on families and healthcare systems as they require a long-term, multidisciplinary care [4]. Additionally, they are associated with increased childhood mortality [5] and can lead to significant challenges in feeding, speech, hearing, and social integration [6].

  Among the syndromes that present OC as one of their phenotypes, Van der Woude Syndrome (VWS) is the most prevalent, accounting for 2 % of cases [7]. VWS is autosomal dominant and is characterized by lower lip pits (small depressions or indentations - embryonic residues - that may occur in the inferior lips) and CL with or without CP or CP. The syndrome presents genetic heterogeneity and is divided into VWS1 (MIM: #119300) caused by variation in IRF6 gene and VWS2 (a rare type, MIM: #606713) caused by variations in the GRHL3 gene and associated mostly with CP [8].

  Another autosomal dominant OC associated syndrome with pathogenic variants in IRF6 as its main cause is the Popliteal Pterygium Syndrome (PPS, #MIM: 119500), with many clinical features that may include: CL with or without CP or CP, lower lip pits, syndactyly, syngnathia, skin and genital anomalies and webs in the lower limbs [9]. Both VWS and PPS have common clinical signs such as the OC and lower lip pits.

  The interferon regulatory factor 6 (IRF6), the focus of this study, is a transcription factor and has 9 exons (1 and 2 are non-coding). IRF6 has a conserved DNA-binding domain (exons 3 and 4), and a less conserved protein-binding domain (exons 7 and 9). These four exons are considered hotspots for mutations in VWS [10], with exon 4 presenting the highest concentration of pathogenic variants.

  IRF6 is a critical gene involved in orofacial development. IRF6 is expressed at the margins of the palatal shelves before and after their fusion in a murine model [11] and plays an essential role in keratinocyte differentiation and epidermal organization [12]. In this study, we aim to investigate IRF6 variants in two syndromic cases of OC and assess how these variants may differentially affect the structure and function of the encoded protein.


## Methods

### Sample selection and ethical considerations
  This study was approved by the local institutional ethics committee (protocol number: 17722319.6.0000.5248), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians. The sample comprised two unrelated probands, both clinically diagnosed with syndromic OC: one of them with VWS and the other with PPS, along with their respective parents. All individuals were recruited from the Craniofacial Anomalies Treatment Center (CTAC), a regional referral center for OC in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil. Biological samples (saliva or buccal swabs) were collected from each family member, alongside comprehensive clinical data. Genomic DNA was extracted using the Oragene® DNA collection kit (DNA Genotek™) and subsequently diluted to a working concentration of 30 ng/µ L for downstream analyses. Genetic kinship was verified to ensure accurate familial relationships and to confirm inheritance patterns.


### IRF6 sequencing, variant annotation and pathogenicity assessment
  Genomic DNA was subjected to PCR amplification targeting exons 3, 4, 7, and 9 of the IRF6 gene using standardized primer sets (Supplementary Table 2). PCR products were purified with ExoSAP-IT™ (Affymetrix Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA) to remove excess primers and nucleotides. Sanger sequencing of the purified amplicons was performed using the automated sequencing platform at the Oswaldo Cruz Institute (Fiocruz Technological Platforms Network – RPT01A, Rio de Janeiro, Brazil). The electropherograms resulting from Sanger sequencing were analyzed using the Ape - A plasmid Editor software (v3.0.6) [13]. Alignment was performed with the GRCh38/hg38 reference sequence (ENST00000367021.8).

  Identified variants were annotated and interpreted using multiple databases and tools. Clinical significance was assessed via ClinVar [14], while pathogenicity predictions were obtained from VarSome [15] and AlfaMissense [16]. Population allele frequencies were compared using data from the 1000 Genomes Project [17], AbraOM [18], and gnomAD [19]. Variant classification followed the guidelines of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) [20] supported by the Genetic Variant Interpretation Tool [21].


### Comparative modeling and structural analysis
  The amino acid sequence of IRF6 was retrieved from the UniProtKB database (accession O14896). A BLASTP search against the Protein Data Bank (PDB) identified the crystal structure of the human IRF4 DNA-binding domain complexed with a GATA motif-containing DNA (PDB ID: 8JKL [22]) as the most suitable template, exhibiting 45 % sequence identity, 68 % sequence similarity and 88 % coverage within the IRF6 DNA-binding motif (residues 1–120).

  Query and template sequences were aligned using the PSI-Coffee algorithm implemented in T-Coffee. Subsequently, 500 homology models were generated using the “automodel” class in MODELLER v10.5 [23], incorporating DNA as a heteroatom. Model optimization was performed using the variable target function method (VTFM) with a maximum of 300 iterations. Molecular dynamics (MD) refinement was conducted in slow mode, with two complete cycles to achieve optimized conformations. The resulting models were ranked according to their discrete optimized protein energy (DOPE) scores, and the top-scoring model was selected for further analysis.

  Rare and missense variations found in the PPS and VWS cases were selected for comparative modeling and structural analysis. The NP_006138.1:p.(Ala16Val) and NP_006138.1:p.(Arg84Cys) variations were introduced into the optimized IRF6 model using the swapaa command in UCSF ChimeraX [24], guided by the Dunbrack backbone-dependent rotamer library [25]. Hydrogen bond interactions and three-dimensional structural representations were analyzed and generated using the default parameters and molecular graphics capabilities of UCSF ChimeraX.


### Molecular dynamics simulations
  Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using AMBER 22 [26]. Prior to simulations, protonation states of titratable residues were assigned using the PDB2PQR server at pH 7.4 [27]. Protein interactions were modeled using the Amber ff19SB force field [28], while DNA interactions were represented using the Amber BSC1 force field [29]. Electrostatic interactions were treated using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) algorithm with a 10 Å cutoff. Each system was simulated under periodic boundary conditions in a triclinic box, with dimensions automatically defined to extend 10 Å beyond the outermost protein atoms in all Cartesian directions. The simulation box was solvated with Optimal Point Charge (OPC) water molecules, and sodium and chloride ions were added to neutralize the systems.

  Energy minimization was conducted in two stages: (i) 2000 steps (1000 steepest descent + 1000 conjugate-gradient) with all heavy atoms harmonically restrained using a force constant of 5 kcal mol-1 Å-2; (ii) 5000 steps (2500 steepest descent + 2500 conjugate-gradient) without positional restraints. Initial atomic velocities were assigned using a Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution corresponding to 20 K. Systems were gradually heated to 310 K over one nanosecond using the Langevin thermostat. During this phase, all heavy atoms were harmonically restrained with a force constant of 10 kcal mol−1 Å−2. Subsequently, systems underwent nine successive 500ps equilibration simulations, progressively reducing positional restraints to zero. Following equilibration, systems were simulated without restraints at 310 K in the isothermal-isobaric (NPT) ensemble at 1 atm pressure, using isotropic coupling. For each system, we conducted four independent MD runs of 500 ns each, totaling 6μs of production dynamics.


### Trajectory analysis and free energy calculations
  Trajectory analysis was performed using tools from the CPPTRAJ package [30] in Amber. Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD) was calculated for each system by aligning heavy atoms to the initial production dynamics structure. Hydrogen bonds (H-bonds) between protein and DNA were identified using geometric criteria: a distance of less than 3.5 Å between two polar heavy atoms (with at least one hydrogen atom attached) and a hydrogen-donor angle exceeding 120∘.

  Binding free energy of IRF6-DNA complexes was estimated using the Molecular Mechanics Generalized Born Surface Area (MM/GBSA) approach. We extracted 500 uncorrelated snapshots from the last 100 ns of each MD simulation trajectory (the period during which simulations mostly stabilized). Interaction energies and solvation free energies for the complex, receptor, and ligand were calculated using the MMPBSA.py module [31] available in the AMBER distribution.


### Statistical analysis
  Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2 (R Core Team, 2024). For RMSD analyses, per-replica mean values calculated from the equilibrated portion of trajectories (final 100 ns) were used as independent observations (n = 4 per system). Normality of distributions was assessed using Shapiro-Wilk tests, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. Based on these assessments, either one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey HSD post-hoc testing or Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s post-hoc comparisons with Bonferroni correction was applied as appropriate. For MM/GBSA energy component analyses, statistical significance was evaluated using propagated standard errors, with differences considered significant when the absolute difference exceeded twice the combined standard error (|Δmean|>2×SEM12+SEM22), equivalent to approximately p< 0.05 for normally distributed data. This approach accounts for uncertainty in both measurements and is appropriate for derived quantities such as free energies. Hydrogen bond occupancy differences were evaluated by comparing mean occupancy values across systems, with significance assessed using the magnitude of change relative to baseline wild-type values. Changes exceeding 15 % in absolute occupancy were considered biologically relevant based on the precision of our sampling protocol. Statistical significance was defined as p< 0.05 for all tests. Data are reported as mean ± standard deviation unless otherwise specified.


## Results

### Identification of pathogenic IRF6 variants in syndromic orofacial cleft cases
  Targeted Sanger sequencing of IRF6 exons 3, 4, 7, and 9 revealed distinct rare heterozygous variants in two unrelated probands presenting with syndromic OC. The first case, a six-year-old male clinically diagnosed with VWS, harbored a missense variant in exon 3: IRF6(NM_006147.4):c.47 C>T | NP_006138.1:p.(Ala16Val) (Fig. 1A). Clinical evaluation revealed bilateral CL, lower lip pits, maxillary hypoplasia, and anterior crossbite. There was no reported maternal exposure to alcohol or tobacco during pregnancy. Although the family history included a paternal second cousin with CL, both parents were clinically unaffected and tested negative for the variant, confirming its de novo origin. This variant is absent in AbraOM and gnomAD. Clinical significance assessment via ClinVar classified this variant as having uncertain significance (1 submission), while computational pathogenicity prediction algorithms, including AlphaMissense and the majority of predictors integrated within VarSome, classified the variant as pathogenic.   The second proband, also male, was diagnosed with PPS and harbored a heterozygous missense variant in exon 4: IRF6(NM_006147.4):c.250 C>T | NP_006138.1:p.(Arg84Cys), corresponding to rs121434226 (Fig. 1B). It is also supported as pathogenic by AlphaMissense, gnomAD, VarSome, and ACMG/AMP criteria (evidence: PS2, PS4, PP3, PM2, PM5).


### Comparative modeling and structural characterization
  To elucidate the molecular consequences of the identified pathogenic variants, three-dimensional models of the IRF6 DNA-binding domain complexed with its cognate DNA sequence were constructed utilizing the IRF4 DNA-binding domain bound to GATA motif-containing DNA (PDB ID: 8JKL) as the optimal structural template. The resultant comparative models demonstrated excellent stereochemical quality and structural validity, providing a robust foundation for subsequent variant analysis and molecular dynamics investigations.

  Structural analysis of the wild-type IRF6-DNA complex revealed that the NP_006138.1:p.(Ala16Val) substitution occurs within a α-helical region distal to the direct protein-DNA interface (Fig. 2A, B). The replacement of the compact, non-polar alanine residue with the bulkier, hydrophobic valine potentially introduces steric perturbations within the α-helical structure (Fig. 2C).   In contrast, the NP_006138.1:p.(Arg84Cys) variant directly affects a residue positioned at the protein-DNA interface within the DNA-binding domain (Fig. 2D). Structural examination revealed that Arg84 engages in direct hydrogen bonding interactions with the DNA backbone in the wild-type complex (Fig. 2D). The NP_006138.1:p.(Arg84Cys) substitution results in the replacement of a large, positively charged arginine residue with a medium-sized, polar cysteine, leading to complete abrogation of the critical hydrogen bonding interaction (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, this substitution significantly alters the electrostatic landscape at the protein-DNA interface (Fig. 2F,G), with the loss of the positively charged guanidinium group potentially destabilizing the electrostatic complementarity essential for high-affinity DNA binding.


### Molecular dynamics analysis of structural stability and dynamics
  Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD) analysis conducted across the extensive molecular dynamic simulations revealed distinct impacts of the NP_006138.1:p.(Ala16Val) and NP_006138.1:p.(Arg84Cys) variants on both IRF6 protein structure and DNA complex stability (Fig. 3). Statistical analysis of the equilibrated trajectories (final 100 ns) was performed using per-replica mean values (n = 4 per system). The wild-type protein demonstrated optimal structural stability, exhibiting the lowest mean RMSD value (4.50 ± 0.69 Å), indicative of a well-equilibrated conformational ensemble (Fig. 3A). Both pathogenic variants manifested increased structural fluctuations relative to the wild type, with the NP_006138.1:p.(Ala16Val) variant displaying the structural perturbations (mean RMSD: 5.28 ± 0.60 Å, 7.5 % increase) (Fig. 3C), followed by the NP_006138.1:p.(Arg84Cys) variant (mean RMSD: 5.39 ± 0.98 Å, 9.8 % increase) (Fig. 3E).Fig. 3Root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) profiles obtained from molecular dynamics simulations of IRF6-DNA systems. (B, D, F) RMSD of DNA bound to WT, NP_006138.1:p.(Ala16Val), and NP_006138.1:p.(Arg84Cys) IRF6, respectively.   Although these differences did not reach statistical significance (Kruskal-Wallis, p = 0.78), the trends were highly consistent across all four independent replicas. The elevated RMSD observed for the NP_006138.1:p.(Ala16Val) variant, despite its localization distal to the DNA-binding interface, provides quantitative evidence for the induction of long-range conformational changes throughout the protein structure. These perturbations likely propagate via allosteric networks, potentially affecting the spatial positioning and dynamic behavior of residues critical for DNA recognition and binding. The NP_006138.1:p.(Arg84Cys) variant exhibited moderately increased structural fluctuations compared to wild type, suggesting that while this variation significantly disrupts local protein-DNA interactions, it may not perturb the overall protein fold as extensively as the NP_006138.1:p.(Ala16Val) substitution.

  Analysis of DNA structural stability within the respective protein complexes revealed unexpected and mechanistically informative patterns. Contrary to initial predictions, the NP_006138.1:p.(Arg84Cys) variant-DNA complex demonstrated the lowest DNA RMSD (4.98 ± 0.47 Å) (Fig. 3F), even exhibiting enhanced stability relative to the wild-type complex (7.12 ± 3.42 Å) (Fig. 3B).

  The wild-type complex exhibited substantial inter-replica variability (SD = 3.42 Å), while one-way ANOVA revealed marginally significant differences among systems (F(2,9) = 3.28, p = 0.085), with the NP_006138.1:p.(Arg84Cys)-NP_006138.1:p.(Ala16Val) comparison approaching significance (p = 0.072). This counterintuitive finding suggests that the NP_006138.1:p.(Arg84Cys) variation may induce formation of a more rigid, albeit functionally suboptimal, protein-DNA interface configuration. In marked contrast, the NP_006138.1:p.(Ala16Val) variant-DNA complex exhibited substantially elevated DNA RMSD (8.83 ± 1.28 Å, 24 % increase vs WT) (Fig. 3D), indicating significant perturbations in DNA structural integrity. These observations suggest that the NP_006138.1:p.(Ala16Val) variation, despite its spatial distance from the DNA-binding interface, engenders more extensive conformational changes in the bound DNA molecule, likely mediated through altered protein dynamics that propagate throughout the entire DNA-binding domain.

  The NP_006138.1:p.(Ala16Val) variant maintained comparable energetic profiles for the primary contributors Arg9 and Arg7, demonstrating minimal direct perturbation of these key interactions (Fig. 4B). However, a notable reduction in the energetic contribution of Arg84 was observed (−4.39 kcal/mol compared to −6.36 kcal/mol in wild type), providing quantitative evidence that the NP_006138.1:p.(Ala16Val) variation, despite its distal localization, allosterically modulates the interaction between Arg84 and the DNA substrate. This finding supports the proposed mechanism whereby the NP_006138.1:p.(Ala16Val) substitution induces conformational changes that propagate through the protein structure to affect the DNA-binding interface.

  The NP_006138.1:p.(Arg84Cys) variation produced the most dramatic alteration in residue energy contributions, with the energetic contribution of position 84 severely diminished from −6.36 kcal/mol to −0.33 kcal/mol (Fig. 4C). These compensatory energetic redistributions suggest significant reorganization of the protein-DNA interface, wherein proximal residues adapt their interactions to partially mitigate the loss of the critical Arg84-DNA contact.

  The NP_006138.1:p.(Ala16Val) variant largely preserved the primary Arg9-DG149 interactions, maintaining mean occupancy above 40 % across multiple contacts, albeit with modest reductions in individual bond occupancies (Fig. 5B). However, a significant reconfiguration occurred in secondary interaction networks, particularly involving Leu10. While the wild-type complex featured a DG149@OP2-Leu10@N interaction with 49.8 % occupancy, the NP_006138.1:p.(Ala16Val) variant exhibited a shift to DG149@OP1-Leu10@N interaction with 40.5 % occupancy, representing a statistically significant reorientation (Δ = 19.3 %, p< 0.05). This subtle but measurable alteration in hydrogen bonding patterns provides molecular-level evidence for the conformational changes induced by the NP_006138.1:p.(Ala16Val) variant and may contribute to the altered DNA dynamics observed in the RMSD analysis.

  The NP_006138.1:p.(Arg84Cys) variant induced the most extensive perturbations in the hydrogen bonding network (Fig. 5C). The substitution of Arg84 resulted in complete elimination of hydrogen bonds with the DNA nucleotide DG126, which demonstrated combined occupancies of 58.3 % in the wild-type complex (31.4 % and 26.9 % for individual contacts).

  This dramatic loss (ΔOccupancy = −58.3 %, p< 0.001) represents the most significant hydrogen bonding disruption observed across all variants. This critical loss was partially compensated through strengthened interactions between Gln82 and DA151, with occupancy increasing from 37.1 % in the NP_006138.1:p.(Ala16Val) variant to 58.9 % in the NP_006138.1:p.(Arg84Cys) mutant (Δ = +21.8 %, p< 0.01). Additionally, novel hydrogen bonding interactions emerged in NP_006138.1:p.(Arg84Cys, including DA127@OP2-ASN88@ND2 with 37.6 % occupancy, which was completely absent in both wild-type and NP_006138.1:p.(Ala16Val) variants (p< 0.001). These alterations in the hydrogen bonding landscape provide direct molecular evidence for the interface reorganization induced by the NP_006138.1:p.(Arg84Cys) variation and offer mechanistic insight into the observed changes in DNA binding affinity and conformational dynamics. Despite these compensatory adaptations, the net result is a more rigid yet functionally compromised binding interface, consistent with the reduced DNA RMSD and severely diminished binding affinity observed for NP_006138.1:p.(Arg84Cys).


## Discussion
  In this study we analyzed two cases with syndromes related to IRF6 gene, both presented distinct phenotypes but had in common the presence of OC (CL or CLP) and lip pits. IRF6 is also a gene highly associated with nonsyndromic OC [32] and is part of a regulatory network for epithelial development together with GRHL3 and TFAP2A [33].

  Our analysis focused on the four established hotspot exons (exons 3, 4, 7, and 9), prioritizing genetic regions with clinical relevance according to our cases. It was sufficient to identify relevant variants in the syndromic families studied, to characterize their clinical presentation and functional consequences. However, future comprehensive genetic evaluations should consider sequencing the entire IRF6 coding region to potentially capture rare or novel pathogenic variants lying outside of these common areas, especially in non-syndromic cases of orofacial clefts [34], where the relevance of variants beyond the known hotspots may be greater than in syndromic cases, for which these hotspots are already well established.

  Both variants found after sequencing are located in the DNA-binding domains of IRF6, responsible for its regulatory function. NP_006138.1:p.(Ala16Val) has two reports in the literature of this variant found in heterozygosity in VWS cases. The first a male with lip pits and CL with or without CP and no family history of OC [35], correlates with our clinical and molecular findings in the VWS case. The second case, also a male with bilateral CL and a positive family history, was found to carry the NP_006138.1:p.(Ala16Val) variant alongside a second variant, p.(Tyr97Cys) [36]. We emphasize that this secondary variant, p.(Tyr97Cys) (classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance in ClinVar), was not detected in our proband.

  A de novo NP_006138.1:p.(Arg84Cys) variation was already described in a similar case report of a Moroccan patient with PPS (presenting: bilateral CLP, lip pits, oral synechias, several achromic spots, nevus on right foot, fimosis, right popliteal pterygium and bilateral syndactyly) [37], although our case did not present nevus or achromic spots. Previously, NP_006138.1:p.(Arg84Cys) was detected in a case of VWS with lip pits and bilateral CLP and no family history [36] as well as in two familial cases of PPS and VWS [11]. A large congolese family with three members (father, son and daughter) presenting the NP_006138.1:p.(Arg84Cys) variant and clinical signs of PPS but with great phenotypic variation has also been reported [38], similar results were found in a Japanese family [39], in all cases NP_006138.1:p.(Arg84Cys) was found in heterozygosity.

  We have other reports in the literature of phenotypic variation in cases of VWS and PPS, with cases of both syndromes in patients with same IRF6 variant [40]. These results show that both syndromes are a spectrum of IRF6 dysfunction that also may be influenced by the multiethnic background, environmental and epigenetic context. The NP_006138.1:p.(Arg84Cys) variant also causes significant impairment of transcriptional function of IRF6 [9].

  NP_006138.1:p.(Arg84Cys) found in the PPS case is located in exon 4, this exon is a hotspot for variants in patients with the syndrome. 72 % of variants in cases of PPS are located in exon 4 and if we focus on missense variants this number goes up to 92,8 % [41]. It is important to highlight that the majority of variations found in PPS are located in an amino acid residue that contacts DNA directly, in contrast the minority of amino acid changes in VWS made direct contact with DNA [42], which agrees with our molecular findings.

  Our comprehensive molecular modeling analysis reveals that NP_006138.1:p.(Ala16Val) and NP_006138.1:p.(Arg84Cys) variations impact IRF6-DNA interactions through distinct mechanisms. NP_006138.1:p.(Ala16Val) induces long-range conformational changes that subtly alter the DNA-binding interface, resulting in moderate reduction in binding affinity. In contrast, NP_006138.1:p.(Arg84Cys) directly disrupts a key protein-DNA interaction, leading to significant rearrangement of the binding interface and more severe reduction in affinity.

  The pathogenicity of NP_006138.1:p.(Arg84Cys) is corroborated by multiple complementary approaches. Recently a comprehensive structural screening of 37 IRF6 variants was performed using static predicted structures from AlphaFold2, SWISS-MODEL, and I-TASSER, demonstrating that pathogenic mutations cluster around the protein-binding domain [43]. While that analysis provided valuable insights into spatial distribution patterns of deleterious mutations, our study employs molecular dynamics simulations to characterize the dynamic mechanisms underlying variant pathogenicity. Our approach reveals that NP_006138.1:p.(Ala16Val), despite being distant from the DNA-binding interface, exerts functional effects through propagated conformational changes that static structural analysis alone cannot capture.

  Additionally, in a murine knock-in model [44], homozygous NP_006138.1:p.(Arg84Cys) mice exhibited complete failure of palatal fusion due to persistence of the medial edge epithelium. Although heterozygous mice alone showed normal palatal fusion, the combination of the heterozygous state with Smad4 deletion recapitulated phenotypes resembling Popliteal Pterygium Syndrome. These findings support the pathogenic role of NP_006138.1:p.(Arg84Cys), although the absence of reported homozygous cases in humans and of SMAD4 variants associated with the syndrome indicates limitations of the murine model. Together, these computational and experimental findings establish NP_006138.1:p.(Arg84Cys) as a high-confidence pathogenic variant, while highlighting how different variants can compromise IRF6 function through mechanistically distinct pathways.

  While our computational analyses provide detailed mechanistic hypotheses for how these variants impair IRF6 function, experimental validation through direct functional assays remains necessary to confirm these predictions, especially for the NP_006138.1:p.(Ala16Val) variant. Future studies employing DNA-binding assays (such as electrophoretic mobility shift assays or surface plasmon resonance), transcriptional activity measurements, and protein stability analyses would be valuable to validate whether NP_006138.1:p.(Ala16Val) operates through allosteric mechanisms and whether NP_006138.1:p.(Arg84Cys) directly disrupts DNA binding as our simulations suggest. The integration of computational predictions with experimental validation represents an optimal approach for understanding variant pathogenicity at the molecular level.

  These findings not only provide a molecular basis for the pathogenicity of these variants in VWS and PPS but also offer detailed insights into the structure-function relationships of IRF6 in craniofacial development, showing how variations could impact its function as transcriptional activator. The differential effects of these variations suggest that they may lead to varying degrees of IRF6 dysfunction, potentially explaining the phenotypic variability observed in VWS and PPS patients.

  While our comprehensive molecular and computational analyses provide deep mechanistic insights into the identified IRF6 variants, the clinical generalizability of our findings is naturally constrained by the study of a limited number of cases. Thus, future studies would benefit from multi-center collaborations or meta-analyses to validate these correlations in larger patient cohorts.

  Some limitations should be considered in the interpretation of our findings, our computational analyses, while employing advanced methodologies, represent theoretical predictions that require experimental validation, the molecular dynamics simulations, despite encompassing 6μs of sampling per system, may not capture rare conformational events or long-timescale allosteric transitions that could be functionally relevant. Finally, our study focused on hotspots of IRF6, potentially overlooking variants in other regions of the gene.


## Conclusion
  Our computational analyses provide mechanistic hypotheses about IRF6-mediated pathogenesis in syndromic OC. The computationally predicted differential mechanisms suggest potential molecular explanations for the phenotypic variability observed in VWS and PPS patients and reinforce the importance of IRF6 in the complex regulatory networks governing craniofacial development. Experimental confirmation through biochemical and cellular assays, especially for the NP_006138.1:p.(Ala16Val), will be essential to definitively establish these proposed molecular mechanisms.
