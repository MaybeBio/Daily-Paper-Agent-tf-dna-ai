# Integrated in-silico and in-vitro analysis of lncRNA H19/miRNA-675/p53 in OSCC: Structural characterization and molecular docking insights


## Background
  Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNA) H19 has drawn special attention because of its varied role in several malignancies, including OSCC. Therefore, this study was conducted to assess the association between H19-miR675-p53 by in-silico analysis, quantify the expression levels of H19, miRNA-675, and target oncogene p53 in cancerous versus normal individuals, and Correlate the Clinicopathological findings with their expression pattern.


## Methods
  The secondary structure of lncRNA H19 was predicted using the RNAfold web server (http://rna.tbi.univie.ac.at/cgi-bin/RNAWebSuite/RNAfold.cgi). The FASTA sequence of H19 was retrieved from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). We performed molecular docking studies to analyze the interaction between miRNA-675 and p53 using the MDockPP (https://zougrouptoolkit.missouri.edu/MDockPP/) web server. Real-time PCR was used to measure the amounts of H19 and miR-675, and Immunohistochemistry was used to analyse the pattern of p53 expression.


## Result
  The study successfully associated miR-675 from the first exon of H19 modulating p53 via in silico analysis. It was found that H19 and miR-675 levels were higher in OSCC patients (3.12 ± 1.16) compared to healthy patients (1.0 ± 0.0), and was statistically significant (p-value < 0.001).


## Conclusion
  The specificity of H19 expression in OSCC compared to normal presents an attractive target for cancer-specific therapies, minimizing the risk of off-target effects.


## Introduction
  Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is the most prevalent subtype of oral cancer, accounting for approximately 4% of all cancer cases worldwide and affecting an estimated 350,000 individuals annually [1]. Commonly, OSCC occurs more frequently in elderly males with tobacco or alcohol-related habits, recent epidemiological trends have shown a shift toward younger individuals and females without such risk factors [2, 3]. The prognosis of OSCC is strongly dependent on the stage of diagnosis, while early-stage cases have a 5-year survival rate of about 50–60%; outcomes are significantly poorer in advanced stages [4]. Among the established risk factors, tobacco use remains the most evident, although other contributors, including alcohol, HPV infection, and genetic predisposition, have been implicated [5].

  OSCC exhibits a wide array of clinical presentations, from smooth swellings to ulcero-proliferative lesions, and can involve multiple sites within the oral cavity, such as the buccal mucosa, tongue, retromolar area, gingivo-buccal sulcus, and floor of the mouth [6]. Prognosis is influenced by several clinicopathological features, among which the pattern of invasion is considered a strong indicator of disease aggressiveness [7]. Standard treatment approaches include surgical excision, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, alone or in combination. Although immunotherapy and targeted therapies are gaining attention, their role in OSCC is under trial, with limited definitive clinical evidence [8]. One of the major challenges in managing OSCC is late-stage diagnosis, often due to its heterogeneous presentation. This underscores the need for reliable molecular biomarkers that can aid not only in early detection but also in prognostication and therapy selection [9, 10].

  Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) have emerged as promising molecular regulators and potential biomarkers in cancer biology [11]. Among them, H19 has garnered significant attention due to its consistent dysregulation across various malignancies, including OSCC [12]. Unlike many lncRNAs, H19 was one of the earliest discovered and is well-characterized in terms of both its structure and functional mechanisms. It is an imprinted gene located on chromosome 11p15.5, highly expressed during embryogenesis and silenced in most adult tissues [13]. Its reactivation in cancerous tissues suggests a pivotal role in tumor biology [14].

  In OSCC, H19 is frequently overexpressed and has been linked to tumor growth, metastasis, and poor clinical outcomes, positioning it as a potential prognostic biomarker [15]. Elevated H19 levels have been correlated with advanced tumor stage, lymph node involvement, and reduced overall survival [16, 17]. These findings highlight its potential utility in risk stratification and personalized treatment planning [18].

  Mechanistically, H19 exerts oncogenic functions through several pathways, including acting as a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) that sequesters tumor-suppressive microRNAs like miR-675, thereby facilitating oncogene expression [19]. Targeting H19 using siRNAs, antisense oligonucleotides, or CRISPR/Cas9 has been shown in experimental models to impair tumor proliferation and metastasis, suggesting therapeutic potential [20]. Several studies, including those by Sekar et al. [21] and Oliveira et al. [22], have demonstrated the functional role of H19 in promoting cancer progression through modulation of cell proliferation, apoptosis, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition [21, 22]. Given its multifaceted involvement in OSCC progression, prognosis, and therapy resistance, H19 stands out among other lncRNAs as a potential non-coding RNA for investigation. Further research into its molecular interactions and clinical applications could pave the way for novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies [23].


## Methodology

### Study design
  This study employed an integrative approach combining in-silico and in-vitro analyses to investigate the relationship between lncRNA H19, miRNA-675, and p53 in patients with OSCC compared to healthy individuals. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Human Ethical Committee (IHEC/SDC/PHD/BIOCHEM-2108/21/TH-035(B)).


### In-silico analysis

#### 2D structure prediction of lncRNA H19
  The secondary structure of lncRNA H19 was predicted using the RNAfold web server (http://rna.tbi.univie.ac.at/cgi-bin/RNAWebSuite/RNAfold.cgi) [24]. The FASTA sequence of H19 was retrieved from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) under Gene ID: 283,120 (species: Homo sapiens) [25]. The sequence was given as input into RNAfold webserver, which predicted the secondary structure based on minimum free energy (MFE) folding and base-pairing interactions.


#### 3D structure prediction of miRNA-675
  The three-dimensional (3D) structure of miRNA-675 was predicted using the RNA Composer web server (https://rnacomposer.cs.put.poznan.pl/) [26]. The secondary structure of miRNA-675, represented in dot-bracket notation, was retrieved from the miRBase database (https://mirbase.org/) and provided as input to RNA Composer [27]. The server generated a 3D model based on its automated RNA structure modeling algorithm.


#### Molecular docking of miRNA-675 and p53
  Molecular docking studies were performed to analyse the interaction between miRNA-675 and p53 using the MDockPP (https://zougrouptoolkit.missouri.edu/MDockPP/) web server [28]. The protein structure of p53 (PDB ID: 1TUP) was retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/) and prepared as the receptor using conventional energy minimization [29–32]. The predicted 3D structure of miRNA-675 and the p53 protein were uploaded into MDockPP for docking analysis. The resulting docked complex was visualized using Discovery Studio. To validate the docking results, the binding pockets of the native complex (1TUP) were compared with the miRNA-675–p53 docked complex, ensuring that the docking occurred in the correct active site.


#### Sample size calculation and grouping
  The sample size was determined using standard statistical parameters with a 95% confidence interval and a 5% margin of error, resulting in 23 participants per group. While modest, this sample size provided sufficient statistical power for preliminary analysis. The study was conducted between 2022 and 2024.


### Group I – Healthy controls

#### Inclusion criteria
  Adults over 18 years of age with no systemic disease and willing to participate.


#### Exclusion criteria
  Individuals with tobacco or alcohol use, systemic illness, pregnancy, lactation, or unwillingness to participate.


### Group II – OSCC patients

#### Inclusion criteria
  Adults above 18 with histologically confirmed well-differentiated OSCC, without recurrence or metastasis, and willing to participate.


#### Exclusion criteria
  Patients with recurrent or metastatic disease, other tumor types, those currently undergoing chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or surgery, individuals with serious medical conditions, or unwilling participants.


### Sample collection and processing
  A 5 ml blood sample was obtained from the antecubital vein of study participants in EDTA coated vacutainer tubes (Purple coded). The samples were centrifuged for three thousand revolutions per minute for fifteen minutes at a temperature of four degrees Celsius. The separated plasma was stored at − 20 °C until further experiments.


### RNA extraction and RT-PCR analysis
  Reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction analysis for the detection of H19, miR-675, U6 and GAPDH was done. Total RNA extraction was done with the Invitrogen RNA isolation kit (catalogue no 4478545). The Thermo Scientific Nano-Drop Lite spectrophotometer was used to determine the quality and yield of RNA that was extracted.

  Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the Takara kit for reverse transcription. Quantification of the obtained cDNA samples was performed with a BioSpectrophotometer. Storage of samples was done at a temperature of − 20 °C until further processing. To amplify for PCR using 2X red dye master mix from ampliqon, a measured amount of approximately 250–500 ng of cDNA was used. For the determination of gene expression, 35 cycles of PCR were done. Relative expression was calculated using the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method. ### Reference gene selection
  For lncRNA H19, GAPDH was used as the internal control, validated for consistent expression across normal and cancerous plasma samples. For miR-675, U6 small nuclear RNA served as the internal control, as it is widely accepted for miRNA normalization.


### Immunohistochemistry (IHC) for p53 protein expression
  To assess p53 protein expression, formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue Sects. (4 µm) were placed on APES-coated slides. Deparaffinization was followed by antigen retrieval using citrate buffer (pH 6). Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with hydrogen peroxide. After protein blocking, slides were incubated with monoclonal anti-p53 antibody (Dako Clone DO7) and subsequently with the secondary antibody. Detection was performed using DAB chromogen and counterstained with Harris hematoxylin.Positive control: Histologically confirmed OSCC tissue with known p53 positivity.Negative control: Slides processed without primary antibody.

  While p53 mRNA expression was not assessed via RT-PCR, protein-level validation via IHC was employed to support the functional impact of miR-675–p53 interaction suggested by in-silico docking.


### Data analysis
  The data was entered in Microsoft Excel and was analyzed using SPSS (Statistical Package for Social Sciences) software version 26.0. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (M ± S.D) and categorical variables were expressed as n and %. Gene Expression Analysis was done by comparing the relative expression levels of H19 and miR-675 between cancerous and normal tissues using chi-square test to evaluate significant differences in expression levels. Correlation analysis between the expression levels of H19, miR-675, and p53 and the clinico-pathological parameters was done using Pearson correlation coefficients to determine the strength and direction of these associations.

## Discussion
  LncRNAs such as H19, and their associated microRNAs like miR-675, have increasingly been recognized as important regulators in tumorigenesis and metastasis across various cancers. They influence key oncogenic processes including epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), cell proliferation, and invasion [33]. Pachnis et al, first identified H19 in fetal tissues [33] and later characterized as a non-protein-coding RNA by Brannan et al. [34], is maternally expressed and paternally imprinted. It is now known to play a dual role acting as either a tumor suppressor or an oncogene depending on the cellular context [35]. In oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), emerging evidence supports its oncogenic function.

  H19 gives rise to miR-675, which is excised from its first exon. The H19/miR-675 axis has been implicated in promoting cancer progression by modulating critical tumor suppressor genes [26, 27]. One proposed mechanism is that H19 acts as a molecular sponge, binding miR-675, which in turn targets p53, resulting in reduced p53 activity and enhanced tumor proliferation. This hypothesis formed the basis of our study.

  In the present study, in-silico analyses successfully predicted the secondary structure of H19 and the tertiary structure of miR-675. Molecular docking demonstrated a stable binding interaction between miR-675 and the tumor suppressor protein p53, suggesting a possible inhibitory effect. Importantly, RT-PCR and IHC analyses showed that expression levels of H19, miR-675, and p53 were significantly upregulated in OSCC patients compared to healthy controls. Furthermore, the expression levels correlated positively with TNM staging, suggesting their roles in disease progression.

  These findings are in agreement with studies such as Guan et al. [36], who reported H19 and miR-675 overexpression in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), and Hong et al. [37], who linked elevated H19 levels with poor clinical outcomes in OSCC. H19 knockdown has been shown to suppress proliferation, migration, and invasion in OSCC and other squamous cell carcinomas, including esophageal SCC [38], and it appears to be particularly overexpressed in tongue cancers [39–41].

  Interestingly, some contradictory reports exist. For instance, two Indian studies and one Chinese study observed downregulation of H19 in OSCC samples using qRT-PCR [42–44]. These discrepancies may reflect ethnic or regional genetic backgrounds, the influence of environmental factors such as tobacco exposure, or differences in methodological design and reference gene selection.

  According to our study, the expression of H19, miR 675 and p53, varied to the clinic-pathological characteristics. Expression of H19, miR-675 and P53(IHC) expression levels increased with increase in level of TNM staging suggesting a significant correlation between H19 expression and tumour recurrences as stated by Esteves et al. in a cohort of 41 head and neck squamous cell carcinoma samples [45]. Eslami et al. by reviewing scientific Literature from 2000—2023 stated that miRNAs can inhibit or stimulate metastasis at all stages, including epithelial-mesenchymal transition, migration, invasion, and colonization, by targeting critical genes. On the other hand, long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) can regulate cancer metastasis by affecting gene expression through cross-talk with miRNAs [46]. Also, a study by Gao liu et al. established the key roles of lncRNA-miRNA functional network in the carcinogenesis of gastric cancer and summarized that H19 regulates the gastric cancer progression via miR-675 [47].

  The expression of p53 in OSCC showed that the expression level increased with increase in level of TNM staging this is in accordance with the study conducted by Gawande et al., who studied 30 OSCC cases and concluded that the expression of p53 increased with the increasing grades of OSCC from well-differentiated squamous cell carcinoma to poorly differentiated squamous cell carcinoma thereby concluding that patient's survival decreases with the increased expression of p53 at the advancing front of the tumor [48]. Our study also found that p53 expression increased with advancing tumor stage. While this may suggest that H19/miR-675 does not inhibit p53, alternative hypotheses must be considered. One possibility is that H19 suppresses wild-type p53 function without affecting its expression levels—consistent with our observation of absent p53 IHC expression in three high-grade OSCC cases. Conversely, in cases with mutant p53, the protein may accumulate despite loss of function. This aligns with Matouk et al., who demonstrated a functional link between p53 and H19 in various cancers [49]. Similarly, Li et al. reported that H19 upregulates miR-675, which in turn inhibits p53 activation in bladder cancer, preventing apoptosis and cell cycle arrest [50].

  Further supporting this regulatory network, Ghapanchi et al. highlighted the oncogenic role of the H19/miR-675/p53 axis in solid tumors, suggesting that this pathway may serve as a critical therapeutic target [51]. As well the same team, Ghapanchi et al. explored lncRNA–miRNA cross-talk in oral cancer models and reinforced the concept of lncRNA-mediated post-transcriptional regulation of p53 [52]. Moreover, Salah et al. emphasized the correlation of H19 with clinicopathological parameters in head and neck cancer, consistent with our findings [53]. Also, Farhadian et al. demonstrated that H19 overexpression promotes chemoresistance and metastasis through EMT modulation, underscoring its clinical relevance [54].

  H19 and miR-675 are significantly overexpressed in OSCC and correlate with TNM stage, suggesting their involvement in tumor progression. Molecular docking confirmed a strong binding affinity between miR-675 and p53, implying functional interference. Protein-level analysis of p53 revealed complex expression patterns, possibly reflecting both H19-mediated inhibition and the presence of mutant p53 in advanced tumors. These findings support the hypothesis that the H19/miR-675 axis modulates tumor behaviour via p53 regulation, highlighting a novel mechanism contributing to OSCC progression.


## Conclusion
  This study provides new insights into the role of the H19/miR-675/p53 regulatory axis in the pathogenesis of OSCC. Our findings demonstrate that H19 and miR-675 are significantly overexpressed in OSCC, with expression levels correlating with advanced TNM stage and p53 protein expression. Molecular docking further supports a direct interaction between miR-675 and p53, suggesting a possible mechanism through which this axis contributes to tumor progression.

  However, the study has certain limitations, including a modest sample size, the absence of p53 mRNA expression analysis, and a lack of functional validation assays. Future research should expand sample size across multicentre cohorts, incorporate quantitative molecular techniques and functional studies, and investigate the diagnostic and therapeutic potential of targeting the H19/miR-675 pathway.

  Our findings underscore the potential of H19 and miR-675 as diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets in OSCC. Further exploration of this regulatory axis may pave the way for more effective risk stratification, personalized therapy, and improved patient outcomes.
