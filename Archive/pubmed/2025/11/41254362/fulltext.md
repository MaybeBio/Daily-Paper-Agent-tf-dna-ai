# Inhibition of DRP1-mediated Mitochondrial Fission and NRF2/HO-1/GPX4-mediated Ferroptosis by Mdivi-1 Protects Against Vascular Cognitive Impairment


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12035-025-05484-2.


## Introduction
  Vascular cognitive impairment (VCI), encompassing a spectrum of cognitive dysfunction stemming from ischemic cerebrovascular diseases, represents a significant and growing public health concern [1]. Chronic cerebral ischemia (CCI), a fundamental hemodynamic characteristic of ischemic cerebrovascular disease, refers to a state of persistently reduced blood flow to the brain, which deprives neurons and glial cells of essential oxygen and nutrients, leading to progressive neuronal dysfunction and damage [2, 3]. This prolonged deprivation triggers a cascade of detrimental events, including excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis, all of which contribute to the cognitive decline characteristic of VCI [4]. While acute stroke events cause immediate and dramatic changes, CCI has a more insidious and progressive effect, often leading to subtle cognitive changes that may initially go unnoticed. The prevalence of VCI increases markedly with age, making it a particularly important consideration for the aging population [5]. The insidious nature of CCI-related VCI underscores the importance of early detection and intervention strategies to the prevention of further neurological deterioration [6]. However, early diagnosis and intervention for VCI remain challenging, and its underlying pathological molecular mechanisms are still not well understood.

  Ferroptosis, a form of regulated cell death, is characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation [7]. This process is regulated by pathways associated with redox homeostasis, iron handling, and mitochondrial activity [8]. Ferroptosis can be initiated by iron overload or the inactivation of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), a critical enzyme responsible for the reduction of lipid hydroperoxides [9]. Conversely, the activation of GPX4 can prevent ferroptosis. Thus, iron metabolism and GPX4 activity are major determinants of sensitivity to ferroptosis. Ferroptosis plays a role in a range of conditions, including VCI and CCI-induced neuronal injury [7], by disrupting critical cellular metabolic pathways and affecting redox homeostasis, iron regulation, and mitochondrial function. In CCI, reduced cerebral blood flow fosters an environment conducive to ferroptosis, which exacerbates the initial ischemic injury and leads to secondary neuronal death [10]. This initiates a vicious cycle in which damaged neurons become increasingly susceptible to ferroptosis, resulting in widespread ischemic injury that extends beyond the initial hypoperfused area [11]. As lipid peroxidation progresses, it spreads to adjacent neurons, aggravating tissue damage and contributing to the cognitive deficits associated with VCI [12, 13]. Understanding the mechanisms of ferroptosis in the context of CCI is therefore essential for developing neuroprotective strategies. Moreover, targeting key components of the ferroptosis pathway offers significant promise for mitigating ischemic neuronal damage and improving outcomes in VCI.

  Mitochondria are essential organelles for neuronal function and survival, and are responsible for the generation of most cellular ATP, which serves as the energy source for neuronal processes such as synaptic transmission, ion transport, and signal transduction [14, 15]. They also regulate reactive oxygen species (ROS) homeostasis and participate in apoptotic and ferroptotic processes [16]. The maintenance of normal mitochondrial function relies on mitochondrial quality control mechanisms, encompassing processes such as fission and fusion. The dysregulation of mitochondrial dynamics, particularly excessive fission, leads to mitochondrial fragmentation and impaired mitochondrial function, which contributes to the occurrence of neurodegenerative diseases, stroke, and VCI [17]. In this context, Mdivi-1, a selective small-molecule inhibitor of dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1, also known as DNM1L)—a key regulator of mitochondrial fission—shows promise as a neuroprotective agent in cerebral ischemia [18]. Mdivi-1 prevents excessive mitochondrial fragmentation, thereby decreasing the release of pro-apoptotic factors and potentially reducing cerebral infarct size [19]. While targeting dysregulated mitochondrial fission may offer a novel therapeutic strategy for VCI, the efficacy and precise mechanisms of action of Mdivi-1 in VCI remain unclear.

  In this study, we investigated the role of neuronal ferroptosis in ICI and explored the mechanism by which Mdivi-1 modulates mitochondrial fission and ferroptosis.


## Materials and Methods

### Animal Model and Treatment Groups
  Thirty-five male Sprague–Dawley rats (6 weeks old, 200 ± 10 g) were obtained from Shanghai Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd (SCXK2023-0004; Shanghai, China) and housed in a SPF facility under a 12-h:12-h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water for 1 week before experimentation. A VCI model was established via permanent bilateral common carotid artery occlusion (BCCAO), as previously described [20]. Briefly, anesthesia was induced in rats with 2% isoflurane (v/v) in 0.5 L/min oxygen, and analgesia was achieved via the intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of chloral hydrate (3.5%, 400 mg/kg). Both carotid arteries were occluded via double ligation with 4–0 sutures following a median cervical incision. Sham controls underwent the same procedure but without BCCAO. Three rats died post-surgery due to either anesthesia or surgical complications. The remaining 32 rats were randomly assigned to the following four groups (n = 8/group): Sham, VCI, Mdivi-1 (VCI + Mdivi-1 [No. HY-15886, Med Chem Express, Shanghai, China]) [20], and Mdivi-1 + ML385 co-treatment (VCI + Mdivi-1 + ML385 [No. HY-100523, Med Chem Express, Shanghai, China]) [21]. Mdivi-1 (3 mg/kg/day), ML385 (30 mg/kg/day), or vehicle were administered daily for 4 weeks via i.p. injection. Two hours after the final injection, the animals were euthanized for further analysis.


### In Vitro Ischemia Model and Treatment Groups
  The HT22 mouse hippocampal neuronal cell line was obtained from ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA). Cells were cultured in special culture medium (GZ20011, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) with 5% CO2. To generate an in vitro ischemia model, oxygen–glucose deprivation (OGD) was induced by exposing HT22 cells (2 × 105 cells/well in 6-well plates) to a hypoxic environment (1% O2, 5% CO2, 94% N2) in a hypoxia chamber (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 4 h. The cells were then returned to normoxic conditions (21% O2, 5% CO2) in fresh medium and incubated for 24 h before subsequent experiments. Control cells underwent the same procedure but without OGD exposure. The experimental groups included (1) control (Con), (2) OGD, (3) OGD + Mdivi-1 (5 μM) (Mdivi-1) [22], (4) OGD + ferrostatin-1 (10 µM, No. S7243, Selleck, Shanghai, China) (Fer-1) [23], and (5) OGD + ML385 (2 µM) (ML385) [24]. Drug dosages were selected based on the literature [22–26] and our preliminary experiments, and were assessed for effectiveness and non-toxicity under experimental conditions.


### Morris Water Maze Test
  To assess the effects of Mdivi-1 on spatial learning and memory in adult rats, a Morris water maze test was conducted as previously reported [27]. A circular pool (diameter: 150 cm) was filled with opaque water, which was maintained at a constant temperature (25 °C–27 °C). Distinctive visual cues were placed around the pool. A submerged platform (10 cm in diameter) was placed in the pool, always in the same quadrant. For the acquisition phase (days 1–4), each rat underwent four trials per day, with the platform submerged 1 cm below the water surface. The rats were released from one of four randomly assigned starting positions and allowed 60 s to locate the platform. If a rat failed to locate the platform within the allotted time, it was gently guided to the platform and allowed to remain there for 15 s before being removed. The inter-trial interval lasted at least 10 min. For the probe trial (day 5), the platform was removed from the pool, and the rat was released from the starting position opposite the platform’s usual location and allowed to swim for 60 s. The escape latency (time to find the platform), the number of times crossing the platform’s previous location (platform crossings), and the swimming speed were recorded and analyzed.


### Histological Examination via Hematoxylin–Eosin (H&E) Staining
  Brain architecture was evaluated through H&E staining. Briefly, cerebral specimens were post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), and then sequentially dehydrated, paraffin-embedded, and sliced in a microtome into 4-μm-thick sections. The prepared sections were subsequently stained with H&E using a kit (G1076, Servicebio) following the manufacturer’s protocols. After air-drying, the tissue sections were mounted on coverslips using mounting medium, and imaged using a light microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) at × 200 magnification.


### Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)
  Hippocampal mitochondria were analyzed by TEM as previously reported [28]. Rats were transcardially perfused with 1 × PBS containing 4% PFA 3 h after the last treatment. Hippocampal tissue (2 mm2 fragments) was fixed in Karnovsky’s fixative (4% PFA, 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 7.4) for 7–8 h at 4 °C [13]. After three 15-min washes in 0.1 M phosphate buffer at 4 °C, the tissue was incubated in 1% osmium tetroxide for 3 h at 4 °C, followed by six 1-min washes in deionized distilled water. Ultrathin Sects. (60–90 nm) were prepared using an ultramicrotome (Leica Ultracut UCT), mounted on copper grids, and counterstained with Reynold’s lead citrate. Mitochondrial damage scores were determined according to Flameng’s scoring system [29].


### Immunofluorescence Staining
  Immunofluorescence staining was performed on brain sections and HT22 cells. Samples were fixed in 4% PFA (20 min, 4 °C), permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (30 min), blocked with 5% BSA in PBS (30 min, room temperature), and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against caspase-3 (1:150, ab179517, Abcam, MA, USA), NeuN (1:200, 24307 T, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), p-Nrf-2 (1:150, AF1609, Beyotime Biotech, Shanghai, China), GPX4 (1:200, HY-P80450, MCE, Shanghai, China), DRP1 (1:200, HY-P80644, MCE), MFN1(1:300, sc-166644, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA), MFN2 (1:200, CY6638, Abways, Shanghai, China), and TOMM20 (WH0009804M1, Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA). After a 2-h incubation with secondary antibodies in the dark, the samples were counterstained with DAPI (C1341S, Beyotime Biotech). Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss, Jena, Germany). Mean fluorescence intensity was quantified across three fields of view per group by an investigator blinded to the experimental conditions.


### Western Blotting
  Total protein was extracted from the hippocampus using the Total Protein Extraction Kit (C006225, Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). Protein concentration was determined using the Pierce BCA protein assay kit (P0012S; Beyotime Biotech). Western blotting was performed using the following primary antibodies: cleaved caspase-3 (1:2000, 9664 T, Cell Signaling Technology), DRP1 (1:1000, HY-P80644, MCE), MFN1 (1:1000, ab221661, Abcam), MFN2 (1:1000, CY6638, Abways), Nrf-2(1:1000, bs-1074R, Bioss, Beijing, China), p-Nrf-2 (1:1000, AF1609, Beyotime Biotech), HO-1 (1:1000, CY8761, Abways), GPX4 (1:1000, HY-P80450, MCE), and tubulin (1:5000, AF2835, Beyotime Biotech). HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:7500; R&D) were used. Blots were developed using an ECL kit (C500044, Sangon Biotech), imaged using an enhanced chemiluminescence system (Millipore, Watford, UK), and quantified via ImageJ software.


### Terminal Deoxynucleotidyl Transferase Biotinylated-dUTP Nick End Labeling (TUNEL)
  Apoptosis was assessed using the TUNEL assay (G1516, Servicebio) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, brain sections were permeabilized and incubated with the TUNEL reaction mixture, followed by counterstaining with Hoechst 33,342 (10 μg/mL). Images of FITC-labeled apoptotic cells were acquired using an Olympus BX51 fluorescence microscope (× 400 magnification), and apoptotic neurons were quantified per field of view.


### Determination of ROS, Malondialdehyde (MDA), and Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP) Contents
  The levels of ROS, MDA, and ATP were quantified using the respective kits (S0033S, S0131S, and S0026, Beyotime Biotech). Measurements were obtained with a microplate reader and analyzed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.


### Reverse Transcription-Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR)
  Total RNA was isolated from tissue or cells using the Total RNA Extraction Kit (BYT430, Beyotime Biotech). cDNA synthesis and amplification were carried out with AceQ qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Q121-02, Beyotime Biotech) in a 10-µL reaction mixture containing 5 µL of 2 × SYBR Green Mix, 0.5 µL each of forward and reverse primers (10 µM), 2 µL of cDNA, and nuclease-free water to 10 µL. Primers (Nrf-2 forward 5ʹ-CAACCCTTGTCACCATCTCAG-3ʹ and reverse 5ʹ-AGCGGCTTGAATGTTTGTCT-3ʹ [product 136 bp]); PTGS2 forward 5ʹ-TCAATGAGTACCGCAAACGC-3ʹ and reverse 5ʹ-TGGTCTCCCCAAAGATAGCA-3ʹ [product 177 bp]); GAPDH forward 5ʹ-CTCAGTTGCTGAGGAGTCCC-3ʹ and reverse 5ʹ-ATTCGAGAGAAGGGAGGGCT-3ʹ [product 120 bp]) and hsa-Drp1 (NM_001363730.2)-siRNA (hsa-Drp1-siRNA1 forward 5ʹ-ACAUUAUGUUGUUAGACAAtt-3ʹ and reverse 5ʹ-UUGUCUAACAACAUAAUGUtt-3ʹ; hsa-Drp1-siRNA2 forward 5ʹ-GGAAUUUGUUGCUCCAGAAtt-3ʹ and reverse 5ʹ-UUCUGGAGCAACAAAUUCCtt-3ʹ) were synthesized by Sangon Biotech. Negative control (NC) and Drp1 siRNA were transfected according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Amplification was performed as follows: an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, 44 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s, and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. The PCR products were resolved on a 2.5% agarose gel at 120 V for 30 min. Relative mRNA levels of target genes were normalized to those of GAPDH.


### mRNA Sequencing (mRNA-seq)
  RNA was extracted after conventional cell culture and OGD treatment using a kit (B511311, Sangon Biotech). mRNA-seq was performed by APExBIO Technology LLC (Shanghai, China) on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).


### Molecular Docking
  Molecular docking of Midvi-1 (CID:3,825,829, C15H10Cl2N2O2S) to NRF2 (ID: 7K2F, Protein Data Bank) was performed using the Maestro docking module (Schrödinger 12.8). Docking results were visualized with PyMOL (Schrödinger LLC, New York, NY, USA). Interactions with a binding energy of − 5 kcal/mol or less were considered for further analysis.


### Measurement of ROS Levels
  ROS levels in the hippocampus were evaluated using the ROS Assay Kit for Superoxide Anion with dihydroethidium (DHE) (S0064S, Beyotime Biotech), following a previously established protocol [30]. Briefly, brain tissue sections were deparaffinized, incubated with 5 μmol/L DHE for 30 min at 37 °C, and subsequently washed with PBS (5 min). Images were captured using a Zeiss fluorescence microscope (Jena, Germany). Quantitative analysis of DHE fluorescence intensity was performed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA), as described in previous studies [28, 31].


### Statistical Analysis
  Data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 7.0 (GraphPad Software) and SPSS v.22.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test was used for intergroup comparisons. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.


## Results

### Mdivi-1 and the Ferroptosis Inhibitor Fer-1 Reduce OGD-Induced Neuronal Apoptosis and ROS Contents In Vitro
  To further investigate the role of ferroptosis and the effects of Mdivi-1, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed on hippocampal HT22 cells under both control and OGD conditions (Fig. 1a). Analysis of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between OGD-treated and control cells revealed that 702 genes were upregulated while 672 were downregulated in the OGD group. A volcano plot illustrating these changes is shown in Fig. 1b. Gene ontology (GO) term enrichment analysis indicated that many of these DEGs were associated with cellular processes, including ion channel activity, calcium ion transport, and cellular responses to external stimuli, consistent with mitochondrial function and stress responses (Fig. 1c).   To investigate the role of ferroptosis in ischemic neuronal injury and the effects of Mdivi-1 and ML385 on neuronal survival, we performed double immunofluorescence staining for caspase-3 and NeuN in HT22 cells following the respective treatments. The results showed that Mdivi-1 and Fer-1 treatment significantly reduced the OGD-induced increase in caspase-3 expression, indicative of decreased neuronal apoptosis, whereas ML385 enhanced the caspase-3 immunofluorescence signal, suggesting that apoptosis was exacerbated (Fig. 1e, f). In addition, considering the complex relationship among ferroptosis, mitochondria, and oxidative stress, we further measured ROS levels in both treated and untreated cells. We found that OGD led to an increase in cellular ROS levels, an effect that was suppressed following Mdivi-1 or Fer-1 treatment, and enhanced with exposure to ML385 (Fig. 1g, h). The protein level of cleaved caspase-3 was also evaluated using western blotting. Mdivi-1 and Fer-1 markedly suppressed the OGD-induced upregulation in cleaved caspase-3 expression, whereas the opposite trend was observed with ML385 treatment (Fig. 1i, j). These results suggested that Mdivi-1 and Fer-1 protect against OGD-induced neuronal apoptosis and oxidative stress, whereas ML385 has the opposite effect.


### Mdivi-1 Ameliorates CCI-induced Vascular Cognitive Dysfunction
  Studies have linked CCI to cognitive deficits [27, 32]. To assess the cognition-enhancing effects of Mdivi-1, rats in the respective treatment groups were subjected to the Morris water maze test. Figure 2a presents representative swimming trajectories, reflecting spatial exploration patterns such as path tortuosity and time spent moving versus time spent pausing. The lowest trajectory density was observed in the VCI group, which may reflect either shorter periods of exploration or a tendency to pause more frequently (e.g., due to cognitive impairment), leading to shorter total distance covered. Mdivi-1 treatment significantly reduced the escape latency (Fig. 2b), which was consistent with improved spatial learning. Additionally, the number of platform crossings (Fig. 2c) and the time spent in the target quadrant were both increased in the Mdivi-1 group compared with those in the VCI model group (Fig. 2d). However, Mdivi-1 treatment did not impact the active swimming speed of VCI rats—calculated as the average velocity during active movement, excluding pauses—despite the tendency for more frequent pauses in the VCI group. However, the Mdivi-1-induced cognitive improvement was significantly attenuated by co-treatment with ML385, an Nrf-2 inhibitor. These findings demonstrated that Mdivi-1 ameliorates VCI, an effect that is dependent on Nrf-2 signaling.Fig. 2Morris water maze test. (a) Representative swimming trajectories. ### Mdivi-1 Mitigates CCI-induced Neuronal Damage

Significant reductions in the number of eosinophilic neurons and nuclear condensation were observed in the Mdivi-1 intervention group relative to those in the VCI model group. Conversely, concomitant Mdivi-1 + ML385 administration exacerbated cellular atrophy, manifesting as pronounced cytoplasmic retraction and nuclear hyperchromasia.   Given the critical role of the hippocampus in learning and memory, we investigated the effects of Mdivi-1 on neuronal apoptosis within the CA1 region of rats. TUNEL staining revealed that Mdivi-1 treatment significantly reduced CCI-induced apoptosis in hippocampal CA1 neurons (Fig. 3b). Nevertheless, this protective effect of Mdivi-1 was reversed by co-treatment with ML385, reflected in an increase in the number of TUNEL-positive cells (Fig. 3c). These results suggested that Mdivi-1 inhibits CCI-induced neuronal apoptosis, an effect that is counteracted by ML385 administration.

To investigate the effects of Mdivi-1 on mitochondrial dynamics, we measured the expression levels of DRP1, MFN1, and MFN2 in brain sections of rats using immunofluorescence and western blotting. Compared to sham controls, CCI led to an increase in the protein expression of DRP1 alongside a decrease in that of MFN1/2 (Fig. 4a, d).   To validate the findings relating to mitochondrial dynamics, hippocampal mitochondrial ultrastructure was examined using TEM in the four groups of rats (sham, VCI, Mdivi-1, and Mdivi-1 + ML385). The extent of mitochondrial injury was assessed using Flameng’s scoring system [29]. While sham-operated rats exhibited intact mitochondrial membranes and cristae (Fig. 5d), rats subjected to CCI displayed severe mitochondrial swelling, cristae disruption, and vacuolation in neurons, accompanied by a swollen endoplasmic reticulum and fewer organelles (Fig. 5d). Notably, Mdivi-1 treatment ameliorated these morphological defects in both mitochondria and the endoplasmic reticulum (Fig. 5d, e), whereas co-treatment with ML385 resulted in disrupted and concentric cristae. Consistent with the ultrastructural observations, the ATP level, a key indicator of mitochondrial function, was significantly reduced in rats of the CCI group. Mdivi-1 administration significantly increased ATP levels in CCI rats (P < 0.05), an effect that was blocked by ML385 co-treatment (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5f). These results confirmed that Mdivi-1 inhibits ROS accumulation and protects mitochondria from ultrastructural damage, thereby improving their function, including ATP production.


### Mdivi-1 Attenuates CCI-induced Ferroptosis via the Nrf-2/HO-1/GPX4 Pathway
  Considering that CCI can trigger mitochondrial dysfunction and, consequently, neuronal ferroptosis, we investigated the effects of Mdivi-1 on ferroptosis using immunofluorescence in the hippocampal CA1 region. The results revealed that compared to sham-operated animals, the expression of GPX4 was significantly reduced in CCI-exposed rats (Fig. 6a, b), as were the levels of p-Nrf-2 (Fig. 6a, c). However, Mdivi-1 treatment significantly reversed these effects, whereas co-treatment with ML385 blocked these ameliorative effects of Mdivi-1 (Fig. 6a–c). Western blot analysis further confirmed these findings, showing that the levels of p-Nrf-2, HO-1, and GPX4 were lower in the CCI group than in the sham-operated controls. However, while Mdivi-1 treatment increased the levels of these proteins (Fig. 6d–g), ML385 co-treatment blocked this effect. To assess lipid peroxidation, we measured MDA levels in rat hippocampal neurons. While rats in the VCI group showed elevated MDA levels relative to the sham group (P < 0.05), Mdivi-1 treatment decreased the contents of MDA; however, ML385 reversed this effect (Fig. 6h). In addition to MDA, we further detected the mRNA levels of Ptgs2. We found that the mRNA levels of Ptgs2 were higher in the VCI group than in the sham-operated group. While this increase was inhibited by Mdivi-1 treatment, ML385 promoted the occurrence of ferroptosis (Fig. 6i). Finally, we knocked down DRP1 using siRNA, followed by Mdivi-1 treatment, and then quantified the Nrf-2 mRNA expression levels. Compared with the DRP1 knockdown group, Mdivi-1 intervention resulted in a further increase in Nrf-2 mRNA levels (Fig. 6j). Molecular docking studies further demonstrated that there was a high binding affinity between Mdivi-1 and Nrf-2, supporting that the two directly interacted (Fig. 6k, l). ## Discussion
  Ferroptosis is implicated in a variety of pathologies, including neurodegenerative disorders (Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease), cancer, stroke, intracerebral hemorrhage, traumatic brain injury, and ischemia–reperfusion injury [33, 34]. In a rat model of middle cerebral artery occlusion and reperfusion (MCAOR), neuronal ferroptosis was shown to be activated, which, along with the associated oxidative stress, exacerbated ischemic injury [11, 35, 36]. Similarly, in HT22 cells subjected to oxygen–glucose deprivation/reperfusion (OGD/R), the levels of both GPX4 and endogenous antioxidants (NADPH, GSH, SOD) were decreased, which promoted ferroptosis and apoptosis [37, 38]. In the present study, RNA sequencing of HT22 neurons after 4 h of OGD revealed that the expression levels of ferroptosis-related genes were upregulated compared with those in control cells. In vitro, exposure to the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 suppressed OGD-induced neuronal apoptosis and promoted cell survival. One limitation of this study was the absence of in vivo validation of the effects of Fer-1. Future studies should evaluate the efficacy of Fer-1 in vivo to further support our in vitro findings regarding brain tissue damage.

  Emerging evidence indicates that inhibiting ferroptosis can mitigate ischemic nerve injury and cognitive dysfunction. For example, the inhibition of Toll-like receptor 4 in neonatal rats was reported to prevent hippocampal hypoxic-ischemic injury and neuroinflammation by suppressing ferroptosis [39, 40]. Propofol, an anesthetic, has also been shown to suppress OGD/R-induced neuronal ferroptosis, potentially via the inhibition of the HIF-1α pathway [41]. However, the role of ferroptosis in chronic ischemic cerebrovascular disease and VCI remains unclear. Potential mechanisms linking ferroptosis to neuronal damage include ferroptosis-associated inflammation and ferroptosis-induced, ROS-mediated mitochondrial damage [12, 42]. Our findings of significant mitochondrial ultrastructural damage in the hippocampus of mice subjected to CCI, potentially linked to ferroptosis, support such a mechanism. Furthermore, our finding that the mitochondrial fission inhibitor Mdivi-1 blocked OGD-induced ferroptosis suggests that mitochondrial protection may represent a therapeutic strategy for ischemic injury.

  Dysregulated mitochondrial quality control, encompassing fission, fusion, and mitophagy, has been strongly implicated in ferroptosis [43]. Liu et al. demonstrated that ferroptosis, marked by increased DRP1 protein levels, is activated in cerebral ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury [44]. DRP1, a key mediator of mitochondrial fission, is inhibited by Mdivi-1. Similarly, Yang et al. reported that Mdivi-1 alleviates hypoxia/propofol-induced ferroptosis in HT22 cells by inhibiting excessive mitophagy [45]. Our study showed that Mdivi-1 significantly activates the Nrf-2/HO-1 pathway, resulting in increased expression of GPX4—a negative ferroptosis regulator—as shown by both immunofluorescence staining and western blot. GPX4 protects against ferroptosis by directly reducing phospholipids and cholesterol hydroperoxides to their benign lipid alcohols. The inactivation of GPX4, either via the depletion of GSH or exposure to direct inhibitors, leads to the derepression of ferroptosis [46]. Mdivi-1 was also reported to improve diabetes-associated cognitive dysfunction through its modulatory effects on mitochondrial fission-mitophagy, consistent with our previous findings that CCI induces the activation of mitophagy [47]. Mechanistically, mitophagy regulates ferroptosis by removing damaged mitochondria and reducing ROS concentrations [48]. Here, we confirmed that Mdivi-1 significantly reduces the cerebral ischemia-induced increases in ROS levels. Furthermore, Mdivi-1 promoted mitochondrial fusion, as evidenced by the Mdivi-1-induced upregulation of MFN1/2 expression—consistent with prior studies—which also contributed to its ferroptosis-inhibiting effects.

  Nrf-2 is a master regulator of cellular antioxidant responses, controlling the expression of numerous genes involved in combating oxidative stress, ischemia, and hypoxia [49, 50]. Dysregulation of the Nrf-2 pathway has been implicated in various neurological disorders. Nrf-2 has been shown to regulate the expression of over 250 genes, thereby influencing cellular processes such as antioxidant defense, neuroinflammation, apoptosis, and neurodegeneration. Notably, it also acts as a key suppressor of ferroptosis by directly regulating the expression of most anti-ferroptosis genes [51, 52]. For instance, the flavonol kaempferol ameliorates OGD-induced neuronal ferroptosis by activating the Nrf-2/SLC7A11/GPX4 axis [53]. Furthermore, in APP/PS1 mice, inhibiting ferroptosis-mediated neuroinflammation via Nrf-2/GPX4 activation mitigates Alzheimer’s-like pathology [37]. In this study, we revealed that Mdivi-1 directly interacts with and activates Nrf-2. This leads to an increase in the expression of HO-1, a Nrf-2 target gene, which, in turn, promotes the inhibition of ferroptosis by upregulating GPX4 and PTGS2 expression. These effects and the cognitive improvements conferred by Mdivi-1-mediated Nrf-2/HO-1 activation were partially reversed when Nrf-2 was inhibited with ML385. Furthermore, Nrf-2 can play both pro-survival and pro-death roles, depending on the level of cellular stress [54]. The inhibition of Nrf-2 observed in our study might promote apoptosis under the mitochondrial stress induced by treatment with Mdivi-1, which is known to alter mitochondrial metabolism. ML385 has been shown to affect mitochondrial ROS production and ROS interactions with apoptotic regulators [55]. These observations imply that the underlying mechanism of action may be more complex than initially hypothesized, potentially involving cross-talk with other stress signaling pathways.

  Chronic reductions in cerebral blood flow in VCI can lead to hypoxia, inflammation, and oxidative stress, which amplify DRP1 activity and suppress Nrf-2. DRP1-driven mitochondrial fission exacerbates ROS production and worsens bioenergetic deficits [22]. However, inhibiting DRP1 preserves mitochondrial integrity, diminishes oxidative stress, and prevents neuronal apoptosis and neurodegeneration, all of which are key contributors to the neuronal loss seen in conditions such as Alzheimer’s disease [56]. For example, tau induces the mislocalization of DRP1, which promotes neurodegeneration [57]. Nrf-2 activation boosts the expression of antioxidant genes, such as those encoding HO-1 and quinone oxidoreductase 1, directly counteracting ROS. Nrf-2 can also suppress DRP1 expression through KEAP1-independent pathways, establishing a feedback loop that stabilizes mitochondrial dynamics while concomitantly improving cellular redox balance [58]. Additionally, Mdivi-1 can suppress mitochondrial Complex I independently of DRP1, thereby disrupting the electron transport chain, diminishing ATP synthesis, and disrupting iron homeostasis [59]. Importantly, Mdivi-1 has been shown to lessen oxidative stress and provide vascular protection in ischemic/hypoxic injury by enhancing Nrf-2 expression [60]. In the present study, Mdivi-1 treatment elevated Nrf-2 mRNA expression beyond the levels observed with DRP1 knockdown. This suggests that the effects of Mdivi-1 are not solely dependent on DRP1 inhibition and may also involve direct interaction with Nrf-2. These pathways synergistically attenuate mitochondrial dysfunction, ferroptosis, and oxidative damage in VCI. The reduction in ROS levels, achieved through Nrf-2 activation, complements the protection of mitochondrial networks afforded by DRP1 inhibition, thereby preserving blood–brain barrier integrity and neuronal function in cerebral ischemia [61, 62]. Recent studies have provided evidence for the existence of such cross-talk, suggesting that combining therapeutic approaches could amplify neurovascular protection [63, 64].

  Despite these insights, our study had several limitations. Here, we demonstrated that chronic cerebral ischemia induces ferroptosis in VCI animal models, as evidenced by the observed increase in ROS accumulation and mitochondrial contraction, alongside the reduction in GPX4 expression. However, directly measuring ferrous iron (Fe2+) overload in HT22 cells using an iron assay kit or a calcein-AM probe would significantly strengthen our conclusions, as ferroptosis is known to be triggered by Fe2+ via Fenton reactions and the consequent ROS accumulation [65]. While our current data establish a functional link between Mdivi-1 and the suppression of ferroptosis, the exact underlying molecular mechanisms require deeper exploration. Future research should evaluate the crosstalk between mitochondrial fission and ferroptosis regulators (e.g., GPX4) or profile lipid peroxidation dynamics and iron metabolism in DRP1-deficient models.


## Conclusion
  In summary, neuronal ferroptosis and mitochondrial dynamics are key contributors to ischemic neuronal injury and cognitive decline (Fig. 7). Our study is the first to demonstrate that Mdivi-1 activates the Nrf-2/HO-1/GPX4 signaling pathway, improving VCI by modulating mitochondrial fission and reducing ferroptosis.
