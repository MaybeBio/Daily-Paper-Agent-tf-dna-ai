# A novel sulfonamide derivative suppresses gastrointestinal cancer progression by targeting Axin2 and β-catenin


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25904-6.


## Introduction
  Gastrointestinal cancer constitutes a significant global health challenge, accounting for over a quarter of all cancer cases worldwide and nearly one-third of cancer-related deaths1. The highest incidence and mortality rates are observed in East Asia2. The incidence of gastrointestinal cancers has been identified as an upward trajectory. By the year 2040, approximately 1.8 million new diagnoses and 1.3 million fatalities related to these cancers would occur. This represents a stark increase of about two-thirds in cases and nearly three-quarters in mortality compared with the investigation from 20203. Current treatment approaches encompass surgical resection, radiation therapy, chemotherapy, and their combinations, with surgery remaining the cornerstone for colorectal, gastric, and esophageal cancers4–6. At present, drug resistance and adverse off-target effects of anticancer drugs are the main challenges faced by cancer treatment, which has prompted medicinal chemists to continuously develop more highly effective and low-toxicity anticancer drugs.

  Sulfonamide drugs were among the earliest to be widely used and employed as preventive and chemotherapeutic agents for various diseases7. Initially developed as antibiotics in the mid-20th century, these compounds have been extensively utilized to combat bacterial infections. However, emerging evidence indicates that sulfonamide derivatives exhibit additional biological activities, including antitumor8, anti-carbonic anhydrase9 or protease inhibitory activity10. In particular, the antitumor activity of sulfonamide compounds has garnered considerable attention11,12. The sulfonamide scaffold, as a unique bioactive unit, plays a crucial role in the development of antitumor drugs13,14. Sulfonamides have been used in clinical trials for more than 30 years, some of which are approved by the FDA for cancer treatment. For instance, a topoisomerase II inhibitor, amsacrine, is used for acute leukemia and malignant lymphoma treatment by binding to DNA of tumor cells15. Currently in clinical trials, Indisulam (N-(3-chloro-7-indolyl)-1,4-benzenedisulfonamide, E7070) is a novel sulfonamide anticancer drug. It exerts its effects through various mechanisms, such as inducing cell cycle arrest in the G1 phase, potent inhibition of carbonic anhydrase, and significant modulation of gene expression, impacting at least 60 transcripts16. Due to a wide range of biological activity and multifunctional structure of sulfonamides, they have emerged as excellent candidates for updating current drugs or developing multi-targeted drugs in polypharmacology17. The unique molecular structure of sulfanilamide derivatives allows them to interfere with critical cellular processes, such as, cell cycle arrest, carbonic anhydrase inhibition, disruption of microtubule assembly, and inhibition of angiogenesis, involved in cancer development and progression13,14,18. Sulfanilamide derivatives show diverse mechanisms of action, including inhibiting enzymatic pathways, disrupting cell signaling cascades, and modulating the tumor microenvironment19–21. Therefore, they hold potentials in targeting various types of cancer, both solid tumors and hematological malignancies22. However, most sulfonamide-based anticancer agents remain confined to preclinical animal studies, with their efficacy and stability in humans lacking systematic evaluation. There is an urgent need to develop a greater variety of sulfonamide derivatives for a broader spectrum of tumor treatment.

  The complex pathogenesis of gastrointestinal cancers involves the dysregulation of various signaling pathways, including the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, which is pivotal in tumor initiation, growth, and metastasis23. Although the disorder of Wnt/β-catenin pathway can be found in various types of tumors, gastrointestinal cancers (especially colorectal cancer) have higher frequency of gene mutations in this pathway (for example, the inactivation of the APC gene occurs in more than 80% of cases)24. In parallel, a clear causal relationship has been confirmed between its carcinogenic mechanism and the constitutive activation of the Wnt pathway. Aberrant activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway is frequently implicated in the onset and progression of gastrointestinal cancers25,26.The β-tubulin protein, essential for cell division, is also a target for several anticancer drugs27,28. Targeting the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, in conjunction with other relevant targets such as β-tubulin, has shown potential in inhibiting cancer cell proliferation, suppressing tumor growth and inducing apoptosis. In previous studies, N-substituted piperidinyl diphenylsulfonyl sulfonamides have been found to inhibit secreted frizzled-related protein I (sFRP-1) and modulate Wnt signaling29. This work thus aim to explore whether the novel synthesized sulfonamide derivatives we identified can affect Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, even driving the progression of cancer.

  Our study aims to confirm the potent anticancer activity of the novel sulfonamide derivative 2406 against gastrointestinal cancers. This sulfanilamide derivative exhibits promising inhibitory effects on both β-tubulin and the Wnt/β-catenin pathways. We conducted a comprehensive evaluation of its anticancer properties, including assessments of cytotoxicity, inhibition of cell proliferation, and cell cycle arrest, underscoring its potential as a multi-targeted therapeutic approach. Developing compounds with dual inhibitory properties holds significant promise for overcoming drug resistance and improving treatment outcomes in gastrointestinal cancers.


## Results

### 3D structure
  Compound 2406 (Fig. S1) is a novel sulfanilamide derivative synthesized and identified using1H nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) and13C nuclear magnetic resonance (13C NMR). The1H NMR and13C NMR spectra are provided in Figs. S2 and S3.


### Anti-proliferative activity
  We conducted cell proliferation assays on GES-1, EC-9706, HT-29, and SGC-7901 cells at 24, 48, and 72 h, using 5-FU as a positive control (Fig S4 A-H). The drug concentration ranged from 0 nM to 20,000 nM, with intermediate concentrations at 10 nM, 100 nM, 500 nM, 1000 nM, 5000 nM, and 10,000 nM. Our results indicate that compound 2406 significantly inhibited EC-9706 and SGC-7901 cell proliferation at the 48-hour mark and HT-29 cell proliferation was enhanced at 24 h while showed increased cytotoxicity at 72 h. We thus selected a 48-hour intervention period for evaluating compound 2406 on EC-9706, SGC-7901 and HT-29 cells. Additionally, the CCK-8 assay found that the IC50 values for 5-FU after 48 h of exposure were substantially higher for SGC-7901 (17.2 µM ), EC-9706 (57.5 µM) and HT-29 (27.9 µM) compared to those for compound 2406 (8.8 µM for 7901, 7.8 µM for EC-9706 and 3.6 µM for HT-29). This suggested that compound 2406 was more effective at inhibiting EC-9706 and HT-29 cells than 5-FU and compound 2406 has a more pronounced effect on the proliferative capacity of EC-9706 and HT-29 cells compared to SGC-7901. Based on IC50 results, we selected concentrations of 1 µM, 2 µM, and 3 µM for HT-29, and 1 µM, 3 µM, and 6 µM for EC-9706 for further experiments to evaluate the potential inhibitory effect of compound 2406 on gastrointestinal tumor cells.


### Compound 2406 inhibited colony formation
  The inhibitory effects of compound 2406 on tumor cell proliferation were further assessed using a colony formation assay. Given that the colony formation assay spans up to 7 days, we optimized the drug concentrations based on IC50 values. EC-9706 and HT-29 cell lines were treated with various concentrations of compound 2406 (0 µM, 0.25 µM, 0.5 µM, 1 µM for HT-29 and 0 µM, 1 µM, 2 µM, 3 µM for EC-9706 ). The results indicated that increasing concentrations of compound 2406 led to a gradual decrease in colony formation ability for both cell lines compared to the control group. When the concentration of compound 2406 reached 0.25 µM for HT-29 and 1 µM for EC-9706, the proliferative ability of both cell lines was inhibited, with a statistically significant difference observed (Fig. 1A-D, P < 0.05).

### Effects of compound 2406 on EC-9706 cell migration and invasion
  The effects of compound 2406 on tumor cell migration and invasion were further assessed using the cell scratch assay and Transwell assay. EC-9706 and HT-29 cells were treated with compound 2406 at various concentrations (0 µM, 1 µM, 3 µM, 6 µM) for 24 h (Transwell assay) or 48 h (cell scratch assay). The results indicated that, compared to the control group, the wound healing ability of EC-9706 cells diminished as the concentration of compound 2406 increased. Additionally, the Transwell assay revealed that compound 2406 significantly inhibited the migration and invasion of EC-9706 cells (Fig. 2A–E, P < 0.05).

### Effects of compound 2406 on tumor cell morphology and apoptosis-related proteins
  Furthermore, we investigated whether compound 2406 induced changes in cell morphology and apoptosis. EC-9706 and HT-29 cells were treated with varying concentrations (0 µM, 1 µM, 2 µM, 3 µM for HT-29 and 0 µM, 1 µM, 3 µM, 6 µM for EC-9706) for 48 h. The results showed a significant reduction in the number of EC-9706 and HT-29 cells as the concentration increased (Fig. 3A–B). Notable morphological changes included rounding and shrinkage of the cells. DAPI staining revealed increased nuclear pyknosis and fragmentation in EC-9706 cells (Fig. 3C), while no significant changes were observed in the nuclear morphology of HT-29 cells (Fig. 3D). Additionally, we examined apoptosis-related proteins and found that a high concentration (3 µM for HT-29 and 6µM for EC-9706 ) of compound 2406 significantly increased the expression of cleaved PARP and cleaved caspase 9 (Fig. 3E-H).

### Effects of compound 2406 on cell cycle
  To determine whether compound 2406 exerts antitumor effects by regulating the cell cycle, we evaluated its impact on EC-9706 and HT-29 cells. Tumor cells were treated with varying concentrations of compound 2406 (0 µM, 1 µM, 2 µM, 3 µM for HT-29 and 0 µM, 1 µM, 3 µM, 6 µM for EC-9706 ) for 48 h, and flow cytometry was used to analyze cell cycle distribution. Compared to the control group, the treated cells showed a gradual increase in G2/M phase arrest of EC-9706 cells as the concentration of compound 2406 increased. At a concentration of 6 µM, significant arrest at the G2/M phase was observed in the EC-9706 cell line (Fig. 4A, C). HT-29 cells also showed G2/M phase arrest, with the effect being concentration-dependent (Fig. 4B, D). Moreover, the result confirmed changes in cell cycle-related proteins, showing that compound 2406 inhibited the expression of Cyclin A2 and CDK1, leading to cell cycle arrest (Fig. 4E–H).

#### Molecular Docking of compound 2406 with β-tubulin and Wnt/β-catenin signaling proteins
  To investigate the potential molecular targets of compound 2406, we performed molecular docking analyses with β-tubulin and key components of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. For β-tubulin, compound 2406 exhibited a docking energy of − 8.3 kcal/mol and formed hydrogen bonds with specific amino acid residues, indicating a spontaneous and favorable interaction. In contrast, the docking energy of 5-FU with β-tubulin was − 5.1 kcal/mol (Fig. 5A, Fig. S5, Table S1). For β-catenin, a central effector in the Wnt pathway, compound 2406 displayed a docking energy of − 7.2 kcal/mol, which was also more favorable than that of the positive control 5-FU (− 5.2 kcal/mol) (Fig. 5B, Fig. S5, Table S1). Taken together, these results indicate that compound 2406 exhibits stronger predicted binding affinities than 5-FU toward both β-tubulin and β-catenin, supporting its potential as a dual-target anticancer agent. Furthermore, compound 2406 was docked with four upstream regulators of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway—Wnt3a, GSK-3β, APC, and CK1—all of which also exhibited negative docking energies (Fig. S6, Table S1), indicating the compound’s potential to interfere with multiple nodes within the Wnt signaling cascade.

(B) Molecular docking studies revealed a significant negative binding energy between compound 2406 and β-catenin.


### Molecular dynamics simulations and MM-PBSA binding free energy (ΔG) analysis
  To further validate the docking results and evaluate the binding stability of compound 2406, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed, followed by MM-PBSA binding free energy (ΔG) calculations. For the β-tubulin–compound 2406 complex, the MD trajectory remained stable, and the calculated binding free energy (ΔG) was − 28.16 kcal/mol (Figs. S7–S8), indicating a thermodynamically favorable and stable interaction. This result is consistent with the docking energy and supports β-tubulin as a potential primary target of compound 2406. Similarly, the β-catenin–compound 2406 complex also exhibited a negative binding free energy (ΔG = − 2.51 kcal/mol). Although the binding free energy (ΔG) value for β-catenin is relatively small, its negative sign still indicates a thermodynamically favorable interaction, supporting the possibility of binding under certain conditions. This is consistent with the molecular docking prediction and the observed downregulation of β-catenin expression in subsequent biological assays.


### Compound 2406 inhibits tubulin polymerization and modulates Wnt/β-catenin signaling in cancer cells
  To experimentally validate the predicted molecular interactions, we examined the functional effects of compound 2406 on microtubule organization and Wnt signaling activity in cancer cells. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that β-tubulin filaments in untreated EC-9706 and HT-29 cells were normally arranged around the nucleus. In contrast, treatment with compound 2406 resulted in a concentration-dependent disruption of tubulin polymerization, with significant impairment observed at 3 µM in HT-29 and 6 µM in EC-9706 (Fig. 6A–B). In parallel, Western blot analysis demonstrated that compound 2406 induced a dose-dependent decrease in β-catenin protein levels and a concomitant increase in Axin2 expression, consistent with inhibition of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway (Fig. 6C–F). These results support the dual mechanism of action of compound 2406 and provide biological evidence for its inhibitory effects on both structural and signaling targets in gastrointestinal cancer cells.

## Discussion
  In this study, compound 2406, a novel sulfonamide derivative, was found to effectively inhibit the proliferation and migration of gastrointestinal tumor cells, specifically EC-9706 and HT-29 cells. It arrests the cell cycle as well as induces apoptosis. Researchers determined effective treatment concentrations (0 µM, 1 µM, 2 µM, 3 µM for HT-29 and 0 µM, 1 µM, 3 µM, 6 µM for EC-9706). Compound 2406 significantly inhibited cell proliferation and migration, increased apoptosis, and enhanced cell cycle arrest. It also inhibited tubulin and affected the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway by decreasing β-catenin and increasing Axin2 protein levels. These results suggest that compound 2406 has substantial anticancer potential, making it a promising candidate for cancer therapy8,30.

  In recent years, numerous novel sulfonamide derivatives have demonstrated significant antitumor activity both in vivo and in vitro22,31. Zengin Kurt et al. synthesized a series of coumarin - sulfonamide derivatives. Bioactivity assays revealed that one of their derivatives selectively inhibited the proliferation of human colon cancer HT-29 cells, with an IC50 of 17.01 µM. Our synthesized compound 2406, however, showed a significantly stronger inhibitory effect on HT-29 cell proliferation, with an IC50 of 3.6 µM, indicating compound 2406 is a promising candidate32. Sulfonamides can inhibit cancer by targeting Lemur tyrosine kinase 3 (LMTK3) enzyme activity, a serine-threonine-tyrosine kinase, which is significantly involved in the β-catenin pathway and leukemia cell survival33. In parallel, sulfonamide drugs can act as tyrosine kinase inhibitors, disrupting key signaling pathways in cancer progression34.

  We found compound 2406 can inhibit the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, thereby affecting cancer progression. The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway plays a crucial role in regulating various cellular processes, including proliferation, differentiation, migration, and apoptosis. Axin2, recognized as a tumor suppressor gene, is a key component in this pathway35–37. It functions by facilitating the degradation of β-catenin, thereby acting as a negative regulator of Wnt/β-catenin signaling. Axin2 achieves this by forming a complex with β-catenin and TCF, which in turn suppresses the expression of Myc, a known target gene of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway that is associated with tumor development38. β-catenin is a key moleculer in the Wnt signaling pathway. A decrease in its accumulation within the nucleus will lead to the inhibition of the transcriptional activation of downstream target genes of the Wnt signaling pathway (such as Myc, Cyclin D1, etc.), thereby suppressing the malignant behaviors of tumor cells, including proliferation, invasion, and metastasis39. Research has indicated that elevated levels of nuclear β-catenin and heightened Wnt/β-catenin signaling activity are frequently observed in various cancer types, underscoring the importance of Axin2’s tumor-suppressing role in maintaining cellular homeostasis. The upregulation of Axin2 and inhibition of β-catenin provide more therapeutic targets for certain cancers36. This is consistent with our findings that compound 2406 can inhibit gastrointestinal tumors by upregulating Axin2 as well as inhibiting β-catenin. Tubulin, a heterodimeric protein, is a fundamental component of the cytoskeleton, which is composed of heterodimers of microtubule proteins (α-tubulin and β-tubulin), the crucial factor for maintaining cellular morphology, cell movement, cell division, and intracellular material transport in life activities28,40,41. The dynamic assembly and dissociation of microtubules are related to the binding of GTP and GDP, and this process is essential to the dynamics of cytoskeleton41,42. Not only one anticancer drug, including periwinkle alkaloids, vincristine, vinblastine, colchicine, and paclitaxel, exert their effects by binding to tubulin43–45. Our findings indicate that compound 2406 can strongly bind to tubulin confirming that it can potentially exert anti-cancer effects.

  Previous studies have shown that sulfonamide drugs can induce cell apoptosis, thereby inhibiting tumor growth. The N-(7-indazolyl) benzenesulfonamide derivative, a promising anticancer agent with a sulfonamide moiety, is known to induce cell cycle arrest and apoptosis46. Additionally, the 4-((6-([1,1’-biphenyl]-3-yl)-9 H-purin-2-yl)amino) benzenesulfonamide compound demonstrates significant antitumor activity against CDK247. However, most sulfonamide anticancer drugs up to date are still undergoing at the animal stage, and their effects in humans are not systematically explored, even unstable. Therefore, developing more types of sulfonamide derivatives for a wider range of tumor treatments is necessary, particularly in different patients from various populations (diverse genetic backgrounds). Sulfonamide compounds generally show good chemical stability due to the strong covalent bonds of the sulfonamide group. Compared with other sulfonamide derivatives, the presence of fluorine atoms in compound 2406, especially the fluorine atom bonded to the benzene ring, may enhance its stability48. Fluorine substituents usually have a strong electron-withdrawing effect49. This may improve the stability of the molecule by reducing the nucleophilicity of the sulfonamide group. Therefore, under different conditions, compound 2406 is likely to possess high stability. In the present work, treatment with compound 2406 induced apoptosis in tumor cells and arrested the cell cycle at the G2/M phase, indicating its significant anticancer potential and its promise as an effective therapeutic agent. Furthermore, compound 2406 reduced cell proliferation, migration, and colony formation, as well as impaired wound healing in gastrointestinal cancer cells. These effects are consistent with those observed in effective anticancer drugs, highlighting the capability of compound 2406 as a therapeutic agent. However, the absence of animal studies and clinical trial data for compound 2406 partially limits our understanding of its pharmacokinetic profile, potential toxicological effects, side effects and therapeutic efficacy in humans. Although sulfonamide derivatives have been widely studied and used, a new compound like compound 2406 still needs to be rigorously tested in preclinical (animal) models and clinical trials to determine its safety and efficacy in humans in the future.


## Conclusion
  This study successfully synthesized a novel sulfonamide derivative, namely compound 2406, and further evaluated its inhibitory effects on the proliferation of gastrointestinal cancer cell lines (EC-9706 and HT-29). The results show that compound 2406 significantly inhibits cell proliferation by inducing apoptosis and cell cycle arrest. In vitro experiments demonstrated that the compound disrupts tubulin polymerization by downregulating the β-tubulin expression and promoting Axin2-dependent β-catenin degradation, supporting its potential as an anticancer therapeutic.


## Materials and methods

### Synthesis and identification of compound 2406
  A 100 mL round-bottom flask was charged with methanol (20 mL), isoindoline (16.8 mmol), pentafluorobenzenesulfonyl chloride (21.8 mmol), and sodium bicarbonate (25.2 mmol), and heated at 65–70 °C under stirring. The progress of the reaction was monitored by TLC. Upon completion, 20 mL distilled water was added to the reaction mixture to quench the reaction. The mixture was then extracted with ethyl acetate (20 mL × 3). The organic phase was dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate, filtered, and the solvent was removed under reduced pressure. The resulting crude product was subjected to dry blending and then purified by silica gel column chromatography using hexane: ethyl acetate (10:1) as the eluent to obtain compound 2406. And compound 2406 was identified using1H NMR and13C NMR.


### Cell culture
  Four human cancer cell lines were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). These included colon cancer cells (HT-29), esophageal cancer cells (EC-9706), gastric adenocarcinoma cells (SGC-7901), and gastric mucosal epithelial cells (GES-1). All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The HT-29 cells were cultured in McCoy’s 5 A medium, the GES-1 and SGC-7901 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium, and the EC-9706 cells were cultured in DMEM medium.


### Cell viability assay
  GES-1, EC-9706, SGC-7901, and HT-29 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5,000 cells per well and incubated for 24 h to determine the anti-proliferative properties of the compound 2406 and 5-Fu. Subsequently, the cells were treated with the compounds and 5-FU at varying concentrations (0 nM, 10 nM, 100 nM, 500 nM, 1000 nM, 5000 nM, 10,000 nM and 20,000 nM) for 24, 48, and 72 h in a CO2 incubator (37 °C, 5% CO₂). Following this, the wells were incubated for four hours after the addition of 10 µl of CCK-8 solution to each well. The absorbance at 450 nm was quantified using a multi-functional microplate reader (Bio-Rad, iMark, USA), and the IC50 values were calculated through non-linear regression analysis using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).


### Wound healing test
  HT-29 and EC-9706 cells were seeded and cultured until they reached 90% confluence. A scratch was made in the cell monolayer using a pipette tip, followed by the addition of varying concentrations of the compounds (0 µM, 1 µM, 2 µM, 3 µM for HT-29 and 0 µM, 1 µM, 3 µM, 6 µM for EC-9706 ) to the culture medium. Cell migration and wound healing were observed and recorded at 0, 24, and 48 h using a Zeiss microscope (Axio Observer, Germany) to assess the effects of different drug concentrations.


### DAPI staining
  The HT-29 and EC-9706 cells were cultured for 48 h in basal medium with varying concentrations of the compounds (0 µM, 1 µM, 2 µM, 3 µM for HT-29 and 0 µM, 1 µM, 3 µM, 6 µM for EC-9706 ). Subsequently, the samples were stained with DAPI for 5 min in the dark, after which images of the staining were captured using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM800, Germany).


### β-tubulin Immunofluorescence
  The HT-29 and EC-9706 cells were seeded on slides in a basal medium containing varying concentrations of 2406 (0 µM, 1 µM, 2 µM, 3 µM for HT-29 and 0 µM, 1 µM, 3 µM, 6 µM for EC-9706 ). Following a 15-minute fixation period with 4% paraformaldehyde and a 10-minute permeabilization period with 0.5% Triton X-100, the slides were prepared for further analysis. The samples were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for one hour and incubated overnight with a fluorescent secondary antibody against β-tubulin (ab32572, Abcam, UK) (diluted 1:100 in 2.5% BSA) at 4 °C. On the subsequent day, the cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), stained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for three minutes, and visualized using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM800, Germany) for imaging and analysis.


### Cell invasion assay
  Prior to use, the basement membrane gel should be thawed at 4 °C and diluted with ice-cold complete cell culture medium. This solution should then be added to the Transwell chambers. To the upper chamber, add serum-free cell suspensions containing 105 EC-9706 cells. In the lower chamber, add 500 µl of basal medium or serum-free medium with varying concentrations of the compounds (0 µM, 1 µM, 3 µM, 6 µM). Following a 24-hour incubation period, the culture medium was removed, and the basement membrane gel and cells in the upper chamber were gently wiped off with a cotton swab. PBS was used to rinse the cells twice, then 4% paraformaldehyde was applied for 25 min, 0.1% crystal violet solution was applied for 30 min, and the cells were air dried at room temperature. On the following day, five fields were observed and counted under a high-power microscope (Zeiss Axio LAB A1, Germany).


### Cell migration assay
  Transwell chambers were utilized for the seeding of EC-9706 cells into 24-well plates. The upper chamber of the Transwell was filled with a serum-free cell suspension containing 10⁵ cells, while the lower chamber was filled with serum-free medium containing various concentrations of compound 2406 (0 µM, 1 µM, 3 µM, 6 µM). After 24 h of incubation, the medium was removed, and the cells were gently wiped away with a cotton swab. The remaining cells were washed three times with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 25 min, and stained with 1 ml of 0.1% crystal violet solution for 30 min. The subsequent day, images were captured, and further analysis was conducted using Image J (Bio-Rad, USA).


### Colony formation assay
  Both cell lines were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 3,000 cells per well and cultured in basal medium with varying concentrations (0 µM, 0.25 µM, 0.5 µM, 1 µM for HT-29 and 0 µM, 1 µM, 2 µM, 3 µM for EC-9706 ) of the compounds. After 6–7 days of incubation, the number and morphology of the cell colonies were observed under a microscope (Zeiss Axio LAB A1, Germany). The samples were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, photographed, and analyzed using Image J.


#### Western blot analysis
  The HT-29 and EC-9706 cells were cultured for 48 h in medium containing varying concentrations of the compounds (0 µM, 1 µM, 2 µM, 3 µM for HT-29 and 0 µM, 1 µM, 3 µM, 6 µM for EC-9706 ). The cell lysates were then separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes after being lysed with lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk at room temperature for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against β-actin (ab8226, Abcam, UK), GAPDH (ET1601-4, Huabio, China), CDK1 (ET1607-51, Huabio, China), Cyclin B1 (ab32053, Abcam, UK), Cleaved PARP (ET1608-10, Huabio, China), Cleaved Caspase-9 (ab2324, Abcam, UK), β-catenin (ab32572, Abcam, UK), and Axin2 (ab109307, Abcam, UK) diluted in 5% BSA. On the following day, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies (goat-anti-rabbit: ZB-2301, ZSGB-bio, China and goat-anti-mouse: ZB-2305, ZSGB-bio, China) in 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. The proteins were visualized using Image Lab (Bio-Rad, USA).


### Cell cycle experiment
  HT-29 and EC-9706 cells were seeded for 48 h in medium containing different concentrations of the compounds (0 µM, 1 µM, 2 µM, 3 µM for HT-29 and 0 µM, 1 µM, 3 µM, 6 µM for EC-9706 ). Following this, the cells were washed once with pre-chilled PBS, digested with trypsin, and collected. The cell pellets were fixed with pre-cooled 70% ethanol and stained with propidium iodide in the dark. Flow cytometry was employed to analyze the DNA content at a wavelength of 488 nm.


#### Molecular docking
  The protein structures of interest were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB). Molecular docking was conducted using AutoDock Vina 1.5.750, and the results were visualized with PyMOL 2.4.0 and Discovery Studio 201951.


#### Molecular dynamics simulation
  This study employed Gromacs 2022 for molecular dynamics simulations. The pdb2gmx tool and AutoFF web server were used to obtain force field parameters. The receptor protein was parameterized with the CHARMM36 force field, and the CGenff force field was used for the ligand52. A 1 nm TIP3P water box was added around the system for solvation. Ions were introduced with the gmx genion tool to neutralize the system. Long-range electrostatic interactions were handled by the PME method with a 1 nm cutoff. All bonds were constrained using the SHAKE algorithm, and the Verlet leapfrog algorithm set the integration time step at 1 fs. Energy minimization was performed before simulation, including 3000 steepest descent steps and 2000 conjugate gradient steps. The process involved restraining the solute and minimizing water energy, then restraining counterions and minimizing energy, and finally minimizing the entire system without constraints. The simulation was run under NPT conditions at 310 K for 500 ns. GROMACS tools like g-rmsd, g-rmsf, g-hbond, g-rg, and g-sasa were used to calculate RMSD, RMSF, HBonds, Rg, and SASA. The MM-PBSA binding free energy of the complex was computed with the g_mmpbsa package in GROMACS.


### Statistical analysis
  Data analysis was carried out using SPSS 20.0 software (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) on the results of three independent experiments. Data from the treated group were compared with the corresponding control group to ensure standardized processing. A Dunnett’s t-test (comparing multiple treated groups to the same control group) was used to evaluate significant differences between the experimental and control groups. Our statistics were displayed as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD), with P < 0.05 referred to significance.
