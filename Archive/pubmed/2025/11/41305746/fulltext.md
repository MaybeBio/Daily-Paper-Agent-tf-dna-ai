# Integrative bioinformatics analysis for identifying the mitochondrial-related gene associated with immune infiltration in sarcopenia


## Abstract
  The increasing prevalence of sarcopenia (SAR) has raised significant concerns in healthcare. Although mitochondrial dysfunction and immune disorders are recognized as risk factors, the interactions between them remain unclear. This study aims to identify potential diagnostic biomarkers associated with both phenotypes in the progression of SAR. Three transcriptional datasets were obtained from the GEO database. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed to explore the features of the training set, followed by filtering the differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Weighted gene co-expression network analysis was applied to select gene modules closely related to SAR. SAR-mitochondria-related DEGs were then determined by intersecting the DEGs, weighted gene co-expression network analysis, and mitochondrial-related genes from the MitoCarta3.0 database. Hub genes were further explored using LASSO and random forest machine learning algorithms. The regulatory molecules of these hub genes were predicted using the NetworkAnalyst database. Subsequently, receiver operating characteristic analysis was performed and the immune infiltration was analyzed using the CIBERSORT algorithm. A SAR model was established in C2C12 cells using d-galactose, and RT-qPCR experiments were performed for further validation. Gene set enrichment analysis results revealed that the training set genes are mainly enriched in mitochondrial function and energy metabolism. Through the machine learning methods, 5 hub genes were screened out from 32 SAR-Mito DEGs, namely MTRF1L, MICU1, DHTKD1, ACADM, and FHIT. A total of 42 transcription factors and 10 miRNAs strongly associated with the hub genes were detected. These hub genes demonstrated solid diagnostic potential in both training and validation sets. Furthermore, immune infiltration analysis indicated a significant reduction in neutrophil levels in SAR patients. The downregulation of the hub genes in d-galactose-induced C2C12 cells was confirmed. Collectively, our findings identified 5 potential biomarkers for the diagnosis and therapy of SAR and emphasized the interaction between mitochondrial function and the immune response in the development of this condition.


## 1. Introduction
  Sarcopenia (SAR) is a skeletal muscle disorder characterized by the accelerated loss of skeletal muscle mass and function.[1] Although the prevalence of SAR varies greatly due to the differences in the diagnostic criteria, race, and region, conservative estimates indicate that it still affects 5% to 10% of the general population.[2] Patients with SAR face an increased risk of falls, fractures, and premature mortality,[3,4] which significantly diminish their quality of life and contribute to higher healthcare costs. Additionally, SAR is often accompanied by the comorbidities such as diabetes and osteoporosis.[5,6] Therefore, early detection and prompt intervention are essential in managing SAR. According to the latest Delphi consensus from the Global Leadership Initiative in Sarcopenia (GLIS), muscle mass, muscle strength, and muscle-specific strength were considered key indicators for diagnosing SAR.[7] This highlights that current diagnostic criteria focus primarily on macroscopic features, with a notable lack of universally recognized biomarkers. Furthermore, no drugs have been reported to have specific benefits for the prevention and treatment of SAR.[8] Thus, further research is urgently needed to identify critical pathogenic targets, which could serve as the basis for early diagnosis and targeted therapeutic interventions.

  The pathogenesis of SAR is intricate and complex, with key factors including muscular mitochondrial dysfunction, redox imbalance, disruption of protein homeostasis, chronic inflammation, and alterations in hormonal status.[9,10] Previous research has highlighted mitochondrial dysfunction as a central regulator in the aging of skeletal muscle and the development of SAR.[11] Mitochondria are the powerhouse of the cell, responsible for generating the ATP required for normal muscle contraction. Mitochondrial dysfunction is acknowledged as one of the hallmarks of aging,[12] which can disrupt energy metabolism in muscle cells, leading to declines in both muscle structure and function.[13] Meanwhile, muscular mitochondrial dysfunction also results in an excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which may increase the risk of cellular damage by targeting mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA).[14,15] Notably, ROS generated by dysfunctional mitochondria can also initiate an inflammatory response by activating the NF-κB signaling pathway.[16] Inflammation is regarded as the impetus behind muscle wasting.[17] Most elderly individuals experience inflammaging, a chronic condition marked by elevated levels of inflammatory markers in the blood, which is considered a risk factor for SAR.[18] While mitochondrial dysfunction and immune inflammation are closely associated with SAR, the interaction and regulatory mechanisms between these factors remain insufficiently elucidated.

  This study aims to investigate the molecular regulatory mechanism of mitochondrial-immune axis in SAR using bioinformatics methods. Gene sets related to SAR were sourced from the GEO database, while mitochondrial-associated genes were retrieved from the Mitocarta3.0 database. Enabled by differential expression analysis, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) analysis, and machine learning algorithms, we identified 5 hub genes strongly associated with SAR and mitochondrial dysfunction. Moreover, the NetworkAnalyst database was utilized to identify possible upstream and downstream regulatory molecules of these genes. The correlation between these genes and immune cell infiltration was also examined. And the hub genes were verified by the reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). These findings provide a novel perspective for better understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying SAR pathogenesis, offering valuable insights into the identification of promising biomarkers and therapeutic targets.


## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1. Data collection
  The flowchart of the study is detailed in Figure 1. Regarding the control of confounding factors, the study primarily relied on rigorous data selection and preprocessing. Micro-array data (accession number GSE38718, GSE1428, GSE25941) were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) public database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) by GEOquery package, ensuring consistency in the same sample sources and diagnostic criteria for SAR. In the analysis, we utilized GSE38718 as the training set, while the others served as validation sets. To investigate the variations between samples, we conducted dimensionality reduction of the data by principal component analysis. A total of 80 samples were included, including 39 control vastus lateralis muscle samples and 41 SAR muscle samples. The specific details of the selected datasets are presented in Table 1. Additionally, a total of 1136 human genes encoding strong supportive proteins for mitochondrial localization were retrieved from the MitoCarta 3.0 database (http://www.broadinstitute.org/mitocarta).[19]


### 2.2. Identification of DEGs and GSEA analysis
  Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the control and SAR muscle samples were identified by the R package “limma.”[20] The screening criteria were set to P < .05 and |log FC| > 0.5. The differential analysis results are visualized by a volcano plot using the R package “ggplot2,” while significantly expressed molecules are visualized by a heatmap using the “ComplexHeatmap” package.

  To further explore the characteristics of the training set, we performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)[21] using the R package “clusterProfiler.” This method was applied to determine whether a predefined gene set exhibits statistically significant and consistent differences between the 2 biological states. For this purpose, we selected “c2.cp.all.v2022.1.Hs.symbols.gmt” and “c2.cp.kegg.v2022.1.Hs.symbols.gmt” gene sets from the MSigDB database (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/collections.jsp).[22] Significantly enriched results were identified based on the cutoff values of P.adj < .05 and FDR (q-value) < 0.25.


### 2.3. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)
  WGCNA is a bioinformatics tool used for constructing and analyzing weighted gene co-expression networks, enabling the identification of gene modules and their correlations with clinical traits.[23] In this study, we used the R package “WGCNA” to construct a co-expression network based on the gene expression data of GSE38718. First, genes with a standard variance greater than the top 25% were extracted, and the outliers were removed. Second, an optimal soft threshold was selected to establish a scale-free co-expression network. Third, a topological overlap matrix (TOM) was constructed to group genes with similar expression profiles into gene modules. Subsequently, we assessed the dissimilarity among module eigengenes, set a threshold for the module dendrogram, and merged closely related modules. Lastly, core genes in the most relevant modules were identified based on the criteria that module membership (MM) > 0.5 and that gene significance (GS) > 0.5.


### 2.4. Functional enrichment analysis of the SAR-Mito DEGs
  The intersection of DEGs, mitochondrial-related genes, and genes in the key module from the WGCNA analysis was determined using the R package “VennDiagram.” The overlapping genes from these datasets were designated as SAR-Mito DEGs for subsequent analysis. To refine the selection and minimize the risk of high false discovery rates,[24] the Wilcoxon test was applied to these candidate genes, comparing their expression levels between the control and SAR groups. Potential biological roles and pathways associated with the SAR-Mito DEGs were investigated through gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses (P < .05). The GO analysis focused on identifying enriched biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF), while the KEGG analysis provided insights into the metabolic and signaling pathways.[25,26]


### 2.5. Machine learning algorithms for the selection of the hub genes
  To further identify the hub genes for diagnosing SAR, machine learning algorithms, namely least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression and random forest (RF), were utilized via the R package “glmnet” and “randomforest,” respectively. The LASSO algorithm performs both regularization and feature selection, enhancing predictive accuracy and interpretability.[27] Meanwhile, the RF algorithm addresses potential overfitting issues inherent in a single decision tree by aggregating predictions from multiple decision trees, thereby improving predictive reliability. The intersection of the results from both algorithms was used to identify potential hub genes for diagnosis.


### 2.6. Construction of interaction networks between the hub genes and related TFs and miRNAs
  The NetworkAnalyst database,[28] a powerful online visual analysis platform for transcriptome profiling, network analysis, and meta-analysis of the gene expression data, was employed in this section. The platform is capable of generating protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks, gene regulatory networks, gene co-expression networks, as well as networks for toxicogenomics and pharmacogenomics research. In this study, the transcription factors (TFs) and the microRNAs associated with the hub genes were identified using the JASPAR[29] and the TarBase v9.0[30] databases. For the miRNA analysis, the network tool of the minimum network was selected. The TF-gene and the gene-miRNA interaction networks were visualized using the Cytoscape software.[31]


### 2.7. Construction and validation of the diagnostic model
  To evaluate the diagnostic potential of the hub genes, their expression levels were compared between the control and SAR groups, and their diagnostic values were assessed through the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis by the R package “pROC.” The area under the curve (AUC) and the 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated and represented the diagnostic and predictive value of these genes. Validation of the diagnostic model was subsequently performed using the independent datasets GSE1428 and GSE25941.


### 2.8. Immune infiltration analysis
  To characterize the immune cell profiles between the control and SAR groups, the cell-type identification by estimating relative subsets of RNA transcripts (CIBERSORT) algorithm was applied to estimate the immune infiltration based on twenty immune cell markers available from the CIBERSORTx platform (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/). CIBERSORT uses gene expression profiles to calculate and infer the composition of immune cells in the tissue samples.[32] The relative proportions of immune cells were visualized using bar plots, while a heatmap illustrated correlations between different immune cell types. The differences in immune infiltration between the 2 groups were illustrated via a box plot.


### 2.9. Cell culture
  C2C12 mouse myoblasts were procured from China Center for Type Culture Collection (CCTCC, Wuhan, China), and maintained in the humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, SH30022.01, Hyclone, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, 11011-8611, Four Seasons Green, Zhejiang, China) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution (15140122, Gibco, USA) was used for cell culture. To establish a SAR cell model, C2C12 cells were exposed to d-galactose (40 g/L; ST1219-25g, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) for 48 hours.


### 2.10. Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)
  Total cell RNA was extracted using the RNA extraction kit (TSP413, Tsingke Biotech, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. After the assessment of the concentration and quality, the extracted RNA was subjected to reverse transcription into cDNA using the SynScript®III RT SuperMix for qPCR kit (TSP314M, Tsingke Biotech, China). RT-qPCR was conducted using a ArtiCanATM SYBR qPCR Mix kit (TSE501, Tsingke Biotech, China). Expression levels of the target genes were normalized against the endogenous reference gene GAPDH. The data were calculated with the 2−ΔΔCT method. The primer sequences used for RT-qPCR are listed in Table 2.


### 2.11. Statistical analysis
  All statistical analyses were conducted using the R software (version 4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Differences between the 2 groups were evaluated using either nonparametric tests or t-tests, depending on the data distribution. Comparisons between multiple groups were analyzed by 1-way ANOVA with correction by Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Different enrichment analyses were statistically analyzed and corrected according to the R package described above. The difference of the continuous variables was assessed using the Wilcoxon test, while the correlation of the continuous variables was determined by the Pearson correlation. All tests were 2-tailed, with P < .05 considered statistically significant.


## 3. Results

### 3.1. Identification of the DEGs and GSEA analysis
  Principal component analysis of the GSE38718 dataset revealed a clear distinction between the control and SAR samples (Fig. 2A). Based on the specified criteria, 750 DEGs were identified, comprising 304 upregulated genes and 446 downregulated ones (Fig. 2B). Genes with the most significant expression differences were displayed using a heatmap (Fig. 2C). GSEA was performed to investigate significantly enriched functional terms between the control and SAR samples in the training set. Results indicated that the genes primarily involved in GO terms related to mitochondrial protein-containing complex, inner mitochondrial membrane protein complex, mitochondrial respiratory chain complex assembly, extracellular matrix structural constituent and mitochondrial matrix, as well as enriched in the KEGG pathways including Parkinson’s disease, oxidative phosphorylation, Leishmania infection and Huntington’s disease (Fig. 2D, E).


### 3.2. Identification of the key module genes by WGCNA
  As depicted in Figure 3A, B, the soft threshold of 6 was chosen based on the scale independence and average connectivity (scale-free R2 = 0.86). Using a module merge threshold of 0.35 and a minimum size of 30, 15 gene co-expression modules were generated and labeled with different colors (Fig. 3C, D). Furthermore, correlation analysis of the gene modules and clinical trait suggested that the bisque4 module was highly associated with SAR (r = −0.77, P = 3.0e−5; Fig. 3E). We also conducted a correlation analysis between the module membership (MM) and gene significance (GS), which revealed a significant positive correlation (Fig. 3F). Therefore, the 1541 genes in the bisque4 module were considered SAR-related genes and were selected for further analysis.


### 3.3. Selection and functional enrichment analysis of the SAR-Mito DEGs
  The SAR-Mito DEGs were defined as the intersection of the DEGs, SAR-related genes, and mitochondria-related genes from the MitoCarta 3.0 database (Fig. 4A). Differential expression of these 32 genes between the control and SAR groups was confirmed by the Wilcoxon tests (Fig. 4B). The function of these genes was investigated by GO and KEGG enrichment analyses afterwards. The most enriched GO terms included BP terms such as cellular respiration, mitochondrial translational termination, mitochondrial gene expression, energy derivation by oxidation of organic compounds, and translational termination; CC terms included mitochondrial protein complex, mitochondrial inner membrane, mitochondrial matrix, inner mitochondrial membrane protein complex, and mitochondrial intermembrane space; MF terms included nucleotide diphosphatase activity; oxidoreductase activity, acting on the aldehyde or oxo group of donors; structural constituent of ribosome; flavin adenine dinucleotide binding; and translation factor activity, RNA binding (Fig. 4C–E). KEGG analysis revealed that lipoic acid metabolism, 2-oxocarboxylic acid metabolism, thermogenesis, Parkinson disease, and Prion disease were most enriched (Fig. 4F).


### 3.4. Selection of the hub genes of sarcopenia using machine learning
  The LASSO regression and RF machine learning algorithms were applied to select the potential hub genes associated with the diagnosis of SAR. LASSO regression analysis identified 9 crucial genes with the lowest binominal deviance (Fig. 5A, B). After ranking the SAR-Mito DEGs according to the gene importance score, 10 potential candidates were detected by the RF algorithm (Fig. 5C, D). By integrating the 2 results, 5 hub genes were yielded, namely mitochondrial translation release factor 1 like (MTRF1L), mitochondrial calcium uptake 1 (MICU1), dehydrogenase E1 and transketolase domain containing 1 (DHTKD1), acyl-CoA dehydrogenase medium chain (ACADM), and fragile histidine triad diadenosine triphosphatase (FHIT; Fig. 5E).


### 3.5. Prediction of the TFs and miRNA associated with the hub genes
  After identifying the hub genes, we longed to explore their potential upstream and downstream regulatory factors. Enabled by the online tool NetworkAnalyst and Cytoscape software, we constructed a TF-gene interaction network, which contains 42 nodes and 53 edges (Fig. 5F). Specifically, MTRF1L was regulated by 8 TFs, MICU1 was regulated by 16 TFs, DHTKD1 was regulated by 5 TFs, ACADM was regulated by 15 TFs, FHIT was regulated by 9 TFs. The gene-miRNA interaction network includes 10 nodes and 17 edges (Fig. 5G). miRNA hsa-mir-34a-5p, hsa-mir-26b-5p, hsa-mir-128-3p, hsa-let-7b-5p, and hsa-mir-217 were detected. The first 3 of these miRNAs all targeted 4 hub genes.


### 3.6. Construction and validation of the diagnostic model
  To gain a clearer insight into the diagnostic significance of hub genes in SAR, we carried out ROC analyses. As depicted in Figure 6A–E, all 5 hub genes showed excellent diagnostic value in the training dataset. The AUC values of MTRF1L, MICU1, DHTKD1, ACADM, and FHIT were 1.000, 0.973, 0.973, 0.982, 0.964, respectively. Furthermore, the GSE1428 and GSE25941 datasets served as the test sets for validation. In the GSE1428 dataset, the AUC values of MTRF1L, MICU1, DHTKD1, ACADM, and FHIT were 0.625, 0.775, 0.708, 0.675, 0.567, respectively (Fig. 6F). In the GSE25941 dataset, the AUC values of MTRF1L, MICU1, DHTKD1, ACADM, and FHIT were 0.625, 0.527, 0.587, 0.654, 0.902, respectively (Fig. 6G).


### 3.7. Immune cell infiltration analysis
  The levels of the immune infiltration were estimated by the CIBERSORT algorithm. The relative proportion of the immune infiltrating cells in the control and SAR groups was depicted in Figure 7A. Next, the correlation between different immune cells in SAR progression was assessed (Fig. 7B), demonstrating that the naive B cells had the strongest positive correlation with the naive CD4 T cells (r = 0.74, P < .0001), and the plasma cells had the highest negative correlation with the neutrophils (r = −0.67, P < .001). Among the 20 types of immune cells, the CD8 T cells and neutrophils showed significant differences between the 2 groups (Fig. 7C). The former was more expressed in the SAR group, while the latter had the opposite result.


### 3.8. Preliminary validation of the hub genes
  To validate the bioinformatics predictions, RT-qPCR experiments were performed to evaluate the relative mRNA expression levels of MTRF1L, MICU1, DHTKD1, ACADM, and FHIT in a d-galactose-induced SAR cell model. Compared with the control group, the SAR group exhibited significant downregulation of these 5 hub genes (P < .05; Fig. 8A–E).


## 4. Discussion
  Since 2016, SAR has been officially defined as a muscle degenerative disease,[33] marking a significant milestone that has greatly advanced the development and refinement of its management strategies. Against the backdrop of the accelerating global population aging, this condition is increasingly emerging as a critical public health concern.[34] Mitochondrial dysfunction[35] and immune inflammation[36] have been identified as major risk factors. However, their specific roles and the mechanisms through which they interact in the pathological progression of SAR remain insufficiently elucidated. Therefore, further research is eagerly needed to identify potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for SAR.

  In this study, we performed an integrative bioinformatics analysis using data from the GEO and the MitoCarta 3.0 databases. GSEA analysis revealed that the SAR-related gene set GSE38718 was predominantly enriched in mitochondrial function and energy metabolism. Using WGCNA analysis, we identified gene modules closely linked to SAR. By intersecting key gene modules, DEGs, and mitochondrial-related genes, we screened out 32 SAR-Mito DEGs. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses indicated that they were primarily involved in the biological functions such as mitochondrial biogenesis, protein metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation, and ribosome function. To further refine the candidate gene selection, we applied machine learning algorithms, including the LASSO and the RF, resulting in 5 hub genes: MTRF1L, MICU1, DHTKD1, ACADM, and FHIT. We predicted potential regulatory factors for these genes and validated their diagnostic potential through ROC analyses and RT-qPCR. Furthermore, immune infiltration analysis suggested significant differences in CD8 T cells and neutrophils between control and SAR groups.

  Mitochondrial dysfunction is widely recognized as a key contributing factor to the development of SAR. The downregulation of the 5 hub genes are predicted to have positive correlations with SAR. Mitochondrial translation release factor 1 Like (MTRF1L or MTRF1a) is involved in mitochondrial translation termination by facilitating the release of the nascent polypeptide chain from the ribosome upon recognition of the termination codons UAA and UAG.[37] It indirectly affects mitochondrial function and cellular energy metabolism by participating in the mitochondrial protein synthesis.[38] The decrease in mitochondrial biogenesis can therefore result in muscle atrophy.[39]

  Mitochondrial calcium uptake 1 (MICU1) plays a critical role in maintaining mitochondrial calcium homeostasis and serves as a gatekeeper in regulating mitochondrial Ca²+ uptake.[40,41] The loss of MICU1 can lead to mitochondrial Ca2+ overload, which in turn triggered the accumulation of ROS.[42] The disruption of mitochondrial Ca2+ homeostasis is also closely linked to muscle disorders.[43] Besides, the loss of the bioenergetic role of MICU1 contributes to muscle weakness and fatigue, and muscle-specific lack of MICU1 in mice recapitulates the skeletal muscle symptoms observed in patients.[44]

  Dehydrogenase E1 and transketolase domain containing 1 (DHTKD1) is a nuclear-encoded mitochondrial precursor protein, which can promote mitochondrial ATP production and contribute to mitochondrial biogenesis.[45] It functions as a core enzyme within the mitochondrial tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. Its deficiency directly impairs mitochondrial respiratory chain function, reduces ATP synthesis efficiency, and increases ROS production.[45] This metabolic impairment may consequently lead to abnormal function in skeletal muscle fibers. Furthermore, previous study has reported that mutations in DHTKD1 can affect neurological development and the DHTKD1Y486* knock-in mice could show an accumulation of mitochondria and an elevated energy metabolic state.[46]

  Acyl-CoA dehydrogenase medium chain (ACADM) is an enzyme that catalyzes the initiation of mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation.[47] During aging, adipose inflammation causes fatty infiltration in skeletal muscle, disrupting the β-oxidation of fatty acids and contributing to a decline in muscle strength and function.[48] In the skeletal muscle of aged mice, significantly lower mRNA and protein levels of ACADM were observed compared to young mice, suggesting its potential involvement in aging-associated muscle functional decline (e.g., SAR).[49] Additionally, in the diabetic mouse models, the expression of ACADM in the skeletal muscle can be regulated by the STING signaling pathway. It’s reported that STING deficiency or inhibition upregulates PPARγ, thereby increasing the expression of ACADM, ameliorating the disorders of lipid metabolism, and attenuating diabetes-associated muscle atrophy.[50]

  Fragile histidine triad diadenosine triphosphatase (FHIT), often referred to as a guardian of the genome, plays a tumor-suppressive role by promoting apoptosis.[51,52] Additionally, FHIT can enhance the sensitivity of Ca²⁺ transporters, thereby facilitating the increased mitochondrial calcium uptake.[53] However, the relationship between FHIT and aging-related muscle changes remains underexplored and warrants further investigation.

  Of note, chronic inflammation is potentially a significant factor in the pathogenesis of SAR.[36] Senescent cells can release the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), which contributes to chronic inflammation and drives the senescence of normal cells.[54] In SAR, the accumulation of senescent muscle cells drives chronic inflammation and muscle wasting. Prolonged chronic inflammation can induce the senescence of the immune cells, impairing immune function and reducing the body’s ability to eliminate senescent cells and inflammatory factors. This ultimately creates a self-perpetuating cycle of inflammation and aging.[55] The systemic immune inflammation index (SII), calculated from neutrophil, lymphocyte, and platelet counts, serves as an objective metric for assessing inflammation and provides a more accurate assessment of inflammatory changes within the body.[56] Shi et al[57] reported a significant association between elevated SII levels and an increased risk of low muscle mass, suggesting that anti-inflammatory therapy could be a potential strategy to mitigate muscle mass loss. Meanwhile, a recent meta-analysis has suggested a positive correlation between SII and SAR.[58] However, the interplay between the immune inflammation and the mitochondrial dysfunction, both of which are critical risk factors for SAR, remains poorly understood. To explore this relationship, we conducted an immune infiltration analysis of genes associated with mitochondria and SAR utilizing the CIBERSORT algorithm. Neutrophils are key players in innate immunity, responsible for phagocytosing pathogens, cellular debris, and senescent cells. A reduction in neutrophils may compromise their ability to clear these senescent cells, allowing SASP factors to persist and exacerbate muscle atrophy. This aligns with our observation of the immune cell infiltration analysis. Previous studies have shown that the dysregulation of the PI3K-Akt pathway in neutrophils may promote the development of SAR by decreasing muscle synthesis and increasing catabolism.[59] In inflammatory myopathies, CD8+ T cells can invade non-necrotic muscle fibers and directly destroy myocyte structure by releasing cytotoxic molecules such as perforin. This invasive behavior leads to muscle fibrosis and loss of function, which is an important pathological basis of SAR.[60] This is also consistent with our observation that CD8+ cells were highly expressed in the SAR group.

  To investigate the regulatory elements of the 5 hub genes, we constructed a network of TF-gene and gene-miRNA interactions. The top 5 transcription factors in terms of betweenness centrality are NFIC, GATA2, and ELF5. Of them, the knockdown of NFIC promoted bovine myoblast proliferation.[61] GATA2 aligned with a previous study identifying regulators of SAR.[62] There is currently no direct evidence for the relationship between ELF5 and SAR. In terms of microRNA, hsa-mir-34a-5p, hsa-mir-26b-5p, hsa-mir-128-3p, hsa-let-7b-5p, and hsa-mir-217 were identified. The first 3 of these miRNAs all targeted 4 hub genes. The actual regulatory effects of the above regulatory factors on SAR may require further experimental and clinical verification.

  However, there are still some limitations in the study. Firstly, due to the limited availability of SAR datasets, the sample size included in our study was relatively small, which may lead to low statistical validity and increase the risk of false-negative results, particularly for detecting subtle but biologically significant differences in gene expression or immune cell infiltration. Secondly, this research primarily involves secondary data mining and analysis of previously published datasets, where variations in selections of the datasets and analytical methods may lead to differing outcomes. Thirdly, the hub genes and their potential regulatory factors have not yet undergone further clinical validation. Furthermore, the precise mechanism through which these biomarkers influence inflammatory and immune responses, contributing to the development of SAR, remains unclear. This gap hinders a comprehensive understanding of the “mitochondrial-immune axis” in SAR pathogenesis. Therefore, more sample should be included in the future analysis with comprehensive cross-validations. Further in vitro and in vivo experiments, as well as multicenter large-scale clinical trials, are also required to investigate their practical effects and precise mechanisms in the management of SAR. Addressing these limitations through expanded sampling, standardized analyses, and mechanistic experiments will enhance the reliability of our findings and advance the translation of identified hub genes into clinical practice.


## 5. Conclusion
  In conclusion, this study conducted an integrative bioinformatic analysis to systematically explore the biological significance of mitochondrial-related biomarkers in SAR and their relationship with immune cell infiltration. Five hub genes, MTRF1L, MICU1, DHTKD1, ACADM, and FHIT, have been identified as mitochondrial-related biomarkers for SAR. Furthermore, we demonstrated their fine diagnostic potential and their correlation with neutrophil infiltration. Overall, these discoveries offer new perspectives on the underlying mechanisms of SAR, enhancing our comprehension of its pathogenesis and facilitating the identification of potential biomarkers for diagnosis and therapy.
