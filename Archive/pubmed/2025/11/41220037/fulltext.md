# Unravelling butyrate metabolism in sepsis: identification of key genes


## Background
  Sepsis is an infection-induced systemic inflammatory response. Given the potential link between changes in butyrate metabolism (BM) and sepsis development, this study aimed to preliminarily identify potential BM-related genes and explore their possible relevance to sepsis treatment strategies.


## Methods
  The GSE54514, GSE175453 and GSE65682 datasets, as well as BM-related genes (BMRGs), were obtained from public databases. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) obtained through differential expression analysis were intersected with BMRGs to acquire candidate genes. The key genes were obtained using two machine learning algorithms and expression verification. A series of analyses of key genes were subsequently conducted, including Friends analysis, enrichment analysis, impact assessment on sepsis clinicals and survival, nomogram construction, immune infiltration analysis, molecular regulatory network, and molecular docking. In vitro experiments were conducted to establish a sepsis model in which THP-1 cells were stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) to verify the differences in the expression of key genes. Furthermore, the heterogeneity of sepsis cells was analysed at the single-cell level.


## Results
  Three key genes (ID2, ZFP36L1, and ZNF148) were selected from among 11 candidate genes. ID2 had relatively strong functional similarity with the others. These genes were enriched in 9, 3, and 6 pathways, respectively, including neuroactive ligand–receptor interaction and ribosome. Differences in ID2 expression were observed among the age subgroups. A difference in survival was observed between the high- and low-expression groups of the three genes in sepsis patients. The nomograms exhibited good predictive and clinical value. Four differentially expressed immune cell types were detected between the sepsis and control groups. The key genes showed potential regulatory relationships with multiple transcription factors (TFs) and microRNAs (miRNAs). For instance, bioinformatics analysis suggested that ID2 might be influenced by HOXA9, ZFP36L1 could be targeted by hsa-miR-27a-3p, and ZNF148 may be modulated by hsa-miR-365b-3p. Binding energies of -8.5 kcal/mol were observed between ZFP36L1 and amarogentin and andrographolide. In vitro experiments revealed significant variations in the expression levels of key genes—ID2, ZNF148, and ZFP36L1—in sepsis. These genes have potential as biomarkers for the diagnosis of this condition. Finally, neutrophils were identified as key cells, and the expression of key genes showed a dynamic distribution during their development.


## Conclusion
  ID2, ZFP36L1, and ZNF148 may serve as potential candidate regulators of sepsis-associated butyrate metabolism, and the nomogram model showed preliminary predictive value for clinical assessment in sepsis patients. These findings provide exploratory insights that may aid in further investigations of the pathological mechanisms underlying sepsis.

## Background
  Sepsis is characterized by organ dysfunction resulting from an imbalanced host response to infection [1]. When the body’s immune response to infection is not properly regulated, the immune system can become overactivated, releasing large quantities of inflammatory mediators, including cytokines. This leads to a systemic inflammatory response that adversely affects various organ systems [2]. In contemporary intensive care units, sepsis is a major contributor to mortality and significantly increases health care costs, representing a substantial global burden of illness and death [3, 4]. Approaches to treating sepsis include timely fluid resuscitation, anti-infective medications, vasoactive agents, glucocorticoids, and nutritional support [5]. Although these strategies have led to some improvement in survival rates among sepsis patients, therapeutic outcomes are often limited by the variability of the condition [6, 7]. Consequently, rapid identification, risk assessment, and early treatment of sepsis can significantly increase patients’ chances of recovery [8, 9] Numerous biomarkers, including inflammatory mediators, microRNAs (miRNAs), and various clinical indicators, contribute to the clinical diagnosis and management of sepsis [10]. However, owing to the heterogeneity of sepsis, these biomarkers often exhibit insufficient sensitivity and specificity, leading to misdiagnosis and overlooked cases [1]. In light of this information, biomarkers that can accurately diagnose sepsis and effectively guide treatment are urgently needed. Additionally, it is essential to discover new key genes and develop more precise diagnostic tools and effective therapeutic strategies.

  Butyrate metabolism (BM) refers to the process by which intestinal bacteria produce butyrate through fermentation, followed by a series of biochemical reactions that facilitate its absorption, utilization, and transformation within the body [11]. This process is crucial for maintaining the integrity of the intestinal barrier and microbial ecosystem, modulating inflammation, regulating immune responses, sustaining host energy metabolism, and influencing the functions of various organ systems [12]. Additionally, it is closely linked to the development of several diseases. Butyric acid generated in the intestine plays a key role in enhancing intestinal barrier function, promoting the development and repair of intestinal epithelial cells, modulating the immune response within the intestine, and effectively reducing intestinal inflammation [13]. This process is particularly critical during sepsis, when the intestinal barrier becomes compromised, allowing the translocation of bacteria and toxins. Butyric acid can strengthen tight junctions in the intestine, thereby reducing intestinal permeability and decreasing the risk of bacterial and toxin translocation, which can aid in alleviating this condition [14]. Furthermore, a hallmark of sepsis is the dysregulation of the systemic inflammatory response. By modulating the activity of immune cells, such as macrophages and T cells, butyrate reduces the release of proinflammatory cytokines, mitigates excessive inflammatory responses, and enhances immune cell functionality, thereby improving the immune status of patients with sepsis [15]. Butyrate serves as an essential energy source for cells, providing the energy necessary to enhance metabolic processes in individuals with sepsis. Consequently, butyric acid has considerable potential for alleviating the effects of sepsis and facilitating patient recovery [16]. During both the initiation and progression of sepsis, alterations in butyrate metabolism may be intricately linked to the disease’s pathological state. By managing butyrate metabolism, novel treatment strategies for sepsis could be developed. However, the precise roles of genes associated with butyrate metabolism in the context of sepsis remain poorly understood, highlighting the critical need for further investigations to elucidate their mechanisms of action.

  This study investigated the potential role of butyrate metabolism in sepsis through the integration of bioinformatics analysis and experimental validation. We detected a set of butyrate metabolism-related genes (BMRGs) with expression patterns that exhibit characteristic alterations in patients with sepsis. A diagnostic model constructed on the basis of these genes, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.929, demonstrated excellent discriminative ability, with a predictive performance that was comparable to or slightly superior to that of existing methods. In terms of mechanism, this study revealed that butyrate metabolism may be associated with the MAPK signalling pathway, and a correlation with the functional status of gamma delta T cells and CD56bright natural killer cells was observed. Bioinformatics predictions indicate that transcription factors such as HOXA9, POU2F2, and FLI1 may play a role in regulating ID2 gene expression. Additionally, data analysis revealed regulatory relationships between microRNAs, such as miR-27a-3p, and target genes, including ZFP36L1. Molecular docking revealed that the natural compounds amarogentin and andrographolide potentially bind to ZFP36L1. These findings provide new insights into the pathophysiological mechanisms of sepsis, suggesting that modulating butyrate metabolism may represent a novel avenue for exploring sepsis treatment.


## Methods

### Data capture
  GSE54514 (GPL6947), GSE65682 (GPL13667) and GSE175453 (GPL18573/GPL24676) were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) and downloaded on November 7, 2024 and August 11, 2025. Among them, the GSE54514 dataset was used as the training set [17]. Both survivors and nonsurvivors were included, and sample data on the first day (D1) were selected. In contrast, sample data from the second day, third day, fourth day and fifth day were excluded, which included 35 samples from sepsis patients and 18 control samples. The GSE65682 dataset was used as the validation set, which included 760 samples from sepsis samples (479 samples with survival information) and 42 control samples [18]. GSE175453, a single-cell dataset, contained 4 samples of peripheral blood mononuclear cells from sepsis patients and 5 control samples [19]. Moreover, all these samples were whole blood samples. Three hundred forty-eight BMRGs were retrieved from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/human/search.jsp) using “butyric acid” as the keyword [20] (Additional file 1). This is a widely used, reliable database with comprehensive gene sets, diverse sources, and gene sets obtained through a combination of manual curation and automated computational methods.


### Screening of potential candidate genes
  In the training set, the limma (v 3.50.1) package [21] was used to conduct a differential analysis between the sepsis group and the control group to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (|log2 fold change (FC) | >0.5, p < 0.05). The ggplot2 (v 3.3.5) [22] and pheatmap (v 1.0.12) [23] packages were subsequently used to construct a volcano plot and a heatmap, respectively, to visualize the DEGs. In the volcano plot, the top 10 up- and downregulated DEGs ranked according to the log2FC are labelled, and the DEGs shown in the heatmap correspond to them. Moreover, the VennDiagram (v 1.7.1) package [24] was used to determine the intersection of the DEGs and the BMRGs, and genes that were related to both sepsis and BM were obtained as candidate genes.


### Functional enrichment and interaction analysis of candidate genes
  To survey the biological functions and signalling pathways of the candidate genes, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed on the candidate genes (p < 0.05) using the clusterProfiler (v 4.6.0) package [25]. GO consisted of three parts, namely, biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). The enrichment results were ranked in ascending order in line with the p value, and the top 5 GO terms for each aspect were illustrated and presented via the enrichplot (v 1.14.2) package [26]. Concurrently, the significant pathways were identified using KEGG. In addition, the Gene Multiple Association Network Integration Algorithm (GeneMANIA) (http://www.genemania.org/) was employed to prognosticate genes associated with the functions of candidate genes as well as the functions in which they were involved.


### Selection of key genes
  In the training set, a least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis was executed on the candidate genes using the glmnet (v 4.1-2) package [27]. Upon reaching the minimum lambda value, the LASSO genes with regression coefficients that did not decrease to zero were retrieved. Subsequently, a support vector machine-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) analysis was performed on the candidate genes using the caret (v 6.0–91) package [28]. SVM-RFE analysis identified the genes corresponding to the highest accuracy (Kappa coefficient = 1.0) as SVM-RFE genes. Thereafter, the VennDiagram (v 1.7.1) package [24] was used to intersect LASSO genes and SVM-RFE genes, thereby yielding candidate key genes.

  In both the training set and the validation set, the Wilcoxon test was applied to determine the expression levels of key candidate genes in the sepsis and control samples (p < 0.05). The genes that manifested meaningful variances and congruent expression trends in the two datasets were designated the key genes. Box plots were generated to present the results using the ggplot2 (v 3.3.5) package.


### Friends and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) analysis of key genes
  In the training set, the GOSemSim (v 2.24.0) package [29] was used to conduct Friends analysis to evaluate functional similarity among key genes (those with scores greater than 0.5). In addition, to explore the functions in which key genes are involved, Spearman correlation analysis was conducted between each key gene and the entirety of the remaining genes using the psych (v 2.2.9) package [30], thereby determining the association between key genes and other genes. Here, “c2.cp.kegg.v7.4.symbols.gmt” was retrieved from MSigDB to serve as a reference gene set, and a GSEA was executed by the clusterProfiler (v 4.6.0) package (|Normalized Enrichment Score (NES) | >1, p < 0.05). The top five pathways were subsequently selected and presented in accordance with enrichment scores by means of the enrichplot (v 1.14.2) package [23].


### Clinical characteristics and survival analysis of patients with sepsis
  In the training set, the Wilcoxon test was employed to assess the expression disparities of key genes among diverse clinical subgroups, which were specifically categorized by age (≤ 60 and >60) and sex (female and male). In the validation set, the expression level of each key gene was divided into a high-expression group and a low-expression group derived from the optimal cut-off value using the survminer (v 0.4.9) package [31]. The log-rank test was subsequently applied to compare the overall survival (OS) period between patients in the high-expression group and those in the low-expression group of sepsis patients for whom survival information was available (p < 0.05). Thereafter, a Kaplan‒Meier (K‒M) curve was plotted to present the results using the survival (v 3.3-1) package [32].


### Construction and validation of the nomogram
  In the training set, a nomogram model of key genes was established using the rms (v 6.2-0) package [33]. Each key gene was associated with a line segment, and the length of the line segment indicated the extent to which the key gene contributed to the development of sepsis. The probability of a sample being afflicted with sepsis was appraised by summing the individual scores corresponding to diverse values of each key gene to obtain a total score. Thereafter, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve of the nomogram was plotted using the pROC (v 1.18.0) package [34] to estimate the diagnostic efficacy of the nomogram (area under the curve (AUC) >0.7). Additionally, decision curve analysis (DCA) curves of individual key genes and the nomogram were generated using the rms (v 6.2-0) package to assess the practical clinical applicability of the nomogram. To evaluate the stability and generalizability of the nomogram, ROC analysis of the nomogram was performed in the validation set. Furthermore, by means of 1000 Bootstrap resamplings, the difference in the AUC between the training set and the validation set was assessed.


### Immune infiltration analysis
  In the training set, a single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA) algorithm was used to assess the infiltration abundances of 28 types of immune cells in both sepsis samples and control samples. The Wilcoxon test was subsequently applied to compare disparities in immune cell infiltration abundances between sepsis samples and control samples. The number of differentially infiltrated immune cells (p < 0.05) was thereby determined. Afterwards, the psych (v 2.2.9) package [34] was used to perform Spearman correlation analyses, not only between the differentially infiltrated immune cells but also between the differentially infiltrated immune cells and key genes (|connection (cor)| >0.3, p < 0.05).


### Molecular regulatory network of key genes
  To elucidate the regulatory mechanisms of key genes in the development of sepsis, the transcription factors (TFs) of key genes were identified using the Transcriptional Regulatory Relationships Unraveled by Sentence-based Text mining (TRRUST) database (http://www.grnpedia.org/trrust). The miRNAs of key genes were subsequently predicted using the miRWalk database (http://mirwalk.umm.uni-heidelberg.de/). Finally, TF‒mRNA (key gene) and miRNA‒mRNA regulatory networks were constructed using Cytoscape (v 3.5.0) software [35].


### Drug prediction and molecular docking of key genes
  To investigate the feasibility of treating sepsis with drugs predicted in accordance with key genes, the Drug–Gene Interaction Database (DGIdb) (https://www.dgidb.org/) was used to predict potential drugs corresponding to key genes. Molecular docking analyses were subsequently performed between key genes and the associated effective active ingredients of drugs. In the first step, protein crystal structures corresponding to each individual key gene were obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) database (https://www.rcsb.org/). Three-dimensional structures of the active compounds of drugs were subsequently downloaded from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). CB-Dock (http://cao.labshare.cn/cb-dock/) was subsequently used to predict binding activities between proteins and compounds. A binding energy lower than − 5 Kcal/mol indicated relatively favourable binding. Finally, PyMOL (v 2.5.8) software [36] was used to visualize the results.


### Chemicals and reagents
  RPMI-1640 medium and foetal bovine serum (FBS) were purchased from Gibco (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Lipopolysaccharide (LPS; L4391) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). The primary antibodies used included anti-ZFP36L1 (68038-1-Ig; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), anti-β-actin (20536-1-AP; Proteintech), anti-ZNF148 (AF0792; Affinity, Jiangsu, China), and anti-ID2 (DF6178; Wanleibio, Liaoning, China). The secondary antibodies used were CoraLite488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (RGAM002; Proteintech), CoraLite594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (RGAR004; Proteintech), and HRP-conjugated rabbit (7074) or mouse (7076) IgG (Cell Signaling Technology; Danvers, MA, USA). The Hifair II First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (gDNA digester plus, 11121ES60) and Hieff qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (11203ES03) were obtained from YEASEN (Shanghai, China).


### Cell culture and stimulation
  Human myeloid leukaemia mononuclear cells (THP-1) were obtained from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (Beijing, China) and maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated foetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere. Cells were passaged every 2–3 days at 70–80% confluence and maintained at densities between 1 × 10⁵ and 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL to ensure logarithmic growth.

  For the in vitro sepsis model, THP-1 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded at 2 × 10⁵ cells/well in 6-well plates and stimulated with LPS at a final concentration of 1 µg/mL for 6 h [37]. Untreated cells (cultured in parallel without LPS) served as negative controls. All experiments included three biological replicates with technical triplicates per condition to ensure reproducibility. The cell viability was confirmed to exceed 95% using a Trypan blue exclusion assay prior to treatment.


### Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT‒PCR) analysis
  Total RNA was isolated from all six biological replicates per group using TRIzol reagent following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were verified using a Nanodrop device (A260/A280 ratio > 1.8). First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using 1 µg of total RNA with the Hifair II First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit, including gDNA digester treatment. qPCR was conducted in technical triplicates using Hieff qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix on a Roche LightCycler 480 system. The sequences of primers used were as follows: ID2-DP (5’-CGCATCCCACTATTGTCAGC-3’) and ID2-RP (5’-GAAGGGAATTCAGAAGCCTGC-3’); ZNF148-DP (5’-TGATGATGCCATGCAGTTTT-3’) and ZNF148-RP (5’-TCCCTGCTGTTGTTACTTGCT-3’); ZFP36L1-DP (5’-GAACGCACAGGATGACCACCA-3’) and ZFP36L1-RP (5’-CCTACCCTGGCTTAGTCATCTGA-3’); and β-actin-DP (5’-CATGTACGTTGCTATCCAGGC-3’) and β-actin-RP (5’-CTCCTTAATGTCACGCACGAT-3’). The relative expression of each target gene was calculated using the 2 − ΔΔCT method, with β-actin serving as the internal reference gene (Sangon, Shanghai, China).


### Western blotting
  Approximately 1 × 10^6 cells from each group (n = 3) were collected, centrifuged to remove the supernatant, and lysed, followed by sonication. Protein samples were mixed with sample buffer, and appropriate volumes were subjected to total protein isolation using sodium dodecyl sulfate‒polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‒PAGE, Epizyme, PG113) with 12.5% sodium dodecyl sulfate. The proteins were then transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF, Millipore, 239690) membranes. Blocking was performed with milk for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation with primary antibodies (ID2, ZNF148, ZFP36L1, and β-actin). The membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, after which the bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) luminescence detection kit (Biosharp, BL520B). The grey values were analysed using ImageJ software with β-actin normalization.


### Immunofluorescence
  THP-1 cells (1 × 10^6 cells/well; n = 3) in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into six-well plates and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. After being washed with PBS, the cells were permeabilized with 0.4% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min and blocked with 1% BSA in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies against ID2, ZNF148, and ZFP36L1 were diluted in blocking buffer and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After being washed with PBS, the cells were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with species-matched secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 or Alexa Fluor 594. DAPI staining was applied for 10 min before the cells were transferred to slides. After being sealed with an anti-fluorescence quenching sealer, the cells were imaged using a laser confocal microscope with identical threshold settings applied across all the experimental groups.


### Single-cell analysis
  The GSE175453 dataset was analysed using the Seurat package (v5.0.1) [38]. First, the raw data were subjected to quality control with the following criteria: nFeature_RNA, 200–4000; nCount_RNA, 200–15,000; percentage of mitochondrial genes (pctMT) < 10%; and percentage of haemoglobin genes (pctHB) < 5%. Genes and cells that passed quality control were normalized using the NormalizeData function. The FindVariableFeatures function was subsequently used to identify 2000 highly variable genes. The ScaleData function was used for data scaling, and the RunPCA function was used for dimensionality reduction. To cluster cells into different clusters, the FindNeighbors function was applied to select principal components (PCs) that significantly contributed to variation (p < 0.05); the FindClusters function was used for clustering analysis (resolution = 0.4). Finally, cell clusters were annotated into different cell types using canonical marker genes from the SingleR database.

  On the basis of different cell types, the expression of key genes in various cell types was analysed and visualized using UMAP plots. The cell type with the highest coexpression of key genes was defined as the key cell type. Cell communication between key cells and other cells was analysed using the CellChat package (v 1.6.1) [39], whereas the differentiation trajectory of key cells and the dynamic expression of key genes in these cells were analysed using the Monocle package (v 2.28.0) [40].


### Statistical analysis
  In this study, bioinformatics analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.2). Statistical analyses were conducted with GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.0) and SPSS (version 17.0). Statistical differences between the two groups were evaluated using the t test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, whereas one-way ANOVA was used to compare multiple groups. All the data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05.


## Results

### Enrichment analysis and interaction among 11 candidate genes
  Transcriptomic analysis revealed 104 DEGs in sepsis patients compared with controls (|log2 FC | >0.5, p < 0.05), 44 of which were upregulated and 60 of which were downregulated in sepsis patients (Fig. 1a-b, Additional file 2). The intersection of these 104 DEGs and 348 BMRGs was subsequently determined, and 11 candidate genes (MYH9, BTF3, GADD45A, NAMPT, STAT2, ID2, ZFP36L1, ZFP36, ZNF148, HMGB2 and S100P) were identified (Fig. 1c). GO enrichment analysis of candidate genes was performed, and 221 results were obtained. Among them were 211 BPs, such as regulation of B-cell differentiation, positive regulation of fat cell differentiation, and positive regulation of myeloid cell differentiation. Four CCs, euchromatin, P-body, cytoplasmic ribonucleoprotein granule, and ribonucleoprotein granule, were detected. Six MFs, namely, transcription regulator inhibitor activity, mR3’-UTR AU-rich region binding, 14-3-3 protein binding, molecular function inhibitor activity, and transmembrane and transporter binding, were also obtained (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1d, Additional file 3). KEGG enrichment analysis of the candidate genes revealed 5 genes related to the Hippo signalling pathway, cellular senescence, and transcriptional misregulation in cancer (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1e, Additional file 3). A total of 20 function-related genes were predicted on the basis of 11 candidate genes. For example, interactions were observed between ID2 and MOK, as well as between ID2 and OLIG1. Interactions were also detected between STAT2 and TRPM7 and between STAT2 and IFITM3. The functions in which candidate genes and genes interact include nephric duct development, the receptor signalling pathway via STAT, and the regulation of cell cycle G1/S phase translation (Fig. 1f).

  The above results revealed that candidate genes potentially play roles in sepsis through the regulation of these biological pathways. Clues were provided for further understanding the mechanisms of candidate genes in the occurrence and development of sepsis. For example, these genes are potentially involved in the regulation of cell proliferation, differentiation and cancer-related processes.


### ID2, ZFP36L1, and ZNF148 are regarded as key genes
  When lambda.min was 0.009036365, seven LASSO genes with regression coefficients that were not penalized to 0 were obtained: MYH9, GADD45A, STAT2, ID2, ZFP36L1, ZNF148 and HMGB2 (Fig. 2a-b). When the accuracy rate was 0.95, eight SVM-RFE genes, ID2, ZFP36L1, ZFP36, ZNF148, MYH9, NAMPT, STAT2 and HMGB2, were obtained (Fig. 2c). Then, genes obtained with LASSO and SVM-RFE were intersected, and six key candidate genes, namely, MYH9, STAT2, ID2, ZFP36L1, ZNF148 and HMGB2, were obtained (Fig. 2d). In the training set and validation set, three key genes (ID2, ZFP36L1, and ZNF148) with consistent expression trends were obtained. Notably, ID2 and ZFP36L1 expression levels were downregulated in patients with sepsis, whereas ZNF148 expression levels were upregulated in patients with sepsis (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2e-f).

### Potential pathway associations and exploratory analysis of ID2, ZFP36L1 and ZNF148
  Friends analysis results demonstrated that ID2 and ZFP36L1 had relatively high functional similarity (scores > 0.5). Among them, ID2 demonstrated the highest association score among the analysed genes, suggesting its potential as a hub gene with multiple pathway interactions (Fig. 3a).

  Fig. 3Analysis of key genes. (a) Friends raincloud plot of key genes. (b) GSEA enrichment analysis of ID2. (c) GSEA enrichment analysis of ZFP36L1. (d) GSEA enrichment analysis of ZNF148

  The GSEA results of key genes revealed that ID2, ZFP36L1 and ZNF148 were enriched in 9, 3 and 6 pathways, respectively (Additional file 4). ID2 was enriched mainly in pathways such as the cell cycle, MAPK signalling pathway, neuroactive ligand–receptor interaction, and VEGF signalling pathway (Fig. 3b). ZFP36L1 was enriched mainly in basal transcription factors, Leishmania infection, and ribosomes (Fig. 3c), and ZNF148 was enriched mainly in pathways such as lysosome, neuroactive ligand–receptor interaction, basal transcription factors, pathogenic Escherichia coli infection and Parkinson’s disease (Fig. 3d). Notably, neuroactive ligand–receptor interactions were commonly enriched in ID2 and ZNF148, and basal transcription factors were commonly enriched in ZFP36L1 and ZNF148. These findings suggest that key genes might have synergistic effects or shared regulatory mechanisms in these commonly enriched aspects, providing clues and points of focus for subsequent in-depth research on sepsis occurrence and development.


### Impact of ID2, ZFP36L1 and ZNF148 on clinical features and survival in patients with sepsis
  ID2 expression levels differed markedly among the age subgroups and were greater in the group aged younger than or equal to 60 years than in the group aged older than 60 years (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4a). Among the sex subgroups, there were no notable differences in the expression of key genes (Fig. 4b). K‒M curves revealed notable differences in OS between the high-expression group and low-expression group for key genes in patients with sepsis (p < 0.05), and the survival rate of the low-expression group was lower than that of the high-expression group. Preliminary analysis revealed that ID2, ZFP36L1, and ZNF148 expression levels may be correlated with the survival status of patients with sepsis (Fig. 4c-e).

### A nomogram with relatively good diagnostic value
  The nomogram constructed with ID2, ZFP36L1 and ZNF148 revealed that the higher the total score was, the greater the probability of sepsis occurrence (Fig. 5a). The ROC curve indicated that the nomogram had relatively good diagnostic value (AUC = 0.929) (Fig. 5b). The DCA curve revealed that the net benefit of the nomogram model was greater than that of the diagonal line (All), the horizontal line (None) and individual key genes (Fig. 5c), demonstrating that the nomogram model had relatively good predictive performance and practical application value in clinical decision-making for patients with sepsis. In the validation set, the nomogram also exhibited good diagnostic ability (AUC = 0.91) (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Furthermore, the results of 1000 Bootstrap resampling analyses performed in the training set and the validation set showed that the difference in the AUC value between the two datasets was only 0.015, with P > 0.05 (Supplementary Fig. 2B). These findings indicated that the nomogram constructed had good stability and generalizability.

### Four types of differentially abundant immune cells
  The infiltration abundances of 28 types of immune cells were analysed, and the infiltration levels of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) were relatively high in both the disease group and the control group (Fig. 6a). Four different immune cell types, namely, activated B cells, CD56bright natural killer cells, effector memory CD4 T cells and gamma delta T cells, were detected between the sepsis disease group and the control group (p < 0.05). The infiltration level of activated B cells was greater in the sepsis group than in the control group, whereas the infiltration levels of CD56bright natural killer cells, effector memory CD4 T cells and gamma delta T cells were lower in the sepsis group than in the control group (Fig. 6b). In addition, gamma delta T cells were relatively strongly linked with the other three immune cells. A significantly strong connection was present between ID2 and gamma delta T cells (cor = 0.31, p = 0.001) (Fig. 6c, Additional file 5). These preliminary findings suggest that the detected candidate genes and differentially abundant immune cells may collectively be correlated with sepsis pathogenesis. This exploratory analysis provides initial insights that could inform future investigations into immune mechanisms and potential therapeutic strategies for sepsis.

### Molecular regulatory networks, drug prediction and molecular docking of ID2, ZFP36L1 and ZNF148
  A TF‒mRNA network revealed that a total of 11 TFs targeting ID2 were predicted, whereas no TFs were predicted for ZNF148 or ZFP36L1. For example, ID2 is potentially regulated by HOXA9, POU2F2, and FLI1 (Fig. 7a). The miRNA‒mRNA network further indicated that 3 miRNAs were predicted for ZNF148, 1 miRNA was predicted for ZFP36L1, and none were predicted for ID2. Notably, ZFP36L1 was regulated by hsa-miR-27a-3p, and ZNF148 expression was altered by the expression of hsa-miR-365a-3p, hsa-miR-365b-3p, and hsa-miR-425-5p (Fig. 7b). Bioinformatic predictions suggested that candidate key genes might undergo expression modifications mediated by regulatory factors, which could affect disease progression, although their specific regulatory mechanisms still need to be further explored through experiments.

  In addition, two drugs related to ZFP36L1, amarogentin and andrographolide, were predicted. The binding energy between ZFP36L1 and amarogentin was measured to be -8.5 kcal/mol. ### Differential expression of key genes in sepsis
  Given the critical role of macrophages in the inflammatory response associated with sepsis, as well as their involvement in immune activity, host defence, and tissue repair, we chose the THP-1 cell line for an in vitro study to confirm the functions of genes significantly related to sepsis [41]. Using bioinformatics results, we examined ID2, ZNF148, and ZFP36L1 expression levels in both the control group and the LPS-stimulated THP-1 cells. qRT‒PCR analysis (n = 6) revealed significant dysregulation of key transcriptional regulators following LPS challenge. ZNF148 mRNA expression was upregulated 1.57-fold (p < 0.01), whereas ZFP36L1 and ID2 were markedly downregulated 0.69-fold (p < 0.01) and 0.46-fold (p < 0.01) compared with the control levels, respectively (Fig. 8a).

  Fig. 8Validation and experimental results of key genes (a) qRT-PCR was conducted to examine the expression of ID2, ZFP36L1, ZNF148. (b) Immunofluorescence staining for key genes. (c) Protein expressions of ID2, ZFP36L1, ZNF148 in THP-1 cells.(Full-length blots are presented in Supplementary Fig. 6)

  These transcriptional changes were corroborated at the protein level by Western blot analysis (n = 3). After β-actin normalization, the ZNF148 protein abundance increased 4.2-fold (p < 0.01), in contrast with the reduced expression of ZFP36L1 (0.73-fold, p < 0.01) and ID2 (0.40-fold, p < 0.05) (Fig. 8b).

  Immunofluorescence quantification (n = 3) confirmed these expression patterns through mean intensity measurements: the ZNF148 signal increased 1.77-fold (p < 0.05), whereas the ZFP36L1 and ID2 intensities decreased to 57% (p < 0.05) and 31.6% (p < 0.01) that of the controls, respectively (Fig. 8c).

  The concordance across transcriptional and protein-level analyses validated the initial bioinformatic predictions. These findings suggest that ID2, ZNF148, and ZFP36L1 are mechanistically relevant candidates for sepsis biomarker development.

### Neutrophils exhibit heterogeneity in sepsis
  In terms of cell communication between key cells and other cells, compared with those in the control group, neutrophils in the sepsis group communicated more closely with other cells (such as monocytes and NK cells). This more active and frequent interaction indicated that neutrophils might undertake stronger “communication” tasks in processes such as immune response and participate in complex intercellular signal transmission. In contrast, the cell communication of neutrophils in the control group was relatively less active, remaining at a relatively “quiet” or basic communication level (Fig. 10a-b). Moreover, secondary clustering divided the neutrophils into 4 cell clusters (Supplementary Fig. 4) and 4 cell subtypes, namely, N2-neutrophil, classical-neutrophil, resting-neutrophil, and immature-neutrophil (Fig. 10c). Overall, the neutrophils were divided into 3 developmental states. At the early stage of development, they were mainly of the classical-neutrophil subtype. After the middle stage of development, they differentiated into 2 developmental directions. Among them, ID2 was highly expressed in the early stage of development, ZFP36L1 was highly expressed in the middle stage of development, and ZNF148 was highly expressed in the late stage of development (Fig. 10h).

## Discussion
  Sepsis is a prevalent and severe complication that can be disabling and fatal in critically ill patients and is characterized by a complex pathogenesis [1]. Timely identification, effective risk stratification, and the initiation of appropriate treatment for individuals with sepsis can significantly improve patient outcomes [8, 9]. The intricate pathophysiology of sepsis and the limited availability of biomarkers in clinical practice emphasize the importance of exploring new therapeutic targets. Butyrate metabolism, which plays a vital role in maintaining intestinal barrier function and immune regulation, has been associated with sepsis [12, 14–16]. Nevertheless, the mechanisms governing butyrate-related gene networks under these conditions remain unclear. A deeper understanding of these molecular interactions may provide insights into sepsis pathology.

  In this research, our machine learning model and expression validation revealed three key genes associated with butyrate metabolism in sepsis: ID2, ZFP36L1, and ZNF148. We constructed a nomogram model incorporating the expression levels of key genes for sepsis risk prediction. Using integrated bioinformatics analyses, we investigated potential molecular mechanisms, pathway associations, and pharmacological targets related to these genes. Preliminary in vitro experiments using THP-1 cells revealed differential expression patterns of these candidate markers under sepsis-like conditions.

  Our findings position ZNF148 as a pivotal molecular nexus connecting butyrate metabolism with sepsis pathophysiology. As a Krüppel-type zinc finger transcription factor, ZNF148 serves as the primary effector of butyrate signalling through multiple well-characterized mechanisms: it binds GC-rich promoter elements (e.g., p21WAF1) to recruit the histone acetyltransferase p300, orchestrating chromatin remodelling via histone H3/H4 acetylation [42–47], while simultaneously stabilizing p53 to reinforce cell cycle control [48, 49]. These molecular interactions collectively enable ZNF148 to transduce the HDAC inhibitory effects of butyrate into coordinated transcriptional responses that govern cell proliferation and differentiation. Importantly, our prognostic analysis revealed significant correlations between ZNF148 expression patterns and sepsis outcomes, suggesting that its metabolic regulatory functions may substantially influence disease progression. The butyrate–ZNF148 axis further extends to mucosal immunity, where it upregulates antimicrobial peptides (e.g., defensins) and suppresses enteric pathogen virulence [44, 49], with deficiency states predisposing against gastrointestinal barrier dysfunction and susceptibility to sepsis. Emerging evidence indicates that during sepsis, the interaction between ZNF148 and HDAC3 [50, 51] may disrupt inflammatory gene expression programs, as impaired HDAC activity leads to pathological histone hyperacetylation and sustained proinflammatory signalling [52, 53]. This context-dependent duality—where ZNF148 mediates beneficial metabolic regulation under physiological conditions but may correlate with immune dysregulation in sepsis—informs the need for further investigation into its precise role in critical illness. Future studies should focus particularly on elucidating how ZNF148 coordinates butyrate signalling with immunometabolic pathways, including its potential effects on regulatory T-cell differentiation [49] and HDAC3-mediated inflammatory responses, which may reveal novel therapeutic targets for modulating metabolic‒epigenetic crosstalk in sepsis management.

  Inhibitor of DNA binding 2 (ID2), a key regulator of immune cell differentiation and function [54–60], has emerged as a critical molecular interface between butyrate metabolism and immune regulation. Our in vitro experiments demonstrated significantly decreased ID2 mRNA expression in LPS-stimulated THP-1 cells, suggesting that inflammatory signals may disrupt the butyrate-ID2 regulatory axis. These findings align with growing evidence that butyrate, a microbe-derived HDAC inhibitor, directly modulates ID2 expression in a cell type-specific manner, enhancing its expression in CD8 + T cells to promote antitumour immunity through IL-12/IFN-γ signalling [61, 62], whereas in hepatocytes, ID2 upregulation confers resistance to butyrate-induced apoptosis via BCL2 activation [63]. The essential role of ID2 in maintaining effector T-cell populations [57, 64–66] and Treg stability [67, 68] may be fundamentally linked to its responsiveness to microbial metabolites such as butyrate, particularly at mucosal interfaces such as the intestinal barrier [69, 70]. In summary, ID2 serves as a molecular integrator of butyrate signalling across multiple physiological systems, coordinating metabolic inputs with immune cell function. Its expression levels not only reflect immune status but also represent a measurable outcome of host–microbiome metabolic interactions, suggesting its potential as a biomarker for metabolic–immune dysregulation.

  ZFP36L1, a critical member of the TTP family, plays a pivotal role in sepsis pathogenesis by regulating the mRNA stability of key inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α and IL-6 [71–73]. Genetic deficiency of ZFP36L1 results in hyperactivation of T/B lymphocytes [74, 75] and multiorgan inflammatory responses [76], with particularly significant regulatory effects on monocyte/macrophage differentiation [77]. Our in vitro sepsis model demonstrated downregulation of ZFP36L1 expression, suggesting its potential contribution to disease progression through amplification of the cytokine storm. However, direct evidence linking ZFP36L1 to butyrate metabolism remains limited, and the literature supports a plausible mechanistic connection. Butyrate, a gut microbiota-derived metabolite and known HDAC inhibitor, may epigenetically regulate ZFP36L1 expression, analogous to its effects on other TTP family members [78, 79]. This regulation could establish a “butyrate-ZFP36L1-anti-inflammatory” feedback loop, which is potentially crucial for maintaining intestinal immune homeostasis. On the basis of the current evidence, we propose that ZFP36L1 represents a critical molecular nexus connecting sepsis pathophysiology with butyrate metabolism. Dysregulation of ZFP36L1 expression may be correlated with sepsis onset in humans, but targeted modulation of its activity could inform novel therapeutic strategies for cytokine storm management in sepsis.

  We constructed a nomogram based on three key genes to predict the risk of sepsis, achieving an AUC value of 0.929, indicating high predictive accuracy. Compared with the AUROCs of ferroptosis-related hub genes in sepsis diagnosis reported by Li et al. [80], which were 0.770, 0.941, and 0.774, our model demonstrated superior discriminatory power. Furthermore, our model outperformed the AUC value of 0.881 from the C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio (CAR)-based nomogram developed by Zhou et al. [81]. This enhanced performance may be attributed to the inclusion of additional predictors and more complex interaction effects within our model. Our preliminary results suggest that the nomogram demonstrates promising accuracy for sepsis prediction, although further validation is needed to assess its clinical utility.

  To identify significant immune-related pathways associated with key genes in sepsis, we conducted GSEA. Our findings highlight that the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway is closely linked to the functional expression of these genes. The MAPK pathway transmits extracellular signals to the nucleus through a series of cascade phosphorylation reactions, thereby regulating various biological processes, including gene expression, cell proliferation, differentiation, survival, and apoptosis [82]. This pathway is crucial for mediating the inflammatory response, immune regulation, and metabolic processes during sepsis [83]. In the early stages of sepsis, both the MAPK and STAT3 pathways are activated, either directly or indirectly, to regulate the expression of early inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α [84]. Activation of the MAPK pathway is associated with a survival advantage in sepsis patients, suggesting a potential protective role in the immune response [85]. With respect to butyrate metabolism, butyrate enhances β-cell proliferation and function while reducing apoptosis by modulating the p38/ERK MAPK and apoptosis pathways [86]. Additionally, high-dose butyrate influences the regulation of AKT and p38 MAPK, thereby affecting the expression of inflammatory cytokines [87]. Proinflammatory cytokines can alter colonocyte butyrate metabolism via the p38 signalling pathway [88]. The GSEA results suggested that the screened candidate genes may participate in multiple sepsis-related biological pathways, potentially highlighting mechanistic targets worthy of further investigation for therapeutic development.

  In this study, we employed ssGSEA to quantify the abundance of 28 immune cell populations across two sepsis microarray datasets. Compared with those in controls, significant alterations in activated B cells, CD56bright natural killer cells, effector memory CD4 + T cells, and gamma delta T cells were observed, suggesting profound immune dysregulation in patients with sepsis. Specifically, B-cell dysfunction is correlated with poor clinical outcomes, whereas CD56bright natural killer cells likely contribute to innate immunity via IFN-γ secretion [89]. The transient decline in effector memory CD4 + T cells may reflect immunosuppressive mechanisms [90], whereas gamma delta T cells appear to be critical for balancing inflammatory and cytotoxic responses [91]. Although ssGSEA offers a standardized framework for immune characterization, its dependence on canonical gene sets may fail to capture sepsis-specific immunological dynamics, particularly transitional states such as immune activation or exhaustion. These limitations could be addressed using multimodal approaches combining single-cell RNA-seq with proteomic validation (e.g., CITE-seq).

  Furthermore, we observed a significant relationship between butyrate metabolism and immune cell infiltration, indicating that this metabolic pathway may influence sepsis progression through immune activation. Zimmerman et al. demonstrated that butyrate can suppress T-cell growth and induce apoptosis via the Fas-mediated pathway [92]. Additionally, we observed a strong correlation between the butyrate metabolism gene ID2 and gamma delta T cells, suggesting potential regulatory mechanisms. These insights underscore the importance of investigating immune regulation by key genes and imply potential mechanistic links between butyrate metabolism and immune regulation in sepsis pathogenesis. Single-cell RNA sequencing revealed elevated expression of key genes in neutrophils, linking butyrate metabolism to the cellular basis of immune regulation in sepsis. As the primary effector cells involved in early sepsis responses, neutrophils exhibit functional dysregulation closely tied to systemic inflammatory dyshomeostasis [93]. The observed high expression of these genes in neutrophils suggests a potential role for butyrate metabolism in modulating the activity of neutrophils, including chemotaxis, phagocytosis, and cytokine release [94]. Notably, the stage-specific enrichment of ID2, ZFP36L1, and ZNF148 during neutrophil development implies their participation in a temporally regulated network. These findings offer a dynamic perspective on how these genes may influence sepsis progression through regulation of the lifespan of neutrophils.

  To elucidate the transcriptional regulation of key genes involved in butyrate metabolism during sepsis, we performed bioinformatics analyses and identified 11 TFs associated with ID2. Computational predictions suggest that HOXA9, POU2F2, and FLI1 may play pivotal roles in modulating ID2 expression during sepsis pathogenesis. Notably, HOXA9 has been implicated in regulating inflammatory mediator release, which influences disease progression [95]. POU2F2, a member of the POU TF family, is critical for immune response regulation [96], whereas FLI1, an ETS family TF, is involved in cell proliferation, differentiation, and immune modulation, with reported roles in autoimmune processes [97]. These findings suggest that sepsis-associated changes in ID2 expression may modulate inflammatory responses and cell survival through downstream gene regulation, thereby impacting disease outcomes. However, these computational predictions require experimental validation. Future studies should employ chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays and functional experiments—such as targeted knockdown or overexpression—in appropriate sepsis models to confirm these regulatory mechanisms.

  The miRNAs are considered promising biomarkers, and numerous miRNAs have been shown to play roles in sepsis and sepsis-related organ damage [98]. Our study demonstrated that hsa-miR-27a-3p regulates ZFP36L1 expression, whereas ZNF148 is influenced by hsa-miR-365a-3p, hsa-miR-365b-3p, and hsa-miR-425-5p. These findings suggested that the predicted miRNAs may participate in regulating the expression of candidate sepsis-associated genes, with potential downstream effects on disease progression. Previous studies have shown that miR-27a is upregulated in sepsis and contributes to the inflammatory response. Reducing miR-27a expression can significantly regulate TNF-α and IL-6 levels, thereby decreasing lung inflammation and increasing the survival of septic mice [99]. These findings suggested that ZFP36L1, potentially modulated by hsa-miR-27a-3p, may participate in the posttranscriptional regulation of inflammation-associated miRNAs in immune cells. Such regulation could correlate with altered inflammatory mediator production and potentially influence sepsis pathophysiology.

  Computational screening revealed two phytochemicals—amarogentin and andrographolide—as potential ligands with favourable binding characteristics with ZFP36L1 in silico. Amarogentin, a secoiridoid glycoside extracted from the roots of Swertia and Gentiana [100], has antioxidant, anticancer, hepatoprotective, immunomodulatory, and antidiabetic effects [101–103]. Amarogentin inhibits TNF-α secretion in a dose-dependent manner and offers protective benefits against sepsis-induced brain injury by modulating AMPK/SIRT1/NF-κB signalling [104]. Andrographolide has various beneficial effects in combating sepsis, such as increasing the levels of antimicrobial peptides, facilitating autophagy, and preventing NLRP3 inflammasome activation, thereby providing anti-inflammatory and multiorgan protective effects [105]. In particular, andrographolide reduces the levels of proinflammatory substances such as TNF-α and IL-6 in the peritoneal fluid and blood of mice with sepsis triggered by caecal ligation and puncture (CLP) [106] while also improving bacterial clearance and increasing survival rates [105]. The significant role of ZFP36L1 in butyrate metabolism during sepsis indicates its potential as a therapeutic target. Consequently, these medications may affect the progression of sepsis through their interaction with ZFP36L1.

  Nevertheless, this study is subject to several limitations. First, heterogeneity in the public dataset—including variations in sample sources, inclusion criteria, disease severity grading (e.g., SOFA score), and sampling time points—may compromise the interpretability of gene expression profiles. Moreover, the in vitro validation was confined to LPS-induced M1 macrophage polarization and did not encompass other immune cell types, such as T cells and neutrophils, or polarization states like M2 macrophages. Additionally, the nomogram model has yet to be validated in independent clinical cohorts.

  To address these issues, we plan to establish a high-quality patient cohort with uniform diagnostic criteria, detailed clinical scores, and standardized sampling time points. This will allow validation of the robustness of genetic signatures and their associations with disease severity and prognosis. In parallel, we intend to systematically examine the functional roles of these genes across sepsis stages and within key immune cell subtypes—including T cells, neutrophils, and M2 macrophages—using in vitro models or cell-specific gene knockout animals. Furthermore, the predictive performance of the nomogram will be rigorously evaluated in prospective clinical cohorts.

  Despite these challenges, our findings offer valuable insights into the molecular underpinnings of sepsis and highlight new potential therapeutic targets. Our findings suggest a potential link between butyrate metabolism-related genes and sepsis pathogenesis, although additional studies are needed to establish definitive mechanistic connections. Furthermore, the nomogram we designed may facilitate the risk stratification of sepsis patients in clinical settings and provide innovative concepts for future precision treatment methodologies.


## Conclusion
  Integrative analyses suggest that the candidate genes ID2, ZFP36L1, and ZNF148, which are computationally associated with butyrate metabolic pathways, may have potential relevance for early sepsis identification and prognostic assessment. These findings contribute to the growing understanding of metabolic–immune interactions in sepsis, and the developed gene signatures (AUC = 0.929) show preliminary promise for clinical risk stratification pending further validation.
