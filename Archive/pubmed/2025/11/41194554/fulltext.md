# Molecular basis of antibiotic sensing by the TetR family regulator CecR – a structural perspective


## Abstract
  Escherichia coli HTH‐type transcriptional dual regulator CecR belongs to TetR family regulators (TFRs), which regulate the expression of genes enabling bacteria to survive under stress conditions. Previous studies (Yamanaka et al., Microbiology 2016; 162: 1253–1264) showed that CecR senses the presence of antibiotics, cephalosporins and chloramphenicol, in the cell and activates the expression of a putative drug efflux pump. Although CecR is present in many pathogenic strains of Escherichia and Salmonella genera, this regulator is poorly characterized. Here, we report the first crystal structure of E. coli CecR. Each protomer of the CecR homodimer is composed of an N‐terminal DNA‐binding and a C‐terminal ligand‐binding domain. In addition to nine canonical TetR α‐helices, CecR contains structural elements characteristic of TetR subfamily D. The ligand‐binding cavity of CecR has a tunnel‐like shape, not common in TFRs. Unexpectedly, the CecR–ligand‐binding cavity contained polyethylene glycol (PEG) fragments, originating from crystallization solution, and suggesting a potential site for effector binding. Additionally, the affinity of CecR to various antibiotics was determined. The strongest interactions were observed for CecR and cefepime, a representative of the fourth‐generation cephalosporins. Molecular docking of the analyzed antibiotics into the ligand‐binding tunnel of CecR indicated the amino acid residues important for ligand recognition. The CecR structure reported here provides the first structural information on the ligand‐binding cavity and ligand recognition by CecR. As CecR is an important regulator, widespread among pathogenic bacteria belonging to the Enterobacteriales order, the results of our study are an important contribution to the understanding of the CecR‐related mechanisms underlying antimicrobial resistance.


## Introduction
  The TetR family regulators (TFRs) belong to a widespread family of one‐component transcription factors in bacteria [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. More than half a million protein sequences have been assigned to this family, so far. TFRs are involved in controlling various processes like multidrug resistance [7, 8, 9], metabolism [10, 11, 12], antibiotic biosynthesis [13, 14], biofilm formation [15, 16], response to stress conditions [17, 18], or cell‐to‐cell signaling [19, 20]. The most studied group of TFRs involves proteins responsible for the regulation of genes that help bacteria survive in the presence of antibiotics or other antimicrobials [6]. The TFRs belonging to this group usually act as repressors, similarly to the great majority of other TFRs. In the absence of the specific ligand (the effector), a TFR binds to DNA, to a specific operator sequence, and blocks the transcription of the target genes. However, when the effector appears in the cell, it is recognized by the TFR and the TFR‐effector interaction causes the release of the regulator from DNA, allowing for the transcription of target genes [1, 3].

  All TFRs are homodimers with the shape of the Greek letter ‘Ω’. The TFR protomer representing a canonical fold of the TetR family is composed of nine α‐helices organized in two domains, a conserved N‐terminal DNA‐binding domain (NTD) and a highly variable C‐terminal domain that recognizes specific effectors. Nevertheless, many TFRs exhibit some discrepancies from the canonical fold [2, 3, 4, 6]. Hence, the TetR family is often described as heterogeneous [5]. Therefore, in our recent work, we have proposed a classification of TFRs and divided them into fourteen subfamilies (subfamilies A‐N), based on a phylogenetic tree calculated for the whole family [6]. Additionally, all TFR structures available in the Protein Data Bank (PDB) [21] were analyzed and some structural similarities, characteristic of particular subfamilies, were observed. For example, the members of subfamilies D and K contain extended loops or additional α‐helices [6]. The proposed classification should help in the identification of close structural homologs of newly identified TFRs. Currently, only a small percentage of the identified 600 thousand TFRs is well characterized.

  Many TFRs that control efflux pumps are present in the proteomes of Gram‐negative pathogens, enabling the removal of antibiotics and other antimicrobials from bacterial cells. Colclough et al. performed a wide‐scale phylogenomic study across the Escherichia and Salmonella genera and identified five genes encoding TFRs that were present in all tested strains [5], among them, the ybiH gene, whose product was partially characterized by Yamanaka et al. [22]. The high‐throughput phenotype microarray system was employed to compare the growth of the wild‐type and ybiH‐deleted E. coli strains. The studies revealed that the deletion mutant was much more sensitive to antibiotics like chloramphenicol and various cephalosporins: cefazolin, cefoxitin, and cefoperazone, corresponding to the first‐, second‐, and third‐generation cephalosporins, respectively. The authors used chloramphenicol and cefoperazone, the latter one as a cephalosporin representative, in further experiments. Thus, YbiH was renamed to cefoperazone and chloramphenicol regulator (CecR). Additionally, the DNA sequence recognized by CecR was identified. It is located within the intragenic spacer of the divergently transcribed cecR‐ybhGFSR and rhlE operons [22]. The ybhG gene encodes a predicted inner membrane protein, whereas the ybhFSR genes encode an uncharacterized ATP‐binding cassette (ABC)‐type transporter [23] that most likely acts as a drug efflux pump [22]. The other operon contains a gene encoding RhlE, an RNA helicase of an unknown function [24]. Initial studies on CecR suggest that YbhG and YbhFSR are involved in response to chloramphenicol and cefoperazone, respectively. Interestingly, the results of the reporter assay showed that the cecR‐ybhGFSR operon is under the negative control of CecR, while the rhlE operon is positively controlled, indicating that most likely CecR acts as a bifunctional regulator [22].

  Another report also highlights the important role of CecR in bacterial survival under stress conditions. It was shown that the exposure of E. coli K‐12 to metformin, a medication for type‐2 diabetes, present also as a pollutant in wastewater, promotes the development of acquired antibiotic resistance in E. coli. The expression of the cecR gene was upregulated in bacteria exposed to metformin, which most likely contributed to the increased efflux of antibiotics [25]. In contrast, evolution experiments with E. coli K‐12 W3110 in the presence of a compound being an uncoupler to the proton motive force, required to move protons outwards, across the membrane, revealed mutations in the cecR gene leading to downregulation of the putative YbhFSR pump. Such mutations enabled bacteria to adapt to the particular stress condition—the presence of the uncoupler. Its efflux was increased by the EmrA pump. In contrast, the genes encoding efflux pumps involved in the removal of other drugs, like YbhFSR putatively involved in cephalosporin efflux, were downregulated [26]. These examples show the importance of CecR in the gene regulation appropriate to the environmental conditions. As CecR is still poorly characterized, we performed structural and biophysical studies to gain more information about this TFR. Here, we report the first crystal structure of CecR and the analysis of its affinity to various antibiotics.


## Results and discussion

### Crystal structure of CecR reveals structural elements characteristic of TetR subfamily D
  CecR was crystallized in the P212121 space group with two polypeptide chains, forming the functional homodimer, present in the asymmetric unit. The crystal structure of CecR was solved at 1.91 Å resolution. Chains A and B contain residues 1‐222 and 7‐220 (out of 223 amino acid residues present in the full‐length protein), respectively. Additionally, at the N terminus of chain A, two residues remaining after tag cleavage are modeled. The dimer shows a typical all‐helical TFR fold, and each of the protomers consists of an N‐terminal DNA‐binding domain (NTD) and a C‐terminal ligand recognition domain (CTD) (Fig. 1A). The NTD, comprising helices α1‐α3 with a conserved helix‐turn‐helix (HTH) motif, represents a canonical TFR DNA‐binding domain. In contrast, the CTD of CecR slightly differs from a typical TFR–ligand‐binding domain. The long helices α4 and α7 are kinked at residues 73‐74 and 138‐139 in α4 and α7, respectively. In other words, the carbonyl oxygens of these residues fall out of the helical conformation, causing the helices to bend at this position. Thus, the two halves of the helices were annotated as α4/α4a and α7/α7a (Fig. 1A). CecR also contains an additional helix α8a, followed by an extended loop that is not present in all TFRs. This helix covers the entrance to the CecR binding site (see next section).

  To classify CecR into one of the TetR subfamilies, we performed the search for the conserved motifs characteristic of subfamilies using MAST from MEME‐Suite [29] and the motif data for the whole TetR family reported by Filipek et al. [6]. The search identifies characteristic motifs in the amino acid sequence of a TFR. The motifs identified in the CTD of CecR classified CecR as the TFR belonging to subfamily D. As the NTDs are highly conserved among various TFR subfamilies, the motifs found in the NTD do not enable the proper differentiation between the TetR subfamilies [6]. This finding is in good agreement with the structural analysis. The additional structural elements of CecR, like helix α8a and the succeeding extended loop, seem to be characteristic of the members of subfamily D [6], as it was observed for E. coli RutR [30] and Pseudomonas fluorescens PhlH [31]. It is of note that additional structural elements between helices α8 and α9 were also reported for the family name‐giving TetR from E. coli [32]. However, according to our previous analyses [6], this protein and its homologs were assigned to subfamily K, as they contain a much more expanded region between helices α8 and α9, including an extended helix α8, a long helix α8a, and a long loop between these two helices, compared to TFRs of subfamily D.


### CecR has a tunnel‐like ligand‐binding cavity
  The ligand‐binding cavity of CecR can be described as a long interior tunnel that covers the inner part of the chain A and B CTDs, mainly the area between helices α4‐α8 in each chain and the helix α8a of the opposite protomer. The tunnel is oriented vertically with respect to the axis of the dimer (Fig. 1A). Such a shape of the ligand‐binding cavity is highly similar to the one described for PhlH, another member of the TetR subfamily D. PhlH regulates the biosynthesis of 2,4‐diacetylphloroglucinol (2,4‐DAPG), a secondary metabolite of Pseudomonas fluorescens [31]. The length of the tunnel is about 35 Å and its volume, calculated with the CASTp server [33, 34], is 399 Å3, whereas these values for PhlH are 30 Å and 354 Å3, respectively. In the crystal structure of PhlH in complex with 2,4‐DAPG, two molecules of the ligand per dimer are bound at both ends of the tunnel [31]. Unexpectedly, the crystal structure of CecR contains two polyethylene glycol (PEG) fragments (Fig. 1A,B), originating from the crystallization solution, in its ligand‐binding cavity. However, both PEG fragments are present inside the CTD of chain B. Although PEG fragments occupy a large part of the cavity, no specific interactions between these molecules and the amino acid residues forming the tunnel can be observed. For instance, the only hydrophilic interactions of PEG and amino acid residues are mediated via water molecules. It is difficult to judge how the presence of the PEG fragments in the ligand‐binding cavity affects the overall CecR structure and whether the CecR‐PEG complex represents the apo or ligand‐bound state of this protein. In TFRs, the distance between helices α3 and α3’ belonging to the HTH motif is a factor that determines the state of the protein. For the known structures of TFRs, it was observed that this distance is longer in protein–ligand complexes compared to protein–DNA complexes. Interestingly, the distance between helices α3 and α3’ in the known unliganded/apo TFR structures is highly variable [2, 4]. It is of note that there are known cases where the structures of TFR complexes with a physiological ligand or a compound from the crystallization solution are almost identical. For example, the structures of the multidrug binding transcriptional repressor CgmR in complex with methylene blue (PDB ID: 2YVE, [35]) and with glycerol originating from the crystallization solution (PDB ID: 2ZOY, [36]) superpose with an RMSD of 0.22 Å over 347 Cα atoms, indicating that the CgmR‐glycerol complex is nearly identical to the ligand‐bound state of this TFR. However, other reports show that the binding of compounds from the crystallization solution, for instance the PEG fragments, can still be related to the apo state of the TFR. The binding of the PEG fragments might only be an entropic effect, replacing some water molecules [37]. However, as any other CecR structures for comparison (either unliganded, bound to a physiological ligand or bound to DNA) are not available, it is difficult to determine the CecR state with high confidence.

  According to Cuthbertson and Nodwell [3], the entrance to the ligand‐binding cavity of a TFR can be located at the front, side, or top of the dimer. However, in some cases, it is not possible to identify the entrance location, as the cavity is fully buried in the CTDs or the dimer interior [3, 6]. This also applies to CecR; the thorough analysis of the CecR surface (Fig. 1C) did not reveal any cleft that would lead to the ligand‐binding cavity. Similarly, the cavity entrance was not found for the PhlH [31]. Thus, we performed the analysis with HingeProt, which predicts the hinge movements [38] to identify the potential tunnel opening. The software divides the protein into the rigid parts and the hinge regions linking them. Interestingly, the most significant movement predicted in CecR involves the residues 183‐196 that correspond to structural elements characteristic of TetR subfamily D, the helix α8a and the succeeding loop (Fig. 2). Possibly, the hinge movements of these elements might open and close the entrance to the ligand‐binding cavity. This assumption can partially be supported by the fact that in other members of TetR subfamily D, the additional structural elements could not always be tracked in electron density maps. An example is the structure of HlyIIR (PDB ID: 2FX0) from Bacillus cereus, where the loop between the helix α8a and α9 was not modeled, indicating the significant flexibility of this element [39].


### CecR affinity varies for different antibiotics
  CecR recognizes cephalosporins and chloramphenicol [22]. Cephalosporins are a class of β‐lactam antibiotics that contain the β‐lactam ring fused to a six‐membered dihydrothiazine ring, forming the 7‐aminocephalosporanic acid skeleton, also called cephem skeleton. In most cases, alterations at the C‐3 and 7‐amino positions affect the pharmacokinetic or antibacterial properties of the cephalosporins, respectively. The large class of cephalosporins is grouped into generations, mainly based on their antimicrobial activity [40]. In contrast, chloramphenicol is a slightly smaller molecule containing a nitrobenzene ring and dichloro‐substituted acetamide. For testing the interactions of CecR with antibiotics, we selected two cephalosporins representing different generations, cefepime (the fourth‐generation cephalosporin) and cefoxitin (the second‐generation cephalosporin), as well as chloramphenicol (Fig. 3A). Initially, it was also planned to test cefoperazone (the third‐generation cephalosporin) in our studies, but due to its low solubility in assay buffer or dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), it was excluded from further experiments. Our selection of antibiotics for further experiments was based on previous studies by Yamanaka et al. [22], which utilized high‐throughput phenotype microarray screening to compare the growth of the wild‐type and cecR‐deleted E. coli strains. The results showed that deletion mutants are sensitive to chloramphenicol, cefazolin, cefoxitin, and cefoperazone. In general, with each successive generation, cephalosporins show improved activity against Gram‐negative bacteria, but often reduced effectiveness against Gram‐positive species [40]. Thus, we did not include the first‐generation cefazolin in our experiments but instead we tested a cephalosporin representing the fourth‐generation. We chose cefepime, which is widely used worldwide and has an extended spectrum of activity against Gram‐negative and Gram‐positive bacteria.

  The binding affinity of CecR for each antibiotic was determined using spectral shift. This method relies on analyzing molecular interactions directly in solution, avoiding the need for surface immobilization. Moreover, in contrast to microscale thermophoresis, these measurements proved to be more robust, enabling reliable data collection even in the presence of aggregates [41]. For each protein–antibiotic complex, dissociation constants (K  d ) were determined from three independent experiments. Individual K  d  values with confidence intervals, as well as a mean with standard deviation, are presented in Table 1. Examples of spectral shift dose–response curves representing the interactions between the CecR and antibiotics were presented in Fig. 3C,E,G. CecR exhibited the strongest interaction with cefepime, as indicated by the relatively low K  d  value of 79.27 ± 7 μm. The interaction of CecR with cefoxitin resulted in a significantly weaker binding affinity, with a K  d  value of 6.07 ± 2.8 mm. The weakest binding affinity was identified for chloramphenicol. However, the precise determination of K  d  value was not possible. In the case of chloramphenicol, the ligand was dissolved in DMSO, and that is why specific considerations must be addressed to ensure reliable and reproducible results. DMSO is a common solvent for ligands, particularly hydrophobic compounds. However, its presence in the sample can influence protein stability and binding interactions. In the spectral shift experiment, the final concentration of DMSO must be kept low, typically below 5%. That is why the ligand stock solution in DMSO must be highly concentrated to ensure that, after dilution into the assay buffer, both the required ligand concentration and an acceptable DMSO percentage are maintained. Unfortunately, chloramphenicol solubility in DMSO was insufficient to prepare a highly concentrated stock, which, in consequence, prevented reaching ligand saturation in the spectral shift assay. A higher concentration of DMSO (up to 20%) was also tested, but saturation was not reached, and moreover, it affected the fluorescence intensity in a concentration‐dependent manner. That is why the determination of binding affinity was not possible, but we could conclude that K  d  was higher than 50 mm.

  The K  d  for specific TFR–ligand complexes is often in the range of 0.1‐10 μm, according to many reports on TFRs [31, 42, 43, 44]. However, it is of note that this usually concerns regulators that recognize one particular effector, like PhlH sensing levels of 2,4‐DAPG. The interaction between TFR and its ligand can be slightly weaker when the regulator is able to recognize various compounds. For instance, the K  d  for Salmonella enterica RamR complexes with drugs was in the range of ~15–25 μm [9]. However, some ligands can only weakly interact with the regulators, an example is the RcdA‐trimethylamine N‐oxide complex, of which K  d  was 7 mm [17]. The K  d  values determined for CecR‐antibiotic complexes indicate that CecR strongly interacts with cefepime, similarly to other reported TFR‐drug complexes, whereas the CecR interactions with cefoxitin and chloramphenicol are much weaker.

  The K  d  for the CecR‐chloramphenicol complex was higher than 50 mm, which raises concerns regarding physiological relevance. However, it is of note that, considering in vivo conditions, much more variable factors should be taken into account. For instance, it is possible that although the extracellular chloramphenicol concentration might be low, the cells might be able to accumulate this antibiotic. It was shown for another Gram‐negative bacterium, Haemophilus influenzae, that while the extracellular chloramphenicol concentration was 0.03 mm, the drug concentration in bacterial cells was 1000‐fold greater (~30 mm) [45]. Unfortunately, such studies were not performed for E. coli; however, this shows a potential possibility of high chloramphenicol concentration occurrence under in vivo conditions.


### Docking of antibiotics to the CecR–ligand‐binding site reveals potential regulator–effector interactions
  To understand why CecR shows different affinity to various antibiotics, we tried to cocrystallize the protein with antibiotics. However, the collected data always represented the CecR‐PEG complexes, which might be due to the high PEG content (25% w/v) in the crystallization solution, as this compound was the main precipitant. Similarly, we were unsuccessful in growing the unliganded form of CecR that could be used for soaking the crystals with antibiotics, as all collected data of potentially unliganded CecR also represented the CecR‐PEG complexes. Finally, the trials of soaking the CecR‐PEG crystals with antibiotics did not provide the CecR‐antibiotic complexes. The analysis of the CecR‐PEG structure revealed that no cavity entry is available in the CecR‐PEG complex, which makes the potential exchange of the ligands impossible.

  Thus, we used AutoDock Vina [46, 47] to identify the potential interactions between CecR and antibiotics. The predicted binding site for all studied ligands covered the site occupied by one of the PEG fragments located closer to the dimer interface in the ligand‐binding cavity of CecR (Fig. 3B). The docking simulations were designed in a way that several residues within the binding site were flexible. For cefepime (Fig. 3D), one potential hydrophilic interaction was detected between the side chain of Q126 and the oxygen of the amide group attached to position C7 of the cephem skeleton. The identical interaction was predicted for cefoxitin (Fig. 3F), involving Q126 and the same amide group of the antibiotic. However, in the case of cefepime, hydrophobic interactions are also possible. Such interactions could potentially occur between the pyrrolidinium ring at position 3 of the cephem skeleton and the side chains of F74 and L109. In contrast, for chloramphenicol, only hydrogen bonds were predicted (Fig. 3H). These involve the hydroxyl groups of S119 and S123 and the nitro group of chloramphenicol and again Q126 and the oxygen of the antibiotic amide group. Although the modeling of S119 and S123 with cefepime at the binding site did not provide the interactions between these residues and the ligand, S119 and S123 could also have other conformations of the side chains that would place their hydroxyl groups in the proximity of cefepime. Then, hydrogen bonds between the S119 and S123 hydroxyl groups and the 2‐amine group of the cefepime thiazole ring can be formed. Some conformations of the side chain of S123 were observed in the structure of the CecR‐PEG complex (Fig. 3B) which supports this assumption. The detailed analysis of the antibiotics docked at the ligand‐binding cavity of CecR might explain why CecR has the highest affinity for cefepime. The chemical structure of this antibiotic allows for the formation of hydrophilic and hydrophobic contacts with the regulator; hence, it is strongly bound by CecR.


### CecR conserved motifs contain residues responsible for ligand recognition
  Identification of potential interactions between antibiotics and CecR has indicated the amino acid residues of the ligand‐binding site that might play an important role in ligand recognition. Our analysis highlighted the importance of Q126, which potentially interacts with all analyzed ligands. Additionally, S119 and S123 can also be involved in hydrophilic interactions with ligands, whereas F74 and L109 might be important for hydrophobic interactions. S119, S123, and Q126 are a part of the motif spanning the residues 118‐132 (Fig. 4) identified in the CecR sequence. L109 is present in the motif covering residues 104‐113. Both sequence motifs are conserved and classify the TFR to subfamily D. It is of note that it does not mean that all subfamily D members have the discussed motifs, but rather a group of proteins belonging to this subfamily contains them [6]. In contrast, F74 was not identified as a part of any conserved motif.

  The search for structural homologs in the PDB using PDBeFold [48] provided only one hit with RMSD below 1.0 Å and it was a putative TFR of Salmonella enterica (PDB ID: 1T33, Midwest Center for Structural Genomics). This structure was used for molecular replacement, as the sequences of the putative TFR and CecR show 85% identity. The RMSD for the superposition of chain A of these structures was 0.67 Å for 215 out of 223 common Cα atoms (the superposition of dimers: 0.82 Å for 428 out of 446 Cα atoms). As the structure of this putative TFR was the result of a structural genomics project, it was deposited in the PDB without a corresponding article and its structure was not analyzed. The second position on the list of structural homologs was a relatively better characterized PhlH (PDB ID: 7E1N) [31] with RMSD of 2.5 Å for 185 out of 223 common Cα atoms (2.6 Å over 345 out of 446 Cα atoms for the dimers). Although PhlH and CecR have a similar tunnel‐like ligand‐binding cavity, the RMSD value of their superposition is relatively high compared to a putative TFR of S. enterica. The sequence identity between PhlH and CecR is low (18%) implying involvement in different regulation mechanisms. It is noteworthy that the sequence identity in the TetR family is usually high in the NTD, but it can be very low for the CTDs of various TFRs (representing even random‐like identity) due to enormous differences of the recognized ligands. Hence, it is not surprising that the sequence identity between PhlH and CecR is only 18%, as PhlH recognizes different ligands than CecR.

  To further search for CecR homologs and to identify the most conserved parts of CecR, we performed the analysis with the ConSurf server [49, 50, 51]. The most conserved parts of the structure were the N‐terminal domain, the most conserved domain among TFRs, and the large fragments of helix α4, α6, and α8 (Fig. 4A,E). Interestingly, the relatively high conservation score was calculated for the additional helix α8a, which means that the TFRs selected for comparison by ConSurf, based on the HMMER search, most likely have the additional structural elements characteristic of subfamily D. The analysis of the protein surface of a single polypeptide chain forming the dimer shows that the residues present at the surface of the CTD exposed to the solvent are not conserved (Fig. 4B), in contrast to the highly conserved residues located at the dimer interface and forming the ligand‐binding cavity (Fig. 4C). Finally, the five residues predicted to be involved in ligand recognition are highly conserved (Fig. 4D). S119, S123, and Q126 are part of helix α6, of which all amino acid residues have high conservation scores. The additional analysis of the motif comprising these residues, created with WebLogo [52] for the set of sequences from ConSurf, revealed that S119 and Q126 were present in all analyzed proteins, whereas S123 was replaced with alanine or threonine in about 20% of the protein sequences (Fig. 4F). L109 is located at the C‐terminal part of helix α5 and only this end of the helix is conserved compared to its other part. Although F74 was not mapped within any conserved motif, it has a very high conservation score, whereas this score is low for the neighboring residues. F74 is one of the residues present at the kink of helix α4. Its high conservation suggests that it might play an important role not only in ligand recognition but also in conformational changes related to the unliganded/liganded state of the protein. A similar kink was observed in helix α4 of SCO3201, another member of the TetR family [18] belonging to TFR subfamily J and of PhlH, a member of the subfamily D like CecR. Interestingly, the helix α4 of PhlH is intact in the apo structure, while it becomes kinked after binding of the ligand due to the interaction of the hydrophobic residue V80 with the effector [31]. This kink is more pronounced compared to the bending of the helix α4 in CecR; however, this might suggest that also in CecR, the kink might be linked to conformational changes related to the protein state.


### Site‐directed mutagenesis of CecR confirms the role of Q126 and F74 in ligand binding
  To confirm our assumption about key residues of CecR, predicted to be involved in ligand binding, we performed site‐directed mutagenesis at the two critical positions (Q126 and F74). Two obtained protein variants, Q126A and F74A, were subjected to a ligand‐binding assay using spectral shift and cefepime, the antibiotic for which the strongest interaction was determined. Although the antibiotic concentration used was 10‐fold higher than in the study with the wild‐type protein, ligand saturation could not be achieved (Fig. 4G,H), and K  d  could not be determined. Unfortunately, a higher concentration of cefepime could not be tested due to its limited solubility in the assay buffer. However, it strongly indicates that the tested protein variants with introduced mutations bind cefepime more weakly than the wild‐type protein, which supports a proposed binding mode in which Q126 and F74 stabilize ligand interactions.


### Homologs of CecR are widely spread among the Enterobacteriales order
  A wide‐scale phylogenomic study performed by Colclough et al. revealed that CecR was identified in all analyzed strains of clinically relevant pathogens across Escherichia and Salmonella genera [5], indicating an important role of this regulator in pathogenic bacteria. We analyzed the set of sequences from the ConSurf analysis. Almost all homologs selected by the software were from the species belonging to the Enterobacteriales order, comprising Gram‐negative rod‐shaped bacteria. The distribution of the analyzed homologs among bacteria families (Fig. 5) revealed that the great majority of them were classified into Enterobacteriaceae (57.5%). It is not surprising, as the studied CecR is a TFR from Escherichia coli, which also belongs to this family. Among the identified species of Enterobacteriaceae, there were species of Salmonella and Klebsiella, which are dangerous pathogens. Some species of Citrobacter also appeared on the list of homologs and for instance, Citrobacter rodentium is a highly infectious mice pathogen [53]. The homologs from bacteria belonging to the Yersiniaceae family constituted 19.2% of the analyzed sequences. Among them, the bacterial species represented mainly two genera, pathogenic Yersinia and Serratia, often being opportunistic human pathogens [54]. The next family of homologs belonged to Morganellaceae (15.8%). Here, many species of Providencia, of which some are responsible for traveler's diarrhea [55], were found. Another large group within this family was constituted by genera Photorhabdus and Xenorhabdus, bacteria associated with nematodes that infect insects [56]. The last family of bacteria belonging to the Enterobacteriales order was Pectobacteriaceae (6.7%), which mainly includes plant pathogens [57]. In this group, the most represented genus was Brenneria, pathogenic bacteria of woody plants like oak or poplar [58]. Finally, there was also one representative from another bacteria order, Pseudomonadales (0.8%), representing the Pseudomonadaceae family and the Pseudomonas genus.

  The analysis of the distribution of the CecR homologs among bacteria families showed that this regulator is present in pathogenic species. However, this concerns not only human but also nematode/insect and plant pathogens. The most surprising was the identification of the CecR homologs in plant‐infecting bacteria from the Pectobacteriaceae family. Interestingly, CecR of E. coli and Brenneria goodwinii, an oak pathogen, share 43% of sequence identity. Out of five ligand‐binding residues of CecR, identified in this study, three were conserved in B. goodwinii CecR, corresponding to L109, S119, and Q126 in E. coli CecR, whereas the residues corresponding to the positions of F74 and S123 are tyrosine and threonine residues in B. goodwinii CecR, respectively. These results raise the question of what the effectors of CecR are in plant pathogens. It might also be speculated that CecR, in general, is able to recognize some other ligands in addition to cephalosporins and chloramphenicol.


## Conclusions
  This work reports the first crystal structure of CecR from E. coli. The detailed analysis of its amino acid sequence and structure revealed that it belongs to subfamily D of TFRs. Hence, it contains additional TetR‐like structural elements, helix α8a and the succeeding extended loop, characteristic of this subfamily. Additionally, the ligand‐binding cavity of CecR has a tunnel‐like shape. This kind of cavity is not common for TFRs, but it was reported for PhlH from P. fluorescens that also belongs to subfamily D. It is of note that not many representatives of subfamily D are well characterized. The structural analysis of CecR confirms that the CTD of TFRs is highly variable and although the overall shape of the dimer is similar to all TFRs, the small differences in their CTD structures cause these regulators to be able to recognize various effectors. Thus, the conserved residues of the CTD of a particular TFR might indicate the group of ligands interacting with the regulator.

  Previous studies on CecR showed that cecR deletion mutants are more sensitive to cephalosporins and chloramphenicol [22]. In addition to structural studies, we have determined the dissociation constants of the CecR complexes with selected cephalosporins (cefepime and cefoxitin) and chloramphenicol. The affinity of CecR varies depending on the antibiotic and is high for cefepime (K  d  of 79.27 ± 7 μm), whereas cefoxitin and chloramphenicol are bound weakly (K  d  in mm range). To explain these differences, the molecular docking of the analyzed antibiotics was performed, and five residues of the ligand‐binding cavity, which potentially could bind the antibiotics, were identified, S119, S123, and Q126, able to form hydrogen bonds, and F74 and L109 which could be involved in hydrophobic interactions. It seems that Q126 is the most important residue among them, as it was predicted that Q126 forms contacts with all analyzed molecules. However, F74 was assumed to be critical for hydrophobic interactions and its presence at the kink of the helix α4 suggests that it might also play a role in conformational changes of the CecR CTD. Cefepime is the only antibiotic among the analyzed ones that would be able to form hydrophilic and hydrophobic interactions due to its chemical structure, which could explain the high affinity of CecR to this antibiotic. Thus, this antibiotic was tested in the binding assay of the CecR variants containing mutations Q126A or F74A. It was shown that both variants bound cefepime more weakly compared to the wild‐type protein which confirmed an important role of these residues in the interactions with the ligand.

  The comparison of the CecR amino acid sequence with sequences of its homologs indicated that all predicted ligand‐binding residues are conserved and most of them are the parts of conserved motifs characteristic of a group of proteins of subfamily D. Finally, the homologs of CecR were identified mainly in various families (Enterobacteriaceae, Yersiniaceae, Morganellaceae, and Pectobacteriaceae) of the Enterobacteriales order. Interestingly, CecR is present not only in human pathogens but also in bacterial species that infect higher plants. This shows that further studies on this regulator are needed, as it is intriguing which effector might target this regulator in plants.


## Materials and methods

### Protein production and purification
  The cecR gene was amplified by PCR from a colony of E. coli BL21 Gold and integrated via EcoRI and NcoI restriction sites into the pETM‐11 vector (EMBL Heidelberg), which adds a TEV‐cleavable N‐terminal His6‐tag to the produced protein. The primers used in PCR are presented in Table 2. The constructs containing mutations were prepared using the polymerase incomplete primer extension (PIPE) method [59] and primers listed in Table 2. E. coli BL21 Gold competent cells were transformed with the vector and grown in autoinducing medium (LB Broth Base including trace elements, Formedium) for 4 h at 37°C and afterwards the culture was further incubated at 20°C overnight for 16 h. The cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4000  g  for 25 min.

  Protein purification was performed on the basis of the previously described protocols for other E. coli proteins [60, 61, 62, 63]. The harvested bacterial cells (suspended in 20 mm HEPES pH 7.5, 500 mm NaCl, 10 mm imidazole, 0.5 mm TCEP and 0.1 mm PMSF) were disrupted by sonication using a Sonopuls GM 3200 ultrasonic homogenizer (Bandelin). Insoluble cell debris was removed by centrifugation at 21 000  g  for 40 min. The supernatant was loaded onto 2 mL of Ni‐NTA agarose beads (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and after several washing steps, the target protein was eluted with buffer containing a high concentration of imidazole (20 mm HEPES pH 7.5, 500 mm NaCl, 400 mm imidazole, 0.5 mm TCEP). The His6‐tag was cleaved off with TEV protease at 4°C during overnight dialysis against 20 mm HEPES pH 7.5, 500 mm NaCl, 0.5 mm TCEP. The protein was then loaded again onto 2 mL of Ni‐NTA agarose beads to remove the cleaved His6‐tag, the His6‐tagged TEV protease and the uncleaved protein. The flow through contained the target protein, which was concentrated to a volume of 1 mL. Subsequently, the concentrated protein was subjected to gel filtration (10/300 Superdex 200 Increase column, coupled to AKTA Pure system, GE Healthcare, Upsala, Sweden) in buffer containing 20 mm HEPES pH 7.5, 250 mm NaCl, 0.5 mm TCEP.


### Protein crystallization and data collection
  The purified CecR protein was concentrated to 13 mg·mL−1. Initial screening was performed using the hanging‐drop vapor diffusion method and Index Screen (Hampton Research, Aliso Viejo, CA, USA). Crystals of CecR were obtained under the following conditions: 0.15 m magnesium chloride, 0.05 m sodium acetate, 0.1 m Bis‐Tris pH 6.5, 25% (w/v) PEG 3350. The cocrystallization of CecR with antibiotics was performed by adding the 10‐50 molar excess of the particular antibiotic to the protein solution used for crystallization. The obtained crystals were cryoprotected by soaking in crystallization solution containing 22.5% (v/v) PEG 400 and flash‐cooled in liquid nitrogen.

  X‐ray diffraction data were collected on beamline P13 of the PETRA III storage ring (EMBL/DESY, Hamburg, Germany) [64]. The collected data were processed with XDSAPP (Table 3) [65].


### Structure determination and refinement
  The crystal structure of CecR was determined by the molecular replacement method with Phaser [66]. The structure of a putative transcriptional repressor (TetR family) from Salmonella typhimurium LT2 (PDB ID: 1T33; Midwest Center for Structural Genomics) was used as a search model. The final CecR structure was generated by iterative rounds of manual model building with Coot [67] and automated refinement with phenix.refine [68] with TLS parameters [69]. The quality assessment of the structure was performed in MolProbity [70]. Refinement statistics are listed in Table 3. The electron density omit map Fo‐Fc indicating the presence of the PEG fragments in chain B of CecR, the map was calculated with Phenix Polder [27]. Atomic coordinates and structure factors for CecR have been deposited in the PDB under accession code 9I1X.


### Structural analysis
  The ligand‐binding cavities were analyzed using the CASTp server [33, 34]. The search for conserved motifs was performed using MAST from MEME‐Suite [29]. The conservation score for the amino acid sequence of CecR was calculated using ConSurf [49, 50, 51]. WebLogo was used for detailed analysis of the motif involved in ligand binding [52]. All figures presenting the CecR structure were created in PyMOL software [28].


### Molecular docking
  Molecular docking of drugs was conducted using AutoDock suite with AutoDock Vina [46, 47], as described by Forli et al. [71]. Briefly, PDBQT files of both the rigid and flexible parts of the regulator, as well as docked ligands were generated using AutoDock Tools (ADT). The grid center, size, and space were the same for every docked ligand and set using ADT. Molecular docking was executed using AutoDock Vina with exhaustiveness 50 and 10 poses. Exhaustiveness was set to 50 due to a combination of factors: ligand size, number of its rotatable bonds and size of docking space. It was done to improve sampling thoroughness, especially considering the presence of a large number of flexible residues. Flexible residues for every docking attempt were set to: F74, L109, S119, S123, Q126, H170, L177, and R180, as selected based on structural analysis of the binding site. Solvent molecules and PEG fragments originating from crystallization solutions were not included. The docking poses were selected based on the calculated binding affinity values; the poses with the lowest value were chosen. Validation was performed by redocking of the PEG fragment.


### Spectral shift analysis
  Spectral shift measurements were conducted using the Monolith X instrument (NanoTemper, Munich, Germany). CecR protein and its variants bearing mutations (Q126A and F74A) were fluorescently labeled with the RED‐NHS Protein Labeling Kit (NanoTemper) and used at a final concentration of 25 nm in the assay. Ligands tested were the antibiotics: cefepime, cefoxitin, and chloramphenicol. Cefepime and cefoxitin were initially dissolved in assay buffer (20 mm HEPES pH 7.5, 250 mm NaCl, 0.5 mm TCEP), while chloramphenicol was dissolved in DMSO to generate stock solution (20 mm, 100 mm, and 500 mm, respectively). Later each ligand stock solution was subsequently diluted into the assay buffer (in the case of chloramphenicol in assay buffer with 10% DMSO to maintain a consistent DMSO concentration across the entire dilution series). Labeled CecR protein was mixed with a 1:1 (v/v) dilution series of antibiotics, which served as the nonfluorescent ligands. The highest concentrations of antibiotics in the assay were 1 mm and 10 mm for cefepime (1 mm in interaction studies with wild‐type protein and 10 mm when Q126A and F74A variants were tested), 50 mm for cefoxitin, and 250 mm for chloramphenicol. The mixtures were loaded into MST premium capillaries and measured at 25°C, with excitation power set between 80% and 100%, and medium IR laser power. Fluorescence signal ratios (670 nm/650 nm) were plotted against the concentration of the antibiotic. Data analysis was performed using MO.Control 2 software (v2.5.4, NanoTemper). The spectral shift experiment was repeated at least three times for each protein–antibiotic complex.
