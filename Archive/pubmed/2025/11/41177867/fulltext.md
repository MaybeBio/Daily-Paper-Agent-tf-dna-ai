# Apigenin regulates CCR5/JAK1/STAT1/MMPs signaling to alleviate secondary brain injury after intracerebral hemorrhage and its enhanced delivery via targeted nanoparticles


## Graphical Abstract

## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03748-6.


## Introduction
  Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is a type of hemorrhagic stroke caused by the rupture of cerebral blood vessels, accounting for approximately 15% of all strokes, with a mortality rate as high as 70% [1, 2]. Following ICH, the mechanical compression of surrounding brain tissue by the hematoma is considered the cause of primary brain injury, whereas secondary brain injury (SBI) results from the lysis of red blood cells within the hematoma. This process triggers inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, neuronal apoptosis, and disruption of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) [3–5]. Although current surgical interventions can partially reduce mortality in ICH patients, the persistent presence of SBI significantly worsens clinical outcomes. Statistical data indicate that only about 20% of ICH patients regain basic self-care within six months [6], burdening families and society. To date, no pharmacological treatments have been proven effective in specifically targeting SBI after ICH, making the development of new therapeutic agents and targets for mitigating ICH-induced SBI an urgent medical challenge.

  Apigenin (API) is a natural flavonoid compound widely found in nature, primarily derived from medicinal plants such as celery, parsley, thyme, and chamomile. Pharmacological studies have shown that API possesses significant anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-fibrotic properties [7–9]. Notably, research has also demonstrated that API can promote neurogenesis by upregulating brain-derived neurotrophic factors, enhancing neurofilament protein synthesis, and increasing the expression of PSD-95 and synaptotagmin [10]. In addition, API has been shown to protect BBB integrity in a subarachnoid hemorrhage model by inhibiting TLR4-mediated inflammatory responses [11]. However, the role and underlying mechanisms of API in ICH remain unclear.

  Janus kinase 1 (JAK1) is an intracellular non-receptor tyrosine kinase composed of FERM, SH2, pseudokinase, and kinase domains, and is capable of phosphorylating various downstream genes and signaling molecules, particularly the signal transducers and activators of transcription (STATs) [12]. In addition to JAK1, the JAK protein family includes JAK2, JAK3, and TYK2, which are widely expressed in multiple cell types, especially in immune-related cells. The JAK-STAT signaling pathway is activated upon the binding of cytokines to membrane receptors and plays a key role in regulating macrophage polarization, mediating signaling by interleukins (ILs), interferons (IFNs), and chemokines [12–14]. As a downstream effector of JAK1, STAT1 regulates the secretion of various proteolytic enzymes, including matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP9) [15]. The JAK-STAT-MMPs (matrix metalloproteinases) signaling pathway is a classical inflammatory pathway that regulates tight junction proteins in the BBB, such as zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) and claudin-5 (CLDN5), thereby contributing to hydrolytic and neuroinflammatory injury [16, 17].

  As innate immune cells, macrophages play critical roles in phagocytosis, antigen presentation, and cytokine production [18, 19]. Previous studies have confirmed substantial macrophage infiltration around the hematoma following ICH, where these macrophages cooperate with microglia to promote hematoma clearance through phagocytosis [20, 21]. However, under stimulation by hemoglobin degradation products, macrophages can polarize toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, releasing harmful factors such as MMPs, thereby exacerbating SBI [22]. Therefore, promoting macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype while preserving their phagocytic capacity and reducing the production of harmful factors like MMPs is a promising strategy to mitigate SBI after ICH.

  Nanodrug delivery systems have been widely applied in treating neurological diseases due to their favorable targeting ability, biodegradability, and tissue penetration. PLGA is an FDA-approved biodegradable material that effectively encapsulates and sustains the release of small-molecule drugs. To precisely target inflammatory macrophages, we developed a PLGA-based apigenin-loaded nanoparticle system (RVG/FA-NPs@API) modified with the blood–brain barrier ligand RVG29 and the macrophage-targeting ligand folic acid (FA). The system evades reticuloendothelial clearance, prolongs circulation, and integrates neutrophil-mediated chemotaxis with macrophage-driven responsiveness, enabling spontaneous blood–brain barrier penetration and selective targeting of M1 macrophages, thereby enhancing drug accumulation and bioavailability at lesion sites [23]. In addition, we employed molecular dynamics simulation, bioinformatics analysis, and both in vitro and In vivo functional experiments to systematically evaluate the mechanism of action and neuroprotective effects of API in ICH, aiming to provide novel therapeutic strategies and a translational foundation for the treatment of SBI following ICH.


## Materials and methods

### Animals
  Female C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old, Cavens Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd., Changzhou, China) were housed under controlled conditions (22 ± 1 ℃, 55 ± 10% relative humidity, 12-h light/dark cycle) and acclimated for at least three days before surgery. Food and water were provided ad libitum. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the School of Medicine, Jiangnan University, Wuxi (Ethics Approval No: JN.No20220330c0480512[108]) and were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the U.S. National Institutes of Health.


## Experimental design
  The experimental timeline and design are shown in Fig. 1A. The Grid Walking test, Rotarod test, Forelimb Placing test, and brain water content measurement were performed on days 1, 3, and 7; the Forelimb Placing test was repeated on days 14 and 21; and the Morris Water Maze was conducted on day 35. Experimental groups, animal numbers, and observed mortality rates are summarized in Table S1.


## ICH model
  As previously described, the ICH model was established by injecting collagenase into the lateral ventricle [24]. Mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 3% sodium pentobarbital (30 mg/kg; Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China). Each mouse was placed in a stereotaxic frame (RWD Life Science Co., Ltd., China), and a burr hole was drilled in the skull (1 mm anterior to bregma, 2 mm lateral to the midline). A 10 µL microsyringe (Gaoge Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) was inserted 4 mm deep into the right striatum. Then, 0.5 µL of normal saline containing 0.075 U of type IV collagenase (100162037, Sigma, USA) was slowly injected over 5 min. After injection, the needle was kept in place for 5 min before being withdrawn. The burr hole was sealed with bone wax, and the scalp was sutured. Postoperatively, mice were maintained at 37.0 ± 0.5 °C using a heating pad. The sham group underwent the same procedure, except that only normal saline was injected. In the nanoparticle efficacy study, mice subjected to ICH were randomly assigned to five groups (PBS, API, PLGA@API, RVG/FA-NPs@API). At 24 h post-surgery, a single dose was administered via tail vein injection (API-equivalent 1 mg/kg) to assess systemic circulation and targeting capacity.


## Drug administration
  Edaravone (HY-B0099, MedChemExpress, USA), a clinically approved neuroprotective agent for stroke treatment, was used as the positive control. Edaravone and apigenin (A800500, Macklin, China) were dissolved in a vehicle composed of 10% DMSO, 40% PEG300, 5% Tween-80, and 45% saline. At 24 h after ICH induction, small-molecule drugs, including apigenin at low (5 mg/kg) and high (20 mg/kg) doses and edaravone (30 mg/kg), were administered intraperitoneally to promote rapid absorption. Maraviroc (MVC), a selective CCR5 antagonist (100 mg/kg for animal experiments or 5 µL for cell experiments, administered for 6 h; S2003, Selleck Chemicals, USA), was dissolved in 1% DMSO. In animal studies, MVC was administered intraperitoneally at three time points: 25 h before ICH induction and 2 h and 23 h after ICH induction. Recombinant mouse MIP-1β/CCL4 (rCCL4) (P6776, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) treated macrophages for 12 h. Following ICH, treatment groups received daily intraperitoneal injections of API or edaravone for either 7 or 3 consecutive days. Sham-operated and ICH groups received equal volumes of vehicles. GM6001, a broad-spectrum MMP inhibitor, was dissolved in 5 µl DMSO and administered to mice via carotid artery injection. A negative control compound (EMD Biosciences, La Jolla, CA, USA) was used in parallel [25].


## Neurological behavioral testing
  Short- and long-term behavioral tests were conducted to assess neurological deficits in mice following ICH. Short- and long-term neurobehavioral tests were conducted to evaluate behavioral deficits after ICH in mice. Standardized assessments were performed in a double-blind manner: (1) neurological function using the 5-point Longa scale; (2) sensorimotor integration by the forelimb placing test (correct response rate of the left forepaw); (3) motor coordination and endurance by the accelerating rotarod test (5–40 rpm/300 s, mean latency of three trials); (4) limb coordination by the grid walking test (frequency of left forepaw missteps); and (5) spatial memory by the Morris water maze on day 35, including 7 days of hidden platform training (3 trials/day) and a 60-s probe trial, with measurements of target quadrant time, platform crossings, and swimming speed. Before surgery, all mice underwent training thrice daily for three consecutive days. Behavioral assessments were then repeated on postoperative days 1, 3, and 7. The adhesive removal test and MWM were extended to day 28.


### Assessment of cerebral edema
  As previously described [26], brain water content (BWC) was measured using the wet/dry weight method. Briefly, brains were rapidly harvested 1, 3, and 7 days after collagenase injection. Each brain region’s wet weight (WW)—including the basal ganglia, cortex, cerebellum, hippocampus, and brainstem—was recorded. Samples were then dried in an oven at 65 °C for 72 h to determine the dry weight (DW). The extent of cerebral edema was evaluated by calculating the WW to dry DW ratio. The formula used to determine BWC was as follows: BWC (%) = [(WW - DW)/WW] × 100%.


## Assessment of blood–brain barrier permeability
  Coronal brain Section (20 μm) were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature and then incubated for 2 h with Cy3-conjugated, affinity-purified goat anti-rat IgG (1:400; Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, Inc.). After mounting, images were acquired using an LSM 700 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss) and reconstructed with Adobe Photoshop [25].


## Nissl and hematoxylin & Eosin (H&E) staining
  Nissl staining assessed neuronal loss after ICH [27]. Specifically, 16 μm-thick coronal frozen brain sections were immersed in Nissl staining solution for 10 min, followed by 2 min of differentiation in graded ethanol. Sections were then mounted and observed under a microscope. Nissl-positive cells were quantified at 20× magnification using ImageJ software (ImageJ 1.5, NIH, USA). H&E staining was performed to evaluate hematoma volume [1]. The section containing the largest hematoma area—typically at the level of the needle track—was selected for staining. The stained sections were scanned using a Leica DM6 microscope, and the hematoma volume was measured using ImageJ software (ImageJ 1.5, NIH, USA).


### TUNEL staining
  To quantify neuronal apoptosis 72 h after ICH, TUNEL staining (green) was performed using an in situ apoptosis detection kit (C1088, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). TUNEL-positive neurons in the hematoma region were manually counted. Three random sections were analyzed for each brain, and the average number of TUNEL-positive neurons was calculated using ImageJ software (ImageJ 1.5, NIH, USA) at 200× magnification. The results were expressed as the percentage (%) of TUNEL-positive neurons.


### Western blot (WB)
  Protein samples were mixed with loading buffer and heated at 95 °C for 8–10 min, followed by electrophoresis on a 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gel (E303-01, Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). Proteins were transferred to methanol-activated PVDF membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST at room temperature for 2 h and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (1:1000 dilution). After three washes with TBST (15 min each), membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:3000) at room temperature for 2 h. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence reagents (WBULP-100ML, Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) and analyzed for band density using ImageJ software (ImageJ 1.5, NIH, USA). Antibody details are provided in Table S2.


### RNA isolation and quantitative PCR (qPCR)
  Total RNA was extracted from brain tissue 72 h after ICH using Trizol (79306, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the 5× All-in-One RT MasterMix Kit (R333-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). qPCR was performed in triplicate using SYBR Green qPCR MasterMix (Q711-02, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). mRNA expression levels were normalized to β-actin and calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Table S3.


### Immunofluorescence staining
  Paraffin-embedded brain tissues were sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm for immunofluorescence staining. Sections were first deparaffinized and rehydrated in xylene and graded ethanol, followed by antigen retrieval in sodium citrate buffer at boiling temperature for 1 h. After preparation, the sections were blocked with 5% BSA for 2 h and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (1:150 dilution). On the following day, sections were incubated at room temperature for 1.5 h with secondary antibodies—goat anti-rabbit Cy3 (1:200, ab6939, Abcam, USA) and goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (1:200, ab150077, Abcam, USA). Finally, the sections were counterstained with DAPI (C0065, Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 15 min, rinsed, and imaged using a Zeiss fluorescence microscope (Zeiss Axio Imager 2, Germany). Cell immunofluorescence was carried out according to established protocols [21]. Neuronal fibers were quantified by MAP2 immunofluorescence staining, and fiber length was analyzed by tracing with ImageJ software. RAW264.7 or HT-22 cells (3 × 10⁴ cells per well) were seeded on coverslips in 24-well plates and cultured overnight. After drug treatment, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were washed three times with cold PBS. Cells were then fixed with paraformaldehyde for 30 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 15 min, and blocked with 5% BSA for 2 h. Fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies were applied and incubated at room temperature for 1.5 h. Cells, stained with DAPI (C0065, Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 15 min, were thoroughly washed and mounted with coverslips. Fluorescence images were captured using a Zeiss Axio Imager 2 microscope (Germany).


### Tissue distribution of RVG/FA-NPs@API in ICH model mice
  ICH model mice were established and intravenously injected with Cy5.5-labeled RVG/FA-NPs@API (200 µL, 2 mg/mL) via the tail vein 24 h after ICH induction. In vivo fluorescence imaging was performed at 1, 12, and 24 h post-injection using the IVIS Spectrum imaging system (PerkinElmer, USA). Following euthanasia, major organs, including the brain, liver, spleen, lungs, heart, and kidneys, were harvested for ex vivo imaging. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software to analyze the tissue distribution pattern of the nanoparticles.


### In vivo targeting evaluation of RVG/FA-NPs@API
  After euthanasia, brain tissue was harvested and dissociated into single cells using the Adult Brain Dissociation Kit (130-107−677, Miltenyi Biotec, Germany). CD11b-positive cells were then enriched using CD11b MicroBeads (130-049−601, Miltenyi Biotec, Germany). The enriched cells were subjected to flow cytometry analysis using PE-conjugated anti-mouse CD86 antibodies (105007, BioLegend, USA) at a dilution of 5 µL per 1 million cells in a 100 µL staining volume. Cy5.5 fluorescence was used to evaluate nanoparticle uptake. The proportion of Cy5.5⁺CD86⁺ double-positive cells was analyzed using a BD LSRFortessa™ X-20 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA) to assess the enrichment efficiency of RVG/FA-NPs@API in M1-type macrophages.


### Network pharmacology analysis
  Network pharmacology analysis was conducted as previously described [28]. We used databases, including SwissTargetPrediction, PharmMapper, and DrugBank, to identify potential targets associated with API. In the SwissTargetPrediction database, targets with a probability score greater than 0.1 were selected as initial candidates. Targets related to ICH were retrieved from DisGeNET, OMIM, and GeneCards. For the GeneCards database, targets with a relevance score of ≥ 20 were included. The keywords used for the search included “intracerebral hemorrhage,” “basal ganglia hemorrhage,” “hemorrhagic stroke,” and “intracranial hemorrhage.” Drug-related and disease-related targets from each database were compared, and duplicate entries were removed. The intersection of the two target sets was identified and visualized using a Venn diagram from the online tool (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/).


### Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network and enrichment analysis
  The overlapping targets of the drug and disease were imported into the STRING database, with the organism set to Homo sapiens and the confidence score threshold set to medium (0.4) to generate a PPI network. The resulting interaction data were then imported into Cytoscape software (version 3.10.1), and network topology parameters, including degree, betweenness, and closeness centrality, were analyzed using the CytoHubba plugin. Functional enrichment analysis, including Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment, was performed using the Metascape database (https://metascape.org/). The screening criteria were set to Homo sapiens and p < 0.05. Final visualizations were generated using the WeChat online charting tool.


### Molecular docking
  Molecular docking between API and JAK1 protein—including the full-length protein and each of its four individual domains—was performed using AutoDock (version 4.2.6). The protein structure files in PDB format were obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/). After removing water molecules from solvents and ligands, hydrogen atoms were added. The PDBQT files of the JAK1 protein and its domains were prepared as receptor files, while the PDBQT file of API was used as the ligand for docking analysis. Docking results were analyzed using AutoDockTools (version 1.5.7), and visualizations were generated using PyMOL (version 2.4.1) and Liplot (version 2.2.8). The root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) between the docked conformations of API and its original structure was calculated using PyMOL. A successful docking result was one with an RMSD ≤ 2.0 Å (0.2 nm).


### Molecular dynamics simulation
  Molecular docking and dynamics simulations of API with the JAK1 protein and its kinase domain were performed using Discovery Studio (BIOVIA, Dassault Systèmes, Discovery Studio Modeling Environment, version 2019). The molecular dynamics simulation involved the following steps: (1) The protein-ligand complex was imported into Discovery Studio, and the “Prepare Protein” tool was used to preprocess the protein structure. (2) The ligand file was opened, and the CHARMM36 force field was applied for energy minimization of the protein and ligand before simulation. (3) The dynamics process was initiated using the “Standard Dynamics Cascade” protocol, with the solvent system window (Complex.dsv) set as the active window for the molecular dynamics simulation. (4) Hydrogen bond fluctuations within the protein-ligand complex, along with the RMSD and root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) values of the protein backbone and side chains, were analyzed using Discovery Studio.


### In vitro kinase activity assay
  Active human JAK2 protein (Cat# J62-53G, Signal Chem, Canada) was diluted to a final concentration of 0.1 µg/ml in Kinase Dilution Buffer III (Cat# K23-09, Signal Chem, Canada), and then incubated with His-tagged STAT3 substrate (3 µg), purified from E. coli M15 cells using Ni-NTA magnetic beads (Cat# 30210, Qiagen, Germany), together with 5 µl ATP. Different concentrations of apigenin (10 µM and 20 µM; Cat# A800500, Macklin, China) were added to the reaction system, which was maintained at 30 ℃ for 30 min. Reactions were terminated by adding 1× Laemmli buffer (Cat# 1610747, Bio-Rad, USA) and boiling at 95 ℃ for 5 min. Western blotting was performed using an anti-phospho-STAT3 antibody (Cat# 9145, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) to detect JAK2-mediated phosphorylation of STAT3 at Tyr705 [29].


### Cell culture and cell viability assay
  Cell viability was assessed as previously described [6]. RAW264.7 and HT-22 cell lines (CL-0190/CL-0595, Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were treated with various concentrations (0, 12.5, 25, 50, 100, and 200 µM) of API or biotinylated API for 12, 24, and 48 h. After treatment, CCK-8 reagent (C0038, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was added, and cells were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h in a 5% CO₂ incubator. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).


### In vitro live/Dead cell staining
  Cell apoptosis was assessed using a live/dead cell staining method [30]. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, a Calcein-AM/PI Cell Viability and Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (C2015M, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was used. Briefly, cultured neurons were washed to remove residual esterases from the medium. A working solution containing Calcein-AM (for viable cells) and Propidium Iodide (PI, for dead cells) was added to the cultured cells, followed by incubation at 37 ℃ for 30 min in the dark. After staining, live/dead cell status was assessed under a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss Axio Imager 2, Germany). The ratio of PI-positive (dead) cells to total cells was quantified to evaluate cell membrane integrity and viability.


### Pull-down assay for API-binding proteins
  RAW264.7 cell lysates were incubated overnight at 4 °C with biotin, biotinylated API, or biotinylated API pre-incubated with free API. After incubation, streptavidin-conjugated magnetic beads (21115, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) were used to pull down proteins from the lysates at 4 °C for at least 6 h. The beads were thoroughly washed with PBS, and 5× loading buffer was added. Samples were boiled, and the supernatant was collected to detect JAK1 protein.


### Surface plasmon resonance (SPR)
  The binding affinity between API and JAK1 protein (HY-P700583, MedChemExpress, USA) was evaluated using the SPR-based Biacore T200 instrument (Cytiva, Sweden). A CM5 sensor chip was used for the experiment. The sensor surface was activated with a mixture of 50 mM NHS and 200 mM EDC for 7 min. JAK1 protein (420 µg/mL), diluted in 10 mM acetate buffer (pH 4.5), was immobilized on the chip surface at a 10 µL/min flow rate. The surface was then blocked with 1 M ethanolamine (pH 8.5). Multiple binding cycles were performed, and response signals were recorded with time on the x-axis and response units on the y-axis. The collected data were fitted using the Biacore T200 evaluation software with a 1:1 Langmuir binding model to determine kinetic parameters, including the association rate constant, dissociation rate constant, and equilibrium dissociation constant. The experimental procedures and data analysis were carried out by TGTMED Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).


#### Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)
  CETSA was performed as previously described [31]. Briefly, ICH mice were intraperitoneally injected with API (20 mg/kg), and RAW264.7 cells were treated with API (25 µM) for 8 h. After treatment, brain tissue from the hemorrhagic side of mice and protein lysates from RAW264.7 cells were collected. Protein stability was assessed at temperatures ranging from 50 °C to 71 °C. Following thermal denaturation, samples were subjected to three freeze-thaw cycles using liquid nitrogen, then centrifuged at 20,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were collected for further analysis.


### Preparation of PLGA nanoparticles and API loading
  API-loaded PLGA nanoparticles were prepared using the solvent evaporation method. Briefly, 50 mg of PLGA-COOH (LA: GA = 50:50, Mw = 30,000–60,000; P2191, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was dissolved in 4 mL of dichloromethane (DCM; D807825, Macklin, China), followed by the addition of 10 mg of API. The mixture was thoroughly stirred to form the organic phase. This solution was then added dropwise into 40 mL of an aqueous phase containing 1% polyvinyl alcohol (PVA, Mw = 30,000–70,000; P8136, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) under ice bath conditions. A probe-type sonicator (Scientz-IID, Ningbo Scientz Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) was used to ultrasonicate the mixture at 200 W for 3 min (2-second pause, 1-second pulse). The resulting emulsion was stirred at 500 rpm under ventilation for 4 h to allow DCM evaporation. The formed nanoparticles were collected by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min (Model: 5810R, Eppendorf, Germany), washed three times with deionized water, and freeze-dried for storage [32, 33].


#### Surface functionalization with RVG29 and FA
  Dual modification was achieved by incorporating DSPE-PEG2000-RVG29 (HY-172705, MedChemExpress, USA) and DSPE-PEG2000-FA (PS2-DEFA, Pengshuo Biotech, China). Briefly, 5 mg of lyophilized PLGA nanoparticles were dispersed in 10 mL PBS (pH 7.4), followed by the addition of 0.5 mg DSPE-PEG2000-RVG29 and 0.5 mg DSPE-PEG2000-FA. The mixture was sonicated in a 37 °C water bath for 10 min and stirred at room temperature for 12 h. The modified nanoparticles were washed three times by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm, lyophilized, and designated as RVG/FA-NPs@API [34]. To evaluate the respective targeting roles of RVG29 and FA, single-modified nanoparticles were also prepared, namely RVG-NPs@API and FA-NPs@API. The procedure was identical to that of the dual-modified group, except that only 0.5 mg DSPE-PEG2000-RVG29 or 0.5 mg DSPE-PEG2000-FA was added, followed by lyophilization for storage.


#### Characterization of particle Size, zeta potential, and morphology
  Nanoparticle morphology and size were characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and dynamic light scattering (DLS). TEM: Approximately 3 µL of nanoparticle suspension was dropped onto a carbon-coated 200-mesh copper grid, allowed to stand at room temperature for 5 min, excess liquid removed, and negatively stained with 3 µL of 1% (w/v) uranyl acetate for 5 min. After drying, samples were observed at 80 kV using a TEM (Tecnai-10, Philips, The Netherlands). DLS: Hydrodynamic diameter, polydispersity index (PDI), and ζ-potential were measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern Instruments, UK) with three independent replicates [35]. Structural Confirmation: RVG/FA-NPs@API (6 mg) was dissolved in DMSO-d6/D2O (3:2) and analyzed by 1 H NMR spectroscopy to verify conjugation [36].


#### X-ray diffraction (XRD)
  XRD patterns were obtained using a diffractometer equipped with a Cu target and graphite monochromator (Rigaku D/max 2500/PC, Japan). Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.54 Å) was used as the incident beam, with a scanning range of 5°−90°, operating at 40 kV and 200 mA, and a scan rate of 5°/min with a 1-s counting time.


### Determination of drug encapsulation efficiency (EE%) and loading efficiency (LE%)
  To evaluate the drug EE%, 5 mg of freeze-dried RVG/FA-NPs@API was dissolved in 1 mL of acetonitrile by sonication. The concentration of API was then determined using a high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system (Agilent 1260, USA). A C18 column (4.6 mm × 250 mm, 5 μm; Waters, USA) was used with a mobile phase of methanol: water (60:40, v/v) at a 1.0 mL/min flow rate. The detection wavelength was set at 340 nm. EE% and LE% were calculated based on the standard calibration curve [34].


### Stability evaluation
  To assess the stability of RVG/FA-NPs@API, the nanoparticles were incubated in PBS buffer and cell culture medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) for 0 and 24 h. At the designated time points, aliquots of the nanoparticle suspension were collected, and particle size was measured using DLS. For long-term storage stability evaluation, freshly prepared RVG/FA-NPs@API were stored at 4 °C in the dark. Samples were taken on days 0, 15, 30, 45, and 60, and particle size was measured via DLS to assess changes over time.


### In vitro drug release study
  A total of 5 mg of RVG/FA-NPs@API was resuspended in 1 mL of PBS (containing 0.5% Tween-80, at either pH 7.4 or pH 5.5) and placed into a dialysis bag (MW cut-off 10,000 Da; Spectrum Labs, USA). The bag was then immersed in 50 mL of the corresponding buffer and incubated at 37 °C with constant shaking at 100 rpm. At 1, 2, 5, 7, 10, 25, and 50 h, 1 mL of the release medium was collected and replaced with an equal volume of fresh buffer. The concentration of API in the release samples was determined using HPLC.


### In vitro biocompatibility assessment of nanoparticles
  RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of1 × 10⁵ cells/well and polarized into M1 macrophages by stimulation with LPS (1 µg/mL, L4391, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 12 h, or into M2 macrophages by stimulation with IL-4 (20 ng/mL, 200-04, PeproTech, USA) for 24 h [37]. Polarized RAW264.7 cells and HT22 cells were then seeded into 96-well plates (5 × 10³ cells/well) and cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 ℃ in a 5% CO2 incubator. Cells were treated with RVG/FA-NPs@API at different concentrations (5–25 µg/mL) for 24 h, followed by the addition of 10 µL/well CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo, Japan). After incubation for 2 h at 37 °C, absorbance was measured at 450 nm [38].


### In vitro cellular uptake of nanoparticles
  RAW264.7 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells per well and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere for 24 h. The cells were then transferred to a fresh DMEM medium containing FITC-labeled RVG/FA-NPs@API (FITC conjugation achieved via covalent grafting; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and incubated for 6 h. After incubation, all cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min and washed with PBS. The nuclei were stained with DAPI for 15 min. Fluorescence images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM), and cellular uptake of RVG/FA-NPs@API was quantitatively analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD FACSvantage SE, USA) [39].


### Statistical analysis
  All experiments were conducted in a randomized and blinded manner. Each in vitro experiment was independently repeated at least three times, and each In vivo group included more than three mice. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 software (GraphPad Software Inc.). For comparisons between two groups, Tukey’s multiple comparison test was used. For multiple group comparisons, one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied, followed by appropriate post hoc tests. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD).


## Results

### Animal mortality and exclusion
  A total of 285 female C57BL/6 mice were used in this study, including 261 in the ICH induction group and 24 in the sham group. Nineteen mice did not exhibit neurological deficits after ICH modeling and showed no evidence of hematoma in brain tissue; thus, they were excluded from the analysis. The overall mortality rate was 7.4% (21/285). The ICH + MVC group exhibited the highest mortality (2/11), while the ICH + API 20 mg/kg group had the lowest (2/60). #### Side effects and toxicity of API
  API is a flavonoid compound, with its structure shown in Figure S1. Compared with edaravone (Eda), it additionally exerts specific effects on macrophage polarization and the CCR5/JAK1/STAT1 signaling pathway. Given its antitumor properties, its potential toxicity to normal tissues was evaluated. Toxicity prediction using online tools indicated that API possessed good biocompatibility and was unlikely to induce toxic effects in mice at appropriate concentrations (Figures S1A and S1B). Further safety evaluation was performed based on previous studies [40, 41], with mice receiving daily intraperitoneal injections of API at 5 mg/kg and 20 mg/kg for 7 consecutive days. Histological analysis (H&E staining) of the liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys revealed no significant structural changes, confirming its favorable biocompatibility (Figures S2A and S2C). In addition, biochemical analysis of serum from mice treated with high and low doses of API for 7 days showed no significant differences in aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), serum creatinine (CREA), or urea (UREA) levels compared to the control group (Figure S2B).


#### API improves neurological deficits and cerebral edema induced by ICH in mice
  The effects of API were evaluated in a mouse model of ICH (Fig. 1 A, B). Longa’s 5-point scoring system was used to assess short-term neurological deficits during behavioral evaluations. Compared to the sham group, mice exhibited significant neurological impairments one day after ICH induction. However, daily administration of API at 5 or 20 mg/kg for three or seven consecutive days partially alleviated these deficits (Fig. 1 C). In addition to behavioral scores, API improved sensory and motor function in ICH mice, as assessed by the foot-fault test, accelerating rotarod test, and adhesive removal test. The foot-fault test revealed a significantly higher error rate in the left forelimb of ICH mice, particularly on day 3 post-ICH, compared to sham controls. Treatment with API at both 5 and 20 mg/kg significantly reduced the foot-fault rate (Fig. 1D). In the rotarod test, API-treated ICH mice remained on the rotating rod for longer durations, indicating partial recovery of motor coordination (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, API significantly reduced the time required to detect and remove adhesive tapes in the adhesive contact and removal test, with marked improvement observed up to day 21 post-ICH. This suggests that API promoted the recovery of somatosensory and motor function (Fig. 1 F). To assess spatial cognitive function, the MWM test was performed. The probe trial was conducted on day 35 after ICH. Swimming path analysis (Supplementary Video https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/8zx49bh5gb/1) showed that sham mice predominantly swam within the platform-containing quadrant, while ICH mice had difficulty locating the target. API treatment markedly improved performance in this task (Fig.  1 J). Compared to untreated ICH mice, API-treated mice showed a higher frequency of platform crossings (Fig. 1L) and increased time spent in the target quadrant (Fig. 1M). Swimming speeds were similar across all groups (Fig. 1 K), indicating comparable motor abilities. These findings suggest that API enhances spatial learning and memory in ICH mice, with dose-dependent effects on neurological recovery. BWC was measured at 24, 48, and 72 h after ICH to evaluate cerebral edema. Compared to the sham group, water content in the basal ganglia and cortex was significantly elevated at days 1, 3, and 7 post-ICH (Figs. 1G-I), whereas no significant changes were observed in the cerebellum, hippocampus, or brainstem. Notably, marked cerebral edema was observed in the basal ganglia and cortex on day 3 post-ICH, which was effectively alleviated by API treatment (Fig. 1H). Evans blue extravasation performed on day 3 post-ICH further demonstrated increased BBB permeability in the ipsilateral hemisphere of ICH mice. This disruption was partially reversed by API administration (Figs. 2 A-I). In summary, short-term neurological deficits, cerebral edema, and BBB disruption were most severe on day 3 after ICH, which was therefore selected as the primary endpoint for subsequent animal experiments.

  To further investigate the neuroprotective effects of API against ICH-induced SBI, we evaluated the expression of apoptosis-related markers, including Bax, Bcl-2, and cleaved caspase-9. To assess drug accumulation, serum and brain tissues surrounding the hematoma were collected 4 h after high-dose API administration and analyzed using targeted LC-MS. Results revealed that API was highly enriched in the blood and brain tissue, particularly in the peri-hematomal area (Figure S3). MAP2 immunofluorescence staining showed that high-dose API treatment reversed cavitary necrosis caused by ICH, preserving neuronal fibers that appeared “superficially disconnected but structurally continuous.” Compared with normal neurofilament organization, high-dose API treatment effectively preserved neurofilament quantity but displayed a more “disordered” arrangement, indicating partial retention of damaged axonal structures and suggesting ongoing neural regeneration (Fig. 3D). These findings support the hypothesis discussed in the introduction, suggesting that API promotes neurofilament preservation and regeneration [10]. Next, on day 3 after ICH, coronal brain sections from each group were subjected to H&E, Nissl, and TUNEL staining (Fig. 3 K). API treatment dose-dependently restored neuronal morphology, particularly in the basal ganglia region (Fig. 3 J). TUNEL staining demonstrated many apoptotic cells surrounding the hematoma in the ICH group. API treatment significantly reduced the number of apoptotic cells in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3L).

### API strongly co-localizes and tightly binds with JAK1, suggesting an irreversible covalent binding pattern between them
  API is directly bound to JAK1 and affects its phosphorylation. Immunofluorescence with biotin-labeled API showed colocalization with JAK1 in macrophages (Fig. 7A-B; Figure S9). Pull-down assays further confirmed the interaction (Fig. 7 C). In contrast, pre-incubation with API prevented subsequent JAK1 capture by API-beads (Fig. 7 J). In vitro kinase assays showed no significant effect of API on JAK2 activity (Fig. 7 K). We further performed molecular docking analyses of API with the kinase domains of JAK1 (RCSB PDB ID: 5E1E), JAK2 (RCSB PDB ID: 2B7A), and JAK3 (RCSB PDB ID: 3PJC) (Fig. 7L). The results revealed that API exhibited the lowest binding energy with JAK1 (–9.4 kcal/mol). The docking model indicated hydrogen-bond formation with GLU, LEU, and ARG residues, suggesting the strongest binding affinity. By contrast, the binding energies with JAK2 and JAK3 were markedly higher (–9.3 kcal/mol for both), implying weaker or less stable interactions.

### API binds to the kinase domain of the JAK1 protein, triggering a conformational change involving elevation of the leg and downward movement of the head
  To investigate the covalent interaction between API and JAK1, we conducted a detailed analysis of JAK1’s structural domains. The JAK1 protein comprises four domains: FERM, SH2, pseudokinase, and kinase domains. The FERM and SH2 domains are responsible for signal transduction through interactions with extracellular receptors. The pseudokinase domain enhances the catalytic activity of the kinase domain, while the kinase domain itself possesses a catalytic function, converting ATP to ADP and promoting JAK1 phosphorylation, thereby activating the JAK-STAT signaling pathway. Comparative analysis revealed that API exhibits the strongest binding affinity among the four domains to the kinase domain (Figure S10A; Figure S11A). This interaction is mediated by hydrogen bonds formed with amino acids such as Asp, His, and Glu, with binding scores showing consistent results (Figure S10A). This interaction explains API’s capacity to modulate JAK1 phosphorylation.

  Molecular dynamics simulation further revealed that the RMSD values of the backbone and side chains of the API-JAK1 complex and its components remained stable within 2.0 Å (Figure S10C-D). These findings indicate that the API-JAK1 kinase domain complex maintained a stable conformation under dynamic conditions. The time-dependent variation in hydrogen bond length within the small molecule-protein complex trajectory was also analyzed (Figure S10B). Molecular dynamics simulation demonstrated that API was deeply inserted into the cleft of the JAK1 kinase domain, causing a tightening of the entire complex. This insertion further led to an elevation of the left side chain and bending of the upper side chain, resulting in a “Leg-up” and “Head-down” conformation (Figure S10F). These API-induced conformational changes in the JAK1 kinase domain may be critical in regulating its catalytic activity. To further identify key amino acids involved in the API-JAK1 kinase interaction, we performed in silico mutagenesis (binding free energy computation) to identify crucial residues within the active site. The results showed that Gly887, His918, Asp921, Leu1024, and Lys908 were key residues mediating the interaction with API through hydrogen bond formation (Figure S11B-D). This finding is consistent with the molecular docking results. Although hydrogen bonding is a reversible non-covalent interaction, it represents the initial recognition step that may facilitate the subsequent covalent binding between API and the JAK1 kinase.


### The α,β-unsaturated carbonyl group in the API structure forms a thiol-covalent reaction with cysteine 1052 in the JAK1 kinase domain
  Structural analysis of API identified three critical features: (1) three phenolic groups, (2) one ether bond, and (3) an α,β-unsaturated carbonyl group. Molecular docking suggested that phenolic hydroxyl groups served as hydrogen bond donors, while the ether bond functioned as a hydrogen bond acceptor (Figure S10A). Given previous reports [46, 47], the α,β-unsaturated carbonyl was hypothesized to covalently interact with cysteine residues in the JAK1 kinase domain. To test this, JAK1 was incubated with API-beads in the presence or absence of β-mercaptoethanol (BME), dithiothreitol (DTT), or iodoacetic acid (IAA), a cysteine alkylating agent. API-beads failed to capture JAK1 when any of these reagents were present, indicating thiol competition (Figure S12A). CETSA revealed that API-induced thermal stability of JAK1 was significantly reduced after IAA treatment (Figure S12B-C), and DARTS showed that BME or DTT abrogated API-mediated stabilization, resulting in enhanced JAK1 degradation (Figure S12D). Consistently, BME or DTT impaired the inhibitory effect of API on JAK1 phosphorylation (Figure S12E). These findings highlight the essential role of the α,β-unsaturated carbonyl group in covalent binding between API and JAK1 cysteine residues.

  To explore structure-activity relationships, two derivatives were synthesized: catechin (CAT), partially lacking the α,β-unsaturated carbonyl, and naringin (NAR), completely lacking it (Figure S12F). Compared with API, CAT exhibited reduced suppression of JAK1 phosphorylation, whereas NAR almost completely lost inhibitory activity (Figure S12G). Pull-down assays further showed that API-beads precipitated JAK1 protein and that excess free API blocked this effect, while CAT and NAR failed to show similar activity (Figure S12H). Cytotoxicity assays demonstrated that Biotin-Linker-API and its derivatives exhibited no significant toxicity at the tested concentrations (Figure S13A-E). Furthermore, ATP measurements showed that CAT mildly reduced ATP levels, whereas NAR caused a marked reduction (Figure S13F).

  Analysis of the JAK1 sequence identified eight conserved cysteine residues within the kinase domain (Figure S12I). To determine their potential contribution, site-directed mutagenesis was performed to substitute each cysteine with alanine (Cys892Ala, Cys944Ala, Cys988Ala, Cys1052Ala, Cys1078Ala, Cys1116Ala, Cys1120Ala, and Cys1131Ala) (Figure S14A-H, Figure S15). Structural modeling suggested Cys1052 as the most strategically positioned for Michael addition with API’s α,β-unsaturated carbonyl group, lying in closest proximity to the reactive moiety. Site-directed mutagenesis of all eight cysteines to alanine confirmed this prediction: only the mutation of Cys1052 abolished API-JAK1 covalent binding (Figure S12J-Q). API inhibited phosphorylation of wild-type JAK1, but this effect was markedly attenuated in the Cys1052Ala mutant (Figure S12S). No comparable effect was observed on JAK2, and API-beads failed to pull down JAK2 protein (Figure S12K, R). Sequence alignment demonstrated that Cys1052 is highly conserved across species (Figure S12L).

  Functionally, mutation of JAK1 Cys1052 reduced the ability of API to suppress MMP9 expression in RAW264.7 cells (Figure S12U) and abolished its indirect neuroprotective effect against apoptosis in HT-22 neurons (Figure S12T). Live/dead staining further confirmed that supernatants from LPS-treated RAW cells carrying the Cys1052Ala mutation induced marked HT-22 cell death despite API treatment (Figure S12V).

  Together, these results indicate that API inactivates JAK1 through a Michael addition reaction, in which its α,β-unsaturated carbonyl group covalently binds to Cys1052 within the JAK1 kinase domain, a modification critical for API-mediated neuroprotection against macrophage-induced injury.


### Successful construction of RVG/FA-NPs@API targeting inflammatory macrophages
  An Apigenin nanodelivery system with central nervous system targeting and inflammatory microenvironment responsiveness (RVG/FA-NPs@API) was successfully constructed (Fig. 8 A). XRD analysis showed that NPs@API exhibited amorphous broad peaks at ~ 16.7° and 22.5°; FA-NPs@API displayed an additional peak at ~ 28.5°; and RVG/FA-NPs@API further presented new peaks at ~ 19.5° and 32.1° (Fig. 8B). 1 H NMR spectra confirmed the successful conjugation of FA and RVG29, with characteristic proton signals observed at δ 6.6–6.8 ppm and δ 8.6–8.7 ppm for folate, and at δ 7.2–7.4 ppm and δ 3.0–3.2 ppm for RVG29 amino acids (Fig. 8 C).

  TEM imaging revealed spherical, uniformly dispersed nanoparticles with clear boundaries (Fig. 8D). DLS measurements showed mean sizes of ~ 170 nm, 187 nm, and 195 nm, and Zeta potentials of −20.1 mV, −24.02 mV, and − 28.6 mV for NPs@API, FA-NPs@API, and RVG/FA-NPs@API, respectively (Fig. 8E-F). HPLC analysis indicated an encapsulation efficiency of 68% and a drug loading capacity of 7.5% for RVG/FA-NPs@API (Fig. 8G). Drug release assays demonstrated that API release was markedly accelerated under pH 5.5 compared with pH 6.5 and 7.4 (Fig. 8H), indicating acid-responsive behavior. Acidic microenvironments (pH < 6.8) are common in brain diseases, including glioma, inflammation, and ischemic stroke. In glioma and ischemic stroke models, extracellular pH has been reported to drop to ~ 6.5, and in severely ischemic or necrotic cores, even to ~ 6.0 [48]. Given hematoma mass effect, iron toxicity from erythrocyte lysis, and intense neuroinflammation, the pathological microenvironment of ICH is likely to reach, or even fall below, this level of acidification. Stability studies showed no significant change in particle size over 24 h in PBS or DMEM containing 10% serum (Fig. 8I), and RVG/FA-NPs@API remained stable for at least 60 days at 4℃ under light-protected conditions (Fig. 8 J). Collectively, these data demonstrate that RVG/FA-NPs@API possesses a stable structure, efficient drug loading, and responsiveness to the acidic microenvironment of ICH, providing a foundation for subsequent functional validation.

### RVG/FA-NPs@API exhibit favorable in vitro and in vivo biocompatibility and targeting capability
  In vitro and In vivo studies systematically evaluated the biocompatibility and targeting ability of RVG/FA-NPs@API (Figure S16A). CCK-8 assays showed no cytotoxicity toward RAW264.7 or HT22 cells (Figure S16B). In LPS-treated M1 macrophages, FITC-labeled RVG/FA-NPs@API displayed markedly stronger cytoplasmic fluorescence than NPs@API (Figure S16C), with flow cytometry confirming significantly higher FITC-positive rates (Figure S16D). Uptake was time-dependent and showed a clear preference for M1 over M2 macrophages, with fluorescence intensity ~ 2.5-fold higher, whereas non-targeted NPs@API showed no difference (Figure S16E). Receptor blocking with MVC or free FA significantly reduced uptake, with MVC exerting stronger inhibition (Figure S16E), indicating that RVG29 targets CCR5 while FA targets FR, jointly mediating M1-specific delivery.

  In vivo imaging demonstrated that ICH + RVG/FA-NPs@API mice exhibited markedly stronger brain fluorescence compared with ICH + NPs@API (Figure S16F). Distribution analysis revealed that RVG-NPs@API enhanced brain signals but accumulated in the liver, while FA-NPs@API mainly enriched in the liver and spleen with weaker brain signals. By contrast, RVG/FA-NPs@API showed the strongest brain fluorescence with reduced peripheral accumulation (Figure S16G). Flow cytometry further confirmed that the proportion of CD86 + Cy5.5 + M1 macrophages was highest in the RVG/FA-NPs@API group, exceeding that of single-ligand particles (Figure S16H). Together, these findings demonstrate that RVG/FA-NPs@API possesses excellent biocompatibility, robust brain-targeting capacity, and specific delivery to M1 macrophages, providing a strong foundation for targeted immunomodulation in ICH.


#### RVG/FA-NPs@API inhibit the macrophage CCR5/JAK1/STAT1/MMPs pathway and alleviate macrophage-Mediated neurotoxicity
  The mechanism by which RVG/FA-NPs@API regulate macrophage inflammatory states and influence neuronal injury was investigated using an in vitro three-step protocol: LPS-induced macrophage activation, RVG/FA-NPs@API intervention, and subsequent neuron co-culture (Fig. 9 A). In LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells, RVG/FA-NPs@API significantly reduced CCR5 mRNA and protein expression, as well as p-JAK1 and p-STAT1 levels, without affecting total JAK1 or STAT3. MMP9 and MMP3 expression was also markedly suppressed (Figs. 9B). TNF-α and IL-1β were significantly downregulated (Fig. 9D). Immunofluorescence revealed decreased iNOS and increased Arg1, indicating a shift from M1 to M2 polarization (Fig. 9E). In the Transwell co-culture model, neuronal viability was significantly increased when exposed to supernatants from RVG/FA-NPs@API-treated macrophages compared with LPS-treated macrophages (Fig. 9 F). Live/dead staining confirmed reduced neuronal necrosis and apoptosis, with preserved morphology (Fig. 9G). WB further demonstrated downregulation of Bax and upregulation of Bcl-2, NF200, PSD95, and GAP43 in neurons (Fig. 9H). Collectively, RVG/FA-NPs@API inhibited macrophage-derived cytokines and MMPs, promoted M2 polarization, and conferred neuroprotection by alleviating inflammation and preserving neuronal structural integrity.

### RVG/FA-NPs@API Alleviate SBI
  In the collagenase-induced ICH model, PBS, Apigenin, and PLGA@API were used as controls to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of RVG/FA-NPs@API (Fig. 10 A). In the Longa score, rotarod test, grid walking test, and forelimb placing test, both the ICH + Apigenin and ICH + PLGA@API groups showed significant improvement compared with the ICH group, whereas the RVG/FA-NPs@API group demonstrated further enhancement across all behavioral measures (Figure S17A-D). In the Morris water maze on day 35, mice in the RVG/FA-NPs@API group exhibited a significantly higher number of platform crossings and longer time spent in the target quadrant compared with the ICH + Apigenin and ICH + PLGA@API groups, indicating superior recovery of cognition and spatial memory (Figure S17E-F). Direct comparison of targeting strategies showed that dual-modified nanoparticles (RVG/FA-NPs@API) outperformed single-modified nanoparticles (RVG-NPs@API or FA-NPs@API) in all behavioral assays, while both single-modified groups were superior to non-targeted NPs@API but exhibited clear limitations (Figure S18A-F).

  Brain water content analysis revealed that Apigenin and PLGA@API reduced water accumulation in the basal ganglia and cortex, whereas RVG/FA-NPs@API provided additional reduction in cerebral edema (Fig. 10B). Histological analyses with HE and Nissl staining showed smaller hematoma volume, reduced tissue damage, and more intact neuronal arrangement in the RVG/FA-NPs@API group (Figs. 10C-D). TUNEL staining (green fluorescence) indicated the lowest proportion of apoptotic neurons in the RVG/FA-NPs@API group (Fig. 10E). Immunofluorescence further demonstrated stronger downregulation of iNOS (green) and upregulation of Arg1 (red) in the RVG/FA-NPs@API group, confirming a pronounced shift from M1 to M2 macrophage polarization (Fig. 10 F). Western blot results supported these findings, showing the greatest reduction in CCR5, p-JAK1, p-STAT1, MMP9, and MMP3, alongside marked upregulation of ZO-1 and CLDN5 in the RVG/FA-NPs@API group (Fig. 10G).

## Discussion
  Following ICH, peripheral blood enters the brain through ruptured vessels, leading to hematoma formation. Subsequently, immune cells from the peripheral blood migrate into the brain and differentiate into macrophages/microglia, actively participating in subsequent biological processes (BP). Due to the complex pathophysiology of ICH and safety concerns, no neuroprotective agents have yet demonstrated efficacy in clinical trials. This study highlights the promising therapeutic potential of the API, particularly in alleviating BBB disruption and reducing neuronal apoptosis caused by ICH. However, translating these findings from the laboratory to clinical application faces significant obstacles. APIs still encounter various limitations in clinical settings, including water solubility, biological half-life, and dose optimization challenges. Therefore, there is an urgent need for structural modifications and the development of new formulations. Moreover, APIs have shown multiple effects in various cell types, further underscoring their potential as multi-target therapeutic agents [49]. This study primarily focused on the functional role of API in macrophages, indirectly contributing to neuroprotection. Future research will explore the effects of API on other cell types to enhance its therapeutic potential in neurons.

  Over the past few decades, JAK has become an important target in drug development, particularly for treating immune and inflammatory diseases. In clinical trials, JAK inhibitors have been identified as effective agents for suppressing inflammatory responses [50]. Consequently, significant efforts have been devoted to identifying small-molecule inhibitors of JAK proteins for treating immune inflammation-related disorders [51]. JAK proteins comprise several functional domains: FERM (band four-point-one, ezrin, radixin, moesin), Src homology 2 (SH2), a pseudokinase domain, and a kinase domain. These domains are further divided into seven subdomains, referred to as JH1-JH7. JH1 and JH2 are located at the C-terminal end, while JH3-JH7 reside at the N-terminal region [14]. JH1 encodes the catalytic kinase domain responsible for ATP phosphorylation and is essential for kinase activity. JH2 is a pseudokinase domain lacking intrinsic enzymatic activity, but it plays a key regulatory role in modulating JH1 function. JH3 and JH4 contribute to maintaining the structural integrity of the kinase, while JH5, JH6, and JH7 are involved in modulating the activity of both JH1 and JH2. The interaction between JAK and its receptor is crucial [52, 53]. Cytokines—including IFNs, ILs, and growth factors—and their corresponding receptors serve as primary activators of JAK. Upon ligand binding, the receptor-ligand complex activates the receptor-associated JAK, catalyzing the phosphorylation of tyrosine residues on the receptor. This phosphorylation event triggers the recruitment of downstream STAT proteins, which are essential for mediating various biological functions [54].

  In this study, we were the first to identify and describe the role of API in promoting innate hematoma clearance and facilitating both short- and long-term neurological recovery in a mouse model of ICH. Further investigations revealed that the CCR5-JAK1-STAT1-MMPs axis was activated in the perihematomal region following ICH. It was found that API binds to the activated form of JAK1, leading to JAK1 kinase inactivation, inhibition of its phosphorylation, and suppression of downstream signaling pathways. These findings support the hypothesis that API selectively targets and inhibits the CCR5-JAK1-STAT1-MMPs axis, significantly improving SBI following ICH in mice.

  Identifying drug targets is critical for therapeutic development [55, 56]. To investigate the direct cellular target of API, we designed a small-molecule probe (API-biotin) based on the structure of API and found that it is effectively bound to the JAK1 protein. Interestingly, in the presence of API, this binding occurred irreversibly in a “first-in” manner. Subsequent molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations indicated that API formed a covalent bond with the kinase domain of JAK1. Notably, the binding of API to the catalytic domain induced conformational changes, including “leg-raising” and “hump-back” motions. These conformational transitions may be associated with the loss of JAK1’s catalytic kinase activity. Residues Gly887, His918, Asp921, Leu1024, and Lys908 were found to be critical in this binding process. Although our study demonstrated conformational alterations within the kinase domain upon API binding, it did not fully explore the effects on all four domains of JAK1 or define the precise structural changes within each domain. Nevertheless, these findings suggest that API primarily exerts its effects by modulating the structural domains of JAK1.

  We hypothesized that the chemical environment formed by interactions between the API molecule and amino acids within adjacent binding pockets plays a critical role in covalent binding. Based on previous literature [57], we focused on the unsaturated carbonyl group in API and the cysteine residues within the kinase domain of JAK1. Using strategies such as reducing α-unsaturation, alkylating cysteine residues, and performing alanine mutations on all eight cysteine residues within the kinase domain, our results identified the thiol group of Cys1052 as the covalent binding site of API in JAK1. This binding exhibited structural specificity, enabling API to recognize and attach to JAK1 selectively.

  Notably, our findings demonstrated that API selectively targeted JAK1 but not JAK2. This selectivity was attributed to a glycine residue at the corresponding site in JAK2, whose carboxyl group is unreactive toward API. Structural analyses of JAK3 and TYK2 further revealed that the corresponding sites contain glycine and histidine residues, respectively, neither of which meets the structural criteria required for stable binding with API. One limitation of this study is that, due to time constraints and other factors, we did not experimentally validate the API’s specificity against other JAK family members, such as JAK3 and TYK2. Nevertheless, to our knowledge, API represents the first small-molecule allosteric inhibitor that directly targets and modulates the Cys1052 residue within the kinase domain of JAK1 to block its phosphorylation. Notably, the Cys1052 residue was not previously identified as a druggable site for JAK1 inhibition.

  This study developed an RVG/FA-NPs@API, which was engineered for specific delivery to inflammatory M1-type macrophages. The system exhibited favorable characteristics in both in vitro and In vivo experiments, including uniform particle size, stable surface charge, and sustained-release behavior. It also demonstrated excellent biological and storage stability under various pH and FBS conditions. In a mouse model of ICH, RVG/FA-NPs@API showed substantial targeting accumulation at inflammatory sites and good biocompatibility. It significantly suppressed the expression of p-JAK1, p-STAT1, MMP3, MMP9, TNF-α, and IL-1β, promoted polarization from M1 to M2 macrophages, and enhanced neuronal viability and survival. Animal experiments further confirmed that this nanoparticle system effectively reduced hematoma volume, preserved the integrity of the BBB, and improved behavioral outcomes. Furthermore, integration with programmable light-switchable microcapsules may enable precise and customized drug delivery via microfluidics, providing a potentially valuable concept for programmable light-release systems and future clinical applications [58]. Nevertheless, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Long-term biosafety requires further validation, clinical sample verification is lacking, and certain signaling mechanisms remain incompletely resolved. In particular, the role of the CCR5/JAK1/STAT1/MMP axis in mediating BBB disruption and motor dysfunction has not been directly verified.

  This study still has several limitations. First, the findings are mainly based on a mouse ICH model, and differences in pathology and pharmacokinetics between animals and humans limit direct clinical translation. Second, only limited doses and treatment windows were tested, leaving the optimal dosage, safety profile, and long-term efficacy unclear. Third, the work focused primarily on macrophage CCR5/JAK1/STAT1/MMPs signaling, while the roles of neurons, astrocytes, and endothelial cells remain insufficiently explored. Fourth, although the RVG/FA nanoparticle system showed favorable targeting, large-scale production, long-term stability, and potential immunogenicity may pose challenges for clinical application.

  Future studies may be expanded in the following directions. First, validation in large animal models and preclinical trials is required to assess efficacy and safety for clinical translation. Second, single-cell sequencing and spatial transcriptomics should be used to comprehensively elucidate the effects of apigenin across multiple cell types and signaling pathways. Third, optimized treatment strategies, including dose gradients, therapeutic windows, and combination therapies, should be investigated to achieve maximal efficacy with minimal side effects. Fourth, the RVG/FA nanoparticle platform may be extended to deliver other drugs or genetic molecules for ICH and broader neurological diseases. Fifth, gene editing and clinical sample analyses should be employed to validate the critical role of the JAK1-Cys1052 site, while CCR5/MMPs or peripheral inflammatory markers may be explored as potential biomarkers to guide precision therapy.

  In conclusion, the RVG/FA-NPs@API nanoparticle system targeted the conserved cysteine residue Cys1052 in the kinase domain of JAK1, blocking its phosphorylation and inhibiting the downstream CCR5/JAK1/STAT1/MMPs signaling pathway. This resulted in anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects, significantly alleviating SBI following ICH. This study provides new insights and a potential therapeutic strategy for the intervention of SBI after ICH.


## Conclusion
  In this study, by constructing RVG/FA-NPs@API targeting inflammatory macrophages, we systematically validated their significant neuroprotective effects against SBI in a mouse model of ICH. The results demonstrated that RVG/FA-NPs@API effectively targeted inflammatory M1-type macrophages in the brain, significantly alleviated neurological deficits, reduced cerebral edema, and inhibited neuronal apoptosis. The underlying mechanism primarily involved downregulating the CCR5/JAK1/STAT1 signaling axis, which suppressed the expression of MMPs, stabilized the BBB and mitigated inflammatory responses and cytotoxicity.
