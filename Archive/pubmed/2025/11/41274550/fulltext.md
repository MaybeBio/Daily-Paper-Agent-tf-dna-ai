# DOCKGROUND: Expanding the resource to protein-DNA complexes


## Abstract
  Proteins and nucleic acids function by interacting with each other and with other molecules. Protein interactions with DNA are the basis of key cellular processes, such as replication, transcription, packaging, and DNA repair. To understand the mechanisms of these processes, it is important to know the structure of the molecular components and their interactions. Structures of protein complexes with nucleic acids are especially difficult to solve experimentally due to their greater instability. Thus, relatively few experimentally determined structures of such complexes are available. Computational modeling is essential for understanding biomolecular mechanisms and for the development of our ability to modulate them. Advances in the protein structure prediction field have shown that protein models can routinely reach unprecedented levels of near-experimental accuracy. In this context, modeling macromolecular interactions has become a focal point in structural biology. The Dockground project increases our knowledge of macromolecular interfaces and provides data resources for the development of techniques for structure-based modeling of macromolecular interactions. The resource, which contains protein-protein and protein-RNA complexes, has now been expanded to protein-DNA interactions. To advance the scope and functionality of the resource, the expansion includes new automatic weekly update procedures and user-friendly web interface for search, analysis and download of the protein-DNA complexes. The utility of the new release of the resource is illustrated by an example of benchmarking AlphaFold3 predictions. Dockground is publicly available at https://dockground.compbio.ku.edu.


## Introduction
  Protein-protein interactions and protein interactions with other biomolecules are essential for many biological processes. Protein interactions with DNA are the basis of key cell processes, such as replication, transcription, packaging, and DNA repair [1]. To understand the mechanisms of these functions, it is important to know the structure of the molecular components and their interactions. For example, high quality structures of DNA-binding proteins, especially those in complex with DNA, can provide valuable insights into the stereochemical principles of protein-DNA binding, including recognition of certain base-pair sequences and DNA modification upon binding [2, 3]. Insights into the structure of DNA-binding proteins is also important for treating diseases, such as cancer and neurological disorders [4, 5].

  Solving DNA structures experimentally, especially DNA in complex with proteins, is expensive and time consuming [6]. Protein-DNA structures can be routinely modeled computationally, using tools like protein docking and machine learning [7]. Protein-DNA structures have been modeled using docking procedures, such as pyDockDNA [8], NPDock [9], and HDOCK [10]. More recently, protein-DNA structures were successfully predicted by machine learning methods, such as AlphaFold3 [11] and RoseTTAFoldNA [12]. The machine learning-based modeling of macromolecules has become much more popular since the first release of the AlphaFold. As new versions are released and new machine learning methods developed, it is important to benchmark these programs on large representative sets of experimentally determined structures in order to accurately characterize the predictive performance. Good benchmarking datasets contain a large number of non-redundant structures with a broad scope of biological relevance [13].

  There are several published protein-DNA benchmark datasets [13–15]. However, such sets often are not updated and have relatively few structures. The new Dockground protein-DNA dataset, presented in this paper, is the largest, most comprehensive dataset of protein-DNA complexes available for use for benchmarking and training of machine learning procedures. It is regularly updated on a weekly basis with new structures from the Protein Data Bank (PDB). A full set with redundant structures, and the non-redundant versions are available for download. Users may also search the dataset for complexes which meet their search criteria and download the resulting subsets. To illustrate the use of the Dockground resource, we benchmarked AlphaFold3 procedure on a non-redundant version of the protein-DNA set (691 structures). The dataset is available on the Dockground website at https://dockground.compbio.ku.edu.


## Database Content and Description

### Database curation
  The Dockground protein-protein database was described earlier [16–19], along with its extension to protein-RNA complexes [20]. In this paper, we focus on further developments of the Dockground resource to incorporate protein-DNA complexes. For initialization of the protein-DNA dataset, similarly to the protein-protein and protein-RNA complex sets, all suitable structures from CIF biounit files in the PDB were added. New structures are incorporated on a weekly basis on Tuesdays, following the PDB’s updates on Mondays. In the extracted structures, we detect all interacting pairs of protein-protein and protein-DNA chains with at least one interface residue/nucleotide in each chain. In order to be considered, protein chains must have at least 30 residues and DNA chains - at least 6 nucleotides. Unlike protein-protein and protein-RNA complexes which consist of two interacting chains, a protein-DNA complex in the dataset consists of one protein chain and two DNA chains, which form the interacting double-helix with the protein. Two DNA chains are considered to be within the same helix if they have a greater buried surface area between them than with any other DNA chain in the structure. If a structure has single-stranded DNA (ssDNA), then the protein-DNA complex will consist of one protein chain and one DNA chain. Examples of proteins in complex with ssDNA include structures like 7lw8, an exonuclease, and 4tyn, a helicase. Table 1 shows the content of the database with protein-DNA complexes generated from the full PDB as of June 16, 2025.

  The following information is retained for each chain of a pairwise complex: chain ID (as in the PDB CIF assembly file) and biounit model number,type of chain (protein, RNA, or DNA),total number of residues/nucleotides (as in the PDB CIF assembly file),number of interface residues/nucleotides,buried solvent accessible surface area (as determined by the VoroContacts program [21]),range of interface residues (by sequence number, as in the PDB CIF assembly file).

  For the protein chains, we also include: UniProt ID(s) (as specified in the PDB CIF assembly file),canonical UniProt accession,sequence of the canonical UniProt accession,alignment of the SEQRES sequence of the PDB chain to the canonical UniProt sequence,number of domains (drawn from the Pfam/InterPro database [22]).

  Other annotations for each entry: species of origin,experimental method and its resolution (if applicable),multimeric state of the original PDB structure (including RNA and DNA chains),type of the complex (homomeric or heteromeric),PDB keywords and release date,presence of a membrane,presence of sulfide bonds and ligands at the interface.

### User interface
  The Dockground database main page (https://dockground.compbio.ku.edu/bound) provides a form to search the database for various complexes using either a single PDB entry code, or a set of filters, described in previous Dockground publications. For the DNA expansion, an additional filter was implemented to search for protein-DNA complexes alone or in combination with other complex types. The main page also lists available downloads for the various datasets from the database. The downloads are for the full and non-redundant sets of all complexes, protein-protein complexes, and protein-RNA complexes, along with the new full and non-redundant sets of protein-DNA complexes.

  Upon conducting a search from the main page, if filters are used, a table of relevant complexes is shown. If protein-DNA complexes are included in the search, the resulting table will list the “Complex Content” having protein and DNA, providing the name of the protein chain, and the two DNA chains which form the helix together separated by a slash (unless the DNA is single stranded). For example, if a protein-DNA complex has protein chain A, and DNA chains B and C, the table will list chain 1 as A, and chain 2 (or the second part of the interface) as B/C.

  Entry pages for a structure (which can be reached by clicking an entry on the search results table or by searching directly by PDB code) display the same information as in previous releases. The new information on the DNA chains is displayed similarly to protein and RNA chains, with the chain name, first and last nucleotide number, and the nucleotide sequence. From an entry page users can reach the page listing information on the entry’s interacting chains. For protein-DNA complexes, DNA chains which form a helix are listed together. To use the above example of protein chain A interacting with DNA chains B and C, interacting chains will be listed on the page as A/B/C with the type of chains Protein/DNA/DNA.

  As with protein-protein and protein-RNA complexes, visualization of the interface of protein-DNA complexes is provided using Jmol [23]. The visualization can be reached from a link either in the search results table or from the page listing the entry’s pairwise complexes. An example of the visualization page for a protein-DNA complex is shown in Figure 2. For a given set of interacting chains, the protein chain is shown in red, and the DNA chains in dark and light blue. Interacting residues in the protein are highlighted in green and interacting nucleotides in the DNA chains are highlighted in yellow. Any chains in the structure not involved in the displayed interface are in gray. The visualization interface was modified for all complexes so that, if the option is marked, the surface of the selected chains will be shown (previously only the surface of the interface could be displayed).


### Redundancy reduction
  Similarly to the earlier releases, redundancy reduction on the protein-DNA complexes was performed in two different ways, producing two types of non-redundant datasets. One type is based on the sequence-identity. MMseqs2 [24] was used to cluster protein and DNA chains together based on several sequence identity thresholds (30%, 40%, or 50% for proteins, and 70% for DNA). A sequence identity of 70% is used for DNA following its use in reduction of the protein-RNA dataset in the earlier Dockground release. We could not identify current publications that suggest a sequence identity cutoff for DNA structural similarity. Cutoffs for functional similarity were not considered since the Dockground protein-DNA dataset does not focus on function.

  Once the chains are clustered with their respective cutoffs, each interacting pair is re-clustered, so that each representative has at least one unique chain. For example, let us assume that chains A = B (e.g. chain A is equal/similar to chain B), C = D, and E = F, and the chains are paired as AC, AD, and AF. Then pairs AC and AD will be in the same cluster and AF in its own cluster. Since three different sequence identity cutoffs can be used for the proteins, there are three corresponding sequence-based non-redundant datasets.

  The other type of non-redundant dataset is based on structure similarity. Protein chains are clustered using Foldseek [25] according to TM-score 0.6 or 0.9, a common metric for characterizing structural similarity [26]. Currently there is no fast way to structurally align many nucleotide structures. So for protein-DNA complexes, DNA chains are clustered using the 70% sequence alignment cutoff. The clusters of interacting chains are generated as described above. According to the two different TM-score cutoffs used for proteins, there are two corresponding non-redundant datasets.


## Benchmarking Example with AlphaFold3
  To illustrate the utility of the protein-DNA dataset for benchmarking, AlphaFold3 was used to generate five models (as in a standard AlphaFold3 setup) for 691 non-redundant protein-DNA structures in the Dockground database (as of June 16, 2025). All structures used were deposited in the PDB after September 30, 2021, which ensured that they were not included in the training set for the AlphaFold3. The redundancy reduction of the set was based on the sequence identity with 30% cutoff for proteins and 70% for DNA. The 691 structures contained 2042 pairwise protein-DNA complexes. The DockQ program [27] was used to calculate the DockQ score, the interface root mean square deviation (i-RMSD) and the ligand root mean square deviation (L-RMSD) for each pairwise interaction. Each score was averaged across all five models for each interaction. The quality of the modeled interfaces was categorized in terms of CAPRI-like criteria as shown in Supplemental Table S1. The breakdown of the model quality by score is in Figure S2. The higher percentage of incorrectly modeled interactions assessed by i-RMSD compared to those assessed by L-RMSD suggests that AlphaFold3 modeling may yield an overall accurate structure with a less accurate or even possibly wrong interface between the subunits.


## Conclusion
  Dockground is a comprehensive resource for protein recognition studies including modeling of protein complexes. It contains a number of datasets for the development of computational methodologies. The Dockground resource has been expanded to include protein-DNA interactions. The database updates on a weekly basis to incorporate relevant new structures from the PDB. The future plans for Dockground development include adding new derivative datasets (unbound/modeled, docking decoys, and templates) for the protein-nucleotide structures, with regular updates, to maximize the resource utility for the research community in the rapidly evolving field of structural biology.
