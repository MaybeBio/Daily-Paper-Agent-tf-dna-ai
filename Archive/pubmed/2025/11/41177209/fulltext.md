# Sequence and supercoiling-dependent effects on the structural dynamics of DNA minicircles


## Abstract
  The degree of over-/underwinding of the DNA double helix, quantified by the superhelical density, is a key feature modulating critical biological processes such as gene expression and regulation. In fact, DNA molecules are able to channel the excess levels of mechanical stress into local defective and denatured states that are promptly detected by, e.g., transcription factors and nuclease enzymes. The occurrence and stability of these motifs are dictated by a complex interplay between topological and sequence-dependent effects, ultimately affecting the global conformational dynamics of the DNA molecule itself.

  Here, we characterize the impact of the sequence and of the superhelical density on the structural evolution of a 672-bp DNA minicircle via classical molecular dynamics simulations employing the coarse-grained oxDNA force field. We observe that moderately-to-highly undercoiled regimes are associated with the occurrence of stable, somewhat broad denaturation bubbles, typically co-localizing with flexible nucleotide sequences on the DNA minicircle. These defects are hardly reabsorbed by the system, thereby pinning the subsequent dynamics of the molecule. In fact, a similar behavior was recapitulated by enforcing “synthetic,”adjoining DNA mismatches, regardless of the underlying nucleotide sequence, suggesting an effective manner of DNA manipulation.


## Why it matters
  The degree of over-/undertwisting of a DNA molecule, expressed by the DNA supercoiling, modulates the structural and mechanical properties of the double helix and is reckoned to play a critical role in key biological processes such as DNA transcription and replication. Here, we explore how the dynamical interplay between supercoiling and sequence influences the occurrence and stability of local structural defects (i.e., “bubbles”) in a DNA minicircle via molecular simulations at a low level of resolution. We establish a general mechanism by which these structural defects—whether naturally occurring or artificially enforced—reshape the structural and dynamical behavior of a DNA molecule, thereby achieving significant insights for bio-/nanotechnology and synthetic biology.


## Introduction
  DNA holds the hereditary information for cells to replicate and develop, and adopts a variety of structural and functional frameworks varying across species. Key to this polymorphism is the degree of over-/underwinding of the DNA double helix about its axis, or supercoiling, which critically affects its structural compartmentalization as well as diverse cellular processes (1,2,3). In prokaryotes, negative supercoiling drives the compaction (4,5) and local denaturation of DNA, the latter modulating the expression of genes (2,6,7); similarly, supercoiling emerges in eukaryotic DNA from its packing about histone cores in nucleosomes, with topoisomerases regulating the degree of torsional stress within key processes such as DNA transcription and replication (8).

  The levels of DNA supercoiling are expressed via the superhelical density (σ):(1)σ=Lk−Lk0Lk0,where the linking number (Lk) denotes the number of crossings between the (oriented) strands of a closed, double-stranded DNA molecule—with Lk0 defining its reference value (associated with a torsionally relaxed conformation, i.e., σ = 0), which typically depends upon the thermodynamic environment (9). The Călugăreanu-White-Fuller theorem expresses the linking number as sum of two contributions, namely Lk = Tw + Wr, with the “twist” (Tw) quantifying the wrapping of the two strands of DNA about the axis of the double helix and the “writhe” (Wr) the coiling of the helical axis in space (10,11). In closed dsDNA moieties, the linking number is a topological invariant of the conformational ensemble explored by the system—and is conserved as such (3).

  Several efforts have been devoted to characterizing the spontaneous occurrence of DNA defects induced by excess supercoiling levels, and the mechanical and biological implications thereof. Coarse-grained, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of linear DNA molecules in a magnetic-tweezers setup have shown that force-mediated denaturation bubbles typically spawn at the tips of plectonemes (i.e., the apical loop of a coiled DNA structure) and consistently so at AT-rich stretches, acting as channels of stress release (12). DNA defects have been observed in regimes of zero external force alike, both by atomistic MD simulations (13) and by experimental assessments of circular DNA molecules (14,15,16). Yet, the dynamical interplay between topological and sequence-dependent features—which drive the nucleation of local DNA defects and contribute to reshaping the conformational ensemble of the DNA molecule—has been explored to a lesser extent.

  By means of classical MD simulations employing the oxDNA2 coarse-grained force field, here we characterize the impact of the sequence and of excess supercoiling levels on the conformational dynamics of a 672-bp DNA minicircle, as well as their influence on the likelihood and lifetime of denaturation phenomena. DNA minicircles are small, circular dsDNA molecules—less than a few hundred base-pairs (bps)—often employed as structural proxies to complex DNA topologies (1,17). On account of their reduced size and degree of correlation, the thermodynamical and mechanical behavior of DNA minicircles might be engineered by designing suitable sequences and adjusting the levels of superhelical density (15,16,18); in fact, these systems have been broadly characterized, both experimentally (14,15,16,19,20) and in silico (7,21,22,23,24,25).

  We observe that the amount of (negative) supercoiling significantly affects the likelihood of occurrence of DNA bubbles, with the nucleotide sequence modulating their stability and distribution. In line with earlier works, these defects are most likely associated with AT-rich stretches, on account of their inherent flexibility, and consistently co-localize with apical loops on the DNA molecule (12,26). Conversely, DNA bubbles at CG-rich stretches are short (typically involving the failure of a few base-pairings) and swiftly reabsorbed by the system.

  Furthermore, we verify that, although spawning stochastically and homogeneously along the minicircle, denaturation bubbles nucleating at AT-rich stretches often exhibit extended lifetimes—specifically so under highly negative supercoiling regimes—and exert an effective bias on the molecular structure, thereby “pinning” the conformational dynamics of the minicircle (in line with earlier experimental data (15)). This behavior is recapitulated by placing multiple adjoining DNA mismatches along the sequence: In fact, DNA defects as small as 5 bps disrupt the equilibrium dynamics of the minicircle by forcing the migration of (otherwise stable) supercoiled regions into apical loops, suggesting an effective manner of structural and topological DNA manipulation, e.g., enhancing such processes as DNA binding and recognition—as similarly inferred about the action of indirect (or shape) DNA readout complexes (21).


## Materials and methods

### System setup and simulation protocol
  The 672-bp circular DNA molecule employed in this work matches the template reported by Fogg and co-workers (15), who joined the sequence of a known 336-bp DNA minicircle in a tandem orientation (see Section 1 of the supporting material for the full DNA sequence and density of AT/CG pairings along the DNA template). In fact, the authors have inferred the distribution of locally defective motifs on the 672-bp moiety by tracking the digestion pattern of two nuclease enzymes—namely Bal-31 and S1—with the former recognizing isolated base-pairing defects and the latter binding largely denatured DNA stretches (15). Six degrees of superhelical density have been characterized, namely σ = −0.1, −0.08, −0.06, 0, 0.06, and 0.1—corresponding to a linking difference ΔLk = −6.4, −5.1, −3.8, 0, 3.8, 6.4, respectively. All simulations have been performed employing the oxDNA2 CG force field (27), taking into account sequence-specific hydrogen-bonding and stacking interactions. Ten independent MD replicates have been carried out for each level of superhelical density, at a physiological monovalent salt concentration of 0.15 M (which oxDNA2 models effectively via a Debye-Hückel electrostatic screening) and temperature T = 310 K, employing the GPU-accelerated, native oxDNA code (28,29). Particularly, a three-step MD protocol has been followed before each replicate:(1)The (perfectly circular) starting configuration of the DNA minicircle is generated via tacoxDNA (30). For each supercoiling regime, the proper topological constraint is enforced by fixing the corresponding value of σ, homogeneously distributing the twist imbalance along the DNA filament;(2)Subsequently, the DNA minicircle is subjected to a swift relaxation stage of 1 × 107 MD steps via the Bussi thermostat (31), associated with a timestep Δt = 9.09 fs (details of the conversion factor between oxDNA and SI units are reported in Ref. (32)). As suggested by the oxDNA developers, the correlation time and the timestep frequency of the thermostat have been set to 1000 and 53 MD steps, respectively. Moreover, at this stage, a modified version of the backbone potential (33) has been enabled, ensuring a higher stability of the system toward the large displacements of the CG sites;(3)A production run of 5 × 108 MD steps (∼4.5 μs)—with a sampling frequency of 5 × 105 MD steps—is thus performed starting from the equilibrated DNA conformation, employing the Langevin thermostat. The diffusion coefficient and timestep frequency of the thermostat have been set to 2.5 and 103 MD steps respectively, according to the recommendations of the oxDNA developers (32). The overall sampling time per replicate has been established upon the decay of the autocorrelation of the gyration radius of the DNA minicircle at the diverse degrees of superhelical density (refer to Section 2 of the supporting material for details).


### Structural analysis of the DNA minicircle
  Circular DNAs attain diverse configurations upon the seamless interconversion of twist and writhe, driven by the degree of superhelical density (34). In fact, the dynamical equilibrium of twisted and writhed conformations is fairly well reproduced by the oxDNA2 force field, whereby the DNA minicircles feature the sharply bent apical loops and highly interwound supercoiled tracts of the plectonemic coiling (see Fig. 1).Figure 1Plectonemic coiling of a DNA minicircle at superhelical density σ = −0.06, with the color bar defined upon the value of the (unsigned) local writhe about the i-th nucleotide. Two regions of topological significance are highlighted, that is, the apical loops and a broad supercoiled tract, the latter typically characterized by a highly interwound DNA bundle.

  The evolution of loops and supercoiled tracts along the MD replicates is tracked via the (unsigned) “local writhe” |ω(i,t)| (35,36) (or the local average crossing number, ACN (37)), defined as:(2)|ω(i,t)|=∑k=i−lwi∑l=ii+lw|tk,k+1(t)×tl,l+1(t)·(rk(t)−rl(t))|2π|rk(t)−rl(t)|3,with ri the coordinate vector of the i-th nucleotide pairing along the centerline of the DNA helix, ti,i+1 the tangent vector to the helical axis at location i, and lw = 150 bps—corresponding to the persistence length of DNA in solution. From this definition, it follows that a flat DNA segment centered at location i contributes zero to the summation in Eq. (2), whereas local maxima of |ω(i,t)| along the helix centerline are associated with highly writhed DNA tracts and/or apical loops. An example calculation of the local writhe on a DNA minicircle at σ = −0.06 (and the topologically-significant regions thereof) is shown in Fig. 1, whereas Fig. S4 of the supporting material depicts the time evolution of |ω(i,t)| along a MD replicate at σ = −0.06.

### Supercoiling and sequence-dependent effects on the dynamics of DNA minicircles

### Supercoiling and sequence-dependent effects on DNA denaturation bubbles
  As briefly mentioned earlier, the behavior shown by ⟨|ω(i)|⟩ at highly-negative regimes of superhelical density is accounted for by the occurrence of local denaturation bubbles—i.e., the metastable dissociation of the DNA helix driven by the local failure of the hydrogen-bonding interactions (see Fig. 3). We hereby define an effective free-energy difference ΔGBF, associated with the likelihood that each topoisomer spawns a denaturation bubble, as follows:(3)ΔGBF(σ)=−NAkBTln[fb(σ)fnb(σ)],with kB the Boltzmann constant, NA the Avogadro’s number, and fb and fnb the fraction of frames in the dynamics of a DNA minicircle exhibiting bubbles or the absence thereof, respectively.Figure 3(Top) Profile of the effective free energy ΔGBF(σ), associated with the propensity of the DNA minicircle to spawn denaturation bubbles as function of the superhelical density—with details in the main text and in Section 5 of the supporting material. A snapshot of a DNA minicircle at superhelical density σ = −0.1 exhibiting a DNA denaturation bubble is shown in the inset. (Bottom) Distribution of bubble sizes under negative supercoiling regimes, associated with values of ⟨Bs⟩ = 1.1 ± 0.6 (σ = −0.06), 2 ± 2 (σ = −0.08), and 7 ± 6 (σ = −0.1) failed base-pairings respectively.

  The likelihood of occurrence of DNA bubbles as function of the superhelical density and the free-energy profile ΔGBF are shown in Table S1 of the supporting material and in Fig. 3, respectively, illustrating the response of the minicircles subjected to the diverse supercoiling regimes.

  The inherent propensity of negatively supercoiled DNA molecules to yield denaturation bubbles is consistent with the observation that these topological regimes facilitate the strand separation of the double helix, thereby enhancing sequence recognition and DNA binding processes (38,39,40). Particularly, values of σ between −0.08 and −0.06 seemingly mark a transition between two thermodynamic regimes, that is, either favoring (σ < −0.07 (15)) or inhibiting the spontaneous occurrence of DNA bubbles. The behavior shown in Fig. 3 agrees with the experimental data of Fogg and co-workers, who observed that the topoisomers of the 672-bp minicircle exhibiting highly-negative supercoiling levels are cleaved promptly by Bal-31 (15). In fact, the frequency and size distribution of the DNA denaturation phenomena recapitulate the cleavage selectivity of the nuclease enzymes, with Bal-31 and S1 binding shorter (single-nucleotide) and larger (≥ 4-nucleotide) bubbles respectively (15).

  Conversely, under both zero and positive supercoiling regimes, the likelihood of denaturation events is lower and mostly ascribable to single-nucleotide phenomena. This is in line with the experimental data of Irobalieva and co-workers, based on the enzymatic activity of Bal-31 on 336-bps minicircles at Δ Lk = +3 (σ = 0.093) (16).

  As similarly inferred from the distribution and stability of loops and supercoiled regions, denaturation bubbles favorably co-localize with flexible sequences along the DNA centerline, and consistently so in moderately-to-highly undercoiled regimes. In fact, the occurrence of bubbles is broadly favored at AT-rich DNA stretches, as shown in earlier works alike (20,41,42), on account of their inherent capability to bear excess torsional stresses. A significant correlation exists between the (average) locations of apical loops and denaturation bubbles—as shown by Figs. 4 and S6 of the supporting material—in line with earlier AFM measurements (14,43), wet-lab experiments (15,16,44), and numerical simulations (12,41,45). Consistently, the distribution of denaturation phenomena peaks at diametrically opposed sites of the DNA centerline, that is, about nucleotides 300 and 600 (i.e., the tandem cleavage sites shown in Fig. S1). Of note, the off-diagonal datapoints shown in Fig. 4 and observed at highly-negative supercoiling regimes (σ ≤ −0.08) are associated with DNA bubbles spontaneously spawning about nucleotides ∼300 and ∼600 but not perfectly co-localizing with the apical loops closeby. By visual inspection of the trajectories, we verified that, if these bubbles are sufficiently stable, they drive the subsequent translocation of the loops along the DNA centerline (and about nucleotides ∼300 and ∼600), ultimately “pinning” the dynamics of the DNA minicircle.Figure 4Scatter plot highlighting the co-localization of apical loops and of DNA bubbles over all independent MD replicates, at varying degrees of superhelical density. The color code is associated with the local AT density upon a rolling average of 10 nucleotides. For the purpose of clarity, denaturation bubbles of size Bs = 1 are not shown; in fact, they are homogeneously distributed along the DNA centerline.


### Lifetime of DNA denaturation bubbles
  We have shown that the likelihood and stability of denaturation bubbles are markedly affected by the sequence and by topological constraints, favoring their co-localization with AT-rich DNA stretches and apical loops—and consistently so at highly-negative supercoiling regimes. This bears an impact on both the size distribution and the lifetime of the bubbles, as shown by Fig. 5 (as well as Figs. S7–S9 of the supporting material). In fact, their average dwelling times (⟨Δτ(i)⟩) vary significantly between the diverse regimes of (negative) supercoiling and along the DNA centerline, with the DNA bubbles co-localized with AT-rich stretches exhibiting the highest stability.Figure 5(Top) Evolution of denaturation bubbles along the centerline of a DNA minicircle, over a single MD replicate of the σ = −0.1 scenario. (Middle) Histograms showing the distribution of DNA bubble sizes within 1-μs time windows (TW) along the trajectory reported above. A color code is defined upon (the statistical mode of) the distribution of AT-density values co-localizing with bubbles of a specific size: smaller defects spawn stochastically along the minicircle, whereas broader bubbles co-localize consistently with AT-rich DNA stretches. For the purpose of clarity, all events involving the failure of single nucleotide pairings have been neglected. (Bottom) The average lifetime of denaturation bubbles about each nucleotide of the DNA minicircle, at diverse degrees of (negative) superhelical density. Values have been rescaled upon an experimental benchmark to take into account the kinetic bias enforced by the coarse-graining procedures—with details in the main text. For the purpose of statistical significance, the values of ⟨Δτ(i)⟩ have been grouped by windows of 50 nucleotides about a reference index i. Likewise, all events associated with the failure of single base-pairings have been neglected in the calculation.

  It is well acknowledged that the integration of multiple degrees of freedom associated with the coarse-graining procedure smoothens the free-energy landscape of a system (with respect to their atomistic counterpart), thereby enhancing its dynamics and favoring the transitions between thermodynamic basins (46,47,48). Moreover, the oxDNA force field is bundled with an implicit description of the solvent, thus lacking a proper characterization of hydrodynamic effects (49,50). In light of their kinetic implications, a rescaling of the time steps of the CG simulation is typically performed upon an atomistic MD benchmark or wet-lab experiment, under the approximation that the modifications induced by the model are quantifiable in terms of a global redefinition of the characteristic times of a system (46).

  By assuming that a DNA bubble might be described as a local melting/hybridization process, an additional rescaling factor of 3000 has been adopted (51), based on the agreement between the oxDNA simulations and the experimental rates of the DNA hybridization (27).

  Notably, our rescaled lifetime estimates are consistent with the values reported by Altan-Bonnet and co-workers, who inferred the characteristic timescales of bubble (un)zipping ranging between 20 and 100 μs (52) (see Fig. 5). Moreover, we observe that the shape of the bubbles seamlessly adjusts to the DNA sequence (i.e., with the bubble sliding across the centerline), broadening about AT-rich DNA stretches—as shown by Figs. S10 and S11 of the supporting material. This tallies with the work of Hwa and co-workers, who characterized the dynamics of twist-induced bubbles in random DNA sequences, showing that smaller defects are distributed ubiquitously along the DNA sequence, whereas larger bubbles co-localize consistently with AT-rich DNA stretches (53). Additionally, highly-negative supercoiling regimes favor the occurrence of larger defects that are hardly reabsorbed by the system, which accounts for their extended lifetime.


### Bubbles drive the structural dynamics of DNA minicircles
  We have described the process whereby denaturation bubbles affect the dynamics of DNA minicircles by migrating and co-localizing with apical loops. In fact, this was similarly observed in silico by Desai and co-workers, who inferred that DNA mismatches (MMs) beyond a specific size pin the dynamics of a DNA plectoneme—within a magnetic tweezer setup and under specific regimes of supercoiling, external force, and saline concentration (54).

  Here, we further characterize this phenomenon at a negative degree of superhelical density by enforcing (adjoining) mismatches upon a DNA minicircle at σ = −0.06 as “synthetic bubbles” of size Bs = 1, 2, 5, and 10 bps. To this effect, we have selectively replaced nucleotide pairings along the DNA sequence with “special” nucleotides (55) that retain all interactions of the oxDNA force field but the Watson-Crick hydrogen bonding (refer to the oxDNA documentation for details). We shall remark that, although mismatched nucleotides establish (weak) noncanonical interactions (56), we will hereby assume that their significance is negligible in this context. Moreover, to try to avoid any interference with spontaneous denaturation events, all synthetic bubbles have been placed on supercoiled regions of the minicircle showing an inherently low denaturation propensity in the equilibrated trajectories (see Table S2 of the supporting material).

  Overall, the impact of synthetic bubbles is proportional to the extent of the mismatch and affects the behavior of the DNA minicircle on both a structural and dynamical level. In fact, Fig. 5 shows that the propensity of DNA mismatches to migrate and co-localize with apical loops increases with the size of the defect (an example process is shown in Fig. 6).Figure 6(Top) A synthetic bubble of size Bs = 5 bps is enforced on the supercoiled region of a 672-bp DNA minicircle (characterized by a superhelical density σ = −0.06) and drives a concerted, structural adjustment, whereby the defect steadily migrates toward an apical loop. (Bottom) Time evolution of the (unsigned) local writhe about the centroid of a synthetic bubble BCM (and the distribution thereof), upon diverse MD replicates and bubble sizes Bs = 1, 2, 5, 10 bps. ## Discussion
  The degree of superhelical density is a critical feature driving the (mechanical, thermodynamical) stability of the DNA double helix and playing a variety of roles in the regulation, expression, and organization of the genome. In this work, we have characterized the structural dynamics of a 672-bp DNA minicircle subjected to diverse levels of supercoiling via a broad MD simulation campaign with the oxDNA2 force field. Specific efforts have been devoted to assess the correlations between the sequence, the topological constraints of the DNA molecule (defined by the superhelical density), and the likelihood and stability of local DNA defects, such as denaturation bubbles. In fact, the release of the excess mechanical stress associated with moderate-to-high levels of negative supercoiling occurs through local strand separation phenomena—induced by the failure of hydrogen bonds—triggering a global conformational adjustment of the DNA molecule in turn.

  Particularly, major denaturation events characterized by an extended lifetime spontaneously occur at σ = −0.08 and −0.1 and consistently co-localize with AT-rich DNA stretches at apical loops, thereby enforcing a bias on the conformational ensemble of the minicircle. To this concern, we notice that, despite the nucleation of bubbles being an inherently stochastic phenomenon, the degree of supercoiling and the sequence ultimately modulate the structural dynamics of the DNA minicircle.

  The influence of denaturation defects has been further verified by enforcing nonreversible, “synthetic” bubbles (as single or adjoining DNA mismatches) on the supercoiled region of the DNA minicircles. Notably, beyond a threshold bubble size ≥5 bps, these defects significantly bias the conformational ensemble of the minicircles, forcing the translocation of a target DNA stretch into an apical loop, regardless of the nucleotide sequence and the equilibrium dynamics underneath. In fact, a similar behavior is attributable to spontaneous bubbles alike, despite major denaturation events rarely spawning but at higher levels of DNA supercoiling.

  In conclusion, these observations emphasize the role of the dynamical interplay between the DNA sequence and topology, both as a critical factor in biological processes such as sequence recognition and DNA binding and as an effective manner of structural DNA manipulation by design.
