## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** The abstract presents a logically structured and policy-relevant approach to PFAS prioritization. The integration of computational, in vitro, and literature-based evidence within an IATA framework is timely and aligns with regulatory needs. However, the abstract alone does not provide sufficient quantitative or methodological detail to evaluate the robustness of the conclusions. Key claims regarding consistency across platforms, the specificity of receptor interactions, and the biological plausibility of the ranking cannot be verified from the supplied material. The work is potentially valuable, but the evidence base as presented is insufficient to establish the case.

Academic researchers in endocrine disruption, computational toxicology, and PFAS fate and effects would also find the comparative docking and assay data useful. Industry stakeholders involved in chemical substitution and regulatory compliance may use the ranking to inform product stewardship decisions.

The use of multiple docking tools and two OECD guideline assays adds methodological triangulation. The explicit integration of adverse outcome pathway concepts and a weight-of-evidence matrix is a strength, as it moves beyond single-endpoint screening toward biologically plausible ranking.

### Major Concerns

- **Concern ID** R1-M1  
- **Severity** Major  
- **Blocking** Yes  
- **Axis** Evidence sufficiency  
- **Claim pointer** The abstract claims that molecular docking using AutoDock Vina, CB-Dock2, and Endocrine Disruptome consistently revealed strong binding affinities of long-chain PFAS, particularly PFDA, PFOA, and 10:2 FTOH, to estrogen and androgen receptors.  
- **Evidence pointer** Abstract text; location not provided  
- **Concern** No docking scores, binding energies, or statistical comparisons are reported. The term "strong binding affinities" is not defined, and no thresholds or benchmarks are provided to distinguish strong from weak binding. The consistency across three tools is asserted but not demonstrated with data.  
- **Why it matters** Without quantitative docking results and predefined criteria, the claim of consistency and strong affinity cannot be independently evaluated. This is a central pillar of the prioritization, and unsupported docking claims undermine the entire ranking.  
- **Resolution test** Provide docking scores for all 10 PFAS against ER and AR, with standard deviations or confidence intervals, and define a priori thresholds for strong, moderate, and weak binding. Show correlation or agreement metrics across the three tools.

- **Concern ID** R1-M2  
- **Severity** Major  
- **Blocking** Yes  
- **Axis** Evidence sufficiency  
- **Claim pointer** The abstract claims that ER and AR transactivation assays (OECD TG 455/458) demonstrated ER agonist and AR antagonist activity for the same compounds (PFDA, PFOA, 10:2 FTOH).  
- **Evidence pointer** Abstract text; location not provided  
- **Concern** No assay results, potency values (e.g., EC50 or IC50), efficacy percentages, or statistical significance are reported. The claim of "activity" is qualitative, and it is unclear whether the observed effects meet OECD guideline acceptance criteria or are within a biologically meaningful range.  
- **Why it matters** The in vitro data are the functional confirmation of the docking predictions. Without quantitative results and evidence of assay validity, the claim of corroboration is unsubstantiated. This is critical for establishing the biological relevance of the prioritization.  
- **Resolution test** Report EC50 or IC50 values with confidence intervals, maximum response relative to positive controls, and statistical comparisons to vehicle controls. Confirm that assay performance met OECD guideline acceptance criteria.

- **Concern ID** R1-M3  
- **Severity** Major  
- **Blocking** Yes  
- **Axis** Methodological transparency  
- **Claim pointer** The abstract claims that all lines of evidence were systematically integrated into a weight-of-evidence matrix, enabling a transparent and biologically plausible ranking of PFAS.  
- **Evidence pointer** Abstract text; location not provided  
- **Concern** The weight-of-evidence matrix is described but not shown or described in terms of scoring criteria, weighting factors, or how conflicting evidence was resolved. The integration process is not reproducible from the abstract.  
- **Why it matters** The weight-of-evidence approach is the core methodological contribution. Without transparency on how evidence was weighted and integrated, the ranking cannot be audited or compared with other prioritization schemes. This is essential for regulatory acceptance.  
- **Resolution test** Provide the weight-of-evidence matrix as a figure or table, with explicit scoring rubrics, weighting rationale, and sensitivity analysis showing how rankings change under alternative weighting schemes.

- **Concern ID** R1-M4  
- **Severity** Major  
- **Blocking** Yes  
- **Axis** Evidence sufficiency  
- **Claim pointer** The abstract claims that short-chain and sulfonated PFAS (PFHxA, PFBS, PFOS) exhibited limited to no activity across platforms.  
- **Evidence pointer** Abstract text; location not provided  
- **Concern** The claim of "limited to no activity" is presented without quantitative data for these compounds. It is unclear whether "limited" refers to weak but measurable activity or activity below detection limits, and whether this was consistent across all three docking tools and both assays.  
- **Why it matters** The contrast between active and inactive compounds is central to the prioritization. If the negative results are not robustly demonstrated, the ranking may be biased. This is particularly important for PFOS, which has known endocrine effects in other studies.  
- **Resolution test** Report quantitative results for all 10 PFAS, including negative data, with detection limits and statistical power analysis. Clarify the criteria for classifying a compound as inactive.
