# Exploration of purine metabolism-related genes in the development of osteoporosis by integrated analyses and experiments


## Objective
  Metabolomics analyses suggest abnormal purine metabolism during the development of osteoporosis. This study aimed to investigate the role of purine metabolism-related genes in osteoporosis.


## Methods
  Three microarray datasets for osteoporosis were used in this study. Purine metabolism activity score was quantified by GSVA. Differential expression analysis and weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) were employed to screen dysregulated purine metabolism activity-related genes in osteoporosis. Three machine learning algorithms were employed to screen potential biomarkers, and a predictive nomogram based on biomarkers was then established. Drugs that target the biomarkers were predicted based on the DGIDB database. An ovariectomized rat model causing osteoporosis was established to validate the expression of biomarkers and the purine metabolism in vivo.


## Results
  Purine metabolism activity of osteoporosis samples was significantly different from controls. Based on differential analysis and WGCNA, 122 dysregulated purine metabolism-related genes were found, and they were mainly involved in immune inflammation-related functions. PDPK1 and FOXO3 were identified as potential biomarkers, and the nomogram based on PDPK1 and FOXO3 showed well performance to predict the onset risk of osteoporosis, with a clinical net benefit greater than PDPK1 and FOXO3 each alone. Two approved drugs, resveratrol and celecoxib, were predicted to target FOXO3 and PDPK1, respectively. Expression of FOXO3 and PDPK1 was enhanced in osteoporosis both in bioinformatics analysis and in vivo experiments. Rat model showed higher serum levels of xanthine and hypoxanthine as well as high level of uric acid.


## Conclusion
  FOXO3 and PDPK1 might be biomarkers and targets for the prevention and treatment of osteoporosis.


## Introduction
  Osteoporosis is a systemic bone metabolic disease characterized by reduced bone mass and damage to the microstructure of bone tissue, which results in skeletal fragility and thus greatly increases the risk of fractures [1, 2]. Osteoporosis has primary and secondary two forms, of which, postmenopausal and age-related osteoporosis are primary [3, 4]. Among people over the age of 50, approximately 10.2% are affected by osteoporosis, and which rate is estimated to enhance to 13.6% by 2030 [5]. Osteoporosis is more common in postmenopausal women [6], that is, postmenopausal osteoporosis, with osteoporosis-related fracture during their lifetime occurring in approximately half of these [7, 8]. The bone formation function of osteoblasts in bone tissue and the bone resorption function of osteoclasts maintain the balance of homeostasis within bone tissue. After estrogen deficiency, this balance is disrupted [9, 10]. The bone resorption capacity of osteoclasts exceeds the bone formation capacity of osteoblasts, leading to bone loss. This is the pathogenesis of postmenopausal osteoporosis [9, 11, 12]. Studies have shown that this imbalance is caused by oxidative stress resulting from estrogen deficiency. The oxidative stress state can induce the anti-apoptotic effect of osteoclasts and the pro-apoptotic effect of osteoblasts, thereby accelerating the rate of bone loss [13–15].

  Purines are a class of important metabolites that function as the basis of nucleotides and constitute the genetic material and energy units of organisms. Under the catalysis of xanthine oxidase, purines are gradually decomposed into uric acid. Purine metabolism disorders are widely present in various forms of osteoporosis [16]. Uric acid levels within the physiological range are positively correlated with bone density, even after adjusting for confounding factors [17]. Under normal levels, blood uric acid levels protect against osteoporosis through their own antioxidant capacity or by directly acting on bone metabolism-related cells [18]. Based on a cross-sectional study of 3465 participants, Chen et al. indicate that an increase in physiological uric acid levels is associated with 23–26% reduction in incidence of osteoporosis [19]. Nevertheless, there are noticeable destructive effects of blood uric acid on bones when its level exceeds the normal range [18]. Hyperuricemia inhibits the expression of osteogenic factors and induces bone erosion, thus leading to bone mass loss and destruction of bone microstructure [16].

  Furthermore, excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) is generated during the process of purine metabolism [16]. ROS itself and its derived inflammatory factors not only directly attack osteoblasts and induce cell apoptosis, but also promote the differentiation of osteoclasts [20–22]. Based on a large Asian cohort, Li et al. proposed that hyperuricemia was linked to a reduced risk of osteoporosis in women but not in men, and such protective effects were lost when serum uric acid level exceeded 8 mg/dL [23]. Therefore, the disorder of purine metabolism may be the fundamental cause for the generation of large numbers of free radicals in the female body after menopause, which in turn leads to an imbalance in the bone remodeling process and causes osteoporosis.

  Two bioinformatics analyses of metabolites demonstrate the involvements of purine metabolism disorders in the development of osteoporosis [24, 25]. However, the role of genes related to purine metabolism in the development of osteoporosis has not yet been studied. To fill this research gap, we conducted this study to investigate the potential of purine metabolism-related genes as biomarkers in osteoporosis. Besides, we established a rat model of postmenopausal osteoporosis by means of bilateral ovariectomy to validate the main findings from bioinformatics analysis.


## Methods

### Data sources and data preprocessing
  Microarray datasets for osteoporosis, including GSE7158, GSE56815 and GSE13850, were downloaded from the gene expression omnibus (GEO) database. Data in both GSE7158 (including 12 osteoporosis and 14 control samples) and GSE56815 (including 40 osteoporosis and 40 control samples) was generated on the GPL570 platform. The data in these two datasets were derived from the blood monocytes, which are precursors of osteoclasts and can secrete various cytokines involved in the differentiation, activation, and apoptosis of osteoclasts [26, 27]. Using R package sva (version 3.50.0) to eliminate batch effects, these two datasets were merged as a larger dataset, termed as training set. GSE13850 contained 20 osteoporosis and 40 control samples, which was used as an external validation set. Besides, 163 purine metabolism-related genes (PMRGs) were retrieved from a previous study [28].


### Purine metabolism activity score
  Single sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) is a method commonly used to estimate the relative enrichment of each gene set in that sample by comparing the gene expression data of each sample with a specific gene set, thus reflecting the active state of cells or tissue in a specific biological process. In this study, we quantified the purine metabolism activity of each sample into specific score with the aids of ssGSEA method. Briefly, based on the gene set of 163 PMRGs, ssGSEA was conducted utilizing the “ssgsea” function provided in R package GSVA to obtain enrichment score for each sample, that is purine metabolism activity score. Subsequently, difference on the score between osteoporosis and control samples was analyzed by Wilcoxon test.


### Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)
  WGCNA is a system biology algorithm frequently used to screen genes with highly similar expression patterns by analyzing the coordinated change patterns among genes from large-scale data (such as gene expression profiles or RNA-Seq data), and to further explore the gene sets related to specific diseases or phenotypes [29]. Highly correlated gene modules related to purine metabolism activity in osteoporosis were identified utilizing R package WGCNA (version 1.72–5.72). Specifically, a scale-free network was constructed, and an appropriate soft threshold power (the correlation coefficient square values of connection degree k and p(k) reached 0.85) was determined to ensure the connections between genes in the network following the scale-free network distribution. Subsequently, gene hierarchical clustering and dynamic tree cutting (minmoduleSize setting as 100) were employed to cluster the genes into highly correlated gene modules. Afterwards, correlations between each gene module and trait (purine metabolism activity score) were calculated to identify key gene modules with cut-off values of |r|> 0.3 and P < 0.05.


### Candidate genes associated with purine metabolism in osteoporosis
  Differential expression analysis across osteoporosis and control samples was conducted utilizing R package Limma, and the genes with P < 0.05 and |log2FC|> 0.2 were defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in osteoporosis. The overlapped genes between the module genes screened by WGCNA and DEGs were screened by Venn analysis. The involved gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways of these overlapped genes were enriched using R package clusterProfiler (version 4.4.4). Besides, the protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed for these overlapped genes based on the predicted interactions (score > 0.4) in STRING database. The genes with interactions with others in the PPI network were considered as candidate genes.


### Machine learning for screening feature genes
  From the above candidate genes, feature genes were further screened based on three machine learning algorithms. LASSO is a generalized linear model that reduces model complexity and overfitting by shrinking coefficients of insignificant variables to zero through introducing L1 regularization term. R package glmnet (version 4.1–8.1) was employed to conduct LASSO analysis with tenfold cross-validation and 1000 iterations to select feature variables based on the minimum criteria (lambda.min). SVM-RFE is a feature selection method based on support vector machines (SVM), which utilizes recursive feature elimination (RFE) to progressively eliminate the features with the smallest weight. SVM-RFE was performed with fivefold cross-validation utilizing R package e1071 (version 1.7–14.7), and the feature genes in model with highest accuracy and lowest error rate were selected. XGBoost, an extreme gradient boosting method, was performed to screen feature genes using R package SHAPforxgboost (version 0.1.3) in this study. XGBoost model sorted feature genes according to the extent to which feature genes contribute to the model output, and the feature genes with larger contributions were selected. The shared feature genes identified by all three algorithms were selected.


### Screening and validation of potential biomarkers
  Expression of the identified feature genes was validated in GSE13850 validation set, and the genes with the same expression trend in both training and validation sets were considered as potential biomarkers in osteoporosis. The top 20 interacting genes with the biomarkers and their involved functions were predicted utilizing GeneMANIA website. The subcellular localization of the biomarkers was investigated by utilizing the mRNALocater database. Besides, the drugs that target the biomarkers were predicted based on the DGIDB database.


### Molecular docking
  To confirm the binding of key drugs to biomarkers, we implemented molecular docking analysis. Briefly, the protein accession numbers of biomarkers were obtained based on Uniport database, and their crystal structure (PDB format) was further downloaded from the PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org/). The three-dimensional (3D) structures (SDF format) of the key drugs were acquired from the PubChem database. Subsequently, CB-Dock2 (http://183.56.231.194:8001/cb-dock2/) was employed to simulate molecular docking.


### Establishment of a clinical predictive nomogram
  To easily use the biomarkers in clinic, we established a nomogram using R package rms (version 6.7-1.7) based on the identified biomarkers to predict the risk of osteoporosis. The accuracy and power of the nomogram were evaluated by the plotting decision curve analysis (DCA) and calibration curve.


### Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)
  GSEA was conducted to explore the signal pathways affected by the dysregulation of biomarkers in osteoporosis. Specifically, the correlation between biomarkers and the other genes in the training set was calculated, and these genes were sorted by the correlation coefficients. Using the sorted results, GSEA was conducted based on the KEGG gene set “c2.cp.kegg_medicus.v2024.1.Hs.symbols” in MSigdb database, and the significantly enriched pathways were screened by P < 0.05 and |normalized enrichment score (NES)|> 1.


### Establishment of ovariectomized rat model causing osteoporosis
  Female Sprague–Dawley rats (Aged 6–7 weeks, weighted 250 ± 20 g) were obtained from the Beijing HFK Bioscience Co., Ltd (Beijing, China), and were raised in a controlled room with a temperature of 22 ± 3 °C, a humidity of 55–75%, and a light/dark cycle of 12 h. All rats were given adaptive feeding for 1 week, and then were assigned into sham-operated (sham) group and model (OVX) groups. We established an ovariectomized rat model causing osteoporosis by means of bilateral ovariectomy. Specifically, rats were anesthetized by tail vein injection of 0.5 g/100 g of Zoletil. A longitudinal incision of approximately 1.5 cm was made 1.5 cm below the rib. Subsequently, the abdominal cavity and the kidneys were exposed after separating the subcutaneous tissue. After separating the fatty tissue below the kidneys, ovaries were exposed, and the ovaries were completely removed. Incision was then sutured using absorbable sutures. For rats in sham group, longitudinal incision, ovaries exposure and suture were conducted as the same methods of model group, in exception of ovaries removal. To avoid infection, all rats were intramuscularly injected with penicillin G for 3 consecutive days after surgery, and wounds were coated with erythromycin ointment. Postoperative recovery was allowed for 8 weeks. Subsequently, rats were sacrificed after general anesthesia, and the femoral tissues were collected for further experiments. Blood samples were collected from orbital, and serum uric acid was measured by automatic analyzer.


### Micro computed tomography (micro-CT)
  After fixing the collected femoral samples with 4% paraformaldehyde, the microstructure of rat femurs was scanned utilizing micro-CT on a SkyScan1176 system (BRUKER, USA). The femurs microstructure parameters were determined for bone microstructure analysis, including bone mineral density (BMD, mg/cm2), trabecular bone volume fraction (bone volume/total volume, BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb. Th, mm), trabecular separation (Tb. Sp, mm), trabecular number (Tb. Num, 1/mm), and trabecular connectivity density (Conn. dens, 1/mm3).


### Hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining
  For histological assessment, femoral samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, and were decalcificated with 10% ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA) for 4 weeks. Afterwards, the samples were embedded in paraffin, and were prepared into sections. Paraffin sections were dewaxed and hydrated routinely, and the sections were stained by hematoxylin and eosin in accordance with the manual of the Hematoxylin and Eosin Staining Kit (#C0105S, Beyotime).


### RNA extraction and qRT-PCR
  After adding liquid nitrogen, the femurs were ground into powder. Total RNAs from femurs were then isolated by Trizol reagent, followed by determination of RNA concentration and purity. Then, RNAs were reverse transcribed into cDNA. PCR amplification was then carried out with the aids of SYBR Green PCR Master Mix under reaction conditions: 95 °C for 10 min and 40 cycles of 95 °C for 12 s and 60 °C for 40 s. The used primers were as follows: PDPK1-F 5′-ACTTCCACCATTCAGAGCCG-3′, PDPK1-R 5′-GCCAAACTGGCTCAGGAGAT-3′; FOXO3-F 5′-CGAGGAGGACGACGATGAAG-3′, FOXO3-R 5′-TCGTTCTGAACCCGCATGAA-3′; GAPDH-F 5′-GACATGCCGCCTGGAGAAAC-3′, GAPDH-R 5′-AGCCCAGGATGCCCTTTAGT-3′ species: rat.


### Western blot
  After adding liquid nitrogen, the femurs were ground into powder. Then, lysis buffer and proteinase inhibitor were added to the bone tissue powder for protein extraction. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C, supernatant was collected, that is total proteins. Total proteins were quantified utilizing BCA assay. After electrophoretically separation, proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes, followed by membranes blocking. Subsequently, primary antibodies: RUNX2 (1:500, #PA5-86506, Thermo Fisher), OPN (1 μg/mL, #PA5-34579, Thermo Fisher), COL1A1 (0.5 μg/mL, #PA5-95137, Thermo Fisher), PDPK1 (1:500, #PA5-99497, Thermo Fisher), FOXO3 (1 μg/mL, #720128, Thermo Fisher), and GAPDH (1: 1000, #ab181602, Abcam), were added to co-incubate with the membranes overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies for 60 min at dark. Ultimately, the blotting bands were visualized by ECL reagent followed by film exposure.


### Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC–MS)
  Level of xanthine and hypoxanthine in serum was detected by LC–MS. Analysis of standard substances for hypoxanthine (HPLC ≥ 98%, CAS: 68–94-0; #B20211) and xanthine (HPLC ≥ 98%, CAS: 69–89-6; #B20561) were purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The standard substances were prepared into solution (18.052 μg/mL and 18.077 μg/mL, respectively) and were mixed to obtain working solution. The whole blood samples were stand left for two hours, and serum samples were collected. After centrifugation at 3000 rpm/min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected for analysis. Both standard substance solutions and samples were filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane filter before injection. The system injection volume was 0.4 μL. Separation was performed utilizing Agilent Eoltpse PlusC18 RAHD chromatographic column (2.1*50 mm) at a column temperature of 30 °C and a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. Mobile phases A and B were 0.1% (v/v) formic acid aqueous solution and acetonitrile. Mass Spectrometer (MS) was operated for quantification of xanthine and hypoxanthine, with the MS conditions set as follows: Gas temperature: 325 °C, Gas flow: 6 L/min, Nebulizer: 40 psi, Sheath gas temperature: 350 °C, Sheath gas flow: 11 L/min, Capillary: 4000 v, and Nozzle voltage: 500 v.


### Data statistics
  All bioinformatics analyses were conducted utilizing R software (version 4.2.2). All experimental data were displayed as mean ± standard deviation, and were analyzed and graphed using the GraphPad software (version 7.0). Unpaired t test was employed for comparison between two groups, with P < 0.05 showing the statistical significance.

### Candidate genes associated with purine metabolism in osteoporosis
  To screen purine metabolism activity-related genes, we performed WGCNA. Soft threshold power of 5 was determined where r2 reached 0.85 for the first time (Fig. 1c). A total of 8 gene modules were obtained (Fig. 1d), of which three modules showed significant correlations with purine metabolism activity score (Fig. 1e), including “brown” module (r = 0.374, P < 0.001), “yellow” module (r = − 0.377, P < 0.001), and “turquoise” module (r = − 0.462, P < 0.001). These three modules contained a total of 7941 genes. Besides, we screened the DEGs in osteoporosis by differential analysis (Fig. 1f, g), and 275 DEGs (129 up- and 146 down-regulated genes) were obtained with the cut-off values of P < 0.05 and |log2FC|> 0.2. Among 7941 module genes and 275 DEGs, 122 overlapped genes were screened by Venn analysis (Fig. 1h), which were considered as candidate genes associated with purine metabolism in osteoporosis.

Similarly, KEGG pathways, such as Th1 and Th2 cell differentiation, IL–17 signaling pathway and antigen processing and presentation, were also enriched by these 122 genes (Fig. 2b). The PPI network contained 81 nodes and 125 interaction edges, indicating that among the 122 genes, 81 genes interacted with each other (Fig. 2c). ### FOXO3 and PDPK1 identified as biomarkers in osteoporosis
  Expression of these five genes were analyzed. Expression of ACTC1, CPNE1 and TFPI was down-regulated, while expression of FOXO3 and PDPK1 was up-regulated in osteoporosis (Fig. 4a). In GSE13850 external validation set, expression of FOXO3 and PDPK1 was up-regulated in osteoporosis, which was completely with the expression pattern in training set (Fig. 4b). Therefore, FOXO3 and PDPK1 were selected as potential biomarkers in osteoporosis. ROC curves indicated that these two genes show well power for predicting the risk of osteoporosis, with an AUC of 0.809 for FOXO3 and an AUC of 0.81 for PDPK1 (Fig. 4c). In GSE13850 validation set, the AUC for FOXO3 and PDPK1 was 0.698 and 0.682, indicating a moderate predictive performance (Fig. 4d). For convenience of clinical use, we established a predictive nomogram based on FOXO3 and PDPK1 (Fig. 4e), which showed well predictive performance (C-index = 0.859). Calibration curve showed a high coherence between predictive probability and actual probability (Fig. 4f). DCA curve revealed that the clinical net benefit of predictive nomogram was greater than that of FOXO3 and PDPK1 each only (Fig. 4g). ## Discussion
  Purine metabolism disorders are present in different forms of osteoporosis [24, 25]. Purine metabolic disorder disrupts the balance of bone remodeling through the “uric acid-ROS-inflammation” axis, and is one of the important metabolic mechanisms for the occurrence of osteoporosis [16]. Restoring purine metabolism (such as activating HPRT1) may become a new strategy for the treatment of osteoporosis in the future.

  In this study, we systematically analyzed the expression and function of genes related to purine metabolism in osteoporosis. We firstly observed the dysregulation of purine metabolism activity in osteoporosis patients compared to that of controls, which was consistent with the findings in bioinformatics analyses of metabolites in osteoporosis [24, 25]. WGCNA is a systems biology approach used to identify co-expressed gene modules from high-throughput transcriptome data, and to further explore the relationships between these modules and specific sample traits, which has been widely used for studies to screen specific gene modules in multiple diseases [30–32]. Based on WGCNA and differential expression analysis, we screened 122 genes that dysregulated in osteoporosis and associated with the purine metabolism activity in osteoporosis. These genes were mainly involved in immune inflammation-related biological processes, such as regulation of inflammatory response, positive regulation of cytokine production, and leukocyte mediated immunity. This could be explained by the “uric acid-ROS-inflammation” axis. Specifically, the damage to bone tissue caused by purine metabolism mainly results from the excessive production of terminal uric acid and ROS during the decomposition process [16]. Uric acid shows both antioxidant and pro-oxidant effects, which relies on the microenvironment [33]. The accumulation of ROS mediates the pathological state of oxidative stress and inflammation, which is the main pathogenic factor for purine disorders that cause bone imbalance [16].

  Further machine learning algorithms and expression validation identified PDPK1 and FOXO3 as two potential biomarkers in osteoporosis. PDPK1 (3-phosphoinositidedependent protein kinase 1, also termed PDK1) has been extensively studied in the fields of oncology [34–36], but there are relatively few research reports in the field of orthopedics. As an important gene in the PI3K/AKT pathway, PDK1 can regulate multiple signal processes through activating AKT by phosphorylation [37]. PDK1 was reported as a positive modulator for the differentiation and function of osteoclasts [38]. PDK1 specific inhibitor BX-912 was found to repress the generation and differentiation of osteoblasts by activating the phosphorylation of AKT at Thr308, thereby increasing the number of osteoclasts and leading to the occurrence of osteoporosis [39]. Both in vitro activation of PDK1 by PDK1 activator PS48 and adenovirus-mediated in vivo delivery of PDK1 were found to reverse the inhibition of osteogenic activity of osteoblasts in models of glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis, which was achieved by regulating the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway [40]. Forkhead box class O (FOXOs) proteins are evolutionarily conserved transcription factors, including FOXO-1, −3, −4 and −6. Among them, FOXO-1, −3 and −4 are widely expressed in osteocytes and regulate osteocyte function and intercellular signal transduction [41]. Dysfunction of FOXOs may result in multiple bone diseases, including osteoporosis [41]. Li et al. indicated that Zuogui Wan could prevent bone loss and improve the ovariectomy-induced osteoporosis by elevating the expression of FOXO3 [42]. Zhang et al. proposed that expression of FOXO3 was reduced in osteoclasts in the context of osteoporosis, which leaded to an accumulation of ROS, thereby enhancing the generation of osteoclasts and bone resorption [43]. The deficiency of FOXO3 could facilitate the systemic bone loss, while the restoration of FOXO3 expression could reverse such bone loss in animal model, indicating that FOXO3 is a potential therapeutic target for alleviating bone loss in osteoporosis [43]. Besides, another study suggested that in senile osteoporosis condition, FOXO3 was GlcNAcylation modified by glutamine synthetase at the Ser296 site, which could enhance the stability of FOXO3, reduce oxidative stress, and thereby promote osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), while the absence of this GlcNAcylation modification could weaken the osteogenic potential of BMSCs [44].

  The above studies demonstrated the importance of PDPK1 and FOXO3 in osteoporosis, suggesting the potential of these two genes as biomarkers in osteoporosis. For convenience of clinical use, we established a predictive nomogram based on these two genes, which showed well performance to predict the onset risk of osteoporosis. The clinical net benefit of the nomogram was greater than PDPK1 and FOXO3 each alone. Besides, we further predicted the drugs for targeting PDPK1 and FOXO3, and two approved drugs, including resveratrol and celecoxib were predicted. Celecoxib is a selective cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) inhibitor, which can inhibit the differentiation and activity of osteoclasts and reduce bone resorption in ovariectomized mice [45], indicating that celecoxib might be useful for preventing estrogen deficiency-caused bone loss. In rat model with short-term celecoxib administration (for 6 days), it was observed that bone formation increased, bone resorption decreased, and the bone trabecular structure improved (with an increase in bone volume fraction BV/TV) [46]. Resveratrol, a natural polyphenol compound, has been reported to have well therapeutic effects on osteoporosis in animal experiments. A meta-analysis of preclinical studies suggested that resveratrol could markedly enhance the BMD of osteoporosis rat models [47]. A randomized, placebo-controlled trial indicated that resveratrol supplementation for 12 months could observably increase the BMD in the lumbar spine and neck of femur, while it decreased the level of bone resorption marker, CTX-1 of postmenopausal women [48]. The regulation of these drugs on PDKD1 and FOXO3 in the context of osteoporosis should be further investigated in future studies.

  Several limitations should be admitted. We identified PDKD1 and FOXO3 as potential biomarkers and targets for the prevention and treatment of osteoporosis and further explored the expression of these genes by in vivo experiments. However, the exact functional effects of FOXO3 and PDPK1 on osteogenic and osteoclastic differentiation as well as purine metabolism should be further explored by a series of functional experiments. In addition, the biomarker potential of PDKD1 and FOXO3 should be validated based on serum samples analysis of large-scale of clinical samples.

  In summary, to best our knowledge, this study investigated the roles of purine metabolism-related genes in osteoporosis for the first time. Dysregulated purine metabolism-related genes in osteoporosis were primarily involved in inflammation-related processes, which suggested that purine metabolism disorder might damage osteocyte and disrupt the balance of bone homeostasis. Two genes, PDKD1 and FOXO3, were identified as potential biomarkers and targets for the prevention and treatment of osteoporosis. Overall, this study contributed to understanding the roles of purine metabolism in the development of osteoporosis.
