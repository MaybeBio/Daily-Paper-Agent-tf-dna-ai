# Identification of lactylation-related biomarkers in HNSC by integrating machine learning and Spatial transcriptomics analysis


## Objective
  Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC) is a common malignant tumor with a 5-year survival rate of less than 60% and lactylation modification plays a key role in its occurrence and progression. This study aims to identify key lactylation-related genes (LRGs) in HNSC and their prognostic impact by combining machine learning methods and spatial transcriptomics analysis.


## Methods
  High-throughput gene expression data of HNSC were obtained from the TCGA database. The “Limma” R package was used to screen differentially expressed genes (DEGs), and their biological significance was investigated through GO and KEGG functional enrichment analyses. Subsequently, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was employed to identify disease trait-associated modules. We used 12 machine learning methods to screen for key genes and validated them in the GSE6631 dataset. Survival curves were plotted to evaluate the prognostic ability of key genes. Transcriptional regulation analysis via the “RcisTarget” package identified transcription factors associated with motifs exceeding NES  3.5. Single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analysis was performed, which involved dimensionality reduction, clustering via UMAP, cell type annotation and the plotting of spatial expression distribution maps of key genes. Finally, for immune infiltration analysis, the CIBERSORT algorithm was used to calculate the proportions of 22 types of immune cells, and then the correlations between key genes and immune-related genes were analyzed.


## Results
  Our study identified three key LRGs (MSN, KIF2C, and RFC4), which exhibited strong diagnostic performance, with AUC values of 0.986 for KIF2C, 0.964 for RFC4, and 0.89 for MSN in the TCGA-HNSC cohort. These findings were validated in the GSE6631 dataset. Western blotting showed a significant upregulation of the expression of these genes in the HNSC group, which was consistent with the RT-qPCR results. Survival analysis showed that the high expression of key genes was closely associated with a poorer overall survival (p < 0.05). Spatial transcriptomics analysis revealed that KIF2C, RFC4, and MSN were preferentially expressed in tumor core regions, indicating their involvement in the malignant microenvironment. Additionally, we discovered that 10 types of immune cell infiltrations were significantly changed in HNSC and well correlated with key genes.


## Conclusion
  MSN, KIF2C and RFC4 may serve as potential biomarkers for diagnosing and predicting the prognosis of HNSC, providing new insights into targeted therapy.

## Introduction
  Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC) is a malignant tumor originating from squamous epithelium located above the clavicle to anterior to the cervical vertebrae (excluding intracranial, ocular, and cervical vertebra regions). Head and neck cancer ranks as the seventh most common cancer worldwide, with squamous cell carcinoma comprising 90% of all cases [1, 2]. It is strongly associated with smoking, alcohol consumption, and HPV infection [3, 4]. HPV-related cases (especially oropharyngeal cancer) tend to respond more favorably to radiotherapy and chemotherapy, with significantly better survival rates and lower recurrence risk compared to HPV-negative cases caused by smoking [5–7]. Despite the rapid advancements in comprehensive treatment options for HNSC in recent decades, including surgery, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy, more than 50% of cases still experience recurrence or metastasis, and the global overall 5-year survival rate remains below 60% [8, 9].

  Recent studies have shown that lactate in the tumor microenvironment (TME) is not a “metabolic waste,” but plays a key role in shaping the TME by multidimensionally regulating various cell populations in various ways through immune, metabolic, signaling, and epigenetic mechanisms [10]. In tumor cells, lactate provides a direct substrate for lactylation modification, which drives malignant progression through epigenetic regulatory mechanisms [11]. For example, since the first discovery of histone lactylation by Zhang et al. [12], it has been reported in various cancers, especially histone H3 lysine 18 lactylation (H3K18la) [13]. Ma Hailong et al. demonstrated that knocking out IL-11, a downstream target gene of H3K9la, reverses lactate-induced CD8+ T cell exhaustion. This suggests that increased histone lactylation is linked to a poor response of immunotherapy in HNSC [14]. As lactylation research in cancer continues to advance, strategies targeting lactate/lactylation as potential anticancer therapies have attracted widespread attention. Therefore, identifying lactylation-related targets in HNSC has important clinical significance for disease treatment.

  Notably, machine learning has emerged as a powerful tool for developing prognostic biomarkers in HNSC. Xing et al. constructed a prognostic model for HNSC using machine learning approaches, identifying a five-small nucleolar RNA (snoRNA) signature that effectively predicts overall survival with high sensitivity and specificity [15]. In this study, we analyzed the key biomarkers of HNSC based on bioinformatics and machine learning methods, mined potential lactylation-related genes (LRGs) as therapeutic targets, and analyzed the independent prognostic value of LRGs for HNSC patients. Furthermore, we investigate the spatial distribution of LRGs in tumors and their correlation with immune cell infiltration in HNSC patients. This provides a foundation for predicting patient prognosis and planning personalized treatment.


## Methods

### Data sources
  RNA sequencing gene expression data and survival data were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/), which includes 495 tumor samples and 44 paired normal samples. In addition, RNA sequencing data of the GSE6631 dataset, consisting of 22 tumor samples and 22 normal samples, were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). For spatial transcriptomics analysis, we used the spatial transcriptomics dataset GSE252265-GSM7998252, which was derived from fresh samples of human cancer patients collected in 2022 at Helsinki University Hospital. A total of 400 lactylation-related genes (LRGs) were obtained from the GeneCard database (search term “lactylation”) and previously published studies, and these genes were further refined by removing duplicates.Differential Analysis [16]. The list of LRGs is presented in Supplement Table.

  Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were analyzed using the “limma” package [17]. The threshold was set at Adjusted p-values < 0.05 and |Log2FoldChange| >1. Heatmaps and volcano plots were used to visualize the differential gene expression profiles. Furthermore, to explore the functions and potential characteristics of the differential genes, Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was performed using the “clusterProfiler” package [18], including molecular function (MF), biological process (BP), and cellular component (CC). Simultaneously, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis was conducted to screen signaling pathways potentially involved with hub genes, investigating their roles in HNSC biological processes.


### WGCNA
  Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) was performed using the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} 				\usepackage{amsmath} 				\usepackage{wasysym}  				\usepackage{amsfonts}  				\usepackage{amssymb}  				\usepackage{amsbsy} 				\usepackage{mathrsfs} 				\usepackage{upgreek} 				\setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} 				\begin{document}$$\:\text{R}$$\end{document} package [19]: first, duplicate genes were merged using the limma package, and low-expression as well as low-variance genes were filtered out based on thresholds of average expression >0.2 and standard deviation >0.2. Hierarchical clustering was then used to remove outlier samples. Next, the pickSoftThreshold function was applied to select the optimal soft-thresholding power (β = 5). Based on this value, adjacency and topological overlap matrices (TOM) similarity matrices were calculated. Co-expression modules were identified by dynamic tree cutting (minimum module size of 60), and highly correlated modules were merged at a module eigengene correlation distance of 0.25. Finally, Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to evaluate the relationships between module eigengenes and disease phenotypes, with significantly associated modules defined by |correlation coefficient| >0.5 and p < 0.05.


### Machine learning
  Twelve machine learning algorithms were implemented for disease diagnostic feature gene selection using R packages. “caret” [20], including random forest (randomForest), support vector machine (kernlab), XGBoost (xgboost), logistic regression (glm), GBM boosting trees (gbm), K nearest neighbors (knn), neural networks (nnet), LASSO regression (glmnet), decision trees (rpart), naive Bayes (klaR), AdaBoost (ada), and Bagging (ipred). All models employed fivefold repeated cross-validation (trainControl to assess algorithm robustness and generalization ability. Model training parameters used default settings, and the binary classification threshold was set at the default 0.5 probability cutoff. The DALEX package was used for performance evaluation, constructing residuals and variable importance interpretation frameworks. The pROC package plotted ROC curves on the test set and calculated the area under the curve (AUC) and 95% confidence intervals using the DeLong method; The PRROC package was used for Precision-Recall curve and AUC analysis.


### Survival analysis
  Based on the TCGA-HNSC dataset and its corresponding clinical information, we evaluate the impact of key gene expression levels on patient prognosis. TCGA-HNSC samples were divided into high-expression and low-expression groups according to the median gene expression; Kaplan-Meier method was used to plot survival curves to visualize differences in overall survival between the two groups. For all statistical analyses, p < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.


### Sample collection
  We validated the expression of key genes using patient samples. This study collected tumor and adjacent tissue from three head and neck squamous cell carcinoma patients at the Department of Otolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University. All patients had not received radiotherapy or chemotherapy prior to tumor resection. The research was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (Approval No. 2024-KY-0405-002).


### RNA extraction and real-time PCR
  Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Cat. No. 15596018) and transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript II Q RT SuperMix Kit (Vazyme, Cat. No. R222-01), following the manufacturer’s instructions. One microgram of total RNA was used for reverse transcription to obtain cDNA. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) on a QuantStudio™ 5 Real-Time PCR Instrument. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2^(−ΔΔCt) method, with target gene expression levels normalised against the housekeeping gene GAPDH. The primer sequences are as follows: MOESIN-F: TCAACCAGCCATCAGTCCCTT; MOESIN-R: ACCAAACTTCCCTCAAGCCAA. RFC4-F: AAACCTGAGGAACGAGCCG; RFC4-R: AGGGAACGGGTTTGGCTTTC. KIF2C-F: ACGCAGTAATGGTTTAATTCACAGT; KIF2C-R: TTCCTGCAAGGGCAGATTGT. GAPDH-F: TCCTCCGGGTGATGCTTTTC; GAPDH-R: TGAAGGGGTCATTGATGGCA.


### Western blot analysis
  Tissue samples were homogenized at 4 °C using a cryogenic homogenizer with pre-chilled lysis buffer. After centrifugation at 12,000×g for 10 min, the supernatant was collected, mixed with loading buffer, and denatured. Samples were then separated by 10% SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis. Gels were electrophoresed at 70 V for 30 min, followed by 110 V for 1 h. Proteins were then transferred to PVDF membranes at 110 V for 2 h. Membranes were blocked in 5% skim milk for 30 min before incubation with anti-MOESIN antibody (Proteintech #82009-1-RR; 1:1000), RFC4 (Proteintech #10806-1-AP; 1:1000), KIF2C (Proteintech #28372-1-AP; 1:1000), and Kla (PTM Bio #PTM 1401; 1:1000) and GAPDH (Proteintech #60004-1-Ig; 1:1000) overnight at 4 °C. Wash three times with TBST (8 min each), then incubate at room temperature with HRP-labeled secondary antibody Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H +L)-HRP Conjugate (Bio-Rad #1706515; 1:10,000) for 2 h. Images were quantified using Image J.


### Regulatory network analysis of hub genes
  Regulatory network analysis was conducted based on motif enrichment using the RcisTarget package to systematically identify potential master regulatory transcription factors of the target gene set. The analysis first calculated the weighted AUC values of the input gene set across all known motifs, using the mean plus three times the standard deviation as a preliminary screening threshold. Further, normalized enrichment scores (NES) were used with a key threshold of NES > 3.5 to select high-confidence significant motifs, which were then mapped to upstream transcription factors based on motif annotations. Subsequently, significant regulatory genes corresponding to each motif were identified and regulatory effects were demonstrated via cumulative recovery curves… Further visualization of enrichment results included motif logos and high-confidence TF labels, summarizing significant motifs in interactive HTML tables.


### Spatial transcriptomics analysis
  Systematic analysis of 10x Genomics spatial transcriptomics data was performed using R packages (Seurat, ggplot2, patchwork, dplyr, Rfast2, hdf5r, viridis, glmGamPoi, SingleR, celldex, and RColorBrewer). First, a Seurat object was constructed and spatial information was attached. Then, normalization was performed using SCTransform, followed by dimensionality reduction with PCA, neighborhood finding, clustering, and UMAP dimensionality reduction for clustering analysis. Clustering results were visualized by UMAP and spatial distribution maps to output the spatial distribution of each cluster. Spatially variable gene analysis was completed using NormalizeData, FindVariableFeatures, and FindSpatiallyVariableFeatures.Spatial expression distribution maps were drawn for key genes. Cell type annotation was performed using SingleR combined with human reference datasets provided by celldex (HumanPrimaryCellAtlasData), and the annotation results were integrated into the Seurat object. Cell type distribution was displayed through UMAP and spatial distribution maps.


### Immune infiltration analysis
  The CIBERSORT algorithm was applied using the LM22 immune cell signature matrix with 1000 permutations to estimate the relative fractions of 22 immune cell types in each sample [21]. The sum of scores for the 22 immune cell types in each sample equals 1. For each sample, deconvolution p-values were generated, and only those with p < 0.05 were considered reliable and included in downstream analyses; non-significant samples were excluded. Next, correlation analysis was performed between immune cell infiltration and key gene expression levels. Further, spearman correlation tests (significance level p < 0.05) were used to calculate correlation coefficients between target genes and various immune genes. Finally, a correlation matrix plot was constructed using the linkET package for data visualization.


## Results

### Differential analysis and enrichment analysis
  A total of 1183 genes were identified through differential analysis, of which 677 were downregulated and 506 were upregulated (supplementary Table 1). The heatmap displays the top 50 upregulated and downregulated differentially expressed genes (Fig. 1A , B). GO enrichment analysis was performed on the differential genes, with significance defined as adjusted p-value < 0.05. In the BP category, (Fig. 1C), differential genes were significantly enriched in “external encapsulating structure organization,” “extracellular matrix organization,” etc., suggesting that extracellular matrix-related biological processes are abnormally active during the occurrence and development of HNSC; In the CC category (Fig. 1D), differential genes were mainly localized to cellular components such as “contractile fiber” and “sarcomere,” which are involved in the collagen-related extracellular matrix and the actin cytoskeleton, among other structures. MF category (Fig. 1E) showed that proteins encoded by differential genes have significant activities such as “extracellular matrix structural constituent” and “actin binding.” KEGG pathway analysis (Fig. 1F) revealed that differential genes are significantly associated with pathways like “ECM-receptor interaction” and “Focal adhesion,” which may affect tumor invasion and metastasis (supplementary Table 2).

### WGCNA analysis
  To identify gene modules related to disease traits, we performed WGCNA analysis on the TCGA-HNSC cohort. A soft threshold of 5 achieved a high scale-free topology fit (R² >0.9) with appropriate connectivity (Fig. 2A). Hierarchical clustering yielded 15 distinct gene modules (Fig. 2B, C) (supplementary Table 3). Module–trait relationships revealed strong positive correlations of HNSC status with MEtan (r = 0.54, p = 2.7 × 10−42), MEred (r = 0.50, p = 2.0 × 10−35), and MEmagenta (r = 0.60, p = 7.7 × 10−54) (Fig. 2D–F), implicating these modules as disease-relevant hubs (Fig. 2D, E).

### Machine learning identification of key genes
  A total of 6 candidate genes were identified through the integration of the WGCNA module, DEGs, and LRGs (Fig. 3A). To evaluate their diagnostic value, we compared the performance of 12 machine learning models. Feature importance analysis highlighted MSN, KIF2C, and RFC4 as the key genes (Fig. 3B). Residual distribution curves showed that the GLM model achieved the most stable performance with minimal error (Fig. 3C). The PR curve shows that the GLM model achieves high precision and recall (Fig. 3D). Additionally, ROC analysis further confirmed the superior diagnostic ability of GLM (AUC = 0.994), outperforming the other models (Fig. 3E). Taken together, these findings identify MSN, KIF2C, and RFC4 as robust biomarkers.

### Diagnostic performance and survival analysis
  In the TCGA-HNSC cohort, KIF2C, RFC4, and MSN demonstrated strong diagnostic performance with AUCs of 0.986, 0.964, and 0.897 (Fig. 4A). Independent validation in the GSE6631 dataset yielded consistent results, with AUCs of 0.850, 0.848, and 0.801 (Fig. 4E). In the TCGA-HNSC cohort, boxplot showed that compared with normal tissues, the expression levels of KIF2C, RFC4, MSN in tumor tissues were significantly higher (Fig. 4B–D). This result was further validated in the GSE6631 validation set (Fig. 4F–H). Survival analysis further revealed that patients with high KIF2C (p = 2.74 × 10⁻3), high RFC4 (p = 3.32 × 10⁻5), and high MSN expression (p = 4.26 × 10⁻²) had significantly shorter overall survival compared with those with low expression (Fig. 4I–K).

### Motif transcriptional regulation analysis results
  Motif enrichment analysis identified several transcription factor (TF) binding motifs significantly associated with the key genes (Fig. 5A , B). Among them, transfac_pro_M05418 and transfac_pro_M05904 both achieved the highest enrichment (NES = 7.72, AUC = 0.331), corresponding to ZNF521 and ZNF564. The predrem_nrMotif380 motif (NES = 7.69, AUC = 0.330) and tfdimers_MD00359 motif (NES = 7.65, AUC = 0.328) were also highly enriched, the latter linked to multiple TF complexes including CEBPA, CEBPB, CEBPE, CEBPD, and STAT6. In addition, cisbp_M5134 (NES = 7.63, AUC = 0.327) was identified as another enriched motif. These results suggest that transcription factors such as ZNF521, ZNF564, and members of the CEBP family may act as upstream regulators of the identified key genes, potentially driving transcriptional programs involved in HNSC progression.

  A total of 13 different cell types were identified in the single-cell analysis, including chondrocytes, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, keratinocytes, macrophages, T cells, and other stromal and immune subsets (Fig. 6A). Analysis of spatial gene expression patterns showed that KIF2C, RFC4, and MSN were preferentially expressed in the tumor core regions, whereas their expression was much lower in adjacent non-malignant areas (Fig. 6D–F). These findings highlight the tumor-specific spatial enrichment of the three key genes and suggest their potential involvement in the malignant microenvironment.

### Immune infiltration analysis
  CIBERSORT algorithm revealed significant heterogeneity in the infiltration of 22 immune cell types across the tumor and normal samples (Fig. 7A) (supplementary Table 5). Notably, CD8⁺ T cells and neutrophils exhibited significant differences between the tumor and normal tissues (Fig. 7B). Correlation analysis between three key genes (MSN, KIF2C, RFC4) and 22 immune cells revealed distinct patterns. KIF2C showed positive correlations with T cells CD8 and M1 macrophages, while negatively associated with M0 Macrophages and Neutrophils. RFC4 expression was positively correlated with T cells follicular helper and plasma cells, and negatively correlated with neutrophils and M0 macrophages. MSN expression was positively correlated with T cells CD4 memory resting and NK cells resting, but negatively associated with CD8⁺ T cells and B cells memory (Fig. 7C) (supplementary Table 6). A further analysis of the correlations between these key genes and immune-related genes indicates a significant association (Fig. 7D–F). These results suggest that MSN, KIF2C, and RFC4 significantly influence immune cell infiltration and immune-related gene expression, contributing to the immune regulatory network in HNSC.

### Experimental verification
  To verify the expression of key LGRs in HNSC, we further performed qRT-PCR and Western blotting. qRT-PCR results showed that the mRNA expressions of MSN, KIF2C and RFC4 were significantly high in HNSC group (Fig. 8A–C). Western blotting results showed that the protein levels of MSN, KIF2C and RFC4 were significantly increased in the HNSC group (Fig. 8B). Furthermore, using the Pan-kla antibody to detect lactylation, we observed that proteins across multiple molecular weight ranges exhibited lactoylation modifications in the HNSC group (Fig. 8C). This finding suggests that lactoylation may play a significant role in regulating the function of these proteins, thereby influencing the metabolic and cellular biological processes of HNSCs.

## Discussion
  This study analyzed transcriptomic data of HNSC from the TCGA database and identified lactate-related key genes (MSN, KIF2C, RFC4) associated with HNSC prognosis. These findings provide a foundation for exploring metabolic and epigenetic interactions in HNSC and suggest potential therapeutic strategies. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed significant enrichment of terms such as “ECM assembly disorder” and “cytoskeletal dynamics abnormalities”. Lactylation modifications can directly target core components of the extracellular matrix (ECM), such as collagen and fibronectin, altering their cross-linking status or integrin-binding capacity. Recent research by Sun et al. further elucidated the mechanism by which lactate promotes type I collagen deposition through two complementary pathways, thereby regulating ECM remodeling [22].

  MSN (encoding the Moesin protein, ERM family) connects the cell membrane to the actin cytoskeleton [23, 24], and may regulate the membrane localization of lactate transporter MCT4, thereby changing intracellular lactate concentration and protein emulsification level. Studies have found that MSN is specifically overexpressed in various tumors, such as in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) tissues and cell lines, and is associated with poor prognosis [25] .Our findings are consistent with those of Schlecht NF et al., which demonstrated that MSN (moesin) is associated with poor overall survival in HNSC. This further strongly suggests that MSN could be a potential therapeutic target for HNSC [26]. The KIF2C (kinesin-13 family) acts as a microtubule depolymerase involved in regulating mitotic spindle dynamics [27, 28]. The upregulation of KIF2C in HNSC is consistent with other reports of solid tumors [29–31]. Studies have shown that in hepatocellular carcinoma cells, KIF2C overexpression leads to enhanced mTORC1 activity and elevated lactate levels that subsequently promote histone H3K18 lactylation and activate transcription of genes associated with cell proliferation and migration [32]. Recent literature reports that KIF2C may regulate microtubule plus-end dynamics as well as the composition and phosphorylation status of focal adhesion (FA) proteins, thereby modulating tumor cell motility and migration [33]. However, whether KIF2C regulates metastasis and invasion of HNSC through the mechanism of lactylation remains to be verified. RFC4, a subunit of the RFC complex, regulates DNA replication and repair [34]. Similarly, RFC4 is highly expressed in tumor cells, and the demand for proliferation drives a shift towards glycolytic metabolism (Warburg effect), resulting in an increased production of lactic acid. For example, in breast cancer, RFC4 and lactate-associated genes are co-enriched in glycolysis and cell cycle pathways, suggesting that it may provide substrate for lactate by promoting glycolysis [35]. In tumors with high RFC4 expression, infiltration of regulatory T cells (Treg) and M2 macrophages increases, while the proportion of CD8+ T cells decreases [36], consistent with our findings. Moreover, RFC4 co-expresses with immune checkpoint molecules such as PD-L1 and CTLA-4, suggesting that tumors with high RFC4 expression may respond poorly to immunotherapy. This study systematically identified key LRGs MSN, KIF2C, and RFC4 in HNSC for the first time through integrated transcriptomics analysis, machine learning, and spatial transcriptomics, revealing their potential regulation by lactylation epigenetics and providing new perspectives for targeting metabolic-epigenetic crosstalk.

  Further functional analysis revealed that these three genes are strongly correlated with immune infiltration and immune-related genes. In the mechanism of immune microenvironment remodeling, MSN, KIF2C, and RFC4 exhibit dual regulatory roles. MSN positively regulates M2 macrophages and activated mast cells, negatively regulates CD8+ T cell infiltration, and is significantly positively correlated with immune checkpoints LAG3/TIGIT and chemokines CCL2/CCL5, suggesting it may promote immune escape by recruiting immunosuppressive cells and activating exhaustion pathways; KIF2C is associated with Tregs, M1 macrophage infiltration and PD-L1/CTLA4 expression synergistically, possibly mediating cancer progression through dual pathways involving the PLK1–MMP9 pathway and immune checkpoint upregulation [37]. Spatial heterogeneity analysis further revealed that high expression of key genes in the tumor core overlaps with a hypoxia-lactate microenvironment. These findings suggest that these genes can be used as potential diagnostic markers and therapeutic targets, especially in the context of lactic acid metabolism, which affects tumor growth, invasion and metastasis.

  However, this study has several limitations. First, our analysis is mainly based on TCGA data, which may introduce selection bias due to samples coming from specific cohorts. Second, the biological functions and regulatory mechanisms of the identified key genes remain to be fully elucidated. Third, although we explored the relationship between key genes and immune cell infiltration, the complex interactions between these genes and the tumor microenvironment require further in-depth study. Future research should address these limitations to better understand the roles of these genes in HNSC.

  In summary, this study establishes MSN, KIF2C, and RFC4 as core hubs of the lactylation regulatory network in HNSC, driving tumor microenvironment remodeling and poor prognosis through the “epigenetic-immune” axis, providing new targets and biomarker systems to overcome the current bottleneck of immunotherapy resistance and offering deep insights into the molecular pathogenesis of the disease.
