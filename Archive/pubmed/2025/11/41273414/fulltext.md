## Introduction
  Type 2 diabetes is a common, complex disease and represents a clinically heterogeneous composite of various pathophysiological disruptions that converge into the common final pathway of chronic hyperglycaemia. In 2021, type 2 diabetes affected over 500 million people worldwide, with projections indicating a rise to 1.3 billion by 2050 [1, 2]. It is a leading cause of global morbidity due to its broad metabolic impact and frequent complications, including CVD, chronic kidney disease, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), neuropathy and retinopathy. Pathophysiologically, type 2 diabetes is a highly heterogeneous disorder and manifests through multiple interconnected mechanisms, including (1) insulin resistance in peripheral tissues (e.g. liver, fat, skeletal muscle) [3], often but not always concomitant with adiposity and ectopic fat deposition in visceral organs; (2) progressive dysfunction of beta cells, signifying the inability of the organismal beta cell complement to preserve euglycaemia in the face of mounting insulin resistance [4]; (3) hypertriglyceridaemia, reflecting ectopic lipid overflow and impaired lipid storage [5]; (4) chronic low-grade inflammation in peripheral tissues (e.g. adipose, pancreatic islets) associated with increased immune cell infiltration and inflammatory cytokine secretion [6]; and (5) hyperglucagonaemia [7]. Importantly, these processes occur in varying degrees and combinations across individuals, contributing to disease heterogeneity and variable therapeutic response [2], opening the way to more targeted therapeutic approaches [8]. Environmental and behavioural factors, including diet, physical activity and socioeconomic context, strongly modulate type 2 diabetes risk and progression [9, 10]. Yet twin and family studies consistently estimate that 40–70% of the interindividual risk is attributable to heritable factors [11–13]. Over the past two decades, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified 1289 loci associated with type 2 diabetes, providing a foundation for mechanistic discovery [14].

  This review outlines recent advances in decoding the biology of type 2 diabetes through human genetics. We highlight examples of genetically anchored functional insights across key metabolic tissues and describe emerging variant-to-function (V2F) tools, including fine-mapping, single-cell profiling, chromatin-based methods and CRISPR perturbations, that enable systematic dissection of disease mechanisms. These approaches are beginning to translate genetic signals into mechanistic understanding and, ultimately, more precise avenues for therapeutic development.


## Twenty-five years of type 2 diabetes genetic discovery
  The history of type 2 diabetes genetics spans over two decades. Early population-level and familial data have long suggested a strong genetic component to type 2 diabetes, but only when population-scale genetic analysis methodologies emerged were the first reproducible genetic associations with type 2 diabetes discovered [15–18]. Since then, GWAS have revolutionised the discovery of type 2 diabetes-associated genetic risk loci. GWAS scan the genomes of many individuals to identify common genetic variants statistically associated with the risk of disease, or specific disease traits, without requiring prior biological assumptions [19, 20]. These large-scale genetic studies together with successive multi-ancestry meta-analyses have identified an increasing number of genetic loci that are robustly associated with type 2 diabetes [14, 21, 22]. Over the previous 12 years, type 2 diabetes genetic associations have progressed from the 63 loci supported in the 2012 DIAGRAM GWAS to the most recent and largest type 2 diabetes GWAS meta-analysis performed in 2024, which analysed over 2.5 million individuals across six major ancestry groups, identifying 1289 independent genetic signals [14, 23] (Fig. 1a). These extensive genetic associations established the basis for probing the complex biology behind type 2 diabetes susceptibility. Yet, challenges remain. The common variants identified through GWAS to date explain only 18–20% of total type 2 diabetes heritability [24], underscoring the polygenic nature of the disease and suggesting that additional risk likely stems from variants of smaller effect, gene–gene and gene–environment interactions, and rare variants yet to be uncovered.Fig. 1The complexity of type 2 diabetes from a V2F perspective.   As the field of type 2 diabetes genetics moves forward in the genomic era, the complexity and scale of genetic discovery is simultaneously a great achievement and a scientific challenge. As sample size and diversity in large-scale genetic studies continue to increase, not only new population-wide associations but also sex- and ancestry-specific genetic associations will be identified. With the increasing scale of genetic discovery, V2F approaches have emerged to ascertain the mechanistic effects of disease-associated variants. A V2F approach connects genetic variants to disease mechanisms by linking variants to regulatory elements, effector genes/proteins, cellular states and ultimately cell function or morphology in disease-relevant contexts [25]. To date, only a minority of type 2 diabetes genetic associations have been isolated to one or more causal variant(s) in a well-defined tissue context, with a clear downstream mechanistic impact on disease risk. In type 2 diabetes, the V2F challenge is a result of layers of complexity (Fig. 1).

  A key aspect of this challenge is that most type 2 diabetes-associated variants fall in non-coding regions of the genome. Rather than altering protein sequences, these variants can regulate gene activity through sequences known as cis-regulatory elements (CREs) in a cell type- or cell state-dependent manner [26]. Consortia such as ENCODE and the Roadmap Epigenomics Consortium have mapped over 2.3 million CREs across hundreds of human cell types and tissues over the last two decades, enabling systematic annotation of the non-coding genome [27, 28] (Fig. 1b). However, many candidate CREs (cCREs) are shared among cell types, and context-specific regulatory activity is often required to assign tissue specificity. For example, comparison of an ENCODE liver and muscle sample revealed over 40,000 shared cCREs, with 38,000 and 64,000 unique cCREs, respectively. The cCRE map alone will not reveal cell type specificity or tissue of action for a given genetic association. In addition to improved regulatory element annotation is the parallel increase in non-coding gene annotation. In the past two decades, the number of annotated genes has nearly tripled, mostly driven by long non-coding RNA, pseudogenes and small RNAs [29, 30] (Fig. 1c). The increasing complexity of genome annotation opens the door to new hypotheses for non-coding type 2 diabetes variation in loci containing newly annotated cCREs and non-coding genes. An additional layer of complexity in type 2 diabetes stems from the growing recognition of cell type diversity and cell state heterogeneity within each relevant metabolic tissue [31–34] (Fig. 1d). A recent adipose tissue cell atlas identified seven adipocyte subtypes that differ in abundance and metabolic function between adipose tissue depots [32], possibly explaining how genetic variants exert differential effects depending on cell state. An additional study looked at adipose heterogeneity in paired individuals to profile cell state changes within adipose tissue pre- and post-weight loss using single-nucleus and spatial transcriptomics [35]. These studies exemplify methods under development to capture transcriptional and phenotypic heterogeneity at scale, advancing resolution in V2F studies (Fig. 1e).

  Together, these findings have expanded the genetic landscape of type 2 diabetes and revealed key biological contexts in which genetic risk unfolds. Here, we review recent progress and current challenges in type 2 diabetes genetics and discuss computational and experimental technologies advancing V2F studies that operate across cell types, developmental windows and disease-relevant stimuli. As the field transitions from association to mechanism, such frameworks will be essential for translating genetic discoveries into actionable biological and clinical insight.


## Dissecting type 2 diabetes genetic heterogeneity
  Type 2 diabetes is a clinically and biologically heterogeneous disorder composed of multiple, partially overlapping pathophysiological processes. Some individuals present with dominant features of insulin resistance and lipid overflow, while others exhibit primary beta cell dysfunction or glucagon dysregulation. Genetic studies are increasingly used to parse this disease heterogeneity and suggest potential disease subtypes. One approach involves clinically informed genetic clustering in which type 2 diabetes-associated variants are grouped based on their co-association with intermediate metabolic traits such as LDL-cholesterol, fasting insulin, lipid levels and waist-to-hip ratio. Recent studies have used several approaches to cluster phenotypes and variants, such as k-means clustering and Bayesian non-negative matrix factorisation (bNMF), and annotated clusters of variants by their clinical associations [8, 14, 36–39]. Despite methodological differences, these analyses tend to converge on a core set of biological ‘processes’ or endotypes, including beta cell dysfunction, liver/lipid metabolism, obesity, insulin signalling and lipodystrophy. Individual approaches have refined these clusters even further: Smith et al [37] identified two independent lipodystrophy-related clusters; and Suzuki et al [14] identified two beta cell clusters, one with increased proinsulin levels (suggesting beta cell stress) and one with decreased proinsulin (suggesting reduced beta cell mass or function).

  Findings from monogenic forms of diabetes provide biological validation for several of these clusters. For example, mutations in HNF1A, GCK and PDX1 that cause MODY implicate beta cell development and glucose-sensing pathways, which also harbour common non-coding variants in type 2 diabetes GWAS [40]. Similarly, mutations in genes such as PPARG and LMNA that cause familial partial lipodystrophy (FPLD) mirror genetic clusters associated with insulin resistance and abnormal fat distribution [41, 42]. These parallels suggest that rare, high-penetrance coding variants and common, low-effect regulatory variants converge on overlapping pathways of type 2 diabetes pathogenesis.

  The DGKB locus illustrates how genetic clustering and functional annotation can jointly inform disease mechanisms. Smith et al [37] identified DGKB as a top-weighted locus in a beta cell cluster marked by elevated proinsulin levels and was similarly classified in a beta cell stress cluster by Ghatan et al [36]. Complementary work has identified likely causal DGKB expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) colocalising with the lead GWAS signal in pancreatic islet cells [43]. Diacylglycerol kinase β (DGKB), a diacylglycerol (DAG) kinase that regulates cellular DAG levels, is a key second messenger in several cell signalling pathways. While the precise molecular function of these risk variants has not been elucidated, the combination of genetic clustering and functional studies suggests a role for DGKB in regulating beta cell proinsulin levels, paving the way for future mechanistic studies in beta cells.

  In the context of a complex heterogeneous disease such as type 2 diabetes, such clustering of loci can provide a powerful tool to focus experimental efforts on subsets of variants operating within shared biological pathways and, ultimately, a path towards patient sub-segmentation and more tailored therapeutic strategies.

### Pancreatic islets
  Type 2 diabetes involves a fundamental defect in pancreatic islet function, resulting in insufficient insulin secretion from pancreatic islet beta cells. The expected importance of pancreatic beta cells in type 2 diabetes pathophysiological processes is supported by studies showing a significant enrichment of type 2 diabetes GWAS signal in pancreatic-islet-specific regulatory elements [44]. Recent work using human embryonic stem cell models of pancreatic beta cell differentiation evaluated 22 type 2 diabetes-associated genes predicted to function in beta cells [45]. This study identified candidate functional variants at two loci (SEC16B and RALY) and confirmed a previously identified variant in the ADCY5 locus [46]. However, not all genetic associations in the context of beta cell function are straightforward. For example, functional dissecting of the ARAP1/STARD10 locus has proven challenging. Conflicting reports suggest distinct effector gene targets: one implicates downregulation of ARAP1 expression through paired box 6 (PAX6)- and paired box 4 (PAX4)-mediated transcriptional repression [47]; the other points to STARD10, a lipid transfer gene, the loss of which leads to reduced proinsulin levels in mice [48]. While the mechanism of the ARAP1/STARD10 locus in regulating proinsulin regulation remains unresolved, these two studies reveal the power of genetically anchored investigation into beta cell function and underscore the complexity of V2F in type 2 diabetes.

  The TCF7L2 locus contains the strongest common genetic association with type 2 diabetes, with up to 32 independent signals now identified in the region [14]. Since its discovery, the tissue specificity and mechanisms underlying the role of transcription factor 7 like 2 (TCF7L2) in type 2 diabetes has remained controversial and uncertain. TCF7L2 is proposed to act primarily through impairment of insulin secretion in beta cells and early studies suggested pleiotropic roles across multiple tissues, including islets, liver and adipose tissue (comprehensively reviewed previously [49, 50]). More recently, single-cell profiling across pancreatic beta cell differentiation identified a stage-specific role for TCF7L2, with enhancer activity localised to developmental windows rather than mature beta cells [51]. Specifically, this work identified a stage-specific enhancer overlapping the rs7903146 linkage disequilibrium (LD) block, providing additional evidence for the role of TCF7L2 during beta cell development. Genetic clustering approaches described earlier have placed TCF7L2 variants primarily in the beta cell-related clusters (beta cell 2 in Smith et al [37]; beta cell + proinsulin in Suzuki et al [14]), though some index variants also fall in clusters linked to adiposity and glycaemic traits (body fat, residual glycaemic and metabolic syndrome clusters described in Suzuki et al [14]). These multi-cluster assignments suggest that additional context-dependent effects may be present regarding TCF7L2. Future work exploring TCF7L2 activity across developmental stages and metabolic states may clarify its pleiotropic contributions to type 2 diabetes.

  A power of genetic discovery is the ability of GWAS to identify novel genes and disease mechanisms. One striking example is the MTNR1B locus, first identified in 2009 and associated with increased fasting glucose [52]. In 2015, statistical fine-mapping and variant overlap with FOXA2 ChIP-seq peaks prioritised rs10830963 as a functional risk variant in the MTNR1B locus. This risk allele creates a novel NEUROD1 motif leading to an increase in MTNR1B expression in islets [53]. MTNR1B encodes a melatonin receptor and follow-up studies established a role for melatonin as an inhibitor of cAMP in beta cells, thereby impairing insulin secretion (a mechanism amplified by the MTNR1B risk allele) [54]. This genetic evidence paired with functional genomics helped reinvigorate study of the circadian control of insulin secretion. Subsequent studies have linked MTNR1B and the circadian clock gene CRY2 to diurnal regulation of glucose levels, suggesting that common variants may influence temporal dynamics of insulin action [55]. Larger cohorts and precise time-resolved longitudinal phenotyping of glucose levels will be needed to comprehensively uncover genetic control of the circadian function of beta cells.


### Liver
  The liver is a major metabolic organ that plays a central role in glucose and lipid metabolism by tightly regulating glycogen storage, gluconeogenesis and systemic insulin sensitivity. Insulin resistance and excessive lipid accumulation in hepatocytes can contribute to the development of MASLD. Despite the liver’s key metabolic role, relatively few common non-coding variants have been functionally validated as acting specifically through hepatocytes. Below, we discuss the recent progress in type 2 diabetes-relevant discovery of genetically anchored mechanisms in the liver.

  A notable case is the 17p13 type 2 diabetes risk locus that drives two genetic mechanisms linking SLC16A11 regulation and function in hepatocytes to type 2 diabetes risk [56]. This locus harbours both coding and non-coding risk variants linked to decreased expression and function of solute carrier family 16 member 11 (SLC16A11) in hepatocytes, respectively. These findings support SLC16A11 as an effector gene at this locus and illustrate how converging evidence from both coding and non-coding variation at a disease risk locus for a particular disease process can strengthen confidence in V2F effector gene assignments. SLC16A11 encodes a category I monocarboxylate transporter, with a potential to import pyruvate yet the precise tissue-/cell type-specific substrate is unknown. The combination of genetic and experimental evidence for SLC16A11 suggests decreased expression and function leads to increased DAG and triacylglycerol (TAG) levels in hepatocytes, which is consistent with lipid phenotypes present in individuals with type 2 diabetes [57].

  Proper maintenance of hepatic lipid levels requires balance of lipid-droplet biogenesis and breakdown. PNPLA3 encodes a lipid-droplet-associated protein with triacylgylcerol lipase activity, highly expressed in hepatocytes and adipocytes, and is known to mediate lipid-droplet mobilisation. Like SLC16A11, both coding and non-coding PNPLA3 variations have been associated with liver fat content, and with MASLD and type 2 diabetes [14, 58–60]. While PNPLA3 is most highly expressed in the liver, its expression in adipose tissue complicates tissue-of-action assignments. Given that both liver and adipose tissues are key insulin-sensitive metabolic tissues that have the potential to store and metabolise lipids, disentangling the tissue-specific impact of genetic variation between these two tissues remains challenging [61]. Additionally, these similarities suggest additive cross-tissue mechanisms, where variation in both adipose and liver together contribute to clinically observable phenotypes, as opposed to a genetic signal acting in a tissue-specific manner. This raises a broader question: to what extent are in vitro models limited to uncover a subset of V2F connections whose function depends on the integration of metabolic processes across tissues?

  The gap between genetic and mechanistic discovery remains large across diseases. Characterisation of type 2 diabetes genetic associations in the liver is an example of the challenges that remain in metabolic disease genetics. The liver is a complex organ composed of multiple cell types and states arranged in a spatial pattern that dictates metabolic function and response to external stimuli [62, 63] (Fig. 1d). Development of new in vivo and in vitro models to study hepatic differentiation, organisation and function in the context of human genetics is needed to further V2F efforts in this tissue context. Approaches such as spatial transcriptomics of human liver samples that preserve cellular structure and intracellular relationships, coupled with eQTL analysis to identify hepatic zone-specific eQTLs would introduce greater cell type and context resolution than current bulk tissue datasets. In addition, in vitro approaches have now emerged to study hepatocytes. In vitro models of induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived hepatocytes allow for 2D or 3D models that recapitulate hepatic zonation and in vivo phenotypes [64–66]. Utilisation of these approaches offers the potential to further V2F discovery in the liver.


### Adipose tissue
  Adipose tissue is a key insulin-sensitive metabolic tissue involved in energy storage and balance, glucose and lipid homeostasis, and immune function. Type 2 diabetes risk is modified by changes to the mass, distribution and function of adipose tissue. GWAS of fasting insulin and insulin resistance have robustly linked genetic variants to target genes implicated in adipose biology [67–70]. Adipose tissue is classified as white adipose tissue (WAT) and brown adipose tissue (BAT), with WAT being further divided into subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) and visceral adipose tissue (VAT), which differ markedly in their metabolic function. An important role of SAT is lipid storage through accumulation of triacylglycerols and formation of lipid droplets. SAT also plays an important role in insulin control through secretion of adipokines. VAT, however, has been reported to have higher rates of lipolysis and altered secretion of paracrine factors, including reduced leptin, increased adiponectin and increased IL-6, compared with SAT [71].

  Although increased adiposity is a well-established type 2 diabetes risk factor, recent Mendelian randomisation studies have implicated not just obesity but also unfavourable adipose distribution (increased VAT at the expense of SAT) as an independent casual risk factor for the development of type 2 diabetes and other cardiometabolic diseases [72, 73]. A large fraction of type 2 diabetes GWAS signals co-localises with signals for fat mass distribution and insulin resistance traits. These findings highlight adipose function, integrity and anatomical distribution as a central node in type 2 diabetes pathophysiology.

  Inherited and acquired lipodystrophies, characterised by selective or global perturbation of WAT function, mass and distribution, result in severe forms of insulin resistance and diabetes. In addition, molecular mechanisms shared between rare FPLD type 1 and common forms of insulin resistance at the genetic level have been previously suggested [74]. In these conditions, the loss of sufficient energy storage in adipose leads to ectopic lipid deposition in insulin-sensitive organs, including the liver, skeletal muscle and the heart, contributing to severe insulin resistance, dyslipidaemia and hepatic steatosis [74, 75].

  This failure of lipid partitioning highlights the essential buffering role of adipose tissue in metabolic homeostasis. FPLD, caused by rare coding mutations, provides mechanistic insights into type 2 diabetes subtypes characterised by impaired adipogenesis, lipid storage and insulin responsiveness. Examples include coding mutations in PPARG, a master regulator of adipogenesis, that impair adipocyte differentiation and limit the capacity for peripheral lipid storage, leading to ectopic fat deposition and insulin resistance. In contrast, rare variants in AKT2 primarily affect insulin receptor signalling [41, 42]. In addition, rare coding variants in CAV1, a ubiquitously expressed gene encoding a scaffolding protein in caveolar membranes [76], impair lipid trafficking and cell membrane dynamics contributing to a systemic lipodystrophic phenotype characterised by insulin resistance, dyslipidaemia and fatty liver disease. Monogenic lipodystrophies and the pathways identified from driver mutations now offer insights into common variation driving lipodystrophy-like subtypes of type 2 diabetes. For example, coding mutations in PPARG drive monogenetic lipodystrophy, and both coding and non-coding common genetic variants are linked to dysregulated PPARG transcription and impaired adipogenesis [16, 70].

  Insulin-stimulated glucose uptake is an important process that is dysregulated in type 2 diabetes. SLC2A4 (GLUT4) encodes a glucose transporter isoform that mediates insulin-stimulated glucose uptake in adipose and muscle. Variants such as rs117643180 that modulate SLC2A4 expression have been shown to directly impact insulin uptake at the transcriptional level, while additional genetic evidence implicates regulators of GLUT4 trafficking to the cell membrane. A recent study found that perturbation of 14 genes within eight insulin-fold-change (IFC) GWAS loci leads to altered insulin-stimulated GLUT4 surface expression [77]. Translocation of GLUT4 to the cell surface is a tightly controlled process involving an intricate interplay between the insulin receptor signalling pathway, vesicle transport, microtubule networks and the actin cytoskeleton [78]. One of these 14 genes, FLII (encoding FLII actin remodelling protein), is a known regulator of the actin cytoskeleton [79]. Additional evidence for the role of GLUT4 trafficking in type 2 diabetes has emerged from rare coding variation in TBC1D4, identified in a Greenlandic population GWAS [80]. TBC1D4 (also known as AS160) is a Rab-GTPase-activating protein, which responds directly to Akt activation to promote GLUT4 storage vesicle transport to the plasma membrane [80, 81]. Together, these data reveal several unique mechanisms by which genetic variants lead to impaired GLUT4 surface expression and subsequent decreased insulin-responsive glucose uptake.

  Molecular studies in adipocytes have revealed a role for cell shape and cytoskeletal dynamics in the regulation of adipogenesis and lipid signalling [82–85]. Genetic studies have the power to add additional information and identify key regulatory nodes of processes associated with disease. Recent work found that non-coding variants intronic to GRB14 decrease COBLL1 expression, impair actin organisation and lead to decreased lipid-droplet formation and insulin-responsive glucose uptake [86]. Cordon-Bleu WH2 Repeat Protein Like 1 (COBLL1) is an understudied actin-binding protein without a clear function in adipocytes. Genetic and functional studies have nominated COBLL1 as an important regulator of adipocyte differentiation and actin structure but future work will need to identify the specific molecular mechanism that connects non-coding COBLL1 variation to type 2 diabetes. These converging genetic findings offer support for important cytoskeletal regulatory nodes of insulin-sensitive glucose uptake.

  Beyond molecular mechanisms, recent studies highlight adipose tissue heterogeneity as an important determinant of metabolic function, uncovering significant developmental heterogeneity of adipose precursors and mature adipocytes [32, 35, 87] (Fig. 1d). While bulk adipose may provide one level of evidence to link genetic variation to gene expression and phenotypic changes, cellular specificity of V2F effects likely depend on resolving subpopulation and cell states. The FTO locus dissection is an example of how investigation into developmental trajectories and chromatin context can improve V2F discovery in adipocytes. The causal variant rs1421085 was found to exert an effect on adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells early in differentiation, where it disrupts a conserved AT-rich interaction domain 5B (ARID5B) repressor motif, leading to de-repression of a mesenchymal super enhancer and increased expression of IRX3 and IRX5 [88, 89]. This shift favours white over beige adipocyte formation, reducing thermogenesis and promoting adiposity. This finding illustrates how the functional impact of non-coding variants can depend on developmental timing and cellular context, highlighting the need for high-resolution functional maps. Additional work has also uncovered a functional role for FTO locus risk variants in neurons, acting through the same distal IRX3 and IRX5 connections as uncovered in adipocytes [90, 91]. These studies offer further evidence for pleiotropic effects between tissues and underscore the complexities in V2F effects in a multi-tissue disease such as type 2 diabetes.

  The single-cell data revolution has improved the discovery of cell type- and cell state-specific effects in type 2 diabetes-relevant tissues through integration of single-cell transcriptomic data with genetics [92]. Applied to current and future adipose cell atlases, these methods promise to improve V2F resolution by localising variant effects not only to adipose tissue broadly but also to specific adipocyte subpopulations. Additionally, adipose tissue is distributed across anatomically and metabolically distinct depots in the body [93]. Adipose tissue distribution is a heritable phenotype with strong associations to metabolic health; however, very few studies have resolved the depot-specificity of a variant effect [94]. Building more comprehensive datasets and equally comprehensive cell models to tackle adipose complexity will enable future, context-specific discovery.


### Muscle
  Skeletal muscle is another key insulin-sensitive tissue with a critical role in systemic glucose homeostasis. Upon becoming insulin resistant, skeletal muscle contributes to impaired glucose uptake and the pathogenesis of type 2 diabetes. As the insulin signalling pathway is a major driver of disease, uncoupling the tissue-specific effects of genetic variation across muscle, adipose and liver insulin signalling can be challenging.

  In muscle, a splicing variant of ANK1 (encoding ankyrin 1) was nominated as causal [95]. ANK1 is necessary for sarcoplasmic reticulum assembly, a complex that regulates GLUT4 translocation and glucose uptake. However, one study found no evidence for ANK1 splicing but instead proposed that variants within the ANK1 locus decrease expression of NKX6–3 [96], encoding a transcription factor (TF) linked to islet beta cell development. The tissue-specific differences of NKX6–3/ANK1 variants prompted further dissection of this locus to reveal two independent genetic signals within a 25 kb region in ANK1 that were in strong LD with the lead eQTL for ANK1 in skeletal muscle and NKX6–3 in pancreatic islets [21]. These studies underscore the importance of dissecting genetic loci down to individual, independent genetic signals, and investigating the tissue-shared or specific nature of each variant across disease-relevant time scales. This offers a warning for V2F research in type 2 diabetes: if a genetic signal is proposed to be functional in one tissue context, a different tissue context may respond uniquely to the same genetic signal or nearby independent genetic signals.

  Recent advances in human skeletal muscle profiling have begun to improve resolution of disease-specific mechanisms. Single-cell and multi-omic technologies and massively parallel reporter assays (MPRAs) are now being applied to models of muscle differentiation and human muscle samples [31, 97]. In addition to offering greater insights into muscle heterogeneity, a recent study profiled 287 muscle samples at single-nucleus resolution to identify cell type-specific gene signatures, eQTLs and chromatin accessibility QTLs (caQTLs) [98]. This approach allowed for identification of 13 cell types and thousands of e/caQTLs within this large dataset. One example from this study involves the C2CD4A/B type 2 diabetes locus, containing the variant rs7163757, which is a caQTL and alters enhancer activity. Previous studies implicate this variant in pancreatic cells, with an impact on C2CD4A/B expression; however, Varshney et al [98] found that rs7163757 may impact VPS13C, a gene that can impact cell surface GLUT4 localisation. This example illustrates the power of cell type- and cell state-resolved multi-omic profiling in the muscle.

  As these resources grow, the integration of GWAS with cellular-resolution expression and chromatin maps will be essential for resolving skeletal muscle-specific mechanisms in type 2 diabetes. In parallel, improvements of in vitro myogenic models and perturbation-based screening platforms will support systematic functional validation of candidate effector genes.


## Emerging technologies to bridge the gap between variant discovery and disease-driving mechanisms in type 2 diabetes
  Over the past decade, advances in statistical genetics, functional genomics and high-throughput perturbation platforms have increasingly enabled lineage-, tissue- and context-specific dissection of type 2 diabetes-associated variants. These technologies, when applied to disease-relevant cell types, offer a promising path towards resolving V2F links and illuminating disease-driving mechanisms.


### Genetic discovery and fine-mapping
  The advent of GWAS has transformed our understanding of the genetic basis of type 2 diabetes; however, these studies face inherent limitations. GWAS remain a signal discovery tool. They identify genetic variants associated with a trait or disease but do not pinpoint causal variants regulating effector genes and cellular programmes, or their relevant cell types [99]. An added challenge is that the majority of disease-associated variants are in the non-coding part of the genome [100]. To overcome these challenges, several statistical and computational approaches have been developed for prioritising candidate causal variants for subsequent functional characterisation. Traditional fine-mapping techniques, including heuristic algorithms, penalised regression, and conditional association analyses [101, 102], have been widely applied to define credible sets of variants. More recently, with the advent of large-scale genetic association mapping across an increasing number of ancestries, Bayesian-based fine-mapping tools such as SuSiE [103], CARMA [104], FINEMAP [105], CAVIAR [106] and PAINTOR [107], have enhanced variant prioritisation by leveraging probabilistic models to navigate the complexities of LD.

  While statistical fine-mapping has become increasingly powerful, significant challenges remain in translating these signals into mechanistic understanding. In many loci, multiple variants are in high LD, and credible sets can span several kilobases overlapping many regulatory elements. Moreover, statistical confidence does not always align with functional relevance: fine-mapped variants often reside in putative enhancers that lack cell type-specific activity or show no concordant eQTL effect. This is further complicated by loci where multiple genes may contribute to a phenotype, or where gene regulation is context-dependent, such as under metabolic stress or during cell differentiation. Therefore, integrating statistical fine-mapping with functional data, epigenomic annotations, chromatin interaction maps, gene expression and perturbation studies is essential for accurate variant interpretation. These integrative approaches are beginning to resolve the causal biology at complex loci but current tools and models are still evolving, and a comprehensive, unified framework remains a key goal in V2F research.


### Functional annotations
  Most common variant associations in type 2 diabetes map to non-coding regions in the genome, which may or may not have gene regulatory activity. A recent type 2 diabetes meta-analysis identified 184 single-nucleotide variants with a posterior probability of greater than 50% but of these likely causal variants only 10.3% (19/184) were missense coding variants, which aligns with previous findings that ~90% of GWAS variants are in non-coding regions [99, 100]. This reinforces the need to look beyond protein-coding changes and examine the non-coding genome, where many variants may exert their effects through gene regulatory mechanisms.

  To interpret these non-coding variants, epigenetic annotations, which indicate whether a variant is in a regulatory element such as a promoter or enhancer, offer valuable insights into variants’ potential role in gene expression and regulation. Evaluating these annotations in a cell type-specific context provides an added layer of information, highlighting the involvement of specific cells or tissues. This perspective is particularly important for understanding complex diseases like type 2 diabetes, where regulatory mechanisms often vary across biological contexts. Publicly available variant annotation tools and reference epigenome maps generated by large-scale consortia provide datasets on chromatin accessibility, 3D chromatin conformation, gene expression, and histone modifications and their segmented chromatin states across hundreds of tissues and cell types, including adipose, liver, muscle and pancreas, aiding in the prioritisation of causal regulatory variants [108–111]. The wealth of information gained through analysis of reference maps is improving variant annotation but the lack of context and environment-dependent regulatory maps is a piece of the functional annotation puzzle that remains missing. For example, reference maps of adipocytes in basal conditions provide one level of information but dynamic maps across metabolic, immunological or chemical exposures may mimic disease states and inform context-specific functional annotations.

  To functionally annotate non-coding variants, several genomic technologies have been leveraged across tissues and cell types. For example, chromatin accessibility assays such as assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq) [112] have been useful in mapping regulatory landscapes of GWAS variants linked to type 2 diabetes [113, 114]. More recently, single-cell approaches including single-nucleus ATAC-seq (snATAC-seq), SHARE-seq [115], sciCAR-seq [116] and SNARE-seq [117] have enabled simultaneous profiling of chromatin accessibility and gene expression at single-cell resolution (Fig. 2), providing unprecedented insight into the regulatory architecture of type 2 diabetes.Fig. 2Emerging technologies for bridging the gap between variant discovery and disease-driving mechanisms in type 2 diabetes.   Adding a spatial layer to variant interpretation, 3D chromatin interactions provide critical insight into the functional impact of GWAS variants. These interactions reveal the physical connections between non-coding variants and their target genes, enabling the prediction of effector genes regulated by variants in type 2 diabetes-relevant tissues [118, 119]. Chromosomal conformation capture (3C)-based techniques, such as high-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) [120], promoter capture-HiC [121] and Micro-C [122] have supported efforts to map 3D chromatin interactions, including the identification of topologically associating domains (TADs), which are genomic regions with high self-interaction that play a role in gene regulation (Fig. 2). For example, promoter capture-HiC has been used to identify candidate effector genes for obesity by linking non-coding variants to genes such as ORMDL3, LACTB and ACADS in primary adipocytes [123]. By providing a spatial framework for variant-to-gene mapping, chromosomal capture techniques are useful tools in connecting regulatory elements containing GWAS variants to their putative effector genes.


### Integrating statistical and functional fine-mapping approaches
  Several studies have demonstrated the power of integrating statistical fine-mapping with epigenetic annotations to prioritise causal variants and elucidate their functional roles in type 2 diabetes. One study combined Bayesian-based fine-mapping with genomic annotations from diabetes-relevant cell types, including pancreatic islets and hepatocytes, to identify rs10830963 as the likely causal variant at the MTNR1B locus [53]. Another study performed trans-ethnic fine-mapping of 54 loci associated with fasting glucose and insulin in individuals of African and European ancestry, incorporating functional annotations such as active islet enhancers and TF binding sites to refine credible sets [124]. This approach uncovered two novel fasting insulin loci, FAM133A (rs213676) and PELO (rs6450057), highlighting the added resolution gained through trans-ancestral analyses combined with regulatory annotations. Another recent multi-ancestry meta-analysis applied annotation-informed fine-mapping by integrating features such as enhancers, promoters and TF binding sites across type 2 diabetes-relevant tissues [100]. By leveraging population-specific differences in LD, the study was able to define minimal sets of variants explaining each association signal. Collectively, these studies underscore the value of combining statistical fine-mapping with functional context to pinpoint causal variants.

  Ongoing advances in both fine-mapping and functional annotation have further enhanced our ability to resolve causal variation. Improved statistical methods have narrowed association signals with greater precision [125], while new experimental approaches are refining the scale of sequence annotation. For instance, Region Capture Micro-C (RCMC) has narrowed chromatin contacts to nucleosome-level resolution, focusing on key sequences involved in mediating distal regulation [126]. Integration of TF binding profiles with deep learning methods is more precisely defining the size, landscape and organisation of CREs [127]. Together, with many additional efforts, these new approaches are enabling an increasingly precise view of genetic variation and sequence function.


### Quantitative trait loci analysis
  Quantitative trait loci (QTL) analysis is a cornerstone of V2F research, playing a central role in prioritising variants associated with type 2 diabetes. By linking genetic variation to molecular traits, QTL analysis offers a powerful framework for understanding how specific genomic regions influence phenotypes. QTL studies encompass a range of modalities, including expression QTLs (eQTLs), chromatin accessibility QTLs (caQTLs), splicing QTLs (sQTLs), protein QTLs (pQTLs), etc., revealing the genetic architecture underlying regulatory processes (Fig. 2). Among these, eQTLs, which associate genetic variants with gene expression levels, have been the most extensively studied and have been useful in identifying candidate effector genes at type 2 diabetes-associated loci [128–130]. Additionally, large-scale consortia such as GTEx [131], Roadmap Epigenomics [110], ENCODE [27], PsychENCODE [132] and eQTLGen [133] have generated valuable QTL datasets across a wide range of tissues, including those relevant to type 2 diabetes.

  It is important to note that most traditional QTL analyses rely on bulk tissue samples, which can obscure cell type-specific effects due to cellular heterogeneity [43]. Recent advances in single-cell QTL mapping have addressed this limitation by enabling the dissection of variant effects on gene expression at cellular resolution [134–136]. These efforts are uncovering cell type- and cell state-specific variant–gene regulatory relationships across metabolic tissues, including adipose, muscle, pancreas and liver [98, 137–141]. By improving fine-mapping resolution and linking genetic variants to their regulatory impact in specific cellular contexts, single-cell approaches are transforming our understanding of disease mechanisms.

  As data availability and complexity continue to grow, integrative frameworks that combine genetic association data with single-cell transcriptomic and epigenomic profiles are systematically dissecting the regulatory architecture of type 2 diabetes [114, 142, 143]. For example, building on bulk analyses in the pancreas, recent single-cell efforts have enabled population-level dissection of functional variants in beta cells [144]. In that study, stratified LD score regression was used to identify cell type- and cell state-specific enrichment of GWAS signals in the pancreas, leading to the prioritisation of a beta cell population enriched for regulatory activity at the KCNQ1 locus. Variant rs231361 was implicated in modulating INS expression and, subsequently, beta cell function, underscoring the value of single-cell epigenomic profiling for uncovering functional mechanisms of disease-associated variants.

  QTL mapping has provided a foundational framework for linking genetic variation to gene regulation and additional molecular traits. And although the power of colocalisation of GWAS signals with QTL has enabled V2F discovery, the promise of QTL mapping to ‘solve’ the V2F problem has not lived up to its potential. Less than 50% of GWAS associations co-localise with eQTLs [145–148], challenging the prevailing assumption that most disease-associated variants act by altering gene expression. Improvements in statistical power and resolution may increase colocalisation rates; however, current findings suggest that existing models may oversimplify the regulatory mechanisms at play. A more nuanced understanding of how genetic variants function, beyond classical eQTL effects, will be essential for advancing V2F efforts.

  A key limitation of many QTL studies is their reliance on baseline, unstimulated conditions in healthy tissues. It is increasingly recognised that disease-relevant regulatory effects may only emerge in specific physiological or environmental contexts. To address this, several studies have modelled disease-relevant stimuli such as cytokines, hormones, or metabolic stressors in vitro to identify response eQTLs (reQTLs) [149]. These reQTL capture variant effects on gene expression in response to various environmental cues [150, 151]. In the context of type 2 diabetes, profiling cells under stress and in response to diverse metabolic stimulations, such as fatty acid exposure, hypoxia, varying glucose concentrations, etc., will be critical for understanding how genetic variants influence gene regulation in dynamic, disease-relevant states, thereby uncovering genetic mechanisms that contribute to disease progression.


### Sequence-based predictive tools
  Recent advances in computational methodologies, particularly through machine learning and deep learning-based approaches, have greatly improved our ability to process large-scale genomic data and integrate diverse data modalities to predict the regulatory impact of non-coding GWAS variants. Central to this progress are sequence-to-function models [152–154], which leverage genomic datasets to infer variant effects and prioritise candidate causal variants. Tools such as DeepSEA [155], Basset [156], Basenji [157] and Enformer [158] use convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and transformer-based architectures trained on epigenomic and transcriptomic data to predict cell type-dependent regulatory activity of genetic variants (Fig. 2). For instance, Basset, combined with conservation scores, has been used to refine association signals at type 2 diabetes loci, highlighting regulatory variants at the PROX1 locus (in pancreas) and ADCY5 (in adipocytes and osteoblasts) [159, 160].

  Other tools focus on linking regulatory elements to their target genes by leveraging chromatin interaction data. For example, EpiMap [161] and the activity-by-contact (ABC) model [162] integrate enhancer activity with 3D genome contact frequency to predict enhancer–promoter interactions across tissues and cell types. Additional gene prioritisation frameworks, such as combined SNP-to-gene (cS2G) [163] and polygenic priority score (PoPS) [164], incorporate multi-omic features, including chromatin accessibility, histone modifications and chromatin conformation, to systematically prioritise likely causal variants and their putative effector genes in complex traits (Fig. 2).

  To further dissect gene regulatory mechanisms, TF binding prediction tools such as ChromBPNet can offer a more nuanced understanding of how genetic variants influence gene regulation. ChromBPNet analyses DNA sequences at base-level resolution to identify disrupted TF binding motifs and prioritise regulatory variants, revealing the intricate connections between genetic variation and transcriptional activity [165]. Complementary resources like JASPAR [166] provide a curated database of TF binding motifs, offering tools for enrichment analysis and visualisation of binding sites. These tools make it easier to explore how TFs interact with DNA and interpret the functional consequences of variants in the context of their gene regulatory circuitries at the cellular and physiological levels (Fig. 2).


## Functional validation
  Importantly, genetics and descriptive catalogues of molecular activity are, for non-coding signals at least, not sufficient to build compelling proof for a chain of causation that stretches from variant to gene and on to phenotype. To address this, technologies that support the functional characterisation of sequence perturbations at scale are rapidly maturing.


### MPRAs/reporter-based techniques
  Experimental V2F approaches have undergone significant innovation, enhancing their ability to validate computational predictions and elucidate the roles of genetic variants within physiologically relevant systems at increasing scale and resolution. Individual reporter assays, such as luciferase assays, have long been used to evaluate the regulatory activity of single non-coding elements by linking a candidate enhancer to a promoter and measuring transcriptional activity via luminescence [167]. While effective, these assays are limited in throughput and lack scalability for genome-wide studies. High-throughput approaches such as MPRAs [168, 169] and self-transcribing active regulatory region sequencing (STARR-seq) [170] overcome these challenges by testing thousands of regulatory elements simultaneously (Fig. 2). STARR-seq has not been widely performed in type 2 diabetes-relevant tissues but studies are emerging that use STARR-seq to map enhancers in pancreatic islets and liver [171–173]. MPRA studies in type 2 diabetes-relevant tissues have provided insights into disease-associated regulatory elements, offering a functional readout of genetic variants implicated in metabolic traits [174]. A growing body of work highlights the importance of using cell type-specific rather than generic promoters to improve MPRA performance, ensuring functional relevance in disease contexts [175–177]. Recent advancements in MPRA technology have also led to the integration of computational sequence-based models for refining regulatory predictions, further enhancing the functional interpretation of genetic variants [176].


### Genome engineering via CRISPR
  While plasmid-based assays offer a valuable platform to investigate the regulatory activity of GWAS-nominated variants, they are unable to study genetic loci within their endogenous genomic context. The advent of CRISPR-based technologies has revolutionised functional genomics, providing precise tools to directly perturb variants within a native genomic locus. Classical CRISPR-Cas9 systems primarily introduce targeted double-strand breaks, which can be leveraged for precise nucleotide modifications through homology-directed repair (HDR) with an exogenous template or introduce insertions/deletions via non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) [178]. More recent iterations of CRISPR, including CRISPR activation (CRISPRa) and interference (CRISPRi), have enabled the modulation of gene expression without altering the DNA sequence, making them particularly useful for interrogating enhancer regions that harbour regulatory variants associated with gene expression changes [179]. In addition, emerging technologies such as base editing and prime editing now enable precise sequence modifications without introducing double-strand breaks, further expanding the CRISPR toolkit [180].

  Beyond locus-specific editing, pooled CRISPR screens have significantly broadened the scale of variant interrogation by enabling high-throughput functional studies. Pooled screens are increasingly paired with high-dimensional readouts, such as single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), proteomics and chromatin accessibility, to dissect the molecular consequences of genetic perturbations. Single-cell CRISPR platforms such as Perturb-seq [181], CROP-seq [182], CRISP-seq [183], Mosaic-seq [184] and TISCC-seq [185] combine targeted perturbations with scRNA-seq to resolve gene regulatory networks at single-cell resolution (Fig. 2). Technologies such as STING-seq and BeeSTING-seq extend this capability by integrating multi-omic measurements with CRISPRi and base editing, respectively [186]. Other multimodal profiling platforms such as CITE-seq [187], DOGMA-seq [188] and, more recently, CRAFT-seq [189] integrate CRISPR perturbations with different combinations of simultaneous measurements of transcriptomes, surface proteins, chromatin accessibility, etc., enabling direct and high-resolution mapping of genetic perturbations to molecular phenotypes (Fig. 2).

  These CRISPR-based tools are accelerating our ability to connect genetic variants to their molecular and phenotypic effects and remain valuable resources for studying the genetic mechanisms of type 2 diabetes. While base-editing screens are a recent development, early studies are already applying these methods in metabolic cell types. For example, a recent study used base editors to investigate common variants that impact LDL-cholesterol uptake and cis-regulation of the LDLR gene, demonstrating the potential of these approaches in studying cardiometabolic traits [190].


### Phenotypic characterisation
  Connecting genetic variants directly to cellular phenotypes is instrumental in identifying disease-driving causal mechanisms/biology underlying metabolic diseases. For example, cardiometabolic disease oriented high-content imaging approaches, such as LipocyteProfiler, generate rich morphological and cellular profiles across multiple cell states to connect genetic variation to disease-relevant, functional cell programmes [191, 192]. Similarly, optical pooled screening (OPS) technologies offer a scalable approach to associate image-based phenotypes with specific genetic perturbations, enabling the functional interrogation of variant effects through high-resolution microscopy at single-cell resolution [193–195]. These unbiased platforms broaden the range of phenotypes accessible to V2F studies and offer a robust framework to connect genetic variants, regulatory elements and genes to cardiometabolic disease pathways such as insulin resistance, fat distribution and lipodystrophy.

  Other high-throughput phenotypic assays, particularly flow cytometry-based methods, have further expanded the toolkit for functional variant characterisation. For example, lipid-based FACS integrates CRISPRi/CRISPRa with lipid staining and flow cytometry to sort cells into distinct bins based on lipid content (or other relevant phenotypes), facilitating the study of variants and effector genes that influence lipid accumulation in metabolically relevant cells such as hepatocytes [196] (Fig. 2). Combining such phenotypic readouts with gene expression measurements, particularly in spatial contexts, can help link observable traits to underlying regulatory mechanisms. To this end, transcript-level assays can serve as key complements to phenotype-based screens. FlowFISH, which couples RNA fluorescence in situ hybridisation with flow cytometry, is one such technique that allows transcript-level quantification in pooled CRISPR screens, enabling direct measurement of how regulatory variants affect gene expression [162]. For spatially resolved insight, smFISH provides single-molecule detection of RNA transcripts within intact tissues or complex culture systems [197, 198]. By localising gene expression changes to specific cell types or microenvironments, smFISH adds valuable contextual information that can enhance interpretation of genetic perturbations for phenotypic outcomes (Fig. 2).

  Natural genetic variation screens, or ‘GWAS-in-a-dish’, provide another powerful approach for phenotypic characterisation by leveraging genetically diverse cells to functionally investigate variants in controlled environments. These systems can involve co-culturing multiple cell lines or donor-derived cells in shared ‘cell villages’, which enhance scalability and minimise batch effects by enabling simultaneous analysis of multiple genetic backgrounds [199]. When combined with scRNA-seq, transcriptional profiles can be deconvolved, linking cells back to their donor genotypes and uncovering context-specific effects, such as cell type-specific QTLs or dynamic regulatory changes during differentiation. This strategy can bridge GWAS findings with cellular mechanisms, providing crucial insights into the functional consequences of genetic variation.

  Advances in 3D tissue systems, such as organoids and organs-on-a-chip (OoC), have added an additional layer of depth to the study of genetic variation in physiologically relevant contexts. In the context of type 2 diabetes, 3D modelling of relevant tissues, such as adipose and liver, may allow for incorporation of additional cell types to study the impact of genetic variation in a system more similar to in vivo tissue [200]. Additionally, re-creation of tissue-specific microenvironments by incorporating nutrient gradients and mechanical forces, make OoC particularly suited for modelling dynamic, organ-level processes [201]. These innovative systems provide a valuable extension to traditional high-throughput phenotypic assays, offering the ability to study the downstream effects of genetic variation in more complex, physiological-like environments.


## Conclusion
  The path from genetic variant discovery to mechanistic interpretation in type 2 diabetes has seen remarkable progress, yet it remains a complex, non-linear and iterative process. A core challenge lies in resolving the roles of non-coding variants, which regulate gene expression in highly tissue-, cell- and state-specific contexts and respond dynamically to diverse disease-relevant conditions. The scale and intricacy of these interactions have historically impeded progress but breakthroughs in experimental and computational technologies are rapidly bridging this gap. These advancements are unlocking opportunities to decode the molecular mechanisms underlying type 2 diabetes pathogenesis, transforming genetic insights into actionable biological and clinical knowledge. Looking ahead, the integration of artificial intelligence and machine learning into V2F pipelines offers potential to streamline this process. These technologies can rapidly process and integrate vast genetic, clinical and multi-omics datasets, prioritise causal variants and model complex regulatory networks across tissues and cellular states. Additionally, as the scope of multi-ancestry datasets expands, these tools will allow us to capture ancestry-specific and sex-specific genetic associations, thereby providing a more nuanced understanding of type 2 diabetes risk factors across diverse populations. Improved methodologies for studying gene–environment interactions under various contexts will further elucidate how genetic risk converges with lifestyle and environmental exposures to drive disease progression. By combining these computational innovations with advanced experimental systems, the field is poised not only to bridge the gap between genetic discovery and functional biology but also to inform the development of targeted therapeutic interventions. Ultimately, this progress brings us closer to achieving precision medicine in type 2 diabetes, with treatments tailored to an individual’s genetic and molecular profile and specifically dysregulated pathways, improving outcomes for patients worldwide.
