# RNF185 promotes esophageal squamous cell carcinoma progression by regulating BAK1 ubiquitination and activating the cGAS–STING–IRF3 pathway


## Objective
  RNF185 is a mitochondrial RING-type E3 ubiquitin ligase known to regulate mitochondrial homeostasis, apoptosis, and innate immune signaling through ubiquitination of substrates such as BNIP1, JWA, and cGAS. Although dysregulation of RNF185 has been reported in several cancers, its role in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) remains unclear. The present study aimed to investigate the function of RNF185 in ESCC progression and assess its potential as a therapeutic target.


## Methods
  We analyzed RNF185 expression in the TCGA-ESCA dataset and ESCC cell lines. RNF185 knockout cell lines were generated using CRISPR/Cas9. Functional assays including CCK-8, colony formation, apoptosis analysis, transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and ELISA were performed. Activation of the cGAS–STING pathway and downstream transcription factors was assessed by Western blot, qPCR, luciferase assays, and ChIP-qPCR. BAK1 ubiquitination and interaction with RNF185 were evaluated using immunoprecipitation. In vivo tumor growth inhibition was tested using a subcutaneous mouse xenograft model with Honokiol treatment.


## Results
  RNF185 was significantly overexpressed in ESCC tissues and correlated with worse patient outcomes. RNF185 knockout suppressed cell proliferation, induced apoptosis, and caused mitochondrial damage and mtDNA release, activating the cGAS–STING–IRF3 pathway. Mechanistically, RNF185 mediated BAK1 ubiquitination to maintain mitochondrial integrity. Loss of RNF185 led to BAK1 accumulation and IRF3-dependent transcriptional upregulation of BAK1, forming a positive feedback loop promoting apoptosis. Knockdown of BAK1 or IRF3 rescued mitochondrial structure and cell survival. Honokiol, identified via molecular docking, targeted RNF185, reduced BAK1 ubiquitination, activated innate immune signaling, and inhibited tumor growth in vivo.


## Conclusion
  RNF185 sustains mitochondrial homeostasis and ESCC cell survival by promoting BAK1 ubiquitination and limiting cGAS–STING–IRF3-mediated immune activation. Targeting RNF185 with small molecules like Honokiol may represent a promising therapeutic approach in ESCC.

## Introduction
  Esophageal cancer ranks as the seventh most common cancer and the sixth leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with more than 600,000 new cases and 540,000 deaths annually. Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) is the predominant histological subtype, particularly in East Asia and sub-Saharan Africa, accounting for nearly 85% of all cases [1, 2]. Despite advances in surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immune checkpoint blockade, the 5-year overall survival rate remains below 20% for advanced ESCC [3]. However, the molecular mechanisms that drive ESCC progression and therapeutic resistance remain incompletely understood, necessitating the identification of novel oncogenic drivers and actionable vulnerabilities.

  Recent studies have highlighted that, beyond their metabolic roles, mitochondria are central regulators of cell death and innate immunity in cancer [4, 5]. Mitochondrial stress and membrane destabilization can lead to the cytosolic release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), a potent immunostimulatory molecule capable of activating the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)–stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway [6, 7]. Upon mtDNA binding, cGAS catalyzes the synthesis of cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP), which subsequently activates STING, leading to the phosphorylation of TBK1 and IRF3, and culminating in a type I interferon (IFN-I) response [4, 8]. While this pathway plays a critical role in host defense and immunosurveillance, tumor cells can exploit or suppress cGAS–STING signaling depending on context. Notably, the intrinsic activation of this axis via mtDNA leakage has been observed in certain malignancies, where it contributes to either antitumor immunity or immunosuppression [9]. Nevertheless, how mitochondrial instability induces cGAS–STING signaling in ESCC and whether this can be therapeutically exploited remains largely unexplored.

  RNF185 is a RING-type E3 ubiquitin ligase anchored on the mitochondrial outer membrane. Physiologically, RNF185 contributes to mitochondrial quality control and stress adaptation by mediating the ubiquitination of substrates such as BNIP1 and JWA, thereby regulating mitophagy and endoplasmic reticulum stress responses [10, 11]. Pathologically, dysregulation of RNF185 has been linked to human cancers. For example, RNF185-mediated ubiquitination of JWA promotes gastric cancer metastasis, while aberrant RNF185 expression has been associated with poor prognosis in lung and other malignancies. Beyond cancer metabolism, RNF185 has also been implicated in immunity: Wang et al. reported that RNF185 can facilitate cGAS ubiquitination, thereby enhancing cGAS–STING activation in the context of viral infection [12]. These findings suggest that RNF185 is a multifunctional regulator at the intersection of mitochondrial integrity, tumor progression, and innate immune signaling. However, whether RNF185 contributes to esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) through coordinating mitochondrial stability and immune activation remains unknown.

  BAK1, a pro-apoptotic member of the Bcl-2 family, mediates mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP), enabling the release of apoptogenic factors such as cytochrome C and mtDNA [13]. Recent evidence suggests that BAK1 not only regulates apoptosis but also facilitates mtDNA egress to the cytoplasm, linking mitochondrial damage to cGAS–STING activation [14]. The stability and activity of BAK1, however, are subject to post-translational regulation, including ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal degradation [9, 15], yet the upstream E3 ligase responsible for this in ESCC remains unidentified.

  Here, we demonstrate that RNF185 is highly expressed in ESCC and that its loss suppresses tumor growth by destabilizing mitochondrial integrity, enhancing BAK1 accumulation, and promoting mtDNA leakage. This process activates the cGAS–STING-IRF3 pathway, leading to tumor-intrinsic type I interferon signaling and apoptosis. Moreover, we identify IRF3 as a transcriptional activator of BAK1, establishing a positive feedback loop that reinforces mitochondrial destabilization. We further show that the natural compound Honokiol binds to and inhibits RNF185, recapitulating the antitumor effects observed with RNF185 knockout. This defines a novel RNF185–BAK1–mtDNA–cGAS–STING–IRF3 signaling circuit in ESCC and identifies mitochondrial destabilization-induced immune activation as a promising therapeutic avenue.


## Material and methods

### Cell lines and culture conditions
  Human esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) cell lines KYSE30 (Cat. No. JCRB0188, RRID:CVCL_1351) were obtained from the JCRB Cell Bank. And EC9706 (Cat. No. SB-HU3378, RRID:CVCL_E307) were obtained from the Share-bio Biotech Inc. The murine esophageal carcinoma cell line mEC25 (Cat. No. ATCB63314, RRID:CVCL_C6JH) was obtained from the ATCELBIO Biotech Inc. The immortalized human esophageal epithelial cell line Het-1A was purchased from ATCC and used as a normal esophageal epithelial control. All cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Cells were passaged at a ratio of 1:3 to 1:5 and only used in experiments during the logarithmic growth phase.


### Gene knockout and overexpression
  To investigate the roles of RNF185, BAK1, and IRF3 in ESCC cell function, stable knockout cell lines were constructed using the CRISPR-Cas9 system. Specific sgRNAs targeting human RNF185, BAK1, and IRF3 were designed using an online CRISPR design tool and cloned into the lentiCRISPR v2 vector (Addgene #52961). Lentiviruses were produced by co-transfecting the constructed plasmids and packaging plasmids psPAX2 and pMD2.G into HEK293T cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Supernatants containing viral particles were collected to infect KYSE30 and EC9706 cells. Puromycin (2 µg/mL) selection was performed for 7 days to obtain stable knockout clones, which were validated by qPCR and Western blotting.

  To assess the regulatory effects of IRF3 and IRF7 on BAK1 promoter activity, their coding sequences were cloned into the pcDNA3.1 + eukaryotic expression vector. The expression plasmids were transfected into HEK293T cells using Lipofectamine 3000 according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection for downstream luciferase reporter and molecular assays.


### RNA extraction and qPCR
  Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, 15596026) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were measured with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), ensuring an A260/A280 ratio of 1.8–2.1. cDNA synthesis was performed with 1 µg of total RNA using the HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA wiper) (Vazyme, R323-01). qPCR was conducted using ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q311-02) on an ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech. Reactions were set up in 20 µL volumes in triplicate, with NTC controls included. The cycling conditions were 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2^-ΔΔCt method with GAPDH as the reference gene. All experiments were performed in biological triplicates.


### Western blot analysis
  Cells were lysed in ice-cold RIPA buffer (Beyotime, P0013B) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime, P1050/P1080) for 30 min. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min, and protein concentrations were measured using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 23227). Equal amounts of protein (30 µg) were resolved by SDS-PAGE (10% or 12% gels) and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, IPVH00010). Membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (e.g., anti-RNF185, anti-BAK1, anti-cGAS, anti-STING, anti-p-STING^S365, anti-TBK1, anti-p-TBK1; CST or Abcam). HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (CST, 1:5000) were used for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using ECL reagent (Millipore, WBKLS0500) and imaged with a ChemiDoc MP system (Bio-Rad). Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ.


### Immunoprecipitation and ubiquitination assays
  Cells were lysed in IP lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 1 mM EDTA) with protease/phosphatase inhibitors for 30 min. After centrifugation (12,000 × g, 15 min), equal amounts of protein were incubated with anti-BAK1 (CST) or anti-HA (Abcam) antibodies at 4 °C for 2 h, followed by 50 µL Protein A/G magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher, 88802) for 24 h. Complexes were washed and boiled in 1 × SDS loading buffer for Western blotting. Ubiquitination was detected using an anti-Ubiquitin antibody (Abcam, ab7780).


### Luciferase reporter assay
  A ~ 2 kb region upstream of the BAK1 transcription start site was amplified by PCR and cloned into the pGL4-basic luciferase vector (Promega). The reporter construct was co-transfected with IRF3 or IRF7 expression vectors (pcDNA3.1-IRF3/IRF7) into HEK293T cells using Lipofectamine 3000. After 48 h, luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System (Promega, E1910). Firefly luciferase signals were normalized to Renilla luciferase, and the results were reported as Firefly/Renilla ratios. Experiments were performed in triplicate.


### Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)
  ChIP was performed using the Millipore ChIP Kit (17–295). Cells were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min, quenched with 125 mM glycine, and lysed. Chromatin was sheared to 200–500 bp using a Bioruptor (Diagenode). Sheared chromatin was incubated overnight with anti-IRF3 (CST #11904) or IgG, followed by Protein A magnetic beads. After washes, DNA was eluted and subjected to qPCR for the BAK1 promoter. Input-normalized enrichment was used to assess IRF3 binding.


### CCK-8 cell proliferation assay
  Treated EC9706 and KYSE30 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2,000 cells per well, with 3–5 technical replicates per group. After 24 h of adherence, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo, Japan) was added to each well at designated time points (0, 24, 48, and 72 h). Following 12 h of incubation, absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek). The experiment was independently repeated three times, and growth curves were plotted based on the averaged absorbance values to assess proliferative capacity.


### Colony formation assay
  EC9706 and KYSE30 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 500–1,000 cells per well and cultured for 14 days until visible colonies formed. Cells were gently washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 30 min. Excess dye was removed, plates were air-dried, and colonies with a diameter > 1 mm were counted using ImageJ software. Experiments were performed in triplicate.


### Apoptosis detection
  Apoptotic cells were detected using the Annexin V-FITC/7-AAD Apoptosis Detection Kit (BD Biosciences, 556547) according to the manufacturer's instructions. In brief, both adherent and floating cells were harvested, washed with PBS, resuspended in 1 × binding buffer, and incubated with Annexin V-FITC and 7-AAD in the dark at room temperature for 15 min. Samples were analyzed using a BD FACSCalibur flow cytometer, and the proportion of early (Annexin V⁺/7-AAD⁻) and late apoptotic cells (Annexin V⁺/7-AAD⁺) was quantified using FlowJo software. At least 10,000 events were recorded per sample.


### MitoSOX and ROS detection
  To measure mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS), cells were stained with MitoSOX™ Red Mitochondrial Superoxide Indicator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, M36008). The dye was prepared at a final concentration of 5 μM and added to the cell culture, followed by a 10-min incubation at 37 °C in the dark. After PBS washes, cells were observed under a fluorescence microscope (Ex/Em: 510/580 nm) or harvested for flow cytometric analysis of fluorescence intensity. Each experiment was independently repeated three times.


### Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) of mitochondrial ultrastructure
  To observe the effects of RNF185 or BAK1 knockout on mitochondrial structure, EC9706 and KYSE30 cells were collected and fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C overnight, followed by postfixation with 1% osmium tetroxide for 1 h. After graded ethanol dehydration, cells were embedded in epoxy resin, sectioned into ultrathin slices (70–90 nm), and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Sections were examined under a Hitachi H-7650 transmission electron microscope at 80 kV. At least 20 fields per group were evaluated for mitochondrial membrane integrity, cristae density, and structural disruption.


### Cytochrome C release detection
  To assess apoptosis-induced cytochrome C release from mitochondria into the cytosol, a Cytochrome C ELISA Kit (Cloud-Clone Corp., SEB315Hu) was used. Treated EC9706 and KYSE30 cells were lysed and centrifuged to isolate the cytoplasmic fraction, which was subjected to ELISA per the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was read at 450 nm and cytochrome C concentration was calculated based on a standard curve.


### mtDNA quantification
  Cytosolic mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) release was quantified as an indicator of cGAS–STING pathway activation using an mtDNA ELISA Kit (Abcam, ab287877). Cells were lysed by sonication, and cytosolic fractions were obtained for total DNA extraction. ELISA was performed following the kit protocol. OD450 values were converted to mtDNA concentrations and compared to standard curves. Each group included three technical replicates and the assay was independently repeated three times.


### cGAMP quantification
  To evaluate intracellular cGAMP production as a measure of cGAS enzymatic activity, a cGAMP ELISA Kit (Cayman Chemical, Item No. 501700) was used. Cells were lysed, and cytosolic extracts were processed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured and cGAMP concentrations were calculated using a standard curve.


### RNA sequencing and data analysis
  To explore the transcriptomic impact of RNF185 knockout in esophageal cancer cells, total RNA from sgNTC and sgRNF185 KYSE30 cells was extracted using TRIzol and evaluated for integrity using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (RIN > 7.0). Library construction was performed using the NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit (NEB), and sequencing was conducted on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw reads were quality-checked with FastQC, aligned to the human genome (hg38) using HISAT2, and counted with featureCounts. Differentially expressed genes were identified using DESeq2 (|log2FoldChange|> 1, FDR < 0.05). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted with ClusterProfiler using MSigDB v7.5 Hallmark gene sets, with focus on pathways such as INTERFERON_ALPHA_RESPONSE, DNA_SENSING_PATHWAY, and INFLAMMATORY_RESPONSE.


### Immunoprecipitation–mass spectrometry (IP–LC–MS/MS)
  To identify proteins interacting with RNF185, 293 T cells were transfected to overexpress Myc-RNF185. Following cell lysis and anti-Myc IP, complexes were enriched using Protein A/G magnetic beads, eluted, and digested with trypsin (Promega) overnight. Peptides were purified via C18 columns and subjected to LC–MS/MS (Thermo Orbitrap Fusion). Raw data were processed with MaxQuant for protein identification. Candidate interactors were cross-referenced with cGAS–STING-related pathways (e.g., DNA sensing, interferon responses) in the MSigDB and BioGRID databases to prioritize targets like BAK1.


### Molecular docking analysis (AutoDock Vina)
  To screen for potential small-molecule inhibitors of RNF185, FDA-approved drugs were downloaded from DrugBank and prepared for docking using AutoDockTools (v1.5.6). Predicted RNF185 structures (from AlphaFold or Swiss-Model) were prepared in pdbqt format. Blind docking targeting the E3 ligase pocket was performed using AutoDock Vina. Compounds with binding energies <  − 7 kcal/mol were shortlisted. Binding modes were visualized using PyMOL and Discovery Studio to assess key interactions (e.g., hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic contacts), and Honokiol was selected for further validation.


### Mouse allograft tumor model
  For in vivo tumorigenicity assays, 1 × 106 mEC25 cells (in 100 μL PBS:Matrigel = 1:1) were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old, 18–22 g; n = 6 per group). Tumor growth was monitored every 3 days by caliper measurement, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: V = (length × width2)/2. Mice were randomly assigned to receive vehicle or Honokiol (10 or 20 mg/kg, intragastric gavage, every 3 days starting from day 7 post-inoculation). At day 28, mice were sacrificed, and tumors were excised and weighed. All animal experiments were performed in accordance with protocols approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Wenzhou Medical University (Approval No. xmsq2025-0108).


### Statistical analysis
  All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and R version 4.3.1 with relevant Bioconductor packages. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise indicated. For two-group comparisons, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used. For multiple group comparisons, one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied. Survival analyses (TCGA-ESCA cohort) were performed using the Kaplan–Meier method with log-rank test. Differential gene expression analysis of RNA-seq data was conducted using DESeq2, with false discovery rate (FDR) correction by the Benjamini–Hochberg method. A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Each experiment was independently repeated at least three times unless otherwise stated, and representative results are shown.


## Results

### RNF185 loss suppresses the growth of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) cells
  We first noted that RNF185 is significantly overexpressed in tumor tissues compared to normal esophageal tissues within the TCGA-ESCA dataset (Fig S1A), and that patients with high RNF185 expression showed markedly reduced survival duration (Fig S1B). Similarly, we observed that the expression level of RNF185 was significantly higher in esophageal cancer cell lines compared to immortalized esophageal mucosal epithelial cells (Fig S1C–D). These findings suggest that RNF185 may be of critical research interest in ESCC. To further evaluate whether targeting RNF185 could hold therapeutic potential for esophageal cancer, we designed sgRNAs to generate RNF185-knockout EC9706 and KYSE30 cell lines (Fig. 1A, B). We found that RNF185 deletion significantly inhibited ESCC cell proliferation (Fig. 1C), and markedly reduced the number of colonies formed (Fig. 1D). Additionally, the proportion of Annexin V⁺/7AAD⁺ double-positive apoptotic cells was notably increased in sgR185 cells (Fig. 1E), accompanied by elevated expression of cytochrome C (Fig. 1F). These results attracted our attention, since cytochrome C is a key component of the mitochondrial electron transport chain, and its increased release indicates mitochondrial structural disruption. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) confirmed that mitochondria in sgR185 cells were significantly damaged (Fig. 1G). Furthermore, we observed a substantial increase in mtDNA levels in sgR185 cells (Fig. 1H), suggesting that mitochondrial damage may trigger activation of cytosolic DNA sensing pathways. Cytosolic mtDNA, as an endogenous immune stimulant, has been widely recognized to activate innate immune responses via the cGAS–STING pathway. Therefore, we hypothesized that RNF185 deficiency may activate cGAS–STING signaling through mitochondrial damage and mtDNA leakage [16, 17]. ### RNF185 deficiency activates the cGAS–STING pathway to promote mitochondria-mediated apoptosis in ESCC cells

### RNF185 promotes BAK1 ubiquitination to maintain mitochondrial stability
  RNF185 is a mitochondrial outer-membrane E3 ligase that promotes mitophagy and mitochondrial homeostasis via K63-linked ubiquitination of BNIP1 and recruitment of the p62–LC3 machinery; it also functions in ER protein quality control as part of an ERAD complex, and has been implicated in cancer progression and innate immune regulation through cGAS ubiquitination [10, 12, 16]. To identify proteins interacting with RNF185, we performed IP–LC–MS/MS analysis. Among several candidates, we focused on BAK1, a pro-apoptotic member of the Bcl-2 family, because of its well-established role in mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP) and release of cytochrome C and mtDNA. Given our observation that RNF185 loss induces mitochondrial disruption and mtDNA leakage, BAK1 represented the most mechanistically relevant interactor for further investigation (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, RNA-seq analysis of sgNTC vs. sgRNF185 KYSE30 cells revealed differentially expressed genes (Fig. 3B, C), and GSEA indicated a positive correlation between RNF185 and HALLMARK_INTERFERON_ALPHA_RESPONSE as well as HALLMARK_INTERFERON_GAMMA_RESPONSE (Fig. 3D, E). We then observed that BAK1 ubiquitination was markedly reduced in sgR185 cells (Fig. 3F). To validate the interaction, we co-transfected 293 T cells with HA-BAK1 and Myc-RNF185 plasmids and found that anti-HA could co-immunoprecipitate Myc-tagged RNF185, and vice versa (Fig. 3G, H). ### IRF3 functions as a transcriptional activator of BAK1
  In our earlier results (Fig. 3I, J), we observed that BAK1 mRNA and protein levels were significantly elevated in sgR185 cells. Given that RNF185 is an E3 ligase typically involved in post-translational regulation, the increase in BAK1 mRNA expression suggests possible transcriptional regulation via downstream cGAS–STING signaling. Notably, RNF185 expression was positively correlated with IFN-alpha and IFN-gamma Hallmark pathways. Among downstream effectors of STING, IRF3 and IRF7 are key transcription factors. We cloned the BAK1 promoter into a pGL4 luciferase vector and co-transfected it with IRF3 or IRF7 overexpression constructs into 293 T cells. Overexpression of IRF3 in KYSE30 and EC9706 cells led to increased BAK1 expression at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 5D, E). In the TCGA-ESCA dataset, RNF185 expression was negatively correlated with BAK1, cGAS (MB21D1), STING (TMEM173), TBK1, and IRF3 (Fig. 5F). ### sgIRF3 preserves mitochondrial integrity by limiting BAK1 transcription in sgR185 cells
  To further confirm that RNF185 deletion leads to IRF3-mediated transcriptional activation of BAK1, which disrupts mitochondrial homeostasis, we generated IRF3-knockout cells (sgIRF3) in the sgR185 background (Fig. 6A, B). IRF3 loss led to significantly reduced BAK1 expression (Fig. 6C, D), decreased mtDNA and cytochrome C levels (Fig. 6E, F), and preservation of mitochondrial structure (Fig. 6G, H). Additionally, the apoptotic rate was markedly reduced (Fig. 6I), and cell viability was restored (Fig. 6J). Collectively, these results support a model wherein RNF185 maintains mitochondrial homeostasis by controlling BAK1 protein stability; its loss leads to increased BAK1, mitochondrial damage, mtDNA leakage, and cGAS–STING–IRF3 activation. ## Discussion
  In this study, we identify RNF185 as a previously unrecognized mitochondrial gatekeeper that restrains innate immune activation and apoptosis in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC). We demonstrate that RNF185 promotes the ubiquitin-dependent degradation of BAK1, thereby preserving mitochondrial integrity and preventing the release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), cytochrome C, and reactive oxygen species (ROS). RNF185 loss leads to BAK1 accumulation and mitochondrial destabilization, which in turn triggers the activation of the cGAS–STING–IRF3 pathway and results in tumor-intrinsic type I interferon responses and cell death. Furthermore, we discover a positive feedback loop wherein IRF3 transcriptionally enhances BAK1 expression, amplifying the cytosolic DNA sensing response. Pharmacologic inhibition of RNF185 using Honokiol recapitulates these effects in vivo, offering a promising strategy to restore innate immune signaling and suppress ESCC progression.

  Our findings highlight a crucial link between mitochondrial homeostasis and immune activation in tumor cells, mediated by RNF185. Although RNF185 was previously implicated in mitophagy and ER stress responses via targeting BNIP1 and JWA [11], its role in restraining BAK1 stability and mitochondrial DNA release has not been reported. Given that BAK1 is a central effector of mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP) [18, 19], its stabilization in RNF185-deficient cells provides a mechanistic basis for the observed release of mtDNA and subsequent immune activation. This positions RNF185 as a dual regulator of mitochondrial integrity and innate immune evasion in ESCC.

  Activation of the cGAS–STING pathway in tumor cells has largely been attributed to exogenous stressors such as genotoxic damage or viral infection [16, 20, 21]. Our study supports a growing body of evidence that tumor-intrinsic triggers, such as mitochondrial damage and mtDNA leakage, are sufficient to initiate this pathway [17, 22]. Notably, the marked upregulation of cGAS expression and phosphorylation of STING and TBK1 in RNF185-deficient cells strongly supports this endogenous activation model. Previous studies have proposed that mtDNA is a potent ligand for cGAS [23], and our findings confirm that RNF185 depletion unleashes this innate immune trigger, resulting in robust cGAMP production and downstream IFN-I responses.

  A particularly novel aspect of our study is the identification of a transcriptional feedback circuit linking innate immune activation to mitochondrial destabilization. IRF3, a canonical downstream effector of STING, is traditionally recognized for its role in inducing antiviral and interferon-stimulated genes [24]. Here, we show that IRF3 directly binds to and activates the BAK1 promoter, thereby enhancing mitochondrial apoptosis and further mtDNA leakage. This feedback loop creates a self-reinforcing cycle that amplifies tumor-intrinsic immune activation—a mechanism that may be particularly advantageous in “cold” tumors such as ESCC, which often lack immune infiltration and are resistant to immunotherapy [23–25].

  The identification of Honokiol as an RNF185 inhibitor adds a valuable translational dimension to our study. Honokiol, a natural compound derived from Magnolia officinalis, has been reported to exhibit antitumor properties in various cancer types [26], but its direct molecular targets remain poorly defined. Our docking analysis and functional validation suggest that Honokiol may inhibit the E3 ligase activity of RNF185, leading to BAK1 stabilization and immune reactivation. These findings raise the possibility of developing small-molecule RNF185 inhibitors to convert immune-resistant tumors into immunogenic ones, either as monotherapy or in combination with immune checkpoint blockade. Indeed, recent work has emphasized the importance of restoring STING activity in tumors to overcome PD-1 resistance [27], and our data suggest that RNF185 suppression may represent a novel strategy to achieve this.

  While our findings provide compelling evidence for a RNF185–BAK1–cGAS–STING circuit in ESCC, several limitations warrant discussion. First, our study relies primarily on subcutaneous tumor models; orthotopic or transgenic models may better capture the tumor microenvironment and immune interactions. Second, although Honokiol shows promise, its specificity for RNF185 remains to be fully characterized through direct binding assays and structural studies. Third, we did not examine the role of RNF185 in immune cells, where it may exert distinct functions. Future investigations should explore whether RNF185 expression correlates with immune evasion signatures in clinical ESCC cohorts and whether its inhibition can synergize with PD-1 or CTLA-4 blockade in immunocompetent models.


## Conclusion
  Our work uncovers a previously unappreciated role for RNF185 in maintaining mitochondrial integrity and suppressing innate immune activation in ESCC. By controlling BAK1 ubiquitination and limiting mtDNA release, RNF185 prevents the activation of the cGAS–STING–IRF3 axis and subsequent apoptosis. Targeting this pathway through genetic deletion or pharmacologic inhibition rewires tumor cell fate, converting an immune-silent phenotype into an immunogenic one. These findings not only broaden our understanding of tumor-intrinsic innate immunity but also highlight RNF185 as a viable therapeutic target for ESCC and potentially other mitochondria-dependent cancers.
