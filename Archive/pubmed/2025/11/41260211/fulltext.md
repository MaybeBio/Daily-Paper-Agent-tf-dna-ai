# Structure of the transcriptional co-activator SAGA complex, including the histone acetyltransferase module


## Summary
  The SAGA (Spt-Ada-Gcn5 acetyltransferase) complex, a 1.8MDa multi-subunit assembly comprising 19 subunits, is required for RNA polymerase II transcription in eukaryotes. The complex consists of four modules: Tra1 (Transcription-associated protein 1), Core, DUB (Deubiquitination) and HAT (Histone Acetyltransferase). Although the structures of the Tra1, Core, and DUB modules have been determined, the overall architecture of the HAT module remained elusive due to its inherent flexibility. To address this, we conducted cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) analyses on SAGA purified from the thermophilic fungus Chaetomium thermophilum, yielding structures of Tra1 and Core modules at 2.6 Å and three of the four HAT subunits at 3.7 Å. The structure of HAT module was informative about the aspects of histone acetylation and the interface of HAT-Core modules, contradicting earlier AlphaFold predictions. Our structure-guided genetic and biochemical analyses confirmed the roles of Ada1 and Spt7 in anchoring the HAT module within the SAGA complex.


## Introduction
  The eukaryotic transcription co-activator SAGA (Spt-Ada-Gcn5 acetyltransferase) is a highly conserved 1.8 MDa, 19 subunit protein complex composed of four modules: the Tra1 (1 subunit) module, which interacts with activators, the core module (10 subunits), which deposits TBP onto the promoter, the histone H3 acetyltransferase module (4 subunits), which acetylates several lysines of the histone H3 tails, and the DUB module (4 subunits), which possesses histone H2B deubiquitinase activity 1–3. Discovery of the histone acetyltransferase activity of the Gcn5 component of the HAT module attracted great interest because of the previous identification of Gcn5 as a transcription factor 4,5. Previous structural studies of yeast and human proteins have solved structures of Tra1 (hTRRAP), core plus Tra1, DUB, and DUB plus nucleosome core particle (NCP) 6–10. The structure of the HAT module was long sought, but remained unsolved, due to flexibility 6.

  The HAT module comprises the catalytic Gcn5 (hKAT2) subunit and three additional proteins, Ada2 (hTADA2b), Ada3 (hTADA3) and Sgf29 (h in parentheses indicates human orthologs). Gcn5 contains a bromodomain that binds to lysines and confers specificity for the lysines of the histone H3 amino-terminal tails 11,12. Ada2, Ada3 and Sgf29 modulate the activity and specificity of Gcn5 13–16, which alone acetylates H3 tail peptides but fails to acetylate nucleosomes. The addition of Ada2 and Ada3 enables activity on nucleosomes 17,18. Sgf29 contains a Tudor domain that recognizes H3K4 trimethylated histones and is important for H3 acetylation in vivo, although not required in vitro 15,19,20.

  Structural information about the HAT module is limited to crystal structures of the Gcn5 catalytic domain and fragments of other HAT module subunits 21–24. The overall structure of the HAT module and its spatial relationship to the rest of the SAGA complex was not determined. Recently, an attempt was made using AlphaFold-Multimer prediction combined with biochemical data to determine the position of the HAT module in relation to SAGA 25. Due to the lack of structural information, the prediction was not tested.

  We sought to overcome the problem of flexibility of the HAT module by structure determination of the SAGA complex from a thermophilic fungus, Chaetomium thermophilum 26. Here we report the structure of ctSAGA including the HAT module. The structures of ctTra1 and core resemble those of SAGA from Saccharomyces cerevisiae (sc) 6,10, with minor differences (Figure 1A–1E). The structure of the HAT module accords with previous genetic and biochemical findings. Additional electron density was observed connecting the Spt8/DUB region to the HAT module. (Figure 1F and 1G).


## Results

### Purification and structure determination of the SAGA complex from Chaetomium thermophilum
  Previous structural studies have been performed with SAGA complexes isolated from S. cerevisiae, human cell lines, and Pichia pastoris, or subunits co-expressed in E. coli 6–9,27,28. To address the problem of flexibility and conformational heterogeneity of the HAT module 6,10, we turned to a thermophilic fungus, as thermophiles have previously shown advantages in the structure determination of large protein assemblies 26,29–31. We isolated the SAGA complex from the thermophilic fungus, Chaetomium thermophilum. The gene encoding the Spt7 subunit was identified by sequence homology and a C-terminal FLAG-TEV-Protein A tag was introduced, cloned and transformed into ct protoplasts as described 32 with modifications (see STAR Methods). In the last step of purification, size exclusion chromatography, a single peak was observed, eluting at 1.2 ml, immediately following the void volume (1ml) of a Superose 6 3.2/300 column, as expected for the 1.8MDa SAGA complex (Figure S1A and S1B). The peak fraction was crosslinked and plunge frozen for cryo-EM data collection. The protein preferred thick ice and the 2D class averages showed SAGA in multiple orientations (Figure S1C and S1D). In parallel, the peak fraction was subjected to mass spectrometric proteomic analysis, which identified the subunits of the ctSAGA complex (Figure 1H and Table S1).

  Cryo-EM data acquisition and image processing resulted in an electron density map of ctSAGA at 2.7 Å resolution, in which Tra1 and core were well resolved, with distinct amino acid side chain densities. Focused 3D classification of Tra1 and core resulted Tra1 at ~2.6 Å (Figure 1, S2, S3 and Table S2). The HAT and DUB modules were poorly resolved due to flexibility, as expected 6,10. However, upon focused 3D classification in the HAT module region, an electron density map of the minimal HAT module at a nominal resolution of 3.7 Å was obtained (Figure S2). A model was built in most of the ctSAGA electron density map; parts that could not be built were either disordered, as predicted by the Prediction of Natural Disordered Regions (PONDR) tool, or missing, possibly due to flexibility (Figure 1H).


### Structure of ctSAGA, Tra1 and core
  To explore the conformational diversity within ctSAGA, we conducted 3D variability analysis (3DVA) using CryoSPARC 33. This analysis generated a series of movie frames depicting the conformational landscape of various components of SAGA. Notably, the HAT module exhibited significant mobility, exploring a wide spatial range, while connected to the tip of the SAGA core (see Movie 1).

  Despite sequence identity of less than 40% to scSAGA, the ctSAGA map could be aligned with the published scSAGA map (emdb: 10412), showing similar overall architecture (Figure 1A and 1B). By contrast, human SAGA differs significantly in structure from scSAGA 27. When the high-resolution electron density maps and structures of ct and scSAGA were aligned, a shift of about 36 Å of the SAGA core tips was observed (Figure S4B), suggesting a slightly tilted conformation of ctSAGA relative to scSAGA. The structural resemblance of ct and scTra1 (Figure S4C) was especially noteworthy. Within the ctSAGA core, an asymmetric histone-like fold composed of Taf6–Taf9, Taf10–Spt7 and Taf12–Ada1, and two histone folds of Spt3, were almost identical to the histone-like folds in scSAGA (Figure S4D). The Taf5 and Taf6 subunits of ct exhibited strong structural similarity to their sc counterparts, particularly in the C-terminal WD40 repeats of Taf5 and the N-terminal HEAT repeats of TAF6 (Figure S4E, upper panel). Alignment of N-terminus of ctAda1 (hTADA1) and ctSpt7 (hSUPTL7L) with the corresponding subunits of scSAGA was not possible because they were not resolved in the scSAGA structure. Focused 3D classification in the core tip region resolved secondary structural features in ctSpt7. A best fit model, obtained with AlphaFold-Multimer followed by Phenix Real Space refinement, corresponded to the N-terminal region of Spt7 (Figure 2), a highly negatively charged protein with an N-terminal bromodomain 34. The best fit model contained a four-helix bundle (aa 332–435) resembling a canonical bromodomain, cantilevered by two helices, α1 (aa 208–229) and α2 (aa 238–262), with two helix extensions, Helix 1 (aa 286–321) and Helix 2 (aa 433–455), stretching towards the HAT module (Figures 2B and 2C and Movie 2). The sequence and structure predicted by AlphaFold for the yeast bromodomain region are highly conserved between sc and ctSpt7 (Figure S4A). Deletion of the scSpt7 bromodomain has no detectable phenotype, so its role in Spt7 function is unknown 35. The Ada1 subunit, which together with Taf12 is involved in the formation of a histone-like fold in SAGA core, was thought to reside entirely within the SAGA core 6, but our structure shows that the N-terminal region of Ada1 is part of the HAT module, detailed in the next section.


### Structure of the HAT module
  The overall architecture of the SAGA complex shows the minimal HAT module of Gcn5, Ada2 and Ada3 positioned adjacent to the SAGA core tip with Spt7 extensions interacting with Ada3 (Figure 2). Within the HAT module, Gcn5 is nested between Ada2 and Ada3, while the base of the HAT module containing the Ada2 SWIRM domain interacts with the Ada1 subunit of the SAGA core (Figures 3C and 3D). Focused 3D classification of the HAT region of ctSAGA resulted in an electron density map at a nominal resolution of 3.7 Å (Figure S2). Density for amino acid side chains was apparent in a significant portion of the map (Figure S5A). Models could be built for three of four subunits of the HAT module, Gcn5, Ada2 and Ada3, with a very good map-to-model fit (Figure 3B and Movie 2). There was no electron density that could be assigned to the fourth subunit of the module, Sgf29, likely due to its flexible association with the rest of the module, consistent with previous reports of Sgf29 as peripherally associated with the HAT module 15,25.

  Gcn5 comprises an N-terminal catalytic HAT domain, an Ada2 interaction domain, and a C-terminal bromodomain (Figure 3A). In our structure, the Gcn5 HAT domain (aa 65–226) consists of four anti-parallel β strands followed by an α helix and another β strand, with catalytic residue E139 at the center, all bracketed by Ada2 (Figure 3D). These observations are consistent with a previous crystal structure of a complex of truncated domains of Ada2 and Gcn5 23. The HAT domain of Gcn5 is followed by an Ada2 interaction domain (aa 227–277) that passes alongside the SANT domain of Ada2 and forms many contacts with the ZnF region of Ada2 (Figures 3D and S5C). Finally, the C-terminal region of Gcn5 (aa 295–405) forms a canonical bromodomain at the periphery of the HAT module adjacent to the active center region (Figure 3D). The Gcn5 bromodomain is known to interact with histone H3 and H4 tails 36,37 and has been implicated in the recognition of acetylated lysines of target proteins, and in cooperative and cross tail acetylation of nucleosomes 38.

  Ada2 comprises three chromatin interaction domains, an N-terminal Zinc Finger (ZnF) domain of no known function, a SANT domain that contributes to Gcn5 HAT activity by enhancing binding of Gcn5 to acetyl Co-A 23, and a SWIRM 39 domain implicated in chromatin remodeling and gene expression 3. The structure suggests that Ada2 serves a scaffolding function, with many contacts to Ada3 and Gcn5 (Figures 3C and S5B). This observation is in agreement with a previous crosslinking and mass spectrometric study in yeast, showing that deletion of Ada2 caused loss of association of the other three subunits of the HAT module, whereas deletion of Gcn5 or Sgf29 did not 40. The C-terminal SWIRM domain of Ada2 interacts with Ada3 and the N-terminal region of Ada1, part of the SAGA core. The N-terminal region of Ada1 extends from the base of the SAGA core and interacts with Ada3, as well as the SWIRM domain of Ada2. This region of Ada1 is hereafter referred to as the SWIRM-interacting domain (SID). Together, Ada1-SID (residues 61–129), the SWIRM domain of Ada2 (residues 453–514), and Ada3 (residues 394–530) form a multi-helical bundle (Figures 3E and 3F). The SWIRM domain of Ada2 features a helix-turn-helix motif, with four helices that align closely with those of a SWIRM domain in Swi3, a subunit of the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex (Figures S5D and S5E). Ada3 contains an elongated coiled coil region (aa 573–634) that interacts with most of the of C-terminal region of Ada2 (aa 233–326) and the SWIRM domain of Ada2 (Figures 3C and 3E).

  Focused 3D classification in the HAT region of ctSAGA revealed a subset of particles showing electron density that was not previously reported, a prominent electron density connecting the Spt8/DUB and Ada2/Gcn5 regions (Figures 1F and 1G). The resolution was not sufficient for model building. The density might correspond to an elongated coiled coil structure of Sgf29 predicted by AlphaFold (data not shown).


### The HAT module is tethered to the SAGA core via Ada1 and Spt7.
  Recently, Alphafold-Multimer prediction combined with biochemical data led to the proposal that the HAT module is tethered by the SWIRM domain of Ada2 to Spt7 of the SAGA core 25. On the contrary, our structure revealed different interactions between the HAT module and the SAGA core (see Movie 2). The N-terminal region of Spt7 makes two contacts with C-terminal region of Ada3 (Figures 4A and 4B). Two Spt7 helices (Helix 1 and Helix2) extend from the core and clamp Ada3. Helix 1 makes many contacts with Ada3, and Helix 2 is in close proximity to the C-terminal region of Ada3. Based on sequence homology and AlphaFold structure prediction, scSpt7 Helix 1 and Helix 2 exhibit high structural similarity to their ct counterparts (Figures S4A left and S6). In support of our results, previous studies in sc showed crosslinks between the C-terminal region of Ada3 and the bromodomain region of Spt7. No cross-links were observed between the SWIRM domain of Ada2 and Spt7 2. In addition to the interactions with the two Spt7 helices, the N-terminal Ada1 SID, extending from the base of SAGA core, interacts with the SWIRM domain of Ada2 and with Ada3 (Figures 3C, 3E and 3F). The Ada1 interaction with the HAT module is significant as it forms a multi-helical bundle with Ada2 SWIRM and Ada3 helices (Figures 3E and 3F). Our results clearly demonstrate that the HAT module is anchored to the SAGA core via Spt7 and Ada1 (Movie 2).


### Functional validation of structural observations and organization of SAGA
  To obtain genetic and biochemical evidence for anchoring of the HAT module to the SAGA core via Ada1 and Spt7, we turned to sc, as such studies in ct are hindered by the limited availability of selectable markers. Through sequence homology and AlphaFold structure predictions, we identified the regions in sc corresponding to Spt7 Helix 1 and Helix 2, and the Ada1 SID (Figures S4A, S6 and S7). Our structure suggests that deleting one or more of these sites of interaction will disrupt the association of the HAT module with the rest of the SAGA complex. We proceeded by generating an sc double knockout yeast strain lacking both ADA1 (HfI1) and SPT7 and expressing 3xFLAG-tagged Gcn5 (strain: ZRM022), via an intermediate strain (ZRM017) lacking ADA1 and expressing 3xFLAG-tagged Gcn5 (Table 1 and see STAR Methods for details). We then constructed a panel of Spt7 deletion mutants targeting Helix 1 (M1), Helix 2 (M2), or both (M3), as well as broader deletions encompassing regions flanking the helices. These deletions included the bromodomain (M4), partial removal of the N-terminal region (M5), and complete deletion of the N-terminal region (M6) (Figure 5A). All Spt7 variants were cloned into plasmid p416-ADH (Ura3). These constructs were analyzed in the Ada1 SID deletion (expressed from pRS403, His3) background and compared to wild-type Ada1 using plasmid-based expression systems in yeast.

  We first performed spot assays of the yeast transformed with the Spt7 mutants and ΔSID-Ada1 plasmids for possible growth defects on the selective synthetic media (Figure 5B, left). Since the SAGA complex plays a dominant role in stress-inducible gene expression, we also tested mutants on plates supplemented with Sulfonyl urea herbicide, Sulfometuron Methyl (SMM). The SMM herbicide inhibits acetolactate synthase encoded by the ILV2 gene, resulting in stress due to amino acid starvation for isoleucine and valine 41,42. Of all the mutants tested, only mutants M5/ΔSID-Ada1 and M6/ΔSID-Ada1, which have the longest deletions in Spt7, showed pronounced growth defect on defined auxotrophic media (Figure 5B, left). The Ada1 SID deletion alone showed a modest growth defect. However, on plates supplemented with SMM all Ada1-SID deletion mutants showed growth defects, which were more pronounced when combined with Spt7 Helix 1 deletion (ΔH1-Spt7/ΔSID-Ada1). The combination of Spt7 Helix 2 deletion with ΔSID-Ada1 (M2/SID-Ada1) showed a growth defect comparable to Ada1-SID deletion alone (Figure 5B, right).

  To further assess the roles of Spt7 Helix 1 (H1), Spt7 Helix 2 (H2), and the Ada1 SID in the SAGA complex, we purified all the mutants with Spt7 C-terminal Twin-StrepTagII using StrepTactin-XT resin followed by western blot against StrepTagII (Spt7), Anti-FLAG (Gcn5) and Anti-Taf5. The western blot showed that Ada1 SID deletion alone caused a substantial loss of Gcn5 from the SAGA complex without affecting the SAGA core (revealed by Anti-Taf5). The combined deletion of Spt7 helix 1 and Ada1-SID (M1/ΔSID-Ada1) resulted in near-complete loss of Gcn5 from the complex. In contrast, deletion of Helix 2 with ΔSID-Ada1 (M2/ΔSID-Ada1) showed comparable Gcn5 loss to ΔSID-Ada1 alone. The Spt7 mutant with deletion of both the helices and ΔSID-Ada1 (M3/ΔSID-Ada1) showed near total loss of Gcn5, like M1/ΔSID-Ada1. Other mutant combinations (M4–M6/ΔSID-Ada1) also showed Gcn5 loss comparable to M1/ΔSID-Ada1. M5/ΔSID-Ada1 and M6/ΔSID-Ada1, which contain more extensive deletions of Spt7, led to the destabilization of the SAGA core, as indicated by the loss of Taf5. When the M3 mutant (ΔH1/ΔH2-Spt7) was expressed in a wild-type Ada1 background, a substantial amount of Gcn5 was retained within the SAGA complex (Figure 5C).


### Superposition of the Tetrahymena thermophila HAT domain with the ctHAT domain revealed the path of the histone H3 tail in the HAT module
  Tetrahymena thermophila (tt) Gcn5 and ctGcn5 exhibit a high degree of amino acid sequence conservation, with over 50% sequence identity in the HAT domain region (Figure S8A). We superposed the crystal structure of a ttGcn5 catalytic domain-histone H3 tail peptide-acetyl CoA complex with our ctGcn5 catalytic domain structure (Figures 6A–6C) with the use of ChimeraX matchmaker 43,44, and nominal fits were obtained with RMSD 1.56 for overall atom pairs and RMSD 1.045 with long atom pairs removed (Figures S8B–S8D). After structure fitting, the HAT domain region of ttGcn5 was removed, leaving the H3 peptide and acetyl CoA complexed with the ctHAT module. A surface representation showed no apparent stereochemical clashes in the complex (Figures 6D and 6E). The H3 peptide was seen to run down a narrow channel in the HAT domain and emerge upon encountering a protein loop (aa 192–204) of Ada2 (Figures 6E and 6F). Sequence alignment of sc and ctAda2 showed this loop is part of a highly conserved region of Ada2 (Figure S8E). The superposition of structures also revealed that the Gcn5 bromodomain is in proximity to the histone H3 tail bound to the active site of the HAT catalytic domain (Figure 6F).


## Discussion
  The overall architecture of ctSAGA, including the Tra1 and core modules, closely resembles that of its sc counterpart. Notably, a slight tilt observed in the ctSAGA core suggests an inherent structural flexibility, which may have functional significance. This is consistent with previous structural studies of scSAGA 6, which demonstrated that the core becomes more flexible upon nucleosome interaction, potentially implying a conserved dynamic feature linked to chromatin interaction.

  Among the multiple functions of the SAGA complex, the histone acetyltransferase activity has attracted particular attention, because of the central role of histone acetylation in gene activation and transcription. The lack of evidence for the HAT module has therefore been a major deficiency of SAGA structures determined to date. The problem could arise from mobility of the HAT module with respect to the rest of the SAGA structure or disorder of the module itself. We therefore pursued an approach that has been effective in some cases in the past, structure determination of the corresponding proteins from thermophiles, expected to be less flexible under ambient conditions. We investigated the structure of the SAGA complex from the thermophilic fungus Chaetomium thermophilum, which has an optimal growth temperature of 50–55°C. We were able to identify and isolate ctSAGA and determine its structure by cryo-EM. In contrast with structures from other organisms, including sc, also a fungus but with an optimal growth temperature of 30°C, the ctSAGA structure revealed the HAT module. Variability analysis of ctSAGA by CryoSPARC showed the HAT module still exhibited motion with respect to the rest of the SAGA structure, but a high-resolution electron density map of the HAT module could be obtained by focused classification. The ctSAGA structure further revealed that the HAT module is anchored to the SAGA core through previously unresolved regions of Spt7 and Ada1 subunits. The Spt7 region includes a bromodomain and at least two helical extensions, Helix 1 and Helix 2 that embrace the HAT module (Figures 2 and 4). The N-terminal region of Ada1 (Hfi1), previously thought to reside solely within the SAGA core 6, extends outward to form a multi-helical bundle with the SWIRM domain of Ada2 and with Ada3 (Figures 3C, 3E and 3F). These interactions collectively stabilize the interface between the HAT module and the core of the complex.

  A model for the HAT module led to several notable findings:

  First, the bromodomain of Gcn5 is located immediately adjacent to the HAT catalytic domain. Previous biochemical studies have shown that the Gcn5 bromodomain is required for cooperative acetylation of nucleosomes, and that it enhances acetylation on one H3 tail when the other H3 tail of the nucleosome is already acetylated 38. Physical proximity of the bromodomain to the catalytic domain (Figure 6F) may play a role in this cooperative acetylation behavior.

  Second, the structure of the N-terminal domain of the Spt7 subunit of the core, a point of connection to the HAT module, includes an additional bromodomain, previously unresolved. Deletion of this bromodomain in yeast did not show any phenotype 35,45. This Spt7 bromodomain is nevertheless capable of binding H3K9ac 46, and when substituted for the bromodomain of Gcn5, the SAGA complex retains the ability to bind nucleosomes. Inasmuch as the Spt7 bromodomain lies on the same face of SAGA and nearby the catalytic center, it may play a yet unrecognized role in cooperative acetylation. While an earlier AlphaFold-based model placed the Spt7 bromodomain far from the Gcn5 bromodomain and catalytic center 25, our structure reveals a much closer spatial relationship among the Spt7 and Gcn5 bromodomains and the catalytic center, offering possible insights into the cooperative or regulatory mechanism of SAGA-mediated acetylation. This newfound proximity of the bromodomains to the HAT catalytic center may have significant functional implications and lays a basis for future studies.

  Third, superposition of the structure of a ttGcn5-histone peptide-acetyl CoA complex on the structure of ctGcn5 revealed a path of the peptide that abuts a conserved loop of the Ada2 subunit (Figure 6F). Movement of the histone tail through the peptide binding groove of Gcn5 would appear to be obstructed. Previous biochemical studies have shown that the Gcn5 bromodomain is involved in cooperative acetylation of nucleosomes and that it enhances acetylation of one histone H3 tail when the other H3 tail within the nucleosome is already acetylated 38. Structural alignment of the ttHAT domain with the ctHAT module reveals proximity of the Gcn5 bromodomain to the histone tail in the catalytic center of the HAT domain. This positioning raises the possibility of an acetylation mechanism in which the bromodomain anchors an acetylated histone tail, allowing dissociation and rebinding to additional sites, thereby overcoming the obstruction by the Ada2 loop. In this way, the bromodomain would enhance the retention of the SAGA complex and promoting acetylation elsewhere in the same tail or on other tails of the same or adjacent nucleosomes.

  Fourth, the structure of the Ada2 subunit suggests a scaffolding role in the HAT module. Ada2 interacts with Ada1, Ada3, and Gcn5. The structure thus explains the finding that deletion of Ada2 causes loss of association of the other three subunits of the HAT module with SAGA 40. In addition to this structural role, Ada2 contains a SANT domain positioned close to the Gcn5 catalytic domain. The SANT domain is thought to contribute to Gcn5 acetylation activity by enhancing the binding of acetyl Co-A 23. Moreover, a helical region of Ada2 (residues 230–260) interacts with an α-helix on the side of the HAT domain opposite to the SANT domain. This strategic positioning suggests a potential regulatory role for the Ada2 helix in modulating HAT activity (Figures 3C and 3D).

  Fifth, a previous AlphaFold-Multimer model proposed that the SWIRM domain of Ada2 directly interacts with the tip of the SAGA core, specifically with Spt7, thereby anchoring the HAT module to the SAGA complex 25. In this model, Gcn5 is positioned distally, projecting away from the core. In contrast, our structure reveals a markedly different organization in which Gcn5 is situated near the tip of the SAGA core in proximity to Spt7, and the Ada2 SWIRM domain connects to the base of the SAGA core via the Ada1 SID. As a result, the predicted orientation of the HAT module is inverted with respect to that in our structure. AlphaFold-Multimer prediction in this case does not accurately reflect the true architecture of the complex.

  To functionally validate our structural model of HAT module-SAGA interaction, we employed genetic and biochemical approaches in sc, a system with suitable genetic tools. Using a yeast strain lacking both SPT7 and ADA1, and expressing FLAG-tagged Gcn5 (ZRM022), we tested a panel of Spt7 mutants (M1–M6) in combination with ΔSID-Ada1 for functional consequences. Under normal growth conditions, only mutants with extended deletions in Spt7 (M5 and M6) in the ΔSID-Ada1 background exhibited significant growth defects. However, under stress conditions induced by Sulfometuron Methyl (SMM), Spt7 Helix 1 deletion in combination with ΔSID-Ada1, led to pronounced growth defects. Spt7 Helix 2 deletion did not exacerbate the phenotype beyond that of ΔSID-Ada1 alone, and reintroducing wild-type Ada1 in a mutant bearing deletion of both Helix1 and Helix 2 (ΔH1/ΔH2 Spt7) rescued cells from severe growth defect (Figure 5 B, right).

  Biochemical analyses confirmed that deletion of the Ada1 SID alone significantly reduced the association of Gcn5, and thus of the HAT module, with the SAGA complex. When combined with deletion of Spt7 Helix 1, this effect was exacerbated, resulting in nearly complete loss of Gcn5. In contrast, the combined deletion of Ada1-SID and Spt7 Helix 2 led to a Gcn5 reduction comparable to that of ΔSID-Ada1 alone, suggesting that Helix 2 does not play a substantial role in anchoring the HAT module. Indeed our structure, which showed a well-defined interface of Helix 1 with the HAT module, show no clear interface with Helix 2. Moreover, sc spot assays demonstrated that deletion of Helix 2 combined with ΔSID-Ada1 exhibited a similar growth defect under stress conditions to ΔSID-Ada1 alone (Figure 5B, right). The loss of Gcn5 observed in extended Spt7 deletion mutants (M5/M6) combined with ΔSID-Ada1 is attributed to destabilization of the SAGA core, as evidenced by the loss of core subunit Taf5 (Figure 5C). This structural disruption correlates with the severe growth defects exhibited by these mutants under normal conditions (Figure 5B, left). Notably, the interactions of Ada1-SID and Spt7 Helix 1 with the HAT module are conserved across fungal species. Previous biochemical studies have also shown that deletion of the SWIRM domain of Ada2 leads to a marked reduction in HAT components of the SAGA complex 25. The yeast strains generated in this study (ZRM017 and ZRM022) may be useful in further studies for separating the roles of the acetylation and deubiquitination functions of the SAGA complex in vivo (for details on yeast strains see Table 1).

  Finally, variability analysis revealed a remarkable range of motion of the HAT module with respect to the SAGA core. The HAT module itself is not flexible or disordered but rather moves as a unit, likely accounting for the failure of previous studies to reveal its structure. This movement of the HAT module may be important for SAGA function, enabling acetylation of histone tails by a SAGA complex bound at a promoter.


### Limitations of the Study
  Although the majority of the ctSAGA structure was resolved to sub-3 angstrom resolution, certain regions, specifically the SAGA core-tip-HAT interface and the peripheral regions of the HAT domain, were poorly resolved. The Sgf29 subunit of the HAT module could not be resolved and the DUB module was not captured within the context of the entire SAGA complex. Furthermore, our analyses identified discrepancies between the protein annotations for ctTaf9 (G0SF39) in the UniProt database and the validations we obtained from our structural and sequencing data. Although these discrepancies have been reported to the UniProt, they underscore concerns regarding the reliability of sequence annotations of ct proteins within the database. Considering these annotation issues, we exercised caution by refraining from building model in regions where the data did not support the validation of side chains. However, this exclusion does not impact the conclusions of our study.

### Experimental model and study participant details

### Method details

#### Cloning and purification of ctSAGA.
  To purify the endogenous ctSAGA from C. thermophilum, a FLAG-TEV-ProtA (FpA) was introduced into C-terminal end of the SPT7 gene (Spt7-FpA), annotated and identified based on homology. The SPT7 gene was put under the expression of actin promoter. The colonies from the selection plate were picked and grown multiple rounds in liquid media with the Terbinafine drug for the stability test. No difference in growth or morphology was observed between wild type vs SPT7-FpA transformed cells. The stable colony was picked and 6ml culture in CCM media without drug was grown overnight for the expression test. The cells from the overnight grown culture were ground in cryogenic conditions using mortar and pestle. The ground powder was used for DNA and protein extraction. The protein sample was prepared by adding 2x loading buffer and boiled at 95ºC for 8 min followed by centrifugation at maximum speed in tabletop centrifuge. The supernatant was loaded directly onto SDS-PAGE followed by immunoblot using Peroxidase Anti-Peroxidase (PAP) soluble complex.

  To scale up the production of ctSAGA, 200 mL of C. thermophilum cell culture was grown overnight. Next day, cells were water washed, blended, and inoculated in a 100L fermenter and grown at 52°C overnight. These cells were harvested and resuspended with buffer A (60 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 400 mM ammonia sulfate, 0.02% NP40 and 2 mM DTT). The resuspended cells were blended and then subjected to Dyno Mill (Federal Equipment Company) for cell disruption. The cell extract was first centrifuged at 9,000 rpm for 15 minutes, followed by treatment polyethyleneimine (0.15%) and centrifugation at 9000rpm to remove DNA. The supernatant was precipitated with 50% saturated Ammonium Sulfate. The Ammonium Sulfate pellet was resuspended in buffer A minus Ammonium sulfate until the conductivity was equal to 300mM Ammonium sulfate. The sample was centrifuged at 150,000 g for 30 min. The supernatant was passed through a 30 mL IgG Sepharose column and then washed 5CV with buffer B-400 (30 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 400 mM ammonia sulfate, and 1 mM DTT) followed by buffer B-200 (30 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 200 mM ammonia sulfate, and 1 mM DTT). After cleavage with TEV protease (20ug/ml resin) at 4°C for 12 hours in buffer B-200, the eluent was further purified by HiTrap Q HP anion-exchange column (GE Healthcare). Peak fractions were analyzed by SDS-PAGE and stained by Coomassie blue. The fractions corresponding to pure ctSAGA were concentrated and subjected to size exclusion chromatography (SEC) using Superose 6 Increase 10/300 column (GE). A main peak with a shoulder was obtained. All the fractions of the main peak were pooled, concentrated, and stored as Glycerol stock for the subsequent steps.


#### Sample preparation for Cryo-EM.
  The frozen glycerol stock of purified ctSAGA was subjected to analytical scale size exclusion chromatography using Superose-6-Increase 3.2/300 in buffer 20mM HEPES pH 7.5, 150mM Potassium Acetate, 1.5 mM TCEP (Figure S1A). The peak fraction at 0.9mg/ml was used for grid preparation. The protein sample was mixed with equal volume of 0.5% glutaraldehyde to get the final concentration of ctSAGA as 0.45mg/ml and glutaraldehyde at 0.25%. The crosslinking reaction mix was incubated on ice for 1h and 3ul of the sample was directly applied onto 200 mesh Quantifoil gold R2/1 (Quantifoil Micro Tools GmbH). The grids were glow discharged for 35s at 0.4 mBar and 15mA using Pelco EasiGlow glow discharge cleaning system. Leica automatic grid plunger was used to prepare grids under following conditions: Temperature 20°C, Relative Humidity 95%, and blot time 1.3s. The grids were stored in liquid nitrogen for data collection.


#### Cryo-EM data collection and image processing.
  Preliminary dataset was collected on a 200kV Tecnai F20 microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) with a dose rate of 10e−/pixel/s and defocus range of −2.0 to −3.0 μm. For automated data acquisition, SerialEM package47 was used, and data was collected in movie-mode, and image stacks with 50 frames were recorded on a Gatan K2 Summit direct electron detector camera in counting mode with magnification of 14,500x and pixel size of 2.53Å. MotionCor2 48 was used for motion correction and CTFFIND450 was used for the estimation of the contrast transfer function. Particles were initially picked using EMAN2’s e2boxer.py 49 from a couple of hundreds of images to generate a small dataset followed by Relion’s 2D classification 51. Then a good 2D template was selected for the template- based particle picking for all the images. Relion and or CryoSPARC 33 were used for the 2D and 3D classifications for the large dataset.

  To further improve the resolution, data was collected on two 300kV Titan Krios (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) equipped with Gatan K3 Summit direct electron detector cameras: one has no energy filter and the other has BioQuantum energy filter (Gatan Inc). The movie-mode images were recorded in super-resolution mode with −1.2 to −2.5 μm defocus range and each image was dose-fractionated to 40 frames. First 46,224 movie-mode images were collected from the Titan Krios without energy filter with the total dose 68.9 e/Å2 and exposure time of 2 seconds at the nominal magnification of 29,000× and the pixel size of 0.839 Å/pixel on the specimen level. The other 122,545 movie-mode images were collected from the Titan Krios with the energy filter of 20eV slit width and with the total dose of 67.2 e− /Å2 and exposure time of 2 seconds at the nominal magnification of 105,000× and pixel size 0.8677 Å/pixel on the specimen level. Again, the motion correction was performed using MotionCor2 and the defocus values were determined using CTFFIND4. The subset selection in Relion resulted in 161,760 micrographs after the severely drifted images due to the liquid nitrogen auto-refill into the microscope autoloader were eliminated. Of the total micrographs, 117,458 were at the pixel size 0.8677 Å/pixel and 44,302 were at 0.839 Å/pixel (Figure S2). Particles were automatically picked by the previous 2D template in Relion. The extracted particles were processed in CryoSPARC for 2D classification, heterogeneous and non-uniform (NU) refinements to eliminate the bad ones (Figure S2). In total, 3.56 million particles yielded a 3D map of ctSAGA at an overall resolution of 2.7 Å after the two datasets were binned to the same pixel size of 1.041 Å/pixel and merged. The external program pyem (csparc2star.py, Asarnow, D., Palovcak, E., Cheng, Y. UCSF pyem v0.5. Zenodo https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.3576630 (2019)) was used to convert the particles processed in cryoSPARC to the Relion-compatible format for further processing in Relion. To further improve the resolution of Tra1, Core, Core-HAT interface and HAT module, appropriate masks were generated for the regions, and after particle subtraction focused 3D classification was performed for each region marked by dotted overlays (Figure S2). The particles of the best resolved classes of Tra1, Core and HAT were combined and CryoSPARC NU refinement was performed yielding maps of Tra1, Core at 2.6 Å and HAT module at 3.7 Å estimated from their FSC curves (Figure S3). In addition, to improve the low-resolution regions of Tra1 and Core, further Focused 3D classification was done on different segments (not depicted in the Figure S2 to simplify presentation). All the high-resolution maps of different parts of SAGA were scaled and combined using volume maximum function of ChimeraX to obtain a composite map of the entire ctSAGA. Also, the ctSAGA particle stack was subjected to CryoSPARC 3DVA analyses using a mask around HAT module and the frames were displayed in intermediates mode from 3D Variability Display that allowed to see the volume explored by HAT module and the relative motion of the rest of the components of ctSAGA (see Movie 1).


#### Model building.
  The structure of the ctTra1 was built by first generating homology model using SWISS Model followed by model fitting using Rigid-body fitting in ChimeraX 43,44. Adjustments in protein sequence was made in COOT53 and the model was subjected to iterative Real Space Refine in PHENIX52 and COOT until the best fit model was obtained. For ctSAGA core, initial model was generated using either SWISS Model, Alphafold or Alphafold-Multimer 54, the predicted structures with portions of the model with high confidence (pLDDT score >80) were rigidbody fitted in ChimeraX followed by COOT adjustments. Significant portion of ctSAGA core Taf6, Taf5 and Spt7 were manually built using COOT as the amino acid side chains were well resolved. The ctSAGA Core tip containing Spt7 subunit was built using Alphafold-Multimer predicted model with the sequence of Spt7 and Taf6 subunits. The predicted model with low confidence region were trimmed out and the high confidence model was rigidbody fitted in ChimeraX followed by PHENIX Real Space Refine and COOT adjustments. The alignment of the N-terminal region of the ctTaf9 sequence revealed discrepancies while fitting in the electron density map. Subsequent sequencing of the cDNA amplicon indicated that the UniProt annotation (G0SF39) was inaccurate due to a sequencing error, specifically the omission of a cytosine at position 20 relative to the start codon. This error resulted in the characterization of a shorter splice variant consisting of 221 amino acids55. In contrast, the corrected ctTaf9 variant identified in this study consists of 277 amino acids without any splicing events. Notably, the sequence of the corrected ctTaf9 matched perfectly with the electron density map. The pairwise alignment between G0SF39_CHATD and the corrected ctTaf9 sequence are presented in Figure S9 and Uniprot was communicated to update the entry. The HAT module structure was built initially using PHENIX Map-to-Model with Ada3 sequence. To build the rest of the HAT module, Alphafold-Multimer predicted model of Ada2, Ada3 and Gcn5 was used as starting model. The portion of Ada3 built by using PHENIX Map-to-Model was validated by the Alphafold-Multimer predicted structure.


#### Mass spectrometric analyses of ctSAGA.
  The protein sample in solution was subjected to Mass spectrometric analyses following the method described previously 31. The protein solution was first reduced by incubating with 10mM DTT at 55°C for 30 minutes. Following reduction, alkylation was performed at room temperature with 30mM acrylamide for 30 minutes and protein solution was subjected to overnight protease digestion with Trypsin/LysC (Promega) in presence of 0.02% of ProteaseMAX (Promega) at 37°C. Next day, digestion reaction was quenched by supplementing 1% formic acid and the sample containing peptides was desalted using C18 MonoSpin reversed phase columns (GE Life Sciences). The desalted peptides were dried in a SpeedVac and resuspended in 20ul of reconstitution buffer (2% Acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid), and 2μl of the resuspended sample was injected into the Mass spectrometry instrument, Orbitrap Fusion Tribrid mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). The mass spectrometer was operated in a data dependent fashion where MS1 survey spectra was collected in the Orbitrap and MS2 fragmentation using CID in the ion trap. For data anaylsis, the RAW data files were processed using Byonic (Protein Metrics).


### Preparation of yeast strains
  To validate the structural observations in ct, genetic experiments were designed in yeast. An Ada1 knock out strain (host strain BY4741) was purchased from Horizon Discovery (cat # YSC6273–201917658). First, the 3xFLAG tag was introduced at the C-terminal of Gcn5 via homologous recombination using LEU2 selectable marker thus creating an HFI1 knock out strain with flag tagged Gcn5 (Strain ZRM017). Subsequently, the ZRM017 strain was used to create a double knockout of SPT7 and HFI1 with C-terminal FLAG tagged Gcn5 (ZRM022), using Hygromycin resistance cassette. For the expression of Spt7 and Ada1 mutants, plasmid p416-ADH (Ura3) and pRS403 (His3) respectively were transformed into ZRM022 strain followed by selection on auxotrophic media (sc -Ura, -His, -Leu). The Spt7 mutants have C-terminal Twin-Strep-TagII for purification.


### Yeast growth and biochemical Assays
  Following transformation of Spt7 mutants and ΔSID-Ada1 into the ZRM022 yeast strain, yeast spot assay was performed. Saturated cultures of all mutant strains were serially diluted (1:4) across a 96-well plate and spotted onto agar plates using a pin tool. The plates containing a defined media (sc –Ura –His –Leu) either without or with the drug 0.3ug/ml Sulfometuron Methyl (SMM). Growth was monitored at 30 °C.

  For the purification of mutant or wildtype SAGA complex, Spt7 with C-terminal Twin-Strep-TagII was used as bait for binding to Strep-Tactin-XT 4Flow resin (IBA, cat # 2–5010-025). For each mutant 2L cell cultures were grown and harvested at around OD600= 3.5. The cells were pulverized using cryo-mill (Retsch) followed by resuspension in lysis buffer (Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 200mM Ammonium Sulfate, 5% Glycerol, 1mM EDTA, 1mM DTT) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail and PMSF. The lysate was briefly sonicated and centrifuged at 38000×g for 25 minutes. The supernatant was supplemented with NeutrAvidin (0.2 mg/ml) and incubated for 30 minutes to block any free Biotin or Biotinylated proteins from binding to the resin. The resin was incubated in a disposable column with the lysate for 2 hours on a rotary mixer at 4°C. After incubation, lysate was allowed to pass through by gravity flow and column was washed with lysis buffer followed by washing with wash buffer (lysis buffer + 400mM Ammonium Sulfate) and finally washed with elution buffer (Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 100mM NaCl, 5% Glycerol, 1mM DTT). The protein was eluted from the column using elution buffer supplemented with 50mM Biotin (Sigma Aldrich, cat # 4501). The eluted protein was concentrated, and western blot was performed, after the transfer, the membrane was cut at the expected molecular weight and incubated with HRP conjugated Anti-Strep-TagII antibody (sigma Aldrich, cat # 71591–3) and Anti-FLAG (M2) antibody (1:500). For the detection of Taf5, anti-Taf5 antibody (Rabbit, 1:5,000) was used followed by HRP conjugated Goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:10,000).
