# Honokiol inhibits gastric cancer via tumor microenvironment modulation: a bioinformatics and single-cell analysis


## Abstract
  Honokiol, a natural polyphenol from Magnolia bark, exhibits antitumor effects in various cancers. This study aimed to elucidate its pharmacological mechanisms in gastric cancer. Active components and their targets were retrieved from the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology Database and SwissTargetPrediction databases. Gastric cancer-related genes were collected from the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database and GeneCards databases. Overlapping targets were analyzed using STRING to construct a protein–protein interaction network. Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analyses were performed using DAVID and visualized using Cytoscape. Thirty-four overlapping targets were identified, with CHUK as a key hub gene. Functional enrichment analysis revealed multiple pathways, including NF-κB signaling. Single-cell analysis confirmed CHUK expression in tumor clusters, and immunohistochemistry validated its downregulation following honokiol treatment. These findings suggest that honokiol exerts multi-target and multi-pathway effects in gastric cancer, with CHUK-mediated inhibition of NF-κB signaling identified as a critical mechanism.


## Introduction
  Cancer metastasis is the primary cause of death in cancer patients, with the peritoneum being the most common site of metastasis in gastrointestinal cancers1. Among these malignancies, gastric cancer (GC) is a major global health concern1,2, ranking as the fifth most frequently diagnosed cancer and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, according to the latest Global Cancer Observatory estimates1–4.

  Helicobacter pylori infection is the most significant risk factor for GC 3. H. pylori plays a critical role in the development of chronic gastritis, peptic ulcers, gastric adenocarcinoma, and gastric mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma3. Although it is a well-established risk factor for GC, only 1%–3% of infected individuals develop the malignancy, suggesting that other molecular mechanisms contribute to gastric carcinogenesis5–9. Despite treatment advances, the prognosis for GC remains poor, especially when lymph node metastasis is present10, highlighting the need for a better understanding of its underlying molecular mechanisms.

  In recent years, natural bioactive compounds have gained significant attention as potential therapeutic agents for cancer treatment. Honokiol, a natural bioactive polyphenol derived from Magnolia officinalis, has attracted considerable attention due to its diverse pharmacological properties, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antimicrobial, and neuroprotective effects11. Its anticancer potential, in particular, has become a key area of research12. Honokiol has demonstrated potent antitumor effects across multiple cancer models—including breast, lung, prostate, liver, and colorectal cancers—through mechanisms such as apoptosis induction, angiogenesis inhibition, immune modulation, and suppression of tumor cell proliferation and metastasis11–13. Additionally, honokiol has been found to modulate critical oncogenic signaling pathways, including PI3K/AKT, NF-κB, and STAT3, all of which are implicated in cancer progression and metastasis13–16. Despite these promising findings, the specific molecular mechanisms underlying honokiol’s therapeutic effects in GC remain largely unexplored. Given the strong correlation between H. pylori infection and gastric carcinogenesis, further investigation into honokiol’s role in this context is crucial.

  This study integrates network pharmacology and bioinformatics approaches to systematically analyze the active components of honokiol and elucidate its molecular mechanisms in GC. By identifying key molecular targets and signaling pathways, this research aims to provide valuable insights into honokiol’s potential as a novel therapeutic agent for GC. Ultimately, these findings could contribute to the development of innovative therapeutic strategies, thereby improving long-term treatment outcomes for patients with GC.


## Methods
  This study employed a multi-step bioinformatics and experimental validation workflow to identify key targets of honokiol in GC. First, honokiol’s active components and potential targets were retrieved from the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology (TCMSP) and SwissTargetPrediction. GC-related genes were obtained from the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD) and GeneCards databases. A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using the STRING platform and analyzed with Cytoscape (version 3.10.3). Functional enrichment analyses (Gene Ontology [GO] and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes [KEGG]) were performed using the DAVID database. CHUK was identified as a core hub gene. CHUK expression was validated using single-cell analysis of GC datasets and confirmed by immunohistochemistry (IHC) on tissue microarrays. This study was approved by Institutional Review Board of Tungs’ Taichung MetroHarbor Hospital.


### Screening for honokiol’s active components and action targets
  Honokiol composition was determined using the TCMSP database (https://www.tcmsp-e.com/) 17. Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) is typically administered orally and acts on organs and tissues following absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME)—a process known as TCM pharmacokinetics17–20. Oral bioavailability (OB) and drug-likeness (DL) are key ADME parameters21,22. In this study, the criteria for bioactive components of honokiol were OB ≥ 30% and DL ≥ 0.15. Additionally, PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) was searched for honokiol’s SMILES file. The SwissTargetPrediction database (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/; probability > 0) was searched for potential targets of the bioactive components of honokiol23,24.


### Identification of GC-related genes
  The keywords “Stomach Neoplasms” and “Stomach Neoplasms Helicobacter pylori infection” were used to retrieve gene lists from GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/; Relevance score ≥ 1)25 and to identify relevant genes from the CTD (https://ctdbase.org/)26. From CTD, disease-related targets with an Inference Score ≥ 30 were selected. After merging these targets and removing duplicates, a final set of honokiol-associated targets was obtained.

  Next, the TIMER database was used for a comprehensive assessment of various immune cell infiltrations and their clinical implications27. GC-related targets obtained from the previous search were analyzed for relational value and then imported into the TNMplot database (https://tnmplot.com/analysis/) to identify potential GC target hub genes28. This approach provided further data support for the association between these genes and tumor progression or prognosis. In this study, the gene module was used to assess the relationship between hub gene expression levels and immune cell infiltration, while the survival module was employed to evaluate the association between clinical outcomes, immune cell infiltration, and the hub genes.


### Survival analysis of GC patients
  The prognostic significance of mRNA expression in GC patients was evaluated using the Kaplan–Meier Plotter online tool (www.kmplot.com)29. This database integrates data from several sources, including Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), the European Genome-phenome Archive, and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). Briefly, patient samples were divided into high and low expression groups using auto-selected best cutoff values. The corresponding mRNAs or their target genes were then entered to generate Kaplan–Meier survival curves. Additionally, hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals and log-rank p-values were calculated. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.


### Construction and analysis of the protein–protein interaction (PPI) network
  The previously identified GC-related targets were uploaded to the STRING database (version 12) (https://string-db.org/)30 to analyze protein–protein interactions. A PPI network of the overlapping targets (between honokiol’s predicted targets and GC-related genes) was constructed using Cytoscape (version 3.10.3)31.


### GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses
  GO (http://www.geneontology.org)32 and KEGG (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/)33 pathway enrichment analyses were conducted using the Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) Bioinformatics Resources (2021 version)34. GO analysis included three domains: molecular function, cellular component, and biological process (BP)35.


### Single-cell and immune analyses
  The Tumor Immune Single Cell Hub (TISCH2) database was used for single-cell analysis36 to identify the specific GC cell types expressing CHUK. Subsequently, independent datasets from the scTIME Portal37, comprising cells from GC patients, were analyzed with GSE134520 and GSE167297 visualized using UMAP. GSE12345 and GSE167297 were downloaded from the GEO on January 15, 2024.The ligand-receptor network from the scTIME Portal was used to analyze cellular communication among different T cell subsets. GEPIA2 was employed to assess the correlation between IMMT expression and immune cell signatures38.


### Cell culture
  The GC cell line MKN45 was supplied by the Taipei Veterans General Hospital (Taiwan). Cells were cultured in RPMI medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (complete medium) and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. For experiments, cells were plated in six-well plates and cultured in serum-free medium (starvation medium) overnight. They were then treated with various concentrations of honokiol for different time periods. In some experiments, cancer cells were transfected using Lipofectin reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Transfection efficiency (approximately 90%) was measured using an equivalent amount of a plasmid containing the green fluorescent protein gene, driven by the cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter. Honokiol was purchased from Wako Chemical Company (Osaka, Japan). The product (Product Name: Honokiol; Product Number: 083–08511; CAS Number: 35354-74-6) was supplied as white crystals or crystalline powder with a reported purity of ≥ 99.0% as determined by high-performance liquid chromatography. No human participants were involved in the study.


### Xenograft mouse model
  All animal experiments were approved by the Committee for Animal Experiments of National Chung Hsing University (Approval No. NCHU-100-26). MKN45 cells were not pretreated with honokiol before inoculation. Four- to six-week-old male BALB/c nude mice (National Laboratory Animal Center, Taipei, Taiwan) were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions. To establish a peritoneal metastasis model, MKN45 cells (1 × 10⁵) were injected intraperitoneally. The mice were randomly assigned into a control group (vehicle only) and a honokiol treatment group (n = 4 per group). Honokiol was administered intraperitoneally at doses of 1 or 5 mg/kg twice weekly, starting on day 3 post-inoculation. No oral administration of honokiol was performed during the study period. Mice were monitored for 28 days and then euthanized under anesthesia: mice were first anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) to achieve deep anesthesia, followed by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital (150 mg/kg) to induce death, which was confirmed by cessation of respiration and heartbeat. Tumors were collected for histological analysis.


### Ethics statement
  All animal experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations and approved by the Committee for Animal Experiments of National Chung Hsing University (Approval No. NCHU-100-26). The study complies with the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org). All procedures were conducted following institutional policies for the care and use of laboratory animals, and every effort was made to minimize animal suffering and to reduce the number of animals used.


### Immunohistochemistry (IHC)
  All animal experiments were approved by the Committee for Animal Experiments of National Chung Hsing University (Approval No. NCHU-100-26). Mice were euthanized under anesthesia. Tumors were excised, cut into small pieces, and either fixed in 10% formalin and embedded in paraffin or rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen.

  Paraffin-embedded tissue Sect. (5-µm thick) were deparaffinized in xylene for 20 min and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100%, 95%, 75%, and 50% ethanol in water) for 5 min each, followed by rinsing with double-distilled water (ddH₂O). Antigen retrieval was performed by boiling sections in 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 15 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubating sections with 5% hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS).

  After blocking with 10% FBS in PBS, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber with a primary antibody against CHUK, diluted in PBS containing 5% FBS. The following day, sections were incubated with ExtrAvidin-peroxidase (1:1,000; Sigma) diluted in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Color was developed using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (DAB; Roth), and nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Finally, slides were dehydrated through graded ethanol, incubated in xylene, and mounted with coverslips.


### Western blotting analysis
  MKN45 cells were treated with honokiol (40 µM) and harvested at 4, 8, 12, and 24 h post-treatment. The total protein was extracted using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Equal amounts of proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in TBS-T for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CHUK (1:1000, Senta, Catalog #sc-7606) and β-actin (1:1000, Senta, Catalog # sc-47778). After washing, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000, Senta, catalog # sc-2004) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence kit (Beyotime Biotechnology) and quantified by Image Lab (v4.1, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).


### Cell migration assay
  Cells were seeded in 24-well plates with or without prior transfection, as indicated, and cultured at 37 °C for 24 h. A linear scratch was made through the cell monolayer using a 200-µL sterile pipette tip. After washing with PBS to remove detached cells, fresh medium containing honokiol was added, and cells were further incubated for 8–12 h. Images of the wound area were captured at 0 and 24 h after honokiol treatment using a phase-contrast microscope. The width of the wound was measured using ToupView software (ToupTek Photonics, China).


### Cell viability assay
  Cell viability was evaluated using the MTT assay (Beyotime Biotechnology). MKN45 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After treatment, 10 µL of the MTT solution (5 mg/mL) was added to each well and then incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. The supernatant was then carefully removed, and 200 µL of DMSO was added to dissolve the formazan crystals. The absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (Dynatech Laboratories, Chantilly, VA, USA).


### Statistical analysis
  All graphs were generated and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.2; GraphPad Software, Inc.). Student’s t-test and repeated measures (analysis of variance) were performed to assess differences between GC tumor and adjacent normal tissues. A significance level of α = 0.05 was used for all analyses. Differences between groups were considered statistically significant when p < 0.05.

#### Upregulation of CHUK in GC tissues
  We assessed CHUK mRNA expression across a panel of GC cell lines and observed a notably elevated level in MKN45 cells. Following honokiol treatment, CHUK mRNA expression in MKN45 cells was significantly reduced, indicating a strong suppressive effect (Fig. 9A, p < 0.05). To ensure data reliability and reduce variability, the experiment was performed once with four technical replicates (N = 3). Western blotting further confirmed an increase in CHUK expression following plasmid transfection and reduced levels following honokiol exposure (Fig. 9B). In the wound-healing assays, CHUK overexpression enhanced cell migration, which was effectively reversed by honokiol (20 or 40 µM) without affecting cell viability (Fig. 9C and D). To corroborate these findings at the protein level, IHC staining was performed on a tissue microarray. The results demonstrated strong CHUK protein expression in untreated MKN45 cells, which was markedly diminished after honokiol exposure. In contrast, normal gastric tissues showed significantly lower CHUK protein expression compared to untreated MKN45 cells (Fig. 9E).

## Discussion
  In this study, we employed network pharmacology and bioinformatics approaches, integrated with clinical data and single-cell analysis, to comprehensively investigate honokiol’s potential mechanisms in GC and the overall results are summarized in Fig. 10. The in vitro experimental validation was conducted using MKN45, a single GC cell line, which represents as a representative model for functional assays. Given the highly invasive nature and metastatic potential of GC, identifying novel targets remains crucial for improving patient outcomes10,40. We identified CHUK as a significantly upregulated gene in GC tissues whose expression was effectively reduced by honokiol, suggesting its role in tumor progression. CHUK, a highly conserved kinase containing a kinase domain, leucine zipper, and helix-loop-helix motifs, plays a central role in both canonical and noncanonical NF-κB signaling pathways36,43,44. Our analysis revealed that CHUK expression was significantly lower in Helicobacter pylori-infected patients (p = 0.0094). a finding consistent with previous evidence suggesting that CHUK mediates immune and inflammatory responses through NF-κB activation43–45. The activated NF-κB, in turn, binds DNA consensus sequences to regulate target genes46.

  Further analysis revealed that CHUK expression was markedly elevated in MKN45 cells among the GC cell lines tested and was significantly reduced following honokiol treatment (p < 0.05). Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining corroborated these findings at the protein level, showing strong CHUK protein expression in untreated MKN45 cells and a significant reduction after honokiol exposure. Normal gastric mucosal tissues exhibited significantly lower CHUK protein levels compared to MKN45 cells (p < 0.05), supporting the positive association between CHUK overexpression and GC progression. Single-cell analysis indicated that CHUK upregulation is associated with an immunosuppressive TIME, possibly mediated by co-expressed genes in the PI3K/AKT pathway.

  The enrichment analyses (GO and KEGG), conducted using DAVID and visualized in Cytoscape, showed that honokiol-regulated targets are significantly enriched in biological processes related to cell proliferation, inflammation, and apoptosis. Particularly, PI3K-Akt, MAPK, and NF-κB signaling pathways emerged as the primary pathways through which honokiol exerts its therapeutic effects47,48. These findings align with previous studies indicating honokiol’s capacity to regulate multiple oncogenic pathways, including STAT3, EGFR, and others44,49–52. Interestingly, our study predicted that honokiol has strong binding affinity for RAF1, AKT1, EGFR, BRAF, MDM2, ERBB2, CHUK, PDGFRB, MAPK14, and KDR, and may inhibit the activation of the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway.

  Interestingly, the expression level of CHUK was lower in H. pylori-infected patients, despite its proposed pro-tumorigenic role. This apparent paradox may reflect dynamic regulation of CHUK in different stages of infection or tumorigenesis. H. pylori may initially suppress CHUK expression as an immune evasion strategy. Indeed, CHUK is a key component of both canonical and noncanonical NF-κB pathways, and its modulation may be context-dependent. Moreover, H. pylori can activate alternative NF-κB signaling through CHUK-independent pathways, such as NIK–RelB, which may compensate for CHUK downregulation53–55. Therefore, CHUK downregulation may represent a specific phase of infection or immune adaptation. Further mechanistic studies are needed to clarify these temporal dynamics and the potential compensatory mechanisms involved.

  In conclusion, this study integrates in silico, in vitro approaches, and analysis of clinical datasets to uncover CHUK as a potential regulator of the pathogenesis of GC and a candidate target for honokiol. These findings offer molecular insights into possible mechanisms of action of honokiol and support its exploration in future preclinical models. Future preclinical and translational research may further explore honokiol derivatives and combination strategies aimed at targeting CHUK and the NF-κB pathway, including CHUK-knockout or overexpression studies to elucidate its functional role and therapeutic relevance in GC.


## Conclusion
  This study comprehensively investigated the potential mechanisms by which honokiol exerts therapeutic effects on GC. Through integrative bioinformatics analysis, the predicted targets of honokiol were primarily kinases (20%), followed by oxidoreductases, family A G protein-coupled receptors, epigenetic erasers, and other cytosolic proteins, each accounting for 13.3%. Network topology analyses identified RAF1, AKT1, EGFR, BRAF, and MDM2 as key hub genes. Notably, CHUK was identified as a potential functional target of honokiol, and its role was further confirmed by in vitro experiments, showing reduced cell migration and colony formation upon honokiol treatment. Additionally, single-cell transcriptomic analysis revealed the potential influence of honokiol on the tumor microenvironment and immune cell dynamics. Collectively, these findings provide a theoretical framework for understanding the multifaceted actions of honokiol in GC and lay the foundation for future mechanistic and therapeutic investigations.


### Limitations
  This study has several limitations. First, all computational analyses were based on publicly available datasets, which may introduce biases owing to sample heterogeneity and batch effects. Second, although the analytical tools employed are well-established, they rely on assumptions that may not fully reflect the complexity of the tumor microenvironment. Third, functional validation was conducted in MKN45, a single GC cell line; thus, further validations using additional cell lines are necessary to ensure reproducibility and generalizability. Lastly, in vivo experiments and clinical data correlation are required to confirm the biological relevance of the present findings.
