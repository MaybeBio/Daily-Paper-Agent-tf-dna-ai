# Development of a diagnostic model for ovarian cancer based on machine learning algorithms and functional analysis of key biomarker SOX17


## Background
  Ovarian cancer (OC) demonstrates the poorest prognosis among gynecological malignancies, with five-year survival rates below 45%, primarily due to late-stage diagnosis. To address this challenge, we systematically identified OC-specific differentially expressed genes (DEGs) to develop a robust diagnostic model based on eleven machine learning algorithms. Furthermore, we explored the potential mechanism of key DEG in OC.


## Methods
  We acquired RNA sequencing data of 426 tissues (352 °C tumor and 74 adjacent non-tumor) from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) repository. Following rigorous batch effect correction and normalization procedures, DEGs were screened between tumor and non-tumor specimens. Furthermore, the resultant DEGs underwent comprehensive functional characterization, including Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway, protein-protein interaction (PPI) network, and immune microenvironment analyses. To optimize diagnostic feature selection, we implemented a tiered analytical approach combining F-test, LASSO regression and Pearson correlation. The curated gene subset served as input for developing machine learning classifiers, with the cohort partitioned into stratified training (70%) and validation (30%) subsets. Eleven distinct algorithms were evaluated through iterative 10-fold cross-validation, with model performance quantified via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, precision-recall (PR) metrics, calibration curve fitting, learning curve profiling, and decision curve analysis (DCA) assessment. Finally, we investigated the biological functions of one key gene, SRY-box containing gene (SOX17) in OC cell lines by in vitro experiments.


## Results
  We delineated 27 DEGs exhibiting distinct expression patterns in OC, with 16 upregulated and 11 downregulated genes. GO enrichment analysis suggested that DEGs were significantly enriched in response to folic acid, blood microparticle and alcohol dehydrogenase [NAD(P)+] activities. KEGG pathway analysis indicated that these DEGs were mainly involved in tyrosine metabolism, fatty acid degradation, ABC transporters and pyruvate metabolism. Immune microenvironment profiling revealed substantial M2 macrophage polarization and cytotoxic T-cell exhaustion in tumor tissues.The optimal diagnostic model was established based on five key genes (CD24, CLEC4M, SOX17, ADH1C and CHRDL1) and Logistic Regression algorithm was the optimal algorithm. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and accuracy of the model were 0.93 and 0.875, respectively. SOX17 was upregulated in OC tumor tissues and knockdown of SOX17 obviously suppressed tumor cell proliferation and migratory.


## Conclusion
  Our multivariable diagnostic model based on five genes through logistic regression optimization, demonstrated robust discriminative capacity for OC. SOX17 functions as a suppressor and potential therapeutic target for OC.

## Introduction
  Ovarian cancer (OC) remains among the most prevalent malignancies and the leading cause of gynecological cancer mortality globally [1]. Due to the absence of specific early symptoms and effective early detection strategies, more than 70% of patients are diagnosed at advanced stages [2]. Although primary treatment combining cytoreductive surgery with platinum-based chemotherapy achieves a 90% cure rate in early-stage ovarian cancer, the five-year survival rate for advanced cases remains stubbornly low at approximately 30% [3, 4]. Consequently, there is an urgent need to explore efficacious biomarkers for diagnosis of OC.

  Machine learning (ML) is the subset of artificial intelligence (AI) which enables computers to gradually learn from data, examples and experience. It is an effective method for instructing computers on how to better manage big biological data [5]. Technological advancements in AI and ML have paved the path towards diagnosis tools by utilizing big data sets to meet the future challenges for disease detection at a very early stage especially in cancer [6]. Ahmed Hammad et al. used machine learning technology to identify 10 key genes with diagnostic and prognostic values in colorectal cancer [7]. Al Mudawi N et al. presented an astute way to predict cervical cancer with ML algorithms. In terms of cervical cancer prediction, the random forest (RF), decision tree (DT), adaptive boosting (AdaBoost), and gradient enhancement algorithms in the prediction model achieved 100% of the highest classification score [8]. Sundar et al. developed a gene signature, which could represent as predictive biomarker for paclitaxel benefit in gastric cancer [9]. These studies provide a direction for us to look for genes to diagnose OC.

  In this study, we identified key genes for OC based on publicly available databases and established a diagnostic model by selecting the optimal machine learning algorithm. The diagnostic efficacy was evaluated and validated in training and validation groups. Furthermore, one of the key genes, SOX17, which was up-regulated in OC, was selected to explore its effects on cell proliferation and migration by cell function assays. Our findings provide a novel model for predicting diagnostic and clinical treatment application in OC.


## Materials and methods

### Datasets downloading and processing
  Six OC microarray datasets (GSE18521, GSE26712, GSE29450, GSE38666, GSE66957, GSE105437) comprising 352 tumor tissues (OC) and 74 non-tumor tissues (nOC) were retrieved from the GEO database. Following data acquisition, probe-level data were converted to gene-centric expression matrices using Perl-based annotation pipelines. Then, we used the R package “Sva” to merge these six gene sets.


### Identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)
  We identified DEGs between OC and nOC using the R package “Limma”. The cutoff criteria were |log2 fold change| >2 and adjusted P-value < 0.05. Then, the packages “pheatmap” and “ggplot2” were performed to draw volcano and heat maps of the DEGs.


### Genes enrichment and protein-protein interaction analyses
  GO function and KEGG pathway analysis were carried out to predict the potential functions of the DEGs using R packages “clusterProfiler” and “enrichplot”. A PPI network was constructed by the STRING database to further investigate the interactive associations between DEGs.


### Immune cells infiltration analysis
  Immune infiltration profiles quantifying 22 kinds of cells were computationally derived for all specimens using dedicated R packages, with results visualized through stratified bar plots. Systematic correlation analyses were subsequently conducted between immune cell fractions and pivotal DEGs.


### Identification of key DEGs of OC
  F-test and Lasso regression analysis were used to screen key DEGs, then the intersection of the obtained DEGs were taken. The correlation among the key DEGs was evaluated by Pearson analysis. Multiple genes with correlation coefficients greater than 0.7, only one should be retained to exclude co-expression.


### Establishment and validation of the optimal diagnostic model based on machine learning algorithms
  The OC samples were randomly divided into a training cohort and an independent validation cohort at a 7:3 ratio. During model development, 10-fold cross-validation was implemented within the training set to ensure robust parameter optimization. The expression matrices of key DEGs were enrolled as the input files, and 11 machine learning algorithms, including Naive Bayes, K-nearest Neighbor, Logistic Regression, Random Forest, Decision Tree, Artificial Neural Networks, Support Vector Machine cross-validation, Integrated Learning Gradient Boosting Decision Tree, LightGBM, AdaBoost and XGBoost, were utilized to establish diagnostic models. To identify the optimal algorithm, a comprehensive validation framework was employed incorporating five complementary analytical approaches: ROC curve analysis, PR curve assessment, calibration curve evaluation, DCA and learning curve monitoring.


### Cell culture
  Two human OC cell lines (SKOV3 and A2780) were purchased from Pricella (Wuhan, China). SKOV3 cells were cultured in McCoy’s 5 A Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, China), and A2780 cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, China). All culture media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (ZQXZBIO, China), and incubated at 37 °C under a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere.


### Cell transfection
  Silencer™ Select siRNAs targeting SOX17 (si-SOX17-1283:5’-GCACGGAAUUU GAACAGUA-3’; si-SOX17-424:5’-GCUUUCAUGGUGUGGGCUA-3’) and a scrambled negative control siRNA (5’-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGU-3’) were procured from GeneBio Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Henan, China). Cell transfection was performed using Lipofectamine™ 3000 transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Knockdown efficiency was assessed 24 h post-transfection on mRNA and protein levels through quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) and Western blot analyses.


### RNA extraction and real-time PCR (qPCR)
  According to the instructions of the manufacturer, RNA was isolated with EpiQuik Total RNA Isolation Fast Kit (Feijie Bio-Technology, Shanghai). The purity and concentration of RNA were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. cDNA was synthesised using High Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Vazyme Biotech), and qPCR was performed using Blaze Taq qPCR Mix according to the manufacturer’s instructions. GAPDH was used as an internal reference. The primers were designed and synthesized by GENE-Bio. Relative expressions were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Statistical analysis was performed based on data from three replicates.


### Western blot
  Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) supplemented with 1% protease inhibitor cocktail. Protein concentration was quantified with a BCA™ Protein Assay Kit (Solarbio Life Sciences, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Equal amounts of protein lysates were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Merck Millipore, Germany) using a semi-dry transfer system. After blocking with 5% non-fat dried milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: rabbit anti-SOX17 (1:1000, ABclonal Biotechnology, China)and rabbit anti-GAPDH (1:1000, ABclonal Biotechnology, China).Following three washes with TBST, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:2000, goat anti-rabbit IgG, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using Enhanced Chemiluminescence (ECL) Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and quantified by Image Lab™ Software (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA).


### Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) analysis
  For the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay, cells were counted and seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 3,000 cells per well. After the appropriate treatment/intervention, 10 µl of CCK-8 reagent (MedChemExpress) was added to each well according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Following a 2-hour incubation at 37 °C under protected light conditions, the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using an Epoch Microplate Spectrophotometer (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA).


### Colony formation
  OC cells from each experimental group were counted and seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 3,000 cells per well. The cells were cultured for 14 days in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. The colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) for 30 min at room temperature and then stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) for 15 min. After thorough washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), colonies containing > 50 cells were quantified using a microscope.


### Transwell assay
  OC cells from each experimental group were counted and seeded at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells per well in the upper chamber of 8 μm pore-size polycarbonate transwell inserts (Corning Costar, Cambridge, MA, USA). The cells were suspended in serum-free medium, while the lower chamber of the 24-well plate contained 600 µl of complete medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) as a chemoattractant. Following 24 h of incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2, non-migrated cells on the upper membrane surface were removed using cotton swabs. The migrated cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) for 15 min. Representative photomicrographs were captured under an inverted fluorescence microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and cell counting was performed using ImageJ software (Version 1.53t, NIH, USA).


### Wound-healing assay
  OC cells in 6-well plates were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. When cells reached 80% confluency, three parallel linear wounds per well were created in the confluent monolayer using a sterile 200 µl pipette tip. After washing twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove cellular debris, fresh serum-free medium was added to minimize proliferation-related effects. The plates were returned to the incubator for 24 h with periodic monitoring. Wound closure was documented at 0 h and 24 h post-scratching using inverted fluorescence microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Migration distance was quantified using ImageJ software (Version 1.53t NIH, USA), and healing rate (%) was calculated as: [(Initial wound area - Final wound area)/Initial wound area] × 100.


### Statistical analysis
  All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2) for advanced multivariate analyses, complemented by GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) for data visualization and basic hypothesis testing. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) after confirming normal distribution through Shapiro-Wilk test, while non-parametric data were analyzed using Mann-Whitney U test. Intergroup differences were evaluated by two-tailed Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc correction for multiple comparisons, with P < 0.05 (95% confidence interval) considered statistically significant. All experiments were conducted three replicates to ensure the reliability and consistency of the findings.


## Results

### Identification of DEGs
  A total of 27 DEGs were screened including 16 up-regulated and 11 down-regulated genes in OC tumor tissues comparing with non-tumor tissues which were shown in volcano map (Fig. 1A). The heatmap indicated that the expression of DEGs had obvious differences between two groups (Fig. 1B).

### Gene enrichment and protein-protein interaction analyses of DEGs
  GO analysis revealed three hierarchical functional clusters (Fig. 2A). In the Biological Process (BP) category, the DEGs were significantly enriched in response to folic acid and cardiac outflow tract morphogenesis. The Cellular Component (CC) analysis demonstrated predominant localization in blood microparticles. Molecular Function (MF) annotations mainly involved in alcohol dehydrogenase [NAD(P)+] activity, extracellular matrix structural constituent, NAD-retinol dehydrogenase activity. We identified four core metabolic pathways: tyrosine metabolism, fatty acid degradation, ABC transporters and pyruvate metabolism using KEGG pathway analysis (Fig. 2B). The protein-protein interaction (PPI) network constructed via STRING database (version 12.0, confidence score > 0.7) contained 28 nodes with 67 edges, exhibiting a clustering coefficient of 0.412 (Fig. 2C).

  Fig. 2Gene enrichment and protein-protein interaction analyses. (A) GO enrichment analysis results. (B) KEGG enrichment analysis results. (C) Protein network of the DEGs


### Immune cell infiltration
  We used CIBERSORT to analyze the relative proportions of 22 immune cells in each tissue (Fig. 3A). Immune cell correlation was then visualized in the form of a heatmap (Fig. 3B). Further analysis of the difference in the degree of cellular infiltration between nOC and OC groups indicated that follicular helper T cells (P < 0.001), regulatory T cells (Tregs, P = 0.001), M1-polarized macrophages (P < 0.001), dendritic cells resting (P = 0.006), activated dendritic cells (P < 0.001) and Mast cells (P < 0.001) were significantly higher in OC group than those in nOC group. However, B cells native (P = 0.026), T cell CD4 memory resting (P = 0.044), T cells gamma delta (P < 0.001), monocytes, mast cells resting (P < 0.001) and neutrophils (P < 0.001) infiltration were significantly lower in OC group (Fig. 3C).

### The key DEGs of OC
  Feature selection was performed through a multi-step computational framework (Fig. 4). Initial F-test screening identified 15 protein-coding genes with differential expression scores exceeding 130 quantile thresholds (Supplementary Table 1). Parallel LASSO regression analysis (λ = 0.016 via 10-fold cross-validation) selected 15 transcripts demonstrating minimal mean-squared error in predictive modeling (Supplementary Table 2; Fig. 4A). Intersection of these two independent selection sets revealed 10 consensus genes: CLEC4M, CD24, WFDC2, ADH1C, SOX17, NELL2, FOLR1, PRG4, MNDA, and CHRDL1 (Fig. 4B). Co-expression network analysis using Pearson’s correlation matrix applied stringent linkage criteria: gene pairs with |r| >0.7 underwent elimination, retaining the transcript showing higher betweenness centrality in protein-protein interaction networks. This refinement process yielded five non-redundant biomarkers: CD24, CLEC4M, SOX17, ADH1C and CHRDL1 (Fig. 4C).

### The establishment of the optimal diagnostic model
  The abovementioned five key DEGs were utilized to develop diagnostic models through eleven distinct machine-learning algorithms. Learning curve analysis revealed that both the Logistic Regression algorithm and Artificial Neural Networks achieved stable convergence between training and validation groups as sample size increased, with performance scores consistently exceeding 0.92 (Fig. 5). DCA demonstrated superior clinical utility of the Logistic Regression model, as evidenced by the largest net benefit across threshold probabilities (Fig. 6A). The Logistic Regression algorithm further exhibited outstanding discriminatory power, with the AUC of 0.93 (Fig. 6B). Comprehensive evaluation integrating PR curves and calibration plots confirmed that the Logistic Regression model provided the optimal balance between predictive accuracy and reliability for OC diagnosis (Fig. 6C, D).

  To operationalize our findings, we engineered a web-based predictive interface (https://eaakxrd76vbb87gkwewfnp.streamlit.app/). This interactive platform enables clinicians to predict the diagnostic risk for an individual by inputting quantitative expression profiles of SOX17, CD24, CLEC4M, ADH1C, and CHRDL1.


### Upregulation of SOX17 in OC tissues
  To validate the diagnostic model’s biological relevance, we analyzed the expression levels of SOX17 in 5 paired OC tissues and non-tumors. Results from RT-qPCR and Western blot demonstrated that the expression levels of SOX17 at both mRNA and protein levels were significantly upregulated in OC tissues compared to adjacent non-tumor tissues.


### Knockdown of SOX17 significantly suppressed the proliferative and migratory abilities of OC cells
  We employed RNA interference in two OC cell lines (SKOV3 and A2780) to explore the function of SOX17. RT-PCR quantification revealed > 70% reduction in SOX17 mRNA levels (P < 0.005 vs. scramble siRNA controls), a finding corroborated at the protein level by Western blot analysis (Fig. 7A, B). CCK8 assays and colony formation experiments indicated that knockdown of SOX17 suppressed the proliferation capacity ability of SKOV3 and A2780 cells (Fig. 7C-F). Meanwhile, wound-healing and transwell experiments demonstrated that knockdown of SOX17 inhibited the migration abilities of SKOV3 and A2780 cells (Fig. 8A-D). Collectively, these orthogonal experimental approaches mechanistically link SOX17 depletion to dual suppression of proliferative and metastatic potential, thereby providing preclinical validation for its therapeutic targeting in OC.

## Discussion
  In the present study, comparative transcriptomic analysis revealed 27 consensus DEGs (16 upregulated and 11 downregulated) between OC and nOC. GO enrichment analysis of these DEGs demonstrated significant enrichment in three biological processes: folate response pathways, blood microparticle and alcohol dehydrogenase [NAD(P)+] activity. Duthie et al. [10] found that folic acid is related to the development of cancer. Folate deficiency increases the risk of malignancy by reducing intracellular S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) and causing an imbalance in DNA precursors, uracil misincorporation into DNA and chromosome breakage. Microparticles (MP) are small membrane vesicles that sprout from plasma membrane cells in response to cell activation or apoptosis [11]. Most MPs in human blood originate from platelets called platelet-derived MPs (PDMPs). Recently, a study revealed the positive relationships between PDMPs levels and breast cancer grade and metastasis [12]. Alcohol is mainly oxidized to acetaldehyde by alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH). International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classified alcohol consumption as group 1 of human carcinogens [13]. Many studies have found that disturbances of the alcohol dehydrogenase activity play an important role in alcohol-related neoplasms [14]. Furthermore, KEGG pathway analysis indicated that these DEGs were mainly involved in tyrosine metabolism, fatty acid degradation, ABC transporters and pyruvate metabolism. Previous studies demonstrated that these pathways were closely related to OC development and progression. Cancer cells adapt to increase energy demands required for limitless proliferation and protect themselves against intrinsic or microenvironmental stressors by altering their dependencies on different metabolic pathways [15]. Highly invasive OC cells uptake significantly more pyruvate than less invasive cells to enhance metastatic potential [16]. Yang et al. [17] evidenced that aberrantly upregulation of fatty acid desaturases can accelerate lipid metabolic activities and tumor aggressiveness of ascites-derived OC cells. Several ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters are well correlated with OC progression and aggressive [18]. These findings collectively demonstrate the multilevel pathogenesis of OC, spanning epigenetic regulation (SAM-dependent methylation), intercellular communication (PDMP-mediated microenvironment remodeling), and metabolic adaptation (acetaldehyde toxicity/energy imbalance), thereby establishing a molecular target cluster for diagnostic model development.

  The tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) serves as a critical regulator of OC progression [19]. Our analysis revealed significantly elevated infiltration levels of activated dendritic cells, mast cells, M1-polarized macrophages, regulatory T cells (Tregs), and follicular helper T cells in OC tissues compared to nOC. Dendritic cells (DC) are professional antigen presenting cells, in the steady state DC continually process and present antigens on MHCI and MHCII. Conejo-Garcia et al. [20] firstly demonstrated that DCs largely infiltrate advanced human and mouse ovarian tumors. Recent studies have shown that OC dynamically promote intratumoral DC accumulation to support malignant progression via pro-angiogenic and immunoregulatory mechanisms [21]. Mast cells (MCs) are innate immune cells and able to secrete pro-angiogenic and growth factors [22]. Therefore, MCs possess both pro-tumorigenic and anti-tumorigenic capabilities. Meyer et al. [23] demonstrated that MCs have a suppressive effect on ovarian tumor growth. Macrophages are versatile immunocytes necessary for maintaining tissue homeostasis [24]. To maintain the balance between inflammation and regeneration, macrophages are polarized from the inflammation status (M1-like) to anti-inflammation status (M2-like), high M2/M1 ratio status are associated with poor prognosis for most solid tumors, such as OC [25]. For OC patients, the more tumor-associated regulatory T-cells, the worse the prognosis for all tumor stages [26].

  Five candidate genes (CD24, SOX17, ADH1C, CHRDL1, and CLEC4M) were identified as diagnostic biomarkers for OC through F-test, LASSO regression analysis and Pearson analysis. These genes were subsequently integrated with 11 machine learning algorithms to develop diagnostic models, among which Logistic Regression demonstrated optimal predictive performance. Significantly, ROC curve analysis validated the robust diagnostic efficacy of these model genes, establishing them as valuable diagnostic biomarkers for OC. To bridge the gap between computational modeling and clinical implementation, we developed and deployed a web-based interface termed the Ovarian Cancer Diagnostic Prediction Model, providing predictive diagnostic capabilities for OC. This interactive platform is capable of generating personalized diagnostic risk scores by analyzing the quantitative expression profiles of SOX17, CD24, CLEC4M, ADH1C, and CHRDL1. This facilitates the integration of molecular diagnostics into clinical practice.

  Previous studies have revealed that CLEC4M, CHRDL1, and ADH1C exhibited multifaceted roles in oncogenesis across multiple cancer types. CLEC4M, also designated as DC-SIGNR, is a calcium-dependent lectin predominantly expressed in hepatic sinusoidal and lymph node endothelial cells [27]. A previous study discovered that CLEC4M overexpression contributed to the inhibition of HCC progression [28]. Another study reported that high level of CLEC4M was associated with poor clinical prognosis in lung cancer patients and enhanced the resistance of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cells to cisplatin [29].

  CHRDL1 is a kind of secretory protein belonging to the Chordin family [30]. As a bone morphogenetic protein (BMPS) antagonist, CHRDL1 has been proven to be associated with various cancers. In oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), its downregulation promotes epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and metastasis via MAPK pathway activation, effects reversible through CHRDL1 re-expression [31]. CHRDL1 could inhibit the migration and invasion of BC cells to alleviate the prognosis of BC through inhibiting the bone morphogenetic protein 4 (BMP4) signal [32]. In addition, CHRDL1 has been identified as a potential biomarker for the prognosis of gastric cancer [33].

  ADH1C is a member of the alcohol dehydrogenase family and catalyzes the oxidation of ethanol to acetaldehyde, which is a carcinogenic metabolite, and have relation to the etiology and prognosis of various malignant tumors [34]. Overexpression of ADH1C inhibited the growth of CRC tumors by down-regulating PHGDH and PSAT1 expression in the serine synthesis pathway and reducing intracellular serine [35]. ADH1C is associated with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) survival and is considered as a promising biomarker for predicting the prognosis of LUAD [36]. Liu et al. found that the expression of ADH1C was significantly downregulated in hepatocellular carcinoma samples compared to normal liver samples. High expression of ADH1C was associated with good prognosis for HCC patients [37].

  We also found compelling evidence confirming the upregulated genes (CD24 and SOX17), with malignant proliferation and poor prognosis of OC. CD24, also known as small cell lung carcinoma cluster 4 antigen or Heat Stable Antigen (HSA), is a highly glycosylated protein which expresses in diverse malignant tumor cells, including myelocytes, keratinocytes, macrophages, renal tubular epithelial cells, muscle fibers, activated T cells, neurons, and ganglion cells [38]. CD 24 could promote immune evasion by interacting with the inhibitory receptor sialic acid binding IG-like lectin 10 (Siglec-10) expressed by tumor associated macrophages (TAMs) [39]. The increase of CD24 is an independent factor in ovarian serous adenocarcinomas for shortened survival rate [40].

  SRY-box containing gene (SOX17), as a member of the SOX transcription factor family, which has an HMG, β-catenin–binding and transactivation domains, plays an important role in cancer progression [41]. Reddy et al. [42] developed the CaCTS (Cancer Core Transcription Factor Specificity) algorithm, which identified PAX8, SOX17, and MECOM as master regulators in high-grade serous ovarian carcinomas (HGSOCs) and demonstrated their essential role in maintaining HGSOC cell viability. The current research results indicate that SOX17 is a highly sensitive marker for ovarian non-mucinous carcinomas and endometrial carcinomas and demonstrate its outstanding specificity and sensitivity for distinguishing metastatic gynecologic carcinomas from other cancers in cytology specimens [43, 44]. Zalfa et al. [45]developed a gene expression biomarker detection method that accurately discriminates ovarian serous adenocarcinoma (OSCA) from healthy ovarian tissue and other malignancies. And, they identified a diagnostic signature based on MUC16, PAX8, and SOX17 RNA expression, which differentiates OSCA from 29 additional tumor types with high specificity. In OC cells, PAX8 and SOX17 proteins stimulate tumor angiogenesis by suppressing SERPINE1 expression. Consequently, targeting the PAX8/SOX17 pathway may provide an anti-angiogenic basis for OC treatment [46]. This study further explored the role of SOX17 in OC. We validated the significant upregulation of SOX17 in OC tissues comparing with paired adjacent non-tumor tissues, consistent with our initial bioinformatic transcriptomic findings. The cell proliferation and migration assays supported that knockdown of SOX17 inhibited proliferation and migration abilities of OC cell. Collectively, these findings not only demonstrate SOX17’s regulatory role in OC cell but also underscore the potential of SOX17 as a promising therapeutic target for OC intervention.

  This study also has several limitations. Firstly, its heavy reliance on public databases combined with the lack of comprehensive clinical validation may restrict the generalizability of findings. Secondly, although our in vitro experiments with SOX17 successfully demonstrated its significant impact on OC cell proliferation and migration capacity, the exclusive focus on this single gene precluded deeper exploration of its upstream regulatory mechanisms and downstream signaling cascades, thereby leaving critical molecular pathways unexamined. This lack of mechanistic understanding prevented us from defining SOX17’s position within OC pathogenesis and developing precise targeted treatment strategies.


## Conclusion
  In summary, our integrated bioinformatics approach identified five DEGs (CD24, CLEC4M, SOX17, ADH1C, and CHRDL1) demonstrating robust diagnostic potential for OC, with a Logistic Regression -based predictive model achieving superior discriminative performance. Notably, cellular experiments underscore the pivotal role of SOX17 in OC cell proliferation and migration, indicating its potential as a new therapeutic target.
