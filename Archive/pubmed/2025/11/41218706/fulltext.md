## Introduction
  The cell nucleus, which governs the intricate regulation of cell proliferation, metabolism, gene activation and cell cycle management, has been considered as the most promising target for a large range of therapeutic drugs in cancer therapy. [1] DNA, the genetic material primarily housed within the nucleus, is a critical key for regulatory processes. It carries all the genetic information that an organism needs for development, functioning, growth and replication. [2] Up to now, numerous DNA-toxin chemotherapeutic anticancer drugs such as cisplatin, daunomycin and doxorubicin that bind to DNA or its associated enzymes have been widely investigated and applied in clinic medicine. [[3], [4], [5]] These drugs have ability to disrupt DNA replication and transcription which are essential for cell growth and division. Regarding the anticancer drugs that directly bind with DNA, covalent and noncovalent binding mechanisms are involved. [6,7] The most used covalent binders are alkylating agents such as cisplatin, melphalan and mechlorethamine. These alkylating agents attach an alkyl group to DNA, causing a specific reduction in DNA synthesis, chromosomal aberrations and genetic mutations. [8] With respect to the non-covalent binding anticancer drugs, the drug-DNA interactions can be divided into two categories: intercalation and groove binding, forced by π-π stacking, van der Waals, hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions between drugs and DNA. [6,9] The intercalation involves the insertion of the drugs with planar aromatic or heteroaromatic ring between adjacent DNA base pairs without disturbing hydrogen bonding of base pairs, resulting in a decrease of DNA helical twist and lengthening of the DNA. The groove binding drugs are usually crescent-shaped molecules that bind to the minor groove of the DNA. In such binding, the conformation of the DNA will not be largely changed. It is regarded as a standard lock-and-key model for ligand-macromolecular binding. Notably, some of the anticancer drugs such as anthracyclines and daunomycin take advantages of both binding modes. [7,10].

  However, due to the structure properties, many drugs still have difficulty in penetrating the nuclear membrane. Additionally, drug resistance may occur during the treatment process. In this regard, to enhance the anticancer efficacy of drugs, the development of drug delivery systems capable of directly delivering the drugs or genes to the cell nucleus has attracted significant attention in medical research. [11,12] In which, nanoparticle-based nucleus-targeting drug delivery systems have emerged as a pivotal strategy. Nanoparticles or modified nanoparticles with nucleus-targeting capabilities in this system may function as drug delivery carriers, anticancer and bioimaging reagents. Pan et al. reviewed cancer cell nucleus-targeting nanocomposites for advanced tumor therapeutics. [1] According to publications, various nanomaterials, including gold (Au), copolymer, mesoporous silica, iron oxide and carbon-based nanoparticles, have been investigated. [[13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]] Among them, the Au particles smaller than 9 nm can enter the nucleus via passive diffusion, while ligand modifications (TAT peptide, SV40 antigen, adenoviral, etc.) are needed to promote the active nuclear transport for particles with relatively large size. Meanwhile, the surface charges determined by functional groups of nanoparticles play a vital role in nuclear membrane penetration. A positively charged surface typically enhances nuclear membrane penetration efficiency. [19] Therefore, selecting nanoparticles with appropriate size and structure is significantly important in nucleus targeting.

  Carbon dots (CDs), a type of carbon-based nanoparticles with sizes less than 10 nm, high photoluminescence (PL), ease of functionalization and good water solubility, have been extensively applied in photocatalysis, biosensing, bioimaging and drug/gene delivery. [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25]] Owing to ultrasmall sizes, many CDs are enabled to enter the nucleus easily, holding great potential in nucleus imaging and nucleus-targeted drug/gene delivery. [[26], [27], [28]] In addition, the properties of CDs are tunable by changing the precursors and synthesis approaches. With different precursors, specific functional groups such as hydroxyl, amine and carboxyl groups are filled on the surface of CDs, which provides potential of CDs in further modifications for drug/gene delivery or binding with genetic materials in the cell nucleus. [28] Regarding the CDs with ability to bind with genetic materials, for instance, Liu et al. synthesized hydrogen-binding-induced emission CDs (HBIE-CDs) using folic acid (FA), m-phenylenediamine (m-PD) as precursors. The obtained HBIE-CDs showed strong affinity toward DNA and RNA by H-bonding without interference from other biological species, achieving wash-free nucleus imaging. [29] Qu’s group prepared the fluorescent F and N co-doped CDs (FNCDs) with positive charges (zeta potential of + 6 mV) by utilizing glycine and 2, 4-difluorobenzoic acid as precursors. Owing to the positively charged surface, FNCDs could absorb into DNA/RNA by electrostatic interactions and label the nucleolus and chromosome. As a result, the living cell cycle in real time and the whole cell division process of 4 T1 cells can be monitored by the FNCDs. [30] Besides the typical electrostatic interactions between positively charged CDs and negatively charged DNA, CDs with negative surface may also exhibit DNA binding capability. Wu et al. reported one type of N-doped CDs (MTCDs) derived from 1,2,3-propanetricarboxylic acid (tricarballylate) and m-phenylenediamine(m-PDA), with zeta potential of −12.7 eV. Based on the spectroscopic methods, after entering nucleus, the MTCDs could bind with DNA via groove binding modes. [31] Therefore, CDs not only function as nanocarriers but also show potential in DNA-binding-related applications.

  There are many publications investigating CDs-DNA interactions for antibacterial. Jhonsi et al. reported that TCDs derived from tamarind could bind to DNA via intercalation and exhibit excellent antibacterial activity to E.coli and C.albicans. [32] Arun’s group also demonstrated that mushroom derived CDs could bind with DNA through electrostatic intercalative interactions and exhibit the efficient antibacterial activity against Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. [33] These works suggest that, as one of the mechanisms, CDs may disrupt the DNA synthesis of bacteria. So far, research related to the inhibition of cancers by pure CDs with DNA binding abilities is still limited. Triggered by the widespread use of CDs as nanocarriers in cancer therapeutics and their potential to bind with DNA, we are highly interested in investigating whether CDs themselves could affect the cell cycle, which are associated with DNA synthesis, replication and cell division, in cancer cells. This effect could be similar to anticancer drugs with DNA binding ability, which may offer a synergistic effect when loaded with other drugs or even acting as an anticancer agent on their own.

  Based on structural investigations, it is widely accepted that CDs are composed of a π-conjugated core and a surface shell containing abundant functional groups. [20,34] Among all the functional groups, carboxylic and amine groups are the most commonly used ones to conjugate with other molecules applied in cancer therapy or bioimaging. [35] Meanwhile, CDs with a large number of amine groups and carboxylic groups normally exhibit positive charges and negative charges, respectively, which shows great potential in binding with anticancer drugs by electrostatic interactions for drug/gene delivery. In the cell nucleus, they may also interact with DNA/RNA by electrostatic interactions or hydrogen bonding. [28,29,36] Therefore, in this project, to comprehensively understand DNA binding mechanisms of CDs and their effects on the cell cycle arrest in cancer cells, two types of CDs reported in our previous work, [37,38] Y15-CDs and Black-CDs (B-CDs), which predominantly possess amino and carboxylic groups on their surfaces, respectively, were selected to represent typical CDs used in cancer therapy and bioimaging. Their comparable sizes also make them ideal candidates for this study, as this minimizes the influence of particle size on DNA-binding ability. Furthermore, our previous study already demonstrated that Y15-CDs can induce cancer cell death due to the presence of Y15 in their structure. It is important to investigate whether Y15 on the surface of CDs participates in DNA binding, serving as one of the mechanisms that promote cell cycle arrest in cancer cells. B-CDs, synthesized from carbon powder and characterized by a simpler structure, can provide an ideal comparison to Y15-CDs.

  To achieve the purpose, the prepared Y15-CDs and B-CDs were characterized by UV–vis, fluorescence emission and Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, transmission electron microscope (TEM), atomic force microscope (AFM) and zeta potential measurement to confirm successful synthesis. The DNA binding abilities and binding modes of CDs were systematically evaluated through fluorometric competitive displacement assays, circular dichroism spectroscopy, molecular docking, all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations and binding free energy calculations. Subsequently, the intracellular distribution, cytotoxicity, cell cycle distribution and DNA damage detection of osteosarcoma (U2OS) cells treated with CDs were assessed. Finally, zebrafish embryos were employed as an in vivo model to investigate the effects of CDs on embryonic development.


## Materials and methods

### Materials and reagents
  Carbon nanopowder (<100 nm particle size), calf thymus sonicated DNA, folic acid (FA, ≥ 97 %), 1,2,4,5-benzenetetramine tetrahydrochloride (Y15, ≥ 95 %), thiazolyl blue tetrazolium bromide (MTT), 4′,6-diamidine-2′-phenylindole dihydrochloride (DAPI), methyl green (MG, ∼85 %), 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU, 95 %), cy5-azide (95 %), BioTracker NIR694 Nuclear Dye (dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)), RNase (DNase-free), and mouse monoclonal anti-phospho-histone H2AX (Ser139) antibodies (Cat#: MABE1095) were ordered from Sigma-Aldrich (St Louis, MO, USA). Donkey Anti-Mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 647) was bought from Abcam (Cambridge, MA, USA). 1,2-ethylenediamine (EDA, ≥ 99 %), ethidium bromide (EB), propidium iodide (PI) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). The deionized (DI) water was purified by a Modulab 2020 water purification system obtained from Continental Water System Corporation (San Antonio, TX, USA), achieving a resistivity of 18.2 MΩ.cm and a surface tension of 72.6 mN·m−1 at 22 ℃. All reagents above were used as received without further purification.


### Synthesis of CDs
  The B-CDs and Y15-CDs were synthesized by the methods reported in our previous publications. [[37], [38], [39]] Briefly, to synthesize B-CDs, 1 g of carbon nanopowder was dispersed in a mixture containing 36 mL of concentrated sulfuric acid and 12 mL of concentrated nitric acid, followed by refluxing for 15 h at 110 ℃. After the reaction, the mixture was cooled down to room temperature and neutralized by the gradual addition of a saturated sodium hydroxide solution in an ice bath. The neutralized suspension was vacuum filtered multiple times to remove unreacted carbon nanopowder. The filtrate was then cooled in an ice bath to induce the crystallization of salts (sodium sulfate and sodium nitrate), with sodium sulfate used as seed crystal when necessary. The precipitated salts were removed by vacuum filtration, and this process was repeated several times to ensure complete purification. Subsequently, the purified solution was transferred to a separational funnel and extracted three times with 15 mL of chloroform to remove organic impurities. The aqueous phase was collected and centrifugated at 3000 rpm for 30 min. The supernatant was dialyzed in a dialysis membrane tubing with a molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) of 3500 Da for 5 days against DI water, during which the DI water was replaced every 4–10 h. Finally, the dialyzed solution was concentrated and dried by using a rotary evaporator to obtain B-CDs.

  Regarding the preparation of Y15-CDs, 50 mg of Y15, 10 mg of FA and 30 µL of EDA were dissolved in 10 mL of DI water under continuous stirring for 1 h. The prepared solution was then transferred into a 20 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and heated at 180 ℃ for 8 h. Upon completion of the reaction, the autoclave was cooled down to room temperature, and the resulting suspension was centrifuged twice at 9000 rpm and 4 ℃ for 20 min. The collected supernatant was filtered through a 0.2 µm membrane filter and dialyzed in a 100–500 MWCO dialysis membrane tubing for 3 days to remove unreacted precursors. The DI water for the dialysis was replaced every 24 h. Thereafter, the purified Y15-CDs solution was freeze-dried to acquire the Y15-CDs powder.


### Characterization
  The UV–vis absorption and fluorescence emission spectra of the prepared CDs were measured using a Cary 100 UV/vis spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies, USA) and a Fluorolog-3 fluorometer (Horiba Jobin Yvon, USA), respectively, with a quartz cell of 1 cm pathlength. The size of CDs was characterized by a Cypher S Atomic Force Microscope (AFM, Oxford Instruments, Asylum Research, Santa Barbara, CA) and a 1200X transmission electron microscope (TEM, JEOL, USA). Topographic AFM images with a resolution of 256 × 256 pixels were acquired by utilizing an AC160TSA-R3 AFM probe. For AFM sample preparation, the CDs diluted in water with concentration of 50 µg/mL were incubated on the freshly cleaved Mica surface for 5 min, followed by drying with nitrogen before the AFM imaging. TEM sample was prepared by adding one drop of CDs aqueous solution (50 µg/mL) to Formvar Film 150 Mesh (Cu), followed by drying in air. The functional groups of CDs were obtained from Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectra which measured by a PerkinElmer FTIR spectrometer (Frontier, PerkinElmer, USA) coupling with Smart Orbit attenuated total reflectance accessories (Thermo Scientific, USA). The zeta potential was acquired from a Malvern Zetasizer (Malvern Panalytical, USA).


### Fluorometric competitive displacement experiments
  The fluorometric competitive displacement experiments were performed to investigate the DNA binding mechanisms of B-CDs and Y15-CDs. 1 µg/mL of DNA solution was separately incubated with 0.6 µg/mL of EB and 0.1 µg/mL of DAPI for 30 min at room temperature to form DNA-EB and DNA-DAPI complexes through intercalative binding and minor groove binding, respectively. Then, varying concentrations of Y15-CDs or B-CDs were introduced into the complex solutions and further incubated for 30 min. Fluorescence spectra of obtained solutions were recorded at excitation wavelength of 510 nm and 358 nm, respectively. The MG based competitive displacement experiment, corresponding to the major groove binding, was conducted using a similar approach with 10 µg/mL of DNA and 1.0 µg/mL of MG.


### Structural modeling
  Based on the available information, the initial structure of Y15-CDs and B-CDs were created (Fig. S1) and optimized using the Gaussian 16 software package. [40] The charges and electrostatic surface potentials (ESP) were computed utilizing B3LYP (Becke, 3-parameter, Lee-Yang-Parr) hybrid functional. [41] Carbon, hydrogen were treated using 6-31G(d) basis set, while nitrogen and oxygen are treated with 6–31 + G(d) basis set. [[42], [43], [44], [45]] Frequency calculations were carried out following the single point calculations of QM-optimized structures using the B3LYP/6-311G(d, p) level of theory. [46] Calculated IR spectra were plotted employing the Multiwfn 3.8 [47,48] program with scaling factors of 0.967. The initial structure of the DNA was obtained from the protein data bank (PDB ID: 1D54) with chain 5′-TGTACA-3′. [49] The AutoDock Vina 1.5.7 software was used to obtain the possible binding modes of Y15-CDs and B-CDs onto the DNA double helices. [50] The rigid docking protocol was used where the structure of receptor DNA was maintained, while the CDs ligands were flexibly docked on either major or minor groove. The grid size was set and centered to cover the whole DNA and most of the CDs. The spacing remained at the standard value of 0.375 Å. An exhaustiveness value of 20 was calculated for increasing the chance of optimal sampling. The number of modes was set to 20 to produce 20 different binding modes. The PDB files of CDs and DNA were saved separately from the docking procedure and missing hydrogens on CDs were added. These PDB files of CDs were submitted to Automated Topology Builder (ATB) and Repository 3.0 [51] for creating all-atom parameterized topology files and pre-formatted PDB files for GROMACS. [52,53].


### Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations
  The highest-ranked groove and intercalative binding modes provided by molecular docking were further explored using all-atom MD simulations. The CDs-DNA system was saturated by TIP3P water molecules in a cubic simulation box with 2.0 nm spacing to prevent artificial self-interactions across the periodic boundaries. Sodium and chloride ions were added to neutralize the charge of the system and mimic the physiological saline concentration of 0.154 mM. [54] The solvated and electroneutral systems were subsequently relaxed via steepest descent algorithm for 5000 steps. This energy minimization step was the starting point for real time-dependent dynamics. The MD simulations were then carried out with a canonical ensemble (NVT) with distance restraints imposed between the first two base pairs from the 5′ termini to equilibrate the temperature. [55] The long-range electrostatics were treated using Particle-Mesh Ewald (PME) approach. [56] To stabilize the pressure of the system, an isothermal-isobaric ensemble (NPT) was carried out with the same restraints and PME method as in NVT step for 100 ps. A constant pressure of 1 atm and constant temperature of 300 K were set for coupling groups of DNA, CDs, water molecules and ions in a 50 ns NPT production simulation. Periodic boundary condition (PBC) was then utilized to mimic an infinite system to avoid self-interaction artifacts and prepare the trajectory for analysis and visualization. [57] Cluster analysis was conducted to derive the most representative structure during the simulation. Root-mean-square-deviation (RMSD) values of the CDs-DNA systems were calculated using VMD 1.9.4 software. [58] All trajectory frames had the backbones of target portions aligned to the reference frame for this calculation, and then the RMSD values were averaged with standard deviations. The binding free energies of major-groove, minor-groove and intercalate binding modes were computed using the molecular mechanics Poisson-Boltzmann surface area (MMPBSA) approach seperately. [59].


### Cellular distribution of CDs
  U2OS cells were seeded in an 8-well chamber slide at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well in 300 µL of complete Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) which contained 10 % of fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1 % of penicillin–streptomycin and incubated for 24 h. The medium was then replaced with fresh DMEM containing 1 mg/mL of B-CDs or Y15-CDs. After incubation for 2 h, the cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde (PFA) in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.5 % Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min and stained by BioTracker NIR694 Nuclear Dye (DMSO) for 30 min at room temperature. Finally, the cells were mounted using a mounting medium and covered with Gold SealTM Cover Glass lids for cell imaging.


### Cytotoxicity of CDs
  The cytotoxicity of CDs was evaluated by the MTT assay. Specifically, U2OS cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 5 × 103 cells/well in 200 µL of complete DMEM. After incubation for 24 h at 37 °C, the culture medium was removed and replaced with fresh DMEM containing different concentrations of B-CDs and Y15-CDs (0, 6.25, 12.5, 25, 50, 100 µg/mL), followed by an additional 72 h incubation. Thereafter, 20 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL) were added to each well and incubated for 4 h. The culture medium was discarded, and 150 µL of DMSO was added to solubilize the formed crystals. Tecan Microplate Reader was used to record the absorbance of each well at 595 nm.


### Cell cycle analysis
  The cell cycle distribution of U2OS cells treated with B-CDs and Y15-CDs were determined by flow cytometry analysis. U2OS cells were seeded in 6-well plates (4 × 105 cells/well) with complete DMEM. Upon incubation of 24 h, the medium was removed and the fresh DMEM without 10 % FBS was added, followed by incubating another 24 h. Next, the cells were treated with 50 and 100 µg/mL of B-CDs and Y15-CDs in complete DMEM. Following a 24-hour incubation, the cells were harvested using 0.25 % trypsin-EDTA solution, centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C and washed twice with 1 mL of ice-cold PBS. After that, the cell pellets were kept on ice, resuspended in 100 µL of PBS and fixed by adding 1 mL of cold methanol for 30 min. Prior to flow cytometry analysis, the fixed cells were centrifuged again, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in 200 µL of RNase solution (20 µg/mL) along with 10 µL of PI solution (200 µg/mL). The obtained samples were then incubated in darkness at 4 ℃ for 30 min and transferred into a 96-well plate for flow cytometry analysis.

  The detection of cells in S phase were further performed by EdU labeling approach. U2OS cells were seeded in 35 mm glass-bottom dishes (1.5 × 105 cells/dish). After being incubated with B-CDs and Y15-CDs in medium at the concentrations of 10, 50 and 100 µg/mL for 24 h, the cells were treated with 10 µM of EdU for 1 h, fixed by cold 4 % PFA for 15 min and permeabilized with permeabilization buffer (0.5 % Triton X-100 in PBS) for 20 min. The permeabilization buffer were removed, 200 µL of “click” reaction mixture consisting of 1 mM copper (II) sulfate, 5 µM Cy5-azide, and 1 mg/mL sodium ascorbate were added with further incubation for 30 min in dark environment. The cells were then stained with 1 µg/mL DAPI and mounted for imaging.


### Cell imaging for DNA damage
  U2OS cells were plated in an 8-well chamber slide at a density of 1.5 x 104 cells/well in 300 µL of complete DMEM. Following a 24-hour incubation, the cell culture medium was replaced with fresh DMEM supplemented with 0.1 mg/mL of B-CDs or Y15-CDs. After further incubation of 48 h, the cells were fixed with 4 % PFA, permeabilized in 0.5 % Triton X-100, blocked with blocking buffer for 1h and incubated with mouse anti-γH2AX antibody (1:200) overnight at 4 ℃. The following day, the cells were incubated with goat anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 647 s antibody (1:2000) for 1 h, stained with 1 µg/ml DAPI for 10 min and mounted for imaging.


### Effect of CDs on zebrafish embryonic development
  Fertilized wild-type zebrafish (Danio rerio) eggs were obtained from the University of Miami Zebrafish Core Facility. To investigate the distribution of CDs in zebrafish embryos, the embryos at 24 hpf (hour post-fertilization) were incubated in B-CDs or Y15-CDs solution (100 µg/mL) for 6 h. Then, the zebrafish embryos after soaking with B-CDs or Y15-CDs were observed under confocal microscope at the excitation wavelength of 488 nm. For evaluation of the impact of CDs on zebrafish embryonic development, freshly laid eggs (< 2 hpf) were incubated in B-CDs or Y15-CDs solutions at the concentrations of 1.0, 10, 50, and 100 µg/mL. The incubation temperature was controlled at 28 ℃. The survival rate of zebrafish embryos after exposure to different concentrations of B-CDs or Y15-CDs for 4, 8, 24, 48 and 72 h were recorded. Meanwhile, the body length, eye size and head size of zebrafish embryos after incubation with CDs for 72 h were measured under the microscope. These zebrafish experiments were performed in adherence to an animal care protocol (IPROTO202400000598) approved by the University of Miami Animal Care and Use Committee and complies with the guidelines of the National Science Foundation.


## Results and discussion

### Synthesis and characterization of CDs
  In this study, B-CDs were synthesized via a “top-down” approach using carbon nanopowder with the help of sulfuric acid and nitric acid as oxidizing agents, while the Y15-CDs were prepared by a “bottom up” method using Y15, FA and EDA as precursors. To confirm the successful synthesis, the morphologies, optical and structure properties of as-prepared B-CDs and Y15-CDs were characterized. Fig. 1 shows the AFM and TEM images, along with size distribution histograms (more than 300 particles were counted) representing the height (z-axis) and diameter in the x-y plane of the CDs, respectively. The results illustrate that both B-CDs and Y15-CDs are well dispersed with spherical or elliptical shapes. The mean particle size of B-CDs is 2.0–2.4 nm in height and 2.0–3.0 nm in the x-y plane (Fig. 1A), while Y15-CDs exhibit an average height of 1.8 nm and a diameter of 3 nm in the x-y plane (Fig. 1B). It demonstrates that both B-CDs and Y15-CDs possess ultrasmall sizes, which may provide great potential in penetrating the nucleus membrane of cells in further studies.Fig. 1(i) AFM and (ii) TEM images of (A) B-CDs and (B) Y15-CDs.

  The optical and structural properties of B-CDs and Y15-CDs were evaluated through UV–vis absorption, fluorescence emission and FTIR spectroscopies. The obtained spectra are displayed in Fig. 2. In the UV–vis spectra (Fig. 2A), B-CDs exhibit two typical peaks at 228 and 265 nm, corresponding to π − π* transitions of C=C bonds and n − π* transitions of C=O bonds, respectively. Y15-CDs show two characteristic peaks at 274 and 470 nm, which can be ascribed to the π − π*/ n − π* transitions involving C=C/C=O bonds and the presence of a large π-conjugated system in the structure of Y15-CDs, respectively. By varying excitation wavelength from 325 to 575 nm in 25 nm intervals, photoluminescence (PL) emission spectra of B-CDs and Y15-CDs were recorded. The spectra in Fig. 2, Fig. 2 demonstrate that both B-CDs and Y15-CDs possess an excitation-dependent PL emission. For B-CDs, the maximum emission peak appears at 515 nm when excited at 425 nm, which corresponds to the green fluorescence. In contrast, Y15-CDs display two distinct emission peaks at 450 and 575 nm, indicative of the blue and green fluorescence, respectively, under excitation wavelength of 375 and 450 nm.Fig. 2Characterization of CDs. (A) UV–vis spectra; Fluorescence emission spectra of (B) B-CDs and (C) Y15-CDs; and (D) FTIR spectra. Insets in the B and C are normalized fluorescence emission spectra.

  The functional groups of B-CDs and Y15-CDs were determined by FTIR, with the results shown in Fig. 2D. B-CDs exhibit a broad band in the range of 3689–3000 cm−1 and a peak at 1711 cm−1, which can be assigned to O–H and C=O stretching vibrations, respectively, from carboxyl groups (–COOH). Meanwhile, the C=C stretching and O–H bending vibrations were observed at 1584 and 1400 cm−1, respectively. These characteristic peaks altogether confirm the presence of –COOH groups in the structure of B-CDs. As for Y15-CDs, in addition to the C=C stretching band, several typical peaks were observed at 3360, 2908, 2803, 1662 and 1501 cm−1. Among them, the bands at 3360 and 1501 cm−1 correspond to N–H stretching and N–H bending vibrations of amine groups. The peaks at 2908 and 2803 cm−1 are attributed to the C–H stretching vibrations, while the peak at 1662 cm−1 is ascribed to the C=O/C=N stretching vibrations from amide bonds and nitrogen-containing heterocyclic ring. Thus, different from B-CDs that are rich in carboxyl groups, Y15-CDs predominantly contain amine groups, consistent with our previous publications. [37,39] The zeta potential of B-CDs and Y15-CDs are −30.4 ± 5.95 and 16.9 ± 3.9 mV, respectively, which further confirms our successful synthesis of B-CDs and Y15-CDs in this project.


### DNA binding mechanisms of CDs
  CDs possess abundant π bonds and diverse functional groups on their surfaces. Owing to these π bonds and specific functional groups such as carboxyl, hydroxyl and amine groups, they can interact with many biomolecules and ions through electrostatic interactions, π-π stacking, hydrogen binding and hydrophobic interactions. Up to now, some CDs have been developed as probes for nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) detection and nucleolar imaging. [60] However, regarding the interaction between CDs and DNA, the specific binding sites of CDs on DNA have not yet been systematically investigated. Since many anticancer drugs show the therapeutic efficacy by binding with DNA via intercalative, major groove or minor groove interactions, understanding the DNA binding modes of CDs is significantly important in this study. In this regard, the fluorometric competitive displacement experiments, circular dichroism studies and molecular docking simulations were performed to investigate the DNA binding mechanisms of B-CDs and Y15-CDs.


#### Competitive displacement studies
  To experimentally investigate the DNA binding modes of B-CDs and Y15-CDs, competitive displacement studies were conducted by using EB, DAPI and MG as fluorescent DNA binding probes. Among them, EB is a typical intercalative molecule that binds strongly to DNA through intercalative interactions, while DAPI and MG bind to DNA via minor groove and major groove binding modes, respectively. Fluorescence of EB, DAPI and MG are very weak in water. Once they bind with DNA, their intrinsic fluorescence will be significantly increased because of formation of EB-DNA, DAPI-DNA and MG-DNA complexes. However, when other molecules with the same DNA binding mechanism as that of EB, DAPI or MG are introduced to the solution of complexes, some of the EB, DAPI or MG will be displaced from DNA, resulting in a reduced fluorescence. [[61], [62], [63]].

  The fluorescence spectra of EB, DAPI and MG bound to DNA were measured with the addition of increasing concentrations of B-CDs or Y15-CDs. As displayed in Fig. 3, Fig. 3, EB exhibits a maximum fluorescence emission at 612 nm when excited at 510 nm. Upon binding to DNA, the fluorescence intensity increases, and the emission peak shifts from 612 to 600 nm, indicating the formation of EB-DNA complexes. Upon the addition of B-CDs to the EB-DNA solution (Fig. 3A), the fluorescence intensity remains nearly unchanged, inferring that B-CDs are unbale to replace EB from the EB-DNA complexes. Such results suggest that the intercalative binding between B-CDs and DNA is either unlikely to occur or is too weak to be detected. In contrast, when Y15-CDs were added to the solution at increasing concentrations from 0.2 to 2.0 µg/mL, a significant fluorescence quenching was observed. At the highest concentration of Y15-CDs (2.0 µg/mL), the maximum emission peak decreased from 9.8 × 105 to 6.8 × 105, corresponding to a quenching of 30.6 %, suggesting that Y15-CDs can effectively intercalate into DNA and displace some EB from the EB-DNA complex.Fig. 3Competitive displacement assays. Fluorescence emission spectra of 0.6 µg/mL DAPI bound 1.0 µg/mL DNA with addition of different concentration of (C) B-CDs and (D) Y15-CDs at excitation wavelength of 358 nm. Fluorescence emission spectra of 1.0 µg/mL MG bound 10 µg/mL DNA in the presence of different concentration of (E) B-CDs and (F) Y15-CDs at excitation wavelength of 633 nm.

  Fig. 3, Fig. 3 show the results of the DAPI competitive binding assay, which targets the DNA minor groove. The maximum emission peak of DAPI-DNA complex appears at 455 nm under the excitation wavelength of 358 nm. When B-CDs were added at concentration below 1.0 µg/mL, no obvious change of fluorescence intensity was observed (Fig. 3C). However, upon addition of 2.0 µg/mL B-CDs, the fluorescence was quenched around 12 %. Compared to B-CDs, Y15-CDs exhibit a stronger displacement effect (Fig. 3D), with fluorescence intensity decreasing progressively as the concentration increased, reaching a maximum reduction of 51 %. These results suggest that both B-CDs and Y15-CDs can displace DAPI from DAPI–DNA complexes, binding to DNA through the minor groove. Since the reduction in fluorescence intensity upon addition of Y15-CDs was significantly greater than that observed with B-CDs, Y15-CDs were considered to exhibit a stronger binding affinity toward DNA.

  Fig. 3, Fig. 3 illustrate the fluorescence spectra from MG-based competitive study which assesses the binding of B-CDs and Y15-CDs to DNA by major groove binding mode. The fluorescence spectra were recorded with excitation at 633 nm and emission at 672 nm. The results show that both B-CDs and Y15-CDs are able to displace MG from MG-DNA complexes, demonstrating their capability for major groove binding. The fluorescence intensity of MG-DNA reduced by 7.5 % and 56 % with addition of 2.0 µg/mL B-CDs and Y15-CDs, respectively. Similar to the minor groove binding, Y15-CDs show higher binding efficiency than B-CDs. Since Y15-CDs possess positive charges, this stronger interaction might derive from the electrostatic interactions between Y15-CDs and DNA, which is one of the major forces to stabilize the groove binding. [9].


#### Circular dichroism studies
  Circular dichroism spectroscopy is a widely used technique for determining the secondary structure of DNA. It has been reported that circular dichroism spectra of DNA are highly sensitive to conformational and/or morphological changes caused by interactions with small molecules. In general, when molecules bind to DNA through intercalation, the base stacking and helicity bands are significantly perturbated. However, groove bindings result in minimal or no perturbation of these bands. [37,63,64] Thus, to further investigate the DNA binding mechanisms of B-CDs and Y15-CDs, the circular dichroism spectra of DNA in the absence and presence of different concentrations of B-CDs and Y15-CDs were recorded, with the results shown in Fig. 4. The obtained spectrum of DNA exhibits a positive band at 274 nm and a negative band at 245 nm, representing base stacking and right-handed helicity of B-form DNA, respectively. From Fig. 4A, when B-CDs were added to DNA solution, only a slight decrease in the intensity of the band at 245 nm was observed, while no obvious change was found in the band at 274 nm, indicating that the primary interaction between B-CDs and DNA might be groove binding modes. For the interaction between Y15-CDs and DNA (Fig. 4B), with the concentration of Y15-CDs increasing, both bands related to base stacking and right-handed helicity are reduced significantly. These noticeable perturbations confirm the presence of an intercalation mechanism when Y15-CDs bind to DNA. Therefore, the DNA binding mechanisms of B-CDs and Y15-CDs identified though circular dichroism spectroscopy are consistent with those determined by competitive displacement studies.Fig. 4Circular dichroism spectra of 50 µg/mL of DNA with addition of different concentrations of (A) B-CDs and (B) Y15-CDs.


#### Modeling of CDs-DNA interactions

##### Structural modeling of CDs
  According to experimental data reported in this study and in our previous publications, B-CDs and Y15-CDs can exist in either spherical or elliptical forms with different sizes. [[37], [38], [39]] B-CDs were proposed to have multiple graphene-like layers with mostly sp2 carbons with carboxyl and alcohol groups distributed on the edge-plane. [39] The overall dimensions of B-CDs were expected to be around 2.0–2.4 nm in height and 2.0–3.0 nm in length according to AFM and TEM data. A representative model of B-CDs was established with five layers in a nearly parallel stacked pattern (Fig. 5A). A gradual decrease in size of the hexagonal graphitic sheet from the center to the terminal position makes the model more realistic. There are 20 –COOH groups in the peripheral area of B-CDs structure supported by 21 –COOH groups/B-CDs particles from the experimental data. Besides that, several carbonyl and ether group linkages are located either on the edges of the graphene sheets or within interlayer domains to form a robust multilayer framework with dimensions in agreement with the experimental data. Additionally, several covalent C-O-C and C–C bonds between lattices tightened the spacing between minor graphitic planes and major plane to around 0.35 to 0.45 nm. The presence of oxygen-containing functional moieties also endows B-CDs with good water solubilities and high polarities, as well as greater possibility of being anionic CDs.Fig. 5Representative structures and dimensions of CDs obtained from DFT calculations and MD simulations: (A) B-CDs and (B) Y15-CDs.

  Y15-CDs from FA, Y15 and EDA were proposed to possess nitrogen-doped aromatic domains with branched derivatives of precursors. [37,[65], [66], [67]] Similar to B-CDs, Y15-CDs were established by assembling 5 layers in a descending order of size from the middle. In Fig. 5B, a representative structure of Y15-CDs after MD simulations is shown to demonstrate a π-π stacking model with extra benzenetetramine groups provide dispersion effects. An intercalative binding mode is possible considering the insertion of the benzenetetramine group between base pairs of the DNA. The nature of peripheral –NH2 functional groups enables enhanced solubility and electrostatic binding with negatively charged DNA. The maximum dimension of this representative Y15-CD model can reach 2.8 to 3.0 nm in length and 1.7 to 1.9 nm in height which correspond with the most populated sizes according to AFM and TEM. In comparison to experimentally determined FTIR spectra in Fig. 2D, theoretically calculated IR spectrum in Fig. S2A exhibits the corresponding typical peaks around 3400, 2800 and the range of 1500–1700 cm−1, which are attributed to N–H stretching, C–H stretching and C=O/C=N/C=C bonds in Y15-CDs. Similarly, the computed IR spectrum of B-CDs (Fig. S2B) shows peaks in the range of 3000–3800 cm−1 and 1400–1700 cm−1, which agrees with the functional groups represented by FTIR spectra including –OH and –COOH.


##### CDs-DNA interactions
  The structures of B-CDs and Y15-CDs were subsequently used to explore their interactions with DNA and compare the binding affinities. Fluorometric competitive displacement experiments show that Y15-CDs possessed higher binding affinities from either major or minor groove than B-CDs, which is due to the cationic nature of Y15-CDs. However, the molecular-level identification of interactions between CDs and DNA was still unavailable. To address this issue, molecular docking and MD simulations were performed for different binding modes followed by binding free energies calculations. During the simulation, π-π stacking interactions were maintained as the major non-covalent effects in CDs. [68] Multiple docking modes of B-CDs and Y15-CDs onto DNA model were observed: major and minor groove binding modes for B-CDs, major and minor groove binding modes for Y15-CDs, intercalative binding modes from major groove side or from minor groove side for Y15-CDs. Regarding Y15-CDs, the intercalation between planar benzenetetramine groups and adjacent base pairs are supported by fluorometric competitive displacement experiments and circular dichroism data. However, the groove binding was less pronounced than intercalative binding mode. This was later confirmed by binding energy calculations. From Fig. 6, hydrogen bonding interactions are found to be dominant in MD equilibrated structures. The negatively charged phosphate groups of DNA also associate with positively charged Y15-CDs through electrostatic attractions. [37,[69], [70], [71], [72]].Fig. 6MD derived binding modes of CDs to DNA (PDB ID = 1D54) with: (A) major groove binding mode of the Y15-CDs-DNA complex, (B) major groove intercalative binding of the Y15-CDs-DNA complex, (C) minor groove binding of the Y15-CDs-DNA complex, (D) minor groove intercalative binding of the Y15-CDs-DNA complex, (E) major groove binding of the B-CDs-DNA complex, and (F) minor groove binding of the B-CDs-DNA complex.

  The RMSD values and binding energies of CDs-DNA systems using the MMPBSA method were calculated for all six binding modes (Table S1). Y15-CDs have similar binding energy to B-CDs with major groove binding (−34.8 kcal/mol compared to −34.4 kcal/mol). However, for Y15-CDs, benzenetetramine or other branched aromatic groups could further intercalate between base pairs. In this case, extra π-π stackings strengthened the binding affinity of Y15-CDs. Their intercalative binding free energy in the major groove side was calculated as −53.5 kcal/mol, which is the most stable binding mode. The stability of this binding mode was evidenced by the RMSD value of 1.49 ± 0.45 Å throughout the equilibration period of MD trajectory (Table S1). The minor groove binding of Y15-CDs is 10.4 kcal/mol more stable than that of B-CDs. Notably, the intercalative binding mode from the minor groove side shows an even higher binding affinity i.e. 24.4 kcal/mol higher binding affinity. Thus, Y15-CDs show stronger binding affinity to DNA, which is in line with experimental observation that cationic Y15-CDs preferentially interact with DNA through electrostatic and intercalative dispersion interactions.

  The cellular distribution of the as-prepared CDs in U2OS cells was studied using a fluorescence microscope. After incubation with B-CDs and Y15-CDs for 2 h, the nuclei of cells were stained with NIR 694 dye. Cell imaging was performed under green and red channel of the fluorescence microscope to capture the fluorescence signals from CDs and the NIR 694 dye, respectively. The fluorescence images are shown in Fig. 7, Fig. 7. Both B-CDs and Y15-CDs are capable of crossing the cell membrane and penetrating the nuclear membrane, with distributing in both the cytoplasm and the nucleus. For B-CDs (Fig. 7A), the green fluorescence signals in the nucleus are comparable to or slightly lower than those in the cytoplasm. However, Y15-CDs (Fig. 7B) exhibit stronger fluorescence signals in the nucleus than in the cytoplasm. These results indicate that Y15-CDs may have better nucleus targeting ability than B-CDs. Since B-CDs and Y15-CDs possess similar sizes in the characterization of TEM and AFM, this difference might derive from their surface structures. From zeta potential measurement, we have known that B-CDs carry a negative charge due to carboxylic groups, whereas Y15-CDs carry a positive charge attributed to amine groups. The better nucleus targeting capability of Y15-CDs might be because Y15-CDs could interact with the negatively charged nuclear membrane through electrostatic interactions, thereby enhancing their penetration and accumulation into the cell nucleus. Thus, the cellular distribution study proves the nucleus targeting ability of both B-CDs and Y15-CDs.   The in vitro cytotoxicity of B-CDs and Y15-CDs to U2OS cells was evaluated using the MTT assay. As shown in Fig. 7C, Y15-CDs exhibit higher cytotoxicity toward U2OS cells compared to B-CDs. ### Cell cycle analysis
  The cell cycle is an intricate sequence of events that regulates the genomic DNA replication and cell division, which is mainly divided into two distinct stages: interphase and mitosis (M). Interphase consists of the G1, S and G2 phase which correspond to pre-DNA synthesis, DNA synthesis and preparation for division, respectively, while the M phase is related to the actual process of cell division. In addition to actively cycling cells, there are also some non-growing or non-proliferating cells that are not in the cell cycle but have the potential to re-enter. They are classified as being in the G0 phase. As the cell cycle involves DNA replication and cell division, the DNA content of cells varies depending on the phase. G1 is regarded as the initial step in cell cycle progression, with DNA content of 2 N in cells. During the S phase, DNA synthesis and replication occur, resulting in a gradual increase in DNA content from 2 N to 4 N. Once DNA replication is complete, cells in the G2 and M phases maintain a DNA content of 4 N. Thus, by determining the DNA content, the cells in the different cell cycle phases can be determined. [73,74].

  In this project, to evaluate the influence of CDs on the cell cycle, U2OS cells treated with B-CDs and Y15-CDs for 48 h were analyzed by flow cytometry using PI as a nucleic acid staining dye. As shown in Fig. 7D-F and Fig. S3, the proportions of cells in the G0/G1, S and G2/M phases are 57 %, 17 % and 26 %, respectively, in the control group. In cells incubated with B-CDs, no significant changes in the cell cycle distribution were observed compared to control. In contrast, cells treated with Y15-CDs show an increase in the proportion of cells in S phase, rising to 46 % and 51 % at the concentration of 50 and 100 µg/mL, respectively. Correspondingly, the proportion of cells in the G2/M phase decreases to 16 % and 2 % after treatment with 50 and 100 µg/mL of Y15-CDs. The proportion of cells in the G0/G1 phase also reduces to 38 % and 47 % at 50 and 100 µg/mL. As a summary, the flow cytometry data suggests that Y15-CDs can arrest the U2OS cell cycle primarily at the S phase which corresponds to the DNA synthesis and replication process. To further confirm S phase arrest, the quantification of U2OS cells in S phase was also performed by EdU labeling approach followed by confocal microscopic imaging. In which EdU can incorporate into replicating DNA, recognizing the cells in S phase. As illustrated in Fig. S4, the nuclei of cells were stained with DAPI and the cells in S phase were labeled with a Cy5 dye coupled with EdU by a click reaction. The proportion of cells in the S phase was calculated and is displayed in Fig. S5. Consistent with the flow cytometry results, B-CDs did not affect the proportion of cells in S phase, while Y15-CDs induced a significantly increased cells proportion. These cell cycle analysis results imply that Y15-CDs may interfere with DNA synthesis of cells after strongly binding to nuclear DNA through both intercalative and groove binding modes. The minimal influence of B-CDs on the cell cycle may be due to their weak interaction with nuclear DNA, which is insufficient to cause disturbances.


### DNA damage detection
  Cellular genomic integrity is often threatened by various chemical and physical factors that are able to alter nucleotide bases or induce breaks in the sugar-phosphate backbone. If DNA is damaged or abnormally structured, cells typically activate checkpoints that delay the cell cycle, providing time for DNA repair, chromatin restructuring, and other metabolic response, or triggering cellular death. Numerous studies have reported that compounds such as reactive oxygen species (ROS), intercalating agents, alkylating agents and base analogues can induce the DNA damage in cells, thereby serving as effective DNA damaging agents in cancer therapy. [[75], [76], [77]] From flow cytometry and DNA binding experiments, we have proven that both B-CDs and Y15-CDs can bind to DNA, with Y15-CDs inducing S phase arrest in U2OS cells. To investigate whether this cell cycle arrest is also induced by DNA damage, DNA damage detection was performed. Phosphorylation of the Ser-139 residue of the histone variant H2AX, forming γH2AX, is a marker for double-strand breaks and an early cellular response signal to repair DNA double-strand break damage. [78] The confocal images for detection of γH2AX in U2OS cells after treating with B-CDs and Y15-CDs at 100 µg/mL for 48 h are shown in Fig. 8. The nuclei of cells were stained with DAPI and the γH2AX foci were labeled with Alexa Fluor 647 (red) antibody. The fluorescence signals of γH2AX from nuclei of control group is negligible. After being treated with B-CDs and Y15-CDs, cells exhibit the γH2AX foci within the nucleus. The mean fluorescence intensities of γH2AX foci across different groups are presented in Fig. S6. It shows that the fluorescence intensity in cells treated with Y15-CDs is appropriately 2.7-fold higher than that observed in B-CDs-treated cells, indicating a greater extent of DNA damage. Therefore, after entering the cellular nucleus, both B-CDs and Y15-CDs can induce DNA damage in U2OS cells, but Y15-CDs exhibit greater DNA-damaging capability. Considering that γH2AX (a sensitive and quantitative indicator of DNA damage under standardized conditions) alone does not fully resolve the type or origin of DNA lesions. In the future, incorporate complementary markers and orthogonal assays will be further applied to investigate the mechanisms underlying CDs-induced genomic stress.Fig. 8Confocal microscopic images for detection of γH2AX in U2OS cells after treating with (A) B-CDs and (B) Y15-CDs at 100 µg/mL for 48 h. ### Zebrafish embryonic development study
  Zebrafish is a small teleost fish species which has been extensively used as an in vivo model in the field of developmental biology, genetics, molecular biology, anticancer drug screening and toxicologic studies, owing to its genomic (> 70 %), anatomical and physiological similarities to humans as well as high fertilization rate. Its developmental stages can be divided into four major periods: embryos (within 72 hpf), larvae (72 hpf to 29 days), juvenile (30 days to 4 months) and adult (sexually mature stage). [[79], [80], [81]] In Danio rerio, cells divide rapidly and are easily observable in the first few hours after fertilization, with embryos developing very quickly. During the early developmental stages, both the embryo and chorion are transparent, allowing the scientist to monitor the developmental process from the first moments of fertilization to the free-swimming larval stage. [82] As cell division is rapid in zebrafish embryos and all major organ systems form within the first 5-days post-fertilization, they are highly sensitive to compound exposure, often showing morphological alterations and other developmental changes. [80,83] In this situation, zebrafish have been employed for screening of anticancer drugs such as cell cycle inhibitors, angiogenesis inhibitors, apoptosis inducers and cytotoxic agents. [81].

  The in vivo distribution, toxicity and impact of CDs on zebrafish embryonic development were investigated by incubation of zebrafish embryos/eggs in B-CDs and Y15-CDs solutions, followed by microscopic observations. Confocal microscopy images (Fig. 9, Fig. 9) show that both B-CDs and Y15-CDs can penetrate the chorion membrane and throughout the embryonic body, especially in the yolk sac. This indicates that B-CDs and Y15-CDs may directly interact with the embryos and potentially affect their growth. To evaluate the toxicity of CDs, freshly laid eggs (< 2 hpf) were incubated in B-CDs or Y15-CDs solutions at various concentrations, and embryo survival rates were recorded over different incubation periods by observation of 120 zebrafish embryos for each group. As illustrated in Fig. 9, Fig. 9, when the incubation time reached or exceeded 24 h, some zebrafish failed to survival at a high concentration (50 and 100 µg/mL) of B-CDs and Y15-CDs, leading to a reduced survival rate. Meanwhile, Y15-CDs resulted in a lower survival rate compared to B-CDs. Notably, no significant decrease in survival rate was observed as the incubation time increased from 24 to 72 h, suggesting that Y15-CDs and B-CDs primary terminate the growth of zebrafish at the first 24 h of embryonic development, during which the embryos undergo a remarkable transition from a single cell to a highly developed body. ## Conclusion
  In this project, the DNA binding mechanisms of Y15-CDs and B-CDs as well as their effects on the cell cycle arrest were investigated. Morphological and structural characterization confirmed that both B-CDs and Y15-CDs have sizes around 2–3 nm, carrying negative and positive surface charges, respectively, which is consistent to our previous publications. Based on a combination of experimental and computational approaches including fluorometric competitive displacement assays, circular dichroism spectroscopy, molecular docking, MD simulations and binding free energy calculations, positively charged Y15-CDs were found to bind with DNA through both intercalative and groove-binding modes, while the negatively charged B-CDs primary bind to DNA via groove binding. Moreover, because of the positive charges and benzenetetramine groups in structure, Y15-CDs exhibit a higher binding affinity compared to B-CDs. Cellular studies using U2OS cells as an in vitro mode demonstrate that both B-CDs and Y15-CDs are capable of penetrating the nuclear membrane and entering the nucleus. Upon targeting nucleus and binding with DNA, Y15-CDs exhibit the ability to induce DNA damage and arrest U2OS cells in the S phase which correlates to the DNA synthesis and replication in cells. In contrast, B-CDs did not cause significant changes in the cell cycle profile. Using zebrafish as an in vivo model, the effect of B-CDs and Y15-CDs on zebrafish embryonic development were investigated. Both types of CDs are able to penetrate the chorion membrane and distribute throughout the embryonic body. Following 72 h of incubation of embryos (< 2 hpf), those treated with Y15-CDs exhibit reduced body length, eye size, and head size, indicating the developmental inhibition ability of Y15-CDs, which may correspond to the cell cycle arrest ability of Y15-CDs. Overall, the results in this project demonstrate that compared to negatively charged B-CDs, Y15-CDs, which carry positive charges and possess both intercalative and groove binding abilities to DNA, exert a stronger influence on cell cycle arrest in cancer cells and inhibit the development of zebrafish embryos. Our findings offer insight into DNA binding mechanisms of CDs and the design of CDs which can be used for inducing cell cycle arrest, highlighting their potential in cancer therapy.
