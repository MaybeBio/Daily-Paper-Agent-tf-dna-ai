# CD44v5 enhances the IL-4/IL-4Rα/STAT6/SAM68 aix inducing M2 polarization and promoting triple-negative breast cancer progression


## Background
  Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is an aggressive subtype with a poor prognosis and limited treatment options. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), the predominant and abundant immune cells in the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), critically drive TNBC progression. Consequently, TAM reprogramming has emerged as a promising therapeutic approach. However, a major barrier remains the incomplete understanding of the molecular mechanisms governing TAM reprogramming.


## Methods
  The role of CD44v5 in TAM polarization was evaluated with a CD44v5 monoclonal antibody and CD44v5-knockdown cell lines. Subsequently, cell functional assays, including wound healing, invasion, and colony formation assays, were performed to assess changes in the MDA-MB-468 cell line. Cytokine secretion levels (IL-4 and IL-6) were measured by electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA).


## Results
  We found that M2 macrophages and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) polarized through the IL4/IL4R signaling pathway and exerted similar protumorigenic functions, and that IL4 is the key protumorigenic factor secreted by M2 macrophages. Interestingly, CD44v5 blockade effectively inhibited M2 polarization and promoted the phenotypic shift to M1 macrophages, which was supported by increased CD86 expression and decreased IL-4 secretion. Furthermore, molecular docking analysis and colocalization microscopy confirmed that CD44v5 colocalized with IL-4Rα, preventing its internalization.


## Conclusion
  CD44v5 promotes M2 macrophage polarization by stabilizing and enhancing the IL-4Rα/STAT6/IL-4 signaling pathway, thereby facilitating the progression of triple-negative breast cancer. CD44v5 serves as an important therapeutic target for the reprogramming of both TAMs and M2 macrophages, thereby providing a novel strategy for the treatment of TNBC.


## Introduction
  Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is an aggressive malignancy characterized by high invasiveness, frequent recurrence, and substantial mortality, for which current conventional therapies often yield unsatisfactory outcomes.1-3 Consequently, the identification of novel therapeutic targets is of paramount importance.

  Therapeutic interventions for triple-negative breast cancer largely revolve around non-coding RNAs, epigenetic regulation, and RNA metabolism; however, the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) continues to represent an area with unmet targeting needs.4-6

  Tumor progression is known to be critically regulated by components of the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME),7-9 wherein tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) constitute the most abundant population of immune cells.10-12 TAMs are rarely polarized into M1-like TAMs with antitumor effects, but more often into tumor-promoting M2-like TAMs, which mainly play the roles of promoting angiogenesis, immunosuppression, and facilitating metastasis in tumor progression.13-16 TAMs, as an important part of TIME, provides a new therapeutic approach for treating tumors,12,17-19 but the mechanism and regulatory target of TAMs polarization are still unclear.

  Interleukin-4 (IL-4) plays an important role in inflammatory responses and macrophage differentiation.20-22 Several studies have demonstrated that IL-4 exerts antitumor effects through multiple mechanisms, including functional remodeling of intratumoral terminally exhausted CD8+ T cells, synergistic induction with IL-1β of a novel Th9 cell subset characterized by enhanced cytotoxicity and reduced exhaustion, as well as mediating CAR-T cell exhaustion.23-25 However, our previous study revealed that IL-4 contributes to the TIME promoting TNBC cell metastasis.26 Mollaoglu et al.27 found that IL-4 derived from ovarian cancer cells modulates the immunosuppressive TIME, thereby enhancing resistance to anti-PD-1 therapy. The source of IL-4 in tumors or TAMs in the TIME is still unclear.

  CD44 is a transmembrane glycoprotein. The cd44 gene undergoes alternative splicing to produce canonical (CD44s) and variant (CD44v) isoforms, and the expression of CD44v is correlated with tumor aggressiveness and metastasis.28-30

  Our previous study indicated that CD44v5 regulates helper T cell differentiation and promotes a type II immune response via the IL-4/IL-4R signaling pathway.31 In this study, we revealed that CD44v5 stabilizes and enhances the IL-4/IL-4Rα/STAT6 positive feedback axis by colocalizing with IL-4Rα, promoting M2 macrophage polarization and tumor progression by secreting IL-4. Interestingly, CD44v5 blockade inhibited M2 macrophage polarization and reprogrammed M2 macrophages from the protumorigenic phenotype to the tumor-suppressive phenotype. Taken together, our results revealed that CD44v5 is a potential target for a macrophage-targeting strategy against TNBC.


## Materials and methods

### Cell lines
  The triple-negative breast cancer cell line MDA-MB-468 and the human monocyte cell line THP-1 (sourced from Pricella) were cultured in Leibovitz's L-15 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Lonsera S711-001S) and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin. THP-1 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco). All the cell lines were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂ and were periodically tested for mycoplasma contamination. The identities of all the cell lines were verified via short tandem repeat (STR) sequence analysis. The cells were thawed and cultured for two passages before the experiments and subcultured for up to ten passages.


### Immunoblot analysis
  The cells were lysed with RIPA buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology P0038), and protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Biosharp BL612A and Beyotime Biotechnology P1082) were added at the recommended concentrations to prevent protein degradation. The protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (Boster AR0197). Proteins were separated by SDS‒PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore ISEQ00010). The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk powder for 2 hours at room temperature before being incubated overnight at 4 °C on a shaker with primary antibodies at the recommended dilutions. The following day, the membranes were washed three times with TBST (10 minutes per wash) and then incubated with the appropriate secondary antibodies for 2 hours at room temperature. After further washing with TBST, the membranes were developed using an ultrasensitive ECL chemiluminescent substrate (Biosharp BL520A) for 5 minutes. The primary antibodies used included anti-CD44v5 (Invitrogen BMS115), anti-IL-4Rα (Proteintech 67051-1-IG), anti-phosphorylated STAT6 (Abclonal AP1309 and Abcam), anti-CD163 (Nature Biosciences A17011), anti-CD86 (Abmart T40013) and SAM68 (Proteintech 10222-1-AP) antibodies. The secondary antibodies used were goat anti-mouse IgG (H+L) HRP (Affinity S0002) and goat anti-rabbit IgG (H+L) HRP (Affinity S0001).


### Co-culture experiments
  Human monocytic THP-1 cells were treated with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA; 100 ng/mL, MCE HY-18739) for 24 hours to induce differentiation. The following day, the cells were polarized into M2 macrophages by adding IL-4 (20 ng/mL, PeproTech 200-04-20 μg) or polarized into M1 macrophages by adding IFN-γ (20 ng/mL, PeproTech 300-02-20 μg) and bacterial lipopolysaccharides (LPS; 100 ng/mL, Sigma L2880-10MG). Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) were induced by co-culturing THP-1 cells with MDA-MB-468 cells for 48 hours using 0.4 μm porous inserts (Corning 3412).


### Cell migration assay
  MDA-MB-468 cells were seeded into six-well plates. Once the cells reached 80–90% confluence, a 10 μL pipette tip was used to create a scratch perpendicular to the surface of the plate at a uniform speed. The wells were washed twice with PBS to remove detached cells, and images of the scratch area were captured under a microscope. After a 24-hour incubation, the scratch area was photographed again. The distance migrated by the cells was measured and quantified using image analysis software.


### Cell invasion assay
  The cells were starved for 24 hours, and then trypsinized and resuspended in serum-free medium. A total of 5 × 10⁴ cells were seeded into 24-well transwell chambers precoated with a solidified layer of Matrigel matrix (Acro Biosystems AC-M082704). Complete medium (500 μL) was added to the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. After 48 hours of incubation, the transwell membranes were collected, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 minutes, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 10 minutes. Images were captured under a microscope, and three independent fields of view per well were analyzed for cell invasion quantification.


### Experiments on colony formation
  Control and experimental group cells were taken, digested and counted, and 600 cells per well in the final six-well plate wells were cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 temperature chamber for 12–15 d, washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 minutes, stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution, photographed, and counted using software for statistical analysis.


### siRNA sequences and transfection
  Transfection was carried out using an RNA transfection reagent (Sangon Biotech E607402-0100) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The siRNA targeting CD44v5 had the following sequence: 5′-CCACUGCUUAUGAAGGAAAdTdT-3′.


### Immunofluorescence staining
  THP-1 cells were differentiated into M2 macrophages on cell culture slides, washed with phosphate-buffered saline (Boster AR0032), and blocked with rapid immunocontainment solution (Beyotime Biotechnology P0260) for 15 minutes. Primary antibodies against IL-4Rα and CD44v5 were diluted proportionally and incubated with the cells overnight at 4 °C. The slides were washed three times with PBS (5 minutes per wash), followed by incubation with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 hours. The slides were stained with DAPI staining solution (Beyotime Biotechnology P0131) for nuclear visualization. After staining, the slides were washed, mounted with the stained coverslips, and imaged using a laser confocal scanning microscope (Cellsens Microsystems).


### Cytokine detection
  The conditioned culture medium was removed, and the cells were washed three times with PBS. A total of 1 mL of fresh complete culture medium was added, and the cells were incubated for 24 hours. The following day, the supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 minutes at 4 °C. The cell pellet was discarded, and the supernatant was diluted tenfold for analysis. Cytokine levels of IL-4 and IL-6 were quantified using the Ranos IMS1200 and Roche magnetic particle chemiluminescence immunoassay systems.


### Calculations and statistical analysis
  Cell migration, invasion, and colony formation assays were analyzed using Image J. Statistical analyses were performed using the t-test and one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test (GraphPad Prism version 9.0 and IBM SPSS Statistics version 23). All the experiments were performed in triplicate and independently repeated at least three times to ensure reproducibility, with the data presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). A p value of less than 0.05 (p < 0.05) was considered statistically significant.


## Results

### M2 macrophages with increased CD44v5 expression exert protumorigenic effects
  As outlined in our polarization schematic (Figure 1A), macrophages were polarized into the M1 and M2 phenotypes. We assessed the expression of the M1 macrophage marker CD86 and the M2 macrophage marker CD163. The results confirmed that the polarizations were successful and that CD44v5 expression was significantly upregulated in M2 macrophages (Figure 1B).

  To investigate the effects of different macrophage types on tumor cells, we conducted scratch assays and invasion assays on TNBC cell lines. Compared to control tumor cells, the migratory and invasive abilities of MDA-MB-468 cells were suppressed when they were cocultured with M1 macrophages but significantly enhanced when they were cocultured with M2 macrophages (Figure 1C and D). Similarly, colony formation assays demonstrated that the proliferative capacity of MDA-MB-468 cells was suppressed in the M1 macrophage coculture group but increased in the M2 macrophage coculture group (Figure 1E). Consistent results were obtained in the MDA-MB-231 cell line (Supplementary Figure S1D and S1E).


### M2 macrophage polarization and CD44v5 expression are induced by the IL-4Rα/STAT6/GATA3 pathway
  Our data revealed that M2 macrophages were polarized via the IL-4Rα/STAT6 signaling pathway (Figure 2A). To further confirm these findings, dupilumab (an IL-4R inhibitor) was applied, which resulted in reduced STAT6 phosphorylation and GATA3 expression, as well as the inhibition of M2 macrophage polarization (Figure 2B). AS1517499 (a STAT6 phosphorylation inhibitor) also suppressed M2 macrophage polarization and decreased the expression of CD44v5 and IL-4Rα (Figure 2C). The results indicated that M2 macrophages were polarized through the IL-4Rα/STAT6/GATA3 pathway, which upregulated CD44v5 and IL-4Rα expression in a positive feedback way. We also found that M2 macrophages and TAMs were both polarized through the IL-4Rα/STAT6 signaling pathway (Supplementary Figure S1A) and exhibited similar protumorigenic effects (Figure 2D, E, F).


### CD44v5 regulates M2 macrophage polarization and protumorigenic functions
  To further clarify the role of CD44v5 in M2 macrophage polarization, we employed an anti-CD44v5 monoclonal antibody (CD44v5 mAb) for CD44v5 blockade during the polarization of M2 macrophages. We found that the levels of IL-4Rα, phosphorylated STAT6 and GATA3 were correspondingly reduced, and M2 macrophage polarization was ultimately inhibited, whereas CD44 blockade had no such effect (Figure 3A). These data support the hypothesis that the CD44v5 domain participates in inducing M2 macrophage polarization rather than the CD44 common region.

  Compared with those in the control group, the migration, invasion, and proliferation of tumor cells were suppressed when co-cultured with CD44v5 blockade M2 macrophages (Figure 3B, C, and D). Consistent results were obtained using the MDA-MB-231 cell line (Supplementary Figure S1F and S1G). Additionally, CD44v5 knockdown in a cell line (Figure 3E) confirmed that CD44v5 knockdown inhibited M2 macrophage polarization and tumor-promoting effects (Figure 3F, G, and H), which was consistent with CD44v5 blockade. In addition, we found that M2 macrophages exerted a tumor suppressor function similar to that of M1 macrophages when we blocked CD44v5. Thus, we speculated that CD44v5 not only promoted polarization but also induced repolarization of M2 macrophages.


### CD44v5 blockade promotes M2 macrophage repolarization
  Blocking CD44v5 decreased CD163 expression and conversely increased CD86 expression during M2 macrophage polarization. Similar results were obtained in the CD44v5-knockdown cell line, further confirming that CD44v5 blockade could inhibit M2 macrophage polarization (Figure 4A, B). Next, we examined cytokine secretion levels in M0, M1, and M2 macrophages. The results revealed that M2 macrophages secreted IL-4, but IL-6 was secreted by M1 macrophages (Figure 4C). We used IL-4 and IL-6 as M2 and M1 macrophage markers, respectively, which is consistent with their known roles. After blocking CD44v5, M2 macrophages underwent repolarization, as evidenced by decreased IL-4 secretion and increased IL-6 secretion (Figure 4D, E). Collectively, the above findings suggest that CD44v5 is a potential therapeutic target for repolarizing M2 macrophages to the M1 phenotype.


### Colocalization of CD44v5 with IL-4Rα enhances M2 macrophage polarization signaling and promotes SAM68 expression
  Binding prediction of the IL-4R ECD (green) to the ECD of the CD44 variant combination (red) by AlphaFold3 and visualization of the prediction results by PyMOL showed that the IL-4Rα ECD could bind to the CD44v5 ECD (Figure 5A). The presence of the CD44v5 ECD led to a significant increase in its binding rate with IL-4Rα (Figure 5B, C).

  We found that CD44v5 colocalized with IL-4Rα on the cell surface of M2 macrophages by immunocytochemistry (Figure 5D). We found that blocking CD44v5 did not affect IL-4Rα expression levels when the microtubule system was inhibited by paclitaxel (Figure 5E), which revealed that CD44v5 plays a major role in binding to IL-4R by inhibiting its internalization.

  SAM68, a protein specifically involved in the alternative splicing of CD44v5, was also highly expressed in M2 macrophages (Figure 5F). Inhibition of the M2 macrophage polarization signaling pathway significantly reduces SAM68 expression levels. (Figure 5G, H). Furthermore, CD44v5 blockade led to a reduction in SAM68 expression (Figure 5I, J). In summary, CD44v5 promotes M2 macrophage polarization and exerts protumorigenic effects by stabilizing and enhancing the IL-4Rα/STAT6/IL-4 positive feedback loop (Figure 5K).


## Discussion
  According to our above results, CD44v5 was highly expressed in M2 macrophages and played a critical role in enhancing M2 macrophage polarization through its stable enhancement of the IL-4R/STAT6/IL-4 signaling pathway. Accordingly, blockade of CD44v5 inhibited the IL-4R/STAT6/IL-4 signaling pathway, polarized macrophages from M2 toward M1 macrophages, and then inhibited tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, which suggested that CD44v5 can act as a "control switch" for macrophage polarization.

  We observed that blockade of either CD44v5 or IL-4Rα downregulated GATA3 expression and induced the repolarization of M2 macrophages to M1 macrophages. We hypothesized that in macrophages, GATA3 plays a role analogous to its function in T helper cells, where it promotes Th2 cell differentiation and inhibits Th1 cell differentiation.32,33 Therefore, we propose that GATA3 may not only promote M2 macrophage polarization but also suppress the expression of key proteins involved in M1 macrophage polarization. The downregulation of GATA3 could thereby alleviate this suppression to facilitate macrophage repolarization, a mechanism that requires further experimental validation.

  STAT3 is a multifunctional protein regulated by a variety of cytokines and growth factors.34,35 While numerous studies indicate that STAT3 activation promotes M2 macrophage polarization,36-38 other evidence paradoxically shows that inhibiting the STAT3 pathway can also enhance this process.39,40 Our results demonstrated that the level of phosphorylated STAT3 was higher in M1 macrophages than in M2 macrophages (Supplementary Figure S1B). Furthermore, an increase in phosphorylated STAT3 was observed during M2 macrophage repolarization (Supplementary Figure S1C). Therefore, we hypothesized that STAT3 phosphorylation is involved primarily in regulating the polarization process of M1 macrophages.

  Our previous study revealed that IL-4 promotes the massive secretion of IL-6 by breast cancer cells IL−6 and exerts protumorigenic functions through IL-6.26 However, in this study, IL-6 was preferred to be the signature cytokine of M1 macrophages, whereas IL-4, a cytokine that performs protumor biological functions rather than IL-6, was the signature cytokine of M2 macrophages.

  In M2 macrophages, IL-4 induces polarization and promotes autocrine secretion via a positive feedback loop involving IL-4R/STAT6/GATA3. Conversely, in tumor cells, IL-4 enhances malignant functions and stimulates IL-6 secretion.26 These distinct cell-specific actions demonstrate that IL-4 is a multifunctional cytokine. Our research elucidates the potential mechanism by which IL-4 and IL-6 regulate the formation of the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME).

  The results from our predictive model suggest that the CD44v5 domain is the critical determinant for the binding of IL-4R to the CD44 extracellular region. CD44v5 not only promotes M2 macrophage polarization through stable enhancement of the IL-4/IL-4R/STAT6/GATA3/IL-4 signaling pathway but also promotes its own CD44v5 splicing via IL-4R/STAT6/SAM68. Although we applied IL-4Rα inhibitor effects similar to those of the CD44v5 monoclonal antibody, IL-4Rα is widely expressed in other tissues, and CD44v5 is expressed in tumor tissues with much higher specificity than is IL-4Rα. In addition, the functions of CD44v5 as a cell adhesion molecule and its therapeutic effects need to be further compared. In addition, we explored the functional differences between M2 macrophages and M2-like TAMs, both of which promote TNBC invasion, migration, and proliferation in TNBC and thus worsen the TIME. Our study also confirmed that IL-4 is the initiating cytokine and, more importantly, revealed that the formation of TAMs and CD44v5 could be a potential target to regulate TAMs, which would be a more promising therapeutic target for TNBC as well.
