# Selective disruption of DNMT1/ELK1 interactions induces DGKI re-expression and promotes temozolomide sensitivity of MGMTmethylated/DGKImethylated glioblastoma


## Background
  DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) inhibitors are emerging as a promising class of agents for personalized and targeted cancer therapy, particularly in malignancies with limited therapeutic options such as glioblastoma (GB). In GB, the MGMT/DGKI methylation profile serves as a biomarker for stratifying patients by treatment response. Specifically, the MGMTmethylated/DGKIunmethylated profile is associated with favorable outcomes, whereas the MGMTmethylated/DGKImethylated profile correlates with poor outcome. These findings suggest that selective demethylation of DGKI without altering MGMT or inducing widespread genomic hypomethylation, which may promote tumorigenesis, could represent a novel and more effective therapeutic strategy.


## Results
  Current DNMT inhibitors lack specificity for glioblastoma relevant methylation profiles, thereby limiting their therapeutic efficacy. To address this challenge, AlphaFold-based protein–protein interaction predictions were integrated with sequential chromatin immunoprecipitation assays and established DNMT1 interactome data. This integrative approach led to the identification of a DNMT1/ELK1 complex as a critical regulator of DGKI methylation. A peptide mimicking the DNMT1/ELK1 interface, designated EXDDNMT1/ELK1, was designed and shown to induce selective DGKI demethylation without altering MGMT or inducing global DNA hypomethylation. Notably, EXDDNMT1/ELK1 did note promote cellular proliferation or invasion, and successfully restored sensitivity to standard glioblastoma therapy in both cellular and in vivo models. These findings also support the use of MGMT and DGKI methylation levels in cell-free DNA as potential biomarkers to identify patients likely to benefit from EXDDNMT1/ELK1 treatment.


## Conclusion
  This study identifies a clinically actionable biomarker (MGMTMethylated/DGKIMethylated), detectable in both solid and liquid biopsies, enabling patient stratification. Furthermore, it establishes EXDDNMT1/ELK1 as a highly selective epigenetic therapeutic agent to treat GB patients.

## Introduction
  Glioblastoma (GB) is the most aggressive primary neuroepithelial tumor of the central nervous system, characterized by a highly unfavorable prognosis. Despite standard-of-care treatment, median overall survival remains limited to 12–15 months, with only 3–5% of patients surviving beyond five years after diagnosis [1, 2].

  Standard therapy for GB consists in maximal surgical resection, rarely complete due to the tumor’s infiltrative growth, followed by radiotherapy combined with both concomitant and adjuvant administration of temozolomide (TMZ) [3]. The advent of next-generation sequencing has facilitated the identification of diagnostic, prognostic, predictive, and theranostic biomarkers relevant to GB pathogenesis and precision medicine [4].

  Several biomarkers are now routinely assessed in clinical settings of GB, including O6-methylguanine DNA methyltransferase (MGMT) promoter methylation, isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 or 2 (IDH1/2) mutation, epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) amplification, Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) expression, global and gene-specific DNA methylation levels and immune checkpoints like Programmed cell death 1 ligand 1 (PDL1) [5]. However, despite these molecular insights, the therapeutic landscape of GB has remained essentially unchanged since 2005, and the disease remains incurable. As a result, few clinical decision-making algorithms currently incorporate these biomarkers to personalize therapy by combining the standard protocol with targeted small-molecule inhibitors [6].

  In this context, the development of a therapeutic strategy aimed at improving treatment response in GB patients exhibiting the MGMT-methylated/DGKI-methylated profile, a subgroup previously shown to respond poorly to conventional therapy [7, 8].


## Methods

### Cell line culture
  The human GB cell line U251 (Sigma, France), U373 (Sigma, France) U87 (ATCC-LGC, France) A172 (ATCC-LGC, France) were obtained from Sigma and was cultured in 100 mm culture dish in presence of recommended medium (DMEM#21,969,035, Thermo Fisher, France) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Eurobio, France), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Thermo Fischer Scientific, 15,140,122, France) and 1% glutamine (Thermo Fischer Scientific, 25,030,024, France) in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2 atmosphere (Panasonic#MC0-19AIC-PE, France).


### Primary cultured tumor cells derived from glioblastoma (PCTC-dGB)
  Within 30 min following resection, fresh brain tumor tissues were collected and processed from the neurosurgery department of the Laennec Hospital (Nantes/Saint-Herblain). All subjects gave their informed consent, and the clinical protocol was authorized by French ethics regulations. Following mechanical dissociation, the initial cultivated tumor cells were collected. Using DMEM with 10% FBS and antibiotics, tumor tissue was sliced into 1–5 mm3 pieces and plated in a 60 mm2 tissue culture dish. Furthermore, and concurrently, 200 U/ml collagenase I (Sigma, France) and 500 U/ml DNase I (Sigma, France) were incubated with chopped tumor pieces in PBS for one hour at 37 °C with vigorous, continuous agitation. After passing through a 70 mm cell strainer (BD Falcon, France), the single-cell suspension was rinsed with PBS and then suspended in DMEM-10% FBS. Following confluence, cell cultures were divided 1:2, and tests were conducted prior to passages 3–5.


### Temozolomide treatment
  Temozolomide/TMZ (CAS 85622-93-1, MedChemExpress, France) was dissolved in 100% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma-Aldrich, France) to prepare a stock solution at a concentration of 100 mM. Stock solution was stored at − 80 °C. Final concentration (IC50 = 50 M) was obtained after dilution in cell medium culture.


### Cell viability assay
  Cell viability was measured using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay. Cells were seeded into 96-well plates overnight at a density of 500 cells per mL of complete medium. Subsequently, chemotherapeutic agents were incorporated for 72 h. After incubation, 10 μL of MTT (10 μg/ml) was added to each well and incubated for 3 h. After removing the medium containing MTT, 200 μL/well of DMSO was added. Optical density was measured at 570 nm using a spectrophotometer (VICTOR, Perkin Elmer, France). Graphpad PRISM-9 software was used to calculate IC50 value.


### Epigenetic drugs
  The epigenetic dugs are sourced from the following manufacturers: Decitabine (Tocris/BioTechne#2624/10, CAS#2353-33-5, France), Procainamide (Santa-Cruz#sc-202297, CAS#614-39-1, France) and EXD/peptide (GeneCust, France).


### Tumor and plasma samples
  Tumor samples were obtained from the French Glioblastoma Biobank (FGB) [8]. The French Ministry of Health and Research received notification of the FGB network (declaration number: DC-2011-1467, cession permission number: AC-2017-2993). The CNIL (no. 1476342, date of approval: 10 October 2011) and the CPP OUEST II ethics commission (CB 2012/02, date of approval: 20 December 2011) both authorized the FGB network's protocols and rules.

  Plasma samples were collected from patients treated at the Institut de Cancérologie de l'Ouest (ICO, http://www.ico-cancer.fr). All enrolled patients provided written informed consent. The database used to register the collected samples and associated clinical data was approved by the French Ministry of Research (No. DC-2018-3321). The "Centre de Ressources Biologiques-Tumorothèque” of “Institut de Cancérologie de l’Ouest” housed biological resources [9]. Briefly, Blood was collected in K⁺EDTA tubes, and plasma was isolated from 10 mL of whole blood within 30 min via a two-step centrifugation protocol (10 min/1900 g/4 °C and 10 min/16000 g/4 °C). After transfer to a new tube, plasma was stored at − 80 °C until analysis.


### DNA extraction and qMSRE
  A QIAcube automate, QiaAmp Circulating Nucleic Acid kit and QIAmp DNA Mini QiaCube kit (Qiagen, France) were used to isolate DNA from plasma. MGMT promoter methylation was quantified using OneStep qMethyl Kits (Ozyme, France). DGKI promoter methylation was quantified by qMSRE with the on the -173/-68 DGKI promoter region with the primers: AACTGCGTCCCCGCCT and GAGGGCCCTGTGATCCG. Digestions were performed with HhaI and AciI (NEB, France). Typically, 100 ng of genomic DNA were digested with 40U of enzymes at 37 °C for 2 h in 50 μL of reaction. Control samples were processed identically, except without enzyme addition. 5μL of digestion mixture were used for qPCR. The QuantiFast SYBR Green PCR Kit and Rotor-Gene Q (Qiagen, France) were used to perform the qPCR.


### Alphafold analyses
  Protein–protein complex structure prediction was performed using the public available ColabFold. Amino acid sequences for human DNMT1 (UniProt: P26358) and ELK1 (UniProt: P19419) were retrieved from UniProt. Selected interactions sequences were pasted to colab_batch connected by colon using the FASTA file from Uniprot. The default parameters for MSA generations and the number of recycles (3) were used. For relaxed prediction, only the first model was relaxed using num_relax: 1. The colabFold was executed using T4 GPU under ColabFold pro account. Five models were generated for each prediction with the pLDDT (predicted Local Distance Difference Test) score, a per-residue confidence score between 0 and 100. Generally, pLDDT scores above 90 are considered ‘‘very confident,’’ pLDDT scores between 70 and 90 are considered ‘‘confident,’’ pLDDT scores between 50 and 70 are ‘‘low confidence’’ and scores below 50 are considered ‘‘very low confidence’’ and may be unstructured. ColabFold ranks the five models based on pLDDT scores, pTM (predicted Template Modeling), and ipTM (interface predicted Template Modeling). A pTM score above 0.5 indicates good overall folding of the complex, while a score below 0.5 suggests an incorrect structure. For the ipTM score, values above 0.8 denote high-confidence, high-quality predictions. Scores between 0.6 and 0.8 should be interpreted with caution, as the predictions may be accurate or incorrect. Scores below 0.6 likely indicate a failed prediction. To evaluate how a prediction is reliable or not, a model confidence score could be calculated as follow: Model confidence = (0,2 × pTM + 0,8 × ipTM) × 100 [10]. A heatmap representation of the MSA indicates all sequences mapped. The color scale indicates the identity score. White regions are not covered. The black line identifies the relative coverage of the sequence with respect to the total number of aligned sequences. Top-ranked model from the generated PDB file was used for further molecular visualization using Pymol. Each protein was distinguished by coloration. Protein–protein interaction was determined using Modify/Around/Atoms within 4 Å. Synthetized peptide was represented using stick. Pymol picture was downloaded using Ray (slow) at 300 DPI.


### siRNA invalidation
  siRNA-DNMT1 (Santa Cruz, # sc-35204, France), siRNA-ELK1 (Santa Cruz, # sc-35290, France) and siRNA-cEBPα (Santa Cruz, #s sc-37047, France) were used to specifically silence the expression of DNMT1, ELK1 and cEBPα, respectively. siRNA-A (Santa Cruz, #sc-37007, France), a scrambled sequence devoid of specific degradation of any cellular message, was used as control. 2 × 105 cells were seeded in six-well plates and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in a CO₂ incubator. Subsequently, 60 pmol of siRNA was diluted in 100 µL of siRNA transfection medium (Santa Cruz, #sc-36868), and 8 µL of siRNA transfection reagent (Santa Cruz, #sc-29528) was diluted in 100 µL of siRNA transfection medium. These two solutions were then mixed by pipetting up and down. After incubating for 45 min at room temperature, 0.8 ml of siRNA transfection medium was added. After one cell wash with 2 ml of siRNA transfection medium, the total mixture was incubated on the cells for 7 h at 37 °C in a CO₂ incubator. Without removing the transfection mixture, 1 mL of standard growth medium supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin was added, followed by a further 24-h incubation. Cells were then cultured for an additional 48 h in standard growth medium. Analyses were performed approximately 72 h after siRNA exposure.


### Proliferation assay and doubling time
  Doubling time (i.e., the period of time required for a quantity to double in size) was calculated by using the Doubling Time Online Calculator website (Roth V. 2006, http://www.doubling-time.com/compute.php) and counting the proliferation of 103 cells over 120 h. Cell number was determined, every 24 h over 120 h, using the Countess Automated Cell Counter (Life Technologies, France).


### Invasion assay
  All procedures were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (QCM 24-Well Collagen-Based Cell Invasion Assay, Millipore, France). Briefly, 200 μl serum-free medium containing 2 × 105 cells were seeded into an invasion chamber and placed into a 24 well plate containing 500 μl complete medium. After 72 h of incubation at 37 °C, medium was removed, and cells were stained by immersing the chamber in staining solution for 20 min at room temperature. Non-invading cells on the membrane upper side were gently removed. The stained chamber was then placed in a clean well containing 200 μL of extraction buffer for 15 min at room temperature. Subsequently, 100 μL of the extracted dye solution was transferred to a 96-well plate, and optical density was measured at 560 nm using a spectrophotometer.


### In vivo experiments
  Cells were harvested by trypsinization, washed and resuspended in PBS/50% Matrigel buffer. Cell suspensions (105 in 150L of buffer) were injected s.c. into the flank of 6-weeks old SCID mice (Charles River Laboratories International, catalog number: 236). Tumor volume based on caliper measurements was calculated using the modified ellipsoidal formula (Tumor volume = 1/2(length × width2)). At the end of the experimentation, mice bearing patient-derived xenograft (PDX) tumors were euthanized, and tumor tissues were collected for analysis.

  All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and the regulations of the French National Committee of Ethics. Experiments were performed at the Plate-forme Animalerie of the Institut de Recherche en Santé de l’Université de Nantes (IRS-UN) and were approved by the French National Committee of Ethics.


### Chromatin immunoprecipitation (reChIP) experiments
  Briefly, cells were cross-linked by adding 1% formaldehyde (from a 37% stock solution) and incubated on a rotator for 10 min at room temperature (RT). Cross-linking was quenched by the addition of 125 mM glycine (from a 1.25 stock), followed by rocking for 5 min at RT. Cells were then washed with cold PBS, and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Lysis was performed in buffer containing 1% SDS, 5 mM EDTA, and 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0) supplemented with freshly added protease inhibitor. Chromatin was fragmented to 200–500 bp with a Bioruptor plus sonicator (Diagenode, France) at 4 °C. Sequential ChIP experiments were performed using Re-ChIP-IT kit (Active Motif, France). Sequential chromatin immunoprecipitation was performed using anti-DNMT1 (Abcam #Ab13537, France) in the first immunoprecipitation, followed by anti-ELK1 (Abcam #Ab32106, France) in the second. ChIP-grade IgG (Abcam #Ab2410, France) was used as a negative control, replacing anti-DNMT1 and anti-ELK1 in the respective reactions. DNA recovered from the second immunoprecipitation was analyzed by quantitative real-time PCR using QuantiFast SYBR Green PCR mix (Qiagen, France) on a Rotor-Gene Q system (Qiagen, France).


### Statistical analysis
  All statistical analyses (i.e., log rank test and Student’s t test,…) were performed using GraphPad Prism-9 (version 9.0; GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). Differences between means were considered statistically significant when p < 0.05*, p < 0.01** and p < 0.001***.


## Results

### DGKI expression and methylation status are implicated in the response to standard glioblastoma therapy
  Previous studies demonstrated that DGKI promoter methylation defines a subset of MGMT-methylated GB patients with poor response to standard therapy, refining conventional MGMT-based stratification of GB patients [7, 8]. Based on these findings, a new short cohort of 22 GB patients was stratified into four molecular subgroups according to their methylated (M) or unmethylated (U) status: MGMTM/DGKIM, MGMTM/DGKIU, MGMTU/DGKIM, MGMTU/DGKIU (Table 1). Methylation levels of DGKI and MGMT were assessed using a quantitative methylation-sensitive restriction enzyme (qMSRE) assay (Fig. 1A).   Given the known repressive effect of DNA methylation on gene expression, it was hypothesized that reduced DGKI expression in MGMTM/DGKIM GB cells may impair the efficacy of temozolomide- and irradiation-induced cell death.

  To test this, TMZ-irradiation-induced cell death in 6 primary-cultured tumor cells (PCTC) was analyzed. The six PCTC derived from GB having different levels of DGKI/MGMT expression and methylation. As expected, PCTC-dGB with an MGMTM/DGKIU signature were sensitive to TMZ/IR-induced cell death (Fig. 1C, orange panel), whereas PCTC-dGB harboring the MGMTM/DGKIM signature (Fig. 1C, blue panel) exhibited resistant or limited sensitivity to TMZ/irradiation-induced cell death, like PCTC-dGB characterized by the unmethylated MGMT (Fig. 1C, gray panel).

  To directly assess whether DGKI expression enhances therapy response, DGKI was re-expressed in the MGMT⁽M⁾/DGKI⁽M⁾ U251 GB cell line via plasmid transfection (pCMV5-DGKI, Addgene). U251 glioblastoma cell line was selected for this study due to its MGMTM/DGKIM methylation profile (Fig. 1C, yellow panel). DGKI overexpression in U251 cells transfected with the pCMV5-DGKI plasmid was validated by ELISA. (Fig. 1C, yellow panel). As shown in Fig. 1C, restored DGKI expression significantly increased TMZ/IR-induced cell death in U251 cells (Fig. 1C, yellow panel).

  Together, these experiments indicate that DGKI expression contributes to increased sensitivity to TMZ and irradiation-induced cell death.


### Currently used DNA demethylating agents are inefficient at demethylating DGKI without demethylating MGMT
  Given that DGKI expression enhances TMZ- and irradiation-induced cell death in MGMTM/DGKIM cells, it was hypothesized that selective demethylation of DGKI could improve the therapeutic response in this molecular subtype. To test this, effects of two non-selective DNA demethylating agents were assessed on MGMT and DGKI methylation levels, as well as on the extent of TMZ- and irradiation-induced cell death. Thus, two distinct DNMT inhibitors were used: the nucleoside analog Decitabine [11], which incorporates into DNA and traps all DNMTs, and the catalytic inhibitor procainamide [12], which specifically blocks the active site of DNMT1.

  First, qMSRE assays confirmed that Decitabine and Procainamide induced the demethylation of both DGKI and MGMT genes (Fig. 1C, green panel), consistent with their non-selective mechanism of action. In this context, MGMT and DGKI expression increased but TMZ/IR-induced cell death decreased (Fig. 1C, green panel). In other words, despite promoting DGKI demethylation and re-expression, these agents failed to enhance TMZ sensitivity, likely due to concomitant reactivation of MGMT, a key mediator of TMZ resistance.


### Design of EXDDNMT1/ELK1, a peptide that selectively demethylates DGKI without affecting MGMT
  Since both non-selective DNA demethylating agents failed to specifically target DGKI without affecting MGMT, efforts were directed toward identifying a molecule capable of selectively demethylating the DGKI promoter. Based on previous findings from our group and others, DGKI methylation was hypothesized to be regulated through a DNMT1–transcription factor interaction [13–19], specifically by a mechanism called TF-directed DNA methylation. To identify TFs potentially interacting with DNMT1 to regulate DGKI methylation, the PROMO predictive software (https://alggen.lsi.upc.es/cgi-bin/promo_v3/promo/promoinit.cgi?dirDB=TF_8.3) was used. Thus, 22 TFs were identified (Supplementary Fig. 2). Among these 22 TF, only two have been reported to interact with DNMT1, based on the work of Hervouet et al.   To assess whether DNMT1, CEBPα, or ELK1 contribute to DGKI methylation, DGKI methylation level was analyzed following siRNA-mediated silencing of each factor (Supplementary Fig. 3). qMSRE analysis revealed that silencing of DNMT1 or ELK1 reduced DGKI methylation levels, whereas CEBPα knockdown had no detectable effect (Fig. 2A). These observations support the hypothesis that DGKI methylation is regulated through functional interaction of DNMT1 and ELK1.

  AlphaFold2, the deep learning-based structural prediction tool developed by DeepMind, was employed to identify and design a peptide targeting the DNMT1/ELK1 interaction. Initially, protein regions were analyzed and selected to enable accurate prediction of the DNMT1/ELK1 interaction interface. For DNMT1, residues 349–1130 were selected for complex prediction, corresponding to its structured core excluding the catalytic C-terminal domain (residues 1130–1600). ELK1 remains poorly characterized structurally; however, Mo et al. identified its DNA recognition domain (residues 1–94) as critical for DNA interaction [20]. These considerations led to the hypothesis that DNA recognition domain of ELK1 mediates its interaction with DNMT1. Accordingly, this domain was selected for AlphaFold-based structural prediction. An initial AlphaFold prediction using DNMT1 (349–1130) and ELK1 (1–95), although low in confidence (~ 30%) and not shown here, suggested a potential ELK1 interaction site within residues 350–500 of DNMT1. A second prediction was therefore performed, focusing on this region. In the second model, a very high confidence score (82%) was observed, suggesting high reliability in predicting ELK1 interaction with DNMT1 residues 461–471 (Fig. 2B). In other terms, these data suggest that the 461–471 amino acid region of DNMT1 is engaged in the DNMT1/ELK1 interaction. Moreover, the predicted interaction suggested that electrostatic forces and hydrogen bonding may occur between DNMT1 residues N462 and E463 and ELK1 residues D58 and K75. (Supplementary Fig. 4). Additionally, this prediction also indicated that W454 residues of DNMT1 could interact with phenylalanine F81 of ELK1 through π-stacking interactions (Supplementary Fig. 4). Based on these findings, a peptide mimicking DNMT1 residues 461–471 was synthesized. To facilitate intracellular delivery and nuclear localization, a cell-penetrating sequence and a nuclear-targeting signal were appended. This peptide construct was subsequently named EXDDNMT1/ELK1.

  Then, ChIP and reChIP experiments showed that EXDDNMT1/ELK1 inhibited both DNMT1 and ELK1 recruitment to DGKI gene, compared with a control peptide (EXDctrl) (Fig. 2D).

  In comparison, a peptide targeting DNMT1/PCNA/UHRF1 (EXDDUP), essential for DNMT1 activity and known for his unspecificity, did not alter of DNMT1 and ELK1 recruitment to DGKI gene (Fig. 2D), showing the specific effect of EXDDNMT1/ELK1. In addition, qMSRE assays showed that EXDDNMT1/ELK1 decreased DGKI methylation level without affecting MGMT methylation level (Fig. 2E).

  In conclusion, EXDDNMT1/ELK1 inhibits both DNMT and ELK1 recruitment to DGKI gene, resulting in reduced DGKI methylation without affecting MGMT methylation level.


### EXDDNMT1/ELK1 increases anti-GB effect of TMZ in PDX model of GB without promoting pro-tumoral phenotypes
  EXDDNMT1/ELK1 was next evaluated for its impact on TMZ/irradiation-induced cell death, doubling time, invasion and global level of DNA methylation, three cancer hallmarks and one signature associated with GB tumorigenicity [21–23], were carried out.

  Treatment with EXDDNMT1/ELK1 significantly increased TMZ/IR-induced cell death, while having no detectable effect on invasion index, doubling time and global DNA methylation level of U251 (Fig. 3A). On the contrary, Decitabine, Procainamide and EXDDUP, various non-specific methylation inhibitors reduced TMZ/IR-induced cell death. This effect could be explained by multiple molecular mechanisms triggered upon DNMT inhibition, notably the re-expression of MGMT, a DNA repair enzyme that counteracts DNA damage. To quantify the therapeutic index of each compound, we developed a Score of Modulation of Cancer Hallmarks (SMoCH). Notably, EXDDNMT1/ELK1 was the only one DNA demethylating agent presenting a positive SMoCH (Fig. 3A), indicating that it suppressed more cancer hallmarks than it promoted, unlike other tested DNA demethylating agents.   The anti-tumor efficacy of EXDDNMT1/ELK1 was further validated in TMZ-treated PDX model of GB. Subcutaneous implantation of three MGMT⁽M⁾/DGKI⁽M⁾-classified PCTC-dGB lines was performed in nude mice. ### EXDDNMT1/ELK1 prevents the TMZ resistance acquisition
  The potential impact of prolonged TMZ exposure on DGKI methylation was next examined. To address this at cellular level, three GB cell lines were exposed to TMZ at their respective IC₅₀ concentrations for eight weeks. All cell lines acquired TMZ resistant and only A172-TMZ8w cells displayed an increase in DGKI methylation level (Fig. 4A). Interestingly, MGMT methylation level was unchanged under these conditions in three cell lines (Fig. 4A). To investigate whether TMZ treatment might modulate DGKI methylation level in patients, two series of experiments were performed. DGKI methylation levels were first assessed in a cohort of 15 GB patients who underwent two surgical resections: one before treatment (R1) and one at recurrence after TMZ therapy (R2) (Table 1). Importantly, DGKI methylation level increased in six of the fifteen patients between the two surgical resections, remained unchanged in eight of the fifteen patients and decreased in only one patient (Fig. 4B). In addition, MGMT methylation levels remained unchanged between first and second resections in these patients. (Fig. 4B). Then, based on these results, methylation profile evolution between the two resections was examined. For this, genes with methylation levels below 50% were classified as unmethylated, while those with levels equal to or above 50% were classified as methylated. Thus, five patients have a methylation profile that evolved from MGMTM/DGKIU to MGMTM/DGKIM between the two resections (Fig. 4C). This means that five patients shifted from a favorable to an unfavorable prognosis for response to standard anti-GB therapy between the two tumor resections (Fig. 4C).   In a second investigation based on 5 new GB patients, evolution of DGKI and MGMT methylation levels in cell-free circulating DNA (cfcDNA) during treatment in five GB patients was analyzed. Analysis of DGKI and MGMT methylation in cfcDNA is supported by the well-established idea that cfcDNA partially reflects tumor-derived DNA (tDNA). In this study, results showed strong correlations between methylation levels of MGMT and DGKI of tDNA and cfcDNA in a cohort of 18 GBM patients (Supplementary Fig. 5). Importantly, no correlation was observed between MGMT and DGKI methylation levels in cfcDNA and those in buffy coat leukocyte DNA. This suggests that cfcDNA-based methylation measurements predominantly reflect tumor-derived DNA rather than leukocyte DNA, which may constitute up to 76% of the total cfcDNA [24] (Supplementary Fig. 5). Moreover, DGKI methylation levels in cfcDNA increased in three of the five patients analyzed (Fig. 4D), while MGMT methylation levels remained unchanged across all five (Fig. 4D). Together, these analyses indicate that TMZ treatment can alter DGKI methylation. Notably, three patients shifted from a favorable (MGMTM/DGKIU) to an unfavorable (MGMTM/DGKIM) methylation profile associated with reduced response to standard GB therapy (Fig. 4D).

  Finally, A172 cells were co-treated with EXDDNMT1/ELK1 and TMZ for 8 weeks to assess whether this approach could prevent the emergence of TMZ resistance. Resistance was evaluated by measuring the IC₅₀ of cells that survived the co-treatment. These data show that TMZ + EXDDNMT1/ELK1 co-treatment prevents the emergence of TMZ-resistant A172 cells after 8 weeks of exposure, since the IC50 of these cells is 37 ± 6 μM, whereas it is 399 ± 36 μM when A172 cells are treated with TMZ alone (Fig. 4E).


## Discussion
  As in other cancer types, the integration of precision medicine as a complementary approach in GB therapy has gained momentum over recent years [25, 26]. Current research focuses on identifying theranostic biomarkers that can serve dual roles in patient stratification and targeted intervention. Reflecting this trend, the 2016 World Health Organization classification of central nervous system (CNS) tumors emphasized incorporating molecular biomarkers into the diagnostic criteria, particularly for GB [27], with the goal of improving diagnostic accuracy and optimizing therapeutic strategies. Accordingly, several clinical studies proposed treatment algorithms, particularly for elderly patients with GB [28–30].

  Building on this paradigm, our previous work identified DNMT/protein-X complexes as both stratification biomarkers and actionable epigenetic in GB [13, 21, 23, 31]. These findings support the clinical utility of epigenic marks (epimarks) in guiding personalized therapy. In this context, several epimarks have already been associated with clinical outcomes. For instance, Chiang et al. report that hypomethylation of hESC-associated genes predicted poorer clinical outcome in GB [32]. Yin et al. identified six CpG signatures as a robust and independent prognostic indicator for GBs, with potential value for guiding personalized treatment strategies involving bevacizumab [33, 34].

  Using a bedside-to-bench approach, this study proposes the MGMTM/DGKIM profile as a molecular signature identifying a subgroup of GB patients who, despite being considered as good responders due to MGMT methylation, exhibit poor response to standard therapy. This subgroup may benefit from treatment with EXDDNMT1/ELK1, a rationally designed peptide that promotes DGKI demethylation and enhances responsiveness to standard therapy.

  Moreover, EXDDNMT1/ELK1 reduced glioblastoma cell invasion without promoting proliferation or other pro-tumorigenic phenotypes. Notably, EXDDNMT1/ELK1 was the only epidrug exhibiting a positive SMoCH score, indicating a global anti-tumor activity. Pro-tumoral hallmark lacking is attributed to selective disruption of DNMT1/ELK1 complex by EXDDNMT1/ELK1, an interaction involved in the process of “Transcription-factor directed DNA methylation.” In contrast, Decitabine, Procainamide and EXDDUP treatment induced pro-tumoral effect such as decrease in TMZ-IR-induced cell death, decrease in doubling time, increase in invasion and decrease in global DNA methylation level. One possible explanation for the accumulation of these pro-tumor effects is that these three epidrugs have a broad spectrum of action, as they inhibit all DNMTs, all DNMT1-including complexes, and DNMT1/PCNA/UHRF1 complex, as the main complex responsible for DNA inheritance methylation, respectively. Furthermore, the sequence specific nature of methylation driven by DNMT/transcription factor complexes, occurring within or near the transcription factor’s binding site, may explain the selective demethylation and genre reactivation observed with EXDDNMT1/ELK1, including re-expression of GDKI. This reactivation likely contributes to its anti-tumor effects. A dedicated research program is currently underway in our laboratory to investigate this mechanism further.

  Tumor volume reduction following TMZ/EXDDNMT1/ELK1 co-treatment was associated with EXDDNMT1/ELK1-induced DGKI demethylation, as detected in both tumor-derived DNA and circulating cell-free DNA (cfcDNA). Quantifying this epigenetic change in cfcDNA is essential for determining when EXDDNMT1/ELK1 should be administered and supports its role in reprogramming MGMTM/DGKIM tumors into a more treatment-responsive MGMTM/DGKIU state.

  Interestingly, 40% of GBs initially classified as MGMTM/DGKIU shifted into the MGMTM/DGKIM unfavorable signature between the two resections (5/15) or during the first phase of their treatment (3/5). The MGMTM/DGKIU >  > MGMTM/DGKIM shift appears to mark emerging resistance to TMZ and radiotherapy. Notably, this shift was observed in only one of the three cellular models of TMZ resistance acquisition, specifically in A172 cells, but not in U373 or U87 cells. Therefore, it may be the dynamic changes in MGMT and DGKI methylation levels, rather than their absolute levels that are biologically relevant and warrant further consideration. These results support the early use of EXDDNMT1/ELK1 in patients exhibiting the unfavorable profile or its emergence in cfDNA, enabling therapeutic adjustment before relapse or second surgery.

  Multiple studies identified gene and protein expression signatures linked to temozolomide resistance in subclones of human glioblastoma cell lines, and delineated distinct molecular pathways driving acquired resistance in glioblastoma [35–37]. However, to our knowledge, no prior report has identified a methylation signature shift associated with therapy-induced resistance in clinical GB samples. These findings provide a first proof-of-concept for an epimark/epidrug pairing, where a dynamic epimark linked to acquired resistance is coupled with an epidrug that reverses the underlying resistance mechanism. Further validation in larger GB cohorts, as well as technological advances in cfDNA methylation profiling, will be necessary to establish clinical relevance of the proposed epimark/epidrug pair. In this context, tracking changes in MGMT and DGKI methylation status throughout TMZ and radiotherapy could help tailor treatment strategies in GB.

  To identify and design the EXDDNMT1/ELK1 peptide targeting the DNMT1/ELK1 interaction, the state-of-the-art artificial intelligence of DeepMind, Alphafold2 (AF2) was used. Alphafold merged in 2021 at CASP14 and was developed for protein structure prediction and considered as the groundbreaking application of AI in science [38–40]. AF2 shows remarkable performance with high accuracy prediction and demonstrated a root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of 0,8 Å. AF2 was adapted to predict the structures of protein–protein interactions (PPIs) through Alphafold Multimer [41, 42]. This tool has shown promising results in the study of PPIs, notably for the development of SARS-CoV-2 inhibitors [43] or for diverse interactions involved in cancer development [44], as SHLD3-RIF1 [45] and for the design of peptide targeting PPI [46]. By validating the DGKI-specific demethylating activity of EXDDNMT1/ELK1, this study provides, to our knowledge, the first proof-of-concept for structure-guided design of an epidrug using AlphaFold2-predicted protein–protein interactions. More broadly, this tool could support the development of DNMT/protein-X interaction inhibitors and advance design of highly selective DNMT inhibitors.


## Conclusion
  This study highlights the potential of EXDDNMT1/ELK1—MGMT/DGKI methylation profile as a functionally matched epidrug—epimarker pair capable of enhancing therapeutic response in MGMTmethylated/DGKImethylated glioblastoma (MGMTM/DGKIM). In addition, this targeted approach could limit therapeutic failure by preventing the MGMTM/DGKIU >  > MGMTM/DGKIM” epigenetic shift, which occurs in up to 50% of GB relapses.


## Limitations
  Although our findings contribute to the growing understanding of the molecular mechanisms of DNA methylation and may inform future studies aimed at designing novel, highly selective DNA demethylating therapeutic agents, our studies have several limitations. Further experimental validation and mechanistic investigation of the DNMT1/ELK1 complex are essential to confirm its highly selective activity and putative role in TMZ sensitivity. Additionally, the EXDDNMT1/ELK1 pharmacodynamic parameters must be defined and optimized prior to translation into clinical insights.
