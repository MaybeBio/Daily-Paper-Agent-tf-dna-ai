# Discovery of natural RORγt inhibitor using machine learning, virtual screening, and in vivo validation


## Graphical abstract
  Graphical abstract


## Introduction
  Psoriasis is a chronic and immune-mediated skin disease that primarily affects the skin and joints, with an estimated global prevalence of up to 4.4 % [1]. The predominant symptoms include itching, burning, and soreness on the skin, and the disease presents with various phenotypes, such as psoriasis vulgaris, guttate psoriasis, inverse psoriasis, pustular psoriasis, palmoplantar psoriasis, and erythrodermic psoriasis [2]. Severe or exacerbated psoriasis can lead to rheumatologic complications, such as psoriatic arthritis, as well as cardiovascular and psychiatric comorbidities [3].

  Retinoid-related orphan receptor gamma-t (RORγt), a member of the nuclear receptor superfamily, plays a crucial role in regulating the development and function of T-helper 17 (Th17) cells, which are implicated in the pathogenesis of various autoimmune disorders [[4], [5], [6]]. Inhibitors of RORγt that modulate Th17 cell function have emerged as promising therapeutic candidates for treating Th17-related autoimmune diseases such as multiple sclerosis, rheumatoid arthritis, and psoriasis [5,7]. Ursolic acid and digoxin are two well-studied natural products that act as RORγt inverse agonists and selectively inhibit RORγt, thereby suppressing IL-17 production [8]. However, digoxin exhibits potent carcinogenic, extracardiac, and coronary vascular effects, while ursolic acid acts on multiple targets, potentially leading to off-target side effects. Consequently, efforts are ongoing to identify additional RORγt inhibitors, including both natural and synthetic compounds [[9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14]].

  Natural products (NPs) offer unparalleled chemical diversity shaped by evolution, making them a valuable source for drug discovery [15,16]. NPs and their derivatives have historically made significant contributions to the development of newly approved therapeutics [17]. However, identifying novel bioactive compounds within the chemical space of NPs remains a major challenge in drug discovery, often requiring laborious and resource-intensive experimental screening efforts [18].

  Recent advancements in machine learning (ML), encompassing a range of techniques from traditional algorithms to deep learning (DL), have revolutionized the drug discovery process [19,20]. Artificial intelligence (AI)-powered methods streamline various stages of drug discovery, including hit identification and lead optimization, by efficiently analyzing large datasets and predicting molecular properties, thereby reducing timelines and costs [[21], [22], [23]]. Specifically, ML models trained on chemical structures and associated bioactivity data can rapidly screen extensive virtual libraries to prioritize compounds for experimental validation [22]. Among these, ligand-based virtual screening (LBVS) approaches are widely used, particularly when ligand structure and properties are known but the target structure is not available [23].

  Herein, we present an integrated in silico strategy to identify novel RORγt inhibitors from NPs, combining ML-based virtual screening, chemotaxonomic analysis, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulations. ML models were developed to predict RORγt inhibitory activity and applied to a large NP database. The top-scoring hits were subjected to hierarchical chemical classification and literature-based investigation, which led to the prioritization of six protoberberine alkaloids. Using in vitro and ex vivo assays to assess IL-17 production by T cells, we identified berberine (Ber) and coptisine (Cop) as potent and selective inhibitors of Th17 differentiation and function. Importantly, in vivo studies demonstrated that Cop significantly ameliorates Th17/γδT17-driven skin inflammation in a mouse model of psoriasis, correlating with RORγt inhibition.


## Materials and methods

### Data preparation
  Bioactivity data for model training were sourced from the PubChem database [24]. Specifically, data originated from assays associated with Assay ID (AID) 2604 (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioassay/2604) that include the primary cell-based RORγt reporter assay (AID 2551) and a counter-screen assay utilizing the VP16 transactivation domain (AID 2546). These assays aimed to identify inhibitors of RORγt-mediated transcription while enabling the filtering of compounds potentially causing non-specific reporter activation or inhibition. Compound activity was defined based on the 'Activity Score' provided by PubChem, ranging from 0 to 100, reflecting the percentage inhibition of activation across a concentration range of 0.05 nM to 150.4 µM. For the primary RORγt screen (AID 2551), compounds with scores between 40 and 100 were designated as 'active' inhibitors (label 1), while all others were labeled 'inactive' (label 0). An analogous labeling procedure was applied to the counter-screen dataset (AID 2546) to identify compounds active in the non-specific assay. The resulting RORγt dataset comprised 270,773 unique compounds, containing 10,704 active inhibitors and 268,069 inactive compounds. The counter-screen dataset contained 300,701 compounds, with 32,933 compounds labeled as active and 267,768 as inactive.

  To ensure robust model evaluation based on chemical similarity, both datasets were partitioned into training (∼80 %) and test (∼20 %) sets using a scaffold splitting, where we have used the RDKit library to generate the scaffold for each molecule [25]. Approximately 20 % of the training set was further reserved as a validation set during hyperparameter optimization. Model performance was evaluated by averaging results from three independent scaffold-based splits.


### Model training and hyperparameter tuning
  Three distinct molecular representations were generated for model inputs. Two of these—AttentiveFP and Canonical featurizers—were used for GNN models. In these representations, molecules were encoded as undirected graphs where atoms serve as nodes and chemical bonds as edges; associated atom features (including element type, degree, formal charge, hybridization state, aromaticity, and number of attached hydrogens) and bond features (such as bond type, conjugation status, and ring membership) were transformed into numerical vectors suitable for graph-based learning. The third representation was based on molecular fingerprints. Specifically, molecular structures were converted into fixed-length binary vectors using the Extended-Connectivity Fingerprint (ECFP) algorithm from RDKit library. GNN models were built using the Deep Graph Library leveraging the utilities within the DGL-LifeSci package [26,27]. RF classifiers with ECFP fingerprint representations were implemented using the scikit-learn library [28]. The predictive performance of the optimized RF models and the GNN ensemble models was evaluated on the independent test set. Counter-screen models were similarly evaluated, and the corresponding GNN ensemble was used for filtering. For robust prediction, we selected the top three performing hyperparameter configurations for each model based on validation results. To further evaluate the generalization ability of our ensemble model, we conducted external validation experiments using three binary classification datasets (BBBP, HIV, and BACE) from the MoleculeNet benchmark [29]. Each dataset was split into training, validation, and test sets at an 8:1:1 ratio using scaffold-based partitioning. The model was trained with the best hyperparameter setting identified in our primary experiments, and predictive performance was assessed by calculating the area under the ROC curve (AUC) on the validation and test sets. For each dataset, three independent training runs were performed under the same hyperparameter configuration, and the resulting models were combined into an ensemble. The results demonstrate consistently high predictive performance across datasets, supporting the model’s generalization capability (Supplementary Table S1).


### Preparation of six protoberberine-type compounds via virtual screening
  276,518 compounds from NPs were downloaded from an open source database, LOTUS (https://lotus.naturalproducts.net/) [30]. Of these, 196,450 compounds with the molecular weight of more than 200 were prepared using the OPLS4 force field, the Epik mode for ionization, and up to 32 combinations per a ligand generated using the LigPrep module in Maestro. Each compound in the prepared library was scored using the GNN ensemble models for both RORγt inhibition and non-specific activity (VP16 counter-screen). Candidate compounds were prioritized based on a high predicted probability of RORγt inhibition after filtering a low predicted probability of activity in the counter-screen (i.e., average ensemble probability <0.2). The top 1,000 compounds meeting these criteria were selected for further analysis. The selected 1,000 high-priority hits were subjected to automated chemical classification using the ClassyFire web server [31] to obtain a hierarchical chemical ontology (superclass, class, subclass) (Supplementary Files 1).

  To quantitatively evaluate drug-likeness of 1,000 high-priority hits, we employed ‘Lipinski’s rule of five’ (no more than one violation among: molecular mass ≤500 Da, no more than 5 hydrogen bond donors, no more than 10 hydrogen bond acceptors, logP ≤5) via the QikProp module within in Maestro Schrödinger suite version 2024–2 (Schrödinger, LLC, New York, NY) [32]. Additionally, drug-likeness was also quantified using the Quantitative Estimate of Drug-likeness (QED) as implemented in RDKit library [25]. Compounds exhibiting Lipinski’s rule of five scores of 0 or 1, together with those with a QED score of 1, were preferentially selected as filtering criteria (Supplementary Files 2).

  After filtering hits based on drug-likeness results and literature survey, we prioritized the ‘Alkaloid and derivatives’ superclass and used the NPASS database (https://bidd.group/NPASS/) to manually review target information of compounds within each subclass that had more than 30 activity records. Among these, we ultimately selected the ‘Protoberberine alkaloids and derivatives’ subclass. Six commercially available protoberberine alkaloids (phellodendrin, palmatine, coptisine, columbamine, jatrorrhizine, berberine) were selected for structural diversity within the subclass and purchased from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA; Purity ≥95 %).


### Mice
  C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Orient Bio (Seongnam, South Korea) or KOATECH (Pyeongtaek, South Korea). Il17aEGFP reporter mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratories (USA). Il17aCrexRosa26YFP mice were previously described [33]. All the animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the animal use guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Seoul National University (SNU-191210-3-11), Lee Gil Ya Cancer and Diabetes Institute (LCDI-2022-0088), and Incheon National University (INU-ANIM-2024-0026-01).


### Th17 and Th1 differentiation in vitro
  Splenic naive CD4+ T cells were isolated from Il17aEGFP mice or C57BL/6 mice using naive CD4+ T cell isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec). Purified naive CD4+ T cells were cultured in a flat-bottomed 96 well plate pre-coated with anti-CD3ε (1.0 µg/ml, 145-2C11, BioXCell) and anti-CD28 (1.0 µg/ml, 37.75, BioXCell) antibodies in the presence of recombinant human TGF-β (0.5 ng/ml, Peprotech), recombinant mouse IL-6 (50 ng/ml, Peprotech). Titrated doses of candidate compounds or vehicle (DMSO) were added to the culture from day 0. On day 4, cells were harvested and were subjected to flow cytometric analysis to detect EGFP expression levels. IL-17 levels in the culture supernatant were measured by mouse IL-17A ELISA kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). To detect IL-17 expression by flow cytometry, cells were further stimulated with PMA (50 ng/ml, Sigma) and ionomycin (1 μg/ml, Sigma) for 3–4 h in the presence of Brefeldin A (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and monensin (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After fixation with IC Fixation buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), cells were permeabilized and intracellularly stained with APC anti-mouse IL-17 (TC11-18H10.1, Biolegend) and Alexa 488 anti-mouse IFN-γ (XMG1.2, Biolegend). For Th1 differentiation, naive CD4+ T cells were stimulated with plate-bound anti-CD3ε (1.0 µg/ml) and anti-CD28 (1.0 µg/ml) antibodies in the presence of recombinant murine IL-12 (10 ng/ml, Peprotech) and recombinant murine IL-2 (10 ng/ml, Peprotech). On day 4, IFN-γ levels in the culture supernatant were measured by mouse IFN-γ ELISA kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The cultured cells were subjected to intracellular cytokine staining with Alexa 488 anti-mouse IFN-γ (XMG1.2, Biolegend) after restimulation with PMA and ionomycin in the presence of Brefeldin A and monensin.


### MOG-specific Th17 restimulation assay
  Il17aCrexRosa26YFP mice were immunized with myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein peptide (MOG35-55, RS synthesis, USA) emulsified in CFA as previously described [33]. Eight days after immunization, single cells prepared from draining lymph nodes were restimulated with MOG35-55 peptide (25 µg/ml) and recombinant mouse IL-23 (20 ng/ml, Thermo Fisher Scientific) in the presence of titrated doses of Cop, Ber, or vehicle (DMSO). At day 5 after culture, IL-17 levels in the culture supernatant were measured by ELISA. Cells were further restimulated with PMA and ionomycin in the presence of Brefeldin A and monensin for flow cytometric analysis of cytokine production. Cell surface staining was performed with Pacific Blue anti-mouse CD4 (GK1.5, Biolegend), PerCP-Cy5.5 anti-mouse CD44 (IM7, Biolegend), and eFluor506-fixable viability dye (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After cell surface staining, cells were permeabilized followed by intracellular staining with PE anti-mouse IL-17 (TC11-18H10.1, Biolegend).


### Imiquimod-induced psoriasis-like skin inflammation
  C57BL/6 mice were treated daily with Aldara cream (5 % imiquimod, w/w, BK Pharm, South Korea) in the left and right ears for six consecutive days. Cop (50 mg/kg) was administered intraperitoneally (i.p.) daily from day 0 (prevention model) or day 2 (therapeutic model) after disease induction. The same volume of vehicle (DMSO) was injected i.p. as a control. Ear thickness of both ears was measured daily. On day 6 after disease induction, mice were sacrificed for analysis. Single cell suspensions prepared from draining cervical lymph nodes were incubated with PMA and ionomycin for 3 h in the presence of Brefeldin A and monensin for flow cytometric analysis or 24 h without Brefeldin A and monensin for detection of IL-17 levels in the culture supernatant by ELISA. For surface staining of ear cells, cells were stained with PE anti-mouse TCRγδ (GL3, Biolegend), BUV395 anti-mouse CD3ε (145-2C11, BD Bioscience), PE-Cy7 anti-mouse CD4 (GK1.5, Biolegend), APC-Cy7 anti-mouse CD45 (30-F11, Biolegend) for 30 min at 4 °C. After surface staining, cells were fixed with IC fixation buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 20–30 min at 4 °C, and then intracellular staining was performed with FITC anti-mouse-IL-17A (TC11-18H10.1, Biolegend), APC anti-mouse-IFN-γ (XMG1.2, Biolegend). Surface staining of restimulated lymph node cells was performed using PE anti-mouse TCRγδ (GL3, Biolegend), APC anti-mouse/human CD44 (IM7, Biolegend), and Pacific Blue anti-mouse-CD4 (GK1.5, Biolegend). Intracellular staining was performed using FITC anti-mouse-IL-17A (TC11-18H10.1, Biolegend) and PE-Cy7 anti-mouse IFN-γ (XMG1.2, Biolegend).


### Histological and immunohistochemical analysis
  For histological analysis, tissues were fixed in 10 % neutral-buffered formalin, processed using standard paraffin embedding, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) on an automated slide stainer (MICROM HMS 70) at the Core-facility for Cell to In-vivo imaging, Gachon University. The stained slides were digitized with a whole-slide scanner (PANNORAMIC SCAN II, 3DHISTECH). Images were randomly selected and analyzed at 30× magnification using the CaseViewer software (3DHISTECH). For immunohistochemistry, paraffin sections were incubated with a mouse anti-Ki-67 primary antibody (B56, BD Biosciences), followed by a biotinylated secondary antibody. The sections were then treated with the VECTASTAIN Elite ABC-HRP kit (PK-6200, Vector Laboratories), and immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) as the chromogen. Finally, the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin to visualize cell nuclei.


### Protein purification
  Complementary DNA encoding the RORγt ligand binding domain (RORγt-LBD) (residues 262–507) was cloned into the pET-28a vector and transformed into E. coli strains BL21 (DE3) cells. The cells were grown in LB medium at 37 °C. Overexpression of RORγt-LBD was induced with 0.5 mM isopropyl-β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) at 15 °C for 16 h when the optical cell density reached an OD600 of 0.8. The cells were collected and resuspended in a buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl, 500 mM NaCl, 5 % Glycerol, 0.4 mM Tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine hydrochloride (TCEP), 0.1 % Nonidet P-40 (NP40), pH 7.3 with Protease inhibitor cocktail tablet (Roche) to be disrupted by sonication. Cell debris was removed by centrifugation and the supernatant was loaded onto a Ni-affinity column. Poly-histidine-tagged RORγt-LBD was eluted with raising concentration of imidazole up to 500 mM. The poly-histidine tags were cleaved by thrombin protease at 4 °C for 16 h. After that, the RORγt-LBD was diluted to 50 mM NaCl and purified by ion exchange chromatography with gradient increasing concentration of NaCl up to 1 M. The eluate was concentrated and further applied to size exclusion chromatography using Superdex 75 increase 10/300 GL with PBS buffer (pH 7.4). The peak fractions of the purified proteins were collected and concentrated to 1 mg/ml for SPR experiments.


### Surface plasmon resonance
  SPR was employed to measure binding affinities between RORγt-LBD protein and Cop and Ber using Biacore T200 (Cytiva). Cop and Ber were prepared to concentration of 50 mM in 100 % DMSO for complete dissolution. The ligand protein was diluted with 10 mM sodium acetate and immobilized on CM5 chip (Cytiva) at a flow rate of 30 μl/min. Each of Cop and Ber was associated with running buffer containing PBS, 0.005 % Tween20, and 5 % DMSO. The binding of Cop and Ber to RORγt-LBD was assessed across a series of concentrations: 6.25, 12.5, 25, 50, 100, 200, 400, and 800 μM and 3.125, 6.25, 12.5, 25, and 50 μM, respectively. The binding curves for SPR measurement were collected and analyzed by using Biacore T200 Control and Biacore T200 evaluation software.


### Docking study
  We employed a structure-based virtual screening through molecular docking approach using the crystal structure of RORγt protein with ligand-binding domain in complex with an inverse agonist (PDB code: 4QM0) in the RCSB PDB homepage (https://www.rcsb.org/, December 2021). The protein structure was prepared with the default parameters including hydrogens addition, water deletion, protonation state adjustment and restrained minimization using the Protein Preparation Wizard workflow in Maestro Schrödinger suite version 2024–2 (Schrödinger, LLC, New York, NY) [32]. After the Receptor Grid Generation step for limiting the ligand-binding site using the original ligand for 4QM0, Glide SP (standard precision) workflow was used for the ligand docking study. Ber and Cop, which showed inhibitory effects on IL-17 production by T cells in in vitro and ex vivo assays, were docked on the crystal structure of RORγt protein using Glide SP.


### Molecular dynamics study
  Molecular dynamics (MD) study was performed to investigate the stability of Cop in the binding pocket of RORγt protein (PDB code: 4QM0) for 200 ns using the Desmond software implemented in Maestro [32]. The protein–ligand complex was prepared with the TIP4P solvent model, the boundary condition of 10 Å distances using Orthorhombic Box Shape function and neutralized using the OPLS4 force field. After solvation, the complex was subjected to equilibrium runs using the Nose-Hoover chain thermostat and Martyna-Tobias-Klein baristat methods with NPT ensemble class [34,35]. The equilibration of the protein–ligand complex was analyzed using the root mean square deviation (RMSD). The interactions between the protein and Cop were monitored using Protein-Ligand contacts and Ligand-Protein contacts plots throughout the simulation time.


## Results

### Development of Machine learning models for RORγt inhibitor discovery
  To identify novel RORγt inhibitors, we first established robust ML models to predict compound activity based on chemical structure (Fig. 1A). We explored different modeling strategies, including traditional algorithms using molecular fingerprints and deep learning approaches utilizing molecular graphs. Specifically, random forest (RF) was trained using extended-connectivity fingerprints (ECFP) [36], while graph neural networks (GNNs), such as graph convolutional network (GCN) [37], graph attention networks (GAT) [38], Attentive FP [39], MPNN [40], NF [41], and Weave [42], were trained directly on molecular graph representations (Supplementary Table S2). Both RF and GNN models were trained to distinguish RORγt inhibitors using the screening dataset of RORγt transcriptional activity (PubChem ID: AID2551) and were also trained on a counter-screen dataset (AID2546) to identify and filter out compounds exhibiting non-specific activity (Fig. 1B). Model performance was evaluated three times using distinct scaffold-based splitting, which is described in detail in the Materials and methods section. Comparing the performance on a test set, the GNN-based models, especially AttentiveFP, and Weave, demonstrated superior predictive capability based on AUC-ROC and F1 score for this specific task (Fig. 1C and D). Due to their comparatively lower accuracy, RF and MPNN models were excluded from further prediction tasks. To evaluate the robustness and reproducibility of our GCN model predictions, we employed the Integrated Gradients (IG) method for an atom-level importance analysis [43,44]. This interpretability assessment was performed on pairs of structurally similar active compounds identified by Tanimoto similarity to test whether the model learned consistent chemical patterns. The analysis revealed that the GCN model consistently attributed high importance to analogous atoms and substructures across structurally similar molecules (Supplementary Fig. S1).   Additionally, to investigate the potential selectivity of Cop for RORγt over its related isoforms, RORα and RORβ, we performed an in silico structural similarity analysis against curated libraries of known active compounds from PubChem. Structural similarity was quantified using the Tanimoto coefficient based on Morgan fingerprints. While the absolute Tanimoto scores were modest, the top-ranked compounds in the RORγt active set shared significant and visually apparent structural scaffolds with Cop (Supplementary Fig. S2, top row). In contrast, the most similar compounds identified in the active sets for RORα and RORβ displayed both low similarity scores and a clear lack of structural analogy to Cop (Supplementary Fig. S2, middle and bottom rows). These results suggests that Cop is preferentially aligned with established RORγt ligands, providing indirect evidence for its selectivity.

  Consequently, an ensemble model averaging the predictions from the best-performing models was constructed for the subsequent virtual screening procedure. The corresponding ensemble model trained on the counter-screen data was used for filtering non-specific compounds.


### Virtual screening of natural products and selection of protoberberine alkaloids
  We utilized a combination of an ensemble model to selectively identify NPs targeting RORγt and employed an integrative strategy incorporating multiple in silico screening steps to discover potential RORγt inhibitors (Fig. 2A). We performed an ML-based virtual screening using our ensemble model against a library of 196,450 processed NPs derived from the LOTUS database [30]. The top 1,000 high-scoring candidate compounds underwent further in silico analysis via hierarchical chemical classification tool, ClassyFire [31], and manually inspected in the view of the chemotaxonomy by the comparison with literature (Fig. 2B). The structures of 1,000 candidates were classified into 11 superclasses, with the two largest groups being ‘Lipids and lipid-like molecules’ and ‘Phenylpropanoids and polyketides’. These groups were deprioritized due to their low drug-likeness scores according to the drug-likeness properties, such as Lipinski's Rule of Five and QED scores (Supplementary Files 2), as well as lack of novelty. Moreover, lipid-like compounds have been reported to bind members of the ROR subfamily, such as RORβ, suggesting potential off-target effects [45,46]. Similarly, compounds in the ‘Phenylpropanoids and polyketides’ class, many of which are terpenoid or steroid derivatives such as hydroxycholesterol, ursolic acid, and digoxin, have been shown to act as agonists or inverse agonists of RORα and RORγt [[47], [48], [49]]. We decided to focus on the ‘Alkaloid and derivatives’ superclass, known for its diverse pharmacological roles, and manually investigated the activity records of compounds within its subclasses. Using the NPASS database (https://bidd.group/NPASS/), which links chemotaxonomy information with activity records, we examined targets of compounds with more than 30 activity records. Among these, we focused on ‘Protoberberine alkaloids and derivatives,’ which have not been studied in relation to RORγt and represent the largest subset within the ‘Alkaloid and derivatives’ superclass (Fig. 2C). Protoberberine alkaloids are recognized as bioactive compounds with low toxicity and have been experimentally validated in various disease models [[50], [51], [52], [53]]. Accordingly, we selected six commercially available protoberberine alkaloids for further evaluation of their biological activity related to RORγt (Fig. 2D).Fig. 2Chemical classification and structures of candidate compounds. ### Inhibitory effects of protoberberine alkaloids on Th17 cell differentiation in vitro
  To investigate the potential of the six protoberberine alkaloids as RORγt inhibitors, each compound was tested for its effect on Th17 differentiation in vitro. Naive CD4+ T cells isolated from Il17aEGFP reporter mice were subjected to Th17 polarization with titrated doses of each compound (Fig. 3A). Among the six protoberberine alkaloids, berberine (Ber) demonstrated the most profound inhibition of IL-17-EGFP expression in CD4+ T cells on day 4 after culture in a dose dependent manner. Coptisine (Cop) also significantly inhibited IL-17-EGFP expression at 3.3 μM as well as at 10 μM, while palmatine marginally inhibited IL-17-EGFP expression at 10 μM (Fig. 3B and C). To confirm the inhibitory effect of Cop and Ber at the protein level, naive CD4+ T cells isolated from C57BL/6 mice were subjected to Th17 differentiation in the presence of two-fold serially diluted compounds (Fig. 3D). Flow cytometric analysis of IL-17 expression in CD4+ T cells confirmed the dose-dependent inhibition of Th17 differentiation by Cop or Ber treatment at the single cell level (Fig. 3E and F). When the IL-17 levels in the culture supernatant were measured by ELISA, Cop and Ber resulted in the IC50 values of 1.859 μM and 0.8561 μM, respectively (Fig. 3G and H). To investigate whether the inhibitory effects of Cop and Ber were specific to Th17 differentiation, their effects on Th1 differentiation were tested. Intriguingly, Cop and Ber did not significantly inhibit Th1 differentiation at all tested doses, suggesting that the inhibitory effects of Cop and Ber are Th17-specific (Supplementary Fig. S3). ### Coptisine and berberine inhibit expansion of antigen-specific Th17 cells

Eight days after immunization with myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein 35–55 (MOG35-55) peptides in CFA, lymphocytes isolated from draining lymph nodes were restimulated with MOG35-55 peptides and recombinant IL-23 to enrich and expand MOG-specific Th17 cells. Titrated doses of Cop or Ber were added to the culture to investigate their effects on MOG-specific Th17 cell expansion (Fig. 4A). After 5 days of restimulation, flow cytometric analysis revealed a significant reduction in the frequencies of IL-17 fate-mapped cells (IL-17 fm-YFP+) as well as currently IL-17 expressing cells (IL-17+) after treatment with either Cop or Ber in a dose dependent manner (Fig. 4B–D). Consistent with these results, a dose-dependent reduction in IL-17 production in the culture supernatant was also observed after Cop or Ber treatment (Fig. 4E). ### Docking study and molecular dynamics simulation
  We conducted docking studies to investigate whether Cop and Ber effectively bind to the ligand-binding site of RORγt (PDB: 4QM0), which is the crystal structure of the human RORγt-LBD in complex with a tertiary sulfonamide that serves as an inhibitor with lower lipophilicity and higher selectivity than other candidates (Fig. 5C and D) [54]. Cop formed 4 pi interactions with Phe388 and His479, which were the binding residues of other inhibitors [5]. A stable pi-cation interaction formed between Phe388 and the nitrogen atom within Cop [55,56]. Furthermore, the unique pi-pi stacking interactions of two aromatic rings and the dioxolane ring with His479 were considered to potentially stabilize the interaction network [56]. In contrast, Ber, possessing dimethoxy residues instead of the dioxolane ring found in Cop, exhibited a pi-pi interaction between the ring with dimethoxy group and His479. However, this conformation resulted in an increased distance between the nitrogen atoms in Ber and Phe388. Next, we performed a molecular dynamics study of Cop to better understand the interaction with RORγt. During the simulation time of 1,000 ns, the protein–ligand complex remained stable (Supplementary Fig. S4A). The protein backbone RMSD against Cop showed only minor fluctuations, remaining below 1.5 Å at a steady state. Analysis of the interaction fraction between protein residues and Cop revealed consistent interaction with Phe388 and His479, averaging 31 % and 35 % contact strength, respectively, over the total simulation time (Supplementary Fig. S4B and C). Interestingly, we observed a variation in the ligand RMSD around 300 ns. During 0–300 ns, the interaction with Phe388 was dominant with an average contact strength of 75 % (Supplementary Fig. S4D and E). However, between 400 and 1,000 ns, the pi-pi interaction with His479 reached an average contact strength of 53 % (Supplementary Fig. S4F and G). These results are consistent with the docking study indicating that both Phe388 and His479 are the main interaction residues with Cop.

## Discussion
  The discovery of novel, and effective RORγt inhibitors remains a critical goal for treating Th17-mediated autoimmune diseases like psoriasis. This study successfully demonstrates an integrated computational and experimental workflow for identifying candidate compounds from the complex chemical space of NPs. By combining ML-based virtual screening, chemotaxonomic analysis, molecular docking, molecular dynamics, and biological validation, we identified two protoberberine alkaloids, Cop and Ber, as direct RORγt inhibitors with therapeutic potential.

  To ensure a well-curated dataset for the construction of ML model, we collected RORγt-related bioassay datasets from the PubChem repository. The largest dataset (AID: 2551) had an imbalanced ratio of inactive molecules, which we addressed by randomizing chemical similarities to achieve a more balanced dataset with active molecules. Additionally, to exclude compounds that may yield false positive results by binding to the Gal4 DNA binding domain or the UAS luciferase reporter, we employed a dataset (AID 2546) using a vector with the VP16 transactivation domain instead of the RORγt transactivation domain to develop the ML model for selecting candidates that inhibit RORγt. To validate the reliability of the GCN model's predictions, we systematically assessed its interpretability using the IG method. This analysis provided strong evidence for the model's robustness and the reproducibility of its predictions, suggesting that it learns meaningful and generalizable chemical representations that support the stability of the GCN framework. Therefore, this interpretability analysis complements the model's predictive performance metrics, offering an additional layer of validation for the trustworthiness of the machine learning workflow applied in this study.

  A key contribution of our approach was the subsequent filtering and prioritization of ML hits using chemotaxonomy. The structural diversity of NPs can pose challenges for the finding of novel candidates on the specific targets. Many natural products share common structural features derived from representative biosynthetic pathways. Grouping compounds based on similar core structures can serve as an effective strategy for identifying candidates with comparable bioactivities. The chemotaxonomic approach helped navigate the diversity of predicted NP hits and focusing experimental resources efficiently. By classifying the top 1,000 ML candidates, we could strategically deprioritize scaffolds with poor drug-like properties (e.g., lipids) or those containing known RORγt modulators with liabilities (e.g., certain terpenoids/steroids related to digoxin/ursolic acid). Through this rational filtering process, we prioritized protoberberine alkaloids, which are a class with known bioactivities but relatively unexplored potential against RORγt. Considering that the structural diversity of protoberberine derivatives is largely determined by the presence and position of hydroxyl, methoxy groups or 1,3-dioxolane rings on both benzene rings, we selected six representative protoberberine derivatives with these structural modifications for further study. Among six protoberberine derivatives, both Cop and Ber demonstrated potent and selective inhibition of Th17 differentiation in vitro. SPR experiments unequivocally established their direct binding to the RORγt ligand-binding domain, confirming them as RORγt ligands. Cop exhibited a higher binding affinity (KD∼60 µM) than Ber (KD∼108 µM), consistent with molecular docking and dynamics simulations suggesting potentially more favorable interactions for Cop, particularly involving pi-pi stacking with His479 and pi-cation interactions with Phe388. Although a transition was observed around 300 ns, the RMSD between the protein and ligand contacts remained stable throughout the 1,000 ns simulation, indicating that this shift does not result from conformational instability. Instead, the temporal transition between Phe388- and His479-dominated binding modes suggests an induced-fit binding mechanism, in which Cop adapts to local conformational changes within the RORγt binding site. This dynamic may contribute to an extended residence time and potential allosteric effects, highlighting the complex interplay between ligand flexibility and protein plasticity.

  Ber is a well-known natural product with diverse pharmacological activities, including inhibition of Th17 differentiation [[57], [58], [59], [60], [61]]. However, the underlying mechanism has remained unclear. Our study provides strong evidence that Ber directly engages RORγt, identifying a likely mechanism responsible for its effects on Th17 cell differentiation. To our knowledge, this is the first demonstration of direct RORγt binding by Ber, offering mechanistic insight into its selective action on Th17 differentiation [62]. Nonetheless, we do not rule out the possibility that RORγt binding is not solely responsible for Th17 inhibition, as previous studies have reported that Ber can also interact with TGFBR1 and JAK2, both of which are critical regulators of Th17 differentiation [62]. Cop, being less extensively studied, represents a more novel RORγt-targeting scaffold identified through our workflow. Based on its stronger binding affinity compared to Ber, along with its relative novelty, Cop was advanced to in vivo testing. In the preclinical imiquimod-induced psoriasis model, Cop demonstrated significant therapeutic efficacy, reducing skin inflammation and suppressing IL-17 production by γδT cells and CD4+ T cells. This in vivo activity strongly supports the potential of Cop as a therapeutic candidate for psoriasis and potentially other RORγt+ T cell-driven autoimmune diseases.

  Pharmacokinetic (PK) studies of Cop have reported that its oral bioavailability is low, mainly due to poor membrane permeability and rapid metabolism [63,64]. Intraperitoneal (i.p.) administration may achieve higher plasma concentrations than oral delivery. Therefore, further PK studies following i.p. administration in an in vivo psoriasis animal model are needed to address these bioavailability limitations. In addition, the development of Cop derivatives or novel formulations will be important to overcome its poor bioavailability and enhance its therapeutic potential.


## Conclusions
  In summary, this study validates an efficient, multi-faceted strategy combining advanced ML, chemotaxonomic knowledge, and targeted experimental work for NP-based drug discovery. We successfully identified and characterized Cop as a novel, direct-acting RORγt inhibitor with significant in vivo efficacy against psoriasis-like skin inflammation. Although the binding affinities of Ber and Cop were not sufficiently potent for direct drug development, confirmation of their interaction with RORγt highlights the potential for developing natural product-based RORγt inhibitors through structure-guided optimization and derivative design. This work not only delivers a promising lead compound but also provides a robust approach applicable to discovering modulators for other challenging biological targets by leveraging the rich chemical diversity of natural products. Further investigation of the PK properties and structural optimization of Cop derivatives is warranted to comprehensively evaluate their therapeutic potential.
