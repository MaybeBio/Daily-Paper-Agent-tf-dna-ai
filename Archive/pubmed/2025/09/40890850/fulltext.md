# Mammalian conservation of endogenous G-quadruplex reveals their associations with complex traits


## Background
  DNA G-quadruplexes (G4s) are four-stranded DNA structures. Endogenous G-quadruplexes (eG4s) have been identified as pivotal regulatory elements for gene expression in the human genome. The measurement of evolutionary conservation can be employed to ascertain the functional relevance of putative regulatory elements. However, the evolutionary profiles of human eG4s remain largely unknown.


## Results
  Here, we construct mammalian evolutionary profiles of human eG4s based on a comprehensive reference annotation of human eG4s from the integration of the eG4 database EndoQuad covering 41 human cell lines and our home-made G4 CUT&Tag data covering seven cell lines. We find that transposable elements contribute substantially to the evolutionary spread of primate-specific eG4s. A total of 92,910 highly conserved human eG4s were identified under mammalian constraint. By developing and utilizing the eG4 prediction tool eG4finder, which is based on a large language model, we verify the high structural conservation of highly conserved eG4s. The enrichment of highly conserved eG4s in developmental and aging pathways highlights their potential significance in key biological processes. Notably, highly conserved eG4s exhibit higher regulatory potential, regulatory activity and affinity for transcription factors. We demonstrate that highly conserved eG4s are the most powerful transcriptional activation elements in the total eG4 collection. Meanwhile, trait-associated variants and variants affecting the expression of high phenotypic severity genes are most enriched in highly conserved eG4s.


## Conclusions
  Our study highlights the important regulatory functions and close association with complex human traits of human eG4s that are highly conserved in the mammalian lineage.

## Background
  DNA G-quadruplexes (G4s) are non-B DNA secondary structures that are formed by G-rich sequences [1]. The potential G-quadruplex sequences (PQSs) typically comprise four G-tracts and loop sequences that are connected between the G-tracts. G4s are formed by stacked planar quartets, which are stabilised by Hoogsteen hydrogen bonds connecting the Gs of adjacent G-tracts [2, 3]. Initially, G4 prediction software developed based on the straightforward sequence characteristics of PQSs, was employed to identify PQSs within the genomic context [4–6]. Furthermore, G4-seq has been developed and employed for the detection of genome-wide in vitro G4s in G4-promoting conditions [7, 8]. However, the majority of PQSs and in vitro G4s do not form endogenous G4s (eG4s) that are actually present in vivo [9, 10]. Consequently, G4 ChIP-seq/CUT&Tag based on G4 antibodies were developed and used to detect genome-wide eG4s in vivo [11, 12]. The genome-wide characterization of eG4s has revealed its enrichment in regulatory regions of chromatin and its function as a transcription factor (TF) binding hub [13, 14]. This has led to the proposal that eG4s plays a role in shaping the transcriptome landscape [15–17]. In a previous study, we established EndoQuad, a database of eG4s defined by PQSs overlapping with G4 peaks [18], and highlighted the greater conservation and regulatory potential of eG4s in comparison to non-eG4s (PQSs that do not form G4 structures in vivo). In conclusion, eG4s are acknowledged as a pivotal regulatory element of gene expression [19, 20].

  The quantification of evolutionary conservation represents an efficacious methodology for the identification of functional regions within the genome. Genes with conserved sequences often indicate that they are under purifying selection and are functionally important [21]. Furthermore, conserved non-coding elements have been demonstrated to be critical for development and have been associated with a variety of diseases [22, 23]. Recent studies have demonstrated that evolutionary conserved human candidate cis-regulatory elements and TF binding sites (TFBSs) exhibit heightened regulatory functions and activity, and exert a significant impact on human traits [24]. However, the evolutionary landscape of eG4 remains largely unknown. A reference-free alignment across 241 mammalian genomes produced by the Zoonomia Consortium [25] and the comprehensive eG4 database EndoQuad provide an opportunity to further enhance our comprehension of eG4 evolution and function. We postulate that human eG4s that are evolutionarily conserved in mammals may have enhanced regulatory functions compared to other eG4s. Therefore, these conserved eG4s are likely to have a closer association with complex human traits.

  In this study, we integrated our home-made G4 CUT&Tag data with the EndoQuad database, thereby producing the most comprehensive annotation of human eG4s. By utilizing the reference-free 241 mammalian genome alignment developed by the Zoonomia project, we were able to characterize the evolutionary profiles of human eG4s within mammals and identify highly conserved eG4s under mammalian constraint. Furthermore, we developed an eG4 prediction tool, designated eG4finder, which is designed to generically predict the potential of PQSs to form eG4s in vivo based on DNA sequence alone. The cross-species eG4 prediction with eG4finder verified the high structural conservation of highly conserved eG4s. Moreover, the integration of functional genomics datasets provided compelling evidence for the enhanced regulatory potential and powerful transcription enhancement of highly conserved eG4s. Importantly, we demonstrated the important function of highly conserved eG4s in driving complex human traits.


## Results

### Mammalian evolutionary profiles of human eG4s
  A comprehensive reference set of human eG4s is the prerequisite for their evolutionary study. We previously developed the EndoQuad database to comprehensively identify eG4s spanning 41 human cell lines. We separated each cell type from EndoQuad and found that the vast majority of eG4s in each separated cell type (mean: 95.5%) were also eG4s in the remaining cell types in EndoQuad (Additional file 1: Fig. S1A). This finding indicates that the majority of eG4s identified in any new G4 ChIP-seq/CUT&Tag data are likely to have been annotated in EndoQuad. In conclusion, the human eG4s in the EndoQuad database tended to be saturated (Additional file 1: Fig. S1A-C), thereby constituting a potential reference set of human eG4s.

  To validate the saturation of human eG4s from EndoQuad and further refine the human eG4 reference set, we further performed G4 CUT&Tag with a pre-assembled Tn5 transposome based on the previous work [12] to profile native eG4s in seven human cell lines (Methods; Fig. 1A). Of these, four cell lines (HT29, HCT116, NCM460, SW620) were not included in EndoQuad, and three other cell lines (HepG2, HEK293T, A549) were included in EndoQuad to provide positive controls. Briefly, we purified the eG4 structure-specific scFv antibody BG4 and combined it with the Tn5-based CUT&Tag approach [26]. In this protocol, eG4 structures are specifically recognized by BG4 in situ and eG4-containing DNA fragments are cut and released by Tn5 transposome-assisted tagmentation.Fig. 1Mammalian evolution of the human eG4s.   Prior to conducting an integrative analysis, quality control of the G4 CUT&Tag data was performed. The results of the hierarchical clustering analysis demonstrated that the biological replicates of the same cell types were clustered together (Additional file 1: Fig. S2A), indicating the high quality and reproducibility of the data. Furthermore, it was observed that the proportion of regions containing at least one PQS and the number of PQSs were both higher in the G4 peaks than in the shuffled regions (Additional file 1: Fig. S2B-C). The G4 peaks demonstrate a notable enrichment of PQSs, particularly within their centers (Additional file 1: Fig. S2D). In addition to exhibiting substantial overlaps with PQSs, the majority of G4 peaks contained known eG4s from EndoQuad (Additional file 1: Fig. S3A). The genome browser tracks shown the large pileup of G4 CUT&Tag signals at known promoter eG4s (Additional file 1: Fig. S3B). Consistent with previous reports [13], the majority of G4 peaks were localized to promoters, while most of the remaining peaks were distributed among introns and distal intergenic regions (Additional file 1: Fig. S4). These findings demonstrate that our data have enabled the mapping of eG4 structures with high confidence. Subsequently, eG4s were identified in each cell line (Methods). The eG4s demonstrated a low degree of overlap among the seven cell lines and a high proportion detected in only one cell line (Additional file 1: Fig. S5), indicating that eG4s exhibited high cell-type specificity among different cell lines. Furthermore, the proportions of known eG4s were within the expected range in three cell lines as positive controls (Additional file 1: Fig. S6A). In the four cell lines not included in EndoQuad, from 91.2% (in HCT116) to 95.5% (in HT29) of eG4s are known in EndoQuad (Additional file 1: Fig. S6B), indicating that the human eG4 reference set in EndoQuad has reached saturation. The newly identified eG4s from the G4 CUT&Tag data were integrated with the existing eG4s in the EndoQuad database (Fig. 1A; Additional file 1: Fig. S7), resulting in the most comprehensive human eG4 annotation.

  Subsequently, to examine the evoluationary patterns of eG4s, we used the reference-free 241 mammalian genome alignment, as provided by the Zoonomia project [25], to calculate the base alignment rates of each human eG4 across 240 non-human genomes (Methods; Fig. 1A). Based on the number of genomes with base alignment rates of ≥ 90% (N1) and the number of genomes with base alignment rates of ≤ 10% (N2) for each eG4, the 404,538 eG4s were mapped in a triangular plane with N1 and N2 as the y-axis and the x-axis, and three major evolutionary categories are shown (Fig. 1B; Additional file 1: Fig. S8). Category 1 (C1) eG4s represent highly conserved eG4s, exhibiting base alignment rates of ≥ 90% in the majority of mammalian genomes and ≥ 50% in the vast majority of mammalian genomes. Category 2 (C2) eG4s are actively evolving eG4s, which have base alignment rates of ≥ 90% almost exclusively in primate genomes but can also have base alignment rates of ≥ 50% in nonprimate genomes. Category 3 (C3) eG4s are primate-specific eG4s with base alignment rates of ≤ 10% in almost all nonprimate genomes. The three categories of eG4s collectively constituted approximately 68.1% of the overall eG4s.

  We then investigated evolutionary conservation patterns of human eG4s within the mammalian lineage based on the comparisons among C1-C3 categories. In comparison to non-eG4s and shuffled regions, eG4s exhibited a higher percentages in C1 and C2, while displaying a lower percentages in C3 (two-sided Fisher’s exact test, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1B, C), which underscores the heightened functional potential of eG4s. To visualize the evolutionary landscape of human eG4s, we employed Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) [27] for dimensionality reduction on eG4s according to their base alignment rates in 240 mammalian genomes. The majority of eG4s formed a continuous large cluster in UMAP, with highly conserved C1 eG4s at one end and primate-specific C3 eG4s at the other end, connected by actively evolving C2 eG4s (Fig. 1D). The UMAPs were colored according to the number of genomes with base alignment rates of ≥ 90% (N1) and the number of genomes with base alignment rates of ≥ 50% (N3) for each eG4. It was observed that the two UMAP maps were highly similar except for the color of C2 eG4s (Fig. 1E, F). Furthermore, the UMAP was colored according to the average PhastCons score of eG4s, revealing that C1 eG4s situated at the extremities of large clusters exhibited the highest sequence conservation (Fig. 1G). The quantitative results also indicate that C1 eG4s exhibited much greater conservation than C2 eG4s, and that C2 eG4s were more conserved than C3 eG4s (two-sided Wilcoxon rank sum test, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1H). In conclusion, these results suggest that the evolutionary landscape of eG4s can be constructed through the alignments of human eG4s across mammalian genomes. The C1, C2, and C3 categories reliably summarize the patterns of evolutionary conservation of human eG4s in the mammalian lineage, including highly conserved, actively evolving, and primate-specific patterns.


### Evolutionary contribution of transposable elements to primate-specific eG4s
  Transposable elements (TEs) have been demonstrated to widely drive the evolutionary innovation of regulatory elements, particularly in primates [28]. To evaluate the contribution of TEs to the evolution of human eG4s, we explored the distribution of TEs in each evolutionary category of eG4s.

  We found that primate-specific eG4s exhibit a higher proportion of overlap with TEs (51.5%) compared to highly conserved and actively evolving eG4s (7.0% and 12.1%) (two-sided Fisher’s exact test) (Fig. 2A). Specifically, SINE-VNTR-Alus (SVAs) and endogenous retroviruses 1 (ERV1s) demonstrated significant enrichment in primate-specific eG4s and depletion in highly conserved and actively evolving eG4s (Fig. 2B), indicating the substantial contribution of SVAs and ERV1s to the evolutionary spread of primate-specific eG4s. Primate-specific eG4s contain multiple groups with different evolutionary conservation patterns. We identified five such groups, including one that is human-specific and others that are conserved only in hominoidea, catarrhini, haplorhini, and primates (Methods; Fig. 2C). We found that SVAs are only enriched in human-specific eG4s, while ERV1s are enriched in three groups that are conserved only in hominoidea, catarrhini, and haplorhini (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, long interspersed nuclear elements 1 (LINE1s) are slightly enriched in the group that is conserved only in catarrhini. These results suggest that the evolutionary spread of various primate-specific eG4 groups is driven by different TE families.Fig. 2Contribution of TEs in the evolution of primate-specific eG4s. ### The tool eG4finder: an effective eG4 predictor
  It is essential to evaluate whether sequence-conserved C1 eG4s are conserved across mammals at the structure level. Given the dearth of G4 ChIP-seq/CUT&Tag data for mammals other than human, it is challenging to obtain a comprehensive eG4 reference set for these species. Meanwhile, existing G4 prediction tools are either used to predict G4 in vitro, such as Quadron, G4detector, and PENGUINN [29–31], or aimed at predicting eG4s in specific human cell types based on DNA sequence and chromatin accessibility data from ATAC-seq, such as DeepG4 and G4Beacon [32, 33]. Accordingly, we have developed an eG4 prediction tool, eG4finder, which is a large language model-based method and is designed to generically predict the potential of genome-wide PQSs to form eG4s in vivo based on DNA sequence alone.

  The framework of eG4finder is illustrated in Fig. 3A. In the first step, to train the eG4finder, we prepare the human PQS sequences with their corresponding recurrence, which is defined by EndoQuad database and refers to the number of cell types in which a given PQS has been detected to form eG4. Moreover, considering that the flanking sequences have a pivotal effect in influencing the formation of eG4s [34–37], we extended the PQS sequences to 500 nt on both sides to obtain the input PQS sequences consisting of original PQS sequences and flanking DNA. In the second step, we utilize the DNABERT-2 [38], which is an advance pre-trained genome foundational model trained by human genome and the multi-species genome, to encode the input PQS sequences. Then, the embeddings of PQS are inputted to a 3-layer Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) for outputting the recurrence. In the third step, after training the eG4finder model, we utilize it for predicting the recurrences of novel PQSs in human and other species. The evaluation of the model is regarded as a binary classification task, whereby PQSs with predicted recurrences of less than 1 are categorized as non-eG4, and all others are designated as eG4.Fig. 3Model architecture and prediction performance of eG4finder. A Overview of the eG4finder architecture and training workflow. B Pearson correlation analysis between PQS recurrence and eG4finder score in the human test sets. C AUROCs for eG4finder and other G4 prediction tools in the human test sets. D AUPRCs for eG4finder and other G4 prediction tools in the human test sets. E Comparison of performance between eG4finder and other G4 prediction tools, in terms of AUROC, AUPRC, accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score. F AUROCs for eG4finder in mouse, pig, and chicken. G Proportion of predicted eG4s for eG4s and non-eG4s in mouse, pig, and chicken. H eG4finder score for eG4s and non-eG4s in mouse, pig, and chicken. The outliers are hidden

  We then evaluated the prediction performance of eG4finder. In the human test sets, PQS recurrence was found to be positively correlated with the eG4finder score (Pearson r = 0.78, P < 0.001), indicating that eG4finder is an effective tool for predicting the potential of PQSs to form eG4s in vivo (Fig. 3B). Accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), and area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) were used to evaluate the performance of eG4finder. The excellent prediction performance of eG4finder is demonstrated (AUROC = 0.94, AUPRC = 0.86, Accuracy = 0.89, Precision = 0.84, Recall = 0.71, F1 score = 0.77). A comparison of eG4finder with other G4 prediction tools using the same test sets revealed that the AUROC, AUPRC, and accuracy of eG4finder were 23–106%, 45–269%, and 18–166% higher than those of the other tools, respectively (Fig. 3C, D, E). Additionally, the prediction scores of other tools cannot reflect PQS recurrence (Additional file 1: Fig. S10A). These results suggest that eG4finder is the most effective tool for predicting whether a PQS will form an eG4 based solely on its DNA sequence.

  Next, we evaluated the prediction performance of eG4finder in other genomes. To generate datasets for an effective evaluation of eG4finder, we performed G4 CUT&Tag in two mouse cell lines (C2C12, 3T3) and two pig cell lines (PK15, ST). These G4 CUT&Tag data are of high quality and reliably capture eG4s (Additional file 1: Fig. S9). The eG4s identified by the G4 CUT&Tag data and the eG4s from four mouse cell lines in EndoQuad were integrated for performance evaluation of eG4finder (Additional file 1: Fig. S7). To extend the applicability demonstration of eG4finder to other genomes, the eG4s from one chicken cell line in EndoQuad were also employed for evaluation. The eG4finder was utilized to predict eG4s in mouse, pig, and chicken using genome-wide PQSs as inputs, and the results demonstrated that eG4finder can predict eG4s well (Fig. 3F). Although the AUPRC and precision are suboptimal, especially in pig and chicken (Additional file 1: Fig. S10B-C), this is likely due to the fact that the set of non-eG4s still encompasses a considerable number of experimentally unobserved eG4s, which is caused by the identification of eG4s in a limited number of cell lines. To simulate low eG4 saturation in pig and chicken, we gradually reduced the number of cell lines used to identify mouse eG4s and reevaluated the performance of eG4finder in mouse. We found that as the number of cell lines decreased, the AUPRC and precision decreased, while recall increased (Additional file 1: Fig. S10D-E), indicating that discrepancies in performance between species are largely due to differences in the eG4 saturation. To avoid the bias caused by the inaccurate number of false-positive samples in the performance evaluation of eG4finder, we compared the prediction results of eG4s and non-eG4s to reflect the prediction performance of eG4finder. The proportion of predicted eG4s and the eG4finder scores in eG4s (medians of eG4finder scores: ~ 4.32 to ~ 6.21) were found to be higher than those in non-eG4s (medians of eG4finder scores: ~ 0.00 to ~ 0.14) (two-sided Fisher’s exact test, P < 0.001; two-sided Wilcoxon rank sum test, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3G, H; Additional file 1: Fig. S10F), indicating that eG4finder is particularly effective in predicting eG4s in other species. In conclusion, eG4finder is a general eG4 prediction tool that excellently predicts the structural potential of PQSs in forming eG4s based on DNA sequences only.


### Evolutionary conservation patterns indicate the structure conservation and functions of eG4s
  In order to evaluate the conservation of each category of eG4s with different evolutionary conservation patterns at the structure level, we employed eG4finder to predict genome-wide eG4s for six mammalian species, including the chimp, macaque, rabbit, mouse, rat, and pig. The predicted eG4s were integrated with those observed experimentally in mouse and pig, resulting in eG4 reference sets of 106,037 (in rat) to 503,603 (in pig) eG4s for each species (Additional file 1: Fig. S11).

  We next evaluated whether each human eG4 is structurally conserved in other mammals (Additional file 1: Fig. S12; Methods). In total, 17% (in rat) to 73% (in chimp) highly conserved eG4s are structurally conserved in each species (Fig. 4A). By contrast, 4% (in mouse and rat) to 71% (in chimp) actively evolving eG4s are structurally conserved in each species, and only 1% (in rabbit, mouse and rat) to 43% (in chimp) primate-specific eG4s are conserved in structure level for each species. In comparison to actively evolving and primate-specific eG4s, highly conserved eG4s exhibited a higher proportion of structurally conserved eG4s in each species (two-sided Fisher’s exact test, P < 0.001), particularly in non-primate species, where the odds ratios exceeded 3 and 24, respectively (Fig. 4B). These results confirm that the evolutionary conservation patterns of eG4s are consistent with their structure conservation level. To eliminate the potential confounding influence of sequence conservation on the evaluation of structure conservation, we solely evaluated whether those eG4s with a base alignment rate of ≥ 90% in a given species were structurally conserved. The proportion of structurally conserved eG4s was still higher in highly conserved eG4s than in actively evolving and primate-specific eG4s, with the odds ratios all exceeding 1 (Fig. 4A, B).   To investigate the relationship between evolutionary conserved patterns and functions of eG4s, we conducted a GREAT analysis [39] on each category of eG4s using all eG4s as background (Additional file 2: Table S1). Highly conserved eG4s are enriched in a large number of developmental processes, including embryonic organ morphogenesis, cell fate determination, stem cell differentiation and various organ development (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, actively evolving eG4s are also enriched in the development of specific organs (Additional file 2: Table S1). However, the biological processes with the highest enrichment of actively evolving eG4s are related to glucose metabolism, such as glucose metabolic process, response to insulin, glucose homeostasis (Fig. 4C). Additionally, primate-specific eG4s are enriched in olfaction-related biological processes, such as detection of chemical stimulus involved in sensory perception of smell, sensory perception of smell (Fig. 4C). This result is consistent with the established fact that the human olfaction pathway adapts to rapid changes in chemicals in the environment mainly through evolving regulatory elements [24]. In conclusion, evolutionary conserved patterns indicate the functionalities of eG4s. The enrichment of highly conserved eG4s in developmental and aging pathways suggests their potential importance in fundamental biological processes.


### Highly conserved eG4s are enriched in promoters and exhibit epigenomic signatures of transcriptional activation
  The genomic location of a DNA element can be used to infer its potential regulatory functions. Consequently, we calculated the proportion of eG4s mapped to each type of genetic element and discovered that the majority of primate-specific eG4s have a genomic location analogous to that of non-eG4s, situated in distal intergenic and intronic regions (Fig. 5A). In comparison to actively evolving and primate-specific eG4s, highly conserved eG4s exhibited a greater enrichment in promoters (two-sided Fisher’s exact test, P < 0.001), particularly in the vicinity of transcription start sites (TSSs) (Additional file 1: Fig. S13).   DNA regulatory elements frequently necessitate a considerable degree of chromatin accessibility in order to bind with chromatin modifiers and transcription factors, thereby directing gene transcription [40, 41]. Meanwhile, accessible chromatin facilitates the formation and maintenance of eG4 structures [17]. In order to further explore the regulatory potential of highly conserved eG4s, we characterized chromatin accessibility near the center of eG4s. Highly conserved eG4s showed more signals by ATAC-seq, and higher DNase accessibility by DNase-seq than actively evolving and primate-specific eG4s (Fig. 5B, C). The ChromHMM states have been demonstrated to accurately reflect the regulatory potential [42]. Our findings indicate that highly conserved eG4s exhibit a higher proportion in active and bivalent promoter states (1_TssA, 2_TssFlnk, 3_TssFlnkU, 4_TssFlnkD, and 14_TssBiv) and a lower proportion in heterochromatic (13_Het) and quiescent (18_Quies) regions compared to actively evolving and primate-specific eG4s (Additional file 1: Fig. S14). Furthermore, both highly conserved and actively evolving eG4s exhibited a higher proportion in putative enhancer states (7_EnhG1, 8_EnhG2, 9_EnhA1, 10_EnhA2, 11_EnhWk, and 15_EnhBiv) in comparison to primate-specific eG4s. Larger distributions in regulatory states and higher chromatin accessibility demonstrate the greater regulatory potential of highly conserved eG4s.

  Histone modification features are responsive to the function of DNA regulatory elements. Regulatory elements with transcriptionally activated functions (promoters and enhancers) are typically marked by H3K4me3, H3K4me2, H3K4me1, H3K27ac, H3K9ac, and H3K79me2 [43–47]. Transcriptionally activated gene bodies are marked by H3K36me3 and H4K20me1 [48, 49]. In contrast, regulatory elements with transcriptional repression (silencers) are generally marked by H3K27me3 and H3K9me3 [50–52]. To explore the differences in regulatory functions of eG4s with different patterns of evolutionary conservation, we evaluated ten histone modifications around eG4s (Fig. 5D). Highly conserved eG4s showed more signals by ChIP-seq for histone modifications associated with transcriptional activation, including H3K4me3, H3K4me2, H3K4me1, H3K27ac, H3K9ac, and H3K79me2, than actively evolving and primate-specific eG4s. Meanwhile, more signals by ChIP-seq for H3K36me3 and H4K20me1 were observed near highly conserved and actively evolving eG4s than primate-specific eG4s. In contrast, primate-specific eG4s showed more signals by ChIP-seq for H3K9me3 associated with transcriptional repression than actively evolving and highly conserved eG4s. Notably, more H3K27me3 signals associated with transcriptional repression were enriched at highly conserved and actively evolving eG4s than at primate-specific eG4s. H3K27me3 together with H3K4me3 marks the bivalent promoters that safeguard the temporal and spatial specific expression of developmentally relevant genes [53, 54]. Highly conserved and actively evolving eG4s are enriched for both H3K27me3 and H3K4me3 signaling, again suggesting their role in development. In conclusion, highly conserved eG4s exhibit the most elevated histone modification features indicative of transcriptional activation.

  DNA methylation levels are often negatively correlated with the activity of regulatory elements [55]. To investigate the regulatory activity of eG4s with different evolutionary conservation patterns, we examined the DNA methylation of each eG4 in 37 consolidated epigenomes. More highly conserved eG4s were maintained in a hypomethylated state (bottom of the heatmap) (Fig. 5E). There were slightly fewer actively evolving eG4s in the hypomethylated state than highly conserved eG4s, whereas primate-specific eG4s were more likely to be in the hypermethylated state (upper right corner of the heatmap). Thus, highly conserved eG4s are more likely to be in the active state than actively evolving and primate-specific eG4s. In addition, eG4s bind DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) and inhibit its methylation activity, which in turn maintains hypomethylation in nearby regions [56, 57]. Therefore, we examined DNA methylation in 200 bp windows within a total of 4 kb regions upstream and downstream of eG4s, and found that the DNA methylation near highly conserved eG4s was lower than that near actively evolving and primate-specific eG4s in each window (two-sided Wilcoxon rank sum test, P < 0.001) (Fig. 5F), indicating that highly conserved eG4s provide greater protection against epigenetic silencing.

  Despite the fact that highly conserved eG4s are enriched in promoters, higher accessibility, more histone modifications of transcriptional activation, and lower DNA methylation levels at highly conserved eG4s were observed in both promoters and non-promoter regions (Additional file 1: Fig. S15), suggesting that their heightened regulatory functions can be explained independently of their preferential localization to promoters. In conclusion, characterization of epigenomic signatures suggests that highly conserved eG4s are transcriptional activation elements with high regulatory activity and enhanced regulatory potential.


### TF-binding proficiency elucidates enhanced regulatory potential of highly conserved eG4s
  eG4s provide non-classical binding sites for a multitude of TFs based on their secondary structure, serving as high-affinity TF binding targets [14]. This represents a well-established mechanism through which eG4s are involved in transcriptional regulation. To further elucidate the reasons for the enhanced regulatory potential of highly conserved eG4s, an evaluation of the binding of 1355 distinct TFs to eG4s was conducted. The diversity of TFs bound to highly conserved eG4s was found to be higher than those bound to actively evolving and primate-specific eG4s (two-sided Wilcoxon rank sum test, P < 0.001) (Fig. 6A), indicating that highly conserved eG4s act as TF hubs with higher affinities. The higher TF diversity at highly conserved eG4s was found in both promoters and non-promoter regions (Additional file 1: Fig. S16A). A multiple linear regression model was also employed to estimate the relationship between TF diversity of eG4s and a set of independent variables, including N1 and N2, which reflect the evolutionary conservation pattern of eG4s, and the distance from eG4s to TSSs (Additional file 2: Table S3). The position of each point is based on the proportion of eG4s with binding sites of the given TF in each eG4 category. C Enrichment analysis of TFs for each category of eG4s. The TFs enriched for each category of eG4s are shown. D Distribution of binding sites of fifteen chromatin modifiers in each category of eG4s and non-eG4s

  To further explore the effects of evolutionary conservation patterns on TF binding patterns, we calculated the proportion of eG4s binding to each TF in each eG4 category. Our findings revealed that highly conserved and actively evolving eG4s exhibited strikingly similar TF binding patterns (R-squared = 0.98) (Fig. 6B). However, the overwhelming majority of TFs (~ 93.2%) demonstrated a higher affinity for highly conserved eG4s. In contrast, the similarity in TF binding patterns between highly conserved and primate-specific eG4s was slightly lower (R-squared = 0.81), indicating that different patterns of evolutionary conservation exert disparate effects on the TF binding patterns of eG4s. Furthermore, we analyzed the enrichment of TFs for each category of eG4s using all eG4s as background. We found that highly conserved eG4s were enriched for the majority of TFs (~ 83.5%) (Fig. 6C), while actively evolving eG4s and primate-specific eG4s were enriched for only 18 and 31 TFs, respectively, with only 4 and 23 TFs being specifically enriched (Additional file 1: Fig. S16D). These results again highlight the TF-binding proficiency of highly conserved eG4s. The higher TF-binding affinities at highly conserved eG4s were observed in both promoters and non-promoter regions (Additional file 1: Fig. S16B-D), indicating that their TF-binding proficiency can be explained independently of their preferential localization to promoters.

  The enrichment of specific TFs facilitates the formation of specific epigenomic signatures [58]. The KMT2A, KMT2B, KMT2C, RBBP5, WDR5, ASH2L, CXXC1, and HCFC1 components of the H3K4 methyltransferase Complex of Proteins Associated with Set1 (COMPASS) [59] were found to be enriched at highly conserved eG4s (Fig. 6D). Additionally, the H3K9/H3K27 acetyltransferase CBP/p300 [60] and the H3K79 methyltransferase DOT1L [61] were found to be highly enriched. Four histone demethylases targeting H3K9, namely KDM4A, KDM4B, KDM4C, and PHF8 [62–65], were also similarly enriched. In conclusion, the high affinity of highly conserved eG4s for specific TFs gives rise to an elevated transcription activation element-like epigenomic signature.


### Highly conserved eG4s most strongly enhance transcription
  To gain further insight into the effects of the three categories of eG4s on gene expression, a comparative analysis was conducted of the expression profiles of genes with promoters containing highly conserved eG4s, actively evolving eG4s, and primate-specific eG4s in six cell lines (Additional file 2: Table S2). Additionally, a control group of genes with eG4-depleted promoters was also included. To exclude the potential influence of chromatin accessibility on gene expression, subsequent analyses were conducted on genes with open chromatin regions at their promoters only. We found that genes with promoters containing highly conserved eG4s demonstrated higher expression proportions (Fig. 7A, B) and expression levels (Fig. 7C) in comparison to genes with promoters containing actively evolving eG4s or primate-specific eG4s.   Despite the observed differences in the number of eG4s among the three groups of eG4-containing genes (Additional file 1: Fig. S17A), no discernible correlation was identified between gene expression levels and the number of eG4s within a gene (Additional file 1: Fig. S17B). When only genes with promoters containing eG4s from a single evolutionary category are considered, it is observed that genes with promoters containing highly conserved eG4s exhibit a further enhancement in the advantage of expression activity (Additional file 1: Fig. S18).

  Furthermore, an evaluation was conducted to ascertain the impact of each category of eG4s on enhancer-related expression regulation. We found that enhancers containing highly conserved eG4s exhibited higher target gene expression proportions and levels in comparison to enhancers containing actively evolving eG4s or primate-specific eG4s (Additional file 1: Fig. S19). These results suggest that highly conserved eG4s may also promote gene expression more strongly in other regulatory regions outside the promoters. In conclusion, highly conserved eG4s are the most powerful transcriptional activation elements in the total eG4 collection.


### Variants in highly conserved eG4s most preferentially affect complex traits
  Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have revolutionised the methods for studying human complex traits, and they have been widely used to identify the genetic variants associated with a wide range of diseases and traits [66–69]. To systematically investigate the association between eG4s and complex traits, we evaluated the enrichment of trait-associated single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) from the GWAS Catalog in each category of eG4s and non-eG4s [70]. We observed that the enrichment and density of GWAS SNPs in highly conserved eG4s was higher than that in actively evolving eG4s and primate-specific eG4s (Fig. 8A, B; Additional file 1: Fig. S20). In contrast, the enrichment and density of human common SNPs in highly conserved eG4s was lower than that in actively evolving eG4s and primate-specific eG4s (Fig. 8A, C; Additional file 1: Fig. S20). In light of the slight disparities in GC-content observed among three categories of eG4s (Additional file 1: Fig. S21A), along with the SNP nucleotide type preferences, a GC-content correction was implemented to evaluate the SNP enrichment. Highly conserved eG4s still exhibited higher enrichment of GWAS SNPs and depletion of common SNPs (Additional file 1: Fig. S21B). Additionally, the derived allele frequency (DAF) of common SNPs found in highly conserved eG4s were lower than those found in actively evolving eG4s and primate-specific eG4s (two-sided unpaired T test, P < 0.001) (Fig. 8D). The higher enrichment of GWAS SNPs, lower enrichment of common SNPs, and lower SNP DAF in highly conserved eG4s were observed in both promoters and non-promoter regions (Additional file 1: Fig. S22).   We further extended our scope to expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL), a tool to efficiently elucidate the mechanisms by which GWAS SNPs affect corresponding traits at the gene expression level [71]. The enrichment of fine-mapped eQTLs from GTEx was evaluated in each category of eG4s and non-eG4s. It was observed that the enrichment and density of fine-mapped eQTLs in highly conserved eG4s was slightly lower than that in actively evolving eG4s (Fig. 8E; Additional file 1: Fig. S23). Subsequent to the categorisation of human protein-coding genes according to phenotypic severity [72], including lethal, lethal & disease, disease (non-lethal), and other, with decreasing phenotypic severity, it was observed that eQTLs affecting the expression of lethal genes exhibited greater enrichment in highly conserved eG4s than in actively evolving eG4s and primate-specific eG4s (Fig. 8E). An illustrative example is variant rs402072, which is located in a highly conserved eG4 on the reverse strand in the vicinity of the TSS of the lethal gene PRKD2 (Additional file 1: Fig. S24A). The variant rs402072 (Alleles: A/G in reverse strand) is fine-mapped eQTL, which is associated with PRKD2 gene expression (Additional file 1: Fig. S24B) and was determined to be associated with type 1 diabetes by GWAS (Risk allele: A) [73]. Moreover, all human genes were ranked according to their level of conservation, and it was observed that eQTLs affecting the expression of highly conserved genes exhibited greater enrichment in highly conserved eG4s (Fig. 8E). Conversely, eQTLs affecting the expression of less conserved genes demonstrated greater enrichment in actively evolving eG4s and primate-specific eG4s. Together, these results suggest that highly conserved eG4s may more generally regulate high phenotypic severity genes and highly conserved genes with pivotal functions, which explains the closer association of highly conserved eG4s with complex human traits.


## Discussion

### Establishment of reference annotation for human eG4s promote exploration of their evolution
  As emerging DNA regulatory elements, the functions and mechanisms of eG4s in the regulation of gene expression are being progressively elucidated and refined [74, 75]. These advancements are largely attributed to the widespread application of G4 antibody-based high-throughput approaches, including G4 ChIP-seq and G4 CUT&Tag. Notably, G4 CUT&Tag demonstrates a superior signal-to-noise ratio as well as enhanced resolution and sensitivity compared to G4 ChIP-seq for in vivo eG4 identification [12]. G4 ChIP-seq/CUT&Tag has generated eG4 profiles for more than forty human cell lines. However, the lack of integrated analysis of large-scale data has resulted in a dearth of reference annotations for human eG4s, which has significantly constrained the investigation of eG4 evolution. In a previous study, we integrated thousands of publicly available G4 ChIP-seq/CUT&Tag samples to classify genome-wide PQSs as eG4s or non-eG4s based on their ability to form G4s in vivo. These results led to the establishment of the comprehensive eG4 database, EndoQuad [18]. In this study, we performed G4 CUT&Tag in seven human cell lines to validate and improve the saturation of the human eG4 reference set in EndoQuad. The establishment of comprehensive human eG4 reference annotation has enabled a detailed exploration of eG4 evolution. Using a reference-free 241 mammalian genome alignment from the Zoonomia Consortium, we quantified the evolutionary conservation of each human eG4 in mammalian species. Our results reveal the existence of three categories of human eG4s with different evolutionary conservation patterns, including highly conserved, actively evolving, and primate-specific patterns.


### Feature and interpretability of eG4finder
  In order to obtain an eG4 reference set for non-human mammals and thus validate the consistency between the evolutionarily conserved patterns of human eG4 and their level of structural conservation, we developed the eG4 prediction tool, eG4finder. The eG4finder is distinct from existing G4 prediction tools. In comparison to in vitro G4 prediction tools such as Quadron, G4detector, and PENGUINN [29–31], eG4finder has been trained using datasets generated based on G4 ChIP-seq/CUT&Tag from EndoQuad, as opposed to datasets generated based on G4-seq. In comparison to eG4 prediction tools such as DeepG4 and G4Beacon [32, 33], eG4finder places greater emphasis on the general prediction of eG4s formed in at least one cell type based on DNA sequences alone, as opposed to the prediction of eG4s in a specific cell type. Specifically, eG4finder does not merely classify, but rather outputs eG4 recurrence (Fig. 3B), which is defined as the number of cell types in which a given eG4 has been detected in EndoQuad and reflects the confidence level of eG4s [18].

  The DNABERT-2 model utilized in eG4finder is a pre-trained foundational model that is trained on a large-scale corpus comprising human and other 134 species genomes [38]. The diverse and comprehensive pre-training data enables the DNABERT-2 to capture a broad spectrum of genomic patterns and enhances its generalization capability. By fine-tuning the DNABERT-2 on the comprehensive human eG4 reference set from EndoQuad, eG4finder benefits from the advantages of the foundation model, resulting in enhanced eG4 prediction performance. The superior performance of eG4finder, trained exclusively on DNA sequences, implies that the sequences of PQSs and their flanking regions are key factors in determining whether PQSs form eG4s. Furthermore, eG4 formation is also subject to regulation by factors such as chromatin accessibility, epigenetic modifications that affect accessibility, G4 stabilizing proteins, and G4 helicases [10, 13, 17, 76, 77]. To assess the interpretability of our model, we extracted all attention scores for each token across all human samples and analyzed the correlation between the attention scores and sample recurrence. We found that the attention of some G-rich tokens that promote G4 stability is strongly positively correlated with sample recurrence (Additional file 1: Fig. S25A), suggesting that the high attention of these G-rich tokens contributes to the prediction of high recurrence. Furthermore, the strongest positive correlation was found between sample recurrence and the attention of a token containing a CCAAT-box (Additional file 1: Fig. S25B), which is one of the most common promoter elements [78], indicating that our model may have learned that eG4s are enriched in promoter regions. These analyses ensure the interpretability of eG4finder. In conclusion, eG4finder provides an inexpensive, simple and reliable way to map eG4s in a genomic context and will greatly contribute to the evolutionary and functional exploration of eG4s in the future.


### Mammalian constraint of human eG4s
  The mammalian constraint of eG4s effectively promises their regulatory functions. In addition to enrichment in various developmental processes, highly conserved eG4s are enriched in a number of aging-related processes [79–82], including circadian regulation of gene expression, regulation of apoptotic signaling pathway, regulation of cell cycle, cellular senescence, aging, and telomere maintenance via telomerase (Fig. 4C). The potential functions of highly conserved eG4s related to development and aging reflect their functional importance. Conversely, primate-specific eG4s, akin to primate-specific genes, are enriched in rapidly evolving pathways. However, primate-specific eG4s are primarily enriched in the olfaction pathway, whereas primate-specific genes are associated with other rapidly evolving processes, including spermatogenesis, immune response, and brain development [83]. This discrepancy is consistent with previous observations that the olfaction pathway adapts mainly by evolving regulatory elements rather than coding sequences [24].

  Although average epigenomic signals and consolidated TFBSs may obscure potential cell type-specific nuances in the epigenomic and TF-binding characteristics of each eG4 category, the higher accessibility, lower DNA methylation levels, higher transcription activation element-like histone modification signals, and higher affinity for TFs across multiple consolidated epigenomes at highly conserved eG4s indicate their generally important regulatory functions.

  Notably, highly conserved eG4s were more enriched for disease- and trait-associated variants, as well as variants affecting the expression of high phenotypic severity genes and highly conserved genes. The variant rs402072, which is situated within a highly conserved eG4 and has been demonstrated to influence the expression of the lethal gene PRKD2 and the risk of diabetes, serves as a pertinent example (Additional file 1: Fig. S24). It has been demonstrated that the knockdown of PRKD2 results in hyperinsulinemia and insulin resistance [84]. Therefore, it is plausible that rs402072 exerts an influence on PRKD2 expression by affecting the structural stability of eG4 (Additional file 2: Table S4), thereby modulating the risk of diabetes through insulin secretion. Consequently, highly conserved eG4s that overlap with genetic variation in the human population are associated with complex human traits.


## Conclusions
  In conclusion, we mapped the evolutionary profiles of human eG4s in mammalian species and identified highly conserved eG4s under mammalian constraint. We also developed an eG4 prediction tool, eG4finder, which was used to verify the evolutionary conservation patterns of eG4s at the structure level. Our findings highlight the important regulatory functions of highly conserved eG4s and their close association with complex human traits. These results broaden our current understanding of the evolution and functions of eG4s.


## Methods

### G4 CUT&Tag experiment
  G4 Cleavage Under Targets and Tagmentation (CUT&Tag) was performed as previously described [85] with some modifications. Specifically, half million cells were washed twice with 1.5 mL wash buffer (20 mM HEPES at pH 7.5, 150 mM KCl, 0.5 mM spermidine and 1 protease inhibitors) by gentle pipetting. 10 μL concanavalin A coated magnetic beads (Smart-Lifesciences) were activated, and added to these cells with a 10 min’ incubation at room temperature. The supernatant was removed and bead-bound cells were resuspended in 100 μL dig-wash buffer (20 mM HEPES at pH 7.5, 150 mM KCl, 0.5 mM spermidine, 1 protease inhibitors, 0.05% digitonin and 0.01% NP-40) containing 2 mM EDTA and a 1:10 dilution of the 0.1 mg/mL BG4 primary antibody. For IgG controls, mouse IgG was used as a primary antibody. Primary antibody incubation was performed on a rotating platform overnight at 4 °C and then the liquid was removed. The Anti-Flag M2 antibody (Sigma-Aldrich) was diluted to 1:100 in 100 μL of dig-wash buffer with 2 mM EDTA and incubated with the cells at room temperature for 30 min followed by removing the liquid. The rabbit anti-mouse IgG antibody was diluted 100 folds and incubated with the cells for another 1 h and washed with dig-wash buffer three times. A 1:200 dilution of pA-Tn5 adapter complex (~ 0.2 μM) was prepared in dig-300 buffer (0.05% digitonin, 20 mM HEPES at pH 7.5, 300 mM KCl, 0.5 mM spermidine, 1 protease inhibitors and 0.01% NP-40). 100 μL pA-Tn5 adapter complex was added to the cells with gentle vertexing followed by one-hour incubation at room temperature. Cells were washed three times in 200 μL dig-300 buffer to remove unbound pA-Tn5 proteins. Next, bead-bound cells were resuspended in 40 μL tagmentation buffer (10 mM TAPS-NaOH at pH 8.5, 10 mM MgCl 2 and 7.5% DMF) and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. To stop tagmentation reaction, 1.5 μL 0.5 M EDTA, 0.5 μL 10% SDS and 0.5 μL 20 mg/mL proteinase K were added and incubated at 55 °C for 1 h and then for 15 min at 75℃. The DNA was extracted with Sera-Mag carboxylate-modified magnetic beads (GE Healthcare) for library preparation. 21 μL DNA was mixed with a universal i5 and a uniquely barcoded i7 primer, and amplified with NEB Q5 high-fidelity 2 master mix. The libraries were purified with 1.1 volume of Sera-Mag carboxylate-modified magnetic beads, and subjected to LabChip DNA analysis and Illumina sequencing. BG4 and pA-Tn5 were produced based on our previous method [12].


### ATAC-seq experiment
  The assay for transposase-accessible chromatin by sequencing (ATAC-seq) was performed as previously described [86] with some modifications. Briefly, 5 × 104 cells were washed with cold PBS and centrifuged at 600 g for 5 min. The cells were resuspended with 50μL cold lysis buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl at pH 7.5, 10 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 0.1% NP-40, 0.1% Tween-20 and 0.02% digitonin) and incubated on ice for 2 min. The cells were furthered added with 200 μL cold resuspension buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl at pH 7.5, 10 mM NaCl and 3 mM MgCl2) and centrifuged at 500 g for 5 min. The pellet was resuspended with 12.5 μL resuspension buffer. 10 μL 5 × tagmentation buffer (50 mM TAPS-NaOH at pH 8.5 and 25 mM MgCl2), 16.5 μL PBS, 0.5 μL 10% Tween-20, 0.5 μL 1% digitonin, 0.75 μL 37.5 μM pA-Tn5 transposome and 9.5 μL nuclease-free H2O were mixed with 12.5 μL cell suspension. The transposition reaction was performed at 37 °C for 30 min with mixing at 1,000 rpm. The reaction was stopped by adding 1.5μL 0.5 M EDTA and 0.5 μL 10% SDS, and digested with 1 μL 20 mg/mL proteinase K (Roche) at 65 °C for 1 h. The DNA was further purified with 1 × Sera-Mag carboxylate-modified magnetic beads and eluted with 20 μL H2O with 0.1% Tween-20. The purified transposed DNA was amplified with NEBNext high-fidelity 2 × PCR master mix, Ad1_noMX primer and Ad2.indexing primers. The DNA libraries were size-selected with Sera-Mag carboxylate-modified magnetic beads and sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 platform.


### High-throughput data processing
  Raw FASTQ files were quality controlled using FastQC (v0.11.9) and Trim Galore (v0.6.7). We applied bowtie2 (v2.3.5.1) [87] for read alignment of G4 CUT&Tag and ATAC-seq samples. The genome and annotation files of human (GRCh38), mouse (GRCm38), and pig (Sscrofa11) were downloaded from ENSEMBL. We only retained reads with the mapping scores (MAPQ) ≥ 20 by SAMtools view (v1.3.1, options: -Sb -q 20) [88]. Duplicates were marked and removed using Picard MarkDuplicates (v2.25.7) for G4 CUT&Tag and ATAC-seq data, and peaks were called for each sample using MACS2 (v2.2.7.1) subcommand callpeak [89]. For data with biological replicates, peak regions were considered as positive if they were confirmed in at least two replicates. Genome-wide PQS datasets were downloaded from the EndoQuad [18]. These PQS datasets were mapped to the genome used in this study using liftOver [90]. We intersected all the PQSs with G4 peaks by Bedtools intersect (v2.25.0) [91], and the PQS that was ≥ 1 base pair overlapped with G4 peaks was defined as eG4 in each cell lines. Within a species, PQSs that form in vivo G4s in at least one cell type were defined as eG4s, otherwise classified as non-eG4s.


### Measurement of evolutionary conservation of human eG4s
  We downloaded the HAL file of the 241-way multiple-genome alignment from the Zoonomia consortium [92]. Using halLiftover from the Cactus (v2.7.0) [93], we calculated the base alignment rates (percentage of nucleotides that could be aligned to a given genome) of each human eG4 in 240 non-human genomes. To avoid any bias resulting from uneven coverage of sex chromosomes, eG4s that overlapped with the Y chromosome and the pseudoautosomal regions of the X chromosome were excluded from the analysis, as some mammalian genomes were constructed based on data from only a single sex. To identify categories of eG4s with distinct patterns of evolutionary conservation, we computed the number of genomes with base alignment rates of ≥ 90% (N1) and the number of genomes with base alignment rates of ≤ 10% (N2) for each eG4. We defined three categories of eG4s based on the previous work [24]: Category 1 (C1): highly conserved eG4s with N1 ≥ 120 and N2 ≤ 25; Category 2 (C2): actively evolving eG4s with 20 ≤ N1 ≤ 50 and N2 ≤ 120; and Category 3 (C3): primate-specific eG4s with N1 ≤ 50 and N2 ≥ 180. We visualized the human eG4s by their base alignment rates in 240 non-human genomes after dimensionality reduction using UMAP with the following parameters: n_neighbors = 30, min_dist = 0.1, seed = 1. Genome-wide nucleotide resolution PhastCons scores were downloaded from the UCSC Genome Browser. Average PhastCons scores were calculated using bigWigAverageOverBed.


### TE analyses
  The RepeatMasker elements were downloaded from the UCSC Genome Browser. All TE families were extracted from RepeatMasker annotations. TE fold enrichments over random (2000 randomizations) were evaluated in each category of eG4s by using the Genomic Association Tester (GAT, v1.3.4). We defined five groups of primate-specific eG4s: human-specific group: eG4s with base alignment rates of < 50% in all nonhuman primates, colugo, and tree shrews; group conserved only in hominoidea: eG4s with base alignment rates of ≥ 50% in all hominoidea and base alignment rates of < 50% in the remaining primates, colugo, and tree shrews; group conserved only in catarrhini: eG4s with base alignment rates of ≥ 50% in all catarrhini and base alignment rates of < 50% in the remaining primates, colugo, and tree shrews; group conserved only in haplorhini: eG4s with base alignment rates of ≥ 50% in all haplorhini and base alignment rates of < 50% in the remaining primates, colugo, and tree shrews; group conserved only in primates: eG4s with base alignment rates of ≥ 50% in all primates and base alignment rates of < 50% in colugo and tree shrews.


### Model and performance analyses of eG4finder

To output the recurrence value, we aggregate all token embeddings of a sequence into an embedding and put it into a 3-layer Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP).

  Since the DNABERT-2 model has a large scale of the parameter, all parameter fine-tuning will bring a large burden of computational resources. Therefore, during training, we employ the AdaLoRA (Adaptive Low-Rank Adaptation) [95] to update the pre-trained weight in a parameter-efficient way. Different from the DNABERT-2’s efficient parameter training method, LoRA, which assigns same rank for all update weights, AdaLoRA can dynamically allocate ranks by importance scoring. This can maximize gains under the same parameter budget. Moreover, the MLP in our method is also trained with the weights in AdaLoRA for applying to our predicting task.

  To evaluate the prediction performance of eG4finder in human genome, we split the human PQS dataset into train, validation and test sets with the rate of 6:1:3, and the results are the average of three random data splits. We downloaded the trained G4 prediction tools from GitHub, including Quadron, G4detector, PENGUINN, and DeepG4 [29–32]. These were then used directly for further comparative analysis. Subsequently, the eG4finder, which had been trained using the complete human PQS dataset, was employed to predict genome-wide eG4s in non-human genomes. This allowed for an evaluation of the performance of eG4finder in non-human genomes.


### Structure conservation and function analyses
  In six mammalian species, we obtained eG4 reference sets for these species by integrating eG4s predicted by eG4finder and experimentally observed eG4s. For human eG4s, we first used liftOver to project their genomic coordinates to these species based on chain files generated by the UCSC Genome Browser. We then applied Bedtools intersect to identify the structurally conserved eG4s, defined as human eG4s that overlap with eG4s from other species. Notably, human eG4s that overlap with non-eG4s or regions other than eG4s in other species are not structurally conserved eG4s. Finally, we calculated the proportions of structurally conserved eG4s for each category of human eG4s in each species. The GREAT approach has been demonstrated to improve the functional enrichment analysis of regulatory regions [39]. In order to evaluate the functions of each category of eG4s, we utilised rGREAT (v2.5.7) [96] to perform GO enrichment analysis on each eG4 category using all human eG4s as the background. Specifically, each gene is assigned a regulatory domain based on the default parameters of GREAT. The 5 kb upstream and 1 kb downstream of the TSS are designated as the basal regulatory domain. This domain is further extended up to the basal regulatory domains of the nearest upstream and downstream genes within 1 Mb. The association of eG4s with genes is facilitated through regulatory domains, and a GO enrichment analysis is conducted based on hypergeometric tests. Additionally, the regulatory domain for each GO term is delineated by the consolidation of the regulatory domains of the corresponding genes. Subsequently, eG4s are associated with GO terms via regulatory domains and undergo GO enrichment analysis based on binomial tests. GO terms with adjusted p-values less than 0.05 in both hypergeometric and binomial tests are considered to be significantly enriched.


### Epigenome and transcription factor analyses
  We obtained human epigenomic datasets for consolidated epigenomes from the NIH Roadmap Epigenomics Mapping Consortium (http://egg2.wustl.edu/roadmap/) [97]: (1) the narrow peaks of DHS (53 consolidated epigenomes), H3K27ac (98 consolidated epigenomes), H3K9ac (62 consolidated epigenomes), H3K4me2 (24 consolidated epigenomes), H3K4me3 (127 consolidated epigenomes); (2) the broad peaks of H3K4me1 (127 consolidated epigenomes), H3K79me2 (21 consolidated epigenomes), H3K36me3 (127 consolidated epigenomes), H4K20me1 (19 consolidated epigenomes), H3K9me3 (127 consolidated epigenomes), H3K27me3 (127 consolidated epigenomes); (3) fractional methylation data from WGBS (37 consolidated epigenomes); (4) ChromHMM states (98 consolidated epigenomes). In addition, we obtained ATAC-seq signals for 33 sample groups from the EpiMap [98]. We calculated the average ATAC-seq signals and average peak count frequency for DHS, and ten histone modifications in a total of 6 kb regions upstream and downstream of the eG4 center by deepTools (v3.5.5) [99]. The fractional methylation of eG4s and regions around the eG4 center was calculated by bigWigAverageOverBed. We utilised ChIPseeker [100] to analyse the genomic distribution of eG4. The GTRD database has generated non-redundant TFBSs for each TF by merging TFBSs identified by different peak callers across multiple ChIP-seq experiments for the same TF [101]. Genome-wide TFBSs for 1355 TFs were downloaded from GTRD. TF diversity is defined as the number of distinct TFs that an eG4 is capable of binding to. The TFBS count frequency for fifteen TFs was calculated around the eG4 center by deepTools.


### Expression analyses
  We obtained RNA-seq and ATAC-seq data for six cell lines (Additional file 2: Table S2). For the RNA-seq data, we directly obtained the transcripts per kilobase million (TPM) matrix generated by NCBI. For the ATAC-seq data, we downloaded the peak data and used Bedtools intersect to obtain consistent peaks. Promoter was defined as the ± 2000 bp region from the TSS of a gene. Expression analyses were conducted on genes with open chromatin regions at their promoters only. The genes with promoters containing each category of eG4s were obtained using Bedtools intersect. To exclude the effect of PQSs on gene expression, eG4-depleted promoters were divided into two groups based on whether they contained non-eG4s (PQSs that do not form eG4s). In each cell line, expressed genes were defined as genes with an average TPM of ≥ 1. We obtained DNase-seq and H3K27ac ChIP-seq data for three cell lines (Additional file 2: Table S2). The prediction of enhancers and their target genes was conducted in three cell lines by using the ABC model (v1.1.2) [102].


### Trait-associated variant analyses
  Trait-associated SNP data were downloaded from the GWAS Catalog [70]. Human common (minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 0.01) SNP data were downloaded from the dbSNP database [103]. Unique SNPs were used for further analysis. We obtained the DAF of SNPs from phase 3 of the 1000 Genomes Project [104]. Fine-mapped eQTL data in normal tissues were downloaded from GTEx (v10). Bigwig file of 241-mammal PhyloP scores were downloaded from the UCSC Genome Browser. Average PhyloP scores for each gene were calculated using bigWigAverageOverBed. Variant fold enrichments over random (2000 randomizations) were evaluated in each category of eG4s and non-eG4s by using the Genomic Association Tester (GAT, v1.3.4). To avoid the potential influence of GC-content, the genome was divided into 50 bp bins using Bedtools makewindows. The GC-content of each bin was then calculated using Bedtools nuc. The randomization step in GAT was replaced by Bedtools shuffle to ensure that the random regions had the same GC-content distribution as the input regions.
