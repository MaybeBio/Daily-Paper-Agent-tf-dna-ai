# INAB: identify nucleic acid binding domain via cross-modal protein language models and multiscale computation


## Abstract
  Protein–nucleic acid interactions play a crucial role in biological processes, including gene regulation and editing. Accurately identifying nucleic acid-binding domains in proteins is essential to unravel these interactions, yet traditional experimental methods like X-ray crystallography remain costly and time-intensive. Computational approaches have thus emerged as indispensable tools to complement wet-lab techniques. Here, we introduce a framework for nucleic acid-binding domain prediction by integrating cross-modal protein language models with a multiscale computational architecture. The proposed method leverages a structurally annotated benchmark dataset, which quantifies binding likelihood through hierarchical, proximity-based labels derived from experimental complexes. Evaluations demonstrate that the approach achieves state-of-the-art performance, providing a new insight into the design of multimodal learning systems in protein-nucleic acid interaction analysis and an open resource to accelerate discoveries in functional genomics and drug design.


## Introduction
  Protein–nucleic acid interactions underpin essential biological processes, including transcriptional regulation, DNA repair, and stress responses [1]. Accurately identifying nucleic acid-binding domains in proteins is critical for understanding these mechanisms, with applications in functional genomics and drug discovery. While experimental techniques like X-ray crystallography and cryo-EM provide high-resolution binding domains, their reliance on costly and time-intensive protocols has driven demand for efficient computational alternatives [2, 3].

  Protein representation is a key factor in computational methods. In this regard, existing computational methods can be categorized into two main approaches: empirical feature-based methods and representation learning-based methods. Early tools, such as DRNAPred [4] (utilizing physicochemical properties) and NucBind [5] (employing evolutionary profiles), were powered by traditional machine learning models with manually curated features. Although interpretable, these handcrafted features often struggle to model complex interaction patterns, limiting their generalizability across diverse protein families.

  Representation learning has emerged as a transformative approach, enabling automated extraction of hierarchical features—from global amino acid composition to local structural motifs—directly from raw protein sequences or structures. For instance, DeepSite [6] maps protein atoms into 3D voxels and employs 3D convolutional neural networks to extract features from the neighborhood of the target residue. GraphBind [7] employs a hierarchical graph neural network to infer structural patterns. GraphSite [8] uses a graph transformer to learn the spatial features from AlphaFold2 [9] predicted protein structures. GeoBind [10] takes the entire point cloud of protein surface as input and learns a high-level representation based on the aggregation of its neighbors in local reference frames. Unlike empirical methods, these models capture non-linear relationships and latent patterns that evade manual annotation, improving adaptability and accuracy.

  Recently, the advent of protein language models (PLMs) has revolutionized feature extraction by leveraging unsupervised pre-training on massive sequence or structure corpora. Models like ESM-2 [11] leverage unsupervised pre-training on vast sequence datasets to learning protein sequence representation. GearNet [12] integrates geometric graph neural networks with contrastive learning to learn protein representation at the residue level. While SaProt [13] incorporates 3D structural tokens into language modeling to advance protein structure representation learning. These PLMs have been successfully applied to various downstream tasks of proteins. In this regard, methods such as DeepProSite [14] and CrossBind [15] harness PLMs to generate structural-semantic embeddings to identify nucleic acid binding residues in protein, and hybrid frameworks like EquiPNAS [16] and GPSFun [17] combine PLMs learned representations with domain-specific knowledge. Despite their success, most methods remain limited to single-modality PLM, leaving the potential of multimodal fusion unexplored.

  Furthermore, current approaches predominantly frame nucleic acid-binding domain identification as a residue-level binary classification task (binding vs. non-binding). However, this oversimplification overlooks the nuanced and continuous nature of residue contributions to interactions. A regression-based analysis, which quantifies the likelihood of binding, may better reflect biological reality.

  To fill these gaps, we developed a hierarchically annotated dataset utilizing a structural proximity-aware labeling system that quantifies binding likelihood based on spatial arrangement in protein–nucleic acid complexes. Residues are annotated using a continuous score derived from their minimum heavy-atom distance to nucleic acid ligands. We predict nucleic acid binding domains using regression rather than binary classification and investigate the effect of systematically integrating semantic features derived from cross-modal PLMs with biophysically interpretable empirical features. Finally, we propose INAB (Identifying Nucleic Acid Binding domain), a multiscale framework that synergizes a state space model (SSM) with geometric deep learning for identifying nucleic acid binding domains in proteins (Fig. 1A). Experiments demonstrate that regression using hierarchical labels outperforms binary classification in accuracy and biological relevance. Cross-modal PLMs integration further enhances prediction robustness, and INAB achieves state-of-the-art performance, offering a versatile tool for functional domain discovery. This work advances computational strategies for analyzing protein–nucleic acid interactions while providing insights into the design of multimodal learning systems.


## Materials and method

### Dataset construction and annotation
  We curated a comprehensive dataset of protein–nucleic acid interactions by collecting 10 869 experimentally resolved complexes from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB) (up to December 2023). Individual protein chains were extracted from these complexes, resulting in 54 523 single-chain protein entries. Sequence redundancy was reduced using CD-HIT [18] with a 90% identity threshold, followed by removal of truncated chains (less than 30 residues) and excessively long sequences (more than 3000 residues). This rigorous filtering yielded a non-redundant dataset of 6158 high-quality protein chains.

  To ensure fair data partitioning and prevent homology bias, we performed structural clustering by using Foldseek [19] with a coverage threshold of 0.3. We divided the training and testing sets based on clusters to ensure that the proteins in the testing set are not in the same cluster as any proteins in the training set. For an objective evaluation, chains sharing structural similarity with the test set of GraphBind’s benchmark dataset were explicitly excluded from our training set, while those overlapping with GraphBind’s training set were removed from our test set. This strict separation protocol eliminated potential data leakage and ensured unbiased performance evaluation.

  Conventional binary annotation (binding/non-binding) fails to capture the nuanced nature of protein–nucleic acid interactions, where binding strength varies significantly due to distinct interaction mechanisms (e.g. strong hydrogen bonds vs. weak van der Waals contacts). To address this limitation, we developed a distance-based hierarchical labeling system that quantifies residue-level binding propensity. ### Cross-modal protein representation
  Traditional computational approaches for protein–nucleic acid interaction prediction have long relied on expert-curated empirical features (Fig. 1B), which remain indispensable for their biophysical interpretability and proven effectiveness. In this study, we systematically integrate these established feature engineering strategies with modern representation learning paradigms to harness their complementary strengths. For sequence-based feature extraction, evolutionary conservation patterns are captured through Position-Specific Scoring Matrices (PSSM) generated via PSI-BLAST [20] against the UniRef90 database and Hidden Markov Model (HMM) profiles derived from UniRef30 using HH-suite [21]. Structural representations are enriched through atomic-level features (e.g. mass, B-factors) and 14 DSSP [22] attributes encompassing solvent accessibility, secondary structure (SS), and backbone geometry, providing localized physicochemical insights.

  The integration of modern PLMs and geometric deep learning has redefined feature extraction paradigms, enabling comprehensive representation learning across sequence and structure modalities. For sequence-based characterization, we leverage ESM-2, a transformer architecture pretrained on 60 million UniRef50 sequences, to generate 1280-dimensional contextual embeddings that encode evolutionary conservation patterns and long-range residue correlations critical for nucleic acid recognition. Structural representations are derived through two complementary approaches: GearNet, a residue-level geometric graph neural encoder pretrained on 800 000 AlphaFold2-predicted structures, employs equivariant message passing to produce 512-dimensional spatial embeddings preserving E(3)-invariance, while SaProt introduces a novel structure-aware vocabulary to tokenize protein conformations into discrete “structural sentences”, pretrained on 40 million predicted structures to capture allosteric communication pathways. This tripartite feature extraction strategy, combining ESM-2’s evolutionary context, GearNet’s geometric fidelity, and SaProt’s conformational semantics, provides rich semantic information about proteins.

  While these pre-trained PLMs have been trained on large-scale protein sequence or structure corpora, including proteins that may overlap with the used benchmark datasets, they are developed through purely unsupervised learning without any access to binding site annotations or downstream prediction labels. Consequently, these models learn generalizable features such as residue co-evolution and structural context, rather than task-specific patterns. Therefore, their use does not constitute information leakage in the binding domain prediction task.

  The cross-modal features of each amino acid residue are concatenated as a 2309-dimensional vector, and then fused through the multi-scale computing framework designed in this study.

  These methods were selected to represent diverse methodological paradigms—graph-based structural modeling, geometric surface learning, equivariant feature integration, and template-guided prediction—ensuring a rigorous evaluation of our framework’s capabilities across complementary computational approaches.

  We evaluated five methods using their official pretrained models and codes. Performance was assessed using three regression metrics: mean absolute error (MAE) and mean squared error (MSE) (lower values indicate better performance) to quantify absolute and squared deviations, respectively, and the Coefficient of Determination (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $R^2$\end{document}) (higher values reflect stronger explanatory power).

  As shown in Table 2, INAB achieved superior performance across all metrics on both DNA and RNA independent test sets. NABind ranked second, benefiting from its extensive template library despite lacking language model integration. The limited performance of EquiPNAS and ESM-NBR reveals that the limitation of a single-scale computational framework with a single PLM (ESM-2), while GraphBind’s dependency on empirical features constrains its predictive capacity. Although GeoBind utilizes equivariant operators, its performance on new data remains unsatisfactory. This may be due to its focus on protein surface point cloud-level predictions, which fail to capture residue-specific binding nuances adequately.

  Different from EquiPNAS, INAB employed a unified 12 Ådistance cutoff for constructing residue graphs, rather than using separate thresholds for DNA- and RNA-binding proteins. INAB adopted a six-layer EGNN architecture, which achieved competitive performance with reduced computational cost by combining with the Mamba module. This was experimentally validated to strike a balance between efficiency and accuracy. For comparison of computational efficiency between EquiPNAS and INAB, please refer to Supplementary Fig. S1.

  For comprehensive validation, we evaluated different methods on two public benchmark datasets: the GraphBind dataset and the GeoBind dataset. INAB was trained separately on each dataset’s training set and evaluated on its corresponding test set. The results of different methods are listed in Table 3 (GraphBind) and Supplementary Table S3 (GeoBind). NABind achieved the best performance, benefiting from its extensive template library, while INAB consistently outperformed all other competing methods. The similar performance trends observed across both datasets demonstrate INAB’s robustness and generalization capability.

  By eliminating the requirement for template-based structural alignments, INAB achieves substantially greater computational efficiency than NABind—a critical advantage when processing large-scale datasets where speed and resource optimization are essential. To quantify these efficiency gains, we conducted a comparative benchmarking study using 50 proteins spanning a broad size range (100, 300, 500, 1000, and more than 1500 residues), with 10 proteins per length category. All tests were conducted on identical hardware to ensure fair performance evaluation.

  As shown in Fig. 2, INAB consistently demonstrated faster annotation times across all protein lengths. For example, for proteins exceeding 1500 residues, INAB completed the analysis in an average of 28.6 min, while NABind required over 4.2 h—an 8.7-fold speed improvement. This efficiency advantage persisted even for smaller proteins (100–300 residues), with INAB reducing annotation time by an average of 86% compared to NABind. The results underscore how INAB’s alignment-free architecture enables scalable processing without compromising accuracy, making it particularly suited for high-throughput functional domain analysis.


### Contributions of multiscale computation and cross-modal learning
  To explore whether multiscale computing and cross-modal PLMs can promote nucleic acid binding domain recognition, we conducted ablation analysis on the model architecture and protein feature representation of INAB. There are seven control models: a model (w/o empirical features) uses only protein features driven from PLMs; a model (w/o PLMs) uses only empirical protein features; three models use empirical protein features and the features driven from only one PLM (ESM-2, GearNet, or SaProt); a model (w/o EGNN) uses only a sequence-level global computing module; and a model (w/o Mamba) uses only a geometric-level local computing module. The ablation experiments (Fig. 3) reveal that the PLM-based features contribute more significantly than the empirical features. Specifically, when PLM features were removed, the model exhibited a marked decline in performance compared to excluding empirical features. This finding underscores that the rich, contextualized information captured by PLMs plays a critical role in enhancing the model’s predictive accuracy. Interestingly, combining empirical features with any one PLM cannot achieve state-of-the-art performance, indicating that the features learned by PLMs of various modalities (ESM-2: sequence, GearNet: residue, and SaProt: structural pattern) are different. Combining cross-modal PLMs can provide a more comprehensive characterization of proteins, thereby enabling the model to achieve better predictive performance.

  Compared to sequence-level global feature learning using Mamba, EGNN for structure-level local feature learning can yield greater performance gains for nucleic acid binding domain identification (w/o Mamba vs w/o EGNN). This is understandable because protein function is primarily determined by its three-dimensional spatial structure, while nucleic acid binding domains are also usually determined by local structure [27, 28]. Due to its equivariant properties, EGNN is well-suited to handle these spatial transformations, enabling it to capture dynamic binding features. By combining global information learning at the sequence level and local pattern learning at the structural level, INAB has achieved better predictive performance in DNA binding domain prediction. However, the performance improvement is not significant in predicting RNA-binding domains. This may be due to the inherent difference between the DNA-binding and RNA-binding modes. DNA-binding proteins typically have stronger sequence specificity [28, 29], which may explain the more significant performance gains of binding sequence space models.

  To further evaluate the necessity of the cross-attention module between the Mamba and EGNN branches, we conducted an additional ablation in which their outputs were simply merged instead of being integrated through cross attention. As shown in Fig. 3, this replacement resulted in a significant drop in predictive performance, suggesting that a simple aggregation of outputs is insufficient. Mamba focuses on modeling sequential dependencies, while EGNN captures structural relationships. The cross-attention mechanism effectively leverages the complementary information learned from these different modeling perspectives, enabling more accurate identification of nucleic acid binding domains.


### Comparison of binary and hierarchical labelling schemas
  To investigate whether a hierarchical label helps the model learn protein binding features and improve predictive performance, we conducted an ablation study on labelling schemas to compare hierarchical and binary labels for identifying nucleic acid binding domains. We evaluate different schemas on the datasets constructed in the study. We obtain the binary labels of the datasets by setting a distance threshold (5 Å). Amino acid residues in a PDB complex with a distance less than this threshold from the nucleic acid are labeled as binding sites (1), while those with a distance more than this threshold are labeled as non-binding sites (0). This is a commonly used strategy in traditional binary classification methods. We set a control model (denoted as INAB-Cls) with a classification framework and train it using the binary labels. Notably, everything else in the control model is consistent with INAB except for the labels of the training data and the model learning type (classification rather than regression). We measured INAB and INAB-Cls using both regression and classification metrics. INAB outperforms INAB-Cls in terms of regression-based metrics (Table 4), while it achieves better classification-based performance than INAB-Cls too (Table 5), despite not being directly optimized for classification. These findings highlight the effectiveness and flexibility of using hierarchical labels in binding domain prediction.


### Case study
  To intuitively demonstrate the capacity of the proposed method, we visualized the prediction result of INAB on a new nucleic acid-binding protein (PDB id: 8w2s_A). We compared it with those of NABind and EquiPNAS (the two best competing methods). Notably, the protein is not included in the training or test datasets, as it was released on RCSB PDB in January 2025. In contrast, the proteins in the constructed datasets were collected up to December 2023. The prediction results of different methods are shown in Fig. 4. INAB’s prediction is more closely aligned with the ground truth (Fig. 4(A)), outperforming the two competing approaches. For instance, in the region of 180–200, INAB assigned appropriately low scores, whereas the other two methods produced unrealistically high scores (Fig. 4(B)). These results confirm the superior ability of INAB to recognize nucleic acid binding domains.


## Conclusion
  In this study, we present a computational framework for predicting protein–nucleic acid binding residues by integrating sequence-scale state space modeling with geometric graph learning. Our contributions lie in three aspects: (i) the systematic integration of multi-modal language models embeddings with interpretable empirical features. (ii) the synergistic combination of Mamba’s long-range sequence dependency modeling and EGNN’s equivariant geometric constraints within a multiscale architecture, and (iii) the development of a distance-stratified hierarchical labeling system that captures nuanced binding affinities based on spatial proximity in crystallographic complexes. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed approach achieves state-of-the-art or comparable performance on both the constructed datasets and two widely adopted benchmark datasets.

  While this work focuses on residue-level nucleic acid binding domain prediction, the proposed hierarchical labeling and multiscale architecture provide a foundation for future extensions toward protein-nucleic acid complex conformation modeling. Recent advances such as AlphaFold 3 [30] highlight the potential of complex structure prediction. The residue-level framework could complement complex structure prediction methods by providing binding interface constraints, while their predicted conformations may serve as geometric priors to refine local interaction details. Although AlphaFold3 can detect protein’ nucleic acid-binding domains with complete complexes when both partners are available. INAB addresses the challenge of identifying binding domains from protein information alone, without requiring knowledge of the nucleic acid partner. These complementary focuses suggest that residue-level binding prediction and complex structure modeling can be integrated, with each approach providing valuable information to enhance the other. Together, these complementary approaches point toward a potential unified framework for biomolecular interaction analysis.

  The proposed method can also promote other related research, such as the prediction of RNA-protein interactions. INAB is designed to be applicable for predicting both DNA- and RNA-binding domains in proteins. The function of predicting RNA-binding domains is expected to become a cornerstone of RNA-protein interaction prediction. It can provide essential information about where and how a protein might bind RNA, significantly narrowing down candidates, informing feature design for machine learning models, enabling structural analysis, and offering clues about binding specificity. While not the only factor and subject to limitations, it can still play as an essential component of the RNA-protein interaction prediction toolkit. Advances in accurately predicting and characterizing RNA-binding domains can directly translate into improvements in RNA-protein interaction prediction accuracy.
