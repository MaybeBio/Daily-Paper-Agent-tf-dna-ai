## Review setup
- **Input scope** Abstract only
- **Assessment boundary** Claims and evidence as presented in the abstract; no full text, figures, tables, or supplementary materials were provided
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors claim that curcumin inhibits HIV-1 by simultaneously suppressing the PI3K/AKT and JAK/STAT pathways, leading to downregulation of CCR5 and FOXP3. They further claim that curcumin disrupts FOXP3 binding to the CCR5 promoter (based on ChIP and EMSA), that network pharmacology and molecular docking identify STAT3 and AKT1 as key targets, and that pathway crosstalk between PI3K/AKT and JAK/STAT constitutes a pharmacological axis for HIV-1 treatment (based on high-throughput sequencing, mass spectrometry, and co-immunoprecipitation).
- **Visible evidence base** Abstract text only; no experimental details, quantitative results, statistical analyses, or methodological descriptions are available
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Full manuscript, all figures and tables, experimental protocols, statistical details, raw data, and any supplementary information

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** The abstract presents a potentially interesting mechanistic hypothesis for curcumin as an HIV-1 inhibitor, but the claims are broad and the evidence base is entirely inaccessible from the supplied material. The causal chain from curcumin treatment to pathway inhibition, transcription factor modulation, co-receptor downregulation, and reduced viral entry is asserted without any quantitative data. The claim of pathway crosstalk as a "pharmacological axis" is particularly ambitious and would require substantial experimental support that is not visible here. The abstract reads more like a summary of a large multi-omics study than a focused mechanistic report, and the lack of any numerical results or statistical measures prevents assessment of effect sizes or robustness.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in HIV-1 pathogenesis and latency, natural product pharmacology, and transcriptional regulation of viral co-receptors would be interested. The potential repurposing of curcumin as an adjunct to antiretroviral therapy could also attract clinicians and translational researchers focused on viral reservoir eradication. However, interest would be contingent on the strength of the underlying evidence, which cannot be evaluated from the abstract alone.
- **Major strengths** The abstract identifies a plausible and testable mechanism linking a natural compound to two well-characterized signaling pathways and a transcription factor with known roles in immune regulation. The use of multiple orthogonal techniques (ChIP, EMSA, network pharmacology, molecular docking, high-throughput sequencing, mass spectrometry, co-IP) suggests a comprehensive experimental approach. The potential clinical relevance of targeting viral reservoirs is timely and of broad interest.

  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Evidence sufficiency
  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that curcumin inhibits HIV-1 through simultaneous inhibition of PI3K/AKT and JAK/STAT pathways, leading to downregulation of CCR5 and FOXP3.
  - **Evidence pointer** Abstract; location not provided
  - **Concern** The abstract provides no quantitative data demonstrating the extent of HIV-1 inhibition, the degree of pathway suppression, or the magnitude of CCR5 and FOXP3 downregulation. No concentrations, time points, cell types, or viral strains are mentioned. The causal ordering (pathway inhibition leading to transcription factor changes leading to co-receptor reduction) is asserted but not supported by any visible experimental result.

  - **Resolution test** Provide dose-response curves for curcumin-mediated HIV-1 inhibition, quantitative measurements of pathway phosphorylation status, and expression levels of CCR5 and FOXP3 with appropriate statistical comparisons and effect sizes.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M2
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Mechanistic causality
  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that curcumin disrupts the binding of FOXP3 to the CCR5 promoter, thereby reducing viral entry.
  - **Evidence pointer** Abstract; ChIP and EMSA experiments mentioned but no results shown
  - **Concern** The abstract states that ChIP and EMSA were used to find that curcumin disrupts FOXP3 binding to the CCR5 promoter, but no data are presented. It is unclear whether this disruption was demonstrated in relevant cell types, whether it was dose-dependent, and whether the reduced binding directly translates to reduced viral entry. The link between FOXP3 occupancy at the CCR5 promoter and CCR5 expression is not established in the abstract.

If this link is not rigorously demonstrated, the entire mechanistic model collapses. The abstract does not clarify whether FOXP3 acts as an activator or repressor at this locus, which is essential for interpreting the direction of the effect.
  - **Resolution test** Show ChIP and EMSA data with appropriate controls, demonstrate that FOXP3 occupancy changes correlate with CCR5 expression changes, and provide functional assays linking CCR5 downregulation to reduced viral entry in relevant cell types.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M3

  - **Blocking** Yes

  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that crosstalk between the PI3K/AKT and JAK/STAT pathways is a pharmacological axis for HIV-1 treatment, identified through high-throughput sequencing, mass spectrometry, and co-IP experiments.

The techniques listed (high-throughput sequencing, mass spectrometry, co-IP) are mentioned without any results. The leap from identifying crosstalk to proposing it as a therapeutic axis is not supported by any visible evidence.

  - **Resolution test** Provide specific data showing the molecular nature of the crosstalk (e.g., phosphorylation events, protein-protein interactions, transcriptional changes), demonstrate that this crosstalk is functionally relevant to HIV-1 inhibition, and show that targeting this axis has therapeutic benefit in relevant models.
