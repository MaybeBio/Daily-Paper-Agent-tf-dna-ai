# Inhibition of oxidative stress and the Neuropilin-2-induced neuroinflammatory pathway by EMO ameliorates epileptic seizures in the preclinical model of epilepsy


## Objective
  Epilepsy is a chronic neurological condition characterized by recurrent seizures, often linked to neuroinflammation and oxidative stress that exacerbate neuronal injury. Neuropilin-2 (NRP2) and Nuclear Factor-Kappa B (NF-κB) are key mediators in these pathways. This study evaluated the neuroprotective effects of emodin, a bioactive anthraquinone with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, in a pentylenetetrazole (PTZ)-induced mouse model of epilepsy.


## Methods
  Seizure severity, anxiety-like behavior (Elevated Plus Maze), and cognitive function (Morris Water Maze) were assessed. Oxidative stress markers including glutathione (GSH), catalase, lipid peroxidation (LPO), and glutathione-S-transferase (GST) were measured. Expression of NRP2, NF-κB, and proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6) was quantified. Docking studies examined emodin's binding affinity to NRP2 and NF-κB.


## Results
  Emodin (200 mg/kg) significantly reduced seizure frequency and severity, improved anxiety-like behavior, and enhanced cognition. Biochemical analysis showed restored oxidative balance, with increased GSH and catalase activity and reduced LPO and GST dysfunction. Molecular studies revealed downregulation of NRP2, NF-κB, and cytokines. Docking confirmed strong binding affinity to NRP2 and NF-κB.


## Conclusion
  Emodin alleviates oxidative stress and neuroinflammation by modulating NRP2 and NF-κB pathways, suggesting therapeutic potential in epilepsy.


## Introduction
  Epilepsy, one of the most prevalent neurological conditions, involves spontaneous recurrent seizures that adversely affect neuronal integrity across multiple areas of the brain. It affects an estimated 50 million individuals worldwide, posing substantial treatment challenges despite ongoing advancements in anti-seizure medications (ASM) [1,2]. A staggering 20%–30% of epileptic patients suffer from resistance to antiepileptic medicine, characterized by persistent seizures and associated cognitive impairments, making its management particularly challenging [3]. Since almost 80% of epileptic patients reside in resource-limited, low-income states, getting access to high-quality epileptic care remains a significant challenge. Inadequate healthcare resources and social stigma are two major systemic obstacles that patients in these areas usually face [4]. Additionally, under-recognition of seizure episodes contributes to diagnostic delays, preventing patients from receiving optimal and timely treatment [4,5]. While surgical options exist, but are underutilized, often due to referral delays and lack of access to specialized centers[3]. Overall, improving epilepsy care necessitates healthcare system strengthening, addressing these systemic barriers and improving patient education.

  The pathophysiology of epilepsy is exacerbated by oxidative stress- a pro-oxidant/antioxidant imbalance. Oxidative stress drives the neuronal damage and significantly contributes to epileptogenesis. [6]. Recent Mendelian randomization studies have identified that higher oxidative stress biomarkers causally increase the risk of focal epilepsy [7], and adjunctive antioxidant (e.g. vitamins E and C) alleviates seizure frequency in drug-resistant cases. [8]. Previous investigations like these highlight the critical role that oxidative stress plays in epilepsy and the necessity of investigating antioxidant-based treatment strategies further [9]. Concurrently, oxidative stress and neuroinflammation reinforce one another: for instance, redox imbalance intensifies neuroinflammation through the activation of NF-κB and triggers TNF-α and IL-6 release, which in turn exacerbates oxidative damage, especially after brain injury [10–12]-. Emerging studies highlight the therapeutic potential of these pathways, e.g, interventions that restore redox balance have demonstrated anti-inflammatory effects and improved seizure outcomes [13], highlighting the importance and promising effects of targeting these intersecting pathways in pharmacoresistant epilepsy.

  Neuropilin-2 (NRP2) is essential for the development and synaptic integration of inhibitory interneurons, such as GABAergic subsets, thereby preserving excitation-inhibition homeostasis within the hippocampus [14]. Altered NRP2 expression is linked to enhanced seizure susceptibility and behavioral deficits commonly linked to epilepsy [15]. Researcher has reported that in transgenic mice, overactivity of NRP2 in NRP2 under activity has been found; parvalbumin interneurons and somatostatin positive neurons diminished inhibitory synaptic interneurons to CA1 pyramidal neurons, precipitating spontaneous seizures in the absence of chemoconvulsants [16,17]. Moreover, NRP2 signaling promotes aberrant mossy fiber sprouting, a key process and hallmark in epileptogenesis, implicating NRP2 as a potential anticonvulsant target [18]. Enhanced activity of NRP-2 receptor also promotes neuroinflammation via NF-κB in the brain. The NF-κB serves as a pivotal transcription factor that, upon activation, translocates into the nucleus and regulates the inflammatory factors, such as IL-16, and TNF-α, which, in turn, induce neuronal excitability and intensify seizure propagation [19,20]. Concurrently, NRP2-induced pathways elevate reactive oxygen species production, which overwhelm the Nrf2-propagated antioxidant response and exacerbate neuronal damage in a diverse pathological condition, including epilepsy [20]. Together, these mechanisms of oxidative stress, NRP2 dysregulation and inflammation underscore its importance as a molecular target for interventions in pharmacoresistant epilepsy.

  Natural compounds have demonstrated enormous therapeutic potential in the management of epilepsy, as evidenced by diverse neuroprotective mechanisms. For instance, phytochemicals, such as amentoflavone, were identified through in-silico molecular docking, as it has high-binding affinity and possesses a favorable pharmacokinetic profile, suggesting multiple modes of action in neurological disorders [21,22]. Similarly, compounds like curcumin and resveratrol modulate the Wnt (wingless/Integration Site) signaling pathway, which is crucial in neuronal survival and synaptic plasticity, thereby offering anticonvulsant and neuroprotective effects [23]. Moreover, phenylpropanoid derivatives demonstrate multi-target activity by effectively modulating neurotransmitter systems involved in seizure generation [24,25]. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that phytochemicals can be used as viable alternative or adjuncts to conventional antiepileptic drugs.

  Emodin (EMO), a naturally occurring antraquinone, has emerged as a favorable neuroprotective agent in depression, Alzheimer’s and Huntington's disease [26]. It has multifaceted mechanisms; it mitigates Aβ oligomer- and tau-induced neurotoxicity through antiinflammatory, antioxidant and antifibrillogenic actions [27,28], modulating TLR4/NF-κB/NLRP3 and Nrf2/GPX4 pathways to reduce cytotoxicity EMO [29]. EMO inhibits neuroinflammation through microglial NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis, promoting neuronal survival [30]. Additionally, it alleviates neuropathic pain and inflammation through the PPAR-gamma pathway and hence underscores its therapeutic potential in chronic nerve injuries [30]. Although there is an increasing body of research on the clinical effects of Emodin (EMO), its precise impact on NRP2-driven neuroinflammation and oxidative stress in the context of epilepsy remains insufficiently explored.

  Therefore, the present study aimed to assess emodin’s neuroprotective effect in a preclinical model of epilepsy by examining its effects on NRP2-driven neuroinflammation and seizure activity. Our findings show that emodin reduces seizure frequency and severity, laying the foundation for NRP2-targeted therapies in drug-resistant epilepsy.


## Materials and methods

### Reagents and chemicals
  Emodin (catalog #E7881, purity ≥95%, PubChem ID: 3220) and pentylenetetrazole (PTZ, catalog #P6500, PubChem ID: 936) were procured from certified reputable chemical suppliers for the study of an experimental epilepsy model. Antibodies employed in the analysis included anti-p-NF-κB (catalog #SC-271908) and Rabbit polyclonal antibody to anti-NRP2 (Antibodies.com, catalog A14081) (Dallas, TX, USA), which were purchased from validated suppliers, ensuring precision in the experimental measurements. Antioxidant Abcam kits (Cambridge, UK) were used for lipid peroxidation and antioxidant tests, such as GSH, SOD, and MDA. We purchased several murine cytokine ELISA kits from Elabscience Biotech Inc. (Houston, TX, USA) that were specific for IL-6 (catalog # 216-16-10UG, Thermofisher Scientific), TNF-α (catalog # E-EL-R0019), and SOD (catalog # E-EL-R0017). Proteinase K (catalog # 02193981-CF) and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (catalog # AB-6789) were obtained from a certified supplier from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). All additional reagents, alongside general laboratory reagents required for RNA extraction and quantitative RT – PCR analyses, were of analytical grade. Custom oligonucleotide primers were synthesized and ordered from an authorized local distributor of Thermo Fisher Scientific.


### Animal housing and experimental design
  Male NMRI mice, aged 6–8 weeks and with a weight of 25–30 g, were used throughout the current study. Standardized laboratory settings were used to keep the animals alive, maintained at a constant temperature of 22 ± 2°C with a 12-hour light/dark cycle. Every animal was given unlimited access to water ad libitum and laboratory chow. Measures to minimize animal discomfort, stress, and pain included seven days of acclimatization, appropriate housing conditions, and sterile surgical procedures performed under anesthesia and analgesia. Animals were distributed into the following experimental groups (6–7 mice) randomly: A standard treatment group received diazepam as a reference medication; drug-treated groups received EMO at doses of 50, 100, 150, or 200 mg/kg before PTZ administration; a control group received saline injections in addition to 0.1% DMSO; and a PTZ group received pentylenetetrazole (PTZ) to induce seizures. 30 min before PTZ injections, EMO, an experimental medication, was administered intraperitoneally after being prepared in a normal saline containing 0.1% DMSO.

  The experimental model involved the administration of PTZ to induce seizures, with behavioral assessments conducted immediately after PTZ injections. Seizure severity and frequency were evaluated using a modified Racine scale. Upon completion of all behavioral assessments, mine were euthanized humanely, and their brain tissues were harvested for downstream biochemical, molecular, and histological analyses. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee of Shanxi Medical University of First Hospital (Ref. NO.DWYJ-2024-224) and conducted in compliance with the NIH guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals.


### Study design and drug treatment
  Mice were randomly assigned to seven experimental groups (n = 6–7 per group): Group 1 (Control): as a vehicle solution, mice were given saline containing 0.1% DMSO. As the epileptic control group, Group 2 (PTZ-only) administered injections of pentylenetetrazole (PTZ) to cause seizures. Group 3 (PTZ + EMO 50 mg/kg): after PTZ-induced convulsions, mice received 50 mg/kg EMO. Group 4 (PTZ + EMO 100 mg/kg): Mice were administered PTZ along with 100 mg/kg of EMO. Group 5 (PTZ + EMO 150 mg/kg): Following PTZ administration, mice received a pretreatment of 150 mg/kg of EMO. Group 6 (PTZ + EMO 200 mg/kg): Following PTZ administration, this group was given the maximum dosage of EMO (200 mg/kg). Group 7 (Standard group): Mice were administered diazepam (1 mg/kg) 30 min before PTZ injections as a standard anticonvulsant treatment.

  Emodin, dissolved in saline containing 0.1% DMSO, was injected intraperitoneally on the day seizures were induced and then repeated every 24 hours for the subsequent fifteen days. The control and PTZ-only groups received equivalent volumes of the vehicle solution without EMO. Behavioral assessments of seizure frequency and severity were conducted throughout the study to assess the dose-dependent effects of EMO. ### Behavior evaluation

#### Racine’s scale for seizure latency, intensity, and convulsion stages
  Seizure severity was evaluated over a 30-minute period following PTZ administration in each mouse. Various behavioral parameters, including seizure intensity, latency, and convulsion stages, were scored using a modified Racine’s scale (Table 1) for 30 min. The seizure intensity mean for each group was determined by averaging individual seizure scores and plotting these values over the treatment period. Seizure latency (period) was defined as the time interval between PTZ injection and the onset of observable seizure activity, such as clonic movements, jerks, or twitches. The frequency of seizures was determined by counting the total number of seizures exhibited by each mouse during the 30-minute observation period, irrespective of the defined stage. Full kindling in animals was confirmed once the animal consistently displayed stage 5/6 seizures following three PTZ administrations at a 48-hour interval. #### Open Field Test (OFT)
  OFT was employed for the assessment of anxiety-like behavior and locomotor activity in mice. Individually, each animal was placed in the center square of the arena measuring 50 × 50 × 40 cm for a 5-minute observation period under uniform lighting. Various behavioral parameters recorded via EthoVision XT software included the total distance covered, frequency of entrances into the central zone, and the time duration in the center compared to the peripheral zone. Greater exploration of the central area was interpreted as reduced anxiety, while overall movement was assessed by measuring the total distance traveled. To prevent olfactory cue bias, the arena was sanitized with 70% ethanol between trials [31].


#### Elevated plus maze (EPM) test
  To further evaluate anxiety-related behavior, mice were tested on an elevated plus maze. Specification of the test equipment included two closed arms (30 × 5 cm) and two open arms (30 × 5 cm) arranged perpendicularly and elevated 50 cm above the floor. Each mouse was put at the maze center, with its face toward an open arm, and was allowed to explore for 5 min. Key parameters measured were the number of entries into, and time spent in, open versus closed arms. Increased exploration and time spent in open arms were indicative of reduced anxiety behavior. The maze was wiped with 70% ethanol after each trial to avoid any cue [32].


#### Morris water maze (MWM) test
  Various parameters, such as spatial learning and memory, were evaluated by utilizing MWM. The apparatus included a circular pool of 1.2 m diameter, filled with opaque water maintained at 24 ± 1 °C, and a hidden probe of 10 × 10 cm, immersed 1 cm below the surface of water at a fixed location. Mice were trained for five consecutive days, with four trials per day, each lasting up to 60 s to allocate the probe. Starting points varied among four equidistant pool quadrants, and escape latency (time to locate the platform) was recorded. Mice failing to find the probe within 60 s were guided to it and allowed to rest for 15 s. On day six, a probe trial was conducted without it, and the time spent in the probe quadrant was recorded as a measure of memory retention in 60 s trial period. This protocol was used to evaluate the impact of EMO on spatial learning and memory in the epilepsy model [33,34].


### Tissue collection, processing and histological preparation
  One day after we administered our last PTZ injection, mice were anesthetized and sacrificed by decapitation. Brains were immediately removed onto a cold surface, and the brain regions like the cortex and hippocampus were carefully segregated. Each region was divided into two: one half was immediately frozen at –80°C for biochemical assays, while the other was immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 24 h at 4°C, followed by routine paraffin embedding for histological and immunohistochemical studies. For biochemical preparations, the frozen tissue was thawed on ice and homogenized in an ice-cold 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), containing phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) at a 1:100 inhibitor-to-sample volume ratio. Subsequently, homogenates were centrifuged at 4000 × g for 10 min at 4°C, and the resulting supernatant was harvested for downstream analyses [34].


#### Antioxidant assays for brain tissues

##### Determination of glutathione level
  Tissue supernatant (0.2 mL) was combined with Ellman’s reagent (DTNB) and 0.2 M phosphate buffer, bringing the total volume to 3 mL. This mixture was left at room temperature for 10 min to allow full development of the yellow 2-nitro-5-thiobenzoate chromophore. Absorbance at 412 nm was then recorded, and GSH concentration was calculated by subtracting the blank reading and is reported as micromoles per milligram of protein (µmol/mg protein).


##### Glutathione-S-transferase (GST) kinetic measurement
  A reaction master mix, containing 1.5 mM reduced GSH and 1 mM CDNB in 0.1 M phosphate buffer, was prewarmed to 23°C. The enzymatic process was initiated by adding 60 µL of tissue homogenate, and the formation of the CDNB–GSH conjugate was followed by the increase in absorbance at 340 nm over 300 s. The reaction’s initial linear rate (ΔA340/min) was used to calculate GST activity, which is expressed as µmol of conjugate formed per minute per mg of protein.


##### Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity
  To assess SOD activity, 100 µL of homogenate was introduced into 2.8 mL of 0.1 M potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) at 25°C. The assay was initiated by adding 0.10 mL of 1 mM pyrogallol, and the ensuing rate of auto-oxidation was tracked immediately by recording absorbance changes at the appropriate wavelength. Enzyme activity was derived from the change in absorbance over time and normalized to protein content.


##### Catalase (CAT) determination
  Catalase function was assessed by combining 50 µL of tissue homogenate with 1 mL of freshly prepared 30 mM H₂O₂ and 1.95 mL of 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) in a quartz cuvette. The breakdown of H₂O₂ was followed by monitoring the decline in absorbance at 240 nm. Using the known molar extinction coefficient for hydrogen peroxide (0.071 mM−¹ cm−¹), the initial rate of absorbance decrease was converted into micromoles of H₂O₂ decomposed per minute per milligram of protein.


##### Lipid peroxidation (LPO)
  Lipid peroxidation was quantified by the thiobarbituric acid – reactive substances (TBARS) assay. 200 µL of sample supernatant was mixed with 580 µL of 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), 20 µL of 1 mM FeCl₃, and 200 µL of 100 mM ascorbic acid. After incubating at 37°C for 60 min to induce lipid oxidation, the reaction was stopped by adding 1 mL of 10% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid and 1 mL of 0.66% (w/v) thiobarbituric acid. Samples were cooled on ice, centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 min, and the absorbance of the clear supernatant was read at 535 nm. Lipid peroxidation levels were calculated as nanomoles of malondialdehyde equivalents per milligram of protein using a standard curve.


### Predictive molecular docking to evaluate the binding interactions of emodin with NRP2 and NF-κB
  To examine the binding interaction of EMO with the target proteins, such as NRP2 (PDB:7M22) and NF-Κb (PDB: 4OT9), the AutoDock software (version AutoDock 4.2) was utilized. The EMO molecular structure was obtained from the ZINC15 docking database (ZINC000003824868). For the preparation of ligand and protein structures, polar hydrogens were added, while Gasteiger charges were assigned using standard protocols. Predefined regions of the target proteins were utilized for docking simulations and then binding energies were computed to analyze the stabilities of the complexes formed. Finally, the docking results focusing on key interacting residues and binding interactions were visualized using Pyrex and Discovery Studio Software (v16.1.0.15350). The validation of the results was based on binding energy scores and interaction profiles.


### Flow cytometric and Western blot analysis NRP2 expression

#### Flow cytometer:
  The expression of NRP2 in brain tissues was analyzed and quantified through flow cytometry. After the final PTZ administration, the hippocampal and cortical areas of the brain were processed by mechanical dissociation using a sterile pipette into single-cell suspensions. Tissue specimens were enzymatically dissociated in Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution containing 0.2 mg/mL Collagenase IV and 0.1 mg/mL DNase I at 37°C for 30 min. Thereafter, the cell suspension was passed through a 70-µm nylon mesh, pelleted by centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 min at 4°C, and the supernatant discarded. The cell pellet was resuspended and incubated in the dark with NRP2 primary antibody (dilution 1:100) for 30 min at 4 °C, following two washes, cells were stained with BB700 fluorophore conjugated secondary antibody (dilution 1:500) under the same conditions for an additional 30 min. After the final wash, the cells were resuspended in 300 µL of FACS buffer, counterstained with DAPI (1:5000) to remove any dead cells. Flow Cytometric measurements for expression were done using the BD A5 Symphony instrument. All tissues for flow cytometry were freshly processed, ensuring the viability and reliability of the flow cytometric data. FlowJo software was used to examine the flow data. The percentage of NRP2-positive cells in each experimental group’s total live cell population was calculated.


#### Western blot analysis
  Freshly preserved cortex and hippocampus tissues were chilled on ice and homogenized in RIPA buffer, fortified with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails to preserve protein integrity. After clearing debris by centrifugation, protein concentration in clear supernatants was determined via the BCA assay. Aliquots, containing 30 µg of total protein, were mixed with loading dye, denatured, and resolved on SDS – PAGE gels. To block nonspecific binding sites, membranes were incubated in TBST containing 5% nonfat dry milk for 60 min at room temperature. They were then incubated overnight at 4°C with an anti-NRP2 antibody (1:1000). Following three washes in TBST, membranes were exposed to an HRP-linked secondary antibody (1: 5000) for 60 min at ambient temperature. Chemiluminescent detection reagents were applied, and protein bands were captured using a digital imaging system. Band densities were quantified in ImageJ, with NRP2 levels normalized to β-actin.


### Histological preparation
  After mouse brain isolation, the tissue fixation was done in 4% paraformaldehyde and subsequently washed. Using a sharp dissection blade, the samples were then sliced into 3 mm coronal sections. The sections were then further embedded in paraffin, and using a microtome, it was converted into 4 µm-thick slices for histological and pathological analyses.


#### Hematoxylin & Eosin Staining for morphological changes in brain tissues
  To know about the morphological changes in the hippocampus and cortex subjected to various drug treatments, we employed H&E staining, using standardized laboratory protocols. In short, the brain’s respective tissue slices were de-paraffinized through xylene and then subjected to a series of ethanol solutions of various strengths for rehydration. Hematoxylin was applied to stain cell nuclei, followed by brief treatments with 1% hydrochloric acid and ammonia water to enhance contrast. Eosin was then used to stain the cytoplasm. The sections were dehydrated using graded alcohol and xylene, then cover-slipped for preservation. Morphological changes, including alterations in cellular structure, density, and edema, were examined by capturing around 4–5 images from a single slide using a light microscope (Olympus, made in Japan). Image analysis was performed with the ImageJ software to quantify morphological changes.


#### Immunohistochemical analysis of p-NF-κB
  Immunohistochemistry was performed to analyze p-NF-κB localization in the cortical region under different treatment conditions. Tissue slices were first passed through xylene and a series of alcohol solutions to ensure proper rehydration, then washed 3 times in PBS, five minutes each. For antigen exposure, Proteinase K was utilized. Endogenous peroxidase was then quenched by a five-minute treatment in 3% hydrogen peroxide. To reduce background, normal serum was applied for one hour at room temperature, tissue was subsequently exposed to a p-NF-κB primary antibody (1:100) and held at 4°C overnight. The next day, sections were washed again in PBS, then incubated with a secondary antibody for two hours, and further processed using an avidin-biotin-peroxidase method. DAB was used to develop the staining for five minutes. Finally, slides were rinsed, passed through ascending ethanol concentrations and xylene to dehydrate, and cover-slipped with a mounting medium.

  Images were collected at 40× magnification using an Olympus microscope. For quantification, p-NF-κB positive cells were counted in five randomly selected fields per section with ImageJ.


#### Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
  As per the manufacturer’s instructions, ELISA kits were used to quantify NF-κB, TNF-α, and IL-6. 2500 µL of PBS with phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), an inhibitor of proteases, was used to homogenize about 50 mg of the brain tissue at 15,000 rpm. The supernatant layer was collected after centrifuging the homogenate mixture for nearly ten minutes at 4000×g. The BCA test measured the protein concentration, and similar amounts of protein were utilized to measure NF-κB, IL-6, and TNF-α. The BioTek EL × 808 ELISA microplate reader recorded the absorbance values. Protein concentrations adjusted to the amount of total protein used were displayed as pg/mg.


### PCR analysis of gene expression in the hippocampus
  Total RNA from mice brain tissues was obtained using TRIzol® (Invitrogen) and quantified on a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. For each sample, 1 µg of RNA was converted into cDNA following the protocol. Real-time SYBR Green PCR was carried out using NF-κB, specific primers (F: 5'-AGCGGAAATCGGAAATCAG-3′; R: 5′-TGAGGTCTTTGGGCAGAGA-3′). Thermal cycling began with a 10-minute activation at 95°C, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 60 s. GAPDH served as the internal reference gene. Through the 2^–ΔΔCT method, relative expression levels were calculated, and each reaction was run in triplicate to ensure consistency [35].


### Statistical analysis
  For statistical comparisons, GraphPad Prism version 8 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) was used. All values in results are reported as mean ± SEM. To analyze differences among groups, one-way ANOVA was employed, with Bonferroni or Tukey post hoc adjustments applied according to the experimental context and characteristics of the data. For studies involving repeated measures or two independent variables, a two-way ANOVA was utilized in combination with Bonferroni correction to address multiple comparisons and minimize Type I error. Quantitative analysis of imaging results was accomplished with ImageJ (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. The following notation was used for significance: * or # for p < 0.05, ** or ## for p < 0.01, and *** or ### for p < 0.001.

#### Open field test (OFT)
  EMO (200 mg/kg), CAPE, and diazepam each significantly mitigated PTZ-induced behavioral alterations in the OFT. ## Discussion
  In this study, we focused on the neuroprotective efficacy of EMO in mitigating PTZ-mediated seizures, neuroinflammation and oxidative stress responses, using CAPE (NF-κB inhibitor) and diazepam as pharmacological positive control or reference anticonvulsant. The results demonstrate that EMO exert potent anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and neuroprotective activities in a dose-dependent way, leading to marked reductions in seizure intensity and notable improvements in behavioral and cognitive performance. EMO.

  Pentylenetetrazole (PTZ) administration in rodents is a reliable model of chronic epilepsy, producing frequent, high-intensity seizures and associated cognitive deficits that can be verified by EPM and MWM. For instance, probiotic supplementation in rats delayed the onset and shortened the duration of PTZ-induced myoclonic-jerk and generalized tonic-clonic seizures, while mitigating inflammation and oxidative stress [35]. Likewise, Esculetin extended the latency of seizure onset, reduced seizure duration and severity, and attenuated cognitive impairment and pro-inflammatory markers [36]. Similarly, serotonin and fluoxetine delayed PTZ-induced seizures and suppressed inflammatory cytokines [37]. Investigations of neuropeptides demonstrated anticonvulsant effects of ghrelin versus proconvulsant actions of obestatin in this model [38,39]. In line with these reports, administration of EMO in the current study, particularly at a 200 mg/kg dose, evidently reduced seizure frequency and intensity in our behavior studies. EMO demonstrated a dose-dependent enhancement in spatial memory and cognitive function, yielding its most pronounced neuroprotective effects, particularly at higher doses (Figures 2, 3, 4, 5; ***p < 0.001). Furthermore, EMO significantly alleviated anxiety-like behavior, as evident by increased time spent in the open arms of the EPM, and improved spatial memory performance in the MWM, suggesting preservation of hippocampal structure and function. These findings align with previous research reporting EMOEMO neuroprotection in other models of neurodegeneration [39]. These behavioral improvements are likely to reflect EMO’s dual ability to reduce oxidative stress and inflammation, both of which contribute substantially to brain injury and cognitive deficits in epilepsy [40].

  EMO’s robust efficacy can be attributed to its simultaneous modulation of neuroinflammatory and oxidative pathways. In the PTZ-induced seizure model, EMO treatment led to a notable reduction in proinflammatory mediators such as TNF-α and IL-1β, and improved antioxidant markers, evidenced by elevating glutathione, GST activity and catalase (Figure 7) [41]. Through research, seizure activity boosts the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which in turn disrupts cellular homeostasis, protein oxidation, and finally DNA damage and mutation that exacerbate neuronal injury [42]. Consistent with these mechanisms, PTZ-treated mice exhibited a significant increase in LPO, and depleted GSH, GST, and catalase S. Previous reports suggest that ROS is one of the hallmarks in epilepsy pathophysiology [43]. Treatment with EMO reversed these changes, reinforcing cellular redox homeostasis and likely contributing to its anticonvulsant efficacy. These findings corroborate prior studies that identified the antioxidant activity of EMOEMO in various neurodegenerative and inflammatory disease models [44,45].

  Neuropilin-2 (NRP2) has a vital function in regulating immune responses and inflammation in the CNS, and its expression is upregulated in neurological disorders, such as epilepsy [46]. Our findings in PTZ-induced seizure models revealed that NRP2 expression showed a significant increase within the hippocampus and cortex, the seizure-prone areas, as demonstrated by flow cytometry and western blotting (Figuress 9 and 10). Rather than being a passive marker, upregulation of NRP2 may amplify neuroinflammation, thereby exacerbating seizure activity and enhancing neuronal damage. This aligns with earlier research that highlights the involvement of NRP2 in sustaining inflammatory responses within a range of neurological conditions [47]. This is not a minor footnote but points toward the possibility that potent neuroprotective effects of EMO may be linked to its action on the modification of NRP2-related signaling pathways. Importantly, our molecular docking studies (Figure 8) revealed favorable binding interaction of EMO with NRP2 and NF-κB, suggesting that EMO may attenuate seizure-induced neuroinflammation by downregulating NRP2-mediated pathways. Therefore, through this dual targeting of EMO neuroinflammatory cascades and NRP2, EMO likely curtails oxidative stress and preserves neuronal viability.

  Extensive research across different neurological disease models indicates a strong link between seizure activity and inflammatory cascades, with NF-κB serving as a central transcriptional regulator in this process [48]. During oxidative stress and elevated ROS, NF-κB translocates to the nucleus where it promotes the transcription of pro-inflammatory mediators, including TNF-α and IL-6. These cytokines exaggerate neuronal excitability and facilitate the seizure’s progression [49]. In our PTZ model, we observed a significant upregulation of NF-κB activation alongside elevated TNF-α and IL-6 (Figure 11), which further validates the role of this pathway in epilepsy-associated neuroinflammation [50]. Remarkably, treatment with EMO, CAPE, and Diazepam each effectively inhibited NF-κB activation and downregulated IL-6 and TNF-α expression, with the most pronounced activity observed in the EMO-treated group (Figures 11 and 14). Immunohistochemical analysis confirmed that EMO significantly reduced phosphorylated NF-κB in cortical neurons (Figure 13). Inhibiting NF-κB not only reduces the expression of IL-6 and TNF-α but also interrupts a deadly cycle of inflammation-driven excitotoxicity that worsens seizure outcomes. These results align with prior evidence, suggesting that NF-κB plays a central role in driving neuroinflammatory processes in epilepsy and that its inhibition can emerge as a potent strategy to mitigate seizure-severity and related brain damage [19]. Thus, the present study adds to the accumulating evidence supporting that positions NF-κB as a viable therapeutic target for epilepsy and other neurodegenerative conditions marked by sustained neuroinflammation.

  Seizure-induced neuroinflammatory and oxidative stress responses frequently precipitate extensive neuronal death, especially in critical brain regions, like the hippocampus and cortex, integral to learning, memory, and seizure control. In our PTZ model, histopathological examination confirmed pronounced neuronal loss in the hippocampus and cortex of PTZ-treated mice (Figure 12), a finding consistent with previous reports, showing the vulnerability of these regions to seizure-induced injury [51]. However, the administration of EMO, CAPE, and diazepam markedly attenuated this damage, as evidenced by the preservation of neuronal structures. Among the treatments, EMOEMO demonstrated the most pronounced neuroprotective effect, yielding the greatest number of surviving neurons across both regions. This neuroprotection likely stems from EMO’s combined antioxidant and anti-inflammatory actions, which help limit ROS accumulation, suppressing NF-κB activation, and reducing TNF-α and IL-6 production, thereby interrupting the inflammation cycle and cell death [52,53].

  EMO markedly mitigated PTZ-induced seizures, neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and neuronal degeneration in mice. The multifaceted mechanisms through which these compounds exert their effects are due to a combination of free radical scavenging, inhibition of NF-κB pathway, and downregulation of NRP2-mediated inflammatory signaling. This finding supports EMO potential as a novel adjunctive therapy for epilepsy. Nevertheless, further research is warranted to elucidate the detailed molecular mechanisms involved and to determine their translational relevance in clinical settings.


### Limitations and future directions
  The current study has several limitations that warrant further investigation. Its reliance on an acute PTZ model, narrow dosing range without accompanying pharmacokinetic or toxicological characterization and absence of experimental validation for in-silico docking predictions. Additionally, it focuses primarily on the NRP2/NF-κB pathway, while omitting other key epileptogenic pathways (e.g. mTOR, Wnt/β-catenin, and TGF-β). The current findings are based on preclinical rodent models and do not fully replicate the complexity of chronic or drug-resistant epilepsy seen in humans. Therefore, future studies should evaluate EMO in chronic and drug – resistant seizure paradigms (pilocarpine, kainic acid), extend dose – response and safety assessments – including blood – brain barrier permeability and metabolism profiles – and confirm direct target engagement via biophysical assays (e.g. SPR, pull – down). Expanding mechanistic analyses to encompass additional molecular cascades and testing EMO in combination with frontline antiepileptic drugs will further clarify its therapeutic viability.
