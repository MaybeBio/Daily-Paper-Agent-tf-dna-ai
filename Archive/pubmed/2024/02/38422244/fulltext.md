# 2ʹ-Hydroxycinnamaldehyde Alleviates Intestinal Inflammation by Attenuating Intestinal Mucosal Barrier Damage Via Directly Inhibiting STAT3


## Background
  The currently available clinical therapeutic drugs for ulcerative colitis (UC) are considered inadequate owing to certain limitations. There have been reports on the anti-inflammatory effects of 2ʹ-hydroxycinnamaldehyde (HCA). However, whether HCA can improve UC is still unclear. Here, we aimed to investigate the pharmacological effects of HCA on UC and its underlying molecular mechanisms.


## Methods
  The pharmacological effects of HCA were comprehensively investigated in 2 experimental setups: mice with dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis and lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-treated fetal human colon (FHC) cells. Furthermore, the interaction between HCA and signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) was investigated using molecular docking. The FHC cells with STAT3 knockdown or overexpression and mice with intestinal epithelium-specific STAT3 deletion (STAT3ΔIEC) were used to evaluate whether STAT3 mediated the pharmacological effects of HCA.


## Results
  2ʹ-Hydroxycinnamaldehyde attenuated dysregulated expression of inflammatory cytokines in a dose-dependent manner while increasing the expression of tight junction proteins, reducing the apoptosis of intestinal epithelial cells, and effectively alleviating inflammation both in vivo and in vitro. 2ʹ-Hydroxycinnamaldehyde bound directly to STAT3 and inhibited its activation. The modulation of STAT3 activation levels due to STAT3 knockdown or overexpression influenced the mitigating effects of HCA on colitis. Further analysis indicated that the remission effect of HCA was not observed in STAT3ΔIEC mice, indicating that STAT3 mediated the anti-inflammatory effects of HCA.


## Conclusions
  We present a novel finding that HCA reduces colitis severity by attenuating intestinal mucosal barrier damage via STAT3. This discovery holds promise as a potential new strategy to alleviate UC.


## Introduction
  Ulcerative colitis (UC), a major prevalent form of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), is a chronic inflammatory disease affecting the colonic and rectal mucosa, with bloody stool and diarrhea as the main clinical manifestations.1–3 The global incidence of UC continues to rise, leading to significant negative effects on patients’ quality of life and imposing substantial economic burdens on individuals and society as a whole.4 Although the specific cause of UC remains unclear, increasing studies support the association of epithelial barrier disruption, genetic susceptibility, dysregulated immune responses, and environmental factors with its pathogenesis.1–3,5 Ulcerative colitis remains a disease that requires continuous treatment owing to its complex etiology.6 The current clinical objectives of UC treatment are to ameliorate inflammation, maintain remission, and address the secondary effects of the disease.6,7 Commonly used agents for UC treatment include 5-aminosalicylic acid, glucocorticoids, immunosuppressants, and monoclonal antibodies. However, these medicines are often associated with suboptimal clinical outcomes, drug dependence, and certain adverse reactions.8–11 Therefore, it is of utmost importance to find new, safe, and effective therapeutic methods to optimize the treatment of UC.

  It is well known that the intestinal mucosal barrier is an important protective mechanism and defense system in healthy individuals. It acts as a barrier between luminal antigens, toxic substances, and the body, while also maintaining the mucosal immune homeostasis. Damage to the mucosal barrier can increase intestinal permeability, allowing external antigens and toxins to invade the body. This invasion triggers an uncontrolled immune response in the intestinal microenvironment and aggravates inflammatory responses.12–15 Increasing evidence suggests that the intestinal mucosal barrier disruption is an important factor in the occurrence and development of UC, and cell tight junctions play a crucial role in maintaining the integrity of the mucosal barrier.15–17 Treatments to improve the impaired barrier effectively ameliorate experimental colitis and are considered a promising therapeutic strategy for UC.6,13

  Signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) is an important member of the STAT signaling protein family and is known to be activated via phosphorylation in response to various cytokines and growth factors, such as interleukin (IL)-6 and epidermal growth factor.18,19 Phosphorylation at tyrosine 705 facilitates STAT3 dimerization, and the dimer can be translocated from the cytoplasm to the nucleus, where it regulates the transcription of target genes.20,21 Aberrant STAT3 activation plays a crucial role in the development of cancer and inflammatory diseases, including UC.22,23 Higher levels of STAT3 phosphorylation are often found in patients with IBD and in other colitis models, and the degree of activated STAT3 is positively correlated with disease activity.24 Therefore, targeting the inhibition of abnormal STAT3 activation has attracted considerable interest as a potential therapeutic strategy in drug research focusing on the treatment of UC.

  Cinnamon, extracted from the cinnamon tree, is not only a commonly used spice but also one of the fundamental herbs in traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), with a history of medical use spanning thousands of years. It has been used in the treatment of gastritis and inflammatory diseases.25,26 Natural small compounds derived from TCM possess valuable pharmacological activities while exhibiting low toxicity.27 Cinnamon extracts have been reported to offer various benefits and contain several active ingredients, such as essential oils (cinnamaldehyde). Cinnamaldehyde, an important active compound, has been extensively investigated and is reported to have various biological activities against bacteria, fungi, and inflammation.28–30 One particular cinnamaldehyde derivative is 2ʹ-hydroxycinnamaldehyde (HCA), which is a natural product isolated from the stem bark of Cinnamomum cassia.25,26,31 2ʹ-Hydroxycinnamaldehyde has demonstrated significant antiproliferative effects on various types of cancer cells, including prostate, colon, and skin cancer cells, as well as anti-inflammatory effects and immunomodulating effects.26,31–34 A previous study has demonstrated that HCA can inhibit lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced nitric oxide production, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α expression, and NF-κB transcriptional activity in vitro.26 Moreover, HCA is known to exert anti-inflammatory effects by targeting lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1),35 and a recent report indicates that HCA can attenuate the overproduction of pro-inflammatory cytokines and improve psoriasis-like dermatitis in mice.36 These findings suggest that HCA can be used as an anti-inflammatory agent. However, the role of HCA in mitigating the progression of UC and its underlying molecular mechanisms remains to be elucidated.

  Based on these data, we hypothesized that HCA could ameliorate intestinal inflammation and innovatively designed experiments to investigate the relevant molecular mechanisms. To achieve this, we established 2 experimental setups: dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis mice and LPS-treated fetal human colon (FHC) cells. Furthermore, a Caco-2 in vitro transwell model was established to assess the effect of HCA treatment on intestinal epithelial barrier function. We found that HCA administration alleviated barrier damage, reduced the apoptosis of intestinal epithelial cells (IECs), and considerably alleviated colitis in vivo and in vitro. Further investigations revealed that HCA exerted its anti-inflammatory effects via binding to STAT3, thereby inhibiting STAT3 activation. Hence, these findings reveal the underlying molecular pathway through which HCA ameliorates colitis, providing a new approach for the clinical treatment of UC.


## Materials and Methods

### Experimental Animals
  The C57BL/6J wild-type (WT) mice (male, 6-week-old) were obtained from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd (Nanjing, China). The C57BL/6J mice with intestinal epithelium-specific STAT3 deletion (STAT3ΔIEC; male, 6 weeks old) were obtained by crossing STAT3-floxed mice (STAT3fl/fl; GemPharmatech CO., Ltd) with Villin-Cre mice (GemPharmatech CO., Ltd). Validation data confirming the knockout of STAT3 in IECs are shown in Supplementary Figure 1A and 1B. The primers used for agarose gel electrophoresis were synthesized by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and are listed in Supplementary Table 1. All mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12 hour/12 hour light/dark cycle, appropriate temperature and humidity, and were provided with adequate autoclaved food and water.


### Preparation of HCA
  The HCA (1 g, purity ≥98%, CAS No. 3541-42-2) was purchased from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China) and dissolved in a solution containing 4% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA), 6% Tween 80 (BioFroxx, Guangzhou, China), 10% dimethylacetamide (Solarbio, Beijing, China), and 80% phosphate-buffered saline (PBS).36 The solution was sterilized using 0.22-µm filters to ensure its suitability for further experiments.37


### Induction of colitis and treatment regimens
  After a 1-week acclimatization period, the 6-week-old WT mice were randomly divided into different groups (n = 6), as follows: control group, drug group, disease group, positive control group, and treatment group. Except for the control and the drug group, mice from the other 3 groups were given sterile drinking water supplemented with 3% (w/v) DSS (MP Biomedicals, Irvine, CA, USA) for 7 consecutive days to induce colitis. Meanwhile, mice in the control, drug, disease, and treatment group were intraperitoneally injected with HCA (30 mg/kg and 50 mg/kg of body weight) or its vehicle every day for 7 days.36 Sulfasalazine (SASP, 200 mg/kg of body weight, Fuda Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China), an agent widely used to treat UC, was administered by gavage to mice in the positive control group. The HCA treatment in the absence of DSS was used to explore its toxicity in healthy mice.38STAT3fl/fl and STAT3ΔIEC mice (male, 6 weeks old) were randomly divided into the control group (vehicle), disease group (3% DSS and vehicle), and treatment group (3% DSS and HCA 50 mg/kg; n = 6). All groups underwent the same treatments as the WT mice, except for the treatment group, in which the dose of intraperitoneal HCA injection was only 50 mg/kg. Throughout the experiment, water and food consumption, general condition, body weight, stool consistency, and hematochezia were observed and recorded daily in each group of mice. On the eighth day, the mice were euthanized, and the whole colon was quickly removed to measure its length. Thereafter, the distal colons were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, Hefei, China) at room temperature for 24 hours and embedded in paraffin for hematoxylin and eosin staining (H&E). The remaining colon sections were placed in a freezing tube and then immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen for subsequent assays. Histological scores and disease activity index (DAI) were assessed according to previous scoring criteria.39,40


### Cell Lines and Cell Transfection
  The normal human colonic epithelial cell line FHC and human colon carcinoma cell line Caco-2, both purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (Rockville, MD, USA), were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution, as previously described.41,42 The FHC cells were maintained in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at a constant temperature of 37°C. Short interfering RNAs targeting STAT3 (siSTAT3), STAT3 overexpression plasmid (pcDNA3.1-STAT3 [plasmid-STAT3]), and corresponding negative controls (nontargeting siRNAs [siNC] and pcDNA3.1-vector [plasmid-NC]) were synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). The FHC cells were seeded onto 6-well plates and then transfected with siRNAs or plasmids using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). RNA and proteins were extracted at 24 and 48 hours, respectively, after transfection to verify the transfection efficiency. The relevant sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 2.


### Cell Counting Kit-8 Assay for Cell Viability
  The HCA (purity ≥98%) was dissolved in DMSO, sterilized using 0.22-µm filters, and diluted to the desired concentrations. The cell viability of FHC cells was assayed by the Cell Counting Kit 8 (BS350B; Biosharp). The FHC cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well. After 24 hours of incubation, the cells were treated with various concentrations of HCA (ranging from 0 to 100 µM) for another 24 hours, followed by the addition of cell counting kit (CCK)-8 reagent in each well and continued incubation for 1 hour at 37°C. The optical density values at 450 nm were measured by the EnSight Multimode Microplate Reader (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA).


### RNA Extraction and Quantitative RT-PCR
  Total RNA was extracted form cells and tissues using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). Next, messenger RNA (mRNA) was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the PrimeScript RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Perfect Real Time; TaKaRa, Shiga, Japan; Cat. No. RR047A). Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was performed in a real-time PCR instrument (CFX connect, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Til RnaseH Plus; TaKaRa; Cat. No. RR820A). The 2-ΔΔct method was used to calculate the relative expression of each gene. All PCR primers (Supplementary Table 3) used in the study were synthesized by Sangon Biotech.


### Biochemical Analysis
  Serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels of the mice were measured by spectrophotometry, using commercially available kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The levels of inflammatory factors in mouse serum or cell-free supernatants were detected using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA, MultiSciences [Lianke] Biotech Co., Ltd, Hangzhou, China). Absorbance was measured using the EnSight Multimode Microplate Reader, and factor concentrations were calculated using standard curves.


### Western Blotting Analysis
  Total protein was extracted from mouse colonic tissues and cells using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Sigma-Aldrich) and protease inhibitors (including protease inhibitor for phosphorylated proteins), followed by centrifugation of the protein samples at 12 000 rpm (Centrifuge 5424R, Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) for 15 minutes at 4°C to obtain the supernatants. The mixture of cell lysates and loading buffer was boiled in a water bath at 100°C for 5 minutes. Nuclear proteins from LPS (Sigma-Aldrich)-treated cells were extracted using the Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (P0027; Beyotime Technology, Shanghai, China), based on the manufacturer’s instructions. The mixture was separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and then quickly transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Bio-Rad).

  The antibodies used for western blotting were as follows: Bax (#60267-1-Ig; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), Bcl-2 (#26593-1-AP; Proteintech), STAT3 (#124H6; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), p-STAT3 (#9145S; Cell Signaling Technology), Janus kinase (JAK)-2 (#3230; Cell Signaling Technology), p-JAK2 (#3776; Cell Signaling Technology), occludin (#91131; Cell Signaling Technology), E-cadherin (#24E10; Cell Signaling Technology), Claudin-3 (#34-1700; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), ZO-1 (#ab276131; Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom), Lamin B (#12987-1-AP; Proteintech), GAPDH (#60004-1-Ig; Proteintech), and horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen). Protein blots were detected by ChemiDocTMXRS + system (Bio-Rad).


### Flow Cytometry for Apoptosis Detection
  The PE Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit I (559763; BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) was used to detect apoptosis according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The FHC cells were inoculated onto 6-well plates for overnight growth. After a series of treatment, cells with different treatments (including those in culture supernatant) were collected and transferred to corresponding tubes. Next, the cells were washed twice with PBS and centrifuged to obtain cell precipitates, which were resuspended in 1X Binding Buffer. Then, PE Annexin V and 7-AAD were added to each tube. The cells were gently vortex, incubated in the dark at room temperature for 15 minutes, and assayed by the CytoFLEX (Beckman Coulter Biotechnology [Suzhou] Co., Ltd, China). The apoptosis rate was analyzed using Flowjo software version 10 (Tree Star, Ashland, OR, USA).


### Immunohistochemistry and Alcian Blue-periodic Acid-Schiff Staining
  Mouse intestinal tissues were embedded in paraffin, cut into 3- to 5-µm-thick slices, and blocked with 10% goat serum (Sigma-Aldrich). The slices were then incubated with primary antibodies targeting ZO-1 (#ab276131; Abcam) and p-STAT3 (#9145S; Cell Signaling Technology), using the UltraSensitive SP immunohistochemistry (IHC) kit (Maxim Biotech, Fuzhou, China), based on the manufacturer’s instructions. Altered mucin secretion from epithelial goblet cells was evaluated using an Alcian blue-periodic acid-Schiff (AB-PAS) staining kit (Solarbio.G1285; Solarbio), according to the supplier’s instructions. Images were captured by an upright microscope (Olympus).


### Electron Microscopy
  The distal colon tissues of freshly euthanized mice were taken and cut into 3- to 5-mm strips using a scalpel. The intestinal tissues were immediately transferred into 2.5% glutaraldehyde and fixed for 2 hours, washed 3 times with 0.1 M phosphate buffer, and then fixed in 1% osmic acid for 2 hours at 4°C. Subsequently, the tissue samples underwent the treatments accordingly, based on the supplier’s instructions. Following counterstaining using 3% uranyl acetate and 2.7% lead citrate, the images were examined under an HT7800 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).


### Immunofluorescence Staining
  The FHC cells were seeded on round coverslips or confocal culture dishes and washed 3 times with precooled PBS after a series of treatments. Subsequently, the cells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 to 20 minutes and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X 100 (Beyotime Technology) for 15 minutes. Finally, the cells were blocked using 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 1 hour and incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight in dark conditions. The next day, cells were incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies (Invitrogen) for 1 hour at 37°C. Immunofluorescence staining images of ZO-1 (#ab221547; Abcam), STAT3 (#124H6; Cell Signaling Technology), and p-STAT3 (#9145S; Cell Signaling Technology) were acquired using an Olympus FV1200 fluorescence microscope (Tokyo, Japan) and a Leica TCS SP8 confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany).


### Measurement of Transepithelial Resistance
  To assess the transepithelial resistance of the intestinal epithelial barrier, Caco-2 cells were seeded onto the top of transwell chambers. The transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) at 24 hours was measured using the Millicell-ERS system (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). A constant temperature was maintained throughout the measurement process. Three different directions were estimated for each transwell for a total of 3 times. The average value was used to calculate the TEER.41,43


### TUNEL Staining
  Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay (C1090; Beyotime Technology) was used to detect apoptotic cells in paraffin-embedded mouse intestinal tissue sections. Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were placed on the slides and subjected to a series of treatments such as dewaxing with dimethyl benzene. Subsequently, tissue sections were incubated with TUNEL detection solution for 1 hour at 37°C in the dark. The nuclei were stained with DAPI (Beyotime Technology). Finally, the images were observed under an Olympus FV1200 fluorescence microscope (Japan), and the proportion of TUNEL-positive cells was calculated using Image J software.43


### Luminex Liquid Suspension Chip Detection
  The supernatant obtained from FHC cells challenged with LPS (1 µg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich) and treated with or without HCA (5 µM) was centrifuged and stored at −80°C until subsequent analysis. Cytokines were detected using the Luminex liquid suspension chip assay by Wayen Biotechnologies (Shanghai, China). Cytokine levels were measured using the Bio-Plex Pro Human Cytokine Grp I Panel 27-plex kit (#M500KCAF0Y), with each cytokine having a distinct lower limit of detection. Briefly, 50 µL of the supernatant were added to 96-well plates embedded with microbeads and incubated at room temperature for 30 minutes, followed by the addition of a detection antibody and incubation for another 30 minutes. Finally, after incubation with streptavidin-PE for 10 minutes, the values of each well were read using the calibrated Bio-Plex 200 System (Luminex Corporation, Austin, TX, USA).


### Molecular Docking
  The crystal structure of STAT3 protein was obtained from RCSB PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org/) under the PDB accession code 6NJS,44 and the structure of HCA was obtained from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Subsequently, molecular docking was performed using AutoDock version 4.2.6. Docking results were visualized and analyzed using the PyMOL 2.2.0 and Discovery Studio Client v19.1.0 software.45


### Statistical Analysis
  Animal experiments in the study were performed in randomized groups, and all experiments were repeated at least 3 times. All animal experiments in this study were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University (Approval number: 20181001). All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Results from multiple experiments are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Student’s t test, Mann-Whitney U test, or Dunnett’s t test were used to evaluate the differences between the 2 groups, as appropriate. Differences in quantitative data between 3 or more groups were compared using 1-way analysis of variance (ANOVA); P < .05 was considered statistically significant.


## Results

### Toxicology of HCA in vivo and in vitro
  To verify its toxicity in vivo, HCA (Figure 1A) was intraperitoneally injected into C57BL/6J WT mice once a day for 7 consecutive days (Figure 1B). Based on previous studies, we preliminarily selected injection doses of 30 mg/kg and 50 mg/kg.31,36 As indicated in Figure 1C, no significant inflammatory damages were observed in the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney of HCA-treated mice compared with healthy mice, following intraperitoneal injection of HCA at doses of 30 mg/kg and 50 mg/kg. Additionally, there were no significant differences in serum ALT and AST levels among the groups, and the levels were within the normal range (ALT, 10.06-96.47 U/L; AST, 36.31-235.48 U/L; Figure 1D, 1E), suggesting that HCA does not have side effects on the liver function of healthy mice. Meanwhile, the CCK-8 assay results showed that pretreatment with HCA (0-100 µM) for 24 hours reduced the viability of FHC cells to 89% when the concentration reached 10 µM and showed no obvious impact at concentrations ≤5 µM. Similarly, the viability of Caco-2 cells was not significantly affected when the concentration of HCA was increased to 5 µM (Figure 1F-1H). Based on these results, we determined the appropriate therapeutic doses of HCA as 30 mg/kg and 50 mg/kg for in vivo studies, and 1 µM and 5 µM for in vitro studies.


### Alleviating Effect of HCA on DSS-induced Experimental Colitis
  To determine whether HCA mitigates intestinal inflammation, we assessed the pharmacological effects of HCA (30 mg/kg and 50 mg/kg) in a DSS-induced colitis model. Sulfasalazine, a commonly used drug for the treatment of UC, was used as a positive control (Figure 2A). Colitis symptoms were observed in mice stimulated with DSS compared with the control group, including weight loss, increased DAI, shortened colon length, elevated histological scores, epithelial damage, and ulceration formation, whereas mice treated with HCA alone showed little influence on these indices (Figure 2B-2G). These data indicated that the colitis model was successfully established. Compared with the DSS group, HCA and SASP alleviated the colitis symptoms, as demonstrated by a slower weight loss (Figure 2B), lower DAI (Figure 2C), reduced colon length shortening (Figure 2D, 2E), and lower histological scores (Figure 2F). Furthermore, histological analysis demonstrated that the inflammatory cell infiltration of the intestinal mucosa and widespread epithelial erosion in mice with DSS-induced colitis were reversed by HCA and SASP (Figure 2G).

  Next, compared with mice stimulated with DSS, the pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α) were significantly downregulated, whereas anti-inflammatory IL-10 levels were upregulated in the serum of DSS- and HCA/SASP-treated mice (Figure 2H). Notably, the effects of HCA (30 mg/kg) were equivalent to those of SASP, and its efficacy was enhanced at high concentrations (50 mg/kg; Figure 2B-2H).

  Taken together, these results suggest that HCA treatment can attenuate the inflammatory response in DSS-induced colitis in mice, without any serious side effects; however, the mechanism of alleviation remains unclear.


### 2ʹ-Hydroxycinnamaldehyde Improves Intestinal Mucosal Barrier in vivo
  Intestinal mucosal barrier damage plays an important role in the progression of UC, and restoring the damaged barrier can effectively reduce abnormal inflammatory responses and alleviate colitis.6,13,46 To examine whether the ameliorating effect of HCA was related to the intestinal mucosal barrier, we analyzed the expression of tight junction proteins and other intestinal mucosal barrier damage indicators in mouse intestinal tissues. Western blotting assays showed that cotreatment with DSS and HCA (30 mg/kg and 50 mg/kg) resulted in increased protein levels of ZO-1, occludin, Bcl-2, E-cadherin, and Claudin-3 and reduced Bax protein levels compared with mice stimulated with DSS (Figure 3A, 3B). Moreover, mucins can protect the mucus barrier, and AB-PAS staining demonstrated that the DSS-induced reduction of mucin secretion from epithelial goblet cells was significantly rescued by HCA in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 3C). Immunohistochemistry analysis further confirmed an increase in ZO-1 expression in the embedded colon tissues of mice, consistent with the western blotting results. The tight junction protein ZO-1 was substantially reduced in the colon of mice prompted by DSS, and the DSS-induced reduction of ZO-1 was restored by the intervention of HCA (Figure 3C).

  Transmission electron microscopy clearly showed that the control group had a normal ultrastructure with neatly arranged, abundant microvilli and normal epithelial tight junctions. In contrast, DSS treatment disrupted tight junctions and significantly reduced the number of microvilli in colonic epithelial cells. The extent of microvillus damage in the groups treated with DSS and HCA improved, and the number of microvilli increased (Figure 3D).47 In addition, accumulated IEC apoptosis damages the integrity of barrier.6,46 Additional intraperitoneal injections of HCA significantly reduced the red signals in the colonic sections of mice with colitis, suggesting a decreased number of TUNEL-positive cells (Figure 3E, 3F). These results strongly suggest that HCA can alleviate DSS-induced colitis by ameliorating intestinal mucosal barrier destruction.


### Alleviates Inflammation, Barrier Damage, and Apoptosis of IECs in vitro
  To investigate the role of HCA in the development of inflammation in vitro, we first examined the levels of different cytokines in the supernatants of FHC cells challenged with LPS alone or LPS and HCA (5 µM) using Luminex liquid suspension chip detection. As indicated in Figure 4A, heat map analysis of cytokine levels in the supernatants showed significant alterations in levels after HCA pretreatment. Among all cytokines, IL-6 showed the least variability within the group but the most significant reduction between the groups. In general, the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines showed a decreasing trend, whereas anti-inflammatory cytokine levels showed an increasing trend in the HCA administration group compared with the LPS group. Notably, the levels of MIP-1α, IL-2, and IL-8 were below the lower limit of detection and were thus excluded from further analysis (Figure 4B). Subsequent ELISA and qRT-PCR analyses confirmed similar trends, showing that HCA alone and DMSO had little influence on the FHC cells. In contrast, LPS increased the transcript levels of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α and decreased IL-10 levels (compared with the control group). However, these effects were reversed by HCA treatment (Supplementary Figure 2A-2H), demonstrating that HCA can alleviate cellular inflammatory responses. Transepithelial electrical resistance is commonly used to assess epithelial barrier integrity and is positively correlated with integrity.43 We found that LPS-induced reduction in TEER value was rescued by HCA pretreatment (Figure 4C). Western blotting analysis showed that LPS resulted in lower protein levels of ZO-1, occludin, Bcl-2, E-cadherin, and Claudin-3 and higher levels of Bax. Pretreatment with HCA inhibited LPS-induced protein changes in ZO-1, occludin, Bcl-2, E-cadherin, Claudin-3, and Bax expression (Figure 4D, 4E). The localization and expression of ZO-1 in FHC cells were detected by immunofluorescence staining. Consistent with the results of western blotting, HCA significantly increased the formation of ZO-1 at the cell border compared with LPS (Figure 4F). Moreover, flow cytometry was used to detect the anti-apoptotic effects of HCA on FHC cells. As shown in Figure 4G and 4H, when FHC cells were incubated with LPS, the rate of apoptosis was significantly increased but mitigated by HCA treatment. Taken together, these data indicate that HCA attenuates LPS-induced cellular inflammation, barrier dysfunction, loss of tight junction proteins, and cell apoptosis.

  Collectively, it is evident that HCA alleviates barrier disruption and reduces IEC apoptosis in both colitis mice and LPS-stimulated inflammatory cells. Nevertheless, the molecular pathway by which HCA relieves the progression of colitis warrants further investigation.


### Inhibits the Activation of STAT3 via Directly Targeting STAT3
  To explore the potential underlying mechanisms, 2 Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) data sets (GSE87466 and GSE87473) were chosen for further analysis. The upregulated differentially expressed genes in UC colon tissues compared with healthy controls were analyzed using the GEO2R online tool. The analysis revealed that a total of 637 genes were significantly upregulated at the mRNA level in the 2 data sets (Supplementary Figure 3A). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis showed significant enrichment of the Janus kinase (JAK)-STAT signaling pathway (Supplementary Figure 3B). Moreover, the search tool for recurring instances of neighboring genes (STRING) analysis was used to explore the interaction networks among these upregulated genes. Subsequently, the Cytoscape 3.10.0 software was utilized to screen and visualize the core genes, including STAT3 (Supplementary Figure 3C). To validate our findings, we performed a differential expression analysis of STAT3 in the 2 data sets (GSE38713 and GSE92415). The expression levels of STAT3 were significantly upregulated in both data sets, consistent with our analysis (Supplementary Figure 3D, 3E). Based on these results, we identified STAT3 as a hub gene in UC, suggesting that the JAK-STAT3 signaling pathway may play an important role in the disease. It is noteworthy that phosphorylation of JAK mediates STAT3 activation.48 Several studies have demonstrated that the activation of STAT3 is associated with the disease activity of UC and can enhance inflammatory responses.24,49,50 Considering that IL-6 is the primary activator of STAT3 in inflammation and that IL-6 levels were significantly downregulated after HCA treatment, we further explored the effect of HCA on the STAT3 signaling pathway.

  Using western blotting analysis and immunofluorescence staining, we confirmed the inhibitory effect of HCA on STAT3 activation in FHC cells. As illustrated in Figure 5A and 5B, the application of HCA significantly reduced p-STAT3 expression and inhibited STAT3 activation compared with LPS stimulation. Furthermore, we investigated the effect of HCA on LPS-induced STAT3 nuclear translocation and found that the translocation of STAT3 from the cytoplasm to the nucleus was significantly blocked by HCA treatment (Figure 5C, 5D and Supplementary Figure 4). Similarly, IHC staining and western blotting analysis of the colonic tissues of mice with DSS-induced colitis further confirmed that HCA pretreatment rescued the upregulated protein levels of p-STAT3 (Figure 5E, 5F). Importantly, we observed that neither JAK2 phosphorylation nor total STAT3 and JAK2 expression was significantly affected by HCA in both inflammatory cells and intestinal tissues of mice with colitis (Figure 5A, 5F), suggesting that HCA may inhibit STAT3 activation via directly interacting with STAT3 itself. Accordingly, the interaction between HCA and STAT3 was analyzed using molecular docking analysis, and the results revealed that HCA showed an interaction with STAT3, with a binding affinity of −5.42 kcal/mol. Further analysis showed that HCA formed hydrogen bond interactions with TYR-446, GLU-444, and LYS-363 residues of STAT3 (Figure 5G). In summary, these data imply that HCA inhibits STAT3 activation via binding to STAT3.


### Alleviates Inflammation, Barrier Disruption, and Apoptosis via Inhibiting STAT3 Activation
  To investigate the role of STAT3 in mediating the therapeutic effects of HCA on colitis in vitro, we conducted knockdown or overexpression experiments in FHC cells. Three siRNAs targeting STAT3 were used to reduce STAT3 expression in FHC cells, and siSTAT3-3 demonstrated the highest knockdown efficacy. Meanwhile, plasmid-STAT3 also effectively increased STAT3 expression in cells (Supplementary Figure 5A, 5B, and Supplementary Figure 6A, 6B). The levels of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α in LPS- and siNC-treated FHC cells were dramatically increased, and IL-10 levels were dramatically decreased. However, this dysregulation of cytokines was reversed by siSTAT3 or HCA treatment (Supplementary Figure 7A-7D). Immunofluorescence staining and western blotting assays showed that STAT3 knockdown and/or HCA pretreatment reduced the LPS-mediated increases in p-STAT3 levels (Figure 6A, 6B). Compared with LPS- and siNC-treated cells, Caco-2 cells incubated with LPS and transfected with siSTAT3, with or without HCA pretreatment, showed significantly increased TEER. The damaged epithelial barrier in Caco-2 cells cocultured with LPS was also restored by cotreatment with HCA and siNC (Figure 6C). Consistent with the TEER changes, the western blotting analysis revealed that ZO-1, occludin, Bcl-2, E-cadherin, and Claudin-3 protein levels were significantly decreased, and Bax levels were significantly increased in LPS- and siNC-treated FHC cells in comparison to control cells, whereas silencing STAT3 and/or HCA counteracted the disruption of intercellular tight junction proteins and the reduction of anti-apoptotic proteins by LPS (Figure 6D and Supplementary Figure 7E). Immunofluorescence staining for ZO-1 showed the same results as the western blotting analysis (Figure 6E). Furthermore, flow cytometry indicated that FHC cells transfected with siSTAT3 had a lower apoptosis rate compared with siNC-transfected cells in the presence of LPS, whereas no difference was observed in siSTAT3-transfected FHC cells compared with cells co-incubated with siSTAT3 and HCA or siNC and HCA (Supplementary Figure 7F, 7G). It is worth mentioning that the barrier restoration and reduced apoptosis did not obviously differ between FHC cells treated with siSTAT3, siNC and HCA, and siSTAT3 and HCA.

  Moreover, a series of subsequent analyses showed that FHC cells stimulated with LPS and transfected with plasmid-STAT3 exhibited higher protein levels of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α; lower levels of IL-10; and increased p-STAT3 expression compared with cells treated with LPS and plasmid-NC. The application of HCA significantly inhibited cellular inflammation and STAT3 activation. Interestingly, when plasmid-STAT3 was introduced, it weakened the mitigating effect of HCA, as shown by the upregulation of IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and p-STAT3 levels and the downregulation of IL-10 levels in FHC cells co-incubated with LPS, plasmid-STAT3, and HCA (compared with LPS, plasmid-NC, and HCA treatment; Figure 6F, 6G and Supplementary Figure 8A-8D). The LPS-induced reduction of TEER in Caco-2 monolayer cells was exacerbated by plasmid-STAT3, which reversed HCA-induced remission (Figure 6H). Accordingly, the destruction of the intestinal epithelial barrier and apoptosis of IECs by LPS were aggravated by STAT3 overexpression, as manifested by marked decreases in ZO-1, occludin, Bcl-2, E-cadherin, and Claudin-3 levels and a significant increase in Bax levels; these LPS-induced disruptive effects were mitigated by HCA, and the pharmacological effects of HCA on inflammation were suppressed after STAT3 overexpression (Figure 6I, 6J and Supplementary Figure 8E). Flow cytometry also verified that the apoptosis rate of LPS-stimulated FHC cells was significantly increased after transfection with plasmid-STAT3, and the rate of LPS-, HCA-, and plasmid-NC-treated cells was decreased vs that in FHC cells cocultured with LPS and plasmid-NC. When cells were treated with LPS and HCA plus plamid-STAT3, the decreased apoptosis rate induced by HCA was inhibited (Supplementary Figure 8F, 8G).

  In summary, the mitigating effect of HCA was not observed in LPS-primed FHC cells transfected with siSTAT3 (Figure 6A-6E and Supplementary Figure 7A-7G). Similarly, STAT3 overexpression weakened the alleviating effect of HCA compared with HCA and plasmid-NC treatment (Figure 6F-6J and Supplementary Figure 8A-8G). These results indicate that STAT3 mediates the therapeutic effects of HCA on colitis.

  Collectively, our study highlights the role of STAT3 in the alleviation of inflammation, barrier restoration, and apoptosis reduction by HCA.


### Knockout of STAT3 Attenuates the Alleviating Effect of HCA on DSS-induced Colitis
  To verify whether HCA alleviates DSS-induced colitis in mice via STAT3 inhibition, we used mice with a conditional STAT3 deletion in enterocyte (STAT3ΔIEC). Neither STAT3fl/fl nor STAT3ΔIEC mice showed obvious intestinal inflammation without DSS treatment. Pretreatment with HCA (50 mg/kg) and/or STAT3 knockout significantly alleviated colitis symptoms in STAT3fl/fl mice stimulated with DSS, as indicated by increased body weight (Figure 7A), decreased DAI (Figure 7B), and longer colon length (Figure 7C). Histological examination of colon tissues revealed that STAT3fl/fl mice cotreated with DSS and HCA and STAT3ΔIEC mice treated with DSS with or without HCA showed less inflammatory cell infiltration, increased numbers of crypts, lower histological scores (Figure 7D), and increased mucin secretion from epithelial goblet cells vs DSS-induced colitis mice (STAT3fl/fl; Figure 7E). However, in STAT3ΔIEC mice treated with DSS, HCA pretreatment had no significant effect on these metrics, and the regulatory effect of HCA on colitis severity was not detected (Figure 7A-7E).

  Subsequent ELISA analysis demonstrated that the upregulation of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α and the downregulation of IL-10 in the serum of STAT3fl/fl mice treated with DSS were reversed by HCA administration and/or the absence of STAT3 expression, whereas the remitting effect of HCA was not observed in STAT3ΔIEC mice (Figure 7F). Moreover, western blotting assays confirmed that HCA treatment and/or STAT3 knockout markedly increased the protein expression of ZO-1, occludin, Bcl-2, E-cadherin, and Claudin-3 and decreased the levels of Bax in the colon tissues of STAT3fl/fl mice treated with DSS. The results of IHC staining for ZO-1 expression in colon sections were also consistent with those of western blotting analysis (Figure 7H-7J). Transmission electron microscopy clearly demonstrated that both HCA treatment and genetic deletion of STAT3 in STAT3ΔIEC mice improved the disruption of tight junctions caused by colitis (Figure 7K). Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick end labeling staining indicated a reduction in the number of TUNEL-positive cells (Figure 7G, 7L). Nevertheless, neither tight junction protein expression nor apoptosis rates were significantly altered in the colonic mucosa of STAT3ΔIEC mice with or without HCA treatment (Figure 7G-7L). In summary, our results indicate that the mitigating effect of HCA on DSS-induced colitis in mice depends on the inhibition of STAT3 activation, which plays a crucial role in the regulation of intestinal inflammation by HCA.


## Discussion
  In conclution, HCA, isolated from Cinnamomum cassia, can attenuate the development of auto-inflammatory and chronic inflammatory diseases such as psoriasis.25,26,36 Although HCA has previously been shown to have anti-inflammatory effects, whether it can alleviate UC has not been investigated. To the best of our knowledge, the present study is the first to demonstrate the protective role of HCA against intestinal inflammation. The results indicate that HCA overtly inhibited the production of inflammatory factors, reduced epithelial cell apoptosis, and improved intestinal epithelial barrier dysfunction in both DSS-induced colitis mice and LPS-treated FHC cells. Further investigations revealed that HCA exerted its anti-inflammatory effects via binding to STAT3 and inhibiting its phosphorylation. Importantly, the efficacy of HCA was significantly affected by the activation levels of STAT3, supporting the conclusion that STAT3 mediates the anti-inflammatory effects of HCA. Our findings demonstrate a novel treatment strategy with potential therapeutic implications for UC.

  In this study, we first explored the toxicity of different concentrations of HCA in vivo and in vitro and determined safe and effective concentrations for treatment. We used a 3% DSS murine colitis model that closely resembles human UC and provides excellent repeatability.51 Subsequently, we found that mice cotreated with DSS and HCA effectively improved the symptoms of DSS-induced colitis in a dose-dependent manner, as evidenced by reduced weight loss, decreased DAI, prevention of colon shortening, decreased histological scores, and less inflammatory cell infiltration. Moreover, cytokines are essential for the mucosa-associated immune system to maintain normal intestinal homeostasis. An imbalance of pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α can lead to disease states of intestinal homeostasis.41,52,53 Our assessment of serum inflammatory factors levels indicated that HCA considerably downregulated the serum levels of these pro-inflammatory factors. These data clearly confirm that HCA can alleviate the severity of colitis.

  The breach of the intestinal mucosal barrier during the development of UC can lead to a series of changes such as mucosal inflammation and immune activation, forming a vicious cycle of development of UC.46 Consequently, maintaining the integrity of the mucosal barrier plays a key role in reducing intestinal inflammation.6,16 Next, we explored whether HCA alleviates colitis by attenuating mucosal barrier dysfunction. As in our study, western blotting analysis, AB-PAS staining, IHC, and transmission electron microscopy clearly showed that HCA treatment reversed DSS-induced reductions in tight junction proteins, increased mucin secretion from epithelial goblet cells, and restored the destruction of tight junction in vivo. Moreover, LPS-stimulated FHC cells were used as an in vitro inflammatory model to evaluate the effects of HCA on inflammation and epithelial barrier function, followed by assessments of inflammatory responses and the expression of tight junction proteins, and measurement of TEER values. The FHC cell line is an ideal in vitro model of IECs and has been increasingly used in studies on colitis.42,54,55 Inflammatory response is an important indicator to evaluate colitis.56 Luminex liquid suspension chip detection was performed to identify differentially expressed inflammatory cytokines between FHC cells incubated with LPS alone and cells co-incubated with LPS and HCA. We found significant differences between the groups, with HCA treatment remarkably downregulating the levels of pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α. Of these, IL-6 was significantly downregulated in LPS-stimulated FHC cells after HCA treatment, which was consistent with the results obtained from ELISA and qRT-PCR analyses. In addition, our in vitro experiments demonstrated that HCA increased barrier-related protein expression and enhanced the TEER values in Caco-2 monolayer cells incubated with LPS. Caco-2 monolayer cells are often used to measure TEER values, and this method is convincing because of its user-friendly operation and high reproducibility.41–43,54,57 Moreover, apoptosis of IECs disrupts the TJ proteins, damaging the integrity of the barrier,6,43 which is a crucial indicator of barrier dysfunction. We observed that HCA reduced IECs apoptosis in vivo and in vitro using TUNEL staining and flow cytometry, respectively. Our results show for the first time that HCA can rescue inflammation, barrier disruption, and epithelial cell apoptosis in vivo and in vitro, providing novel insights into the effects of HCA in UC. However, the exact mechanism by which HCA repairs the intestinal mucosal barrier remains unclear.

  Increasing evidence suggests that inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 can activate JAK2 and subsequently phosphorylate STAT3, followed by their dimerization and nuclear translocation, thus aggravating damage to the intestinal mucosal barrier. Targeted blocking of STAT3 has emerged as a potential therapeutic strategy for alleviating UC.23,33,58 Consistent with these findings, our bioinformatics analysis also revealed that the JAK/STAT signaling pathway plays a crucial role in UC. Based on these insights, we examined the expression of phosphorylated STAT3 and the localization of STAT3 within and outside the nucleus before and after HCA treatment in LPS-induced inflammatory FHC cells as well as STAT3 phosphorylation levels in the colonic tissues of mice treated with DSS to investigate whether HCA affects the activation of STAT3. Our results showed that STAT3 activation was mitigated by HCA pretreatment in LPS-induced inflammatory FHC cells and mice with DSS-induced colitis. Interestingly, HCA had no significant effect on JAK2 phosphorylation or total STAT3 expression, indicating that HCA may interact directly with STAT3 itself. Based on this speculation, molecular docking analysis was performed to evaluate the binding of HCA to STAT3, which revealed a favorable binding energy of −5.42 kcal/mol between HCA and STAT3. These findings confirm that HCA directly interacts with STAT3, inhibiting its activation. These novel insights into the protective role of HCA in UC suggest that STAT3 inhibition may be involved in the HCA-mediated remission of colitis.

  To further investigate the mediating effect of STAT3, either siSTAT3 or plasmid-STAT3 was transfected into FHC cells to knock down or overexpress STAT3 in FHC cells, and the anti-inflammatory effect of HCA was then evaluated in vitro. Based on ELISA, western blotting analysis, immunofluorescence staining, TEER measurements, and flow cytometry, we confirmed that altered STAT3 activation levels due to STAT3 knockdown or overexpression in vitro influenced the ability of HCA to rescue inflammation, intestinal epithelial barrier damage, and cell apoptosis. We also used the Flox-villin-Cre system to delete STAT3 from the intestinal epithelium of mice (STAT3ΔIEC). As predicted, the STAT3ΔIEC mice were significantly less susceptible to DSS compared with STAT3fl/fl mice, as illustrated by less weight loss, lighter DAI score, increased colon length, lower histological scores, and reduced pro-inflammatory factors expression. Knockout of STAT3 in mice mitigated inflammatory responses, ameliorated barrier damage, and reduced cell apoptosis in the DSS-induced colitis model. Interestingly, the ameliorative effect of HCA on colitis was eliminated in the STAT3ΔIEC mice. These findings highlight the indispensable role of STAT3 in mediating the beneficial effects of HCA in alleviating colitis.


## Conclusions
  In conclusion, this study is the first to report the alleviating effects and mechanisms exerted by HCA on experimental colitis. Our data have clearly demonstrated that HCA effectively attenuates the destruction of tight junctions, reduces cell apoptosis, and ameliorates intestinal inflammation both in vivo and in vitro via directly interacting with STAT3 to inhibit its activation and downstream signaling. These results highlight the promising role of HCA as a potential therapeutic strategy for UC.
