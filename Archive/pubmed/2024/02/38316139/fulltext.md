# The SARS-CoV-2
Mpro Dimer-Based
Screening System: A Synthetic Biology Tool for Identifying Compounds
with Dimerization Inhibitory Potential


## Abstract
  

  The COVID-19 endemic remains a global concern. The search for effective antiviral candidates is still needed to reduce disease risk. However, the availability of high biosafety level laboratory facilities for drug screening is limited in number. To address this issue, a screening system that could be utilized at lower biosafety levels remains essential. This study aimed to develop a novel SARS-CoV-2 main protease (Mpro) dimer-based screening system (DBSS) utilizing synthetic biology in Escherichia coli BL21(DE3). We linked the SARS-CoV-2 Mpro with the DNA-binding domain of AraC regulatory protein, which regulates the reporter gene expression. Protein modeling and molecular docking showed that saquinavir could bind to AraC-Mpro both in its monomer and dimer forms. The constructed DBSS assay indicated the screening system could detect saquinavir inhibitory activity at a concentration range of 4–10 μg/mL compared to the untreated control (P ≤ 0.05). The Vero E6 cell assay validated the DBSS result that saquinavir at 4–10 μg/mL exhibited antiviral activity against SARS-CoV-2. Our DBSS could be used for preliminary screening of numerous drug candidates that possess a dimerization inhibitor activity of SARS-CoV-2 Mpro and also minimize the use of a high biosafety level laboratory.


## Introduction
  Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), which is caused by the Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome-Coronavirus-2 (SARS-CoV-2), still continues to pose a significant threat globally despite its recent change into an endemic status. As of July 14th, 2023, there have been 767 million cases and 6.9 million fatalities worldwide.1 Despite various preventive measures, including vaccination, therapeutic approaches must also be considered for the treatment of COVID-19 patients. Antiviral treatments continue to be employed in these efforts, as they are crucial for individuals who are waiting for vaccination, as well as for certain patients who are immunocompromised and unlikely to respond strongly to vaccines.2

  Drug discovery is a complex and time-consuming process that requires a significant investment. It is a challenging and expensive endeavor with a low success rate. Various strategies have been applied to speed up the research and development process of novel drug discovery; one of them is drug repurposing, in addition to utilizing natural products. In the case of COVID-19, drug repurposing has been implemented for existing antiviral drugs to target the viral protease.3,4 The main protease (Mpro) (EC: 3.4.22.69) or chymotrypsin-like protease (3CLpro) of SARS-CoV-2 hydrolyzes polyproteins into various structural and nonstructural proteins.5 The proteolytic activity of the main protease is only active when the single monomer polymerizes to form a dimeric structure.6 Therefore, this dimerization phenomenon is a promising therapeutic target for disrupting SARS-CoV-2 propagation and treating COVID-19.5

  Another major challenge in drug discovery is the requirement of high biosafety level (BSL) laboratories, which are limited in numbers in many lower middle-income countries. For instance, drug screening experiments involving SARS-CoV-2 require the use of BSL-3 laboratories. In addition, the use of such facilities might be costly for screening hundreds to thousands of drug candidates. Therefore, high-throughput drug screening systems that enable the rapid screening of numerous compounds without requiring the establishment of a high biosafety level laboratory are needed. Recently, various drug screening systems have been developed using synthetic peptide or engineered bacteria, for example,7,8 so that the experimental procedures for drug screening processes including the drug repurposing effort could be conducted with minimal interaction with high-risk pathogens.

  Here, we developed a novel synthetic biology-based screening system for identifying drug candidates with a SARS-CoV-2 Mpro dimerization inhibitory potential. The system, also known as dimer-based screening system (DBSS), was initially created based on studies conducted by Furuta et al.9 and Okada et al.10 In general, DBSS identifies potential drug candidate compounds using recombinant Escherichia coli based on their ability to interfere with the dimerization of the target protein. Giri-Rachman et al.11 have implemented this approach with modification of the AraC regulator protein for rapid screening of antitubercular drug candidates (Figure 1). Furthermore, Fibriani et al.12 have employed this screening approach to discover novel antiretroviral drugs for Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV).

  In this study, we developed a DBSS targeting SARS-CoV-2 Mpro to screen antiviral drug candidates for COVID-19. Furthermore, in order to validate the screening system, we used saquinavir, one of the well-known viral protease inhibitors. Recent studies have shown the potential of saquinavir as an antiviral drug for COVID-19 treatment.5,13−15 The known mechanism of saquinavir is it can effectively block the catalytic function of the viral protease by conferring a peptidomimetic scaffold due to the presence of a hydroxyethylene bond.14,16,17 Through molecular docking, our preliminary investigation also supported that saquinavir had the capability to inhibit Mpro dimerization. Overall, this current study demonstrated the potential of the SARS-CoV-2 Mpro DBSS to be further developed and applied to other infectious diseases.


## Results
and Discussion
  We designed a drug screening system for COVID-19 by targeting the dimerization of SARS-CoV-2 Mpro as illustrated in Figure 2. In principle, the technology applied manipulation of gene expression regulation in prokaryotes. To identify a dimerization inhibitory activity of drug candidate compounds, we constructed a fusion protein consisting of SARS-CoV-2 Mpro as the drug target and the DNA-binding domain of AraC as the regulator of the reporter gene expression. Then, for the screening system control, we also used bacteria culture that expresses only the AraC DNA-binding domain as shown in Figure 1. We employed E. coli BL21 (DE3) as the drug screening platform which expressed the AraC-Mpro fusion protein. The dimerization inhibitory activity of the tested drug candidate compound can be inferred from the high fluorescence of the reporter protein (emGFP) in the bacterial culture. Therefore, this system relied on the compatibility of the chemical structure of drug candidate compounds for binding to the dimerization interface of the target viral protein.


### In Silico
Studies: Construction of Fusion Protein and Molecular
Docking
  Amino acid residues involved in the SARS-CoV-2 Mpro dimerization interface were identified and reported in Figure 3.6,18−22 Detailed information about the role of these dimerization residues can be found in Table S1. These amino acids were known to be conserved.23,24 We hypothesized that the dimerization residues would also play a role in facilitating dimerization in the AraC-Mpro fusion protein. To confirm our hypothesis that the same residues in Mpro (Figure 3A) composed the interface in the fusion protein, we conducted a protein–protein docking simulation. The results showed that the dimerization interface of each monomer in the AraC-Mpro fusion protein (Figure 3C) interacted with each other (Figure 3D) and could be compared to the dimerization interface in Mpro (Figure 3B). However, a further in vitro test must be done to validate whether the interface in Mpro is similar to that in AraC-Mpro.

  Molecular docking was performed to examine the interaction between saquinavir, a well-known inhibitor of viral proteases, and the AraC-Mpro fusion protein. The results from molecular docking could provide a prediction on the interactions of saquinavir with Mpro in vitro. Saquinavir exhibited a higher binding affinity to the dimer interface in monomeric Mpro (−7.9 kcal/mol) (Figure 4A) than in monomeric AraC-Mpro (−7.2 kcal/mol) (Figure 4B). These findings suggest that saquinavir is capable of binding not only to the dimerization site of Mpro but also to the fused one (AraC-Mpro).

  Additionally, we also docked saquinavir with the protease in its dimeric form to investigate the possibility that it disrupts the formed dimer. The increased binding affinity of saquinavir to the dimer might indicate the possibility of saquinavir also binding to the already formed dimer besides the monomer (Figure 4C,D). These results indicate that there is a possibility that saquinavir might interact with residues in both monomers and destabilize the dimer, leading to its dissociation into a monomer.

  We hypothesized that saquinavir binds to the Mpro dimer interface, but in AraC-Mpro, there was the possibility that saquinavir binds to AraC instead of binding to the AraC-Mpro dimer interface and prevents AraC interaction with the promoter, leading to emGFP expression. To assess this possibility, we also performed molecular docking with the AraC DNA-binding domain in AraC-Mpro (Figure 4E). The results show that saquinavir is more likely to bind to the Mpro dimer interface in AraC-Mpro than to AraC as it exhibits a significantly higher binding affinity to the Mpro dimer interface compared to AraC (−5.5 kcal/mol).

  Figure 4A,B provides insight into the interactions of saquinavir with the dimerization interface of SARS-CoV-2 Mpro in its monomeric form. In the case of the Mpro monomer (Figure 4A), saquinavir interacted with dimerization residues A285, L286, and E290. Interaction with E290 can disrupt the salt bridge interaction R4, which is necessary for dimer stability.25 Similarly, interaction with L286 or A285 can potentially disturb the interaction with their respective partner residue (A285 and L286 from another monomer) which is crucial for dimer formation and catalytic structure.19,20,27,28 Meanwhile, in monomeric AraC-Mpro (Figure 4B), saquinavir was found to interact with S139 and E290. Interference of the interaction between S139 and its paired residue, such as Q299, plausibly hinders dimerization and affects the catalytic activity of Mpro.29,30 The interaction with E290 in the fusion protein could also affect the dimer stability. These observations might explain why saquinavir is still able to inhibit dimerization, despite its modified structure. There is an indication that the AraC-Mpro dimeric interface is similar to that in Mpro and there is not any major change in the dimeric interface.

  Finally, Figure 4C,D displays the interactions between saquinavir and the protease’s dimeric form. In Figure 4C, saquinavir shows interaction with the dimerization residues R4 and K137 in both Mpro monomers. Moreover, it also interacts with L286 from monomer 1 and E290 from monomer 2. R4 is known to be involved in the binding pocket at the Mpro dimer interface. Besides, this residue is also known to interact with K137 and E290 in neighboring monomer which is important to stabilize the dimer.19,23,25 Saquinavir is shown to interact with E290 in both Mpro’s monomeric and dimeric forms. Meanwhile, saquinavir is found to have interactions with A7 and V125 in one monomer of AraC-Mpro dimer but K137 and L286 in the other monomer (Figure 4D). The interaction with these residues could affect the dimer stability.6,19,33 To summarize, the multiple interactions of saquinavir with both Mpro monomers led to destabilization of the dimer. Both monomer dimerization prevention and dimer dissociation could be indicated by a high fluorescence value in the DBSS assay.


### Dimer-Based Screening System Validation
  We predicted that saquinavir could potentially inhibit dimerization. To test this hypothesis and the accuracy of our screening system, we carried out several in vitro assessments.


### Confirming the Presence
of Arac-Mpro in the Transformant
  The successful transformation to create recombinant E. coli containing the screening construct was confirmed by the presence of DNA bands ranging between approximately 1.5 and 3.0 kb (Figure 5), which demonstrated the presence of the target gene. Band 1 (Figure 5A), corresponding to the 2.50–3.0 kb marker, was estimated to be 2.6 kb in size and contained the AraC-Mpro and emGFP genes. In contrast, band 2 corresponded to the 1.5 kb marker and was predicted to be 1.4 kb in length; it contained only the fusion gene (AraC-Mpro). The presence of two bands was likely due to the use of a common T7 terminator for both genes (AraC-Mpro and emGFP) (Figure 5B).

  Upon sequencing and alignment of the screening circuit sequence, it was found to have 100% identity and similarity to the reference sequence (Figure S1). This result confirmed that the gene contained within the construct was in accordance with its intended design. As such, it could be expected that the system would exhibit its anticipated activity.


### Dimer-Based Screening System
Activity Validation Assay
  In the case where the fusion protein is expressed in optimal amounts, a decrease in fluorescence compared to that of the system control (AraC only) was observed. This phenomenon occurred due to the optimal production of functional AraC-Mpro dimers which repressed the expression of the reporter gene (emGFP) and resulted in a minimal fluorescence signal (Figure 1).

  As shown in Figure 6, the initial DBSS activity assay results indicated that optimal production of AraC-Mpro dimers occurred after a 4 h incubation. It resulted in a reduction in the measured fluorescence compared to the system control (AraC only), where the emGFP expression was not repressed due to the absence of the Mpro dimerization domain (see Figures 1 and 2). However, there was no significant difference in fluorescence between induced and noninduced E. coli. This observation can be attributed to the leakiness of the pET system, which utilizes both the T7 RNA polymerase promoter and lacUV5 promoter.35,36 Previous studies have shown that the leaky expression of T7 RNA polymerase could occur even without IPTG induction.37 This phenomenon led to the expression of the fusion protein as a repressor for the reporter gene expression. However, this phenomenon can be advantageous from practical and economic perspectives, as it eliminates the need for high-cost inducers such as IPTG.


### Saquinavir Inhibits Arac-Mpro Dimerization in the
DBSS Assay
  After that, we validated the developed DBSS using saquinavir to inhibit Mpro dimerization. If saquinavir exhibits inhibitory activity by binding to the dimerization interface residues of the protease, AraC will not bind to its promoter. This will allow for emGFP expression and high fluorescence, as shown in Figure 2. The screening test using DBSS in Figure 7A showed that saquinavir was effective in preventing the dimerization of the AraC-Mpro fusion protein at concentrations ranging from 4 to 10 μg/mL with the highest concentration at 6 μg/mL. At concentrations exceeding 6 μg/mL, the fluorescence level decreased; however, it remained significantly higher than E. coli without saquinavir treatment (P ≤ 0.05). Although the numerical values may differ, the primary focus of our observations is the patterns of fluorescence. Each experiment consistently included a control, namely, the AraC system control (SCC) and the untreated AraC-MproE. coli (baseline). These patterns serve to highlight the reliability of this study, for instance, the comparison between the AraC system control (SCC) and the untreated AraC-MproE. coli and also the treated AraC-MproE. coli compared to the untreated one.

  These results in Figure 7A showed us that the screening system worked as its intended design when inhibited with a dimerization inhibitor (as illustrated by Figure 7B) and in concordance with molecular docking resulted in Figure 4B or 4D. Furthermore, we demonstrated that the dimerization of the AraC-Mpro fusion could also be disrupted by a viral protease inhibitor. Additionally, the DBSS result confirmed the molecular docking result that demonstrated the ability of saquinavir to prevent the dimerization of SARS-CoV-2 Mpro.


### Cytotoxicity and Antiviral Activity of Saquinavir
on Vero E6
Cells
  In order to validate the DBSS results, we evaluated the antiviral performance of saquinavir in vitro. First, we examined saquinavir cytotoxicity on Vero E6 cells using the MTT assay. Viability of the Vero E6 cells decreased as the concentration of saquinavir increased (Figure 8). However, exposure to 10 μg/mL saquinavir for 72 h only reduced the cell viability by 11.01%. According to EN-ISO 10993-5,66 any drug candidate that reduces cell viability by more than 30% should be considered cytotoxic. Therefore, based on our findings, saquinavir could be considered nontoxic for clinical applications on mammalian cells. Additionally, the result is consistent with previous studies. Pereira and Vale67 reported that exposure of saquinavir at concentrations of less than 50 μM (33.54 μg/mL) for 72 h did not significantly reduce viability of A549 cells, human lung epithelial cells. Mahdi et al.68 also showed that the viability of HEK-293 T cells was reduced by more than 50% after the exposure of saquinavir at concentrations above 50 μM.

  Since DMSO is known to be toxic for cells, we also evaluated its toxicity at the concentration used in the assay to avoid biases. We found that DMSO at a concentration of 1% (v/v) increased Vero E6 cell viability compared to the untreated cells, even though it was not significant (P > 0.05) (Figure 8). Notably, another study reported a similar phenomenon where exposure of 1% DMSO for 72 h slightly stimulated cell growth.38

  We also calculated the 50% cytotoxicity concentration (CC50) of saquinavir on Vero E6 cells. CC50 is described as the concentration of drugs or substances that has the capability of reducing the cell viability by 50% after a certain period of exposure compared to untreated controls.39 In this study, saquinavir was shown to possess a CC50 of 114.74 μg/mL. It is higher than the CC50 of Lomibuvir and Telaprevir, another Mpro inhibitor.40,69 Currently, there are several different standards for drug candidate plant extracts to be considered cytotoxic. After exposure for 72 h, referring to the U.S. National Cancer Institute (NCI),41 plant crude extracts with CC50 < 30 μg/mL are considered cytotoxic for mammalian cells. Another reference sets CC50 < 20 μg/mL as a threshold for plant extracts as highly cytotoxic substances.42

  The antiviral activity of saquinavir on the SARS-CoV-2-infected cells was assessed based on the cytopathic effect reduction (CPER) assay. The presence of saquinavir at concentration range of 4–10 μg/mL resulted in the increase of viability of the SARS-CoV-2-infected cells compared to the untreated cells (0 μg/mL saquinavir), showing the inhibition of viral infection (Figure 8). The maximum antiviral activity of saquinavir was reached at a concentration of 4 μg/mL with 75% CPE reduction. However, at higher concentrations, we observed a gradual decrease of the cell viability. Considering the cytotoxicity and antiviral activity assay results, we concluded that saquinavir at a concentration range of 4–10 μg/mL had potential in disrupting SARS-CoV-2 propagation in vitro.

  The results showed that the novel drug screening system for COVID-19, the SARS-CoV-2 Mpro DBSS, was successfully developed, and its performance has been validated using saquinavir. As predicted in the molecular docking (Figure 4), in the DBSS validation assay, the modified Mpro of the fusion protein could not be dimerized in the presence of a protease dimerization inhibitor (Figure 7). Furthermore, the screened antiviral drug candidate, which was saquinavir at a concentration of 4–10 μg/mL, has been proven to effectively disrupt SARS-CoV-2 infection (Figure 8). Interestingly, using a different screening system, recombinant protease-based, Chiou et al.17 showed that the effective concentration of saquinavir mesylate in inhibiting SARS-CoV-2 Mpro was in the same range, which was 9.92 μM (7.61 μg/mL; where the molecular weight is 766.9 g/mol). However, another study showed a higher concentration of saquinavir disrupting viral propagation in vitro. Mahdi et al.68 found that treatment of saquinavir at concentration of 31.4 μM (21.06 μg/mL) inhibited SARS-CoV-2 Mpro in HEK-293 T cells by 50%.

  The current work is a proof-of-concept of the DBBS for drug discovery purposes. Further assessments are needed to verify the accuracy and robustness of the developed drug screening system in identifying antiviral drug candidates from various types of natural compounds and materials. Therefore, DBSS could be used for the preliminary screening of drug candidates. Even though DBSS cannot replace high biosafety level laboratories, DBSS could help minimize their usage for drug candidate screening. As previously shown, the developed DBSS is able to identify the viral protease dimerization inhibitory activity of drug candidate compounds. However, the optimal concentration can only be determined after in vivo experiments using representative model organisms since the in vivo toxicity level and effectiveness of the drug compounds could be different from the in vitro experiments.44 Hence, the preliminary screening approach using DBSS could be further developed for drug discovery purposes.


## Conclusion
  In summary, this study developed a DBSS utilizing a synthetic biology approach to discover a SARS-CoV-2 Mpro dimerization inhibitor. In the screening system, we successfully constructed and expressed fusion protein which consisted of SARS-CoV-2 Mpro and the DNA-binding domain of the AraC regulatory protein. Molecular docking simulation showed that AraC-Mpro still could interact with the same dimerization interface amino acid residues as Mpro. Moreover, the interaction between saquinavir, a viral protease inhibitor, and dimerization residues in AraC-Mpro suggested that the screening system worked as intended. The system could detect the inhibitory activity at a certain range of doses and was further confirmed through cell culture assay. Therefore, our DBSS is deemed suitable for preliminary screening and understanding the mechanism of numerous drug candidates. Moreover, DBSS could also be further developed, including drug discovery endeavors for other infectious diseases.


## Methods

### In Silico Study: DBSS Construction and Molecular
Docking

#### Modeling the Fusion Protein
  We fused codon optimized Mpro gene sequence (Patent No. P00202110062)45 with codon optimized AraC DNA-binding domain gene sequence (Patent No. P00201704939)11 to create fusion protein gene encoding AraC-Mpro. The three-dimensional structure of the fusion protein was modeled using RoseTTA-Fold.46 The intramolecular stability of the fusion protein was refined using the FoldX and mTM-Align algorithm.47,48 Then, residue numbers in AraC-Mpro were modified so that the sequence of Mpro in AraC-Mpro would be in consensus with Mpro.


#### Molecular Docking
  Molecular docking was performed to predict how saquinavir interacts with both Mpro and AraC-Mpro. The chemical structure of saquinavir (CID ID: 441243) was constructed using Chem3D. The grid box was positioned on the protein at the active site and dimerization site of Mpro where the location of the dimerization residue was identified based on literature review.6,18−22 The grid box volume was reduced to 30,000 points with a spacing of 1.0 Å while still encompassing the ligand-binding area to ensure targeted docking. Several algorithms, including POCASA 1.1, CASTp 3.0, and PrankWeb, were used to predict the ligand-binding region.49−51 Subsequently, targeted molecular docking analysis was performed using AutoDock and AutoDock Vina.52 The dimer structure of the fusion protein was visualized using HADDOCK2.4 Web Server.53 Meanwhile, the results of the docking were visualized in 2D using Discovery Studio and in 3D using PyMOL.54,55


#### Construction of Expression
Vector
  An expression vector for the fusion protein was constructed by using pET-23a(+). The codons SARS-CoV-2 Mpro gene (Patent No. P00202110062)45 were optimized using JCat.56 The codon-optimized SARS-CoV-2 Mpro sequence was then fused with a codon optimized DNA-binding domain sequence from E. coli AraC (Patent No. P00201704939),11 creating a synthetic gene encoding the complete AraC-Mpro fusion protein. An emGFP gene was also included into the designed screening circuit, as shown in Figure 1. The synthetic gene was synthesized and cloned into the plasmid vector, creating pET-23a(+)-AraC-Mpro as shown in Figure 2.


### In Vitro Experiment: System Validation and
Cell Culture Assay

#### Transformation of E. coli BL21
(DE3)
  The pET23a(+)-AraC-Mpro plasmid was transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) (ATCC, USA) bacteria using the heat-shock method. The transformants were subsequently cultured in sterile Luria–Bertani (LB) broth containing 100 μg/mL ampicillin at 37 °C with 200 rpm agitation for 1.5 h. Afterward, the recombinant E. coli colonies were grown on LB agar containing 100 μg/mL ampicillin at 37 °C for 16 h. Recombinant E. coli expressing only AraC was also grown as the system control. To confirm the presence of the target gene in the transformant bacteria, colony PCR was performed using T7 universal primer and the PCR results were visualized using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis.

  The DNA sequence of the transformed plasmid was also confirmed by DNA sequencing. Plasmid isolation and purification were carried out using a High-Speed Plasmid Mini Kit (Geneaid, New Taipei City, Taiwan). The purity of the isolated plasmids was determined based on A260/A280 absorption using a NanoDrop Spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher, Waltham, USA). The isolated plasmids were then visualized by using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Target bands visible on the gel, which indicate the screening circuit in the construct (AraC-Mpro to emGFP), were excised and sequenced. Additional primers were designed to cover part of the AraC-Mpro coding region and the AraC promoter (Forward: 5′-GGTGTTCACGCTGGTACTGA-3′; Reverse: 5′-GCGTCACACTTTGCTATGCC-3′). The acquired screening circuit sequences were processed and pairwise aligned to the designed construct sequences using EMBOSS Water.57


#### SARS-CoV-2 Mpro DBSS Activity
Validation Assay
  After being activated twice, the recombinant E. coli was grown in fresh LB medium containing 100 μg/mL ampicillin until their optical density (OD600) reached 0.3–0.4. They were then induced with 1.0 mM isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. The incubated culture was then transferred into a transparent 96-well microtiter plate for absorbance measurement (λ = 600 nm) and subsequently into a black 96-well plate for fluorescence measurement (excitation at λ = 475 nm and emission at λ = 500–550 nm) using GloMax Discover Microplate Reader GM3000 (Promega, Madison, USA). The assay was performed in two independent experiments with three technical replicates in each experiment (n = 20). The fluorescence values were corrected by the absorbance values of the culture at the time of analysis. The relative fluorescence unit (RFU) was calculated as follows.


#### Compound Preparation
  Saquinavir (MW = 670.85 g/mol) was purchased from AdooQ Bioscience, India (purity: ≥98% HPLC). Saquinavir powder was solubilized in 100% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and stored at −20 °C. For the DBSS assay, saquinavir was diluted to final concentrations of 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 μg/mL in DMSO, where the final concentration of DMSO was 5% (v/v). Exposure to 5% DMSO for up to 6 h did not significantly affect the growth of E. coli cells.58 For the cytotoxicity assay, saquinavir was diluted to final concentrations of 4, 6, 8, and 10 μg/mL in complete Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA), while for the antiviral activity assay, complete DMEM supplemented with 2% FBS was used as the diluent instead of 10%. For all assays using Vero E6 cells, the final concentration of DMSO was 1% (v/v), since the DMSO exceeding 1% is considered toxic for mammalian cells.59


#### Dimerization Inhibitory
Activity of Saquinavir using SARS-CoV-2
Mpro DBSS
  Then, we evaluated the performance of the developed DBSS using saquinavir, a viral protease inhibitor. To validate the DBSS working as its intended design, the E. coli BL21 (DE3) expressing AraC only was used as the system control that continuously expressed reporter gene in the presence of a dimerization inhibitor (Figure 1).

  The bacterial culture and saquinavir were added to a transparent 96-well microtiter plate for a total volume of 100 μL. The mixture was then incubated in a shaker incubator at 37 °C and 200 rpm for 4 h. After incubation, absorbance and fluorescence measurements were taken as described before. The assay was performed in two independent experiments with three technical replicates in each experiment (n = 20).


### Cells
and Virus
  African green monkey kidney epithelial cell line (Vero) E6 was maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS; 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) was also added to prevent the growth of microbial contaminants. The confluent cell monolayer was transferred into a new vessel every 2–4 days.

  SARS-CoV-2 (GISAID Accession ID: EPI_ISL_4004658), isolated from Bogor, Indonesia, in May 2020, was propagated in the Vero E6 cell line. The viral titer was determined using the 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) assay, and the TCID50 value was converted into plaque-forming unit (PFU) by dividing it by 0.7.60 The viral culture was preserved at −80 °C. All experiments involving viruses was conducted in a certified BSL-3 facility at the Indonesian National Research and Innovation Agency (BRIN), Indonesia, with the experiment protocols approved by the Biosafety and Ethics Committees of BRIN.


### Cytotoxicity of Saquinavir
on Vero E6 Cells
  The MTT (3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2,5 diphenyl tetrazolium bromide) assay was performed to assess the cytotoxicity of saquinavir on Vero E6 cells. Briefly, Vero E6 cells were seeded in 96-well plates (2 × 104 cells/well) and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. After overnight incubation, the culture medium was discarded and the diluted saquinavir was added to the confluent cell monolayers (100 μL/well) with three technical replicates (n = 3). After incubation for 72 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2, the supernatant was discarded and MTT (0.5 mg/mL) was added 100 μL/well, followed by incubation for 3–4 h, at 37 °C with 5% CO2, in dark conditions. To dissolve the formazan crystals, DMSO was added (100 μL/well) and incubated at room temperature for exactly 10 min. The absorbance was read at 570 nm using a Varioskan LUX Multimode Microplate Spectrophotometer (ThermoScientific, USA). The cells without compound treatment were used as the cell viability control. The cell viability was calculated as follows.The 50% cytotoxicity concentration (CC50) value was obtained by a linear regression calculation.


### Antiviral Activity
of Saquinavir against SARS-CoV-2 on Vero
E6 Cells
  To validate the DBSS assay results, we challenged the SARS-CoV-2-infected Vero E6 cells with saquinavir. The in vitro antiviral activity was determined based on the reduction of the cytopathic effects (CPEs) on the treated infected cells, compared with the untreated infected cells.61 The confluent Vero E6 cell monolayers at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well were infected with SARS-CoV-2 at a MOI (multiple of infection) of 0.1, equal to 2000 PFU/well, in 100 μL of DMEM supplemented with 2% FBS and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. The plate was shaken every 15 min for maximum viral adsorption. After the incubation, the inoculum was removed and the diluted saquinavir solutions were added to the infected cells (100 μL/well), followed by incubation for 72 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The untreated (0 μg/mL saquinavir) noninfected cells were used as the cell viability control (minimal CPEs), while the untreated infected cells were used as the cell infection control (maximum CPEs). For the vehicle control, 1% DMSO-containing medium was used. The assay was performed with six technical replicates (n = 6). After incubation, the cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde and stained with a 0.5% crystal violet (CV) solution. CPE on each well was observed under an inverted microscope before and after CV staining.


### Statistical Analysis
  All data processing and statistical analysis was performed using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft, Redmont, USA) and SPSS Statistics (IBM, New York, USA). Data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation. The corrected fluorescence test results were analyzed using Welch’s ANOVA after checking the assumptions of homogeneity of variance and normal distribution with Levene’s and Shapiro-Wilk’s tests, respectively.62,63 Afterward, the Games-Howell posthoc test was performed, where P ≤ 0.05 was considered significantly different. Statistical differences for cell culture assay were tested using Student’s t test for parametric data or Chi-square test for nonparametric data.
