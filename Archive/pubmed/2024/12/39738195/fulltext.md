# Computational biology assisted exploration of phytochemicals derived natural inhibitors to block BZLF1 gene activation of Epstein–Bar virus in host


## Abstract
  The Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) is widespread and has been related to a variety of malignancies as well as infectious mononucleosis. Despite the lack of a vaccination, antiviral medications offer some therapy alternatives. The EBV BZLF1 gene significantly impacts viral replication and infection severity. The current study performed computer-assisted techniques to analyses the potential drug candidates against Epstein–Barr virus toxin Zta [Human gamma-herpesvirus 4 (Epstein–Barr virus)] from phytochemicals derived natural inhibitors. Various bioinformatics methods were employed to predict and analyze the toxin protein structure obtained from NCBI, and its secondary and tertiary structures were predicted using PSIPRED and the AlphaFold. ProtParam was used to assess physiochemical characteristics. Natural inhibitors were found in the literature and PubChem, tested with PyRx, and performed blind docking by using CB-Dock, then the top selected drug candidate from natural inhibitors was analyzed for possible drug development applications using preADMET, Molinspiration, and MD simulations. Density functional Theory analysis was executed to predict the transition energies and the reactivity. Imperatorin was the best candidate for developing the drug against toxin protein Zta coded by the BZLF1 gene because it exhibited the lowest binding energy (− 6.3 kcal/mol) during natural inhibitor screening. Imperatorin’s compliance with Lipinski’s Rule of 5 and favorable pharmacokinetics make it an ideal therapeutic agent against EBV. Since vaccines and medications are crucial for treating infectious diseases and cancer, computational approaches seem promising and less costly to design and discover potential drug candidates and vaccines with the help of in silico methods but further in vitro research is required for experimental validation.


## Introduction
  The Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) is a prevalent herpes virus in the human population that has been associated with many types of cancer, such as Burkitt’s lymphoma, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, and particular lymphomas in individuals with compromised immune systems1. In addition, EBV is accountable for the occurrence of infectious mononucleosis which is prevalent and self-restricting disease primarily impacts individuals in adolescence and early adulthood2. According to a study, it is estimated that over 90% of the worldwide population has the potential to become in contact with EBV at some point3. Unfortunately, effective vaccines against EBV are still in limited availability4. The treatment options for EBV include the use of antiviral medications and supportive interventions to address symptoms and mitigate potential complications related to the infection5. The life cycle of the EBV may be categorized into two distinct phases, namely latent and lytic6. The replication and dissemination of the virus are heavily contingent upon the lytic phase, during which the BZLF1 protein, produced by the BZLF1 gene, plays a crucial role in activating early lytic genes7. Virus particles remain in host B cells during the latent phase. There, they show limited activation of viral genes to escape being identified by the immune system8. BZLF1 functions as a transcription factor that exhibits special affinity for certain DNA sequences within the viral genome, hence beginning the transcription process of early lytic genes9. BZLF1 demonstrates interactions with biological components of the host and exerts an effect on many cellular pathways, including cell cycle control, apoptosis, and immunological signaling. Consequently, it has the potential to contribute to the pathogenesis of the virus10.

  Natural compounds with antimicrobial activity, such as flavonoids, alkaloids, and terpenes, are important to the immune system’s defense mechanism. Bioactive chemical discovery relies heavily on natural products11. These compounds are inexpensive, less likely to have adverse effects than standard treatments, and safer than certain synthetic compounds12. Natural product drug discovery is a promising field; however, more research is required from clinical studies on using natural products in conjunction with contemporary medications and improving delivery systems13. In order to combat EBV, many compounds have been tested to inhibit its replication in the host14–16. Targeted therapy options for EBV-associated disorders can be developed by learning more about BZLF1’s role in the viral life cycle and how it interacts with host variables. Inhibiting BZLF1 function could avert the transition from latency to the lytic phase, restricting viral replication and lessening the severity of diseases associated with the Epstein–Barr virus17. Despite recent advances in bioinformatics-assisted drug discovery, its application in the context of phytochemicals-derived inhibitors against BZLF1 gene activation still needs to be explored, leaving a significant knowledge gap that this study will address by employing computational biology strategies to design drugs based on natural inhibitors for suppressing EBV replication in the host.


## Materials and methods

### Retrieval and secondary structure prediction of targeted protein
  The targeted toxin protein Zta encoded by the BZLF1 Gene with accession number YP_401673.1 was retrieved by the National Center of Biotechnology Information-NCBI18. Then, the PSIPRED was employed for the prediction of secondary structure. From the different analyses on the server, the secondary structure of the protein and the basic structural characteristics were interpreted19. SOPMA was employed to study the number of alpha sheets, beta sheets and random coils in protein structure20.


### Tertiary structure prediction and validation of toxin protein
  The structure prediction was performed using the AlphaFold with default parameters21. No specific forcefield was applied during the modeling process, and an ab initio approach was used. Discovery Studio Visualizer (Biovia discovery studio 2021) was utilized to visualize the 3D structure of the protein22 Then, tertiary structure was validated by using the PROCHECK tool and results were interpreted in Ramachandran Plot23.


### Physiochemical properties analysis
  The molecular weight, the isoelectric point, the amino acid composition, the instability index, the aliphatic index, and the GRAVY value were calculated using the ProtParam tool on the ExPasy web server. This analysis provided a preliminary idea about the protein and its probable role in a biological system23.


### Acquisition of natural inhibitors from literature
  Natural inhibitors were chosen from the available literature of research conducted by Bencheikh, Cheriet24 that are acceptable due to their drug-like and anti-viral properties. All information was collected from PubChem including 2D and 3D structures of natural inhibitors22. PubChem (NIH) is a free chemistry database maintained by the National Institutes of Health that aids in lead discovery and optimization, compound-target profiling, polypharmacology research, and clarifying unknown chemical identities.


### Screening of natural inhibitors
  Natural inhibitor compounds were screened by employing PyRx to perform multiple ligands docking based on their binding energies with toxin protein22,25. This software is a virtual screening tool for In silico drug development and discovery. Its primary function is to search extensive libraries of potential antidotes to identify compounds that exhibit activity against specific drug targets. The receptor protein was first converted then receptor PDBQT file was saved. Then the ligands were upload and energy of all ligands was minimized and converted into PDBQT files. After this the grid box was set on the on these dimensions center_x = 7.1598, center_y = − 1.2397, center_z = 6.7236, size_x = 90.652547226, size_y = 110.592048035 and size_z = 79.4942625809 and docking was performed at Exhaustiveness 8.


### Docking analysis
  The CB-DOCK platform was utilized to conduct a molecular docking simulation of protein modification26. This web server was chosen due to its simple user interface, which only requires submitting a protein file and a ligand as input. The Discovery Studio software (Biovia discovery studio 2021) was employed to visualize the docking results.


### ADMET analysis
  PreADMET was integrated to analyze ADMET (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity)24. Computational studies of ADMET prediction aim to find molecules likely to fail in subsequent stages before they are synthesized, leading to even higher efficiency gains.


### Lipinski’s rule of 5
  To predict the likelihood that the targeted molecule will become a drug, the Molinspiration tool was used27,28. This method predicts the bioactivity scores of the most important pharmaceutical targets. This states that while the typically orally bioavailable drug has no more than five hydrogen bond donors and ten hydrogen bond acceptors, molecular weight lower than 500 Daltons, and a logP less than 5 means good solubility and permeability. By enforcing these rules, a prediction of a compound’s pharmacokinetic profile can be made.


### Density functional theory analysis
  Density functional Theory analysis was executed to predict the transition energies as well as the reactivity of the selected compound. For this purpose, Gauss view 5.0 and Gaussian 09 W was used. The optimization of the lead compound was done at the ground state, while the basis set for the analysis was selected as 6.31G (d, p,++). The lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) and highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) energies were used. Using this expression, the band energy gap (ΔE) was calculated29.


### Molecular dynamics simulation and free energy calculation
  The AMBER suite of programs was used to perform molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to predicts the dynamic actions of the docked complex between the BZLF1 receptor protein and the Imperatorin ligand. For the protein, the AMBER ff19SB force field was applied, while the ligand structure was parametrized using the General AMBER Force Field 2 (GAFF2)30. The complex was solvated in a TIP3P water model with a simulation box extending 12 Å in all directions around the solute to ensure an adequate aqueous environment31. Sodium and chloride ions (NaCl) were added to neutralize the system and mimic physiological conditions. The system was then subjected to 20,000 steps of energy minimization to resolve any steric clashes or unfavorable interactions, followed by equilibration for 5 nanoseconds under constant temperature (298 K) and pressure (1 bar), using a 2 femtosecond (fs) integration timestep. The production MD simulations were performed under the same temperature and pressure conditions, and 2000 frames of trajectory data were captured at intervals of 10 picoseconds. Post-simulation, the binding free energies of the complex were evaluated using the Molecular Mechanics Generalized Born Surface Area (MMGBSA) and Molecular Mechanics Poisson–Boltzmann Surface Area (MMPBSA) methods32,33. This dual approach allowed for a comprehensive analysis of the structural stability and interaction patterns of the complex in the aqueous environment, offering valuable insights into its potential as a therapeutic candidate.

### Molecular dynamic simulations
  The overall dynamics actions of the BZLF1 with Imperatorin was satisfactory. RMSD plot represents the structural movement and stability of protein, ligand, and binding pocket throughout the dynamical 100 ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulation. The protein RMSD (blue line) appears first to rise sharply and to stabilize out around 10–15 Å, which we know for some while the protein is fluctuating to a stable final conformation (Fig. 4). By observing large fluctuations (orange line, ligand RMSD), we conclude that the ligand explores a number of orientations in the binding site. The other side is the grey line (binding pocket RMSD), which does not change much and remains at about 1–2 Å, indicating the binding site remained constant throughout the simulation. These trends suggest the ligand receives some flexibility, but the protein structure and binding pocket are generally stable.

  Fig. 4RSMD graph of BZLF1 with imperatorin complex at 100 ns MD simulation.

  The fluctuations of individual residues during the simulation are represented in an RMSF plot. In the beginning (residues 1–20), RMSF values are high, close to 18 Å, and reflect a high degree of flexibility, which may be in loop regions or terminal segments. The RMSF values fall around 4–8 Å around residues ~ 50–100, indicating relatively stable regions consistent with forming structured elements, such as helices or sheets (Fig. 5). Another highly flexible segment, perhaps a loop or unstructured region, is indicated by a peak near residue 150 with an approximately 17 Å fluctuation. By contrast, the RMSF values between residues 200 and 250 are pretty low, i.e., 3–6 Å, suggesting that these regions are stable and possibly form part of the protein’s core or structured domain.

  Fig. 5RSMF graph of BZLF1 with imperatorin complex at 100 ns MD simulation.

  The SASA (solvent accessible surface area) plot demonstrates how the protein’s surface is exposed to the solvent over time during the 100 ns simulation. The SASA starts at 1000 Å2, consistent with a relatively open conformation. On the first 40 ns, SASA slightly decreases over the range 850–900 Å2, until it becomes more or less stable, indicating that the protein is getting more compact. The SASA eventually stabilizes between 800 and 850 Å2 some 70 ns after the simulation begins and throughout the remainder, indicating that the protein remains stable but slightly more compact (Fig. 6). A smaller SASA may also mean better folding or tighter packing, increasing binding affinity if the binding pocket is formed and available.

  Fig. 6SASA graph of BZLF1 with imperatorin complex at 100 ns MD simulation.

## Discussion
  The Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) belong to Herpes virus family has been linked to an increased risk of developing Burkitt’s lymphoma nasopharyngeal carcinoma in people with compromised immune systems. Also, it causes infectious mononucleosis, a common, self-limiting illness in young adults and adolescents34. Sir Michael Anthony Epstein and Yvonne Barr were the first to identify EBV in 1964, and they proposed that Burkitt’s lymphoma is a malignancy that mostly affects children in equatorial Africa. The researchers discovered the virus in a line of cells generated from one of these tumors35. BZLF1 functions as a transcription factor by beginning the transcriptional activation of early lytic genes by binding specifically to certain DNA regions within the viral genome36. Flavonoids, alkaloids, and terpenes are just a few examples of naturally occurring phytochemicals having antimicrobial action that play a crucial role in the immune system’s defensive mechanism37. Many phytochemicals have been tried and shown to be effective against EBV and various others pathogens by inhibiting their replication in the host15,24,38–40.

  The Ramachandran plot indicates a high quality for the evaluated protein structure, with 98% of the resulting residues being in the most favored regions, indicating proper folding and the lack of major steric clashes. Deviations of 1.5% in additionally allowed regions and 0.5% in generously allowed regions are minor and accepted for reflecting on the flexibility or local structural variations. Evaluated on the 108 nonglycine/nonproline residues and 6 proline residues, the distribution matches the structural constraints one would expect from prolines, whose nature makes them often introduce kinks. The model is well-refined and can be used for further structural or functional analysis since no residues exist in disallowed regions.

  The secondary structure prediction of the Zta protein, further understandings can be gathered from the physicochemical analysis. The majority of random coils in the protein structure predicts a high degree of flexibility, which may contribute to its role in viral replication and host interaction, as flexible regions often mediate protein-protein interactions. The acidic nature of the Zta protein, indicated by its isoelectric point, suggests that it may preferentially interact with basic cellular proteins or nuclear factors, which could enable its role in the transcriptional activation of early lytic genes. Hydrophobic regions within the protein, typically found in alpha-helices, may indicate potential membrane interaction sites, which could be important for the localization of the Zta protein within the nucleus or other cellular compartments. Prior studies have also showed that the Zta protein’s ability to bind DNA is enhanced by the presence of specific hydrophobic residues, helping its transcription factor activity. Additionally, the instability index calculated for Zta highlights its potential rapid degradation in host cells, which may be a viral strategy to avoid immune detection by quickly cycling through its active forms. Based on its physicochemical properties, Zta likely interacts with nuclear transcription factors and viral DNA, as supported by previous studies that have identified key partners such as host histones and viral promoters41. These findings open new avenues for exploring inhibitors that could disrupt these interactions and further contribute to anti-EBV therapeutic development.

  The Zta protein’s physiochemical examination yielded more information regarding its acidic and unstable characteristics. The Zta protein consists of 245 amino acids and has a molecular weight 26860.21 Da that is also confirmed by previous study conducted by Germini, Sall42. It had a theoretical pI of 5.25, i.e. it is acidic in nature. With an instability index of 62.28, protein is unstable. At 280 nm using one disulfide bond, the extinction coefficient is 21,680 M−1 cm−1; it has 26 negatively and 21 positively charged residues. In mammalian reticulocytes, the estimated half-life of 30 h shows that the compound has moderate stability for experimental and research purposes. This detailed analysis helps to understand the properties of the protein for more drug targeting studies. Out of 5 docked ligands, Imperatorin has garnered attention as a potential pharmaceutical option owing to its notably low binding energy of − 6.3 kcal/mol that is similar to study conducted by Bencheikh, Cheriet24 in which they docked Imperatorin against SARS-CoV-2 and find − 6.8 kcal/mol of binding affinity that showed imperatorin a good therapeutic candidate against viral pathogens. The molecular formula and molecular weight imperatorin are C16H14O4 and 270.28 g/mol, respectively, which occur naturally in plants such as Angelica dahurica, Aegle marmelos(bael fruit), and parsnip seeds. It belongs to the family of polycyclic aromatic compounds biosynthesized from coumarin derivative umbelliferone. Therefore, these properties may suggest therapeutic utility to Imperatorin and make it a possible lead compound for drug development against viruses, such as Zta from Epstein Barr virus. Well-aligned with Lipinski’s Rule of Five, imperatorin ligand shows good potential for good oral availability—the moderate logP value at 3.95 points out balanced hydrophobicity for the membrane permeability with good solubility. The 270.28 g/mol molecular weight is below the 500 g/mol limit, ensuring efficient absorption. The ligand has four hydrogen bond acceptors and no donors, which fulfills the criteria for hydrogen bonding and lowers the chance of poor absorption. Together, these properties indicate that the ligand possesses a drug project profile with desirable absorption, distribution, and overall bioavailability characteristics.

  Upon analyzing the docking results, Imperatorin demonstrates significant binding affinity with the Zta protein, specifically interacting with key residues that are critical for the protein’s function. The molecular docking results revealed that Imperatorin forms hydrogen bonds with residues such as His 239 and Tyr 200, which are located in the DNA-binding domain of the Zta protein. This interaction is particularly important because the DNA-binding domain is essential for Zta’s role in initiating the transcription of early lytic genes. The hydrogen bond formation with His 239 and Tyr 200 likely disrupts the normal functioning of the DNA-binding domain, thereby inhibiting Zta’s ability to bind to viral DNA and activate gene transcription. Hydrophobic interactions between Imperatorin and residues such as Ala 204, Lys 207 and Val 203 further stabilize the binding complex, enhancing its inhibitory potential. These hydrophobic contacts occur within a pocket that is known to be important for Zta’s structural integrity when binding to DNA. By disrupting both the hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, Imperatorin effectively impairs the functionality of Zta, which is essential for EBV’s lytic cycle initiation. These findings shows that the compound not only has strong binding affinity but also targets residues that are important to the functional activity of the Zta protein, making it a promising candidate for therapeutic intervention against EBV. This interaction pattern is supported by previous studies on similar viral proteins, where key hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions at specific active sites were found to disrupt viral replication43–47.

  According to the Density Functional Theory (DFT) analysis, an ELUMO − 0.067 kcal/mol and an EHOMO − 0.226 kcal/mol are observed in imperatorin, and a band energy gap (ΔE) of 0.159 kcal/mol was computed. The energy gap suggested by this mode is relatively small, consistent with high reactivity and capability for efficient interactions with biological targets like the Zta toxin protein of the Epstein–Barr virus, which is also present in the majority of human cancers, and thus provides a possible application for the treatment. DFT-predicted electronic properties suggest that imperatorin should adequately bind to the target site, which can extend its potential as a forthcoming antiviral drug candidate. The MD simulation showed favorable results fluctuation of the ligand is moderate in RMSD analysis, which demonstrates the structural stability of the protein. However, the ligand explores several binding conformations but remains bound to the protein. The stable RMSD of the binding pocket is necessary for maintaining a well-formed active site and ensuring that effective ligand binding interactions are maintained throughout the simulation. Further evidence for the stated stabilization of protein structure leading to possible more favorable ligand-protein interaction is found in the growth of these intramolecular hydrogen bonds over time. A reduction in solvent-exposed surface area from the SASA plot indicates that binding causes the protein to adopt a more compact conformation and, thus, enhances the stability of the ligand and protein complex. Finally, through the PCA analysis, we also find that the conformational flexibility of the protein is large enough to allow the protein to be located in different states associated with higher ligand affinity and protein stability. Finally, the dynamic cross-correlation matrix identifies cooperative and anti-correlated amino acid movements that may locate new allosteric sites involved in enhancing drug binding and inhibition mechanisms. Together, these simulations show that imperatorin could be a suitable BZLF1 inhibitor and, hence, a candidate towards further small molecule development for EBV lytic cycle ablation.

  In the current study, the relatively low binding energies of the natural inhibitors, including Imperatorin (− 6.3 kcal/mol), may appear modest initially. However, detailed molecular dynamics simulations and free energy calculations (MMGBSA and MMPBSA) support the stability and efficacy of Imperatorin in targeting the BZLF1 protein, with total binding free energies of − 28.95 and − 7.68 kcal/mol, respectively. Furthermore, RMSD and SASA analyses demonstrated the ligand-maintained stability within the binding pocket, enhancing protein compactness and ligand affinity. These findings, coupled with ADMET profiling and favorable pharmacokinetics, suggest that despite moderate binding energies, Imperatorin remains a promising lead compound for further development against EBV, warranting additional in vitro validation.

The two studies about natural inhibitors and molecular docking for drug discovery compare favorably by the substance of the target proteins only. These studies use computational methods to investigate antiviral approaches to Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) through viral proteins, which are important for pathogenesis. They both underscore the need for additional in vitro research to determine the validity of the computational findings and to push drug development efforts.


## Conclusion
  The current study performed computational analysis research to identify potential drug candidates against Epstein–Barr virus toxin protein Zta [Human gamma-herpesvirus 4 (Epstein–Barr virus)] encoded by BZLF1 gene by using phytochemicals derived natural inhibitors. A total of five natural inhibitors were screened against toxin protein, including Imperatorin, Bergamottin, Bergapten, Chloroquine, and Hydroxychloroquine. Imperatorin showed the lowest binding energy (− 6.3 kcal/mol). DFT analysis ADMET properties and Lipinski’s rule of 5 analyses showed Imperatorin’s favorable pharmacokinetics, making it an ideal therapeutic agent against EBV. Since vaccines and medicines are necessary for treating infectious diseases and cancer, computational approaches seem promising and less expensive to screen In silico drugs and vaccines candidates for experimental verification.


### Limitations and future prospective
  Despite the promising in-silico results, future studies must address several limitations. One major limitation is the lack of experimental validation to confirm the binding interactions predicted by computational methods. In silico findings can be prone to inaccuracies due to the inherent limitations of the models and algorithms. Thus, it is essential to validate these predictions through experimental techniques. To overcome these challenges, future research will focus on experimental validation of the in-silico findings. In vitro studies will assess the binding affinity of Imperatorin to the Zta protein and its ability to inhibit EBV replication. In vivo studies will further evaluate its antiviral efficacy and toxicity profile in animal models, providing more reliable data to support its therapeutic potential.

  Moreover, the current study has yet to explore Imperatorin’s structure-activity relationship (SAR) in depth. Future research will optimize the compound’s molecular structure, potentially enhancing its binding affinity and therapeutic efficacy. Given the increasing interest in integrating computational and experimental approaches, future studies will combine these methods to provide a more robust understanding of Imperatorin’s therapeutic potential. Additionally, the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of Imperatorin, including its absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) properties, have yet to be fully explored. These aspects will be crucial for evaluating its viability as a therapeutic agent. By addressing these limitations, future research will enhance our understanding of Imperatorin’s mechanism of action and facilitate the development of potent antiviral drugs targeting the Epstein–Barr virus.
