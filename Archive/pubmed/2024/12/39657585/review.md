## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text (abstract, introduction, materials and methods, results, discussion, supplementary data list) provided as a single block. No figures, tables, or supplementary files were available for inspection.
- **Assessment boundary** This review assesses the scientific claims, methodological soundness, and evidentiary support based solely on the text provided. Claims referring to figures, tables, and supplementary materials are evaluated as "not directly verifiable" where those items were not supplied.
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors report the identification and functional characterization of VcAAT1a, a BADH-family anthocyanin acetyltransferase from blueberry, as the major gene underlying a previously mapped QTL on chromosome 2 controlling acylated anthocyanin accumulation. Evidence includes a haplotype-resolved tetraploid genome assembly, gene expression profiling, transient expression in three plant systems, recombinant enzyme assays, AlphaFold-based structural modelling, and promoter transactivation assays with MYB transcription factors.
- **Visible evidence base** Text descriptions of results, methods, and discussion. No raw data, figures, tables, or supplementary files were provided.
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** All figures (Figs 1–9), all tables (Tables 1–2), all supplementary figures and tables (Figs S1–S10, Tables S1–S3), and the deposited genome assembly. ## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript presents a well-structured and technically ambitious study that combines haplotype-resolved genome assembly, QTL fine-mapping, and multi-system functional validation to identify a blueberry anthocyanin acetyltransferase. The experimental design is logical and the range of validation approaches is commendable. However, the strength of the central claim—that VcAAT1a is the causative gene controlling acylated anthocyanin accumulation at this QTL—depends critically on data not visible in the text (figures, tables, supplementary materials). Several interpretive gaps and potential overstatements are apparent from the text alone, particularly regarding the distinction between correlation and causation, the generalizability of the transient expression results, and the structural interpretation of substrate specificity. The manuscript is likely to be of interest to the plant specialized metabolism and berry genetics communities, but the case would be strengthened by addressing specific technical and interpretive concerns.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers working on anthocyanin biosynthesis and modification, particularly in Vaccinium species and other berry crops; plant geneticists interested in polyploid genome assembly and QTL cloning; and breeders targeting improved anthocyanin stability and health-related fruit quality traits. The methodological combination of trio-binning in a tetraploid and multi-system functional validation may also interest those working on polyploid crop genomics.
- **Major strengths** 1) The use of a haplotype-resolved assembly in a tetraploid species to dissect a QTL region is technically advanced and represents a valuable methodological contribution. 2) The multi-pronged functional validation (three transient expression systems plus recombinant enzyme assays) provides convergent evidence for acetyltransferase activity. 3) The inclusion of structural modelling to rationalize substrate specificity adds mechanistic depth. 4) The promoter activation assays extend the work from enzyme function to transcriptional regulation, providing a more complete picture.
- **Major Concerns** See detailed items below.
- **Minor Comments** See detailed items below.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** The causal link between VcAAT1a and the QTL phenotype requires clearer evidence that the favourable haplotype carries a functional allele while the unfavourable haplotypes carry non-functional or poorly expressed alleles. The current text describes sequence differences (e.g., internal stop codon, frameshift mutations) but does not explicitly demonstrate that these differences explain the phenotypic variation in the mapping population. Additionally, the absence of a loss-of-function or complementation experiment in blueberry itself leaves the causal claim partially open.
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria**  
  *Originality*: Moderate to high. The application of trio-binning to a tetraploid blueberry and the identification of a clade III AAT in this context is novel, though the general phenomenon of anthocyanin acylation is well studied in other species.  
  *Scientific importance*: Moderate. The work advances understanding of anthocyanin diversity in blueberry and provides a target for breeding, but the broader biological significance beyond this crop is limited.  
  *Interdisciplinary readership*: Moderate. Will appeal to plant biochemists, geneticists, and breeders, but may have limited appeal outside these fields.  
  *Technical soundness*: Generally sound in design, but the inability to verify key data from the text alone, and the interpretive gaps noted above, prevent full endorsement.  
  *Readability for nonspecialists*: The manuscript is clearly written and the logic is accessible, though some sections (e.g., structural modelling details) assume familiarity with enzymology and protein structure concepts.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The core experimental strategy is sound and the findings are potentially important for blueberry breeding, but the manuscript requires access to the missing data and clarification of the causal logic before the central claim can be fully accepted.

### Major Concerns

- **Concern ID** R1-M1
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** Yes
- **Axis** Evidence sufficiency
- **Claim pointer** The manuscript claims that VcAAT1a is "the major causative gene" controlling anthocyanin acylation at the chromosome 2 QTL, and that the favourable haplotype (h2tg000453l_1) carries the functional allele.
- **Evidence pointer** Results sections "Haplotyped tetraploid genome assembly", "Gene annotation of anthocyanin acyltransferase at the chromosome 2 QTL region", "Quantitative RT-PCR analysis of candidate genes linked to the chromosome 2 QTL"; Figs 1–3; Tables 1–2; Supplementary Figs S2–S5.
- **Concern** The text describes sequence differences between haplotypes (e.g., internal stop codon in HP_hA VcAAT1a, frameshift mutations in VcAAT2b and Nui_hB VcAAT4) and expression differences, but does not explicitly demonstrate that these differences are the causal variants underlying the QTL. The QTL was previously mapped, and the current study identifies candidates within the interval, but the link between specific sequence variants and the phenotypic effect is inferred rather than demonstrated. Without a complementation test, allelic series analysis, or at minimum a clear segregation analysis linking specific haplotypes to the phenotype in the mapping population, the claim of "causative gene" is premature.
- **Why it matters** The central conclusion of the manuscript rests on this causal assignment. If the evidence only supports VcAAT1a as a strong candidate rather than the proven causative gene, the title and abstract overstate the findings.
- **Resolution test** Provide explicit evidence that the favourable haplotype allele is both necessary and sufficient for acetylated anthocyanin production. This could include: (1) demonstration that the unfavourable alleles produce non-functional proteins or are not expressed in relevant tissues; (2) complementation of a heterologous system with the favourable allele; (3) co-segregation analysis in the mapping population; or (4) targeted silencing or knockout in blueberry. At minimum, the manuscript should clearly state the distinction between "candidate gene" and "causative gene" and temper the language accordingly.

- **Concern ID** R1-M3
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** No
- **Axis** Interpretive rigor
- **Claim pointer** The manuscript states that "VcAAT1a was able to produce a range of acylated anthocyanins in planta" in all three transient expression systems, and that the enzyme "could only utilize acetyl-CoA as an acyl donor."
- **Evidence pointer** Results sections "Functional characterization of VcAAT1a", "Biochemical characterization of recombinant VcAAT1a"; Figs 5–7; Table 2.
- **Concern** The text reports that in tobacco leaves, acylated anthocyanins represented only 1.0–1.6% of total anthocyanins. This is a very low conversion efficiency and raises questions about the physiological relevance of the observed activity. Additionally, the statement that VcAAT1a "could only utilize acetyl-CoA" is based on testing three acyl donors; the possibility of other physiologically relevant donors (e.g., malonyl-CoA at different pH conditions, or aromatic CoA thioesters not tested) is not excluded. The structural interpretation (Tyr40 closing the sub-pocket) is plausible but speculative without mutagenesis data.
- **Why it matters** The low conversion efficiency in planta may indicate that VcAAT1a is not the primary enzyme responsible for the high levels of acylated anthocyanins observed in blueberry skin, or that additional factors (e.g., substrate channeling, compartmentalization) are required. The donor specificity claim is stronger than the evidence supports.
- **Resolution test** Clarify whether the low percentage of acylated products in tobacco reflects the natural activity of the enzyme or suboptimal assay conditions. Consider testing additional acyl donors, including those that may be present in blueberry fruit. If structural modelling is used to explain specificity, include site-directed mutagenesis of Tyr40 and other key residues to test the hypothesis.

- **Concern ID** R1-M4
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** No
- **Axis** Generalizability
- **Claim pointer** The manuscript concludes that "VcAAT1a is a functional candidate for anthocyanin acylation in 'Hortblue Petite'" and discusses implications for breeding "blueberries with improved resilience to heat-induced anthocyanin degradation."
- **Evidence pointer** Discussion sections "VcAAT1a is a clade III anthocyanin acyltransferase", "The role of acylated anthocyanins in blueberry"; Figs 5–7.
- **Concern** The functional validation was performed in heterologous systems (tobacco leaves, strawberry fruit, and blueberry fruit flesh of a different cultivar, 'Maru'). While these systems demonstrate that VcAAT1a has acetyltransferase activity, they do not directly demonstrate that VcAAT1a is responsible for the acylated anthocyanin profile in 'Hortblue Petite' skin. The connection between the enzyme activity and the proposed breeding outcome (heat resilience) is also indirect, as no heat-stress experiments were performed.
- **Why it matters** The translational claims in the discussion extend beyond the data presented. The manuscript would be strengthened by acknowledging these limitations and avoiding overstatement of the breeding implications.
- **Resolution test** Add explicit statements about the limitations of heterologous expression systems for inferring endogenous function. If heat resilience is discussed, clearly frame it as a hypothesis based on published literature rather than a finding of this study.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that VcAAT1a is "the major causative gene" for the chromosome 2 QTL is not fully supported by the text alone; the evidence establishes VcAAT1a as a strong candidate but does not demonstrate causation.
- The statement that VcAAT1a "could only utilize acetyl-CoA" is based on testing three acyl donors and may overstate the specificity.
- The breeding implication regarding "improved resilience to heat-induced anthocyanin degradation" is speculative and not directly tested in this study.
- The claim that VcAAT1a is regulated by VcMYBPA1 and VcMYBPA2 is supported by promoter activation assays, but the physiological relevance of the observed 3.3- and 3.9-fold activation is unclear given the high autoactivation of the promoter-only control.
- All quantitative results (expression levels, anthocyanin concentrations, enzyme kinetics, assembly statistics) are unverifiable without access to the figures, tables, and supplementary materials.
