# Haplotyped genome mapping and functional characterization of a blueberry anthocyanin acetyltransferase (AAT) controlling the accumulation of acylated anthocyanins


## Abstract
  Blueberry has a diversity of anthocyanins that confer its characteristic blue-coloured skin. Whilst most cultivars produce only anthocyanin glycosides, some can add aliphatic or aromatic groups to the sugar moiety to create acylated anthocyanins. Due to their enhanced stability, acylated anthocyanins represent an attractive breeding target in blueberry. In this study, a haplotype-resolved assembly of a previously identified quantitative trait locus on chromosome 2 of ‘Hortblue Petite’ (Vaccinium corymbosum) was created to identify candidate anthocyanin acyltransferase genes. One full-length gene (VcAAT1a) was selected based on quantitative PCR expression profiling and transient expression in tobacco leaves and in strawberry and blueberry fruit flesh. In all three systems, VcAAT1a was able to produce a range of acylated anthocyanins in planta. Recombinant VcAAT1a protein demonstrated that, while VcAAT1a was able to act on both anthocyanin 3-O-glucosides and 3-O-galactosides, it could only utilize acetyl-CoA as an acyl donor. Protein modelling using AlphaFold suggested that this restricted range in acyl donors may be due to a spatially restricted sub-pocket in the acyl-binding site of VvAAT1. Finally, LUC/REN promoter activation assays revealed that the VcAAT1a promoter was transactivated by the VcMYBPA1 and VcMYBPA2 transcription factors, further expanding our knowledge of anthocyanin regulation in blueberry.


## Introduction
  The worldwide area of blueberry (Vaccinium sp.) cultivation has been rapidly growing in the last few years, partly due to the increasing interest in the beneficial health properties that have been associated with their consumption (Norberto et al., 2013). Blueberry’s nutritional value is largely attributed to the high content of anthocyanins (Prior et al., 1998; Grace et al., 2019; Kalt et al., 2020), a class of flavonoid compounds that is concentrated in the skin of the blueberry fruit (Zifkin et al., 2012; Scalzo et al., 2015; Günther et al., 2020). Anthocyanins are a product of the flavonoid pathway and include cyanidin-, delphinidin-, pelargonidin-, petunidin-, peonidin-, and malvidin-3-O-glycosides. Apart from pelargonidin-3-O-glycosides, all these anthocyanin types are present in blueberry fruit, largely confined to the skin. The anthocyanin biosynthetic pathway in blueberry has been well documented, from the first committed step, phenylalanine ammonia lyase (PAL), through to the final glycosylation stage of the aglycone, anthocyanidin 3-O-glycosyltransferase (A3GT) (Günther et al., 2020). Additionally, the role of several transcription factors, including MYBA and MYBPA1, in the control of the pathway has been reported (Plunkett et al., 2018; Lafferty et al., 2022b). The glycosylation of anthocyanins involves the sugar moieties galactose, glucose, and arabinose, and these glycosylated compounds may be subject to further decorations such as acylation. Acylation is carried out by anthocyanin acyltransferases (AATs) and there are two distinct classes of AAT genes: the serine carboxypeptidase-like (SCPL)-AAT genes, whose products use acyl-glucose as the donor, and the BADH family, whose products use a range of acyl-CoA groups as the donor (Sasaki and Nakayama, 2014; Curaba et al., 2020). The BADH family is named after the first four members that were discovered; BEAT (acetyl CoA:benzylalcohol acetyltransferase), AHCT (anthocyanin O-hydroxycinnamoyltransferase), DAT (deacetylvindoline 4-O-acetyltransferase), and HCBT (anthranilate N-hydroxycinnamoyl/benzoyltransferase). BADH genes form a large gene family (e.g. 61 predicted members in Arabidopsis and >150 in Vaccinium coryrmbosum) and differ in both function and sequence, sometimes making it difficult to predict their function (Luo et al., 2007; Yu et al., 2009). One of the first berry anthocyanin AATs to be fully characterized was Vv3AT from the grape cultivar ‘Shiraz’, where both in vitro and in planta experiments showed that it was able to acylate a broad range of anthocyanins with both aliphatic and aromatic donors (Rinaldo et al., 2015).

  Acylation has implications for the chemical properties of anthocyanins and their resultant bioactivities in humans. Generally, acylation decreases the polarity, increases the molecular size and pH stability, and modifies anthocyanin spatial structure (Matsufuji et al., 2007). The addition of an aromatic acyl donor leads to increased stability via 𝜋-ring stacking of the A-ring and the aromatic group of the acyl donor, resulting in an intramolecular co-pigmentation effect (Zhao et al., 2017). The effects of adding an aliphatic ester group, acetyl-CoA or malonyl-CoA, are less studied but are linked to the decreased polarity and lower solubility in aqueous conditions and steric hindrance to reduce nucleophilic attack of the flavylium ion (Fenger et al., 2019).

  From a consumer health perspective, acylation of anthocyanins plays a significant role in modifying their mode of action in the gastrointestinal tract. Acylation further decreases the already low bioavailability of anthocyanins; however, increased resistance to deprotonation in the weakly acidic duodenum means an increased flux of intact acylated anthocyanins to the colon (Overall et al., 2017). Gut microbiota can catabolize acylated anthocyanins more readily compared with their non-acylated counterparts to promote the growth of beneficial bacteria, as reviewed in Jokioja et al. (2021).

  Research has focused on understanding the genetic regulation of anthocyanin content in highbush blueberry (V. corymbosum L.), with two complementary studies revealing major quantitative trait loci (QTLs) for anthocyanin concentration and composition (Mengist et al., 2022; Montanari et al., 2022). While Montanari et al. (2022) and Mengist et al. (2022) used two different tetraploid mapping populations, they both identified stable and strong effect QTLs on chromosomes 2 and 4, which were shown to overlap (Albert et al., 2023). The QTL on chromosome 2 appeared to control anthocyanin acylation, while the QTL on chromosome 4 was linked to glycosylation of the molecule. Two BADH AAT genes located within the narrow confidence interval of the chromosome 2 QTL were identified as the main candidates for this locus (Mengist et al., 2022; Montanari et al., 2022).

  The high percentage of phenotypic variance explained by the chromosome 2 QTL (up to 69.1%) suggests that anthocyanin acylation in blueberry is of oligogenic nature. The functional validation of the major gene responsible for this process, and the identification of the causative variant, would provide information for the development of blueberry cultivars with enhanced acylated anthocyanin content. The high-quality phased linkage map used by Montanari et al. (2022) also allowed the identification of the parental haplotype in ‘Hortblue Petite’ carrying the favourable allele linked to an increased concentration of anthocyanins containing acylated sugars. The aim of the present study was therefore to identify the causative variant leading to acylated anthocyanin production in ‘Hortblue Petite’. A chromosome-scale haplotype-resolved assembly was achieved for one offspring of the mapping population described in Montanari et al. (2022). Acyltransferase candidates within the chromosome 2 QTL region were identified on all haplotyped sequences. Gene expression analysis of these candidates allowed the identification of ANTHOCYANIN ACYLTRANSFERASE 2 (VcAAT1a) as a potential causative variant. Transient and enzyme assays functionally validated VcAAT1a as an anthocyanin acetyltransferase, and protein modelling allowed structural insights into the acyl donor preference of VcAAT1a. Finally, promotor activation studies revealed that VvAAT1a was regulated by two MYB transcription factors of the MYBPA-type subgroup (VcMYBPA1) and subgroup 5 (VcMYBPA2).


## Materials and methods

### Plant material and sequencing
  Both parents of the tetraploid segregating population (V06.A002) used by Montanari et al. (2022), V. corymbosum cultivar ‘Nui’ and the V. corymbosum open-pollinated cultivar ‘Hortblue Petite’, as well as one F1 offspring (V06.A002-186) carrying the favourable QTL allele for acylated anthocyanins, were used for whole-genome sequencing. High molecular weight DNA was extracted from nuclei isolated from leaf tissue using the methods of Hilario (2018). Parents’ DNAs were sequenced using Illumina short paired end reads (150 bp) at Neogen (Lincoln, NE, USA). The V06.A002-186 F1 hybrid was sequenced using Pacific Biosciences (PacBio) high-fidelity (HiFi) long reads from two single-molecule real-time (SMRT) cells at the McDonnell Genome Institute at Washington University (St. Louis, MO, USA).


### Genome assembly and trio-binning
  Haplotype-resolved genome assemblies were generated from V06.A002-186’s PacBio HiFi reads using a new trio-binning approach with parental Illumina short reads. First, quality and length distribution of the long and short reads were assessed with Nanopack (v1.1.1) (De Coster et al., 2018) and FastQC (v0.11.7) (Wingett and Andrews, 2018), respectively. The two passes method in Jellyfish (v2.2.10) was then used to analyse the k-mers (k=21) present in all read sets (Marçais and Kingsford, 2011). Unique parental k-mers were identified using trio_binning (v1.0.0) (Koren et al., 2018; Rice et al., 2020), and V06.A002-186’s HiFi reads were binned into maternal and paternal read-sets accordingly. Afterwards, the k-mers for the two binned HiFi read-sets were combined separately with the set of unclassified reads (representing alleles present in both parental genomes) and analysed using the single passes method in Jellyfish (v2.3.0). GenomeScope (v2.0) was used to estimate the rate of heterozygosity for the two binned HiFi read-sets (Ranallo-Benavidez et al., 2020). The classified maternal+unclassified reads and classified paternal+unclassified reads were assembled separately using hifiasm (v 0.19.5) to produce four haplotype assemblies. For each assembly, haplotigs and contaminated contigs were removed (Cheng et al., 2021), and the quality of the cleaned assemblies was evaluated with plant-food-research-open/assemblyqc (v1.4.1) (Laetsch and Blaxter, 2017; Rashid et al., 2024). Finally, the parental Illumina data were mapped to each haplotype using bwa mem (v0.7.17) (Li, 2013, Preprint) with stringent mapping parameters, and mapping rates were calculated with samtools flagstat (Danecek et al., 2021).


### Gene annotation of anthocyanin acyltransferase at the chromosome 2 QTL region
  The assembled V06.A002-186 contig sets were aligned to the blueberry reference genome W85-20 v2.0 (Mengist et al., 2023) using minimap2 (v2.22) (Li, 2018). Contigs mapping to the chromosome 2 QTL region were identified and subsequently screened for AAT genes using the homology search method (tblastn) with default settings in BLAST (v2.15.0) (Altschul et al., 1990). The query sequence (GenBank ID: KM267560.1) that was functionally validated served as the basis for this search (Rinaldo et al., 2015). Predicted full-length gene models were manually curated to ensure proper ORFs, particularly focusing on correcting start and stop codons. Gene models containing a single base causing an internal stop codon or frameshift mutations were also considered as full length. Sequences exhibiting a large deletion (>190 bp) to the query sequence were classified as truncated genes. All the genes were confirmed from blastp searches against the nr database and subsequently plotted on the contigs using the ‘pyGenomeViz’ (v1.2.0) python package (Shimoyama, 2022). Excluding the three truncated genes that contained only single exons, all the sequences were aligned using the MUSCLE (multiple sequence comparison by log-expectation) algorithm with ClustalW as the sequence weighting scheme (Edgar, 2004). Phylogenetic trees were created in Geneious Prime using the UPGMA algorithm as the tree build method with the Jukes Cantor genetic distance model. Consensus trees were constructed with 1000 replicate boot strapping (Sokal and Michener, 1958; Jukes and Cantor, 1969).


### Identification of variants linked to anthocyanin acetylation at the chromosome 2 QTL region
  The V06.A002-186’s contigs that were inherited from ‘Hortblue Petite’ were aligned to the W85-20 v2.0 reference genome for anchoring, and single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were called on each haplotype using bcftools (v1.16). In parallel, the phased linkage map developed by Montanari et al. (2022) was used to estimate identity by descent (IBD) probabilities in the V06.A002-186 offspring, using a Hidden Markov Model (HMM) developed by Zheng et al. (2016) and implemented in the R package polyqtlR v0.1.1 (Bourke et al., 2021).This analysis could be used to determine the inheritance of parental haplotypes in the offspring at each marker position. Using customized Python scripts, the variants that overlapped with the markers segregating as simplex in ‘Hortblue Petite’ (HPxNui=0001x0000) were used to assign the V06.A002-186’s contigs to haplotypes, according to the phasing based on IBD estimates. The output was then plotted using the R package ‘karyoploteR’ (Gel and Serra, 2017).


### RNA extraction from blueberry fruit tissues and quantitative PCR analysis
  Berries were manually harvested from ‘Hortblue Petite’ at developmental stages 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, and 8. Stages were defined by reference to a berry ripening chart (Supplementary Fig. S1) developed from previous studies (Zifkin et al., 2012; Günther et al., 2020) Stages 1 and 2 were combined due to the small size of the berries. Tissue was immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. For RNA extraction, at least four berries were pooled for each of the three developmental stage replicates. Total RNA was isolated from the entire berry using the Spectrum Plant Total RNA Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The QuantiTect® Reverse Transcription Kit by QIAGEN (Hilden, Germany), with integrated removal of genomic DNA contamination, was used for cDNA synthesis of 1000 ng of total RNA.

  Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using the LightCycler® 480 system and LightCycler SYBR Green I Master (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Reactions were of 10 μl total volume and contained 5 µl of Master Mix, 0.5 µl of each primer (10 µM), 2.5 µl of diluted cDNA (1:20), and nuclease-free water. Analyses were performed using the LightCycler® 480 Software Version 1.5.0.39. GLYCERALDEHYDE-3-PHOSPHATE DEHYDROGENASE (GAPDH) and SAND FAMILY PROTEIN (SAND) were used reference genes. Relative gene expression was normalized as the target/reference ratio. Mean relative expression of four technical and three biological replicates was presented as the output result. The primers are listed in Supplementary Table S1.


### Anthocyanin extraction and analysis using ultra high-performance liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry
  Freeze-dried ground tobacco leaf, strawberry fruit, and blueberry fruit flesh (20–50 mg) tissues were extracted using 100% methanol containing 0.1% HCl for 2 h at room temperature in the dark with constant shaking. Three biological replicates were collected for tobacco (two leaves per replicate) and strawberry (up to three infiltrated regions per replicate). Four replicates each consisting of two berries were harvested for the blueberry infiltrations. Cell debris was pelleted by centrifuging at 21 130 g, and the resulting supernatant was evaporated to dryness using a Centrivap concentrator (Labconco, Kansas City, MO, USA). Dry pellets were resuspended in 20% methanol before syringe filtering with a 0.2 µm PTFE syringe filter (Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA).

  Anthocyanin extracts were separated on an Acquity H-Class UPLC system coupled to an Acquity single quadrupole QDa mass spectrometer and a photodiode detector array (PDA) (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). Anthocyanins were separated using an acclaim Polar Advantage II (PA2), 4.6×150 mm, 5 µm, analytical LC column (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), maintained at 35 °C. The solvent system used was (A) 0.1% formic acid in water and (B) 100% acetonitrile (Scharlab, Barcelona, Spain) with a flow rate of 0.35 ml min–1. The following chromatographic conditions were used: 0–5 min 7% B, 5–10 min 12% B, 10–25 min 35% B, 25–28.5 min 100% B, and 28.5–32 min 7% B. Anthocyanins were monitored using absorbance at 520 nm and by using a QDa mass detector in positive ion mode with capillary voltage set to 0.8 kV and a cone voltage of 25 V. Quantification of anthocyanins was carried out using an internal five-point calibration curve employing a cyanidin 3-O-glucoside standard (Extrasynthese, Genay, France).


### Cloning of VcAAT1a from ‘Hortblue Petite’ and Vv3AT
  Gene-specific PCR primers were designed based on the predicted coding sequence of the VcAAT1a gene model. Primers incorporated a NotI and XhoI restriction site for restriction ligation cloning. PCR was performed on cDNA isolated from the skin of ripe stage 8 berries of ‘Hortblue Petite’ using iProof™ High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) and gel purified using a Zymoclean Gel DNA Recovery Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Purified 1.3 kb fragments were blunt-end cloned into the pJet1.2/blunt cloning vector (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for sequencing before subcloning into the pGreen II-0029-62-SK plant transformation vector (Hellens et al., 2000) using the NotI and KpnI restriction sites. Positive colonies were identified by colony PCR and were sequenced by Macrogen (Seoul, Korea) before electroporation into the Agrobacterium strain GV3101 containing the pSOUP plasmid. The Vv3AT positive control was synthesized as a gBlock® fragment (Integrated DNA Technologies Coralville, IA, USA) with a 5' XbaI and a 3' KpnI site based on the GenBank sequence AKF15589 before cloning into pJet1.2 and pGreen II-0029-62-SK as described for VcAAT1a.


### Transient expression of VcAAT1a in plants
  Transient expression assays were performed in three model species, each capable of producing a specific set of anthocyanins: Nicotiana benthamiana, Nicotiana tabacum, and strawberry (Fragaria ×ananassa). Transient expression in N. benthamiana (Northern Territory ecotype) and N. tabacum was carried out using a previously described protocol (Nguyen et al., 2023). Fruits of strawberry cultivar ‘Camarosa’ were infiltrated according to Jia et al. (2020) and Nguyen et al. (2023) with VcAAT1a, Vv3AT, or VcMYBA cloned in pGreen0029-62-SK or GUS cloned into pHEX2. The AmF3'5'H transgene in pHEX2 from Actinidia melanandra was also included to create a dark purple patch, indicative of the accumulation of trihydroxylated anthocyanins, which allowed the infiltrated area to be delineated and excised (Peng et al., 2019). Strawberries were harvested from 4 d to 6 d post-infiltration when fruit was fully ripe.

  Additionally, blueberry fruit infiltration was performed on the ‘Maru’ cultivar (V. virgatum) which was chosen based on a previous publication (Günther et al., 2022). Berries at stage 4 of development were selected for infiltration. Prior to Agrobacterium infiltration, berry skin was pierced 20–30 times to release turgor pressure using a 0.45 mm diameter syringe needle. Berries were infiltrated with Agrobacterium carrying the 35S-VcAAT1a-pGreen0029-62-SK construct or pGreen0029-62-SK empty vector. Infiltrated berries were then bagged in clear plastic bags to avoid moisture loss until harvest, 10–14 d later, once fully ripe. Berries were frozen at harvest before the skin was removed and the fruit cut open for further analysis.


### Recombinant protein expression and enzyme assays
  VcAAT1a and Vv3AT were cloned into the pET30b protein expression vector containing a C-terminal histidine tag and transformed into Escherichia coli BL21 cells. Bacterial colonies were screened by colony PCR before plasmid extraction and sequence confirmation. BL21 cultures were grown at 37 °C with antibiotic selection until the OD600 reached 0.8 and were induced with 0.6 mM isopropyl-β-d-galactopyranoside (IPTG) for 17 h at 16 °C in an orbital shaker (220 rpm). Cell lysates were created using the B-PER® bacterial protein extraction reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol and purified using a His Spin Trap Ni Sepharose column (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA). Protein concentrations were determined using the Pierce™ Bradford Plus protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Enzyme assays were first performed to optimize reaction conditions (temperature and pH) and used 4 µg of recombinant protein, 200 µM acetyl-CoA, malonyl-CoA (Merck, Darmstadt Germany) or p-coumaroyl-CoA (TransMIT, Giessen, Germany), and 400 µM of the anthocyanin acceptor substrate in 100 mM sodium phosphate buffer. Enzyme kinetics were determined at pH 6.5 using 200 µM acyl donor whilst varying the concentration of the anthocyanin acceptor and stopped by adding 60 µl of chloroform:methanol (2:1) at 30 min, which was previously determined to be within the linear range in the optimization assays. Cyanidin 3-O (6ʹʹ-acetylglucoside), cyanidin 3-O (6ʹʹ-acetylgalactoside), and delphinidin 3-O (6ʹʹ-acetylglucoside) standards were made using the recombinant Vv3AT using the reaction conditions outlined above, and authenticity was confirmed using UPLC-MS.


### Three-dimensional protein modelling
  Protein models for VcAAT1a and Vv3AT were built using LocalColabFold, running ColabFold 1.5.2 (Mirdita et al., 2022) and AlphaFold 2.3.1 (Jumper et al., 2021). Protein structures were visualized, superimposed, and analysed using PyMOL 2.5.7 (The PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Version 2.0 Schrödinger, LLC). For the RsVS structure, the VcAAT1a model, and the Vv3AT model, an acetyl-CoA donor was placed in the active site by superimposition of the malonyl-CoA compound bound to Dm3Mat3 and replacement of the malonyl group by the acetyl group. For the NtMat1 structure, the bound malonyl-CoA was partially disordered, therefore the malonyl-CoA from the Dm3Mat3 structure was superimposed onto NtMat1.


### Luciferase reporter assays in tobacco
  A 2 kb VcAAT1a promoter fragment was PCR amplified using iProof™ High Fidelity DNA polymerase (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Primers were designed on the sequence of contig h2tg000453l_1 from V06.A002-186’s haplotype resolved assembly, which was assigned to haplotype 3 of the chromosome 2 QTL region linked to increased acylation of anthocyanins (Montanari et al., 2022). The 3' primer included the ATG start codon of the VcAAT1a ORF. Fragments were ligated into pGreen-0800-LUC using NotI and NcoI restriction sites. The 3ʹ NcoI site allows in-frame translational fusion with the LUC reporter gene.

  The transcription factors used for this assay, PpbHLH3, VcMYBA (Plunkett et al., 2018) VcMYBPA1, and VcMYBPA2 (Lafferty et al., 2022b), have been previously reported. Empty pHex vector was used as negative control. Agrobacterium strains harbouring either the promoter sequence or a transcription factor construct were resuspended individually in infiltration buffer (10 mM magnesium chloride, 10 μM acetosyringone) to OD600=0.5. Culture mixtures were prepared as previously described (Lafferty et al., 2022b). Approximately 200 μl of Agrobacterium mixture was infiltrated into young leaves of 4-week-old N. benthamiana. Four plants were infiltrated per treatment as biological replicates. Four leaf discs per plant were sampled 5 d after infiltration. Dual luciferase assays were performed using DLAR-2B reagents (Targeting Systems, El Cajon, CA, USA) and the Victor X4 plate reader (Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA, USA).


### Statistical analysis
  Data shown in the figures are from at least three biological replicates. Statistical significance was calculated using one-way ANOVA at a significance level of 0.05, and means comparison was performed using a Tukey test to obtain a letter groupings table.


## Results

### Haplotyped tetraploid genome assembly
  PacBio HiFi sequencing of the V06.A002-186 hybrid yielded 2.20 million high-accuracy reads with a mean Q-score of 31.0, totalling 41.5 Gb of data with a median read length of 18.5 kb, corresponding to ~78× coverage, based on an estimated blueberry haploid genome size of 530 Mb (Sakhanokho et al., 2018). Sequencing of Illumina paired end reads of ‘Hortblue Petite’ and ‘Nui’ yielded 56.6 Gb (~105×) and 30.1 Gb (~56×), respectively. K-mer spectrum profiles of Illumina data from both parents and HiFi data from V06.A002-186 are shown in Supplementary Fig. S2. A typical tetraploid k-mer spectrum profile is shown for ‘Hortblue Petite’ (Supplementary Fig. S2A), but not ‘Nui’ because of its lower sequencing depth (Supplementary Fig. S2B). Increased ratios of duplex and triplex variants are shown in the k-mer spectrum of V06.A002-186 (Supplementary Fig. S2C). K-mers generated from parental Illumina short reads contained 391 million and 253 million reads specific to ‘Hortblue Petite’ and ‘Nui’, respectively. These parental specific k-mers were then used to partition the V06.A002-186 HiFi reads into 19.2 Gb (46.4%) ‘Hortblue Petite’ and 21.3 Gb (51.3%) ‘Nui’ read sets. Two typical diploid k-mer spectrum profiles that are similar and highly heterozygous (2.81% and 2.59% heterozygosity for ‘Hortblue Petite’- and ‘Nui’-derived sequences, respectively) were generated from the partitioned V06.A002-186 HiFi reads (Supplementary Fig. S2D, E).

  The four haplotypes of V06.A002-186 were assembled into a total size of 2.11 Gb. The statistics and quality assessment result of each haplotyped assembly are shown in Table 1. The size of each of the four haplotyped assemblies is comparable (ranging from 522.2 Mb to 532.6 Mb), although the ‘Nui’-derived assembly is more fragmented than that derived from ‘Hortblue Petite’. Slightly higher long terminal repeat (LTR) assembly index (LAI) scores were achieved by the ‘Nui’-derived assemblies, compared with those derived from ‘Hortblue Petite’; however, the range of LAI scores qualifies three of these assemblies as ‘gold’ standard (LAI ≥20), and one, HP_hA (‘Hortblue Petite’-derived assembly for haplotype 1), had an LAI=19.75. All assemblies achieved high gene space completeness, as supported by BUSCO (Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs) scores >95%. The ‘Nui’-derived assemblies achieved a higher mapping rate from ‘Nui’ parental reads compared with ‘Hortblue Petite’ parental reads, and a higher percentage of ‘Hortblue Petite’ parental reads were mapped to the ‘Hortblue Petite’-derived assemblies.


### Genome anchoring using synteny and linkage map
  Inheritance of parental haplotypes in V06.A002-186 along the chromosome 2 linkage map was obtained via IBD probability estimate (Supplementary Fig. S3; Supplementary Table S2). Using this information, anchoring of the phased assembly of V06.A002-186 to the high-density linkage map allowed us to determine which of the ‘Hortblue Petite’-derived contigs was linked to the favourable allele of the chromosome 2 QTL (Fig. 1). In the ‘Hortblue Petite’-derived haplotypes, contigs ‘h1tg000119l_1’ and ‘h1tg000296l_1’ in ‘HP_hA, and ‘h2tg000414l_1’, ‘h2tg000453l_1’, ‘h2tg000274l_1’, and ‘h2tg000437l_1’ in ‘HP_hB’, aligned to the region spanning the QTL interval (8–11 Mbp on chromosome 2), based on the W85-20 v2.0 reference genome. In the ‘Nui’-derived haplotypes, contigs in this region are ‘h1tg000862l_1’ in ‘Nui_hA’ and ‘h2tg000258l_1’ in ‘Nui_hB’. The markers used to build the linkage map were retrieved in the V06.A002-186 contigs, which determined that ‘h2tg000453l_1’ is linked to the favourable QTL allele for acylation. The non-favourable alleles correspond to the ‘Hortblue Petite’-derived haplotyped contig ‘h1tg000296l_1’, and the Nui-derived contigs ‘h1tg000862l_1’ and ‘h2tg000258l_1’.


### Gene annotation of anthocyanin acyltransferase at the chromosome 2 QTL region
  AAT genes located within the QTL interval were predicted to form distinct clusters on the assembled haplotypes (Fig. 2). A full-length AAT gene contains two exons. A total of 21 genes, including seven truncated genes, were predicted on the four haplotyped contigs. A phylogenetic tree was constructed from 18 annotated gene models, excluding those that contain only a single exon (Fig. 2). All the gene models are assigned names based on their cluster in the phylogeny, hence alleles share the same number, and differing haplotype numbers are likely to be paralogous copies. On contig h1tg000296l_1 for HP_hA, seven AAT gene copies were annotated, comprising four full-length models (VcAAT1a, VcAAT2, VcAAT3, and VcAAT5) and three truncated models (VcAAT1b, VcAAT4, and VcAAT7). Notably, HP_hA’s VcAAT1a contains an internal stop codon in exon 1. and only exon 2 was found for VcAAT7. There were also seven genes annotated on h2tg000453l_1 for HP_hB, comprising four full-length models (VcAAT1a, VcAAT1b, VcAAT2a, and VcAAT3a) and two truncated models (VcAAT3b and VcAAT7). VcAAT2b has a single nucleotide deletion causing a frameshift in exon 1 supported by mapping back the HiFi raw reads, and is predicted to be non-functional (Supplementary Fig. S4A, B). Conversely, fewer AAT genes were annotated in the ‘Nui’ haplotypes compared with ‘HortBlue Petite’. On contig h1tg000862l_1 for Nui_hA, three genes were predicted, consisting of two full-length (VcAAT1 and VcAAT5) and one truncated model (VcAAT6). On h2tg000258l_1 for Nui_hB, three genes were predicted, comprising three full-length (VcAAT1, VcAAT2, and VcAAT4) and one truncated model (VcAAT7). Nui_hB VcAAT4 has an 8 bp insertion leading to a frameshift in exon 1 supported by mapping back the HiFi raw reads (Supplementary Fig. S5A, B), and only exon 2 was identified for VcAAT7.


### Quantitative RT-PCR analysis of candidate genes linked to the chromosome 2 QTL
  To assess expression patterns of the AAT candidate genes on contig ‘h2tg000453l_1’ (which is linked to the favourable QTL allele for acylation, as shown above), quantitative RT-PCR was carried out using gene-specific primers for VcAAT1a, VcAAT1b, VcAAT2a, and VcAAT3a on cDNA made from whole berry tissue at developmental stages 1–8 (Supplementary Table S1). The remaining three candidates (VcAAT1a*, VcAAT2b*, and VcAAT4*) were discarded due to the presence of an internal stop codon or frameshift mutations. Only VcAAT1a showed a strong increase in transcript abundance during ripening. In contrast, VcAAT2a showed very low transcript abundance throughout development, and VcAAT1b and VcAAT3a peaked at stage 6, then declined in the later ripening stages (Fig. 3A). LC-MS analysis of skin tissues from stages 1 to 8 showed that a rapid 4-fold increase in VcAAT1a expression between stages 6 and 8 coincided with an 8-fold increase in acylated anthocyanins from the onset of ripening to fully ripe (Fig. 3B). The concentration of total anthocyanins increased ~8-fold between stage 6 and 8, and the ratio of acylated to non-acylated anthocyanins remained relatively constant at ~40–44%.


### VcAAT1a is a clade III anthocyanin acyltransferase
  A single 1.3 kb PCR product was obtained from the amplification of the VcAAT1a ORF from the skin of ‘Hortblue Petite’ berries at stage 8 of development. Seven plasmid clones were obtained, and their sequences revealed a predicted protein of 438 amino acids that shared 100% identity with the VcAAT1a gene model. Phylogenetic analysis including 47 previously functionally characterized acyltransferases that encompassed 24 species (D’Auria, 2006; Rinaldo et al., 2015) separated the protein sequences into the five major clades described by D’Auria (2006), with VcAAT1a clustering in clade III (Fig. 4A). VcAAT1a shared 38% amino acid identity with the previously characterized Vv3AT protein isolated from the ‘Shiraz’ grape cultivar (Rinaldo et al., 2015), and 31% identity to the CbBEAT (acetyl-CoA benzylalcohol transferase) from Clarkia breweri (Dudareva et al., 1998), RhAAT (geraniol/citronellol acetyltransferase) from Rosa hybrida (Shalit et al., 2003), and the FvAAT (alcohol acyltransferase) from Fragaria vesca (Beekwilder et al., 2004). VcAAT1a shared relatively low levels of sequence conservation with other members from this clade (28%) but considerably higher levels of sequence conservation (89–91%) with other proteins annotated as AATs from Vaccinium, including VcAAT1b, VcAAT2a, and VcAAT3a, two of which are encoded by genes located on chromosome 2 of the W85-20 v2.0 genome (Vcev1_p0.Chr02.03371 and Vcev1_p0.Chr02.03383) (Mengist et al., 2022; Montanari et al., 2022). VcAAT1a has the functional motifs characteristic of the BADH superfamily, a HXXXD box at residues 151–155 and a DFGWG motif at position 368–372 (Fig. 4B). Its position in clade III was confirmed by the presence of the KPSTPT motif (amino acids 12–17) and the YPLAGR motif (amino acids 71–76), as well as the absence of the YFGNC motif typical of clade I AATs. Interestingly, of the 47 sequences compared, only VcAAT1a had a substitution at the first position of the QVTX (F/L) XCGG motif (Q138 to R138) typical of many BADHs.


### Functional characterization of VcAAT1a
  With co-infiltration of VcMYBA (to induce anthocyanin accumulation), the transient expression of the blueberry VcAAT1a and grape Vv3AT genes in the leaves of N. benthamiana and N. tabacum produced small amounts of delphinidin 3-(6-acetylglucoside) and cyanidin 3-(6ʹʹ-acetylglucoside), respectively, representing between 1.0% and 1.6% of total anthocyanins (Fig. 5). No acylated anthocyanins were detected in the GUS- and VcMYBA-only infiltrated controls. The Vv3AT positive control produced delphinidin 3-(6ʹʹ-p-coumaroylglucoside) and cyanidin 3-(6ʹʹ-p-coumaroylglucoside) in N. benthamiana and N. tabacum, respectively. Closer inspection of the UPLC-MS data for VcAAT1a revealed no evidence of anthocyanins containing other aromatic or aliphatic acyl groups. Acylated derivatives of delphinidin 3-O-rutinoside or cyanidin 3-O-rutinoside were also not detectable. These are the dominant anthocyanins in N. benthamiana and N. tabacum, respectively, when infiltrated with VcMYBA.

  Strawberry fruits were infiltrated with VcAAT1a to test acylation activity in a fruit system containing a broader variety of anthocyanins. In strawberry fruit, expression of the GUS control produced small amounts of both cyanidin 3-O-(6ʹʹ-acetylglucoside) and pelargonidin 3-O-(6ʹacetylglucoside), while tissues infiltrated with VcAAT1a or the Vv3AT control induced a 3- to 16-fold increase in these acetylated anthocyanins (Fig. 6). Again, no acetylated derivative of pelargonidin 3-O-rutinoside was observed, and no other anthocyanins containing other acyl groups were detected.


### Functional validation of VcAAT1a by transient expression in blueberry flesh
  UPLC-MS analysis of the anthocyanin composition revealed the presence of three cyanidin-based anthocyanins in the flesh of blueberries infiltrated with VcAAT1a and the empty vector control: cyanidin 3-O-glucoside, peonidin 3-O-galactoside, and peonidin 3-O-glucoside (Fig. 7). The VcAAT1a-infiltrated berries also contained a peak with an m/z value of 491.1 with a retention time corresponding to a cyanidin 3-(6ʹʹ-acetylglucoside) standard. Cyanidin 3-(6ʹʹ-acetylglucoside) was not detected in the skin of the VcAAT1a- or GUS-infiltrated berries, suggesting that the acylated anthocyanins detected were from de novo biosynthesis in the berry flesh and not from contamination from the cut skin. Interestingly, no acetylated derivatives of peonidin were detected, despite peonidin hexosides being present at similar levels to cyanidin 3-O-glucoside.


### Biochemical characterization of recombinant VcAAT1a
  To confirm the acyl donor and anthocyanin acceptor specificity, a His-tagged recombinant VcAAT1a protein was expressed and purified. Acyl donor specificity was tested using cyanidin-3-O-glucoside with p-coumaroyl-CoA, malonyl-CoA, and acetyl-CoA. Only acetyl-CoA was capable of acting as a donor for VcAAT1a, and no acetylated derivatives of cyanidin-3-O-glucoside were detected for the remaining two acyl donors for the three concentrations tested (Table 2). Reaction conditions were optimized for acyl donor concentration, pH, temperature, and reaction time (Supplementary Figs S6A, B, S7, S8). VcAAT1a was active over a broad pH range (pH 6–8) but had maximum activity at pH 6.5 and 35 °C. As ‘Hortblue Petite’ contains both acylated glucoside and galactoside anthocyanins (Montanari et al., 2022), and the transient expression systems used in this study yielded only anthocyanin glucosides, the relative ability of VcAAT1a to utilize cyanidin 3-O-galactosides or glucosides was also tested. As the reaction rates of VcAAT1a remained linear up to 220 µM for both cyanidin-3-O-glucoside and cyanidin-3-O-galactoside (Supplementary Fig. S9), Lineweaver–Burke plots were used to calculate Km and Vmax. VcAAT1a was able to acylate both the glucoside and galactoside versions of cyanidin (Table 2), and the relative Km and Vmax values for VcAAT1a for both cyanidin-3-O-glucoside and cyanidin-3-O-galactoside were comparable, although VcAAT1a showed a higher affinity for cyanidin-3-O-galactoside (Km 123 µM versus Km 227 µM) but a higher Vmax value for cyanidin-3-O-glucoside (0.11 nmol min–1). VcAAT1a failed to produce acetylated product with delphinidin 3-O-rutinoside.


### Protein modelling using AlphaFold
  To more fully understand how the tertiary structure affects donor and acceptor preference of clade III anthocyanin acyltransferases, including VcAAT1a, AlphaFold was used to build protein models for VcAAT1a and Vv3AT. The structure of VcAAT1a was modelled with very high confidence for the whole chain [predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT)=92.8], except for two solvent-exposed loops (201–214 and 234–242). A Foldseek (van Kempen et al., 2024) search against the PDB (Protein Data Bank) identified the structures of monolignol ferulate transferase from Angelica sinensis (PDB 7W26) (Liu et al., 2022) and of vinorine synthase (PDB 2BGH) (Ma et al., 2005) from Rauvolfia serpentina as the two closest structural homologues of VcAAT1a. Indeed, VcAAT1a shares the exact same topology as these two proteins (Supplementary Fig S10A, B), with differences only observed within loop regions, including the two long loops shaping the entry of the acceptor-binding pocket (120–133 and 201–214). As expected, the characteristic HXXXD and DFGWG motifs of BADH acyltransferases are perfectly predicted in the VcAAT1a model (Supplementary Fig. S10C, D). Similarly, the additional sequence signatures of BADH enzymes [KPSSPT—typical for class III BADH enzymes, YPLAGR (HPLSGR in VcAAT1a), and QVTX(F/L) (RVTF in VcAAT1a)] are also all correctly predicted in the AlphaFold model of VcAAT1a (Supplementary Fig. S10E–G).

  To gain insights into the catalytic mechanism, the VcAAT1a model was superimposed onto the experimental structures of BADH enzymes solved in complex with acyl-CoA donors. AtHCT from Arabidopsis (Levsh et al., 2016) and PvHCT (Eudes et al., 2016) from Panicum virgatum bound to p-coumaroyl-CoA; Gt5,3'AT from Gentiana triflora bound to caffeoyl-CoA (Murayama et al., 2021); and Dm3Mat3 from Dendranthema morifolium (Unno et al., 2007) and NtMat1 bound to malonyl-CoA (Manjasetty et al., 2012). The p-coumaroyl or caffeoyl groups of aromatic acyl-CoA donors all bind in a ‘sub-pocket’ located above the main solvent channel that goes through the enzymes (Fig. 8A–C). Similarly, the AlphaFold model of Vv3AT, which is capable of utilizing both p-coumaroyl-CoA and caffeoyl-CoA as donors, also has a prominent sub-pocket at the same location of its active site (Fig. 8D). In contrast, that sub-pocket is closed in the two malonyl-CoA-bound enzymes, where the side chains of distinct Arg residues (Arg178 in Dm3Mat3 and Arg39 in NtMat1) not only close the sub-pocket to select for the smaller malonyl-CoA donor, but also provide a positively charged group that interacts with the acidic group of the malonyl-CoA donor (Fig. 8E, F). Similarly, the sub-pocket of RsVinorine Synthase (PDB 2BGH) (Ma et al., 2005), which catalyses the transfer of acetyl but not malonyl or aromatic donor groups, is also closed (by the combination of three residues: Ile40, Val165, and Leu168) to create a narrow channel suitable for the smaller aliphatic donor acetyl-CoA (Fig. 8G). However, it lacks a charged group in the vicinity of the active site, in agreement with substrate preference for acetyl-CoA rather than malonyl-CoA donors observed for RsVinorine Synthase. In the AlphaFold model of VcAAT1, the sub-pocket is also completely closed, occupied by the side chain of Tyr40 to create a narrow channel around the active site suitable for a small aliphatic donor (Fig. 8H). As observed in RsVinorine Synthase, no positively charged side chain points towards the active site, possibly explaining the lack of activity of VcAAT1a towards malonyl-CoA. Instead, three polar residues (Tyr298, Ser278, and Thr369) pointing towards the likely position of the acetyl group may provide hydrogen bonding options to stabilize the acetyl donor group within the active site.

  Much larger structural differences are observed in the acceptor-binding pockets of BADH enzymes, owing to their respective activities towards hugely diverse ranges of substrates. In the VcAAT1a model, the lack of confidence in the structure of the long 201–214 loop provides uncertainties regarding the exact shape of the acceptor-binding pocket. Nevertheless, it is clear from the model that the acceptor-binding pocket of VcAAT1a is large and would therefore easily fit the bulky anthocyanidin glucoside substrates (delphinidin-3-O-glucoside, cyanidin-3-O glucoside, and cyanidin 3-O-galactoside).


### Promoter activation by VcMYBPA1 and VcMYBPA2
  Transient promoter activation assays were performed to investigate if VcAAT1a expression is activated by VcMYBA, VcMYBPA1, or VcMYBPA2. The dual luciferase assay showed that VcMYBA was able to strongly activate the VcDFR promoter, with a 35-fold increase in the LUC/REN ratio compared with the promoter-only control. However, no activation of the VcAAT1a promoter by VcMYBA was observed, with a LUC/REN ratio slightly lower than that of the promoter-only control (Fig. 9). In contrast, VcMYBPA1 and VcMYBPA2 were able to induce the VcAAT1a promoter, with a 3.3- and 3.9-fold increase, respectively, in the LUC/REN ratio relative to the VcAAT1a promoter-only control. Interestingly, the VcAAT1a promoter-only control showed a relatively high level of autoactivation compared with the DFR promoter, suggesting that endogenous transcriptional regulators in tobacco may also be able to activate the AAT promoter, and the addition of MYBA reduces this autoactivation either directly, via blocking a DNA binding interaction, or indirectly, via a protein–protein interaction.


## Discussion
  This study investigated the region underlying a previously identified QTL linked to acylated anthocyanin content in a cross between ‘Hortblue Petite’ and ‘Nui’ (V06.A002) (Montanari et al., 2022). A phased annotated assembly of the QTL region on chromosome 2 identified the main AAT candidate gene linked to an increased accumulation of acylated anthocyanins. The role of this gene was functionally validated with three transient overexpression approaches and enzyme activity assays. These showed that although VcAAT1a had a wide acceptor substrate range, it had a strict acetyl-CoA donor requirement. To the best of our knowledge, this is the most comprehensive study on the characterization of the genetic control of anthocyanin acetylation in Vaccinium to date.


### Trio-binning approach for building a haplotyped genome assembly in autotetraploid blueberry
  Generating haplotype-resolved de novo genome assemblies, which involves accurately distinguishing between sequences from different sets of chromosomes, is more challenging in polyploids due to the increased number of homologous chromosomes. In this study, the PacBio HiFi technology was combined with a trio-binning approach to assemble and phase long continuous contigs on the targeted chromosome 2 QTL region of the blueberry tetraploid genome. The use of trio-binning leveraged short reads derived from the tetraploid parental genomes to efficiently segregate long reads from an offspring into sets specific to each parent (Koren et al., 2018; Yen et al., 2020). This approach substantially reduced the assembly complexity from tetraploid to diploid configurations. By partitioning and assembling parental-specific sets, a comprehensive coverage of the gene space across all haplotyped progeny assemblies was achieved. Each phased haplotype could be individually annotated, and differences between alleles in the chromosome 2 QTL region, as well as the parental homologous chromosome they came from, could be identified. This is particularly important in a breeding context, where tracing the inheritance of a haplotype from a parental locus that is linked to the QTL region allows for more targeted selections between plants to improve the desired phenotypes. This approach can be applied to other polyploid species, to reliably map the allelic variation between haplotypes of a target locus and identify the causative variant of traits of interest for cultivar improvement programmes.


### VcAAT1a is a clade III anthocyanin acyltransferase
  Over 140 acyltransferases have been identified to date, the vast majority of these belonging to the BADH subclass. D’Auria (2006) proposed a cladogram to divide the BADH family into five major clades. Almost all of the 16 AAT gene products identified so far are located within clade I and predominantly accept malonyl-CoA or hydroxycinnamoyl-CoA as a donor, and anthocyanins or other flavonoids as the acyl acceptor molecule. The products of AAT genes in clade III mostly utilize acetyl-CoA donors and catalyse a range of acceptor substrates, from medium chain aliphatic esters to monoterpene indole alkaloids. To date, only three AATs have been placed in clade III: Ss5MaT2 from Salvia splendens, which uses malonyl-CoA (Suzuki et al., 2004), Vv3AT from grape (Rinaldo et al., 2015), and the VcAAT1a described here (Fig. 4A). All three lack the YFGNC box which is highly conserved in the clade I AATs, suggesting that they have arisen from a different lineage of the BADH family through a process of convergent evolution (Fig. 4B) . Despite their ability to both act on identical anthocyanin acceptors and utilize similar aliphatic donors, there is little primary sequence conservation between AATs in clade III and clade I (17–18%). Hence the active sites of BADH enzymes may be best described in terms of their three-dimensional shape conservation.


### VcAAT1a has strict donor preference in vivo and in vitro
  Transient expression of VcAAT1a in tobacco leaves and strawberry and blueberry flesh yielded only acetylated derivatives of delphinidin, cyanidin, and pelargonidin (Fig. 5). Furthermore, in ‘Hortblue Petite’, the acylated anthocyanins detected are exclusively conjugated galactose and glucose sugars (Montanari et al., 2022), indicating either strict donor preference or a lack of alternative aliphatic or aromatic donor substrates. The presence of p-coumaroylated anthocyanins when Vv3AT was infiltrated into two tobacco species is consistent with its wide donor range reported in Rinaldo et al (2015), and also suggests that VcAAT1a cannot utilize aromatic donors in vivo. The strict donor preference of VcAAT1a was also reinforced by the enzyme activity results, with neither p-coumaroyl-CoA nor malonyl-CoA being utilized in in vitro reactions (Table 2). Structural modelling of the VcAAT1a protein using AlphaFold suggested that the strict acetyl-CoA preference may be due to the donor-binding sub-pocket being narrowed by the presence of a Tyr40 side chain and the absence of positively charged side chain groups to coordinate with the terminal carboxyl group of the malonyl donor (Fig. 8).

  When VcAAT1a was transiently expressed in blueberry flesh, only cyanidin 3-(6ʹʹ-acetylglucoside) was detected, even though peonidin hexosides were present in similar amounts to cyanidin hexosides, suggesting that VcAAT1a is unable to acylate peonidin (Fig. 7). Possible reasons for this may be spatio-temporal differences between transgene expression and the accumulation of peonidin glycosides, or the impact of O-methylation of the B-ring that negatively affects enzyme activity.

  The lack of acylation of anthocyanins containing rutinoside sugars by VcAAT1a or Vv3AT suggests that addition of a rhamnose sugar group to an anthocyanin glucoside blocks the action of these acyltransferases. Similarly, Montanari et al. (2022) did not detect the presence of acylated derivatives of arabinoside anthocyanins, despite having high levels of arabinose-linked anthocyanins in the skin. Many AATs have strict regio-specificity for the position of the OH group in the acceptor sugar, with almost all AATs using the 6ʹʹ OH, which is missing in pentose sugars such as rhamnose and arabinose.

  Collectively, the results presented in this study suggest that VcAAT1a is a functional candidate for anthocyanin acylation in ‘Hortblue Petite’. To further prove its role in planta, further validation work is required such as stable overexpression or gene knockdown using RNAi or gene editing approaches in blueberry.


### The MYB-mediated regulation of VcAAT1a
  Since the expression of VcAAT1a appears to coincide with anthocyanin accumulation late in berry development, we predicted some form of transcription factor control. The most likely candidate was MYBA, the subgroup 6 MYB transcription factor that activates the anthocyanin biosynthetic gene in blueberry (VcMYBA) and bilberry (VmMYBA) (Plunkett et al., 2018; Karppinen et al., 2021). While VcMYBA is specific for anthocyanin production, there are other interacting MYBs. For example, VcMYBPA1 has a dual role in promoting both flavanol biosynthesis early in berry development and then anthocyanin production during ripening, while VcMYBPA2’s role is restricted to only regulating flavanol biosynthesis (Lafferty et al., 2022a). We tested all three of these MYBs for activation of the VcAAT1a promoter region (Fig. 9). The results showed a strong activation by both VcMYBPA1 and VcMYBPA2, but not by VcMYBA (Rinaldo et al., 2015). In grapevine cell suspension cultures, Vv3AT was activated by the subgroup 6 MYB, VvMYBA. While the authors did not test for activation by other MYBs, the evidence from increased Vv3AT gene expression in VvMYBA-overexpressing transgenic grape lines, together with our promoter activation assays, demonstrated that VvMYBA regulated the expression of Vv3AT (Rinaldo et al., 2015). A study in a teinturier grape also associated the elevated expression of versions of VvMYBA (VvMYBA1, 5, 6, and 7) with Vv3AT expression (Xie et al., 2020). This suggests that the MYB-mediated control of acylation may differ between blueberry and grape. It also further defines the MYB regulation in blueberry. Both MYBA and MYBPA1 are expressed as blueberry fruit ripens and they can both bind and activate the early anthocyanin pathway genes, including CHS and DFR. The major difference is the activation of UFGT, which is only activated by MYBA and not by MYBPA1. Data reported in this work now show that, while MYBA is highly specific to the initial glycosylation of the anthocyanin aglycone, it is MYBPA1 that works downstream to activate VcAAT1a for acetylation. This builds on the model for coordinated and cooperative MYB regulation for anthocyanin composition in Vaccinium species (Albert et al., 2023).


### The role of acylated anthocyanins in blueberry
  Much of our knowledge around the effects of acylation comes from studies directed at improving anthocyanin stability for industrial applications, and our understanding of their relevance in planta remains limited. In a QTL mapping study involving 128 blueberry accessions, Mengist and co-workers noted a positive correlation between fruit pH and the concentration of acylated anthocyanins, with higher pH accessions tending to have greater concentrations than lower pH samples (Mengist et al., 2020). This suggests that either the acylation process is pH dependent, or that the acylation trait has been selected for in accessions with higher vacuolar pH, which would render unmodified anthocyanins less stable. However, a previous heating study in the Merlot grape variety using forced air convection observed an increase in malvidin-based acylated anthocyanins decorated with acetyl and coumaroyl groups when berry cluster temperatures reached 40 °C (Tarara et al., 2008). Furthermore, a study in grape berries has shown that acylated anthocyanins are more resistant to degradation in response to moderately high temperature (35 °C), which may become increasingly relevant with climate change (Mori et al., 2007).

  Other aspects of acylated anthocyanins in blueberry remain unclear. For example, there have been recent advances in understanding the intercellular transport of anthocyanins, and a number of ATP-binding cassette (ABC) and multidrug and toxic compound extrusion (MATE) transporters have been suggested, although not necessarily for acylated anthocyanins (Chen et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2024). Evidence from other species shows that the transport of acylated anthocyanins is specific (Gomez et al., 2009). In grapevine, MATE transporters are responsible for the vacuolar transport of acylated anthocyanins. This acylation-dependent transport is conducted by anthoMATE1 and anthoMATE3 (VvAM1 and 3) and does not function for the non-acylated forms of malvidin 3-O-glucoside and cyanidin 3-O-glucoside. In Arabidopsis, ABC transporter-type mechanisms have been demonstrated to be specific for acylated anthocyanins, with AtABCC1, AtABCC2, and AtABCC14 capable of transport to the vacuole (Dean et al., 2022). To date, there are no reports of specific transporters of acylated anthocyanins in blueberry. Further to this, acylated anthocyanins are tightly associated with anthocyanic vacuolar inclusions (AVIs). AVIs were first described in flower petals by Markham et al. (2000) and subsequently forms of AVIs have been described in some fruits, most notably in grape, both in berries (Mizuno et al., 2015) and in cell suspensions (Conn et al., 2003, 2010). These studies show the selective aggregation of acylated anthocyanins. However, despite the intense blue colour in blueberry skin, and the presence of acylated anthocyanins, there is no clear evidence to date for AVI formation. This may be due to the type of acylation, which requires aromatic rather than aliphatic decoration for AVI formation (Kallam et al., 2017).


### Conclusions
  In this study, we describe the fine mapping and functional validation of the major causative gene (VcAAT1a) controlling anthocyanin acylation in blueberry skin. This finding provides a critical step in developing cultivars with an enhanced concentration of acetylated anthocyanins, creating new opportunities for breeding blueberries with improved resilience to heat-induced anthocyanin degradation and for additional consumer health benefits.
