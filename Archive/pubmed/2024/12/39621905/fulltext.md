# C9orf72-linked arginine-rich dipeptide repeats aggravate pathological phase separation of G3BP1


## Significance
  We address the mechanism of the toxic effect of arginine-rich dipeptide repeats (R-DPRs) poly-PR and poly-GR that arise in neurons of neurodegenerative diseases amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and frontotemporal dementia. We suggest that their likely cellular target is a cytoplasmic protein, Ras GTPase-activating protein-binding protein 1 (G3BP1). Via detailed mechanistic analyses, we show that R-DPRs switch the closed inactive state of G3BP1 to an open activated state, driving its condensation (liquid–liquid phase separation, LLPS) more effectively than its cellular partner RNA. We apply binding studies, biochemical characterization, bioinformatic analyses, and computational modeling to understand the underlying molecular mechanisms and conclude that the R-DPR–G3BP1 interaction represents a general pathological mechanism, which provides effective ways of targeting neurodegenerative diseases.


## Results

### LLPS of G3BP1 Is Aggravated by Arg-Rich C9orf72 Dipeptide Repeats.
  First, we addressed whether the high positive charge of R-DPRs enables their binding to the negatively charged IDR1 and initiates a switch-like activation mechanism conducive of SG formation (Fig. 1B), like that elicited by RNA. To this end, we tested LLPS of recombinantly purified G3BP1 in the absence and presence of toxic R-DPRs, either with or without 1% polyethylene glycol (PEG-10,000), a crowder required for its RNA-driven LLPS (36). As shown by turbidity measurements (Fig. 1C) and microscopy (Fig. 1D), polyU RNA elicits moderate LLPS of G3BP1, the much less toxic ALS-related DPR, PA30 has no effect, whereas both PR30 and GR30 induce LLPS of G3BP1 with a signal 20× increased under the given conditions (cf. SI Appendix, Fig. S1). The much stronger effects of R-DPRs vs. the RNA model polyU (the length distribution of which is not well defined) are supported by complementary experiments with polyA and total cellular RNA extract (SI Appendix, Fig. S2); both are weaker than polyU in promoting LLPS. In accord, LLPS aggravated by R-DPRs in comparison to RNA is likely to be a characteristic cellular phenomenon. LLPS is apparently promoted by the direct physical interaction of R-DPRs with G3BP1, as fluorescent images show their colocalization in the droplets that form (Fig. 1D). The differences are supported by dynamic light scattering (DLS) experiments on the size evolution of LLPS droplets (Fig. 1E and SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Here, polyU induces the rapid formation of very small droplets (about 25 nm in diameter) that do not change for about 5 min, in accord with literature suggesting the appearance of small clusters in RNA-G3BP1 phase separation (36). In contrast, both PR30 and GR30 immediately nucleate large droplets of about 460 nm in diameter, evolving toward droplets of about 1.5 μm in size in 1 h.

  The liquid nature of the droplets is supported by fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) experiments, in which the fluorescence of Alexa-488-labeled G3BP1 recovers within a few seconds with all the partners used (polyU, PR30, and GR30) (Fig. 1F and SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Interestingly, some differences appear: i) in the presence of PEG, the amplitude of recovery in all three systems is only about 50%, whereas in the absence of PEG, DPR droplets recover to a higher extent, about 70%, and ii) GR30-induced droplets appear less dynamic than PR30-induced droplets, recovering somewhat more slowly, both in the absence and presence of PEG, which may indicate differences in their internal dynamics (SI Appendix, Table S1).

  In the presence of 1% PEG, polyU nucleates about three times less droplets than R-DPRs, and these droplets tend to be about 15 times smaller in diameter and/or mature much slower than those forming in the presence of R-DPRs (Fig. 1G). The difference is even more striking in the absence of PEG, where no LLPS occurs with RNA, even at high protein and RNA concentrations. When determining the total fluorescent area of R-DPR-induced condensates (size times their number), it is at least 4 times higher than that of G3BP1 in the presence of polyU (Fig. 1H).

  The dramatic differences between R-DPR- and RNA-induced LLPS become even more apparent when we compare maximal OD values of G3BP1 characteristic of LLPS with GR30, PR30, or PolyU (Fig. 1 C, Inset) and then dissect early effects on LLPS upon controlled mixing in a microfluidic setup (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Indeed, no droplets appear by RNA as opposed to R-DPRs (SI Appendix, Table S2), with characteristic differences between GR30 and PR30. In about 20 s (exact value cannot be calculated due to diffusion and mixing combining in the microfluidics chamber), 107 droplets appeared within the observed area in the presence of GR30, while only 40 when mixing G3BP1 with PR30. Apparently, LLPS-nucleation is more effective by GR30 than by PR30 and the two systems follow different early kinetic trajectories of droplet-growth, as also proven by their different sizes (SI Appendix, Table S2) within the microfluidic channel (Fig. 2B).


### A Switch-Like Activation of G3BP1 Entails Aggravated LLPS.
  It has been shown earlier that G3BP1 is held in a closed conformation by electrostatic interactions between IDR1 and IDR3 (Fig. 1B), and RNA can open this structure and induce LLPS by binding to IDR3 and RRM (36, 37). It was recently suggested that a similar mechanism can be initiated by R-DPRs binding to IDR1 (38), which we are set to dissect in detail. Colocalization experiments (Fig. 1D) suggest the direct physical interaction between G3BP1 and R-DPRs in driving LLPS. We addressed this further by applying biolayer interferometry (BLI) to quantitate the binding of both R-DPRs and RNA to G3BP1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). As shown by the Kds determined (Fig. 2C), the binding of both GR30 and PR30 is about three orders of magnitude stronger than that of polyU (SI Appendix, Table S3, please note that although the Mw of polyU is not defined, polyU and R-DPRs are immobilized on the BLI sensor chip and are titrated with G3BP1, thus the Kd’s determined are absolute, as also explained in Materials and Methods). Of strong relevance to the likely cellular target of R-DPRs in C9-ALS/FTD, the binding of the R-DPRs with G3BP1 is 6 (GR30) or 3 (PR30) times stronger than with NPM1, which is traditionally considered the primary target of R-DPRs (21, 23). Next, we verified whether R-DPR binding is driven by the negatively charged IDR1, as opposed to RNA binding to IDR3 and RRM (37). To this end, we generated two constructs, ΔIDR3 and IDR1 (Fig. 1A), and measured their R-DPR binding strength. The subnanomolar binding strength of IDR1 (Fig. 2C and SI Appendix, Table S3) provides direct evidence that this region is the primary driver of the R-DPR–G3BP1 interaction. The interplay between IDR1 and IDR3 is further supported by the removal of the negatively charged IDR3, which makes R-DPR binding even tighter than that of the full-length protein. Overall, these results suggest a strong allosteric coupling between IDR1 and IDR3 and support the activation model in which competition between IDR3 and R-DPRs regulates the opening of G3BP1 structure and its LLPS (Fig. 1B). Direct demonstration of this conformational change by structural techniques is difficult due to the extreme phase-separation propensity of the system but is suggested by later molecular simulations as well as the effective LLPS of IDR1 (Fig. 2D) and ΔIDR3 (Fig. 2E and SI Appendix, Fig. S7) together with R-DPRs, which was not observed before (38) (although at lower protein concentrations). As expected, the ΔIDR3 construct does not undergo LLPS with polyU (Fig. 2E), underlining the importance of RNA–IDR3 interactions in physiological SG formation (36, 37), which gets probably completely overwritten by pathological R-DPR–IDR1 interactions and LLPS. To strengthen this model further, we have generated a ΔIDR1 construct, which does not bind R-DPRs (Fig. 2C) or phase separate with PR30 or GR30, but undergoes very effective LLPS with polyU (Fig. 2D), supporting that IDR1 is the primary site mediating R-DPR-driven LLPS. The proximity of DPRs and IDR1 in the condensate suggested by the simulations agrees with this model.

  In support of the predominantly electrostatic nature of R-DPR–G3BP1 interaction, it is very sensitive to salt, whereas 1,6-hexanediol, known to interfere with hydrophobic interactions, has a much smaller effect (Fig. 2F and SI Appendix, Fig. S8).

  As the aggravated LLPS propensity of G3BP1 in the presence of R-DPRs precludes small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) experiments, we have carried out coarse-grained Monte Carlo simulations of a G3BP1 monomer alone or in the presence of an RNA or DPR molecule (SI Appendix, Table S4) to approach the suggested structural changes (Fig. 2G). Analysis of 100 equilibrium frames of the trajectories suggests that i) in the absence of a binding partner, the predominant interaction within G3BP1 is between IDR1 and IDR3, with secondary interactions between IDR2 and IDR3, and IDR1 and RRM, in accord with the closed conformation suggested (Fig. 1B); ii) in the presence of RNA, IDR3–RNA interactions lessen the intramolecular IDR1–IDR3 interaction; and iii) DPRs have a much stronger effect: the IDR1–DPR interaction becomes dominant, completely abrogating the IDR1–IDR3 interaction. Most interestingly, when both DPR and RNA are present (Fig. 2 G, Right), the IDR1–DPR interaction remains dominant, but the IDR3–RNA interaction becomes stronger than with RNA alone, suggesting an allosteric coupling between IDR1 and IDR3, resulting in positive cooperativity between DPR and RNA binding. This cooperativity is confirmed by direct binding experiments, in which the binding of GR30 to G3BP1 (Kd = 5.97 ± 0.04 nM) becomes even stronger (Kd = 0.01 ± 0.008 nM) in the presence of RNA (SI Appendix, Fig. S6J). A reciprocal experiment would provide additional evidence for the cooperativity of R-DPR–RNA binding; however, it could not be done due to the extreme LLPS propensity of the R-DPR–G3BP1 system at saturating R-DPRs concentrations.


### Mechanism of R-DPR–Induced LLPS of G3BP1.
  These results underscore that R-DPR binding at IDR1 and RNA binding at IDR3 result in a similar activation mechanism, leaving the question open whether the nature of long-range contacts conducive to LLPS is also similar. First, we have checked whether mutant G3BP1, lacking the IDR3 region (∆IDR3), can undergo LLPS in the presence of R-DPRs. Intriguingly, ΔIDR3 undergoes much more intense phase separation at a concentration, at which LLPS of full-length (FL) G3BP1 is not apparent with microscopy (Fig. 3A). This result suggests that the positively charged IDR3 is inhibitory to R-DPR-induced LLPS and does not provide crosslinks within the condensates. When RNA is present, IDR3 does play a positive role, as the effect of R-DPR and PolyU on FL G3BP1 is more than additive, showing a strong cooperativity (Fig. 3B). The ensuing facilitated binding manifested in Kd values (Fig. 2C and SI Appendix, Fig. S6J) also results in promoting LLPS, apparently due to R-DPRs and RNA operating by the same mechanism (Fig. 1B). This is particularly expressed for GR30, when counting the number of droplets or their mean cross-section area (Fig. 3C), or the total area covered by droplets (Fig. 3D), the numbers are more than additive. Lack of cooperativity—or even additivity—at saturating concentrations of both partners (SI Appendix, Fig. S9) provides further argument for a mechanism shared by R-DPR and RNA since it would not be the case if they induced parallel, additive mechanisms. Arguing for the generality of the pathological mechanisms of R-DPRs is that cooperativity of GR30 and RNA binding is also manifest in the case of the nucleolar R-DPR target nucleophosmin (NPM1, SI Appendix, Fig. S10), much more than in the case of PR30.

  To probe into the possible mechanism of LLPS, we also performed coarse-grained Monte Carlo simulations on a large number of molecules (Fig. 3E and SI Appendix, Fig. S11). We have carried out simulations of 200 dimerizing FL G3BP1 molecules, with either 200 RNA, 200 DPR, or both 200 RNA and 200 DPR molecules. ΔIDR1 or ΔIDR3 deletion constructs were also simulated in the presence of RNA and DPR molecules (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). The ensuing trajectories were analyzed for the distribution of contact density, which is likely to reflect condensation propensity of the system, as high contact number reflects multivalent interactions conducive to LLPS (Fig. 3F). The trends of different combinations suggest that: i) G3BP1 alone or with RNA has little tendency to undergo LLPS, ii) R-DPRs promote G3BP1 LLPS stronger than RNA, iii) R-DPRs and RNA cooperate and have a stronger effect together, iv) ΔIDR1 does not phase separate even in the presence of DPRs and RNA, and v) ΔIDR3 readily phase separates in the presence of DPRs and RNA. Interestingly, we found experimentally that ΔIDR1 cannot phase separate with R-DPRs but does so in the presence of RNA (Fig. 2D). As our simulations suggest that ΔIDR1 would not phase separate with DPR and RNA together, it probably reflects DPR binding directly to RNA, which is not apparent with FL G3BP1 (as DPR binding to IDR1 is 1,000× stronger than to RNA) but comes to play in the absence of IDR1 (and DPR binding there).

  By analyzing the detailed domain–domain or domain–partner contact maps of the multichain trajectories (SI Appendix, Fig. S12), the picture gains some more interesting details: i) the dominant interaction that is likely critical for LLPS in the absence or presence of RNA is between R-DPR and IDR1, ii) when R-DPR is present, there is always IDR1–IDR1 interactions, and iii) even in the presence of R-DPR and RNA together, contact between IDR1 domains, and also bound R-DPRs, prevail and there is a significant interaction between RNA and DPR molecules. Overall, these analyses support our experimental claims that G3BP1 fails to drive LLPS as long as IDR1 is predominantly in contact with IDR3, but if IDR3 is absent or outcompeted by DPRs, G3BP1 will effectively phase separate. By interacting with IDR3, RNA can induce phase separation, but to a lesser extent than R-DPRs, because it can outcompete strong intramolecular IDR1–IDR3 interaction only to a limited extent. With R-DPRs, phase separation is mostly driven by tight stacking between IDR1 and DPR, possibly also including RNA but not that much IDR3. This would contrast the previous model of RNA-driven LLPS in which small G3BP1-RNA clusters are crosslinked by long RNA threads, keeping protein concentration (1 mg/mL) way below that of RNA concentration (64 mg/mL) in the condensates (36). To check on this prediction of the model, we assessed the enrichment of G3BP1 in condensates in microscopic images of G3BP1 LLPS in the presence of polyU, PR30, or GR30 (Fig. 1D): in fact, the ratio of intensities in droplet vs. solution is much higher for R-DPRs than for RNA (Fig. 3G) underscoring the predicted much tighter packing of proteins in the former.


### Pathological implications of R-DPR–G3BP1 interactions.
  Our results that R-DPRs are order(s) of magnitude stronger than RNA in promoting LLPS of G3BP1 via an apparently very different molecular organization, appear to draw strong mechanistic parallels with the profound differences in SG formation under pathological and physiological conditions. To follow up on the ensuing implications on cell physiology and pathology, we have further tested and compared the behavior of G3BP1–polyU and G3BP1–R-DPR droplets. First, a hallmark of C9-ALS/FTD is the appearance of R-DPR-positive inclusions/aggregates (1, 4) also demonstrated by SG induction by R-DPR overexpression in cells (22). To address the appearance of ordered, amyloid-like states positive for Thioflavin S (ThS, a substitute of Thioflavin T (ThT) in the presence of RNA), we measured the evolution of ThS fluorescence for FL G3BP1 or IDR1 mixed with polyU and PR30 or GR30 (Fig. 4A). The differences are striking: R-DPRs cause a fast transition to a ThS positive state of FL G3BP1 while mixing IDR1 with either partners (polyU and PR30 or GR30)—although it undergoes LLPS with DPRs (Fig. 2D)—does not lead to an increase in the ThS fluorescence signal. We have also followed the possible differences by fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 4B). In the presence of polyU, no aggregates are formed over 7 d of incubation, whereas solid-like droplets recovering slowly in FRAP experiments are formed when R-DPRs are also present (cf. Fig. 1F vs. Fig. 4B).

  Second, the recruitment of ALS/FTD-linked RBPs, which often mislocalize into pathological inclusions (41), was considered. To address whether this happens with R-DPR-driven G3BP1 condensates, first we added Alexa-488-labeled hnRNPA2 to RED-labeled G3BP1 condensates forming in the presence of GR30 or polyU. As hnRNPA2 also has a strong tendency to phase separate (42), we kept it under its own saturation concentration (Csat) and visualized its localization by fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 4C). Clearly, it has a strong tendency to partition into R-DPR–G3BP1 condensates, but not into polyU-induced droplets. Whereas distribution of G3BP1 appears homogeneous in the droplets formed, hnRNPA2 is enriched around its periphery, recapitulating earlier observations of the formation of multilayered condensates (43), a key feature of the core-shell architecture of cellular SGs (44). Possible pathological recruitment of other key RBPs implicated in C9-ALS/FTD inclusions, TDP-43, and hnRNPA1 was also tested, by adding a cell lysate to GR30- or PR30-induced droplets and demonstrating the partitioning into droplets by Western blot of the high-density (droplet) phase of the solution (Fig. 4D). Interestingly, both are recruited effectively, but to a different extent in the presence of GR30 and PR30. A similar recruitment was also observed into polyU-induced droplets (Fig. 4D), which may reflect the recently recognized tendency that initial pathological cytoplasmic translocation of TDP-43 may be followed by up-concentration in normal SGs (beyond a threshold), where it then undergoes intracondensate demixing and subsequent transitions into pathological aggregates (45).


### Interactomes of R-DPRs Underscore Their Roles in Impairing Stress-Granule and Nucleolar Function.
  Next, we carried out a detailed analysis of the cellular interactomes of R-DPRs to assess possible further generalities of the features of aggravated G3BP1 LLPS observed, which are also likely shared by the R-DPR-targeted nucleolar protein NPM1. This latter we infer from the strong mechanistic parallels of the switch-like activation of G3BP1 and NMP1, as NPM1 i) also has acidic and basic IDR tracts and an RNA-binding region (RBD), which engage in intramolecular electrostatic interactions, and ii) these interactions are broken by RNA under physiological, and R-DPRs under pathological conditions (21, 23, 32, 33). For a broad comparative analysis, we have compiled the known interaction partners of poly-PR (298 proteins), poly-GR (198), both R-DPRs (374), G3BP1 (135), and NPM1 (433) from high-throughput studies (23, 46, 47) and the BioGRID database (48). As controls, we have taken the entire human proteome (20,449), the interaction partners of ALS/FTD-linked poly-GA, which is much less toxic than R-DPRs (82), and also a subset of R-DPR interactors (87) whose homologs have a strong effect on R-DPR toxicity in a Drosophila RNAi silencing experiment (enhancers or suppressors, hereafter called R-DPR “effectors”) (23) (Dataset S1). We have then compared a broad range of established or predicted, relevant features of these interactomes (SI Appendix, Tables S5 and S6).

  First, by a predictor of LLPS drivers, DeePhase (49), we found that R-DPR-, G3BP1-, and NPM1 interactors are significantly more LLPS prone than the human proteome (Fig. 4E), while interactors of poly-GA are less LLPS prone. Next, we looked for features relevant to binding RNA or having negatively charged patches, as identified for NPM1 partners (21, 23). Interestingly, different RNA-binding motifs, such as an RBD, linked R motifs (at least two RxR or RxxR motifs located maximum 10 residues apart), or linked RGG/RG motifs, are all enriched not only in NPM1 and G3BP1, but also in R-DPR partners, while they are depleted in poly-GA interactors. These preferences suggest strong mechanistic parallels between NPM1 and G3BP1, and possibly other R-DPR interaction partners. As suggested, the switch-like activation of G3BP1 (36, 37) and NPM1 (21, 23) relies on the presence of intrinsically disordered negative and positive patches that can engage in intramolecular electrostatic interactions enabled by IDR flexibility, and RBD(s) that enable RNA to switch the protein (Fig. 1B). To find out whether R-DPRs act on a broader set of such inherently switch-like proteins, we investigated these features next (Fig. 4E). Most interestingly, in comparison to the whole human proteome, R-DPR interaction partners are highly enriched in proteins that show all three features simultaneously (−patch, +patch, and an RBD), and this combined feature is higher for effectors, which modulate R-DPR toxicity. This is also characteristic of the interaction partners of NPM1.

  These strong preferences convey an especially important message: Not only G3BP1 and NPM1 share similar switch-like features making them vulnerable to R-DPR misregulation, but these features are also shared by several other proteins, 23 R-DPR interactors and 7 R-DPR toxicity effectors (SI Appendix, Table S7). When looking into the list of these predicted proteins, one cannot miss their very strong association with MLOs targeted in ALS/FTD, nucleoli (17 proteins), SGs (8), or both (5). An even broader range of targets affected by R-DPR toxicity relying on the noted mechanism may be signaled by R-DPRs targeting similar P-body (7) and nuclear speckle (4) proteins. Probably of high mechanistic relevance is that when we checked the literature of these proteins for expressions “conformational change” or “conformational switch”, we could positively identify NCL (nucleolin) (50), DDX RNA helicases (51), EIF5B, (52) and U2AF2 (53) as proteins in the functioning of which conformational switching plays an important role. The overlaps are put in further context by the similarity of all LLPS-related and switch-like features not only of R-DPR interactors but, surprisingly, also by NPM1- and G3BP1 interactors (Fig. 4E). A large fraction of R-DPR interactors (40.1%) also interact with either G3BP1 or NPM1, while 31.1% of G3BP1 interactors and 28.9% of NPM1 interactors are also binding partners of R-DPRs. These numbers are significantly higher than the ones for poly-GA interactors (SI Appendix, Table S6), which indicate that R-DPRs hit specifically and heavily the interactomes of G3BP1 and NPM1, i.e., they are among the top self-sufficient LLPS drivers that share interaction partners with R-DPRs (SI Appendix, Fig. S14). When analyzing their interactomes in terms of different MLOs, they show a strong preference for SGs, followed by nucleoli and P-bodies, with less inclination toward postsynaptic density and nuclear speckles (SI Appendix, Fig. S15).

  A final intriguing and so-far little appreciated aspect of these generalizations is that there is a significant difference between the behavior of poly-PR and poly-GR, most often considered indiscriminately as “R-DPRs.” However, when we compare their cellular binding partners (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 and Dataset S1), poly-GR shows a strong preference for SG-associated proteins: 25% of its unique interactors are involved with SGs (only 10.8% for poly-PR), whereas the same numbers of nucleolar proteins are in the reverse order: 6.6% interacts with poly-GR, and 13.1% with poly-PR. These differing cellular preferences are unquestionably the manifestation of the vastly different physicochemical characteristics of the two R-DPRs, which is apparent in all aspects studied (Fig. 4F).


## Discussion
  DPRs produced by C9orf72 hexanucleotide repeat expansion arise because of the most prevalent familial mutation in ALS/FTD (1, 3). There are multiple lines of evidence that poly-PR and poly-GR—the two R-DPRs—exert their toxicity and pathological effect via perturbing the physiological balance of the formation, dynamics, and dissolution of cellular MLOs, nucleoli in the nucleus (21, 23) and SGs in the cytoplasm (16, 22, 23). Whereas their effects on nucleoli apparently proceed via perturbing the nucleolar driver NPM1 (21, 23), their target and precise mechanism in SGs have not yet been unveiled. In terms of the underlying molecular mechanisms, NMP1 is kept in a closed, LLPS-incompetent state by electrostatic attraction between its oppositely charged acidic (A) and basic (B) IDRs, and both RNA and R-DPRs can compete off this electrostatic interaction, thereby opening NPM1 structure and enabling a range of homotypic and heterotypic interactions, promoting physiological and pathological LLPS, respectively. We hypothesized that there might be an analogous mechanism in SG misregulation by R-DPRs, as the primary SG driver G3BP1 (and its paralog G3BP2) also occupies a central role in the SG interaction network (37) and operates by a similar electrostatic switch-like activation between closed and LLPS-competent open states promoted by RNA (36, 37).

  In line with these hypotheses, multiple cellular studies imply that G3BP1 could be the prime cytoplasmic target of R-DPRs. First, G3BP1/2 are among the proteins interacting with PR30 in cellular extracts (22, 23). Overexpression of PR100 in HeLa (13) induces strong SG formation, much stronger than that by much less toxic PA100, in a G3BP1/2-dependent manner (13). In another study (23), G3BP1/2 were found to be the prime interaction partners of overexpressed PR50 and GR50, and were also shown to be among the top genetic enhancers of GR50 toxicity in a Drosophila model. Interestingly, similar features have been found for NPM1, the primary driver of the RNA-induced formation of nucleoli (23).

  All these point to G3BP1 as the primary cytoplasmic target of R-DPRs in C9-ALS/FTD. Here, we provide compelling evidence for this notion and dissect the underlying molecular mechanisms of G3BP1 activation by R-DPRs, which leads to aggravated LLPS that explains pathological SG induction in C9-ALS/FTD. We do observe that R-DPRs promote the LLPS of G3BP1 much more intensively than RNA, as they can promote condensation in the absence of a crowder, or about five times more intensively in the presence of a crowder, and typically at a G3BP1 Csat concentration an order of magnitude lower than RNA. These findings probably parallel, and mechanistically underscore earlier observations (16, 22, 23) of the aggravated formation of SGs in cellular models of R-DPR intoxication. The pathological implications of these differences are also underlined by the notable tendency of R-DPR–G3BP1 droplets to evolve toward ThS-positive states and to recruit ALS/FTD-linked RBPs involved in pathological inclusions.

  These strong effects on LLPS stem from a very strong binding of R-DPRs to G3BP1: their binding is about 1,000 times tighter than that of RNA, whereas binding data with ΔIDR1, ΔIDR3, and IDR1 constructs, and molecular simulations point to IDR1 being the primary interaction site of R-DPRs. Salt sensitivity underscores the electrostatic nature of the interaction between positively charged R-DPRs and the negatively charged IDR. Prior data suggest that G3BP1 is held in a compact, LLPS-incompetent state by attractive electrostatic interactions between its two long IDRs, the negatively charged IDR1 and positively charged IDR3 (36, 37). Under physiological stress, when mRNA is released from stalled polysomes, binds to IDR3, opens G3BP1 structure, and promotes SG formation by LLPS by extensive protein–protein, protein–RNA, and RNA–RNA interactions (36, 37). Droplets thus formed in vitro, and SGs probably driven by an analogous mechanism in the cell, are highly dynamic, showing rapid recovery by FRAP and rapid dissolution upon the termination of stress.

  Stark differences of the mechanism of LLPS with RNA and R-DPRs underscore the distinction between physiological and pathological situations, as the LLPS promoted by RNA (36) has many features different from what we observed for R-DPRs. Whereas a short RNA (A60) cannot make G3BP1 phase separate, R-DPRs of only 30 dipeptide units do, and even with long RNA [e.g., total cell extract (36)] LLPS requires crowding, not needed with R-DPRs. In addition, RNA-based LLPS requires many additional protein–protein, protein–RNA (and probably RNA–RNA) interactions, as G3BP1 constructs ΔNTF2L and ΔIDR3 (termed originally ΔRG) do not phase separate. This suggests that besides RNA–RRM interactions, NTF2L–NTF2L, IDR3–IDR3, RNA–IDR3, and RNA–RNA interactions all play a role (36). In the case of R-DPRs, however, even IDR1 and ΔIDR3—but not ΔIDR1—can effectively phase separate with R-DPRs, suggesting a very different mechanism, where the IDR1–R-DPR interaction is sufficient for driving LLPS. With RNA, condensates form by small RNA-G3BP1 clusters crosslinked by long RNA molecules forming an extended network (36), resulting in condensates with high RNA (64 mg/mL), but low G3BP1 (1 mg/mL) concentrations. In contrast, with R-DPRs, molecular simulations suggest the formation of rather dense protein condensates driven by IDR1–R-DPR–IDR1 stacking, explaining why G3BP1 in these R-DPR-driven condensates reaches an order of magnitude higher local concentrations. For the precise mechanism, we may argue that IDR1 is much longer than DPRs (85 vs. 60 AAs), plus its negative charge is distributed along two shorter patches, thus IDR1 and an R-DPR cannot form a strict 1:1 complex. Rather, they probably form a compact but fuzzy complex, with unpaired overhangs enabling to cross-link with other molecules required for condensate assembly.

  A further interesting and highly intriguing aspect of the general perception of pathological R-DPR effects is the differences between the behavior of poly-GR and poly-PR we observe. Due to their similar, strong toxicity and generation from the same extended hexanucleotide repeat region [poly-GR from the sense, and poly-PR from the antisense mRNA strand (10)], the two arginine-rich DPRs are often mentioned and treated together (as R-DPRs), and results with any of them are often generalized (4). Even in the literature, however, there are significant and probably meaningful differences between their pathological actions. For example, when administered externally, GR20 and PR20 peptides enter (U2OS) cells, migrate to the nucleoli, and have a morphological-toxic effect, which is much more pronounced for PR20, which also has much longer cellular half-life (72 h) than GR20 (20 to 30 min) (8). Whereas poly-PR is suggested to be more toxic than poly-GR (8, 54), it could be due to its much higher cellular stability (8).

  Their cellular locations also show characteristic differences. For example, upon transfecting GFP-GR50 and GFP-PR50 into HeLa cells, GFP-GR50 localizes both in the cytoplasm and the nucleus, whereas GFP-PR50 is found predominantly in the nucleus (23). GFP-GR50 tends to colocalize with SG markers, but GFP-PR50 is not detectable in SGs. In addition, the expression of DPRs in primary motor neurons shows a much stronger nuclear aggregation with PR50 than with GR50 (54). A further potentially very interesting difference is that in a Drosophila RNAi screen for genetic modifiers of GFP-GR50 toxicity, NPM1 is a strong suppressor, whereas G3BP1 is a strong enhancer of toxicity, again arguing that the primary target of poly-GR toxicity is not NPM1 but G3BP1 (23). The much tighter binding of both R-DPRs—especially GR30—to G3BP1 than NPM1 may provide a strong argument for this notion.

  In support of all these cellular findings, we do observe characteristic differences in the physicochemical behavior of the two R-DPRs (Fig. 4F). FL G3BP1, IDR1, and ΔIDR3 constructs are all bound much stronger by GR30 than PR30, and more cooperativity is observed between RNA and GR30 than between RNA and PR30, which also applies in the case of NPM1 that probably comes from the very different flexibility of poly-GR vs. poly-PR, poly-PR being extended, rod-like, probably interfering with RNA binding by the downstream IDR3 region of G3BP1 and B region of NPM1, whereas poly-GR is very flexible, thus the complex GR30-G3BP1 and GR30-NPM1 could accommodate binding of RNA molecule(s). Microfluidics show differences in their early kinetics probably relevant for nucleation, and GR30–G3BP1 condensates appear to be denser than PR30–G3BP1 condensates. When analyzing their cellular interactomes, poly-GR interactome shows preference for SGs, whereas poly-PR interactome for nucleoli. These findings suggest a pathophysiological specialization between the two R-DPRs, poly-PR primarily affecting NPM1 in nucleoli whereas poly-GR having more effect on G3BP1 in SGs. Of course, there might be no absolute distinction between NPM1 and G3BP1 by the two R-DPRs, rather preferences that also depend on cellular conditions. Besides the observed binding ratios, these could be driven by internal concentration ratios of poly-PR vs. poly-GR (poly-PR having much longer half-life in the cell than poly-GR), cellular concentrations of G3BP1 vs. NPM1, potential differences in membrane penetrance (such as through the nuclear membrane to affect NPM1), and the presence of other R-DPR binding proteins, which may influence the (relative) effective concentration of the two R-DPRs.

  The strong effects of R-DPRs on G3BP1 provide a mechanistic context for findings that overexpression of GR50 and PR50 promotes spontaneous SG formation without stress, and these SGs are less dynamic, they show an impaired G3BP1 exchange, and are also much less inclined to disassemble than the ones induced by stress (23). As the major pathological hallmark of C9orf72 R-DPR-related ALS/FTD is the appearance of cellular ribonucleoprotein inclusions enriched in R-DPRs and other pathological proteins (4, 5), and a prominent mechanism of R-DPR effects in C9-ALS/FTD is to compromise SG dynamics, it is not far-fetched to conclude that the observed tendency of R-DPR-driven G3BP1 droplets to progress toward ThS-positive aggregates and to recruit C9-ALS/FTD-related hnRNPA1, hnRNPA2, and TDP-43, are also mechanistically linked to pathological processes characteristic of ALS/FTD. This fits into the general framework of our study, i.e., that a great proportion of R-DPR interaction partners display a noted switch-like character shared by G3BP1 and NPM1, thus R-DPRs potentially having an even more profound effect on these and other MLOs.

  As a final note, we should not miss that besides ALS/FTD, RAN translation can also generate potentially aggregation-prone peptide repeats in at least 10 other repeat expansion disorders (55). For example, Poly-GP and poly-PR feature in spinocerebellar ataxia 36 (SCA36) (56), poly-EG and poly-RE in X-linked dystonia parkinsonism (XDP) (57), and poly-LPAC and poly-QAGR in myotonic dystrophy type 2 (DM2) (58). In addition, repetitive, cationic peptides are used by a broad range of organisms as venoms, toxins, and antimicrobials (59, 60), which may similarly target a broad range of host RBPs involved in biomolecular condensation as their primary mechanism of toxicity. In accord, the general mechanistic insight generated here may not only open broad avenues for targeting ALS/FTD, but it may also advance our understanding of these related biological phenomena at the molecular level.


## Materials and Methods

### G3BP1 Constructs.
  The DNA-construct encoding N-terminally GST-tagged and C-terminally polyHis-tagged full-length Ras GTPase-activating protein-binding protein 1 (G3BP1, UniProt Q13283) within a pGEX-2T vector was provided by Prof. Paul-Taylor (St. Jude Children’s Research Hospital, Memphis, TN) (37). This plasmid was used as a template to generate: i) a plasmid (pHYRSF53 vector) carrying a fragment encoding SUMO-tagged G3BP1; ii) a plasmid (pHYRSF53 vector) carrying a fragment encoding SUMO-tagged IDR1 (aa T143-D226); iii) a plasmid (pGEX-2T vector) carrying a GST-tagged deletion construct of IDR3 (aa. 412E-466Q). The plasmid (pHYRSF53 vector) carrying a fragment encoding SUMMO-tagged G3BP1 was used as a template to generate iv) a plasmid (pHYRSF53 vector) carrying a SUMO-tagged deletion construct of IDR1 (aa. 143 T-226D) as described in SI Appendix through Gibson assembly. Note that SUMO-tags are removed after purification (Protein Expressions and Purification).


### Protein Expressions and Purification.
  Escherichia coli Rosetta 2 (DE3) carrying plasmids encoding FL G3BP1, ΔIDR3, ΔIDR1, or IDR1 were grown in TB medium and protein expression was performed as described in SI Appendix. Cell lysis was loaded on a HisTrap HP column (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL) with immobilized Ni2+ beads equilibrated with HisBufferA (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 250 mM NaCl/1 M NaCl (∆IDR1), 0.5 mM TCEP, and 50 mM imidazole). PolyHis-tagged proteins were captured by the column and eluted with a linear imidazole gradient (50 mM imidazole to 500 mM imidazole over 10 column volumes). Eluted GST-G3BP1-ΔIDR3 was then further purified by a GST HP column (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL) and size exclusion chromatography, and tag was cleaved off as explained in SI Appendix. Other constructs were further purified by size exclusion chromatography and tags were cleaved off as explained in SI Appendix. hnRNPA2 was expressed and purified as described in ref. 42. Cleaved product was collected, concentrated, flash frozen, and stored at −80 °C (SI Appendix, Fig. S16).


### Dipeptide Repeats and RNA.
  Three different DPRs related to ALS/FTD were used. Toxic arginine-rich [Pro-Arg]30 (PR30) and [Gly-Arg]30 GR30, and nontoxic [Pro-Ala]30 (PA30), were purchased from SynPeptide (Shanghai, China). All three DPRs were dissolved at 1 mM concentration in water, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C until use. Polyuridylic acid (polyU) and polyadenylic acid (polyA) were purchased from Merck Life Science (Darmstadt, Germany). Total RNA was extracted from HEK293T cells cultured in Dulbecco’s modified eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (GIBCO) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (GIBCO), at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Cells were lysed in PBS buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor by sonication for 45 s (15 s pulse on, 15 s pulse off) at 30% amplification. Next, cell lysis was pelleted by centrifugation for 15 min. at 14,000×g. Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) according to manufacturer protocol.


### Nucleic Acid Labeling.
  Polyuridylic acid (polyU) (Merck Life Science BV, Darmstadt, Germany) was labeled by using Cy5-conjugated cytidine (bis)phosphate (Jena Bioscience, Jena, Germany) and biotinylated cytidine (bis)phosphate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) using T4 RNA ligase enzyme (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA).


### Fluorescence Labeling of Proteins.
  Peptides, hnRNPA2 and G3BP1, at 10 µM concentrations were labeled by using Protein Labeling Kit RED-NHS 2nd Generation (Nanotemper, South San Francisco, CA) and Alexa Fluor 488 NHS Ester (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) by following manufacturer protocol. The labeling sample was dialyzed overnight to storage buffer.


### LLPS Assay, Turbidity.
  To follow liquid–liquid phase separation by turbidity, optical density (OD) at 600 nm was measured at 25 °C for 2 h in a BioTek SynergyTM Mx (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA) plate reader with continuous shaking. Samples were prepared in a total volume of 25 μL in a Cell culture 384-well microplate with clear bottom (Greiner, Kremsmünster, Austria). Each experiment was repeated three times.


### LLPS Assay, Microscopy.
  To visualize droplets, microscopic images were captured by a Leica DFC7000 GT camera connected to a Leica DMi8 microscope (Leica microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Samples were prepared by initiating liquid–liquid phase separation of Alexa-488-labeled G3BP1 unless otherwise mentioned in presence of an excess of nonlabeled G3BP1 with RED-labeled DPRs or polyU unless otherwise mentioned in an excess of nonlabeled DPRS or polyU. After incubation of 5 min on ice, the samples were visualized on a microscopic slide with a 100× oil immersion objective. Fluorescence was visualized by a FITC filter (green) and a Rhodamine filter (red). Experiments with hnRNPA2 LCD were performed similarly with RED-labeled G3BP1 in an excess of nonlabeled G3BP1 and Alexa-488-labeled fluorescently labeled hnRNPA2 and nonlabeled GR30. Quantification was performed as in SI Appendix.


### Thioflavin S Assay.
  In a 384-well microplate with clear bottom (Greiner, Kremsmünster, Austria), samples were prepared in a total volume of 20 μL supplemented with 15 μM Thioflavin S (ThS). The red shift of its emission spectrum upon binding with beta-sheets, was recorded quantitatively in a BioTek SynergyTM Mx plate reader by measuring emission at 490 nm after excitation at 450 nm.


### FRAP Analysis of Dynamics of LLPS Droplets.
  Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) was used for assessing internal dynamics G3BP1-DPR condensates formed by LLPS. To this end, samples were prepared as described in the earlier paragraph. Next, prebleach images were recorded for 10 frames before the bleaching of droplets was performed by a laser shot for 50 ms. Recovery of the fluorescence was then captured for 200 frames. Each experiment was performed in triplicate.


### DLS.
  To measure droplet size, DLS measurements were performed on a DynaPro NanoStar (Wyatt Technology Corp, Santa Barbara, CA). Before measuring, 80 μL of phase separating sample was administered to a disposable cuvette (Wyatt Technology Corp, Santa Barbara, CA), surrounded by a buffer in a separate chamber. Intensity of scattering was then measured at a scattering angle of 95° at 25 °C for 100 min, collecting 10 acquisitions of 8 s. The analysis of the data was carried out by software package DYNAMICS 7.1.9. and hydrodynamic radius (Rh) was calculated. Each experiment was performed in triplicate.


### Measuring Kd of Interactions by Biolayer Interferometry.
  Octet biolayer interferometry (BLI) experiments were carried out in a buffer of 50 mM HEPES, 100 mM NaCl, pH 7.5, 0.05 % Tween 20, pH 7.5, on an Octet RED96 instrument (Sartorius AG, Gottingen, Germany) as described in SI Appendix.


### Droplet Localization Experiments.
  To assess whether ALS/FTD-related protein partners are recruited into G3BP1-R-DPR droplets, HEK293T cells supernatant (SI Appendix) was added to phase-separating G3BP1 (60 µM) by either GR30 (50 µM), PR30 (50 µM), or polyU (40 ng/µM) as mentioned above kept at 4 °C for 30 min. A small sample was stored and rest of the sample was pelleted by centrifugation for 15 min. at max. speed. Supernatant was stored and pellet was resuspended in 8 M urea. All samples were separated by size by SDS-PAGE and protein presence was verified with Western blot (SI Appendix).


### Data Collection for Bioinformatics Analysis.
  Interactors of R-DPRs were collected from various high-throughput screens (HTS). In PR100-FLAG immunoprecipitation, 177 interactors were found (46). In PR50 and GR50 GFP immunoprecipitation, 194 high-confidence interactors were identified, and PR50-interactors and GR50-interactors were distinguished (23). Finally, in a proximity-dependent biotin identification (BioID), interactors of PR100 (73), GR100 (83), and GA100 (82), were identified (47). In the bioinformatic analysis, we have considered i) all high-confidence R-DPR (PR + GR) interactors merged from the above three sources, ii) PR and GR interactors separately, and iii) GA interactors as negative controls (Dataset S1) were taken from (47).

  Of DPR interactors, we have also analyzed proteins identified in a HTS RNAi screen of genetic modifiers of GFP-GR50 toxicity in Drosophila melanogaster, i.e., enhancers and suppressors (23). Human orthologs of 21 D. melanogaster enhancers (with <25% viability) and 66 suppressors (with >48% viability) were obtained from Fig. 2E of Lee et al. (23) (Dataset S1).

  The G3BP1 interactome along the line suggested in ref. 37 was taken from the BioGRID database (48) by only accepting interacting partners supported by low-throughput or at least two high-throughput evidence. A total of 135 proteins were obtained and analyzed (Dataset S1).

  The interactors of NPM1 were taken from the BioGRID database by only accepting interacting partners supported by low-throughput or at least two high-throughput evidence. The obtained list was complemented by the 132 NPM1 interactors identified in a pull-down assay performed as in (32), to finally arrive at 433 unique NPM1 interactors (Dataset S1).

  The stress granule (SG) proteome was obtained from RNAGranuleDB (61) by taking only Tier 1 SG proteins. The nucleolar proteome was derived from ref. 62, by only accepting proteins with “Enhanced” or “Supported” nucleolar localization to gain a high-confidence dataset.

  The human proteome (UP000005640) was obtained from UniProt (63) on 24.01.2023 totaling 20,594 proteins.


### Interactome Analysis of LLPS Drivers.
  Full list (N = 156) of self-sufficient human LLPS drivers were retrieved from PhaSepDB 2.1 on 25 June 2024. Their high-confidence list of interaction partners were retrieved from BioGRID by retrieving the most reliable collection of multivalidated physical interactions from the database compiled as “BIOGRID-MV-Physical-4.4.234.tab3” on 31 May 2024. Matching of protein interactions was carried out by their UniProt accession codes. The figure was created in Python3 using the “matplotlib” module (https://matplotlib.org/)


### Analysis of MLO Proteomes and Their Overlap with the R-DPR Interactome.
  The SG proteome was obtained from RNAGranuleDB’s Tier 1 (ref. 52), while the nucleolar proteome was retrieved from literature (62), as described previously. The P-body (PB) proteome was derived from RNAGranuleDB’s Tier 1-2 on 26 June 2024 by only accepting physical association evidence from discovery approaches (https://rnagranuledb.lunenfeld.ca/). LLPS clients from the postsynaptic density (PSD) were retrieved from the PSINDB database (64) after filtering for PSD clients supported by three-member databases (https://psindb.itk.ppke.hu/) at the time of access (26 June 2024). Last, nuclear speckle (NS) proteome was collected from the literature (65). Overlap with the R-DPR interactome was evaluated by UniProt accession-based matches. The figure was plotted in Python3 using matplotlib and “upsetplot” modules (66).


### Bioinformatic Feature Analyses of R-DPR Interactors.
  Proteins collected in the previous section were assessed for the presence/value of various features (SI Appendix, Table S5) as described in SI Appendix.


### Coarse-Grained Monte Carlo Simulations.
  Coarse-grained multichain Monte Carlo simulations were performed using the LASSI lattice-based simulation engine (67), similarly to earlier studies (36, 67, 68) as described in SI Appendix.


### Microfluidic Experimental Setup.
  Alexa-fluor labeled G3BP1 (180 µM), buffer (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 125 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM TCEP), and GR30 (150 µM), PR30 (150 µM), or PolyU (100 ng/µL) solutions were injected with pressure Fluigent Flow EZ pumps (Fluigent, Le Kremlin-Bicêtre, France) with equal flowrates in the chip (SI Appendix). Data acquisitions are performed at time point where mixing is partly achieved but not finalized (SI Appendix for detailed explanation). An excitation continuous blue laser beam (Coherent, OBIS FP 488LS, Fremont, CA) was used at the maximum output power of about 60 mW. Fluorescent droplets were imaged with a 100× magnification objective (Edmund 100X EO M ULWD, Barrington, NJ) and the data were recorded with a high-speed camera (PCO dimax HS4, Waltham, MA). The emitted light from the droplets was passed through an emission filter (Thorlabs, MF525-39, Newton, NJ) to reduce the background noise. The depth of focus of the objective is much smaller than the depth of the microchannel. Therefore, the microfluidic substrate was oscillated with an electrodynamic magnet (Brüel & Kjær, Mini-shaker type 4810, Nærum, Denmark) perpendicular to the optical axis, effectively displacing the focal plane and scanning the entire VOI. The position of the sample was monitored by a capacitive sensor (Lion Precision, C18, Minneapolis, MN) (SI Appendix, Fig. S4D). The typical scanning frequency was 0.5 Hz and the image acquisition frequency 25 Hz.
