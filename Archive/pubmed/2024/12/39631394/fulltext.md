# DNA hypomethylation promotes UHRF1- and SUV39H1/H2-dependent crosstalk between H3K18ub and H3K9me3 to reinforce heterochromatin states


## SUMMARY
  Mono-ubiquitination of lysine 18 on histone H3 (H3K18ub), catalyzed by UHRF1, is a DNMT1 docking site that facilitates replication-coupled DNA methylation maintenance. Its functions beyond this are unknown. Here, we genomically map simultaneous increases in UHRF1-dependent H3K18ub and SUV39H1/H2-dependent H3K9me3 following DNMT1 inhibition. Mechanistically, transient accumulation of hemi-methylated DNA at CpG islands facilitates UHRF1 recruitment and E3 ligase activity towards H3K18. Notably, H3K18ub enhances SUV39H1/H2 methyltransferase activity and, in colon cancer cells, nucleates new H3K9me3 domains at CpG island promoters of DNA methylation-silenced tumor suppressor genes. Disrupting UHRF1 enzyme activity prevents H3K9me3 accumulation while promoting PRC2-dependent H3K27me3 as a tertiary layer of gene repression in these regions. In contrast, disrupting H3K18ub-dependent SUV39H1/H2 activity enhances the transcriptional activating and antiproliferative effects of DNMT1 inhibition. Collectively, these findings reveal roles for UHRF1 and H3K18ub in regulating a hierarchy of repressive histone methylation signaling and rationalize a combination strategy for epigenetic cancer therapy.


## INTRODUCTION
  DNA methylation is a key epigenetic regulator of gene transcription and is essential to mammalian development1. DNA methylation patterns are established by de novo DNA methyltransferases DNMT3A/3B/3L and are epigenetically inherited through mitotic cell divisions by the replication-coupled maintenance methyltransferase DNMT1. To copy parental DNA methylation patterns to nascent strands, the RING E3 ubiquitin ligase UHRF1 (Ubiquitin-like with PHD and RING finger domains 1) facilitates DNMT1 recruitment to hemi-methylated DNA (a DNA replication intermediate)2–5 through a sequential read-write-read-write mechanism. First, physical interactions of UHRF1 with hemi-methylated DNA and N-terminal tails of histone H3 with di- or tri-methylated lysine 9 (H3K9me2/me3) stimulate its E3 ligase activity that transfers ubiquitin to the H3 tail at positions including lysines 14, 18, and/or 23 (H3K14ub, H3K18ub, and H3K23ub). DNMT1 recognizes these mono- and multi-mono-ubiquitinated histones through two ubiquitin interacting motifs (UIMs) embedded in its replication foci targeting sequence (RFTS) domain6–12. This UHRF1-dependent H3 ubiquitination (H3ub) signaling is crucial for DNA methylation maintenance13 and also for the propagation of abnormal DNA methylation patterns that support oncogenic growth in cancer cells14. However, it is unclear if UHRF1-mediated H3ub signaling has additional functions.

  Histone ubiquitination-methylation crosstalk pathways play pivotal roles in mammalian epigenetic regulation15. A key example is the interplay between mono-ubiquitination of histone H2B at lysine 120 (H2BK120ub) and the histone methyltransferases, DOT1L (disruptor of telomeric silencing 1-like) and COMPASS (complex of proteins associated with Set1). H2BK120ub allosterically stimulates the enzymatic activities of DOT1L and COMPASS, facilitating methylation of transcription-associated H3K79 and H3K4, respectively16–20. Another example of histone ubiquitination-methylation crosstalk is the cooperation between Polycomb repressive complex 1 and 2 (PRC1 and PRC2) as negative regulators of developmental gene expression programs that promote cell differentiation21. PRC1-mediated mono-ubiquitination of histone H2A lysine 119 (H2AK119ub) recruits and activates PRC2 to deposit H3K27me3, a histone post-translational modification (PTM) associated with transcriptional silencing22–24. These established precedents of histone ubiquitination-methylation crosstalk prompted us to consider a potential function for UHRF1-mediated H3ub in the regulation of histone methylation.

  Prior work showed that DNMT1 depletion led to a UHRF1-dependent increase in H3ub6,25. Further analyses indicated that H3K18ub was the most abundant UHRF1 ubiquitination product6,7, suggesting a potential accumulation of H3K18ub following DNMT1 depletion. Despite these observations, mechanisms driving H3ub elevation following DNMT1 depletion, and its functional significance remained unclear. In addition to H3ub changes, studies from us and others demonstrated that genetic or chemical disruption of DNMTs in colon cancer cells causes a re-emergence of the stem-like chromatin state associated with PRC2-dependent H3K27me3 that reinforces transcriptional silencing26–31. These findings underscore a broad epigenetic remodeling following DNA hypomethylation, presumably reinforcing transcriptionally silent states. We therefore reasoned that elevated H3ub in response to DNA hypomethylation might regulate histone methylation dynamics.

  Here, we carried out a comprehensive analysis of histone PTM changes driven by DNMT1 inhibition-induced DNA hypomethylation. We identified simultaneous increases in UHRF1-dependent H3K18ub and SUV39H1/H2-dependent H3K9me3. Integrative epigenomics analysis demonstrated that transient accumulation of hemi-methylated DNA at CpG islands facilitated UHRF1 recruitment and enhanced its E3 ligase activity towards H3K18 while impeding PRC2 activity in these genomic regions. Mechanistically, we showed that H3K18ub interacted with SUV39H1/H2 to enhance its methyltransferase activity and, in colon cancer cells, nucleated new H3K9me3 domains at CpG island promoters of tumor suppressor genes (TSGs) that are epigenetically silenced by DNA methylation. Blocking H3K18ub-directed SUV39H1/H2 activity enhanced the transcriptional activating and antiproliferative effects of DNA hypomethylating agents. Collectively, these findings reveal a previously unrecognized H3K18ub-H3K9me3 crosstalk mechanism, roles for UHRF1 and its E3 ligase activity in regulating a hierarchy of repressive histone methylation signaling, and a strategy for targeting SUV39H1/H2 to improve DNMT1 inhibitor efficacy.


## RESULTS

### DNA hypomethylation leads to simultaneous increases in H3K18ub and H3K9me3
  To study the role of H3ub in DNA methylation maintenance and its broader epigenetic functions, we developed a site-specific H3K18ub antibody that detects UHRF1-dependent ubiquitin ligase activity in vitro and in cells (Figure S1A–C). Consistent with prior studies6,25, DNMT1 knockdown (KD) in HCT116 cells led to UHRF1-dependent elevation of H3K18ub (Figure S1C).

  To identify the potential roles of this elevated H3K18ub, we first analyzed histone PTM changes driven by DNA hypomethylation in RKO colon cancer cells treated with the nucleoside analog DNMT inhibitor 5-aza-2’-deocycytidine (Decitabine; DAC) and the non-nucleoside DNMT1 inhibitor GSK3484862 (GSK862, DNMT1i)32–34. Selected drug doses and treatment time caused significant DNA hypomethylation at several candidate loci (Figure S1D) without cytotoxicity (Figure S1E). The relative abundance of >200 histone PTMs was queried by mass spectrometry (MS), and several histone PTMs showed significant and consistent alterations across the two DNMT inhibitor treatment groups (Figure 1A–C). For example, both DAC and GSK862 treatments elevated H3K27me3 levels and reduced H3K36me2 and H3.3K36me3 levels, aligning with previous findings26–28,30,31,35–38(Figure 1C). Notably, both DAC and GSK862 increased H3K9me3 (Figures 1A–C and S1F), a PTM associated with heterochromatin, transcriptional silencing, and the maintenance of 3D chromatin architecture39.

  Western blot analyses confirmed that DNMT inhibition simultaneously elevated H3K18ub and H3K9me3 in RKO cells (Figure 1D) and across various human cell lines, including those derived from colon cancer (HCT116, HCT15), cervical cancer (HeLa), osteosarcoma (U2OS), embryonic kidney (HEK293), primary fetal lung fibroblast (WI-38), and large intestine epithelial (FHC) cells (Figures 1E, S1G, and S1H). These increases were likewise observed in RKO and HCT116 cells following DNMT1 KD with siRNA and doxycycline-inducible shRNA (Figures 1F, S1I, and S1J), in DAC-treated HCT116-derived xenograft tumors with confirmed DNA methylation loss (Figures 1G and S1K), and in DNMT1 KD RKO cells ectopically expressing mutant forms of DNMT1 that lack catalytic activity13 (Figures 1H and S1L). Notably, H3K18ub and H3K9me3 levels returned to baseline after DNMT inhibitor washout, tracking with DNMT1 re-expression and DNA methylation restoration (Figures 1I and S1M). Collectively, these data demonstrate that genetic and chemical inhibition of DNMT1 catalytic activity leads to simultaneous increases in H3K18ub and H3K9me3 across cultured transformed and non-transformed cell lines in vitro and in vivo.


### UHRF1 E3 ligase activity is responsible for elevated H3K18ub and H3K9me3 levels following DNMT1 inhibition
  Given that elevated H3K18ub levels following DNMT1 KD are UHRF1-dependent (Figure S1C), we next questioned whether UHRF1 also contributes to the increased H3K9me3 levels in DNMT1i-treated cells. We introduced doxycycline-inducible shRNAs targeting UHRF1 (shUHRF1) into RKO, HCT116, and WI-38 cells, and we staggered doxycycline and DNMT1i treatments to achieve UHRF1 KD prior to DNMT1i (Figure S2A). Excitingly, UHRF1 KD prevented DNMT1i-induced increases in both H3K18ub and H3K9me3, as evidenced by western blots (Figures 2A, 2B, and S2B) and MS (Figures 2C, 2D and S2C). MS also detected two additional UHRF1 E3 ligase products, H3K14ub and H3K23ub, in RKO cells (Figure S2D). H3K23ub, but not H3K14ub, showed UHRF1-dependent elevation following DNMT1i, but it was less abundant than H3K18ub, aligning with previous in vitro findings that identified H3K18ub as a predominant UHRF1 product7.

  We next sought to determine which domains of UHRF1 are critical for regulating H3K9me3 following DNMT1 inhibition. We genetically complemented shUHRF1 RKO cells with wild-type (WT) or domain loss-of-function mutant UHRF1 transgenes (Figure S2E and S2F). shUHRF1 RKO cells expressing WT UHRF1, but not an empty vector (EV) control, showed elevated H3K18ub and H3K9me3 levels following DNMT1 inhibition as seen in parental cells (Figures 2E and S2E). Cells with a Tandem Tudor Domain (TTD) mutation (Y188A) that abolishes UHRF1 H3K9me2/me3 reading activity10,40 also showed elevated H3K18ub and H3K9me3 levels following DNMT1 inhibition (Figures 2E and S2F). However, cells expressing UHRF1 mutants that directly or indirectly disrupt its E3 ligase activity towards H3, including Ubiquitin-Like (UBL; F59V), SET and RING-associated (SRA; G448D), and Really Interesting New Gene (RING; H741A) mutants7,41–44, showed impaired elevations in H3K18ub and H3K9me3 following DNMT1 inhibition (Figures 2E and S2E). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that UHRF1 E3 ligase activity is responsible for the simultaneous increases in H3K18ub and H3K9me3 after DNMT1 inhibition.


### UHRF1-mediated H3K18ub nucleates new domains of H3K9me3 following DNMT1 inhibition
  To identify genomic regions where H3K18ub and H3K9me3 accumulate following DNMT1 inhibition, we conducted sans-spike-in quantitative chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (siQ-ChIP45,46) in RKO (UHRF1, H3K18ub, and H3K9me3) and WI-38 (H3K9me3) cells with or without DNMT1 inhibition and/or UHRF1 KD (Figure S2A). Consistent with bulk protein level analyses, thousands of UHRF1 and H3K18ub peaks in RKO, and H3K9me3 peaks in both cell types, showed UHRF1-dependent increases following DNMT1 inhibition (Figure 3A). In addition, model-based analysis of ChIP-seq (MACS47,48) revealed that a substantial proportion of ChIP-seq peaks in DNMT1i-treated cells (Figure S3A, RKO cells: 91% for UHRF1, 80% for H3K18ub, and 45% for H3K9me3; WI-38 cells: 32% for H3K9me3) were unique compared to control or UHRF1 KD samples, indicating the emergence of new UHRF1-dependent chromatin domains marked by H3K18ub and H3K9me3.

  We then integrated these UHRF1-dependent DNMT1i-induced siQ-ChIP peaks (Figure 3A) with Chromatin Hidden Markov Model (ChromHMM) annotations49–51. In RKO cells, these UHRF1, H3K18ub, and H3K9me3 siQ-ChIP peaks were enriched in bivalent promoters (TssBiv), transcription start site flanking (TssFlnk), and repressive Polycomb (ReprPC) regions (Figure 3B). Consistently, DNMT1 inhibition significantly elevated UHRF1, H3K18ub, and H3K9me3 occupancy around the TSS (Figure 3C) and at SUZ12 and EZH2 binding sites (Figure S3B) in a UHRF1-dependent manner. In addition, H3K18ub and H3K9me3 were enriched over DNMT1i-induced UHRF1 siQ-ChIP peaks following DNMT1 inhibition (Figures 3D, 3E, and S3C), and their peak signals showed statistically significant correlations with UHRF1 (Figure 3F), suggesting their colocalization. In contrast to RKO cells, UHRF1-dependent DNMT1i-induced H3K9me3 siQ-ChIP peaks in WI-38 cells accumulated significantly at transcriptionally active gene bodies (Tx) and to a lesser extent at repressed Polycomb regions (ReprPC and ReprPCWk) (Figures 3B, 3G, 3H, and S3D). Collectively, these findings show that UHRF1-dependent H3K18ub nucleates new domains of H3K9me3 following DNMT1 inhibition in RKO cells. The distinct genomic distributions of UHRF1-dependent DNMT1i-induced H3K9me3 peaks in RKO versus WI-38 cells suggested that this may be linked to different DNA methylation patterns between cancer and non-cancer cells52.

  In RKO cells, DNMT1i-induced and UHRF1-dependent increases in H3K9me3 at promoters were accompanied by reduced H3K9me3 occupancy at heterochromatin (Het) and zinc finger repeat (ZNF/Rpts) regions (Figure S3E and S3F), suggesting a redistribution of this PTM. This is consistent with reports of H3K9me3 loss in heterochromatin in DNA methylation-depleted HCT116 cells27,53. Supporting this, DNMT1i-induced H3K9me3 peaks shifted from late (heterochromatin-enriched) to early (euchromatin-enriched) replicating regions, where UHRF1 and H3K18ub also accumulated (Figure S3G). Together, these results demonstrate that DNMT1 inhibition in RKO cells leads to UHRF1 and H3K18ub accumulations at early replicating promoter regions, accompanied by a reorganization of the H3K9me3 landscape.


### Hemi-methylated CpG islands facilitate UHRF1 recruitment and activity following DNMT1 inhibition
  To understand how UHRF1-mediated H3K9me3 remodeling relates to DNA methylation loss, we next performed Infinium MethylationEPIC BeadChip (EPIC array V2) in DNMT1i-treated RKO and WI-38 cells with and without UHRF1 KD (Figure S2A). As expected, pronounced DNA methylation losses were observed in DNMT1i-treated and UHRF1 KD cells (Figure S4A), and DNA methylation patterns were distinct between RKO and WI-38 cells (Figure S4B).

  Integration of DNMT1i-induced differentially methylated regions (DMRs) with siQ-ChIP data revealed that UHRF1 and H3K18ub in RKO cells, and H3K9me3 in both cell types, accumulated across DMRs following DNMT1 inhibition in a UHRF1-dependent manner (Figure 4A). Interestingly, hypomethylated DMRs were clustered into two groups by k-means clustering, with Cluster 1 showing stronger enrichment for UHRF1, H3K18ub, and H3K9me3 than Cluster 2 (Figure S4C). Notably, in both cell types, DMR Cluster 1 CpGs were more enriched for CpG islands and high-density CpG regions compared to those in Cluster 2 (Figure 4B), and DMR Cluster 1 CpGs were predominantly fully methylated in control cells that transitioned to β-values centered around 0.5 following DNMT1 inhibition (Figure S4D).

  Consistently, k-means clustering of UHRF1, H3K18ub, and H3K9me3 siQ-ChIP in RKO cells across all CpG islands revealed two groups of CpG islands with strong (Cluster 1) and weak (Cluster 2) enrichment of these markers (Figure 4C). Cluster 1 CpGs were mostly methylated in control cells with β-values centered on 0.5 following DNMT1 inhibition, while Cluster 2 CpGs were mostly unmethylated in control cells (Figure 4D). Similarly, in WI-38 cells, H3K9me3 showed stronger elevations following DNMT1 inhibition at methylated CpG islands versus unmethylated ones (Figure S4E). Consistently, EPIC array CpG probes covered by DNMT1i-induced siQ-ChIP peaks in both cell types, and UHRF1 siQ-ChIP peaks in RKO control cells, were enriched for CpG islands and high-density CpG regions, with β-values centered on 0.5 following DNMT1 inhibition (Figure S4F–H). This data, together with prior evidence that hemi-methylated DNA stimulates UHRF1 ubiquitin ligase activity in vitro7, suggest that, despite the distinct DNA methylation patterns in RKO and WI-38 cells, inhibiting DNMT1 leads to the accumulation of hemi-methylated DNA at CpG islands, which promotes UHRF1 recruitment, enzymatic activity, and H3K9me3 accumulation in both cell types.

  Next, we sought to further define the role of hemi-methylated DNA in facilitating UHRF1-mediated epigenetic remodeling following DNMT1 inhibition. Treating RKO cells with DNMT1i for up to 12 days, we observed that UHRF1 and H3K18ub levels at candidate CpG islands peaked on Day 2 and returned to baseline by Days 7 and 12, paralleling temporal global increases and decreases in H3K9me3 levels (Figures 4E, 4F and S4I). These temporal dynamics coincide with the predicted accumulation and dilution of hemi-methylated DNA across RKO cell divisions. Additionally, dox-inducible shUHRF1 RKO cells genetically complemented by UHRF1 mutants with disrupted DNA binding (G448D) or impaired hemi-methylated DNA recognition (N489A, finger swap)7,8 did not exhibit H3K18ub elevation or UHRF1 and H3K9me3 accumulations at candidate CpG islands following DNMT1 inhibition (Figures 4G, 4H and S4J). Moreover, cells expressing UHRF1 with compromised E3 ligase activity (H741A) showed significantly diminished H3K18ub and H3K9me3 increases following DNMT1 inhibition while still showing UHRF1 accumulation at hypomethylated CpG islands. In contrast, disruption of UHRF1 H3K9me2/me3 reading activity (Y188A) did not affect H3K18ub elevation or UHRF1 and H3K9me3 accumulations at candidate loci following DNMT1 inhibition (Figures 4G, 4H, and S4J). These results collectively indicate that the ability for UHRF1 to sense transiently accumulated hemi-methylated DNA at CpG islands is essential for its recruitment and enhanced E3 ligase activity in response to DNMT1 inhibition.


### UHRF1-mediated H3K18ub enhances SUV39H1/H2 methyltransferase activity
  We next sought to define which histone lysine methyltransferase(s) contributed to the UHRF1-dependent H3K9me3 increase in DNMT1i-treated cells. In human cells, SUV39H1 (Suppressor of Variegation 3–9 Homolog 1), SUV39H2, SETDB1 (SET Domain Bifurcated histone lysine methyltransferase 1), and G9a/GLP cooperate to methylate H3K939,54. G9a and GLP predominately catalyze mono- and di-methylation of H3K9 in euchromatic regions55, while SUV39H1, SUV39H2, and SETDB1 catalyze H3K9 methylation up to tri-methylation in heterochromatic regions39,54. Individual KDs of SUV39H1, SUV39H2, SETDB1, or SETDB2 minimally reduced H3K9me3 levels (data now shown), likely due to functional redundancy. However, simultaneous KD of SUV39H1 with KD or knockout (KO) of SUV39H2, but not KD of both SETDB1 and SETDB2, significantly attenuated the DNMT1i-induced H3K9me3 increase without affecting UHRF1 and H3K18ub accumulations at hypomethylated CpG islands (Figures 5A, 5B, and S5A–E). These results suggested that UHRF1-mediated H3K18ub promotes SUV39H1/H2-dependent H3K9me3 following DNMT1 inhibition.

  Notably, longer exposures of H3K9me3 blots from RKO cell chromatin extracts revealed a SUV39H1/H2- and UHRF1-dependent ~25 kDa band in DNMT1i-treated cells (Figures 5A, 5C, and S5B), matching the molecular weight of H3K18ub. This suggested a cis-regulatory link between H3K9me3 and H3K18ub on the same H3 tail. In support of this hypothesis, in vitro methyltransferase activity assays revealed that SUV39H1 and SUV39H2 (but not G9a or SETDB1/Atf7IP) methyltransferase activities were significantly enhanced on H3K14ub and H3K18ub nucleosomes relative to unmodified nucleosomes (Figures 5D and S5F), with SUV39H2 showing selectivity for H3ub over other histone mono-ubiquitination sites (Figures 5E and S5F). MS detected H3K14ub or H3K9me3 and H3K14ub dually-marked peptides in RKO cells, but their abundance did not increase following DNMT1 inhibition (Figures S2D and S5G). This suggests that H3K18ub (but not H3K14ub) regulates H3K9me3 in DNMT1i-treated cells. We were not able to detect H3K9me3 and H3K18ub dually-marked peptides in our MS analysis due to the digestion pattern of the trypsin56. Alternatively, reciprocal co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) experiments of micrococcal nuclease (MNase)-digested mononucleosomes from DNMT1i-treated RKO cells confirmed that H3K18ub IP enriched the ~25 kDa H3K9me3 band, and H3K9me3 IP enriched H3K18ub (Figure 5F). These findings parallel discoveries in Saccharomyces pombe where H3K14ub enhances Clr4 (ortholog of human SUV39H1/2)-mediated H3K9me257,58, suggesting an evolutionarily conserved H3ub-H3K9me3 crosstalk mechanism, where UHRF1-mediated H3K18ub enhances SUV39H1/H2 methyltransferase activity in DNMT1i-treated cells.

  Informed by biochemical studies with Clr458 and AlphaFold model predictions59, we next conducted mutagenesis studies to identify a ubiquitin-interacting motif (UIM) in SUV39H1 (Figure 5G). The L173A/V176A/V178A mutation (3A mutant) on a hydrophobic loop preceding the pre-SET domain significantly attenuated or abolished the enhanced SUV39H1 activity on H3K14ub and H3K18ub nucleosomes, respectively, without perturbing its activity on unmodified nucleosomes and H3 peptides (Figures 5G, 5H, and S5H–J). Ectopic expression of HA-3xFlag-tagged wildtype and 3A mutant SUV39H1 in SUV39H1/H2 KD/KO RKO cells showed that 3A mutant SUV39H1 restored basal H3K9me3 but not DNMT1i-induced H3K9me3 at several candidate loci compared to empty-vector and wildtype SUV39H1 (Figures 5I and S5K). ChIP-qPCR with HA tag-enriched chromatin further demonstrated that wild-type SUV39H1 exhibited enhanced occupancy at H3K18ub-accumulated loci in response to DNMT1 inhibition, while the 3A mutant SUV39H1 showed similar occupancy at these loci regardless of DNMT1 inhibition (Figure 5J). These data collectively indicate that H3K18ub enhances the chromatin binding and methyltransferase activity of SUV39H1 through the identified UIM both in vitro and in DNMT1i-treated cells.

  Notably, H3K9me3 increased on both ubiquitinated and non-ubiquitinated histone H3 following DNMT1 inhibition, with the primary increase on the non-ubiquitinated H3 (Figures 5A, 5C, and S5B). ChIP-seq data showed that DNMT1i-induced H3K9me3 peaks nucleate over H3K18ub peaks but extend over 50 kilobases in both directions (Figure 5K). Additionally, the DNMT1 inhibitor treatment time course study suggested that, while H3K18ub accumulation was transient, H3K9me3 levels remained elevated by day 12 (Figures 4E, 4F, S4I, and 5L), supporting a model where focal H3K18ub nucleates broad H3K9me3 domains that propagate through cell divisions without sustained H3K18ub. The established spreading model for H3K9me3 involves H3K9me3 reading activities associated with chromodomains of SUV39H1/H2 and HP1 proteins that complex with SUV39H1/H239. To test this, we engineered SUV39H1/H2 KD/KO RKO cells expressing wild-type or the R24A/K27A mutant SUV39H1 that was previously reported to abolish H3K9me3 recognition through its chromodomain60. ChIP-qPCR analysis of candidate loci showed that the R24A/K27A mutation did not affect DNMT1i-induced H3K9me3 levels compared to wild-type SUV39H1 (Figure S5K and S5L), suggesting that SUV39H1 binding to H3K9me3 is not required for its propagation. However, triple KD of HP1α, HP1β, and HP1γ (Figure S5M) significantly reduced H3K9me3 accumulation in response to DNMT1 inhibition, both in H3K18ub peak regions and flanking loci (Figure 5M). Overall, our data support a model in which focal accumulation of UHRF1-dependent H3K18ub in response to DNMT1 inhibition enhances SUV39H1/H2-mediated H3K9me3 through a cis-regulatory mechanism. This process nucleates H3K9me3 domains that are spread and self-propagated through a read-write mechanism involving HP1 isoforms (Figure 5N).


### UHRF1 E3 ligase activity impedes PRC2 activity at hypomethylated CpG islands
  We next sought to determine how UHRF1-mediated H3K18ub and H3K9me3 remodeling impact the transcriptome of DNMT1i-treated cells. Surprisingly, RNA-seq in RKO cells with combined DNMT1i and UHRF1 KD showed minimal transcriptomic changes compared to DNMT1i alone (Figure S6A and S6B), suggesting another repressive mechanism might compensate. Given that the DNMT1i-induced and UHRF1-dependent H3K18ub and H3K9me3 were enriched at PRC2 target sites (Figures 3B and S3B), we hypothesized that PRC2-mediated H3K27me3 compensates for UHRF1 loss in DNMT1i-treated cells to maintain gene repression.

  To test this, we performed H3K27me3 siQ-ChIP in RKO cells with DNMT1 inhibition and/or UHRF1 KD (Figure S2A). Consistent with prior studies26–30, thousands of H3K27me3 siQ-ChIP peaks showed signal increase following DNMT1i or UHRF1 KD (Figure 6A). Notably, UHRF1 KD, with or without DNMT1 inhibition, led to more pronounced H3K27me3 increases than DNMT1 inhibition alone (Figures 6A and S6C), without causing deeper DNA hypomethylation13 (Figure S4A). This suggests that UHRF1 limits H3K27me3 accumulation in DNMT1i-treated cells, independent of its role in DNA methylation maintenance. ChromHMM analysis revealed that UHRF1 KD elevated H3K27me3 at TssBiv and TSS proximal regions in DNMT1i-treated cells (Figure 6B and 6C), suggesting that PRC2-mediated H3K27me3 compensates for UHRF1 loss at these sites.

  Integrating these results with DNA methylation data revealed that UHRF1 KD enhanced H3K27me3 occupancy at CpG islands, early replicating regions, and a group of hypomethylated DMRs in DNMT1i-treated cells (Figures 6D, 6E, S6D, and S6E), but did not deepen DNMT1i-induced DNA hypomethylation (Figure S6F). Hypomethylated DMRs marked by DNMT1i-induced UHRF1 (Figure 4C, Cluster 1) overlapped with DMRs marked by UHRF1 KD-induced H3K27me3 (Figure S6E, Cluster 1) (Figure 6F, ii), which were significantly enriched for CpG islands (Figure 6G). Integrative analysis of UHRF1, H3K9me3, and H3K27me3 occupancy across CpG islands further revealed that enhanced PRC2 activities in both H3K27me3 pre-marked (Cluster 1) and H3K27me3-free (Cluster 2) CpG islands compensate for the loss of UHRF1 in DNMT1i-treated cells (Figures 6H, 6I, S6G and S6H). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that UHRF1 promotes H3K9me3 but restricts H3K27me3 accumulations at DNMTi-induced hypomethylated CpG islands.

  To determine which domain functions of UHRF1 are crucial for impeding PRC2 activity, we performed H3K27me3 ChIP-qPCR at candidate CpG islands in RKO shUHRF1 cells complemented with wild-type or mutant UHRF1 transgenes (Figure 6J). Following DNMT1 inhibition, SRA domain mutants (G448D, N489A, and finger swap) and the RING domain mutant (H741A), but not the TTD mutant (Y188A), exhibited significantly elevated H3K27me3 at CpG islands compared to wild-type UHRF1 (Figures 6J and S6I). Given that the H741A mutant reduces UHRF1 E3 ligase activity while retaining its hemi-methylated CpG binding activity, and SRA mutants abolish both activities (Figure 4G and 4H), these findings suggest that UHRF1 E3 ligase activity is essential for restricting PRC2 activity at hypomethylated CpG islands in response to DNMT1 inhibition.


### Disrupting H3K18ub-directed SUV39H1/H2 activity enhances DNMT1 inhibitor efficacy in colon cancer cells
  We next sought to determine whether targeting the UHRF1-SUV39H1/H2 signaling axis was a potential strategy for improving DNMT1 inhibitor therapeutic efficacy in solid tumors. Consistent with the role of UHRF1 as an antagonist of PRC2 activity at CpG island promoters (Figures 6), UHRF1 KD partially reversed the antiproliferative effects of DNMT1i treatment in RKO cells (Figure S7A and S7B). On the contrary, SUV39H1/H2 depletion blocked DNMTi-induced H3K9me3 (Figure 5) without enhancing PRC2 activity at candidate CpG islands in DNMT1i-treated RKO cells (Figure S7C), suggesting its potential for improving DNMT1 inhibitor therapeutic efficacy.

  RNA-seq in RKO SUV39H2 KO cells, with or without SUV39H1 KD or DNMT1 inhibition, showed that combined SUV39H1/H2 depletion with DNMT1 inhibition led to stronger transcriptomic changes than either perturbation alone (Figure 7A). Notably, 846 protein-coding genes were uniquely upregulated by combined treatment (Figure 7B), and TSS of these genes exhibited UHRF1-dependent accumulations of UHRF1, H3K18ub, and H3K9me3 following DNMT1 inhibition (Figure 7C). Additionally, genes with DNMT1i-induced H3K9me3 at their TSS (n=464) showed significantly increased expression with combined treatment (Figure 7D), suggesting that SUV39H1/H2-dependent H3K9me3 accumulation functions as a compensatory repressive mechanism when these CpG island promoters are hypomethylated following DNMT1 inhibition.

  Mean Average (MA) plots further demonstrated that combined treatment significantly upregulated genes that were minimally expressed in control cells, including TSGs silenced by promoter DNA hypermethylation in colon cancer (e.g., DKK1, DKK3, APC2, SFRP1, and RUNX3)61 (Figure 7E). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) identified multiple gene sets that were upregulated by combined treatment, including KRAS Signaling Down, Epithelial Mesenchymal Transition, ECM Receptor Interaction, Calcium Signaling, WNT Signaling, and Cytokine-Cytokine Receptor Interaction (Figures 7F, S7D, and S7E). The leading-edge genes in these sets include negative regulators of WNT signaling and Epithelial-Mesenchymal Transition (Figure 7G). Supporting this, SUV39H1/H2 KD/KO RKO cells expressing empty vector or the 3A mutant SUV39H1 showed stronger upregulation of TSGs (e.g., SFRP1, RUNX3) with DNMT1 inhibition compared to those expressing wild-type SUV39H1 (Figure 7H). These findings collectively indicate that disrupting H3K18ub-directed SUV39H1/H2 activity enhances DNMT1 inhibitor efficacy in reactivating epigenetically silenced TSGs.

  Consistent with the combined effects of DNMT1 inhibition and SUV39H1/H2 disruption on gene expression, SUV39H1 KD and SUV39H2 KO enhanced the antiproliferative effects of DNMT1 inhibition in RKO and HCT116 cells (Figures 7I, 7J, and S7F–L). Similarly, DNMT1i with the SUV39H2 inhibitor OTS186935 had greater antiproliferative effects than either treatment alone (Figures 7K and S7M). Finally, in RKO SUV39H1/H2 KD/KO cells, those expressing empty vector or the 3A mutant SUV39H1 were more vulnerable to DNMT1i therapy than cells expressing wild-type SUV39H1 (Figure 7L). These data collectively support a strategy of disrupting H3K18ub-directed SUV39H1 activity to improve DNMT1 inhibitor efficacy.


## DISCUSSION

### UHRF1-mediated H3ub signaling functions beyond DNA methylation maintenance
  UHRF1 plays a critical role in maintaining DNA methylation by interacting with hemi-methylated DNA and H3K9me2/me3 to ubiquitinate H3K14, H3K18, and H3K23, creating docking sites for DNMT16,7,10,11,14,25,40–43,62. However, functions for these PTMs beyond this were unknown entering this study. Enabled by the development of an H3K18ub-specific antibody, we uncovered a cis-regulatory crosstalk mechanism linking H3K18ub to H3K9me3 in response to DNA hypomethylation that applies to both cancer and non-cancer cells. Following DNMT1 inhibition, UHRF1-mediated H3K18ub enhances SUV39H1/H2 activity to nucleate H3K9me3 domains in DNA hypomethylated regions. This crosstalk is independent of the known H3K9me2/me3 reading function of UHRF110,11,40,63 and uncovers a previously undefined role of UHRF1-mediated H3ub signaling in regulating H3K9me3.


### UHRF1-mediated H3K18ub reshapes heterochromatin following DNA hypomethylation
  DNA hypomethylation at intergenic regions is an epigenetic hallmark of cancer, leading to a B-like chromatin compartment shift associated with constitutive heterochromatin64,65. This DNA hypomethylation pattern also occurs in aged and senescent cells and can be induced in vitro during mitosis66–72. Additionally, a similar chromatin compartmental switch is observed in colorectal and leukemic cancer cells after chemical or genetic inhibition of DNMTs53,65,73. However, the mechanism(s) responsible for this hypomethylation-associated heterochromatin remodeling are largely unknown.

  Our analyses show that DNMT1 inhibition in RKO cells causes H3K9me3 redistribution driven by UHRF1 and SUV39H1/H2-mediated H3K18ub-H3K9me3 crosstalk. Specifically, H3K9me3 increases at hypomethylated CpG island promoters and early replicating regions associated with the A compartment74. Meanwhile, H3K9me3 is reduced at late replicating heterochromatic regions associated with the B compartment74. Therefore, the H3K18ub-H3K9me3 crosstalk mechanism defined here may underlie chromatin compartment reorganization seen in cancer, aging, and DNA hypomethylation therapy.


### Hemi-methylated DNA at CpG islands promotes H3K18ub and H3K9me3 through UHRF1 in cells
  While studies in mouse embryonic stem cells reported that, in the absence of DNMT1, prolonged NP95 (mouse UHRF1) binding to hemi-methylated DNA disrupts SETDB1-dependent H3K9me3 deposition75, our study is the first to show that hemi-methylated DNA promotes H3K9me3 at CpG islands through H3K18ub. In both cancer and non-cancer cells with distinct DNA methylation patterns, H3K9me3 accumulates at hemi-methylated CpG islands after DNMT1 inhibition. In RKO cells, hemi-methylated DNA recruits UHRF1 and enhances its E3 ligase activity, which promotes H3K18ub and SUV39H1/H2-mediated H3K9me3. This aligns with previous in vitro findings on UHRF1 binding to hemi-methylated DNA7,42,76,77 and its role in stimulating its E3 ligase activity7,8,43, providing key in-cell evidence of its regulatory function in response to DNA hypomethylation.


### UHRF1 E3 ligase activity limits PRC2 activity at hypomethylated CpG islands
  In addition to its function of promoting H3K9me3, we showed that UHRF1 accumulation at hypomethylated CpG islands following DNMT1 inhibition restricts PRC2 activity in these regions, which is dependent on its enhanced E3 ligase activity. These findings revealed that UHRF1 E3 ligase activity regulates distinct gene repression pathways mediated by DNA methylation, H3K9me3, or H3K27me3. Specifically, during DNA replication, UHRF1-dependent H3K18ub facilitates DNMT1-mediated maintenance of DNA methylation as a primary layer of repressive chromatin modification. When DNMT1 is inhibited, the transient accumulation of hemi-methylated DNA stimulates UHRF1 to deposit H3K18ub, facilitating SUV39H1/2-mediated H3K9me3 as a secondary repressive layer to reinforce heterochromatin states. When both DNMT1 and UHRF1 are inhibited or removed, PRC2 is recruited to hypomethylated CpG islands and deposits H3K27me3 as a third line of defense to maintain a repressive chromatin state.


### H3K18ub-H3K9me3 crosstalk and DNMT1 inhibitor therapy
  The conserved increases in H3K18ub and H3K9me3 levels after DNMT1 inhibitor therapy in multiple cancer types and non-cancer cells suggest that H3K18ub and H3K9me3 could be developed as on-treatment protein-level biomarkers for evaluating DNMT1 inhibitor efficacy in patient biopsy samples. Furthermore, disrupting H3K18ub-H3K9me3 crosstalk by inhibiting SUV39H1 ubiquitin reading activity enhanced tumor suppressor gene expression and improved DNMT1 inhibitor therapeutic efficacy. These studies support the development of SUV39H1/H2 ubiquitin reading antagonists for combination regimens with DNMT inhibitors to block compensatory repression through H3K9me3 signaling. This precision approach to SUV39H1/H2 antagonism may reveal better systemic toxicity profiles than catalytic inhibitors that block the essential function of these enzymes in maintaining constitutive heterochromatin domains.


### Limitations of the Study
  While this study reveals a mechanism of histone ubiquitination-methylation crosstalk in response to DNMT1 inhibition, it does not consider whether this mechanism is operable in other contexts where DNA methylation remodeling events are appreciated, such as during DNA replication. Future studies should investigate this and also clarify how UHRF1 E3 ligase activity restricts PRC2 activity. Additionally, developing SUV39H1/2 inhibitors with improved safety profiles is crucial for assessing the therapeutic potential of disrupting H3K18ub-H3K9me3 crosstalk in vivo.

### Method details

#### Histone acid extraction
  Histone acid extraction for histone PTM MS analysis was performed as previously described78 with modifications. 10 million RKO cells were harvested following the indicated treatment. Cells were lysed using 1 ml hypotonic lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 1 mM KCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM PMSF, supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors) for 30 minutes at 4°C with gentle rotation. This was followed by centrifugation at 10,000g for 10 minutes to pellet intact nuclei and discard supernatant. Histones were then extracted by resuspending the nuclear pellet in 400 μl of 0.2 M H2SO4 and incubated with rotation for 2 hours at 4°C. The mixture was centrifuged at 16,000g for 10 minutes to collect the supernatant containing histones. 200 μl of 100% TCA was then added to precipitate histones followed by a 1-hour rotation at 4°C. Following another centrifugation step at 16,000g for 10 minutes, the histone-containing pellet was washed, first with 500 μl cold acetone/0.1% HCl using glass pasteur pipettes. This was followed by centrifugation at 3,400g for 5 minutes, the supernatant was discarded by inverting the tube. The histone-containing pellet was washed again with 500 μl 100% cold acetone using glass pasteur pipettes. This was followed by final centrifugation at 3,400g for 5 minutes. Supernatant was discarded by inverting the tube. The residual acetone was carefully pipetted out, and purified histone samples were air-dried for 20 minutes.


#### Histone PTM mass spectrometry analysis
  Acid-extracted histone proteins were digested as previously described79. Briefly, precipitated histones were dissolved in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate, pH 8.0 before being subjected to derivatization using 5 μL of propionic anhydride (Sigma Aldrich) and ammonium hydroxide (Sigma Aldrich) to balance the pH to 8.0. The mixture was incubated for 15 minutes, and this derivatization was repeated. Histones were then digested with 1 μg of sequencing-grade trypsin (Promega) diluted in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate (1:20, enzyme: sample) overnight at room temperature. The derivatization reactions were repeated to further derivatize peptide N-termini. Samples were dried in a vacuum centrifuge.

  Prior to MS analysis, samples were desalted using a 96-well plate filter (Orochem) packed with 1 mg of Oasis HLB C-18 resin (Waters). Briefly, samples were resuspended in 100 μl of 0.1% TFA and loaded onto the HLB resin, which was previously equilibrated using 100 μl of the same buffer. After washing with 100 μl of 0.1% TFA, samples were eluted with a buffer containing 70 μl of 60% acetonitrile and 0.1% TFA and then dried in a vacuum centrifuge.

  Samples were resuspended in 10 μl of 0.1% TFA and loaded onto a Dionex RSLC Ultimate 300 (Thermo Scientific), coupled online with an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos (Thermo Scientific). Chromatographic separation was performed with a two-column system, consisting of a C-18 trap cartridge (300 μm ID, 5 mm length) and a picofrit analytical column (75 μm ID, 25 cm length) packed in-house with reversed-phase Repro-Sil Pur C18-AQ 3 μm resin. Peptides were separated using a 30 minutes gradient from 1–30% buffer B (buffer A: 0.1% formic acid, buffer B: 80% acetonitrile + 0.1% formic acid) at a flow rate of 300 nl/min. The mass spectrometer was set to acquire spectra in a data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode. Briefly, the full MS scan was set to 300–1100 m/z in the orbitrap with a resolution of 120,000 (at 200 m/z) and an AGC target of 5×10e5. MS/MS was performed in the orbitrap with sequential isolation windows of 50 m/z with an AGC target of 2×10e5 and an HCD collision energy of 30.

  Histone peptide raw files were manually extracted with Xcalibur (Thermo Scientific) and Skyline80, as well as automatically with the EpiProfile 2.0 software81. From the extracted ion chromatogram, the area under the curve was obtained and used to estimate the abundance of each peptide. In order to achieve the relative abundance of PTMs, the sum of all different modified forms of a histone peptide was considered as 100% and the area of the particular peptide was divided by the total area for that histone peptide in all of its modified forms. The relative ratio of two isobaric forms was estimated by averaging the ratio for each fragment ion with different mass between the two species. Two-tailed student t-test was used to calculate the statistical differences in the relative ratio of each histone PTM between control and drug-treated samples. The MS proteomics data are available via ProteomeXchange. Sample keys are shown in Table S1.


#### Whole-cell and chromatin extract preparations and western blotting
  For whole-cell extracts: Cells were lysed on ice in cold CSK lysis buffer (10 mM PIPES pH 7.0, 300 mM sucrose, 100 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, and 0.1% Triton X-100) supplemented with universal nuclease and protease inhibitor cocktail for 30 minutes with vortexing every 10 minutes. Protein concentration was then quantified by Bradford Assay, denatured in SDS sample buffer (2% SDS, 1% β-mercaptoethanol, 4% glycerol, 0.04 M Tris-Cl pH 6.8 and 0.01% bromophenol blue) and boiled at 95°C for 5 minutes before further analysis.

  For chromatin extracts: 2 million cells were resuspended in 100 μl CSK buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor and incubated on ice for 20 minutes. From the 100 μl mixture, 20 μl was reserved as the whole-cell extract, and the rest (80 μl) was centrifuged at 1,300g for 5 minutes at 4°C. Supernatants containing soluble cytoplasmic and nuclear proteins were transferred to a new tube as soluble fractions. Pellets, containing chromatin and associated factors, were washed with 1 ml CSK buffer and incubated on ice for 10 minutes. The chromatin-containing mixture was centrifuged at 1,300g for 5 minutes and the supernatant was discarded. The pellets were resuspended in 80 μl CSK buffer supplemented with universal nuclease and incubated on ice for 20 minutes to solubilize the chromatin fraction. The 20 μl whole-cell extract was further supplemented with 20 μl CSK buffer containing universal nuclease and incubated on ice for 20 minutes to promote protein solubilization. The whole-cell extract protein concentration was then quantified by Bradford Assay, which was used to infer the relative abundance of chromatin and soluble fractions. These fractions were denatured in SDS sample buffer and boiled at 95°C for 5 minutes before further analysis.

  1–20 μg protein was size-separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membrane (Thermo Fisher). Membranes were blocked for one hour at room temp in blocking buffer (PBS supplemented with 0.1% Tween-20 and 5% BSA) and incubated with primary antibodies (listed in key resource table) overnight at 4°C. Membranes were washed in PBST and incubated in HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 hour at room temp. Membranes were washed in PBST 3 × 5 minutes, incubated in ECL, and imaged with film. ImageJ densitometry was used to quantify band intensity.


#### UHRF1 ubiquitin ligase activity assays
  UHRF1 ubiquitin ligase activity assay reactions were assembled in buffer containing 50 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 66 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM MgCl2, and 2.5 mM DTT. To charge the ubiquitin machinery, the following were mixed at room temp: 50 nM E1 (UBA1), 660 nM E2 (UBCH5A), 1 μM TAMRA_Ubiquitin, and 8 mM ATP. UHRF1 (final concentration 1.5 μM) and its substrate (nucleosomes with unmethylated linker DNA; final concentration 100 nM) were added to the side of the tubes, and tubes were spun down to start the reactions (final volume 50 μL). Reactions were incubated at room temperature for 3 hours, then quenched by adding 5x SDS sample buffer and heating at 95°C for 5 minutes. Reactions were run on SDS-PAGE gels and scanned for TAMRA fluorescence, stained with Coomassie, or transferred to PVDF membranes for western blots.


#### siRNA transfection
  100,000 RKO or HCT116 cells were seeded per well in 6-well cell culture plates 24 hours before the transfection. siRNA SMARTpools targeting the indicated proteins and non-targeting control siRNA pools (Horizon Discovery, Cambridge, UK) were mixed with Lipofectamine RNAiMax (5 μL, ThermoFisher) in a total of 200 μL Opti-MEM (ThermoFisher) and incubated at room temp for 20 minutes. Cells were refreshed with 1.8 ml antibiotic-free cell culture media, with or without indicated drugs, and the siRNA/Lipid mix was added dropwise for a final siRNA concentration of 50 nM. Cells were collected 72 hours after transfection for further analysis.


#### HRM assay for DNA methylation
  DNA was isolated from RKO cell pellets using a DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The EZ DNA Methylation Kit (Zymo) was used to bisulfite convert 500 ng of DNA per sample according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Bisulfite-converted DNA was eluted in 10 μL of M-elution buffer from the kit and brought up to 54 μL total with DNase-Free water. 5 μL of the bisulfite-converted DNA was combined with 10 μL of Precision Melt Supermix (BioRad) for High-Resolution Melt Analysis and 2 μL of forward and reverse primers (2 μM stock) and brought to 20 μL with DNase-Free water. A BioRAD CFX Opus93 Real-Time PCR System was used to amplify the DNA at a 60°C annealing temp for 39 cycles and then perform a melt analysis from 65°C to 95°C with 0.1°C/10 sec increments. The melt temp (Tm) at the maximum reported RFU value was reported for each amplicon. Amplicons from primers indicated in Table S2 were used to report on DNA methylation changes.


#### Mouse xenograft
  Xenografts were designed in accordance with guidelines set by the Van Andel Institute (VAI) Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). All animal studies received IACUC approval prior to initiation. Athymic nude female mice (6–8 weeks old; Charles River, Strain 088) were inoculated subcutaneously with 5×106 HCT116 cells in 100 μL PBS. Once tumors were measurable by caliper (average volume 100–350 mm3), mice were randomized into treatment groups, weighed, and treated with DAC. DAC was administered by Intraperitoneal (IP) injection (0.4 mg/kg dissolved in PBS) once per day for 5 days (Monday through Friday) with a drug holiday the following week. This one-week on/one-week off schedule was repeated for the duration of the study. Tumors were measured with calipers three times per week. When tumors reached 2,500 mm3 (length × width × height), the animal was euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation, and the tumors were harvested and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen.

  For protein analyses, tumors were crushed in liquid nitrogen using a mortar and pestle. A sample of this powder was collected in a microcentrifuge tube and proteins were extracted following the whole-cell extract procedures described above. For DNA extraction, a sample of the powdered tumor was collected, and DNA was isolated using a DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen). This was done according to the manufacturer’s instructions except for the Proteinase K incubation step, where samples were incubated overnight with occasional vortexing.


#### Generation of doxycycline-inducible shRNA knockdown cell lines
  Tet-pLKO-puro vector cloning: Tet-pLKO-puro was a gift from Dmitri Wiederschain (Addgene # 21915)82. shRNA oligos for insertion (Table S3) were synthesized by Eurofins. Oligos were reconstituted in water at a concentration of 100 μM, diluted in 10X annealing buffer (1 M NaCl, 100 mM Tris, pH 7.4), brought to 100°C for 10 minutes, and naturally cooled to 30°C to anneal the oligo pair. Annealed oligos were diluted 1:400 in 0.5X annealing buffer. Tet-puro-pLKO plasmid was digested with AgeI and EcoRI (NEB) restriction enzymes, gel-purified (NEB), and ligated to the annealed shRNA oligos. Ligated plasmids were transformed into stable competent E. Coli (High Efficiency) cells (NEB) and incubated overnight at 37°C on ampicillin LB agar plates. Clones were selected and grown in liquid LB culture with ampicillin, and the plasmid was purified by miniprep (Qiagen). Purified plasmids were sequenced for validation.

  Lentivirus production for Dox-inducible shRNA integration: Lentivirus production for the stable integration of Dox-inducible shRNAs was conducted in HEK 293T cells purchased from ATCC. HEK 293T cells were grown to ~70% confluency on 60 mm cell culture plates prior to transfection. Cells were then transfected with Tet-pLKO-puro plasmids targeting UHRF1, DNMT1, or SUV39H1 transcripts and the accompanying lentiviral packaging (psPAX2) and envelope (pMD.2G) plasmids with Opti-MEM and X-tremeGene 360 transfection reagent (Roche) per the manufacturer’s protocol. After 48 hours of incubation, media containing lentivirus particles was collected and filtered using a 0.45 μm micron syringe filter (Fisher Scientific) to remove potential HEK293T cell debris.

  Lentivirus infection in RKO, HCT116, and WI-38 cells: Cells were grown to ~70% confluency on 60 mm cell culture plates prior to infection. Cells were then infected with media containing 8 μg/mL polybrene and 1 ml of shRNA lentivirus. Media was refreshed 24 hours post-infection, and the cells were allowed to grow for an additional 24 hours prior to puromycin selection (2 μg/mL) for 2 days. An uninfected plate of cells was grown in parallel to test puromycin resistance in the infected cells.

#### Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP)
  Chromatin preparation: For H3K9me3, H3K18ub, UHRF1, and H3K27me3 ChIPs, cells were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde (Thermo Scientific, 16% formaldehyde diluted with PBS) for 10 minutes at room temperature. For HA ChIPs targeting HA-3xFlag-tagged SUV39H1, cells were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde for 30 minutes. Crosslinking was then quenched with 0.125 M glycine for 5 minutes at room temperature. Cells were washed twice with cold PBS, collected in PBS supplemented with 1 mM PMSF, and then centrifuged at 500 g for 5 minutes at 4°C. Cell pellets were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C. Chromatin preparation for H3K9me3, UHRF1, H3K27me3, and HA ChIPs was performed using sonication-based chromatin shearing as follows: 10 million cells were resuspended with 1 ml cell lysis buffer (5 mM PIPES pH 8.0, 85 mM KCl, and 1% NP-40 supplemented with 1 mM PMSF and protease inhibitor) and incubated on ice for 20 minutes, with gentle flicking every 5 minutes to resuspend. Nuclei were collected by centrifugation at 2,000g for 5 minutes at 4°C and resuspended in 1 ml nuclei lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-Cl pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA, and 1% SDS). Chromatin was sheared with a Covaris E220 Evolution Focused-Ultrasonicator for 12 minutes with default settings (Average incident power: 7 Walt; peak incident power: 140 Walt; duty factor: 5%; cycles/burst: 200 count). Chromatin preparation for H3K18ub ChIP was performed using MNase digestion as follows: 10 million cells were resuspended with 1 ml cell lysis buffer and incubated on ice for 20 minutes. Nuclei pellets were collected by centrifugation at 2,000g for 5 minutes at 4°C and resuspended in 1 ml cold MNase digestion buffer (50 mM Tris-Cl pH 8.0, 1 mM CaCl2, 300 mM Sucrose supplemented with protease inhibitor) to wash. This was followed by centrifugation at 2,000g for 5 minutes at 4°C, and the nuclei pellets were resuspended in 1 ml of 37°C pre-warmed MNase digestion buffer. Chromatin was digested by adding 50U MNase (NEB) and incubated at 37°C in a Thermomixer (Eppendorf) for 10 minutes with vortexing at 900RPM. 20 μl of 0.5 M EDTA was added to quench the reaction. The MNase digested mixture was further solubilized with a Covaris E220 Evolution Focused-Ultrasonicator for 1 minute with default settings. Solubilized chromatin from both sonication shearing and MNase digestion was centrifuged at 10,000g for 10 minutes at 4°C to pellet insoluble material, and the supernatant was transferred to new tubes for further analysis.

  Quality control of chromatin preparation: 20 μl soluble chromatin was brought to 100 ul with a final concentration of 1% SDS, 50 mM NaHCO3, 300 mM NaCl, and 200 μg/μl Proteinase K (Thermo Scientific). The mixture was subject to crosslink reversal at 65°C overnight followed by adding 10 μg RNase A/T1 mix (Thermo Scientific) and incubating at 37°C for 30 min. DNA was purified using a QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen). DNA was quantified using a Qubit dsDNA High Sensitivity Assay kit (Invitrogen) and DNA fragment length was checked with 2% agarose gels. Chromatin shearing through sonication produced DNA fragments ranging from 200bp to 1000bp, with a predominant size distribution around 500bp. MNase digestion of chromatin predominantly yielded DNA fragments approximately 150bp in size.

  Immunoprecipitation (IP): 5 μl H3K9me3 antibody (Active Motif) and 5 μg of soluble chromatin, 2.5 μl UHRF1 antibody (Bioworld) and 10 μg soluble chromatin, 5 μl H3K18ub antibody (Cell Signaling) and 20 μg soluble chromatin, 5 μl H3K27me3 antibody (Cell Signaling) and 5 μg soluble chromatin, and 10 μl HA antibody (Cell Signaling) and 30 μg soluble chromatin were incubated at 4°C for 18 hours with constant rotation. Samples were immunoprecipitated with 30 μl Dynabeads Protein A magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 4°C for 2 hours. This was followed by low salt wash X 2 (20 mM Tris-Cl pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X 100, 0.1% SDS, 1 mM PMSF), high salt wash X 2 (20 mM Tris-Cl pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X 100, 0.1% SDS, 1 mM PMSF), LiCl wash X 1 (20 mM Tris-Cl pH 8.0, 250 mM LiCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% NP40, 1% Na-deoxycholate, 1 mM PMSF) and TE wash X 1 (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA). IP’d chromatin was eluted in 100 ul elution buffer (50 mM NaHCO3, 1% SDS) by incubating in a Thermomixer (Eppendorf) for 30 minutes with vortexing at 1,300 RPM. Eluted chromatin was subject to crosslink reversal and DNA purification as described above before further analysis.

  Antibody validation: Specificity of H3K9me3 (Active Motif) and H3K27me3 (Cell Signaling) antibodies were validated using histone peptide microarrays, and these data are available on the Histone Antibody Specificity Database84. H3K9me3, H3K27me3, and HA (Cell Signaling) antibodies used in this study have been widely used and cited for ChIP experiments. As this study reports the first use of UHRF1 (Bioworld) and H3K18ub (Cell signaling) antibodies in ChIP experiments, we included UHRF1 knockdown experiments to validate antibody specificity. Comparing UHRF1 knockdown cells with control cells, both UHRF1 and H3K18ub ChIP showed significant reductions in IP’d DNA mass and siQ-ChIP peak efficiencies (Figures 3 and S3), suggesting the UHRF1 and H3K18ub ChIP signals are UHRF1 dependent.

  ChIP-qPCR: Both input chromatin and IP chromatin were subject to crosslink reversal, DNA purification, and eluted in 30 μl nuclease-free water. 1 μl input DNA and 1 μl IP DNA were individually added as templates for each PCR reaction using KAPA SYBR FAST qPCR Kit (Roche) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. BioRAD CFX Opus93 Real-Time PCR System was used to run PCR reactions. ChIP-PCR was performed on biological duplicate or triplicate samples, and technical duplicates were performed for each gene (Table S4) in each biological replicate. The relative enrichment of the target protein (e.g. H3K9me3) to 2% input was determined by calculating 2−d(Ct), where d(Ct) =IP Ct - 2% input Ct.


#### siQ-ChIP library preparation and sequencing
  Libraries for Input and IP samples were prepared by the VAI Genomics Core (RRID:SCR_022913) from 10 ng of input material, and a minimum of 1 ng IP material using the KAPA Hyper Prep Kit (v5.16) (Kapa Biosystems, Wilmington, MA USA). Prior to PCR amplification, end-repaired and A-tailed DNA fragments were ligated to uniquely barcoded dual indexes (IDT, Coralville, IA USA). Quality and quantity of the finished libraries were assessed using a combination of Agilent DNA High Sensitivity chip (Agilent Technologies, Inc.), QuantiFluor® dsDNA System (Promega Corp., Madison, WI, USA), and Kapa Illumina Library Quantification qPCR assays (Kapa Biosystems). 50 bp, paired-end sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq6000 sequencer using an S2, 100 bp sequencing kit (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) to a minimum read depth of 50 million read pairs per immunoprecipitation library and 100 million read pairs per Input library. Base calling was done by Illumina RTA3 and the output of NCS was demultiplexed and converted to FastQ format with Illumina Bcl2fastq (v1.9.0).


#### siQ-ChIP-seq processing and analysis
  siQ-ChIP sequencing reads were 3’ trimmed and filtered for quality and adapter content using TrimGalore (v0.6.0) with “trim_galore --quality 25 –fastqc --paried” and the quality was assessed by FastQC (v0.11.8). Reads were aligned to human assembly hg38 with bowtie2 (v2.5.1)85 using “bowtie2 -I 0 -X 1000 --end-to-end --sensitive” and were deduplicated using removeDups from samblaster (v.0.1.24)86. SAM files were converted to BAM files, sorted, and indexed using samtools (v1.9)87. BAM files were used for quality control analysis with deeptools (v3.5.0)88 ‘plotFingerprint’ and ‘plotPCA’ functions. To strengthen our conclusions, two different mathematic model-based ChIP-seq peak calling and normalization methods were used independently in the analyses, including MACS247,48 and siQ-ChIP45,46.

  MACS2 peaks were called using “macs2 callpeak -f BAMPE --broad -B” for H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 ChIP-seq datasets and using “macs2 callpeak -f BAMPE -B” for UHRF1 and H3K18ub ChIP-seq datasets, with Input as the control. Intersections of MACS2-called peaks from biological duplicates were performed using bedtools (2.30.0)89 with “bedtools intersect -a rep1 -b rep2 -f 0.5 -r” to select conversed peaks showing over 50% overlap in biological duplicates. These MAC2-called peaks from different treatment groups were further intersected using bedtools (2.30.0)89 and Venn diagrams were generated with meta-chart (https://www.meta-chart.com/venn#/data) as shown in Figure S3A. These MAC2-called peaks were also intersected with RKO replication-timing sequencing data90 (GSE211592) using bedtools (2.30.0)89, and frequency plots depicting the MAC2-peak number and −Log2(Early/Late) ratio were plotted using R (v4.3.1) as shown in Figure S3G. For Figure 3F, MACS2-called peaks were first piled up into bigwig files using bedGraphToBigWig. Then, Spearman’s correlation analysis was performed using the multiBigwigSummary bins program from deeptools (v3.5.0)88 using 10 kb bins, and scatterplots depicting the transformed-Log2 peak signals were plotted using R (v4.3.1) (www.r-project.org).

  For siQ-ChIP normalization, aligned SAM files were processed for paired-end reads with high mapping quality (MAQ ≥ 20), correct pair orientation (Sam Flags = 99, 163), and fragment length as described for siQ-ChIP (https://github.com/BradleyDickson/siQ-ChIP). Param.in files were prepared for each sample with all parameters and measurements required for siQ-ChIP normalization45,46. IP tracks (with H3K9me3, H3K18ub, UHRF1, and H3K27me3 siQ-ChIP efficiency values) and comparative responses between drug treatments (as indicated in the paper) were generated with the execution of “./getsiq.sh > errorfile” with the EXPlayout file (NOTE: params.in and EXPlayout file are provided with the GEO accession). Bigwig pileups of IP tracks with siQ-ChIP efficiency values were generated using bedGraphToBigWig.88 Genome browser views of siQ-ChIP IP tracks were generated using IGV (v2.16.0)91. To determine the change in UHRF1, H3K18ub, H3K9me3, and H3K27me3 distributions between drug-treated samples and DMSO-treated samples, each drug-treated biological replicate was individually compared to the DMSO-treated biological replicate (e.g. DNMT1i-rep1 vs. DMSO-rep1, DNMT1i-rep2 vs. DMSO-rep2) using the comparative response algorithm in siQ-ChIP. Conserved siQ-ChIP peaks from biological duplicates were intersected using bedtools (2.30.0)89 and the averaged log2 fold-change in response (area of peak in drug treatment/area of peak in DMSO treatment) from biological duplicates was calculated. siQ-ChIP peaks were considered to significantly change if the log2 fold-change in response was ≥ 1.0 (increase following indicated treatment) or ≤ −1.0 (decrease following indicated treatment) as shown in Figures 3A, S3E, and 6A. Chromatin Hidden Markov Model (ChromHMM) 18-state annotations49–51 (RKO: ENCODE ENCSR974TXE; WI-38: ENCODE ENCSR062SWN) were used for the siQ-ChIP peak annotation. As shown in Figures 3B, S3F, and 6B, ChromHMM enrichment analysis was performed for the significantly changed siQ-ChIP peaks mentioned above using ChromHMM.jar (https://compbio.mit.edu/ChromHMM/) with “java -mx4000M -jar ChromHMM.jar OverlapEnrichment -labels RKO-18-state-ChromHMM siQ-ChIP-peaks.bed ChromHMM.output”. Heatmaps and average profiles of siQ-ChIP tracks at genes, transcription start sites (TSS), and peak-set were generated using the computeMatrix, plotProfile, and plotHeatmap programs from deeptools (v3.5.0)88. The K-means clustering algorithm from deeptools (v3.5.0)88 was used to group genomic regions based on their siQ-ChIP efficiency. ShinyGo 0.7692 Transcription factor (TF) and protein complex binding enrichment analysis with Encode and ChEA93databases were performed for DNMT1i-induced siQ-ChIP peaks as shown in Figure S3B.


#### Genomic DNA isolation
  Genomic DNA was extracted using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Samples were then treated with 1 mg/ml RNase A at 37°C for 30 minutes. DNA was re-precipitated with 1/10 volume 3 M sodium acetate pH 4.8 and 2.5 volumes 100% ethanol and stored overnight at −20°C. Precipitated DNA was pelleted by centrifugation at 17,090g for 30 minutes at 4°C. The pelleted DNA was washed twice with 70% ethanol, allowed to dry for 15 minutes, and resuspended in nuclease-free water.


#### Infinium MethylationEPIC BeadChip v2.0 (EPIC array)
  Genomic DNA was quantified with the Qubit dsDNA High Sensitivity Assay kit (Invitrogen), and 1.5 μg of genomic DNA was submitted to the VAI Genomics Core (RRID:SCR_022913) for quality control analysis, bisulfite conversion, and DNA methylation quantification using the Infinium MethylationEPIC BeadChIP v2.0 (Illumina) processed on an Illumina iScan system following the manufacturer’s protocol94,95.


#### EPIC array data processing and analysis
  All analyses were conducted in the R statistical software (v4.3.1). Raw IDAT files for each sample were processed using the Bioconductor package SeSAMe (v1.18.4) for extraction of probe signal intensity values, normalization of probe signal intensity values, and calculation of β-values from the normalized probe signal intensity values96–99. The β-value is the measure of DNA methylation for each CpG probe, where a minimum value of 0 indicates a fully unmethylated CpG and a maximum value of 1 indicates a fully methylated CpG in the population. CpG probes with a detection p-value > 0.05 in any one sample were excluded from the analysis. Differentially methylated regions (DMRs) were called using the Bioconductor package ChAMP (v2.30.0) Bumphunter algorithm100,101, and regions were considered differentially methylated if at least seven contiguous CpGs within a 300 base-pair range demonstrated significant (p<0.05) methylation change in biological duplicates comparing drug-treated cells to DMSO-treated cells. The intersection of DMRs was performed using the intersect program from bedtools(2.30.0)89, and the Venn diagram was generated using meta-chart (https://www.meta-chart.com/venn#/data) as shown in Figure 6F.


#### Integrative epigenomic analysis
  As described in the respective sections above, heatmaps and average profiles of siQ-ChIP tracks at DMRs and CpG islands were generated using the computeMatrix, plotProfile, and plotHeatmap programs from deeptools (v3.5.0)88. The K-means clustering algorithm from deeptools (v3.5.0)88 was used to group DMRs based on their siQ-ChIP efficiency. Indicated DMRs, CpG islands, or siQ-ChIP peaks were intersected with EPIC array CpG probe genomic coordinates (hg38) using bedtools (2.30.0)89 to identify individual CpGs covered by these genomic regions. CpGs, including the whole EPIC array and those covered by DMRs or siQ-ChIP peaks, were annotated with CpG island annotation (e.g. Island, Open_sea), CpG context (e.g. WCGW), and CpG content (e.g. solo CpG) as previously described102,103. The enrichment bias calculation104 was then performed for those CpGs to determine the CpG features in indicated DMR clusters and siQ-ChIP peaks. All enrichment bias calculations were normalized to the CpG composition of the DNMT1i-induced DMRs or EPIC array as indicated in the Figure legend, and were done by first determining the following values for each feature:

  q = Number of CpGs covered by indicated genomic regions that are in the feature (e.g. CpGs covered by UHRF1 siQ-ChIP peaks that are in CpG Islands)

  m = Total number of CpGs on the DNMT1i-induced DMRs or EPIC array that match feature (e.g. EPIC array CpGs that are in CpG Islands)

  n = Total number CpGs on the DNMT1i-induced DMRs or EPIC array that do not match feature (e.g., all EPIC array CpGs that are not in CpG Islands)

  k = Total number of CpGs by indicated genomic regions (e.g. CpGs covered by UHRF1 siQ-ChIP peaks)

  Next, the expected number of CpGs covered by indicated genomic regions that would be in that feature by random chance was determined with the following equation:  e=(mm+n)k

  Finally, the percent enrichment bias was calculated with the following equation:  %enrichmentbias=(q−ek)×100

  A positive or negative enrichment value indicates more or less enrichment for a feature in the indicated genomic region than would be expected by random chance, respectively.


#### RNA isolation, cDNA synthesis, and qRT-PCR
  TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen) was added directly to adhered cells, collected, and stored at −80°C. After thawing, total RNA was extracted following the manufacturer’s protocol, and RNA was resuspended in DEPC Nuclease-Free water. The High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems) with the addition of RNase was used according to the manufacturer’s protocol to synthesize cDNA from 2 μg RNA. Using the KAPA SYBR FAST qPCR Kit (Roche) according to the manufacturer’s protocol and run on a BioRAD CFX Opus93 Real-Time PCR System, qRT-PCR was performed on technical duplicates for each gene (Table S5). Data was analyzed using 2−dd(Ct) method105 where the fold change was determined by normalizing to the RPL4 housekeeping gene and an siRNA non-targeting control sample.


#### Construction and sequencing of directional total RNA-seq libraries
  Libraries were prepared by the VAI Genomics Core (RRID:SCR_022913) from 500 ng of total RNA using the KAPA RNA HyperPrep Kit (Kapa Biosystems, Wilmington, MA USA). Ribosomal RNA material was reduced using the QIAseq FastSelect –rRNA HMR Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA). RNA was sheared to 300–400 bp. Prior to PCR amplification, cDNA fragments were ligated to IDT for Illumina TruSeq UD Indexed adapters (Illumina Inc, San Diego CA, USA). Quality and quantity of the finished libraries were assessed using a combination of Agilent DNA High Sensitivity chip (Agilent Technologies, Inc.) and QuantiFluor® dsDNA System (Promega Corp., Madison, WI, USA). Individually indexed libraries were pooled and 50 bp, paired end sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq6000 sequencer to an average depth of 50 million raw paired-reads per transcriptome. Base calling was done by Illumina RTA3 and output of NCS was demultiplexed and converted to FastQ format with Illumina Bcl2fastq (v1.9.0).


#### RNA-seq processing and analysis
  Raw 50 bp paired-end reads were trimmed with TrimGalore! (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/trim_galore/) (RRID:SCR_011847) followed by quality control analysis with FastQC. Trimmed reads were aligned to GRCh38.p12 and indexed to GENCODE v29 via STAR (v2.5.3a) aligner with flags ‘-twopassMode Basic\ -quantMode GeneCounts’ for feature counting.

  ReadsPerGene output count files were constructed into a raw read count matrix in R. Low-count genes were filtered (1 count in at least one sample) prior to edgeR (v3.36.0)106 count normalization. Differential expression analysis and PCA was conducted using DESeq2(1.40.1)107. Genes were considered differentially expressed if |Log2FC| > 1 and FDR < 0.01 (Figures S6B and 7A). A venn diagram (Figure 7B) of overlapping genes that were upregulated by different treatments was generated using BioVenn108. Gene set enrichment analysis was conducted using GSEA (v4.3.2)109,110 using curated gene set databases including HALLMARK and c2.cp.kegg. Phenotype comparisons were set to the combined SUV39H1 KD and DNMT1i treatment versus REST (control, SUV39H1 KD, and DNMT1i) for all analyses with weighted enrichment statistic and Signal2Noise settings for ranking genes. Genes marked as a “core enrichment” gene were used for heatmap row z score analyses of normalized counts per million (CPM) values.


#### Generation of CRISPR knockout cell lines
  CRISPR/Cas9 vector cloning: pSpCas9(BB)-2A-Puro (PX459) V2.0 was a gift from Feng Zhang (Addgene # 62988)111. Single-guide RNA (sgRNA) oligos for insertion (Table S6) were synthesized by Eurofins. Oligos were reconstituted in water at a concentration of 100 μM. 1 μl forward strand and 1 ul reverse strand oligos were diluted in 23 μl annealing buffer (10 mM Tris pH 8.6, 50 mM NaCl), brought to 100°C for 3 minutes, and naturally cooled to 30°C to anneal the oligo pair. Annealed oligos were diluted 1:4 by adding 75 μl water. pSpCas9(BB)-2A-Puro (PX459) plasmid was digested with BbsI-HF (NEB #R3539L) restriction enzymes, gel-purified (NEB), and ligated to the annealed sgRNA oligos. Ligated plasmids were transformed into stable competent E. Coli (High Efficiency) cells (NEB) and incubated overnight at 37°C on ampicillin LB agar plates. Clones were selected and grown in liquid LB culture with ampicillin, and the plasmid was purified by miniprep (Qiagen). Purified plasmids were sequenced for validation.

  CRISPR/Cas9 plasmid transfection: RKO cells were plated in 6-well plates (100,000 cells/well) 24 hours before plasmid transfection. 2 μg plasmid DNA, 8 μl transfection reagent (Roche), and 200 μl OptiMEM (Gibco) were mixed and incubated at room temperature for 30 minutes. RKO cells were refreshed with 2 ml antibiotic-free media, and then the plasmid mix was added. After 48 hours of incubation, cells were subject to puromycin selection (2 μg/mL) for 2 days. An untransfected plate of RKO cells was grown in parallel to test puromycin resistance in the transfected cells.

  CRISPR knockout colony isolation: 1×106 puromycin-selected RKO cells were resuspended in 1 ml PBS supplemented with 1% FBS and filtered using 40 μm filters. Flow cytometry was used to sort 1 cell into each well of a 96-well plate containing 100 μl media. Cells were incubated for 14 days to allow colony expansion. Expanded single clones were collected for western blot knockout validation.


#### Recombinant protein production and purification
  Recombinant full-length UHRF1 was purified as previously described9. For SUV39H1, SUV39H2, and G9a, full-length SUV39H1, full-length SUV39H2, and G9a (912aa-1,210aa of canonical sequence) were cloned into a modified pQE vector as an N-terminal His-MBP fusion separated by a TEV cleavage site112. SUV39H1 mutations (L173A, V176A, and V178A) were introduced by QuikChange site-directed mutagenesis (Agilent). Rosetta (DE3) Escherichia coli were made chemically competent (Zymo Research), and expression constructs were transformed for 30 minutes on ice, heat-shocked at 42°C for 45 seconds, cooled down on ice for 2 minutes, and plated onto prewarmed LB agar plates (ampicillin, 100 μg/mL). Single colonies were picked and grown in 500 ml LB media with ampicillin (100 μg/mL) at 37 °C until the OD600 reached 0.6–0.8. The temperature was then lowered to 16 °C, Isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) was added to a final concentration of 0.5 mM, and cultures were incubated for 18 hours with shaking. Protein expression after IPTG induction was checked by SDS/PAGE, followed by Coomassie blue staining. Cells were collected by centrifugation (5,000 × g, 20 min, 4 °C) and resuspended in 40 ml lysis buffer (50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 20 mM imidazole) supplemented with 1 mM PMSF, 1 mM DTT, and 0.5 mg/mL lysozyme. Cells were incubated on ice for 30 minutes and sonicated at 4 °C (Branson SFX150 Sonifier, 1s ON / 2s OFF, 20% Amplitude, 2 minutesTotal ON). Insoluble material was then cleared by centrifugation (38,000 × g, 30 min, 4 °C). 5 ml His60 Superflow resin (Takara) was washed × 3 in lysis buffer and then mixed with cleared lysate. His60 Superflow resin and cell lysate mixture was rotated at 4 °C for 2 h. The resin and bound protein were washed × 3 with at least 10-bed volumes of lysis buffer, followed by elution of His-tagged proteins in 5-bed volumes of elution buffer (25 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl and 250 mM Imidazole supplemented with 1 mM DTT before use). Proteins were concentrated (Amicon Ultra Centrifugal Filters) and imidazole was significantly reduced by buffer exchange (25 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT). Fractions were checked for purity by SDS/PAGE, followed by Coomassie blue staining, pooled and concentrated, and frozen with 15% glycerol.

  For Setdb1/Atf7IP, cDNAs for Setdb1 and ATF7IP were purchased from Genscript and DNASU, respectively. Flag-tagged Setdb1 and Strep-tagged Atf7IP were individually cloned into pLib vectors and assembled into pBig1a for co-expression in SF9 insect cells according to the BiGBac baculovirus expression system. pLib and pBig1a were gifts from Jan-Michael Peters (Addgene plasmids # 80610 and # 80611)113. DH10Bac E. coli were transformed with pBig1a co-expression vector to generate bacmids for co-expression in SF9 insect cells. ExpiSF9 cells were transfected with the co-expression bacmid, and the virus was amplified to obtain a p2 viral stock at high titer. For protein expression, 500ml of Hi five insect cells were infected with the p2 viral stock at a multiplicity of infection of approximately 4. Protein was induced for 48 hours, harvested by centrifugation, resuspended in lysis buffer (30 mM HEPES pH 7.9, 200 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 1 CoMplete protease inhibitor tablet per 50 ml lysis buffer), and frozen at −80 °C. To purify the Flag-Setdb1/Strep-Atf7IP complex, cells were thawed and lysed using sonication. The lysate was clarified by centrifugation and the clear lysate was applied to M2 FLAG resin in batch, washed, and purified using a gravity flow column. The FLAG elution was concentrated and injected onto a Superose 6 Size exclusion column equilibrated with gel filtration buffer (30 mM HEPES pH7.9, 200 mM NaCl, 5% Glycerol, 2 mM DTT). Peak fractions were pooled, concentrated, flash-frozen, and stored at −80 °C.


#### Methyltransferase activity assay
  1 μM His-MBP-SUV39H1, 1 μM His-MBP-SUV39H2, 1 μM His-MBP-G9a, or 100 nM SETDB1/Atf7IP was mixed with 1 μM recombinant nucleosomes (EpiCypher) or histone H3 peptides with indicated concentrations, and 20 μM S-Adenosyl methionine (SAM) in methyltransferase reaction buffer (20 mM Tris pH 8.0, 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 3 mM MaCl2, 1 mM dithiothreitol, and 0.1 mg/ml BSA) with a total volume of 10 μl in wells of 384-well plates. Reactions were incubated at 30 °C for 18 h and stopped by the addition of 2 μl 0.5% trifluoracetic acid. The MTase-Glo™ methyltransferase assay (Promega) was used following the manufacturer’s protocol to detect and quantify the overall methyltransferase activity in each reaction. MTase-Glo™ assays were done on technical duplicates for each reaction. Meanwhile, western blot analysis was done in parallel using each reaction products to confirm the methyltransferase activity on H3K9 and its methyl order. For the time course study shown in Figure 5H, 1 μM His-MBP-SUV39H1 was mixed with 1 μM of the indicated nucleosomes and then incubated at 30°C for the indicated time before quenching with 0.5% trifluoracetic acid. MTase-Glo™ assay was performed at the end time point (24h) to quantify the overall methyltransferase activity in each reaction.

  To measure the in vitro IC50 of OTS186935 on SUV39H1 and SUV39H2, 1 μM His-MBP-SUV39H1 or 1 μM His-MBP-SUV39H2 was mixed with 10 μM H3 (1–20) peptides, 20 μM SAM, and serially diluted OTS186935 (2-fold dilution ranging from 1 mM to 50 nM) or equivalent amount of DMSO. The reactions were incubated at 30 °C for 4 h and MTase-Glo™ methyltransferase assay (Promega) was used following the manufacturer’s protocol to detect and quantify the overall methyltransferase activity in each reaction.


#### Drug treatment and proliferation assays
  To measure the outgrowth of colon cancer cells after drug treatments, 20,000 RKO or HCT116 cells were seeded in 12-well plates 24 hours before drug administration. For Figure 7I, bulk RKO cells with CRISPR control knockout or SUV39H2 sgRNA2 knockout were treated with or without 100 nM DAC on Day 0. For Figure 7J, RKO cells integrated with Dox-inducible shSUV39H1 were treated with or without 20 ng/ml Dox and/or 500 nM GSK862 on Day 0. Cells were refreshed with drug-containing media every 3 days to maintain SUV39H1 knockdown. For Figure 7K, RKO cells were treated with or without 1 μM GSK862 and/or 200 nM OTS186935 on Day 0; HCT116 cells were treated with or without 1 μM GSK862 and/or 500 nM OTS186935 on Day 0. For Figure 7L, EV, WT, and 3A mutant SUV39H1 were ectopically expressed in RKO SUV39H2 knockout clone 11 with Dox-inducible shSUV39H1. Cells were treated with 20 ng/ml Dox and with or without 300 nM GSK862 on Day 0. Cells were refreshed with drug-containing media every 3 days to maintain endogenous SUV39H1 knockdown. Treatment doses and paradigm for growth results shown in Figure S7 were indicated in Figure Legends. Cell confluency was measured and calculated using the Incucyte® S3 Live-Cell Analysis System (Sartorius). 16 images were captured for each well at each time point, and % confluency results were presented as mean ± SEM of the analysis of 16 images.

  To measure the outgrowth of RKO cells after “prolonged treatment” as indicated in Figure S7A, 100,000 RKO cells were seeded in 6-well plates and drugs were applied to adhered cells 24 hours later. After 72 hours of drug treatment, cells were collected and re-plated directly in fresh media and drugs for another 72 hours. After a total of 6 days of “pre-treatment,” 20,000 RKO cells were plated into a 12-well plate and treated for a final time, which is represented as “Day 6” on the confluency graph shown in Figure S7A. Cell confluency was measured by Incucyte as described above.

  For the colony formation experiment shown in Figure S7B, 200 RKO cells were seeded into 6-well plates 24 hours before drug administration. Cells were refreshed with drug-containing media every 2 days and were fixed with 100% methanol following 14 days of drug treatment. Fixed cells were stained with crystal violet staining solution (0.5% crystal violet and 20% methanol) before imaging.
