# Enhanced Disease
Resistance Mechanism of the CmoAP2/ERF
Transcription Factor in Pumpkin through Genetic Mutations


## Abstract
  

  The squash species Cucurbita moschata has been historically utilized by both animals and humans as a food source. It is an annual dicotyledonous vegetable known for its health benefits, including reducing the risk of various diseases, such as cancer, high blood pressure, diabetes, intestinal disorders, and atherosclerosis, in humans. However, the cultivation of this valuable crop is often challenged by diseases such as powdery mildew (PM), caused by the fungus Podosphaera xanthii. PM not only reduces yield but also impacts photosynthesis rates. A newly identified gene called CmoCh3G009850, which encodes the transcription factor known as AP2-like ethylene-responsive factor (CmoAP2/ERF), has been marked for resistance against PM. A shift in the state from susceptible to resistant can be induced by nonsynonymous SNP mutations at five locations of the CmoCh3G009850 gene. The dynamical studies of the wild-type (WT) and mutated-type AP2/ERF proteins’ interactions with DNA were explored by docking and molecular dynamics simulation studies. These five mutations T105A, S302R, H321R, H335D, and V402A are incorporated in the AP2/ERF transcription factor that makes a stable and compact complex with DNA rather than the WT protein. Overall, the identification and characterization of the CmoAP2/ERF gene and its variants represent a significant advancement for the breeding and cultivation of C. moschata varieties resistant against powdery mildew. This study not only enhances our understanding of plant–pathogen interactions but also provides a potential avenue for developing more resilient squash varieties through genetic improvement.


## Introduction
  Pumpkin (Cucurbita moschata) is a versatile and widely cultivated edible plant enjoyed both as a vegetable and for its medicinal properties across various regions, including Europe, Asia, and parts of the Americas. The plant is valued not only for its fruits but also for its seeds and greens, making it an important multifunctional food source.1 In terms of nutrition, pumpkin is noted for its low-fat content, high protein richness, and good antioxidant compounds, which makes it a favorable choice for dietary inclusion, especially in regions where protein sources may be limited.2,3

  Beyond its nutritional value, pumpkin has gained significant attention in the medical field due to its bioactive compounds. These compounds exhibit a wide range of health-promoting properties, including anti-inflammatory, antiparasitic, antimicrobial, anticarcinogenic, antioxidant, antidiabetic, hepatoprotective, and hypotensive.4,5 These diverse health benefits underline the potential of pumpkin as a functional food and a natural source of bioactive compounds with therapeutic properties. Its widespread cultivation and consumption in various cultures highlight its nutritional and medicinal significance globally.

  Plants have developed regulatory mechanisms to adapt against the dynamic changes in the environment that affect their survival, growth, and reproduction. Globally massive agricultural losses have resulted due to various abiotic stresses.6 These environmental factors, such as high salinity, water scarcity, and extreme temperature, affect particular plant hormone signaling pathways and are controlled through specific hormones and transcription factors belonging to these stress responses. Under abiotic stress, plants produce several hormones such as ethylene (ET) and abscisic acids (ABAs), which are produced as stress hormones, while gibberellins (GAs), brassinosteroids (BRs), and cytokinins (CTK) are produced in different amounts to promote plants’ growth. Changing these substances facilitates the best responses to be achieved.7 During abiotic stress, these hormones are regulated by hormone- and stress-responsive transcription factors.8 The transcription factor belonging to the APETALA2/ETHYLENE RESPONSIVE FACTOR (AP2/ERF) family is now recognized as an important modulator of numerous abiotic stresses along with multiple hormone responses for managing external stress in plants9 and regulating fruit development.10 Some biomarkers are linked with control of biotic11,12 and abiotic stress in the plants and are responsible for several diseases in human beings, like breast cancer.13

  The first reported ERF domain as a conserved motif in four DNA-binding proteins in Nicotiana tabacum (tobacco) is the ethylene-responsive element-binding proteins 1, 2, 3, and 4 (EREBP1, 2, 3, and 4). And now they are renamed as ERF1, 2, 3, and 4, and they interact with the GCC box of DNA sequence.14 The ERF family in plants is classified into four subgroups due to ancient transposition, homing process, and tandem as well as chromosomal/segmental duplication.15 ERF family proteins have several diverse functions to regulate the cellular processes in order to control abiotic stresses such as low temperature, water deficit and drought,16−18 and high-salt- and cold-responsive conditions.19 The AP2 or ethylene responsive element binding factor (EREB) domain of AP2/ERF has 40–70 conserved amino acids for binding with DNA segments.15,20

  The fungus Podosphaera xanthii lowers the rate of photosynthesis and causes powdery mildew (PM) on pumpkin leaves. The novel gene belonging to AP2-like ethylene-responsive transcription factor (CmoAP2/ERF), which is responsible for resistance against PM, was reported in the genome-wide association study (GWAS) study of healthy pumpkin seedlings.21 Nonsynonymous single nucleotide mutations affect the structural and functional properties of proteins.22 Certain protein mutations termed as disease-causing mutations have been shown to improve susceptibility or vulnerability, but sometimes these mutations behave oppositely.

  The study by Alavilli et al., 2022, focused on the AP2/ERF protein extracted from a genome-wide association study (GWAS) of pumpkin (C. moschata), specifically investigating its role in resistance against powdery mildew caused by Pseudomonas xanthii.100 According to their findings, five nonsynonymous mutations were identified within the AP2/ERF protein sequence which were associated with resistance against this fungal disease. These mutations were hypothesized to alter the protein’s structure and function, thereby enhancing its ability to confer resistance. To delve deeper into the molecular mechanisms behind this resistance, we analyzed the DNA–protein interaction patterns of both the wild-type (WT) and mutant-type AP2/ERF proteins. We employed molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to observe the dynamic behavior of these protein–DNA complexes over time.

  MD simulations are computational techniques used to model the movements and interactions of atoms and molecules over a time scale.23 By subjecting the protein–DNA complexes to MD simulations, we aimed to elucidate how these mutations affect the stability and dynamics of the complexes. Specifically, we observed how the mutated AP2/ERF proteins bind to DNA sequences involved in regulating the resistance pathways against PM.

  The mutations identified in the AP2/ERF protein are proposed to play a critical role in regulating these resistance pathways in pumpkins. By understanding the structural and dynamic changes induced by these mutations through computational modeling, we provided insights into how these genetic variations can influence disease resistance in crops like C. moschata. This knowledge would be invaluable in future breeding efforts aimed at developing more resilient pumpkin varieties through targeted genetic manipulation.


## Materials and Methods

### Protein Preparation
  The protein sequence of AP2/ERF was extracted from the Supporting Information of the GWAS study on Pumpkin (C. moschata) as published by Alavilli et al., 2022. Since the protein’s three-dimensional structure was not yet available in the protein data bank, we used an I-TASSER server to model the protein.24,25 The SAVES server (https://saves.mbi.ucla.edu/) was used for the quality assessment of the modeled structure by using ERRAT,26 VERIFY 3D,27,28 and PROCHECK.29 We performed molecular dynamics simulation at 50 ns to refine the modeled structure AP2/ERF using GROMACS30 ·


### Protein–DNA Docking
  DNA sequences have been obtained from the PDB (PDB id 7WQ5), which is bound with an ethylene-responsive transcription factor WRI1 of Arabidopsis thaliana.31 Using the HDOCK server, DNA was docked with the AP2/ERF protein by choosing advanced parameters that specified the binding site residues, which are based on a template-based docking procedure.32 The site-directed mutagenesis at five residues was carried out by PyMol, and subsequently, the structure was equilibrated and minimized by GROMACS.33−36 Finally, the mutated minimal structure was docked with DNA by HDOCK again.


### Molecular Dynamics Simulation
  Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations are a powerful tool for understanding the dynamic behavior, stability, and flexibility.37 We performed MD simulations for the wild-type (WT) and mutated AP2/ERF protein–DNA complexes at atomic resolution, complementing the experimental techniques. The amber99bsc1 force field was used to simulate both the WT and mutant protein–DNA complexes. The force field file was downloaded from the Gromacs Web site.38 The complexes were solvated in a cubic box with edges of 12.39 Å using the TIP3P water model. The system was neutralized by adding Na+ and Cl– ions, and steric clashes were resolved through 50,000 iterations of energy minimization using the steepest descent method with a cutoff of 1000 kJ/mol. Two-phase equilibration was performed to ensure the stability of the solvent molecules and ions. The temperature was stabilized using the NVT ensemble at 300 K, and the pressure was equilibrated using the NPT ensemble at 1 atm, running for 100 ps each. Finally, MD simulations of the WT and mutated protein–DNA complexes were conducted for 100 ns.


### Trajectory Analysis
  Different dynamics parameters of complexes were analyzed by modules available in Gromacs to understand the biomolecular behavior of complexes. It provided a detailed explanation of the behavior of the atoms in the complexes over time. First, we indexed the protein–DNA complexes through the make_ndx module. Four stability parameters, i.e., root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF), the radius of gyration (Rg), and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA),39 were used for the complex analysis. The RMSD, RMSF, Rg, and SASA were determined by rms, rmsf, gyrate, and sasa modules, respectively. The obtained xvg files were analyzed and visualized by Xmgrace.40 Hydrogen bond distribution is another parameter to determine the stability of the complex. Therefore, the hbond module was used for the distribution of hydrogen bonds between the protein and DNA over time. The structural dynamics of the protein–DNA complex during MD simulations were investigated by principal component analysis (PCA). It unravels the collective motions within the protein–DNA complex. Two modules, covar and anaeig, were finally used for the PCA.


## Results and Discussion

### AP2/ERF Structure Modeling
  The protein sequence of AP2/ERF was obtained from a GWAS study of C. moschata.21 They observed that mutation in the AP2/ERF gene was involved in resistance against the powdery mildew caused by the fungus P. xanthii. The proposed protein sequence is very unique and does not show homology with any other proteins present in the PDB database. That is why a three-dimensional structure was modeled for this protein by threading or ab initio (hybrid) modeling methods. I-TASSER was used for hybrid modeling of the AP2/ERF protein sequence (Figure 1a). It gave different predicted models using the folds of various PDB structures (7WQ5, 2OCW, 2NBI, 5FMW, 8WXB, and 5WX9).


### Validation of the Modeled Structure
  Initially, the modeled protein structure did not meet the criteria for a good-quality structure. Only 65.2% of residues were within the allowed regions of the Ramachandran plot, while 27.8% were within additional allowed regions (Figure 1d and Table 1). This indicates that a significant portion of the protein structure deviated from ideal structural conformations. Following MD simulation, which involved analyzing the RMSD, RMSF, and Rg, it was observed that the structure became stable over time (Figure S1), and the dimensional confirmation in the cartoon model is shown in Figure 1b. The RMSD, a measure of structural deviation from the starting structure, indicated that the protein structure stabilized during the simulation. After 50 ns of MD simulation, there was an improvement in the quality of the modeled structure. Now, 82.5% of the residues were in favored regions (indicating they were within favorable conformational parameters), and 14.9% were in additional allowed regions (Figure 1e and Table 1). This suggests that the structure became more closely aligned with the expected structural norms. Comparing the initial modeled structure with the structure after MD simulation, the RMSD value was 12.136 (Figure 1c). This value indicates the average distance between equivalent atoms in the two superimposed structures, reflecting the degree of structural variation between the initial and the simulated structures.


### Mutational Changes in the AP2/ERF
  Proteins’ associated structural topologies support a thorough analysis of the structural perturbations caused by mutations which exhibit the pathogen interactions.41,42 The WT AP2/ERF protein was mutated at five positions, i.e., T105A, S302R, H321R, H335D, and V402A. For stability and structural minimization, the mutated structure was simulated at 5 ns. The RMSD value gives information on the differences between the backbones of the proteins. After mutations, the RMSD between WT and mutated AP2/ERF protein structures was 3.633 nm. The ratio of residues in the Ramachandran plot region can be seen in Figure 2a and the fourth column of Table 1. When compared to the WT protein, the mutated structure’s quality is slightly improved. Three-dimensional conformational changes in the WT (blue) and mutated (magenta) AP2/ERF proteins are shown in Figure 2b. Sequences of proteins have specific structures and functions defined as motifs, also termed as supersecondary structures. The reported protein was 491 amino acids long, and they have two major motifs belonging to the AP2/ERF family (Figure 2c).


### Protein–DNA Docking
  The two most important biological molecules of the cell are proteins and DNA. The protein–DNA interactions regulate many biological processes such as cell regulation, DNA replication, signal transduction, protein synthesis, and many more. Therefore, the docking of protein and DNA helps us to understand the atomic-level behavior of complexes in the biological process used to develop a new variety and resistance crop. The WRINKLED1 (WRI1) regulator is involved in oil synthesis in A. thaliana, and it belongs to the APETALA2 (AP2)/EREBP transcription family.31,43 The structure of the 24 nucleotide long DNA sequence (5′-TACTTCCTCGGTTTCATCGTCCAC) was obtained from the bound form with transcriptional factor WRINKLED1 (PDB: 7WQ5).

  Figure 3 displays both two- and three-dimensional depictions of the WT protein–DNA interaction. Thirteen hydrophobic interactions and seven hydrogen bonds were detected in the complex. Seven residues (Ser25, Glu27, Arg37, Met74, Gln75, Arg397, and Thr418) of protein are involved in the formation of hydrogen bonds with four nucleotides of the 5′ to 3′ strand (5T, 16A, 17T, and 18C) and two nucleotides of the 3′ to 5′ strand (4G and 23T).

  The mutant protein–DNA complex’s interactions (two- and three-dimensional representations) are illustrated in Figure 4. The interactions between the mutated protein and DNA comprise 13 hydrophobic interactions and six hydrogen bonds. Protein residues Pro338, Gly340, Ser341, His346, Arg365, and Asn385 build hydrogen bonds with two nucleotides of the 3′ to 5′ strand (6C and 10G) and four nucleotides of the 5′ to 3′ strand (13T, 14T, 15C, and 21C). ### Molecular Dynamics Simulation Analysis
  The trajectory analysis of the MD simulations for the protein–DNA complex, before and after mutation, reveals significant insights into the structural dynamics and stability of the complexes.


#### Root-Mean-Square Deviation
  Figure 5a illustrates the RMSD values of the protein–DNA complexes over a 100 ns simulation period. The black line represents the RMSD of the wild-type transcription factor-DNA complex, while the red line corresponds to the mutated TF-DNA complex. The average RMSD for the wild-type complex (black) is approximately 1.2 nm, with fluctuations ranging between 0.8 and 1.5 nm. The mutated complex (red) shows a lower average RMSD of around 0.9 nm, with fluctuations ranging between 0.5 and 1.2 nm. Initially, both complexes exhibit similar RMSD values, indicating comparable structural conformations. However, as the simulation progresses, the wild-type complex shows higher RMSD fluctuations, suggesting greater structural instability. In contrast, the mutated complex maintains lower RMSD values, indicating enhanced stability. This suggests that the mutation confers structural rigidity to the protein–DNA complex, potentially contributing to the observed resistance against P. xanthii.

  The observed reduction in RMSD for the mutated complex is consistent with previous studies indicating that specific mutations can lead to enhanced structural stability of protein–DNA complexes.46,47 Enhanced stability often correlates with improved functional performance, particularly in transcription factors where stable binding to DNA is critical for effective gene regulation.48


#### Radius of Gyration
  Rg reflects the compactness of the protein structure. Figure 5b shows that the Rg for the wild-type complex fluctuates around an average of 3.0 nm, whereas the mutated complex maintains a lower average Rg of approximately 2.8 nm. Both complexes display fluctuations throughout the simulation, but the mutated complex (red) exhibits a consistently lower Rg compared to the wild-type complex (black). This decreased Rg for the mutated complex suggests a more compact and stable structure, further supporting the enhanced stability observed in the RMSD analysis. A more compact structure may enhance the ability of the protein to interact with DNA, thereby improving its functional efficiency in binding to DNA and regulating gene expression related to powdery mildew resistance.

  Compactness, as indicated by lower Rg values, is often associated with improved protein–DNA binding affinity and specificity.49 This structural compactness can facilitate the proper positioning of critical residues in the DNA-binding domain, enhancing the transcription factor’s ability to recognize and bind to its target DNA sequences.50


#### Root-Mean-Square Fluctuation
  The RMSF analysis, shown in Figure 5c,d, provides residue-level insights into the flexibility of the protein and DNA, respectively. For the protein (Figure 5c), the wild-type complex exhibits higher fluctuations across several residues, indicating greater flexibility and potential instability. The mutated complex, however, shows reduced fluctuations, particularly in regions critical for DNA binding. For DNA (Figure 5d), the wild-type complex also demonstrates higher RMSF values, suggesting a less stable interaction with the protein. The mutation results in lower RMSF values, highlighting a more stable protein–DNA interaction.

  Reduced RMSF values in the mutated complex suggest that the mutation leads to a decrease in conformational flexibility, thereby stabilizing the interaction between the protein and DNA. This stabilization is crucial for maintaining the integrity of the protein–DNA complex during the dynamic processes of gene regulation.51,52 Enhanced stability in critical binding regions can improve the transcription factor’s ability to modulate gene expression effectively, contributing to increased resistance against pathogens such as P. xanthii.53


#### Hydrogen Bond Distribution
  Figure 5e presents the hydrogen bond distribution over the simulation period. The wild-type complex (black) shows an average of 7 hydrogen bonds, with fluctuations indicating instability. The mutated complex (red) exhibits a higher average of 10 hydrogen bonds with fewer fluctuations, suggesting more stable interactions. This indicates that the mutation enhances the formation of hydrogen bonds, thereby stabilizing the protein–DNA interaction and potentially contributing to the resistance mechanism against P. xanthii.

  Hydrogen bonds play a critical role in stabilizing protein–DNA interactions, and an increase in hydrogen bonding can significantly enhance the binding affinity and stability of the complex.54,55 The observed increase in hydrogen bonds in the mutated complex suggests that the mutation enhances the intermolecular interactions, thereby stabilizing the complex and potentially improving its functional performance in gene regulation.56


#### Solvent-Accessible Surface Area
  The SASA values, illustrated in Figure 5f, reflect exposure of the protein–DNA complex to the solvent. Lower SASA values indicate a more compact and less solvent-exposed complex. Figure 5f shows that the SASA for the wild-type complex (black) is higher, with an average of 410 nm,2 indicating greater exposure to the solvent. The mutated complex (red) has a lower average SASA of approximately 390 nm,2 suggesting reduced solvent exposure and a more compact structure. This compactness may protect the complex from deleterious environmental interactions, enhancing its functional stability.

  A lower SASA value indicates a more buried and compact structure, which can enhance the stability of the protein–DNA complex by reducing the exposure of hydrophobic regions to the solvent.57 This compactness is likely to contribute to the overall structural stability and functional efficiency of the transcription factor in regulating gene expression.58

  The allelic variation in the CmoCh3G009850 gene, encoding the AP2-like ethylene-responsive transcription factor (CmoAP2/ERF), appears to play a crucial role in conferring resistance to powdery mildew in pumpkins. The MD simulation results indicate that the mutation stabilizes the protein–DNA complex, as evidenced by lower RMSD, RMSF, and SASA values along with a higher number of stable hydrogen bonds. This structural stability likely enhances the binding efficiency of the transcription factor to its target DNA, thereby improving the regulation of genes involved in defense responses against P. xanthii.


#### Principal Component Analysis of Protein–DNA
Complexes
  PCA is a powerful statistical tool used to simplify the complexity of high-dimensional data while retaining most of the variation in the data set. In the context of molecular dynamics simulations, PCA helps us to understand the major conformational changes in protein–DNA complexes. Figure 6 presents the PCA results for the wild-type (susceptible) and mutated transcription factor (TF)-DNA complexes, providing insights into their structural dynamics.

  Figure 6a shows the 2D projection of the trajectory onto the first two principal components (eigenvectors) for the wild-type (black) and mutated (red) protein–DNA complexes. The wild-type complex has a broader distribution with projections ranging from −25 to 25 nm, indicating higher conformational flexibility. The mutated complex has a narrower distribution, ranging from −15 to 15 nm, suggesting reduced flexibility and more restricted motion. This compactness of the mutated complex could contribute to its increased resistance to P. xanthii by maintaining a stable interaction with the DNA. Reduced conformational flexibility, as observed in the mutated complex, is often associated with enhanced structural stability and functional efficiency during protein–DNA interactions.59 This stabilization can facilitate more effective binding and regulation of target DNA sequences, contributing to the transcription factor’s role in disease resistance.60

  Figure 6b presents the projection of the trajectories on the first two eigenvectors over the 100 ns simulation period. The wild-type complex shows larger fluctuations along both eigenvectors, with average projections around ±10 nm for the first eigenvector and ±5 nm for the second eigenvector. In contrast, the mutated complex exhibits smaller fluctuations, with average projections around ±5 nm for the first eigenvector and ±3 nm for the second eigenvector. The pronounced fluctuations in the wild-type complex indicate significant conformational changes, reflecting its unstable nature. The reduced fluctuations in the eigenvector projections for the mutated complex indicate that the mutation promotes a more stable conformational landscape, enhancing the functional performance of the protein–DNA complex.61 Stability in the conformational landscape is critical for the efficient binding and regulation of DNA by transcription factors.62 This further supports the notion that the mutation leads to structural rigidity and stability, which could be crucial to its functional role in resistance mechanisms.

  Figure 6c illustrates the RMSF values for the protein–DNA complexes, highlighting the flexibility of each residue. The wild-type complex has higher RMSF values, particularly in critical regions for DNA binding, with average RMSF values around 0.6 nm and peaks reaching 1.0 nm. The mutated complex shows reduced RMSF values, with averages around 0.4 nm and peaks reaching 0.8 nm, indicating increased rigidity and stability in these regions. This stabilization likely contributes to the efficient binding of the transcription factor to its target DNA, facilitating the effective regulation of genes involved in powdery mildew resistance. Figure 6d,e displays the contributions of individual atomic components to the first and second eigenvectors before and after mutation, respectively. The black line represents the total motion, while the red, green, and blue lines correspond to the x, y, and z components of the motion, respectively. The wild-type complex shows higher variability in the contributions, with fluctuations ranging from −0.1 to 0.1 for the first eigenvector and from −0.05 to 0.05 for the second eigenvector. The mutated complex exhibits more consistent and lower variability in the eigenvector components with fluctuations ranging from −0.05 to 0.05 for the first eigenvector and −0.03 to 0.03 for the second eigenvector. This reduction in motion is indicative of a more rigid and stable protein–DNA complex, which is likely to enhance its functional efficiency in resisting P. xanthii.

  The PCA results provide compelling evidence that the allelic variation in the CmoCh3G009850 gene encoding the AP2-like ethylene-responsive transcription factor (CmoAP2/ERF) plays a pivotal role in stabilizing the protein–DNA complex. The mutation leads to a more compact, stable, and less flexible complex, as demonstrated by the reduced RMSF values, lower projections on eigenvectors, and focused 2D projection trajectory.63 These structural characteristics are likely to enhance the binding affinity and specificity of the transcription factor to its target DNA, thereby improving the regulation of genes involved in defense responses against powdery mildew in pumpkin.

  Several species of fungi, bacteria, viruses, and oomycete affect crop yields through the process of host–pathogen interactions. There is a list of fungi which cause various diseases like Alternaria cucumerina (Alternaria leaf blight),64Cercospora citrullina (Cercospora leaf spot),65Pseudoperonosporacubensis (downy mildew),66Fusarium solani (Fusarium crown and foot rot),67Fusarium oxysporum,68Didymella bryoniae (gummy stem blight),69Erysiphe spp. (powdery mildew),70Septoria cucurbitacearum (Septoria leaf spot),71Sclerotium rolfsii (southern blight),72 and Verticillium dahliae (Verticillium wilt).73 Bacteria such as Pseudomonas syringae (angular leaf spot),74Erwinia tracheiphila (bacterial wilt),75 as well as some viruses, for example, cucumber mosaic virus (cucumber mosaic)76 and watermelon mosaic virus (watermelon mosaic),77 cause serious diseases which directly affect crop yields. Phytophthora capsici (Phytophthora blight) and Phytophthora melonis (Phytophthora blight) are oomycetes that act as pathogens responsible for the crown and fruit rotting in pumpkin (Cucurbita pepo), significantly affecting the yields of crops.78−81

  It is anticipated that using pathogen-resistant cultivars will boost the yield while reducing the need for fungicides in farming. The essential components of the plant defense system that combat various pathogens are transcription factors.82 The AP2/ERF family, a crucial transcription factor that has two tandem AP2 domains that bind to a dsDNA region, has emerged as the most viable option for bioengineering to increase the yield by improving the resistivity.

  The 3-D structure and function of proteins are determined by the amino acid patterns.83 The folding, stability, interaction of the protein with other molecules, and overall functionality of the protein can all be affected by alterations in the amino acid sequences.84−87 The structure and functions of the proteins can be changed when the amino acids have been mutated at specific sites, like conserved residues; otherwise, the mutation can change its structure but does not affect protein function. These mutations in the amino acids are a result of DNA markers like SNPs (single nucleotide polymorphism) and changes at single positions in plants’ DNA.88 Finding different SNPs that can be used to build new products is now easier because of recent technological advancements. It demonstrates how SNPs in the gene’s functional regions can alter the expressed region to regulate the levels of biotic and abiotic stress while improving the crops’ variety resistance to both.89

  Compared to the WT protein, the pumpkin mutant AP2/ERF protein binds more strongly with the DNA, leading to a more stable complex. The molecular dynamics simulations reveal that the WT transcription factor-DNA complex is less stable and shows significant instability during the simulation process. This instability likely contributes to the pumpkin’s susceptibility to fungal infection. Conversely, the enhanced stability of the mutant protein–DNA complex suggests its role in activating various pathways that improve resistance to fungal infection. These findings are consistent with studies such as of Murmu and Archak (2024),90 where the mutant form of a protein was reported to form a more stable complex than its WT counterpart, which exhibited instability. Similarly, Panchal et al. (2020)91 observed that mutations in the XPD gene led to altered protein stability, highlighting the importance of structural changes in influencing functional outcomes.


## Conclusions
  Pumpkins are extremely valuable crops that are predominantly utilized globally as food items and rootstocks for cucurbits. Still, P. xanthii hampers pumpkin growth and reduces the level of plant production. A molecular explanation is provided by the in silico investigation of the transcription factor AP2/ERF’s interaction with DNA. The enhanced resistance capabilities against the fungal infection can be credited to the mutated structure, which is more stable than the WT protein, and the same has been confirmed in the present study by docking and MD simulation studies of WT and mutant protein complexes. The combined trajectory analysis and PCA reveal that the mutation in the CmoAP2/ERF gene significantly impacts the structural dynamics of the protein–DNA complex. The mutation results in a reduced conformational flexibility, increased stability, and more coordinated motions within the complex. These changes will likely enhance the functional efficiency of the transcription factor in regulating genes involved in powdery mildew resistance. The findings from these analyses highlight the potential of targeted genetic modifications in the development of resistant crop varieties. Future studies should include experimental validation through in vivo experiments to confirm these computational predictions and further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying powdery mildew resistance in pumpkins.
