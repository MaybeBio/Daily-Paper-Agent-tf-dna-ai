# Untethered Microgrippers for Biopsy in the Upper Urinary Tract


## Abstract
  Untethered microrobots offer the possibility to perform medical interventions in anatomically complex and small regions in the body. Presently, it is necessary to access the upper urinary tract to diagnose and treat Upper Tract Urothelial Carcinoma (UTUC). Diagnostic and treatment challenges include ensuring adequate tissue sampling, accurately grading the disease, achieving completeness in endoscopic treatment, and consistently delivering medications to targeted sites. This work introduces microgrippers (μ-grippers) that are autonomously triggered by physiological temperature for biopsy in the upper urinary tract. The experiments demonstrated that μ-grippers can be deployed using standard ureteral catheters and maneuvered using an external magnetic field. The μ-grippers successfully biopsied tissue samples from ex vivo pig ureters, indicating that the thin-film bilayer springs’ autonomous, physiologically triggered actuation exerts enough force to retrieve urinary tract tissue. The quality of these biopsy samples is sufficient for histopathological examination, including hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and GATA3 immunohistochemical staining. Beyond biopsy applications, the μ-grippers’ small size, wafer-scale fabrication, and multifunctionality suggest their potential for statistical sampling in the urinary tract. Experimental data and clinical reports underscore this potential through statistical simulations that compare the efficacy of μ-grippers with conventional tools, such as ureteroscopic forceps and baskets.


## Introduction
  Untethered biomedical microrobots are small devices designed to navigate hard-to-reach body areas like the vascular system,[1,2]ocular,[3] gastrointestinal (GI),[4,5] respiratory,[6] and urinary tracts.[7,8] Microrobots have been engineered in various designs for medical tasks,[9,10] including drug delivery,[11,12] sensing,[13,14] surgical interventions,[15] and biopsies.[16–18] Biopsy microrobots, in particular, show great potential for diagnosing diseases by obtaining tissue samples from usually inaccessible areas.[19] However, there are challenges, particularly in delivering power for microactuators and enabling tissue collection. [20]

  There are two main classes of untethered robots relevant to biopsy and tissue sampling applications: biopsy capsules and untethered microgrippers. Biopsy capsules powered by external magnetic fields or batteries have been used to perform biopsies using a needle or forceps, predominantly in ex vivo and tissue phantoms.[21,22] Untethered microgrippers have been powered by the triggered release of residual thin film stresses, [16,17] magnetic fields,[23] and differential swelling[24–26] and have been used to biopsy and deliver therapeutics in the GI tract of live animals. [16,17]

  In contrast to the GI tract, designing biopsy microrobots for the urinary system, particularly the upper urinary tract, presents unique challenges. This complexity arises from the urinary tract’s specific anatomy and the narrowness of certain areas, such as the ureter, which has an inner diameter of 3 to 4 mm in human adults.[27] Additionally, the relative smoothness of the urothelium, the lining of the urinary tract, makes it more challenging to grasp than the GI tract’s mucosa. There has been some prior use of untethered microrobots for drug delivery and biofilm cleaning within the urinary system. Lee et al. described a bubble-based microrobot capable of pinning themselves to the bladder walls of rats for sustained local drug release in vivo.[7] Soon et al. reported a soft magnetic microrobot that shoots a spring-powered needle to anchor on ex vivo pig bladder and ureter tissue for potential local delivery and monitoring.[8] Other microrobots have been reported to clean biofilms within the urinary catheters using bubble-based[28] or magnetic-based locomotion.[29] However, these devices lack microactuators necessary for complex tasks such as tearing or cutting, which is required for retrieving tissue samples or performing biopsies in the urinary system.

  Despite the lack of biopsy microrobots in the urinary tract, there is a critical need for more accurate and less invasive biopsies for upper urinary tract diseases. For example, Upper Tract Urothelial Carcinoma (UTUC) is a cancer that affects the renal pelvis and ureters, with an incidence of 1 to 2 cases per 100,000 people globally.[30,31] Traditionally, UTUC has been diagnosed using imaging modalities such as computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging, but they often fail to detect low-grade and small-sized lesions.[32,33] Ureteroscopy (URS) with biopsy is the current gold standard for diagnosing UTUC via direct visualization and sampling of suspicious lesions in the upper urinary tract. However, the effectiveness of ureteroscopic biopsy is limited by several factors. For one, the small caliber of the ureter limits the size of biopsy instruments that can be introduced and, therefore, limits the quality of specimens that can be removed for sufficient pathological analysis. Given the relatively poor tissue yield with traditional, standard cup ureteroscopic biopsy forceps (e.g., Piranha™, Boston Scientific), alternative larger instruments have been used and developed, such as Nitinol baskets (e.g., NCircle®, Cook Medical Inc.) and backloading biopsy forceps (e.g., BIGopsy®, Cook Medical).[34] Additionally, the relatively larger size of these tools results in more significant mucosal trauma, which impairs endoscopic vision and restricts the number of adequate biopsies that can be performed. These limitations affect the precision and adequacy of biopsies, often leading to under-staging, under-grading, and missed lesions.[35] A Cleveland Clinic study found that 43% of patients were under-graded based on URS biopsies compared to subsequent surgical resection findings.[36] Similarly, another study revealed that 65% of patients were upstaged from their initial URS diagnosis after final pathology, with up to 25% of patients having missed lesions.[37] These diagnostic challenges significantly affect treatment decisions and patient outcomes, underscoring the critical need for more accurate and less invasive biopsy methods.

  To address these urgent needs, we report developing and operating first-in-class untethered upper urinary tract biopsy μ-grippers in this work. The micro-robotic grippers have a tip-to-tip size of 717 μm (approximately 2.2 French, Fr) before activation. This small size enables the μ-grippers to be deployed using a urinary catheter or the working channel of a ureteroscope, which typically has a channel size range from 3 to 6 Fr (Figures 1a and b). [38] The μ-grippers are activated autonomously by body temperature, during which the μ-gripper arms self-fold due to the release of differential stress energy stored in the thin metal films like a triggered spring. On self-folding, the tips can penetrate and capture tissue inside the folded μ-gripper with a size of around 200 μm (Figure 1d). We can retrieve the magnetic active μ-grippers with biopsies samples using a magnet mounted on a ureteroscope and collect the tissue samples for histopathology and genomic studies (Figure 1c, d, and e). Using an external magnetic field, we can also remotely steer the μ-grippers to targeted sites. Our proof-of-concept biopsy studies using ex vivo tissues and organs suggest that the μ-grippers effectively penetrated and collected adequate urothelial tissue for histopathology studies. This functionality contrasts with the ureteroscope biopsy baskets and forceps, which have limited maneuverability and sampling time in the upper urinary tract due to size limits. We highlight that the hundreds of μ-grippers can be fabricated and operated simultaneously to conduct distributed and randomized biopsies due to their significantly smaller size and negligible mucosa damage. We anticipate that μ-grippers can revolutionize urinary tract tissue sampling by addressing present-day inadequacies in the biopsy. They can enable uniformly distributed and representative tissue sampling to detect small, low-grade, and hard-to-reach lesions early.


## Results and Discussions

### Design and Fabrication of Biopsy μ-grippers
  We designed the μ-grippers based on previously published protocols with some modifications.[12,16] Briefly, the μ-grippers are inspired by the joints of insects, in which each arm of the μ-gripper consists of three foldable hinges and three rigid panels. While folding, the sharp tip of the μ-gripper arm is positioned at an angle towards the tissue, allowing for better tissue penetration. After folding, each arm turns into a claw with a hook at the tip pressed against the grasped object for better tissue gripping.[39] Each μ-gripper has six arms and was prepared as a 2D star-shaped device with a size of 717 μm. While folding, we estimate based on a prior study, that each μ-gripper arm generates around 5 μN of force. This force is applied on the sharp tapered tip, resulting in a high pressure that allows the arm to penetrate the tissue.[40] After self-folding, the μ-grippers turn into a 3D hexagonal cage-like structure with a size of around 200 μm that captures biopsies inside. We selected this size so that the μ-grippers are small enough to pass through ureteroscopic catheters and move freely in the ureter (3 – 4 mm inner diameter in adult humans) but remain visible under the ureteroscope.

  The μ-grippers comprise a three-layer thin film stack, which includes the stress layer, panel layer, and thermo-responsive layer, as shown in Figure 2a. A differentially stressed bilayer composed of chromium (Cr; high stress) and gold (Au; neutral stress) stores the energy to power the actuation of foldable hinges. The rigid panel segments of the μ-grippers consist of a thick nickel (Ni) and Au layer. The ferromagnetic properties of Ni allow the retrieval of μ-grippers using magnets and navigating them through an external magnetic field. The inert Au coating provides better biocompatibility. We strategically arranged the non-foldable panels and foldable hinges based on origami design principles so that the μ-grippers folded into the desired shape (Figure 2c). A paraffin wax thermo-responsive layer is coated atop to constrain the differential stress in the hinges after release from the substrate. This thin wax layer keeps the μ-grippers flat until the wax is softened by body temperature. We note that all the materials used to fabricate the μ-grippers are generally considered inert and safe in their elemental and alloy forms and have been widely used in urinary tract implants and biomedical devices.[41] We discussed the materials’ safety of μ-grippers in detail in Supporting Note S1.

  We fabricated the μ-grippers using conventional multilayer planar microfabrication. Figure 2b shows a small piece of as-fabricated μ-grippers on a silicon wafer coated with copper (Cu) sacrificial layer. We patterned each layer using photolithography and a computer-aided drawing (CAD) photomask, followed by thin film deposition using thermal evaporation, electroplating, and spinning coating. The fabrication workflow is detailed in the experimental section and Figure S1 and utilizes three photomasks. Due to their submillimeter size, we can accommodate 2,556 μ-grippers on a 3-inch (7.62 cm) silicon wafer. This number can be scaled to about forty thousand per wafer if the fabrication is done on a 12-inch diameter wafer, commonly used in the modern semiconductor industry. Such parallel fabrication can reduce costs.

  After fabrication, we released the μ-grippers from the silicon wafer by dissolving the copper sacrificial layer. Then, we cleaned the μ-grippers in DI water and stored them in a refrigerator (4 °C). When deployed from the cold state, we observed that the μ-gripper arms activated within a few minutes once warmed with the physiological temperature (~ 37 °C, Figure 2e to f). The thickness and type of wax layer offer some control over the actuation time based on its thermal conduction and softening temperature and can be tuned to ensure that the μ-grippers reach the targeted region without premature actuation. Our previous research studied the effect of wax thickness and type on folding.[11,18] Increasing the wax thickness slows the folding rate and decreases the fold angle. A thicker wax layer requires more time to reach temperature equilibrium with body temperature and provides greater mechanical resistance to folding stress. Using wax with a higher melting point at the same thickness will result in a decreased folding angle. Wax with higher melting points has a higher Young’s modulus than those with lower melting points at the same temperature, offering more resistance to folding. These trends depend on deposition methods and can be tuned for specific applications.


### Tissue Gripping Tests on Ex Vivo Urothelial Tissues
  We evaluated the tissue gripping performance of μ-grippers on ex vivo porcine urothelial tissues isolated from the ureter and renal pelvis. We placed μ-grippers on the urothelial tissues that were cut and laid flat on a Petri dish. In Figure 3a, standard cup ureteroscopic biopsy forceps (Piranha™, Boston Scientific) were held next to μ-grippers on urothelial tissues. This photo shows the distinct size between the two biopsy devices in that one μ-gripper is around five times smaller than the jaws of conventional biopsy forceps. After we deployed the μ-grippers on the tissue samples (Figure 2b), we incubated the samples at 37 °C for about 10 minutes to mimic physiological temperature. A small amount of saline covers the tissue samples to keep the moisture and stimulate body conditions. As shown in Figure 3c, all μ-grippers self-folded after incubation. We observed that the μ-grippers landed facing down the tissue, and their arms were inserted into the urothelial tissues, gripping a cluster of urothelial tissue within the μ-grippers, resembling the gripping action of biopsy forceps (Figure 3d). In the zoom-in microscopic photos in Figures 3e and f, we can better visualize the penetration of the μ-gripper arm into the tissue, and the insertion lines are marked with dashed lines in the photos.


### Delivery of μ-grippers Through Ureteral Catheters
  In clinical practice, μ-grippers must be delivered to the pathologic sites within the upper urinary tract for biopsy procedures. To enable deployment in deeper regions and reduce loss, we utilized a previously developed pneumatic-controlled μ-grippers delivery system[42] with standard ureteral catheters commonly used in clinics for accessing the upper urinary tract. As shown in Figures 4a and b, the delivery process involves storing the μ-grippers in a vial with a small volume of saline. Then, a pressurized gas, regulated by a Microfluidic Flow Control System (MFCS operated at 12 psi), is applied to the saline’s surface, propelling the μ-grippers through a ureteral catheter to the targeted sites (Supporting Movie 1).

  We evaluated the efficiency of delivering μ-grippers using three types of ureteral catheters that are frequently utilized for upper urinary tract access: a 5Fr open-end ureteral catheter, a 5Fr Imager™ II urology torque catheter, and a 10Fr dual lumen ureteral catheter. These catheters differ in channel size, length, and shape, as illustrated in Figure 4c. Despite the narrowest channel having a diameter of only 1 mm, it could still accommodate the passage of our 717 μm μ-grippers. For each test, we used approximately 200 μ-grippers passing through each catheter type under consistent delivery pressure, and we counted the number of μ-grippers before and after to determine the passage rates.

  The results indicated that all catheter types achieved about 80% or higher passage rates, with no significant statistical difference. The dual-lumen ureteral catheter demonstrated the highest passage rate at 88%, attributed to its larger channel size (1.27 mm) and shorter length (54 cm). Conversely, the Imager™ II catheter had the lowest passage rate at 79.5% due to its curved tip and longer length (65 cm), which presents more resistance to the μ-grippers. The straight open-end catheter also showed a passage rate of 87% despite its 70 cm length. We observed that most delivery failures were due to μ-grippers adhering to the catheter walls or remaining in the vial. It is important to note that the catheters tested are on the smaller end of the spectrum than those typically used in clinical settings, which can have outer diameters of up to 10 Fr. We anticipate that the passage rate could be further improved using larger catheters.


### Magnetically Controlled Multimodal Motion and Biopsy Transportation
  Other than deploying the μ-grippers through ureteral catheters, the ferromagnetic Ni panel layer of μ-grippers allows us to manipulate them remotely via an external magnetic field. By applying varying magnetic fields, these μ-grippers can perform a range of motions, such as moving, tumbling, and tearing, as depicted in Figure 5a.

  Our demonstrations on ex vivo pig urothelial tissue highlight the diverse magnetic locomotion capabilities of μ-grippers (Supporting Movie 2), controlled by a hand-held magnet positioned about 5 cm from the tissue samples. For instance, in Figure 5b, a non-folded μ-grippers placed on urothelial tissue submerged in saline was moved using a magnet moving in linear motion. This μ-gripper reached an average speed of 7.5 mm/s, ten times its body length. In another experiment shown in Figure 5c, rotating the magnet beneath the μ-gripper induced a tumbling motion on the tissue surface, resulting in a slight displacement at a much slower speed of 0.03 mm/s. Although slower, the tumbling motion enables the μ-grippers to navigate around any uneven protrusions on the tissue surface, unlike linear motion. Additionally, we demonstrated the ability to control an actuated μ-gripper, which grips urothelial tissue to tear it and transport it for biopsy purposes. This capability is shown in Figure S2, where a μ-gripper, controlled by a moving magnet at a distance, pulls on the urothelial tissue. In Figure 5d, a μ-gripper, along with a cluster of urothelial tissue, is moved by a linearly moving magnet at a slower speed of 0.7 mm/s due to the increased drag caused by the tissue cluster.

  As previously reported, more sophisticated and precise control over the magnetic locomotion of μ-grippers is achievable using a multi-axis magnetic coil system, including feedback and haptic control.[43,44] By integrating a magnetic control system with an imaging system, we anticipate precise, wireless, and non-invasive deployment and biopsy of μ-grippers in the urinary system.[44]


### Biopsy Studies Using μ-grippers in Ex Vivo Organs
  After confirming the tissue gripping performance of μ-grippers on flat ex vivo urothelial tissue and demonstrating their deployment using catheters and a magnetic field, we further showcased their biopsy capability in ex vivo upper urinary tract organs such as the ureter, maintaining its original tubular shape to mimic physiological conditions better. As shown in Figure 6a, we infused the μ-grippers into a section of ex vivo pig ureter stained with blue tissue marking dye. The ureter samples with μ-grippers were kept in pre-warmed saline and incubated at 37 °C for around 10 minutes. After incubation, Subsequently, we incised one of the ureter samples and examined the μ-grippers. We observed the μ-grippers were actuated and gripped on the ureter wall.

  We mounted a cylinder magnet rod with a 1/16-inch (1.58 mm) diameter on a catheter to retrieve the biopsies by inserting it into the tubular ureter. By pressing the rod against the ureter wall and moving it back and forth, we used the ferromagnetic properties of the Ni panel to collect μ-grippers with blue-stained tissue samples to the rod, as depicted in Figure 6c. The μ-grippers and the biopsy samples were then carefully removed from the magnet. We observed that most μ-grippers contained a tissue cluster (Figure 6d), and the urothelial cells were visible under an inverted microscope at higher magnification (Figure S3). Figure 6e illustrates a single μ-gripper clasping a cluster of blue-stained tissue. We further stained the μ-gripper sample with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) to enhance cell visibility in the biopsy. We examined it under UV light (Figure 6f), revealing cell nuclei as blue fluorescent ovals against darker cytoplasm and extracellular matrix background. The abundant nuclei suggest that the μ-grippers capture adequate tissue samples for histopathological and cytological analysis.

  Subsequently, we fixed the biopsies and prepared them into histological slides stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and GATA binding protein 3 (GATA3) immunohistochemical staining. The H&E-stained section (Figure 6g) displayed urothelial cells with clearly visible nuclei and eosinophilic cytoplasm. Additionally, some blue staining from the tissue marking dye applied earlier on the surface of the ureter lumen was observable, indicating that the μ-grippers penetrated the superficial umbrella cells (initially stained blue by tissue marking dye) and reached the deeper intermediate cells (initially not stained blue, later stained pink by H&E). This dual staining confirms the μ-grippers’ capability to access multiple layers of urothelial cells. Furthermore, these cells’ morphology and nucleus-to-cytoplasm ratio align with cells in the transitional epithelium of the mammalian urinary tract reported in other studies.[46–48] We note that the H&E slides here only showed the tissue cluster obtained by one μ-gripper, and there is an inherent trade-off between the size of the biopsy tool and the biopsy sample size. Hence, small tools such as the μ-grippers can only retrieve small quantities of tissue. Yet in a biopsy procedure, hundreds of μ-grippers could potentially be delivered and retrieved to obtain tissue samples with similar overall size compared to the traditional biopsy tools (See Supporting Note S3).

  The GATA3-stained section (Figure 6h) showed variable nuclear expression. GATA3 expression is typical for UTUC,[49,50] but GATA3 is also expressed in healthy tissues, including benign urothelial cells,[51,52] as verified by the pig gene ATLAS.[53] This result confirms the μ-gripper’s biopsy procured urothelial tissue, demonstrating its effectiveness in collecting diagnostic-quality samples.

  Similarly, we infused μ-grippers into the renal pelvis of ex vivo porcine kidneys and incubated them until they reached 37 °C and waited for around 10 minutes. We incised the kidneys and observed μ-grippers gripped on the renal pelvis urothelial tissue (Figure S4).


### Comparison of μ-grippers Statistical Biopsy Performance with Existing Tools
  With the promising results above, we developed a statistical model (detailed in Supporting Note S2) to compare the effectiveness of commonly used upper urinary tract biopsy tools with our μ-grippers. The aim was to simulate the probability of successfully collecting tissue from an invisible lesion ranging from 0.1 to 20 mm within a ureter, typically the size range for UTUC lesions.[33]

  As depicted in Figure 7a, the model ureter had a diameter of 3 mm, mirroring the average size of an adult human ureter. Building upon our previous findings, we assumed that one μ-gripper could capture a tissue cluster equivalent to the μ-gripper (0.72 mm). We introduced a specific number of μ-grippers in the simulation, assuming random distribution. In the simulation, we also assumed that 50% landed on their working side based on our μ-grippers ‘good landing’ tests shown in Figure S7, where 51.1% of μ-grippers landed on their working side when deployed on ex vivo urothelial tissue. We note that we can further employ a bidirectional folding design to improve the functional rate of the biopsy μ-grippers.[12] For 50% of μ-grippers landed on their working side, we further assume that 80% were successfully sampled and retrieved. This estimation is based on our previous study using similar μ-grippers conducting biopsies in bile ducts, which had a retrieval of around 95%.[16] Overall, we assumed a 50% failure rate for ‘incorrect landing’, and a 20% failure rate for gripping and retrieving, resulting in a cumulative failure rate of 60%, meaning only 40% of the μ-grippers in the simulation collect the biopsies.

  We leveraged data from a multi-institutional clinical study for the existing upper urinary tract biopsy tools.[34] This study reported biopsy sizes obtained by the three most commonly used tools: standard cup biopsy forceps (Piranha™, Boston Scientific), backloaded cup biopsy forceps (BIGopsy®, Cook Medical Inc.), and Nitinol basket (2.4Fr NCircle®, Cook Medical Inc.), as illustrated in Figure 7b. These tools yielded biopsies with diameters of 2.4 mm, 4.1 mm, and 4.3 mm, respectively. We performed three biopsies using the existing tools for each simulated biopsy procedure based on the average number of biopsies conducted in clinical settings.

  We simulated two scenarios. Firstly, in Figure 7c, we simulated a targeted biopsy from a 5 cm section of the ureter using different tools. The sampling success rates using μ-grippers surpassed those of traditional tools at all points and rose rapidly with increasing lesion size. When detecting a 2 mm lesion, 200 and 400 μ-grippers exhibited success rates of 59.78% and 83.83%, respectively, while all traditional tools achieved less than 15% success rates. For lesions larger than 6.2 mm and 4.1 mm, 200 and 400 μ-grippers achieved success rates exceeding 99%.

  In the second scenario plotted in Figure 7d, we simulated a comprehensive statistical biopsy across the entire length of the ureter (averaging 25 cm in adult humans).[54] Once again, the sampling success rates using μ-grippers outperformed traditional tools consistently. As the detection range extended to cover the entire length, the success rates of conventional tools decreased significantly compared to the 5 cm section scenario. A lesion must reach 16.4 mm for 200 μ-grippers to detect it with a probability exceeding 99%. We could increase the number of μ-grippers to 1,600 to enhance the statistical biopsy performance, thereby reducing the detectable lesion size to 4.5 mm with a 99% probability.


## Conclusions
  In this work, we demonstrated the capabilities of untethered μ-grippers in addressing the challenges of current diagnostic practices for diseases affecting the upper urinary tract, notably UTUC. The small size of the μ-grippers, combined with their autonomous activation by body temperature and magnetic control, enables precise and effective tissue sampling from areas typically inaccessible with conventional tools. Our ex vivo studies showcased the μ-grippers’ ability to navigate the upper urinary tract, execute targeted biopsies, and retrieve tissue samples for histopathological examination. We utilized a statistical model to highlight the μ-grippers’ exceptional performance in identifying small and previously undetectable lesions within the ureter. This capability is vital for the early diagnosis and precise staging of conditions like UTUC, significantly influencing treatment strategies and enhancing patient care.

  As we continue to refine the μ-grippers for clinical application, our future efforts will be directed towards in vivo studies to evaluate the μ-grippers in realistic settings, the development of μ-grippers using bioresorbable materials for improved safety,[55,56] and the enhancement of the deployment system to increase the efficiency of statistical biopsies. Furthermore, we aim to combine the μ-grippers with real-time imaging technologies such as MRI and ultrasound to boost their precision and control during procedures.[43,44] We also note previous studies incorporating drug-delivery patches on the μ-grippers (theragrippers) to enable the sustained release of medications. Such advances could be directed to targeted and sustained drug release within the urinary tract.[11,12] Through these advancements, we believe the μ-grippers have the potential to be translated into practice for diagnostics and therapeutics for various medical conditions.


## Experimental Section

### Biopsy μ-grippers Fabrication
  The fabrication methods of urinary tract biopsy μ-grippers have been adapted from our previous work[12,16] with some modifications. We fabricated μ-grippers on 3-inch (7.62 cm) silicon wafers utilizing planar multilayer microfabrication techniques, as depicted in Figure S1. Initially, we deposited a Cu sacrificial layer of 300 nm thickness, with a 20 nm Cr adhesion layer beneath, through thermal evaporation.

  The μ-grippers used in this study consist of three components: stress layer, panel layer, and trigger layer. We defined the pattern of each layer on the spin-coated photoresist with the aid of CAD-designed photomasks printed by Fineline Imaging (Colorado Springs, CO). For the stress layer, we employed an S1813 photoresist (Microposit, Kayaku) and deposited the layer assembly by thermally evaporating a 60 nm Cr layer followed by a 100 nm Au layer. Subsequently, we patterned the panel layer with SPR220 photoresist (Megaposit, Kayaku). We electroplated the rigid panels with 3 μm of nickel (Ni) and 0.3 μm of Au using nickel sulfamate and gold sulfite solutions from Technic. For the trigger layer, a further step of SPR220 photopatterning is employed, onto which we spin-coated 10 μm of paraffin wax (melting point of 53–58 °C, Sigma-Aldrich) as the trigger layer on the μ-grippers. Following wax deposition, we allowed at least two hours for optimal adhesion before removing any excess photoresist. Finally, we released the μ-grippers from the wafer by dissolving the Cu sacrificial layer in a commercial copper etchant (copper etchant BTP, Transene), which selectively removed the Cu while leaving the rest of the μ-grippers intact. We then thoroughly rinsed the devices in DI water to eliminate any remaining etchant and stored them at 4 °C until needed for deployment.


### In Vitro Actuation of the μ-grippers
  The μ-grippers were typically placed into a glass Petri dish with saline or PBS buffer (pH = 7.4) and heated using a hotplate or incubator to 37 °C to trigger the closing of the μ-grippers. The closure speed is affected by the time required for thermal equilibration, such that the wax is heated to 37 °C to soften it. Hence, pre-warmed solutions can trigger the closing of the μ-grippers faster. We note that waxes are commercially available, which soften at higher temperatures such that μ-grippers could be designed to actuate on-demand using remote wireless heating such as by ultrasound, MR, or inductive coupling techniques.


### Preparation of the Porcine Upper Urinary Tract Samples
  We obtained porcine upper urinary tracts freshly on the day of the experiment from a local slaughterhouse (John Brown General & Butchery, Baltimore, Maryland). We kept the urinary tracts on an ice water bath before use. We measured and cut the ureter to the desired length and removed excess fat around the ureter using a scalpel to obtain smooth ureters (Figure S5). Similarly, we removed and discarded the excess tissue surrounding it to isolate renal pelvis tissue. When needed, we cut open the ureter or renal pelvis using dissecting scissors to expose the urothelial tissue. The isolated tissues were preserved in 0.9% saline and were tested within 12 hours of receiving.


### Ex Vivo Urothelial Tissue Gripping Tests
  We placed μ-grippers in saline on isolated and cut-flat urothelial tissues from the ureter and renal pelvis in a Petri dish. Then, we incubated the tissue samples at 37 °C for around 10 minutes to trigger the self-folding of the μ-grippers. The tissue samples with μ-grippers were imaged using a stereomicroscope (AZ100, Nikon) to check the urothelial tissue penetration.


### μ-grippers Deployment Passage Tests Through Ureteral Catheters
  To test the μ-grippers passage rate when being deployed using standard ureteral catheters. We tested three different catheters manufactured by Boston Scientific (Boston, MA): 5Fr open-end ureteral catheter, 5Fr Imager™ II urology torque catheter, and 10Fr dual lumen ureteral catheter. We removed the connector at the catheter’s rear end, exposed the tube, and attached the tube to a Microfluidic Flow Control System (MFCS-100 1C, Fluigent), as shown in Figure S6.

  We first counted around 200 μ-grippers for each test and noted the initial number as N0. We then transfer the μ-grippers into the MFCS vial with around 1.5 mL saline. A small amount of μ-grippers got lost during transfer due to the adhesion to the pipette. We count that and note that number as NL. We placed the catheter tube around 1 mm above the μ-grippers (Figure 4b) and used a pressure of 12 psi to deliver the μ-grippers through the catheters. Lastly, we collected the μ-grippers at the insertion end of the catheter and counted them using a microscope. We note the delivered amount as NP. We calculated the μ-grippers catheter passage percentage as P = NP / (N0 −NL) × 100.


### Good landing rate test
  To test the ‘good landing’ rate of μ-grippers, which they landed on their working side after deployment. We delivered around 40 to 80 μ-grippers to a piece of incised and flattened urothelial tissue using the MFCS delivery system. We imaged the samples using a microscope and counted the number of μ-grippers landed on the working side and back side manually using ImageJ.


### Magnetic Locomotion Demonstration of μ-grippers
  To demonstrate the magnetically controlled moving and tumbling of a μ-gripper, we place a μ-gripper on a piece of flattened porcine renal pelvis tissue. We covered the tissue with a small amount of saline to mimic the in vivo environment. We hand-held a neodymium magnet disc (D43, K&J Magnetics Inc, Jamison, PA) around 5 cm away from the μ-gripper and moved or rotate at a steady rate to remotely control the motion of the μ-gripper.

  For the magnetically controlled urothelial tissue tearing and transport demonstration, we first dissected the renal pelvis into small pieces and placed μ-grippers on them. We incubated these samples at 37 °C for approximately 10 minutes to actuate the μ-grippers. Then, we selected samples with a μ-gripper gripped on a cluster of urothelial tissue. The tissue sample is kept in saline during the test. We hand-held a neodymium magnet disc (D43, K&J Magnetics Inc, Jamison, PA) around 5 cm away from the μ-gripper. We moved the magnet steadily to remotely control the μ-grippe to pull and transport the tissue cluster.

  We recorded all the tests using a stereomicroscope (AZ100, Nikon) under white light conditions. We used an open-source video analysis software, Tracker (Ver. 6), to manually mark the trajectory of the μ-gripper and calculate the speed.


### Deployment of Biopsy μ-grippers to Ex Vivo Organs
  We prepared ureter samples with lengths of around 5 cm and infused them with blue CDI’s® tissue mark dye (Cancer Diagnostic Inc.) to stain the inner wall of the ureter samples. We allowed the dye to bind with the specimens for 5 minutes at room temperature and used saline to rinse the excess dye. Using a pipette, we deployed μ-grippers in saline into the stained ureters’ lumen. Then, we incubated the ureter samples at 37 °C for around 10 minutes to trigger the self-folding of the biopsy μ-grippers. The ureter samples were immersed in pre-warmed saline during the incubation. After incubation, we cut open one of the ureter samples using dissecting scissors to check the urothelial tissue penetration using a stereomicroscope (AZ100, Nikon).

  Similarly, we deployed μ-grippers into the renal pelvis of a whole porcine kidney by pipetting them from the ureter opening of the kidney. The kidneys were then incubated until the tissue temperature reached 37 °C and dissected to check the μ-grippers tissue gripping performance.


### Retrieval of μ-grippers From Ex Vivo Ureter
  We mounted a 1/16-inch-diameter neodymium cylinder magnet (D1X0, K&J Magnetics Inc, Jamison, PA) on a catheter to retrieve the actuated μ-grippers deployed in the ureter samples. We wrapped the cylinder magnet with a plastic film to allow the separation of the μ-grippers and biopsies with the magnet. We inserted the magnet-mounted catheter into the ureter samples and moved it against the ureter wall. We repeated the retrieval procedure with fresh magnetic cylinders until the catheter returned with no retrieved μ-grippers. We peeled off the plastic film from the magnet and resin the μ-grippers with tissue samples off from the film and collected them in falcon tubes for further characterization.


### Biopsies Imaging and Histopathological Analysis
  To obtain Figure 6c–e, we imaged the retrieved μ-grippers with biopsies by stereomicroscope (AZ100, Nikon) under white light conditions. We prepared a microscope slide with μ-grippers with biopsies for Figure S3 and imaged it with an inverted microscope (TS100, Nikon). To obtain Figure 6f, we stained a group of retrieved μ-grippers with 300 nM DAPI and imaged the sample under UV light using a stereomicroscope (AZ100, Nikon).

  To obtain Figure 6g and h, we fixed the retrieved tissues with 10% formalin (Sigma-Aldrich), embedded them in paraffin wax, and then sectioned them into 2–3-μm slices. We sent the tissue slices to the Johns Hopkins Pathology Department for H&E and GATA3 immunohistochemical staining. We imaged the stained slices with a microscope (ZEISS, Axio Vert A1).


### Statistical Analysis
  All experiments involved statistical data containing at least three samples. We performed statistical analysis using the student’s t-test using GraphPad Prism 9, and two-tailed p-values were used. Differences were labeled as ns if not significant. Data were shown as * if p < 0.05, ** if p < 0.01, and *** if p < 0.001.
