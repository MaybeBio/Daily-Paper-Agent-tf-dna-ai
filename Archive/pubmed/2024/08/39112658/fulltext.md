# Unveiling novel insights into human IL-6 − IL-6R interaction sites through 3D computer-guided docking and systematic site mutagenesis


## Abstract
  The cytokine interleukin-6 (IL-6) plays a crucial role in autoimmune and inflammatory diseases. Understanding the precise mechanism of IL-6 interaction at the amino acid level is essential to develop IL-6-inhibiting compounds. In this study, we employed computer-guided drug design tools to predict the key residues that are involved in the interaction between IL-6 and its receptor IL-6R. Subsequently, we generated IL-6 mutants and evaluated their binding affinity to IL-6R and the IL-6R − gp130 complex, as well as monitoring their biological activities. Our findings revealed that the R167A mutant exhibited increased affinity for IL-6R, leading to enhanced binding to IL-6R − gp130 complex and subsequently elevated intracellular phosphorylation of STAT3 in effector cells. On the other hand, although E171A reduced its affinity for IL-6R, it displayed stronger binding to the IL-6R − gp130 complex, thereby enhancing its biological activity. Furthermore, we identified the importance of R178 and R181 for the precise recognition of IL-6 by IL-6R. Mutants R181A/V failed to bind to IL-6R, while maintaining an affinity for the IL-6 − gp130 complex. Additionally, deletion of the D helix resulted in complete loss of IL-6 binding affinity for IL-6R. Overall, this study provides valuable insights into the binding mechanism of IL-6 and establishes a solid foundation for future design of novel IL-6 inhibitors.


## Introduction
  Human interleukin-6 (hIL-6) is a pleiotropic cytokine, and has diverse physiological functions such as regulating metabolism and the proliferation and differentiation of immune cells1,2. The abnormal activation or dysfunction of the hIL-6 signaling pathway are closely related to a variety of diseases, such as autoimmune diseases, chronic inflammation, malignant tumors2. In addition, the abnormal expression of hIL-6 also substantially contributes to the development of cytokine storm syndrome (CSS) in corona virus disease 2019 (COVID-19)3,4, as well as the severe or life-threatening CSS induced by chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cell therapy5. Moreover, hIL-6 blockade abrogates immunotherapy toxicity and promotes tumor immunity1,6. For example, tocilizumab, an anti-IL-6R monoclonal antibody, has been approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of CSS induced by CAR-T cell therapy5,7.

  hIL-6 consists of 183 amino acids with four antiparallel α Helix. The biological activity of hIL-6 is induced through two receptors: the binding receptor hIL-6R (GP80/CD126) and the signal transduction receptor gp130 (CD130). hIL-6 forms a heterohexamer complex with hIL-6R and gp130 to perform its biological functions, which consists of two molecules of hIL-6, hIL-6R, and gp130 each8. hIL-6 has three binding sites, namely sites I–III. Site I binds to hIL-6R including the C-terminal region of helix D, and a region that encompasses the C-terminal part of the AB-loop/N-terminal part of helix B. Site II binds to gp130 at the elbow formed by the D2 and D3 domains, which are composed of residues from helix A and helix C, and results in the creation of an intermediate trimolecular recognition complex. Site III interacts with the gp130 D1 domain, which ultimately leads to the formation of a functional hexameric complex including and mainly includes residues from the CD-loop/N-terminal end of helix D8.

  In addition to IL-6, there are several other cytokines that can bind to IL-6Rα, including CNTF, the IL-27 subunit IL-27p28 (also known as IL-30), IL-27p28 fused with cytokine-like factors, and human herpes virus 8 IL-6 (vIL-6, an IL-6 analog with approximately 25% sequence identity to mammalian IL-6)9–11. gp130 is a signaling receptor that is shared by IL-6 cytokine family, which includes IL-6, ciliary neurotrophic factor (CNTF), cardiotrophic-like cytokine 1 (CLCF1), leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF), oncostatin M (OSM), cardiotrophin-1 (CT-1), IL-11, IL-27, IL-35, and IL-3912.

  hIL-6 signaling has classical, trans signaling and trans presentation signaling pathways, and all of which are mediated by gp130, which, after the formation of gp130 homodimers, transduces signals via the Janus kinase (JAK)/signal transducer and activator of transduction 3 (STAT3), RAS/mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), PI3K, Yap1, and other intracellular signaling pathways2. Studies have shown that the blockade of hIL-6, hIL-6R, and each of the key molecules in the intracellular signaling pathways may be beneficial for the treatment of hIL-6-related diseases, this includes the blockade of targets such as hIL-6, hIL-6R, hIL-6 − hIL-6R complex, gp130, JAKs, and STAT32,13.

  The formation of the hIL-6 − hIL-6R complex is crucial for the subsequent assembly of hexameric signaling complexes. It is of utmost importance to determine the specific residues that form the contact surface and investigate the underlying interactions that facilitate the binding of hIL-6 with hIL-6R. While there have been numerous research reports on the structure and function of hIL-6, particularly regarding key residues in the C-terminal region, there still exist contradictory findings and debates regarding the functionality of some critical amino acid positions (Table S1)14–24. Therefore, further systematic studies investigating hIL-6 mutations are necessary to advance our current understanding of the binding interface within the hIL-6 − hIL-6R complex.

  In this study, based on the results of homology modeling as well as docking between hIL-6 and hIL-6R, the recognition mode of hIL-6 and hIL-6R was dynamically discriminated through mechanical optimization, kinetic simulation, and the alteration of the accessibility surface area, and the apparent electrostatic distribution was used to theoretically identify the key residues of hIL-6. A series of hIL-6 mutants were designed and expressed, including the elimination of both positive and negative charges, the elimination of hydrogen bonds, reversing the charges, multiple point mutations, and fragment deletions. After that, the affinity of the hIL-6 mutant series with hIL-6R, the affinity of the hIL-6 − hIL-6R complex with gp130, and the biological activity were further investigated. Our data have substantially enhanced the current understanding of the mechanisms underlying the interaction between hIL-6 and hIL-6R, as well as the formation of the hIL-6 − hIL-6R − gp130 complex. Moreover, our study has the potential to offer valuable structural insights that can enhance the design of small molecule blockers, blocking peptides, hIL-6 mutant antagonists and novel antibodies for future research and therapeutic interventions.


## Results

### 3D theoretical structure of hIL-6 − hIL-6R complex and the key amino acids in hIL-6 identified by hIL-6R
  To deeply analyze the intermolecular binding energies and modes between hIL-6 and hIL-6R at the molecular level, this study employed appropriate molecular force field parameters and molecular dynamics optimization techniques. Using the crystal structures of hIL-6 (PDB code: 1il6)25 and hIL-6R (PDB code: 1N26)26 as model, the 3D theoretical structures of hIL-6 and hIL-6R were obtained dealing with Discover_3 program under CVFF force-filed (Insight II 2000, MSI, San Diego) as shown in Fig. 1A, B. Subsequently, the spatial complex structures of hIL-6 and hIL-6R were constructed and optimized using the same program and force field, drawing from the crystal structure of their complex (PDB code: 1p9m)8. According to the 3D theoretical complex structure of the hIL-6 and hIL-6R proteins as shown in Fig. 1C, the key amino acids in the IL-6 binding with IL-6R were predicted using the distance geometry method and computer graphics technology. The distribution of the key residues was located within the complex structure, as shown in Fig. 1D. Based on the van der Waals interactions and coulombic binding activity, the interaction distance between the key amino acid residues of the parent proteins (i.e., hIL-6R and hIL-6) was defined as 6 Å. To further understand the binding between hIL-6R and hIL-6, a local map of the binding between the IL-6R and IL-6 proteins is shown in Fig. 1E. From the docking results, the crucial amino acid residues in hIL-6 that bind to hIL-6R were identified as Q174, R178, and R181. ### Mutant design based on the critical amino acids and amino acid properties
  Considering the properties of the amino acid, acidic or basic amino acids that are prone to form ionic bonds were mutated to alanine. Glutamine, which is prone to forming hydrogen bonds, was also substituted for alanine, which resulted in five single amino acid mutants (R167A, E171A, Q174A, R178A, and R181A). Some special single amino acid mutations were also designed, such as mutant with charge reversal (R178E) and one mutant with a positive charge replaced with a hydrophobic amino acid (R181V). Multiple amino acid mutants in which multiple amino acids were simultaneously replaced with alanine were also generated, which allowed for a comprehensive assessment of the effects of alanine mutations on the elimination of ionic or hydrogen bonds (R167A/E171A/Q174A/R178A/R181A). Furthermore, the binding energies between hIL-6 or its mutants and hIL-6R were calculated and compared, as listed in Fig. 1F. Considering that hIL-6R binds at the site I position of hIL-6, the site I position includes not only the D helix C-terminal end but also the C-terminal end of the AB-loop, according to previous studies27. To evaluate the contribution of the AB-loop amino acids, a D helix deletion mutant (ΔQ155–M183) was simultaneously designed. From the result of the binding energy of hIL-6 to hIL-6R (Fig. 1F), it was found that the IL-6 mutants R167A and E171A had similar binding energy to IL-6R compared to hIL-6 WT. However, the mutants Q174A, R178A, and R181A/V exhibited a higher binding energy than hIL-6 WT. Surprisingly, R178E showed the highest energy, rising from − 118.39 to − 73.99 kCal/mol. This substantial increase was attributed to the loss of electrostatic interactions between the positively charged R178 of hIL-6 and the negatively charged E278 of hIL-6R, as well as the loss of intermolecular hydrogen bonds between R178 and Q281, Y230 of hIL-6R. These findings collectively indicate that the R178E mutant has the lowest affinity for hIL-6R. Moreover, the binding energy of a multipoint mutant (R167A, E171A, Q174A, R178A, and R181A) and a D helix deletion mutant (ΔQ155–M183) could not be calculated, which suggests a loss of binding affinity to IL-6R. These results underscore the critical role of specific amino acid interactions in maintaining the high affinity binding between hIL-6 and hIL-6R.


### Backbone conformation of WT hIL-6 and its mutants
  The backbone conformation of hIL-6 and its mutants was analyzed through CD spectra that was recorded in the far-UV region (190–260 nm). The CD spectra revealed a characteristic negative band at 206 nm and a smaller peak at around 220 nm, which indicates the presence of antiparallel β-sheets and α-helices in both hIL-6 and most of its mutants (Fig. 2A). Comparing the percentage of α-helices to that of the WT hIL-6 (Fig. 2B), most mutants exhibited a high similarity in α-helical content with the WT hIL-6. However, the multipoint mutant and the D-helix deletion mutant showed slightly lower percentages of α-helices. This observation suggests that the deletion in the D-helix region may lead to structural instability, which results in a reduction in the α-helix content. Overall, the CD spectra of the hIL-6 WT and its mutants displayed similar patterns, with overlapping curves and only minor variations in the mean residue ellipticity [θ] at the characteristic peaks. ### Binding kinetics analysis by ELISA of hIL-6R to hIL-6 and its mutants
  The binding kinetics of hIL-6R to hIL-6 and its mutants were first investigated using ELISA (Fig. 3). The hIL-6 and its mutants was first coated, and the hIL-6R with the Fc tag was serially diluted and incubated before performing the color reaction using HRP-conjugated goat anti-human IgG. The coating concentrations of hIL-6 and its mutants and the concentration of the enzyme-labeled secondary antibody were optimized to allow for the generation of a complete binding curve of hIL-6 to hIL-6R. As shown in Fig. 3A, the inability of the hIL-6 mutants Q174A, R178 A/E, and R181A/V to form an efficient engagement with hIL-6R or have decreased binding capacity, which suggests that Q174, R178, and R181 are critical amino acids for hIL-6R binding to hIL-6, which is consistent with findings from previous studies8,16,18. In addition, multipoint mutants (R167A, E171A, Q174A, R178A, and R181A) also failed to bind to hIL-6R, thus demonstrating the role of these amino acids. The D helix deletion mutant (ΔQ155-M183) also failed to bind to hIL-6R, thus illustrating that although the hIL-6 site I includes the C terminus of the D helix as well as that of the AB-loop, the D helix is more important when binding to hIL-6R, such that after deletion, no efficient binding could be formed.Figure 3Binding affinity of hIL-6R to hIL-6 (mutants) as measured by ELISA. Our investigation focused on the functional characteristics of the R167A, E171A, and R178E mutants, analyzing their binding propensities to hIL-6R and the hIL-6R − gp130 complex. ## Discussion
  IL-6 has emerged as an important player in several diseases, thus highlighting its crucial role in disease pathogenesis. The dysfunction and aberrant activation of the IL-6 signaling pathway have been observed in several health disorders, including autoimmune diseases, chronic inflammation, malignant tumors, and CSS associated with COVID-19 and CAR-T cell therapy1–4. As a result, there is considerable potential for developing inhibitors that specifically target IL-6 or its key molecules within these signaling pathways, thus offering potential therapeutic benefits for IL-6 signaling pathway-related diseases1,13,31,32. Consequently, meticulous analysis and investigation of the intricate structure of the IL-6 and IL-6R interactions are essential when designing more effective IL-6 inhibitors33.

  Previous studies have suggested that IL-6 R167, which can form an ionic bond with IL-6R E302, is critical for the interaction between IL-6 and IL-6R15,16. The mutation of R167 to methionine (R167M) has been observed to significantly reduce activity in A375, XG-1, and 7TD1 cells15,16. However, our docking model of hIL-6 − hIL-6R complex (Fig. 1) suggest that the substitution of arginine with alanine at position 167 may lead to a lower binding energy to the hIL-6R interface. Furthermore, the ELISA and SPR results revealed a slight increase in the affinity of R167A for hIL-6R (Fig. 4C), whereas the affinity for hIL-6R − gp130 complex was significantly increased (Fig. 5C), along with boosted p-STAT3 activity (Figs. 6, 7). This finding contrasts with a previous study that reported a loss of binding ability to hIL-6R in the hIL-6 mutant R167M. It is possible that the introduction of methionine to R167 may have affected the structure of hIL-6 or caused cross-linking with other sulfur-containing groups, which led to the loss of binding ability to hIL-6R and biological activity. Interestingly, other studies have examined hIL-6 mutants at non-R167 sites with increased affinity for hIL-6R18,34–37. However, as far as we know, the substitution of alanine in hIL-6 R167 resulting in increased affinity has not been reported.

  Next, we observed that the mutant E171A exhibited a two-fold decrease in its affinity with hIL-6R compared to WT hIL-6. However, its affinity with the hIL-6R − gp130 complex was increased. Moreover, the increased STAT3 phosphorylation inducted by E171A suggests that E171A might enhance gp130 dimerization by influencing the fine conformation of the hIL-6 − hIL-6R and consequently the conformation of the hIL-6 − hIL-6R − gp130 complex. It is likely that E171A affected the conformation of hIL-6 − hIL-6R, which in turn enhanced the hIL-6 − hIL-6R − gp130 affinity and ultimately led to the increased biological activity.

  Considering the crystal structure of the hexameric hIL-6 − hIL-6R − gp130 complex (PDB code: 1p9m)8 shown in Fig. 8, it is evident that R167 and E171 are situated close to the IL-6 AB-loop. Notably, R167 has the potential to affect the interaction between IL-6 K53 (the amino acid at Site1 on the AB-Loop) and IL-6R Q9511. The strategic location of the R167–E171 region, in the near vicinity of the AB-loop region involved in the binding of IL-6R − gp130, strongly implies its crucial role in influencing the formation and functionality of the IL-6 − IL-6R − gp130 complex. Further investigations are required to fully understand the effects of R167A and E171A on biological activity.   Point mutation studies of R178, R181, M183, and F169-M183 and semi-random mutation studies of the amino acids L180-M183 revealed that positively charged arginine at positions 178 and 181 was essential for the biological activity of hIL-617,19,21,22,38. In this study, we observed a notable decrease in binding affinity to hIL-6R when both R178A and R181A mutations were introduced. Although R178A exhibited some measurable affinity (81.9 nM) for hIL-6R (Fig. 4F), R181A revealed no measurable affinity (Fig. 4H). Furthermore, the ELISA binding curves (Fig. 3A) showed that R178A had a higher affinity than R181A, which suggests that hIL-6 R181 may be more critical than R178 for the precise recognition of hIL-6 with hIL-6R. This finding is consistent with a previous study and supports the weaker biological activity observed for R181A compared to R178A19. Surprisingly, the hIL-6 R181A and R181V mutants showed no detectable affinity for IL-6R. However, they maintained their high affinity (3.2 nM and 5.6 nM, respectively, Fig. 5H, I) for the formation of the IL-6R − gp130 complex. This suggests that the hIL-6 mutation of R181 into a hydrophobic amino acid influences the hIL-6 binding to hIL-6R but has no effect on the binding to gp130. Further investigation is necessary to elucidate the underlying mechanisms. Importantly, this observation represents a novel finding not previously reported.

  As expected, the multipoint mutation (R167A, E171A, Q174A, R178A, and R181A) was not able to bind to hIL-6R in the ELISA assay. Further SPR experiments demonstrated that it did not produce any measurable binding signal with hIL-6R (Fig. 4J) nor did it form a hIL-6R − gp130 complex (Fig. 5J). These findings indicate that the mutants exhibited extremely low affinity for IL-6R, rendering them unable to form hIL-6 − hIL-6R − gp130 complex.

  While previous studies have demonstrated that the removal of only four amino acids from the C-terminal region of hIL-6 results in the complete loss of hIL-6 bioactivity and mutants that remove the 15, 26, 36, and 48 amino acids in the C-terminal also abolish activity24, the binding and functional studies of hIL-6 mutants where the entire D-helix (ΔQ155–M183) is deleted have not been investigated. Therefore, we designed a hIL-6 mutant without the D helix (ΔQ155–M183) to observe the effect of the site I AB-loop region on hIL-6R binding and biological activity. As expected, after deleting the entire D helix, the loss of affinity with hIL-6R may be related to the deletion of the hIL-6 binding sites with hIL-6R, which proves that the site I AB-loop region cannot effectively bind to hIL-6R after the deletion of the hIL-6 D helix. Although the hIL-6 Site I includes the AB-loop region, which may be included in the binding interface between hIL-6 and hIL-6R, it does not play an essential role in the binding of hIL-6 and hIL-6R. The binding of hIL-6 to hIL-6R may lead to changes in the conformation of the AB-loop region, which further promotes the binding with gp130 at site III39,40.

  Different affinity values have been reported for the binding of hIL-6 to soluble hIL-6 receptor (shIL-6R), ranging from 0.5 to 34 nM41–44. In our study, we observed an KD value of 2.23 nM for hIL-6 binding to hIL-6R, which is lower than the previously reported value of 22 nM in Baran’s work44. This difference may be attributed to the utilization of dimeric hIL-6R with Fc tag in our experimental setup. Additionally, we also tested the affinity of the monomeric His-tagged hIL-6R, which was found to be 26 nM (Fig S1), consistent with previous findings42,44. Since our primary objective was to evaluate the relative affinities of IL-6 binding to different IL-6R mutants, the incorporation of a commercial dimeric hIL-6R-Fc may not compromise the intent of our study regarding the interaction of hIL-6 and its mutants.

  In summary, this work, systematically studied the affinity of hIL-6 mutants to form complexes with hIL-6R and gp130 and found that both the affinity and conformation of the hIL-6 − IL-6R complexes affected the formation of the hIL-6 − hIL-6R − gp130 complexes and thus impacted their biological activity. Notably, we demonstrated for the first time through mutagenesis that R167 is not a key amino acid for hIL-6 − hIL-6R binding, but rather the fine conformation than the affinity of the mutants R167A and E171A binding to IL-6R resulted in the increased affinity for the gp130 complex, thus leading to increased biological activity. Furthermore, we confirmed that R178 and R181 at the C-terminal of the hIL-6 D helix participates in the precise recognition of hIL-6 and hIL-6R. Although the R181A/V mutants did not show detectable binding to hIL-6R, they exhibited a high affinity for the hIL-6R − gp130 complex, which suggests that R181 is not essential for the formation of the IL-6 − IL-6R − gp130 complexes. Overall, the systematic mutagenesis of the IL-6 D helix based on 3D computer-guided docking has enhanced the understanding of the binding of hIL-6 to hIL-6R and the formation of the hIL-6 − IL-6R − gp130 complex. While our study has elucidated certain aspects of the interaction between hIL-6 mutants and hIL-6R, it is imperative to recognize the limitations in our current understanding. Specifically, we have yet to comprehensively account for confounding variables such as post-translational modifications, cell type-specific responses, and the interplay with other signaling pathways, which may significantly modulate the biological outcomes observed. The potential for off-target effects and cross-reactivity with other receptors also warrants further investigation. Future research endeavors should prioritize comprehensive biological and functional validation studies to address these gaps. These efforts will be crucial in unraveling the intricate mechanisms governing hIL-6 signaling and in refining therapeutic strategies for associated pathologies, thereby advancing the field of cytokine biology and its clinical applications.


## Materials and methods

### Cell lines and reagents
  The human hepatocellular carcinoma cell line, HepG2 cells (ATCC No. HB-8065) were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM) culture medium (Gibco Cat. C11995500BT), supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, Cat. 10,091–148), and 1× penicillin–streptomycin (Solarbio, China Cat. P1400) in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. HEK293F cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were cultured in FreeStyle™ 293 expression medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 2 mM l-glutamine (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). The cells were maintained in a humidified atmosphere with 8% (v/v) CO2 at 37 °C. The cultures were agitated at 125 rpm on an orbital shaker. The primary antibodies, p-STAT, STAT3, and β-actin, were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology. The antibody, PE labeled anti-pSTAT3 (pY705) monoclonal antibody was purchased from BD (Cat. 612569), and horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-human IgG antibody was obtained from Southern Biotech (Cat. 2049–5).


### Computer‑guided homology modelling and molecular docking
  Considering the crystal structure of hIL-6 and hIL-6R and the complex structure of hIL-6 and hIL-6R, the 3D theoretical complex structure of hIL-6 and hIL-6R was studied under consistent valance force field (CVFF) and Charmm force field using Insight II 2000 software (MSI, San Diego). In detail, the 3D modeling structures of hIL-6 and hIL-6R were constructed using the homology module. After minimizing using the molecular dynamics method under CVFF and Charmm force field, the 3D complex structure of hIL-6 and hIL-6R was obtained according to the crystal complex structure of hIL-6 and hIL-6R. Using computer graphics and the distance geometry method, the binding domain was analyzed and point mutations of the key amino acids were performed on the modeling complex structure. The potential binding model and energy were studied and guided in the subsequent experiments.


### Plasmid construction and protein expression of hIL-6 and mutants
  hIL-6 sequence information was obtained from the Uniprot website (P05231). All mutants had an insertion of a 6×His tag at the N-terminus. Briefly, the hIL-6 and hIL-6 mutant gene sequences were synthesized. The synthesized gene sequences were digested with the endonucleases HindIII and EcoRI and then cloned into a modified eukaryotic expression vector pcDNA3.1(+) (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) to construct a universal expression vector of hIL-6 and its mutants. The constructed plasmids were verified through sequencing and extracted using a large-scale plasmid kit. The TA-293/KPM chemical transfection system (Kairui Biotech, cat. K20001/K03125) was used to transfect the protein expression vectors into HEK293F cells, which were cultured for 5 − 7 days. The culture supernatants were collected and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter. The hIL-6 wild-type (WT) and mutants R167A, E171A, Q174A, R178A, and R181A were purified using affinity chromatography and size exclusion chromatography. R167A/E171A/Q174A/R178A/R181A, R178E, and R181V were purified using affinity chromatography and desalting chromatography, D helix deletion (ΔQ155-M183) was purified using affinity chromatography, ceramic hydroxyapatite (CHT) chromatography, and desalting chromatography. The purification equipment used was the GE AKTA series purification systems. The purity of these proteins was analyzed by SDS-PAGE (Fig S2) and the concentrations were determined using the extinction coefficient method.


### Circular dichroism (CD) measurements
  To analyze the secondary structure, the CD spectra measurements were conducted using the MOS-450 spectropolarimeter (Bio-Logic, France) at the wavelength range of 190 − 260 nM. The protein samples at a concentration of 0.5 mg/mL were prepared in 10 mM phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) buffer (pH 7.4). The spectropolarimeter was set to the following parameters: a temperature of 20 °C, a 0.5 nM resolution, a 5.0 nM bandwidth, a 5 s response time, a 50 nM/min scanning speed, three accumulations, and a 1 mm cuvette path length. To ensure accurate results, the CD spectra of the buffer alone were recorded and subtracted from the raw data. The CD intensity is reported as the mean residue molar ellipticity (θ [deg × cm2 × dmol−1]). The percentage of α-helices to the WT hIL-6 was calculated using a specific formula that has been previously reported45.


### ELISA-based analysis of binding kinetics between hIL-6R and hIL-6/mutants
  The affinity differences of the hIL-6 and hIL-6 mutants with hIL-6R were assessed by ELISA. Firstly, WT hIL-6 or hIL-6 mutants were diluted to 1 μg/mL in the coating solution (carbonate buffer, Sigma Cat. C3041), and 100 μL/well was used to coat the ELISA plate, then placed at 4 °C overnight. The coating solution was washed off, and the plate was blocked with Superblock solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. 37515) at 25 °C for 1 h to prevent non-specific binding. hIL-6R with human Fc tag (Sino Biological, Beijing, China, Cat: 10398-H02H) was diluted to 0.075 − 7500 ng/mL. For the hIL-6R series dilution, 100 μL/well were added to the plate and placed at 25 °C for 1 h. After washing, HRP-conjugated goat anti-human IgG diluted to 1:100,000 was added and allowed to react for 1 h at 25 °C. The color reaction was performed by adding 100 μL of 3,3ʹ,5,5ʹ-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate to each well, and the reaction was stopped using 1 M H2SO4. The results were read according to the OD450 nm light absorption assay using a microplate reader (Molecular Device, plus 384).


### Surface plasmon resonance (SPR)-based analysis of binding kinetics between hIL-6R and hIL-6 (mutants)
  The binding kinetics of hIL-6R to rhIL-6 and mutants were characterized using SPR with Biacore™ T-200 (GE Life Sciences). Anti-human Fc antibody was coupled to activated CM5 biosensor chips (GE Life Sciences, Cat. BR100530), followed by blocking of the un-reacted groups with 1 M ethanolamine using the standard amine coupling method, as per the manufacturer’s recommendations (Anti-human Fc kit, Cytiva, Cat. BR-1005-31). The dimeric form hIL-6R with the human Fc tag (hIL-6R-Fc, Sino Biological, Beijing, China) was flown over the chip and captured by the immobilized anti-human Fc antibody. The concentration and binding time of hIL-6R-Fc were pre-optimized. A serial dilution (46.21–0.72 nM) of hIL-6 and its mutants with a His tag were injected into the SPR running buffer (10 mM HEPES,150 mM NaCl, 3 mM EDTA, 0.005% (v/v) surfactant P20, pH 7.4) at 50 μL/min. For the binding kinetics of hIL-6R to rhIL-6 and its mutants, the association reaction time was set at 120 s, and the dissociation reaction time was set at 600 s. After the reaction, the reaction was repeated in 10 mM pH 1.5 glycine HCl for 30 s. Changes in the SPR signal (response unit, RU) caused by the interaction between captured hIL-6R and hIL-6 His were detected and plotted against the time as sensorgrams. The association rates (Kon) and dissociation rates (Koff) were calculated using the 1:1 binding model in the Biacore T-200 Evaluation Software version 3.1 (GE Life Sciences). The equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) was calculated as the ratio of Koff/Kon.


### SPR-based analysis of binding kinetics between gp130 and hIL-6 (or mutants) − hIL-6R complex
  The assay was optimized according to the description provided in reference42. monomeric gp130 (Sino Biological, Beijing, China, Cat. 10974-HCCH1) was immobilized on CM5 sensor chips (GE Healthcare) by standard amine coupling method, as recommended by the manufacturer. After combining the fixed excess concentration of monomeric hIL-6R (His tag, Acro Biosystem, Cat. ILR-H4223) with different concentrations of hIL-6 or its mutants in 25 °C for 30 min, the mixture was flown over the chip with immobilized gp130. The hIL-6R concentration was 1000 nM at the saturated concentration level and the hIL-6 and its mutants were serially diluted with a running buffer solution from 250 to 3.9 nM in the mixture. The association reaction time was set at 180 s, and the dissociation reaction time was set at 360 s. The flow rate was set at 30 μL/min, and the reaction was regenerated for 30 s. The data fitting model and KD calculation method were the same as those of the antibody affinity section.


### Digital western blot analysis
  The method was modified according to the description given in reference46. The HepG2 cells were adjusted to 3.5 × 105 cells/mL and 1 mL of the cell suspension was added per well to six-well cell culture plates. The cells were cultured overnight and allowed to attach to the plates. At least 4 h before cytokine stimulation, the medium was exchanged to DMEM without FBS and penicillin–streptomycin. The cells were stimulated for 15 min with 50 ng/mL hIL-6 or its mutants. The cells were washed with PBS and RIPA lysis buffer (Wuhan Goodbio, China; Cat. G2002), and a complete protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Cat. 04693159001 and 04906837001) was added to the cells. The cells were lysed on ice for 15 min with constant agitation. The lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the total protein concentration was measured using the BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. 23225). The cell lysates were stored at − 80 °C. The levels of p-STAT3, STAT3, and β-actin were evaluated using a ProteinSimple Wes capillary western blot analyzer (JESS, ProteinSimple). A 1:50 dilution of antibodies in an antibody diluent was prepared (ProteinSimple kit), and 25-lane plates were used for the detection in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations.


### Human leukocyte phosphorylation assay using flow cytometry
  We optimized the method for measuring the ratio of phosphorylated cells based on a previously reported protocol30. Briefly, human leukocyte samples (Milestone Biotechnologies) were stimulated with hIL-6 or its mutants (50 ng/mL) for 15 min at 37 °C. Then, 2 mL of cold red blood cell lysing/fixation buffer (Biolegend, Cat. 422401) was added to lyse the erythrocytes and fix the phosphorylated status of STAT3, followed by incubation on ice for 1 h. After fixation, the cells were washed and permeabilized with 1 mL of cold perm buffer III (BD, Cat, 558050) for 30 min on ice. Subsequently, the cells were washed and stained for 30 min at room temperature in the dark with anti-pSTAT3 (pY705)-PE and an anti-mouse immunoglobulin G isotype-matched control (BD, Cat, 558595). The stained cells were washed twice with 2 mL of stain Buffer (BD, Cat, 554656) and analyzed by flow cytometry using the DxFLEX clinical flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter).


### Statistical analysis
  All data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical differences between groups were analyzed using student’s t-test on the Gaussian distribution or Mann–Whitney Wilcoxon on the non-Gaussian distribution. p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001).
