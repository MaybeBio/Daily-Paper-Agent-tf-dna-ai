# Key target genes related to anti-breast cancer activity of ATRA: A network pharmacology, molecular docking and experimental investigation


## Abstract
  All-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) has promising activity against breast cancer. However, the exact mechanisms of ATRA's anticancer effects remain complex and not fully understood. In this study, a network pharmacology and molecular docking approach was applied to identify key target genes related to ATRA's anti-breast cancer activity. Gene/disease enrichment analysis for predicted ATRA targets was performed using the Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery (DAVID), the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD), and the Gene Set Cancer Analysis (GSCA) database. Protein-Protein Interaction Network (PPIN) generation and analysis was conducted via Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING) and cytoscape, respectively. Cancer-associated genes were evaluated using MyGeneVenn from the CTD. Differential expression analysis was conducted using the Tumor, Normal, and Metastatic (TNM) Plot tool and the Human Protein Atlas (HPA). The Glide docking program was used to predict ligand-protein binding. Treatment response predication and clinical profile assessment were performed using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) Plotter and OncoDB databases, respectively. Cytotoxicity and gene expression were measured using MTT/fluorescent assays and Real-Time PCR, respectively. Molecular functions of ATRA targets (n = 209) included eicosanoid receptor activity and transcription factor activity. Some enriched pathways included inclusion body myositis and nuclear receptors pathways. Network analysis revealed 35 hub genes contributing to 3 modules, with 16 of them were associated with breast cancer. These genes were involved in apoptosis, cell cycle, androgen receptor pathway, and ESR-mediated signaling, among others. CCND1, ESR1, MMP9, MDM2, NCOA3, and RARA were significantly overexpressed in tumor samples. ATRA showed a high affinity towards CCND1/CDK4 and MMP9. CCND1, ESR1, and MDM2 were associated with poor treatment response and were downregulated after treatment of the breast cancer cell line with ATRA. CCND1 and ESR1 exhibited differential expression across breast cancer stages. Therefore, some part of ATRA's anti-breast cancer activity may be exerted through the CCND1/CDK4 complex.


## Introduction
  Cancer, as a global health dilemma, has a high incidence and mortality rate, threatening life expectancy [1]. Breast cancer is the second most diagnosed malignancy (11.6 % of all cancer cases) and the leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women worldwide. According to the Global Cancer Observatory (GLOBOCAN) 2022 reports, an estimated 2.3 million new cases and 666,000 deaths have been reported for female breast cancer [2].

  Breast cancer exhibits significant heterogenicity and can be subdivided into different classes based on the expression status of certain hormone receptors, including human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), progesterone receptor (PR) and estrogen receptor (ER) [3]. Breast cancers that are ER+ are managed with endocrine therapies, which are typically administered after surgery. These therapies include estrogen receptor downregulators and modulators like Fulvestrant and Tamoxifen, respectively, and aromatase inhibitors such as Letrozole. For HER2+ cancers, treatment involves HER2-targeting agents like lapatinib and trastuzumab. Also, Programmed cell death protein 1 (PD1) inhibitors, cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) 4/6 inhibitors and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) inhibitors can be administered to improve the efficiency of both HER2-targeting and endocrine therapies [3]. Tripe negative breast cancers (TNBCs), characterized by the absence of ER, PR, and HER2 expression, do not respond to hormonal or anti-HER2 targeted therapies and are associated with a poor prognosis, high rate of metastasis, and relapse [[4], [5], [6]]. The term TNBC was initially used to describe cases that were responsive only to chemotherapy. However, FDA approved Poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors like Olaparib, and immunotherapies like Atezolizumab, can now be used in combination with chemotherapy agents [3].

  Although patients with ER+ or HER2+ breast cancer may initially benefit from hormonal and targeted therapies, 30–40 % of ER+ tumors acquire resistance, and 15–20 % are intrinsically resistant to treatment [7]. Similarly, de novo or acquired treatment resistance to trastuzumab limits its clinical benefit in 27–42 % of HER2+ breast cancers [8,9]. As a result, chemotherapy is often the standard treatment for many breast cancer patients [10]. However, chemotherapy is a non-specific treatment with severe side effects, leading to a considerable reduction in patients' quality of life (QOL) [6,11]. Moreover, prolonged exposure to chemotherapeutic agents can lead to the development of drug resistance [11]. Patients with drug resistant cancers typically receive high doses of chemotherapy drugs with limited or no benefits [12]. Therefore, it is essential to explore novel, alternative, or combinational therapies to improve the clinical outcomes for breast cancer patients [13,14].

  All-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) is the active form of vitamin A extensively utilized in preclinical and clinical studies to explore its anti-breast cancer properties [15]. ATRA has been shown to be effective against breast cancer cells and can prevent mammary carcinogenesis in animal models by triggering apoptosis and inhibiting the cell-cycle progression [16]. ATRA directly or indirectly regulates the transcription of numerous genes, including those involved in proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis. It regulates gene expression mainly through several families of nuclear receptors, including retinoic acid receptors (RARs), retinoid X receptors (RXRs), and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs), as well as coregulators and polymorphic retinoic acid response elements [13,17].

  ATRA also exhibits non-transcriptional (non-genomic or extranuclear) activities, including the activation of Janus kinase/signal transducers and activators of transcription 5 (JAK/STAT5) and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathways. Despite this, the mechanisms underlying ATRA's extranuclear effects are not yet fully understood. The complexity of ATRA's activities extends beyond what was initially expected [18,19], and its exact mechanisms of action remain unclear [20]. To better understand ATRA's mechanisms, in-depth studies are required to identify new mechanisms and novel targets related to its complex functions [19]. Direct binding between ATRA and other targets has been reported; for example, molecular docking simulations indicated that ATRA could directly bind to adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) [21]. Also, ATRA could directly bind to peptidyl-prolyl cis/trans isomerase (Pin1), a protein overexpressed in most cancers, which is thought to contribute to its anti-tumor activity [20,[22], [23], [24]].

  Centritto et al. investigated cellular and molecular mechanisms determining ATRA sensitivity in breast cancer and found that RARα, a key player in the retinoid pathway, is associated with ATRA sensitivity [25]. Despite this, our literature search revealed that no study has undertaken a comprehensive exploration of potential targets of ATRA through a network pharmacology approach to gain a deep understanding of its anti-breast cancer mechanisms. Network pharmacology employs systems biology principles to identify hub genes, which are key drug targets with significant roles in regulating complex biological networks, including protein-protein interaction networks. Traditional approaches typically focus on individual molecules or specific pathways, whereas systems biology aims to integrate and understand the complexity of biological systems [26]. Molecular docking is an in silico technique to simulate the interaction between a small molecule and its targets protein at the molecular level [27,28]. The aim of this study is to identify key target genes related to ATRA's anti-breast cancer activity through network pharmacology and molecular docking analysis.


## Methods

### Drug target prediction
  ATRA target proteins were retrieved from DrugBank (go.drugbank.com/), Binding Database (www.bindingdb.org/), Swiss Target Prediction (www.swisstargetprediction.ch/), The Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD) (ctdbase.org/), and Search Tool for Interactions of Chemicals (STITCH) (stitch.embl.de/) databases using their default options. Simplified molecular input line entry system (SMILES) structure of ATRA was obtained from PubChem (pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/).


### Protein-protein interaction network (PPIN) generation and analysis
  The protein-protein interaction network of ATRA targets was produced using the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING) (https://string-db.org/) with a minimum required interaction score of 0.400 and visualized/analyzed in cytoscape (version 3.10.0). Four parameters-closeness centrality, betweenness centrality, degree and radiality-were considered for analysis, and the top 50 nodes based on these parameters were selected. Overlapped nodes were identified using Science and Research Online Plot (SRplot) (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/srplot) Venn diagram and considered as hub genes. Network modules were identified using the Molecular Complex Detection (MCODE) algorithm in cytoscape, with the following parameters: degree cutoff = 2, K-core = 2, max-depth = 100, and node score cutoff = 0.2.


### Gene enrichment analysis
  Gene ontology (Molecular function (MF), biological process (BP), cellular component (CC)) annotation and pathway analysis (Wiki pathways (WP)) for ATRA targets were performed using Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery (DAVID, https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) via functional annotation tool. Analysis result for MF, BP, CC, and WP were downloaded from annotation summary part. First, the top 15 significant (p < 0.05) terms were reported for each of the MF, BP, CC, and WP analyses. Then, among synonymous and highly overlapping terms, the most significant one was selected. Graphs were plotted using the SRplot.


### Disease enrichment analysis
  Disease enrichment analysis for ATRA targets was conducted using the Set Analyzer tool of CTD (https://ctdbase.org/tools/analyzer.go) and DAVID functional annotation tool. DAVID collects these data from Genetic Association Database (GAD) (geneticassociationdb.nih.gov/) and gene-disease associations network (DisGeNET) (https://www.disgenet.org/) platform, respectively. In DAVID, analysis results for disease enrichment were downloaded from annotation summary part. Default options were used for disease enrichment analysis in the CTD Set Analyzer tool. The top 15 significant (p < 0.05) terms were reported for each database. Then, among synonymous and highly overlapping terms, the most significant one was selected. Graphs were plotted using SRplot.


### Cancer-associated genes
  Cancer-associated genes were identified among the hub genes of ATRA targets within the PPIN using MyGeneVenn of CTD (ctdbase.org/tools/myGeneVenn.go). In the dataset selections, only genes with curated associations were selected. A filter was applied to select only genes with direct relationships (commonalities between exact terms only) with specific types of cancer. The CTD collects curated gene–disease association data from published literature and Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM), using the mim2gene file from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database. Venn diagrams were produced to compare hub genes with genes associated with different types of cancers. Initially, cancers were selected whose associated genes overlapped with at least one-third (∼12) of the hub genes. Subsequently, among cancers with the highest number of shared hub genes, those with the highest 'average degree' of shared hub genes were chosen for further validation, named as final hub genes.


### Annotation of cancer-associated genes
  The PPIN of cancer-associated genes (final hub genes) was produced and visualized as described in section 2.2. Disease enrichment analysis was conducted using the GAD diseases and DisGeNET, as described in section 2.4. Pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using KEGG, Reactome and Wiki pathways, available in DAVID, as described in section 2.3. For disease enrichment analysis, the top seven significant terms were reported for each database. Pathway enrichment analysis yielded the top five significant terms for each database. Furthermore, the activation or inhibition of cancer-related pathways/mechanisms/processes by cancer-associated genes was evaluated in gene set cancer analysis (GSCA) integrated platform (https://guolab.wchscu.cn/GSCA/#/) using breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA) data collection and single gene level analysis section (expression & pathway activity). Each cell number in the analysis reports indicates the degree of association of a specific gene with a particular pathways/mechanisms/process. A significance level of p < 0.05 was used for all analyses.


### Differential expression analysis
  Differential expression analysis of final hub genes was conducted using TNM Plot (https://tnmplot.com/analysis/) based on RNA sequencing data. The expression analysis was performed in paired tumor and adjacent normal tissues (Tumor, n = 112; Normal, n = 112) and also between tumor samples from cancer patients and normal samples from non-cancerous individuals (Tumor, n = 1097; Normal, n = 403). The results of the differential expression analysis were visualized as a violin plot. A significance level of p < 0.05 was used for analysis. TNM plot utilizes gene arrays from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) of the NCBI, or RNA-seq data from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx), Therapeutically Applicable Research to Generate Effective Treatments (TARGET), and the Cancer Genome atlas (TCGA) repositories. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining of breast carcinoma tissue sections and normal breast tissue sections were obtained from pathology and the tissue parts of the human protein atlas (HPA) (www.proteinatlas.org). Normal and cancer tissues in the HPA have been collected from anonymized paraffin embedded surgical specimens. For each gene, two stained tissue sections of carcinoma samples obtained from two different patients and one stained tissue section of normal sample obtained from a non-cancerous individual were randomly selected as examples. Staining level and intensity were also extracted along with IHC sections. In the IHC sections, brown color indicates higher expression resulting from the binding of DAB (3,3′-diaminobenzidine)-labeled antibodies to their corresponding antigens.


### Molecular docking

#### Ligand preparation
  The two-dimensional structures of ATRA and four control inhibitors corresponding to each receptor were generated and optimized using the PM3 [29] semi-empirical method in Hyperchem software (www.hypercubeusa.com). The MOL format of structures with optimized geometry was saved for subsequent analysis with the LigPrep application, which is part of the Schrödinger 2023-2 suite of software (LigPrep, Schrödinger, LLC, New York, NY, 2023). The ionization state of the compounds was determined at pH 7 ± 2 via Epik, according to Hammett and Taft methodologies (Epik, Schrödinger, LLC, New York, NY, 2023). The desalt option was set as the program's default. For each compound, thirty-two conformations were generated at pH 7 using the OPLS4 [30] force field. These acquired conformations were subsequently used in docking analysis.


#### Ligand binding site
  The binding site analysis was conducted using SiteMap (Schrödinger, LLC, New York, NY, 2023). This software identified the top five potential binding sites by default, each requiring a minimum of 15 points. Crop site maps, on the other hand, were set at a distance of 4 Å (Å) from the nearest site point. A site score above 0.80 indicated high druggability and a strong potential for drug-binding. To further validate the top-scoring sites, the binding site of the control inhibitors implemented in the original Protein Data Bank (PDB) files retrieved from Research Collaboratory for Structural Bioinformatics (RCSB), were also used to center the grid in the subsequent step.


#### Generation of grid
  To predict the binding of a ligand to a receptor, it's crucial to generate an accurate 3D boundary using Glide, version 13.2 of Mastero by Schrödinger (Glide, Schrödinger, LLC, New York, NY, 2023). Seven parameters were considered for protein preparation wizard including 1: Removing the original hydrogens and subsequently adding new hydrogens. 2: Assigning bond orders and atomic charges. 3: Removing water molecules with fewer than 3 hydrogen (H)-bonds with non-water molecules or those not contributing to ligand binding. 4: Capping the C and N terminals. 5: Establishing disulfide bonds between sulfur atoms within 3.2 Å. 6: Generating several protonation states at a neutral pH using Epik. 7: Assigning and fine-tuning H-bonds at neutral pH using PROPKA. The structure was then minimized via OPLS4 force field, and Glide was applied to establish a grid centered on the binding domain of receptors according to the top score sitemap site, with a grid box size of 32 × 32 × 32 Å.


#### Molecular interaction and docking studies
  Ligand docking was performed based on Glide protocol. Receptors, along with software-produced conformations, were passed through different filters such as Euler angles, grid-based force filed evaluation, and energy minimization via Monte Carlo simulations. Eventually, the best conformation per ligand was selected according to the docking score, and outputs were generated. Subsequently, standard precision (SP) docking output was used for extra precision (XP) docking. The docking analysis utilized four PDB files: CCND1 (PDB ID: 2w96), ESR1 (PDB ID: 7te7), MDM2 (PDB ID: 4JRG), and MMP9 (PDB ID: 6esm). Navitoclax for CCND1 (2w96), RAD101 for ESR1 (7te7) RO5313109 for MDM2 (4JRG), and Periostat for MMP9 (6esm) were used as the control ligands for comparison with ATRA.


### Treatment response prediction
  Treatment response predication analysis of key hub genes was conducted by generating receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves using ROC plotter (https://www.rocplot.org/) [31]. ROC plotter utilizes cancer datasets from GEO of NCBI to generate ROC plots [31]. ROC curves were plotted using samples from breast cancer patients undergoing different therapies: endocrine therapy (n = 64; 45 responders and 19 non-responders), Anti-HER2 therapy (n = 217; 95 responders and 122 non-responders), and chemotherapy (n = 1632; 532 responders and 1100 non-responders). Responders were defined as patients with complete pathological response to the therapy, whereas non-responders were those with residual disease after treatment. Significance level was set at p < 0.05.


### Clinical profile
  The association between abnormal expression of key hub genes and cancer clinical features was assessed using OncoDB (https://oncodb.org/). OncoDB obtains required data from the TCGA study as well as from the GTEx study. The clinical analysis section of the database was utilized to assess the association between pathological stage and gene expression via the BRCA data collection. The total number of samples was 1071, including stage 1 = 179, stage 2 = 614, stage 3 = 245, stage 4 = 20, and stage X = 13. Finally, the expression levels of genes in different stages were plotted. Significance level was set at p < 0.05.


### In vitro experiments

#### Cell viability assay
  Michigan Cancer Foundation-7 (MCF7), a breast cancer cell line, and human foreskin fibroblast (HFF), a non-cancerous cell line, were purchased from the cell bank of Iran (Pasteur Institute of Iran) and were grown in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Bio Idea Co) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gipco, USA) and 1 % antibiotic (penicillin-streptomycin, Gipco, USA).

  The concentration of ATRA used for treating cells in gene expression studies was determined using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyl-2H-tetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay. Initially, 6 × 103 cells/well were cultured in a 96 well plate. After 24 h, cells were treated with various concentrations of ATRA (∼50–266 μg/ml) dissolved in medium containing 1 % dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Also, medium containing 1 % and 5 % DMSO used as negative and positive controls, respectively. The medium in the plate was replaced with fresh medium containing 0.5 mg/ml MTT (Sigma, USA), after 24 h of incubation. Then, the medium was replaced with 75 μl DMSO (Sigma, USA) after a 4-h incubation. Subsequently, the plate was shaken for 20 min, and the absorbance was measured using a plate reader (CLARIOstar, BMG LABTECH, Germany) at 570 nm and 690 nm (reference) wavelengths. Cell viability at different ATRA concentrations was determined using the viability formula, and subsequently, the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of the cell lines was calculated from the dose-response graph.Viability(%)=OD570(Test−Blank)−OD690(Test−Blank)OD570(Control−Blank)−OD690(Control−Blank)×100

  A fluorescent live/dead cell viability assay was performed following treatment of cells (2 × 105 cells/well in six-well plates) with the IC50 of ATRA for the HFF cell line for 24 h. Cells were stained with acridine orange (AO) and propidium iodide (PI) dyes (Chemometec, Denmark). Stained cells were visualized using an inverted fluorescent microscope (KERN, Germany). Images acquired from the green and red channels were merged, where green cells indicated viability, and cells stained red, orange or yellow were considered non-viable.


#### RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis
  MCF7 and HFF cells were seeded in six-well plates (2 × 105 cells/well) and treated with the IC50 concentration of ATRA for the MCF7 cell line after attachment. After 24 h, the medium was removed, and cells were washed twice with PBS (Sigma, USA). Total RNA isolation was performed using RNX + solution (Sinaclon, Iran) following manufacturer recommendations with slight modifications. Briefly, cells were detached and lysed using 1 ml ice-cold RNX + solution per 107 cells. The cell lysate was vortexed in a microtube for 5–10 s, and then 200 μl of Chloroform per ml of lysate was added. After a 5 min incubation on ice, the microtube was centrifuged for 15 min at 12,000 rpm and 4 °C. The aqueous phase was transferred to a new microtube, and an equal volume of isopropanol was added. The sample was incubated overnight at −20 °C, then centrifuged for 15 min at 12,000 rpm and 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the RNA pellet was washed with 75 % Ethanol. Subsequently, the sample was centrifuged for 8 min at 7500 rpm and 4 °C, the ethanol was removed, and the pellet was allowed to dry. Finally, the pellet was dissolved in nuclease-free water. The integrity and purity of the extracted RNA were confirmed by 1 % agarose gel electrophoresis and by measuring absorbance at 260 nm and 280 nm wavelengths using a nanodrop spectrophotometer (DeNovix, USA).

  cDNA was synthesized using the Thermo Scientific RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). A total of 1 μg of RNA was used in a reaction volume of 20 μl, and reagents were added in a microtube in the amounts and order recommended by the kit instructions. The cDNA synthesis was performed using a thermal cycler, with the samples incubated for 5 min at 25 °C followed by 60 min at 42 °C.


#### Real time polymerase chain reaction (PCR)
  Quantitative assessment of gene expression was performed using the Corbett Research RG-6000 Real Time PCR thermocycler. Reactions were prepared using RealQ Plus 2x master mix green (Ampliqon, Denmark), cDNA and gene specific primers according to the master mix instructions. The sequences of the designed gene-specific primers for CCND1, ESR1, MDM2, and GAPDH (housekeeping gene) are shown in Table 1. The polymerase chain reaction was initiated with a 7-min heating step at 95 °C, followed by 45 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 20 s, annealing at 55°–59 °C for 20 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s. PCR product specificity was confirmed using melting peaks and 1 % agarose gel electrophoresis. #### Statistical analysis
  Data are presented as mean and standard deviation in the graphs. SPSS software version 22 was used for data analysis, employing an independent sample t-test with a significance level set at p < 0.05.

### PPIN of ATRA targets
  Cytoscape was utilized to visualize the PPIN of ATRA targets, with the network constructed in STRING to identify hub gene targets. The constructed PPIN (Fig. 1b) had a clustering coefficient of 0.496 and also shows significantly more interactions than expected (PPI enrichment p < 10−16).


### Gene enrichment findings
  Gene enrichment analysis was conducted to identify molecular functions, biological processes, cellular components, and pathways that were significantly overrepresented among the ATRA target genes. ATRA targets were mainly enriched in molecular functions such as eicosanoid receptor activity and transcription factor activity. These genes were also involved in biological processes including response to xenobiotic stimulus and response to lipid. They were predominantly located in cellular components such as the transcription factor complex and chromatin. Enriched pathway terms included inclusion body myositis and nuclear receptors. A comprehensive report of the 15 significant terms can be found in Figs. S1a–S1d. Among synonymous and highly overlapping terms, the most significant one is reported in Fig. 1c.


### Diseases enrichment findings
  Disease enrichment analysis aimed to identify overrepresented diseases or clinical conditions among the ATRA target genes. This analysis utilized the Set Analyzer from CTD, as well as GAD diseases, and DisGeNET from DAVID. Enriched diseases identified by Set Analyzer included neoplasms and 'pathological conditions, signs and symptoms'. Enriched diseases identified by GAD diseases included Alzheimer's disease, type 2 diabetes, lung cancer, plasma HDL cholesterol level, and breast cancer. Enriched diseases from DisGeNET included adenocarcinoma and hypertensive disease. A comprehensive report of the 15 significant terms can be found in Figs. S1e–S1g. Among synonymous and highly overlapping terms, the most significant one is reported in Fig. 1d.

### Annotation of breast cancer-associated genes
  We analyzed cancer-associated genes by constructing a protein-protein interaction network (PPIN) and performing disease enrichment analysis and pathway enrichment analysis. The constructed PPIN (Fig. 3a) exhibited significantly more interactions than expected (PPI enrichment p < 10−16) and a larger clustering coefficient (0.873) compared to the main network of ATRA targets (0.496). Disease enrichment analysis of cancer associated genes was performed using DisGeNET and GAD diseases databases, available in DAVID. As shown in Fig. 3b, the top five DisGeNET terms indicate breast cancer/carcinoma or its synonyms (e.g., mammary neoplasm/carcinoma), and the first GAD diseases term is breast cancer. ## Discussion
  All-trans-Retinoic Acid (ATRA) is a key bioactive mediator of the physiological functions of vitamin A. Additionally, ATRA plays an important role in regulating several cellular processes, including organogenesis, cell apoptosis, differentiation, and growth [33]. ATRA, known as a differentiation inducer, is approved by United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL) [34]. Moreover, numerous pre-clinical studies have proposed the potential use of ATRA in the treatment of solid tumors, including thyroid [35], ovarian [36], and breast [37] cancers.

  ATRA induces transcriptional activation of different genes through several nuclear receptors, including RARs and RXRs [38,39]. Upon binding to RARs, ATRA regulates gene expression via RXR/RAR heterodimers. Although ATRA has a high affinity for RARs, it does not bind to RXRs directly [40]. ATRA can also bind to other receptors such as PPARs and the Vitamin D Receptor (VDR). PPARs can activate transcription of pro-survival genes [41], which contrasts with proapoptotic gene activation induced by ATRA [39]. Therefore, regarding ATRA's activation of the non-genomic pathways (via regulating multiple kinase signaling pathways) [19], its mechanism of action is more complex than thought. In the present study, a network pharmacology and molecular docking approach was used to identify potential mechanisms, pathways and target genes related to anti-breast cancer activity of ATRA.

  First, 209 unique protein targets were identified using different databases for ATRA. These databases use different algorithms to retrieve protein targets of chemicals (drugs). Generally, identified targets are based on published literature, experimentally confirmed targets and predicted via the "similarity principle", where similar molecules have shared protein targets. Gene enrichment analysis revealed that a significant molecular function of ATRA targets is eicosanoid receptor activity. Eicosanoids, including prostaglandins, play crucial roles in various cancers including breast cancer, where non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) inhibiting cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) show potential in reducing cancer risk [42]. Enriched pathways for ATRA protein included inclusion body myositis and nuclear receptors. The association between inflammatory idiopathic myopathies like inclusion body myositis and malignancy has been documented in previous studies [[43], [44], [45], [46]]. Nuclear receptors have been extensively investigated in cancer biology as promising therapeutic targets across various cancer types due to their druggability [47]. Interestingly, disease enrichment analysis revealed that ATRA targets are implicated in several cancer types, including liver carcinoma, prostatic neoplasms, mammary neoplasms, lung cancer, and breast cancer, highlighting these protein targets' potential for cancer treatment. Subsequently, we constructed and analyzed the protein-protein interaction network of ATRA targets to identify central target genes.

  Through several screening steps, we identified 35 hub genes and three significant modules, with all hub genes contributing to these modules. Hub genes are highly connected nodes identified through network analysis and are expected to play pivotal roles in regulating the overall network structure [48]. Modules comprise closely related genes/proteins that cooperate in specific biological functions through PPIs [49]. Cancer-associated gene analysis using MyGeneVenn of CTD revealed that breast cancer shares 16 genes with hub genes, which also exhibit a desirable average degree. These genes were considered final hub genes, and their involvement in breast cancer was further confirmed through disease enrichment analysis using the DisGeNET and GAD diseases databases. These genes were involved in important signaling pathways such as the androgen receptor signaling pathway, ESR-mediated signaling, endocrine resistance, etc. All of these signaling pathways have an essential role in breast cancer [[50], [51], [52]]. Further analysis showed that the final hub genes are involved in important breast cancer related pathways/mechanisms/processes including apoptosis, cell cycle, EMT, PI3K/AKT, and MAPK. The role of EMT pathway in breast cancer invasion and metastasis has been investigated [53]. Also, dysregulation of PI3K/AKT and MAPK pathways has been reported in breast cancer [54].

  Among the final hub genes, six genes, including CCND1, ESR1, MMP9, MDM2, NCOA3, and RARA (key hub genes), were significantly overexpressed in tumor samples. RARA, or retinoic acid receptor alpha is a member of nuclear hormone receptor superfamily that bind to hormone-responsive elements (HREs) sequences on DNA and regulates expression of multiple genes involved in different signaling pathways such as fibroblast growth factor (FGF), wingless-related integration site (WNT), Transforming growth factor-β (TGFB), and Hedgehog (Hh) pathways [55]. The role of RARA in carcinogenesis is dependent on the tumor microenvironment [56]. While it may act as a tumor suppressor in some malignancies such as prostate cancer, non-small cell lung cancer, gastric cancer, and melanoma [56], Johansson et al. found that high levels of RARA are associated with tamoxifen resistance in breast cancer [57]. Also, Doi et al. indicated that RARA overexpression enhances epithelial‐to‐mesenchymal transition and malformation of acinar structures, making it an important factor in breast carcinogenesis [58]. Molecular docking was not done for RARA because it is a confirmed receptor for ATRA [40].

  NCOA3 or nuclear receptor coactivator-3, collaborates with nuclear receptors to enhance transcriptional activity by acting as a bridging molecule. Actually, NCOA3 facilitates protein-protein interactions between nuclear receptors and other co-regulatory factors, thereby assisting in the assembly of the transcriptome complex at the promoter of target genes [59]. It has been reported that NCOA3 upregulation has a pro-proliferative, anti-apoptotic, and anti-autophagic role and is associated with multiple types of human cancers, including breast cancer [60,61]. In Qiao et al.'s study, NCOA3 played a critical role in conferring resistance to bromodomain and extra-terminal domain inhibitors (BETi) in breast cancer cells via the induction of nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) [62]. Molecular docking was not done for NCOA3 because it has an unsuitable structure.

  MMP9, or matrix metalloproteinase-9, is a member of the zinc-dependent endopeptidases family that has ability to degrade certain types of collagens [63]. MMP9 is involved in all stages of cancer as a tumor microenvironment regulator [64], particularly mediating cancer invasion and metastasis through the degradation of basement membranes [65]. In a study by Kalavska et al., MMP9 was associated with shorter survival and poor prognosis in breast cancer patients [66]. In our docking analysis, we demonstrated that ATRA coordinates with Zn3 and forms hydrophobic interactions with specific residues within a binding site similar to that of Periostat, including His230, His190, Ala189, Leu188, Leu187, Gly186, and Tyr218. Furthermore, this binding site shows potential as a candidate for inhibiting MMP9 in cancer therapy.

  Among the six key hub genes, MDM2, ESR1, and CCND1 were significantly associated with poor treatment response in breast cancer patients. In the present study, we investigated the effect of ATRA treatment on cell viability and the expression changes of MDM2, ESR1, and CCND1 in MCF7 and HFF cells. We found that MCF7 cells are more sensitive to ATRA treatment compared to HFF cells. Previous studies have demonstrated that ER+ breast cancers are more sensitive to ATRA due to its ability to restrict estrogen receptor α (ESR1) action [67,68]. MCF7 is considered an ER+ breast cancer cell line [68].

  ESR1, or estrogen receptor 1, is expressed in 80 % of breast cancers and plays a crucial role in their development and progression [69]. Lei et al. showed that amplification of the ESR1 locus and subsequent overexpression of the oncogenic ER protein can potentially increase resistance of breast tumors to endocrine therapies [70]. Conversely, Kim et al. stated that lower expression level of ESR1 is an indicator of tamoxifen resistance in ER-positive breast cancers [71]. In the present study, the interaction score between ATRA with ESR1 was not satisfactory. Takeyoshi et al. indicated that ATRA does not bind to the ER but exerts estrogenic effects at estrogen response elements (EREs) [72] through degradation of estrogen receptor α [37]. Hua et al. highlighted that ESR1 and RAR have overlapping or closely positioned cis-regulatory elements across the human genome, allowing for competitive binding between these transcription factors. ESR1 promotes signaling pathways associated with proliferation and survival, whereas RARs counteract these pathways by inhibiting ERS1's transcriptional activity [73]. Furthermore, our study demonstrated that ATRA treatment of MCF7 cells can decrease expression of ESR1 at the mRNA level, which is consistent with the findings of Rubin et al. [74].

  MDM2, or murine double minute 2, is a major negative regulator of the p53 tumor suppressor [75]. MDM2 overexpression has been reported in many cancers, including breast cancer [76]. Kalantari et al. stated that MDM2 is overexpressed in human breast cancer and that knocking it down using siRNA induces apoptosis in MCF7 cells [77]. Portman et al. concluded that combinational therapy with MDM2 and CDK4/6 inhibitors, along with estrogen receptor degraders, is effective for treating advanced, endocrine-resistant ER+ breast cancers [78]. In the present study, however, there is no potent interaction between ATRA and MDM2 according to molecular docking studies. Nevertheless, our in vitro gene expression results showed that MDM2 is significantly downregulated after ATRA treatment of MCF7 cells. Zeng et al. also reported downregulation of the MDM2 gene at the mRNA and protein levels in astrocytoma cells following treatment with ATRA [79].

  CCND1, or Cyclin D1, a key hub gene in our study, was linked to poor treatment response and showed differential expression across breast cancer stages. CCND1 regulates the cell cycle by activating CDK4/CDK6, which phosphorylate retinoblastoma (Rb), leading to its inability to bind to the E2 promoter binding factor (E2F) family. This activation of E2F promotes transcription of many genes necessary for the G1 to S phase transition [80]. However, CCND1 overexpression in cancer has a controversial role, sometimes indicating a poor prognosis [81] and other times suggesting a good prognosis [82]. In the study by Manoochehri et al., CCND1 was identified as a key gene involved in the radiation response of colorectal cancer through a network analysis approach [83]. Upregulation of CCND1 is suggested as an important mechanism of therapy resistance in various malignancies. Targeting CCND1 could be a promising strategy for inhibiting tumor progression and overcoming therapy resistance [84].

  Abnormal expression of the CCND1 gene has been reported in breast cancer [85]. Our in vitro gene expression results showed a decrease in CCND1 expression after treatment of MCF7 cells with ATRA, consistent with previous studies. It has been reported that CCND1 mRNA expression was reduced in thyroid carcinoma cell lines following treatment with ATRA [86]. Zanetti et al. also reported downregulation of CCND1 mRNA in breast cancer cells after ATRA treatment [87]. Additionally, Yoshioka et al. demonstrated reduced expression of CCND1 protein after treatment with ATRA through immunoblotting [88]. The human CCND1 promoter contains a binding site for the Activator protein 1 (AP-1) transcription factor [89]. The anticancer effect of retinoic acid is exerted through repression of AP-1 activity and its overexpression is correlated with ATRA resistance in breast cancer [41]. Alam et al. reported that CCND1 itself is not a druggable target and it should be inhibited indirectly by its regulators [84]. Here, in agreement with Alam et al.'s statement [84], our molecular docking analyses also showed that ATRA has a higher affinity towards CCND1/CDK4 than CCND1 alone.

  Although direct inhibition of CCND1 by ATRA through ligand binding has not been reported, several studies have primarily focused on its indirect inhibition by downregulation of CCND1 at protein and/or mRNA levels after treatment with ATRA, as discussed in the previous paragraph. To explore the novelty of CCND1 as a potential protein target for ATRA binding, we conducted a literature review. According to literature review, in addition to RARs, RXRs and VDR [38,39,41], ATRA has been reported to bind to other proteins such as RAR-related orphan receptor beta (RORB) [90] and insulin-like growth factor type 2/mannose-6-phosphate (IGF-2/M6P) receptor [91]. Cytochrome P450 family 26 enzymes (CYP26A1, CYP26B1, and CYP26C1) along with other CYP enzymes such as CYP3A4 and CYP2C8, have binding sites for ATRA [92]. UGT2B7 (UDP-Glucuronosyltransferase-2B7) is another enzyme with affinity toward ATRA [93]. Additionally, ATRA has also been found to directly bind to Pin1 [22], AMPK [21], and Retinol-binding protein 4 (RBP4) [94]. Cellular RA binding proteins (CRABP I and II), as well as fatty acid binding protein 5 (FABP5), are also known direct targets of ATRA [95]. Furthermore, direct binding of ATRA with Protein Kinase C (PKC) [96] and human serum albumin [97] has been reported. Therefore, it becomes clear that CCND1 has not been studied as a direct target of ATRA so far.


## Conclusion
  This study employed various analyses, including PPIN analysis and differential expression analysis, to identify potential protein targets of ATRA and select key hub genes. Our findings revealed that ATRA exhibited significant interactions with CCND1/CDK4 and MMP9, as determined by docking analysis. Moreover, CCND1, ESR1, and MDM2 emerged as strong predictors of chemotherapy response in breast cancer patients. Notably, treatment with ATRA led to a significant decrease in the expression of these genes in breast cancer cells, indicating its potential to modulate key pathways involved in cancer development. CCND1 and ESR1 exhibited varying expression patterns across different stages of breast cancer. Our study highlights CCND1 as a critical target influenced by ATRA treatment, which can be inhibited directly through ATRA's interaction with the CCND1/CDK4 complex or indirectly by reducing its mRNA levels. This finding presents a promising avenue for overcoming resistance mechanisms in breast cancer therapy, particularly when combined with other treatments such as endocrine therapy. However, further experimental validation is necessary to confirm the interaction between ATRA and the CCND1/CDK4 complex. Techniques like circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy can provide valuable insights into the binding dynamics between small molecules and their candidate protein targets. Additionally, gene silencing studies can help evaluate the role of identified hub genes in regulating ATRA's anticancer activity. By silencing genes like CCND1, ESR1, and MDM2, we can determine how they influence ATRA's effectiveness in cancer cells.
