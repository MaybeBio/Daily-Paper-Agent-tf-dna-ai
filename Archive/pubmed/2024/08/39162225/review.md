## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text (abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion, data availability) as provided by the user. No supplementary figures, tables, or referenced external data were available for inspection.
- **Assessment boundary** The review assesses the scientific claims, methodological soundness, and presentation quality based solely on the supplied text. Claims referring to supplementary figures, tables, or external datasets are marked as "not assessable" where the underlying data were not provided.
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors propose that nucleosome architecture within gene bodies can be predicted from a simple signal transmission theory (STT) model, where two emitters at the +1 and -last nucleosome positions generate distance-decaying periodic signals. They further claim that these emitter positions can be identified from a neural network classifier trained on DNA deformation energy and transcription factor binding site (TFBS) density. The model is reported to achieve accuracy comparable to experimental MNase-seq methods, and the authors present evidence for a causal relationship between transcription and nucleosome architecture.
- **Visible evidence base** Main text figures (Figures 1–7) are referenced but not shown. Equations 1–6 are described in the methods. Supplementary Figures S1–S19 and Tables S1–S4 are referenced but not provided. Experimental details for yeast strain construction, RNA extraction, and MNase-seq are described in the methods.
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** All figures, supplementary data, and the referenced external datasets (ArrayExpress EMTAB-13613, GEO GSE255857) were not available. The GitHub and Zenodo code repositories were not inspected. This substantially limits the ability to verify the quantitative claims, the quality of the experimental validation, and the robustness of the machine learning results.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** The manuscript presents an interesting and potentially useful conceptual framework for understanding nucleosome positioning, combining signal transmission theory with machine learning. The idea that nucleosome arrays can be modeled as interference patterns from two boundary emitters is elegant and may offer a parsimonious explanation for phased versus fuzzy nucleosome architectures. However, the current text does not provide sufficient detail to fully evaluate the methodology, the statistical robustness of the predictions, or the strength of the causal claims. The experimental validation is described only qualitatively, and key quantitative comparisons (e.g., accuracy metrics, error bars, statistical tests) are not presented in the visible text. The manuscript would benefit from a more detailed presentation of the model architecture, training procedures, and validation metrics, as well as a clearer discussion of the limitations and assumptions of the STT approach.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in chromatin biology, epigenomics, and computational biology would be the primary audience. The work is relevant to those studying nucleosome positioning determinants, the relationship between chromatin structure and gene expression, and the development of predictive models for chromatin architecture. The potential extension to human genomes, as suggested by the authors, may also interest researchers working on more complex eukaryotic systems.
- **Major strengths**
  - The conceptual framework of using signal transmission theory to model nucleosome arrays is novel and provides a simple, interpretable alternative to more complex mechanistic models.
  - The integration of machine learning with physical descriptors (deformation energy) and sequence annotations (TFBS density) is a sensible approach that leverages readily available data.
  - The authors address the causality question between transcription and nucleosome architecture using both observational and experimental approaches, which is a strength.
  - The model's performance is benchmarked against a state-of-the-art method (NuPoP), which is good practice.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **R1-M1** (Severity: Major, Blocking: Yes, Axis: Technical soundness)
    - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that their full prediction model can reproduce nucleosome architecture at the genome level with an average distance of 32 ± 22 bp from experimental peaks, and that this is within the intrinsic experimental noise (37 bp).
    - **Evidence pointer** Results section, "Predicting nucleosome positioning along the entire genome"; Supplementary Figure S8B.
    - **Concern** The comparison between the model's prediction error (32 bp) and the "experimental noise" (37 bp) is presented as evidence that the model performs at the limit of experimental resolution. However, the definition and calculation of this "experimental noise" are not described in the visible text. It is unclear whether this is a standard deviation of read centers around a peak, a peak-calling uncertainty, or something else. Without a clear definition and a proper statistical comparison (e.g., a distribution of errors, not just a mean), this claim is not verifiable.
    - **Why it matters** This is a central claim of the paper: that the model's accuracy is comparable to experimental methods. If the comparison is not rigorous, the claim is overstated.
    - **Resolution test** The authors should provide a clear definition of the "experimental noise" metric, show the distribution of prediction errors (e.g., a histogram or boxplot), and perform a statistical test to demonstrate that the model's error is not significantly different from the experimental noise.
  - **R1-M2** (Severity: Major, Blocking: Yes, Axis: Technical soundness)
    - **Claim pointer** The authors state that the neural network classifier predicts NFRs with an AUC of 96% and accuracy of 92%.
    - **Evidence pointer** Results section, "NFRs are characterized by unique DNA physical properties..."; Figure 2A, B.
    - **Concern** The details of the neural network training and evaluation are insufficient. The text mentions a train/test ratio of 80/20 and cross-validation on a single chromosome, but does not specify the window size, the number of training examples, the hyperparameters (beyond layer sizes), or the criteria for defining a "positive" (NFR) versus "negative" (non-NFR) example. The AUC and accuracy are reported without confidence intervals or cross-validation standard deviations. The potential for data leakage (e.g., overlapping windows) is not discussed.
    - **Why it matters** Without these details, the reported performance metrics cannot be independently assessed or reproduced. The high AUC may be an artifact of the specific data split or the definition of the classification task.
    - **Resolution test** The authors should provide a complete description of the model architecture, training procedure, and hyperparameter selection. They should report performance metrics with confidence intervals, ideally from multiple cross-validation runs. They should also discuss the potential for data leakage and how it was avoided.
  - **R1-M3** (Severity: Major, Blocking: Yes, Axis: Technical soundness)
    - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that the STT model, using two emitters, can predict nucleosome positions with 85% accuracy (average distance of 19 bp from experimental peaks).
    - **Evidence pointer** Results section, "Nucleosome positioning along gene body is determined by distance-decayed periodic signals"; Supplementary Figure S8B.
    - **Concern** The STT model is described with equations (Eqs. 4–6), but the fitting procedure is not described. How are the parameters α and σ determined? Are they fitted per gene or globally? How is the "accuracy" of 85% defined? Is it the fraction of predicted nucleosomes within a certain distance of an experimental peak, or something else? The text states that α and σ were selected to maximize correlation, but the range of values tested and the sensitivity of the results to these parameters are not reported.
    - **Why it matters** The STT model is the core of the paper. If the fitting procedure is not transparent, the model's predictive power cannot be evaluated.
    - **Resolution test** The authors should describe the fitting procedure in detail, including the optimization criterion, the parameter ranges explored, and the sensitivity of the predictions to α and σ. They should also define the "accuracy" metric clearly and report the distribution of errors.
  - **R1-M4** (Severity: Major, Blocking: No, Axis: Scientific importance)
    - **Claim pointer** The authors conclude that "expression activity → changes in nucleosome architecture" and that RNA polymerase or the elongation complex plays a direct role in reinforcing positioning signals.
    - **Evidence pointer** Results section, "Nucleosome architecture and gene expression are coupled in a complex way"; Figures 6 and 7; Table 3.
    - **Concern** The causal claim is based on two experiments: (1) insertion of an 81-bp sequence into a linker region, which increased fuzziness but did not change expression; and (2) transcription inhibition, which increased fuzziness. The first experiment shows that disrupting the linker can affect nucleosome ordering, but it does not directly test the role of transcription. The second experiment shows a correlation between transcription inhibition and fuzziness, but the use of 1,10-phenanthroline is acknowledged to have an unclear mechanism, and the rpb1-1 data are from a different study. The causal arrow is inferred, not directly demonstrated.
    - **Why it matters** The causal direction is a key conclusion of the paper. If the evidence is only correlational, the conclusion is overstated.
    - **Resolution test** The authors should either soften the causal claim or provide more direct evidence, such as a time-course experiment showing that transcription inhibition precedes changes in nucleosome positioning, or a rescue experiment.
- **Minor Comments**
  - **R1-m1** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Readability for nonspecialists)
    - **Affected element** Introduction, first paragraph.
    - **Evidence pointer** Introduction, paragraph 1.
    - **Issue** The introduction assumes familiarity with terms like "nucleosome repeat length" and "phasing" without defining them. A brief explanation of these concepts would make the paper more accessible to a broader audience.
    - **Required correction** Add a sentence defining nucleosome repeat length and phasing, or provide a schematic figure.
  - **R1-m2** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Technical soundness)
    - **Affected element** Methods, "Machine learning" section.
    - **Evidence pointer** Methods, "Machine learning" section.
    - **Issue** The text states that the neural network was built using scikit-learn, TensorFlow, and Keras, but does not specify the version numbers or the random seed used for reproducibility.
    - **Required correction** Provide version numbers and a random seed, or state that the code is available in the repository.
  - **R1-m3** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Technical soundness)
    - **Affected element** Results, "NFRs are characterized by unique DNA physical properties..." section.
    - **Evidence pointer** Results, "NFRs are characterized by unique DNA physical properties..." section; Figure 1.
    - **Issue** The text states that NFRs have "unusually high" deformation energy, but no quantitative comparison is provided (e.g., a distribution of energies for NFRs vs. non-NFRs).
    - **Required correction** Provide a statistical comparison (e.g., a boxplot with p-values) to support this claim.
  - **R1-m4** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Scientific importance)
    - **Affected element** Discussion, paragraph 2.
    - **Evidence pointer** Discussion, paragraph 2.
    - **Issue** The discussion of the human genome results is very brief and only mentions an AUC of ~70%. It is unclear how this compares to the yeast results or what the implications are.
    - **Required correction** Expand this section to provide more context and a more detailed comparison.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established**
  - R1-M1: Lack of a clear definition and statistical comparison for the "experimental noise" metric.
  - R1-M2: Insufficient detail on the neural network training and evaluation, and lack of confidence intervals for performance metrics.
  - R1-M3: Undescribed fitting procedure for the STT model parameters and unclear definition of the "accuracy" metric.
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria**
  - **Originality** The combination of signal transmission theory with machine learning for nucleosome prediction is a novel approach. The idea of modeling nucleosome arrays as interference patterns from boundary emitters is conceptually original and could inspire new thinking in the field.
  - **Scientific importance** The work addresses a fundamental question in chromatin biology: what determines nucleosome positioning? The potential to predict nucleosome architecture from sequence alone, without requiring experimental data, would be a significant advance. However, the current evidence is not sufficient to fully establish this claim.
  - **Interdisciplinary readership** The work is likely to appeal to a niche but active community of chromatin biologists and computational biologists. The conceptual framework may also be of interest to physicists working on polymer models of chromatin. However, the presentation is not sufficiently accessible for a general scientific audience.
  - **Technical soundness** The technical soundness cannot be fully assessed from the provided text. The lack of detail on the machine learning pipeline, the STT fitting procedure, and the statistical comparisons is a major concern. The experimental validation is described only qualitatively.
  - **Readability for nonspecialists** The manuscript is written in a technical style that assumes familiarity with the field. The abstract is clear, but the introduction and methods would be challenging for a nonspecialist. The use of equations without sufficient context may also be a barrier.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The conceptual framework is interesting and potentially valuable, but the current manuscript does not provide sufficient evidence to fully support the claims. The authors need to provide a more detailed and rigorous presentation of their methods and results, including clear definitions of metrics, statistical comparisons, and a more transparent description of the model fitting and evaluation procedures.
