## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text, including abstract, introduction, results, discussion, methods, and supplementary materials description.
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific validity, technical soundness, reproducibility, and alignment of claims with presented evidence. No assessment of editorial fit or language style beyond readability for a general scientific audience.
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors propose that the topology of core transcription factor (TF) interaction networks, inferred from single-cell RNA sequencing of patient-derived glioma stem-like cells (PD-GSCs), determines whether cells undergo treatment-induced proneural-to-mesenchymal transition (PMT) and acquire resistance to the cytotoxic drug pitavastatin. They further claim that this network topology can be used to predict vulnerabilities, enabling rationally designed sequential treatments (siRNA or secondary drugs) that potentiate killing of surviving cells.
- **Visible evidence base** Results sections describing drug response assays, flow cytometry, bulk and single-cell RNA-seq, GSVA subtype scoring, trajectory analysis (Monocle3, RNA velocity), critical transition index, LOESS regression, scSYGNAL network inference, RACIPE simulations, random forest classification, and drug-target identification via Open Targets. Methods sections for experimental procedures and computational analyses. Supplementary figure and table references are listed but not provided in full.
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Supplementary figures (S1–S17), supplementary tables (S1–S16), raw sequencing data, code, and detailed parameter settings for several computational tools (e.g., Harmony, Monocle3, scVelo, RACIPE) are referenced but not available for direct inspection. This limits verification of data quality, statistical thresholds, and reproducibility of the computational pipeline.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript presents a compelling and ambitious framework linking gene regulatory network topology to drug resistance and treatment escape in glioma stem-like cells. The integration of single-cell transcriptomics, network inference, dynamical modeling, and experimental validation is commendable and addresses a clinically important problem. However, the strength of the central claim—that network topology, rather than initial cell state composition, governs resistance—is not fully established from the provided evidence. Key limitations include the small number of PD-GSC lines deeply characterized (n=2 for most analyses), the indirect nature of some validations, and the lack of clarity on how network topology was quantitatively compared between responders and nonresponders. The therapeutic implications, while promising, are based on in vitro assays and require further validation in more physiologically relevant models.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in cancer systems biology, glioma biology, drug resistance mechanisms, and computational biology of gene regulatory networks. The work is also relevant to clinicians and translational scientists interested in rational combination therapy design and precision oncology, particularly for glioblastoma. The methodological framework (scSYGNAL + RACIPE) may interest method developers in network inference and dynamical modeling.
- **Major strengths** 1) Clinically relevant question addressing a major unmet need in GBM therapy. 2) Sophisticated integration of experimental and computational approaches. 3) Clear demonstration that drug treatment induces PMT in a responder line, with functional consequences (increased IC50). 4) The use of RACIPE to simulate network dynamics and predict intervention targets is innovative and well-executed. 5) The finding that sequential, but not simultaneous, treatment with a secondary agent (vinflunine) potentiates killing is an important and potentially actionable insight.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **Concern ID** R1-M1
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Evidence sufficiency for central claim
  - **Claim pointer** The manuscript claims that "it was not the composition of initial cell states but the topology of the core TF-TF network that governed phenotypic plasticity of GSCs."
  - **Evidence pointer** Results sections "Inference and simulation of the dynamics of TRNs..." and "Trajectories toward acquired resistance..."; Figures 4 and 6; Supplementary Tables S5–S12.
  - **Concern** The central claim that network topology, rather than initial cell state composition, governs resistance is based on a comparison of only two PD-GSC lines (SN520 and SN503). While these lines show distinct responses, this is insufficient to establish a general principle. The authors do not provide a quantitative comparison of network topologies (e.g., graph-theoretic metrics, motif analysis) that would demonstrate a systematic difference between responders and nonresponders. Furthermore, the initial cell state composition of SN520 and SN503 differs substantially (e.g., in subtype proportions), and the authors do not rigorously rule out that these initial differences, rather than topology per se, drive the divergent responses. The RACIPE simulations, while showing that the inferred networks can generate multiple states, do not directly test the counterfactual: would a different initial composition on the same network topology produce the same outcome?
  - **Why it matters** The central claim is the foundation of the paper's conceptual contribution. If the conclusion is not robustly supported, the generalizability of the framework to other PD-GSCs and patients is questionable. The distinction between selection of pre-existing states and topology-driven transition is critical for therapeutic targeting.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should either (a) analyze a larger panel of PD-GSC lines (e.g., 5-10) with varying responses to pitavastatin, demonstrating a correlation between network topology features and resistance phenotype; (b) perform a systematic comparison of network topologies (e.g., degree distribution, modularity, feedback loop structure) between responders and nonresponders; or (c) design a computational experiment where initial conditions are swapped between the two networks to show that topology, not initial state, determines the final attractor. Without such evidence, the claim should be softened to a hypothesis.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M2
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Technical soundness of network inference and validation
  - **Claim pointer** The manuscript claims that scSYGNAL identified "mechanistic" regulatory networks and that RACIPE simulations "recapitulated" observed cell states.
  - **Evidence pointer** Methods sections "scSYGNAL framework" and "RACIPE simulations"; Figures 4 and 6; Supplementary Figures S12–S15.
  - **Concern** The validation of the inferred networks is indirect. The authors show that RACIPE simulations generate states that are "statistically similar" to observed states, but the details of this comparison are not fully described. Specifically, it is unclear how the similarity was quantified (e.g., correlation of expression profiles, overlap of state membership) and what the null distribution was. The claim that knockdowns of specific TFs (e.g., ATF3, IRF1) "validate" the network is based on phenotypic assays (cell viability), but the authors do not demonstrate that these knockdowns specifically disrupt the predicted regulatory circuits rather than having off-target or general cytotoxic effects. The siRNA experiments appear to be performed in bulk cultures, not at single-cell resolution, so it is unclear whether the predicted block in PMT actually occurs at the cellular level.
  - **Why it matters** The predictive power of the framework depends on the accuracy of the inferred networks. If the networks are not mechanistically accurate, the predictions of intervention targets may not be reliable. The validation experiments are crucial for establishing that the computational predictions have biological relevance.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should provide a more detailed description of the statistical comparison between simulated and observed states, including the null distribution and effect sizes. For the siRNA validation, they should ideally perform single-cell RNA-seq or at least flow cytometry for subtype markers after knockdown to demonstrate that PMT is specifically blocked. Additionally, they could test whether knockdown of TFs predicted to be unimportant has no effect, serving as a negative control.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M3
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Generalizability and clinical relevance
  - **Claim pointer** The manuscript suggests that the approach can be used to "rationally predict combinatorial treatments that disrupt acquired resistance in GBM" and that it is a "generalizable approach for formulating N=1 patient-tailored drug regimens."
  - **Evidence pointer** Discussion section; Results section "Trajectories toward acquired resistance..."; Figure 7; Supplementary Table S14.
  - **Concern** The demonstration of generalizability is limited. The vinflunine combination was tested on only two PD-GSC lines (SN520 and SN503) and two additional lines (SN533, SN575) for the combination efficacy. The authors show variable efficacy across these lines, but the sample size is too small to draw conclusions about generalizability. Moreover, the in vitro nature of the experiments (cell viability assays) does not address whether this approach would work in vivo, where pharmacokinetics, tumor microenvironment, and blood-brain barrier penetration are critical factors. The claim of "N=1 patient-tailored" treatment is aspirational but not supported by the current data.
  - **Why it matters** The clinical relevance of the findings is a key selling point of the paper. Overstating the generalizability could mislead readers and future research directions. The approach is promising, but it needs to be validated in more models and eventually in animal studies.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should temper the claims of generalizability and clinical applicability. They could strengthen the paper by (a) testing the combination in a larger panel of PD-GSC lines, (b) including in vivo experiments in orthotopic xenograft models, or (c) clearly stating the limitations of the current study and outlining the necessary steps for clinical translation.
- **Minor Comments**
  - **Concern ID** R1-m1
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Clarity of methods
  - **Affected element** RACIPE simulation parameters
  - **Evidence pointer** Methods section "RACIPE simulations"
  - **Issue** The description of RACIPE simulation parameters is incomplete. The authors mention "default settings" for the sracipeSimulate function but do not specify the number of parameter sets, the range of kinetic parameters, or the criteria for convergence. This limits reproducibility.
  - **Required correction** Provide a detailed description of all simulation parameters, including the number of models, parameter ranges, integration settings, and convergence criteria. If default settings are used, specify what those defaults are.
  - **Concern ID** R1-m2
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Statistical rigor
  - **Affected element** Comparison of simulated and observed states
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section "Inference and simulation of the dynamics of TRNs"; Figure 6; Supplementary Figure S12.
  - **Issue** The statistical comparison between simulated and observed cell states is not described in sufficient detail. It is unclear what test was used, how the null distribution was generated, and what the effect sizes were.
  - **Required correction** Provide a clear description of the statistical methods used to compare simulated and observed states, including the test statistic, null distribution, and significance thresholds. Report effect sizes and confidence intervals where possible.
  - **Concern ID** R1-m3
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Data availability
  - **Affected element** Supplementary materials
  - **Evidence pointer** Supplementary Figures S1–S17 and Tables S1–S16.
  - **Issue** The supplementary materials are referenced extensively but not provided in the manuscript text. This makes it impossible to fully evaluate the data quality, statistical analyses, and additional validations.
  - **Required correction** Ensure that all supplementary materials are accessible and clearly referenced. If possible, include key supplementary figures in the main text or provide a summary of the most important supplementary results.
  - **Concern ID** R1-m4
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Interpretation of results
  - **Affected element** Figure 2I and associated text
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section "Single-cell analysis suggests that drug-induced PMT is likely mechanism of acquired pitavastatin resistance in SN520"; Figure 2I.
  - **Issue** The authors report a significant increase in IC50 for SN520 after treatment, but the clinical relevance of this change (from 0.42 to 2.24 μM) is not discussed. Is this change likely to be clinically meaningful given achievable plasma concentrations of pitavastatin?
  - **Required correction** Add a brief discussion of the clinical relevance of the observed IC50 changes, including a comparison to achievable drug concentrations in patients.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** 1) The central claim regarding network topology vs. initial state composition is not adequately supported (R1-M1). 2) The validation of network predictions via siRNA is indirect and lacks single-cell resolution (R1-M2). 3) The statistical comparison between simulated and observed states is under-described (R1-m2). 4) The generalizability claims are overstated given the limited number of PD-GSC lines tested (R1-M3).
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** **Originality:** High. The integration of network topology inference with dynamical modeling to predict treatment resistance and design sequential therapies is a novel and creative approach. **Scientific importance:** High. Addressing drug resistance in GBM is a critical clinical problem, and the framework could have broad applicability to other cancers. **Interdisciplinary readership:** Moderate to high. The work bridges cancer biology, systems biology, and computational modeling, and should appeal to readers in these fields. However, the heavy computational focus may limit accessibility to purely experimental biologists. **Technical soundness:** Moderate. The computational methods are sophisticated, but the validation is incomplete, and key statistical details are missing. The small sample size (n=2 for the main comparison) is a significant limitation. **Readability for nonspecialists:** Moderate. The manuscript is well-written but assumes familiarity with network inference, dynamical systems, and single-cell analysis. The abstract and introduction are accessible, but the results and methods sections may be challenging for non-experts.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The manuscript presents a promising and innovative framework with potentially important clinical implications. However, the central claim is not fully established from the provided evidence, and the validation is incomplete. The authors should address the concerns raised, particularly regarding the comparison of network topologies, the statistical rigor of the simulation comparisons, and the generalizability of the findings. If these issues are adequately resolved, the paper would be a strong contribution to the field.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that "network topology, not initial cell state composition, governs phenotypic plasticity" is not fully supported by the data, as only two PD-GSC lines were compared and initial state differences were not rigorously excluded.
- The claim that the approach is "generalizable" for N=1 patient-tailored treatment is not supported by the limited number of cell lines tested and the lack of in vivo validation.
- The claim that siRNA knockdowns "validate" the network predictions is not fully supported, as the experiments do not demonstrate specific disruption of the predicted regulatory circuits at single-cell resolution.
- The claim that vinflunine is a rational secondary drug based on network analysis is plausible but not definitively established, as the selection process is not fully described and the efficacy is variable across cell lines.
- The statistical significance and effect sizes of the comparisons between simulated and observed cell states are not reported, making these claims unverifiable from the provided information.
