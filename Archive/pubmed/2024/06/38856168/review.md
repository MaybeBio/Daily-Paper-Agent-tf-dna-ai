## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific content, methodology, dataset construction, model evaluation, and claims as presented in the provided text
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors present new deep learning models (hierarchical and multi-class) for predicting DNA- and RNA-binding proteins from protein sequences, using newly curated datasets with more restrictive annotation criteria. They claim improved prediction accuracy over existing tools, particularly for DNA-binding proteins, and report the first explicit investigation of single-stranded DNA-binding proteins within this prediction framework.
- **Visible evidence base** Abstract, Introduction, Materials and Methods, Results, Discussion, Data availability statement. Figures and tables are referenced but not provided in the supplied material.
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** All figures (Fig. 1-4), all tables (Table 1-2, S1-S7), supplementary figures, and the GitHub repository contents. Without these, quantitative results and dataset details cannot be independently verified.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** The manuscript addresses a relevant problem in computational biology: improving prediction of nucleic acid-binding proteins using deep learning. The authors propose two strategies (hierarchical and multi-class) and introduce new datasets with more stringent annotation criteria. The motivation is clear and the methodological framework is reasonable. However, the provided text lacks the actual figures, tables, and supplementary materials that contain the core quantitative results. This severely limits the ability to assess the validity of the performance claims. The comparison with existing predictors is described but the actual data are not visible. The discussion of SSB prediction is interesting but the reported low accuracy (~40%) raises questions about the practical utility of the models for this subclass. Overall, the manuscript has potential but the evidence base as provided is insufficient for a full evaluation.

- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Computational biologists and bioinformaticians working on protein function prediction, particularly those developing or using machine learning methods for nucleic acid-binding protein annotation. Researchers studying DNA- or RNA-binding proteins, including those interested in single-stranded DNA-binding proteins, may also find the dataset construction and SSB analysis relevant. The work could be of interest to developers of genome annotation pipelines that require accurate functional prediction for uncharacterized proteins.

- **Major strengths** The authors identify a genuine limitation in existing predictors (separate training for DBP and RBP, leading to biased predictions) and propose a multi-class approach to address it. The dataset construction strategy, combining keyword and GO term filtering, is more rigorous than some previous efforts. The explicit inclusion of SSBs as a distinct category is a novel contribution. The use of a deep learning architecture (CNN + LSTM) is appropriate for sequence-based prediction tasks. The authors also perform error analysis, examining wrongly predicted proteins for length bias and GO enrichment, which adds depth to the study.

- **Major Concerns** 
  - **R1-M1** (Severity: Major, Blocking: Yes, Axis: Evidence availability) The core quantitative results are not visible in the provided material. All figures and tables are referenced but absent. Without these, the performance claims (e.g., 81.2% accuracy in step 1, 81.4% in step 2, 72.7% overall for multi-class, 76.6% for DBP) cannot be verified. The comparison with existing predictors in Table 2 is described but the actual numbers are not shown. This is a fundamental barrier to assessment.
  - **R1-M2** (Severity: Major, Blocking: Yes, Axis: Dataset validity) The dataset construction relies on keyword-based annotation from Swiss-Prot. The authors acknowledge that some proteins may be misannotated (e.g., Q9VPT8). The filtering criteria for non-NABPs (absence of specific keywords and GO terms) may not be sufficient to exclude all NABPs, potentially introducing false negatives into the negative set. The authors do not provide an estimate of annotation error rates or a validation of their dataset against an independent gold standard. This affects the reliability of all downstream results.
  - **R1-M3** (Severity: Major, Blocking: Yes, Axis: Technical soundness) The reported SSB prediction accuracy (~40% in the DBP/RBP step, 39.4% in multi-class) is very low. The authors attribute this to small dataset size and feature limitations, but this raises a question: if SSBs are poorly predicted, what is the practical value of including them in the DBP category? The claim that the models are "more practically useful in identifying novel NABPs" is weakened by this poor SSB performance. The authors do not provide a clear strategy for improving SSB prediction, only stating that "new strategies need to be explored."
  - **R1-M4** (Severity: Major, Blocking: No, Axis: Comparison fairness) The comparison with existing predictors is described as using "fair" datasets, but the details are only in supplementary tables (S3-S5) that are not provided. The authors note that their reported accuracies for other predictors are "generally higher than the published accuracy," which is attributed to less ambiguous datasets. This is plausible but cannot be evaluated without the actual comparison data. The choice of 455 proteins for the negative set in comparisons is not justified.

- **Minor Comments** 
  - **R1-m1** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Clarity, Affected element: Methods, Evidence pointer: Materials and Methods, "Datasets" section) The description of the keyword-based selection for SSBs and DSBs is somewhat terse. The exact keywords used for DSB classification (beyond the four listed) are not fully specified. The authors mention "any other four types of descriptions related to transcription factors as shown in Table 1," but Table 1 is not provided. This should be clarified in the text.
  - **R1-m2** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Reproducibility, Affected element: Methods, Evidence pointer: Materials and Methods, "CNN model building" section) The hyperparameter search is described as "we tried different set of parameters for training, and the parameters with the best performance were selected." This is vague. The authors should specify the range of hyperparameters explored, the selection criterion (e.g., validation accuracy), and whether the test set was used only once after final model selection to avoid overfitting to the test set.
  - **R1-m3** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Statistical reporting, Affected element: Results, Evidence pointer: Results, "Prediction of non-NABPs, DBPs, and RBPs with the hierarchical approach" section) The authors report mean and standard deviation over 500 rounds of random data splitting. However, they do not report confidence intervals or statistical significance tests for differences between their model and existing predictors. Given the stochastic nature of the training process, this would strengthen the claims.
  - **R1-m4** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Interpretation, Affected element: Discussion, Evidence pointer: Discussion, paragraph 2) The GO enrichment analysis of wrongly predicted proteins is interesting, but the authors only mention two examples (GO:1901363 for non-NABPs, GO:0003725 for RBPs). A more systematic presentation of the enrichment results, including effect sizes and statistical significance, would be more informative.
  - **R1-m5** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Accessibility, Affected element: Data availability, Evidence pointer: Data availability statement) The GitHub repository is mentioned, but the authors should confirm that the code is well-documented, includes instructions for reproducing the datasets and models, and is publicly accessible without restrictions. This is important for reproducibility.

- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** 
  - R1-M1 (missing figures/tables)
  - R1-M2 (dataset validation)
  - R1-M3 (SSB performance and its implications)

- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** 
  - **Originality**: Moderate. The multi-class approach for simultaneous DBP/RBP prediction is a reasonable extension of existing work, but the underlying architecture (CNN + LSTM) is not novel. The explicit inclusion of SSBs is a new angle, but the poor performance limits its impact.
  - **Scientific importance**: Moderate. Accurate prediction of nucleic acid-binding proteins is important for functional annotation, but the field already has many tools. The improvement claimed (e.g., 1% over DeepDRBP-2L in overall accuracy) is incremental rather than transformative.
  - **Interdisciplinary readership**: Limited. The work is primarily of interest to computational biologists and bioinformaticians. It is unlikely to attract a broad readership from other disciplines.
  - **Technical soundness**: Cannot be fully assessed due to missing figures/tables. The methodology is generally sound in design, but the low SSB accuracy and lack of dataset validation are concerns.
  - **Readability for nonspecialists**: The manuscript is written in a technical style that is appropriate for the target audience. The introduction provides sufficient background, but the methods section assumes familiarity with deep learning concepts.

- **Recommendation posture** Currently not established from the provided evidence. The manuscript has a reasonable structure and addresses a relevant problem, but the absence of all quantitative results (figures, tables, supplementary materials) makes it impossible to verify the core claims. The dataset construction and SSB performance issues also need to be addressed. The authors should be invited to resubmit with the full set of results and additional analyses.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that the models "outperform existing DBP and RBP predictors" is unsupported without Table 2 and the comparison datasets.
- The claim of "~12% improvement" for DBPs in the multi-class approach is unsupported without Figure 4A and Table S6.
- The claim that the datasets are "more accurate and robust" is not verifiable without the dataset details and any validation against independent benchmarks.
- The statement that "this is the first time that SSBs are explicitly investigated as part of DBPs and RBPs prediction" is a strong claim that would require a thorough literature search to verify; the authors do not provide evidence for this novelty.
- The overall accuracy figures (81.2%, 81.4%, 72.7%, etc.) are reported in the text but cannot be cross-checked without the corresponding figures and tables.
- The error analysis (length bias, GO enrichment) is described but the actual results are not shown, making the conclusions from this analysis unverifiable.
