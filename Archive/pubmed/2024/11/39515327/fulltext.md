# Itaconate transporter SLC13A3 impairs tumor immunity via endowing ferroptosis resistance


## Summary
  Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) triggers tumor ferroptosis. However, most patients are unresponsive to ICB. Tumors might evade ferroptosis in the tumor microenvironment (TME). Here, we discover SLC13A3 is an itaconate transporter in tumor cells and endows tumor ferroptosis resistance, diminishing tumor immunity and ICB efficacy. Mechanistically, tumor cells uptake itaconate via SLC13A3 from tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), thereby activating the NRF2-SLC7A11 pathway and escaping from immune-mediated ferroptosis. Structural modeling and molecular docking analysis identify a functional inhibitor for SLC13A3 (SLC13A3i). Deletion of ACOD1 (an essential enzyme for itaconate synthesis) in macrophages, genetic ablation of SLC13A3 in tumors, or treatment with SLC13A3i sensitize tumors to ferroptosis, curb tumor progression, and bolster ICB effectiveness. Thus, we identify the interplay between tumors and TAMs via the SLC13A3-itaconate-NRF2-SLC7A11 axis as a previously unknown immune ferroptosis resistant mechanism in the TME and SLC13A3 as a promising immunometabolic target for treating SLC13A3+ cancer.


## Introduction
  Despite the remarkable clinical efficacy of current cancer immunotherapy, most patients do not respond or develop therapeutic resistance to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB).1 It is paramount to understand the mechanisms of immune resistance in the tumor microenvironment (TME) to identify new therapeutic targets and develop more effective immunotherapies.1–3 Tumors employ metabolic adaptation mechanisms to evade anti-tumor immunity and resist immune stress mediated by immunotherapy.4–7 Abundant levels of immunosuppressive oncometabolites exist in the TME.4,8–11 However, a significant knowledge gap persists regarding how tumor cells utilize these metabolites, whether and which corresponding transporters, such as solute carrier family (SLC) members, are biologically and immunologically implicated in specific metabolite uptake and action in the TME.

  There are more than 400 SLCs that transport diverse metabolic substrates.4 Recent studies have begun to elucidate the importance of transporters for glucose (such as SLC2A1 and SLC2A4)12,13 and amino acids (such as SLC7A11 and SLC43A2)14,15 in T cell-mediated tumor immunity.4,16 However, most SLC family members, including SLC13, are largely understudied in the context of tumor immune responses and immunotherapy. Among 5 members of the SLC13 family, SLC13A1 and SLC13A4 may be transporters for inorganic sulfate, while SLC13A2, SLC13A3, and SLC13A5 are thought to serve as transporters for di- and tri-carboxylates.17 Nonetheless, the expression pattern, immunological significance, and potential role of SLC13 members in transporting oncometabolites, such as itaconate, in the TME, are not yet defined.

  Itaconate is a metabolite derived from the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and catalyzed by aconitate decarboxylase 1 (ACOD1), a metabolic enzyme encoded by immune-responsive gene 1 (IRG1).18,19 During inflammatory responses, IRG1 is upregulated, leading to increased ACOD1expression, catalyzing the conversion of cis-aconitate to itaconate. Endogenous itaconate plays an immunomodulatory role in inflammatory responses, particularly in bacterial infection.20 However, it is unknown whether and how tumor cells can uptake extracellular itaconate in the TME and whether itaconate has a direct effect on tumor cell death triggered by ICB.

  Ferroptosis is a form of regulated cell death driven by iron, reactive oxygen species, and lipid peroxidation of cell membrane lipids.21 ICB-activated T cell-derived IFNγ combined with specific fatty acids function as endogenous ferroptosis inducers, inducing tumor cell ferroptosis and contributing to ICB efficacy.14,22,23 Induction of tumor ferroptosis is a mode of action of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells (CTLs) and an underlying mechanism of ICB.14,22,23 Given that most patients are not responsive to ICB, it is posited that endogenous mechanisms (factors) capable of inhibiting tumor ferroptosis in the TME may exist, thus, enabling tumor immune evasion via ferroptosis resistance.

  In this work we have attempted to address these questions. We have studied the interplay between tumor associated macrophages (TAMs) and tumor cells in the TME. We discovered that SLC13A3 is a transporter for itaconate in tumor cells and plays a previously unreported role in tumor resistance to ferroptosis and ICB.


## Results

### Tumor SLC13A3 expression correlates with ICB efficacy and patient survival
  Oncometabolites, including several di- and tri-carboxylate TCA cycle metabolites, impair anti-tumor immunity in the tumor microenvironment (TME).4,8–11 The SLC13 family is a group of cell membrane transporters that potentially import di- or tricarboxylate metabolites into cells.17 We hypothesized that the SLC13 family might import oncometabolites into tumor cells, contributing to immune suppression and therapeutic resistance. To test this hypothesis, we first assessed the expression of the SLC13 family members in a patient cohort of metastatic melanoma receiving anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody (nivolumab or pembrolizumab) treatment (Cohort 1).24 We found that the expression levels of tumor SLC13A3 were the highest among three major SLC13 family members. Moreover, its expression levels were lower in patients who responded to ICB (R) compared to non-responders (N) (Figure 1A). High expression of tumor SLC13A3 was associated with poor overall survival in these patients (Figure 1B). Thus, SLC13A3 expression negatively correlates with the clinical response to ICB.

  We extended and validated our study to a cohort of patients with advanced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) treated with atezolizumab plus bevacizumab immunotherapy at Cha University Bundang Medical Center, South Korea (Table S1 and S2) (Cohort 2). Consistent with the melanoma data, a negative correlation was observed between SLC13A3 expression and the clinical response to ICB (Figure 1C). High levels of HCC SLC13A3 were negatively associated with the patient’s overall survival (Figure 1D). We similarly analyzed a cohort of patients at the University of Michigan who received ICB treatment across three tumor types, melanoma, head and neck cancer, and bladder cancer (Table S3) (Cohort 3). Again, high expression of tumor SLC13A3 was associated with poor overall survival (Figure 1E). Analysis in TCGA data set covering multiple cancer types, including low-grade glioma (LGG), pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), uterine carcinosarcoma (UCS), cholangiocarcinoma (bile duct cancer, CHOL), breast carcinoma (BRCA), skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM), uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), and kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC) yielded similar results (Figure S1A). Three single-cell RNA sequencing datasets25–27 demonstrated an enriched expression pattern of SLC13A3 in malignant cell clusters compared to other clusters in patients with melanoma and colorectal carcinoma (Figure 1F, S1B–C). Immunohistochemistry staining (IHC) confirmed potent SLC13A3 protein expression in melanoma cells, but not normal skin tissues (Figure 1G). Similar results were observed in human pancreatic, colorectal, liver, and ovarian cancers from the open-source data (Figure S1D). Thus, our findings underscore a potential link between pan-tumor SLC13A3 expression, impaired tumor immunity, and tumor resistance to ICB.


### SLC13A3 diminishes tumor immunogenicity
  To define the functional significance of tumor SLC13A3 in tumor immunity, we generated several murine Slc13a3 knockout (Slc13a3−/−) tumor cell lines (Figure S2A). We observed that genetic deletion of Slc13a3 led to reduced tumor development and progression in C57BL/6J mice bearing Yumm5.2 melanoma, B16-F10 melanoma, and MT3 pancreatic cancer (Figure 2A–F). To further validate this finding, we ectopically expressed Slc13a3 on parental (Slc13a3OE) Yumm5.2 tumors (Figure S2A) or rescued Slc13a3 expression in Slc13a3−/− (Slc13a3−/−+OE) B16-F10 tumors. Restoration of the Slc13a3 expression resulted in tumor progression in both Yumm5.2 and B16-F10 melanoma models (Figure S2B–C). Accordingly, the percentages of tumor infiltrating IFNγ+CD8+ T cells and TNFα+CD4+ T cells were increased in mice bearing Slc13a3−/− tumors compared to Slc13a3+/+ tumors (Figure 2G–H, S2D). In line with this, single-cell RNA sequencing analysis revealed elevated levels of cytotoxic lymphocyte proportion in Slc13a3−/− B16-F10 tumors compared to Slc13a3+/+ tumors (Figure S2E). To understand the importance of the tumor immune response, we inoculated Slc13a3+/+ and Slc13a3−/− B16-F10 cells in NOD-SCID IL2rγnull (NSG) mice. The growth disparity between Slc13a3+/+ and Slc13a3−/− tumors in wild type (immune competent) mice was diminished in the NSG mice (Figure 2I–J). These data indicate that SLC13A3 impairs tumor immunogenicity.


### SLC13A3 imports itaconate to protect tumors from ferroptosis
  To elucidate the potential mechanism by which SLC13A3 impairs tumor immunogenicity, we analyzed single-cell sequencing data on Slc13a3+/+ and Slc13a3−/− B16-F10 tumors in vivo. The Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) demonstrated that the ferroptosis resistance and NRF2 gene signatures were among the top upregulated gene signals in Slc13a3+/+ tumors when compared to Slc13a3−/− tumors (Figure 3A–B). In addition to an increase in cytotoxic T cells in Slc13a3−/− B16-F10 tumors (Figure 2G, S2E), there was an enrichment in the type-I IFN signaling in Slc13a3−/− B16-F10 tumors (Figure 3A), suggesting a potential role of the type-I IFN signaling in Slc13a3−/− tumor regression. We have previously reported that ICB triggered tumor cell ferroptosis, contributing to the anti-tumor immune response.14,22,28 This prompted us to investigate whether SLC13A3, as a metabolite transporter, influences tumor cell ferroptosis, thereby altering tumor immunogenicity. To test the hypothesis, we treated mice bearing Slc13a3−/− and Slc13a3+/+ B16-F10 tumors with Liproxstatin-1, a ferroptosis inhibitor (Figure 3C). Treatment with Liproxstatin-1 had minimal effect on Slc13a3+/+ tumor growth but dramatically rescued Slc13a3−/− tumor progression, diminishing the growth difference between Slc13a3−/− and Slc13a3+/+ B16-F10 tumors (Figure 3C). Thus, SLC13A3 impairs tumor immunogenicity by enabling tumor resistance to ferroptosis in the TME.

  SLC13A3 is thought to be a di- and tricarboxylate metabolite transporter.17 To decipher how SLC13A3 influences tumor ferroptosis, we cultured Slc13a3OE Yumm5.2 cells with a series of di- and tri-carboxylate TCA cycle metabolites and examined their responses to Erastin, a ferroptosis inducer. We discovered that itaconate, unlike other metabolites, strongly protected tumor cells from Erastin-induced ferroptosis (Figure 3D). However, itaconate had no effect on the levels of BODIPY C12 (Figure S3A) and intracellular Fe2+ (Figure S3B) in Yumm5.2 cells in the absence of ferroptosis inducers. A cell-based transporter assay demonstrated that 13C5-labeled itaconate was efficiently transported from the extracellular space into Slc13a3OE Yumm5.2 cells in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 3E), suggesting that SLC13A3 is an itaconate transporter for tumor cells. To validate a role of SLC13A3 in tumor ferroptosis resistance, we pretreated Slc13a3+/+ Yumm5.2 cells and Slc13a3−/− Yumm5.2 cells with itaconate, and subsequently cultured these cells with 2 ferroptosis inducers, RSL3 and Erastin. Treatment with itaconate profoundly protected Slc13a3+/+ Yumm5.2 cells from ferroptosis (Figure 3F) and reduced oxidized lipid production in Slc13a3+/+ Yumm5.2 cells (Figure 3G). Slc13a3−/− Yumm5.2 cells did not exhibit these effects (Figure 3F–G). The protective effect of itaconate against ferroptosis was extended to human melanoma cell lines Colo-679 (Figure 3H) and RVH-421 (Figure 3I). Furthermore, itaconate protected from Erastin-induced ferroptosis in Slc13a3+/+ B16-F10 cells compared to Slc13a3−/− cells, while rescuing SLC13A3 expression in Slc13a3−/− cells (Slc13a3−/−+OE) re-endowed the protective effect (Figure 3J). Transporter assay confirmed a dramatic increase in the import of extracellular itaconate in Slc13a3OE (Figure 3K) and Slc13a3−/−+OE B16-F10 cells (Figure S3C). In line with this, Slc13a3+/+ B16-F10 cells had higher levels of 13C5 itaconate than Slc13a3−/− B16-F10 cells in vivo (Figure S3D). Treatment with cell-permeable itaconate (4-octyl itaconate (OI)) generated similar levels of ferroptosis protection on Slc13a3−/− B16-F10 cells and Slc13a3−/−+OE B16-F10 cells (Figure S3E). Similar results were obtained in Slc13a3−/− Yumm5.2 cells and Slc13a3+/+ Yumm5.2 cells (Figure S3F). To further delve into the ferroptosis protection feature of intracellular of itaconate, we extended our studies to multiple mouse and human tumor cell lines. We confirmed that cell-permeable itaconate enabled ferroptosis resistance to B16-F0 (Figure S3G), A375 human melanoma cell line (Figure S3H), OC8 human ovarian cancer cell line (Figure S3I), and OVCA429 (Figure S3J). Together, these results demonstrate that SLC13A3 is an active itaconate transporter for tumor cells and imports itaconate to protect tumors from ferroptosis.


### Itaconate activates the NRF2-SLC7A11 axis to protect tumors from ferroptosis
  To explore the intracellular mechanisms underlying the impact of itaconate in tumor cells, we performed an RNA sequencing study on Yumm5.2 cells treated with cell-permeable itaconate. In line with our observation on Slc13a3+/+ tumor cells (Figure 3A), we found an enrichment in the NRF2 pathway in tumor cells treated with cell-permeable itaconate (Figure 4A–B). NRF2 plays a pivotal role in cellular antioxidant defense mechanisms29 and serves as a modulator in mediating resistance to ferroptosis.30–32 Notably, the cystine transporter SLC7A11, a critical regulator of ferroptosis, emerged as one of the top NRF2-targeted genes upregulated by cell-permeable itaconate treatment (Figure 4C). In line with this, treatment with permeable itaconate increased Slc7a11 transcripts in Yumm5.2 cells in a dose dependent manner (Figure 4D). Moreover, treatment with permeable itaconate comparably increased expression of SLC7A11 and NRF2 protein in Slc13a3−/− and Slc13a3+/+ Yumm5.2 cells (Figure 4E). However, treatment with itaconate increased expression of SLC7A11 and NRF2 protein in Slc13a3+/+ Yumm5.2 cells, but not in Slc13a3−/− Yumm5.2 cells (Figure 4F). NRF2 could be controlled by protein kinase RNA-like ER kinase (PERK).33 To investigate a potential role of PERK in itaconate-induced NRF2, we used small hairpin RNAs to establish stable Eif2ak3 knocking down Slc13a3OE Yumm5.2 cells. Eif2ak3 knockdown did not affect itaconate-induced NRF2 expression (Figure S4A). It suggests that itaconate upregulates NRF2 through a PERK-independent mechanism. Altogether, SLC13A3 is an essential transporter for importing itaconate to stimulate SLC7A11 and NRF2 expression in tumor cells.

  To determine if SLC7A11 mediates itaconate-induced ferroptosis resistance, we established Slc7a11−/− Yumm5.2 cells (Figure S4B). Cell permeable itaconate stimulated SLC7A11 expression and cystine uptake in Slc7a11+/+ Yumm5.2 cells, but not in Slc7a11−/− Yumm5.2 cells (Figure 4G). In line with this, itaconate increased cystine uptake in Slc13a3+/+ Yumm5.2 cells compared to Slc13a3−/− cells (Figure S4C). In line with this, permeable itaconate induced ferroptosis resistance in Slc7a11+/+ B16-F10 cells, but not Slc7a11−/− B16-F10 cells (Figure 4H). To explore the role of NRF2 in itaconate-induced ferroptosis resistance, we used small hairpin RNAs to generate several stable Nfe2l2 gene knockdown Yumm5.2 cells (sh-Nfe2l2 #1–3) (Figure S4D). We found that permeable itaconate failed to upregulate SLC7A11 expression in sh-Nfe2l2 Yumm5.2 cells (Figure S4D) and was unable to protect Yumm5.2 cells from RSL-3-induced ferroptosis (Figure S4E). Collectively, our data suggest that SLC13A3-transported itaconate activates the NRF2-SLC7A11 axis to protect tumors from ferroptosis.


### Itaconate promotes tumor progression via inducing ferroptosis resistance
  We next evaluated the role of itaconate-induced ferroptosis resistance in tumor progression in vivo. Aconitate decarboxylase 1 (ACOD1) catalyzes cis-aconitate to generate itaconate.18,19 To examine the impact of itaconate on tumor progression, we used the global Acod1 knockout mouse strain (Acod1−/−), which lacks endogenous itaconate.18 We inoculated several syngeneic tumor cell lines into Acod1−/− mice and wild-type Acod1 (Acod1+/+) mice. We observed a reduced tumor progression in Acod1−/− mice compared to Acod1+/+ mice bearing MC38, Yumm5.2, and B16-F10 tumors (Figure 5A–C). Meanwhile, tumor cells isolated from Acod1−/− mice exhibited elevated levels of lipid ROS, suggesting an increase in ferroptosis in vivo (Figure 5D). Notably, treatment with liproxstatin-1 diminished the differences in B16-F10 tumor growth between Acod1−/− and Acod1+/+ mice (Figure 5E), revealing a close correlation between itaconate and tumor ferroptosis resistance in vivo. To assess the involvement of tumor SLC13A3 in this context, we inoculated Slc13a3−/− B16-F10 tumor cells into Acod1−/− and Acod1+/+ mice. Ablation of tumor Slc13a3 mitigated the differences in tumor growth between Acod1+/+ and Acod1−/− mice, as shown by comparable and smaller tumors in both groups (Figure 5F). To test if tumor SLC7A11 expression is crucial for the in vivo effect of itaconate, Slc7a11−/− Yumm5.2 or Slc7a11−/− B16-F10 tumor cells were inoculated into Acod1−/− and Acod1+/+ mice. As expected, Slc7a11−/− tumor progression was no longer different between Acod1−/− and Acod1+/+ mice (Figure 5G, H). To further confirm the role of itaconate in vivo, we detected 3-fold high levels of itaconate in Yumm5.2 tumor interstitial fluids in Acod1+/+ mice than Acod1−/− mice (Figure 5I).

  ACOD1 is highly expressed in myeloid cells following a bacterial or viral infection.20,34–37 To investigate the source of itaconate in the TME, we analyzed human single-cell RNA-seq data sets, including melanoma25,26 and colorectal cancer (CRC).27 Our analysis revealed that macrophages expressed the highest levels of ACOD1 in the human TME (Figure S5A–C). To experimentally test the role of macrophage-derived itaconate in tumor models, we generated mice with macrophage-specific Acod1 knockout mice (Acod1LyzKO) by breeding the LysM-Cre mice with Acod1flox mice.18 We then inoculated Yumm5.2 or B16-F10 tumor cells into Acod1LyzKO mice and wild-type (WT) Acod1flox littermates. Mirroring tumor progression in global Acod1−/− mice (Figure 5A–C), we observed a reduced tumor progression in Acod1LyzKO mice as compared to WT mice (Figure 5J, K). The data indicate a key protumor role of macrophage-derived itaconate in vivo. To directly test the impact of macrophage-derived itaconate on tumor cells, we cultured B16-F10 tumor cells with medium from bone marrow-derived macrophages. Macrophage conditional medium strongly protected Slc13a3OE B16-F10 cells from RSL3-induced ferroptosis as compared to normal medium (Figure 5L). However, this protection was abolished in Slc13a3−/− B16-F10 cells (Figure S5D). Thus, macrophages serve as the major source of itaconate in the TME and tumor SLC13A3 uptakes TAM-derived itaconate in the TME, enabling tumor progression via itaconate-induced tumor ferroptosis resistance.


### SLC13A3 inhibitor treats ICB resistant tumor and sensitizes tumor ferroptosis
  To subvert tumor resistance to ferroptosis mediated by the itaconate-SLC13A3 axis, we explored structural mimics of itaconate which may competitively block itaconate importing through SLC13A3. The structure of SLC13A3 has not been determined. Based on the crystal structure of SLC13A5 complexed with its ligand PF2 (PDB id: 7JSJ),38 we computationally constructed a homology modeling of SLC13A3 in the Molecular Operating Environment (MOE) (Figure S6A).39 Based on the model, the itaconate substructure-based search revealed 460 potential ligands for SLC13A3 in the eMolecules database (https://www.emolecules.com/) for molecular docking into the homology model and processing to generate refined poses and scores for ranking. Based on the molecular docking into the homology model, we generated refined conformations and scores to rank these potential ligands. We tested the top 10 ranked ligands (itaconate-analogs) for their potential to reverse itaconate-induced Yumm5.2 cell ferroptosis resistance. Among these ligands, 2-(3-methylbenzyl) succinic acid showed a maximal capability of reversing itaconate-induced ferroptosis resistance in Yumm5.2 Slc13a3OE cells (Figure 6A). We appointed 2-(3-methylbenzyl) succinic acid as an SLC13A3 inhibitor (SLC13A3i) (Figure S6B). The potential binding site of SLC13A3i was illustrated in the docking model of SLC13A3 (Figure 6B). Transporter assay confirmed that SLC13A3i inhibited the import of 13C5-labeled itaconate in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 6C). SLC13A3i strongly reversed itaconate-induced cell death resistance to Erastin (Figure 6D) in Slc13a3OE Yumm5.2 cells but did not affect Slc13a3−/− Yumm5.2 cells (Figure S6C), and inhibited itaconate-induced SLC7A11 expression in a dose-dependent manner (Figure S6D). We further investigated the impact of SLC13A3i on tumor progression in vivo. Treatment with SLC13A3i inhibited (Slc13a3+/+) B16-F10 tumor progression in WT (Acod1+/+) C57/BL6 mice (Figure 6E). Interestingly, treatment with SLC13A3i failed to inhibit Slc13a3−/− B16-F10 tumor progression in WT (Acod1+/+) C57/BL6 mice (Figure S6E) and had no effect on Slc13a3+/+ B16-F10 tumor progression in Acod1−/− mice (Figure S6F). Lastly, we evaluated whether SLC13A3i treatment could sensitize B16-F10 tumors to anti-PD-L1 monoclonal antibody (mAb) therapy. In the B16-F10 tumor model, known for its limited response to single ICB treatment, we found that the combination of SLC13A3i and anti-PD-L1 resulted in a superior tumor inhibition compared to single treatment or control (Figure 6F). Additionally, the combination therapy led to a robust T cell response as shown by the high amounts of IFNγ+CD8+ T cells in the TME (Figure 6G). To further support that SLC13A3i targets tumor cells, we showed that SLC13A3i had no effect on IFNγ+ expression in the in vitro activated CD8+ T cells with or without itaconate (Figure S6G).

  To evaluate the tolerability and biosafety of SLC13A3i, we administered a high dose of SLC13A3i alone or in combination with anti-PD-L1 mAb to C57/BL6 mice (Table S4–6 and Figure S6H). Under these conditions, both mouse body weights (Figure S6H) and major organ weights (Table S4) remained stable across treated and control groups. Hematologic parameters showed no signs of toxicity (Table S5). However, we observed an increase in mean platelet counts and focal granulocytes (eosinophil and neutrophil) infiltrated in the gastric submucosa, mucosa, and around pancreatic ducts in two out of five mice treated with SLC13A3i and IgG without any tissue destruction (Table S6). These findings were not indicative of toxicity, as platelet counts remained within physiological ranges and focal eosinophil infiltrates were occasionally observed in rodent stomachs as a background finding. This interpretation was further supported by the absence of similar findings in mice treated with SLC13A3i and anti-PD-L1 mAb. Therefore, the anti-tumor effect of SLC13A3i relies on both tumor SLC13A3 and host itaconate. The data suggest that SLC13A3i may be potentially used as a monotherapy and/or in combination with ICB to enhance tumor responses to ICB.


## Discussion
  In this work we have explored endogenous cellular and molecular mechanisms that endow tumor cell resistance to ferroptosis, protecting them from immune-mediated ferroptotic killing in the TME. We have unexpectedly discovered that TAMs protect tumor cells from ferroptosis, thereby enabling tumor resistance to ICB via the SLC13A3-itaconate-NRF2-SLC7A11 axis.

  The SLC13 family is comprised of cell membrane transporters importing di- and tri-carboxylate Krebs cycle intermediates.17 Interestingly, we found that SLC13A3, unlike other SLC13 family members, is highly and selectively expressed in various tumors and negatively correlated with ICB efficacy and clinical outcomes in several cohorts of patients from different hospitals (including our own institutions) across multiple cancer types. Moreover, we have identified that SLC13A3 is a transporter for itaconate in tumor cells, which endows tumor cell resistance to ferroptosis via distinct molecular mechanisms. The expression pattern, selectivity, and functional importance of SLC13A3 suggest that it is a promising immune metabolic checkpoint for cancer therapy. Genetic ablation or pharmacological inhibition of SLC13A3 increases tumor immunogenicity as shown by increased tumor infiltrating cytotoxic T cells and enriched type-I IFN signaling. We have examined this possibility in tumor bearing animal models and in patients with cancer receiving ICB. Human SLC13A3 and SLC13A5 share 48% amino acid sequences. Given the unknown structure of SLC13A3, we have identified a competitive inhibitor for SLC13A3 (SLC13A3i) based on the crystal structure of SLC13A5 and molecular docking analysis.37 SLC13A3i monotherapy can treat ICB resistant tumor and the combination of SLC13A3i and ICB results in a potent inhibition of tumor progression and bolsters the effectiveness of ICB in murine models. The anti-tumor effect of SLC13A3i relies on tumor SLC13A3 and host ACOD1. Thus, our work demonstrates that SLC13A3 is a previously undefined itaconate transporter in tumors, and a potential therapeutic target and disease biomarker for treating SLC13A3+ cancers. The biochemical, immunological, and therapeutic insights we have gained on SLC13A3 and SLC13A3i, may pave the way for the testing, development, and design of SLC13A3 inhibitors or blocking antibody in future studies and clinical trials. The topology predictions of SLC13A3 displays 6 extracellular loops. Targeting these loops with potential antibodies would provide an insight into the major functional epitope responsible for its transport mechanism aiding ferroptosis and ICB resistance. These antibodies may specifically inhibit SLC13A3-mediated itaconate uptake in cancer cells either by competitive blocking or by inducing a conformational change.

  Itaconate has been relatively well-studied in the context of inflammation and infection.18,20 Recent studies have emphasized the pro-tumor effects of several metabolites in the TME.4,8–10 Supporting this line of scientific inquiry, we have detected high levels of itaconate in the TME. Moreover, we have found that TAMs are the major sources of itaconate in the TME in mouse tumors and several human cancer types. Several studies have shown the pro-tumor effect of itaconate.40–44 While it is unclear how T cells import itaconate, evidence suggests that itaconate can be taken up by activated murine CD8+ T cells, inhibiting T cell proliferation and function.42 Additionally, neutrophils can express high levels of ACOD1 (IRG1), producing and consuming their own intracellular itaconate, leading to neutrophil accumulation and promoting tumor metastasis.43 Prior to our current work, if and how tumor cells consume itaconate in the TME and the immunopathological consequences of itaconate inside tumors were unknown. Our study addresses these questions. We demonstrate that itaconate transporter SLC13A3 is predominantly and functionally expressed in both human and mouse tumor cells. This allows tumor cells to efficiently uptake exogenous itaconate from TAMs to escape ferroptotic cell death. Thus, we reveal a previously unreported immune evasion and ICB resistant mechanism by which the interplay between TAMs and tumor cells endows tumor ferroptosis resistance via SLC13A3.

  We have assessed the underlying mechanisms involved in crosstalk between TAMs and tumor cells via the itaconate-SLC13A3 axis. Cytosolic itaconate binds to the cysteine residue of Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1), leading to the activation of NRF2 in macrophages.45 NRF2 is the master transcriptional factor that regulates the antioxidant gene network.29 Consistent with the study on macrophages,45 we show that itaconate can activate the NRF2-SLC7A11 axis in tumor cells, thereby endowing tumor cell resistance to ferroptosis. Notably, tumor cells do not produce itaconate, rather import extracellular itaconate from TAMs via SLC13A3. This reinforces the notion that the interplay between TAMs and tumor cells is critical for programming the tumor immune microenvironment and ICB response. ICB triggers tumor ferroptosis, shaping the fate of tumor cells and the efficacy of ICB.14,22,28,46,47 Indeed, during ICB and radiation therapy, activated immune cell derived IFNγ and specific fatty acids are endogenous ferroptosis inducers in vivo.14,22,28 Given that most patients do not respond to ICB, our current work suggests an intriguing possibility that the acquisition of ferroptosis resistance via SLC13A3 contributes to ICB resistance. Thus, SLC13A3 builds a metabolic bridge between TAMs and tumor cells, resulting in tumor escape from ferroptosis and thereby causing ICB resistance. In summary, our work identifies the SLC13A3-itaconate-NRF2-SLC7A11 axis as a previously unknown immune evasion mechanism in the TME and SLC13A3 as a promising target for combating cancer.

### EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

#### Human studies
  Cohort 1 is a published cohort.24 We recruited Cohort 2 from HCC patients who received immune checkpoint inhibitor at CHA Bundang Medical Center, Korea, between June 2020 and December 2021 (Table S1, S2). All patients had histologically or radiologically confirmed unresectable or metastatic HCC according to the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLC) criteria, had no history of systemic anticancer treatment, and had sufficient baseline tumor tissues for transcriptomic analysis. All patients received first-line atezolizumab and bevacizumab immunotherapy. Objective response to immunotherapy was assessed according to Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1 (RECIST1.1)49 using computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging at 6–9-week intervals. Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the time interval from the initiation of treatment to disease progression or the last follow-up. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time interval from treatment initiation to death or the last follow-up. ### METHOD DETAILS

#### In vivo experiments
  For the tumor models (B16-F10, Yumm5.2, MT3, and MC38), 0.2–2 10^6 tumor cells were subcutaneously injected into the left flank of the mice. Tumor dimensions were regularly measured with calipers, and tumor volume was calculated accordingly. In itaconate blocking experiments, treatment was initiated when tumors reached approximately 100 mm3. 2-(3-methoxybenzyl) succinic acid (SLC13A3i) and controls were administered intraperitoneally daily at a dose of 10 mg/kg mouse body weight throughout the study.

  Anti-PD-L1 and IgG1 isotype monoclonal antibodies were administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 100 μg per mouse on day 3 after tumor cell inoculation, followed by subsequent dosing every 3 days for the duration of the experiment.

  To evaluate tolerability and toxicity, C57/BL6 mice received daily intraperitoneal injections of 20 mg/kg SLC13A3i, either alone or in combination with anti-PD-L1 mAb or IgG (100 μg per mouse every 3 days) for 21 days. Body weights, organ weights, organ histology, and hematological parameters were assessed at the ULAM Pathology Core (RRID: SCR_018823).


#### Flow cytometry analysis (FACS)
  Cell death was assessed by flow cytometry using 7-aminoactinomycin D (7-AAD) to identify 7-AAD-positive cells. For lipid oxidation detection, cells were stained with a lipid peroxidation sensor and C11-BODIPY dye in Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) and incubated at 37°C for 20 minutes. After washing with cold HBSS buffer, the ratio of oxidized C11-BODIPY to total C11-BODIPY was determined using FACS. For the lipid uptake detection, cells were added with a lipid probe, BODIPY FL C12, and cultured at 37°C for 20 minutes in a 5% CO2 incubator. After washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), the median fluorescence intensity (MFI) was determined using FACS. For the intracellular Fe2+ detection, cells were added with a fluorescent probe, FerroOrange, and incubated on ice for 20 minutes. After washing with PBS, the MFI was determined using FACS.

  T cell phenotyping was determined in single-cell suspensions from fresh tumor tissues. Cells were stained with specific antibodies against mouse CD45 (30-F11), CD90 (53–2.1), CD8 (53–6.7), and CD4 (GK1.5) to define CD45+CD90+CD8+ T cells and CD45+CD90+CD4+ T cells. T cell cytokine expression was assessed through intracellular staining using antibodies against mouse IFNγ (XMG1.2), and TNFα (MP6-XT22) from eBioscience or BD Biosciences. Flow cytometry was performed on an LSR Fortessa (BD), and data were analyzed using DIVA software (BD Biosciences).


#### Macrophages (MФs) and conditional medium
  Bone marrow was obtained from the hind legs of mice. Erythrocytes were lysed with Red Blood Cell Lysis Buffer (Sigma Aldrich). Bone marrow-derived macrophages (MФs) were generated from bone marrow cells with M-CSF (20 ng/ml). Culture medium was half changed every 3 days. On day 7, macrophages were stimulated with 10 ng/ml TNFα and 10 ng/ml IFNγ for 48 hours. The cultured medium was collected and centrifuged with 3 kD Microcon centrifugal filters (Sigma Aldrich) to remove cytokines from the culture.


#### In vitro T cell activation
  Splenocytes (1×10^6/ml) from C57B/6J mice were activated for 72 hours with anti-CD3 (ebioscience, 5 μg/ml) and anti-CD28 (ebioscience, 2.5 μg/ml) or other indicated conditions with 1 mM itaconate or 1.5 mM SLC13A3 inhibitor treatments. T cells were subjected to surface marker and intracellular cytokine staining and analyzed by FACS.


#### Ferroptosis detection
  To test the impact of di- and tri-carboxylate metabolites on ferroptosis, 1×10^5 Yumm5.2 Slc13a3OE cells were seeded into wells of a 6-well plate in the presence of 1 mM of individual metabolites. After 48 hours, 5×10^3 cells were seeded into wells of 96-well plates with quadruplicates in the presence of 2 μM Erastin for additional 16 hours. The cell death proportion was analyzed by the AlamarBlueTM Cell Viability assay.

  To examine the impact of itaconate and Slc13a3 on ferroptosis, 1×10^5 Slc13a3+/+ or Slc13a3−/− cells were seeded into the wells of a 6-well plate and treated with control reagent and 2–5 mM itaconate for four days, or 100 μM 4-octyl itaconate (OI) for 2 days. To evaluate the functionality of Slc13a3i, 1×10^5 Yumm5.2 Slc13a3OE cells were seeded into the wells of a 6-well plate, pretreated with Slc13a3i for 30 minutes, and then added with 1 mM itaconate for two days. After the treatments, 2×10^4 cells were re-seeded into wells of a 24-well plate in triplicates. After cells were attached, ferroptosis inducers (Erastin 2–3 μM or RSL3 0.1–0.5 μM) were added for additional 16 hours. Cell death was assessed by flow cytometry using 7-aminoactinomycin D (7-AAD) to identify 7-AAD-positive cells.


#### CRISPR gene targeting and gene overexpression
  CRISPR gene targeting utilized the Double-Nickase plasmid (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc) for mouse Slc13a3 (sc-431118-NIC), human SLC13A3 (sc-416648-NIC), and mouse Slc7a11 (sc-424104-NIC). Successful gene targeting was confirmed by sequencing each cell clone or verification of protein levels through Western Blotting. Multiple deficient clones were pooled.

  SLC13A3 overexpression was achieved by transfecting B16-F10 and Yumm5.2 cells with lentivirus encoding Slc13a3 (VectorBuilder Inc) or a scrambled control. Following transfection, cells were selected with puromycin for 7 days, tested for SLC13A3 expression, and cultured for in vivo experiments.


#### Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining
  Heat-induced epitope retrieval in a pH9 buffer and endogenous peroxidase quenching were performed. Rabbit anti-human polyclonal primary anti-SLC13A3 antibody (Invitrogen, PA5–53224) was applied to an unstained TMA slide (TissueArray_ME204) at a dilution of 1:100 for 4°C overnight. Staining was detected by a biotin-free polymer-based alkaline phosphatase (AP) reagent system (double stain polymer RDS513 and Universal HRP Tertiary Reagent, IPT5002, BioCare Medical) and a red chromogen (IP Fast Red, IPK5017, BioCare Medical). TMA slides were converted to pyramidal digital image files using a digital slide scanner (AT2, Leica Aperio Biosystems) at scanning magnifications of up to 20x (0.5 μm/pixel). Quantitative assessment of immunohistochemical staining in the digitized slide files was performed using the open-source program QuPath v0.4.3.53 Results were reported as the percent of all tumor cells having positivity for the chromogen at levels of 1+ (weak), 2+ (moderate), 3+ (strong), and total positivity. H-scores were calculated as (1 × %1+ staining) + (2 × %2+ staining) + (3 × %3+ staining), giving a potential range of 0 (all cells negative) to 300 (all cells strongly positive).


#### Real-time PCR
  Total RNA extraction utilized TRIzo LS Reagent (Invitrogen, 10296010), and cDNA synthesis was performed with 0.5–1 μg of total RNA using the RevertAid RT Reverse Transcription Kit (Invitrogen K1691). mRNA levels were quantified with gene-specific primers and SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Invitroge, 4368702). Results were normalized to GAPDH using the following primers:

#### Western blotting
  For immunoblot analysis, whole-cell lysates were prepared in RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Scientific, 89900) supplemented with Halt Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Thermo Scientific, 78429). Protein concentrations were quantified using BCA protein assay kits (Pierce, 23227). Subsequently, 20 μg of protein samples were loaded onto SDS-PAGE and transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane. We sourced antibodies from Cell Signaling Technology, Inc: Immune blotting antibodies included anti-SLC7A11 (D2M7A or #98051), anti-PERK (C33E10), anti-NRF2 (D1Z9C), anti-GAPDH (D16H11), and β-ACTIN (D6A8). For SLC13A3 detection, we used an anti-SLC13A3 antibody generated against the extracellular loop 4 of SLC13A3 in the Zhiqiang An lab.


#### RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)
  Yumm5.2 cells underwent treatment with permeable itaconate or DMSO reagent control and were cultured for 20 hours. Total RNA extraction utilized the Direct-zol RNA Miniprep Plus kit (Zymo Research), and mRNA libraries were prepared using Illumina TruSeq technology. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NextSeq sequencer at 150 bp, paired-end reads with an approximate yield of 30 million reads per sample. Each condition was independently sequenced in four experiments. Gene categories were chosen based on primary function or GSEA reference. Differential gene expression was calculated using DESeq2,54 and significance was determined with an adjusted P value per the DESeq2 algorithm (< 0.05).


#### Single-cell RNA-seq
  Samples were fixed and dissociated using the Miltenyi Octo MACS according to 10X Demonstrated Protocol #CG000553. Dissociated cells were counted on Luna Fx7 (Logos Biosystems). Probes were hybridized, and samples were pooled and processed according to manufacturer instructions for 10X Genomics Chromium Fixed RNA Profiling Reagent Kits for Multiplexed Samples (PN 1000568). Final library quality was assessed using the Lab Chip GXII HT (PerkinElmer), and libraries were quantified by Qubit (Thermo Fisher). Pooled libraries were subjected to paired-end sequencing according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Illumina Nova-Seq X Plus). BCL Convert Software (Illumina) was used to generate de-multiplexed Fastq files, and the Cell Ranger Pipeline (10X Genomics) was used to align reads and generate count matrices.


#### Metabolites detection
  Metabolites were detected by IC-MS in the MD Anderson Cancer Center Metabolomics Core. To detect endogenous itaconate in the tumor interstitial fluids, tumor tissues were divided with scissors, wrapped with a 5-mm nylon filter paper (Sterlitech, WA), stuffed into a 1.5-ml conical tube, and centrifuged at 4,000 r.p.m. for 2 hours to collect the interstitial fluids.

  In the in vitro itaconate uptake experiments, 10^6 Yumm5.2 Slc13a3OE cells were seeded into the 25T flask and cultured at 37°C overnight in a 5% CO2 incubator. 0–200 μM 13C5 itaconate was added to the culture medium and incubated for 10 minutes at 37 °C. Cells were quickly washed twice with 150 mM cold ammonium acetate and quenched by pouring liquid nitrogen.

  To evaluate the functionality of Slc13a3i, cells were pretreated with Slc13a3i for 30 minutes. Then 100 μM 13C5 itaconate was added to the culture medium and incubated for 10 minutes at 37 °C. Cells were quickly washed twice with 150 mM cold ammonium acetate and quenched by pouring liquid nitrogen.

  In the in vivo itaconate uptake experiments, 200 μM 13C5 itaconate was intratumorally injected into B16F10 Slc13a3+/+ or Slc13a3−/− tumors with similar sizes. After 30 minutes, fresh tumor tissues were collected and enzymatically digested into single-cell suspensions. We separated the cells with the two-step density gradient by overlying 75% and 100% Ficoll and collected for upper layer cells. We washed the cells with cold PBS and stored them at −80 °C before proceeding to the next detection step. The final reads were normalized by the DNA concentration of each sample and combined from two experiments by internal normalization. Outliers were removed based on the extremely low or high DNA concentration.

  In the cystine uptake experiments, 0.5 × 10^6 Slc7a11−/−, Slc7a11+/+, Slc13a3−/−, or Slc13a3OE Yumm5.2 cells were seeded into a 75T flask with 100 μM OI or 2 mM itaconate for 48 hours. 0.5 × 10^6 cells were seeded into wells of 6-well plates with triplicates and cultured at 37°C overnight in a 5% CO2 incubator. Cells were washed and cultured in cystine-free medium at 37 °C for 30 minutes. Then, medium was replaced with 50 μM 13C2 cystine medium. Cells were incubated for 30 minutes at 37 °C, quickly washed twice with 150 mM cold ammonium acetate, and quenched by pouring liquid nitrogen. The final uptake was counted from the sum abundance reads of 13C2 cystine and 13C1 cysteine.

#### SLC13A3 homology modeling and Ligand docking
  We used the Molecular Operating Environment (MOE) to conduct homology modeling and ligand docking based on the crystal structure complex of SLC13A5 with its ligand PF2 (PDB id: 7JSJ).38 We imported human SLC13A3 (UniProt: Q8WWT9) and SLC13A5 (Q86YT5) protein sequences into MOE and aligned them to match the 7JSJ SLC13A5 template. Incorporating the itaconate substructure-based search, 460 potential SLC13A3 ligands were selected from eMolecules (https://www.emolecules.com/) and prepared for docking into the Chain A pocket of the homology model. The docking procedure involved ligand conformations superimposed onto the protein binding site based on the substructure of the crystallographic ligand. We conducted additional conformational sampling of the docked ligand after removing the crystallographic ligand and then minimized its energy while maintaining the protein’s rigidity. This process generated refined poses and scores for ranking. The prioritization of potential SLC13A3 competitive ligands for functional screening was based on binding scores, docking pose visualization, and compound availability.


#### Bioinformatics analysis
  RNA-seq data analysis in human patients: Transcriptomic analysis included a reported cohort of metastasis melanoma patients treated with ICB (Cohort 1),24 a cohort of patients with advanced HCC treated with immunotherapy at CHA Bundang Medical Centre, Korea (Cohort 2, Table S1,S2), and a cohort from the University of Michigan (UM) of patients with head and neck cancer, bladder (urothelial carcinoma), and melanoma (Cohort 3, Table S3). For the HCC cohort (Cohort 2), RNA extraction and RNA sequencing was performed using the QuantSeq 30 mRNA-sequencing kit (Lexogen, Austria) following the previous protocol.55 The median SLC13A3 expression level in tumor tissue were used as a cut-off value to distinguish between SLC13A3-high and SLC13A3-low tumors. For the UM cohort (Cohort 3), the Maxstar R package was implemented to determine the impact of SLC13A3 expression on survival among the multiple tumor types in the UM cohort. Three tumor types independently yielded patient stratifications, in which low SLC13A3 was associated with prolonged overall survival. Data for patients bearing these three tumor types were combined, and Maxstat was implemented again to determine the appropriate stratification by SLC13A3 expression. We used unpaired, nonparametric t-tests to compare SLC13 transcripts between responders (CR, PR, and SD) and non-responders (PD).

  Single-cell RNA-seq analysis for human datasets: melanoma (GSE72056, GSE115978)25, 26 and colon cancer (GSE146771)27 were downloaded from the GEO database. The SLC13A3 and ACOD1 expression matrix was processed and analyzed by the Seurat package. A dot plot was used to show specific gene expression.

  Single-cell RNA-seq analysis of mouse melanoma tumors: Raw read data were aligned to the mouse reference genome, GRCm38-2020A. Feature barcode matrices from the Cell Ranger multi-pipeline were used for downstream analysis with the Seurat R package (version 4.3.2). To enhance the robustness and reproducibility, triplicates were designed for Slc13a3−/− and wild-type Slc13a3+/+ conditions. Quality control measures included the exclusion of cells with fewer than 200 detected genes and those with over 20% mitochondrial RNA content. After quality control, the Slc13a3−/− and Slc13a3+/+ sample triplicates yielded 25,059 and 25,644 cells, respectively. Filtered gene counts were log normalized with the “NormalizeData” function in Seurat. The top 2,000 highly variable genes were identified with Seurat’s ‘vst’ method. PCA was performed on the above-mentioned scaled preprocessed data for dimension reduction. Unsupervised clustering was done by the graph based “Find Clusters” function, with a resolution of 0.5. The embeddings were visualized using UMAP. Four major clusters were identified from the unsupervised clustering: melanoma cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and immune cells. The cluster identity was assigned based on the log2 transformed expression levels of canonical markers.56 The melanoma cluster exhibited high expression levels of Mlana (Mlana > 1) and Pmel (Pmel > 1), along with low expression of immune-related genes (Ptprc ⩽ 0.2, Gzmb < 1, Prf1 < 1). The fibroblast cluster is marked by the exclusive expression of Pecam1. The endothelial cluster is marked by the exclusive expression of Col1a2. The immune cluster is characterized by high expression of Ptprc (Ptprc > 0.2). Re-clustering of the immune cells with a resolution of 0.8 further identified the detailed composition of immune cells: cytotoxic lymphocytes (Cd8a, Nkg7, Prf1), myeloid cells (Ms4a7), and dendritic cells (Clec9a).

  To explore the biological pathways impacted by SLC13A3, differentially expressed genes between Slc13a3−/− and Slc13a3+/+ conditions were identified in all four cell types using the “Find Markers” function in Seurat. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using GSEA (version 4.1.0)57 on the differentially expressed genes. A two-sided Student’s t-test was used to assess the statistical significance of differences in the average expression levels of ferroptosis-resistance signatures14 (Txn1, Gclc, Txnrd1, Slc1a5, Acsl3, Slc7a11, Nqo1) between Slc13a3−/− and Slc13a3+/+ conditions.


### QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS
  Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software. Two independent groups were compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum and two-tailed t-tests or a two-way ANOVA for tumor growth. Survival functions were estimated employing the Kaplan-Meier methods, and comparisons were made utilizing log-rank test. A significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Sample size determination was based on animal experimental trials and considered previous publications on similar experiments to ensure robust statistical analysis. Unless otherwise specified, the samples analyzed were independent biological replicates.
