## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific content, technical soundness, and claims as presented in the provided manuscript text
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors present a semi-rational engineering strategy combining evolutionary analysis, structural prediction, and mutagenesis to generate transcriptional biosensors with high molecular specificity for tryptophan metabolites and analogs, specifically IAA, IAAld, TRM, IPyA, and PPyA, in probiotic E. coli Nissle 1917.
- **Visible evidence base** Main text figures referenced (Figures 1–6), supplementary figures (Figures S1–S15), supplementary tables (Tables S1–S5), experimental procedures section
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Actual figure images, supplementary data files (Data S1), raw flow cytometry data, detailed plasmid maps, and full supplementary tables were not provided for independent verification

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript describes a systematic approach to engineering transcriptional biosensors with improved ligand specificity for tryptophan metabolites. The work combines computational structural prediction with experimental mutagenesis to generate variants of TrpR and FeaR with altered specificity profiles. The authors demonstrate a "plug-and-play" modular strategy that couples enzyme modules with ligand-specific transcription factors to expand the detectable analyte range. While the engineering workflow is logical and the results are potentially useful for the synthetic biology community, several technical concerns regarding data presentation, statistical rigor, and validation depth need to be addressed before the broader claims of generalizability and practical utility are fully established.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in synthetic biology, metabolic engineering, and biosensor development would find this work relevant. Those working on gut microbiome diagnostics, point-of-care testing, and microbial therapeutics would also be interested given the demonstrated detection of gut-microbiota-derived metabolites in a probiotic strain. The modular engineering strategy may appeal to groups developing transcription factor-based biosensors for other ligand classes.
- **Major strengths** The systematic approach combining evolutionary analysis with structure-guided mutagenesis is well conceived. The demonstration of ligand-specific variants through iterative rounds of rational design and screening is compelling. The modular "plug-and-play" concept, where enzyme modules convert non-target substrates into detectable analytes, extends the utility of the biosensor platform. The identification of previously uncharacterized regulatory mechanisms within ligand-binding domains adds fundamental insight.
- **Major Concerns** 
  - R1-M1: The claim of "exclusive" or "specific" detection is not fully supported by the data presented in the text. The authors report cross-reactivity patterns and interference from structurally similar compounds, yet the language in the summary and discussion implies a higher degree of specificity than the results may warrant.
  - R1-M2: The quantitative performance metrics of the engineered biosensors (detection limits, dynamic ranges, response times) are described qualitatively in several instances. The text mentions "more than 1,000-fold increase" and "detection limits as low as 50 μM" but does not provide systematic comparison across all variants and conditions.
  - R1-M3: The structural predictions and docking simulations are used to explain experimental observations, but the validation of these computational models is limited. The authors acknowledge that the highest-ranked conformations sometimes failed to align with empirical data, raising questions about the reliability of the structural interpretations.
  - R1-M4: The physiological relevance of the detection limits is not thoroughly discussed. For gut metabolite monitoring applications, the relevant concentration ranges in intestinal environments should be compared with the sensor detection limits.
  - R1-M5: The manuscript does not clearly address the stability and reproducibility of the engineered biosensors over time or across different growth conditions, which is important for practical applications.
- **Minor Comments**
  - R1-m1: The abstract and introduction could benefit from a clearer statement of the specific gap in existing biosensor technology that this work addresses, particularly regarding the limitations of previously reported TrpR and FeaR variants.
  - R1-m2: The description of the "plug-and-play" strategy in the results section would be enhanced by a schematic figure that clearly illustrates the modular assembly of enzyme and regulator components.
  - R1-m3: The discussion of TynA enzyme variants would benefit from a more explicit comparison of the kinetic parameters of TynAMG versus TynAKP, as the text mentions "similar substrate promiscuity" but does not provide quantitative kinetic data.
  - R1-m4: The manuscript would benefit from a table summarizing the key performance characteristics (detection limit, dynamic range, specificity) of each engineered biosensor variant for easy comparison.
  - R1-m5: The authors mention that "traditional saturation mutagenesis proved invaluable" but do not provide details on the library sizes or screening throughput, which would be useful for others attempting to replicate the approach.
  - R1-m6: The potential toxicity or growth defects associated with certain enzyme variants (e.g., TynA from K. aerogenes) are mentioned but not systematically characterized.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** R1-M1 (specificity claims), R1-M2 (quantitative performance metrics), R1-M3 (structural model validation)
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** 
  - Originality: The combination of evolutionary analysis, structural prediction, and mutagenesis for biosensor engineering is not entirely novel, but the systematic application to tryptophan metabolites and the identification of specificity-determining residues in both homodimeric and monomeric regulators has original elements.
  - Scientific importance: The work addresses a real need for specific detection of structurally similar gut metabolites. The modular strategy has potential broader applicability, but the demonstrated scope is limited to a few analytes.
  - Interdisciplinary readership: The work bridges microbiology, protein engineering, and biosensor design. The relevance to clinical diagnostics and metabolic engineering broadens the potential audience, though the technical depth may limit accessibility.
  - Technical soundness: The experimental approach is generally sound, but the lack of quantitative rigor in some performance characterizations and the limited validation of computational predictions weaken the technical foundation.
  - Readability for nonspecialists: The manuscript is clearly written but assumes familiarity with transcription factor mechanisms and biosensor design. The figures are referenced appropriately, though the actual figure content could not be assessed.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The core engineering strategy is promising and the results are potentially valuable, but the specificity claims need to be tempered or better supported, quantitative performance data should be systematically presented, and the structural interpretations require additional validation or more cautious framing.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim of "exclusive" biosensing of specific analytes is not fully supported; the text itself describes cross-reactivity and interference.
- The statement that the strategy is "generalizable" is based on application to two transcription factor families and a limited set of analytes; broader generalizability is asserted rather than demonstrated.
- The claim that the biosensors enable "point-of-care diagnostics" is speculative, as no clinical or complex biological matrix testing is described.
- The structural mechanisms proposed for ligand specificity changes are based on computational predictions that the authors acknowledge sometimes disagree with experimental data.
- The statement that the IPyA sensor is "dedicated to IPyA" is qualified by the text noting responses to TRP and IPA through endogenous conversion pathways, making the exclusivity claim ambiguous.
- The detection limit of 50 μM for the IAA sensor is described as "as low as," but the physiological relevance of this concentration in gut environments is not established.
