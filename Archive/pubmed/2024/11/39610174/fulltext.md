# Vitamin E Mitigates
Apoptosis in Ovarian Granulosa
Cells by Inhibiting Zearalenone-Induced Activation of the PERK/eIF-2α/ATF4/Chop
Signaling Pathway


## Abstract
  

  A study aimed to investigate the signaling pathway of zearalenone (ZEA) leading to the apoptosis of ovarian granulosa cells (GCs) and explore the potential of vitamin E (VE) in alleviating ZEA-induced apoptosis of GCs. We constructed an apoptosis model for GCs based on exposure to the environmental toxin ZEA. Transcriptome analysis revealed that ZEA induced endoplasmic reticulum stress by activating the ATF4–Chop pathway. The addition of inhibitors targeting the estrogen receptor (ER) demonstrated that ZEA activates the ATF4–Chop pathway through ER-beta. As a strong antioxidant, VE is thought to mitigate ZEA-induced toxicity. Interestingly, molecular docking analysis at the PERK active site of the endoplasmic reticulum stress revealed a high binding capacity of VE. VE supplementation reduced apoptosis in GCs and decreased the expression of p-eIF-2α, ATF4, and Chop. Mouse tests also demonstrated that VE supplementation effectively mitigated ovarian dysfunction induced by ZEA, as evidenced by increased body weight gain, reduced oxidative stress, and decreased cell death. In summary, the present study demonstrates that ZEA activates the PERK–eIF-2α–ATF4–Chop pathway through ERβ, leading to endoplasmic reticulum stress and apoptosis of GCs. Conversely, VE inhibits the PERK/eIF-2α/ATF4/Chop signaling pathways, mitigating endoplasmic reticulum stress and improving ZEA-induced reproductive toxicity.


## Introduction
  Zearalenone (ZEA), a mycotoxin produced by Fusarium fungi,12 is known to cause damage to animals and humans.34 As an environmental estrogen, ZEA is primarily consumed through corn and other cereals, resulting in reproductive toxicity in organisms.5 In mammalian studies, ingestion of ZEA has been shown to notably decrease the number and weight of fetuses, as well as reduce placental weight in pregnant mice.6 Furthermore, in human studies, the consumption of ZEA during prepuberty has been linked to the occurrence of precocious puberty in girls.7 And reproductive toxicity of ZEA on organisms has been a prominent area of study in the field of ZEA toxicity.

  A recent study utilizing single cell sequencing techniques revealed that ZEA exposure primarily impacts female germ cells and granulosa cells (GCs), as opposed to other cell types such as endometrial cells.8 Ovarian GCs are integral to follicular growth and development, as well as reproductive processes.910 Research has demonstrated that these cells are crucial in determining the fate of follicles, influencing factors such as nutrition and apoptosis.11 Studies have shown that ZEA can disrupt the development and steroidogenesis of ovarian GCs, ultimately impacting female fertility.1213 Specifically, ZEA has been shown to induce apoptosis in ovarian GCs, potentially leading to follicle atresia and other reproductive disorders in females.14

  The structural similarity to endogenous estrogens, enables ZEA to bind to estrogen receptors (ERs), with subsequent exertion of estrogen-like effects.15 ER-alpha (ERα) and ER-beta (ERβ) exhibit distinct functions in various organs and cell types. For instance, ZEA modulates the proliferation, invasion, and migration of cancer cells in the prostate through ERα,16 whereas ZEA also induced oxidative stress in prostate epithelial cells through ERβ.15 Previous research has established the impact of ZEA on ovarian development via ERs,17 yet the specific influence of ZEA on apoptosis of ovarian GCs through ER remains unclear.

  Vitamin E (VE), found abundantly in various food sources such as nuts and vegetable oils, is known for its role in in vivo redox interactions.18 One study demonstrated a cytoprotective effect when cells were pretreated with the antioxidant agent VE prior to ZEA exposure.19 Another study showed that VE can mitigate 30–50% of the toxic effects of ZEA by functioning as an antioxidant.20 Additionally, VE also has the potential to preserve ovarian structure, reduce oxidative damage, and alleviate the toxic effects of external stimuli on the ovary.

  In this research, a model of ovarian GCs was developed to investigate the signaling pathway involved in ZEA-induced apoptosis and to explore the role played by ZEA-targeted binding to the ER. And VE was addition to examine its potential in mitigating the impact of ZEA on GCs apoptosis through in vivo and in vitro experiments. The aim of this study was to explore the possible pathway of GCs apoptosis induced by the environmental estrogen ZEA and to search for a dietary intervention that could alleviate this pathway.


## Materials and Methods

### Reagents and Antibodies
  Zearalenone was purchased from PUSH Bio-Technology (Chengdu, China). CCK-8 (Beyotime, China), DMSO (Gibco, USA), RIPA cell lysate (Solarbio Life Science, China), BCA protein concentration kit (ThermoFisher, USA), SDS-PAGE gel preparation kit (BBI, China), cell cycle and apoptosis analysis kit (Beyotime, China) were used in this study. Mouse monoclonal anti-GAPDH (60004–1–1g), rabbit polyclonal antibody phospho-eukaryotic initiation factor 2α (eIF-2α) (28740–1-AP), mouse antibody activated transcription factor 4 (ATF4) (60035-1-1g), mouse antibody CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein homologous protein (Chop) (66741-1-1g), HRP-conjugated AffiniPure Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (SA00001-2) and HRP-conjugated AffiniPure Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (SA00001-1) were all purchased from Proteintech (Wuhan, China). Vitamin E (≥98.0%) was purchased from Solarbio (Beijing, China).

  ERα antagonist 1,3-bis(4-hydroxyphenyl)-4-methyl-5- [4-(2-piperidinylethoxy) phenol]-1H-pyrazole dihydrochloride (MPP) was purchased from Sigma (USA, M7068). ERβ antagonist 4-[2-phenyl-5,7-bis (trifluoromethyl) pyrazolo [1,5-a]-pyrimidin-3-yl] phenol (PHTPP) was purchased from Selleck (USA, S8686).


### Cell Culture
  Murine immortalized KK1 granulosa cells were kindly donated by Professor Lei An (China Agricultural University). They were cultured in Ham‘s-F12 nutrient medium (Gibco) containing with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Cat#30044333, Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) and 1% antibiotics (Antibiotic-Antimycotic, Cat#C0224, Beyotime, Shanghai, China), and were incubated in 95% humidity and 5% CO2 at 37 °C.


### Calcein/PI Cell Activity and Cytotoxicity
Assay
  After inoculating the KK1 cells on cell crawlers, the cells were dosed. The KK1 cells were stained by adding an appropriate volume of Calcein AM/PI assay (Beyotime, China) working solution, and the staining effect was observed under a fluorescence microscope at the end of incubation. Live cells show green fluorescence and apoptotic cells show red fluorescence.


### Transcriptome Analysis
  Total RNA was extracted from the KK1 cells (n = 3) using TRIzol Reagent (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions, and DNA was removed from genomic DNA using DNase I (Takara) RNA degradation and monitored for contamination on 1% agarose gels. Then, the TruSeq RNA sample preparation Kit from Illumina (Dan Diego, CA) was used for transcriptome database. After Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencer sequencing, each sample was quality controlled and reads mapped. The raw data obtained were subjected to differential expression analysis and functional enrichment.


### Mice
  Six-weeks of age female ICR mice were purchased from SiPeiFu Inc. (Beijing, China). Animals were housed under standard specified pathogen-free laboratory conditions, and received food and water (tap water) ad libitum.


### Animal Test Design
  The animal experimental procedure depicted in Figure 5A involved randomly dividing 40 healthy female ICR mice into four groups (n = 10) based on body weight: a control group (CON, fed basal diet), a ZEA group (fed 10 mg ZEN/kg basal diet), a VE group (fed 500 mg VE/kg basal diet), and a ZEA + VE group (fed 10 mg ZEA and 500 mg VE/kg basal diet). Special feeds (ZEA and VE were dissolved in methanol and diluted with ultrapure water, sprayed on the surface of the feed ingredients. The pellets were then made using cold granulation and the vacuum packed pellets were fed to mice as feed.) were provided by the Beijing Keao Xieli Feed Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China). Prior to the commencement of the formal experiment, the mice underwent adaptive housing for 1 week, with the experiment itself spanning a duration of 28 days. The mice were housed under controlled conditions at a temperature range of 21–23 °C, a relative humidity of 35–65%, and a 12-h light/dark cycle. The concentrations of ZEA and VE administered were determined based on previous studies and reports.2122


### Histopathological Examination
  The ovaries of the mice were removed and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. After embedding the entire ovary with a paraffin block, it was cut into 5-μm sectioned slices and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Images of the H&E staining were captured using the Intelligent Tissue Sectional Imaging Analysis System (Nikon Eclipse E100, Tokyo, Japan) and an Upright optical microscope (Nikon DS-U3, Tokyo, Japan).


### TUNEL Staining
  Ovarian tissues that had been paraffin-embedded were resectioned for TUNEL staining. The sections were deparaffinized by xylene and hydrated with gradient alcohol, then treated with methanol solution containing 0.2% H2O2 dropwise for 10 min at room temperature; TUNEL reaction mixture was added dropwise and incubated at 37 °C for 60 min; the nuclei of the cells were restained by dropwise addition DAPI working solution and treated for 5 min at room temperature. The cells were observed and photographed under the fluorescence microscope (Nikon Eclipse C1, Tokyo, Japan), and five nonrepeat fields were randomly selected, and the rate of TUNEL positive cell was calculated (average number of positive cells/total number of cells × 100%).


### qRT-PCR
  Following RNA extraction, 1 μg of RNA was reverse-transcribed using HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and subjected to qPCR analysis using Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) with primer sequences provided in Table S1 (Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The PCR cycling conditions were as follows: 2 min at 95 °C for polymerase activation, followed by 40 cycles of 15 s at 95 °C and 30 s at 60 °C. Primer specificity was validated through concentration gradient dilution and standard curve analysis, and the 2-ΔΔCt method was employed for qRT-PCR data analysis.


### Western Blot
  KK1 cells were cotreated with ZEA (30 μM) and VE (100 μM) for 24 h. Total cellular proteins were extracted from cells and mice ovaries using protein lysate from Solarbio Life Science (China) and the protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit from Thermo Fisher (USA). The proteins were detached using SDS-PAGE and transferred to a PVDF membrane (0.22 μm). Subsequently, the membrane was blocked with TBST containing 5% skimmed dry milk (phosphorylated strips) and incubated with primary antibodies including anti-GAPDH (1:8000), antiphospho-eIF-2α (1:10,000), anti-ATF4 (1:3000), and anti-Chop (1:5000). Then the membrane underwent three washes with TBST before being probed with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody. The target protein was detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence system from BIO-RAD (USA).


### Molecular Docking
  The 2D structure of VE was generated using ChemDraw 19.0 and subsequently converted into a 3D structure using Chem3D 19.0. The putative target of VE was prepared for molecular docking studies using AutoDockTools 1.5.7, wherein water molecules were removed and hydrogen atoms and charges were added. The 3D structures of PERK core target proteins from the RCSB Protein Data Bank were also prepared for docking studies using AutoDockTools 1.5.7. Semiflexible dockingAuto was performed using Dock Vina 1.1.2 and the optimal conformation was selected as the final docking pose. The conformations with both the lowest docking binding energy and containing hydrogen bonds were chosen to be analyzed for docking binding mode. The final diagram was generated using PyMol software.


### Statistical Analysis
  At least three replicate trials were performed for each result and statistically analyzed and plotted using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. Differences between the data were analyzed using t test, one-way ANOVA, and Kruskal–Wallis test. Tukey’s multiple comparisons were used to separate means when ANOVA was significantly different or had a trend of difference. Results are presented as the means ± SEMs. All statements of significance were based on p < 0.05.


## Results

### Toxic Effects of ZEA on KK1 Cells
  The CCK-8 method was utilized to access the impact of ZEA on KK1 cell viability, as illustrated in Figure 1A. Following a 12-h treated with ZEA, concentrations of 100 μM and 120 μM were found to be cototoxic to KK1 cells (p < 0.05). Upon 24-h exposure to ZEA, all concentrations exceeding 30 μM exhibited toxicity toward KK1 cells (p < 0.05). Subsequent to 48-h ZEA treatment, all concentrations, including the DMSO control group, demonstrated cytotoxicity toward KK1 cells (p < 0.05). In light of these findings, the 12-h treatment duration was deemed insufficient, and the 48-h treatment duration was considered confounded by potential DMSO toxicity. Consequently, the 30 μM concentration of ZEA and a 24-h treatment period were selected for further experimentation. As depicted in Figure 1B,C, the presence of 30 μM of ZEA resulted in a significant decrease in the number of viable KK1 cells, an increase in cell volume, and an expansion of the cell gap compared to the control group, as indicated by green fluorescence. Additionally, there was a notable increase in the number of apoptotic cells (red fluorescence) in the ZEA group (p < 0.05).


### Transcriptome Results Analysis
  To further investigate the impact of ZEA on KK1 cell, cellular samples were collected for transcriptome analysis. Initial analysis revealed 743 upregulated differentially expressed genes and 1558 downregulated genes in the ZEA group (Figure S1). By KEGG annotations, it was observed that differential genes were predominantly enriched in processes related to cell-growth-and-death, folding-sorting-and-degradation, as well as the endocrine-system (Figure 2A). To enhance the precision of our findings, a gene set specifically comprising differential genes associated with cell growth and death was curated for further in-depth examination (Figure 2B). Subsequent KEGG enrichment analysis revealed enrichment of genes involved in apoptosis. Subsequently, a separate gene set focusing on apoptosis-related differential genes was constructed, leading to the identification of enrichment of certain genes in the Chop-ATF4 complex under GO enrichment analysis (Figure 2C,D). Alternatively, a gene set comprising differential genes associated with folding, sorting, and degradation was assembled for in-depth examination. Subsequent KEGG enrichment analysis revealed a higher enrichment of genes in the protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum stress pathway (Figure 2E–G). The Chop-ATF4 complex emerged as a significant pathway in endoplasmic reticulum stress, with the PERK signaling pathway being the primary driver of elevated Chop expression.23 It was (Figure 2H) demonstrated a notable increase in the relative levels of endoplasmic reticulum stress markers PERK, eIF-2α, ATF4, and Chop in the ZEA group, with statistical significance (p < 0.05). Conversely, antioxidant factors Bcl2 and Bcl6 exhibited a significant decrease in the ZEA group (p < 0.05). These results reveal that ZEA has the capability to activate the PERK/eIF-2α/ATF4/Chop signaling pathway.


### ZEA Affects the PERK/eIF-2α/ATF4/Chop
Signaling Pathway through ERβ
  Since ZEA exists in a similar structure to estrogen, it is able to interact with the ERs.24 Our research revealed that ZEA decreased the mRNA levels of ERα while increasing the mRNA level of ERβ (Figure 3A). MPP significantly decreased ERα expression, and the addition of ZEA did not reverse this increase; similarly, PHTPP decreased ERβ expression and the addition of ZEA did not reverse ERβ expression (Figure 3B).

  Interestingly, the addition of MPP resulted in a significant increase in ATF4 expression (p < 0.05), with the expression of ATF4 in the presence of ZEA differing from that of the MPP group. However, the levels of p-eIF-2α and Chop did not show a significant difference (p > 0.05) (Figure 3C,D). Conversely, the addition of the ERβ inhibitor PHTPP reversed the ZEA-induced significant increase in p-eIF-2α and Chop expression (p < 0.05) (Figure 3E,F). These findings suggest that ZEA induces the upregulation of the PERK/eIF-2α/ATF4/Chop signaling pathway through enhanced ERβ expression.


### VE Mitigates the Toxic Effects of ZEA on KK1
Cells
  The selection of conformations containing hydrogen bonds for docking analysis revealed that VE exhibited a binding energy of −7.5 kcal/mol to PERK. This observation suggests that VE has the potential to interact with the active site of PERK. The impact of different concentrations of VE on the viability of KK1 cells is illustrated in Figure 4A.

  Following a 6-h treatment with VE, all concentrations showed no significant effect on KK1 cell activity. However, as the duration of treatment increased, VE at a concentration of 200 μM notably influenced KK1 cell activity after 12 h, and at a concentration of 150 μM, a significant decrease in KK1 cell activity was observed after 24 h of treatment. Therefore, 24 h and 100 μM concentration of VE were used for subsequent experiments. As depicted in Figure 4C,D, the impact of VE in isolation on both viable and apoptotic cells did not yield statistically significant changes (p > 0.05). Conversely, exposure to ZEA alone resulted in a significant decrease in the number of viable KK1 cells and an increase in apoptotic cells (p < 0.05). However, when VE and ZEA were coadministered, VE effectively mitigated the adverse effects of ZEA on viable cells and apoptotic cells (p < 0.05). Furthermore, as illustrated in Figure 4E–G, the addition of VE significantly attenuated the ZEA-induced upregulation of relative protein expression of p-eIF-2α, ATF4, and Chop (p < 0.05), while also reducing the relative mRNA levels of endoplasmic reticulum stress markers ATF4 and Chop (p < 0.05). Meanwhile, VE could mitigate the adverse effect of ZEA on apoptosis factor Bcl6 (p < 0.05).


### VE Mitigates the Toxic Effects of ZEA on Female
Mice
  To further explore the mitigation of ZEA-induced reproductive toxicity by VE, we validated it by constructing a female mouse experiment (Figure 5A). The clinical manifestations demonstrate that mice in the ZEA group exhibited reproductive toxicity, as evidenced by a significant reduction in body weight and oxidative damage (p < 0.05). Conversely, mice in the ZEA+VE group displayed increased body weight and decreased MDA content (p < 0.05) (Figure 5B–D), suggesting that VE supplementation effectively mitigated the effects of ZEA in mice. Histological examination using H&E staining revealed inflammatory cell infiltration and disorganization of the GCs layer in the ovaries of mice in the ZEA group compared to the CON group. The degree of inflammatory infiltration was reduced and the GCs layer was restored to regularity after VE intervention (p < 0.05) (Figure 5E). TUNEL staining revealed a significant increase in cell apoptosis in the ZEA group. However, the number of apoptotic cells was reduced in mice in the group supplemented with VE (p < 0.05) (Figure 5F,G). Western blot analysis indicated that ZEA led to an increase in the protein levels of eIF-2α and Chop (p < 0.05). Conversely, VE supplementation resulted in a significant decrease in the protein levels of p-eIF-2α, ATF4, and Chop (p < 0.05) (Figure 5H,I).


## Discussion
  ZEA is a known estrogenic mycotoxin produced by various strains of Fusarium, posing a threat to the reproductive systems of both humans and animals.25 Ovarian GCs within the ovary play a crucial role in follicular development and overall reproductive function.2627 The adverse impacts of ZEA on reproductive health may be attributed, at least in part, to its effects on GCs. Our study revealed that exposure to 30 μM of ZEA in vitro significantly decreased KK1 cell viability and increased apoptosis. Similarly, previous research by Yi et al. demonstrated that treatment of primary GCs with the same concentration of ZEA resulted in a notable reduction in cell viability after 24 h of exposure.2829 This suggests that 30 μM concentration of ZEA can fully exert the toxic hazard on GCs, both primary and cell lines.

  Apoptosis is programmed cell death controlled by genes.30 Prior studies have identified three primary pathways through which apoptosis is regulated: the mitochondrial pathway, death receptor pathway, and endoplasmic reticulum stress pathway.3132 Endoplasmic reticulum stress-induced apoptosis represents a newly recognized pathway for cell death. While short-term endoplasmic reticulum stress may confer cellular protection, prolonged endoplasmic reticulum stress can activate apoptotic signaling molecules such as Chop and Caspase, ultimately leading to apoptosis.33 GRP78 is a versatile protein with primary functions including protein folding and processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, Ca2+ binding within the endoplasmic reticulum, and the maintenance of endoplasmic reticulum homeostasis.3435 It interacts with three sensors (IRE1, PERK, and ATF6) to remain in an inactive state under normal conditions, but initiates ER stress signals in response to stress.36 PERK, IRE1 and ATF6 are recognized as crucial downstream signaling proteins in the activation of endoplasmic reticulum stress, with PERK serving as a key regulator in this response.37 During ER stress, the activation of PERK leads to the phosphorylation of eIF-2α, subsequently activating specific transcription factors such as ATF4.38In vivo, ATF4 can induce the expression of Chop, a pivotal factor in apoptosis.39 Under conditions of severe endoplasmic reticulum stress, ATF4 triggers the transcription of Chop, initiating a pro-apoptotic program aimed at promoting apoptosis.40,41 Chop acts by negatively regulating the expression of antiapoptotic genes in the Bcl2 family.42,43 Bcl2 is mainly localized to the endoplasmic reticulum and the outer mitochondrial membrane, and protects cells from apoptosis induced by external stimuli.44,45

  The induction of apoptosis by ZEA in various cell types is facilitated through diverse molecular pathways, notably involving endoplasmic reticulum stress.46 In dairy mammary epithelial cells, Fu et al. observed significant up-regulation of Chop and BAX mRNA levels and decreased Bcl2 expression following ZEA treatment compared to the control group.2,47 Consistent with prior research, our study found that apoptosis of ovarian GCs under ZEA exposure was associated with downregulation of antiapoptotic factors Bcl2 and Bcl6. Additionally, our transcriptome analysis indicated that certain differentially expressed genes were enriched in the PERK/eIF-2α/ATF4/Chop pathway upon ZEA treatment, leading to enhanced transcription of Chop and ATF4 and the induction of endoplasmic reticulum stress.

  ZEA is translocated to the nucleus where it competitively binding to nuclear ERs, thereby influencing downstream pathways. ERα and ERβ are classical nuclear ERs with varying expression levels in different tissues and cells,48 leading to distinct functional roles. Specifically, ERα is implicated in mammary gland development, breast tumorigenesis, and progression, while ERβ is not associated with mammary gland tissue.49 In the context of ovarian cancer, research suggests that the downregulation of ERβ expression may contribute to carcinogenesis.50 In our study, ZEA was found to decrease ERα levels while increasing ERβ levels in normal ovarian GCs. This suggests that ZEA has a different direction of regulation of ERα and ERβ in the ovary. Further experiments utilizing MPP (an inhibitor of ERα) and PHTPP (an inhibitor of ERβ) demonstrated that ZEA primarily influences the PERK/eIF-2α/ATF4/Chop signaling pathway through ERβ. Notably, ERβ was found to be more abundant in ovarian GCs compared to ERα, underscoring the significance of ERβ in these cells.

  In order to counteract the adverse effects of ZEA on the reproductive system, researchers have utilized various degradation agents, including degrading bacteria, antioxidants, and plant extracts,29 .51,52 Zhou et al. research demonstrated that incorporating Bacillus subtilisANSB01G culture into the diet effectively reduce ZEA-induced apoptotic state in gestating sows.51 Similarly, Hu et al. study showed that scutellarin could mitigate ovarian GCs injury by suppressing the expression of JNK, c-jun and Cleaved-caspase-3 through the activation of MAPK/JNK signaling pathway.52 In the current study, VE was selected as a mitigating agent to alleviate the ZEA toxicity. Prior research has established that VE has the potential to reduce ZEA-induced reproductive toxicity by 30–50% through primarily engaging the antioxidant pathway.53,54 Our novel experiments have unveiled that VE exhibits a stronger binding energy to the active site of the PERK protein within the endoplasmic reticulum stress pathway through molecular docking analysis. This indicates that VE may effectively bind to the PERK protein, consequently hindering the activation of the PERK signaling pathway induced by ZEA.

  Subsequent to conducting in vivo and in vitro experiments on mice, it was observed that the addition of VE effectively attenuated the ovarian damage induced by ZEA and apoptosis of GCs. This protective mechanism was found to be mediated through the PERK/eIF-2α/ATF4/Chop signaling pathway. Furthermore, our findings suggest that VE may mitigate the reproductive toxicity of ZEA by alleviating endoplasmic reticulum stress. This mitigating effect could be attributed to VE’s superior binding affinity compared to ZEA at the active site of the PERK signaling protein.

  Our data demonstrated that ZEA exposure in vitro can significantly damage ovarian GCs. Furthermore, we observed that ZEA-induced reproductive toxicity in mouse ovarian GCs impacts apoptosis through the endoplasmic reticulum stress markers Chop-ATF4 pathway. Additionally, our results suggest that ZEA activates the PERK/eIF-2α/ATF4/Chop signaling pathway via ERβ. Moreover, it was found that VE alleviates ZEA-induced ovarian toxicity by potentially modulating the PERK/eIF-2α/ATF4/Chop signaling pathway and apoptosis both in vitro and in vivo. These findings suggest that VE could be further explored as a potential therapeutic agent or dietary supplement for preventing reproductive toxicity associated with ZEA exposure in grains and food.
