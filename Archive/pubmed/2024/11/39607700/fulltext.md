# DNA sequence and lesion-dependent mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM)-DNA-binding modulates DNA repair activities and products


## Abstract
  Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) is indispensable for mitochondrial function and is maintained by DNA repair, turnover, mitochondrial dynamics and mitophagy, along with the inherent redundancy of mtDNA. Base excision repair (BER) is a major DNA repair mechanism in mammalian mitochondria. Mitochondrial BER enzymes are implicated in mtDNA-mediated immune response and inflammation. mtDNA is organized into mitochondrial nucleoids by mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM). The regulation of DNA repair activities by TFAM-DNA interactions remains understudied. Here, we demonstrate the modulation of DNA repair enzymes by TFAM concentrations, DNA sequences and DNA modifications. Unlike previously reported inhibitory effects, we observed that human uracil-DNA glycosylase 1 (UNG1) and AP endonuclease I (APE1) have optimal activities at specific TFAM/DNA molar ratios. High TFAM/DNA ratios inhibited other enzymes, OGG1 and AAG. In addition, TFAM reduces the accumulation of certain repair intermediates. Molecular dynamics simulations and DNA-binding experiments demonstrate that the presence of 8-oxo-7,8-dihydro-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-oxodG) in certain sequence motifs enhances TFAM-DNA binding, partially explaining the inhibition of OGG1 activity. Bioinformatic analysis of published 8-oxodG, dU, and TFAM-footprint maps reveals a correlation between 8-oxodG and TFAM locations in mtDNA. Collectively, these results highlight the complex regulation of mtDNA repair by DNA sequence, TFAM concentrations, lesions and repair enzymes.


## Introduction
  Mitochondria are vital subcellular organelles involved in energy production, signaling and metabolism. Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) plays a crucial role in mitochondrial and cellular functions, encoding 37 genes indispensable for mitochondrial functions and cellular signaling (1–3). Defects in mtDNA or the nuclear genes involved in mtDNA maintenance are associated with a variety of mitochondrial disorders (4,5). mtDNA is susceptible to chemical and physical factors from endogenous and environmental sources and is maintained through DNA repair, turnover and the redundancy of mtDNA copies (6,7), in conjunction with mitochondrial dynamics (fission and fusion) and other quality control mechanisms, such as mitophagy (8).

  Base excision repair (BER) is a predominant DNA repair pathway in mitochondria, primarily addressing nucleobase lesions and single-strand breaks (SSBs) (9,10). The sequence of events resembles that of nuclear BER (11,12), involving damaged base excision by a DNA glycosylase, endonucleolytic cleavage at abasic (AP) sites by AP endonuclease, 5′-deoxyribose phosphate (5′-dRp) removal, nucleotide incorporation and ligation of the DNA backbone. Alternative mechanisms include bifunctional DNA glycosylases to excise the lesion and cleave the DNA backbone and long-patch DNA synthesis followed by flap removal (11,12). Several enzymes involved in nuclear BER have distinct mitochondrial isoforms (9), such as human uracil-DNA glycosylase 1 (UNG1) (13,14), APE1 (15,16), human 8-oxoguanine DNA glycosylase 1 (OGG1) (17) and alkyladenine glycosylase (AAG, also known as N-methylpurine DNA glycosylase, or MPG) (18). Mitochondrial BER exhibits cell and tissue-specific variations in repair extent and enzyme involvement, exemplified by the mitochondrial localization of DNA polymerase β (pol β) (19,20). Such effects complicate the understanding of mtDNA repair mechanisms.

  An outstanding question regarding mtDNA repair pertains to the operation of repair machinery within mitochondrial nucleoids. Nucleoids are nucleoprotein complexes housing mtDNA and approximately 37 nucleoid-associated proteins (21,22). Nucleoids are organized primarily by mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM) through a nucleation-and-spreading model (23) and phase separation (24,25). TFAM compacts mtDNA, which has a contour length of approximately 5 μm, into nucleoids roughly 100 nm in diameter (26). Unlike nucleosome core particles, nucleoids lack defined structural characteristics but exhibit specific protein-coating patterns as revealed by DNase-seq footprinting experiments (27). The TFAM/mtDNA ratio regulates the compaction and accessibility of mtDNA in vitro (28) and in cells (23). Additionally, the distribution of TFAM on mtDNA is uneven, featuring high-affinity TFAM sites (23,27,29). The uneven distribution is likely to be contributed by multiple factors, such as TFAM–TFAM interactions (30), cross-strand binding (28), and sequence-specific modulation of TFAM-DNA binding (31). While the effects of chromatin on lesion formation and DNA repair activities have been well recognized (32), the understanding of mtDNA repair in TFAM-compacted DNA remains limited. Gaining insights into this area is essential for understanding the formation and accumulation of mtDNA mutations and heteroplasmy.

  In addition to their roles in nucleoids, TFAM-DNA interactions can modulate mtDNA-mediated immune signaling by influencing the conformation of released mtDNA (33) or masking mtDNA for degradation (34). DNA repair enzymes, OGG1 (35,36) and flap endonuclease 1 (FEN1) (37), play a role in mtDNA-mediated immune signaling by affecting the level of oxidized mtDNA in the cytoplasm. For example, OGG1 can regulate the cGAS-STING pathway by influencing the levels of oxidized mtDNA secreted from mitochondria (35,36). FEN1 is involved in oxidized mtDNA processing pertinent to inflammasome activation (37). Notably, mtDNA containing 8-oxo-7,8-dihydro-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-oxodG) is more proinflammatory than its unmodified counterpart (37–39). Therefore, assessing the ability of repair enzymes to remove DNA lesions from TFAM-bound DNA is also crucial for understanding various mtDNA-mediated signaling pathways.

  In this study, we integrate in vitro biochemical assays, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, and bioinformatic analysis to clarify how TFAM-DNA interactions influence DNA repair activities and products. TFAM cleaves the AP-containing DNA (AP-DNA) to form a variety of DNA terminal modifications depending on the lesion position. TFAM processes the repair product of APE1 by removing 5′-dRp residues, potentially converting toxic repair intermediates to products compatible with downstream processing. Although TFAM has been presumed to have an inhibitory role in DNA repair due to competitive DNA binding (40), our data reveal novel stimulatory effects of optimal TFAM concentrations on DNA repair enzyme (UNG1 and APE1) activities with certain DNA substrates. Mechanistic studies indicate that the stimulatory effects likely result from a higher affinity of repair enzymes with TFAM or its ortholog-bound DNA. Conversely, elevated TFAM levels inhibit glycosylase activities of OGG1 and AAG. In addition, DNA-binding assays and MD simulations demonstrate preferential TFAM multimerization with certain 8-oxodG-containing substrates. Furthermore, bioinformatic analysis of published 8-oxodG and TFAM mapping data reveals a correlation between 8-oxodG positions and TFAM high-occupancy sites, supporting a model where TFAM shields 8-oxodG lesions from DNA repair. Overall, our data demonstrate the multi-layer regulation of mtDNA repair and implicate the non-canonical roles of TFAM in mtDNA maintenance and immune signaling by regulating the level of oxidized mtDNA.

  Modified and unmodified oligodeoxyribonucleotides were obtained from Integrated DNA Technologies or TriLink Biotechnologies and were purified by HPLC by the manufacturer. ### Preparation of markers for DNA repair products
  To identify different terminal modifications in TFAM-catalyzed SSBs (Figure 1), we prepared the following SSB markers. AP-containing dsDNA substrates (AP-DNA) were prepared using a dU-containing DNA oligomer followed by enzymatic removal of dU and annealing with a complementary oligomer, as described previously (42). After specific reactions described below, all samples were quenched with 0.2 M NaBH4, followed by incubation on ice for 15 min. 3′-phosphate SSBs from β,δ-elimination of AP sites were prepared by incubating AP-DNA with 0.3 M NaOH at 65°C for 1 h, followed by neutralization with HCl. SSBs containing β-elimination and β,δ-elimination products were prepared by incubating AP-DNA with 0.2 M NaOH at 37°C for 20 min, followed by neutralization with HCl. SSBs containing a terminal 3′-deoxyribose phosphate residue were prepared by reactions with AP-DNA with endo III (45,46). Specifically, 10 pmol of AP-DNA was reacted with 1 uL E.coli endo III at 37°C for 1 h under the condition of 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 1 mM DTT, 1 mM EDTA. SSBs containing a 3′ glutathione adduct were prepared by incubating AP-DNA with 10 mM GSH and 1 mM spermine at 37°C overnight in 50 mM MES buffer pH 6.5. To verify SSBs containing a 3′-deoxyribose phosphate or 3′ glutathione residues, reaction products from a 200 pmol scale were purified using denaturing PAGE (1 mm thickness and 80 cm length). The desired products were excised from the gel, crushed into fine pieces, and extracted with 1 mL 10 mM HEPES pH 7.4 at 4°C overnight. The gel was removed with a Co-Star filter device, and the flow through was cleaned up with a C18 cartridge. The samples from C18 cartridge were dried using SpeedVac and reconstituted in water for mass spectrometry analysis.

  To identify different DNA repair products involving APE1, UNG1 and TFAM (Figure 2), the following SSB markers were prepared. Lane 6 contained 3′-OH SSBs from reactions of 1 μM LSP36-2-AP with 4 nM APE1 under the same conditions as the APE1-UNG1-TFAM reaction.


### DNA repair activity assays
  In general, all DNA repair activity assays were prepared by incubating a fixed amount of 0.5–1 μM DNA substrate and limiting enzyme (UNG1, APE1, OGG1, or AAG) with varying concentrations of TFAM (or mTFAM, or HMG-5). The reaction buffer is dependent on the glycosylase and designed to be optimal to their respective activities. For APE1-UNG1-TFAM reactions, the 36-nt dsDNA substrates (LSP36-1-dU, LSP36-2-dU and LSP36-3-dU) were used. The reaction buffer was designed for optimal UNG1 and APE1 activities: 20 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 50 mM NaCl (30 to 60 mM NaCl for optimal UNG1 activity (48)), 5 μM of MgCl2 (for optimal APE1 endonuclease activity and minimal exonuclease activity at a molar ratio of 1:3000 for APE1/MgCl2) (49,50), 0.1 mg/mL BSA. Around 1 μM of dsDNA was pre-incubated with 0, 0.6, 1.0, 2.0 and 6.6 μM of TFAM for 2 min at 37°C before adding hUNG1 (4 nM) to initiate the uracil excision reaction for 15 min. A final concentration of 4 nM of APE1 in MgCl2 was added To initiate APE1 reactions and allowed for 30-min reaction followed by quenching with 50 mM EDTA. The reaction mixture was then placed on ice for 2 min followed by quenching with 100 mM NaBH4. Samples were then stored at -20°C for gel electrophoresis analysis. Samples were diluted to 0.1 pmol DNA/μL with a kinetic stop solution (90% formamide, 50 mM EDTA, ultrapure water and 0.005% bromophenol blue, 0.005% xylene cyanol) and loaded (0.2 pmol/well) onto a denaturing PAGE gel (18% (19:1), 7 M urea, 38 × 50 cm, 0.4 mm). The gels were scanned with a Typhoon imager. For reactions with AP-DNA and APE1, LSP36-2-AP was used. Reactions were run in the same conditions as the APE1-UNG1-TFAM assay, except without UNG1.

  For reactions with 22-nt substrates, uracil-containing LSP22, X22 and R22 were used. Around 1 μM of dsDNA was pre-incubated with varying amounts of TFAM (0, 0.08, 0.13, 0.22, 0.37, 0.62, 0.77, 1.0, 1.2, 1.5, 1.9, 2.4, 4.4 and 6.6 μM) at 37°C for 2 min. The reaction was initiated by adding 0.2 nM of UNG1 followed by a 15-min incubation at 37°C in a buffer containing 20 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA and 0.1 mg/mL BSA. The reaction was quenched with 100 mM NaBH4 or 100 mM NaOH to convert UNG1-generated AP sites to SSBs. For reactions quenched with NaOH, the sample was denatured at 90°C for 2 min, then neutralized with HCl before flash-freezing on dry ice for storage at −20°C. Unless otherwise specified, reactions with mTFAM, HMG-5 and E. coli UDG were carried out under similar conditions. The HMG-5 reaction buffer contained 50 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 100 mM NaCl, 0.1 mg/mL BSA and 10 mM MgCl2.

  For OGG1-TFAM assay, 0.5 μM of 8-oxodG-containing substrates (LSP22-8-oxodG, HSP22-oxodG, or X22-2-oxodG) was incubated at 37°C in a 10 μL total reaction mixture volume with 50 nM of wild-type human OGG1 and 0, 0.06, 0.13, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4 and 8 μM of human TFAM. 5 μL of DNA and 2.5 μL of TFAM stocks are preincubated for 2 min at 37°C in a reaction mixture with a reaction buffer comprised of 70 mM MOPS pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 0.15 μg/μL BSA, 5% glycerol, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM EDTA and ultrapure water. After preincubation, the reaction was initiated by adding 2.5 μL OGG1 stock, followed by incubation at 37°C for 5 min. The reaction was quenched with NaOH (similar to UNG1 reactions) and then placed immediately on dry ice to store at −80°C for gel electrophoresis analysis.

  For AAG-TFAM Assay, 1 μM of DNA (LSP22-7-dI or LSP22-8-dI) was incubated with 0, 0.01, 0.03, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, 1, 2, 4 and 6 μM of TFAM at 37°C for 2 min. The reaction was initiated by adding 0.4 μM of AAG in a reaction buffer containing 50 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 100 mM NaCl, 0.1 mg/mL BSA, 1 mM EDTA, and 5% (v/v) glycerol, followed by incubated for 30 min at 37°C. ### Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA)
  The TFAM-DNA binding stoichiometry was evaluated with EMSA. Varying amounts of TFAM were incubated with 4 μM of DNA with or without an 8-oxodG lesion. In the case of LSP36-2-dU substrate, 1 μM of DNA was used. Reactions were run under a buffer containing 30 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 10 mM EDTA and 88 mM NaCl, where protein and ligand were incubated for 30 min at 25°C then loaded onto a 6% native PAGE (acrylamide:bis-acrylamide, 55:1) to separate the TFAM-DNA complexes from free DNA. The gel electrophoresis was performed in 0.35X TBE buffer (45.5 mM Tris, 15.75 mM boric acid and 0.875 mM EDTA) for a total of 50 min at 100 V, 4°C, with a pre-run of 45 min and 60V at 4°C. Following electrophoresis, the FAM-labeled DNA complexes were visualized on a Typhoon imager and quantified using ImageQuant TL software.


### Steady-state kinetic analysis of UNG1
  To examine the effect of TFAM on UNG1 enzyme kinetics, steady-state kinetic analysis was performed in the absence and presence of TFAM. A molar ratio of 0.6 (TFAM/LSP22-4-dU) was used, whereby the strongest stimulatory effects were observed in TFAM concentration dependent experiments. The DNA substrate was pre-incubated TFAM (or without TFAM) for 2 min at 37°C before adding UNG1 to initiate the reaction. The assays contained limiting UNG1 (0.2 nM) and varying concentrations of LSP22-4-dU (25, 50, 100, 200, 400 and 600 nM) in the presence of 20 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA and 0.1 mg/mL BSA in 20 uL reactions at 37°C. At varying times, aliquots were quenched in equal volumes of 0.2 M NaOH and kinetic stop solution (90% formamide, 50 mM EDTA, ultrapure water, and 0.005% bromophenol blue, 0.005% xylene cyanol). Samples were frozen immediately on dry ice for storage at −20°C or prepared for subsequent analysis by denaturing at 95°C for 5 min to generate strand-break products. Following denaturation, samples were spun down at 15 000 g at 4°C for 5 min and neutralized with equimolar amounts of HCl. To visualize products, a minimum of 0.05 to 0.1 pmol of DNA was loaded per well into a 18% denaturing PAGE (19:1 acrylamide and 7M urea). Initial velocities were calculated based on linear regression under varying DNA concentrations and were plotted as a function of DNA substrate concentrations to obtain kcat and Km based on the Michaelis–Menten equation.


### MD simulations
  The TFAM-DNA co-crystal structures (PDB: 3TQ6 for the TFAM:LSP complex; PDB:4NOD for the TFAM:HSP complex) (30,51,52) were used as starting models in MD simulations in the AMBER 22 package with GPU acceleration (53,54). Force fields used for different molecules were AMBER ff14SB (55) for the protein, DNA.BSC1 for DNA and AMBER general force field (GAFF2) (56) for 8-oxodG. 8-oxodG was parametrized using Amber's antechamber program with AM1-BCC charge calculation methods (57). The sequential minimization steps were performed for the hydrogen atoms, side chains and the entire complex with 1000,1000 and 5000 minimization steps, respectively, to eliminate any potential clashes. In addition, the minimization was performed in a Generalized Born implicit solvent to avoid unrealistic sidechain interactions (58). All systems were then solvated in TIP3P water box system (59), with an extension of 12 Å from the solute edge. Salt ions were added to neutralize the system. After adding the water and ions, the water molecules were minimized for 10 000 steps, followed by the minimization of the whole system for 20 000 steps. The entire system was equilibrated in the isothermal-isobaric (NPT) ensemble from 50 to 275 K in 25 K increments for 100 picoseconds (ps) each, and at 298 K for 2 nanoseconds (ns). Finally, production runs were performed in the NPT ensemble at 298 K using the Langevin thermostat with 2-femtosecond timesteps for 400-ns, and each frame was collected at 1-ps time intervals (60). A 12 Å cutoff was set for short-range non-bonded interaction, which includes van der Waals (vdW) and electrostatic components. The long-range electrostatic interactions were computed by the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method (61). The MD trajectories were aligned to the protein backbone and re-saved every 100 picoseconds for further analysis. The first 40 ns of MD simulation were treated as equilibrium, and all analyses were performed on the following 360ns. CPPTRAJ and visual molecular dynamics (VMD) were used for data analysis (62,63).

  Gromacs force distribution analysis (FDA) was used to compute non-bonding forces with Leonard-Jones’ potential and Coulomb potential (65). ### Bioinformatic analysis
  The mitochondrial genome analysis of 8-oxodG and dU distributions was based on the published datasets BioProject PRJNA751557 (66) and PRJNA728500 (67), respectively. Paired-end reads were aligned to the hg19 reference genome using BWA MEM (v0.7.17). Duplicates were removed using Picard MarkDuplicates (v3.2.0). Read counts were obtained with multiBamSummary in deeptools (v3.5.5) (68), then Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated using cor, the built-in function of R. BAM files were filtered and converted to BED format with Python scripts, selecting records with a guanine base 1 nt upstream of the read start (or end for negative strand reads). These were converted back to BAM format with bedtobam function of Bedtools (v2.30.0) (69). Samples were merged using Picard MergeSamFiles. BigWig files were generated with bamCoverage in deeptools with parameters of normalized using RPKM. The RPKM value of the experimental group was divided by the average value of the control group within the same interval. The top 5% of 8-oxodG and dU sites were selected as high-frequency sites. Statistical tests were used to compare high 8-oxodG sites and high TFAM regions (27) for the overlap. The comparisons were carried out using the fisher function of Bedtools. Statistical analysis for the overlap comparisons was carried out using Bedtools ‘fisher’ function.


## Results

### Design of DNA substrates
  All DNA substrates are double-stranded (ds) DNA. For gel-based analysis, a 5′ FAM or 3′ FAM label was used to follow the different terminal modifications at the 3′ end or 5′ end, respectively, after strand cleavage. Two categories of substrate sequences were used. The first category contains DNA sequences based on TFAM footprinting experiments (70), analogous to cognate transcription factor binding sites. The two best characterized 22-nt substrates are human mitochondrial light-strand promoter (LSP) and heavy-strand promoter (HSP) sequences, denoted as LSP22 and HSP22. Based on the solved co-crystal structures of TFAM:DNA complexes (30,51,52), TFAM binds to these substrates with a defined polarity to activate transcription (71). Therefore, these substrates were used to probe different interacting environments in TFAM:DNA complexes. Another 22-nt substrate with a solved crystal structure (31) contains the site X sequence (also derived from TFAM footprinting experiments) and is denoted as X22. The substrates are numbered based on their appearance in the text, and site-specific DNA modification is indicated if present. For example, LSP22-1-AP indicates a 22-nt dsDNA substrate containing the LSP sequence and an abasic (AP) site. The second category of substrates containing random sequences was used to test the generality of the reaction. For example, R22-1-AP indicates a 22-nt dsDNA substrate with a random sequence and an AP lesion. AP-DNA refers to a dsDNA substrate containing a site-specific AP lesion, prepared using enzymatic removal of uracil from a dU-containing substrate using E coli. UDG (42). The substrate sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1. A set of 36-nt dsDNA substrates was also used to accommodate interactions with multiple proteins. The sequence encompasses the LSP sequence, with a 14-nt extension at the 5′ end based on the human mtDNA sequence, denoted as LSP36-1-AP. The substrate sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S2.


### Complexity of DNA terminal chemistry in TFAM-cleaved AP-DNA products
  Because TFAM is the major mtDNA-packaging protein and has AP-lyase activities (42,45), we seek to establish the types of cleaved DNA products formed with TFAM alone to provide a baseline for comparison with products from DNA repair enzymes. TFAM-mediated strand cleavage at AP sites is facilitated by Lys residues via Schiff base chemistry (Figure 1A) to form two categories of products, i.e. DNA-protein cross-links (DPCs) and SSBs (3,6). DPCs are formed via imine or Michael addition reactions, and SSBs are formed upon β- or β,δ-elimination. The resulting 3′-PUA reacts with biological thiols, such as glutathione (GSH), to form GSH-adducted SSBs (3′-PUA-GSH, product 1 in Figure 1A), competing with TFAM-DPC formation (45). It is known that SSBs derived from AP sites can yield different terminal chemistry in the presence of different catalysts (e.g. NaOH, amine and repair enzymes) (72). To clarify the DNA terminal modifications of SSBs by TFAM, LSP substrates with a 5′ FAM label were used to follow the different terminal chemistry at the 3′ ends of SSBs. Three substrates with the same sequence vary in the AP position (sequence and nomenclatures shown in Supplementary Tables S1, LSP22-1-AP). LSP22-1-AP was used to probe the high-mobility group (HMG) box 1 (HMG1) domain, LSP22-2-AP for the HMG box 2 (HMG2) domain, and LSP22-3-AP for the linker region (Figures 1B, 1C and 1D).

  TFAM-mediated AP-DNA cleavage occurs in a matter of hours (42,45). With LSP22-1-AP, TFAM converted the substrate to 5-nt fragments after a 24-h reaction. As shown in Figure 1B (lane 6), SSBs with different functional groups on the 3′ terminus can be resolved using denaturing PAGE. In the absence of GSH, DPCs were the main products (recalcitrant to migration into the gel), resulting in overall lower product intensities for SSBs in lane 6. SSBs with different terminal modifications were assigned by comparing the migration with markers and mass spectrometry analyses. For example, the fast-migrating product 5 (Figure 1B, lane 6) was assigned as the 3′-phosphate (3′-p) SSB, based on its co-migration with the marker from β,δ-elimination (Figure 1B, lane 2). The slow-migrating products contained three major bands (Figure 1B, lane 6, products 2, 3, 4). Of these, the top two bands co-migrated with products formed in endo III reactions (Figure 1B, lanes 4 and 5) (6,7) and were assigned as 2,3-didehydro-2,3-dideoxyribose-5-phosphate (3′-ddRp, product 2) and 3′-deoxyribose phosphate (3′-dRp, product 3). The 3′dRp product was also confirmed by mass spectrometry (Supplementary Fig. S1). When GSH was added at the end of the reaction, product 2 remained unchanged, and product 4 was converted to GSH adduct. Therefore, product 4 was assigned as 3′-trans-PUA (Figure 1B, lane 8) (72). Additional support for the assignment of product 4 was from TFAM-mediated reactions in the presence of 10 mM GSH (Figure 1B, lane 7). Under such conditions, a minimal level of product 4 was observed, consistent with the trapping of 3′-trans-PUA in situ. The 3′-PUA-GSH SSBs were also confirmed using mass spectrometry (Supplementary Fig. S1).

  With LSP22-2-AP and LSP22-3-AP, similar product profiles were observed albeit with different relative abundance. For simplicity, cropped images are shown in Figures 1C and D, and complete images with markers are shown in Supplementary Fig. S2. Compared to LSP22-1-AP, LSP22-2-AP yielded a greater amount of product 5 (Figure 1C, lane 6), likely due to a higher abundance of Lys (K183, K186 and K190) near the AP lesion to facilitate β,δ-elimination (42). Relative to LSP22-1-AP (Figure 1B, lane 7), 3′-trans-PUA products were formed at a lower yield, especially under in situ trapping by GSH (Figure 1C, lane 7). Fragments containing products 2 and 3 were not resolved due to the length of oligomers (15 nt). On the other hand, with LSP22-3-AP, fragments containing 3′-p were the major products regardless of the reaction condition. This is likely due to β,δ-elimination reactions given the overall high abundance of Lys in the TFAM linker region (22%, compared to 13% for HMG1 and 14% for HMG2). Together, the data demonstrate the rich chemistry of TFAM-cleaved AP-DNA and provide a reference for comparison with reaction products in the presence of DNA repair enzymes.


### TFAM affects the product profile of base excision repair
  Next, we examined the effect of TFAM-DNA binding on BER, the primary mtDNA repair pathway. We used dU as a representative DNA lesion, and UNG1 and APE1 as prototypical repair enzymes. Both UNG1 (13,14) and APE1 (15,16) have mitochondrial isoforms. The level of dU in nuclear DNA is on the order of 1 dU per 106 to 107 nt in human cells based on LC/MS/MS analysis (73,74). Although the abundance of dU in mtDNA is unknown, the presence of mitochondrial UNG1 isoform hints at the importance of regulating dU in mtDNA. Two substrates, denoted as LSP36-1-dU and LSP36-2-dU, contain a FAM label at the 5′ end or the 3′ end to follow different chemistry of the resulting SSBs. The substrates harbor a dU in the same sequence context as the AP lesion in LSP22-1-AP (dU near HMG1), whereas a control substrate LSP36-3-dU bears a dU outside the preferred TFAM-binding site (Figure 2B, right panel). The corresponding AP-DNA substrates are denoted as LSP36-1-AP and LSP36-2-AP. Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) was used to obtain the TFAM-DNA binding stoichiometry (Supplementary Fig. S3). In the presence of 1 μM LSP36-2-dU, the highest yield of 1TFAM:1DNA complex was observed at a TFAM/DNA molar ratio of 2, similar to results obtained with a 22-nt LSP substrate (42). Based on the preferential binding of TFAM with the LSP sequence in TFAM footprinting analysis (70), the 1TFAM:1DNA complex can preferentially form at the LSP sequence motif, which was validated further using a control substrate LSP36-3-dU (see below). As TFAM concentration increased, the binding of multiple TFAM molecules to the substrate was observed. The free DNA was nearly depleted at a TFAM/DNA molar ratio of 2.5 (Supplementary Fig. S3A).

  The DNA substrate (1 μM) was pre-incubated with varying concentrations of TFAM to mimic different extents of mtDNA packaging. Reactions were initiated by adding a limiting amount of UNG1 (4 nM), as described in Figures 2A and B. Although the physiological concentrations of DNA repair enzymes are unknown in mitochondria, none of the repair proteins was on the list of nucleoid proteins (21,75), suggestive of their low abundance or transient interactions with DNA. With LSP36-1-dU, the major products were LSP36-1-AP (pink arrow in Figure 2C, LSP36-1-dU, left panel, and Supplementary Fig. S4B) in a 20-min reaction, indicating UNG1 can compete with TFAM for DNA-binding and excise uracil from the substrate. Because UNG1 is a monofunctional glycosylase and does not cleave AP-DNA, the small amount of SSBs (green arrow) was attributed to the AP-lyase activity of TFAM, albeit relatively low in abundance. The relative proportion of these products is consistent with the fast enzymatic turnover by UNG1 and the relatively slow AP-lyase reactions by TFAM. The separation between LSP36-1-dU and LSP36-1-AP became apparent when adjusting the image contrast (Figure 2D). Under a TFAM/DNA molar ratio of 6 (Supplementary Fig. S4B), the yield of LSP36-1-AP was much lower than those observed with lower TFAM/DNA molar ratios. Nonetheless, the formation LSP36-1-AP indicates that UNG1 can remove dU even at excess TFAM conditions.

  In BER, APE1 cooperates with UNG1 to complete the repair (Figure 2A). Similar reactions containing UNG1 and APE1 (Figure 2C, LSP36-1-dU, right panel) produced greater amounts of SSBs due to the very efficient AP-lyase reaction of APE1. The two predominant SSB bands correspond to products containing 5′-dRp (orange arrow) or 5′-p (green arrow) residues based on their comigration with markers (lanes 2 and 3 in Figure 2C, LSP36-1-dU, right panel and Supplementary Figure S4B). The 5′-dRp-containing products were canonical products by APE1, and the 5′-p-containing products were attributed to the dRp-lyase activity of TFAM (47) on the basis of comparison with reaction products from controls without TFAM (Supplementary Figure S4B). Although the AP-lyase activity of TFAM is relatively slow compared to APE1, the dRp-lyase activity of TFAM is reasonably fast (47) (labeled as fast and slow reactions in Figure 2A). With LSP36-2-dU, besides the similar product profiles, the 5′-FAM label provided additional insights into the TFAM-specific products. For example, in the presence of APE1 (Figure 2C, LSP36-2-dU, right panel), TFAM-specific products (yellow arrow) represent a small portion of the total products, in keeping with the relatively slow AP-lyase reactions by TFAM. Such products were assigned as 3′-PUA-DTT derivatives due to reactions with DTT in the TFAM stock solution, based on their co-migration with the 3′-PUA-DTT marker (blue arrow in Figure 2C, lane 5). Due to the slow kinetics of TFAM-mediated reactions relative to APE1-catalyzed reactions, a separate reaction with LSP36-2-AP and 6-fold higher TFAM was used to generate TFAM products at higher yields to facilitate the assignment of different products. The resulting products are shown in lane 8 of Supplementary Figure S4B. Consistent with observations with LSP36-1-dU, APE1 played a major role in forming SSB products, as evidenced by a predominant product band corresponding to a 3′-p (red arrow in Figure 2C, LSP36-2-dU, right panel). A separate reaction with LSP36-2-AP provided additional support to the assignment of these products (Supplementary Figure S5A). Therefore, careful analysis of these products allowed us to unambiguously assign the contributions of different repair enzymes in the reaction. In TFAM-bound substrates, UNG1 and APE1 can compete for their canonical substrates and form repair products; however, the relative portion of products is affected by the molar ratio of TFAM/DNA in the reaction.

  To quantify contributions by respective repair enzymes and TFAM, we compared the product percent yields with LSP36-1-dU (Figure 2E) or LSP36-2-dU (Figure 2F). Overall, in a 20-min reaction, TFAM contributes to the formation of SSB minimally; however, TFAM facilitates the 5′-dRp removal in a TFAM concentration-dependent manner (green arrow in Figure 2C, and product quantification in Supplementary Fig. S5C), consistent with fast kinetics of TFAM-mediate dRp-lyase reactions (47). These results provide additional support for such activities in the presence of APE1. To further examine the change of enzymatic activities of UNG1 and APE1 under different TFAM concentrations, we combined TFAM products (SSBs) with UNG1 products (AP-DNA) in UNG1-TFAM reactions, considering that TFAM-derived products are sourced from AP-DNA. Similarly, in UNG1-APE1-TFAM reactions, TFAM products were counted toward APE1 products due to the dRp lyase activity of TFAM (47). Interestingly, the product yields with both UNG1 and APE1 showed a hormetic effect as the concentration of TFAM increased. Although the inhibition of repair at high TFAM concentrations has been reported (40), careful titration of TFAM in reactions with UNG1 and APE1 revealed optimal TFAM concentrations in each case. The highest yield of UNG1-derived products was observed at a TFAM/DNA molar ratio of 0.6, corresponding to the sub-saturating TFAM-DNA binding according to EMSA with approximately 20% substrate in the TFAM-bound form and 80% in TFAM-free form (Figs. S3A and S3B). Similar experiments under a more gradual change of TFAM concentrations showed similar effects (Supplementary Fig. S6). On the other hand, the highest yield of APE1-derived products was observed with a TFAM/DNA molar ratio of 2.0, a ratio whereby the highest yield of 1TFAM:1DNA complexes was formed (Figs. S3A and S3B). Notably, a similar trend of the product yield at varying TFAM concentrations disappeared when dU was positioned outside the preferred TFAM-binding site (Figure 2G and H), indicating that the stimulating effects with both UNG1 and APE1 were due to specific TFAM-DNA interactions. Together, these data demonstrate that the repair products and activities are modulated by TFAM concentrations and the sequence context of DNA lesions.


### TFAM stimulates UNG1 activities
  To test the generality of the stimulatory effect, we carried out experiments with UNG1, APE1, 8-oxoguanine DNA glycosylase (OGG1) and AAG (also known as N-methylpurine DNA glycosylase or MPG) using the 22-nt DNA substrates containing respective DNA lesions. The 22-nt substrates were chosen to minimize the effects of non-specific interactions with DNA by TFAM and repair enzymes. For UNG1, experiments were performed with 22-nt dsDNA substrates, LSP22-4-dU or LSP22-5-dU, which are equivalent to LSP22-1-AP and LSP22-2-AP, respectively, regarding the lesion position and sequence, except for a 3′ FAM label. The 3′ FAM label is chosen to simplify the product profile because the yield of the 3′-remnant oligomers represents the total product yield. Reactions were conducted in a manner similar to the previous section with the incubation of DNA substrate (1 μM) with a range of TFAM concentrations (0–6.6 μM) in the presence of limiting amounts of UNG1 (0.2 nM). As shown in Figure 3A and B (gel images shown in Supplementary Figure S7), similar stimulatory effects were observed under sub-saturating conditions (0.6 μM), consistent with results with longer substrates LSP36-1-dU and LSP36-2-dU. Statistical analyses show that the increase in activities was significant (Figure 3A and B, right panels). The highest yield of 1TFAM:1DNA complexes is known to occur under a molar TFAM/DNA ratio of 2.0 (41,42,45). The optimal activities observed at a molar TFAM/DNA ratio of 0.6, a condition with sub-saturating TFAM levels. Due to the comparable Kd,DNA of TFAM (∼ 10 nM) (42) and UNG1 (15 ± 3.2 nM, Supplementary Figure S10C), it is conceivable that TFAM and UNG1 compete for the 22-nt substrate. Similar effects were observed with equivalent DNA substrates containing a 5ʹ FAM-label (Supplementary Figure S8).

  Further, we carried out experiments with random 22-nt DNA substrates R22-1-dU and R22-2-dU (Supplementary Figure S9). Despite their random DNA sequences, these substrates contain a hypothetical binding motif (GN10G) (76), which is thought to facilitate TFAM-DNA binding. Notably, a hermetic effect was also observed with R22-1-dU as TFAM concentration varied (Supplementary Figure S9A), but not with R22-2-dU (Supplementary Figure S9B), corroborating DNA sequence-dependent effects observed in Figure 2. No stimulatory effects were observed with a different GN10G-containing substrate X22-dU (Supplementary Figure S10), derived from a different TFAM-binding site in the non-coding region of mtDNA (31). This suggests factors other than the GN10G motif contribute to TFAM-DNA conformations and stimulatory effects. One explanation is that compared to the LSP sequence, X substrate shows considerable stiffness and a lower TFAM-binding affinity (31). The lack of stimulation with the site X substrate is likely due to the difference in conformational dynamics of TFAM–DNA complexes (31,77). Notably, it has been shown that the sequence context of the dU lesion contributes minimally to the excision efficiency of UNG1 with synthetic DNA substrates (48). Therefore, the varying extent of modulation of UNG1 activities by TFAM concentrations is likely due to the different conformation dynamics of TFAM–DNA complexes.

  Because TFAM can form stable DPCs with AP-DNA in the presence of NaBH3CN (42,45), we seek to quantify AP-DNA generated by UNG1 via TFAM-DPCs in situ instead of solely replying on SSB products converted from AP-DNA upon quenching with 100 mM NaOH. We carried out experiments with LSP22-4-dU or LSP22-5-dU in the presence of NaBH3CN. Gel electrophoretic analyses revealed the formation of DPCs, with a higher yield observed for LSP22-5-dU relative to LSP22-4-dU (Figures 3C and D). The higher yield is consistent with the higher concentration of Lys in the vicinity of AP sites in TFAM:LSP22-5-dU complexes. In the presence of NaBH3CN, the yield of DPCs represents the total product yield because the reaction is halted upon Schiff base formation without proceeding to SSBs. The yield of DPCs under varying TFAM concentrations demonstrates a similar stimulatory pattern, although the highest yield was observed at a TFAM/DNA molar ratio of 2.0 (compared to a ratio of 0.6 without NaBH3CN). A shift in the optimal TFAM/DNA ratio can be explained by the different rate-limiting steps under the two conditions. That is, without NaBH3CN, the reaction rate is limited by the UNG1 turnover, whereas in the presence of NaBH3CN, the reaction is limited by the formation of TFAM Schiff-base intermediates (41). Therefore, in situ trapping of AP-DNA generated by UNG1 corroborate stimulatory effects exerted by optimal TFAM levels.

  Because UNG1 assays were carried out under multiple turnover conditions (limiting UNG1 relative to DNA), the rate-limiting step of the reaction is known to be DNA product release (44,78). The stimulatory effects could be attributed to an increased enzyme turnover, an enhanced DNA-binding of UNG1, or both. To distinguish effects from catalytic turnover from binding, we carried out steady-state kinetic analysis of UNG1 with LSP22-4-dU with and without TFAM. In experiments with TFAM, TFAM was present at a ratio of 0.6 (TFAM/DNA) due to the greatest extent of stimulation observed under this condition. As shown in Table 1 (Supplementary Figure S10E), compared to reactions without TFAM, the catalytic turnover (kcat) was reduced by approximately 2-fold, and Km was reduced by approximately 4-fold. The reduced kcat is likely due to the competition for free DNA substrates in the presence of TFAM. The more dramatic effects on Km indicate that the higher DNA-binding affinity of UNG1 plays a greater role in the stimulation. Compared to the control, an overall 2-fold higher catalytic efficiency in the presence of TFAM is consistent with the approximately 2-fold stimulation with LSP22-4-dU in the presence of TFAM discussed earlier.

  We attempted to capture potential TFAM-UNG1 interactions by pull-down experiments and were unsuccessful (data not shown), suggesting that if exists, the interaction is transient or functional. This is different from the direct interactions observed with NEIL1 and TFAM (79). Alternatively, if the stimulatory effects are due to direct TFAM-UNG1 interactions, the effect is likely to be specific to human UNG1 and TFAM pairs. Therefore, we carried out experiments with UNG1 and TFAM orthologs, E. coli UDG, mTFAM and C. elegans HMG-5. Similar to human UNG1 and other orthologs, E. coli UDG has a rate-limiting product release step during catalysis (80); however, E. coli UDG is known to be inhibited by reaction products, AP-DNA (81), whereas human mitochondrial UNG1 isoform is resistant to product inhibition (44). With LSP-DNA substrates LSP22-4-dU and LSP22-5-dU, we observed no stimulation or inhibition for E. coli UDG at similar TFAM concentrations to produce the stimulatory effect observed with human UNG1 (Supplementary Figure S11). The lack of stimulation is likely due to the difference in product inhibition between the two UNG enzymes. On the contrary, substituting human TFAM with mTFAM (Supplementary Figure S12) or C. elegans HMG-5 (Supplementary Figure S13) resulted in similar stimulating patterns with UNG1. These experiments demonstrate that the stimulatory effects are not unique to human UNG1 and TFAM pairs. Collectively, the effect on Km and similar stimulatory effects observed with TFAM orthologs suggest that it is likely that a higher affinity of UNG1 with TFAM–DNA complexes leads to higher product yields under optimal TFAM concentrations.


### TFAM stimulates APE1 activities but inhibits OGG1 and AAG activities
  We examined the stimulation of APE1 activates in the presence of varying concentrations of TFAM with 22-nt substrates. An AP site analog, tetrahydrofuran (THF), was used to probe the APE1 endonuclease activities without complications from the AP- and dRp-lyase activities of TFAM (42,45,47). Under limiting APE1 (4 nM) and excess DNA (1 μM), the rate of APE1-catalyzed AP-DNA incision is limited by product release (82). Substrates LSP22-4-THF and LSP22-5-THF are equivalent to LSP22-4-dU and LSP22-5-dU, respectively. With LSP22-4-THF (Figure 4A), the endonuclease activity of APE1 increased moderately by about 50% relative to the control, whereas a more dramatic increase was observed with LSP22-5-THF (2-fold increase in relative activity, Figure 4B). The difference can be attributed to the THF position within TFAM:DNA complexes, because LSP22-5-THF may allow for easier access of APE1 to THF due to the lower DNA-binding affinity of TFAM HMG2 domain relative to HMG1 (83,84). The highest activity of APE1 was observed at a TFAM/DNA molar ratio of 2.0 (DNA nearly fully bound) (42), similar to those observed with LSP36-1-dU and LSP36-2-dU. The ability of APE1 to access the substrate under a higher TFAM/DNA molar ratio (2.0) compared to UNG1 (0.6) can be explained by the higher DNA-binding affinity of APE1 (sub-nanomolar) (42) and the difference in the rate-limiting steps compared to UNG1 (85). On the other hand, product yields with R22-1-AP (random sequence) showed a competitive inhibition pattern as TFAM concentration increased (Figure 4C). The different product yield patterns with different substrates indicate that APE1 activity is modulated by lesion position, DNA sequence and TFAM concentrations.

  Moreover, we assessed two additional DNA glycosylases, OGG1 and AAG. The substrates of OGG1 and AAG include some of the most prevalent DNA lesions in cells, such as 8-oxo-7,8-dihydro-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-oxodG) for OGG1, and alkylated DNA modifications, N7-methyl-2′-deoxyguanosine and N3-methyl-2′-deoxyadenosine for AAG. In the case of OGG1, substrate HSP22-1-oxodG contains a heavy-strand promoter (HSP) sequence and a lesion near the TFAM HMG1 domain. Testing for changes in glycosylase activity of OGG1 revealed an overall inhibition trend under increasing TFAM concentrations (Figure 4D, representative gel electrophoresis images in Supplementary Fig. S14). Similarly, with AAG, an overall inhibition pattern was observed under increasing TFAM concentrations using substrates containing a deoxyinosine (dI). LSP22-6-dI and LSP22-7-dI contain a dI near HMG1 or HMG2 domains, respectively (Figs. 4E and S15). Although the lack of stimulation can be explained by the relatively weak DNA binding of AAG (Kd,DNA ∼ 100 nM) (86,87), the inhibition pattern of OGG1 cannot be fully explained by its Kd,DNA (23 nM with a substrate containing an 8-oxodG:dC pair) (88). Therefore, we carried out the additional TFAM-DNA binding analysis (see below). Together, these data demonstrate that in the presence of TFAM, DNA repair activities can be modulated by multiple factors, such as the DNA sequence, extent of TFAM compaction of mtDNA and repair enzymes.


### DNA lesion and sequence context affect TFAM-DNA binding
  TFAM has been shown to bind more strongly to certain DNA substrates containing lesions compared to canonical DNA (reviewed in (89) and references therein), potentially affecting lesion accessibility. Since equilibrium binding experiments offer limited insight into the local interactions and stability of TFAM:DNA complexes, we conducted MD simulation experiments with TFAM:DNA complexes containing either lesioned or canonical DNA. Given the stimulatory effects observed with UNG1, we conducted experiments with two dU substrates (equivalent to LSP22-1-dU and LSP22-2-dU), As observed in Supplementary Figure S16, the dU-containing complexes exhibited minimal differences in total binding energy (1.92 kcal/mol), interaction energy (-6.38 kcal/mol), or the root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) of the Cα carbons of TFAM amino acid residues compared to the canonical substrate. RMSF measures the flexibility of individual residues or the extent of their fluctuation during the simulation. Similar effects were observed when dU was positioned near the HMG2 domain (Supplementary Figure S16E through Supplementary Figure S16H). Additionally, DNA-binding experiments have demonstrated that TFAM exhibits comparable Kd,DNA values and binding stoichiometry with dU-containing and unmodified substrates (42). Therefore, it is unlikely that the presence of a dU significantly impacts the TFAM–DNA binding.

  Next, we examined the TFAM–DNA binding with 8-oxodG substrates using biochemical assays and MD simulations. TFAM binds to certain 8-oxodG-bearing substrates preferentially in vitro (40,90). Although little difference was observed in Kd,DNA between the 8-oxodG and control substrates based on fluorescence polarization measurements with the 22-nt substrates (Supplementary Table S3), EMSA revealed a greater tendency to form 2TFAM:1DNA complexes with LSP22-8-oxodG (Supplementary Fig. S17). Such complexes may promote TFAM oligomerization during nucleoid packaging. In an effort to demonstrate the effect in sequences other than the LSP sequence, experiments with two additional substrates containing the HSP (Supplementary Fig. S18) and site X sequences (Supplementary Fig. S19) were performed. Similar effects were also observed with these substrates. Notably, all the substrates have an 8-oxodG at the first guanine position in the GN10G motif, suggesting the potential of 8-oxodG in promoting TFAM–DNA binding in certain sequence motifs.

  MD simulations provided additional support for this notion. Molecular mechanics Poisson-Boltzmann surface area (MM/PBSA) calculations were employed to evaluate binding energy. In addition to the GN10G motif, we focused on regions near the DNA-intercalating residues (L58 and L182, see Figure 5A) observed in TFAM:DNA crystal structures (51,52). TFAM does not interact significantly with nucleobases in other regions of the complex. For LSP, when the first G of the GN10G motif was substituted with 8-oxodG (blue box in Figure 5A), the total binding energy of the 8-oxodG-bearing system showed a -7.4 kcal/mol difference compared to the system containing the unmodified DNA, indicating a stronger binding affinity for the 8-oxodG substrate (Figure 5B). The TFAM:DNA interaction energies of the 8-oxodG system also showed lower values (approximately −20 kcal/mol) than the unmodified control (Figure 5C). Similar effects were observed when 8-oxodG was positioned at the first G of the GN10G motif in an HSP substrate (Supplementary Figure S20). The stability of the TFAM:DNA complexes was assessed using RMSF of Cα carbon of TFAM amino acid residues. As shown in Figure 5D, the presence of 8-oxodG resulted in lower RMSF values in certain local regions, such as the HMG2 domain (near 8-oxodG) and the linker region (distant from 8-oxodG), suggesting greater stability of the lesion-containing complexes. A greater extent of stabilization was observed with the HSP substrate containing an 8-oxodG in the first G of the GN10G motif (Supplementary Figure S20D). In contrast, no stabilizing effect was observed when 8-oxodG was placed at the last G or the middle of the GN10G motif (LSP in Supplementary Figure S21 or HSP in Supplementary Figure S22), highlighting the sequence-specific impact of 8-oxodG on the stability of TFAM:DNA complexes. Interestingly, the attractive forces are predominantly distributed around the lesion site rather than the lesion itself (Figure 5E and Supplementary Figure S20E). Thus, the MD simulation results corroborate biochemical data and support a model in which 8-oxodG stabilizes the TFAM:DNA complex when located at the first G of the GN10G motif. These findings provide an explanation for the resistance to repair observed when 8-oxodG is present in TFAM:DNA complexes.


### Correlation of 8-oxodG and TFAM locations in mtDNA in cells
  Previously, the colocalization of TFAM with potential 8-oxodG sites was inferred from the correlation of TFAM sites with FPG-sensitive sites in aged rat tissues based on qPCR analysis (91). To assess the correlation between 8-oxodG sites and TFAM footprint, we analyzed the published genome-wide 8-oxodG maps (66) and TFAM ChIP-seq data (27,29). Given the cell-type specificity of many mitochondrial observations, we selected 8-oxodG (66) and TFAM (27,29) mapping data from the same cell type, i.e. HeLa cells. An et al. used CLAPS-seq to map 8-oxodG positions in nuclear DNA (66) under endogenous (untreated) and oxidative stress (treated with KBrO3). We examined these five datasets under both conditions, focusing on mtDNA. Due to mtDNA heterogeneity, we use the top 5% of mapped sites to represent high-frequency 8-oxodG sites. Our results reveal an uneven distribution of 8-oxodG under untreated and stressed conditions (Figure 6). High-frequency 8-oxodG sites were localized to several genes, including ND1, ND2, COX1, ATP8, ND4, ND5 and CYTB. A majority of observed 8-oxodG regions are common with both endogenous sites and those from KBrO3 treatment. Notably, 8-oxodG is highly enriched in the non-coding region of mtDNA in HeLa cells under both conditions. A different study using MCF10A cells found a lack of accumulation of 8-oxodG in the non-coding region (92). The difference may be due to the cell lines used. Furthermore, we analyzed the correlation between the high-frequency 8-oxodG sites and high TFAM sites (27,29). A significant overlap was observed between high TFAM and 8-oxodG sites in two of the three untreated samples (WT rep 2 and rep 3) (Table 2). These data indicate a correlation between the TFAM high-occupancy sites and 8-oxodG sites, supporting a model of TFAM-binding to 8-oxodG-containg mtDNA. Conversely, the mapped dU sites do not show any correlations with high TFAM sites (Supplementary Table S4 and Supplementary Figure S23), corroborating the lack of preferential binding of TFAM with dU substrates observed in DNA-binding assays and MD simulations.


## Discussion
  Characterizing mtDNA repair enzymes in the context of TFAM-packaged DNA is fundamental to understanding the biological consequences of damaged mtDNA and its contribution to mtDNA heteroplasmy. In addition, given the emerging roles of TFAM (34) and mtDNA repair enzymes (35–37) in innate immunity, insights into how TFAM–DNA binding regulates DNA repair are also essential for identifying the molecular signatures of mtDNA-mediated immune response and inflammation. In this study, we used in vitro biochemical assays, MD simulations, and bioinformatics to examine the impact of TFAM-packaged DNA on repair processes. Our data reveal novel stimulatory effects of UNG1 and APE1 under optimal TFAM concentrations with specific DNA substrates, while inhibitory effects were observed for OGG1 and AAG. Furthermore, MD simulations and bioinformatics analyses corroborate a model that TFAM binds preferentially to certain 8-oxodG-containing substrates, thereby modulating the accessibility of 8-oxodG to repair enzymes.

  The levels of DNA repair enzymes are a crucial for the efficacy of mtDNA repair. Nearly all mtDNA repair enzymes exist as multiple isoforms that can localize to both the nucleus and mitochondria. The distribution can vary depending on factors such as the cell-cycle (14), cell types (19,20) and type of stress (18). Interestingly, for some of these enzymes, the mitochondrial localization does not dependent on the presence of mtDNA, as observed with ρ° cells (15). While direct comparisons of the protein levels between TFAM and repair factors in mitochondria are rare in the literature (likely due to the need for careful cellular fractionation and validation) TFAM is presumed to be one of the two most abundant proteins, the other being mitochondrial single-stranded DNA binding protein. TFAM has been used as a marker for nucleoids in immunostaining studies (93,94). Historically, various methods, including cross-linking and proximity labeling, have been employed to characterize nucleoid-associated proteins, resulting a current list of approximately 37 proteins (reviewed in (22) and references therein). Notably, none of the repair proteins appear on these lists, suggesting that the repair factors are either present in very low abundance or only transiently interact with mtDNA. Nonetheless, robust DNA repair activities of mitochondrial extracts have been demonstrated in various studies (15,42,95). Clearly, additional research is needed to characterize the different pools of DNA repair proteins and factors influencing their distribution.

  While the macroscopic Kd,DNA values of TFAM from ensemble studies show little difference between mtDNA promoter sequences and random sequences, single molecule analyses reveal different DNA bending dynamics with different DNA sequences (77). The distribution of TFAM along the mtDNA molecule is not uniform, supported by TFAM ChIP-seq data (27,29) and single-molecule DNA sequencing data (23). In addition, the single molecule long-read sequencing data demonstrate that approximately 80% of nucleoids are inaccessible, while the remaining 20% show variability in mtDNA packaging states (23). Considering the significant variation in TFAM levels across different tissues and pathological conditions (96), DNA accessibility may vary considerably under different biological circumstances. These studies highlight the potential for variable TFAM occupancy along mtDNA and under different biological conditions. In this study, we analyzed the DNA repair activities under varying TFAM concentrations to mimic the different degrees of TFAM coverage on mtDNA. Detailed biochemical analyses revealed optimal TFAM/DNA molar ratios that accelerate the enzymatic turnover of UNG1 and APE1. Despite the moderate increase and sequence dependence, our results suggest multiple layers of regulation in mtDNA repair capabilities, including DNA sequence, DNA modifications, TFAM concentrations and DNA glycosylases.

  The stimulatory effects of UNG1 under optimal TFAM concentrations are attributed to primarily the increased Km as evidenced by steady-state kinetic analyses. Although a hormetic effect was also observed with APE1 and TFAM assays, the optimal TFAM concentration differs from those observed in UNG1 assays, suggesting distinct underlying mechanisms. A displacement model has also been used to explain the stimulatory effects observed with other BER enzyme pairs (97). Because many DNA glycosylases have a product release step that is rate-limiting during catalysis, efficiently shuttling repair intermediates through the BER machinery without exposing toxic intermediates to the cellular environment is presumed advantageous (12). Interestingly, single molecule studies have shown that a significant portion of DNA-binding events by APE1 and pol β do not necessarily facilitate substrate channeling (98). Given the abundance of TFAM in mitochondrial nucleoids, it is plausible that TFAM aids in channeling or shielding certain BER intermediates. Additionally, TFAM’s dRp lyase activity may facilitate DNA repair (47), given the high abundance of TFAM, the relatively weak dRp lyase activity of pol γ (99) and the comparatively weak DNA-binding activity of pol β (98).

  TFAM has been shown to bind preferentially to DNA substrates with modifications, including 8-oxodG (40,90), 5-methylcytidine (100) and O4-alkylthymidine (101). The binding preference is DNA sequence dependent (40,90). A minimal binding motif, GN10G, has been proposed for TFAM-DNA binding (76), and there are thousands of such motifs in mtDNA. It should be noted that although the initial report identifies the 1.9-fold higher binding affinity of TFAM with the GN10G substrate compared to the AN10A substrate under high salt conditions (500 mM NaCl), the affinity of TFAM with the two substrates are comparable under physiological conditions (150 mM NaCl) (76). Recent analyses of TFAM-binding patterns based on the single molecule long-read sequencing data found no correlation with the GN10G motif (23). Therefore, there are other unknown sequence effects contribute to TFAM binding.

  Our data demonstrate that TFAM preferentially binds to 8-oxodG substrates, particularly when 8-oxodG is located at certain sequence motifs. This finding partially explains the resistance to repair by OGG1 and suggests that TFAM may protect 8-oxodG from repair at certain sites. This model is supported by the colocalization of 8-oxodG positions and high TFAM sites in HeLa cells. Furthermore, over-expression of mitochondrial-targeting OGG1 reduces the level of 8-oxodG in the mitochondrial genome (35), corroborating the competitive binding model between TFAM and OGG1. In contrast, no correlation was found for dU, consistent with the lack of preferential binding of TFAM with dU-containing DNA substrates in MD stimulations and DNA-binding assays. Notably, colocalization between mitochondrial AP positions and low TFAM sites has been reported (102), underscoring the importance of DNA modifications in determining the preferential binding of TFAM.

  The shielding of DNA lesions by TFAM can be functionally important in at least two ways. First, the model provides a plausible explanation for the resistance of mutagenesis of mtDNA under repair-deficient conditions or after exposure to known nuclear mutagens. For example, knock-out of OGG1 or MUTYH in mice does not lead to an increase in the mtDNA point mutation load (103). Similarly, exposure to known mutagens, benzo[a]pyrene or N-ethyl-N-nitrosourea (ENU) did not increase point or deletion mutation frequencies in mice (104). Exposing C. elegans aflatoxin B1 has no effect on mtDNA mutation frequency, spectrum, or trinucleotide context signature (105). The damaged mtDNA could be sequestered by TFAM from mutagenesis. Alternatively, there may be unknown mechanisms to regulate mtDNA mutagenesis. Second, considering the role of 8-oxodG in immune response (37–39), the shielding of 8-oxodG-containing mtDNA (whether intact or fragmented) can serve as a means for modulating immune signaling. The preferential accumulation of 8-oxodG in the fragmented mtDNA (106) and the elevation of 8-oxodG-containing DNA in mitochondria and the cytoplasm under suppressed OGG1 expression (37) support a model that at least a subpopulation of the 8-oxodG in mtDNA escapes repair and triggers inflammation pathways. Clearly, additional studies are needed to test these hypotheses.

  In summary, our study reveals the complexity of mtDNA repair, which is regulated by the abundance of TFAM, DNA sequence, lesion types and the level and type of repair enzymes. TFAM can remove certain types of DNA repair intermediates, such as 5′-dRp. The preferential binding of TFAM to specific lesion-containing DNA may help protect mtDNA from mutagenesis. Considering its recently reported role of facilitating autophagy (34), there is much more to learn about the involvement of TFAM in mitochondrial biology and human pathology.
