# An in silico approach uncovering the competency of oncolytic human adenovirus 52 for targeted breast cancer virotherapy


## Abstract
  Breast cancer remains a major health threat throughout the world specifically in women above 30 years of age however, it is rarely known to affect men as well. It is characterized by the abnormal division of cells in the breast tissue resulting in the development of breast malignancies. Various risk factors contributing to breast cancer include age, family history, genetic mutations (chiefly in BRCA1 and BRCA2 genes) along with hormonal imbalances (oestrogen, progesterone, HER2). Early detection which can be obtained through frequent rounds of self-examination, mammographic scanning, and clinical assessment plays a crucial role in the prevention of the disease. In addition, appropriate diagnosis assists in better therapeutic responses. This study highlights the considerable health risks associated with the conventional treatment procedures which arise and increased demand of advanced, secure, and risk-free treatment alternatives. Oncolytic viruses are potentially apparent for the aim of improving cancer therapeutics with reduced side effects. These viruses act as the fundamental therapeutic agent themselves that selectively target and kill malignant cells without harm to healthy tissues. The key objective of the research is to provide evidence that Human Adenovirus 52 is a potent oncolytic virus and to highlight its capacity to target and eliminate cancer cells with precision while causing the least amount of harm to healthy tissues. Validating the in-silico method entails evaluating the precision and dependability of the computational modelling by contrasting the in-silico predictions with the findings from the experiments rank as the secondary objective. The workflow of this research utilized in-silico computational drug designing approaches including retrieval of tertiary structures of both the target Breast Cancer Type 1 Susceptibility Protein (BRCA1) and the viral Human Adenovirus 52 protein, their validation generating Ramachandran Plots determining favoured amino acid residue angles and prediction of their active residues. Furthermore, the study focused on the molecular dynamics docking of proteins, interpretation of molecular interactions between the docked complex, as well as the assessment of the molecular dynamic simulations (MD) in addition to their MMGBSA binding energy calculations. A successful docking between BRCA1 and Adenovirus protein provided a significant score of 329.2 +/- 24.3, furthermore, MD simulations showed a high RMSD peak at 2.8 Å, RMSF were maximum at 3.5 Å with highest protein–protein interaction, the radius of gyration was stable throughout the simulation representing elastic stability along with a high energy interaction value of - 7882 kCal/mol. Moreover, the MMGBSA calculation results showed a notable release of binding free energy of - 68.96 kCal/mol demonstrating effective bond formation between the docked complex. These findings propose the effectiveness of Human Adenovirus 52 to treat cancer. The selected oncolytic Human Adenovirus 52 is a potential candidate for the target specific treatment of breast cancer through virotherapy. This computer-aided drug discovery presents significant potential in targeting cancer cells and would assist in the development of potent drug reagents for the cancer therapy.


## Introduction
  Breast cancer is among the top three leading cancers worldwide in addition to colon and lung cancer and is a significant cause of malignancy in females such that, one in ten women are susceptible to this disease in their course of life1. Although less common, male breast cancer is still a serious health concern. Even though it only makes up around 1% of all cases of breast cancer, it is crucial to increase knowledge and awareness of this illness2. Different populations have varied rates of male breast cancer. It is, nevertheless, often less common than the frequency in females. According to certain research, men with Klinefelter syndrome, those with a family background in breast cancer, and those exposed to specific environmental conditions have a marginally increased risk of developing breast cancer. Breast malignancy causes symptoms such as a breast lump, nipple inversion, breast secretions (blood/watery substances), red patches and irritation in the skin surrounding the breast as well as the presence of a tumour mass3. Male breast cancer shares many of the same clinical signs and symptoms as female breast cancer. On the other hand, there might be some variations in the physiology and management of breast cancer in men and women. Male breast cancers, for instance, may react differently to specific treatments and have a greater probability to be identified at an advanced stage. The likelihood of survival for male breast cancer patients might differ based on various factors, including the cancer’s kind, stage, and overall health. Male cancer of breasts is less prevalent, but it is curable, and many men who have it go on leading long lives4. Furthermore, in case of metastasis (spread of cancer); signs of lymphadenopathy, breathing problems, bone soreness and discomfort may appear5. This disease is common in women below 30 or between 40 and 49 years of age. In 2020, there were over 2.3 million new cases of breast cancer identified, according to the World Health Organisation (WHO). The breakthroughs in early identification and treatment have led to a dramatic improvement in breast cancer survival rates in recent decades. However, a few variables, including the cancer’s form, stage, and overall health of the patient, might affect the overall survival rate. Genetic mutations, obesity, heredity factors, elevated mitotic indices further contribute to breast cancer6. Genetically, breast cancer is associated with mutations particularly in BRCA1 and BRCA2 genes, causing the disease in nearly 45% of inherited breast cancer cases. In addition, BRIP1 (BRCA1-Interacting Protein 1), ATM (Ataxia telangiectasia mutated) and BARD1 (BRCA1-Associated Ring Domain 1) gene mutations increase the risk by two-four folds7. Not all women are more susceptible to breast cancer than others, even though BRCA1 and BRCA2 are well-known risk factors for the disease. Breast cancer can also arise as a result of a wide range of additional hereditary variables, lifestyle decisions, and environmental exposures8. Breast cancer risk may be influenced by the hormone receptors (progesterone and oestrogen) on or in breast cancer cells. In general, women who develop hormone receptor-positive carcinoma of the breast are more vulnerable than those who develop hormone receptor-negative breast cancer. Lifestyle factors that may increase the risk of developing breast cancer include the following: An increased risk of breast cancer has been associated with a diet low in vegetables, fruits, and whole grains and rich in processed meat and red meat9. Drinking too much alcohol can raise your risk of breast cancer, especially in women who have just gone through menopause. Furthermore, especially after menopause, regular exercise can lower the risk of breast cancer. On the other hand, after menopause, being overweight or obese can raise your risk of breast cancer.

  Early prognosis and undetected metastasis increase treatment potential. Conventional treatment follows medical and radiation oncology along with surgical approaches. Medical oncology includes endocrine therapy, chemotherapy, adjuvant/neo-adjuvant therapy and HER-2 targeted therapy. Radiation oncology treatment post-surgery encompasses; whole breast radiation, post lumpectomy/postmastectomy radiations as well as accelerated partial breast radiations. Surgical procedures involve breast-conservation surgery (lumpectomy), breast removal and reconstruction surgery due to breast removal10. Although the above-mentioned treatment modalities are effective in terms of first line of action, their associated limitations pose a serious threat to human health11. Even with the tremendous advancements in breast cancer treatment, several obstacles still exist. Breast cancer that has migrated to other parts of the body known as metastatic breast cancer, affects 20–30% of those diagnosed with breast cancer at the time of diagnosis. Currently, there is no cure for metastatic breast cancer; instead, the goal of treatment is to manage symptoms and extend survival. Chemotherapy and targeted treatments can cause cancer cells to become resistant, which reduces their efficacy. Hair loss, exhaustion, nausea, and hot flashes are just a few of the serious side effects that many cancer treatment treatments can cause. The quality of life of a patient may be considerably impacted by these adverse effects. The biology and behaviour of breast cancer can vary greatly, making it a heterogeneous illness. This makes it difficult to develop treatments that are effective for all patients.

  Chemotherapeutics cause adverse damage including, fatigue, vomiting, allergic reactions, peripheral neuropathy, mouth dryness and ulcers, hair loss (alopecia) and decreased kidney functions etc12. Radiotherapy induced side effects may include; fibrosis (scarring of the tissue), breast oedema and radiogenic dermatitis (rashes)13. Henceforth, development of targeted therapies and anti-cancer drugs with selective activity on cancerous cells sparing healthy tissues has become a necessity. A constructive approach towards this objective is to take advantage of the naturally-occurring oncolytic viruses having the characteristic ability of selective replication in cancerous cells without impairing healthy tissues14. Oncolytic virotherapy comprises of virally induced cellular cytotoxicity to kill the cancerous cells and is integrated with immune mediated cytotoxic responses. Moreover, the apoptotic death machinery of the cancerous cells is directed and regulated by these target-specific viruses accounting to cellular demise however, only after there is maximum utilisation of the infected cells’ resources for viral development15. In addition, the ineffective interferon-beta signalling pathway in cancer cells facilitates viral infection16. This pathway performs a critical role of activating antiviral defence systems in healthy tissues which are known to significantly reduce metabolic activities of the virus as well as restrict its replication and multiplication. However, in cancerous cells these antiviral defences are impaired hence aid in the survival of the viral entity17. Moreover, characteristics such as indefinite division of malignant cells irrespective of growth inhibitors, absence of cellular apoptosis and lack of immune feedback further promote the viability of this therapy18. Furthermore these viruses trigger immune system activation through antigenic release. Tumour-associated antigens are released by oncolytic viruses upon infection and lysis of cancer cells (TAAs). The immune system may be prompted by these TAAs to identify and combat cancerous cells. In addition, immune checkpoint inhibitors can be expressed by certain oncolytic viruses, which can improve immunity against tumours and help combat immune suppression19. Oncolytic virus-induced immune responses can have a major effect on how well the treatment is effective. Although the immune system can be a potent weapon against cancer, tumours can also use it to their advantage to avoid being discovered and eliminated. One example would be the emergence of anti-tumour immunity, which enables a strong immune response to destroy cancer cells at the original tumour site as well as in distant metastases20. Benefits to long-term survival may arise from this. Tumours, however, can evolve defence strategies against the immune system, like producing an immunosuppressive environment or expressing immunosuppressive chemicals21. Strategies to boost the immune response such as combining immune checkpoint inhibitors or other immunotherapies with oncolytic viruses are being investigated as a means of countering immune evasion. Furthermore, comprehending the principles underlying tumour immune evasion can aid in the discovery of biomarkers that inform tailored therapy and forecast treatment response22. This research emphasizes the use of the Human Adenovirus 52 for the purpose of targeted breast cancer oncolytic virotherapy. In addition, the human body consists of a CAR receptor - coxsackie and adenovirus receptor which is a transmembrane cell attachment protein that will promote the adhesion of adenovirus to target cells. This interaction is facilitated by the presence of a fibre knob on the Human Adenovirus 52 known to efficiently bind with the CAR receptor23. Henceforth, this mechanism plays a significant role in allowing the viral entry into the breast cancer cells. Furthermore, the expression of the CAR receptor on breast cancer cells24 additionally facilitates the process. Human Adenovirus 52 (Ad52) is unique among oncolytic viruses for the following reasons: Recognition of Polysialic Acid (PSA) is a key feature of the adenovirus serotype 52. Ad52 binds to polysialic acid (PSA) as its principal receptor, in contrast to other adenoviruses that typically bind to the coxsackie-adenovirus receptor (CAR). Since many cancer cells overexpress PSA, Ad52 is more distinguishing when it relates to targeting tumours25. Furthermore, it has broad tissue tropism such that Ad52 exhibits a broad spectrum of infection, including the ability to infect cancer cells, which may confer broad-spectrum efficacy against a variety of tumour types. Along with this it has a characteristic of deeper tissue penetration as compared to other oncolytic viruses, research has demonstrated that Ad52 is more capable of penetrating tumours than other adenoviruses, which enables deeper infection and the death of cancer cells25. In addition, it has the ability of potent immune activation in comparison to other oncolytic viruses. Ad52 infection and cancer cell lysis can elicit a stronger anti-tumour immune response. This is explained by its enhanced capacity to activate immune cells and release a higher quantity of tumour-associated antigens26. Compared to other oncolytic viruses, Ad52 has demonstrated a generally favourable safety profile with little adverse effects. Its distinct receptor binding and diminished off-target effects could be contributing factors of this27. Owing to its clinical progress, studies have suggested positive preliminary findings that Ad52 is presently being assessed in clinical trials for several cancer types, and promising initial outcomes have been observed. The preliminary results indicate that Ad52 may offer a therapeutic benefit over other oncolytic viruses, but additional research is required.

  Adenoviruses have previously been used as viral vectors for gene therapy in cancer treatment. These viruses have been genetically engineered to express various tumour antigens as well as immune specific molecules. Moreover, adenoviruses have also been modified with the characteristic ability to deliver transgenes encoding p53 - a tumour suppressor gene which upon expression halts the cell cycle of tumours28. However, the primary focus of this research is to employ the Human Adenovirus 52 as a pharmaceutical drug itself16. Due to their distinguished ability of significant and targeted amplification in malignant cells they are a potential candidate for cancer treatment. Moreover, till date any significant detrimental side effects have not been reported because of virotherapy29. Various oncolytic viruses are currently being studied in clinical trials for a range of malignancies, such as glioblastoma, melanoma, prostate cancer, and breast cancer. Researchers have the ability to increase the virus’s selectivity for tumour cells by modifying viral envelope proteins to target particular receptors found on cancer cells30. Clinical investigations for breast cancer are presently evaluating several oncolytic viruses. Among the more noteworthy candidates are T-VEC, or Talimogene Laherparepvec: This modified form of the herpes simplex virus is being studied in conjunction with chemotherapy for the treatment of breast cancer and has been permitted for the management of metastatic melanoma31. Reolysin: In clinical studies for metastatic breast cancer, this unaltered reovirus has demonstrated encouraging outcomes32. Adenovirus: ONYX-015 and Ad5/3-Δ24 are two adenovirus-based oncolytic viruses that are being researched for breast cancer.

  With advancements in technology and bioinformatic tools, in silico approaches have gained considerable potential in providing preferable treatment options. Computational techniques have increased efficacy in comparison to accustomed drug designing as these are less time-consuming and come with reduced expenses33. In-silico computational methods have significantly accelerated drug discovery and cancer therapeutics by providing efficient and cost-effective ways to identify and optimize potential drug candidates. Rapid screening of possible therapeutic candidates is one such advancements that allows researchers to promptly uncover prospective therapeutic candidates by screening enormous libraries of compounds against target proteins via virtual screening. Vast molecular libraries have been screened against the SARS-CoV-2 virus using deep learning methods, which have proven effective in identifying possible COVID-19 treatment options34. An example of in-silico drug design includes Sitagliptin. It is a medication that is used to treat type 2 diabetes. It was found by in-silico screening against the enzyme dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) using a large molecular library35. Another such example of the effective drug discovery through computational tools includes the antiretroviral drug doravine, which is used to treat HIV infection36. Moreover, compared to common wet-lab screening, in-silico procedures can save a significant amount of time and money37. This research aims to design a therapeutic oncolytic adenovirus through in silico tools, using Human Adenovirus 52 for a targeted treatment of breast cancer corresponding to BRCA1-BRCT mutations with the purpose of alleviating the several health risks accompanying conventional treatment methods and to provide a safer mode of treatment for cancer patients. The key perspective of this therapy is that the virus fundamentally acts as the operative therapeutic agent rather than being utilized as a carrier for drug delivery16. The goal of this study is to suggest the competency of oncolytic adenoviruses for targeted cancer virotherapy. The development of oncolytic viruses as possible cancer therapeutics requires a vital step: moving from in-silico observations to in-vivo testing. Before the virus is tested on humans, it must pass several stringent assessments and optimisations to guarantee its efficacy and safety. We need to select animal models for preclinical in-vivo testing by choosing suitable animal models capable of reliably assessing the safety and efficacy of the virus and that closely resemble the human disease. Following which would be the dose optimisation which requires establishing the oncolytic virus’s ideal dosage to maximise effectiveness and reduce toxicity. In addition, the most suitable delivery route of administration would need to be selected such as intratumoral injection, intravenous injection, or other techniques. Furthermore, assessment of efficacy and safety is a major factor to determine whether the virus can specifically target and kill cancer cells. We would also need to consider any possible negative consequences. For this purpose, we would require regulatory aspects to be considered including development of a preclinical research file that include details on the manufacturing process, safety, and effectiveness of the virus. Along with which we need to ensure regulatory acceptance by sending the preclinical research file for evaluation and approval to regulatory bodies, such as the European Medicines Agency (EMA) or the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in the United States. Moreover, the medical trials need to be conducted in phases such that the following aspects are brought into account: Phase I Trials to evaluate safety and tolerability, conduct preliminary clinical trials with a limited number of participants. Then proceed with Phase II Trials to assess the virus’s effectiveness in a greater number of patients suffering from the intended malignancy. The last would be Phase III Trials to carry out extensive randomised controlled trials to verify the virus’s safety and effectiveness in relation to conventional therapies. This study will prove to be an effective strategic outlook to bring forth such risk-free cancer therapeutics.


## Materials and methods

### Retrieval of breast cancer protein
  The tertiary structure of the target protein was obtained from RCSB PDB – Protein Data Bank (available at: https://www.rcsb.org) with PDB ID: 1N5O, a cancer-causing mutated anti-tumour protein38. RCSB PDB is a database that provides experimentally curated three-dimensional structures of proteins and other macromolecules. Using information on this database scientific research can be carried out for analysing and identifying various biological complexes associated ligands as well as sequence annotations.


## Viral protein structure retrieval
  The tertiary structure of the drug candidate; oncolytic viral protein – Adenovirus Serotype 52 was retrieved from RCSB Protein Data Bank (available at: https://www.rcsb.org) with PDB ID: 6G4739.


## Purification of tertiary structures
  The purification of the retrieved 3D structures: 1N50 (Breast Cancer Type 1 Susceptibility Protein) and 6G47 (Adenovirus Serotype 52) was done using PyMol (available at: https://pymol.org/2/). During the purification process, the water molecules and naturally occurring ligands were removed. This software allows virtual visualization of three-dimensional structures of small molecules, their complexes and of macromolecules. It aids in generating characteristic animations of protein structures along with high-quality images. Furthermore, PyMol is a suitable tool for the analysis of proteins through computational approaches40.


## Validation of 3D structures
  In order to validate the tertiary structures, PROCHECK (available at: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/thornton-srv/databases/pdbsum/Generate.html) was used to develop Ramachandran plot for both the Breast Cancer Type 1 susceptibility protein and Adenovirus Serotype 52. This tool aids in the quality assessment and validation of protein structures. It recognizes errors in the geometry of the models and assists in ensuring the accuracy of protein structures. Furthermore, it also examines the stereochemical characteristics of the structures. The Ramachandran Plot indicates the dihedral orientation angles of the constituent amino acid residues of the proteins therefore the results of these plots indicate the allowed regions where the amino acids can be positioned and regions where the orientations are disallowed41.


## Active site prediction
  CAST p (Computed Atlas of Surface Topography) an online server (available at: http://sts.bioe.uic.edu/castp/index.html?1bxw) allows the prediction of the active sites of protein structures. This software facilitates the identification of the functional sites of the proteins. The tool was used to discover the active sites of the Breast Cancer Type 1 susceptibility protein as well as the Adenovirus Serotype 52 viral protein. The server assists in the visualization of protein pockets that are known to cognate with binding activities of proteins. It provides a guide to the possible binding sites for candidate ligands42.


### Protein-protein interaction analysis
  To get insights of the functional protein network of the receptor protein – BRCA1, a STRING analysis was carried out. STRING is an online tool (available at: https://string-db.org) that facilitates the study of known and predicted protein partners of a particular protein of interest. A detailed STRING analysis gives information about direct and indirect interactions of proteins obtained from a vast variety of resources ranging from experimental data, information suggested through scientific literature, as well as co-expression and in-depth text-mining43.


## Molecular dynamics docking
  The molecular dynamics docking was carried out to dock the target – Breast Cancer Type 1 Susceptibility protein and the drug candidate – Adenovirus serotype 52 using the HADDOCK web server (available at: https://wenmr.science.uu.nl/haddock2.4/). HADDOCK – High Ambiguity Driven Biomolecular Docking is a characteristic tool used in computer-aided drug designing to carry out docking studies of molecular complexes. It allows the interpretation of protein-protein interactions resulting in the selection of suitable drug candidates. The tool utilizes experimentally curated data along with information in bioinformatics to carry out docking of the given complexes44.


## Molecular interaction analysis
  To understand the interactions of the docked complex between the target protein and the adenovirus, two-dimensional and three-dimensional interactions were analysed using ligplus + and PyMol respectively. Ligplus + provides insights to the interactions between the ligands and proteins by generating two-dimensional graphical visualizations45.


### Molecular dynamic simulations
  The molecular dynamic simulations are critical in analysing the movement of the various atoms in the proteins that allow a better understanding of the atomic interactions and behaviour of atoms. This information aids in comprehension of different molecular activities such as binding of ligands, changes in protein conformations, and folding of proteins. It further provides information about the Root Mean Square Deviations, Root Mean Square Fluctuations, The Solvent Accessible Surface Area, Radius of Gyration, and the Energy values of the proteins throughout the simulations which are discussed in detail in the results section.

  Desmond gmx, version 2021.4 was used to carry out molecular dynamic simulations. The tool is extensively used due to it being feasible, well-regulated and freely accessible. The results of the simulations will then be verified by comparison with experimental evidence46. Furthermore, a calculation of MMGBSA – Molecular Mechanics with Generalised Born and Surface Area Solvation was also carried out, which is a known method for the computation of the energy generated upon the binding of ligand molecules with larger biomolecules. It is a calculate of the binding affinity of the receptor-ligand complex and indicates the energy released because of bond formations between the two molecules. The findings of MMGBSA assist in improving the outcomes of the molecular dynamics docking and interactions during drug designing47. Hawdock a freely accessible server (available at: http://cadd.zju.edu.cn/hawkdock/) was used to calculate the MMGBSA energy of the molecular docked complex. It is a popular web server used to predict protein-protein interactions of the receptor-ligand complex48.

### Validation of tertiary structures
  The purified structure of the target protein 1N5O was validated using PROCHECK. The respective Ramachandran plot was generated which predicted 157 residues i.e. 87.2% in the most favoured regions, 21 residues i.e. 11.7% in the additionally allowed regions and 0 residues in the disallowed regions. Likewise, the Ramachandran Plot for the Adenoviral protein 6G47 predicted 376 residues i.e. 88.5% in the most favoured regions, 49 residues i.e. 11.5% in the additionally allowed regions and 0 residues in the disallowed regions. The Ramachandran Plots of the tertiary structures are given in Fig. 2(A and B). The most energetically advantageous combinations of phi (φ) and psi (ψ) dihedral angles for amino acid residues in a protein structure are represented by preferred regions in a Ramachandran plot. These areas line up with conformations that reduce steric collisions between the atoms in the structural framework of the peptide. A high proportion of residues in the preferred regions are shown in both the plots, indicating that the protein models are of generally high quality. This is encouraging for the stability and functionality of the protein. The structural integrity of the proteins is improved by these conformations, which permit the best possible hydrogen bonding and van der Waals interactions among amino acid residues. To ensure protein stability, enable appropriate folding, and maintain adequate protein function, amino acids must be located within the favoured regions of a Ramachandran plot. Deviations from these regions frequently point to possible problems with the quality of the protein model or the structure of the protein which has not been observed during the validation of the tertiary structures.

### Molecular dynamics docking
  Using the HADDOCK web tool, we conducted molecular docking between the BRAC1 and the drug candidate adenovirus 52, as illustrated in Fig. 5. The docking results of all clusters can be seen in Table 1. Scoring Function and Determination of the Score of HADDOCK utilizes a scoring function that combines various energy terms (Van der Waals Energy, Electrostatic Energy, Desolation Energy, and Restraints Violation Energy). The final HADDOCK score is a weighted sum of these energy components, which gives an overall indication of the binding affinity and stability of the docked complex. In our study, the significant docking score of 329.2 ± 24.3 was determined by averaging the scores from the top cluster of poses, ensuring statistical robustness. The docking score of 329.2 ± 24.3 suggests a strong and favourable interaction between the breast cancer protein and the viral protein. When compared to other similar systems, this score falls within the expected range for effective protein-protein interactions, which typically exhibit HADDOCK scores between 200 and 400, depending on the system and conditions. For instance, studies involving docking of similar protein complexes have reported scores in the range of 300–350, indicating that our result is consistent with known effective interactions. This high score reflects the stability and binding affinity of the docked complex, suggesting that the adenovirus 52 could be a potent inhibitor of the target breast cancer protein.

### Molecular dynamic simulations
  The molecular dynamic simulations depict various factors of the docked complexes including RMSD which is the Root Mean Structure Deviation. The RMSD of the following docked complex illustrates conformational changes with significant fluctuations from 0 to 18 nanoseconds, however at nearly 20 nanoseconds the deviations became stabilized throughout 100ns simulation. The highest peak was observed at around 2.8 Å at 90 nanoseconds. The stabilization of the RSMD of protein and ligand after 20ns indicated that the Adenovirus 52 in fit in the pocket of the BRAC1 protein. The RMSD plot helps distinguish between the major structural variations throughout the simulations. These deviations are represented in Fig. 8.

  Fig. 8The Root Mean Deviations of Docked Complex (RMSD).

  Secondly, the simulation shows the Root Mean Square Fluctuation – RMSF, the high peaks at around the center of the graph represent the protein-protein interactions. The values of the fluctuations indicate most of the energetic regions in the complex, providing a better understanding of the structural dynamics of the system. RMSF is an average estimate of protein residue divergence from a point of reference over a period. ## Discussion
  Amongst women in 140 countries, breast cancer is the most common cancer to be diagnosed. The reality that over half of breast cancer fatalities and over half of diagnoses occur in developing nations cannot be ignored, even though industrialized countries have high incidence rates of breast cancer49. The quality of life for patients receiving current treatments is rapidly declining due to their significant side effects and inability to completely cure metastatic breast cancer. This means that to cure breast cancer, new therapeutic strategies like virotherapy must be developed.

  While radiation and chemotherapy are frequently used as adjuvants in the treatment of breast cancer, surgery is frequently utilized to remove primary breast tumours. Systemically administered adjuvant medicines are intended to eliminate cancer cells that have spread throughout the body to reduce the likelihood of new cancer cells developing50. The most popular adjuvant regimens for chemotherapy involve cyclophosphamide plus doxorubicin, as well as docetaxel or methotrexate plus 5-fluorouracil. On the other hand, off-target effects and the emergence of treatment resistance restrict the application of radiation and chemotherapy. These altered gaps are used by the treatment approach known as “virotherapy” to specifically target cancer cells51. In general, there are two sub-classes of cancer virotherapy: (I) using replicating viruses as oncolytic agents and (II) using non-replicating viruses as vectors for cancer gene therapy.

  Our in-silico analysis of oncolytic human adenovirus 52 suggest good findings for targeted breast cancer virotherapy, complementing experimental findings in similar studies. Hemminki et al. (2017) employed a miRNA-targeting strategy, inserting miR-145-5p binding sites into the adenoviral genome to selectively replicate in breast cancer cells without affecting normal cells52. Their work demonstrated that increasing miRNA binding sites enhanced the virus’s selectivity for cancer cells, particularly in breast cancer cell lines such as MDA-MB-453 and MCF-752. Similarly, Zhu et al. (2020) evaluated a Cox-2 promoter-controlled OAd designed to encode the human sodium iodide symporter (hNIS), targeting breast cancer stem cells (BCSCs) resistant to conventional therapies. Their results highlighted the vector’s ability to inhibit tumorsphere formation and reverse paclitaxel resistance in TaxR cells53. While these studies involve complex in-vitro methods and modifications, our in-silico approach focuses on the stability, binding affinities, and interaction profiles of adenovirus 52 with relevant cancer biomarkers. This computational analysis serves as an important precursor to experimental validation, providing a basis for further exploration of adenovirus-based therapies52,53.

  Oncolytic adenovirus transductionally targeted via chimeric fibre and designed to encode a full-length antibody against HER2 was described in a study by Liikanen et al. The virus was shown to have a strong anti-tumour effect on a HER2 + tumour in mice and to efficiently kill HER2 + BRCA cells in vitro. To decrease liver toxicity and increase infection of BRCA cells, which typically exhibit large levels of Lyp-1 receptor, a study by Xu et al. examined another oncolytic adenovirus for BRCA in which the fibre protein was changed to incorporate Lyp-1 peptide. Likewise, in this study, the approach to utilizing virotherapy for breast cancer was employed. Therefore, human adenovirus serotype 52 was used in this study as a therapeutic oncolytic adenovirus treatment employing in silico methods for the targeted treatment of breast cancer corresponding to BRCA1-BRCT mutations. The goal is to develop a safer form of treatment while reducing the many health concerns associated with traditional approaches.

  Adenovirus Serotype 52 (PDB ID: 6G47) and Breast Cancer Type 1 Susceptibility Protein (PDB ID: 1N5O) tertiary structures were obtained. A stable docked complex with a HADDOCK score of 329.2 +/- 24.3 was produced because of active site prediction, which identified important amino acid residues essential for molecular docking. Protein-protein interactions are facilitated by hydrogen bonds between amino acids, as demonstrated by molecular interaction studies [52–56]. Conformational changes in the docked complex were shown by molecular dynamics simulations; RMSD indicated stability after 20 nanoseconds, RMSF highlighted regions of protein-protein interaction. The RMSF values also showed expected trends, with fluctuations generally remaining below 2 Å, except for loop regions, which may exhibit higher variability. Our results fall within these ranges, supporting the conclusion that the BRCA1-Adenovirus 52 complex is stable under the simulated conditions. The radius of Gyration suggested overall stability, and SASA indicated variations in solvent accessibility. The stability of energy graphs was seen throughout, indicating the robustness of the system. Collectively, these discoveries shed light on the structural dynamics and interactions between the viral and breast cancer proteins, with considerable implications for future treatment approaches.


## Conclusion
  Breast cancer is a significant health challenge accounting to the increased rate of mortality worldwide. Extensive research efforts aid in the deeper understanding of the biology and epidemiology of the disease along with advancements in the field of medicine which have led to outstanding progress. Furthermore, in silico tools are emerging as a promising approach for drug discovery, increasing patient response, likewise this research reveals the competency of the oncolytic adenoviruses as a candidate for drug development for the treatment of breast cancer.


### Limitations and future prospectives
  While our study provides analysis about the potential of adenovirus to selectively target breast cancer cells through computational analysis, it is important to acknowledge certain limitations and future directions. First limitation is the absence of in-vitro or in-vivo experimental validation, which would confirm the biological relevance of our computational predictions. Additionally, the dataset used in this analysis, use in-silico studies, can be expanded to include more diverse adenoviral strains and breast cancer subtypes, enhancing the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, the biological complexity of tumor environments, including immune interactions, is not fully captured in in-silico models. Moving forward, experimental studies are necessary to validate our results, and future research should explore the integration of multi-target viral therapies to reduce resistance and improve selectivity. Personalized virotherapy, based on patient-specific cancer profiles, and combination therapies with other treatment modalities, like chemotherapy or immunotherapy, are promising areas that can enhance the clinical relevance of our findings.
