# STK39-mediated amplification of γ-H2A.X promotes homologous recombination and contributes to PARP inhibitor resistance


## Abstract
  The phosphorylation of histone H2A.X into γH2A.X is a crucial early event in the DNA damage response, marking DNA damage sites and initiating repair processes. While ATM kinase is traditionally recognized as the primary mediator of H2A.X phosphorylation, our study identifies serine/threonine kinase 39 (STK39) as a novel enhancer of this critical signaling pathway. We demonstrate that after DNA damage, STK39 undergoes phosphorylation by the ATM kinase, facilitating its interaction with the Mre11-Rad50-Nbs1 complex and subsequent recruitment to chromatin. This recruitment enables STK39 to further phosphorylate H2A.X, thus amplifying γH2A.X production and promoting homologous recombination repair. Notably, we observe a significant upregulation of STK39 in pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD) tissues, correlating with heightened resistance to PARPi therapy. Furthermore, we demonstrate the synergistic efficacy of combining STK39 inhibition with PARP inhibitors in suppressing and reversing PAAD growth. This study not only provides new insights into the molecular dynamics of H2A.X phosphorylation but also highlights the therapeutic potential of targeting STK39 to enhance PARPi sensitivity in PAAD (created with BioRender).


## Introduction
  The DNA damage response (DDR) pathway orchestrates the cellular response to DNA damage through intricate repair mechanisms and presents a promising target for cancer therapy (1). Among these, homologous recombination repair (HRR) and non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) are pivotal in maintaining genomic integrity (2). The Mre11-Rad50-Nbs1 (MRN) complex, a primary sensor of DNA damage, recruits and activates ATM kinase at damage sites, initiating a cascade of reactions including the phosphorylation of H2A.X to form γH2A.X, a key step in the DDR pathway (3,4). γH2A.X recruits mediator of DNA damage check point protein 1 (MDC1) through the breast cancer 1 (BRCA1) C-terminal (BRCT) domain (5–7). Following this, Ring finger protein (RNF) 8 recognizes phosphorylated MDC1 and facilitates the ubiquitination of histone H1 or L3MBTL2, which in turn recruits RNF168 (8–10). RNF168 orchestrates the ubiquitination of histone H2A on K13-15 sites (8,9,11), enabling BRCA1 and 53BP1 recruitment onto DNA damage sites, enabling BRCA1 and 53BP1 recruitment onto DNA damage sites, thus facilitating the HRR and NHEJ pathways respectively (12–15).

  The dependency of cancer cells on such intricate repair mechanisms provides a unique therapeutic opportunity, particularly through the strategy of synthetic lethality (16). For example, BRCA1/2 mutations impair HRR, rendering cells highly susceptible to poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARPis) (17). This principle has been validated in multiple cancers, including pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), through extensive clinical trials (18–21). However, somatic BRCA1 and BRCA2 mutations were reported to be observed in 1.3% and 3.1% of cases, respectively (22,23) limiting the benefits of PARPi to a small subset of PAAD patients, thus restricting its wider application in PAAD treatment. This underscores the urgency to identify and target other critical molecules within the HRR pathway to expand the therapeutic potential of PARPis.

  In response to this challenge, our research introduces serine/threonine kinase 39 (STK39, also known as SPAK), a member of the Ste20-like kinase family, as a novel synergistic target within the HRR pathway. Specifically, our findings indicate that STK39 is phosphorylated by ATM following DNA damage, followed by recruitment to damage sites. By facilitating the phosphorylation of H2A.X, STK39 amplifies the γH2A.X signaling to enhance HRR processes. Importantly, we demonstrate that STK39 is overexpressed in PAAD tissues, correlating with increased resistance to PARPi treatment. Conversely, inhibiting STK39 markedly enhances PARPi sensitivity in PAAD cells, both in vitro and in vivo, suggesting that targeting STK39 could significantly broaden the therapeutic efficacy of PARPis, transcending the limitations imposed by BRCA mutation status.


## Materials and methods

### Cell culture
  HEK293T and U2OS cell lines were purchased from ATCC (American Type Culture Collection). PANC-1, SW1990, BxPC-3, Mia Paca-2 and SW1990 were purchased from Institute of Basic Medical Sciences, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (Beijing, China). Cell lines 293T, PANC-1, SW1990 and BxPC-3 were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) medium with 10% (volume/volume; v/v) FBS and 1% (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin. Cell lines Mia and PaCa-2 were cultured in DMEM medium with 5% (volume/volume; v/v) horse serum and 1% (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin. U2OS was cultured in McCoy’s 5A medium supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS and 1% (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin. All cells were incubated in a humidified ThermoFisher incubator with 5% CO2.


### Plasmids and antibodies
  pCMV-STK39 vector was purchased from WZ Biosciences (Gene ID: NM_013233) and sub-cloned into pLVX3 and pGEX4T-1 vectors. All STK39 site mutants were generated using mutagenesis polymerase chain reaction, and the sequences were verified by DNA sequencing. Short hairpin RNA (shRNA, WZ Biosciences) sequences are listed below:

  shRNA1 GTACGGCAAGTCCTTTAGAAATTCAAGAGATTTCTAAAGGACTTGCCGTACTTTTTT

  shRNA2 CGGTCAGATTCACAGGGATTTTCAAGAGAAATCCCTGTGAATCTGACCGTTTTTT


### Lentiviral production and cell transduction
  Lentiviruses were generated using the second-generation packaging system. Briefly, 2.8 μg of the vector of interest, 0.72 μg of pMD2.G and 1.42 μg of psPAX2 were thoroughly mixed and co-transfected into 293T cells in 60-mm cell culture dishes using Hieff Trans® Liposomal Transfection Reagent (Yeasen), following the manufacturer’s guidelines. At 48 h post-transfection, the supernatant was collected and utilized to infect the specified cells. Subsequently, 293T, U2OS, PANC-1, SW1990, BxPC-3 or Mia PaCa-2 cells were infected with the designated STK39 wild-type (WT), mutant or shRNA constructs. Forty-eight hours post transfection, cells were selected with 1 μg/ml puromycin and analyzed by western blotting for subsequent experimentation.


### DNA damage repair reporter assays
  DR-GFP and EJ5-GFP reporter systems were employed to assess DNA double-strand break repair efficiency. 293T cells stably expressing indicated WT STK39, mutant, or shRNAs were seeded in triplicate in each well of a six-well plate. Within 24 h, cells in each well were transfected with Lipofectamine mixed with plasmids containing 500 ng of DR-GFP (or 500 ng of EJ5-GFP), 50 ng of mCherry and 500 ng of I-Sce1. ZT-1a was added to the culture medium 8 h post-transfection. At 48 h post-transfection, cells were collected and subjected to flow cytometric analysis (FACS). The relative percentage of mCherry/GFP-positive cells was calculated by dividing the number of mCherry/GFP-positive cells by the number of mCherry single-positive cells.


### Immunofluorescence and quantification of DNA damage foci
  Cells were exposed to X-ray irradiation (1 Gy). After 1 h, cells were washed with 1 × PBS, fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton Buffer for 10 min, blocked with 5% Goat Serum for 1 h and incubated with primary antibodies (γH2A.X, MDC1, FK2, 53BP1 and BRCA1, 1:1000 dilution in 5% Goat Serum) overnight. Subsequently, cells were washed three times with 1 × Tris-Borate-Sodium Tween-20 (TBST), treated with secondary antibodies (donkey anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 594 and donkey anti-mouse IgG Alexa Fluor 488 from Invitrogen, 1:200 dilution in 5% Goat Serum) for 20 min at 37°C, co-stained with 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Invitrogen) and observed under a fluorescent microscope. The number of nuclear foci signals was quantified using ImageJ, with at least 100 cells counted for each cell line.


### Colony assay
  Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a concentration of 500–1000 cells per well. The following day, cells were treated with varying amounts of ZT-1a, olaparib or X-ray radiation (1/2/4/8 Gy). Culturing continued for 10–14 days, followed by staining the colonies with a 0.1% crystal violet solution. The number of colonies in each well was quantified, and the surviving fraction was calculated by comparing the number of colonies in treated wells with that in untreated wells.


### Cell viability assay
  Cell viability was estimated using a cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). About 2 × 104 cells were seeded into each well of 96-well white plates in 24 h before drug treatment. Cisplatin (Invitrogen, USA) or etoposide (Invitrogen, USA) dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and diluted in the growth medium was added to wells and incubated for 72 h. Then, 10 μl of CCK-8 reaction solution was supplemented into each well according to the manufacturer’s protocol, and the optical density (OD) values at 450 nm of each well were measured.


### Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) analyses
  Cells were lysed in NETN buffer containing protease inhibitor and phosphatase inhibitors and sonicated to release endonuclear components. The lysates were incubated with the indicated antibody-conjugated beads (Flag beads) overnight at 4°C. The recovered immuno-complexes were rinsed three times with NETN buffer before being resolved by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and immunoblotted with the indicated antibodies.


### Molecular docking
  The 3D structures of the target proteins were downloaded from RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB) database (http://www.rcsb.org/), and the species selected was ‘Homo sapiens’: STK39 (PDB ID: 7O86) and MRN complex (PDB ID: 7ZR1). Then, AutoDock 1.5.6 (http://autodock.scripps.edu/) was used to dock the hub targets and active ingredients. The protein–ligand binding potential of molecular docking was evaluated by binding energy as docking score. Among these results, the minimum value was selected, and the corresponding structure was considered to have moderate binding potential and tight combination.


### COMET assays
  U2OS cells pretreated with ZT-1a for 1 h and stable U2OS cell lines expressing indicated WT STK39, mutant or shRNAs were all treated with irradiation (10 Gy). Cells were then gently scraped and resuspended at 1 × 105 cells/ml in cold PBS, followed by combining cells with molten LMAgarose at a ratio of 1:10 (v/v) and immediately pipetting 100 μl onto a Comet Slide. The slide was placed flat at 4°C in the dark for 10 min, immersed in prechilled lysis solution, and left at 4°C for 1 h. The slide was then immersed in prechilled Neutral Electrophoresis Buffer for 30 min at 4°C before being transferred to an electrophoresis apparatus for tris borate EDTA (TBE) electrophoresis. Samples were immersed in 70% ethanol for 30 min at room temperature, then dried and placed in 50 μl of diluted propidium iodide for 30 min in the dark. Images were recorded by epifluorescence microscopy and analyzed using CometScore software.


### In vitro kinase assay
  GST-fused full-length WT STK39 and mutations were reconstructed, and the proteins were purified from Escherichia coli BL-21. His-tagged H2A.X was also expressed and purified from bacteria. H2A.X protein was incubated with STK39 WT or kinase-dead mutant proteins in kinase buffer (10 mM HEPES, 5 mM MgCl2, 5 mM MnCl2, 1 mM DL-Dithiothreitol (DTT), 1 mM ATP) at 30°C for 30 min. Subsequently, 5 × SDS loading buffer was added to the reaction mixtures and boiled. Samples were subjected to SDS-PAGE and either stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue or immunoblotted with the indicated antibodies.


### Genomic analysis and patient survival assessment
  The genomic profiles and clinical attributes of PAAD samples were extracted from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database, specifically dataset GSE183795. For the comparison of RNA expression levels between cancerous and non-cancerous tissues, we utilized the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) web-based platform. Survival outcomes associated with PAAD were evaluated using the KM-plotter, an online resource for survival analysis.


### PAAD cell-derived and patient-derived xenograft models
  Female athymic nude mice (BALB/c-nu) aged between 5 and 6 weeks were utilized for the establishment of cell-derived xenograft (CDX) models. A suspension containing 2 × 106 PAAD cells (including PANC-1, SW1990, STK39-defect PANC-1 and SW1990 cell lines) mixed with 50% Matrigel was subcutaneously injected into the anterior region of each mouse. Approximately 12 days post-inoculation, mice were divided into different treatment groups, including STK39 inhibition (ZT-1a, 20 mg/kg, intraperitoneally, every other day for 2 weeks), olaparib (20 mg/kg, intraperitoneally, every other day for 2 weeks) and a combination of STK39 inhibitor with olaparib.

  Patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models of PAAD were obtained from Nanchang Royo Biotech Co. Ltd. Those models with STK39-high expression (PDX-893 and 848) were selected and explanted. The viable portions of the PDX tumors were cut into pieces and implanted subcutaneously into female BALB/c-nu mice. Once the average tumor volume of PDX models reached 200 mm3, mice were randomly allocated to treatment cohorts, including controls receiving only the vehicle, STK39 inhibition (ZT-1a, 20 mg/kg, intraperitoneally, every other day for 2 weeks), olaparib (20 mg/kg, intraperitoneally, every other day for 2 weeks) and a combination of ZT-1a with olaparib.

  In all mice studies, electronic calipers were employed to measure the length and width of each tumor every other day. Tumor volumes were calculated using the formula: volume = 0.5 (length × width2). At the culmination of the experiment, animals were euthanized, and some tumor tissues were promptly fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for subsequent immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis with the rest stored in −80°C.


### Specimen microarray and immunohistochemistry assays
  Tissue microarray (TMA) slides containing primary PAAD cancers and adjacent normal tissue specimens from 43 PAAD cancer cases were purchased from Shanghai Outdo Biotech Company (Shanghai, China). Representative areas of each invasive carcinoma were identified on the corresponding hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)–stained slides. The TMA sections were immunohistochemically stained for STK39 and γH2A.X with a standard immunostaining. The percentages of staining-positive cells were calculated by ImageJ. Correlation between tissue STK39 and γH2A.X staining level was tested by Pearson’s rank correlation analysis.

  Tumor tissues from CDX and PDX were collected and performed in IHC staining. The fresh tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde fix solution overnight, followed dehydrated and embedded in paraffin. Samples were subjected to IHC staining. The primary antibodies included Ki-67 and cleaved caspase 3. Images were acquired at 200× magnification, and semi-quantitatively assessed by an immunoreactive score system.


### Statistical analysis and data visualization
  For the statistical evaluation of our data, we employed various analytical methods. Differences in gene expression levels and other continuous variables were assessed using the unpaired Student’s t-test and two-way ANOVA, as appropriate. A threshold of P-value < 0.05 was set to indicate statistical significance, with specific levels denoted as *P< 0.05, **P< 0.01, ***P< 0.001; NS indicated a lack of statistical significance. Combination index (CI) and synergy score under the highest single agent (HSA) model were employed to represent joint effect of combinational therapy in vivo or in vitro; CI < 1 or HSA synergy score > 10 indicates a synergetic effect. All data are presented as mean values ± standard error of the mean to provide a clear representation of variability. The determination of sample sizes was guided by precedent studies in the field, ensuring adequate power for detecting significant differences. For graphical representation and analysis of the data, we used FlowJo v.10 for flow cytometry data, CometScore Pro v.1.01.44 for DNA damage quantification, ImageJ v.1.8.0 for image analysis, and GraphPad Prism v.9.0 for statistical analysis and graphing. Diagrammatic representations were crafted using Biorender.

## Results

### STK39 is upregulated in PAAD and involved in DNA repair
  STK39 is part of the Ste20-like kinase family (24). It contains an N-terminal series of proline and alanine repeats (PAPA box) and a serine/threonine kinase catalytic domain (25). STK39 plays a crucial role in ion homeostasis by regulating the activities of cation chloride cotransporters, essential for salt transport in kidneys and circulatory system stability in mammals (26,27). Aberrant expression of STK39 has been linked to several human diseases, including hypertension (28), stroke (29), type 1 diabetes (30) and various cancers (31–34). In the TCGA database, quantitative analyses revealed that STK39 kinase is significantly overexpressed in PAAD tissues compared to adjacent non-tumorous tissues (Supplementary Figure S1A). Also, higher STK39 expression corelates to lymph node metastasis and advance stage of PAAD (Supplementary Figure S1B and C). Kaplan–Meier analysis of PAAD patients revealed that STK39 overexpression is associated with reduced overall survival (Figure 1D) and progression free survival (Supplementary Figure S1E). Similarly, STK39 is up-regulated and predicts an adverse prognosis in other cancers such as colon cancer and liver cancer (Supplementary Figure S1F–I), indicating STK39 may serve as a prognostic marker across these malignancies. We further analysis the expression of STK39 with immunohistochemistry on PAAD tissue microarrays. Consistent with the TCGA data, STK39 protein level is upregulated in tumor tissues compared to normal ones and is localized in the nucleus (Supplementary Figure S1J–L). Notably, STK39 level is strongly correlated with the presence of the double-strand break marker γH2A.X (Supplementary Figure S1K–N). Given γH2A.X’s crucial roles in DNA repair, we proposed that STK39 might be involved in DNA repair process.

  To scrutinize STK39’s potential function in DNA repair, we first employed two independent shRNAs to knock down endogenous STK39 in cells (Figure 1A) and checked the DNA repair efficiency with the GFP-based reporter system (Figure 1B). The reporter assay revealed a decrease in HR and NHJE efficiency upon STK39 knockdown (Figure 1C). We further tested whether STK39 affects cellular sensitivity to genotoxic stress. As shown in Figure 1D–I, compared to control cells, cells expressing STK39 shRNA showed increased cellular sensitivity to irradiation (IR, Figure 1D and E), olaparib (Figure 1F and G), cisplatin (Figure 1H) and etoposide (Figure 1I). Together, these data demonstrated that STK39 is involved in cellular response to DNA damage.

  The DSB repair process is mediated through the recruitment of a series of DNA repair factors, including MDC1, RNF8, RNF168 and BRCA1. Given that STK39 regulates DSB repair, we thus checked the foci formation of several key DNA repair factors (γH2A.X, MDC1, FK2, 53BP1 and BRCA1) in STK39-depleted U2OS cells by immunofluorescence assay. As shown in Figure 2A–K, STK39 depletion significantly impaired the foci formation of all these DNA repair factors. Notably, this modulation of the DNA repair pathway by STK39 does not result from altered DNA damage levels, as indicated by comparable tail moments of comet assay in STK39-depleted and control cells (Figure 2L and M). These observations suggest that STK39 modulates the very early step of DDR.

  Given that STK39 is a protein kinase, we wondered whether its kinase activity is pivotal for promoting HRR. To test this, we reconstituted STK39 knockdown cell lines with WT and catalytically inactive STK39 mutant (D212A (35)) (Supplementary Figure S2A) and checked HRR efficiency in these cells. As shown in Supplementary Figure S2B, cells expressing WT STK39 displayed enhanced HR and NHEJ, whereas those expressing D212A mutant did not, indicating the activity of STK39 is essential for its function in DNA repair. We further checked the effect of STK39 mutants on DNA repair foci formation by immunofluorescence. While cells expressing STK39 WT displayed enhanced γH2A.X, MDC1, FK2, 53BP1 and BRCA1 foci, cells expressing D212A mutant failed (Supplementary Figure S2C–L). This was not attributable to varied levels of DNA damage induction since the overexpression of either WT or D212A mutant showed no significant change in tail moments in neutral comet assays compared to control cells (Supplementary Figure S2M and N). To further test whether the activity of STK39 regulates cellular sensitivity to genotoxic agents, we treated cells expressing STK39 WT or mutant with IR and olaparib. Clonogenic survival assays indicated that cells expressing STK39 WT were much more resistant to IR and olaparib treatment than cells expressing D212A mutant (Supplementary Figure S2O and P). These results demonstrated that the kinase activity of STK39 is required for its function in DNA repair.

  To further test the effect of STK39 activity in DDR, we utilized a selective STK39 inhibitor (ZT-1a (36)) to diminish its activity in cells. Treatment with ZT-1a led to a significant reduction in STK39 phosphorylation at Ser373 site (the activating site phosphorylated by WNK1 (37)), indicating ZT-1a efficiently inhibited its kinase activity (Supplementary Figure S3A). We further treated cells with ZT-1a and checked DNA repair efficiency. Consistent with previous results, STK39 inhibition resulted in diminished cellular HR and NHEJ efficiency (Supplementary Figure S3B). Also, the formation of repair foci involving all assessed molecules (γH2A.X, MDC1, FK2, 53BP1 and BRCA1) declined after STK39 inhibitor ZT-1a treatment (Supplementary Figure S3C–L), mirroring the effects observed in STK39-depleted cells. Notably, this effect was not due to altered DNA damage levels, as ZT-1a treatment did not result in expanded comet tail moments (Supplementary Figure S3M and N). Correspondingly, colony formation assays showed that STK39 inhibitor sensitized cells to IR and olaparib (Supplementary Figure S3O and P). Together, these results further confirmed that STK39 regulates DNA repair through its kinase activity.


### STK39 directly phosphorylates H2A.X in vitro and in cells
  Given that STK39 affects the formation of critical DDR molecules (γH2A.X, MDC1, FK2, 53BP1 and BRCA1), we proposed that STK39 might directly modulate γH2A.X phosphorylation. To test this, we knocked down STK39 in U2OS cells and checked the γH2A.X phosphorylation by western blot. As shown in Figure 2N and O, compared to controls, STK39-depleted cells exhibited a significant reduction in γH2A.X levels. We further reconstituted the STK39 knockdown cells with STK39 WT and D212A mutant and checked the γH2A.X phosphorylation by Western blot. Cells expressing STK39 WT exhibited elevated γH2A.X levels relative to those expressing kinase-deficient D212A mutant (Figure 2P and Q). Additionally, inhibiting STK39 activity with ZT-1a markedly reduced γH2A.X phosphorylation (Figure 2R and S). In these experiments, we also examined ATM phosphorylation and found that it also exhibited reduction in STK39-depleted cells (Figure 2N). Taken together, these results indicate STK39 may directly regulates H2A.X phosphorylation, or indirectly phosphorylate H2A.X by modulating ATM activity, or through other mechanisms after DNA damage in cells. To test whether STK39 directly phosphorylates H2A.X in vitro, we purified STK39 and H2A.X from bacteria and performed in vitro kinase assays. As shown in Figure 2T, STK39 WT, but not the D212A mutant, directly phosphorylated H2A.X, establishing a direct enzymatic activity of STK39 on γH2A.X (Figure 2U). In response to the changes in ATM phosphorylation observed in cells, we propose that γH2A.X reinforces the phosphorylation of ATM at the damage sites (38) after being phosphorylated by STK39. These results established that STK39 phosphorylates H2A.X both in cells and in vitro.


### ATM phosphorylates STK39 at S401 and promotes the recruitment of STK39 onto chromatin after DNA damage
  Previous large scale mass spectrometry analysis indicates that the S401 site on STK39 might undergo phosphorylation after DNA damage (39). This phosphorylation site matches with the ATM substrate SQ/TQ motif and is conserved across human and several other species (Figure 3A). We postulated that STK39 might be phosphorylated by ATM after DNA damage. To test this hypothesis, we immunoprecipitated STK39 from cells treated with IR (10 Gy) and checked the phosphorylation with ATM substrate antibody (pSQ/TQ). As shown in Figure 3B, STK39 was highly phosphorylated after DNA damage, which was abrogated by ATM inhibitor KU-55933, indicating ATM phosphorylates STK39 after DNA damage. To validate whether STK39 is phosphorylated at S401, we mutated this S401 site to alanine (S401A) and checked its phosphorylation after DNA damage. As shown in Figure 3C, S401A mutant totally abolished phosphorylation after DNA damage, confirming that STK39 is phosphorylated by ATM at S401 after DNA damage.

  Given STK39 is phosphorylated at S401, we next studied the functional significance of STK39 phosphorylation. Interestingly, we found the interaction between STK39 and two critical MRN complex components, Rad50 and NBS1, increased after DNA damage, suggesting these interactions might be mediated by STK39 phosphorylation (Figure 3D). To test this hypothesis, we mutated STK39 S401 to A and checked the interaction between STK39 and MRN complex. As shown in Figure 3E, while the interaction between STK39 WT and MRN complex increased after DNA damage, these interactions were disrupted by the S401A mutant, indicating the phosphorylation of STK39 at S401 is essential for the interaction between STK39 and MRN complex. The molecular docking model also showed the interaction between phosphorylated STK39 and MRN complex (Figure 3F). We proposed that these interactions might affect STK39 recruitment onto the chromatin after DNA damage. To test this, we isolated the chromatin binding fraction and checked the loading of STK39 onto chromatin after DNA damage. As shown in Figure 3G, while STK39 WT was recruited onto chromatin after IR, STK39 S401A mutant failed. These results indicate that the phosphorylation of STK39 at S401 is indispensable for its recruitment to chromatin following DNA damage (Figure 3H).


### Phosphorylation of STK39 at S401 is essential for its function in DNA damage response
  To further investigate the function of STK39 S401 phosphorylation in HRR, we reconstituted STK39 knockdown cell lines with STK39 WT and S401A mutant (Figure 4A). With these stable cell lines, we conducted assays to evaluate HRR efficiency. The HRR report assay revealed that the S401A mutant was unable to promote HRR, while STK39 WT significantly increased HR and NHEJ efficiency (Figure 4B). Next, we checked whether STK39 S401 phosphorylation affected H2A.X phosphorylation after DNA damage. Following DNA damage, only cells expressing WT STK39, but not cells expressing STK39 S401A mutant, showed increased γH2A.X levels, indicating that S401 phosphorylation of STK39 is critical for initiating DDR signaling (Figure 4C). Furthermore, cells expressing S401A mutant failed to increase formation of γH2A.X, MDC1, FK2, 53BP1 and BRCA1 foci (Figure 4D–M). Additionally, compared to cells expressing WT STK39, cells expressing S401A mutant were more sensitive to IR and olaparib treatment (Figure 4N and O). Together, these findings suggest STK39’s recruitment and function in HRR are contingent upon its phosphorylation at S401 by ATM.


### STK39 regulates cellular sensitivity to PARPi in PAAD
  Considering that STK39 enhances DNA repair efficiency, it is plausible that STK39 could be used as a target to sensitize cancer cells to DNA-damaging agents. To validate this hypothesis, we firstly assessed STK39 expression levels in different human PAAD cell lines by western blot and grouped PANC-1, SW1990 and CFPAC-1 cells as STK39 high expression cells and BxPC-3 and MIA PaCa-2 as STK39 low expression cells (Figure 5A). Subsequently, we depleted STK39 in the STK39-high PAAD cell lines (PANC-1 and SW1990) and checked the cellular sensitivity to genotoxic agents. The colony formation assays revealed that STK39 depletion rendered these cells more sensitive to IR-induced DNA damage and olaparib (Figure 5B–E). Conversely, to test whether STK39 overexpression could lead to resistance to genotoxic stress, we overexpressed WT or mutant forms (D212A and S401A mutant) in the STK39-low PAAD cell lines (BxPC-3 and MIA PaCa-2). Overexpression of WT STK39 increased cells’ tolerance to IR-induced DNA damage and olaparib. However, this resistance was not observed with the overexpression of phosphorylation deficient (S401A) or catalytically inactive (D212A) mutants (Figure 5F–M), indicating that the protective effect of STK39 overexpression against genotoxic stress is contingent upon its kinase activity and specific phosphorylation at S401.

  To further confirm that STK39 regulates PARPi sensitivity in PAAD, we knocked down STK39 in PANC-1 and SW1990 cells and generated cell-derived xenograft (CDX) models. These tumors were treated with olaparib and monitored (Supplementary Figure S4A). As shown in Figure 5N–Q, tumors with STK39 knockdown showed significantly increased sensitivity to olaparib compared to those with WT STK39. Additionally, depletion of STK39 decreased proliferation potential and increased apoptotic ratios in both CDX models treated with olaparib (Supplementary Figure S4B–I). These findings support the hypothesis that low STK39 expression is synthetically lethal with PARPi treatment in PAAD.


### Inhibiting STK39 induces cellular sensitivity to PARPi in PAAD
  Considering STK39 is a potential therapeutic target to enhance PARPi sensitivity, we investigated whether the STK39 inhibitor ZT-1a could sensitize PAAD to PARPi. Inhibiting STK39 decreased survival rate of PANC-1 and SW1990 cells to irradiation-induced DNA damage (Figure 6A and C). Colony formation assays also demonstrated a synergistic effect between olaparib and STK39 inhibition in STK39-high cell lines (PANC-1 and SW1990), underscoring the therapeutic promise of this combination (Figure 6B and D). We further tested this synergy in cell-derived xenograft (CDX) models from PANC-1 and SW1990 cell lines (Figure 6E). As shown in Figure 6F–K, a combination of olaparib and ZT-1a dramatically inhibited the tumor growth. Additionally, the combination of olaparib and ZT-1a decreased proliferation potential and increased apoptotic ratios in CDX models treated with olaparib (Supplementary Figure S5A–F). Notably, no noticeable change in mice weight was observed across these four groups (Supplementary Figure S5G and H), confirming the safety of this combination strategy.

  Additionally, we utilized patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models established from PAAD tumors with high STK39 expression (Figure 7A) to further test the efficiency of the combination strategy (Figure 7B). As shown in Figure 7C–H, the combined treatment of olaparib and the STK39 inhibitor substantially improved anti-tumor efficacy. This combination strategy significantly reduced proliferation potential and increased apoptotic trend in the PDX model (Supplementary Figure S6A–F). Consistent with the CDX models, no noticeable change in mice weight was observed across these groups (Supplementary Figure S6G and H). These findings confirm that STK39 inhibition enhances PARPi sensitivity in PAAD, presenting a promising therapeutic strategy.


## Discussion
  In the process of HRR, γH2A.X serves as a crucial marker of DNA DSBs and initiates the subsequent repair process. While it is well-established that ATM is responsible for H2A.X phosphorylation (2,40,41), our study reveals that the protein kinase STK39 also contributes to H2A.X phosphorylation, introducing an additional layer to this pathway (Supplementary Figure S7). Specifically, after DNA damage, ATM phosphorylates STK39 at the S401 site, which enhances STK39’s recruitment through its interaction with the MRN complex. This action facilitates STK39’s role in amplifying γH2A.X signaling through further phosphorylation of H2A.X. Our findings reveal a novel mechanism that significantly boosts γH2A.X signaling, providing new insights into the modulation of DNA repair. Given that STK39 activity is regulated by multiple physiological conditions, including hypertension, diabetes and different cancers (27,28,30–34), future studies exploring how these conditions regulate DNA repair could provide significant advancements in our understanding of cellular responses to DNA damage.

  PAAD remains one of the deadliest cancers worldwide, characterized by late diagnosis and poor response to conventional therapies. Despite advancements in cancer treatment, the 5-year survival rate for PAAD patients continues to languish below 10% (42–44). This alarming statistic highlights the critical urgency for innovative and more effective therapeutic strategies in PAAD to combat the aggressive progression and relentless nature of this disease (45,46). The therapeutic landscape for PAAD is rapidly evolving, with homology recombination deficiency (HRD) emerging as a pivotal determinant of cancer cell susceptibility to PARPi. Despite the promising efficacy of PARPi in patients harboring germline mutations in BRCA1 or BRCA2, the rarity of these mutations in the PAAD population restricts the widespread utility of PARPi in this setting (47). Recent advancements in the understanding of DNA repair mechanisms have spotlighted the HRR pathway as a critical modulator of cancer cell survival and chemoresistance (2,48,49). Targeted inhibition of key HRR components has been explored as a viable approach to induce synthetic lethality in cancer cells, particularly in conjunction with PARPi therapy (50). For instance, the combination of ATM inhibitors with PARPi has demonstrated synthetic lethality in proliferating cancer cell lines (51). Similarly, our study demonstrated that the pharmacological inhibition of STK39 also synergizes with PARPi. The combination of STK39 inhibitor with PARPi significantly enhances cancer cell lethality, inhibits clonal expansion of PAAD cells and suppresses tumor growth across both in vitro and in vivo PAAD models (including PDXs and CDXs). This synergy not only highlights STK39 as a critical determinant of HRD but also positions it as a strategic target to broaden the applicability of PARPi therapy beyond the confines of BRCA mutation status.

  Moreover, the interplay between HRD and cellular sensitivity to conventional chemotherapeutic agents, such as cisplatin, provides a compelling rationale for exploring combination therapies that inhibiting STK39 to enhance the cytotoxic effects of these agents. The exploration of STK39 inhibition as a strategy to enhance PARPi and cisplatin efficacy underscores the importance of understanding DNA repair pathways in cancer therapy. Future preclinical and clinical investigations are essential to delineate the safety profile, optimal dosing regimens and therapeutic efficacy of these combination strategies. Such endeavors will not only expand the therapeutic options for treating PAAD but also pave the way for precision medicine approaches by exploiting specific vulnerabilities within the tumor’s genetic and molecular landscape.

  Additionally, the relationship between DNA repair pathway deficiencies and PARPi sensitivity is complex. Previous studies have indeed reported that the loss of the NHEJ regulatory 53BP1 in ovarian or breast cancer cells with BRCA1 mutations can lead to resistance to PARPi and dual deficiency in HR and NHEJ typically results in reduced sensitivity to PARPi compared to a single HR deficiency (52,53). In our study, inhibition of STK39 appears to suppress both HR and NHEJ yet still sensitizes cells to PARPi. This phenomenon might be due to the differential impact of STK39 inhibition on these pathways—particularly, a more pronounced defect in HR compared to NHEJ. This uneven suppression may still leave cells partially dependent on the compromised HR pathway, which is critical for the repair of PARPi-induced damage. Additionally, it’s plausible that STK39 inhibition might impact alternative DNA repair mechanisms, such as alternative end-joining (Alt-EJ) and single-strand annealing (SSA) (54–56), which may become more important when both HR and NHEJ are compromised. These alternative pathways could potentially alter the cellular response to PARPi. Elucidating how STK39 modulates these pathways is essential for understanding its role in DNA repair and PARPi sensitivity. Further research is needed to thoroughly explore these interactions, particularly how STK39 affects the balance between HR, NHEJ and other compensatory repair mechanisms.

  In summary, our investigation has illuminated a critical function of STK39 in the DDR, particularly in enhancing γH2A.X signaling to facilitate the effective repair of double-strand breaks. Inhibition of STK39 induces homologous recombination deficiency in PAAD, thereby extending the benefits of PARPi therapy beyond the subset of patients with BRCA mutations.
