# A theoretical systems chronopharmacology approach for COVID‐19: Modeling circadian regulation of lung infection and potential precision therapies


## Abstract
  The COVID‐19 pandemic, caused by SARS‐CoV‐2, has underscored the urgent need for innovative therapeutic approaches. Recent studies have revealed a complex interplay between the circadian clock and SARS‐CoV‐2 infection in lung cells, opening new avenues for targeted interventions. This systems pharmacology study investigates this intricate relationship, focusing on the circadian protein BMAL1. BMAL1 plays a dual role in viral dynamics, driving the expression of the viral entry receptor ACE2 while suppressing interferon‐stimulated antiviral genes. Its critical position at the host‐pathogen interface suggests potential as a therapeutic target, albeit requiring a nuanced approach to avoid disrupting essential circadian regulation. To enable precise tuning of potential interventions, we constructed a computational model integrating the lung cellular clock with viral infection components. We validated this model against literature data to establish a platform for drug administration simulation studies using the REV‐ERB agonist SR9009. Our simulations of optimized SR9009 dosing reveal circadian‐based strategies that potentially suppress viral infection while minimizing clock disruption. This quantitative framework offers insights into the viral‐circadian interface, aiming to guide the development of chronotherapy‐based antivirals. More broadly, it underscores the importance of understanding the connections between circadian timing, respiratory viral infections, and therapeutic responses for advancing precision medicine. Such approaches are vital for responding effectively to the rapid spread of coronaviruses like SARS‐CoV‐2.


## INTRODUCTION
  The rapid and relentless spread of Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID‐19) has catalyzed a global health emergency, significantly straining healthcare systems worldwide. 1 ,  2 ,  3  This pandemic is attributed to the pernicious Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS‐CoV‐2), which primarily afflicts the respiratory system, affecting both the upper and lower respiratory tracts. 3  In its severe manifestations, COVID‐19 is notorious for instigating lung complications, such as pneumonia and acute respiratory distress syndrome, placing significant strain on healthcare resources, including hospital beds, ventilators, and medical personnel. 4 ,  5 ,  6  Moreover, the long‐term ramifications of COVID‐19 have been well‐documented, underscoring the urgency for innovative therapeutic interventions. 7  In response to this pressing need, novel antiviral drugs are being actively developed to combat COVID‐19. 8  Recently, an intriguing avenue of investigation has unveiled the interplay between the infection and replication of SARS‐CoV‐2 and the circadian clock within the lung. 9  Notably, the clock protein component BMAL1 has been implicated in this intricate relationship. 9 ,  10 ,  11 ,  12  On the one hand, BMAL1 orchestrates the positive regulation of angiotensin‐converting enzyme 2 (ACE2), effectively facilitating virus infection. 9  On the other hand, BMAL1 concurrently exerts a negative influence on the expression of various interferon‐stimulated genes (ISGs), inadvertently promoting viral replication. 9  This duality has sparked interest in BMAL1 as a potential target for the development of therapeutics against SARS‐CoV‐2. However, it is imperative to acknowledge that BMAL1 plays an indispensable role in the circadian clock, and its disruption may give rise to unforeseen complications. Furthermore, the timing of drug administration adds a layer of complexity to this multifaceted challenge. In light of these intricacies, a comprehensive model is requisite to unravel the dynamic relationship between circadian rhythms, SARS‐CoV‐2 infection, and the administration of antiviral agents, aiming to explore targeted, time‐dependent modulation rather than complete inhibition, potentially offering a means to disrupt viral replication while minimizing systemic circadian disruption.

  The circadian system in mammals comprises both the central, or master, circadian clock and various peripheral tissue‐specific clocks. 13  The master clock resides within the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), where its neurons generate rhythmic electrical activity and orchestrate the release of synchronizing signals like temperature variations and rhythmic hormone secretion. These signals serve to adjust or reset the phase of peripheral clocks according to local time. At the molecular level, the mammalian circadian system relies on interlocked transcription/translation feedback loops involving several clock components. 14  Clock, Bmal1, Dbp, and Ror genes (Rorα, Rorβ, and Rorγ) act as positive elements, while Period (Per1, Per2, Per3), Cryptochrome (Cry1 and Cry2), and Rev‐Erb (Rev‐Erbα and Rev‐Erbβ) genes function as negative elements. 15  In the core loop, the heterodimer transcription factor CLOCK:BMAL1 activates the transcription of Per and Cry genes. 16 ,  17  The expressed complex of PER:CRY proteins enter the nucleus and repress the activity of CLOCK:BMAL1. 18  In addition, CLOCK:BMAL1 can also activates the transcription of Dbp and Rev‐Erb. 19 ,  20  The transcription factor DBP promotes the transcription of Per, Ror, and Rev‐Erb genes. 21  ROR and REV‐ERB are nuclear receptors. ROR promotes Bmal1 and Cry genes, whereas REV‐ERB represses Bmal1 and Cry genes. 22

  The circadian clock system usually comprises a complex network of many interacting components and processes. This complexity makes it challenging to intuitively predict and explain the clock's function and response to environmental cues. To better understand this system, we need a rigorous, quantitative model that captures current knowledge of the circadian gene network. Such a model could be used to simulate experimental perturbations, revealing key components and mechanisms that give rise to core clock properties. In short, a computational model of the circadian clockwork would allow more systematic testing of the system's dynamics than intuition alone permits. Quantitative models of the lung clock have been built previously. 23 ,  24  Mavroudis et al. 23  used delay differential equations to describe the phase variabilities of liver, muscle, adipose, and lung clock models. These models have effectively examined inter‐tissue differences in the circadian rhythms of identical clock‐regulated genes. Such temporal variability across tissues is an integral part of homeostatic regulation. 23  In 2021, Abo and Layton developed a lung clock model to understand the molecular mechanisms connecting circadian rhythms to immune function in a sex‐specific manner relevant to the health impacts of shift work. Their model examines interactions between the lung clock and the immune system, aiming to elucidate differences in how disrupted circadian cycles affect immunity in males versus females exposed to unusual work hours. 24

  To develop effective and precise antiviral strategies, it is crucial to explore the interactions between the lung clock and SARS‐CoV‐2, incorporating insights into the model. More known clock components, such as ROR and DBP, and more virus interaction components, such as ACE2 and ISG, should be added to the model. By doing so, we can introduce antiviral drugs into the model to assess their actions and systemic effects. Notably, we can also evaluate the timing of drug administration within the model, considering its impact on efficacy and reducing side effects. This multifaceted approach is poised to yield valuable insights into the complex interplay between the circadian system, lung health, and viral infections.


## METHODS

### Model construction
  The lung circadian clock mathematical model was manually developed using the CellDesigner™ 4.4.2 software. 25 ,  26  We used CellDesigner™ 4.4.2 software for model construction and simulation. While this software provides a user‐friendly interface for building and analyzing complex biological models, we acknowledge that all modeling software has inherent limitations. To mitigate potential biases, we carefully validated our model against experimental data from the literature and conducted sensitivity analyses to ensure the robustness of our results. The construction of the model was built upon theoretical models employed for comprehending genetic regulatory interactions and quantifying the variations in biological molecules. 27 ,  28  Specifically, we initiated our model construction by adapting the foundational Goodwin's 29  negative genetic feedback loop model and integrating insights gleaned from previous literature. 23 ,  30 ,  31 ,  32  To establish the lung circadian clock model, we began by determining model parameters related to the degradation rates of key components, including Per, Cry, Bmal1, Dbp, Ror, and Rev‐Erb RNA. These parameters were either directly derived from experimental data 33 ,  34 ,  35 ,  36 ,  37  or referred to published models. 23 ,  32  For components with non‐fixed parameters, we systematically adjusted their values and initial conditions through empirical fine‐tuning. This process aimed to ensure that the model's simulations align with experimental observations in terms of the relative levels and phase relationships among clock components, enhancing the model's accuracy and predictive capabilities. The kinetic equations utilized within the model are detailed in the Data S1, and the model itself is available in SBML format Level 2 version 4 (File S1). Simulations were conducted in CellDesigner, employing SOSlib as the numerical solver. SOSlib was chosen for its efficiency in handling stiff ordinary differential equations (ODEs) typically encountered in biochemical systems and for its compatibility with our model's SBML format.


### Modeling cortisol pulses in the model
  To simulate phase response curves (PRCs) within the model, we implemented 1‐h cortisol pulses at intervals of every four circadian hours. These cortisol pulses were initiated by modifying the transcription rate (kact_pc_cortisol) of Per and Cry genes, transitioning it from 0 to 10, and then returning it to 0 to conclude the pulse. Simultaneously, the repression constant (Krep_rev_cortisol) governing the effect of cortisol on Rev‐Erb transcription was altered from 1 to 0 during the pulse initiation and restored to 1 upon pulse termination. The timing at which Per mRNA reached its peak both before and after the cortisol pulse was meticulously recorded, allowing us to calculate any shifts in the clock's advance or delay.


### Model robustness and sensitivity analysis
  To assess the stability of our model and its sensitivity to parameter variations, we conducted comprehensive robustness and sensitivity analyses using custom Python scripts. For the robustness analysis, we systematically varied each parameter of the model from 0 to 100 times its original value, using 1000 equally spaced points on a logarithmic scale. For each parameter value, we simulated the model for 200‐time units with 40,000 data points. We then calculated the period and amplitude of the pc_RNA oscillations, as well as the peak and trough levels. The period was determined by measuring the time between peaks in the pc_RNA time series, while the amplitude was calculated as the difference between peak and trough values. This analysis allowed us to identify which parameters most strongly influence the model's oscillatory behavior and stability. For the sensitivity analysis, we employed a local sensitivity analysis approach using response coefficients. For each parameter, we calculated the response coefficient (RC) by perturbing the parameter value by ±3% and measuring the resulting change in six key model outputs: period, amplitude, peak viral load, time to peak viral load, peak ACE2 level, and peak ISG level. The response coefficient for each output was calculated as: RiO=δOOδPiPi where δO is the change in output, O is the original output value, δP  i  is the change in the parameter value (±3% in our case), and P  i  is the original parameter value. This formulation of the response coefficient quantifies the relative change in the output with respect to the relative change in the parameter, providing a dimensionless measure of sensitivity.

  Both analyses were performed using parallel processing to improve computational efficiency. The robustness analysis results were visualized using line plots for period and filled area plots for amplitude, while the sensitivity analysis results were presented as tornado plots.

  These analyses provided insights into the model's behavior under parameter perturbations and identified the most critical parameters influencing various aspects of the model's dynamics, including circadian rhythms and viral infection processes. The robustness analysis revealed the stability of the circadian period across a wide range of parameter values, while also identifying parameters that significantly affect oscillation amplitude. The sensitivity analysis, through the calculation of response coefficients, allowed us to quantify the impact of small parameter changes on key model outputs, highlighting the parameters most crucial for maintaining circadian rhythms and influencing viral dynamics.


### Modeling drug effects in the model
  In the context of SR9009 treatment, we manipulated the activation constant (Ka_rev_SR9009) responsible for Rev‐Erb transcription due to SR9009. This constant was adjusted from 0 to 1 to initiate the treatment and subsequently returned to 0 to conclude it. The treatment was initiated at circadian time (CT) 0, extended over a 4‐h duration, and repeated at 24‐h intervals for the specified experimental duration.


## RESULTS

### The lung circadian clock model
  We developed a model of the lung circadian clock to provide a platform for investigating virus and clock interactions and antiviral drug action mechanisms. The scheme of the model is shown in Figure 1. For the purpose of simplicity, we made several key assumptions in our model. First, we considered Period (Per1, 2, 3) and Cryptochrome (Cry1, 2) genes as a single Per/Cry gene component. Clock and Bmal1 genes are considered as a single Clock/Bmal gene component. Similarly, Ror (Rorα, β, γ) genes and Rev‐Erb (Rev‐Erbα, β) genes are represented by a single Ror gene and a single Rev‐Erb gene, respectively. This simplification is based on the largely redundant functions of these genes in the circadian clock. 30  Second, we used first‐order degradation kinetics for mRNA and protein degradations, which is a common approximation in systems biology models. 38  These simplifications allow for a more tractable model while still capturing the essential dynamics of the system. The transcription of Per/Cry, Clock/Bmal, Ror, Dbp, and Rev‐Erb genes are modeled in step 1–5, respectively, and the translation of Per/Cry, Clock/Bmal, Ror, Dbp, and Rev‐Erb genes are modeled in step 6–10, respectively. The Per/Cry (pc), Clock/Bmal (cb), Ror, Dbp, and Rev‐Erb mRNAs and the PER and CRY (PER/CRY, PC), CLOCK and BMAL1 (CLOCK/BMAL, CB), ROR, DBP, and REV‐ERB proteins are the mRNA and protein products of the above genes, respectively. The PER:CRY complex (PC complex, PCC) formation is modeled in step 11, and the CLOCK:BMAL1 complex (CB complex, CBC) formation is modeled in step 12. For simplicity, first‐order degradation kinetics are used to model mRNA and protein degradations (step 13–24). The basic Hill equation is used to describe the activation and the repression of transcription. This model is based on the core loop consisting of the cb and pc genes and the interlocking loop consisting of the Dbp, Ror, and Rev‐Erb genes. The transcription of the pc gene is positively regulated by CB, DBP, and ROR and negatively regulated by PC and REV‐ERB. The transcription of the cb gene is positively regulated by ROR and negatively regulated by REV‐ERB. The transcription of the Dbp gene is positively regulated by CB and negatively regulated by PC. The transcription of the Ror gene is positively regulated by DBP. The transcription of the Rev‐erb gene is positively regulated by CB and DBP and negatively regulated by PC. The full set of kinetic equations, including Hill equations, and parameter values used in the model are presented in Data S1 and Table S1, respectively.

  The simulated results are shown in Figure 2a. We first validated this model against experimental phenotypes. The period of the simulated results is 24 h and the phase of cb, pc, Dbp, Ror, and Rev‐Erb mRNAs are inside the range of experimental results (Figure 2b). 33  The antiphasic expression of Bmal1 and Per2 mRNA is successfully reproduced in the model. Furthermore, in order to validate the clock mechanisms and behaviors are in agreement with experimental data, we introduce cortisol into the model. A peripheral clock should be able to respond to external stimulus, such as glucocorticoids (cortisol in humans), which has been widely recognized as an entrainer of the clock. 39  At a molecular level, experimental data shows that glucocorticoids can directly regulate the clock by enhancing Per1 expression and repress Rev‐Erb expression. 40 ,  41  To examine the effect of cortisol pulses on our model clock, the system was pulsed with cortisol (1 h duration) at 4‐h intervals covering one circadian day. The results are shown in a phase response curve in Figure 3. In the model, large phase shifts are induced during the early morning, which is in agreement with the published literature. 42


### Model robustness and parameter sensitivity analysis
  To assess the stability and sensitivity of our model, we conducted comprehensive robustness and sensitivity analyses. The core circadian oscillator demonstrated remarkable stability across a wide range of parameter values (0 to 100 times the original value), with the period remaining constant at approximately 24 h for many parameters (Figure S1). However, the amplitude of oscillations showed greater sensitivity to parameter variations (Figure S2). Our local sensitivity analysis, using response coefficients, revealed that the circadian period was most affected by kd_cb (RC = −0.262) and kd_CBC (RC = −0.274) (Figure S3), while the amplitude was particularly sensitive to ROR‐related parameters (e.g., k_ror and k_ROR, RC ≈ 3.22) (Figure S4). We identified critical parameters for maintaining circadian rhythms, such as k_cb, Ka_cb_ROR, and Ki_cb_REV. Interestingly, parameters related to viral dynamics showed minimal impact on the core circadian oscillations, suggesting a degree of resilience in the clock mechanism to viral perturbations. However, viral dynamics were highly sensitive to specific parameters, with kd_Vendo (RC = −2.629) and k_ISG (RC = 0.861) having the most significant effects on peak viral load and ISG levels, respectively (Figures S5–S7). Figure 4 illustrates the distribution of parameters based on their period and amplitude response coefficients, revealing a positive correlation between these two measures. Key parameters affecting both circadian rhythms and viral dynamics include k_ror, k_ROR, k_cb, Ka_pc_ROR, kd_ROR, kd_ror, Ka_rev_CBC, kd_REV, kd_CBC, and kd_cb, highlighting the interconnected nature of the circadian and viral response systems in our model.


### Modeling with virus and drug
  To model the lung circadian clock and its interactions with SARS‐CoV‐2, the components related to virus infection and replication are needed to be introduced in the model. From the literature, ACE2 is the major cell entry receptor for SARS‐CoV‐2. 43  Recently, it has been reported that the expression of ACE2 and the entry of SARS‐CoV‐2 are regulated by BMAL1. 9  BMAL1 also down‐regulates interferon‐stimulated genes' (ISG) expressions and promotes the replication of SARS‐CoV‐2. 9  The entry, replication, maturation, and degradation of the virus are modeled in steps 25–27 and 30 in Figure 1, respectively. The translation of Ace2 is modeled in step 28 and is positively regulated by CBC and negatively regulated by PCC. ISG production is modeled in step 29 and ISG is made from the precursors (ISGpre). Virus entry is promoted by ACE2 and Virus replication is repressed by ISG in the model. The production of ISG is negatively regulated by CBC and positively regulated by PCC. The degradation of ACE2 and ISG are modeled in steps 31 and 32, respectively. Hill equation is used to describe the above regulations. The parameter values used to model clock‐virus interactions are presented in Table S1. The simulated results are shown in Figure 5a. The level of SARS‐CoV‐2 is rhythmic after virus entry according to the circadian rhythm and is repressed by ISG. The behaviors of virus entry and replication are successfully reproduced in the model. 9  Next, we test the model by introducing SR9009 effects into the model. Consistent SR9009 administration can lead to clock abolishment, which can result in significant adverse effects. To reduce the adverse effects of SR9009, the timing and duration of SR9009 should be optimized. To address the exposure‐response relationship for SR9009 and to find out the best course of treatment with the best efficiency and minimal adverse effects, we modeled different times of administration at 2‐h intervals from CT 0–24 (24–48 h after virus infection). We simulated the levels of Per and Cry mRNA (pc_RNA) and virus titer during drug administration at different circadian time points (Figure 5b; Figure S8). Each course of treatment lasts 5 days, with medication administered at the same time every day for 1 h. Importantly, these simulations reveal a non‐linear, time‐dependent efficacy in viral load reduction, with administration at CT 18 showing the strongest virus repression efficiency (Figure 5b; Figure S8). This complex, time‐dependent response highlights the potential of chronotherapy in optimizing antiviral strategies. It should be noted that because of the chronotherapy, the circadian clock is still running robustly, which greatly maintains the body's natural rhythms and biological processes. Figure S8 provides a detailed illustration of these time‐dependent responses, showing the levels of pc_RNA and virus titer during SR9009 administration at 2‐h intervals across the circadian cycle.


## DISCUSSION
  Our comprehensive model validation, incorporating comparisons with experimental phenotypes and cortisol pulse responses, demonstrates the biological relevance of our model. The robustness and sensitivity analyses further support its validity and reliability. The stability of the circadian period across parameter variations aligns with known biological characteristics, while the identification of critical parameters provides insights into key regulatory mechanisms. The model's ability to reproduce the antiphasic expression of Bmal1 and Per2 mRNA, along with its appropriate response to cortisol pulses, validates its capture of essential clock behaviors. Furthermore, while the clock mechanism showed robustness to viral perturbations, we identified key viral dynamics parameters (e.g., kd_Vendo) that significantly impact viral replication without disrupting circadian rhythms. This highlights the complex interplay between circadian and viral response mechanisms.

  The interplay between the circadian clock and viral infections has become an intriguing avenue of chronotherapy research, especially amid the COVID‐19 pandemic. 11 ,  44 ,  45  Several evidence has demonstrated that components of the molecular circadian clock machinery directly interact with viruses and regulate their replication. 46 ,  47 ,  48  For example, the circadian transcription factor BMAL1 modulates SARS‐CoV‐2 entry and replication in lung cells by controlling the expression of the ACE2 viral receptor and antiviral interferon‐stimulated genes. 9

  Our focus on BMAL1 as a therapeutic target is based on its dual role in SARS‐CoV‐2 infection. BMAL1 not only enhances viral entry by upregulating ACE2 expression but also suppresses host antiviral responses by downregulating interferon‐stimulated genes. This unique position makes BMAL1 a potentially powerful target for antiviral interventions. However, given BMAL1's crucial role in maintaining circadian rhythms, any therapeutic approach must carefully balance antiviral efficacy with the need to minimize disruption to the circadian system.

  The REV‐ERB agonist SR9009 was chosen for our model due to its ability to modulate BMAL1 activity. SR9009 has shown promise in altering circadian regulation and exhibiting antiviral properties against influenza. 49  Our model suggests that carefully timed administration of SR9009 could potentially inhibit SARS‐CoV‐2 replication while minimizing circadian disruption. Additionally, REV‐ERBα, an integral circadian repressor, has been implicated in the life cycle of viruses including influenza. 49  These findings have sparked interest in leveraging chronotherapies to treat viral infections in a timed manner that accounts for circadian effects. 50 ,  51  The REV‐ERB agonist SR9009 exhibits antiviral properties against influenza by altering circadian regulation. 49  As this research describes, SR9009 also shows the potential to inhibit SARS‐CoV‐2, 9  however, its circadian disruption side effects need to be considered. Quantitative systems pharmacology models integrating virus‐clock interactions, like the lung circadian clock model presented here in our study, will provide crucial insights guiding optimal timing and dosing of chronotherapies for COVID‐19. Moving forward, further unraveling the circadian control of viral replication through both experimental and modeling approaches will pave the way for novel circadian‐based antiviral therapies.

  Here, we developed an extensive mathematical model of the lung circadian clock, integrating core clock components (e.g., Per, Cry, Bmal1) with other regulatory elements (e.g., Dbp, Ror, Rev‐Erb). 14  To quantitatively capture the circadian regulation of these genes and proteins, the model uses kinetic equations to describe the molecular interaction mechanisms of the clock. The parameters used in the model were either directly derived from experimental data 33 ,  34 ,  35 ,  36 ,  37  or referred to published models. 23 ,  32  We validated the model by demonstrating its ability to generate ~24‐h oscillations with phase relationships consistent with literature findings. 37 ,  42  Furthermore, they show the model can be reset by simulated cortisol pulses, recapitulating a defining property of circadian clocks. 42

  We extended the model to incorporate components related to SARS‐CoV‐2 infection, including viral entry via the ACE2 receptor 52  and regulation of replication by interferon‐stimulated genes. 53  The validation of this extended model, as described in our “Results” section, provides several key insights: Resilience of circadian rhythms to viral perturbations: Our model reveals that virus‐related parameters have negligible effects on circadian oscillations, suggesting a robust clock mechanism that maintains its function even in the presence of viral infection. This finding aligns with the general understanding of circadian rhythm robustness. Importantly, this observation provides a testable hypothesis for future studies investigating the potential impacts of viral infection on circadian rhythms. It opens up new avenues for research into the complex interplay between viral infections and circadian rhythms, potentially leading to novel insights into chronobiology and virology. Future experiments could validate or challenge this prediction, furthering our understanding of how the circadian system responds to pathogenic challenges.Circadian regulation of ACE2 and ISG expression: Our model's predictions regarding the regulation of ACE2 and ISG expression by clock components are consistent with experimental findings. 9  This supports the model's validity and suggests potential mechanisms for circadian influence on viral susceptibility and immune response.Viral load dynamics: The model's reproduction of experimentally observed viral load patterns 9  and their relationship to clock components provides a framework for understanding how circadian rhythms might influence the course of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection.

  These results demonstrate that our extended model captures key interactions between the circadian clock and SARS‐CoV‐2 infection processes. The model not only reproduces known regulatory relationships but also provides new, testable hypotheses about the interplay between circadian rhythms and SARS‐CoV‐2 infection.

  While our model reproduces the rhythmic viral load observed experimentally, 9  we acknowledge that the exact timing of peak viral load is not yet fully understood and requires further investigation. We have therefore refrained from making specific predictions about this aspect in our current findings.

  Importantly, our model predicts optimized timing and duration for the Rev‐Erb agonist SR9009 to maximize viral inhibition while minimizing clock disruption side effects. 9 ,  49  This highlights the potential of the model for guiding antiviral chronotherapy and opens new avenues for experimental investigation. Recent experimental work by Zhuang et al. 9  provides support for our modeling approach. They demonstrated that treatment with the REV‐ERB agonist SR9009 reduced SARS‐CoV‐2 replication in Calu‐3 lung epithelial cells, with an IC50 of 8.3 μM. This experimental data provides a crucial starting point for understanding the pharmacodynamics of circadian‐based interventions against SARS‐CoV‐2. Our model incorporates this information to simulate the potential effects of SR9009 on viral replication in the context of the lung circadian clock. Incorporating these infection dynamics reproduces the rhythmic viral load observed experimentally. 9  However, we acknowledge that the exact timing of peak viral load is not yet fully understood and requires further investigation. We have therefore refrained from making specific predictions about this aspect in our current findings. The model predicts optimized timing and duration for the Rev‐Erb agonist SR9009 to maximize viral inhibition while minimizing clock disruption side effects. 9  This highlights the potential of the model for guiding antiviral chronotherapy and opens new avenues for experimental investigation.

  A key limitation of this study is the lack of comprehensive pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) data for both BMAL1‐targeting compounds and SR9009 in the context of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection and circadian modulation. Our computational model provides a theoretical framework for exploring potential interventions, but it is constrained in its ability to make precise predictions about dosing and concentration effects. Our simulations are based on theoretical dose–response relationships derived from our understanding of circadian biology and the limited available data on SARS‐CoV‐2 infection dynamics. 9  This limitation affects our capacity to determine optimal dosing strategies that effectively inhibit viral replication while minimizing disruption to circadian rhythms. Future research should focus on addressing these gaps. Experimental studies are needed to establish dose–response relationships for BMAL1‐targeting compounds and generate comprehensive PK/PD data for SR9009 in the context of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection. Such data would allow for more accurate predictions of efficacy, optimal dosing strategies, and better assessment of potential safety profiles. Future work should incorporate experimentally determined PK/PD parameters to refine the model's predictions and better inform potential therapeutic applications. While our model provides a theoretical basis for understanding the potential impact of these interventions, it is designed as a framework that can be refined and expanded as experimental data becomes available. We emphasize that these results should be interpreted as proof‐of‐concept explorations rather than definitive dosing recommendations. Despite these limitations, our study offers valuable insights into the potential of chronotherapy‐based approaches for COVID‐19 treatment and provides a platform for future experimental work in this promising area. Future studies focusing on in vivo PK/PD data for SR9009 in the context of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection and circadian modulation will be crucial for validating and refining our model's predictions.

  A key strength of this work is the integration of multi‐scale knowledge into a sophisticated in silico representation quantifying infection‐circadian interplay. 54  The model generates specific, testable predictions about optimizing the timing of antiviral therapy considering efficacy and toxicity. 55 ,  56  However, justifying the simplifying assumptions would strengthen confidence in the completeness of this complex model.

  In summary, this systems pharmacology modeling approach shows promise for understanding infection‐circadian interactions and aiding precision medicine. 55 ,  57  With experimental validation, this framework could be expanded to study COVID‐19 in other cell types and augmented to incorporate immune and pharmacokinetic considerations. Through this study, we have established a strong platform for an integrative, quantitative understanding of the circadian regulation of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection.
