# Single-cell multiomics reveals simvastatin inhibits pan-cancer epithelial-mesenchymal transition via the MEK/ERK pathway in XBP1+ mast cells


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-80858-5.


## Introduction
  Malignant tumors, ranking as the second leading cause of global population mortality, present significant challenges to public health systems worldwide1. Distant metastasis from primary tumors is the predominant cause of cancer-related fatalities2, with prevalent cancers like lung, colorectal, breast, and liver cancers exhibiting 5-year survival rates of less than 10% in advanced stages3. Presently, systemic therapy predominantly centered on molecular targeted drugs and immune checkpoint inhibitors is advancing rapidly; however, achieving further survival benefits remains elusive4,5. Therefore, addressing the current treatment bottlenecks is imperative for enhancing systemic therapy in advanced malignancies.

  Distant metastasis of malignant tumors is intricately linked to the suppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). Historically, macrophages, regulatory T cells (Tregs), tumor-associated fibroblasts, and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) have been identified as pivotal components of the suppressive TME. These cells modulate immune evasion, vascular remodeling, metabolic reprogramming, extracellular matrix degradation, and other changes by secreting adhesion molecules, cytokines, chemokines, and inflammatory factors or by inducing epigenetic alterations, thereby shaping the suppressive TME and pre-metastatic niche, which promotes tumor progression and metastasis6,7. While mast cells have conventionally been linked to allergic and autoimmune conditions due to the release of granules containing inflammatory mediators8, recent studies propose their significant role in the pre-metastatic niche of solid tumors like breast cancer9,10. Mast cells play a dual role in the TME; releasing inflammatory mediators through degranulation to activate CD4+/CD8+ T cells and natural killer (NK) cells for cytotoxic effects11, while their infiltration is associated with the metastasis and advancement of gastric, colorectal, and pancreatic cancers12–14. Mast cells activate matrix metalloproteinases via protease secretion, facilitating extracellular matrix degradation and enhancing tumor cell migration and invasion15,16. Additionally, mast cells release various factors like vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), platelet-derived growth factor-beta (PDGF-β), endothelin-1, granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), and IL-8, which contribute to tumor vessel remodeling17–19. Furthermore, mast cells disrupt choroidal epithelial cilia through the trypsin-PAR2-FoxJ1 pathway, promoting cerebrospinal fluid accumulation and causing tumor-associated peritumoral edema surrounding brain metastases20.

  The diverse roles of mast cells in the TME suggest the existence of distinct phenotypic subgroups with varying effects, including hypersensitivity reactions, anti-tumor responses, and pro-tumor effects. Targeted therapies against mast cell subgroups inducing hypersensitivity reactions involve tyrosine kinase inhibitors (e.g., Nilotinib and Sunitinib) used for treating both clonal and non-clonal mast cell-related disorders21. However, research targeting mast cell subgroups promoting tumorigenesis remains limited, highlighting a research gap. This study leverages single-cell transcriptomics data to characterize different mast cell subgroups and, employing multi-omics and Mendelian randomization methodologies, predicts potential targeted therapies, offering novel insights and evidence for future research investigations.


## Methods and materials

### Data collection and preprocessing
  The transcriptomic data used in this study were obtained from public databases. Bulk RNA-Seq data were sourced from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) pan-cancer dataset, which includes 33 types of malignancies (n = 10,434). scRNA-Seq data were obtained from GSE161529, GSE178318, GSE186344, GSE205506, and GSE234832, encompassing samples of breast cancer brain metastasis (BCBM, n = 6), breast cancer lymph node metastasis (BCLN, n = 7), colorectal cancer brain metastasis (CRCBM, n = 1), colorectal cancer liver metastasis (CRCLM, n = 16), lung cancer brain metastasis (LUBM, n = 5), melanoma brain metastasis (MBM, n = 3), ovarian cancer brain metastasis (OVBM, n = 1), renal clear cell carcinoma brain metastasis (RCCBM, n = 1), rhabdomyosarcoma brain metastasis (RMSBM, n = 1), and unknown primary brain metastasis (UNKBM, n = 1), totaling 42 samples.


### Experimental reagents and consumables
  Reagents and consumables for in vitro experiments included Transwell plates (WG3422) from Servicebio (Wuhan, China), fetal bovine serum, penicillin-streptomycin, and DMEM/F12 medium from Gibco (New York, USA). Streptavidin Magnetic Beads (P2151), BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0010), and Enhanced Chemiluminescence (ECL) Kit (P0018FM) were purchased from Beyotime (Shanghai, China). The mast cell degranulation assay kit (IMM001) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Missouri, USA). Antibodies used in the experiments included IRE1α (3294 S) from Cell Signaling Technology (Massachusetts, USA), and E-Cadherin (ab231303), N-Cadherin (ab245117), Vimentin (ab16700), β-Actin (ab8227), goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (ab205718), and goat anti-mouse secondary antibody (ab205719) from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). Commercial cell lines used in this study included HMC-1 human mast cells (BFN60808685) from Bluefbio (Shanghai, China), A549 human lung adenocarcinoma cells (C6053), HCT116 human colon cancer cells (C6309), and MCF7 human breast cancer cells (C6547) from Beyotime (Shanghai, China).


### Immune cell infiltration analysis
  Using TCGA pan-cancer Bulk RNA-Seq data, the CIBERSORT algorithm22 was employed to estimate the infiltration abundance of 22 immune cell types, including T cells, NK cells, macrophages, dendritic cells, B cells, and mast cells, in each sample. Based on the clinical information of the samples, differences in immune cell infiltration abundance between primary tumors (M0 stage) and metastatic tumors (M1 stage) were compared. Additionally, using overall survival (OS) as the dependent variable and the infiltration abundance of resting mast cells as the independent variable, a univariate COX regression analysis was performed across 33 types of cancer to identify survival-related cancer types. Samples from different cancers were divided into high/low infiltration groups based on the median infiltration abundance of mast cells, and Kaplan-Meier survival curves were plotted using the Log-Rank test to compare survival differences between the two groups.


### Cell dimensionality reduction, clustering, and annotation
  scRNA-Seq data from five different datasets were first quality controlled and SC-Transform normalized using the Seurat package (v4.3.0)23 in R (v4.2.2). Sample integration and batch effect correction were performed using the Harmony package (v0.1.1)24. Dimensionality reduction and clustering followed, with principal component analysis (PCA) applied to the integrated data. The Louvain algorithm optimized modularity to determine the specific number of cell clusters. PCA-reduced data were further visualized using UMAP25.

  Differential gene expression analysis between different clusters was conducted using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with conditions set at Log2Fc > 0.5 and Benjamini-Hochberg-adjusted P < 0.05 to identify differential expressed genes (DEGs). Cell annotation was performed using the SingleR package (v1.10.0)26, and manual corrections were made based on DEGs and cell biological functions, resulting in the annotation of nine main cell types. Visualization was done through UMAP, calculating the proportion of each cell type in the samples.

  Finally, data of myeloid cells were extracted for secondary dimensionality reduction, clustering, and annotation, following the main cell types annotation process to annotate myeloid cells into four subtypes. Mast cells were identified and extracted for further analysis.


### Cell trajectory analysis
  To further reduce dimensionality and cluster mast cells, identifying different anti-tumor/pro-tumor subtypes, cell trajectory analysis was performed to determine the developmental trajectory of mast cells. The Monocle3 package (v1.3.1)27–29 was used for cell trajectory analysis. SimplePPT and iterative algorithms were utilized for cell trajectory recognition and pseudotime analysis, plotting the pseudotime cell trajectory map of mast cells and identifying cell subtypes with different differentiation outcomes. Following the main cell types annotation process and considering cell differentiation trajectories, mast cells were ultimately annotated into three subtypes.


### Enrichment analysis
  To validate the biological functions of different mast cell subtypes and identify those with distinct roles in the tumor microenvironment (TME), we performed enrichment analysis. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test to identify DEGs for the three mast cell subtypes. Enrichment analysis based on these DEGs utilized Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG)30 gene sets, with results presented in bubble charts. To further confirm biological functions, we conducted gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using the fgsea package (v1.22.0)31 with Hallmark gene sets.


### Cell communication analysis
  We integrated data from mast cells, epithelial cells, sarcoma cells, and melanoma cells to perform cell communication analysis and identify interactions between mast cell subtypes and malignant tumors. This analysis, conducted using the CellChat package (v1.6.1)32, predicted interactions between mast cell subtypes and malignant tumors. Communication networks were visualized based on the number and probability of ligand-receptor pairs, which were displayed using bubble charts.


### Weighted gene co-expression network analysis
  To identify characteristic genes of pro-tumor mast cell subtypes and potential therapeutic targets, we conducted Weighted Gene Co-Expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) using the WGCNA package (v1.72-1)33,34. Dynamic tree cut algorithms identified and calculated different modules and module eigengenes (MEs), with hierarchical clustering performed and distinct gene modules represented by different colors. A module refers to a set of genes with similar characteristics obtained through clustering analysis, MEs represent the characteristic genes of these modules. A correlation heatmap was then plotted to identify MEs associated with pro-tumor mast cell subtypes for further analysis.


### Machine learning algorithm for potential target screening
  Using the WGCNA results, we identified MEs associated with pro-tumor mast cell subtypes. To predict potential targets for treating malignant tumor metastasis by targeting mast cells, we constructed a prognostic risk model using machine learning algorithms. Twelve machine learning algorithms (Ridge, Enet, Stepglm, Lasso, RandomForest, GBM, XGBoost, NaiveBayes, SVM, glmBoost, LDA, plsRglm) were employed, with one algorithm used for variable selection and another for constructing classification prediction models. A total of 113 algorithm model combinations were cross-validated. The prognostic model was constructed using clinical information and transcriptome expression data from TCGA pan-cancer samples, with gene expression levels of the identified MEs as independent variables and overall survival (OS) as the outcome variable. Pan-cancer samples were randomly divided into training and validation sets, and the area under the curve (AUC) of receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves was calculated for the 113 algorithm model combinations. The best algorithm model combination was selected based on AUC values to construct the prognostic risk model. ### Drug sensitivity prediction
  Following the construction of the prognostic risk model, potential therapeutic targets were identified. To predict drugs targeting these potential targets, the CellMiner database35,36 was utilized for drug sensitivity prediction. Gene expression data from CellMiner was extracted based on the potential targets. Pearson correlation analysis was initially performed to verify the correlation between the expression levels of potential targets and drug sensitivity, followed by linear fitting and generation of correlation scatter plots. Subsequently, potential targets were categorized into high and low expression groups based on the median expression level, and differences in drug sensitivity between these groups were compared. The intersection of the correlation analysis and differential analysis results, combined with the risk coefficients of potential targets, was used to screen for potential targeted therapies.


### Mendelian randomization analysis
  Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis was conducted using single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as instrumental variables (IVs) for eight genes: CBX1, HES6, KIT, KITLG, MAP1B, MARCKS, and MLLT11. SNPs related to expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) were obtained from the eQTLGen consortium37. Since NGFRAP1 is located on the X chromosome, we are unable to obtain SNPs associated with the eQTL of this gene. Consequently, we will exclude it from further analysis. SNPs were screened for genome-wide significance with FDR < 0.05 and located within ± 500 kb of the mentioned gene loci, ensuring strong correlation between IVs and eQTL. To exclude the influence of linkage disequilibrium (LD), r^2 < 0.3 was used as the threshold38. A strong correlation between the instrumental variable and the exposure is a prerequisite for Mendelian randomization analysis. Therefore, we used the formula (F = BETA2exposure / SE2exposure) to assess the correlation of the instrumental variables (IVs)39. In this formula, BETA and SE represent the effect size and standard error of the SNP on the exposure, respectively. IVs with an F-Value < 10 are considered with weak correlation and are excluded from further analysis. Details of the IVs included in the analysis are provided in Table S1. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) data on cancer were obtained from the Finnish r10 database. Five cancers were selected as outcomes: Lung and Bronchus Cancer (case number 6340, control number 314193, ID C3_BRONCHUS_LUNG_EXALLC), Breast Cancer (case number 18786, control number 182927, ID C3_BREAST_EXALLC), Colon Cancer (case number 4143, control number 314193, ID C3_COLON_EXALLC), Rectum Cancer (case number 2490, control number 314193, ID C3_RECTUM_EXALLC), and Hepatocellular Carcinoma (case number 500, control number 314193, ID C3_HEPATOCELLU_CARC_EXALLC). Inverse variance weighted (IVW), MR Egger, and Weighted median (WM) methods were used for MR analysis, with IVW as the primary model and MR Egger and WM for robustness analysis. P < 0.05 indicated a significant causal relationship. Heterogeneity was tested using IVW and MR-Egger methods, calculating Cochran’s Q statistic; P > 0.05 indicated no heterogeneity. The MR-Egger regression intercept was used to assess pleiotropy; if the intercept approached 0 and P > 0.05, no horizontal pleiotropy was assumed. Sensitivity analysis was performed using a leave-one-out approach, removing SNPs one at a time and repeating IVW analysis. All Mendelian randomization analysis were performed in R (version 4.3.1).


### In vitro experiment validation

#### Co-culture system
  In vitro experiments utilized a Transwell chamber to establish a co-culture system of mast cells and tumor cells. XBP1+ mast cells were derived from the HMC-1 human mast cell line, characterized by high expression of the surface marker IRE1α. XBP1+ mast cells were sorted using immunomagnetic beads targeting IRE1α, and RT-qPCR was used to detect XBP1 mRNA expression levels for identification. A549 human lung adenocarcinoma cells were used as lung cancer cells, MCF-7 human breast cancer cells as breast cancer cells, and HCT-116 human colorectal cancer cells as colorectal cancer cells.


#### Transwell migration assay
  A 24-well Transwell plate was used to construct the co-culture system, with tumor cells in the upper chamber and mast cells in the lower chamber, incubated for 48 h. The upper chamber was then removed, washed with PBS, fixed, and stained with crystal violet. Random fields were photographed under an inverted microscope (200×), and the number of cell-migrated pores was counted.


#### Wound healing assay
  A 24-well Transwell plate was used to construct the co-culture system, with mast cells in the upper chamber and tumor cells in the lower chamber. After digestion and counting, approximately 1 × 10^5 tumor cells were added to each upper chamber well, and approximately 5 × 10^4 tumor cells were added to each lower chamber well, incubated overnight. After removing the medium and washing three times with PBS, a scratch was made, and photographs were taken. After 48 h and 72 h of incubation, photographs of the same field (100×) were taken to compare the scratch width at 0 h, 48 h and 72 h.


#### Degranulation capacity assay
  Cellular chymase activity was measured using spectrophotometry to assess degranulation capacity. Log-phase HMC-1 cells and XBP1+ mast cells were digested, resuspended, and counted, with cell concentration adjusted to 5 × 10^6/mL. 1mL of each cell suspension was added to centrifuge tubes and treated with the calcium ionophore A23187. Cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 1 h, and the supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 1000 g. Control groups (blank, negative, positive) and experimental groups were set up in 96-well plates according to the instructions, incubated at 37 °C for 2 h, and the OD value at 405 nm was measured using a UV-visible spectrophotometer.


#### RT-qPCR
  HMC-1 cells and XBP1+ mast cells were cultured in 6-well plates. Total RNA was extracted from collected cell suspensions, and RNA levels and purity were measured using a UV spectrophotometer. Extracted total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA, and RT-qPCR was performed after adjusting the cDNA concentration. β-Actin was used as the reference gene to detect the relative expression level of XBP1 mRNA. Primer sequences and the RT-qPCR system are detailed in Supplementary Tables.


#### Western blot
  Experimental groups were set up in 6-well plates, with XBP1+ mast cells treated with 50 mg/L simvastatin. Control groups included untreated HMC-1 and XBP1+ mast cells. Western blot was used to detect the effect of simvastatin on MEK/ERK signaling pathway activity. The co-culture system in 24-well Transwell plates was treated with 50 mg/L simvastatin, with control groups including untreated tumor cells, co-culture system, and simvastatin-treated tumor cells. Western blot was used to detect the effect of simvastatin on EMT-related proteins E-cadherin, N-cadherin, and Vimentin. Tumor cell suspensions were collected, centrifuged, and the supernatant was discarded. Total protein was extracted from resuspended cells using cell lysis buffer, and protein concentration was measured. Protein samples were denatured at 95 °C for 10 min, loaded (10–15µL per well) on 10% PAGE gel, and electrophoresed. Membranes were transferred and blocked with 5% BSA in PBS at room temperature for 1 h, incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, washed with PBST, and incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Membranes were treated with ECL reagents and signals were observed, with images processed using ImageJ software (v1.54i). β-Actin was used as the reference protein to detect the relative expression levels of MEK 1/2, p-MEK 1/2, ERK 1/2, p-ERK 1/2, E-cadherin, N-cadherin, and Vimentin.


#### Statistical analysis
  For differential analysis between groups, independent sample t-tests were used for normally distributed data with homogeneity of variance, while the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for non-normally distributed data. One-way ANOVA with Bonferroni correction was employed for multiple group comparisons. Pearson correlation coefficients were used for correlation analysis. All in vitro experimental data were repeated three times to ensure reproducibility. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.


## Results

### Immune infiltration analysis reveals that mast cell activation is associated with pan-cancer survival
  We utilized TCGA pan-cancer transcriptomic data to predict the abundance of 22 types of immune cells and compared immune cell infiltration between primary and metastatic samples. The results (Fig. 1A) reveal that metastatic tumors exhibit higher infiltration levels of M0 macrophages, activated mast cells, neutrophils, and resting NK cells compared to primary tumors. Conversely, naive B cells, M2 macrophages, resting mast cells, monocytes, activated NK cells, resting memory CD4+ T cells, and CD8+ T cells show higher infiltration in primary tumors. These findings suggest that features like NK cell dormancy and reduced infiltration of CD8+ T cells and memory CD4+ T cells characterize the pre-metastatic microenvironment of malignancies. Significantly, we found that resting mast cells show higher infiltration in primary tumors, while activated mast cells show higher infiltration in metastatic tumors. This finding underscores the dual role of mast cells in the TME and confirms that mast cell activation contributes to the formation of the pre-metastatic microenvironment.

  Fig. 1Analysis of immune cell infiltration. (A) Differences in the infiltration abundance of 22 immune cell types between primary and metastatic pan-cancer; (B) Univariate COX regression analysis showing the correlation between resting mast cell infiltration and overall survival (OS) in pan-cancer; (C-L) Kaplan-Meier survival curves illustrating OS differences between high and low resting mast cell infiltration in various cancer types, based on univariate COX regression analysis results.

  To further explore the relationship between mast cell infiltration and survival, we conducted univariate Cox regression analysis using the abundance of resting mast cells as the independent variable. The forest plot (Fig. 1B) indicates that increased infiltration of resting mast cells serves as a prognostic protective factor in solid tumors such as breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), and uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC). Survival analysis results (Fig. 1C-L) corroborate these findings, suggesting that higher infiltration of resting mast cells predicts longer overall survival (OS) for patients with these solid tumors.


### Cell dimension reduction, clustering, and annotation
  This study analyzed scRNA-Seq data from 5 datasets comprising 46 different metastatic tumor samples. After rigorous data quality control, normalization, and batch effect removal preprocessing, the study included a total of n = 164,094 cells. Following dimension reduction and clustering, cells were categorized into 9 major types (Fig. 2A). Specifically, Myeloid cells underwent secondary dimension reduction clustering and were classified into 4 distinct cell subtypes (Fig. 2B): Dendritic cells, Macrophages, Monocytes, and Mast cells. Differential gene expression analysis was performed for each major cell type, and the volcano plot (Fig. 2C) highlights the top 5 upregulated and downregulated DEGs. The study identified specific marker expressions for different major cell types (Fig. 2D), including B cells (CD79A, IGKC, JCHAIN), Endothelial cells (VWF, PECAM1, PLVAP), Epithelial cells (KRT19, KRT18, KRT8), Fibroblasts (COL1A1, COL1A2, ACTA2), Melanoma cells (TYRP1, MLANA, MITF), Myeloid cells (CD163, LYZ, FCER1G), Neurons (TUBA1B, SOX4, TUBB), NK/T cells (CD3D, CD3E, GZMA), and Sarcoma cells (KCNQ1OT1, IGF2, MEST). Additionally, the density plot (Fig. 2E) illustrates the marker expression characteristics specific to major cell types in the UMAP plot, while Fig. 2F provides the proportional representation of the 9 major cell types across different metastatic tumors.

### Cell annotation identifies distinct mast cell subtypes
  To delineate mast cell subtypes with distinct anti-tumor/pro-tumor characteristics, initial cell trajectory analysis was conducted. This analysis categorized mast cells into three subtypes (Fig. 3A): HSPA6+ (HSPA6, LTB, SPOCK2), CCL4+ (CCL4, CCL3, CCL4L2), and XBP1+ (XBP1, NEAT1, EGR1) mast cells. Figure 3B illustrates the cell differentiation trajectories and pseudo-temporal ordering of mast cells. The volcano plot (Fig. 3C) displays gene expression characteristics specific to each subtype. HSPA6+ mast cells were identified as the starting point for mast cell differentiation within the tumor microenvironment (TME), while CCL4+ and XBP1+ mast cells represented distinct differentiation branches, indicative of their varied biological functions.

(A) Mast cells were re-annotated as HSPA6 + mast cells, CCL4+ mast cells, and XBP1 + mast cells; (B) Cell differentiation trajectory analysis of mast cells using Monocle3; (C) Volcano plot showing DEGs expression levels in the three mast cell subtypes; (D, E) Bubble plots showing GO (D) functional enrichment and KEGG (E) pathway enrichment results for the three mast cell subtypes; (F) GSEA enrichment analysis results for mast cells based on HALLMARK gene sets; (G) GSEA line plot of enrichment scores for the epithelial-mesenchymal transition pathway; (H) Cell communication network between the three mast cell subtypes and three types of malignant tumors; (I) Bubble plot showing the ligand-receptor pairs for cell communication between XBP1+ mast cells and other cells.


### Gene enrichment and cell communication analyses reveals functions of mast cell subtypes
  To further elucidate the functional differences among these mast cell subtypes, differential gene expression analysis followed by GO (Fig. 3D) and KEGG (Fig. 3E) enrichment analyses was performed. CCL4+ mast cells were notably associated with conditions such as inflammatory bowel disease, asthma, autoimmune thyroid disease, type 1 diabetes, and allograft rejection, linked to allergic reactions or autoimmune diseases. In contrast, HSPA6 + mast cells exhibited involvement in antigen presentation, cytotoxic effects mediated by T cells or neutrophils, and cellular senescence, suggesting their role in activating anti-tumor immune responses and inducing tumor cell senescence. Meanwhile, XBP1 + mast cells showed associations with transcription factor dysregulation and extracellular matrix degradation, potentially influencing the pre-metastatic microenvironment. Using HALLMARK gene sets, GSEA analysis (Fig. 3F) underscored a strong link between XBP1 + mast cells and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), supported by the GSEA plot (Fig. 3G).

  Cell communication analysis was employed to explore the interaction network between XBP1 + mast cells and tumor cells (Fig. 3H). This analysis highlighted a closer relationship between XBP1 + mast cells and tumor cells compared to other mast cell subtypes, particularly evidenced by ligand-receptor pairs promoting tumor metastasis (Fig. 3I). Notably, XBP1 + mast cells were implicated in regulating epithelial cells, sarcoma cells, and melanoma cells through pathways such as KITLG-KIT and SELE-CD44, with KITLG-KIT interactions being notably significant.


### Prognostic risk model identifies potential mast cell targets
  To identify potential therapeutic targets for XBP1 + mast cells, we further screened for the most likely targets using Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) and machine learning algorithms. First, we used WGCNA to identify module eigengenes (MEs) most associated with XBP1 + mast cells. The module clustering dendrogram from WGCNA (Fig. 4A) showed three main modules (blue, brown, and turquoise) in mast cell subtypes. The heatmap of the correlation between MEs and mast cell subtypes (Fig. 4B), combined with the co-expression heatmap of MEs (Fig. 4C), indicated that the MEbrown (Fig. 4D) is the characteristic module of XBP1 + mast cells. MEbrown contains 15 characteristic genes, including CKB, MAP1B, BNIP3, HES6, MARCKS, BASP1, NGFRAP1, MLLT11, PAFAH1B3, RNASEH2A, CENPV, RTN3, CBX1, WBP5, and RDX.

  Fig. 4Potential target screening for XBP1 + mast cells. (A) Module clustering dendrogram from weighted gene co-expression network analysis; (B) Heatmap showing the correlation between different module eigengenes (MEs) expression levels and mast cell subtypes; (C) Heatmap showing the co-expression level correlation between MEs; (D) Heatmap showing the expression levels of MEs in the MEbrown module across different samples; (E) Prognostic risk model construction based on 12 machine learning algorithms (MR), with a heatmap showing the AUC values of different MR combinations in the Training and Testing sets, where the optimal algorithm model combination is LASSO + RF; (F) Correlation coefficients of 15 prognosis-related genes in the prognostic risk model; (G-I) Kaplan-Meier survival curves showing OS differences between high and low risk groups in all samples, the Training set, and the testing set.

  We used the expression levels of these 15 characteristic genes from MEbrown as independent variables and OS data from TCGA pan-cancer dataset as the outcome variable. TCGA pan-cancer samples were randomly divided into training and testing sets. Prognostic modeling was conducted using 113 combinations of algorithm models. By calculating the AUC for each sample, we determined that the optimal algorithm model combination was LASSO + RF (Fig. 4E). The prognostic risk model included the 15 characteristic genes of MEbrown, with correlation coefficients of MLLT11 (0.2737), MARCKS (0.1327), HES6 (0.1201), CENPV (0.0948), RNASEH2A (0.0913), WBP5 (0.0891), BASP1 (0.0559), RDX (0.0125), CKB (-0.0214), RTN3 (-0.0251), BNIP3 (-0.0543), PAFAH1B3 (-0.065), MAP1B (-0.1211), NGFRAP1 (-0.164), and CBX1 (-0.2374), as detailed in Fig. 4F. In the prognostic risk model, a positive correlation coefficient indicates a risk factor for OS, while a negative coefficient indicates a protective factor. Each sample’s risk score was calculated according to the prognostic risk model, and samples were divided into high/low-risk groups based on the median risk score in all samples, training, and testing sets. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were used to compare OS differences between the high/low-risk groups. The results (Fig. 4G-I) showed that the OS of the high-risk group was significantly lower than that of the low-risk group (P < 0.001) in all samples, the training set, and the testing set, confirming the reliability of the prognostic risk model in predicting survival benefit.


### Drug sensitivity prediction screens targeted drugs
  We selected genes with the highest correlation coefficients, including risk factors (MLLT11, MARCKS, HES6) and protective factors (CBX1, NGFRAP1, MAP1B), along with potential targets obtained from cell communication analysis (KITLG, KIT), to predict drug sensitivity using the CellMiner database. Based on correlation and differential analysis results, we identified potential drugs. Figure 5A-L illustrates some of the drug sensitivity prediction results according to the correlation coefficients. Notably, the identified drugs mainly fall into four categories: BRAF/MEK/ERK pathway inhibitors such as Selumetinib (P < 0.001, R = -0.47), ARRY-162 (P = 0.001, R = -0.41), and Trametinib (P < 0.01, R = -0.38); Aurora kinase inhibitors such as AMG-900 (P < 0.001, R = -0.45) and ENMD-2076 (P < 0.001, R = 0.39); topoisomerase I inhibitors such as Camptothecin (P < 0.01, R = 0.38) and Topotecan (P < 0.01, R = 0.33); HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors such as Fluvastatin (P < 0.01, R = 0.36), Lovastatin (P < 0.01, R = 0.36), and Simvastatin (P < 0.01, R = 0.37). We observed that several drugs target multiple genes, such as Selumetinib, ARRY-162, and Simvastatin. Detailed drug sensitivity prediction results are available in Supplementary Table S2.

### Mendelian randomization analysis reveals the causal relationship between potential drugs and pan-cancer
  Our study involved the analysis of 684 SNPs across 7 genes and 5 cancers, totaling 35 drug target MR analyses. Detailed information on the SNPs included in the analysis is presented in Table S1, and the results of all MR analyses are thoroughly documented in Table S3. A significant causal association was identified when the P-value derived from the IVW method was less than 0.05 and the 95% CI for the OR did not include 1. We visualized the significant causal relationships between target genes and diseases (Fig. 6).

  Fig. 6Results of Mendelian randomization analysis. The dots in the figure represent the OR value. The larger the OR value, the larger the dot. The color of the dots represents different methods: red for inverse variance weighted, yellow for MR Egger, and blue for weighted median.

  We found that the expression levels of MARCKS were significantly associated with metastasis in lung cancer (P = 0.006, OR = 0.822), breast cancer (P = 0.008, OR = 1.138), and rectal cancer (P = 0.001, OR = 1.477). Similarly, the expression levels of KITLG/KIT were significantly associated with metastasis in lung cancer (P = 0.027, OR = 1.145), breast cancer (P = 0.001, OR = 1.1), and rectal cancer (P = 0.044, OR = 1.109). These findings suggest that MARCKS and KITLG/KIT could serve as potential targets for mast cell-targeted therapy in pan-cancer distant metastasis. Notably, our results imply a possible regulatory relationship between MARCKS and KITLG/KIT signaling. Additionally, the expression level of MAP1B was causally associated with lung cancer (P = 0.035, OR = 1.038). No significant heterogeneity was detected in the heterogeneity analysis (all P-values > 0.05), as detailed in Table S4. In the horizontal pleiotropy analysis (Table S5), the MR Egger intercepts were close to 0, with all P-values > 0.05, indicating no significant horizontal pleiotropy in our MR analyses. Finally, sensitivity analysis using the leave-one-out method (Figure S1) showed stable results, with no substantial impact observed after removing any single SNP.

  Based on multi-omics analysis, drug sensitivity prediction, and Mendelian randomization analysis, we hypothesize that XBP1 + mast cells may activate the downstream MEK/ERK pathway via the transcription factor XBP1, thereby regulating mast cell degranulation to release KITLG, which targets KIT on tumor cells and induces EMT. HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors may inhibit mast cell degranulation by suppressing the MEK/ERK pathway, ultimately inhibiting EMT.


### In vitro experiments reveal that simvastatin inhibits EMT in pan-cancer through the MEK/ERK pathway
  To preliminarily validate our hypothesis, we conducted various in vitro experiments, including Transwell migration assays, wound healing assays, and chymotrypsin-like activity assays. HMC-1 cells specifically express XBP1, and we isolated XBP1 + mast cells from HMC-1 cells using immunomagnetic beads. These cells were identified via RT-qPCR (Fig. 7A), which revealed significantly higher XBP1 mRNA expression in XBP1 + mast cells compared to HMC-1 cells (P < 0.001). The reaction system formulation for RT-qPCR and the mRNA primer sequences for β-Actin and XBP1 can be found in the Table S6-S9.

  Fig. 7Effects of simvastatin on XBP1 + mast cells and tumor cells. (A) Relative mRNA expression levels of XBP1 in HMC-1 cells and XBP1 + mast cells; (B, C) Transwell migration assay showing the effects of simvastatin intervention on the migration ability of A549, MCF7, and HCT116 cells (200×); (D-G) Wound healing assay demonstrating the impact of simvastatin intervention on the migration ability of A549, MCF7, and HCT116 cells (100×); (H) Tryptase activity assessment showing the effect of simvastatin on the degranulation ability of XBP1 + mast cells; (I-K) Effects of simvastatin intervention on MEK/ERK signaling activation in XBP1 + mast cells; (L-O) Effects of simvastatin intervention on the expression levels of EMT-related proteins in A549, MCF7, and HCT116 cells. * P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01, *** P < 0.001, **** P < 0.0001.

  The Transwell migration assay results (Fig. 7B-C) showed that simvastatin alone had minimal impact on the migration ability of A549, MCF7, and HCT116 cells. In contrast, the migration ability was significantly enhanced in the XBP1 + mast cell co-culture system compared to the HMC-1 co-culture system (PA549 < 0.001, PMCF7 < 0.01, PHCT116 < 0.001). Simvastatin intervention in the XBP1 + mast cell co-culture system significantly inhibited the migration ability of tumor cells (PA549 < 0.0001, PMCF7 < 0.0001, PHCT116 < 0.0001).

  The wound healing assay results (Fig. 7D-G) were consistent with the Transwell migration assay results. At 48 h, the XBP1 + mast cell co-culture system exhibited stronger migration ability than the HMC-1 co-culture system for A549 and MCF7 cells (PA549 < 0.001, PMCF7 < 0.05), but not for HCT116 cells (PHCT116 > 0.05). Simvastatin intervention significantly inhibited the migration ability of tumor cells in the XBP1 + mast cell co-culture system (PA549 < 0.0001, PMCF7 < 0.0001, PHCT116 < 0.0001) at 48 h. At 72 h, the XBP1 + mast cell co-culture system still showed stronger migration ability compared to the HMC-1 co-culture system (PA549 < 0.0001, PMCF7 < 0.001, PHCT116 < 0.001), and simvastatin intervention significantly inhibited the migration ability of tumor cells (PA549 < 0.0001, PMCF7 < 0.0001, PHCT116 < 0.0001) at 72 h.

  Next, we assessed the tryptase activity among different groups. The results (Fig. 7H) indicated that XBP1 + mast cells exhibited stronger degranulation ability compared to HMC-1 cells (P < 0.001), which was significantly inhibited by simvastatin intervention (P < 0.0001).

  We also used Western blot analysis to examine the effect of simvastatin on MEK/ERK signaling activity in XBP1 + mast cells. The results (Fig. 7I-K) showed significant phosphorylation of MEK 1/2 (P < 0.001) and ERK 1/2 (P < 0.001) proteins in XBP1 + mast cells compared to HMC-1 cells. Simvastatin intervention significantly reduced the activity of p-MEK 1/2 (P < 0.001) and p-ERK 1/2 (P < 0.001) proteins. The original western-blot images can be found in the Figure S2-S6.

  Finally, we investigated the impact of targeting XBP1 + mast cells on tumor cell EMT. The results (Fig. 7L-O) showed that simvastatin alone had minimal effect on the expression levels of E-cadherin and N-cadherin but significantly increased Vimentin protein expression (P < 0.0001) in A549, MCF7, and HCT116 cells. Co-culture with XBP1 + mast cells had minimal effect on E-cadherin but significantly increased the protein expression levels of N-cadherin (P < 0.001) and Vimentin (P < 0.001). Simvastatin intervention in the co-culture system significantly increased E-cadherin protein expression (P < 0.001) while decreasing N-cadherin (P < 0.001) and Vimentin (P < 0.001) protein expression levels. The original western-blot images can be found in the Figure S7-S9.


## Discussion
  Distant metastasis of malignant tumors is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths, making targeted inhibition of TME a primary research focus. Significant breakthroughs have been achieved with immune checkpoint and chimeric antigen receptor therapies targeting T cells and NK cells. With deeper insights into the TME, the dual role of mast cells can be more clearly understood. Our study suggests that the TME may contain an XBP1 + mast cell subtype that promotes tumor progression and metastasis. Using machine learning algorithms for target screening and Mendelian randomization validation, we identified KITLG/KIT, MARCKS and MAP1B as potential targets for XBP1 + mast cells. Combined with drug sensitivity prediction, we propose HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors as potential targeted drugs.

  Depending on the tumor type and TME state, various strategies can be employed to target mast cells due to their dual role. These strategies include regulating KITLG/KIT signaling, stabilizing degranulation, targeting surface activation/inhibition receptors, limiting mast cell infiltration, and modulating mast cell-secreted mediators40. KITLG, primarily secreted by mast cells, fibroblasts, and stromal cells, binds to KIT (c-Kit) on mast cell surfaces, promoting adhesion, migration, proliferation, survival, and the release of histamine and chymase, thus participating in allergic and autoimmune responses41. Cell communication results suggest that KITLG/KIT signaling may be involved in malignant tumor metastasis. Since KITLG/KIT signaling is crucial for mast cell development and activation, tyrosine kinase inhibitors are potential non-specific drugs targeting this pathway. In neuroendocrine prostate cancer, imatinib inhibits mast cell proliferation and degranulation, enhancing endocrine therapy efficacy42. Similarly, in melanoma immunotherapy, imatinib or sunitinib can improve immune checkpoint therapy outcomes43. Barzolvolimab, a newly developed KIT monoclonal antibody, is in clinical trials and shows potential for treating gastrointestinal tumors44 and chronic urticaria45. Beyond KITLG/KIT signaling, SELE/CD44 is another significant pathway through which mast cells promote malignant tumor progression. CD44, an adhesion molecule and tumor stem cell marker, interacts with hyaluronic acid (HA) to degrade the extracellular matrix, promoting tumor progression, metastasis, and drug resistance46. Targeting the CD44/HA axis is a current focus in tumor drug development47.

  Our results indicate that XBP1 + mast cells exhibit transcription factor dysregulation and regulate epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). The transcription factor XBP1, abnormally activated by the unfolded protein Myristoylated alanine-rich C kinase substrate (MARCKS), activates MEK/ERK signaling, promoting degranulation and the release of mediators such as KITLG or SELE, which act on invasive receptors like KIT or CD44 on malignant tumor cells, inducing EMT. In vitro experiments confirmed that XBP1 + mast cells promote EMT, while simvastatin intervention inhibits this process by stabilizing degranulation. In mastocytosis with high XBP1 expression, activation of the transcription factor XBP1 and its upstream sensor IRE1α is involved in the unfolded protein response (UPR)48,49, promoting mast cell degranulation and the release of inflammatory mediators50. Statins have been shown to inhibit degranulation of mast cells51–53 and basophils54,55, supporting our hypothesis. Additionally, statins can inhibit platelet degranulation, stabilizing cell membranes56. MARCKS, an inherent unfolded protein, can induce UPR and mediate crosstalk among various signals. MARCKS regulates pro-inflammatory cytokine expression in macrophages, and its derivatives can inhibit inflammation57. MARCKS also interacts with actin, playing a crucial role in cytoskeleton regulation and is associated with malignant tumor metastasis58. It participates in Wnt and NF-κB signaling activation, promoting the migration and invasion of melanoma and chronic lymphocytic leukemia cells59,60. Notably, XBP1 + mast cells can also interact with CCL4+ mast cells and HSPA6 + mast cells through MIF-(CD74+CXCR4), MIF-(CD74+CD44), and SEMA3C-(NRP2 + PLXNA1) pathways, regulating anti-tumor and autoimmune-related subtypes. This suggests that XBP1 + mast cells might promote tumor progression by interfering with the anti-tumor functions of normal mast cells. Evidence shows that mast cells can release mast cell extracellular traps (MCETs) expressing macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF)61–64, which induces and maintains inhibitory TME characteristics. MIF targeting CD74+CXCR4 or CD74+CD44 activates signals like AKT, NF-κB, ERK1/2, and JNK, promoting tumor progression65,66.

  In the results of this study, we identified several interesting phenomena that warrant further investigation. During immune cell infiltration analysis based on bulk RNA-Seq data, we observed that in low-grade glioma (LGG), the Kaplan-Meier survival curves revealed that higher infiltration of quiescent mast cells was associated with poorer survival, contrary to findings in other solid tumors. We hypothesize that this is mainly related to the unique immune microenvironment of LGG. Due to the less aggressive nature of LGG, it has a reduced impact on the blood-brain barrier, providing a natural advantage for the formation of a unique TME in LGG. This may explain the limited effectiveness of new therapies, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors, against LGG. Glioma cells can induce local microenvironmental changes through signaling and genetic alterations, and the crosstalk between malignant cells and the microenvironment is crucial for tumor cell proliferation, migration, chemoresistance, and immune evasion67. In gliomas, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) are involved in initiating or regulating the functions of other immunosuppressive cells while impairing the functions of normal immune components, including inhibiting M1 macrophage polarization, dendritic cell antigen presentation, NK cell cytotoxicity, and T cell activation68. Therefore, we speculate that MDSCs’ remodeling of the TME obscures the dual role of mast cells, leading to this intriguing result.

  Additionally, in experiments testing the effects of simvastatin on EMT-related protein expression, Western blot analysis showed that simvastatin alone appeared to promote the EMT state of malignant cells. This phenomenon was also observed in another study69, where researchers found that statins induced a mesenchymal morphology in 2D-cultured human pancreatic cancer cells and reduced the ability of 3D-cultured cells to form spheres, with looser cell-cell connections. It is important to note that statin treatment did not inhibit the growth and proliferation of cancer cells, suggesting that the morphological changes are unrelated to these processes. The study concluded that when the cholesterol pathway is inhibited by statins, cancer cell plasticity decreases, making them more prone to an EMT state. When free cancer cells need to undergo a mesenchymal-to-epithelial transition (MET) to colonize, statins prevent MET formation, thereby inhibiting cancer cell metastasis. In our study, we observed increased expression of N-cadherin and Vimentin, indicating a tendency toward the EMT state after simvastatin treatment alone. However, in co-culture systems, simvastatin inhibited the interaction between XBP1 + mast cells and tumor cells, thereby suppressing the transition of tumor cells to the EMT state. This phenomenon highlights the importance of interactions between immune cells and tumor cells in the TME as a significant factor influencing cancer cell behavior.

  This study focuses on the impact of simvastatin targeting XBP1 + mast cells on pan-cancer EMT, though there are limitations. The results imply a close relationship between XBP1 + mast cells and UPR, with MARCKS and XBP1 being key UPR molecules. However, the complex mechanisms of UPR were not deeply explored. Furthermore, XBP1 + mast cells also have the potential to regulate other mast cell subtypes, including anti-tumor HSPA6 + mast cells, which are closely related to tumor progression and metastasis. The role of XBP1 + mast cells in shaping the inhibitory TME is complex and diverse. Future research will address these limitations and further explore the mechanisms involved.


## Conclusion
  This study confirms that abnormal activation of MEK/ERK signaling in XBP1 + mast cells can induce EMT in tumor cells by regulating degranulation and activating KITLG/KIT signaling. Simvastatin can inhibit EMT by stabilizing degranulation and inhibiting KITLG/KIT signaling.
