# Transient interactions modulate the affinity of NF-κB transcription factors for DNA


## Significance
  The nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) dimers regulate the transcription of hundreds of target genes, influencing a broad spectrum of cellular programs, in particular immune responses and cell survival. The biochemical, structural, and long-timescale molecular dynamics simulation studies presented here reveal intricate transient interactions dictating binding of RelA, a member of the NF-κB family of transcription factors (TFs), to closely related DNA sequences with varying affinities. The understanding of discriminatory recognition of DNA sites by NF-κB TFs provides clues to why an overexpression of certain members could trigger cancer pathogenesis.


## Results

### Dimers of RelA Bind A- and T-Centric κB Sites with a Higher Affinity Than G- and C-Centric Sites.
  Most NF-κB driven promoters contain κB sites with an A or a T at the central position, although recent studies indicate the existence of G- and C-centric κB DNA sites (17). We examined the binding of RelA homodimer to four κB sites present in the promoters of four well-studied genes: Cxcl10, Nfkbia, Cxcl1, and Ccl2. These κB site sequences differ only in the central position (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C) (24), and were named A-κB, T-κB, G-κB, and C-κB DNA, respectively, based on the identity of the central base (position 0) in the forward stand (Fig. 1A). A luciferase-based transcription reporter assay revealed that RelA-dependent transcription driven by both A- and T-κB sites is significantly higher compared to that by G- and C-κB sites (Fig. 1B). EMSA using TNFα-activated nuclear extracts (containing predominantly p50:RelA heterodimer and a minor proportion of RelA homodimer) indicated that binding of NF-κB dimers to both G- and C-κB DNAs is much weaker than that to A- and T-κB DNAs (Fig. 1C). We also qualitatively assessed the binding affinity of purified full-length RelA (RelAFL) homodimer and p50:RelA heterodimer to all four κB DNAs using EMSA (Fig. 1D and SI Appendix, Fig. S1D). Further detailed analysis by EMSA indicated that RelAFL bound A-κB DNA with a ~sixfold higher affinity than G-κB DNA (116 nM vs. 699 nM) and RelA-RHR bound A-κB DNA with a ~10-fold higher affinity than G-κB DNA (16 nM vs. 167 nM) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1E) (2, 25).

  For an in-depth quantification of binding, we performed biolayer interferometry (BLI) assays with biotin-tagged κB DNAs bound to streptavidin chips. The time courses of association and dissociation of RelAFL to the four κB DNAs were measured at different RelA concentrations (Fig. 1E). The apparent equilibrium constants (KD) for binding of RelA homodimer to the A- and T- κB DNAs calculated from the dependence of the plateau values of the BLI signal on protein concentration were highly similar (KD ~ 25 nM). The apparent binding affinities to the G- and C-κB DNA were also nearly equal (KD ~ 300 nM) but about 10-fold weaker than that to the A- and T-κB DNA sites, tallying with results of earlier SELEX studies (SI Appendix, Table S1a). The association kinetics indicate an apparent single-exponential accumulation of RelA-DNA complexes (SI Appendix, Fig. S1F). However, the dissociation kinetics, performed by transferring the chip from a near-equilibrium state for the association experiment to the binding buffer without protein, are double-exponential. The initial, faster dissociation component is highly evident at and above 50 nM RelA for its complexes with the A- and T- κB DNA, and it is not prominent at low protein concentrations and for less stable complexes. For example, for the RelA complex with T-κB DNA, the population of the rapidly dissociating complexes is indistinct at 12.5 nM and 25 nM protein concentrations, and the dissociation kinetics appear to be single-exponential at these concentrations. Similarly, for weaker RelA complexes with G- and C-κB DNA where only ~30% DNA saturation is reached at the highest protein concentration, the dissociation kinetics appears to follow an apparent single-exponential behavior since the faster component is not prominent. The BLI experiments with the p50:RelA heterodimer also displayed an apparent single-exponential association and double-exponential dissociation kinetics as observed for RelA homodimer (SI Appendix, Fig. S1G). And similarly to the RelA homodimer, the A- and T-κB DNA bound the heterodimer strongly (~25 nM) but binding to the G- and C-κB sites was approximately 8- to 10-fold weaker (~225 nM) (SI Appendix, Table S1b).

  The double-exponential kinetics also reflect the likely binding of a second dimer to a nonspecific site, which is interacting directly or through DNA with the first dimer bound at the specific site. Overall, the data from the binding experiments clearly show a striking effect of the identity of the base pair at the center, which appears to make no direct contact with the protein, on binding affinity.


### Crystal Structures Reveal Nearly Identical Binding Modes of the RelA Homodimer to the A-, T-, G-, and C-κB DNA.
  To elucidate the physico-chemical principles behind the observed differences in binding affinities of RelA homodimer to various κB sites, we crystallized the RelA RHR homodimer bound to the four abovementioned κB sites and determined their structures (Fig. 2A; wwPDB accession codes 9BDU, 9BDV, 9BDW, and 9BDX (26–29)). The global architectures of RelA dimer and DNA duplexes are highly similar in all four complexes (Fig. 2A). Crystals of RelA homodimer bound to the T- and G-κB sites resolved beyond 1.9 Å—thus revealing finer details of the protein–DNA contacts in an NF-κB:DNA complex (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 and Table S2a) (30, 31). The structural models clearly show that two half-sites separated by the central bp within the full κB site are positioned asymmetrically to engage with the DNA-interacting residues of the two RelA protomers (Fig. 2 B and C). The first half site −5G−4G−3G−2A−1A interacts with several residues of the protomer I—R41, R35, and R33 recognizing G bases at positions −5, −4, and −3 of the forward strand, respectively, and Y36 forming van der Waals contact with −2T and −1T of the reverse strand. E39, in addition to stabilizing R33 and R187, contacts −3C of the reverse strand. All base-specific contacts, except for the contact by the R41 of protomer II are preserved in the four bp 1T2T3C4C half-site. R41 of protomer II contacts the phosphate of the DNA backbone instead. It is unclear whether the presence of 5G could enable efficient direct contact with R41 of protomer II due to the asymmetric positioning of RelA protomers on DNA. The interactions of R187 from protomer I and protomer II are also distinct, likely triggered by the asymmetric positioning of P189 in the global dimeric complex; R187 of protomer II (and not protomer I) contacts −2A directly. Side chains of several DBD residues (Y36, C38, S42, K56, K123, and R124), and some from the DD (K218, K221, and R246), contact the phosphates of the DNA backbone, i.e., make non-base-specific contacts. A schematic depicting base-specific and non-base-specific contacts is illustrated in SI Appendix, Fig. S2.

  Overall, the protein–DNA interfaces of all four κB complexes are nearly identical, with stably bound water molecules facilitating several interactions. The RMSD of backbone atoms of protomer I DNA-binding regions (19 to 190) bound to DNA is ~0.87 Å (Fig. 2A), and that of protomer II DNA-binding domains is similar. However, a superimposition of protomer I subcomplex on protomer II subcomplex (e.g., in the T- or the G-κB complex) indicate significant differences (Fig. 2B). A departure of subcomplexes from mirror symmetry in the arrangement of bases around the pseudodyad axis through the central base pair, creates a differential strain in linker region (loop L3, residue range 185 to 193) between the DD and the DBD. Earlier studies indicate that the DD and the DBD are noninteracting, and the DD is highly rigid and symmetric (32). P189 at the domain interface, which appears to interact allosterically with R41 through R187, is positioned differently in the two protomers, potentially distinguishing the two half-site complexes. P189 makes discriminatory van der Waals interactions with the base next to the central pair, and thus the stacking potential of the base pairs could possibly influence its position/interaction. To accommodate both protomers within the dimer around the central bp, P189 of protomer II must translate by about 4 Å, generating a strain that is incorporated through the phosphodeoxyribose backbone of the central and +1 base pair. This strain affects interactions of R187, as it interacts directly with E39 but shifts away to interact with the C=O of F34 in protomer II. These subtle changes in turn influence the K37-S45 loop area, whose flexibility is facilitated by two strategically placed glycine residues (G40 and G44), thereby regulating the positioning of R41. In p50, the flexibility of this loop is dampened by the presence of a structured proline residue (P62). The domain movement also exerts a long-range effect on binding enabling additional protein–DNA contacts, such as K56 interacting with the phosphate backbone (and bending distal DNA toward the protein) in protomer I, while R41 of protomer II pulls the DNA from the opposite direction (Fig. 2B). Overall, a dynamic nature of RelA around the central DNA-binding interface is evident from multiple conformations of P189, R187, N186, and D185, which allow protomer I and protomer II to engage differently. Additionally, R124 of protomers I and II approaching DNA through the minor groove near the central bp cannot make identical contacts. K218 of the protomers appears to make a water-mediated contact with the central bp. These interactions collectively create a distinct asymmetry in the binding, the propensity for which must be dictated naturally by the sequence (stacking between base pairs) all throughout the DNA, including that of the central base pair.

  We analyzed differences in the B-factor values, representing thermal atomic displacements, focusing on T- and G-κB complexes due to their resolution beyond 1.9 Å. Most DNA-contacting residues of protomer I of the T-κB complex display a higher average B factor compared to those in the G-κB complex, except for three residues (E39, R41, and S42) which exhibited a higher B factor in the G-κB complex than the T-κB complex. These residues of protomer II from both complexes displayed similar average B-factor values. Interestingly, the B-factor values of DNA nucleotides indicated increased flexibility around the periphery (−4, −5, and beyond) compared to the central base pairs (56 vs. 14), and a greater flexibility is observed in the protomer I-binding half-site (SI Appendix, Table S2b). Taken together, high-resolution structures of the RelA:κB DNA complexes unveiled intricate details of protein–DNA contacts, shedding light on the sources of asymmetric binding to the two half-sites and revealing distinctive side chain dynamics of the A/T- and G/C-κB DNA complexes.


### Crystal Structures of the Free κB Sites Reveal Their Nearly Identical Structural States.
  We determined crystal structures of all four κB DNA duplexes in the unbound state to observe whether variation of a single bp at the center influenced their structures and how the structure of the free DNA was altered upon its binding to RelA. Crystals were obtained with DNA duplexes of 12-bp length. The crystal structures were determined by the single-wavelength anomalous diffraction method using the anomalous signal of Zn2+ atoms coordinated to the N7 atoms of guanines (33) (Fig. 3A and SI Appendix, Table S3). The structure of the DNAs was determined in the resolution range of 2.15 to 2.5 Å thus enabling a detailed comparison among them and with RelA-bound DNA (wwPDB accession codes 9BDY, 9BDZ, 9BE0, and 9BE1 (34–37)). The diffraction of the A- and T-κB DNA crystals was slightly inferior to that of G- and C-κB DNA crystals, which might be indicative of a higher flexibility of the former DNAs. The last base of each strand was flipped out of base-paring to make lattice contacts. Despite these minor variations, all four DNAs exhibited a high degree of structural similarity (Fig. 3A). Interestingly, the placement of the two sides around a central base was strikingly symmetrical such that it caused a minor packing ambiguity in the crystal lattice. The unit cell contained nine independent, almost identical duplexes stacked in three rows. Three duplexes of one row were positioned in shifted alternate lattices (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A and B). A comparison of the DNA in the free and the RelA-bound states revealed that to be accommodated within the protein–DNA complex where the position of RelA protomers (especially its N-terminal domain) within the dimer is asymmetrical, the DNA needs to be deformed around the central base pair (Fig. 3 B and C). The deformation is not narrowly localized, as exhibited by the values of various DNA parameters in base pair and base pair steps all along the DNA axis. If the Gs at positions −5, −4, and −3 interacting with protomers I are superimposed, the G3, G4, and T5 of the unbound DNA are placed at 3.5, 4.3, and 5.2Å away to be interacting with protomer II. We reasoned that free κB DNA likely adopts various conformations, and RelA dimer must deform and/or capture these target DNAs in the observed deformed state during engagement. The differential dynamics around the central base pair, which are influenced by the differential stacking of the central base pair with neighboring base pairs, likely alter deformation propensity of the DNAs for engaging to RelA that is reflected in binding affinity. Given that flexibility at positions −4/4G and −5/5G was pronounced upon RelA binding, the base-specific interactions mediated by R41/R35/R33 were likely highly dynamic.


### Microsecond MD Simulations of Unbound κB Site DNAs and RelA:κB DNA Complexes.
  The high structural similarity among κB sites described above is suggestive of additional factors underlying discriminatory affinities. To address the likelihood of sequence-specific differential dynamics at the atomic level, we performed all-atom MD simulations of κB DNA sites both in the unbound state and in complexes with the RelA homodimer. Five independent 2 µs simulations were performed for each of the four unbound κB sites, and six independent 6 µs simulations were performed for each of the A- and G-κB DNA:RelA dimer complexes, thus a total simulation time of 112 µs. The backbone RMSDs of the κB sites (10 bp region containing position −5 to +4) simulated in unbound and RelA-bound forms were measured with respect to their corresponding structures determined by crystallography. The unbound κB sites are similar to their respective crystal structures and RMSDs are small (within 2 Å). RelA-bound κB sites are even more similar to the crystal structures and RMSDs are small (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A, panel A). The groove geometry parameters of simulated DNAs in their unbound form indicate mostly symmetrical differences of the half-sites around the center (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A, panel D). The A- and the T-κB DNA sites display narrowing of minor grooves at −4 to +1 and −1 to +4 pairing positions, respectively, indicative of a kink due to the A-tract in the central region. The narrowing of the minor groove and widening of the major groove is observed for both the A- and the G-κB sites upon binding to RelA.


### Discriminatory Major Groove Binding by RelA.
  The overall structures of both G- and A-κB DNA complexes observed in the 6 × 6 µs MD simulations appear to be similar to the crystal structures (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A, panels A–C). To assess whether key protein residues could contribute to differences in binding dynamics, we measured the closest distances between protein residues and nucleotides in pairs and obtained the residue-based importance profiles using two machine learning models, namely, logistic regression and random forest (38) (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). The importance profiles obtained from A- and G-κB models are highly similar, and both identified the large differences in protein–DNA contacts between protomers I and II at the primary region of interaction (position −5 to +4). Residues R33, R124, R187, and K218 on protomer I provide the primary differential contacts for RelA protomers (Fig. 4A).

  The loop L3 of RelA, which connects the N-terminal DBD and C-terminal DD, contains the critical R187 which is projected into the DNA major groove for dynamic interactions and is coupled to R33, R35, and E39. Notably, R187 on protomer I (R187-I) scored highest in importance for differentiating binding patterns to A- and G-κB (Fig. 4A), hinting at a stronger contribution of protomer I in the DNA-binding specificity than protomer II. In A-κB, the guanidino group of R187-I binds predominantly D185-I and, in 14.5% of simulations, binds E39-I and contacts the DNA nucleobases directly; in contrast, in the G-κB, the guanidino group of R187-I predominantly (60.1%) flips to bind the backbone phosphate between 0C and +1A and never interacts with the DNA nucleobases (Fig. 4 B and C). The phosphate binding of R187-I in the G-κB appears to allow R33-I to swing and bind to E39-I in 26.1% of the simulations (Fig. 4 D and E). This R33–E39 interaction repositioned the side chain of E39-I, thus, disrupting the binding of E39-I to R35-I and the base of −3C and promoting the interaction with R33-I. Consequently, this may have weakened the interaction between R33-I and the base of −3G, leading to a decrease in the total number of direct hydrogen bonds formed by R33/R35/E39 with DNA bases, although contact area estimation reflects a slight increase (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C). The different poses of R187-I lead to K218-I interacting with main chain oxygen of R187-I in A-κB and carboxylate of D217-I in G-κB (SI Appendix, Fig. S4D), which were identified by the machine learning models. Overall, the flip and phosphate binding of R187-I recorded in G-κB simulations appear to have weakened the interactions between R33/R35/E39 and the DNA, while the R187-D185 interaction observed in A-κB simulations enables R33–R35–E39 to strengthen their interactions with the flanking G/C base pairs of A-κB.


### Discriminatory Minor Groove Binding by RelA.
  The KKRD sequence patch within loop L2 (residues 122 to 125) contributes R124-I, which interacts with DNA through the minor groove. R124-I displays three distinct binding states in both the A- and G-κB simulations (Fig. 4F). In state 1, R124-I inserts into the minor groove. Notably, the carbonyl oxygens of 0T and +2T in the A-κB is twisted to bind the inserted guanidino group, while the carbonyl of 0C is not, which remains paired with 0G; in state 2, R124-I contacts a backbone phosphate in the reverse-stand DNA; and in state 3, it remains unbound from the DNA. Such insertion of R124-I in state 1 caused increase in area of DNA contact and increased direct hydrogen bonds with DNA bases, thereby strengthening the interactions between the RelA and DNAs (SI Appendix, Fig. S4E). Stabilized by direct binding to DNA nucleobases, the adoption of state 1 by R124-I appears to be four times more likely in the A-κB (27.3%) than in the G-κB (6.5%) (Fig. 4G), possibly underlying a stronger binding affinity of RelA to the A-κB, at least in part. In G-κB, R124-I remains predominantly (67.2%) in state 2 where contacts are made with the backbone phosphate of the cross-strand DNA. It is worth noting that states 2 and 3 of R124-I are the higher populated states in both the A-κB and the G-κB simulations (Fig. 4G). The conformation of R124-I captured in the crystal structures appears to be an average of these two states. Collectively, these observations suggest that the specificity of RelA might arise, at least in part, from a sequence-dependent shift in distribution of protein side-chain conformers, e.g., an increased population of state 1 and a decreased population of state 2 in the A-κB complex, and an opposite trend in the G-κB complex. Such sequence-specific alteration in the population of conformers could contribute to the strengthening of contacts between the RelA homodimer and the A-κB while weakening those between RelA and the G-κB.


### DNA Binding by the RelA Mutants.
  To explore the potential influence of differential electronic environments in the major groove and hydrogen-bonding potential in the minor groove on the disparate binding affinities, arising from variations in the central base pair, we performed experiments with κB DNA duplexes where A and G of the central A:T and G:C were substituted with inosine. The substitution to I:T replaced N6 of A with O6 and that to I:C eliminated N2 of G and a minor groove H-bond (Fig. 5A). The affinity to I:T κB DNA was reduced approximately fivefold compared to A:T, while the affinity to I:C κB DNA was increased about 1.3-fold compared to G:C (Fig. 5B). These findings suggest that variations in the central base pair induce subtle structural changes, influencing the dynamics of protein–DNA interactions and thereby modulating the binding affinity. Furthermore, we investigated the binding affinities of RelA mutants for A:T and G:C κB DNAs. Considering the ability of arginine and lysine to mediate distinct interaction, we explored whether swapping RelA R124 with a lysine could alter discriminatory specificity of RelA (39). The homodimer of p52, where the R124 counterpart is a lysine (K143), displayed similar affinities for A/T- and G/C-centric κB DNAs (12). We replaced the sequence patch KKRD of RelA (containing R124) with the corresponding region TKKN of p52 to test differences in selectivity. Additionally, we generated R187K and R187K, TKKN mutants of RelA. The DNA-binding activities were tested by EMSA with nuclear extracts isolated from TNFα-treated HEK293 cells transfected with wild-type (WT) and mutant RelA expression plasmids. We observed a modest increase in the binding of G- and C-centric κB DNA to the RelAFL-TKKN mutant compared to the WT (Fig. 5C). In contrast, the binding to the A- and T-κB DNAs was reduced with the mutant. Although a partial gain in the stability of the RelA-TKKN:G/C-DNA complex is observed, the resulting stability is still significantly lower than that of the RelA-TKKN:A/T-DNA complex. The affinities of the R187K mutant to all κB DNAs were weaker than those with WT RelAFL. The R187K, TKKN double mutant was also severely defective in binding any of the κB DNAs. Together, this information suggests a detrimental role of G/C or C/G bp at the center on the conformational and electronic environment of the DNA such that it cannot be overcome by the base-specific contacts at the DNA periphery. We also tested reporter gene expression potential of the RelA TKKN mutant on A- and G-centric κB DNA. We observed that the mutation reduced the reporter gene expression from not only the A-centric site but also the G-centric site (SI Appendix, Fig. S5).

  To explore the effects of lysine substitutions, we generated models of R124K, R187K and R124K/R187K mutants of RelA using the A-κB-bound crystal structure and conducted four 3 µs MD simulations on each of these systems (Fig. 6). The mutation of three-state R124 (Fig. 4F) to lysine results in a loss of its direct interactions to the base of 0A on A-κB (Fig. 6 A and B). However, a partial insertion of K124 is observed in 9.5% of the simulations, with the lysine side chain hydrogen bonding with the backbone phosphate. This might explain the slightly enhanced affinity of TKKN mutant to G-κB, with lysine-mediated bond partially compensating for the absence of R124 insertion in G-κB. Interestingly, despite R187 being distant from R124, the R187K mutation considerably decreases the insertion of R124 into the minor groove (Fig. 6 A and B). Conversely, the R124K mutation suppresses direct interactions of R187 to the major groove compared to the WT RelA (Fig. 6 C and D). These observations might be indicative of a coupling between interactions in the minor and major grooves.

  In the R187K, flexibility and dispersed poses of lysine will perturb normally observed interactions in the DNA major groove e.g., R187-D185. In the R124K, R187K double mutant, both the minor- and major-groove interactions will be perturbed, resulting in severe disruption to the RelA binding, as observed in the mutagenesis assays (Fig. 5C). Notably, in R187K or R124K/R187K double mutant, an increased population of direct K187-DNA base interaction compared to the WT RelA (~28% vs. 14.5%) was observed. The flexibility of lysine sidechains could provide interaction promiscuity, which could possibly generate steric hindrance to other interactions such as binding of R33-I and E39-I to DNA bases (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A), leading to an overall instability in the major groove.


### Differential Selectivity of κB DNAs by RelA and cRel.
  Since residues R124 and R187 appeared to be critical in DNA-binding and MD simulations revealed their multiple states, we explored their possible contributions to a discriminatory selection of κB sites by NF-κB family members. RelA is unique among NF-κB subunits, having arginine residues at both 124 and 187, while cRel has an arginine at 187 and a lysine at 124. Since RelA and cRel are reported to have unique gene specificities, we compared their site selectivity and DNA-binding affinity. We plotted Z-scores vs. ranked motifs reported in a protein binding microarray of homodimers of RelA and cRel along with the homodimer of a RelA mutant (residues 103 to 185 swapped with the corresponding part of cRel) and the p50:RelA heterodimer (5). The analysis revealed a much steeper decline in Z-scores of binding for RelA than cRel. Interestingly, RelA mutant behaved closer to cRel indicating its broader selectivity. The slope was even steeper for p50:RelA heterodimer, indicating its stricter sequence selection (Fig. 7A). These findings align with a previous report of promiscuity of cRel homodimer in binding to κB sites (40). To probe discriminatory binding properties of cRelA and RelA homodimers, we filtered sequence motifs that fit to the NGGNNNNNCC pattern of canonical κB sites, where N is any nucleotide. In this targeted comparison, we further selected 185 top-scoring motifs (Z-scores of 7 to 14 for cRel) to reduce experimental artifacts in data of weak binding sites and sorted these based on the decreasing order of the positive score differential between cRel and RelA. The Z-score cutoff above 14.0 eliminates the very high-affinity binding sites of cRelA and RelA homodimers that include identical sequences (e.g., GGGAAATTCC and GGGAATTTCC). Of the 185 sequences, those with a Z-score differential of 4.0 and above, 61% (73/119) contained at least one G:C base pair within the central 3 positions (−1, 0, and +1). In contrast, for sites with a Z-score differential below 4.0 only 29% (19/66) contained one G:C base pair (Fig. 7B). This is suggestive of the easier ability of cRel to bind κB sites with a G:C bp in the central three positions (Fig. 7C). Overexpression of cRel is associated with cancer and autoimmune disorders, likely due to sustained activation of inflammatory cytokines and enrichment of immunosuppressive regulatory T cells (41–43). T cell (Treg) development is reported to be through the regulation of the FoxP3 TF by the cRel homodimer (44). The κB sites present in the enhancer and promoter regions of FoxP3 and the cytokine IL-12 are rich in G:C bp in the central region (45). A study by Sanjabi et al. reported a preference of cRel for G/C-rich sequences at the central region (46) and identified the role of a segment containing the minor groove binding residue K113 in cRel (R124 in RelA). A swap of this segment into RelA, changed its DNA binding property akin to cRel. A crystal structure of the cRelA homodimer bound to the CD28 response element of the IL-2 promoter is available (47); however, the absence of MD simulations data does not allow comparison of its dynamics to RelA. These findings suggest that binding interactions at the minor groove could provide sequence selectivity to NF-κB dimers. Overall, our results along with published reports suggest that a minor difference in interfacial residues (or distal residues controlling these interfacial residues) may have provided a significantly enhanced promiscuity to cRel for κB sites, leading to the expression of unwanted genes and unregulated growth, development, and proliferation.


## Discussion

### Long Timescale MD Simulations of 75 kDa RelA:κB DNA Complexes.
  How DNA-binding TFs regulate transcription differentially through binding to seemingly similar DNA sequences has remained unclear. The complexity appears deeper when we consider different transcriptional outcomes upon binding of an identical DNA sequence by different members of a TF family with a high sequence conservation (21). In this study, we explored underlying mechanistic possibilities in the context of RelA, a member of the NF-κB family TFs, binding to four cognate κB sites differing by just a single bp at the center. This bp does not contact RelA directly. We observed that the dimer of full-length RelA binds to the A- and T-centric κB sites significantly better than the G- and C-centric κB sites. The reason behind this observation is unclear. The crystal structures provide possible clues, such as a higher B-factor of protein side chains engaging to the central region of T-κB DNA compared to G-κB. Perhaps a greater dynamicity of A- and T-κB DNA confers its facile adaptability to bind RelA, and conversely, a higher rigidity of G- and C-κB DNAs confers constraints (48). However, for the most part, the static structures do not inform about the intricate structural fluctuations occurring in either the protein or DNA during the binding process. Prior MD simulation studies have revealed details of atomistic dynamicity during protein–DNA recognition processes (49–59). To investigate whether a difference in structural flexibility among NF-κB-DNA complexes could lead to affinity discrimination, we performed MD simulations of several microsecond duration on ~75 kDa RelA-κB DNA (A- and G-centric) complexes, multiple complexes of RelA mutants bound to A-κB DNA, and unbound κB DNAs. These simulations yielded insights into sensing of subtle differences in DNA by RelA residues, and provided a likely explanation for the differential binding. Among these, R187, a major groove associated residue in the central region, is the most critical. It communicates directly with the R33–R35–E39 triad in the major groove at the edges of κB site and indirectly with R124 in the minor groove at the center via the DNA. In complex with G-κB, R187-I binds predominantly to the backbone phosphate and avoids interactions with the DNA nucleobases. This binding mode destabilizes interactions between R35 and interacting DNA base in the flanking region. In the A-κB complex, R187-I engages with D185 allowing the R33–R35–E39 triad to occupy optimal positions for making base contacts. In this complex, R124-I frequently inserts into the minor groove for a base contact, a phenomenon highly infrequent in the G-κB complex. The concerted changes observed in MD simulations of the R124K and R187K single and double mutants also reflect a communication between the minor and groove binding residues. These differences in interactions may explain the stark difference in the affinity of RelA for the A-κB vs. G-κB. As anticipated from interaction transiency/flexibility of R187 and R124, the R to K mutations (both within single and double mutants) displayed altered binding. Interestingly, the RelA R124K mutant exhibited a slight improvement in affinity for G/C-κB DNAs compared to RelA-WT hinting at the detrimental effect of even a small fraction of R124 inserting into the minor groove of the G-κB, possibly due to a charge repulsion. Our experiments also support the delocalized positive charge of the guanidino group of arginine allowing its frequent insertion into the negatively charged minor groove of DNA, as reported before (60). A longer timescale and further improved simulation studies would likely reveal additional interaction possibilities, justifying the role of apparently noninteracting bp in influencing structure of the entire κB sequence to regulate binding. A recent NMR study on µs–ms transitions in DNA interacting loop residues supports this possibility (32).


### Transcriptional Activity of Diverse Natural κB Sites.
  The promoter/enhancer regions contain not only strong κB sites, but they are also littered with weak κB sites or even half-sites. The role of these sites in transcription is unclear. The reporter-based assay revealed that the weak κB sites rendered little transcriptional activity on their own, and a slight increase in their binding affinity did not enhance the reporter activity significantly. We recently observed an essential role of these weak sites in transcription in vivo (in preparation). These sites may have evolved 1) to assist recruitment of NF-κB dimers to strong κB sites in promoter, and 2) to recruit other TFs that collaboratively regulate transcription with NF-κB dimers, thus expanding the regulatory spectrum. The sequences of κB sites do overlap with binding sites of TFs from other families such as NFAT (nuclear factor of activated T cells) and STAT (signal transducer and activators of transcription) (61, 62). Critical roles of weak binding sites of TFs are well documented in Drosophila and vertebrate development (63–66). It has been observed that variation of a single nucleotide with a slight change in affinity could be highly detrimental. However, the selection and recruitment i.e., equilibrium occupancy of TFs to these weak targets in vivo is not clear, which is likely determined by binding kinetics, dynamics, and placement of neighboring sites.

  The biochemical, structural, and computational studies presented here set the groundwork for understanding the interaction dynamics of NF-κB dimers in selecting κB DNA sites. The understanding of discriminatory recognition of DNA sites by TFs bridges the gap in our knowledge of correlating DNA binding to transcriptional regulation. Furthermore, this work could contribute to unraveling how the regulatory scope of NF-κB factors is broadened through posttranslational modifications or their interactions with other cofactors.


## Materials and Methods
  Detailed procedures are provided in “SI Appendix.”


### Luciferase Reporter Assay.
  CMXTK-Luciferase reporter plasmids under κB DNA promoter elements were transiently transfected along with HA-RelA (1-551) or empty HA-vector in HEK293 cells using PEI. Renilla luciferase expression plasmid was also cotransfected as an internal control. To compare transcription efficiencies of reporter plasmids, luciferase activities were measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega catalog # E1910).


### Protein Expression and Purification.
  Untagged Mouse RelA RHR (19-304) and His-tagged mouse RelA RHR (19-304; wild-type and mutant versions) were expressed in Escherichia coli using pET3a expression vector for crystallization and electrophoretic mobility shift assays, respectively. His-tagged full-length RelA (1-551) for in vitro assays was expressed in Spodoptera frugiperda Sf9 insect cells using baculoviral expression vector pFastBac-HTa. To obtain p50:RelA heterodimer, E. coli expressed His-tagged p50(1-435) was incubated with His-tagged RelA(1-551) in a 1:1 molar ratio at an approximate final concentration of 1.5 micromolar in buffer containing 25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 250 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM DTT.


### Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assay.
  Recombinant RelA homodimer or p50:RelA heterodimer was incubated with radiolabeled DNA duplexes (SI Appendix, Table S1c) at RT for 15 min in binding buffer containing 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 50 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 1% NP-40, 1 mM EDTA, and 0.1 mg/mL sonicated salmon sperm DNA (as a nonspecific competitor). Complexes were resolved in a 5% nondenaturing polyacrylamide gel by electrophoresis for quantitation.


### BLI Assay.
  DNA duplexes (SI Appendix, Table S1c) with one strand biotinylated were immobilized onto hydrated Octet Streptavidin (SA) biosensors (Sartorius) for BLI assays using the Octet K2 system (Sartorius AG). The single-exponential accumulation RelA-DNA complexes at various concentrations of recombinant His-RelA(1-551) or p50:RelA were assessed for 120 s (or longer) for association kinetics. The double-exponential decay of complex in buffer was observed for 180 s (or longer) for dissociation kinetics. The best-fit values and the respective best-fit curves of kinetics are shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S1F (described in detail in SI Appendix).


### Crystallization of κB DNAs and RelA:κB Complexes.
  The κB DNA (SI Appendix, Table S1c) crystals were formed by hanging drop vapor diffusion method at 18 °C. DNAs at a concentration of 0.5 mM were mixed with a reservoir solution containing 100 mM sodium acetate-HCl pH 3.75, 8% PEG3350, and 50 mM zinc acetate in a 1:1 ratio. For cryoprotection, crystals were soaked in a buffer containing the mother liquor and 20% ethylene glycol and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. For crystallization of RelA:κB-DNA complexes, untagged RelA(19-304) at a final concentration of ~150 µM was mixed with κB-DNA at a 1:1.2 molar ratio in buffer containing 25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 50 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT followed by incubation at room temperature for 15 min. Crystals were formed by the hanging drop vapor diffusion method at 18 °C by mixing complexes with reservoir solution containing 100 mM MES pH 5.5, 2 mM calcium chloride, 50 mM ammonium chloride, 14% PEG3350, 1 mM spermine, and 0.05% octyl β-D-glucopyranoside in a 1:1 ratio. Crystals of the complexes were cryoprotected by soaking in buffer containing the mother liquor without DTT supplemented with either 16% glycerol or 21% ethylene glycol and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen.


### MD Simulations.
  Free-form structures of A-, T-, G-, and C-κB DNAs were extracted from corresponding crystal structures of RelA-κB complexes. Simulation systems for A- and G-κB DNAs in complex with RelA were constructed from their corresponding complex crystal structures. Structures were solvated by TIP3P water in a dodecahedron box with a margin of 12 Å and neutralized by 0.15 M concentration of Na+ and Cl− ions. GROMACS 2021.7 package (67) and AMBER ff19SB force field (68) with OL15 parameters for DNAs (69) were used in all simulations. Five 2 µs simulations were performed for each free κB DNAs; six 6 µs simulations were performed for each wild-type RelA-bound DNA complex; four 3 µs simulations were performed for each RelA mutant-bound A-κB DNA complex. MD results were analyzed using integrated trajectories from all replicas of each simulation system, and the centroid structures of top clusters are used to represent the typical structures of DNAs and RelA residues.


### Key Residue Screening by Machine Learning.
  The key-residue screening protocol is based on the machine learning models from ref. 38. Machine learning via the logistic regression and random forest models were used to identify key residues distinguishing the binding of A- vs. G-κB DNAs to RelA. The minimum heavy-atom distance from each residue of RelA dimer to each DNA nucleotide on the 5+1+4 κB sites was measured in pairs from RelA-DNA complex simulations (A-κB-RelA and G-κB-RelA). The pairwise distances were then inverted and normalized to render a distribution with a mean of 0 and a SD of 1. Next, the matrix of correlation coefficients for pairwise distances was calculated and only the upper triangular part was retained. The importance profile (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C) was obtained by summing the importance of pairwise features for each residue and normalized to be ranging from 0 to 1.
