# Significance of Zn2+ in RyR1 for Structural
Integrity and Ligand Binding: Insight from Molecular Dynamics


## Abstract
  

  Ryanodine receptor type 1 (RyR1) is a Ca2+-release channel central to skeletal muscle excitation–contraction (EC) coupling. RyR1’s cryo-EM structures reveal a zinc-finger motif positioned within the cytoplasmic C-terminal domain (CTD). Yet, owing to limitations in cryo-EM resolution, RyR1 structures lack precision in detailing the metal coordination structure, prompting the need for an accurate model. In this study, we employed molecular dynamics (MD) simulations and the density functional theory (DFT) method to refine the binding characteristics of Zn2+ in the zinc-finger site of the RyR1 channel. Our findings also highlight substantial conformational changes in simulations conducted in the absence of Zn2+. Notably, we observed a loss of contact at the interface between protein domains proximal to the zinc-finger site, indicating a crucial role of Zn2+ in maintaining structural integrity and interdomain interactions within RyR1. Furthermore, this study provides valuable insights into the modulation of ATP, Ca2+, and caffeine binding, shedding light on the intricate relationship between Zn2+ coordination and the dynamic behavior of RyR1. Our integrative approach combining MD simulations and DFT calculations enhances our understanding of the molecular mechanisms governing ligand binding in RyR1.


## Introduction
  Ryanodine receptor type 1 (RyR1) is a calcium-release channel predominantly expressed in skeletal muscle cells.1−3 It holds a pivotal role in regulating calcium signaling and muscle contraction. Other isoforms of RyR1 are found in distinct muscle types, with RyR2 present in cardiac muscles and RyR3 expressed in the brain and other tissues.4,5 The primary function of the RyR1 channel is to release stored calcium ions (Ca2+) from the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) into the cytoplasm of muscle cells. When an electrical signal or action potential propagates along the muscle cell membrane and reaches the specialized region known as the triad, where the transverse tubules make contact with the SR, it triggers the opening of RyR1 channels. This, in turn, causes the efflux of calcium ions from the SR into the cytoplasm. This release of calcium through RyR1 is a crucial event in the process known as excitation–contraction (EC) coupling. Calcium signaling via RyR1 is tightly controlled by various factors, including action potentials, intracellular calcium levels, and the binding of modulators such as adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and magnesium. Dysregulation or mutations in RyR1 can perturb the usual calcium-release process, potentially resulting in irregularities in calcium handling. These disruptions have been associated with various skeletal muscle disorders, including malignant hyperthermia (MH), central core disease (CCD), and specific forms of muscular dystrophy.6−8

  RyR1 is one of the largest known ion channels, boasting an impressive mass of approximately 2.2 MDa. Its structure comprises four identical subunits arranged in a symmetrical tetrameric configuration, forming a large cytoplasmic domain and a transmembrane domain (Figure 1). This cytoplasmic domain encompasses various distinct regions crucial to its function and regulation. Notable domains within the cytoplasmic region of RyR1 encompass the N-terminal domain, SPRY domains, handle domain, helical domain (HD), central domain (CD), and C-terminal domain (CTD). Within this cytoplasmic region, several ligand binding sites have been pinpointed through structural studies. The binding of ligands like calcium, zinc, ATP, and caffeine to their respective sites plays a pivotal role in modulating RyR1 activity, facilitating calcium release, and maintaining cellular calcium homeostasis.9−14 The precise functionalities and interactions of these domains continue to be the focal point of ongoing research in the realms of calcium signaling and muscle physiology.

  The atomic-level elucidation of the 3D structure of RyR1 through cryo-EM provided crucial insights into its closed conformation and identified the Zn2+ binding site at the CTD.16 Building on this foundation, a comprehensive structural analysis of RyR1 incorporated activators such as Ca2+, ATP, and caffeine.9 They elucidated the interactions between the protein and these activators, proposing a molecular mechanism for RyR1’s large conformational rearrangement between the closed and open conformations described earlier.17 Meissner’s team explored the interactions between RyR1 and activators (Ca2+, ATP, and caffeine), shedding light on their roles in the dynamic interplay between the open and closed states of the pore.18,19 Their findings underscored the synergistic activation induced by Ca2+ and ATP, and the enhancing effect of caffeine in orchestrating the calcium-release process through allosteric regulation. Throughout all these studies, the specific contributions of Zn2+ to the protein’s structural dynamics remain unexplored, despite the proximity of this cation to important regulatory sites in the channel, presenting an avenue for further investigation.

  Cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) has been instrumental in unraveling the comprehensive structure of RyR1, offering insights into its architectural intricacies and molecular functionality. Within the tetrameric configuration of RyR1, the cryo-EM structure has exposed a zinc binding site nestled in the cytoplasmic domain of each subunit. In each binding site, Zn2+ is coordinated by two cysteine residues and two histidine residues (Zn(II)Cys2His2), forming a bona fide zinc-finger motif. Zn2+ coordination adheres to specific bond lengths and angles crucial for stabilizing the geometric structure of the zinc-finger motif.

  Zn2+ plays crucial roles in various physiological processes, including immune function, wound healing, DNA synthesis, and cell division.14,20 Despite the subnanomolar concentrations of Zn2+ ions in the cell,21 RyR’s zinc-finger motifs efficiently coordinate zinc ions during folding of the polypeptide chain into RyR’s three-dimensional structure. Mutagenesis studies of two ligand residues within the zinc-finger motif have demonstrated their pivotal role in preserving a stable conformation of the RyR1 channel.22 On the other hand, it has been suggested that Zn2+ exhibits biphasic modulation in skeletal muscles, similar to calcium,23,24 implying the existence of both activation and inactivation sites. Notably, physiological cytosolic concentrations of Zn2+ ranging from 100 pM to 1 μM can increase the open probability of the RyR1 channel, while higher concentrations can inhibit ryanodine binding.24 This dual modulation extends to cardiac muscles, where RyR2 also exhibits zinc concentration dependence, capable of both activating and inhibiting calcium release. Activation likely occurs by binding to the Ca2+-activation site, while the inhibitory effect of Zn2+ may be mediated by the Ca2+/Mg2+-inhibitory site of RyR1. These findings highlight the intricate and environment-dependent regulatory role of zinc in RyR.21,25 In this paper, we focus on the role of zinc fingers as a structural scaffold, promoting the folding and maintenance of the protein’s three-dimensional conformation.26,27 The cryo-EM structures of RyR1 revealed the presence of a zinc finger within the C-terminal domain.16 Despite this observation, there exists a limited research exploring the roles of zinc in RyR.

  The limited resolution and static representation of cryo-EM structures of RyR1 make it challenging to precisely understand its gating mechanism, the role of activators (ATP, Ca2+, and caffeine), and regulatory interactions at the atomic and temporal levels. To address this, we have utilized MD simulations and the density functional theory (DFT) method to refine the representation of the zinc binding site and its coordination geometry within the RyR1 protein. This approach aims to complement experimental cryo-EM data by providing a more accurate depiction of the metal binding site, thereby enhancing our understanding of the protein structure and function. Furthermore, MD simulations unveil the impact of Zn2+ on the modulation of activator binding in RyR1. By examining the fluctuations in the protein structure over time, we gained a comprehensive perspective on the conformational dynamics associated with the presence and absence of zinc ions. These findings contribute valuable information for elucidating the intricate interplay between the zinc finger and the functional dynamics of RyR1.


## Computational Methods

### Structure Model
  The initial template model was derived from the cryo-EM structure of the RyR1 channel, resolved at a resolution of 4.4 Å (PDB ID: 5T9V).9 We have strategically utilized a low-resolution structure of the protein to serve as the starting point for refinement. This deliberate choice enables us to highlight the efficacy of our methodology in enhancing the structural definition, with a specific focus on elucidating the coordination structure of the zinc finger within the protein. Given that the RyR1 protein is exceptionally large, comprising multiple domains, the majority of which are not directly associated with the zinc binding site, we chose to focus on specific regions of the RyR1 channel for our study rather than analyzing the entire structure. This enables us to concentrate on the domain surrounding the zinc binding site and helps in reducing computational demands. In this study, the starting model for MD simulations comprised three distinct regions from the cytoplasmic domain: the central domain and U-motif (residues 3667–4253), the S2–S3 loop (residues 4666–4786), and the C-terminal domain, encompassing the C-terminal S6 (S6c) helical segment (residues 4938–5037), as shown in Figure 2a. This combined entity is referred to as CDL23CTD for simplicity. Each domain selection was based on discernible structural features and functional annotations present in the cryo-EM structure.2,9,28 Truncated residues within each domain were selected to ensure the inclusion of essential structural motifs and key functional regions. Notably, the inclusion of different domains (or part of domains) of the CDL23CTD structure was due to their proximity to the Ca2+, ATP, and caffeine binding site, as identified in the cryo-EM structure. The C-terminal domain houses critical amino acid residues involved in the Zn2+ and ATP recognition sites.


### Preparation for the MD
Systems
  The starting model of the selected domains obtained from the cryo-EM data revealed an unresolved region (residues 3741–3747) in the protein structure. To address this, we utilized the ModLoop server, which employs a loop-modeling routine in MODELER to predict plausible loop conformations by satisfying spatial restraints.29 Subsequently, the generated model of the unresolved region was integrated into the initial protein structure, resulting in a comprehensive starting model. Next, to address the absence of hydrogen atoms in the protein structure, the psfgen module in VMD (visual molecular dynamics) software30 was employed using CHARMM36 amino acid topology.31 This process systematically added the missing hydrogen atoms to achieve a more accurate and complete representation of the protein structure. The protonation states of ionizable residues (Lys, Arg, Asp, and Glu) at neutral pH were assigned through pKa prediction using the PROPKA software.32 Following this, the protein was immersed in a simulation box filled with TIP3P water molecules.33 The overall charge of the systems was neutralized by adding counterions and maintaining a salt concentration of 0.1 M NaCl using VMD’s Autoionize plugin.30


### MD simulations
  MD simulations of all systems were conducted using the NAMD software.34 The CHARMM36 all-atom force-field parameters were utilized to describe the interactions and behavior of the protein, ATP, and the metal ions.31,35−38 Parameters for caffeine atoms were generated with the CHARMM topology and generalized force field using the CHARMM-GUI Web server.39 The TIP3P force-field parameters accounted for interactions and properties of water molecules in the simulation systems.33 Rigid bonds to hydrogen atoms were maintained using the LINCS algorithm, while the SETTLE method constrained water geometry as rigid.40 A distance cutoff of 12 Å governed electrostatic and van der Waals interactions, with particle mesh Ewald summation for electrostatic calculations via fast Fourier transform.41 The Langevin thermostat, with a damping coefficient of 1 ps–1 was used to maintain a constant temperature of 300 K. Pressure was held constant at 1 atm using the Nosé-Hoover Langevin piston method.42 Energy minimization was performed to eliminate any bad contacts between atoms. Two equilibration steps prepared the system for production MD simulations. In the first step, the protein, ATP, Ca2+, and Zn2+ remained fixed, while water molecules and counterions underwent positional adjustments to form a stable solvation shell around the protein. In the subsequent equilibration step, the entire system, including the protein, water molecules, and counterions, was allowed to move freely. Protein atoms, excluding terminal residues (4783–4786 and 4938–4941) of the truncated structure, were gradually released from restrained positions to achieve a stable state of thermal and dynamic equilibrium. Restraining terminal residues prevented aberrant conformational changes, ensuring the conformation of the truncated protein structure remained representative of the native state. Simulations were carried out for 100 ns in three independent runs to enhance statistical significance and reproducibility.

  We conducted MD simulations on various structural models containing modified residues crucial to the coordination structure of the [Zn(II)Cys2His2] complex in RyR1. Specifically, we altered the side-chain-state configuration for histidine residues 4978 and 4983 as well as for cysteine residues 4958 and 4961 (Figure 1b). In simulation system I–IV, we employed the residue identifiers HSE, HSD, CYS, and CYM in accordance with the CHARMM topology (Figure 2b). HSD and HSE denote the histidine residue with the hydrogen atom placed at the delta and epsilon positions of the imidazole ring of the histidine side chain, respectively, representing the tautomeric forms of histidine residues. Similarly, CYS and CYM refer to the cysteine residue, where the side chain is in the neutral form (thiol, –SH) and the negatively charged form (thiolate, –S−), respectively. Additionally, MD simulations of system V were conducted on the structural model without the zinc ion, enabling us to investigate the effects and contributions of the metal ion to the protein’s structure and dynamics. Lastly, MD simulations were conducted on the high-resolution crystal structure of a zinc finger-DNA-binding protein (Zif268) to establish a baseline for comparison. Table 1 provides a summary of the model systems employed for MD simulations.


### Trajectory Analysis
  MD trajectory analyses such as the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF), hydrogen bond (H-bond), geometry of the complex, secondary structure content, and hydration structure were performed using in-house TCL scripts in VMD. The RMSD value was calculated by aligning the protein backbone atoms of residues within 15 Å of the metal center (residues 4958–4983) from each snapshot in the MD trajectory with the initial reference structure. The deviation in atomic positions between the aligned structures and the reference was then measured. To illustrate the dynamic behavior of individual residues in the protein structure, RMSF per residue was computed with respect to the average structure. To identify hydrogen bonding interactions in the system, we introduced the distance and angle criteria. The distance between the donor and acceptor heteroatoms is within a cutoff 3.5 Å, and the angle formed by the donor, hydrogen, and acceptor atoms falls within a range of 120°–180°. The structural geometry of the zinc-coordination complex was determined by analyzing the bond lengths and bond angles involving the metal and ligand atoms. Analysis of secondary structure content was achieved using DSSP (dictionary of protein secondary structure) algorithms via a TCL script in VMD. Unless otherwise specified, MD trajectories were extracted from the production phases during the last 20 ns of simulations.


### Binding Energy of the [Zn(II)Cys2His2]
Complex by DFT Calculations
  We utilized the density functional theory (DFT) method to evaluate the findings obtained from MD simulations. For this assessment, the initial model of the complex, consisting of only Zn2+ and two cysteine and two histidine residues, was derived from an MD snapshot of system II. Subsequently, we adjusted the side-chain configurations for histidine and cysteine residues of the [Zn(II)Cys2His2] complex in the configurations defined in systems I–IV for MD simulations. This procedure yielded the structural coordinates of the Zn2+ complex, as presented in Table 1, for DFT computations. Geometry optimization using the PM3 method was then performed on all constructed models. All computations were performed using the Gaussian16 software package.43 Convergence was achieved when the maximum force acting on any atom fell below 0.00045 hartree/Bohr. The maximum allowed displacement was set at 0.0018 Å. Following geometry optimization, single-point calculations were conducted on the optimized structures using the DFT method at the B3LYP level and a 6-31+G(d,p) basis set to determine the binding energy of both complex and isolated systems. Grimme’s dispersion correction (DFT-D3)44 was also incorporated to account for van der Waals interactions and enhance the accuracy of the binding-energy calculations. The CPCM model was employed to account for the solvent effect.45 The binding energies (ΔEbind) were derived using the following equationwhere Ecomplex is the energy of the [Zn(II)Cys2His2] complex, and Eligand and EZn(II) are the energies of the isolated system Cys2His2 and the Zn2+ ion, respectively. The total energies of the complex and isolated systems were computed separately.


## Results

### Optimal Binding Characteristics
of the [Zn(II)Cys2His2] Complex in RyR1

#### Ion Displacement and Backbone RMSD
  The stability of Zn2+ in the zinc-finger-binding site was assessed through ion-displacement analysis. Notably, Zn2+ displacements in systems I and IV were significantly large (Figure 3a), exceeding 10 Å by the end of the simulations, indicating Zn2+ instability. Both [Zn(CYS)2(HSE)2]2+ and [Zn(CYS)2(HSD)2]2+ configurations failed to retain the metal in the binding site. In contrast, ion displacements in systems II and III were relatively small, showing fluctuations of magnitude similar to that of Zif268. This suggested that the [Zn(CYM)2(HSD)2] and [Zn(CYM)2(HSE)2] configurations effectively maintained the stability of Zn2+ in the binding site.

  The RMSD of the protein backbone at the zinc-finger region, as depicted in Figures 3b and S1, provides crucial insights into the impact of types of residue ligands on structural changes within the metal binding sites. Initially, the RMSD curve exhibited an upward trend as the structure adjusted to simulation conditions. Notably, in system II, the curve levels off, reaching a plateau with minor fluctuations as the simulation progresses, indicating inherent flexibility in the protein within the model system over the simulation duration. In contrast, systems I, III, IV, and V exhibited higher RMSD values, signifying greater structural variability. The results suggested that [Zn(CYM)2(HSD)2] offers the most suitable configuration for accommodating the Zn2+ ion with minimal structure perturbation.


#### Evaluation of Bond Geometry
  In zinc-finger structures, typical bond lengths are around 2.3–2.5 Å for Zn–S(Cys) and 2.0–2.2 Å for Zn–N(His), with bond angles approximately spanning 90–120° for S(Cys)–Zn–S(Cys), S(Cys)–Zn–N(His), and N(His)–Zn–N(His). The distance profiles of Zn–S and Zn–N bonds over time offer valuable insights into the structure analysis of the zinc complex. In system II ([Zn(CYM)2(HSD)2]), the Zn2+ demonstrated favorable coordination with cysteine and histidine residues, presenting bond distances of approximately 2.5 Å for Zn–S and 2.2 Å for Zn–Nε (Figure 4b). These bond distances, falling within the typical range for zinc-finger complexes, remained stable throughout the simulations. Additionally, the Zn–Nδ bond distance appeared to be greater than that of Zn–Nε, suggesting a preference for Zn2+ in RyR1 to form a coordination bond with the ε-nitrogen rather than the δ-nitrogen of the imidazole ring of the histidine ligands. In contrast, a notable divergence in Zn–S and Zn–N bond distances was observed in systems I, III, and IV (Figure 4a,c,d, respectively). This observation suggests that Zn2+ is incapable of establishing stable bonds with two cysteine and two histidine ligands. The average distance and its standard deviation for Zn–S and Zn–N across all systems are summarized in Table 2. Consequently, the results suggested that the negatively charged cysteine (CYM) and the delta histidine (HSD) configuration are the most suitable forms for establishing coordination bonds at the zinc-binding site in the RyR1 protein.

  In the case of system III ([Zn(CYM)2(HSE)2]), the Zn2+ displayed a tendency to disengage from the binding site, indicating that the side-chain configuration of the ligands in system III is not appropriate to maintaining Zn2+ in place. Detailed analysis revealed that the distances between Zn and S at 2.62 and 2.68 Å fell within an acceptable range for Zn2+-coordination bonds (Table 2), suggesting that the anionic cysteine is more favorable in complex formation compared to the neutral cysteine. This indicated that Zn2+ forms more stable coordination with the thiolate group compared to the thiol group of the cysteine ligand. However, longer distances between Zn and N imply a cause for the loss of the coordination bond in the zinc-finger complex. The disruption of the Zn–N bonds resulted from significant conformational changes of the histidine residues. The results suggested that Zn2+ does not form a coordination bond with Nδ of the histidine side chain.

  Crystallographic and computational studies consistently reveal that Zn-finger structures adopt a tetrahedral shape, with the Zn2+-metal center forming coordination bonds with the specific side-chain state of residue ligands, mainly cysteine and histidine.46−49 In our study, manipulating the side-chain states of cysteine and histidine ligands allowed us to identify residue side-chain configurations compatible with stable metal-ion coordination. As depicted in Figure 3, it is evident that Zn2+ in systems I ([Zn(CYS)2(HSE)2]) and IV ([Zn(CYS)2(HSD)2]) were unable to maintain their positions at the binding site, resulting in a significant conformational shift in that location. This observed geometric distortion of the zinc-finger structure is supported by the data in Table 2 and Figure S2, highlighting significant deviations in bond distances and angles in these cases. One major factor contributing to the disruption of coordination between Zn2+ and histidine or cysteine ligands was primarily attributed to unfavorable interactions with the thiol group of the neutral cysteine.

  It is worth emphasizing that throughout the investigation, the configuration of system II, [Zn(CYM)2(HSD)2], emerged as the most likely form of the zinc-finger structure. Despite this, an intrusion of water molecules into the first coordination structure of Zn2+ was observed (system II in Figure 5). This interference led to a notable deviation of bond angles from the expected tetrahedral structure, with values ranging between 90° and 100° for ∠S1–Zn–S2, ∠S1–Zn–NE1, ∠S1–Zn–NE2, ∠S2–Zn–NE1, ∠S2–Zn–NE2, and ≈170° for ∠NE1–Zn–NE2 (Figure 4e). However, to ensure the accurate geometry of the zinc complex in simulations, we conducted additional MD simulations (system IIbond). This involved constraining the bonds between Zn2+ and the ligand atoms: Zn–S(Cys) and Zn–Nε(His), through the “patch residue” command in a VMD TCL script. This patching bond process involved modifications to the CHARMM topology and force field parameters associated with the newly introduced bond in the zinc complex.50 The simulation results of system IIbond ([Zn(CYM)2(HSD)2]bond) led to bond lengths of 2.34 ± 0.04 Å for Zn–S and 2.02 ± 0.04 Å for Zn–Nε and bond angles that closely approached an ideal tetrahedral geometry (109.5°, Figure 4e). Furthermore, it exhibited minimal impact on the protein structure, secondary structure, and the region surrounding the Zn2+-binding site. It preserved an accurate coordination geometry to ensure the stability of Zn2+ in the binding site, with no observed intrusion of water molecules into the coordination sphere of Zn2+ (Figures S1–S4). Based on these MD results, we proposed that the zinc-finger configuration, where the Zn2+ forms two bonds with the ε-nitrogen of histidine and another two bonds with the anionic cysteine, accurately represents the most suitable state of the zinc-finger complex in RyR1. The implementation of bond constraints in the MD simulation effectively refined the model of the zinc-finger complex within the context of RyR1’s structure. This refinement addresses the observed geometric distortions and provides a more accurate representation of the coordination geometry in the simulated system.


#### DFT-Derived Binding Energy
  To complement the MD results described above, an investigation into the structure of zinc finger complexed with different side-chain states of histidine and cysteine ligands was conducted using an alternative approach. The structural coordinates, encompassing Zn2+ and two histidine and two cysteine ligands, were extracted from the simulation snapshot of system II and utilized as the initial model. This model initially exhibited a poorly defined geometry for the zinc-finger complex, as described in the preceding section. This model transformed into a tetrahedral shape through geometry optimization using the PM3 semiempirical method. The convergence achieved through this quantum calculation method was confirmed, and the observed tetrahedral shape closely matched the results from MD simulations of system IIbond (Table 3). This alignment provides robust support for the conclusion that Zn2+ in RyR1 strongly coordinates to the Nε of histidine ligands and the –S– of cysteine residues, resulting in the formation of a stable tetrahedral complex.

  Following optimization, the refined structure served as the initial model for modifying the side-chain states of cysteine and histidine ligands. This process generated the zinc-finger complex that replicated the configurations designed for conducting MD simulations in systems I–IV, as described earlier (Table 1). Subsequently, each configuration underwent geometric optimization at the same level of theory. Notably, the results indicated that the geometry optimization of configuration I failed to converge, while that of configuration III resulted in an incorrect geometry for the Zn2+ complex, suggesting its unfavorable zinc-finger configuration (Table 3). Conversely, both optimized structures of configurations II and IV displayed a tetrahedral coordination geometry, as evidenced by the bond lengths and bond angles between the Zn2+ ion and ligand atoms of the two histidine and cysteine residues (Table 3). However, the ΔEbind of configuration II was found to be lower than that of configuration IV, suggesting a stronger binding energy. Consequently, the [Zn(CYM)2(HSD)2] complex was determined to be more stable than the [Zn(CYS)2(HSD)2]2+ complex.

  The comparison of ΔEbind with and without Grimme’s dispersion corrections reveals interesting insights into the stability of the zinc complexes. For configuration II, the inclusion of Grimme’s dispersion corrections yields improved binding energy (i.e., a more negative value) compared to standard DFT. This indicates dispersion interactions further enhance the stability of the [Zn(CYM)2(HSD)2] complex. Similarly, for the [Zn(CYS)2(HSD)2]2+ complex, incorporating DFT-D3 leads to a better binding energy, accurately capturing dispersion interactions in this system. These findings are consistent with previous studies, indicating that DFT-D3 can enhance the binding energies of metal complexes with bioorganic ligands by improving both inter- and intramolecular interactions, thereby achieving a balanced contribution of exchange–repulsion, electrostatics, and dispersion.44,51 Despite these differences in binding energy values with and without dispersion corrections, both scenarios exhibit the same trend in binding affinity, with the binding energy of configuration II remaining significantly higher (i.e., more negative) than that of configuration IV. This suggests that electrostatic interactions between oppositely charged species (Zn2+ and two charged cysteines) in the [Zn(CYM)2(HSD)2] complex contribute more to the binding energy compared to those between Zn2+ and two cysteines in the [Zn(CYS)2(HSD)2]2+ complex.


### Impact of Zn2+ on the ATP Binding
Site
  Pairwise residue contact maps derived from MD simulations provide a visual representation of interactions between amino acid residues within protein domains, offering insights into the interdomain interactions and conformational changes occurring within the protein. In this analysis, the maps depicting the pairwise distances between the residue center of masses were generated from the simulations in the presence (system IIbond) and absence (system V) of Zn2+ in the zinc binding domain. The examination primarily focused on the U-motif, CTD, and S6c domains, particularly the ATP binding site proximal to the zinc-finger region. Figure 6 illustrates the residue contacts between the U-motif and CTD. Examining the contact maps of U-motif/CTD and U-motif/S6c of the MD in the presence of Zn2+ revealed a pattern consistent with the cryo-EM structure of the open RyR1 (Figure S5). Notably, in the presence of Zn2+, the residues 4178–4184 of the U-motif engage in interactions with the residues 4980–4992 of the CTD (Figure 6a), particularly, the hydrogen bonding of H4983/E4182, N4987/R4180, and Y4988/E4182. However, the absence of Zn2+ led to the change in the U-motif/CTD interactions, resulting in disappearance of these hydrogen bond interactions. Moreover, in our MD simulations conducted in the absence of Zn2+, a peculiar displacement of the S6c helix was observed (Figure 6c), presenting a noticeable deviation from the simulations with Zn2+. This distinct movement resulted in a consequential alteration in the residue contacts between S6c and the U-motif (Figure 6b). The conformational distortion of the S6 helix was notably linked to the disappearance of hydrogen-bonding interactions, both among protein domains and between ATP and the residue on the S6c segment. The absence of Zn2+ induced structural changes, particularly in the S6c helix, underscoring the role of Zn2+ in stabilizing the conformational dynamics and intermolecular interactions within the protein. The difference in the interdomain contacts occurred near the zinc-finger region, emphasizing the crucial role of the Zn2+ in maintaining structural integrity and interdomain interactions within RyR1.

  ATP has been identified as an activator of RyR1,12 with cryo-EM structures revealing two ATP binding pockets. The first is situated at the interface of the U-motif, CTD, and S6c segment,9,52 while the second is located on the SPRY domain.53 Establishing the binding geometry and hydrogen-bond interactions between ATP and RyR1 has proven challenging. Cryo-EM structures of the RyR1–ATP complex reveal diverse interactions based on RyR1 conformational states (open and closed), ATP analogues, and the buffer conditions in which the cryo-EM samples were prepared; such interactions can be observed with higher precision in the better-resolved structures. Modifications to ATP molecules also influence the binding affinity and hydrogen-bond patterns compared to native ATP.

  Native ATP carries an overall negative charge of −4, distributed as −2, −1, and −1 for γ-(PG), β-(PB), and α-phosphate (PA), respectively. Interactions with RyR1 are predominantly governed by these phosphate groups, influencing the channel-opening activities in the order of ATP > ADP > AMP > adenosine ≈ adenine.54,55 In the presence of Zn2+, MD simulations demonstrated robust hydrogen bonds between the phosphate groups of ATP and K4211, K4214, and R4215 (Figures 7a and S6), mirroring cryo-EM structures of open RyR1 activated by Ca2+, caffeine, and ATP (e.g., 5T9V, 7M6L).9,52 Specifically, the γ-phosphate forms strong hydrogen bonds with the amino groups of K4211 and K4214, as well as the guanidinium group of R4215, approaching 100% occupancies. In contrast, MD simulations of RyR1 without Zn2+ exhibited only two hydrogen bonds from K4211 and K4214 with the γ-phosphate. At β- and α-phosphates, hydrogen bonds observed in the presence of Zn2+ significantly decreased compared to the γ-phosphate due to charge density or reactivity, aligning with native ATP chemistry. Simulations in the absence of Zn2+ alters the ATP binding pocket’s geometry, with reduced interactions with the γ-phosphate, and an attempt to maintain ATP stability by increasing hydrogen bonds at the core phosphate (Figure 7a,b). Consequently, the total numbers of interactions at phosphate groups follow the order PG > PB > PA for MD with Zn2+ and PG ≈ PB ≈ PA for MD without Zn2+.

  The interactions involving the ribose moiety of ATP with RyR1 demand careful consideration, particularly given the challenges posed by the limited resolution of cryo-EM structures. In numerous structures, hydrogen bonds at the ribose site often go undetected. However, our MD analysis revealed a robust hydrogen bond between the hydroxyl group (O2′H) of ribose and the carbonyl oxygen from M4954 in the S6c segment, with an approximately 90% occupancy (Figure 7b). Notably, this observation aligns with a cryo-EM structure (7M6L), capturing the open state with full-ligand activation.52 On the contrary, in the closed state, ribose establishes a hydrogen bond with T4979, as evidenced in a cryo-EM structure (8SEO).55 Our simulations without Zn2+ reveal that ATP lacks its interaction at the ribose site (Figure 7b), resulting in abnormalities in U-motif/S6c interactions and a misalignment of S6c segment (Figure 6b,c). In contrast, the S6c segment from MD simulations with Zn2+ maintains an appropriate configuration. This underscores the significance of the ribose/M4954 hydrogen bond in preserving the orientation of the S6c segment in the open state of RyR1.

  The interactions at the adenine site of ATP present intriguing dynamics. Cryo-EM structures of RyR1 revealed that in closed or inactivated states, adenine facilitates the amino group of N6 to form a hydrogen bond with H4983.9,28,52,55 Conversely, it has been suggested that in the open state, adenine predominantly participates in hydrophobic interactions.9,18,52 Our MD results with Zn2+ similarly demonstrate a preference for a hydrophobic cleft involving F4217, I4218, V4221, and V4222 from the U-motif, M4954 of the S6c segment, and F4959 and L4985 of the CTD. Therefore, the Zn-finger plays a crucial role in maintaining the U-motif/CTD interaction and the shape of the ATP binding pocket.

  Comparisons of distance profiles between adenine and amino acids forming the hydrophobic cleft from cryo-EM structures (Figure S6) suggested that the presence of Zn2+ can sustain the adenine-binding pocket in the open state. In contrast, the absence of Zn2+ led to high fluctuation in adenine interactions. Superimposed structures (Figure 7c) potentially propose that native ATP exhibited two binding modes with RyR1 during the activation process. The first mode represents the initiation of activation, where the adenine group of ATP formed a hydrogen bond with H4983 and the ribose formed a hydrogen bond with T4979 in the closed state without strong interactions with the S6c segment. Mutation T4979 results in the failure of this binding mode of ATP.56 After RyR1 activates, inducing a structural rearrangement in the ATP binding site, ATP switches to the second binding mode. In this mode, ATP maintains an open conformation, enabling adenine to switch its interaction to the hydrophobic cleft, and ribose changes the location of hydrogen bonding to M4954, preserving the direction of the S6c segment in the open state. Figure 7c,d indicates that the geometry of ATP in the absence of Zn in MD simulations is similar to the closed state. Consequently, ATP plays a role in enhancing the interactions between the U-motif, S6c, and CTD, allowing them to move together during channel activation based on the conformation supported by the zinc finger.


### Impact of Zn2+ on the Caffeine-Binding
Site
  Caffeine, as an exogenous ligand, facilitates the release of calcium from the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) by activating RyR1,57−59 with its binding site situated at the convergence of four subdomains: CD, CTD, U-motif, and the S2−S3 loop domain.9 I4996 from CTD plays a pivotal role in this interaction, directly residing on the caffeine binding site and establishing contact with caffeine. The proximity of I4996 to H4983, a Zn2+ ligand residue, suggests that the loss of interdomain contact between the U-motif and CTD could potentially impact caffeine stability. Analysis of residue contact maps obtained from MD simulations in the absence of Zn2+ reveals a diminished interaction between CD and the S2−S3 loop compared to its presence (Figures 8a and S7). Notably, the interaction between W4716 and F3753 is lost. In MD simulations with Zn2+, caffeine demonstrates direct hydrophobic interactions with F3753 from CD, I4996 from CTD, and engages in π–π stacking with the indole ring of W4716 from the S2−S3 loop (Figure 8b). Additional amino acid residues, including E4239, I4242, F4243, and Q4246 from the U-motif, also interact with caffeine. A significant disruption of the interaction between caffeine and I4996 is observed in MD simulations without Zn2+ (Figure S8a). Ultimately, the fluctuating interaction with F3753, the sole amino acid from CD, may impact the conformation and direction of the central domain, potentially influencing the Ca2+ binding site due to the proximity of T5001 to I4996.

  Another crucial residue pair contact within the CD/S2−S3 loop involves the formation of a salt bridge between E3757 and K4723 (Figure 8b), positioned in close proximity to F3753. The mutation K4723Q has been linked to central core disease (CCD), as it disrupts the E3757/K4723 interaction, leading to abnormal CD/S2−S3 loop connections.60,61 The effect of this disruption has been shown by MD simulations in the absence of Zn2+, where a weakened interaction between E3757 and K4723 is observed, which we attribute to the highly fluctuating location of F3753 (Figure S8a). Consequently, the presence of Zn2+ in the CTD-binding pocket of RyR1 plays a crucial role in maintaining the proper orientation of the CTD. This orientation is essential for the correct binding geometry of caffeine and stability of the CDs. This facilitates interdomain interactions between the CD and the S2−S3 loop. Our MD simulations reveal a pivotal role of Zn2+ in maintaining the stability of the caffeine-binding site in RyR1.


### Impact
of Zn2+ on the Ca2+-Binding Site
  Calcium plays a crucial role as an activator for opening the RyR1 channel,62 with its activation sites located at the interface between CD and CTD.9,28,52 Upon binding of Ca2+ at the activation site, it serves as an initiator, enhancing the interaction between CD and CTD. Subsequently, CTD moves toward CD during the activation process. The interactions among the U-motif, S6c, and CTD, facilitated by ATP lead to the displacement of the S6c segments, thereby enlarging the pore through direct mechanical force. In our comprehensive analysis of the residue contact map between CD and CTD, we sought to elucidate the influence of Zn2+ on the Ca2+-binding site through the interactions between CD and CTD. Our investigation revealed a compelling and significant impact of Zn2+ at the Ca2+-binding site, illustrated by a clear distinction in residue contact patterns between conditions with and without Zn2+. Specifically, in the absence of Zn2+, we observed a significant and substantial loss of contacts in five distinct regions compared to the state in the presence of Zn2+ (Figures 9a and S8b,c). These regions encompassed the caffeine-binding site (region 1), the space between caffeine and Ca2+-binding sites (region 2), the Ca2+ activation sites (regions 3 and 4), and the region proximity to E4032 (region 5). MD simulations in the absence of Zn2+ revealed a disruption in connections essential for maintaining the structural integrity at the Ca2+-binding pocket, in contrast to MD simulations with Zn2+ present in the binding site. Notably, in the presence of Zn2+, Ca2+ located at the CD/CTD interface formed ionic interactions with E3893, E3967, T5001, and Q3970, involving carboxylate side chains and carbonyl backbones (Figure 9b). These interactions align with the known structures of RyR1 in the open activated state (Figure S9).18,28,52 The Zn2+ also prevented conformational changes in residues within the Ca2+-binding pocket. This ensured the maintenance of electrostatic interactions between Ca2+ and T5001, thereby preventing the expansion of the gap at region 2 (between G3807 and T5001) and preserving the strength of CD–CTD interactions. This intricate interplay between Zn2+ and the domains sheds light on the dynamic structural adjustments that underlie the functional regulation of the protein.


## Discussion
  Our study highlights the efficacy of our computational approach in refining the less well-defined regions of the metal-binding site in protein structures. Specifically, our focus lies on enhancing the coordination structure of Zn2+ within the zinc finger of RyR1. This deliberate choice of a lower-resolution starting point allows us to emphasize the robustness of our methodology in improving the accuracy and clarity of the structural details, especially in the context of metal coordination within RyR1’s zinc-finger domain. By beginning with a less well-defined structure, we aim to highlight the robustness of our computational refinement process in accurately capturing the intricate details of the metal–amino acid interactions. This approach not only emphasizes the versatility of our methodology but also underscores its potential applicability in addressing challenging scenarios where structural information is limited.

  The evaluation of Zn2+ stability within the zinc-finger-binding site of RyR1 presented here uncovers the critical role played by the state of specific side chain of residue ligands involved in the coordination of Zn2+. The comprehensive structural analysis extended to examining the RMSD of the protein backbone, ion displacement, and the profiles of bond distances and bond angles within the tetrahedral geometry of the Zn2+ complex. The [Zn(CYM)2(HSD)2] configuration, wherein Zn2+ coordinates with the thiolate groups of cysteine and the Nε-imidazole rings of histidine, emerges as the most favorable arrangement. This configuration exhibited stability in ion displacement, lower RMSD values, and consistent bond distances and angles. Conversely, alternative forms of histidine and cysteine ligands yielded configurations with more substantial structural perturbations and significant distortion in the metal complex, indicating that such forms would not be conducive to proper coordination of Zn2+. Moreover, the alignment of results from the DFT approach and MD simulations further strengthens the robustness of the findings regarding the stability and binding energy of the [Zn(CYM)2(HSD)2] complex. The agreement between these two independent computational methods enhances the confidence in the accuracy and reliability of the drawn conclusions. These results substantiate the conclusion that the [Zn(CYM)2(HSD)2] configuration within the context of the protein’s structure offers an optimal arrangement for accommodating Zn2+.

  The RyR channel is inherently large. To manage computational costs effectively, we made deliberate choices, focusing our study on the relevant regions surrounding the zinc-finger motif of the channel. This decision allowed us to explore specific interactions and conformational changes relevant to our research question. However, we acknowledge that this approach may have limitations, particularly regarding the functional relevance of observed conformational changes. Our strategy involved isolating domains from the larger RyR structure and imposing constraints on chain terminal residues to maintain system stability. Nevertheless, we recognize the potential for these constraints to influence system behavior and possibly magnify disruptions observed in the presence and absence of Zn2+. To address this issue, we conducted additional analyses of RMSF per residue to validate the stability of the isolated structure. These analyses provide further insight into the structural dynamics of the domains and help assess the impact of constraints and isolation on the observed disruptions. As illustrated in Figure S10, the RMSF profiles of the individual domains exhibit good agreement with the reported conformational changes detailed in the Results section. Notably, domains lacking Zn2+ (−Zn) consistently display greater fluctuations compared to those with Zn2+ present (+Zn). Despite initial concerns about the impact of applied constraints on terminal residues, the RMSF analyses consistently reveal coherent behavior across all individual domains, encompassing the CD, U-motif, S2−S3 loop, S6c, and CTD. The observed differences in RMSF between simulations with and without Zn2+ highlight the role of Zn2+ in modulating the structural dynamics of these domains. Furthermore, the resemblance in fluctuation patterns suggests that while the presence of Zn2+ influences the magnitude of fluctuations, it does not significantly alter the overall conformational dynamics of the system.

  The comparison of the zinc-binding site between our studies and high-resolution X-ray crystallography of various zinc-finger proteins reveals strong agreement, as evidenced by Table 4. This convergence highlights the reliability of our computational approach in capturing key structural features observed experimentally. While our computational approach provides a detailed molecular-level understanding of zinc-binding dynamics, high-resolution crystallography offers precise structural information. This comparative analysis enriches our understanding of zinc-finger proteins’ structural biology.

  It is worth noting that the Nε of the histidine side chain is more commonly involved in metal coordination in proteins due to its greater accessibility and reactivity.63,64 While the Nδ nitrogen of histidine is less common than Nε, there are instances where the Nδ of histidine can also form coordination bonds with metals in proteins. Examples of metalloproteins where the Nδ nitrogen of histidine coordinates with metal ions are Zn2+ in carbonic anhydrase and in nitric oxide synthase and Cu2+ in cytochrome c oxidase and in copper–zinc superoxide dismutase. Additionally, in some metalloenzymes and metalloproteins, histidine residues may undergo metal coordination through both the Nε and Nδ nitrogens, forming a bidentate coordination mode. This dual coordination can provide stability to the metal-binding site and influence the reactivity of the metal center within the protein. The introduction of bond constraints in the simulations of the Zn2+ complex within RyR1, particularly in the [Zn(CYM)2(HSD)2] configuration, has yielded insightful observations regarding the coordination geometry of the zinc-finger complex. In simulations where the metal and ligand atoms of amino acid residues did not interact, significant distortions in the coordination geometry were observed. However, the incorporation of bond constraints not only rectified these distortions but also improved the model. The MD results consistently support the pivotal role of bond constraints in refining the model of the zinc-finger complex, providing a more accurate representation of the coordination geometry within the simulated system. This emphasizes the critical importance of exercising precaution in the simulation protocol when dealing with metal–protein complex systems. Specifically, instructing the MD simulation program to preserve the bonds between the metal ion and residue ligand atoms is crucial. This precautionary step serves to prevent bond breakage or ligand detachment, ensuring that the complex remains intact and accurately represents the actual interactions of the metal complex within proteins.

  The series of observations from our detailed analysis sheds light on subtle structural intricacies within the RyR1 protein, particularly on the role of the Zn-finger in maintaining the U-motif/CTD interaction and the overall shape of the ATP binding pocket. The investigation into the pairwise residue contacts between the protein domains in the presence and absence of Zn2+ revealed distinct alterations, notably the loss of contacts in specific regions without the metal ion. The presence of Zn2+ appears to influence the interactions at the adenine site, sustaining the adenine-binding pocket in the open state, while the absence of Zn2+ results in high fluctuations in adenine interactions. The absence of Zn2+ induced conformational distortion, particularly in the S6c region, which is notably linked to the disappearance of hydrogen-bonding interactions between protein domains and ATP on the S6c segment. The comprehensive exploration of ATP interactions and the influence of Zn2+ reveals a dynamic interplay between the ligand and the protein domains during the activation process. The observed structural rearrangements and switch in ATP-binding modes underline the intricate role of ATP in enhancing connections between the U-motif, S6c, and CTD, facilitating their coordinated movement during channel activation. This observation suggests a pivotal role for Zn2+ in stabilizing interdomain interactions and highlights its crucial contribution to the overall structural integrity of the protein. While the current study sheds light on essential structural intricacies and coordination aspects of Zn2+ within RyR1, it is important to recognize certain limitations. The study primarily relies on computational approaches, including molecular dynamics simulations and density functional theory. While these methods provide valuable insights, experimental validation using techniques such as site-directed mutagenesis or spectroscopy could further validate and refine the proposed models. Additionally, the study predominantly focuses on the structural aspects, and future works could incorporate functional assays to elucidate the functional consequences of Zn2+ binding on RyR1 channel activity.


## Conclusions
  Our comprehensive investigation into the impact of Zn2+ on RyR1 provides valuable insights into the intricate interplay between this essential trace element and the protein’s structural and functional dynamics. The detailed analysis of residue contact maps, conformational changes, and coordination aspects has revealed the significance of Zn2+ in maintaining the stability of RyR1, particularly in the context of ATP- and Ca2+-binding sites. The observed loss of contacts and conformational distortions in the absence of Zn2+ underscore its crucial role in preserving the native architecture of RyR1. The utilization of computational approaches, such as molecular dynamics simulations and density functional theory, has offered a platform for exploring these interactions, but experimental validation remains essential for further refinement. This study not only advances our understanding of the role of Zn2+ in RyR1 but also opens avenues for future research, including functional assays and in-depth experimental validations, to unravel the full spectrum for the role of Zn2+ in RyR1 function and regulation.
