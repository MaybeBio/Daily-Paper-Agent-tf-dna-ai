# A finely balanced order–disorder equilibrium sculpts the folding–binding landscape of an antibiotic sequestering protein


## Significance
  Deciphering the molecular mechanisms underlying multidrug resistance (MDR) in bacteria is crucial for developing novel therapeutic interventions. One such mechanism is the sequestration of antibiotics via protein-based sensors. Here, combining experiments, simulations, and statistical modeling, we highlight the presence of a highly conserved sensory mechanism involving an interplay between ordered and disordered subdomains in an antibiotic-sequestering protein, TipAS, from Streptomyces lividans. Our results showcase how TipAS exists on the verge of both order and disorder, enabling it to sample multiple conformations which facilitate the binding to thiopeptide-antibiotics. The conservation of order–disorder equilibrium in TipAS-like proteins in bacteria that infect humans opens up the possibility of targeting these proteins to control bacterial infections.


## Materials and Methods
  Detailed methods for TipAS purification, equilibrium and time-resolved spectroscopy including NMR, scanning calorimetry, small angle X-ray scattering measurements and modeling, thiostrepton binding experiments, statistical mechanical model predictions, and MD simulations are provided in SI Appendix, Materials and Methods.


## Results

### Global and Local Probes Reveal Stark Differences in the Unfolding Cooperativity.
  The far-UV circular dichroism (CD) spectra of TipAS display features of a helical protein, with the characteristic minima at 208 and 222 nm (Fig. 1C). Near-UV CD is typically employed to support or rule out the existence of molten-globule-like conformations (15). The near-UV CD spectra spanning the physiological range are indicative of a well-folded protein (Fig. 1D). Specifically, the fine spectral features of aromatic residues are evident in wavelength range 275 to 290 nm (Fig. 1D). The intensity of the near-UV CD spectrum, however, decreases strongly in the temperature range 278 to 308 K (Fig. 1D). Since TipAS harbors two tryptophan residues (W30 and W51) and seven tyrosine residues, the precise structural origins of these spectral changes are difficult to pinpoint via this technique.

  The thermal unfolding profile as monitored by far-UV CD at 222 nm presents a characteristic sigmoidal unfolding profile of a two-state-like system (Fig. 2A). The pretransition region, on the other hand, exhibits a steep temperature-dependence suggesting that there are distinct structural changes in the native ensemble at low temperatures (<310 K). Similarly, the near-UV CD monitored thermal melts have a noticeably steeper pretransition (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). It is unusual to observe strong temperature-dependence in the near-UV CD pretransition region for a two-state system, unless these are associated with structural changes. One reason for this is the presence of aromatic residues W30 and W51 in the N-terminal subdomain (NTSD; residues 1 to 51) previously determined to be disordered. However, if the residues in the NTSD are indeed fully disordered even at the lowest temperatures, such structural changes are not expected. In fact, the fluorescence emission maximum of the tryptophan residues at 278 K is 340 nm, revealing that one or both the tryptophan residues are fully or partially buried in the NTSD (Fig. 2B). On temperature increase, the emission maximum reaches ~349 nm suggesting complete solvent exposure, and by extension full unfolding.

  Interestingly, the shape of the unfolding curve monitored via fluorescence emission maxima and far-UV CD at 222 nm do not match, with the former displaying a steeper pretransition and broader unfolding. This is apparent in the comparisons of enthalpy of unfolding at midpoint (ΔHm; a measure of unfolding cooperativity or the apparent sharpness of the transition) and pretransition slopes from two-state model fits (Fig. 2 C and D). The unfolding observed via tryptophan fluorescence is ~37% less cooperative than that from far- or near-UV CD (Fig. 2C), with a pretransition slope which is 30% steeper (Fig. 2D), emphasizing the thermodynamically weakly cooperative melting of the NTSD. To further confirm this observation, we generated the mutants W30A and W51A and monitored their thermal unfolding. If the NTSD is less stable, the W30 should display a longer emission wavelength which is exactly what we observed (Fig. 2E), despite only small stability perturbations (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). Similarly, broad unfolding profiles with no pretransition regions can also be observed in urea melts (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C) showcasing that the TipAS structure is at the verge of disorder. Finally, 1H-15N HSQC spectra of TipAS at 278 K (blue) and 308 K (orange) are shown in Fig. 2F. The spectrum at 278 K is well resolved in both 1H and 15N dimensions, indicating that TipAS is folded at this temperature. In contrast, a number of broad overlapped resonances appear in the random coil region between 7.8 and 8.4 ppm at 308 K, signifying the presence of heterogeneity and partial unfolding of the TipAS ensemble at 308 K.


### The Conformational Landscape of TipAS.
  To obtain a probe-independent view of the unfolding behavior, we measured the absolute heat capacity profile of TipAS from the concentration dependence of apparent heat capacities (Fig. 3A; see Materials and Methods and SI Appendix, Fig. S2). The absolute heat capacity values at low temperatures approach the empirical baseline of folded proteins proposed by Freire and coworkers (16), and this agreement further confirms that TipAS is well-folded at temperatures <290 K. The calorimetry profile of TipAS is distinctly asymmetric around the peak heat capacity temperature, unlike a two-state unfolding system. This asymmetry is more pronounced at lower temperatures, i.e., <305 K, that constitute the pretransition region in far-, near-UV CD, and fluorescence experiments (Fig. 2). Finally, a two-state model fit to the calorimetry profile results in the crossing of folded and unfolded baselines, thus ruling out a two-state unfolding mechanism (SI Appendix, Fig. S2).

  A quantitative picture of the underlying conformational landscape is obtained by characterizing the calorimetry profile by the statistical mechanical block Wako–Saitô–Muñoz–Eaton (bWSME) model (17, 18) that considers an ensemble of 1,702,915 microstates (Materials and Methods). The model predicts the NTSD to be intrinsically unstable with the residues 1 to 15 corresponding to helix 1 already disordered at 278 K (Fig. 3B). An increase in temperature promotes progressive unfolding of the NTSD (278 to 298 K) following which the C-terminal subdomain (CTSD) unfolds (>305 K). Fig. 3B plots only the population averages, but a more detailed view of the unfolding mechanism can be generated by projecting the residue folding probability of the conformations onto the number of structured blocks, which is a natural reaction coordinate (RC) of the bWSME model. NTSD is fully folded only at very high values of the RC (Fig. 3C), and as one moves to the left more residues in CTSD unfold, following which the central fourth helix corresponding to residues 50 to 65 unfold (at very low RC values).

  The one-dimensional free energy profile at 278 K points to a broad native ensemble defined by a collection of substates with different degrees of folding spread over 45 to 65 structured blocks (Fig. 3D). The most probable state at 58 structured blocks accounts for nearly 125 folded residues. The ensemble shifts to the left at 298 K with the NTSD exhibiting a higher degree of disorder spanning 40 to 60 structured blocks. Similarly, at the melting temperature, the folded ensemble is not the same as the one at 278 K, but is significantly heterogeneous spanning a range of RC values. The folding free energy surface employing two coordinates showcases a similarly broad native ensemble encompassing varied degrees of order in the NTSD (Fig. 3F). An interesting feature of the TipAS conformational landscape is that the macrostate at ~64 structured blocks with all the helices folded (F) appears as an excited state over a barrier of 10 kJ mol−1 even at 278 K, and which increases to ~20 kJ mol−1 at 315 K (Fig. 3G). The fully folded state is only weakly stable with a population at most 2% at 278 K that goes down to zero at 310 K. Taken together with the other spectroscopic probes, we establish that TipAS is nearly fully folded at low temperatures (<290 K), while increasingly sampling partially structured states associated with NTSD that unfolds first, followed by the unfolding of the CTSD (residues 52 to 135).


### Temperature-Driven Equilibrium between Binding-Competent and Incompetent States.
  At a macroscopic level, the conformational space of TipAS can be split into three substates: the folded-like ensemble N termed the binding incompetent state as binding site is occluded, partially structured ensemble N* in which the NTSD exhibits different degrees of structure (binding-competent state as the binding site residues are accessible), and the unfolded state U (Fig. 4A). Each of the three substates should display a unique temperature dependence on (de)stabilization as discussed below. Consider two starting ensembles, one which is more stable than the other, represented by dashed and continuous lines in Fig. 4A. On subjecting the less stable ensemble (dashed line in Fig. 4A) to temperature perturbation, the population of N decreases continuously while that of N* increases, reaches a maximum, and decreases further (Fig. 4B). The population of U, however, follows a sigmoidal profile with temperature. The more stable ensemble (continuous line in Fig. 4A) reveals a similar dependence but shifted to the right on the temperature axis.

  To test this feature in TipAS, we monitor the environment and conformational properties of tryptophan residues, W30 and W51 (Fig. 1A), through time-resolved fluorescence experiments. The life-time traces shorten with increasing temperatures suggestive of faster de-excitation by virtue of competing nonradiative processes at higher temperatures (Fig. 4C). The fluorescence traces were best fit by triexponential functions resulting in three lifetimes (τ1, τ2, and τ3). The longer lifetime, τ1, reports on the features of folded-like rotameric states or backbone conformations (or both) of one or both of the tryptophan residues (Fig. 4D; see below). The shortest lifetime, τ3, reports on the environment around tryptophan residues in unfolded-like conformations. The corresponding amplitudes of τ1 and τ3 follow the trends expected of N and U populations, respectively (filled circles Fig. 4E). The intermediate life-time (τ2) which also exhibits a marginal temperature dependence, on the other hand, likely reports on conformational status of tryptophan residue(s) that are neither fully folded or fully unfolded. The amplitude of this lifetime exhibits a bell-shaped curve peaking at ~305 K, and by comparison with the predictions from the bWSME model (Fig. 4B), this life-time reports on the state N*. If this is indeed the case, then stabilization of the native ensemble should shift the curves to the right along the temperature axis. This is exactly what is observed when TipAS is stabilized in the presence of 5% DMSO (conditions chosen for thiostrepton binding experiments)—the shape of the curves are maintained but are merely shifted to the right, except for the amplitude of N* that not only shifts to the right but also has a marginally higher amplitude (open circles in Fig. 4E), as expected for a state with the properties of N* (Fig. 4B). We thus demonstrate the presence of a pre-equilibrium between the binding-competent (N*) and binding-incompetent (N) state, which is summarized in Fig. 4F.


### NMR Confirms the Presence of Multiple Substates in the Native Ensemble.
  Tryptophan indole side-chain resonances are well-separated from other backbone resonances in a 1H-15N HSQC spectrum and we exploited this to monitor conformational diversity in TipAS. W51 is located at the C-terminal end of the NTSD and at the N terminus of helix 4, which is highly structured as per the bWSME model predictions. W30, however, is located in helix 2 (in the NTSD) which is conditionally disordered (i.e., temperature dependent). Excluding degeneracies in chemical shifts, we therefore expect a maximum of six indole side-chain resonances in TipAS, one each for W30 and W51 in the N, N*, and U states. In accordance with this expectation, the 1H-15N HSQC-detected temperature titration of TipAS shows multiple resonances in the indole region as a function of temperature (Fig. 5).

  There are two indole side-chain peaks at the highest temperature of 318 K at 1HN chemical shifts of 10.03 and 10.06 ppm. These are assigned to W30 and W51 in the unfolded state, respectively (W30U and W51U in Fig. 5A, green), based on the spectra of W30A and W51A TipAS. At the lowest temperature (278 K), there are two prominent resonances which we assign to the binding-incompetent native state N. Using W30A and W51A variants, we assigned the resonances at the 15N chemical shifts of 126.1 ppm and 129.2 ppm to W30N and W51N respectively (Fig. 5A, red). The chemical shifts of W30N in particular (15N: 126.1 ppm and 1HN: 10.7 ppm) are very different from the random coil value (15N: ~129.5 ppm and 1HN: 10.2 ppm) (19, 20), indicating that this resonance does not belong to the unfolded state.

  Among the four peaks from the N and U substates, only the peak intensities of W30N and W51U can be independently quantified at all temperatures, as W30U and W51N overlap with each other during the titration. The intensity of W30N increases with temperature till ~298 K and then decreases, with the peak completely disappearing at 318 K (Fig. 5 A, B, and D). The bWSME model, however, predicts that the substate N decreases with temperature between 278 and 318 K (Fig. 4B). In order to determine the possible cause for this discrepancy, we measured 15N R2 for the nonoverlapped Trp indole sidechain resonances from various substates of TipAS at 293 K (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). R2 values are sensitive to global tumbling as well as to microsecond-millisecond (µs-ms) timescale conformational fluctuations that result in transitions to higher free-energy conformations. The R2 for W30N is significantly higher than R2s for W30N*, W51N*, and W51U (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A), suggesting that W30N is broadened because of µs-ms conformational exchange. Such exchange broadening would result in inefficient coherence transfer in a HSQC pulse train and the temperature-dependence of the exchange broadening could result in aberrant intensities as a function of temperature. On the other hand, the intensity of W51U increases monotonically with temperature from 278 to 318 K (Fig. 5 A–D), in line with the bWSME model predictions. This is consistent with W51U having small R2s, indicative of a sidechain in a disordered polypeptide containing significant local mobility and lacking micro-millisecond conformational fluctuations (see SI Appendix, Supporting Text for further discussion).

  In addition to resonances from the N and U substates, there are two other Trp indole sidechain resonances observed at 308 K [(15N ppm, 1HNppm): (128.6, 9.97); (128.9, 10.18)]. Both these resonances are below noise level at 278 K and increase in intensity from 288 to 308 K (Fig. 5 C and D). However, both peaks disappear at 318 K (Fig. 5A, green) indicating that they are not resonances from the unfolded state, but from the binding-competent N* substate. Based on the spectra of W30A and W51A TipAS, we assign the peak at 9.97 ppm to W30N* and the peak at 10.18 ppm to W51N*. Taken together, 1H-15N HSQC spectra attest to the presence of pervasive conformational heterogeneity in TipAS and with the existence of at least three kinetically distinct conformations in exchange with each other. The populations of the three conformations, as inferred from their peak intensities (Fig. 5D), change in broad agreement with results from fluorescence lifetime measurements and bWSME model predictions. The Trp side-chain region of the 1H-15H HSQC also suggests the existence of additional conformational substates belonging to states N and N*. For example, both resonances W30N and W51N* consist of two closely spaced peaks. However, the structural differences between these substates and whether they correspond to static heterogeneity or can interconvert with each other remains unknown.


### Molecular Simulations Highlight the Differential Torsional Mobility of Tryptophan Residues and the Weakly Coupled Nature of NTSD.
  We performed a series of explicit water MD simulation runs at different temperatures (four runs for each temperature) for 2 µs each to enhance the sampling of conformational substates and to extract the behavior of the two tryptophan residues. The Cα RMSD of the protein domains (Fig. 6 A and B) reveals a significant deviation from the native structure at 320 K, with enhanced structural stability of the two central helices (helices 4 and 5) as well as the pronounced fluctuations of helices in the N- and C-terminal area (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). This observation is consistent with the bWSME model predictions that the region between residues 50 to 70 as the most stable.

  The fluctuations of the N-terminal residues are always higher than those corresponding to the C-terminal residues at all the temperatures investigated (Fig. 6 A and B). The N-terminal region shows a bimodal RMSD distribution even at 280 K which becomes broader and more pronounced at 320 K, demonstrating the intrinsic flexibility of these residues. Accordingly, the number of residues in helical conformation decreases more in the N-terminal region than in the C-terminal subdomain (Fig. 6C). The differential thermal response was also investigated by a principal component analysis on the concatenated trajectories at three representative temperatures (280, 320, and 360 K). The projection of the Cα positions on their essential subspace, defined for both by the first two eigenvectors (representing more than 50% of total variance), reveals that the C-terminal region samples similar conformational states (i.e., the observed probability density maxima are close to each other, SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). The N-terminal region explores a different area of the essential subspace at 320 K in comparison to 280 K (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B), further confirming the weakly coupled nature of the NTSD. The fraction of native contacts (Q) follow a similar trend (Fig. 6D and SI Appendix, Figs. S4C and S5C) with the NTSD exhibiting lower Q-values at all simulation temperatures.

  To substantiate the findings of time-resolved fluorescence experiments, we quantified the torsional flexibility of the tryptophan residues via the χ1 dihedral angle. At the lowest temperature of 280 K, the χ1 dihedral of W30 falls primarily in a region centered around 0° with a minor population at −30° (blue in Fig. 6E). At 320 K, the dihedral angle flips primarily to the −60° rotameric state, while at 360 K all the allowed values between −180° and 180° are sampled (Fig. 6 E and F). On the contrary, the dihedral angle of W51 samples a unique range of values at both 280 and 320 K, centered around −75°. The partial unfolding of NTSD is also sensed by W51 as can be observed from the sampling of all allowed dihedral angles at 360 K. The local environment of W30 and W51 residues is further probed by evaluating the fraction of native contacts, Q, over the course of the simulation. While W51 experiences a local native environment up to 320 K, W30 maintains only a fraction of the native contacts highlighting higher mobility. However, at higher temperatures (340, 360 K), W51 has a similar contact density as W30 (SI Appendix, Fig. S5D). Additionally, the integral of the radial distribution function of the distance between the indolyl nitrogen atom and the oxygen atom of the water molecules indicates a major solvent exposure of W30, with respect to W51, at temperatures higher than 300 K (SI Appendix, Fig. S5E). We can thus conclude that both the tryptophan residues are sensitive to the conformational status of the NTSD. Specifically, the longer life-time of τ1 would be a mix of the life-times of W30 and W51 in the folded subensemble N, whose population decreases with temperature. The enhanced torsional mobility of W30 is likely captured by the remaining life-time components τ2 (indicative of N*) and τ3 (U and/or W30 in an unfolded-like environment at lower temperatures). As the temperature increases to 360 K, both the tryptophan residues exhibit large fluctuations and this feature is compatible with the monotonically increasing amplitude of τ3 at higher temperatures.


### The Native Ensemble of TipAS Swells on Temperature Increase from 298 to 313 K.
  To extract the average size of TipAS in solution and to probe overall structural changes in the temperature interval 283 to 313 K, we resorted to small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) measurements (Fig. 7A and SI Appendix, Tables S1 and S2). The P(r) functions (Inset to Fig. 7A) display a significant expansion between 303 and 313 K with a higher probability density for distances above 4 nm. The resulting radius of gyration (Rg) remains near constant at ~2.4 nm below 303 K, following which it rises sharply reaching ~2.7 nm at 313 K (Fig. 7B). The SAXS profiles also show a second linear region in the Guinier plot at higher q, from which the Rg is extracted to be ~1.8 nm (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A–C), which is largely constant with temperature, possibly due to a subportion of the protein which stays compact within this temperature range.

  The Kratky plot representation further highlights that the characteristic maximum of the bell-shaped plot, expected for compact particles, becomes less pronounced with increasing temperatures (Fig. 7F), in agreement with a structural transition involving an increase in the degree of flexibility. The dimensionless Kratky plot also shows that the profiles become closer to exhibiting a plateau behavior (expected for flexible chains) at temperatures above 308 K while the maximum deviates from the position theoretically expected for a fully compact object, (intersection of gray lines in Fig. 7 D and E), even at the lowest temperature.

  The fact that TipAS is not fully compact even before the “transition” (298 to 313 K) can also be arrived at from a comparison between the experimental SAXS profiles and MD simulation ensembles (SI Appendix, Table S3). The simulated ensemble at 300 K, wherein the protein structural fluctuations are rather limited, predicts a SAXS profile with a distinguishable secondary maximum in Kratky plot representation (Fig. 7 E and F). This feature is entirely absent in the experimental data (Fig. 7E). In contrast, the calculated scattering profile from the 320 K ensemble with a partially unfolded NTSD is in line with the experimental profile at 298 K. Simulations at 340 K predicted a further deviation of the SAXS profile from the fully compact form, in agreement with the experimentally observed trend. We also notice that the theoretical profiles obtained from the MD simulations at 320 and 340 K are also slightly shifted from the maximum expected for a compact particle in Kratky plots. Thus, the high temperature MD ensemble that samples a larger conformational space is better at reproducing the room temperature SAXS data.

  These conclusions are further confirmed upon modeling the SAXS data following the ensemble optimization method (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 and Table S4). The agreement is reasonably good only if the NTSD is allowed to be fully flexible or free to change orientation with respect to the CTSD. The size distributions for the optimized pools compared to the random pools show a maximum shifted to smaller Rg values and are overall sharper but exhibit a tail toward larger Rg values. This suggests that the conformational space of TipAS is not as broad as sampled by the random pool approach. As expected, the optimized size distributions to fit the TipAS SAXS data at 283 and 298 K substantially overlap, whereas they are appreciably shifted to larger sizes and have a broader maximum for the 313 K data (SI Appendix, Fig. S7).

  We next ask whether the ensemble dimensions can also be captured by the bWSME model. To do so, we assume a simple linear Rg dependence on the RC, the number of structured blocks, defined by Rg of the folded and unfolded states (Fig. 7G). The temperature dependence is then calculated as RgT=∑RgRCp(RC,T), where p is the temperature-dependent probability distribution (shown for 298 K in Fig. 7G). The Rg of the unfolded state is estimated from Flory’s scaling law (21) (Rg=0.2N3/5 nm where N is the number of residues in the protein) to be 3.9 nm for TipAS. The second parameter is the Rg of the fully folded state (RC value = 1), which is calculated to be 1.54 nm from the PDB file. However, a slightly larger value of 1.8 nm is required to obtain a better agreement with experimental results (Fig. 7H). This is likely a consequence of the unfolded nature of H1 (Fig. 3B and vide infra) and the extra solvation layer that is not considered in the calculation of Rg from unsolvated PDB file. Despite this, the agreement is reasonable with a larger change in ensemble dimensions observable only at temperatures >300 K. This steep dependence arises primarily due to progressive melting of helices H2 and H3 in the NTSD, as the rest of helices remain intact in the temperature range 298 to 313 K (Fig. 7I).


### Thiostrepton-Induced Population Shifts of Substates.
  To explore how the pre-equilibrium between N and N* is perturbed in the presence of TSN, we monitored the changes in fluorescence intensity of TipAS with different concentrations of TSN spanning the nM to μM range. A red shift of the tryptophan fluorescence emission maximum is observed (from ~340 nm to 347 nm) accompanied by strong quenching at TSN concentrations beyond 8 μM, indicative of binding (Fig. 8A). The fluorescence life-time traces accordingly shift to shorter time-scales (Fig. 8B), with the triexponential fits revealing life-time magnitudes comparable to that in the absence of TSN at lower concentrations (Fig. 8C, compare with Fig. 4D at lower temperatures). At higher [TSN], the intrinsic life-times shift to lower values due to fluorescence quenching and the traces approach the instrument response function.

  As more TipAS molecules bind TSN, the population of binding-competent N* state is depleted, leading to an altered equilibrium between N and N*, and with more of N shifting to N*. At sufficiently high concentrations, the ensemble in its entirety is dominated by the bound conformation, as the binding is an irreversible process (14). The proportional depletion of N and N* population is observed in the amplitudes of the longer (τ1) and intermediate life-times (τ2) that drop to near zero (Fig. 8D). The shortest life-time (τ3) under this condition (fixed temperature and increasing concentrations of TSN) reports on the population of the bound conformation, as the fluorescence is quenched when TipAS binds TSN. The amplitude of this component increases with [TSN] and accounts for nearly 90% of the population at 100 μM TSN. Also, note that τ2 and τ3 shift to lower life-times at [TSN]>1 to 5 μM indicating structural changes in the states defined by these life-times.

  The experiments above at a fixed temperature and varying TSN concentrations point to a conformational-selection-like (CS) mechanism of binding, with one of the substates being selected to bind TSN. Nonetheless, deviations from pure CS-mechanism are observed when thermal melts are carried out in the presence of the ligand. Specifically, we accumulated fluorescence life-time traces of TipAS at a protein:ligand molar ratio of 1:1 and at a range of temperatures. The resulting life-times magnitudes decrease for the state N* (Fig. 8E), and this feature is also observed in Fig. 8C. More importantly, the substate N* (amplitude of the phase τ2) is stabilized by 20 K in the presence of TSN (Fig. 8F). This is likely a consequence of partial binding of TSN to N* which in turn lowers the life-time–due to competing nonradiative processes as seen in the bound conformation–of this state and concomitantly stabilizes it (Fig. 8G). Since both the bound and unfolded-like conformations are characterized by similar life-times, the amplitude of this phase starts to increase only beyond 330 K, conditions where both N* and N convert to unfolded-like conformations.


### Disorder Determinants and a Conserved Weakly Stable NTSD Across Orthologs.
  The charge-hydropathy Uversky plot (22) reveals that the sequence of NTSD is characterized by lower mean hydropathy and higher net charge and falls well into the disordered region (Fig. 9A). On the other hand, CTSD sits right at the interface between ordered and disordered regions in the phase diagram. The disorder determinants of the NTSD in TipAS are therefore equivalent to the features exhibited by many other intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs), with low hydrophobicity and higher charged residue content promoting disorder. Additionally, the long fourth helix, that is, the sequential link between the NTSD and CTSD, displays large helical propensity [predicted by AGADIR (23)] indicating that this region could nucleate the folding of TipAS at low temperatures (Fig. 9B). The higher intrinsic stability of this region is also observable in bWSME model residue folding probability predictions and in the lower RMSF in MD simulations. Together, we conclude that the fourth helix acts as a “backbone” nucleating the folding of the regions C-terminal to it, which in turn promotes the folding of NTSD residues only at lower temperatures. At higher temperatures (>300 K), the NTSD is entropically stabilized sampling numerous conformations thus being intrinsically less stable. This feature is partly a consequence of the weak packing between the NTSD and the CTSD—only loop connecting helices 2 and 3, and helix 3 (both in NTSD) interact with segments of helices 5 and 6 (in CTSD). The consequence of these weakly coupled structural features is that the NTSD encompasses a large cavity with a volume of 957 Å3 suitable for binding thiopeptide-based ligands (Fig. 9C).

  To generalize these observations across orthologs, we first predict the structure of TipAS from AlphaFold2 (AF2) via the ColabFold interface (25, 26). The pLDDT scores are generally low for the first three helices (the NTSD; Fig. 9D). As pLDDT scores are related to the degree of disorder (lower scores mean higher disorder tendency) (27), these results are consistent with the experimental observations of disorder in the NTSD. We then proceeded to predict the structure of TipAS from seven organisms, six of which are either found in the human gut or are infectious (SI Appendix, Fig. S8): Bacillus subtilis (found in the gut of humans), Clostridium botulinum (produces the neurotoxin botulinum), Clostridium leptum (found in human feces), Corynebacterium diphtheriae (that causes diphtheria), Listeria monocytogenes (foodborne pathogenic bacterium), Streptococcus pneumoniae (causes pneumonia) and Halobacillus halophilus (marine lactic acid bacteria). The predicted structures show similar overall architecture but with large deviations in the NTSD (Fig. 9E). The pLDDT scores of the orthologs follow a trend similar to TipAS (Fig. 9F) pointing to the fact that the NTSD could potentially be disordered.

  As the final step, we predict the ensemble features of these seven orthologs under isostability conditions of 25 kJ mol−1 at 298 K by employing the bWSME model (Materials and Methods). The resulting free energy profiles exhibit broad native ensembles for the majority of the orthologs and with marginal thermodynamic barriers separating the unfolded and folded states (Fig. 9 G–M). The native ensemble of C. leptum is predicted to be the most heterogeneous, displaying significant probability even for macrostates with just 50% of the residues folded (Fig. 9I). The only profile that displays a sharp native state is that of L. monocytogenes (Fig. 9L), while the partially structured macrostates in H. halophilus appear as excited states (arrow in Fig. 9K). Despite these minor differences, the NTSD is uniformly less stable, or more unfolded, for all the orthologs employed in the current study compared to the CTSD (Fig. 9N). In the extreme case of C. leptum, both the subdomains are partially structured in the native ensemble contributing to the large heterogeneity observed in Fig. 9I.


## Discussion
  We show that the NTSD of TipAS is conditionally folded depending on the prevailing thermodynamic conditions. In other words, TipAS is nearly fully folded at temperatures <290 K (except for H1), with only the NTSD progressively unfolding in the temperature range between 298 and 313 K, following which the other protein regions unfold. The convenient location of tryptophan residues solely in the NTSD enabled us to deconvolute the time-resolved fluorescence signal into distinct life-times and amplitudes. By interpreting these through the lens of NMR, quantitative statistical modeling, and simulations, we show that TipAS is best described to coexist in equilibrium with multiple conformational ensembles, which can be very coarsely partitioned into a native state ensemble (N), a partially structured ensemble N* and an unfolded ensemble U. At 298 K, the population ratio of N:N*:U is 25:70:5 which changes to 5:60:35 at 310 K. Interestingly, the fully folded state F, a substate within the N ensemble is minimally populated even at 298 K (<0.5%) and thus appearing as an excited state on the free-energy landscape. The observation of conformational broadening via NMR at 308 K is suggestive of N* being composed of numerous substates which interconvert in the millisecond timescale, in agreement with earlier NMR studies (13).

  Disordered regions in proteins (IDRs) or IDPs are promiscuous binders presenting different interaction surfaces to their partners (28). The native ensemble is therefore entropically stabilized in absence of the ligand, though transient secondary structures (29–32) and even fully folded state can be populated (33, 34). On binding the ligand, the complex is stabilized via specific interactions (enthalpy dominates over entropic stability) or through a combination of favorable enthalpic and entropic effects [i.e., “fuzzy” complexes (35)]. TipAS is a unique case wherein the NTSD is conditionally disordered and not fully unfolded, while the CTSD is relatively more rigid and folded under physiological conditions. It appears that the CTSD provides the rigidity and interactions with one face of the ligand, and interactions with the other ligand face are acquired by structuring of the NTSD that “folds on binding.” Flexibility in the ligand binding pocket is an attribute of most proteins and is required for “accommodating” the ligand through adjustments of side-chain positions (36, 37). This feature is taken to an extreme in TipAS wherein nearly one-third of the protein folds on binding the antibiotic, thiostrepton or other thiopeptide-based antibiotics.

  One fundamental advantage of being disordered or partially structured is the ability to sample multiple binding-competent conformations in the native ensemble. In fact, extensive dynamics in the NTSD of TipAS has been reported as a piece of evidence for conformational selection mechanism of binding (13). Given the large size of the thiostrepton ligand that nearly occupies the entire cavity, it is unlikely that TSN fits right in when NTSD samples a binding-competent conformation, i.e., after a conformation is “selected” to bind. We observe that the populations of N (binding-incompetent) and N* (binding-competent ensembles) decrease at higher concentrations of TSN, while the population of the bound state concomitantly increases. However, the property of N* (monitored via the intermediate life-time τ2) is altered, and importantly, the corresponding amplitude is stabilized by nearly 20 K in the presence of the ligand. Consequently, this reports on a mechanism wherein the TipAS binds the ligand that stabilizes the N* substate, while it “accesses” the perfect binding pose. The mechanism we report here is similar to that proposed by Tawfik and coworkers on the likely prevalence of such “encounter” complexes, that dissociate slowly and likely aid in not only tight binding, but also in enabling promiscuity (38).

  From a (un)folding mechanistic perspective, steep pretransitions are signature outcomes of structural rearrangements in the “native ensemble” upon temperature increase. Such structural changes determine the unfolding mechanisms (39, 40), the degree of binding to a partner protein (41), phosphorylation extents (42), and are evolutionarily selected to enable fine-tuning of activity. In TipAS, the steep pretransition signals a population shift from a nearly fully folded ensemble to an ensemble in which the NTSD unfolds continuously between 298 and 313 K. While this is observable in CD and fluorescence experiments, SAXS provides a direct measure of the extent to which the ensemble dimensions can be modulated in the pretransition regime with the Rg varying from 2.4 nm to 2.7 nm between 280 and 310 K. The change in dimensions arises primarily from the melting of helices H2 and H3 with H1 being unfolded even at the lowest temperatures. Thus, TipAS is a distinctive example wherein structural changes in the pretransition region can encompass large unfolding events that aid in ligand binding.

  Finally, the conservation of broad native ensemble and weakly structured NTSD (and by extension large dynamics) across orthologs from bacteria that infect humans and some of those found in the human gut, highlights an evolutionary selection of these features to enable sequestration of thiopeptide antibiotics. Given the strong connection between conformational and functional landscapes, it should be possible to integrate the plethora of structural models currently available (from AlphaFold2, for example) to address questions on structural and conformational determinants that drive selectivity and affinity to different but related thiopeptide ligands.
