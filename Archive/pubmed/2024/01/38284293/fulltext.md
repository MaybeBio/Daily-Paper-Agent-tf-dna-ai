# Tanshinone IIA, a component of the self‐made Xiao‐Yin decoction, ameliorates psoriasis by inhibiting IL‐17/IL‐23 and PTGS2/NF‐κB/AP‐1 pathways


## Background
  Psoriasis is a persistent inflammatory dermatological disorder. Tanshinone IIA (tan‐IIA) is a biologically active compound in the self‐made Xiao‐Yin decoction (SMXYD) and exhibits diverse biological properties, such as anti‐proliferative and anti‐inflammatory effects. The objective of this investigation was to assess the potential of tan‐IIA as a therapeutic agent against psoriasis.


## Methods
  Network pharmacology was employed to ascertain the active constituents and potential pathways associated with SMXYD and psoriasis. We conducted CCK‐8, qRT‐PCR, and western blotting to assess the proliferation of HaCaT keratinocytes and the expression of IL‐17/IL‐23 and PTGS2/NF‐κB/AP‐1 pathways. Additionally, we used H&E staining, western blotting, and ELISA to evaluate the therapeutic effects and signaling pathways of tan‐IIA in psoriasis‐like mice induced by imiquimod (IMQ).


## Results
  Network pharmacology analysis identified eight hub compounds. The Th17/IL‐17 signaling was found to be a potential therapeutic pathway of SMXYD against psoriasis, with JUN (AP‐1) as the core molecule. Next, PTGS2 was selected as the target of tan‐IIA against psoriasis using network pharmacology analysis. Molecular docking showed a high affinity between PTGS2 and tan‐IIA. Tan‐IIA treatment attenuated M‐5‐induced hyperproliferation and inflammation in HaCaT keratinocytes. Additionally, Tan‐IIA downregulated the PTGS2/NF‐κB/AP‐1 pathway in HaCaT keratinocytes. In the IMQ‐induced psoriasis‐like mouse, tan‐IIA significantly reduced the severity of skin lesions and downregulated the PTGS2/NF‐κB/AP‐1 pathway. Moreover, the combination of methotrexate (MTX) and tan‐IIA further inhibited the IL‐17/IL‐23 and PTGS2/NF‐κB/AP‐1 pathways.


## Conclusion
  The administration of tan‐IIA has shown a positive effect on psoriasis by inhibiting the IL‐17/IL‐23 and PTGS2/NF‐κB/AP‐1 pathways. The findings suggest that it has promising qualities that make it a potential candidate for the development of future anti‐psoriatic agents.


## INTRODUCTION
  Psoriasis is a prevalent and persistent skin disorder characterized by papulosquamous lesions. It is a global health concern that imposes a substantial burden on individuals and society. 1 ,  2  The most common form of psoriasis, known as chronic plaque or psoriasis vulgaris, is influenced by genetic factors, particularly the presence of the HLA‐C*06:02 risk allele. 3  Various environmental factors, such as streptococcal infection, stress, smoking, obesity, and alcohol consumption, have also been identified as contributing to the onset and progression of psoriasis. 1 ,  4  Immunological and genetic research has highlighted the significance of IL‐17 and IL‐23 in the development of psoriasis. 5 ,  6 ,  7 ,  8  Furthermore, the NF‐κB signaling pathway plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of this disease. 4  Psoriasis has garnered increasing attention in recent years, leading to the emergence of several innovative therapeutic approaches. Despite extensive research on psoriasis, the exact mechanism responsible for the development of this disease is still not fully comprehended.

  Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) is widely utilized for the treatment of psoriasis, with numerous clinical and experimental studies providing evidence of its effectiveness. 9 ,  10 ,  11  According to TCM theory, psoriasis is analyzed in terms of the concept of “blood”, which encompasses three main syndrome types: blood heat, blood dryness, and blood stasis. One commonly used TCM formula for psoriasis is Xiao‐Yin decoction, which has been shown to effectively alleviate heat, eliminate toxins, cool the blood, and improve blood circulation. However, the specific mechanism by which Xiao‐Yin decoction achieves its therapeutic effects on psoriasis has not yet been investigated.

  Tanshinone IIA (tan‐IIA) is a lipophilic compound that is primarily found in Salvia miltiorrhiza. Research suggests that tan‐IIA has significant immunomodulatory effects by suppressing the production of inflammatory mediators and restoring abnormal signaling pathways, such as the TLR/NF‐κB pathway  12 ,  13  and the MAPKs/NF‐κB pathway. 14 ,  15  Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that tan‐IIA possesses anti‐tumor properties through the induction of differentiation and apoptosis, as well as the inhibition of carcinoma progression. 16 ,  17  The precise involvement of tan‐IIA in the immune response mediated by IL‐17/IL‐23 in psoriasis has yet to be fully elucidated.

  In this research investigation, network pharmacology was employed to investigate the effects of the self‐made Xiao‐Yin decoction (SMXYD) on psoriasis. Furthermore, a molecular docking analysis was performed to assess the interaction between the principal constituents and the target genes within the pathway. The primary objective of our study was to evaluate the anti‐psoriatic properties of tan‐IIA and explore the underlying mechanisms involved.


## MATERIALS AND METHODS

### Preparation of SMXYD
  SMXYD, composed of Radix Rehmanniae Praeparata (20 g), Salvia miltiorrhiza (20 g), Radix et Rhizoma Cymbopogonis (20 g), Spreading Hedyotis Herb (15 g), Tree Peony Bark (10 g), Suberect Spatholobus Stem (20 g), Redroot Gromwell (15 g), and Sophora japonica (10 g), was obtained from the Hainan Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine. The preparation process involved immersing a total of 130 g of mixed SMXYD crude herbs in 2 L of double‐distilled water for 30 min, followed by a 1‐h decoction. The resulting liquid was then filtered through a double layer of gauze, with the filtration process repeated twice. The obtained filtrate was combined and concentrated to a specific volume. The final aqueous extracts of the crude drug had concentrations of 3.03 g/mL and 6.07 g/mL and were stored at 4°C for future use.


### Network pharmacology

#### Component target finding
  Initially, the TCMSP database was used to query Radix Rehmanniae Praeparata in order to obtain the relevant targets of its components. Only the component targets that satisfied the criteria of having satisfactory oral bioavailability (OB ≥ 30%) and drug‐likeness (DL ≥ 0.18) were included in this study. The remaining components were then queried in the Batman‐TCM database to retrieve their corresponding targets. Targets with a likelihood score greater than 20 were selected for further analysis. To eliminate non‐human targets, all targets were cross‐referenced with the UniProt database.


#### Disease target finding
  To ascertain the human genes linked to psoriasis, a comprehensive search was conducted in three databases: GeneCards, NCBI gene database, and DisGeNET. The keyword “psoriasis” was used as the search criterion. The genes retrieved from these databases were merged, yielding a cumulative count of 2568 genes associated with the disease.


#### Protein‐protein interaction (PPI) network construction
  The Venny 2.1 software was used to visualize the 185 overlapping targets obtained from the searches of both the component and disease targets. The common targets identified between the components and disease were further analyzed using the String database to construct a PPI network. The biological species chosen for this analysis was Homo sapiens.


#### Topology analysis
  To enhance comprehension of the intricate interplay among components, the disease, and their respective targets, the PPI network was visualized using Cytoscape 3.8.0. Subsequently, a topology analysis was performed to rank the components according to their degree scores. Components with degree scores exceeding the average were identified as significant targets, resulting in a total of 78 key targets.


#### Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis
  The enrichment analysis of biological processes (BP), molecular functions (MF), and cellular components (CC) was conducted using the String database to identify the shared targets between the components and disease. The screening threshold for GO and KEGG pathways was defined as a P‐adjust value below 0.05. The results were then visualized using the clusterProfiler package in R version 4.0.3.


### High‐performance liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry (HPLC‐MS)
  The metabolomics analysis was conducted using the UPLC system (30A, SHIMADUZU, Japan) in conjunction with the 5600 Q‐TOF/6500 Q‐TRAP mass spectrometer (AB sciex, Canada). A 5 µL volume of each sample was injected onto a T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm with 1.7 µm particle size, Waters, USA) at a column temperature of 55°C. The mobile phases consisted of 25 mM CH3COONH4 and 25 mM NH4OH in H2O (mobile phase A) and ACN (mobile phase B). The flow rate of the mobile phases was set at 0.3 mL/min. The mass spectrometer was operated using an electrospray ionization (ESI) source, with a spray voltage of −4500 V in the negative mode and 5500 V in the positive mode. The source temperature was maintained at 600°C and the curtain gas was set at 20 psi. Scanning was performed using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM).


### Molecular docking
  The ligands of tan‐IIA were employed as crucial constituents and subjected to molecular docking against their respective receptor binding domains, with prostaglandin‐endoperoxide synthase 2 (PTGS2) being utilized as the docking receptor. The crystal structures of the constituents were obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank. Molecular docking investigations were carried out using Autodock Vina 1.1.2, and the docking process was computed employing the Genetic Algorithm.


### Cell culture and treatment
  Human HaCaT keratinocytes (iCell‐h066, iCell, China) were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (11995065, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (10099141C, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin solution (AWH0529b, Abiowell, China). The cell cultures were maintained in a CO2 incubator at 37°C. When the cells reached 80%–90% confluence, they were divided into four groups: (1) Control group: Cells were cultured under standard conditions. (2) M‐5 group: To create a psoriasis‐like keratinocytes model, HaCaT keratinocytes were exposed to a combination of cytokines (IL‐17A, IL‐22, oncostatin M, IL‐1α, and TNF‐α) (PeproTech, USA) at a concentration of 2.5 ng/mL for 12 h. 18  (3) Tan‐IIA group: The cells were treated with 10 µg/mL of tan‐IIA (HY‐N0135, MedChem Express, USA) for 24 h, followed by a 12‐h M‐5 treatment. (4) MTX group: The cells were treated with 2 µM MTX for 24 h, followed by a 12‐h M‐5 treatment.

  To transfect HaCaT keratinocytes treated with M‐5, oe‐PTGS2 (HG‐HO000963, HonorGene, China) was used. The transfected cells were cultured in a medium containing puromycin to select stable cells. The negative control (NC) group of human HaCaT keratinocytes was transfected with the empty vector (oe‐NC).


### Cell viability assay
  To assess the impact of tan‐IIA on cell viability, a cell counting kit‐8 (CCK‐8) (Abiowell, China) assay was performed. About 10 µL of CCK‐8 solution was added to each well and incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 4 h. The measurement of absorbance at 450 nm was conducted using a Microplate Reader (Heales, China).


### Quantitative real‐time PCR (qRT‐PCR)
  Total RNA samples were isolated from cellular samples using Trizol reagent (15596026, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Subsequently, the mRNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a mRNA reverse transcription kit (CW2569, CWBIO, China). qRT‐PCR was performed using the SYBR method. Primers were designed using Primer5 software (Table S1). The GAPDH gene was used as a reference gene. The data were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method.


### Animal experiments
  Male Balb/c mice, aged 6–8 weeks, were obtained from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co. Ltd. The mice were housed in a controlled environment with a 12‐h light/dark cycle and were free from any pathogens. The mice were randomly assigned to one of four groups: Sham group, Model group, Tan‐IIA group, MTX group, and Tan‐IIA+MTX group, with five mice in each group. The dorsal hair of the mice, except for those in the Sham group, was shaved, and a topical application of 50 mg IMQ cream (5%, Jian Xuyitang Bioscience Co., Ltd, China) was applied to their dorsal skin for seven consecutive days, following a previously established protocol. 19  A solution of Tan‐IIA (2 mg/mL, HY‐N0135, MedChem Express, USA) was prepared by dissolving it in a solution containing 0.1% DMSO and 0.9% NaCl. The mice in the Tan‐IIA and Tan‐IIA+MTX groups were treated with tan‐IIA (20 mg/kg/day) by gavage for seven consecutive days. Additionally, the mice in the MTX group and Tan‐IIA+MTX group were intraperitoneally administered MTX (1 mg/kg/day, 59‐05‐2, APExBIO, USA) for seven consecutive days. At the end of the treatment period, the mice were euthanized by intravenous injection of barbiturate (150 mg/kg). Skin and serum samples were collected for further analysis. The animal experimental protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Hainan Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine (IACUC‐HPHCM‐2210001).


### Scoring the severity of psoriatic skin lesions
  The evaluation of murine psoriatic skin lesions was conducted by employing the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) score, which was modified from the scoring system utilized for assessing the severity and extent of psoriasis in humans. The updated PASI includes three parameters: skin erythema, scales, and thickness. Each parameter was independently scored on a scale ranging from “0” to “4”. The numerical scale assigns specific values to different levels of severity. Specifically, the value “0” denotes the absence of a particular characteristic, “1” indicates a slight presence, “2” signifies a moderate level, “3” represents a marked degree, and “4” indicates a very marked intensity. The scoring criteria mentioned were previously outlined. 20  The scores of the three parameters were combined to calculate the overall PASI score for each mouse.


### Histological analysis
  Hematoxylin‐eosin (H&E) staining was employed to assess the morphological alterations in the skin tissues. Paraffin‐embedded tissue sections were subjected to a heat treatment and subsequently dried for 20 min at 75°C. The specimens were immersed in a series of solutions (100%, 100%, 95%, 85%, and 75%) for 5 min, repeated twice. After the drying process, the sections were subjected to staining using hematoxylin (AWI0020, Abiowell, China) and eosin (AWI0020, Abiowell, China).


### Western blotting analysis
  Cells and tissues were collected and lysed using lysis buffer (AWB0136, Abiowell, China) at 4°C for 20 min. The resulting lysates were then subjected to centrifugation at 14,000 rpm for 15 min, and the supernatant was collected for protein concentration measurement. Approximately 15 µg of protein were separated using SDS‐PAGE and subsequently transferred to nitrocellulose (NC) membranes. The membranes were blocked with a 5% nonfat milk solution at room temperature for 2 h. Following this, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies including anti‐PTGS2 (AWA45173, Abiowell, China), anti‐activator protein‐1 (AP‐1) (AP1M1, ab194384, Abcam, USA), anti‐p‐AP‐1 (AWA42707, Abiowell, China), anti‐p65 (66535‐1‐Ig, Proteintech, USA), anti‐p‐p65 (S536) (ab76302, Abcam, USA), anti‐IL‐17 (66148‐1‐Ig, Proteintech, USA), anti‐IL‐23 (ab190356, Abcam, USA), and anti‐β‐actin (66009‐1‐Ig, Proteintech, USA). After being washed three times with TBST, the membranes were incubated with a secondary antibody at room temperature for 2 h. Finally, the membranes were treated with a chemiluminescence substrate (Abiowell, China) and visualized using the ChemiScope 6000 Series (CLINX, China). The protein bands were quantified using ImageJ software, with β‐actin serving as an internal reference.


### Enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) analysis
  The serum was acquired from peripheral blood through centrifugation at 1000 × g for 15 min. The concentrations of IL‐17A, IL‐23, TNF‐α, and IL‐6 in the serum were quantified using mouse IL‐17A kit (KE10020, Proteintech, USA), mouse IL‐23 kit (CSB‐E08463m, CUSABIO, China), mouse TNF‐α kit (KE10002, Proteintech, USA), and mouse IL‐6 kit (KE10007, Proteintech, USA). The measurement of absorbance at 450 nm was conducted using a Microplate Reader (Heales, China).


### Statistic analysis
  The data is presented in the form of mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. The t‐test was employed to compare two groups, while one‐way analysis of variance was utilized to compare more than two groups. A P‐value less than 0.05 is deemed to be statistically significant.


## RESULTS

### Network pharmacology was employed to forecast the herb‐compound‐target interactions associated with the therapeutic effect of SMXYD on psoriasis
  To explore the various constituents, targets, and pathways underlying the therapeutic properties of SMXYD for psoriasis, we utilized network pharmacology. Firstly, we gathered psoriasis‐related targets, as well as SMXYD‐related targets. By comparing the 2568 targets related to psoriasis with the 448 targets related to SMXYD, we identified 185 potential targets for SMXYD in the treatment of psoriasis (Figure S1A). A PPI network was constructed to identify the hub genes associated with SMXYD concerning psoriasis. Figure S1B provides an overview of the relationships among the 185 targets. The hub genes were determined using a Network analyzer, and the top five genes (AKT1, IL‐6, INS, IL‐1β, JUN) were identified as hub genes. Additional information can be found in Figure S1B and Supplementary Table S2. Moreover, the network diagram between compounds, disease, and their targets was imported into Cytoscape for topology analysis, which identified eight key compounds of SMXYD (Table 1).

  Next, to comprehend the therapeutic functions of the potential targets, we enhanced the annotation of the GO terms for the shared drug‐disease targets. Subsequently, a total of 2563 BP terms, 64 CC terms, and 165 MF terms were obtained (Figure S1C). Among these, “response to lipopolysaccharide” (GO: 0032496), “membrane raft” (GO: 0045121), and “nuclear receptor activity” (GO: 0004879) were the most significant GO terms, respectively. Additionally, a KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was conducted, revealing the top 20 pathways (Figure S1D), with the AGE‐RAGE signaling pathway in diabetic complications demonstrating a significant impact. Furthermore, the IL‐17 signaling pathway exhibited enrichment with 24 genes, and Th17 cell differentiation showed enrichment with 21 genes. The overlapping genes between these two pathways included HSP90AA1, JUN, RELA, FOS, MAPK1, IL6, NFKBIA, IL1B, CHUK, MAPK14, HSP90AB1, IL4, and MAPK8. Of particular significance, JUN is extensively studied as a protein within the AP‐1 complex, 21  whereas NF‐κB 22 ,  23  and AP‐1 24  have been found to play crucial roles in psoriasis. Thus, we hypothesized that SMXYD may exert effects through IL‐17/Th17 and NF‐κB/AP‐1 pathways. Moreover, we constructed a compound‐target‐pathway network (Figure S1E). In conclusion, network pharmacology data demonstrated the potential regulatory effect of SMXYD on NF‐κB/AP‐1 signaling, subsequently impacting the psoriasis pathway.

  To gain deeper insights into the molecular mechanism of SMXYD in treating psoriasis, we constructed a compounds‐IL‐17 signaling pathway‐Th17 cell differentiation network (Figure S1F). Tan‐IIA (MOL007154) was discovered that have close interactions with IL‐17/Th17 signaling. Furthermore, the major pharmaceutical ingredients of SMXYD, identified via HPLC‐MS analysis, are presented in Tables S3, S4, and Figure S2. The data highlighted the high abundance of tan‐IIA in SMXYD. Additionally, previous study has revealed that tan‐IIA inhibited growth of keratinocytes through cell cycle arrest and apoptosis. 25  The above results suggested that tan‐IIA may be the core compound of SMXYD against psoriasis.

  Therefore, we then utilized network pharmacology to predict the interactions between tan‐IIA and psoriasis. As displayed in Figure 1A, the network represented the intricate connections between tan‐IIA, the disease, and their respective targets. Concurrently, we performed a network topology attribute analysis and identified PTGS2 as the central node with a higher degree (Figure 1B). Additionally, to investigate the potential interaction between tan‐IIA and PTGS2, we conducted a molecular docking study. The binding energy of PTGS2 with tan‐IIA was found to be −10.1 kcal/mol. During the interaction with tan‐IIA, PTGS2 exhibited alkyl interaction with VAL46, PRO153, ARG469, and LEU152, pi‐alkyl interaction with PRO153, CYS36, and LEU152, and pi‐donor hydrogen bond interaction with CYS47 (Figure 1C). The docking result indicated the strong affinity between PTGS2 and the core compound, tan‐IIA. Therefore, tan‐IIA was selected for further investigation.


### Tan‐IIA improved psoriasis and downregulated PTGS2/NF‐κB/AP‐1 signaling
  Next, the viability of HaCaT keratinocytes subjected to treatment with tan‐IIA was assessed. No significant cytotoxic effects were observed at concentrations below 10 µg/mL following a 24‐hour exposure to tan‐IIA, as shown in Figure 2A. Therefore, a concentration of 10 µg/mL of tan‐IIA was used in the present study. The impact of tan‐IIA treatment on M‐5‐stimulated HaCaT keratinocytes was evaluated by assessing cell viability. The study found that the excessive cell proliferation caused by M‐5 was reduced when tan‐IIA or MTX was present (Figure 2B). Additionally, when comparing the treatment with M‐5 alone to the co‐treatment of tan‐IIA and M‐5, a notable reduction in the levels of inflammatory cytokines (IL‐17, IL‐23, TNF‐α, and IL‐6) was observed in HaCaT keratinocytes (Figure 2C). To investigate how tan‐IIA inhibits proliferation and inflammatory response, we conducted western blotting to assess the inactivation of PTGS2/NF‐κB/AP‐1 signaling. M‐5 stimulation significantly activated PTGS2, AP‐1, and p65 phosphorylation, which were restored in the presence of tan‐IIA and MTX (Figure 2D). These findings suggest that tan‐IIA, an active component of SMXYD, improved the condition of psoriasis by regulating the PTGS2/NF‐κB/AP‐1 signaling pathway.


### Tan‐IIA improved psoriasis through PTGS2
  Based on the above results, PTGS2 was the potential target of tan‐IIA against psoriasis. Cyclooxygenase‐2 (COX‐2), encoded by the PTGS2 gene, has also been reported to participate in the regulation of inflammatory response in psoriasis. 26  To further explore the regulatory effect of PTGS2 on the proliferation and inflammatory response of HaCaT keratinocytes, oe‐PTGS2 was transfected into HaCaT keratinocytes. As expected, the expression level of PTGS2 was significantly increased (Figure 3A). Meanwhile, PTGS2 expression was found downregulated by tan‐IIA (Figure 3B). Subsequently, the impact of PTGS2 overexpression on M‐5‐stimulated HaCaT keratinocytes was evaluated. The cell viability assay revealed that the reduced cell proliferation caused by tan‐IIA was elevated when PTGS2 was overexpressed (Figure 3C). Additionally, compared to the M‐5+Tan‐IIA+oe‐NC group, a notable increase in the levels of inflammatory cytokines (IL‐23, TNF‐α, and IL‐6) was observed in HaCaT keratinocytes (Figure 3D). Furthermore, we conducted western blotting to evaluate the NF‐κB/AP‐1 signaling. The data show that PTGS2 overexpression significantly activated AP‐1 and p65 phosphorylation (Figure 3E). These findings suggest that tan‐IIA downregulated the NF‐κB/AP‐1 signaling through PTGS2 and improved the condition of psoriasis.


### Tan‐IIA attenuated the psoriasis‐like phenotype in the IMQ‐induced murine model
  We conducted additional assessments to examine the impact of tan‐IIA on a psoriasis‐like mouse model induced by IMQ, which has been shown to elicit dermatitis in mice that closely mimics human psoriasis. 27  The evaluation of skin lesions, which are characterized by redness, flaking, and thickness, was assessed using the PASI scores on day 7. The mice treated with tan‐IIA showed lower scores compared with the Model group (Figure 4A). Additionally, the mice that received only IMQ treatment consistently showed symptoms similar to psoriasis, such as redness, scaling, and thickening of the skin. The administration of tan‐IIA effectively reduced these symptoms, and the concurrent administration of tan‐IIA and MTX resulted in even greater improvement (Figure 4B). The histopathological analysis revealed that the mice subjected to IMQ treatment exhibited a statistically significant increase in the thickness of their epidermal layers compared with the Sham group. Although no significant difference in epidermal thickness was observed between the tan‐IIA and Model group, the concurrent administration of tan‐IIA and MTX resulted in a significant reduction in the severity of skin lesions and epidermal thickness (Figure 4C and D). These findings suggest that tan‐IIA is effective in improving psoriasis‐like skin lesions induced by IMQ in mice, particularly when combined with MTX.


### 3.5 Tan‐IIA attenuated IMQ‐induced inflammation, IL‐17/IL‐23 and PTGS2/NF‐κB/AP‐1 signaling in mice skin
  We then assessed the expression of IL‐17/IL‐23 and PTGS2/NF‐κB/AP‐1 pathways in mice skin. Western blot analysis revealed a notable upregulation in the expressions of IL‐17, IL‐23, PTGS2, p‐AP‐1/AP‐1, and p‐p65/p65 after IMQ treatment. Treatment with tan‐IIA, however, reduced the observed increases in Figure 5A and B. Additionally, the co‐administration of tan‐IIA and MTX led to an additional decrease in protein expression compared to treatment with MTX alone. Furthermore, the inflammatory status in mice was assessed by measuring the levels of IL‐17A, IL‐23, TNF‐α, and IL‐6 using ELISA. The administration of IMQ resulted in a notable upregulation of inflammatory cytokine mRNA levels. However, treatment with tan‐IIA partially reduced this upregulation, as depicted in Figure 5C. The co‐administration of MTX and tan‐IIA resulted in a notable reduction in the expression of inflammatory cytokines, surpassing the effects observed in the MTX group. In conclusion, the results of this study suggest that tan‐IIA has a beneficial effect on the inflammatory response induced by IMQ in mice, which is achieved by regulating the IL‐17/IL‐23 and PTGS2/NF‐κB/AP‐1 signaling pathways.


## DISCUSSION
  Psoriasis is a multifaceted inflammatory dermatological condition. 28  Systemic immunosuppression, including the use of cyclosporine, MTX, and other biological agents, has been proven to alleviate symptoms in individuals with psoriasis. TCM takes a unique approach to diagnosing and treating psoriasis. This approach involves eliminating pathogenic heat and toxins from the body, promoting blood circulation, and nourishing the blood while also eliminating toxins. One specific treatment, called Huanglian Jiedu Decoction, has been found to alleviate symptoms in 45 out of 50 individuals with psoriasis. This treatment includes ingredients such as Radix Scutellariae, Cortex Phellodendri, and Rhizoma Coptidis. 29  Certain conventional remedies and bioactive compounds derived from Chinese herbs have been developed into pharmaceutical agents used in hospitals because of their remarkable effectiveness. The effectiveness of a composite ointment containing Indigo naturalis, Scutellaria baicalensis Georgi, and Cortex phellodendri in treating pediatric psoriasis has been proven. 30  Similarly, the therapeutic effectiveness of Xiao‐Yin decoction in treating psoriasis has been proven. Therefore, we have developed our formulation called SMXYD and investigated the most active compound and potential action pathway of SMXYD using network pharmacology.

  The present study utilized molecular docking and HPLC analysis to demonstrate that tan‐IIA, a bioactive compound found in SMXYD, plays a crucial role in the therapeutic effectiveness against psoriasis. Tan‐IIA, a fat‐soluble compound derived from Danshen, has gained recognition for its therapeutic potential in managing various clinical conditions. These conditions include cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, cancer, diabetes, central nervous system disorders, and liver disease. 31 ,  32  Previous research has shown that tan‐IIA can reduce inflammation and tissue damage caused by various pathogens. 33  Our study confirmed that the administration of tan‐IIA effectively suppressed excessive proliferation and inflammation induced by M‐5 in HaCaT keratinocytes. Additionally, treatment with tan‐IIA significantly inhibited the development of skin lesions and inflammation in psoriasis‐like mice induced by IMQ. When tan‐IIA and MTX were combined, the therapeutic effect was improved, which was a significant finding. Likewise, the combination of MTX and baricitinib is more effective and safe than MTX alone as the initial treatment for patients with active rheumatoid arthritis. 34  JAKi combined with MTX demonstrated superiority to JAKi monotherapy in terms of ACR responses, low disease activity and remission achievement. 35  These findings suggest that the combination of tan‐IIA and MTX has a high potential for future application in clinical psoriasis patients and further investigation may require exploring combinations of two or more therapies.

  The causeof psoriasis is not completely understood, but there is increasing evidence suggesting that the IL‐17/IL‐23 axis contributes to its development. 27  Clinical trials assessing the effectiveness of anti‐P40 treatment, a subunit of IL‐23, have shown promising results in managing psoriasis. 36  Our network pharmacology findings support the therapeutic effectiveness of SMXYD in targeting the Th17/IL‐17 pathway. In addition, Yan et al. showed that tan‐IIA is correlated with the reduction of serum and brain IL‐17 and IL‐23 in rats with experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis. 37  In a study by Meng et al., it was discovered that tan‐IIA can inhibit the inflammatory responses caused by LPS in vitro. This inhibition is achieved by partially suppressing the TLR4/TAK1/NF‐κB signaling cascade in vascular smooth muscle cells. 13  Moreover, Zhao et al. suggested that tan‐IIA may help stabilize vulnerable atherosclerotic plaques in ApoE −/− mice. This effect is achieved by reducing inflammation and immune response in a dose‐dependent manner. It is mediated through the TLR4/MyD88/NF‐κB signaling cascade. 38  Meanwhile, PTGS2 was one of the crucial inflammatory and psoriatic markers. 39  Based on these findings, it was hypothesized that tan‐IIA could alleviate psoriasis symptoms by regulating the IL‐17/IL‐23 and PTGS2/NF‐κB/AP‐1 signaling pathways. A series of experiments, both in vitro and in vivo, were conducted to assess the protein expression of these pathways. As expected, our findings confirm that tan‐IIA has immunomodulatory properties and provides protection against inflammation through the IL‐17/IL‐23 and PTGS2/NF‐κB/AP‐1 signaling pathways.

  To the best of our knowledge, the present study represents the first attempt to investigate the effects of tan‐IIA on psoriasis in mice induced with IMQ. However, it is imperative to recognize the constraints of this study. In addition to the IL‐17/IL‐23 and PTGS2/NF‐κB/AP‐1 pathways, tan‐IIA may have other regulatory targets in mice induced with IMQ. Further investigation is warranted to examine the roles of tan‐IIA in individuals with psoriasis.


## CONCLUSION
  The study concludes that tan‐IIA can reduce the proliferation and inflammatory reactions caused by M‐5 cytokines in HaCaT keratinocytes. The observed effects were found to be linked to the inactivation of the IL‐17/IL‐23 and PTGS2/NF‐κB/AP‐1 signaling pathways. Additionally, the administration of tan‐IIA improved the severity of skin lesions and reduced inflammation in an IMQ‐induced mouse model of psoriasis‐like condition. The findings suggest that tan‐IIA exhibits promising potential as an anti‐psoriatic agent, thereby warranting further exploration for its application as a therapeutic intervention for psoriasis.
