# Simultaneous binding of quercetin and catechin to FOXO3 enhances IKKα transcription inhibition and suppression of oxidative stress-induced acute alcoholic liver injury in rats


## Graphical abstract
  Graphical abstract


## Introduction
  Alcoholic liver disease (ALD) is a disorder of liver metabolism, which is caused by acute or chronic excessive alcohol consumption and results in the generation of products that damage the liver. ALD in advanced/late stages was found difficult to be cured (liver transplantation would probably be the only option at these stages) [1]. Therefore, monitoring the acute alcoholic liver injury (AALI) in early stage is a proactive approach for preventing and controlling ALD. Moreover, ALD caused by acute alcohol consumption was found in close association with an abnormal increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS), causing oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, inflammation, and ultimately liver injury [2], [3]. Since oxidative stress is one of the major triggering factors of AALI, the supplementation of dietary antioxidants, especially those naturally occurring in foods (which have minimal side effects), is an important strategy to prevent and treat oxidative damage-induced ALD [4], [5]. Flavonoids, as plant-based antioxidants found abundantly in human diet, have a characteristic phenylbenzopyran structure and several phenolic hydroxyl groups. These antioxidants possess diverse biological and pharmacological properties including anti-oxidant capacities, anti-inflammatory activities, anti-cancer efficacies, liver-protecting function and cardiovascular protective effect to influence cellular events, metabolic activities and signaling processes [6], [7], [8].

  Quercetin and catechin are flavonoids that occur naturally in plant foods. Both are well known effective antioxidants against oxidative stress and associated damage. Quercetin can inhibit oxidative stress, glutathione depletion, and pro-inflammatory cytokine production, promote lipophagy, and improve steatosis in ALD [9], [10]. Catechin could protect ethanol-induced liver injury via regulating lipid metabolism of hepatocytes in alcoholic fatty liver disease mice [11]. At the molecular level, quercetin and catechin can influence the intracellular signaling pathways via interacting with functional proteins including transcription factors (TFs), intracellular enzymes and receptors, to provide protective effects on cells and the body [12], [13]. Quercetin was reported to ameliorate ethanol-induced AALI through up-regulating nuclear factor-erythroid-2-related factor-2 (Nrf2)/HO-1 to reduce oxidative stress, and down-regulating the nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB)/NLRP3 expression to inhibit inflammation [14]. Another green tea catechin, epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG), was found able to mitigate ALD-associated liver injury by directly targeting TLR2/3 to induce interleukin 10 (IL-10) production [15]. Furthermore, the combined use of quercetin and catechin was reported to offer an enhanced, additive or synergistic effect in desired bioactivities with minimized toxicity [16]. In lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-stimulated macrophage RAW 264.7 cells, quercetin and catechin demonstrated synergistic anti-inflammatory actions through their abilities to suppress TLR4-MyD88-mediated NF-κB and mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPK) signaling pathways [17]. Quercetin and catechin combined at 15 μmol/L and 20 μmol/L, respectively (these concentrations were too low to be effective for them to be used singly), could exert a synergistic effect in suppressing angiotensin II-induced redox-sensitive signaling pathways in hypertensive rats vascular smooth muscle cells [18]. Compared with quercetin alone or catechin alone, the quercetin-catechin combination was able to attenuate to a greater extent MetS-associated adipose inflammation, oxidative stress and insulin resistance in fructose-fed rats and in 3T3-L1 adipocytes [19]. Accordingly, it is possible that quercetin and catechin work in concert to prevent oxidative stress-induced AALI through a synergistic or additive action.

  The NF-κB signaling pathway is known to modulate the expression of genes involved in various cellular processes including innate stress, immune responses, cell proliferation and apoptosis, and there is a complex molecular interplay between NF-κB and Nrf2 in response to oxidative stress, inflammation, acute injures and other stimuli [20], [21]. NF-κB can be activated through the canonical NF-κB pathway (which requires IKKβ but does not need an inhibitor of κB kinase α (IKKα)), or the non-canonical NF-κB pathway (which requires its key activating factor, IKKα, and NF-κB inducing kinase, NIK, but does not involve IKKβ or IKKγ) [22]. Cross-talks between these two pathways were reported previously [23], in particular, the activated canonical pathway can trigger NF-κB signal transduction while limiting the non-canonical signaling in immune cells [24]. Moreover, the family of forkhead box class O (FOXO) function as TFs to maintain cellular and organismal homeostasis [25]. FOXOs are significant regulators of cellular stress response, through acting as cellular redox sensors and scavengers of reactive oxidative species via phosphorylation, acetylation and ubiquitination [26], [27], while enhancing cellular antioxidant defense and mediating various cellular events such as cell cycle arrest, apoptosis and metabolic processes [28]. As a member of the FOXO family, FOXO3 (also known as FOXO3a) has multiple important biological functions, including roles as an inducer of macrophage apoptosis and inhibitor of the release of the anti-inflammatory cytokine, IL-10 [29]. FOXO3 participates in the upregulation of IKKα, and downregulation of FOXO3 can obstruct neuronal damage and inflammatory response via suppressing the carboxy-terminal domain 2/IKKα axis [30]. In the meantime, there is a signal transduction crosstalk between FOXO3 and p53, including the stabilization of p53 by FOXO3 [31], activation of p53 through up-regulating its upstream regulator p19ARF (CDKN2A) [32], or the binding of p53 to FOXO3 to activate FOXO3 directly [33]. FOXO3 and p53 share similarities in their functions related to DNA repair, cell cycle arrest and apoptosis [34].

  Following ingestion and entry into the human body, phenolics and their derived phenolic products will interact with functional proteins in dynamic biological systems including cells. As reviewed recently by our research team [35], phenolics can interact with TFs to prevent TFs from binding to DNA to inhibit the expression of downstream genes, or block TF-protein interactions to influence the expression of TFs and cell signaling. Like all phenolic-protein interactions, flavonoid-FOXO3 interactions depend on the chemical structures of flavonoids and environmental factors such as the type of system medium and the matrix composition. Flavonoids possess hydrophobic benzene rings and several phenolic hydroxyl groups, thus can interact with proteins via covalent bonds and non-covalent bonds (including hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, π bonds, van der Waals forces and ionic/electrostatic bonding). Also, covalent interactions are possible between oxidized flavonoids and proteins or the systems involving organosulphur compounds [36]. Accordingly, the interactions between FOXO3 and quercetin and/or catechin are anticipated to take place, and such interactions would become increasingly complex in a dynamic biological system under oxidative stress.

  This research aimed to investigate the association between the abilities of quercetin and catechin to counteract oxidative stress-induced AALI, the changes in Nrf2 expression and IKKα transcription, and the possible bindings of quercetin/catechin to the TF, FOXO3. The protective effects of quercetin alone, catechin alone and the quercetin-catechin combination against oxidative stress in the rat liver, HepG2 and HHL-5 cells were examined for the first time. This research is the first study to show whether or not FOXO3 can directly bind to the IKKα promoter to regulate the transcription of IKKα. The enhanced effect exhibited by the quercetin-catechin combination was further investigated at the molecular level through examining the interactions of FOXO3 with quercetin and/or catechin by molecular docking, cellular thermal shift assay, UV–Vis absorption spectroscopy, fluorescence spectroscopy, and circular dichroism spectroscopy.


## Materials and methods

### Materials
  Quercetin (purity > 98%), catechin (purity > 98%) and H2O2 were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). All other chemical and reagents were of analytical grade and purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) unless indicated otherwise. Full-length FOXO3 proteins were expressed and purified [37]. Briefly, the FOXO3 coding sequences were inserted into the pET32a plasmid along with a His-tag via homologous recombination (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), and the resulting plasmid was transformed into E.coli BL21(DE3) cells (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). The protein expression was induced by 0.5 mM β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) at 16 °C for 16 h, and the protein was purified with a Ni2+-NTA column (GE Healthcare Bio-Sciences AB, Uppsala, Sweden). The final purity was determined as > 95% by sodium dodecyl-sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE).


### Animal experiments
  Twenty-five male SPF-Wistar rats (200 ± 20 g, aged 6 weeks) were purchased from Pengyue Laboratory Animal Breeding Co., Ltd (Jinan, China). Rats were kept in cages with unrestricted access to food and water under a controlled environment with a temperature of 23 ± 1 °C, a relative humidity of 50 ± 10%, and a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle. After 7-day acclimation, the 25 rats were randomized into 5 groups (as shown in Table S1). Quercetin and catechin were added to 0.4% sodium carboxymethyl cellulose solution, and their individual doses (50 mg/kg body weight for quercetin/catechin alone group and 25 + 25 mg/kg body weight for quercetin-catechin combination group) without causing a toxic effect were selected according to the previous study [14], [38]. The acute liver damage model was established by given 50% (v/v) ethanol 5 g/kg body weight every 12 h for a total of three times. On the last day of trial, the serum of each experimental rat was taken from the rat heart after the rat was anesthetized, and the liver samples were collected after decapitation for further analysis. Every experiment was in compliance with the European Community Guidelines for using animals in studies, and the ethical committee guidelines of Shandong Agricultural University (ethical number: 2007005).


### Cell culture and treatments
  Gaining Biological (Shanghai, China) provided the HepG2 cells, and Shanghai Cell Bank (Shanghai, China) provided the HHL-5 cells. The cells were cultured at 37 °C under a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 and 95% room air in fresh minimum essential medium (MEM) (Solarbio, Beijing, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin (100 U/mL)-streptomycin (100 μg/mL) mixture. The cells were seeded in 96-well or 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL/well before being treated with quercetin alone (25 or 30 μM), catechin alone (25 or 30 μM) or a combination of quercetin and catechin (12.5 μM + 12.5 μM, or 15 μM + 15 μM) for 24 h. Then, the cells were treated with H2O2 (500 μM) for 2 h. In parallel, the control group was treated only with the basic culture medium. After the treatments, all the cells were collected for further experimental examination.


### The CCK-8 assay for cell viability
  The Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) (Solarbio, Beijing, China) was used to determine cell viability in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. A multi-detection microplate reader (SpectraMax® M5, Shanghai, China) was used to record the absorbance at 450 nm.


### Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) assay
  The ROS generated in cells was measured with the use of 2′,7-dichlorodihydro-fluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA). The ROS generated in the liver tissue or liver cells was quantified with an ROS assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. The fluorescence measurements were performed by a fluorescence microscope (Nikon Instruments, Tokyo, Japan) and a multi-detection microplate reader (SpectraMax® M5, Shanghai, China) with an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and emission wavelength of 525 nm.


### Measurements of malondialdehyde (MDA) level and activities of antioxidant enzymes
  The cells were collected after the above-mentioned treatments. Then, the cellular level of MDA and activities of antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT) and glutathione peroxidase (GPx) were determined using the matching kits (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions.


### Measurement of mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψ)
  Mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψ) was determined using the mitochondria-specific membrane permeable lipophilic, cationic fluorescent dye 5,5,6,6′-tetrachloro-1,1′,3,3′ tetraethylbenzimi-dazoylcarbocyanine iodide (JC-1) kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and a fluorescence microscope (Nikon Instruments, Tokyo, Japan). The Δψ values were used to evaluate the mitochondrial function to indicate cell health.


### Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) assay
  From the liver tissues or cells, total RNA was extracted using the Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The cDNA was reversed from RNA using PrimeScriptTM RT reagent Kit with gDNA eraser (Takara, Dalian, China). Green Premix Ex Taq (TliRNaseH Plus) (Takara, Dalian, China) was used for qRT-PCR. Using GAPDH as a reference (for normalization), the 2−ΔΔCT technique was used to assess the gene expression levels. The specific primers used are listed below: Keap1 (forward), 5′-ATTCAGCTGAGTGTTACTACCC-3′; Keap1 (reverse), 5′-CAGCATAGATACAGTTGTGCAG-3′; Nrf2 (forward), 5′-AGTCCAGAAGCCAAACTGACAGAAG-3′; Nrf2 (reverse), 5′- GGAGAGGATGCTGCTGAAGGAATC-3′; IKKα (forward), 5′-GACCTCTACAGCACCAGCATTTCC-3′; IKKα (reverse), 5′-GCATGACCTCCTGAGCAGTGATG-3′.


### Western blotting
  The total proteins and nuclear proteins were extracted using the kit for total protein extraction (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and nuclear/cytosol fractionation (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), respectively. A 10% SDS-PAGE was used for separating proteins, and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes were used for immobilizing proteins. After blocking the membranes for two hours at room temperature using 5% skim milk in Tris buffered saline (TBST) with 0.1% Tween-20, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies for an additional night at 4 °C. The primary antibodies used were: IKKα (Abcam, ab32041, 1/1000), Nrf2 (Abcam, ab137550, 1/1000), IKKβ (Abcam, ab32135, 1/2000), p-IKKβ (Abcam, ab194519, 1/1000), IκBα (Abcam, ab32518, 1/50000), p-IκBα (Abcam, ab133462, 1/10000), p65 (Abcam, ab32536, 1/50000), p-p65 (Abcam, ab76302, 1/1000), Bcl-2 (Abcam, ab182858, 1/2000), Bax (Abcam, ab182733, 1/2000), p53 (Abcam, ab179477, 1/2000), p53-phospho S20 (ab157454, 1/5000), β-actin (Abcam, ab179467, 1/5000), and Lamin A (Abcam, ab108595, 1/20000). After the incubation, goat anti-rabbit IgG with peroxidase (HRP) was incubated on the membranes for two hours at room temperature. Finally, the bands were visualized with an enhanced chemiluminescence reagent (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and examined by the Image LabTM 5.1 version software (Bio-Rad ChemiDocTM MP Imaging Systems, Hercules, USA).


### RNA-seq and bioinformatics analysis
  Five micrograms of total RNA was extracted from each sample in the control (CK), H2O2 (H), H2O2 + Quercetin (HQ), H2O2 + Catechin (HC), and H2O2 + Quercetin + Catechin (HQC) group as the input material. RNA purity and concentration were checked using NanoDrop (Implen, Germany). The Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer was used to evaluste the integrity of RNA. Sequencing libraries were constructed using NEBNext® UltraTM RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB, USA). The RNA sequencing service was provided by Lianchuan Biotechnology Co. Ltd (Hangzhou). For the investigation of signaling pathway and functions, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Gene and Genomes (KEGG) were utilized. For bioinformatic analysis, OmicStudio tools (https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool) was utilized.


### Transfection of siRNA and overexpression plasmids
  The LipofectamineTM 2000 Reagent (Invitrogen) was used for the transfections of siRNA and overexpression plasmids following the manufacturer’s guidelines. The siRNA-IKKα (final concentration: 20 μM) and overexpression plasmids pcDNA3.1-IKKα (0.5 μg) were transfected into HepG2 cells for 24 h. Then, the cells were treated with quercetin, catechin or their combination, followed by H2O2 damage, according to the above-described procedure. After the treatment, follow-up experiments were carried out. The siRNA-related sequences used are listed below: siRNA-NC sequence (forward), 5′- UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3′; siRNA-NC sequence (reverse), 5′- ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT-3′; siRNA-IKKα sequence (forward), 5′- GCACUAUGUGUCUGGACUATT-3′; siRNA-IKKα sequence (reverse): 5′-UAGUCCAGACACAUAGUGCTT −3′.


### Yeast growth and yeast one-hybrid (Y1H) assay
  The core promoter sequence (-500/-1) was cloned into the pAbAi vector via homologous recombination, and the pBait-AbAi construct was obtained by sequence comparison. The plasmids were linearized by the BatBI restriction enzyme before transfection for stable integration of plasmid into Y1H yeast genome using the YeastmakerTM Yeast Transformation System 2 (Clontech, USA). The generated Y1H bait yeasts were analyzed using the Matchmaker Insert Check PCR Mix 1 (Clontech, USA) to obtain Y1H Gold [Bait/AbAi]. The Y1H Gold [p53/AbAi] was used as a positive control. Then, the minimum aureobasidin A (AbA) concentration (AbA*) for inhibiting self-activation of the AbA reporter was determined. The Y1H Gold [Bait/AbAi] were transferred into competent cells. The library plasmids were constructed by Accurate Biology company. After the transformation of constructed library plasmids (10 μg) into the competent cells, the transformed cells were coated on SD/-Leu/AbA* plates. The plates were incubated at 30 °C for 5 days until yeast colonies appeared. Then, the single colonies were selected for PCR with T7 and 3AD primers. The samples with amplified sequence bands were used for sequencing. After sequence comparison, potential TFs were screened out.


### YIH rescue assays
  The whole coding sequences of potential TFs were cloned into vector pGADT7 and verified via sequencing. Then, the pGADT7-prey plasmids were transferred into the constructed Y1H Gold [Bait/AbAi] competent cells and verified on the SD/-Leu/AbA* solid medium. The negative control was pGADT7 and the positive control was pGADT7-p53.


### Luciferase reporter assay
  Firstly, the core promoter sequence of IKKα was identified using luciferase reporter assays. Four promoter fragments of 500 bp were fused to the luciferase (Luc) reporter of pGL3 vector. Luciferase activities were measured by co-transfecting the reporter pGL3 and pRL-TK (renilla luciferase vector) into HEK293T cells. Secondly, the binding of potential TFs to IKKα core promoter was confirmed by luciferase reporter assays. The whole coding sequences of potential TFs were cloned into vector pcDNA3.1 and verified via sequencing. The pcDNA3.1-TF, the reporter vector pGL3-IKKα promoter and pRL-TK were co-transfected into HEK293T cells. The LipofectamineTM 2000 (Invitrogen, USA) was used to carry out the transfections. The dual-luciferase reporter assay kit (Promega, USA) and the SpectraMax® M5 microplate reader were used to measure the luciferase activities. For each sample, the activity of renilla luciferase was used to normalized the firely luciferase activity.


### Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA)
  The Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai) was utilized to isolate nuclear protein extracts from HepG2 cells. The LightShift Chemiluminescent EMSA Kit (Thermo Fisher, Shanghai) was used to perform EMSA. There are the sequences of probe senses that were used: wide-type probe, 5′-GAGTACCTGTTTGTTTCCCCATCCCTTAGC-3′; mutant probe, 5′-GAGTACCTGTTTCAAAGCATTTCCCTTAGC-3′. The 5′ and 3′ ends of the wild-type probe were labeled with biotin, and double-stranded probes were synthesized. The nuclear protein (2 μg) was incubated at room temperature for 20 min in the presence of poly [d(I–C)] (1 μg), the binding buffer and labeled wild-type probe with or without an unlabeled probe. The 5.5% nondenaturing PAGE was performed to separate the bound DNA complexes. The nylon membrane was used to transfer the bound DNA complexes and cross-link transferred DNA. The signals were detected using a chemiluminescent solution by the FluorChemE system.


### Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay
  The Pierce Magnetic ChIP Kit (Thermo Scientific, Shanghai) was used to perform the ChIP experiment following the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde and then terminated with a glycine solution (2.5 M). An aliquot of cross-linked DNA was kept as the input control (the reference). The remaining cross-linked cells were sonicated until the small chromatin fragments (sized about 500 bp) were generated. The sonicated chromatin was centrifuged at 9000 g and 4 °C for 5 min. Immunoprecipitation was carried out with anti-FOXO3 antibody for 12 h at 4 °C. A reaction with IgG or without an antibody was used as the negative control. After that, ChIP Grade Protein A/G Magnetic Beads were used to incubate the DNA-protein-antibody complex for 2 h at 4 °C. After the elution and reverse-crosslink of the immunoprecipitation (IP) DNA following manufacturer’s instructions, the IP DNA was subjected to column-based DNA purification and qRT-PCR. The primer sequences were as follows: Forward, 5′-ACAGGAGAGACTGGGCTGCTT-3′; Reverse, 5′- GCCTCAGGTTCCACAGTTGTTCCT-3′.


### Molecular docking
  Molecular docking studies were carried out to examine the binding of FOXO3 with quercetin and/or catechin using AutoDock Vina 1.2.3 with the support of AutoDock Tools-1.5.6. The three-dimensional (3D) structure of FOXO3 (PDB ID: 2UZK) was obtained from Protein Data Bank (http://www.rcsb.org/). The 3D structure of quercetin and catechin were downloaded from the PubChem Compound database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pccompound/). The geometry optimization for FOXO3 and quercetin/catechin was performed using AutoDock Tools-1.5.6 to generate the docking input files. The water molecules in the structure of FOXO3 were removed, and the Gasteiger partial charges were assigned, while the nonpolar hydrogen atoms being merged. The protein structure was kept rigid. The ligands were prepared by merging non-polar hydrogen atoms and defining rotatable bonds. The size of the grid box was set to 100 × 90 × 126 Å, and the x, y, and z coordinates of the box center were 19.6, −21.7, and 27.0 Å, respectively. Fifty docked conformations were obtained for each docking calculation. The lowest-energy docked complex was chosen and further analyzed using PyMoL 2.5.0 + openvr software [39].


### The cellular thermal shift assay
  Quercetin and catechin were dissolved in DMSO to obtain high-concentration stock solutions (final concentration: 20 mM and 20 mM, respectively). The cells were seed in 6-well plates and digested with trypsin. After digestion, the cells were collected, given two PBS washes, and then put back into the PBS solution with protease inhibitor and phosphatase inhibitor. The suspension of cells was placed in liquid nitrogen and then in a water bath at 37 °C, and this freeze–thaw cycle was repeated three times to lyse cells. After centrifuging the resultant sample for 20 min at 20,000 g and 4 °C, the collected supernatant was separated into four parts. The four portions of the supernatant were mixed with an equal volume of DMSO, quercetin (100 μM), catechin (100 μM) and the quercetin-catechin combination (50 μM + 50 μM), respectively, and the four mixtures were allowed standing at room temperature for 30 min. Each mixture was divided into eight equal parts and placed into eight separate tubes, and these eight tubes were heated at different temperatures for 3 min using a thermal cycle meter. After the cooling of these samples, their FOXO3 protein contents were determined by Western blotting (with the FOXO3 in DMSO as an internal control).


### UV–Vis absorption spectroscopy
  Quercetin and catechin were dissolved in DMSO to obtain high-concentration stock solutions (final quercetin and catechin concentrations: 6 mg/mL and 6 mg/mL, respectively). The FOXO3 protein solution (3 mg/ml in PBS) and quercetin/catechin solution were diluted with PBS, respectively, and then mixed to generate FOXO3-quercetin/catechin at different ratios, with the final protein concentration being the same (0.1 mg/mL). The concentrations of the diluted solutions of quercetin or catechin alone were 0–12 μg/mL, and the concentrations of quercetin and catechin in the quercetin-catechin solutions were half those of quercetin alone solution and catechin alone solution. The UV–Vis absorption spectra were acquired at 298 K over the wavelength range of 220–340 nm using a multi-function microplate reader (SpectraMax® M5, Shanghai, China). The spectra of PBS in the absence and presence of quercetin/catechin was used as the control for subtraction.


### Fluorescence spectroscopy
  The protein fluorescence spectra were acquired at 298 K with an initial excitation wavelength of 280 nm, the emission wavelength from 300 to 460 nm and the width of slit as 5.0 nm. The FOXO3 protein solution, quercetin/catechin alone solution, and FOXO3-quercetin/catechin solutions were prepared following the above-described methods in the Section “UV–Vis absorption spectroscopy”. To make the fluorescence intensity curves correct, PBS was used as a corresponding blank. The final concentration of the FOXO3 protein in various test systems was constant (0.1 mg/mL). The concentrations of quercetin or catechin alone solutions were 0–12 μg/mL, and the concentrations of quercetin and catechin in the quercetin-catechin solutions were half those of quercetin alone solution and catechin alone solution. All fluorescence experiments were performed in triplicate.

  The fluorescence quenching was examined using Stern-Volmer equation [40]:(1)F0/F=1+KsvQ=1+Kqτ0Q(2)logF0-F/F=logKa+nlogQwhere F0 and F are the protein fluorescence intensities at the emission maximum with or without quercetin/catechin, respectively; Ksv is the quenching constant; [Q] is the concentration of quercetin/catechin; Kq is the biomolecular quenching rate constant; τ0 is the average lifetime of the fluorophore without a quencher (τ0 = 10-8 s). Ka is the binding constant and n is the number of binding sites. Ka and n can be calculated from the intercept and slope values of the plot of log[(F0-F)/F] versus log[Q].


### Circular dichroism (CD)
  CD spectra of FOXO3 protein solutions (in PBS) with or without quercetin and/or catechin were acquired using a Chriascan CD spectropolarimeter (Applied Photophysics Inc., Leatherhead, Surrey, UK). The FOXO3 protein solution, quercetin/catechin alone solution, and FOXO3-quercetin/catechin solutions were prepared following the above-described methods. The test samples had a consistent protein content of 0.02 mg/mL. The concentration of quercetin or catechin alone solution was 0.5 μg/mL, and the concentrations of quercetin and catechin in the quercetin-catechin solutions were half those of quercetin alone solution and catechin alone solution. CD spectra were recorded at 298 K from 200 to 260 nm using a quartz cuvette with a path length of 1 mm and a scan speed of 100 nm/min. Each final spectrum was the average of three consecutive spectra. The FOXO3 protein spectra were obtained through subtracting the PBS background. The spectra of the two flavonoids acquired under the same conditions were subtracted as the background spectra from the spectra of FOXO3 - quercetin/catechin samples. The data were calculated using the protein secondary structure calculation software (CDNN program).


### Statistical analysis
  Each experiment was conducted three times, and the data were expressed as “mean ± standard deviation”. The SPSS 16.0 software was used to analyze the data. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to perform the statistical analysis. At p < 0.05, the differences were deemed statistically significant.


## Results

### The combination of quercetin and catechin alleviated AALI in ethanol (EtOH)-treated rats through inhibiting oxidative stress cooperatively

#### The enhanced suppressing effects of the quercetin-catechin combination on EtOH-induced liver injury
  Using a rat model, the in vivo liver-protecting effects of quercetin alone (50 mg/kg body weight), catechin alone (50 mg/kg body weight) and quercetin-catechin combination (25 mg/kg quercetin + 25 mg/kg catechin body weight) on EtOH-induced AALI were compared. Significant differences were detected in the liver tissue sections between the EtOH group and the control group, including hepatocyte steatosis, small vacuolation in the cytoplasm, local necrosis of hepatocytes and infiltration of inflammatory cells. The administration with quercetin alone or catechin alone significantly (P < 0.05) inhibited the EtOH-induced pathological abnormalities, and the quercetin-catechin combination further enhanced the protective action (Fig. 1A). Compared to the quercetin alone or catechin alone group, the quercetin-catechin combination led to a significant (P < 0.05) enhancement in reducing alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels in rat serum (Fig. 1B). It was reported previously that oxidative stress is a major triggering factor of ALD, and ethanol metabolism would generate acetaldehyde and ROS (mainly H2O2), deplete glutathione, inhibit the antioxidant enzymes expressions (e.g., SOD1), eventually reducing cellular antioxidative ability [41]. As shown in Fig. 1C, EtOH stimulation significantly (P < 0.05) increased the level of generated ROS in the liver, especially the H2O2 level (which accounted for 71.04% of total ROS), compared with the control group. This result indicated that the ROS resulting from ethanol metabolism was mainly in the form of H2O2, and the generated ROS accounted for the oxidative damage in the rat livers. EtOH stimulation also significantly decreased the activities of SOD, GPx and CAT (Fig. 1D–F), and increased MDA level (Fig. 1G). However, the pretreatment of quercetin, catechin or their combination counteracted the EtOH-initiated oxidative damage, with their counteracting effects in the order of quercetin-catechin combination > quercetin alone > catechin alone. To examine the mechanisms involved in the protecting effects provided by the phenolic treatment on the rat liver tissues against oxidative stress in AALI, the signaling pathways relevant to the regulation of oxidative stress such as the Nrf2 routes in the liver tissues were investigated herein. As shown in Fig. 1H-1I, quercetin alone, catechin alone or quercetin-catechin combination was able to counteract EtOH-induced decrease of Nrf2 mRNA and protein expression, with the quercetin-catechin combination being more effective than quercetin alone or catechin alone. Quercetin alone, catechin alone or quercetin-catechin combination also decreased significantly (P < 0.05) the protein expression of Keap1, with the quercetin-catechin combination exerting greater influence than quercetin alone or catechin alone (Fig. 1I). These results indicated that quercetin and catechin acted jointly to exert an enhanced suppression on the expression of Keap1, leading to a greater promotion of the release of Nrf2 from Keap1 and subsequent translocation of Nrf2 to nuclear, thereby accelerating the activation of the expression of Nrf2 and its downstream target genes. #### Quercetin and catechin provided an enhanced relieving effect on AALI through inhibiting IKKα/p53 pathway
  IKKα as a member of IκB kinase (IKK) complex regulates the canonical or noncanonical NF-κB signaling pathway and exhibits a range of functions during NF-κB-independent signaling. At present, many IκBα- and NF-κB-independent targets of IKKα have been identified or are being confirmed. IKKα can regulate several target genes in cell apoptosis, cell transformation, angiogenesis and tumor progression [44]. ROS are closely associated with transient decrease in IKKα level due to redox-sensitive signaling and modification [45]. In this study, EtOH significantly (P < 0.05) induced IKKα mRNA expression and protein expression in rat liver tissues, and quercetin alone or catechin alone suppressed such EtOH-induced IKKα mRNA expression and protein expression, with the quercetin-catechin combination leading to a greater suppression (Fig. 4A-C). Cellular ROS generation is essential to redox signaling, and ROS can interact with NF-κB signaling pathways, with IKK as a primary target for ROS and a converging point of NF-κB activation [46], and redox active p53 directing and arranging cellular responses [47]. Excessive ROS can activate injury-related pathways such as the NF-κB inflammatory pathway and p53 apoptotic pathway [48]. Therefore, the signaling pathways relevant to the regulation of oxidative stress such as NF-κB and p53 routes were investigated here. In this study, there was no significant difference (P < 0.05) between quercetin alone and catechin alone in reducing protein expression and phosphorylation of IKKβ, IκBα and NF-κB p65 (Fig. S1). The expressions of both p53 and phospho-p53 (phosphorylation on Ser20) were inhibited by quercetin alone, catechin alone or their combination, with the inhibition effectiveness decreasing in the order of quercetin-catechin combination > catechin > quercetin (Fig. 4B and D). The expression of pro-apoptosis protein, Bax as a downstream effector of p53, was suppressed by each of the three flavonoid treatments, with the quercetin-catechin combination having the greatest suppressive effect (Fig. 4B). EtOH induced the decrease of Bcl-2/Bax ratio, compared with the control group. The three flavonoid treatments all made the Bcl-2/Bax ratio up to the level much higher than that of the EtOH alone-treated group (the quercetin-catechin combination led to the largest increment) (Fig. 4E). These results showed that quercetin and catechin acted jointly to exert an enhanced ameliorating effect against oxidative stress, possibly through regulating cooperatively the IKKα/p53 signaling pathway in AALI. Similar to the results of the animal experiments, all the three flavonoid treatments inhibited significantly (P < 0.05) H2O2-induced IKKα mRNA and protein expression, with the quercetin-catechin combination showing a greater inhibition in HepG2 and in HHL-5 cells (Fig. 4F-G). The quercetin and catechin worked jointly to provide an enhanced suppression on the expressions of p53 and its phosphorylation level. ### The interaction of FOXO3 with quercetin and/or catechin

#### Quercetin and catechin bound to FOXO3 at different sites revealed by molecular docking
  As shown above, both quercetin and catechin were found able to inhibit the binding of FOXO3 to IKKα promoter and these two flavonoids could work together to exert an enhanced inhibitory effect. Accordingly, understanding the interactions between quercetin and/or catechin and FOXO3 protein becomes imperative. In this section, such interactions were examined by molecular docking. The docking results suggested that quercetin and catechin mainly interacted with the FOXO3 protein via hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions. The surface, 3D and 2D docking poses of quercetin and/or catechin with the FOXO3 protein are shown in Fig. 6. The docking score (to predict binding affinity) for quercetin and catechin were −5.23 kcal/mol and −5.06 kcal/mol, respectively. The more negative score of quercetin suggests its stronger binding to FOXO3, compared with catechin. According to the chemical structure of quercetin, quercetin can act as a donor (via its hydroxyl groups) and an acceptor (via its oxygen atoms) for the formation hydrogen bonds with amino acids of proteins. Quercetin interacted with the amino acids, Ser218, Asn237, Asp239, Gly244 and Arg248, of FOXO3 to form hydrogen bonds. Besides its hydrophilic surface region, quercetin is generally hydrophobic due to the two aromatic rings. Therefore, quercetin might adsorb onto the hydrophobic amino acid residues of the FOXO3 protein, including Arg219, Trp234, Pro238, Lys242, Ser243 and Lys245. Such hydrophobic interactions enhanced the quercetin-FOXO3 binding and stabilize FOXO3 protein. Given the similarity between quercetin and catechin in their chemical structures (Fig. 6C), it is not surprising that both hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions have also taken place between catechin and FOXO3. Catechin bound to FOXO3 via hydrogen bonds with Pro247, Arg249, Phe220 and Leu214 as well as hydrophobic interactions with Ser215, Ser218, Ala246, and Met221 of FOXO3. Therefore, both flavonoids mainly bound to the DNA-binding domain (DBD) (residues 158–240) of FOXO3 [37]. The stronger quercetin-FOXO3 binding than catechin-FOXO3 binding was likely due to quercetin’s additional C2 = C3-double bond conjugated to the 4-oxo group in its C ring (which is able to delocalize an unpaired electron from its B ring).Fig. 6The interaction between quercetin and/or catechin and FOXO3 protein by molecular docking.   Previous studies [49], [50] reported that FOXO3 has a centrally located, winged helix folded and structured DNA-binding forkhead (FH) domain that contains three α-helices (H1-H3), a short twisted three-stranded antiparallel β-sheet (S1–S3) and two wing-like loops (W1, W2) in the configuration of H1-S1-H2-H3-S2-W1-S3-W2. This FH domain is flanked by a flexible -N terminal loop region and -C terminal coil region (which contains a transactivation domain). Both the termini have intrinsically disordered regions containing different types of secondary structure elements, involving unevenly distributed hydrophobic and charged amino acids. Therefore, FOXO3 has a number of sites for various ligands. In this study, the molecular docking revealed that quercetin and catechin could interact simultaneously with the FOXO3 protein: Quercetin bound to the groove of FOXO3 formed between the winged helix H3 and C-terminal coil, whilst catechin primarily bound to the C-terminal coil via hydrogen bonds. Besides its unique ketocarbonyl group, quercetin has an expanded planar structure through its 2, 3-double bond, the C3–OH group and C-ring. In comparison, catechin possesses a non-planar structure. Such differences in molecular structure and spatial arrangement between quercetin and catechin accounted for their different bindings (binding scores and binding sites) to FOXO3.

#### Systems containing FOXO3 in the absence and presence of quercetin and/or catechin examined by UV–Vis absorption spectroscopy
  The absorption of UV radiation over 230–300 nm by intrinsic chromophores in proteins arises from the aromatic side-chains of Tyr and Trp residues (and to a small extent from the Phe residue and disulfide bonds if present). Trp contributes the most to the absorbance change at 230 nm, with less contributions from Tyr and Phe. Absorption around 230 nm is sensitive to protein conformation especially protein unfolding, which correlates well with the absorbance change at 280 nm [52]. As shown by the native FOXO3 absorption spectra when quercetin or catechin was present (Fig. 7B and C), the absorbance at 280 nm increased by 34.45% and 27.32% for the FOXO3-quercetin system and FOXO3-catechin system, respectively, compared with the FOXO3 protein alone. According to the docking results (Fig. 6), FOXO3′s amino acid residues, Ser218, Gly244, Asn237, Asp239, Arg248, Arg219, Trp234, Pro238, Lys242, Ser243 and Lys245 were the key binding sites for quercetin, and FOXO3′s Ser215, Phe220, Pro247, Arg249, Ala246, Leu214, Ser218 and Met221 were the key binding sites for catechin.

  To further confirm the binding of quercetin and catechin to FOXO3, the respective amino acid residues for hydrogen bonds were mutated to alanine. The mutation of FOXO3′s binding sites (Ser218, Asn237, Asp239, Gly244 and Arg248) for quercetin yielded “mutated FOXO3-1”, and the mutation of FOXO3’s binding sites (Leu214, Phe220, Pro247 and Arg249) for catechin yielded “mutated FOXO3-2”. When quercetin interacted with mutated FOXO3-1, the absorbance at 280 nm increased by 31.42% (Fig. 7D). The absorbance at 280 nm for the system of catechin and mutated FOXO3-2 increased by 25.07% (Fig. 7E). Therefore, the presence quercetin/catechin led to a small absorbance increment for the mutated FOXO3 than for the native FOXO3 protein, suggesting that the mutated sites in FOXO3 were the binding sites for quercetin/catechin. As shown in Fig. 7F, the absorption peak at 280 nm exhibited an intensity increase (by 41.26%) and a slight blue shift for the native FOXO3 in the presence of the quercetin-catechin combination. While the co-occurrence of FOXO3 and quercetin and/or catechin could increase the absorbance at 280 nm, the substantial UV–Vis absorption at 280 nm could also come from quercetin/catechin and some of their derivatives. Consequently, the limitations of current UV–Vis absorption approach prevented it from conclusively confirming the existence of a direct interaction between FOXO3 and quercetin and/or catechin, though the UV absorption experiments here revealed the difference in quercetin/catechin-induced absorbance increment between the native FOXO3 system and the mutated FOXO3 system.


#### Fluorescence quenching in FOXO3 induced by quercetin and/or catechin
  Protein fluorescence quenching (emission spectral change) has been used to examine protein–ligand interactions especially non-covalent binding, since the fluorescence of aromatic chromophores in proteins (Trp, Tyr and Phe) may be utilized as probes of protein structure [53]. As shown in Fig. 8, when excited at 280 nm, the FOXO3 protein exhibited a high fluorescence emission peak at 330 nm. After quercetin was added, the fluorescence intensity of FOXO3 dropped (fluorescence suppression became greater for an increased quercetin concentration; 81.15% suppression at 12 μg/mL) (Fig. 8A), addition of catechin alone (fluorescence suppression became greater for an increased catechin concentration; 64.45% suppression at 12 μg/mL) (Fig. 8B), or addition of quercetin-catechin combination (fluorescence suppression became greater for increased quercetin and catechin concentrations; 84.78% suppression at 6 μg/mL for quercetin and 6 μg/mL for catechin) (Fig. 8C). A small red shift (4 nm) was detected in the maximum emission wavelength when a quercetin-catechin combination was used, indicating that molecular interaction caused conformational changes in proteins and/or alterations in the fluorophores’ microenvironments (Trp, Tyr and/or Phe) [54]. Therefore, the reduction in FOXO3′s fluorescence intensity with quercetin was due partially to the involvement of the Trp234 residue of FOXO3 in the hydrophobic interaction with quercetin. It was also possible that the interactions of FOXO3 with both quercetin and catechin made the microenvironments around the fluorophores more polar, induced charge redistribution during excitation, altered FOXO3 conformation, and enhanced the exposure of buried residues (Trp, or Tyr and/or Phe) of FOXO3 (including the Trp157 in the -N terminal region, Trp160 in H1, Trp186 in H2, Trp209 in H3, and Trp236 in –C terminal coil region) [55].Fig. 8The interaction of FOXO3 protein with quercetin and/or catechin by fluorescence spectroscopy.   The Stern-Volmer equation was used to investigate the fluorescence quenching mechanisms in more detail. As shown in Fig. 8D-F, the quenching constant Kq values for FOXO3 with quercetin alone, catechin alone or the quercetin-catechin combination were 1.0800 × 1013, 6.5804 × 1012 and 1.4033 × 1013 M−1S−1, respectively. The Kq value was approximately 100 or 1000 times the maximum scatter collision quenching constant of dynamic quenching (2.0 × 1010 M−1S−1), indicating that the static quenching was the initial cause of the FOXO3 fluorescence quenching caused by quercetin and/or catechin (which favored the development of a protein–ligand complex) [56]. The results obtained based on the double logarithmic equation (Fig. 8G-I) revealed that the binding constant Ka values for FOXO3 with quercetin alone and with catechin alone were 1.3329 × 105 M−1 and 5.1109 × 104 M−1, respectively. In comparison, the Ka value for FOXO3 with the quercetin-catechin combination was 1.3452 × 106 M−1. These results indicated a higher affinity for the FOXO3-quercetin + catechin complex than for the FOXO3-quercetin complex or FOXO3-catechin complex. The enhanced effect achieved in the co-presence of quercetin and catechin was confirmed.

  The above-mentioned mutant FOXO3 proteins were also used for the fluorescence quenching experiments. Compared with the native (wild type) FOXO3, the interaction of quercetin or catechin with the mutated FOXO3 protein was weaker (Fig. 8J-O). The fluorescence intensities of the mutated FOXO3 decreased by 64.45% and 46.50%, respectively, in the presence of quercetin or catechin (Fig. 8J and M), with their Ka values being reduced to 2.0739 × 104 and 1.3380 × 103 M−1, respectively. These findings suggested that the binding affinity of the mutated FOXO3 with quercetin or catechin was impaired due to the mutation of the corresponding binding sites for quercetin or catechin. These results again confirmed the occurrence of separate, simultaneous and coordinating bindings of quercetin and catechin to the native FOXO3 protein.


#### Circular dichroism (CD) tracked the changes in the secondary structures of FOXO3 induced by quercetin and/or catechin
  Protein secondary structure changes in the far UV region (190–250 nm) and tertiary structure changes in the near UV region (250–390 nm) can both be tracked by CD. Thus, CD has been used for examining the interaction of the protein with small compounds [57]. Fig. 9 shows the CD spectra of FOXO3 with quercetin alone, catechin alone and the quercetin-catechin combination. When the native FOXO3 interacted with quercetin, FOXO3′s α-helix content decreased from 12.6% to 6.6%, FOXO3′s β-sheet content increased from 30.3% to 44.5%, and the content of random coils in FOXO3 increased from 34.9% to 37.2% (Fig. 9A). In comparison, the interaction between the native FOXO3 and catechin significantly reduced FOXO3′s α-helix content (from 12.6% to 7.8%) with minimal change in the content of random coils (from 34.9% to 34.7%) (Fig. 9A). The binding of quercetin or catechin to FOXO3 promoted the stretching of peptide chain, making FOXO3 adopt a less compact and more extended conformation. Moreover, the interaction of the native FOXO3 with the quercetin-catechin combination caused the decrease of α-helix content from 12.6% to 3.0%, increase of β-sheet content from 30.3% to 67.6%, and increase of random coils from 34.9% to 38.6%. These conformational changes led to a more stretchable and extendable FOXO3-quercetin + catechin complex, indicating the joint actions of quercetin and catechin towards the different secondary structures of the native FOXO3 protein. As reported previously [36], the interaction between a protein and phenolic compound is affected by their initial structures, and the dynamic structures alterations in response to the environment (such as changes in ionic strength and matrix composition). Consequently, the alteration in FOXO3′s conformation caused by the simultaneous FOXO3-quercetin and FOXO3-catechin interactions would differ from FOXO3′s conformational changes caused by the sole interaction between FOXO3 and quercetin/catechin.Fig. 9The interaction of FOXO3 protein with quercetin and/or catechin revealed by circular dichroism.   Some differences were found in the CD spectrum of the native FOXO3 (Fig. 9A) and the CD spectra of mutated FOXO3-1 and mutated FOXO3-2 (Fig. 9B and C), indicating that either of the two mutations of FOXO3 caused alterations in the secondary structures of FOXO3 protein. When quercetin bound to the mutated FOXO3-1, the content of β-sheet increased from 35.8% to 59.0%, the contents of β-turn and random coils decreased from 25.8% to 15.2% and from 38.0% to 34.7%, respectively, as well as the α-helix content didn’t change. The binding of the mutated FOXO3-2 with catechin reduced the α-helix content (from 11.0% to 4.4%), enhanced the β-sheet content (from 34.3% to 53.5%), β-turn content (from 16.7% to 18.7%), and random coils content (from 33.6% to 37.3%). These results suggested that besides the binding of quercetin and/or catechin to their respective binding sites in the native FOXO3, other types of interactions (involving other amino acid residues instead of those identified binding sites) might also take place between the two flavonoids and native FOXO3.

  Taken together, quercetin and catechin could interact simultaneously with the different sites around DBD of FOXO3, thereby influencing cooperatively the DNA binding to suppress its downstream genes transcription.


## Discussion
  Oxidative stress is a key contributor to AALI, causing intracytoplasmic lipid accumulation, inflammatory infiltration and local necrosis of hepatocytes [14]. Quercetin and catechin are two flavonoids with potent chemopreventive and therapeutic properties, including the ability to scavenge ROS derived from unhealthy food consumption or abnormal food metabolism inside the human body. Quercetin and catechin have demonstrated synergistic or enhanced effects in antioxidation [38], anti-inflammation [17] and hypertension prevention [18] processes. However, the molecular mechanism underlying these synergistic/enhanced effects of quercetin and catechin against oxidative stress to ameliorate AALI, especially their influences on associated signaling pathway(s) and their interactions with relevant TFs, remain unclear. This study firstly showed the underlying mechanisms for the enhanced protection provided jointly by quercetin and catechin on alleviating AALI in EtOH-treated rats. Quercetin and catechin worked together to exert enhanced effectiveness to inhibit the EtOH-induced pathological abnormalities of livers, reduce ALT and AST levels, scavenge intracellular ROS (especially H2O2), enhance the activities of antioxidant enzymes (CAT, SOD and GPx), and reduce MDA level. Previous research showed that in patients with ALD, the production of alcohol-mediated ROS in the liver, primarily H2O2, contributed to disease development and even hepatocellular carcinoma formation [58]. In oxidative stress, H2O2 is also a crucial redox signaling molecule [59]. The findings of this investgation also confirmed that EtOH-initiated oxidative stress in the rat livers was mainly caused by H2O2. Then, the protective effect and mechanism of quercetin and catechin against H2O2-induced oxidative stress were further examined in HepG2 and in HHL-5 cells. Quercetin and catechin were also found to work jointly to provide an enhanced protective effect against the cell damages caused by H2O2 in HepG2 and in HHL-5 cells. The combination of quercetin and catechin enhanced significantly the efficacies to increase cell viability, suppress ROS generation, promote SOD, CAT and GPx activities, and inhibit MDA level and cell apoptosis in H2O2-treated HepG2 or HHL-5 cells, compared with quercetin alone or catechin alone.

  ROS is an essential component of redox signaling and can activate TFs and other functional proteins, including Keap1-Nrf2, AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) and NF-κB in response to stress [60], [61]. The Keap1-Nrf2 signaling pathway represents an intracellular defense mechanism to counteract oxidative stress, as this pathway is involved in numerous physiological and pathological processes and its activation can trigger a protective antioxidant response [62]. In this study, quercetin and catechin significantly worked together to enhance the release from Keap1 and nuclear translocation of Nrf2 to alleviate EtOH-initiated liver injury, suggesting that Nrf2 might play a critical role in the enhanced antioxidative effect provided by quercetin and catechin. In this study, IKKα was found to be a differentially expressed gene among quercetin alone, catechin alone and their combination by RNA-sequence analysis, suggesting that IKKα might be another potential target of the enhanced antioxidant action. Quercetin and catechin could act jointly, providing an enhanced inhibition on IKKα's mRNA and protein expressions in the rat livers and the hepatocytes (as revealed by RT-PCR and Western blot analyses). IKKα is well known as a member of the NF-κB signaling pathways. IKKα was found able to regulate the transcription activity of p53 by phosphorylation at Ser20 in response to ROS [39]. IKKα-mediated phosphorylation can cause cAMP response element-binding protein preferentially bind to NF-κB rather than p53, thereby up-regulating NF-κB-mediated anti-apoptotic genes while down-regulating p53-mediated pro-apoptotic genes [63]. In this study, quercetin and catechin not only worked together to enhance the activation of Nrf2, but also acted jointly to exert an enhanced inhibition towards IKKα expression via the NF-κB-independent pathway. Quercetin and catechin could exert an enhanced inhibitory effect on the expression of p53 and phosphor-p53 (Ser20), reduction of Bax protein, and increase in Bcl-2 protein expression, to alleviate cell apoptosis.

  Oxidative stress and inflammation are known as “evil twins of aging” [64]. Crosstalk and interplay between inflammation and oxidative stress were found in ALD. The Nrf2 and NF-κB pathways are co-regulators for oxidative stress and inflammation, and the interplay between these pathways through both transcriptional and post-transcriptional mechanisms offers dynamic responses to the changes of the biological environment (e.g. cellular redox balance) [65]. Therefore, it is of interest to find out whether or not there was an enhanced effect provided by quercetin and catechin in regulating Nrf2 and IKKα signaling pathways, and how IKKα and p53 interacted in response to oxidative stress. In this study, silencing IKKα inhibited p53 expression, whilst overexpression of IKKα promoted p53 expression. In particular, IKKα responded stronger to the joint action of quercetin and catechin compared with Nrf2. The inhibition of IKKα led to the elimination of the enhanced effect exerted by quercetin and catechin on cell viability and ROS production. These results of this investigation further confirmed the critical role of IKKα in the joint action of quercetin and catechin. Quercetin and catechin could exert an enhanced inhibition towards IKKα transcription and translation to suppress downstream p53 and apoptosis-related proteins, leading to the amelioration of H2O2-induced oxidative damage. The findings were in agreement with the published reports. Previous research found that IKKα responded to canonical and non-canonical NF-κB stimuli, and exhibited a range of functions during NF-κB-independent signaling [66]. Inhibition of IKKα was reported to protect neuronal cells against oxygen-glucose deprivation damage [67]. IKKα was found to be downregulated by CRSP8 via the binding of CRSP8 to IKKα promoter region (-257 ∼ -143), causing the suppression of the growth, migration and invasion of anaplastic thyroid cancer cells [68]. The binding of ETS1 to IKKα promoter was reported to enhance IKKα transcription in cisplatin (DDP) resistant TNBC cells [69]. The transcriptional co-activator CITED2 could regulate IKKα expression through binding directly to IKKα promoter to modulate the metastasis of human breast cancer cells [70].

  The associations of FOXO3 with IKKα, p53 and NF-κB have been described in the Introduction section. FOXO3 can function as a protective factor preventing alcoholic liver injury [71]. Activating FOXO3 by JNK phosphorylation was found in several mouse model of liver fibrosis [72]. As a multispecific TF, FOXO3 accounts for multiple and conflicting transcriptional events, and plays a proapoptotic role in the liver under oxidative stress [73]. The CITED2/IKKα axis can be regulated by FOXO3 binding to the enhancer of CITED2, causing the acceleration of apoptosis in neuronal cells triggered by oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD) [70]. In the presence of a stressful stimulus, FOXOs can either encourage apoptotic signaling cascades or activate stress resistance genes (such as SOD, CAT or GADD45) to reduce oxidative damage [74]. Dihydroquercetin was found able to inhibit FOXO3 and FOXO3-activated NF-κB signaling pathway via miR-132-3p, resulting in the mitigation of LPS-induced inflammation and apoptosis [75]. Overexpression of miR-27a-3p could inhibit FOXO3 to attenuate LPS-induced inflammation and apoptosis in alveolar epithelial cells through suppressing NAPDH/ROS activation [76]. Though both the unphosphorylated FOXO3 and the FOXO3 phosphorylated on serine 574 (p-574-FOXO3) can bind to the Bcl-2 promoter, these two forms of FOXO3 exhibit different functions: the unphosphorylated FOXO3 acts as a transcriptional activator, whilst the p-574-FOXO3 serves as a transcriptional repressor [77]. In this study, FOXO3 was able to bind directly to IKKα promoter region to facilitate the transcription of IKKα. Quercetin and catechin acted jointly to inhibit the binding of FOXO3 to IKKα promoter sequence. The molecular docking experiments in this study revealed that quercetin and catechin could interact with the different sites around the DBD of FOXO3 protein. It is known that FOXO3 contains a conserved FH domain (from Gly158 to Ser251) with a winged-helix fold to recognize DNA. In this study, quercetin likely bound to the major groove of FOXO3-DBD (via amino acid residues Ser218, Asn237, Asp239, Gly244, Arg248, Arg219, Trp234, Pro238, Lys242, Ser243 and Lys245), thereby blocking the interaction of FOXO3 with the target gene to restrain its transcription. Catechin might interact with FOXO3 at the C-terminus, thereby being enveloped by the C-terminal coil (via amino acid residues Leu214, Phe220, Pro247, Arg249, Ala246, Ser215, Ser218 and Met221), which helped stabilize the protein-DNA complex. It was pointed out previously that the absence of the C-terminal domain could cause almost complete loss of the binding affinity to DNA [37]. The biological activities and health regulations of flavonoids are inseparable from the interaction with functional proteins in vivo [35]. EGCG promoted tumor cell apoptosis and inhibited tumor development by directly interacting with tumor suppressor p53 protein. EGCG was mainly bound to the N-terminal domain (NTD) of p53, which is the binding region of p53 regulated kinase MDM2. EGCG-p53 binding inhibits p53-MDM2 binding, thereby inhibiting p53 ubiquitination and degradation [78]. In this study, quercetin and catechin interacted directly with different sites of FOXO3 protein, and altered FOXO3′s secondary structures. Such joint interactions of quercetin and catechin would affect the binding of FOXO3 to the target gene, and ultimately influence the stability of FOXO3-DNA complex and the transcription of its downstream genes.

  In addition, the direct binding of quercetin and/or catechin to FOXO3 protein was also examined by the thermal shift assay, UV–Vis absorption spectroscopy, fluorescence spectroscopy and CD spectroscopy, in addition to the above-discussed molecular docking experiments. The native FOXO3 protein along with the two mutant FOXO3 proteins (whose binding sites for quercetin and catechin were mutated respectively) were employed to elucidate FOXO3′s binding sites for quercetin and catechin as well as other possible FOXO3-quercetin/catechin interactions. The significantly decreased affinity of quercetin or catechin to the corresponding mutant FOXO3 protein indicated again that quercetin and catechin bound directly to FOXO3 protein at different sites, and there existed other factors affecting the interactions between FOXO3 and the two flavonoids. Besides the direct bindings between FOXO3 and the two flavonoids, other types of interactions also took place between the two flavonoids and FOXO3 via other amino acid residues (instead of those identified binding sites for quercetin and catechin, respectively). The conformational changes of FOXO3 would cause these other kinds of FOXO3-flavonoid interactions to vary dynamically. It was possible that the interactions of FOXO3 with the two flavonoids altered dynamically the microenvironments around the fluorophores (e.g. becoming more polar) and FOXO3 conformation (e.g. changing the proportions of α-helix, β-sheet, β-turn and random coils), thereby exposing the initially buried amino acid residues of FOXO3 such as the Trp157 in the -N terminal, Trp160 in H1, Trp186 in H2, Trp209 in H3 and Trp236 in –C terminal, as well as other residues like Tyr and Phe. Previous research revealed that quercetin, ferulic acid and cinnamic acid acted synergistically to induce greater inhibition of tyrosinase to suppress enzymatic browning in food through their respective bindings to the different sites of the tyrosinase to inhibit synergistically tyrosinase’s activity [79]. Apigenin and curcumin were found to exert a synergistic anticancer activity through their bindings to the different sites on tubulin, thereby inhibiting the formation of microtubules and inducing apoptosis in cancer cells [80]. Therefore, the direct binding of quercetin and catechin to FOXO3 at different sites is not surprising, initiating an enhanced inhibition towards IKKα transcription to ameliorate apoptosis initiated by H2O2-induced oxidative damage in HepG2 cells.


## Conclusion
  This study demonstrated the enhanced protection provided by the quercetin-catechin combination against EtOH-induced oxidative stress to alleviate AALI in rats. Quercetin and catechin could directly bind to the FOXO3 protein at different sites, thereby inhibiting the interaction of FOXO3 with IKKα promoter to suppress IKKα transcription, inhibit IKKα-p53 expression, ameliorate cell apoptosis, and ultimately alleviate oxidative damage to the liver (Fig. 10). In addition to the direct binding of quercetin and catechin to FOXO3, other forms of interactions between FOXO3 and the two flavonoids also exist, and these interactions would change due to conformational changes of FOXO3 initiated by the direct FOXO3-flavonoid binding and other environmental factors. The regulatory actions of quercetin and catechin are cooperative and multi-targeting, though further studies are needed to corroborate this finding. This research is the first study to find that FOXO3 can directly bind to the IKKα promoter to regulate the transcription of IKKα. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive investigation into the molecular mechanisms underlying the enhanced defense of quercetin and catechin against oxidative stress and associated damage at the transcriptional level, and from the perspectives of the relationships between polyphenols and functional proteins as well as the crosstalk between signaling pathways. This study demonstrates a new strategy for examining the joint effect and underlying molecular mechanism for multiple dietary flavonoids towards the functional proteins inside biological systems (e.g. humans and animals) to provide chemopreventive and chemotherapeutic efficacies.Fig. 10Possible mechanisms underlying the enhanced protection offered by quercetin and catechin against EtOH-induced oxidative damage in the rat liver, including their influences on FOXO3, IKKα and p53.
