# A complex network of interdomain interactions underlies the conformational ensemble of monomeric TDP‐43 and modulates its phase behavior


## Abstract
  TAR DNA‐binding protein 43 (TDP‐43) is a multidomain protein involved in the regulation of RNA metabolism, and its aggregates have been observed in neurodegenerative diseases, including amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and frontotemporal dementia (FTD). Numerous studies indicate TDP‐43 can undergo liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) in vitro and is a component of biological condensates. Homo‐oligomerization via the folded N‐terminal domain (aa:1–77) and the conserved helical region (aa:319–341) of the disordered, C‐terminal domain is found to be an important driver of TDP‐43 phase separation. However, a comprehensive molecular view of TDP‐43 phase separation, particularly regarding the nature of heterodomain interactions, is lacking due to the challenges associated with its stability and purification. Here, we utilize all‐atom and coarse‐grained (CG) molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to uncover the network of interdomain interactions implicated in TDP‐43 phase separation. All‐atom simulations uncovered the presence of transient, interdomain interactions involving flexible linkers, RNA‐recognition motif (RRM) domains and a charged segment of disordered C‐terminal domain (CTD). CG simulations indicate these inter‐domain interactions which affect the conformational landscape of TDP‐43 in the dilute phase are also prevalent in the condensed phase. Finally, sequence and surface charge distribution analysis coupled with all‐atom simulations (at high salt) confirmed that the transient interdomain contacts are predominantly electrostatic in nature. Overall, our findings from multiscale simulations lead to a greater appreciation of the complex interaction network underlying the structural landscape and phase separation of TDP‐43.


## INTRODUCTION
  A large majority of proteins (>70%) encoded by the eukaryotic genome are multidomain in nature (Han et al., 2007) and evolved via large‐scale duplication and rearrangement of folded domains derived from a limited set of domain families. In addition to flexible linkers (<30 residues) connecting folded domains, multidomain proteins may also contain longer, disordered regions which can promote intermolecular association via site‐specific (e.g., short linear motifs) and non‐specific interactions (Wright & Dyson, 2015). Multidomain proteins can exhibit high “valency,” referring to the number of interaction sites available to bind their biomolecular partners. The multivalent nature of these proteins allows them to effectively serve as “scaffolds” that transiently anchor and organize key proteins and exert their influence downstream in cellular signaling cascades.

  Multidomain proteins are also implicated in the formation of higher‐order assemblies such as “membraneless organelles” or biomolecular condensates (Banani et al., 2017; Li et al., 2012). Such assemblies may be nuclear (e.g., heterochromatin, nucleoli) or cytoplasmic (e.g., stress granules, P‐bodies), and implicated in a variety of cellular functions such as DNA damage response, RNA splicing, transcription, and translation (Gomes & Shorter, 2019). The assembly and internal architecture of these condensates is dependent on the network of intermolecular interactions formed between multivalent, multidomain proteins and nucleic acids (Lyon et al., 2021; Sanders et al., 2020). Multidomain proteins can undergo liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) in vitro (Boeynaems et al., 2018; Hyman et al., 2014; Mohanty et al., 2022) once the protein concentration exceeds a critical threshold referred to as the saturation concentration (csat) within a defined set of other physical parameters (e.g., temperature, pH, and salt). At the domain level, LLPS behavior can be modulated by two types of interactions: (i) homodomain, that is, interactions between the same domain on different protein molecules and (ii) heterodomain, that is, interactions between different domains on either the same or different molecules.

  TDP‐43 is a multidomain, nucleic acid‐binding protein implicated in various processes related to RNA metabolism such as splicing, transport, and translation (Cohen et al., 2011). TDP‐43 is found in ubiquitin‐positive cytoplasmic inclusions which are a hallmark of neurodegenerative diseases such as Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and Frontotemporal dementia (FTD) (Neumann et al., 2006). TDP‐43 is component of stress granules (SGs)—cytoplasmic condensates which form in response to stress signals (e.g., heat shock) and also comprise other multidomain proteins such as FUS, hnRNPA1/2, UBQLN2, and G3BP1 (Dao et al., 2018; Protter & Parker, 2016; Yang et al., 2020). Recent work by Cleveland and colleagues indicates that the loss of TDP‐43 binding to nuclear RNA either due to ALS‐related mutations (P112H, K181E, K263E) or post‐translational modifications (PTMs) such as lysine acetylation (K145/192) leads to the formation of nuclear condensates called “anisosomes” in association with HSP70 chaperones (Yu et al., 2021). Independent of SGs, TDP‐43 can undergo cytoplasmic de‐mixing in association with the HSP70 chaperone—HSPB1 (Lu et al., 2022). Transient oxidative stress, ATP depletion or proteasome inhibition promotes liquid‐to‐solid transition (Mathieu et al., 2020; Szała‐Mendyk et al., 2023) of TDP‐43 condensates (Chang et al., 2013; Gu et al., 2021; Lu et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2021) which may be related to the onset of TDP‐43 proteinopathies.

  The domain architecture of TDP‐43 (Figure 1) consists of a folded N‐terminal domain (NTD, aa:1–78) (Afroz et al., 2017), two RNA‐recognition motif domains (RRM1/2, aa:104–263) (Lukavsky et al., 2013), and a disordered C‐terminal region (CTD, aa:264–414) that contains an evolutionarily conserved region known to populate a transient α‐helical structure (CR, aa:319–341) (Conicella et al., 2016). Further, short linker regions connect NTD to RRM1 (aa:79–103) and RRM1 to RRM2 (aa:177–191). The NTD is known to oligomerize through head‐to‐tail interactions (Afroz et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2018) while the CTD self‐assembles by forming site‐specific helix–helix contacts (Conicella et al., 2016, 2020) which are enhanced by transient interactions between the flanking disordered regions (Mohanty et al., 2023). PTMs and mutations within either NTD or CTD can modulate LLPS and cellular function (Conicella et al., 2020; Gruijs da Silva et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2018).

  RRM domains were shown to promote the formation of liquid‐like droplets for a TDP‐43 construct lacking the C‐terminal domain and prevented the rapid aggregation of an NTD‐linker construct in vitro (Carter et al., 2021). Although RRM1/2 domains can undergo hetero‐oligomerization upon binding to long UG‐rich RNA sequences (Rengifo‐Gonzalez et al., 2021), it is unclear how these domains influence the phase separation of TDP‐43 upon loss of RNA binding due to disease‐related mutations or PTMs. The N‐terminal linker contains a nuclear localization signal (NLS) recognized by importin‐α (K d ~ 10 nM) (Doll et al., 2022; Guo et al., 2018), which solubilizes TDP‐43 aggregates. The NLS region also interacts with the poly (ADP‐ribose) to promote the phase separation of TDP‐43 in vitro and its localization to stress granules in neurons (McGurk et al., 2018). Nevertheless, the direct contributions of N‐terminal linker in driving the phase separation and its potential interactions with other TDP‐43 domains remain unknown. Additionally, the RRM1‐2 linker harbors an ALS mutation site (K181E) which disrupts RNA binding and sequesters TDP‐43 into inclusion bodies (Chen et al., 2019), suggesting a possible role for this linker in phase separation. In summary, while the contributions of NTD‐ and CTD‐mediated homodomain interactions toward the phase separation of TDP‐43 are well‐established, the nature of hetero‐domain interactions remains to be properly investigated.

  Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations can serve as a valuable complement to experiments (Fawzi et al., 2021), particularly in the case of multidomain proteins such as TDP‐43, which are difficult to characterize in vitro due to their low solubility (Wright et al., 2020). Previous studies utilized all‐atom (AA) and coarse‐grained (CG) molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to characterize the structure, dynamics and interactions implicated in the phase of separation of TDP‐43 CTD and full‐length protein (Conicella et al., 2016, 2020; Ingólfsson et al., 2023; Mohanty et al., 2023). Here, we employ a multiscale simulation approach which utilizes both AA and CG simulations to characterize the monomeric (dilute phase) TDP‐43 full‐length ensemble and nature of inter‐domain interactions which underlie its phase separation. Our study uncovers a diverse array of hetero‐domain interactions which modulate the conformational landscape of TDP‐43 monomer (dilute phase) and occur within the condensed phase alongside homodomain interactions. The hetero‐domains interactions were found to be transient and predominantly electrostatic in nature which is broadly consistent with the known sensitivity of TDP‐43 phase separation to salt (Krainer et al., 2021), pH (Staderini et al., 2022) and charge‐modulating PTMs such as lysine acetylation (Cohen et al., 2015) and serine phosphorylation (Gruijs da Silva et al., 2022). Collectively, our findings unveil a complex network of homo‐ and hetero‐domain interactions which modulate the dilute phase conformational landscape and phase separation of TDP‐43.


## RESULTS

### TDP‐43 populates a diverse conformational ensemble in the monomeric state (dilute phase)
  The conformational flexibility associated with linker and disordered regions coupled with the intramolecular interactions which arise upon tethering to folded domains (Mittal et al., 2018; Taneja & Holehouse, 2021) gives rise to a complex free energy landscape (FEL) for multidomain proteins, comprising of a dynamic ensemble of structures (Tompa, 2012). The function of multidomain proteins in cellular signaling cascades is linked to the modulation of their FEL via mutations, PTMs, and the availability of appropriate interaction partners (Basak et al., 2021; McCann et al., 2012; Yanez Orozco et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2013). To characterize the conformational landscape of wild‐type TDP‐43 (414 aa), we first performed atomistic simulations (3 replicates × 5 μs) at 100 mM NaCl.

  The simulations were performed using the AMBER99SBws force field which provides an accurate description of disordered protein ensembles and prevents excessive association between protein chains (Best et al., 2014; Zerze et al., 2019). Further, dihedral modifications for serine (S), threonine (T) and glutamine residues (Q) were incorporated as these were shown to improve the conformational description of prion‐like, disordered domains such as TDP‐43 CTD (Tang et al., 2020). Based on radius of gyration (R g) calculations, a dynamic ensemble of TDP‐43 conformations was observed on the microsecond timescale across the three simulation trajectories (Figure S1a,b). The stability of folded domains was confirmed based on distance root mean square (dRMS) analysis of Cα atoms and is shown in Figure S2a. In addition, the C‐terminal domain was found to populate a transient α‐helical structure which is consistent with NMR experiments (Figure S2b).

  The mean R g for the full‐length protein computed across three independent trajectories was 3.56 ± 0.40 nm. The R g probability distribution computed for the TDP‐43 simulation ensemble indicated a wide range of conformations which were arbitrarily classified into three categories based on R g‐values (Figure 2a,b): (i) compact (<3.0 nm), (ii) intermediate (3.0–4.0 nm) and (iii) extended conformations (>4.0 nm). Hasnain and colleagues (Wright et al., 2020) characterized a TDP‐43 mutant wherein surface‐exposed tryptophan residues were mutated to alanine (TDP‐43WtoA) to prevent oligomerization and enhance monomer solubility. The authors reported a R g‐value of 4.1 nm based on small‐angle x‐ray scattering (SAXS) which is ~13% more expanded than the MD‐derived ensemble (<R g > =3.56 ± 0.40 nm). However, a direct comparison of our simulation ensemble to SAXS data may not be appropriate due to presence of several Tryptophan to alanine substitutions in the experimental construct which may promote CTD expansion (Li et al., 2018; Martin et al., 2020; Martin, Harmon, et al., 2021) and the possible use of a higher salt concentration (300 mM NaCl) for data collection (Wright et al., 2020). The SAXS‐derived ensemble also indicated that NTD‐RRM1/2 was more compact relative to CTD which populated a heterogeneous ensemble of conformations. In contrast, we observed conformational heterogeneity (i.e., extended, and compact states) for both NTD‐RRM1/2 and CTD across three different trajectories (Figure S1c,d). Classification of the R g‐derived TDP‐43 ensembles (see Section 4) based on end‐to‐end (E–E) distance showed a similar trend to R g, that is, an increase in E–E distance resulted in conformational expansion (Figure S1e). In contrast, solvent accessible surface area (SASA) appeared to be a less effective metric for distinguishing between intermediate and extended ensembles (Figure S1f).

  In summary, our simulations indicated a diverse conformational ensemble for monomeric TDP‐43 due to high conformational flexibility associated with the flexible linkers and the disordered CTD. Given that TDP‐43 appears to sample compact and extended conformations, we hypothesized that specific non‐local intramolecular contacts between domains contribute to the TDP‐43 ensemble.


### A diverse network of inter‐domain interactions underlies the conformational ensemble of monomeric TDP‐43
  The network and relative strength of inter‐domain interactions prevalent in multidomain proteins defines the overall energy landscape of populated conformations and their associated functions across a wide range of contexts (e.g., ligand binding, catalysis, oligomerization) (Tompa, 2012). A detailed characterization of the conformational landscape of multi‐domain proteins also enables a rationalization of the effect of physical parameters (e.g., pH, temperature, salt) and sequence perturbations (e.g., mutations, PTMs) on their ability to form higher‐order assemblies (Mohanty et al., 2022). Within the context of phase separation, studies of disordered, low complexity domains have established that intramolecular interactions which modulate single‐chain conformation in the dilute phase largely resemble intermolecular interactions which promote phase separation (Dignon, Zheng, Best, et al., 2018; Johnson et al., 2023; Lin & Chan, 2017; Martin, Thomasen, et al., 2021; Rekhi et al., 2023). As an extension of this concept, the prevalence of specific intra‐domain interactions in the dilute phase of multidomain proteins are also shown to affect their propensity to undergo phase separation (Larson et al., 2017; Zheng et al., 2021).

  Based on the above findings, we analyzed the inter‐domain interactions prevalent in single‐chain simulations of TDP‐43 full‐length which may account for its phase behavior. We computed the average, pairwise inter‐residue interactions for the TDP‐43 ensemble obtained from three independent trajectories (15 μs in total) which is shown as a contact map in Figure 2c. The contact map averaged over three trajectories indicated the presence of transient interactions involving both folded and disordered regions. Notably, interdomain interactions between NTD‐RRM1, NTD‐CTD, and RRM2‐CTD were observed. Interestingly, transient interactions involving the linker regions also occurred. In particular, the NTD‐RRM1 linker (L1, aa:79–103) participated in numerous pairwise interactions with NTD, RRM1/2 linker (L2, aa:177–191), RRM2 and IDR1 (aa:264–318) of CTD. Further, the RRM1/2 linker also interacted with IDR1 of CTD. Contact map analysis of the R g‐derived ensembles indicated that the compaction of TDP‐43 which was observed in one trajectory (Figure 2a), was correlated with the presence of numerous interactions between NTD and CTD (Figure S3a,b). In contrast, NTD‐CTD interactions are largely absent in intermediate and extended ensembles (Figure S4a,b) and their conformational heterogeneity appeared to be modulated by transient interactions mediated through linkers and CTD.

  Overall, our analysis points toward the existence of a variety of heterodomain interactions which define the conformational landscape of TDP‐43 in the monomeric state, which in turn, may regulate its homoligomerization and interactions with other macromolecules. These findings are broadly supported with a previous study which detected weak interactions between isolated TDP‐43 domains by NMR spectroscopy (Wei et al., 2016).


### CG simulations of TDP‐43 condensates reveal a complex network of homo‐ and heterodomain intermolecular interactions
  To determine whether the heterodomain interactions observed to modulate the monomeric TDP‐43 conformational ensemble also occurred within condensates, we performed CG simulations of TDP‐43 condensates to circumvent length and timescale limitations (Shea et al., 2021), and analyzed the domain‐level interactions between TDP‐43 molecules. As stated in the previous section, the favorable interdomain contacts from our single‐chain, atomistic simulations may also contribute toward intermolecular interactions within the condensed phase of TDP‐43. As an example, the positive correlation between the intra‐monomer contacts and inter‐chain interactions implicated in phase separation was demonstrated recently in case of the HP1 paralogs where CG phase‐coexistence simulations were used to complement the AA simulations and study the protein phase behavior at longer timescales (Her et al., 2022; Phan et al., 2023). We used the HPS model (Dignon, Zheng, Kim, et al., 2018) based on the Urry hydrophobicity scale (Regy et al., 2021) to study the phase behavior of TDP‐43 at a single‐bead per amino acid resolution. Following validation of the single CG ensemble against all‐atom simulations (see see Section 4, Figure S7a–c), the slab geometry was used in the CG phase co‐existence simulations for efficient sampling of two coexisting phases, a dilute phase, and a dense phase (Dignon, Zheng, Kim, et al., 2018; Mammen Regy et al., 2021).

  The 2D‐intermolecular contact map (Figure 3a) from the CG phase co‐existence simulation shows a diverse array of homo‐ and heterodomain interactions. Both NTD and CTD participated in homodomain interactions which is consistent with their propensity to undergo self‐oligomerization. In agreement with previous studies, both CR and IDR regions of the CTD contribute extensively toward favorable interactions in the condensed phase (Conicella et al., 2020; Hallegger et al., 2021; Mohanty et al., 2023; Schmidt et al., 2019). Interestingly, RRM1/2 and disordered linkers‐L1/L2 also participated extensively in contact formation (Figure 3b). In line with AA simulations, both L1/L2 are involved in intermolecular contacts with all other domains. RRM1/2 formed numerous contacts with all CTD regions which is qualitatively consistent with NMR titration experiments (Wei et al., 2016). Overall, these results further confirm the importance of L1/L2 in mediating inter‐domain interactions and predict that RRM1/2 play important roles in TDP‐43 phase separation.


### Heterodomain interactions are predominantly electrostatic in nature and sensitive to salt
  To assess the contribution of various residues toward inter‐domain interactions in our simulations, we computed interactions between possible residue‐type pairs for the R g‐derived ensembles at 100 mM NaCl (Figure S5a‐c). In all‐atom simulations, we observed a prevalence of salt‐bridge residue pairs (R/K‐D/E) in all three types of ensembles (compact, intermediate, and extended). While the compact ensemble also showed an enrichment of polar and hydrophobic interaction pairs (Figure S5d), these interactions were weaker in the intermediate and extended ensembles. The above findings are consistent with the sequence charge analysis of folded and disordered regions in TDP‐43 (Figure S6) which indicates an overall abundance of charged residues in all folded domains and the interconnecting linker regions. Charged residues in CTD are sparser and found in regions flanking the CR (aa:319–341). Further, surface electrostatic potentials calculated for NTD, and RRM1/2 indicated the presence of distinct patches of positive and negative charge for each of these domains.

  In line with AA simulations, we detected electrostatic interactions between oppositely charged R/K and D/E pairs in our CG modeling of the condensed phase. The intermolecular contact map (Figure 3a) demonstrates that positively charged patches in the NTD‐RRM1 linker—KDNKRK (aa:78–84) and KVKRAVQK (aa:95–102), interact dominantly with negatively charged surfaces of NTD—EDENDEPIEIPSEDD (aa:9–23). Further, negatively charged patches in the NTD‐RRM1 linker—DETD (aa:86–89) and in RRM2—EDMTEDELREF (aa:199–210) interact with the positively charged regions in RRM1—KKDLK (aa:135–140), RRM2—PKPFR (aa:223–227), and NTD—RYRNPVSQCMRGVR (aa:42–55). To confirm the electrostatic nature of heterodomain interactions, we also performed all‐atom simulations of the TDP‐43 monomer at a higher salt concentration (300 mM NaCl). The increase in salt concentration led to a clear shift in the R g probability distribution toward more expanded conformations (Figure 4a,b). Further, contact map analysis indicates a substantial loss of heterodomain interactions at 300 mM NaCl (Figure 4c,d) which is consistent with the conformational expansion of TDP‐43.

  Altogether, our findings from both all‐atom and CG simulations confirm the prevalence of charged heterodomain interactions in addition to well‐established homodomain interactions, both of which collectively dictate the phase behavior of TDP‐43 under physiological conditions.


## DISCUSSION
  Recent studies indicate that the phase separation of multidomain proteins including RNA‐binding proteins are significantly influenced by both low and high salt concentration regimes (Krainer et al., 2021). In vitro studies (Staderini et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2018) indicate that an increase in salt concentration beyond 100 mM NaCl considerably reduces TDP‐43 phase separation, indicating an essential role for electrostatic interactions. While a significant electrostatic contribution toward the phase separation of TDP‐43 arises from the asymmetrical oligomerization of NTD (K d ~ 40 μM) (Wang et al., 2018), our simulations also uncover the presence of numerous additional inter‐domain interactions in TDP‐43 which are expected to contribute toward the overall salt‐sensitivity of its phase separation. Specifically, our analysis of single‐chain, all‐atom MD simulations indicated the existence of transient, electrostatic interactions between disordered (linkers‐L1/L2, CTD) and folded domains which modulate the conformational landscape of monomeric TDP‐43. CG simulations also supported these observations in the monomer state and provided further evidence for the prevalence of these heterodomain interactions in the condensed phase.

  Recent studies indicate that heterodomain interactions may play an important role in modulating the phase behavior of multidomain proteins. Mittag and co‐workers (Martin, Harmon, et al., 2021) utilized an integrative modeling approach to uncover heterodomain electrostatic interactions between the RRM and prion‐like domain in driving the phase separation of RNA‐binding protein, hnRNPA1. Notably, the presence of RRM domains reversed the salt dependence of phase separation compared to the prion‐like domain alone. Castaneda and co‐workers (Zheng et al., 2021) also uncovered an intricate network of interactions in the multidomain protein—UBQLN2 based on NMR titrations between folded and disordered domains. Interestingly, their findings revealed the presence of transient intramolecular interactions between disordered and folded domains which were inhibitory toward UBQLN2 phase separation.

  Here, our multiscale simulations combined with previous experimental findings, suggest that a complex network of interdomain interactions control the TDP‐43 condensed phase, as outlined in Figure 5. CTD oligomerization, driven by site‐specific CR and transient IDR interactions, along with NTD self‐association, synergistically promotes TDP‐43 phase separation. In addition, our simulations suggest that both NTD and CTD may further contribute toward TDP‐43 single‐chain compaction and phase separation through heterodomain interactions with each other and with short flexible linkers between the folded domains. Particularly, the NTD‐RRM1 linker, with charge‐segregated patches interact with other oppositely charged patches in disordered regions (RRM1/2 linker and IDR1) or oppositely charged surfaces of the folded domains (NTD and RRM1/2), thereby competing with site‐specific interactions which promote oligomerization in addition to providing conformational flexibility. The NTD‐RRM1 linker region also contains serine/threonine residues modified by phosphorylation and an ALS‐associated mutation (A90V) site which further supports the case for its involvement in LLPS and aggregation (Doll et al., 2022; Doll & Cingolani, 2022). In addition to linker‐mediated interactions, contact map analysis of both all‐atom and CG simulations showed weak interactions between RRM1/2 and CTD whose existence is supported by NMR titration experiments (Wei et al., 2016). Overall, both homodomain and heterodomain interactions may collectively impact the formation and regulation of TDP‐43 condensates.

  In summary, recent successes in the purification of TDP‐43 in vitro coupled with in vivo studies have provided rich insights into the effect of physical parameters and domain mutations on its phase separation and aggregation (Doke & Jha, 2022; Hallegger et al., 2021; Staderini et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2018; Yu et al., 2021). Complementary to these advances, our multiscale simulation methodology (Fawzi et al., 2021; Mohanty et al., 2022) as demonstrated in this study enables a deeper investigation of the inter‐domain interaction network underlying TDP‐43 phase behavior. As an extension, we believe that our demonstrated approach has great potential in unraveling the complex interaction network underlying biologically‐relevant, multi‐component condensates of TDP‐43 such as those formed with HSP70 chaperones (Gu et al., 2021; Lu et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2021) which are relevant to understanding ALS pathology.


## METHODS

### All‐atom MD simulations

#### Initial structure, force field choice and system setup
  The structural model of the TDP‐43 monomer was generated from the structures of the individual domains deposited in RCSB Protein data bank (NTD:5mdi, RRM1/2:4bs2, CTD:2n3x) using the MOE program (Vilar et al., 2008). The protein molecule was simulated in the presence of explicit solvent and ions. The protein was modeled using the AMBER99SBws‐STQ force field (Best et al., 2014; Tang et al., 2020) (https://bitbucket.org/jeetain/all-atom_ff_refinements/src/master/) and solvated with TIP4P/2005 water molecules (Abascal & Vega, 2005). The desired concentration of NaCl was included in the protein‐water system with modified LJ parameters for Na+ and Cl− to improve ion solubility (Luo & Roux, 2010). The protein was solvated in an octahedral box (l = 15.0 nm) with counter ions added to achieve electroneutrality.


#### Simulation protocol
  The solvated protein system was subjected to energy minimization performed via the steepest descent algorithm using GROMACS‐2020.4 (Páll et al., 2020). Following minimization, the Nose‐Hoover thermostat (Evans & Holian, 1985) was used for temperature equilibration at 300 K with a coupling constant of 1 ps. After the NVT equilibration, a 100 ns NPT equilibration was conducted using the Berendsen barostat (Berendsen et al., 1984) with isotropic coupling and a coupling constant of 5 ps for pressure control. Following the equilibration steps, 5 μs production simulations were performed for 5 μs in the NVT ensemble was performed using the Langevin Middle Integrator (friction coefficient = 1 ps−1) (Zhang et al., 2019) in OpenMM‐7.5 (Eastman et al., 2017). Short‐range nonbonded interactions were computed using a cutoff radius of 0.9 nm, while long‐range electrostatics were handled with the PME method (Darden et al., 1993; Essmann et al., 1995). To enable a timestep of 4 fs, the hydrogen mass was increased by 1.5 times (Hopkins et al., 2015), and hydrogen‐containing bonds were constrained using the SHAKE algorithm (Ryckaert et al., 1977).


#### Trajectory analysis
  Compact, intermediate, and extended conformational ensembles of TDP‐43 monomer were generated by filtering the trajectory frames based on the Rg criteria as follows: (i) <3.0 nm = compact, (ii) 3.0–4.0 nm = intermediate and (iii) >4.0 nm = extended. For contact analysis, two residues i and j with sequence separation greater 3 (|i‐j| > 3) were in contact if any two heavy atoms of the residues were within 0.6 nm of each other. Such a cutoff has been extensively tested in our previous studies (Zheng et al., 2020) and is considered large enough to include the diverse contact modes such as hydrogen bonds (<0.35 nm), van der Waal interactions and salt‐bridges (<0.6 nm). Contacts formed within folded, linker and disordered regions were excluded from the analysis. The helical fractions for the C‐terminal domain were calculated based on the DSSP library (Kabsch & Sander, 1983) using the gmx do_dssp program. Distance root mean square deviation (dRMS) for folded domains were computed using gmx rmsdist. The representative snapshots from atomistic simulations were generated using UCSF Chimera (Pettersen et al., 2004). Snapshots from CG simulations were prepared using VMD (Humphrey et al., 1996) and ChimeraX (Pettersen et al., 2021).


### Coarse‐grained (CG) MD simulations

#### System setup
  CG single‐chain simulations of full‐length TDP‐43 were conducted using the LAMMPS software package (Thompson et al., 2022), in cubic boxes (30 × 30 × 30 nm). In the single chain simulations, the folded domains (NTD, RRM1, and RRM2) and the helical CR (aa:320–341) were kept rigid as in their initial PDB structures (NTD:5mdi, RRM1/2:4bs2, CTD:2n3x) as separate rigid bodies, using the fix rigid command. Whereas the linkers between the folded domains and disordered flanks in the CTD remained flexible. Coexistence phase simulations of TDP‐43 were conducted using the HOOMD‐Blue 2.9.3 software package (Anderson et al., 2020) with azplugins (version 0.10.1; https://github.com/mphowardlab/azplugins), according to the protocol described in our previous work (Mammen Regy et al., 2021). The initial slab configuration (24 × 24 × 168 nm) was prepared from 100 chains of the TDP‐43 monomeric sequence based on the HPS‐Urry model (Dignon, Zheng, Kim, et al., 2018; Regy et al., 2021). The folded domains (NTD, RRM1/2) and the conserved helix region (aa:320–341) were constrained as rigid bodies, using hoomd.md.constrain.rigid function. Under the same simulation protocol, NTD‐ and CR‐dimer slab configurations (24 × 24 × 168 nm) were prepared from 50 dimers (100 chains) of TDP‐43. In the NTD‐dimeric slab, two NTDs are kept rigid with respect to each other in a head‐to‐tail dimeric (PDB: 6b1g), whereas two conserved helix regions are constrained in parallel orientation within the CR‐dimeric slab. Apart from those, RRM1/2 and the conserved helix region (aa:320–341) were kept fixed in both cases.


#### Protocol and analysis
  All CG simulations were conducted for 5 μs in the NVT ensemble using a time step = 10 fs. The Langevin thermostat was used for temperature control, with the damping factor (τ) set to 1000 ps for phase coexistence simulations and 1000 fs for single chain simulations. The effect of ionic screening is implicitly modeled using a Debye‐Hückel electrostatic potential: Eijr=qiqj4πDrexp−r/κ, where κ is the Debye screening length which can be changed to mimic different salt concentrations. κ is set to 1 nm, which corresponds to an ionic strength of approximately 100 mM. The first 1 μs was treated as an equilibration period and omitted from all analysis calculations. Radius of gyration, density and contact map analysis were done using the protocol mentioned in our previous studies (Dignon, Zheng, Kim, et al., 2018; Mammen Regy et al., 2021).


#### Validation of monomeric TDP‐43 CG ensemble against all‐atom simulations
  First, the applicability of our CG model for a multidomain TDP‐43 is examined by comparing the single‐chain CG ensemble against atomistic simulations data. As shown in Figure S7a, the R g conformations (3.16 ± 0.02 nm) sampled from our single‐chain CG simulations were more compact than the AA conformations (3.56 ± 0.40 nm). This discrepancy potentially arises from the treatment of folded domains as rigid bodies. Despite the computational efficiency of the rigid‐body assignment method in describing folded domains (Dignon, Zheng, Kim, et al., 2018; Her et al., 2022), it is known to yield stronger interactions than expected due to the substantial contribution from the buried residues within the folded domains to the intermolecular interactions (Kim & Hummer, 2008). One way to address this limitation is by reducing the absolute energy scale of non‐bonded interactions (Dignon, Zheng, Kim, et al., 2018). Alternatively, increasing the simulation temperature could also be employed to compensate for this effect, which we employed in this study. The appropriate temperature for the TDP‐43 FL CG model was selected by comparing the R g distributions obtained from CG single chain simulations performed at various temperatures with the results from AA simulations at 300 K. Conformations of TDP‐43 at temperatures at 320/330 K showed excellent agreement with the AA simulations at 300 K. Further, we also compared the Cα‐based contacts from AA simulations (300 K) with contacts from CG (320 K) simulations (Figure S7b). A 0.9 nm cutoff is used for Cα‐based contact calculation in atomistic single‐chain simulations like the CG contact criteria (1.5 × σij), based on an average amino acid diameter (σij,ave) of 0.6 nm. The interdomain interactions within a single chain, observed at two resolutions, exhibit qualitative agreement for most domains, particularly those involving linker regions (Figure S7c). However, there are sparser intra‐CTD and NTD‐CTD contacts in CG simulations compared to AA simulations. We note that the observed difference may arise from the limitations of the CG model in capturing specific interactions due to its reduced resolution, albeit with increased simulation performance required to model protein condensation. Collectively, the comparison based on R g distributions and contact maps highlight the suitability of CG simulations for studying the phase behavior of TDP‐43.


#### Parameterization of phase‐coexistence simulations against in vitro experiments
  Based on the single chain simulation results, we proceeded with slab simulations of TDP‐43 at four different temperatures: 300 K (as a control), 320, 325, and 330 K (based on R g distributions from CG simulations) and plotted the protein density as a function of the z‐coordinate (Figure S7d). Notably, the dilute phase concentration, which corresponds to the saturation concentration (csat), observed in the simulations at 325 K (2–3 μM) closely aligns with the approximate experimental csat (0.5–2.5 μM at 150 mM NaCl) observed under in vitro conditions (Hallegger et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2018). Encouraged by this agreement, we decided to analyze the trajectory at 325 K to characterize the interdomain interactions of TDP‐43 FL within the condensed phase. It is important to note that CG models for IDRs do not explicitly capture the binding affinity and specificity of NTD dimerization and conserved helical region oligomerization. For that reason, the validity of interdomain interactions from the monomeric slab of TDP‐43 is tested against two TDP‐43 dimeric models that mimic the NTD and CR dimerization (Figure S8a). A similar technique for constraining the folded domains in a dimeric form which was successfully applied in our previous work (Her et al., 2022). The density profiles (Figure S8b) for these two homo‐dimer slabs were computed, revealing that simulations of NTD‐dimer and CR‐dimer exhibit considerably lower csat compared to the monomeric case. In agreement with previous experimental studies (Conicella et al., 2016; Hallegger et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2018) imposing dimerization increases the LLPS propensity of TDP‐43 in our simulations. To characterize the changes in the interaction network upon dimerization, the pairwise intra‐ and intermolecular contacts were calculated from both NTD‐dimeric and CR‐dimeric simulations (Figure S8c). Overall, the two‐dimensional interchain contact maps reveal similar patterns between dimeric and monomeric models. This is further supported by examining the normalized per‐residue contacts (Figure S8d) among three models of the TDP‐43 condensed phase, which showed a higher degree of correlation. In summary, the inter‐domain contacts from the monomeric slab simulations are similar regardless of whether NTD or CTD dimerization is enforced and thus justifies our choice to use the monomeric model to study interdomain interactions within the condensed phase (Figure 3). The normalized per‐residue contacts (Figure S8d) among three models of the TDP‐43 condensed phase showed good correlation. This suggests that the inter‐domain contacts from the monomeric slab simulations are similar regardless of whether NTD or CTD dimers are present and thus justifies our approach to use the monomeric model to study interdomain interactions within the condensed phase (Figure 3).
