## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text, including abstract, introduction, results, discussion, methods, and supporting information references.
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific soundness, methodological rigor, validity of conclusions relative to presented evidence, and clarity of presentation. No assessment of editorial fit or publication policy.
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors propose that context-dependent activity of CRX binding sites in rod photoreceptors is explained by a balance between positive additive contributions of individual TFBSs, negative homotypic interactions between sites for the same TF, and positive heterotypic interactions between sites for different TFs. They support this using MAVE-NN models trained on MPRA data from synthetic CRE libraries, and validate the model against genomic CRX-bound enhancers and silencers.
- **Visible evidence base** MPRA data from three synthetic CRE libraries (CRX+NRL, n=1,299; CDNRM, n=6,600; liver TF, n=4,966), MAVE-NN model fits, parameter estimates with uncertainty, simplified analytical model, and comparisons to genomic data from prior publications.
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Raw MPRA sequencing data, complete model hyperparameter table (Table 1 referenced but not shown), full parameter uncertainty plots (only referenced as supplementary), and code for model fitting and analysis. The manuscript states data availability but specific accession numbers are not provided in the text.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript presents a well-structured and quantitatively rigorous analysis of context-dependent TFBS activity, using an interpretable neural network framework to extract additive and interaction effects from synthetic CRE MPRA data. The central claim, that negative homotypic interactions among CRX sites drive silencing while positive heterotypic interactions and additive contributions from diverse TFs drive activation, is well supported by the model fits and validated against independent genomic observations. The work is technically sound, clearly presented, and offers a mechanistic framework that reconciles enhancer and silencer activity within a single quantitative model. The main limitations are the reliance on synthetic constructs with fixed spacing and orientation, which may not fully capture native chromatin context, and the inability to definitively assign activation versus repression in the liver dataset due to lack of a baseline. Overall, this is a valuable contribution that will interest researchers in gene regulation, computational biology, and retinal biology.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers studying cis-regulatory logic and transcriptional regulation, particularly those interested in quantitative models of enhancer and silencer function. Computational biologists developing interpretable models for functional genomics data will find the MAVE-NN application instructive. Retinal biologists studying photoreceptor gene regulation and CRX function will benefit from the mechanistic insights into how CRX binding sites can have opposing effects depending on context. The general finding that homotypic interactions can be repressive while heterotypic interactions are activating has broad relevance to understanding enhancer evolution and design.
- **Major strengths** 1) The use of MAVE-NN, an interpretable framework, to decompose sequence-function relationships into additive and interaction terms is methodologically appropriate and well-executed. 2) The systematic design of synthetic libraries with controlled TFBS composition enables clean inference of interaction effects. 3) The validation against genomic CRX-bound enhancers and silencers strengthens the generalizability of the findings. 4) The simplified analytical model provides an intuitive illustration of the proposed mechanism. 5) The inclusion of a second cell type (liver) demonstrates that the pattern of positive additive and negative homotypic interactions may be a general feature of TFBS logic.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **Concern ID** R1-M1
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Generalizability of findings to native chromatin context
  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that "the context-dependent activity of CRX sites is thus determined by the balance between positive heterotypic interactions, independent contributions of TFBSs, and negative homotypic interactions" and that this model "explains observed patterns of activity among genomic CRX-bound enhancers and silencers."
  - **Evidence pointer** Results sections "Positive heterotypic and negative homotypic interactions explain the effects of CRX and NRL sites on CRE activity in photoreceptors" and "Balance between positive and negative interactions can explain context-dependent effects of binding sites for transcriptional activators"; Figure 4C.
  - **Concern** The model is trained exclusively on synthetic CREs with fixed spacing (20 bp building blocks), uniform orientation, and no nucleosome positioning or chromatin context. While the model recapitulates trends in genomic MPRA data, the synthetic constructs lack the sequence diversity and structural complexity of native enhancers. The claim that the model "explains" genomic patterns may be overstated, as the comparison in Figure 4C is qualitative and does not account for potential confounding factors such as endogenous chromatin state, DNA methylation, or cooperative binding with additional TFs not included in the synthetic libraries.
  - **Why it matters** If the model's predictive power does not extend to native chromatin contexts, the proposed mechanism may be an artifact of the synthetic design rather than a general principle of CRX-dependent regulation. The authors' conclusion that "enhancers may require diverse TFBSs to overcome negative homotypic interactions" has implications for enhancer evolution and disease variant interpretation, so it is important to establish that the mechanism operates in endogenous contexts.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should either (a) apply the trained model to predict activity of endogenous CRX-bound enhancers and silencers from sequence alone and quantitatively compare predictions to measured MPRA activity, or (b) test whether the model can predict the effects of naturally occurring variants in CRX-bound enhancers, or (c) explicitly acknowledge the limitation and temper the claim from "explains" to "is consistent with."
  - **Concern ID** R1-M2
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Interpretation of negative homotypic interactions
  - **Claim pointer** The authors state that "negative homotypic interactions between sites cause CREs composed of multiple CRX sites to function as silencers" and that "CRX molecules independently contribute to activation while engaging in repressive homotypic interactions with one another."
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section "Positive heterotypic and negative homotypic interactions explain the effects of CRX and NRL sites on CRE activity in photoreceptors"; Figure 2D.
  - **Concern** The mechanistic interpretation of negative homotypic interactions as "repressive" is inferred from model parameters, but the underlying biophysical mechanism is not directly tested. The authors discuss several possibilities (CRX dimerization, co-repressor recruitment, TF sharing) but do not distinguish between them. The claim that CRX "engages in repressive homotypic interactions" implies a specific molecular mechanism that is not supported by the data presented. The model is phenomenological; it captures statistical dependencies in the data but does not identify the molecular basis.
  - **Why it matters** The mechanistic interpretation is central to the paper's conceptual contribution. If the negative homotypic interactions arise from TF sharing or competition for limiting cofactors rather than direct repressive interactions between CRX molecules, the proposed model of "self-inhibition" would be misleading. This distinction matters for predicting how mutations in CRX binding sites would affect enhancer versus silencer activity.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should either (a) perform additional experiments to test the mechanism (e.g., varying CRX concentration, testing mutant CRX lacking dimerization domains, or measuring cofactor recruitment), or (b) revise the language to describe the interactions as "negative statistical interactions" without committing to a specific molecular mechanism, or (c) provide additional analysis from the model that distinguishes between alternative mechanisms (e.g., testing whether the interaction strength depends on spacing in a way that would predict direct protein-protein contact versus TF sharing).
  - **Concern ID** R1-M3
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Model transferability across datasets and cell types
  - **Claim pointer** The authors state that "the pattern of positive additive contributions and negative homotypic interactions may be a recurring feature of transcriptional activators across cell types and species" based on the liver dataset analysis.
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section "Positive additive effects and negative homotypic interactions generalize to a second cell type"; Figures S4 and S5.
  - **Concern** The liver dataset analysis required a different data processing approach (treating replicates separately) and achieved lower predictive performance than the photoreceptor models. The authors note that "we cannot definitively ascribe the effects of the TF binding sites in that library to activation or repression" due to lack of a baseline. This limits the strength of the generalization claim. The liver TFBSs are from a different TF family, and the synthetic constructs use different spacing and design principles, making direct comparison of interaction parameters difficult.
  - **Why it matters** The claim of generalizability is a key part of the paper's significance. If the pattern is not robustly established in the liver system, the conclusion that this is a "recurring feature" is premature. This weakens the broader relevance of the findings beyond CRX and photoreceptors.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should either (a) provide a more rigorous comparison of model parameters across datasets, including uncertainty estimates that allow statistical comparison, or (b) temper the generalization claim to state that the pattern is "observed in two datasets" rather than "may be a recurring feature," or (c) provide additional evidence from published datasets in other cell types to support the generalization.

- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** 
  - Originality: The application of MAVE-NN to dissect additive versus interaction effects in TFBS function is not entirely novel, but the specific finding that negative homotypic interactions among CRX sites drive silencing is a new mechanistic insight. The conceptual framework of balancing positive and negative interactions is a useful synthesis.
  - Scientific importance: The work addresses a fundamental question in gene regulation, namely how the same TF can activate or repress depending on context. The findings have implications for understanding enhancer evolution, interpreting disease variants, and designing synthetic gene circuits.
  - Interdisciplinary readership: The work bridges computational modeling, molecular biology, and retinal biology. The interpretable model framework makes it accessible to a broad audience, though the dense parameter analysis may limit appeal to non-specialists.
  - Technical soundness: The methods are appropriate and well-executed. The use of multiple model architectures, cross-validation, and uncertainty estimation strengthens the conclusions. The simplified model provides an intuitive check on the main findings.
  - Readability for nonspecialists: The manuscript is generally well-written and logically organized. The introduction and discussion effectively contextualize the work. However, the results section assumes familiarity with MPRA and neural network concepts, and some figures are dense.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The core findings are well supported by the data, and the manuscript makes a valuable contribution. The Major Concerns are addressable through additional analysis, revised interpretation, or explicit acknowledgment of limitations. I recommend the authors address the generalizability claims, clarify the mechanistic interpretation of negative homotypic interactions, and provide additional detail on model transferability.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that negative homotypic interactions represent "repressive" interactions between CRX molecules is not directly supported by the data; the model is phenomenological and does not identify a molecular mechanism.
- The claim that the pattern of positive additive and negative homotypic interactions "may be a recurring feature of transcriptional activators across cell types and species" is weakly supported by the liver dataset, which had lower model performance and no baseline for activation versus repression.
- The claim that the model "explains observed patterns of activity among genomic CRX-bound enhancers and silencers" is based on a qualitative comparison (Figure 4C) and may overstate the predictive power of the model for native chromatin contexts.
- The statement that "binding site composition is thus the primary factor distinguishing strong enhancers from silencers" is not fully supported, as the model leaves substantial variance unexplained (R2 = 0.889 for the pairwise model, leaving over 10% unexplained variance).
