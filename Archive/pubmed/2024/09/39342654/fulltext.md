# Uncovering intermolecular interactions driving the liquid-liquid phase separation of TDP-43 low complexity domain via atomistic dimerization simulations


## Abstract
  Liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) of TAR DNA-binding protein 43 (TDP-43), which exerts multiple functions in the splicing, trafficking and stabilization of RNA, mediates the formation of membraneless condensates with crucial physiological roles, while its aberrant LLPS is linked to multiple neurodegenerative diseases. However, due to the heterogeneous and dynamic nature of LLPS, major gaps remain in understanding the precise intermolecular interactions driving LLPS and how specific mutations alter LLPS dynamics. Here, we investigated the molecular mechanisms underlying the LLPS of the TDP-43 low complexity domain (LCD) by simulating the dimerization process using all-atom discrete molecular dynamics with microsecond-long simulations. Our results showed that TDP-43 LCD was intrinsically disordered, with helical structures consistent with prior NMR studies. Phase separation propensity was assessed by simulating the dimerization of TDP-43 LCD and four mutants, showing that A321G, W334G, and M337V inhibited self-association, while G335D promoted it, fully consistent with experimental reports. During the dimerization process, two peptides experienced both elastic and non-elastic collisions, and the self-associated dimer featured both high and low-contact states. These results suggested that the dimerization process of TDP-43 LCD was accordingly dynamic and heterogeneous. Additionally, we identified crucial regions containing hydrophobic clusters and aromatic residues in the N-terminus, central region, and C-terminus that were essential for the self-association of TDP-43 LCD. These residues with high binding affinities can act as stickers to form peptide networks in LLPS. Together, our simulation provides a comprehensive picture of the intermolecular interactions driving the phase separation of TDP-43 LCD, offering insights into both physiological functions and pathological mechanisms.


## INTRODUCTION
  Transactive response DNA-binding protein of 43 kDa (TDP-43) is a primary component of RNA granules and plays functions in the regulation and stability of RNA (e.g., splicing, transcription, translation of RNA, transport of mRNA, etc.), cell proliferation, and apoptosis.1,2. Meanwhile, the insoluble inclusions of TDP-43 in brains have been identified as main hallmarks shared by amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), frontotemporal dementia (FTD), limbic-predominant age-related TDP-43 encephalopathy (LATE), and Alzheimer’s disease (AD).3,4 The full-length TDP-43 contains an N-terminal domain, tandem RNA-recognition motifs (RRM1 and RRM2) and a dominantly disordered C-terminus, which is a glycine-rich low-complexity domain (LCD) responsible for recruiting TDP-43 into stress granules and cytosolic aggregates.2 The LCD (residues 267–414) is of particular importance to the functions and pathogenesis of TDP-43 due to the aggregation-prone properties with sequences similar to yeast prion proteins and being rich with mutations associated with ALS and FTD.5–7 Mounting evidence showed that TDP-43 LCD could undergo liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS), where the protein solution spontaneously de-mixes into a high-density phase coexisting with a low-density phase above a critical concentration. The high-density phase is a reversible and viscous liquid-like droplet, which may further grows by fusion and Ostwald ripening and ultimately mature into irreversible rigid assemblies.8–10 The formation of liquid droplets was associated with the functional activities of TDP-43 LCD, including the generation of membrane-less organelles and the binding and regulation with RNA11, while its rigidification to amyloid fibrils enriched with β-sheet structures was linked with pathologies of neurodegenerative diseases.12–14

  The molecular mechanism for TDP-43 LCD to form phase-separated droplets is essential to understanding the function and dysfunction of TDP-4315. Phase separation of disordered proteins relies on weak and transient multivalent intermolecular interactions. Several possible mechanisms of the LLPS of TDP-43 LCD have been proposed. Nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy suggested that the phase separation of TDP-43 LCD was mediated by the α-helical structure spanning residues 320–343.16,17 By examining the LLPS propensity of a series of mutations, it was found that the phase separation of TDP-43 LCD was dominated by aromatic residues, especially three tryptophan residues W334, W385, and W412.9 Meanwhile, various deletion constructs of TDP-43 LCD, including Δ274–319, Δ316–346, Δ320–366, Δ367–414, and Δ320–414, were found to inhibit the formation of droplets.11 Some of the deletions impairing the phase separation are out of the helix region, implying that the LLPS of TDP-43 are not driven by the helix-to-helix contact alone. Intriguingly, solid state nuclear magnetic resonance measurements on the maturation of liquid droplets formed by TDP-43 LCD showed that the residues 365–400 functioned as the structural core that stabilized the fibrils. The emergence of various reported mechanisms indicates that the phase separation of TDP-43 LCD was likely to be contributed by multiple regions simultaneously, with the details remaining unrevealed. Moreover, the molecular description of the dynamic properties of the droplets formed by TDP-43 LCD, which can fuse, flow and recover fluorescence after photobleaching, is not available. Together, there is still a critical lack of a complete picture of the interactions driving the phase separation of TDP-43 LCD at the molecular level.

  The simulations on the droplet formation of LLPS often adopted coarse-grained models or field-theoretic simulation approaches18,19, such as the hydrophobicity rescale mode20 and LASSI model,21 to give a mesoscopic description of phase separation. Although the formation of droplets was captured, many of the molecular details and sequence-dependencies were missing. All-atom simulations can be used to probe such interactions important for LLPS, but are often limited to capture LLPS-relevant structures and interactions encoded at the monomeric level22 or to focus on the condensate structures and dynamics of short fragments within the whole length protein due to large system sizes and long timescales.23 Therefore, it remains a major challenge for traditional all-atom molecular dynamics simulations to probe the intermolecular interactions driving the self-association of large intrinsically disordered proteins like TDP-43 LCD in LLPS.

  In this study, we investigated the structures and dimerization dynamics of the 296-residue protein and mutants using all-atom discrete molecular dynamics (DMD) with microsecond-long simulations. According to the Flory-Huggins theory of polymer solutions that describes the LLPS in a mean-filed model, the tendency of peptides to undergo phase separation is mediated by the Flory interaction parameter χ.1,2 The Flory interaction parameter χ is a dimensionless parameter quantifying the averaged association energy gain between two composite peptides, which can be reflected by the propensity of dimerization.1,2 Thus, instead of directly simulating the LLPS process that involves a large number of peptides, we focus on the intermolecular interactions driving the dimerization of TDP-43 LCD to reveal the mechanism of LLPS. DMD is a rapid and predictive molecular dynamics algorithm used by many groups to study amyloid aggregation and phase separations of intrinsically disordered proteins.19,24,25 A large number of independent simulations with different initial positions and velocities were conducted to ensure sufficient sampling of the intrinsically disordered proteins. The obtained secondary structure properties of the wild type (WT) and four mutants (i.e., A321G, W334G, G335D, and M337V), whose effects on the LLPS of TDP-43 have been experimentally well established, were consistent with prior experimental results9,16,17. Assessed by the dimerization propensities in silico, the effects of mutations on the phase separation also aligned with experimental measurements. Moreover, the dimerization process was shown to be highly dynamic and heterogeneous, with peptides experiencing elastic and non-elastic collisions and the self-assembled dimers featuring low and high-contact states. Additionally, multiple regions have been identified as having high intermolecular contact propensities to stabilize the condensates of the phase separated TDP-43 LCD, in addition to the reported helical region (320–340) and aromatic residues9,16,17. This observation is consistent with the stickers-and-spacers model of LLPS26, where these identified residues with high binding affinities act as stickers and the intervening residues as spacers for formation of peptide networks in LLPS. Together, the current all-atom DMD simulation study, for the first time, provides a comprehensive picture of the intermolecular interactions driving the phase separation of TDP-43 LCD.


## MATERIALS AND METHODS

### Discrete molecular dynamics simulations.
  Computer simulations were performed with the united-atom discrete molecular dynamics (DMD), a novel fast and predictive simulation engine with enhanced sampling efficiency. The continuous interaction potentials in classic molecular dynamics were reconstructed by optimized discrete stepwise functions in DMD. Atoms moved at constant speeds in DMD until they crossed energy steps, where collisions occurred with the velocities updated through computing the ballistic equations considering conservation laws.27 The system’s dynamics in DMD were dictated by a series of collision events and advanced sort algorithms were employed to find the next collision. Since only the trajectories of colliding atoms are solved in DMD, the sampling and computational efficiencies of DMD are significantly enhanced compared to the classic molecular dynamics where the velocities and positions of atoms are continuously integrated based on the updated forces.

  Interatomic interactions in our all-atom DMD simulations were adapted from the Medusa force field, which encompasses both bonded interactions (i.e., covalent bonds, bond angles, and dihedrals) and non-bonded interactions (i.e., van der Waals, solvation, hydrogen bond, and electrostatic terms). In Medusa force field, the bonded interactions were modeled using infinite square wells, where the covalent bonds and bond angles typically had a single well whereas dihedrals were modeled by multiple wells corresponding to cis- or trans-conformations. The force field parameters for van der Waals, covalent bonds, bond angles, and dihedrals were derived from CHARMM force field.28 To reduce computational burden, the solvation effect was implicitly modelled by the effective energy function 1 (EEF 1) model proposed by Lazaridis and Karplus.29 A reaction-like algorithm considering the distance and angle dependences was employed to explicitly model the hydrogen bond interactions.30 The screened electrostatic interactions between charged atoms were computed by the Debye–Huckel approximation with a physiological monovalent salt concentration of 100 mM, corresponding to the Debye length of ~10 Å. The accuracies of Medusa force field, coupled with the EEF1 model and the reaction-like hydrogen bond algorithm, have been benchmarked for the precise prediction of protein stability change upon mutation and protein–ligand binding affinity comparing with classic MD simulations utilizing various standard force fields (e.g., GROMOS96, OPLS-AA, AMBER99SB-ILDN, and CHARMM36m).31,32 The DMD software is developed and maintained by the author’s research group, which is available free at Molecules In Action, LLC. (www.moleculesinaction.com). Coupled with Medusa force field, DMD has been used by us and others to study protein folding, amyloid aggregation, and interactions with nanoparticles.33–35


### Simulation setups.
  The sequence of WT TDP-43 LCD was taken from UniProt (ID: Q13148) with the animo acids from 267 to 414 presented in Figure 1A. We started from a fully extended monomer constructed by the sequence, which was first relaxed a large box of 50 nm and then equilibrated with counter ions in a cubic box of 15 nm. For the WT and mutations of TDP-43 LCD, the charge states of amino acids were assigned based on their titration states at physiological condition, i.e., positive charges +1e were assigned for Arg and Lys residues, while negative charge −1e were assigned for Asp and Glu residues. Counter ions were introduced to maintain the overall charge neutrality of the system in DMD simulations. The monomeric structures of WT TDP-43 LCD and four mutations were obtained by fifty independent simulations with random initial positions and velocities. Each independent simulation run for 1,000 ns, producing 50 μs simulation time in total for each monomer system. For the dimer simulations, two peptides were randomly selected from the equilibrated monomeric conformations and positioned in a large cubic box with the dimension of 20 nm. The peptides and ions were also placed randomly in the simulation box with a minimal intermolecular distance of 1.5 nm. The temperature of the system was maintained at 310 K by Andersen thermostat in DMD simulations. Fifty independent simulations with different initial configurations and velocities were performed for each system to ensure sufficient conformational sampling. An initial energy minimization was carried out for 50 ps, after which a 750 ns production run was conducted, accumulating 37.5 μs simulation time for each molecular system.


### Computational analysis.
  The dictionary secondary structure of protein (DSSP) program was used to calculate the secondary structure of each peptide.36 The hydrogen bond formation was defined with the cutoff distances between the backbone N and O atoms being 3.5 Å and the angle of NH···O larger than 120°. Residue contacts were established when the minimal distance between the heavy atoms of two nonsequential residues was less than 0.65 nm. The number of residue contacts between two peptides are calculated by counting all the contact pairs between the residues of the two peptides. Two peptides are defined to be in contact if there is any residue contact between the peptides. The two-dimensional potential of mean force (PMF) was computed using the probability distribution function, i.e., -kBTlnPRg,Ncontact, where kB is the Boltzmann constant, T is the simulation temperature of 310 K, and PRg,Ncontact represents the probability of a conformation having a given value of radius of gyration Rg and a total number of residue contacts Ncontact.

  Hierarchical clustering program, oc (www.compbio.dundee.ac.uk/downloads/oc), was used to group similar simulated conformations. Initially, pairwise root-mean-square distances (RMSD) of all equilibrated conformations were computed to constitute the pairwise distance matrix and each conformation constituted a separated cluster. The hierarchical clustering algorithm iteratively joined the two closest clusters into one cluster based on the distances between two clusters and updated the elements in the pairwise distance matrix with respect to the newly formed cluster. The new “cluster distance” was defined as the mean of all pairwise distances between the elements of the two corresponding clusters. The centroid structure of each cluster was selected as the one with the smallest average distance to other elements in the cluster.


## RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

### Structural dynamics of the TDP-43 low complexity domain monomer
  We first examined the secondary structure of the TDP-43 LCD monomer after the simulations reached equilibrium states, which were confirmed by the distributions of the potential energy, secondary structures, radius of gyration (Rg), and residue-wise contacts with different simulation time intervals (Figures S1–S6). As shown by the calculated average secondary structure propensity of each residue (Figure 1B), TDP-43 LCD was intrinsically disordered with coil and bend propensity higher than 50%, except for three helical regions, namely residues 269–275, 320–344, and 350–370 with helical propensity higher than 20%. Specifically, the region 320–344, which is the evolutionarily conserved37, featured high helicity and was divided into two subregions by 335G with the helical propensity of residues 320–334 higher than that of residues 336–344. The helix propensity of our simulations agreed well with nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) results,16,17 which showed that consecutive positive secondary chemical shift for residues 321–330 and weak positive shift for residues 331–342. Similarly, the secondary structure propensities predicted by the δ2D program, a tool determining the secondary structure populations based on the magnetic resonance chemical shifts of TDP-43 C-terminal domain,38 also indicated the α-helix probability of residues 320–340 was up to 80% (Figure S7A–C).10 In addition, the secondary structure propensities obtained by our simulations were in excellent agreement with the predictions by the primary sequence-based program PASTA (Figure S7D),10,39 which also indicated helical structures in regions 269–275, 320–344, 364–367 with nearly the same propensities, highlighting the predictive power of our DMD simulation. The overall β-sheet propensity of TDP-43 LCD was less than 5%, except for the residues 311MNFGAFSIN319 with the β-sheet propensity reaching about 15%. Residues 311MNFGAFSIN319 are among one of the amyloidogenic core of TDP-43 307–319, which was found to adopt β-sheet structure and form amyloid fibril.40,41 Interestingly, this region resembled the amyloidogenic core region of human islet amyloid polypeptide (IAPP) 21NNFGAILS28, suggesting its aggregation-prone property.

  The residue-wise intra-peptide contact frequency map showed high contact frequency in the diagonal region between 269–275, 320–344, and 364–367 (Figure 1C), consistent with the helical structures in these regions. The low contact frequencies between the N-terminal residues 267–319 suggested that this region was primarily disordered with a relatively compact structure. The anti-diagonal pattern between residues 311–313 and 316–318 highlighted by the blue boxes indicated the formation of transient anti-parallel β-sheet structures, as also showed in the β-sheet propensity in Figure 1B. The two-dimensional PMF with respect to the radius of gyration (Rg) and the number of helical residues is shown in Figure 1D. The free energy landscape featured a broad basin with the Rg ranging from 2.3 to 3.5 nm, and helical residues ranging from 15 to 30, suggesting the monomer structure of TDP-43 LCD is dynamic with secondary structures varying with time. The Rg value was experimentally estimated at about 2.8 nm according to the diffusion coefficient, consistent with our simulations17. The representative conformations showed that the central region consisted of a helical structure, with both the N- and C-terminus domains being unstructured (Figure 1E), consistent with dynamic and disordered structures indicated by the PMF (Figure 1D).


### Effects of TDP-43 LCD mutations on the monomeric structure
  We considered the ALS-associated mutants A321G, G335D, M337V, and also an experimentally introduced mutant W334G. The deviations of secondary structure propensities of each residue with respect to the WT were calculated (Figures 2&S8). The variations in secondary structure propensities were localized around the mutation sites, and the helical propensities of mutants A321G, W334G, and M337V decreased at the mutation sites, while G335D increased the helix propensities at the mutation site (top panels of Figure 2). The variations of helix propensities were consistent with the secondary chemical shifts (measured as ΔδCα-ΔδCβ ) revealed by previous NMR experiments (Figure S9).9,16,17 The changes in the intra-peptide contact frequencies correlated with the variations in helix propensities (middle and bottom panels of Figure 2). Mutation W334G reduced the intra-peptide contact frequency in a short range near the mutation site. In contrast, G335D increased intra-peptide contact frequency in a broad region between residues 330 to 350. Mutants A321G and M337V caused the reduction of contacts in the C-termini in addition to the mutation sites, suggesting the existence of the long-range effects. The probability distribution of Rg (Figure S10) showed that the monomeric structures of four mutants all became more extended compared with WT. Considering the secondary structure variations, the increase of Rg of A321G, W334G, and M337V can be attributed to the conversion of helical structure to unstructured coil and bend structure, while the increase in Rg of G335D was due to the elongation of the helix, which made the peptide more rigid.


### The dimerization propensity of TDP-43 LCD wild type and four mutants correlated with corresponding LLPS propensities
  We next investigated the LLPS propensity of TDP-43 LCD by modeling the dimerization process. The ability for peptides to undergo phase separation was measured by the contact probability, which reflected the interaction parameter in Flory-Huggins phase separation theory.42–44 In our simulations, two monomers with equilibrated structures were incubated in a large box with random initial positions and velocities to model the process of dimerization. The simulation results showed that the peptides had a relatively low contact frequency (about 25.12% for the WT) due to the low concentrations (Figure 3A). For mutants A321G, W334G, and M337V, the contact probability reduced to 22.23%, 21.97%, and 20.22%, respectively. In contrast, the contact probability for G335D increased to 33.21%. The contact probability of the five simulated peptides agreed well with their propensity to undergo phase separation in vitro.9,16,17 Experimental measurements showed that mutations of A321G, W334G, and M337V disrupted the phase separation, whereas G335D promoted the phase separation.9,16,17 The good agreements between the dimerization propensities of TDP-43 WT and four mutants with the experimental results further support the correlation between the tendency of LLPS and propensity of dimerization. In addition, we also computed the number of inter-molecular residue-wise contacts in dimer simulations. The distribution probabilities of residue contacts were illustrated in Figure 3B. The contact probability decreased sharply when the residue contact number increased from 0 to 6, after that the probability gradually reduced as the residue contact number increased. For the mutations that disrupted the phase separation (A321G, W334G, and M337V), the probability distributions have similar profiles and the probabilities when the residue contact number was higher than 30 were lower than the WT. In contrast, for the phase separation promoting mutation G335D, the contact probability increased significantly when the residue contact number was within 0–100. Based on the phase separation propensities and also the changes in helical structures of WT and mutant TDP-43 LCD, it was proposed that the helix plays an important role in driving phase separation.9,16,17 Our results also confirmed that the effect of mutation on the helical structure was correlated with the ability to phase separation, i.e., mutations enhancing the helical propensity (e.g., G335D) promoted the phase separation, while mutations that destabilized the helical structure inhibited the phase separation (e.g., A321G, W334G, and M337V).


### Self-association of TDP-43 LCD was highly dynamic with different modes of bound states
  To reveal the dynamic process of TDP-43 LCD dimerization, we monitored the time-evolution of the number of residue contacts in different independent simulations starting from random initial positions and velocities (Figures 4). The residue contacts exhibited large fluctuations over time. Among the 50 independent simulations, various interaction modes were overserved. Based on the contact frequency and duration time, the interaction modes can be divided into three types, namely the elastic collision, inelastic collisions with short- and long-durations. For elastic collision interaction mode, the residue contacts profiles exhibited sharp peaks, indicating the peptides associated and dissociated within a short period (Figures 4A&B). For the inelastic collision with short durations, the peptides stuck with each other for a period before dissociation (Figures 4C&D). In these modes, the peptides frequently switched between the bound and unbound states. A typical trajectory and the corresponding conformations were illustrated in Figure 4G. Initially, two peptides were separated from each other (100 ns). After that, the amyloidogenic core region (residues 307–319) contacted each other and formed an anti-parallel β-sheet structure (t=196.5 ns) before they dissociated in a short time (202.5 ns). The peptides re-associated quickly with the C-terminus interacting with the helix region of the other chain (206.5 ns). The dimer structure re-arranged to the conformation with the amyloidogenic core region (307–319) and helix region contacting each other (244.3 ns). These peptides were kept dissociated (300.0 ns) and reassociated (480.0 ns and 493.4 ns) throughout the simulation. For the inelastic collision with long durations of bound states, the peptides were kept in the bound states with fluctuating residue contacts during the course of simulations after association (Figures 4E&F).


### Self-association of TDP-43 LCD was driven by multiple hot-spots featuring hydrophobic clusters and aromatic residues
  To reveal the inter-molecular interactions driving the dimerization of TDP-43 LCD, we calculated the residue-wise inter-peptide contact frequency map (Figure 5A) and backbone hydrogen bond map (Figure 5B). Residues with peak inter-peptide contact frequency were mainly contributed by hydrophobic interactions, many of which were aromatic residues, as indicated by the peaks at 276F, 283F, 289F, 313F, 316F, 334W, 367F, 374Y, 385W, 397F, 401F, and 412W (Figure 5A). The results correlate well with the mutation analysis that LLPS of TDP-43 were mediated by 334W, 385W, and 412W,9 and the NMR spectrum that 283F, 289F, 374Y, 385W, 401I and 412W were required for the droplet induction45. Four hot regions can be identified in the inter-peptide contact frequency map, including residues 275–289, 311–340, 381–385, and 397–401. By testing the effects of mutations at residues 320–340, experiments showed that the helical region from residues 320–340 is essential for the phase separation of TDP-43 LCD.16,17 Based on our simulations, the amyloidogenic core region, especially residues 311–319, was also crucial in the dimerization of TDP-43 LCD. The inter-peptide backbone hydrogen bond map showed residues 275–285 in the N-terminus, residues 310–320 in the amyloidogenic core region, and residues 381AAIGW385, 397FNGGFGSS404 in the C-terminus region had high propensities to form inter-peptide hydrogen bonds. The propensities for residues 321–380 to form inter-peptide backbone hydrogen bonds were relatively low. The high propensity for residues 381AAIGW385 to form backbone hydrogen bonds was consistent with the NMR results that the fibrilization of TDP-43 LCD was stabilized by the residues 365–400, which was identified as the second core of TDP-43 LCD.46 In addition, high hydrogen bond propensities was observed between residues 381AAIGW385 and residues 269–275, 311–319, and 335–340, agreed with NMR experiments that the interactions between the helical region and residues 382–385 facilitated the oligomerization of TDP-43.46 Together, the exiting experiments suggested that the aggregation of TDP-43 LCD is contributed by multiple regions. Our extensive all-atom DMD simulations identified multiple regions that contribute to the dimerization of TDP-43 LCD, which are consistent with experiments by covering the prior reported regions and residues, thus provide a comprehensive picture and offer new insights to LLPS of TDP-43.

  To investigate the effects of mutations on the intermolecular interactions during LLPS of TDP-43 LCD, the variations of residue-wise inter-peptide contact frequency map with respect to the WT were calculated (Figure S11). The mutants A321G, W334G, and M337V decreased the overall inter-peptide contact frequency of TDP-43 LCD, especially contacts between the helical region 320–340, suggesting that these mutants inhibited the aggregation of TDP-43 LCD by disrupting the helix-helix interactions (Figures S11A, B&D). In contrast, the LLPS-promoting mutant G335D increased the inter-peptide contact in a broad range of residues (Figure S11C). Obvious increments can be observed in the diagonal region of residues 320–340, suggesting the G335D mutant preferred parallelly aligned helix-helix interactions, which further drove the contacts between the N-terminal residues 267–319 and the C-terminal residues 341–414 with no apparent contact patterns.


### TDP-43 LCD peptides involved in elastic collision and also inelastic collisions with both low- and high-contact bound states
  To characterize the energy landscape of the dimerization process, two-dimensional PMF with respect to the number of inter-peptide residue contacts and backbone hydrogen bonds was calculated (Figure 6A). The free energy featured a broad basin with the number of inter-peptide contacts less than 100 and the number of backbone hydrogen bonds below 5, suggesting that weak interactions were energetically favored in the dimerization process of TDP-43 LCD. Strongly bound conformations with a large number of inter-peptide hydrogen bonds could also be observed along with a large number of inter-peptide residue contacts. However, the high contact state was weakly populated, likely because of the high entropic loss associated with the formation of a large number of inter-peptide contacts and hydrogen bonds. Conicella et al. used the well-tempered metadynamics to simulate the dimer system of TDP-43 (310–350) and the corresponding free energy landscape was calculated.17 Since the fragment TDP-43 (310–350) has a high propensity to form inter-peptide helix-helix contacts and a smaller simulation box was adopt, the free energy landscape of Conicella et al. showed a higher probability to form hydrophobic contact and hydrogen bond. Despite the differences, both the results of Conicella et al. and ours predict the existence of low contact state and high contact state.

  To gain a more detailed description of the intermolecular interactions during the dimerization of TDP-43 LCD, we replotted the probability distribution of the number of inter-peptide residue contacts in a semi-log format (Figure 6B). Three states with distinct dependences of probability with respect to the number of inter-peptide contacts could be identified. As mentioned before, the two peptides could undergo elastic collision with few residue contacts, and the probability decreased rapidly with the increase of residue contacts below about xc1=6. The semi-log plot suggested that the bound state can be further divided into two substates based on the observation that the probability decreases slowly when the number of residue contacts is low while it decreases rapidly when the residue contacts are high. To distinguish the two different profiles, we fitted the probability with two lines with different slopes (red lines in Fig. 6B) by minimizing the errors between the simulation results and fitted results. The crossover of the fitting lines was found to be xc2=160 approximately. Thus, the bound states could be further divided into two distinct sub-states, e.g., the low contact state with residue contact number less than 160 and the high contact state with residue contact number higher than 160. For peptides in the bound state, i.e., the low- and high-contact states, the most probable conformations formed about 25 inter-peptide residue contacts, suggesting the interactions were weak in TDP-43 LCD dimers (Figure S12). In the high-density phase of LLPS, the broad distribution of the residue contacts between peptides suggests the heterogeneity of the internal structures and the weak interactions between peptides gave molecular explanations for the liquid-like properties of the droplets, including their fusion and reversibility. The intermolecular interactions in these two sub-states will be discussed in the following part.

  Using the cutoff values in terms of the inter-peptide contact numbers, the secondary structure, residue-wise intra- and inter-peptide contact frequency maps of peptides in the low- and high-contact states were calculated and shown in Figure 7. The magnitudes of secondary structure variations in high contact states were higher than those in low contact states, suggesting that the conformation changes mainly occurred in the high contact states. The changes of secondary structure propensities with respect to the monomeric state (left panels of Figures 7A&B) showed that the dimerization promoted the formation of β-sheet structures in the high contact state, especially for residues 275–288 in the N-terminus, residues 311–319 in the amyloidogenic regions and residues 381–384 in the C-terminus. The increase of β-sheet structure was consistent with the inter-peptide residue-wise contact frequency map, which showed that these three regions had relatively high contact frequency. At the same time, the helical structures were stabilized in both the low and high contact states, which could be attributed to the burial of hydrophobic residues when peptides formed oligomers. The inter-peptide contacts frequency maps in low and high contact states shared similar patterns with high contact states of larger magnitudes (right panels of Figures 7A&B). Meanwhile, the intra-peptide contacts frequency of N-terminal residues 267–300 of both low and high states was reduced compared with the monomeric state (middle panels of Figures 7A&B). Since the elevation of inter-peptide contact was associated with loss of entropy whereas the reduction of intra-peptide contacts in the N-termini led to the gain of entropy, the binding states of TDP-43 LCD were in a balance between these two competing processes. The main difference between the two states was that the intra-peptide contacts of residues 340–360 in the low contact state increased compared to the monomeric state, while in the high contact state it decreased, indicating that the disordered region 340–360 tended to adopt a compact conformation via intra-peptide interactions in the low contact state while it preferred to form inter-peptide interactions in the high contact state. The loss of intra-peptide contact was an essential step for the amyloid fibrillization, suggesting the early step of fibrillization was initiated in the high contact state. The representative conformations of TDP-43 LCD in low contact state and high contact state are shown in Figures. 7C and D, respectively. The typical conformations in the low contact state are extended with peptides loosely connected, whereas the conformations in the high contact state are relatively compact with entanglement between peptides observed. To characterize the conformations in low and high contact states, the probability distribution of Rg for the dimers was calculated. The results indeed showed that the peptides adopted more compact structures in the high contact state than in the low contact state (Figure S13). Taken together, we found that the loss of entropy by forming inter-peptide interaction during the binding of TDP-43 LCD was compensated by the entropy gain through the unfolding of the N-terminus, and the amyloid fibrillization could then be initiated in the high contact state.


### Conclusions
  Liquid-liquid phase separation of TDP-43 plays both physiological and pathological roles. In this paper, we investigated the molecular mechanism underlying the phase separation of TDP-43 LCD using all-atom DMD simulations. Through sufficient sampling by conducting multiple independent simulations with different initial positions and velocities, we demonstrated that the simulation results were highly consistent with the experimental results on the structures and phase separation propensities of TDP-43 LCD and various mutants, underscoring the accuracy and enhanced sampling efficiency of DMD simulations on intrinsically disordered proteins. In addition, analysis of our simulation further suggested that the phase separation process of TDP-43 LCD was highly dynamic and heterogeneous since the peptide self-association experienced both elastic and non-elastic collisions, and the bound state in non-elastic collision featured both low- and high-contact states. The association and dissociation of peptides enabled the droplets to be dynamic and reversible, while the high contact state facilitated the amyloid aggregation. In addition, we identified four regions that are crucial for the LLPS of TDP-43 LCD, namely N-terminal residues 275FGGNPGGFGNQGGF289, the central region 310–340 and C-terminal residues 380AAIGW385, 397FNGG403, in complement to the helical region 320–240 and tryptophan residues reported by previous studies. The residues identified with high binding affinities act as stickers, and the intervening residues serve as spacers to form peptide networks with high flexibility.26 Together, our simulation gave a comprehensive picture of the intermolecular interactions driving the phase separation of TDP-43 LCD at the molecular level.
