# Predicting Gene Comutation of EGFR and TP53 by Radiomics and Deep Learning in Patients With Lung Adenocarcinomas


## Purpose:
  This study was designed to construct progressive binary classification models based on radiomics and deep learning to predict the presence of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) and TP53 mutations and to assess the models’ capacities to identify patients who are suitable for TKI-targeted therapy and those with poor prognoses.


## Materials and Methods:
  A total of 267 patients with lung adenocarcinomas who underwent genetic testing and noncontrast chest computed tomography from our hospital were retrospectively included. Clinical information and imaging characteristics were gathered, and high-throughput feature acquisition on all defined regions of interest (ROIs) was carried out. We selected features and constructed clinical models, radiomics models, deep learning models, and ensemble models to predict EGFR status with all patients and TP53 status with EGFR-positive patients, respectively. The validity and reliability of each model were expressed as the area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, precision, and F1 score.


## Results:
  We constructed 7 kinds of models for 2 different dichotomies, namely, the clinical model, the radiomics model, the DL model, the rad-clin model, the DL-clin model, the DL-rad model, and the DL-rad-clin model. For EGFR− and EGFR+, the DL-rad-clin model got the highest AUC value of 0.783 (95% CI: 0.677-0.889), followed by the rad-clin model, the DL-clin model, and the DL-rad model. In the group with an EGFR mutation, for TP53− and TP53+, the rad-clin model got the highest AUC value of 0.811 (95% CI: 0.651-0.972), followed by the DL-rad-clin model and the DL-rad model.


## Conclusion:
  Our progressive binary classification models based on radiomics and deep learning may provide a good reference and complement for the clinical identification of TKI responders and those with poor prognoses.


## MATERIALS AND METHODS
  Our study got approval from the Institutional Review Board (Approval number: YX-P-2020-085-01). The requirements for informed consent were waived. The study design and results reporting were performed following the TRIPOD guidelines.


### Patients
  In this study, data from 267 patients with lung adenocarcinoma who underwent genetic testing and noncontrast chest computed tomography from our hospital between February 2021 and September 2022 were retrospectively obtained; the group included 127 EGFR-unmutated (EGFR−) patients and 140 EGFR-mutated (EGFR+) patients. In addition, 92 uncomplicated TP53-mutated (TP53−) patients and 48 combined TP53-mutated (TP53+) patients were among the EGFR+ patients. High-throughput sequencing of tumor tissues obtained through puncture or surgery from all patients revealed 169 genes associated with malignancies, including EGFR and TP53. The inclusion criteria for patients were as follows: (1) had a histologic diagnosis of primary non–small cell lung cancer; (2) had a full report containing the results of the EGFR/TP53 genetic test available; (3) had no targeted therapy before genetic testing; and (4) had CT images available 1 month before genetic testing. The exclusion criteria for patients were as follows: (1) no CT images from our hospital, (2) poor image quality with artifacts that interfered with analysis, (3) secondary lung cancer, (4) received targeted therapy before genetic testing, (5) no CT images 1 month before genetic testing, and (6) incomplete or missing clinical data (age, sex, smoking, etc.).


### Methods

#### CT Scanning
  Siemens Somatom Definition dual-source 64-row 128-slice spiral CT equipment was used to scan all the patients who were enrolled. The patient was set up in the supine position with the head advanced. After being instructed to hold their breath at the end of inspiration, the patient was subjected to a series of whole-lung CT scans. The tube current was automatically adjusted, the tube voltage was 120kV, the reconstruction layer thickness was 5 to 7mm, and the image matrix size was 512×512.


#### Data Acquisition and ROI Segmentation
  From the medical records system, we collected the clinical information of the patients, such as sex, age, and smoking history. The semantic features of the patients’ CT images, such as tumor location (left lung or right lung), tumor diameter, presence of ground glass opacity, lobulation, spiculation, vascular convergence, pleural tag, bubble-like lucency, emphysema, pneumothorax, pleural effusion, and distant metastasis (including head, bone, liver, kidney, and adrenal gland), were evaluated by imaging physicians with three and 5 years of experience who were both unaware of the patients’ genetic status. The results of the 2 physicians were then compared, and if there was a disagreement, a physician with a decade of experience was consulted. Notably, tumor metastasis was evaluated via patient medical records, including CT, MRI, and PET/CT images.

  For region of interest (ROI) segmentation, the chest CT DICOM files of all enrolled patients were imported into the open-source image segmentation software 3D-slicer (version: 5.3.0, website: https://www.slicer.org/). An imaging physician with 3 years of experience manually delineated the tumors layer by layer, after which the software automatically constructed three-dimensional reconstructions of the tumors. To further assess intraobserver agreement, all ROIs were reoutlined after at least 1 month. We subsequently randomly selected 50 CT images of the patients, which were outlined by another imaging physician with ten years of expertise. The results of the genetic testing of the lesion were unknown to both physicians.


#### Radiomics
  Feature extraction:  A total of 1246 radiomics features, including shape features, first-order statistical features, second-order statistical features, and higher-order statistical features, were extracted with the “PyRadiomics” package in the Python environment. Shape features demonstrate the shape of the ROI. The mean, median, minimum, and maximum voxel intensities of the image, as well as the homogeneity, skewness, entropy, and flatness, are all described by first-order features. The textural features are second-order features, including gray level dependence matrix (GLDM) features, gray level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) features, gray level size zone matrix (GLSZM) features, and gray level run length matrix (GLRLM) features, which measure the heterogeneity within the lesion.8 After implementing filters or mathematical shifting to images, such as wavelet transform, fractal analysis, Minkowski functionals, and Laplacian transforms of Gaussian-filtered images, higher-order features were extracted.9 The current study used the interclass or intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) calculated using the repeat segmentations to assess whether the interobserver or intraobserver agreement was accurate (an ICC >0.75 indicated good agreement). The obtained radiomics features were evaluated with the ICC, and features with an ICC ≤ 0.75 were removed.

  Feature selection and construction of the radscore:  This study applied the mRMR (Max-Relevance and Min-Redundancy) and LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) algorithms to select the most predictive radiomics features. First, mRMR was applied to remove irrelevant and redundant features. Then, LASSO was performed to decrease the feature dimension, and the most appropriate subset of features was chosen to construct the final predictive model. The LASSO analysis included choosing the regular parameter λ, which involved a 10-fold cross-validation approach. After determining the amount of features, the most beneficial group of features was chosen, and then the corresponding coefficients were assessed. According to the regression weights, the most significant features were used for calculating the radscore. The code for the models trained in this study is available upon request from the corresponding author.


#### Deep Learning
  First, the input CT images were resampled to a 1×1×1 mm resolution interval, and a patch size of 96×96×96 was used to train the network. Here, we used a pretrained benign and malignant nodule classification model to extract features, which used 1715 pathologically verified nodules and 14,735 unlabeled nodules.10 Specifically, for the classification task of this project, a 3D convolutional layer with a kernel size of 3 and a step size of 1 was used as an input block, four 3D convolutional layers with a kernel size of 3 and a step size of 2 were used as down-sampling blocks, and 2 fully connected layers were used as output blocks to obtain deep learning features. In addition, class activation maps (CAM) were utilized to guide the network to focus toward nodule areas, where attention maps are constructed by backpropagating the weights of fully connected layers onto convolutional feature maps.11,12 We implemented networking in PyTorch 2.0.1 and used NVIDIA Apex to reduce memory consumption and speed up computation. All images were cropped according to the size of (128, 128, and 128), with spacing uniformly set to (0.7, 0.7, and 1.0). We also used the Adam13 optimizer, with momentum set to 0.9, weight decay set to 0.0001, and a learning rate of 0.0002. After every 5 periods, the learning rate decreases by 10 times. During training, we set the batch size to 20 and evaluated the model on the validation set at the end of each training period. For more details about how these hyperparameters were selected, please see document Supplemental Digital Content 1, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JTI/A326. All models were trained in 2 NVIDIA TITAN RTX graphics processing units.


#### Clinical and Combined Models
  The univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were applied to clinical information and radiological features, and variables with P≤0.05 were considered to be significant. Then, we used these significant clinical information and radiological features to construct the clinical model. The combined models, including the rad-clin model, the DL-clin model, the DL-rad model, and the DL-rad-clin model, were constructed based on this significant clinical information, radiological features, radscore, and deep learning feature.


#### Construction and Evaluation of the Models
  Two progressive classifications were designed based on gene status: 1. EGFR+ and EGFR−; 2. TP53+ and TP53− in the EGFR positive group, and a total of 4 models were constructed using radiomics and deep learning. We defined the second categorization task in “EGFR+/TP53− and EGFR+/TP53+” to write briefly. The full data set and the EGFR+ data set were split into training and testing cohorts according to the 7:3 ratio for 100 times, respectively. Apart from employing the deep learning algorithm in the construction of deep learning models, we utilized various machine learning algorithms in the construction of other models. For EGFR+ and EGFR−, we used logistic regression in the clinical model, the radiomics model, the rad-clin model, the DL-clin model, and the DL-rad model, while the DL-rad-clin model applied SVM. For TP53+ and TP53− in the EGFR positive group, the clinical model, the radiomics model, and the DL-clin model used logistic regression, while the rad-clin model, the DL-rad model, and the DL-rad-clin model applied SVM. The training cohort was used to select the features and establish the model, whereas the testing cohort was used to verify and evaluate the model’s stability and viability. The validity and reliability of each model were expressed as the area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, precision, and F1 score. DeLong test was used to compare model receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. A flowchart of the experimental steps is shown in Figure 1.


### Statistical Methods
  The statistical analysis was carried out using IBM SPSS (version 23.0). Patient age was a continuous variable described by the mean±SD (conforming to a normal distribution)/median (not conforming to a normal distribution), and statistical analyses were performed using the t test (conforming to a normal distribution) and the Mann-Whitney U test (not conforming to a normal distribution). Frequencies were utilized as descriptions of qualitative variables, such as sex, smoking history, and imaging findings, and the χ 2  test or Fisher exact test was used for statistical analyses. A bilateral P-value ≤0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance. Multivariate logistic regression analyses were subsequently carried out with R software, and variables with P≤0.05 were considered to be significant. The “PyRadiomics” package in Python (version 3.10) was used to extract radiomics features. R software (version 3.6.1) was used for the evaluation of the ICCs, the selection of radiomics features, and the construction of the models. The “psych” package was used to compute the ICC. The “mRMRe” package and “glmnet” package were used to perform the mRMR and LASSO regression, respectively, and the “rms” package was used to plot the nomograms.


## RESULTS

### Clinical Information and Radiological Features
  This study enrolled 267 patients, including 127 (47.6%) EGFR− patients and 140 (52.4%) EGFR+ patients. For patients of the EGFR+ group, there were 48 (34.3%) patients with combined TP53 mutations and 92 (65.7%) patients without combined TP53 mutations. The clinical information and radiological features of patients in training and testing cohorts are summarized in Table 1, respectively. Between the groups of EGFR+ and EGFR−, sex, smoking status, spiculation, pleural tag, and emphysema were considered statistically significant, with P≤0.05 in the training cohort. Distant metatasis was considered statistically significant with P≤0.05 in the training cohort between the groups of EGFR+/TP53− and EGFR+/TP53+. Multivariate logistic regression analyses were subsequently carried out with R software. Sex, spiculation, and emphysema were considered statistically significant with P≤0.05 (Table 2). A multifactorial logistic regression analysis was not carried out because the characteristics from the training cohort were selected for only one meaningful variable when classifying TP53 status, and we used this variable to construct the clinical model.


### Radiomics Models

#### EGFR
  For the binary classification model of EGFR− and EGFR+, this study applied mRMR and LASSO to select the most predictive 9 radiomics features (Fig. 2), including 2 first-order features (log-sigma-5-0-mm-3D_firstorder_10Percentile, wavelet-LHL_firstorder_Skewness) and 7 texture features (wavelet-LHH_glrlm_LongRunLowGrayLevelEmphasis, wavelet-HHH_glcm_MaximumProbability, wavelet-HLH_glszm_GrayLevelNonUniformityNormalized, wavelet-HLL_glcm_MCC, wavelet-HHH_glszm_ZoneVariance, wavelet-HHL_glcm_MCC, and log-sigma-3-0-mm-3D_glcm_InverseVariance). The radscore was computed by summing the selected features weighted by their coefficients. The distributions of the radscore in the EGFR+ and EGFR− patients are shown in Figure 2.


#### TP53
  For EGFR+/TP53− and EGFR+/TP53+, we applied mRMR and LASSO to select 7 radiomics features, including 1 first-order feature (wavelet-HHH_firstorder_Skewness), 1 shape feature (original-shape_Sphericity), and 5 texture features] (wavelet-LHH_glszm_SmallAreaEmphasis, wavelet-LLL_glcm_ClusterProminence, wavelet-LLH_glcm_MCC, log-sigma-5-0-mm-3D_gldm_DependenceNonUniformityNormalized, and original-glszm_SmallAreaLowGrayLevelEmphasis) (Fig. 3). The radscore was calculated for the training and testing cohorts. The distributions of the radscore among the EGFR+/TP53− and EGFR+/TP53+ patients are shown in Figure 3.


### Deep Learning Models
  We employed CAM to interpret the data while predicting gene status with deep learning techniques (Fig. 4). We discovered that deep learning feature extraction focused more on the tumor’s central areas. The color became bluer as the tumor approached the tumor periphery, indicating that this region of the tumor was comparatively less correlated with the gene status.


### Combined Models
  The clinical models were built according to the final results of univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses of clinical information and radiological features. The combinations of radiomics, deep learning, clinical, and radiological features were subsequently used to establish 4 kinds of combined models, namely, the rad-clin model, the DL-clin model, the DL-rad model, and the DL-rad-clin model. When we analyzed what was associated with TP53 status in the EGFR mutation group, among 12 clinical and imaging information that included tumor location, tumor diameter, presence of ground glass opacity, lobulation, spiculation, vascular convergence, pleural tag, bubble-like lucency, emphysema, pneumothorax, pleural effusion, and distant metastasis, only distant metastasis was statistically significant with P≤0.05. As a result, we used it combining radiomics and deep learning features to construct the corresponding rad-clin, DL-clin, and DL-rad-clin models.


### Reliability of 7 Kinds of Models
  In this study, a total of 7 kinds of models for 2 different dichotomies were built, namely, the clinical model, the radiomics models, the DL models, the rad-clin model, the DL-clin models, the DL-rad model, and the DL-rad-clin model. For EGFR− and EGFR+ patients, the combined models had higher AUC values than did the models based on radiomics, deep learning or clinical information alone. Among the combined models, the DL-rad-clin model got the highest AUC value of 0.783 (95% CI: 0.677-0.889), followed by the rad-clin model (0.761, 95% CI: 0.656-0.866), the DL-clin model (0.746, 95% CI: 0.636-0.857), and the DL-rad model (0.739, 95%CI, 0.630-0.849) in the testing cohort. The Delong test indicated that the DL-rad-clin model performed better than did the other three single models, with P-values<0.001. Both our radiomics model and the deep learning model exhibited good performance. Although the DeLong test failed to show a statistically significant variance in efficacy (P=0.442), a comprehensive study of the ROC curves indicated that the DL model was slightly more effective than the radiomics model. In addition, the fusion of radiomics and deep learning model performed better than the radiomics and DL models, with P-values of 0.002 and 0.038, respectively, in the training cohort. The DeLong test showed that the addition of clinical information improved the performance of the radiomics model, deep learning model, and DL-rad model (P=0.032, 0.048, and 0.009, respectively). For TP53− and TP53+ patients in the EGFR mutation group, our results were slightly different. In the training cohort, 4 combined models had higher AUC values. The DL-clin model had the highest AUC value of 0.920 (95% CI: 0.857-0.983), and then the DL-rad-clin model with 0.899 (95% CI: 0.834-0.965), the DL-rad model with 0.879 (95% CI: 0.801-0.956), and the rad-clin model with 0.796 (95% CI: 0.708-0.887). In addition to the DL-clin model’s AUC value (0.753, 95% CI: 0.604-0.901) being lower than those of some single models, including the radiomics model (0.758, 95% CI: 0.591-0.924) and the DL model (0.763, 95% CI: 0.617-0.908), other combined models’ AUC values were better in the testing cohort. The rad-clin model had the highest AUC value of 0.811 (95% CI: 0.651-0.972), followed by the DL-rad-clin model (0.804, 95% CI: 0.667-0.940) and the DL-rad model (0.781, 95% CI: 0.638-0.923) in the testing cohort. Although the AUC value of the DL-rad-clin model was slightly lower than that of the DL-clin model in the training cohort and the rad-clin model in the testing cohort, by combining the performance of the calibration curves and decision curves, we believed that the DL-rad-clin model had better performance and stronger generalizability. Otherwise, the 3 combined models mentioned above all had higher AUC values than did either the radiomics or deep learning models alone, while the DL-clin model did not perform better (P=0.054). The ROC curves of the training cohort (see Figure Supplemental Digital Content 2, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JTI/A327, which demonstrates AUC curves for the training cohorts for each model) and testing cohort (Fig. 5) were plotted, and the area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, precision, and F1 score were computed. Comparisons of the efficacy of each model in the testing cohorts are shown in Tables 3, 4.

  The 4 combined models’ prediction probabilities were presented as calibration curves (Fig. 6 and Figure Supplemental Digital Content 3, Supplemental Digital Content 3, http://links.lww.com/JTI/A328, which demonstrates calibration curves for the testing cohorts of 4 combined models) and decision curves (Fig. 7 and Figure Supplemental Digital Content 4, Supplemental Digital Content 4, http://links.lww.com/JTI/A329, which demonstrates decision curves for the training cohorts of 4 combined models). The calibration curve evaluates the goodness of fit between the predicted probabilities and models with the actual outcomes of EGFR mutation. All models predicting EGFR status showed better goodness of fit in the training cohort, and combined models showed better than single models. We find the DL-rad-clin model model performed better. When predicting TP53 status in the training cohort, the performance of the DL-rad-clin model was steady. For the decision curve, when predicting EGFR status, almost all of the combined models showed more net benefit than “all treat” or “treat none” in the training cohort. The net benefit of the DL-rad-clin model was highest with all the threshold ranges in the training cohort and with the threshold range from 0.35 to 0.65 in the testing cohort. When predicting TP53+ status in EGFR positive group, the net benefit of all combined model were apparent and the DL-rad-clin model had relatively higher net benefit both in the training and testing cohort.


## DISCUSSION
  In the last decade, predictive models using radiomics and deep learning have made considerable progress in identifying benign and malignant lung lesions, pathological typing and staging, predicting associated genes, evaluating treatment effectiveness, and predicting disease prognosis.14–16 As a kind of noninvasive means of extracting high-throughput data from medical images, many studies have explored the value of radiomics and deep learning for molecular prediction.17–20 However, studies on the gene comutation status have been rare. In clinical practice, lung cancer patients with cooccurring mutations in EGFR and TP53 have a poor prognosis, but it is sometimes difficult for physicians to determine the genetic status. The purpose of this study was to develop individualized prediction models based on radiomics and deep learning for overlapping gene mutations to further screen patients with poor prognoses while also identifying those who could benefit from targeted therapy and to provide a reference and supplemental information for personalized treatment.

  Using radiomics and deep learning, we developed and validated progressive binary classification models to differentiate between EGFR+ patients and EGFR− patients as well as between patients with EGFR mutations and patients with or without combined TP53 mutations. The ROIs were manually segmented three times by 2 physicians, and consistency was assessed with ICC to ensure the stability of the extracted features. For predicting EGFR mutations, the radiomics model included first-order features, GLSZM features, GLCM features, and GLRLM features, most of which were texture features. The log-sigma-3-0-mm-3D_glcm_InverseVariance, which quantifies the homogeneity of tumors, had the maximum correlation coefficient and was most strongly associated with EGFR- tumors. EGFR+ tumors were more heterogeneous than EGFR− tumors as previously observed by Zhang et al.21 In addition, we obtained an AUC of 0.701 for the DL model in the testing cohort, while the AUC for the radiomics model was only 0.685. Consistent with the findings of Huang et al,18 the DL model performed better than did the radiomics model, indicating that deep learning algorithms could tap into deeper features of images and that their incorporation expanded the blueprint available for genetic status prediction. When clinical and imaging information was analyzed, data from various training cohorts showed that sex, nonsmoking status, and the absence of emphysema symptoms were significantly associated with EGFR mutations. These findings were in agreement with those of a prior study by Zhu.22 Consistent with the results of previous studies, the rad-clin and DL-clin models, which incorporate clinical and imaging information, achieved higher AUC values in this study than did the other models.18,23 Although there were some differences in the clinical features screened in our study from previous studies, which may be related to differences in the subgroups analyzed, these findings suggest that more attention should be given to some genetically related clinical features in clinical work and that the study cohort should be expanded to improve the reliability of clinical-feature screening in future studies.

  In addition, previous gene status prediction models have mostly focused on EGFR, KRAS, and ALK, while relatively few studies have evaluated TP53.24–26 The prediction of TP53 gene mutations are mostly performed separately, without considering the influence of gene comutation status.27 To further investigate this area, we predicted the gene comutation status of EGFR and TP53 in our study. A triple classification strategy using radiomics was applied in a prior study to explore the gene comutation status of EGFR and TP53, and the integrated model including clinical characteristics, imaging diagnosis and radiomics features achieved an AUC=0.791.28 The authors divided the patients into three groups (EGFR−, EGFR+TP53−, and EGFR+TP53+), then they used the multiclass classification strategy of one-versus-one for prediction based on radiomics and clinical features, respectively. In contrast, in our study, a gradual binary classification strategy was used to construct models, and radiomics and deep learning were used to predict whether TP53 mutations were combined in the cohort with EGFR mutations. We first divided the patients into 2 groups based on EGFR status and then predicted TP53 status in the EGFR positive group. We believe that predicting TP53 status in the presence of consistent EGFR status can control to some extent for effects between genes. And in clinical practice, TKI is applied when a sensitive mutation is detected, but some patients have a poor prognosis, cannot be biopsied repeatedly, and some institutions will not routinely test for TP53 status, so this classification is necessary, which is more relevant to some clinical scenarios. The AUC values of the radiomics and DL models were 0.758 and 0.763 in the testing cohort, whereas the results of the integrated models incorporating clinical and imaging information differed. The addition of clinical and imaging information improved the efficacy of the radiomics model but did not improve the efficacy of the DL model. In our study, distant metastases were more frequently found in patients with EGFR combined with TP53 mutations because TP53 mutations promote cancer cell motility, invasion, and metastasis.29 All of the models in our study achieved good performance in identifying EGFR mutations and EGFR combined with TP53 mutations. As in previous studies,18,23 the fusion model of radiomics features, deep learning features, and clinical information achieved the best efficacy among all our models for identifying EGFR status, indicating that radiomics features, deep learning features, and clinical information are complementary. However, our results were slightly different in terms of identifying the TP53 state; the rad-clin model had the highest AUC. To further explain this discrepancy, we did an analysis of the calibration curve and decision curve. For the calibration curve, all models predicting EGFR status showed better goodness of fit in the training cohort, and combined models showed better than single models, especially the DL-rad-clin model and the rad-clin model. In the testing cohort, although the goodness of fit in predicted probability was slightly poor than training cohort, we can still make out that the DL-rad-clin model and the rad-clin model performed better. However, when predicting TP53 status in training cohort, the performance of the DL-rad-clin model was steady, while the goodness of fit around 0.5 in predicted probability were poor in the rad-clin model. For the decision curve, when predicting EGFR status in the training cohort, almost all of the combined models showed more net benefit than “treat all” or “treat none,” except for a small fraction of the thresholds, and the net benefit of the DL-rad-clin model was highest. In the testing cohort, we similarly found that DL-rad-clin model and the rad-clin model had higher net benefit over a wider range of thresholds. The DL-rad-clin model had more benefits with the threshold range from 0.10 to 0.65. When predicting TP53+ status in EGFR positive group, the net benefit of all combined model were apparent. We can discover that the DL-rad-clin model had higher net benefit than the rad-clin model with the threshold range below 0.90 in the training cohort and in addition to the threshold range of 0.60 to 0.70 in the testing cohort. Also in the testing cohort, we can find that all the combined models were higher except for the DL-rad model in the threshold range of 0.05 to 0.25, where the net benefit was significantly less than “treat all.” This further affirms the value of the clinical application of our models in screening patients with the best prognosis. After a comprehensive analysis of the calibration curves and decision curves, although the AUC value of the rad-clin model was higher when predicting TP53+, considering the more stable predicted probability and the higher net benefit of the DL-rad-clin model, we concluded that the DL-rad-clin model had the best performance. Although there are certain discrepancies between the results of the 2 classification groups, there is no doubt that the fusion models have outperformed the single models, and we can conclude that radiomics features, deep learning features, and clinical features are complementary. Encouragingly, this idea of fusion was introduced, as it has great promise for predicting genetic status.

  In our study, the constructed models have significant potential in clinical practice. First, models built with radiomics and deep learning methods can detect tiny characteristics that the naked eye cannot perceive, allowing for noninvasive prediction of a patient’s genetic status while minimizing patient harm. Second, tumors are heterogeneous, while genetic testing only uses a section of the tumor, which can easily impact the judgment of the results and postpone the patient’s therapy. As well, our models offer a comprehensive assessment of the tumor as a whole. Finally, our models provide an option for therapeutic decision-making in patients who do not respond well to TKI therapy and cannot be rebiopsied for repeat sequencing, as well as in some places where TP53 is not routinely tested. This makes it possible for dynamic molecular diagnosis and timely therapeutic adjustment, which will significantly minimize the burden of care for patients. In conclusion, the combined models based on clinical, radiomics and deep learning features proposed in this study will bring new energy into the individualized therapy of non–small cell lung cancer patients.

  Despite its encouraging findings, our study has the following limitations. First, our study lacks external validation. To further test the robustness and generalizability of the model, our study requires recruiting more patients from various centers. Second, given that deep learning relies on enormous amounts of data support while the samples in this study are still tiny, especially those with EGFR and TP53 comutations, we must enroll more patients in subsequent studies. Third, generalizability to patients with other racial and ethnic backgrounds or from different geographic regions is unclear, so we should validate our models in different populations in future studies. Fourth, this was a retrospective study and all included patients completed genetic testing, which is biased, and in the future we should prospectively include patients to validate our model. Fifth, considering the results of this study are based on a single train-test split, performance might differ across other random splits, which could impact the generalizability of the model’s performance. Furthermore, all of the patients in this study had lung adenocarcinoma, and patients with other pathological types were not studied. Finally, only 2 gene mutation statuses, EGFR and TP53, were examined in this study. It is unknown whether other gene mutations may affect our models, and additional gene data are needed to establish the foundation for future investigations.

  In summary, our progressive binary classification models based on radiomics and deep learning may provide a good reference and complement the clinical identification of TKI responders and those with poor prognoses. In clinical practice, preoperative strategy design can be carried out using decision curves that include all of these critical features.
