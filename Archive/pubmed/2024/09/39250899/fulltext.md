# A Scoping Review of Population Diversity in the Common Genomic Aberrations of Clear Cell Renal Cell Carcinoma


## Introduction
  Previous literature has shown that clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) is becoming a more prevalent diagnosis and that the incidence and mortality differ both regionally and racially. While the molecular profiles for ccRCC are studied regionally through biopsy and sequencing techniques, the genomic landscape and ccRCC diversity data are not well studied. We conducted a review of the known genomic data on 6 of the most clinically relevant DNA biomarkers in ccRCC: von Hippel-Lindau (vHL), Polybromo‐1 (PBRM1), Breast Cancer Gene 1‐Associated Protein 1 (BAP1), Histone-Lysine N‐Methyltransferase Domain‐Containing 2 (SETD2), Mammalian Target of Rapamycin (mTOR), and Lysine‐Specific Demethylase 5C (KDM5C). The review compiled genomic diversity data, incidence, and risk factor differences by geographical and racial cohorts.


## Methods
  The review methodology was created using Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) principles from articles on PubMed and Embase through July 31, 2023, written and published in English, with diagnoses of primary or metastatic ccRCC via cytology or pathology, recorded the incidence of one or more of the 6 biomarkers, explored gene aberration via sequencing, were epidemiological in nature, and/or discussed basic science research, cohort studies, or retrospective studies.


## Results
  Aberrations in vHL, PBRM1, and SETD2 driving ccRCC are studied frequently, but the data are heterogeneous, whereas there is a paucity in the data regarding KDM5C, PBRM1, and mTOR mutations.


## Conclusion
  Studying the genetic aberrations that frequently occur in different regions gives insight into what current research lacks. When more genomic landscape research arises, precision therapy, risk calculators, and artificial intelligence may help better prognosticate and individualize treatment for those at risk for ccRCC. Provided the scarcity of existing data, and the rising prevalence of ccRCC, more studies must be conducted at the clinical level.


## Introduction
  The Global Cancer Observatory (GLOBOCAN) estimated that there were 431,288 new cases of kidney cancer with 179,368 deaths in 2020, making it the 14th most common neoplasm worldwide [1–3]. RCC has the highest incidence in North America, Europe, and Asia, is more common in men than in women, and is more prevalent in higher-income populations [1, 2]. RCC incidence is projected to increase in Asia, Latin America, and Africa [2]. The literature indicates that there has been a significant improvement in the 5-year relative survival rate for RCC from 46.8% in 1977 to 77.6% from 2013 to 2019 [4]. However, the variable genomic landscape makes understanding the pathogenesis and predisposition of RCC challenging [3–5].

  Current studies have improved the understanding of the molecular landscape of ccRCC, the most common subtype of RCC [6]. However, many of these studies are limited to regional analysis. A recent review by Cotta et al. [7] explores the genomic biomarkers that drive the development of ccRCC. This study explores the genomic landscape and elucidates the need for further development of genomic biomarkers to be considered for clinical use. The literature used in their analysis is heavily reliant on the use of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). TCGA has sequenced a comprehensive set of genomic alterations driving various malignancies and is publicly available for researchers to analyze and explore different genomic aberrations [8, 9]. Genome-wide association studies in RCC involving diverse cohorts are often cross-referenced with data from TCGA to understand the genetic basis of cancer development. This can be misleading due to TCGA’s unintentional racial bias [10, 11]. Published diversity data in TCGA overrepresent the Caucasian population and underrepresent patients in minority groups [12]. While Senkin et al. [13] highlight genomic aberration data from different regions across the globe, even this is limited by variation, insufficient power, and unstandardized retrospective data. Similarly, Manley et al. [14] combine ccRCC data among three databases, but only explored three cohorts from Japan, across Europe, and MSKCC. Information from epidemiological studies evaluating the diversity of the cohorts needs to be elucidated more broadly.

  Furthermore, the limitations of current ccRCC treatment methods demonstrate the urgent need for in-depth research on the pathogenesis of the disease. One of the most prominent barriers to accessing care is the scope of biomarker recognition. Our previous work noninvasively identified novel radiogenomic correlations with ccRCC biomarkers through radiomic texture analysis. However, more research is needed to identify gaps in existing biomarkers [15]. Another challenge is the need for more efficacious treatment strategies for kidney cancer. Most patients with advanced disease require multiple lines of therapy, experience disease progression on treatment, or develop intolerance to chemotherapeutic regimens [16]. The cost of such treatments is often prohibitive for patients [17]. Additionally, RCC is characterized by recurrence rates of 20–40% even after nephrectomy for clinically localized disease. As a result, more in-depth research on the pathogenesis of RCC and its regional differences is paramount as this can result in differing therapeutic approaches [18].

  In this systematic scoping review, we aim to clarify and share the differences in the genomic landscape of ccRCC across different geographical cohorts and explore aberrations in von Hippel-Lindau (vHL), Polybromo-1 (PBRM1), Breast Cancer Gene 1-Associated Protein 1 (BAP1), Histone-Lysine N-Methyltransferase Domain-Containing 2 (SETD2), Mammalian Target of Rapamycin (mTOR), and Lysine-Specific Demethylase 5C (KDM5C) within them. Basic science studies exploring genomic aberrations that drive ccRCC have provided useful regional data [11, 13]. In their comprehensive review, Bukavina et al. [1] have elucidated that kidney cancer incidence varies across different regions across the globe. The review highlighted that genomic risk factors were crucial in influencing the development of ccRCC. However, this study was limited because ccRCC incidence was not highlighted by the genomic alterations per region; genomic diversity was not discussed. Genomic diversity is apparent in the aberration of genes leading to ccRCC; therefore, a global perspective needs to be taken to review the landscape of ccRCC. The selected biomarkers are the most pathophysiologically significant in ccRCC and are available on TCGA [11–13]. Of the 894 samples of kidney cancer in TCGA, 537 represent ccRCC subtypes predominantly derived from vHL, PBRM1, BAP1, SETD2, mTOR, and KDM5C [12]. Their diverse epigenetic roles are reflected in the distinct downstream effects of these genes shown in online supplementary Table 1 (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000541370).


## Methods
  The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and checklist followed during the development of this systematic review are shown in Figure 1. COVIDENCE screening tool was used to import, deduplicate, and streamline the screening process between two independent reviewers [19]. Any conflicts were resolved by adjudication with the senior authors.


### Data Sources and Searches
  Two authors independently searched relevant studies from the PubMed Advanced. Search Builder literature database and Embase through July 31, 2023. Systematic searching was restricted to human subjects and literature written in English. The search strategies were developed with the assistance of a research librarian and are found in online supplementary Tables 2 and 3. Additional papers of relevance that were identified were also included in our literature review through a free-hand search. By manually reviewing references from key articles, following citation trails, and using expert knowledge, we identified sources that were directly relevant to our objectives. It was possible to include articles that would not have been captured by automated search strategies due to the free-hand search, ensuring a comprehensive coverage of the literature.


### Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria
  Two authors independently screened the collected literature and included studies that met the following criteria: (1) studies in which patients were diagnosed with primary or metastatic RCC via cytology or pathology; (2) studies that recorded the incidence of one or more the following biomarkers: vHL, PBRM1, SETD2, BAP1, KDM5C, and mTOR; (3) studies that explored gene aberration via sequencing; (4) studies that were epidemiological in nature; (5) articles that discussed basic science research, cohort studies, and retrospective studies; and (6) studies that were written and published in English. Articles were excluded if: (1) studies were duplicated; (2) studies involved financial outcomes; and (3) studies investigated cancers other than RCC.


### Data Extraction and Literature
  Two authors independently extracted the following information through a standardized data collection form found in online supplementary Table 1. The collected data included the first author’s name, publication year, title, form of sequencing, sample size, biomarkers involved in analysis, and biomarker frequency if available. If direct HR or 95% CI was not provided or could not be calculated through the existing data, the corresponding author was contacted to obtain the relevant data. If no response was received from the author, data were extracted from the survival curve.


### Quality Assessment
  Two authors independently evaluated the quality of the included studies in this review via the Newcastle-Ottawa Quality (NOS) assessment scale. Scores of 0–3 were considered low quality, 4–6 were considered moderate quality, and 7–9 were considered high quality, with a mark of 9 being the maximum possible score.


## Results
  In our scoping review, we describe data regarding the mutation frequencies of the six DNA biomarkers of ccRCC. Most of the available global data on the genomic aberrations in literature have been described in online supplementary Table 4 [10, 11, 13, 20–60]. A majority of the studies were from the USA (15) and China (9) followed by Japan (3), South Korea (2), and the Netherlands (2). The cohorts in the studies by Senkin and Kotecha included mutagenic variations from multiple geographical regions [13, 57]. The other cohorts represented were from the UK, Germany, France, Eastern Europe, Africa, India, Pakistan, and Brazil. We have presented the global variations in the mutations of these DNA biomarkers, highlighting their over- or underrepresentation in different geographical regions.

  vHL is responsible for the stability of HIF-1α and HIF-2α, and aberration of this gene is a prominent driver of ccRCC [23, 61]. Genomic studies have shown that co-deletions of vHL with one (or more) of three genes, PBRM1, BAP1, and SETD2, are common and serve as important co-drivers of tumorigenesis. The heterogeneity associated with the evolutionary sequence of these subsequent mutations after vHL needs better understanding and can lead to the development of specific translational therapeutics [62]. For instance, 45 mutations in vHL were reported in the Netherlands Cohort Study on diet and cancer; 15 were co-reported with PBRM1 mutations. This was a significant finding in this study because patients with co-occurrence of vHL and PBRM1 mutations had the best cancer-specific survival among the cohort. Other frequent combinations were not discussed due to limited statistical power and heterogeneity of mutations [47].

  Studies that focus on vHL mutations are the most prominent in the literature [20, 25, 31, 33, 34, 36–38, 44]. The TCGA cohort represents aberrations of the vHL gene as most prevalent at 41% [8]. Mutations in the vHL gene are overrepresented by global cohort studies in China (78%), Korea (47%), Japan (83.3%), and the UK (58.5%) [10, 28, 48, 51].

  In a limited sample size of a particular cohort in the Netherlands, a possible association between body mass index was related to an increased risk of ccRCC with a vHL mutation at the age of 20 years [54]. Batai et al. [32]’s study supports this association, as they found that obesity, among other factors, influences the associations between vHL mutations, ccA/ccB subtypes, and race/ethnicity. The study showed the frequency of vHL somatic mutations in non-Hispanic Whites (∼42%) to be higher. In Hispanics (∼35%) and Japanese (∼40%) patients, vHL somatic mutations were lower, while that of non-Hispanic Blacks (∼31%) were the lowest.


### PBRM1
  PBRM1 is a tumor-suppressor gene that regulates pathways associated with chromosomal instability and cellular proliferation in ccRCC. The co-regulated genes of PBRM1, such as mutations in SETD2, are positively correlated with mutations in PBRM1, and mutations in BAP1 are negatively correlated with mutations in PBRM1. The status of these co-regulated genes may affect the predictor value of PBRM1 in ccRCC [63]. A loss of heterozygosity study suggests that ccRCC tumorigenesis may be influenced by the simultaneous impairment of the PBRM1, BAP1, and vHL genes.

  Next-generation sequencing analysis of cell-free DNA in 83 histopathologically confirmed ccRCC specimens from Poland showed a lower frequency of single nucleotide variations in PBRM1 (11%) as compared to a previously reported number of 26–50% [64]. The advancement of radiogenomic measurement techniques allowed Demirel et al. to analyze the relationship between body muscle and adipose tissue composition. Seventy-seven out of 291 ccRCC patients from the Brazilian cohort had the PBRM1 aberration [26]. A prospective cohort study in South Korea showed a PBRM1 mutation prevalence of 35% versus 52% in a TNM stage-matched TCGA KIRC cohort of RCC. This study only selected patients with advanced RCC (T3, T4, N1, or M1), and multivariate analysis showed that the presence of a PBRM1 mutation was significantly associated with metastatic RCC [10]. Contrary results were found in a Chinese study where 106 participants demonstrated a 27% prevalence of PBRM1 mutation in nonmetastatic samples and were associated with favorable survival. The patients in this study had not received any chemotherapy, anti-VEGFR or mTOR therapy, or immunotherapy. There were no PBRM1 mutations reported in the metastatic ccRCC samples [21].


### BAP1
  BAP1 functions as a critical regulator of various cellular processes, including chromatin modification, gene expression, and tumor suppression. The loss or mutation of BAP1 can disrupt these functions, contributing to the development and progression of certain cancers. BAP1-mutated tumors were associated with poorer overall survival compared to BAP1 wild-type tumors [65], emphasizing the prognostic significance of BAP1 mutations in clear cell RCC [65]. There has been demonstrated BAP1 loss in high-grade tumors associated with increased mTOR activity [66].

  The incidence of BAP1 somatic mutations varied among the studies evaluated. Cohorts from the Netherlands (11.6%), China (19%), Korea (23.3%), Japan (12%), and the UK (10.9%) overrepresented BAP1 somatic mutations compared to the TCGA cohort (10%) [10, 29, 47, 48, 51]. Other variations in mutation frequencies were seen in patients who underwent nephrectomy at the University of Seoul in Korea from 1992 to 2015 and were found to have BAP1 mutations in 17.9% of the cases [58]. Furthermore, Sanger sequencing from a study conducted by Lin et al. identified that 9% of their Taiwanese cohort involved a BAP1 mutation. The cohorts studied by Senkin et al. [13] found that a mutation in BAP1 was among the lowest rates of driving ccRCC in the four genes that they studied, especially in the UK (6%), Lithuanian (6%), Romanian (5%), and Brazilian (5%) cohorts. Since BAP1-mutated ccRCC is associated with worse overall survival, a more detailed investigation is required to address the differences in mutation frequencies and their associated outcomes across different global cohorts to synthesize targeted therapies.


### SETD2
  It has been noted that loss of SETD2 drives genomic instability in the early phases of RCC via replication stress and DNA repair impairment [39]. SETD2 has also been identified to be associated with metastases [35].

  In the integrative genomic study conducted by Wang et al. [48], genetic features associated with tumor thrombus formation were identified in Chinese patients with ccRCC. In addition to emphasizing the heterogeneity of ccRCC among different ethnicities, the studies stressed that it was important to understand the genetic landscape of Chinese ccRCC; 11% of the population studied had an SETD2 gene aberration. The Wang et al. [48] study found that SETD2 mutation was highly prevalent in patients who developed tumor thrombosis. Among all of the Chinese cohorts studied, SETD2 mutations consistently were the third most prevalent in the data of this population. Senkin et al. [13] discovered that SETD2 was also prevalent to a similar extent in their Eastern European cohort that demonstrated that Serbia, Czech Republic, Russia, Poland, Lithuania, and Romania had 13%, 14%, 13%, 0%, 12%, and 16% SETD2 mutation rates, respectively. Lastly, whole-genome sequencing in a Taiwanese cohort conducted by Lin and their team of researchers discovered SETD2 mutations in 22% of their cohort [39].


### KDM5C
  Due to its regulation of hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF2α), loss of KDM5C had a greater tumorigenic effect in vHL-mutated cell lines [67]. KDM5C is also an important driver mutation that regulates glycogenesis and glycogenolysis and a deficiency in the gene has been shown to increase glycogen levels in ccRCC cell lines [68].

  The frequency of KDM5C mutation in ccRCC is about 6% according to a combination of TCGA KIRC, Japanese, and Chinese cohorts [41]. Mutation frequencies vary across different global cohorts and are higher in cohorts from Poland (16%), China (11%), the Netherlands (9.9%), and the USA (15%) [46–48, 64]. Ricketts and Linehan discussed the gender-specific incidence of KDM5C and survival in ccRCC [41]. The TCGA KIRC, Japanese, and Chinese cohorts combined showed a significant mutation rate of KDM5C with male-derived ccRCCs (rate M: 9.4%; F: 1.4%; combined: 6.7%) [41]. Furthermore, this was not true in other RCC-related genes on the X chromosome (KDMC6A and STAG2) [41]. Another study found a significant increase in visceral and total adipose tissue in KDM5C-mutated ccRCC patients as compared to vHL-mutation and controls. VAT has been implicated in the pathogenesis of ccRCC with its growth regulated by HIF-1 [27].


### mTOR
  mTOR has been implicated in the predisposition of RCC in diabetes patients. Oxidative damage in diabetes leads to hyperactivation of Akt protein, which inactivates tuberin, and this leads to the activation of mTOR causing a decreased protein expression of DNA repair enzyme 8-Oxoguanine DNA Glycosylase-1 (OGG1) [69]. This described pathway is also associated with an increased cell turnover due to high levels of Ki67 [69].

  Furthermore, cumulative mTOR mutations were associated with poor clinical outcomes in a retrospective cohort study of 419 nonmetastatic ccRCC nephrectomy samples. Higher and intermediate cumulative biomarker scores were associated with an increased risk for recurrence and death. In the 55.13% of the population with ccRCC that were afflicted with the mTOR aberration [59]. Another prospective cohort study in a Spanish population described mTOR mutations as potential markers for response to rapalogs [60]. The study showed partial responders to have significantly more mutations in the mTOR pathway [60]. Globally, mTOR mutations in ccRCC showed variations from 6.6% in the TCGA cohort to about 5–8% across different Chinese population studies but only 2.5% in a Netherlands cohort [21, 22, 47, 48, 53].


## Discussion
  The most prominent theme of our scoping review indicates that there are discrepancies among mutations in the six most prevalent genes driving ccRCC. Senkin and team’s article is a prime example of the variety in the heterogeneity of mutation prevalence [13]. Despite the variation in prevalence of a driving mutation in ccRCC, it is apparent that the mutation in the vHL gene is the most prominent regardless of geographically or ethnically distinct cohorts. Per our review, PBRM1 and SETD2 are cited as the next most common mutations that are involved in driving ccRCC [15].

  An epidemiological review of RCC written by Bukavina et al. [1] has extensively highlighted that kidney cancer incidence and mortality rates substantially differ based on geography, sex, and age. While the development of kidney cancer is often dependent on a variable number of risk factors, specific germline and hereditary mutations that drive ccRCC warrant future study. In a recent study addressing the lack of literature comparing tumor biology between African American (AA) and Caucasian patients, it was found that predictive immune gene signature expression was lower in AA patients. This suggests that tumor biology potentially differs among racial cohorts [70].

  Although we have discussed that mutations in BAP1, KDM5C, and mTOR are common drivers of ccRCC, the data are underwhelming, and no definite conclusions can be drawn from this information. Aberrations in BAP1, KDM5C, and mTOR do not represent most of the mutations that drive ccRCC [15]. Although not well represented in the literature, BAP1 has recently received more attention due to ccRCC mutations exhibiting coagulative tumor necrosis. These patients often demonstrate poor clinical outcomes and these mutations do not respond well to targeted therapy [71]; a precise mechanism of tumorogenesis involving BAP1 needs to be defined. Deficiency of the KDM5C gene results in glycogen accumulation in ccRCC – an unclear mechanism. The sparsity of information regarding the genetic factors causing glycogen accumulation may explain the lack of data. Future research should aim to explore therapy targeting altered glycogen metabolism in these patients [68]. mTOR’s signaling cascade does not operate in isolation; therefore, its activity cannot solely be pinpointed to ccRCC development [72]. There is potential in combination therapy targeting the complex system of mTOR [73, 74].

  To better address the healthcare disparities involved in researching ccRCC and enhancing healthcare outcomes, more research needs to be conducted on the molecular landscape of ccRCC. Kotecha et al. [57] explore Genetic Ancestry (GA) – an individual’s genetic origin influenced by genetics from different ancestral groups. GA is a prominent contributor in defining why there are varying incidence rates in ccRCC among different ethnic cohorts. TCGA demonstrates that ccRCC samples of African descent were significantly less likely to have altered vHL and PBRM1 genes than those with European ancestry [75]. The Kotecha et al. [57] study was the only article in our database to provide information about genes from African ancestry. Our review discovered that most papers that explored these mutations were from Chinese cohorts [21, 30, 40, 45, 48, 52, 53, 76–78]. Whole-genome sequencing techniques were readily available to these researchers to explore and better define the landscape of Chinese ccRCC. This could be a result of the fact that the Beijing Genomics Institute (BGI) based in China is one of the largest DNA sequencing companies in the world [79].

  Furthermore, the studies explored in our scoping review often make correlations between external influences and gene alterations leading to ccRCC. The Romanian cohort studied by Scelo et al. [56] described the population’s heavy exposure to aristolochic acid. They maintain that the high rate of aristolochic acid exposure is not a cause of the increased PBRM1 aberration in ccRCC development. However, they do call upon future studies to investigate PBRM1’s involvement in aristolochic acid-mutational signatures [56]. vHL studies have further demonstrated that these aberrations can occur in specific populations due to other external factors. Krishnan et al. [22] discuss that end-stage renal disease has a role to play in ccRCC in the AA population. End-stage renal disease is more prevalent in AA populations; therefore, the study suspects that this is the reason for lower rates of ccRCC due to vHL mutations. Other studies have shown that the consumption of fruits and vegetables leads to decreased rates of vHL mutations [31]. More studies need to be conducted to test these external influences on specific genomic aberrations, leading to increased ccRCC development.

  Increasing knowledge of the under-evaluated genomic landscape of ccRCC can lead to the development of better screening tools, improving precision therapy and incorporating relevant artificial intelligence tools. Finding ways to create early detection tools is a top priority among researchers against this notoriously “silent” malignancy [80]. Understanding the genetics behind ccRCC in each patient would optimize indications for early screening, thus preventing metastases or worse prognosis [81] Additionally, clinical characteristics are what influence providers to offer first-line therapy; the aforementioned inter-tumor variability among ccRCC patients demonstrates that tumor profiling is necessary in this encroaching era of precision medicine [82]. As medicine advances, artificial intelligence tools may also improve enough to aid clinicians in detecting the subtype of ccRCC more efficiently via pathology or imaging [83]. Heterogeneity needs to be addressed before research can effectively advance any further.

  Prospective data currently available demonstrate these selected markers’ utility in selecting systemic therapy for advanced or metastatic RCC [84]. For instance, post hoc analyses of the RECORD-3 and the study by Ricketts et al. [41] for PBRM1 mutation showed contrasting results for mTOR inhibitor versus VEGF inhibitor. RECORD-3 showed a prolonged progression-free survival for mTOR inhibitors, whereas the retrospective study showed the same for VEGF inhibitors [85]. In a genomically annotated predictive model including the mutation status in patients enrolled in the COMPARZ trial and RECORD-3 trial, the mutation status of BAP1 and PBRM1 were shown to have an independent prognostic value in mRCC patients treated with first-line TKIs [85]. There has been mixed evidence for predicting survival using these DNA markers in mRCC, but large prospective studies are still required to establish their actual clinical value.

  Our review provides a comprehensive overview of the existing data regarding ccRCC aberrations; however, it does have limitations. Data reviewed in this study are limited by the small number of samples in the cohorts observed, the majority of which are not prospective. Also, most of the data from these studies are from patients treated by academic hospitals, which do not accurately represent general populations. A third limitation review is the fact that the existing data are predominantly from cohorts in North America and Europe; many countries in Asia, Africa, or South America are not well represented. Additional limitations include the following: different surgeons who retrieve biopsy data have different oncological outcomes. Tumor heterogeneity may influence the samples that are studied, and we believe that the incorporation of next-generation sequencing platforms into clinical practice may provide more standardized data.


## Conclusion
  Comprehensive molecular data – including mutations – across diverse ccRCC population groups are lacking overall. While vHL mutations are shown to be the most prevalent drivers of ccRCC, other mutations in PBRM1, BAP1, SETD2, KDM5C, and mTOR are known to be relevant. Researchers and potential end users, such as community oncologists, need to be aware that the published molecular literature for ccRCC may be based on datasets that are not reflective of all ethnicities and geographically diverse communities. Future studies need to be conducted cognizant of the deficiencies in the current literature. As we begin to address these fundamental scientific gaps, progress can be made toward advancement in therapy, screening, and detection.
