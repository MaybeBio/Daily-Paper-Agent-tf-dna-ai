# Discovery of therapeutic targets in cancer using chromatin accessibility and transcriptomic data


## Abstract
  Most cancer types lack targeted therapeutic options and when first-line targeted therapies are available, treatment resistance is a huge challenge. Recent technological advances enable the use of ATAC-seq and RNA-seq on patient tissue in a high-throughput manner. Here we present a computational approach that leverages these datasets to identify drug targets based on tumor lineage. We constructed gene regulatory networks for 371 patients of 22 cancer types using machine learning approaches trained using three-dimensional genomic data for enhancer to promoter contacts. Next, we identify the key transcription factors (TFs) in these networks, which are used to identify therapeutic vulnerabilities either by direct targeting of TFs or proteins that they interact with. We validate four candidates identified for neuroendocrine, liver and renal cancers, which have a dismal prognosis with current therapeutic options.


## Introduction:
  Targeted therapy has dramatically improved the survival outcomes of many cancers, for example the use of imatinib in chronic myelogenous leukemia, vemurafenib in advanced melanoma, tamoxifen in breast and cetuximab in metastatic colon cancer1–4. However, most cancer types currently lack effective targeted therapeutic options and even the cancers where first-line targeted therapies are available, the prevalence of treatment resistance and inevitable disease progression is a continuous challenge5–7. As such, the discovery of novel targeted agents, particularly in aggressive and to date, untreatable diseases, is a high priority. The most commonly used approach to identify drug targets is based upon the presence of genotypic alterations8,9. However, not all tumor types and subtypes exhibit targetable genetic alterations. With this in mind, a recent high-throughput approach was undertaken to repurpose existing compounds by screening thousands of compounds in cell lines10. While this approach is powerful, it usually has low specificity and results in a large number of potential hits both in compounds and druggable gene targets. In fact, in the most comprehensive drug screening resource available, the average cell line was highly sensitive to over 200 drugs targeting ~250 genes.

  It is known that tumors from different cancer types may evolve towards similar lineage driven by the same master regulators or transcription factors (TFs) and indeed lineage plasticity is one of the major causes of resistance to targeted therapies11–14. While master regulators can be identified using gene regulatory networks constructed using RNA-seq data and have revealed successful drug candidates such as mirdametinib in breast cancer, they cannot infer directionality of the interactions and do not leverage the chromatin accessibility data that can now be generated for patient samples15–18.

  Recent advances in technology allow the use of ATAC-seq to identify open chromatin regions directly from patient biospecimens 19. Integration of ATAC-seq and RNA-seq data allows construction of directional regulatory networks20,21. While previous methods for regulatory network construction modeled the relationship between chromatin accessibility and gene expression in simplistic linear correlation-based methods, recently we have developed a computational method that models the joint contribution of multiple ATAC-seq peaks to gene expression using a random forest model resulting in more accurate patient-specific enhancer to target gene connections 22. Here, we find that the increased accuracy of enhancer to target gene connections results in more accurate regulatory networks (with nodes consisting of TFs and target genes) and identification of key regulatory TFs. While the DNA binding activity of TFs can be hard to target therapeutically, it has been shown that identification of master TFs can reveal other potential targets, i.e. the proteins that co-bind/interact with them23,24. This enabled us to develop an approach, CaRNets (Cancer Regulatory Networks and Susceptibilities), that uses knowledge of gene regulatory networks to predict drug targets based upon identification of key regulatory TFs and their interacting proteins. Application of CaRNets on 371 patient samples across 22 cancer types revealed 22 clusters, 13 of which consist of multiple cancer types. Importantly, our approach revealed drug targets for all 22 clusters. Among these neuroendocrine, hepatocellular, and renal cancers are aggressive cancers with no first line targeted therapies. CaRNets predicted NAMPT and PPAT inhibitors for neuroendocrine cancer, an MDM2 inhibitor for renal cancer and a BRD9 inhibitor for hepatocellular cancer as effective therapeutic options, all of which were validated using cell viability assays showing the strong promise of our approach.


## Results:

### Patient clusters based on TF scores
  Patient subtyping based on molecular features can be done using RNA-seq or ATAC-seq. We find that while the total number of clusters is similar using unsupervised methods on these data types, often the assignments of patients to the clusters differ (Supplementary Figure 1A). To overcome this, we conceptualized an approach that enables us to identify features that integrate both accessibility and expression datasets as well as the network structures enabled by our peak-gene predictions. We constructed regulatory networks for 371 patients from 22 cancer types using ATAC-seq and RNA-seq data20. The enhancer to gene edges were constructed using a random forest model that was trained using three-dimensional CTCF/cohesin ChIA-PET data and models the joint contribution of multiple ATAC-seq peaks to gene expression while accounting for confounders such as copy-number variations25. We then identified TF footprints at accessible regions and built edges between TFs and their target genes 26,27 (Figure 1A). We identified likely binding events for 1764 sequence specific motifs corresponding to 850 TFs 28 and find that they bind to 54,153 unique regions predicted to target 17,894 genes. In addition to 371 patient samples, we used ATAC-seq and RNA-seq data for three cell lines (MCF-7, T-47D and MDA-MB-231) for which other available datasets like ChIP-seq can be used for validation (Supplementary Table 1). We used ChIP-seq data for 20 TFs, which have well characterized motifs, are present in the MCF-7 network, and have uniformly processed ChIP-Seq data from ENCODE, as gold standard. We obtain high area under precision-recall curve (AUPRC) in MCF7 cells demonstrating the accuracy of TF binding predictions (Supplementary Table 2, Supplementary Figure 2) 29,30. For validation of the gene targets of TFs, the genes that are dysregulated upon siRNA mediated knockdown of ESR1 in T47D and 4 top TFs (ESR1, TFAP2A, ZFX, and MYB) in MCF7 are significantly enriched for predicted TF targets using our network construction approach 31–33 (Supplementary Table 3). The total number of TF-gene edges and the similarities between patients are shown in Supplementary Figure 3.

  Next, we used a combination of three features to generate a score for each TF in each patient: outdegree centrality “O” (Figure 1A), betweenness centrality “B” (Figure 1A) and the TF expression based on RNA-seq (Figure 1B) (Methods). These TF scores represent the importance of each TF for regulatory network in each patient-specific network and were then used to cluster the patient samples based on an unsupervised, density-based approach (Methods)34–36. The rationale behind using TF scores to cluster samples is to identify features representative of cell lineage and phenotype while enabling us to identify the key regulators responsible for maintaining that lineage. We identify 22 patient clusters with a median size of 14 patients and a maximum of 49 patients (Figure 1C). While 9 out of 22 clusters correspond to only one cancer type, 13 clusters contain patients from multiple cancer types (Supplementary Table 4). For each patient cluster, we identified TFs that differentiate the patient clusters and are potentially important for maintaining the lineage of samples belonging to the cluster. These are identified using in-cluster vs. out-of-cluster comparisons of TF scores using a Wilcoxon rank sum test and are called ‘key regulatory TFs’, a definition shared with other studies 37–39. For example, the largest cluster (BRCA-Cluster2) corresponds to non-basal breast cancer samples with TFAP2A/B/C, ESR1, FOXA1, AR, SPDEF and MYB as key regulatory TFs -- all of which have previous evidence of importance in Estrogen Receptor positive (ER+) breast cancer 40–43. Similarly, we identify ESR1, PGR and FOXJ1 as key regulatory TFs in Uterine and Endometrial cancer (UCEC-Cluster19) 44 and ZFX, FOXA1, HOXB13 and ETS-family members SPDEF and FEV as key regulatory TFs in prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD-Cluster12)45–49 (Figure 1C, top 3 TFs in each cluster are shown and Supplementary Figure 4).

  While the patient clusters based on TF scores and discussed above are similar to those based on ATAC-seq and/or RNA-seq data alone, we identify some distinct clusters that stand out only using TF score-based clustering (Supplementary Figure 1 B &C and Supplementary Table 4). One example is a cluster (LUSC-HNSC-ESCA-CESC-Cluster5) of squamous-like cancers from lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), esophageal carcinoma (ESCA) and Cervical squamous cell carcinoma and endocervical adenocarcinoma (CESC) patients, in which we identify TP73, TP63 and SOX2 as key regulatory TFs, a finding corroborated by other studies (Supplementary Figure 4)12,20,50. Another example is LUAD-PCPG-STAD-Cluster21 which contains samples that were annotated as lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD) and pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma (PCPG). Notably, PCPGs are a known neuroendocrine cancer which raised the possibility that this cluster contained other neuroendocrine samples that had not previously been annotated51. We identify known neuroendocrine markers such as ASCL1, INSM1, SIM1, NEUROD1 and PROX1 as key regulatory TFs in this cluster with ASCL1 and NEUROD1 having been previously identified as key regulatory TFs in neuroendocrine prostate cancer and small cell lung cancer (SCLC) (Figure 1C)52,53. We also observe relatively high expression of known marker genes of neuroendocrine and/or neural-like differentiation such as SYP, PCSK1 and CHGA relative to all other LUAD, STAD and LUSC samples with neuroendocrine differentiation and related pathways enriched in this cluster (Supplementary Figure 5). Comparison of a set of neuroendocrine marker genes across all cancer types further reinforces this observation, with Cluster 21 showing the highest average expression of these genes (z-score:1.93, p=0.0052) across the 22 clusters identified (Figure 2A). As additional validation, the two other neural/neuroendocrine clusters show similarly elevated expression of these genes, low-grade glioma (LGG-TGCT-Cluster14) (z-score:1.59, p=2.71 × 10−12) and Cluster-9 PCPG (z-score:1.53, p=3.08 × 10−9)11,54. Additional validation for this cluster comes from pathological examination of the STAD member of this cluster with the sample assessed as possessing a “mixed gastric adenocarcinoma and small-cell neuroendocrine phenotype”

  ESCA-STAD-COAD-CHOL-Cluster4 is yet another distinct multi-disease cluster observed only using TF scores and includes 28% (7/25) colon adenocarcinoma samples (COAD) and the remainder a mixture of cholangiocarcinoma 8% (2/25), esophageal carcinoma (ESCA) 20% (5/25) and stomach adenocarcinoma 44% (11/25). Comparison to Cluster18, which consists of exclusively COAD samples showed that the COAD samples in Cluster4 featured 85% (6/7) of the Microsatellite Instability High (MSI-H) tumors in the entire COAD cohort and were primarily sampled from the proximal colon, observations not mirrored in the unsupervised ATAC-Seq or RNA-Seq clustering in isolation55 (Supplementary Figure 1B and C). Consistent with this, we observe higher expression of MSI-associated genes and lower expression of WNT activation markers indicating a convergence of regulation of those genes in the TF space (Figure 2B)56,57. Additionally, we see enrichment of gene sets associated with MSI-H in the COAD samples in this mixed cluster relative to other COAD samples (Supplementary Figure 5)58,59. In exploring the function of the TFs exclusive to each cluster we find that while both sets of TFs are strongly associated with DNA-binding, transcription and RNA polymerase II activities, the TFs exclusive to Cluster 4 are associated with immune system functions as well as gut differentiation via NFAT/SMAD and endoderm differentiation. On the other hand, the TFs exclusive to Cluster 18 are associated primarily with RUNX1 related signaling. This is notable, as tumors of the upper and earlier GI tract (Cluster4) occasionally feature deletions of RUNX1 whereas RUNX1 is upregulated in canonical (WNT-driven) colorectal cancers 60,61. (Supplementary Figure 5L-M).

  Another multi-disease cluster observed exclusively using TF scores consists of a mixture of ESCA and STAD samples (ESCA-STAD-Cluster17) and is separate from the previously discussed GI cluster, all of which show low somatic copy number alterations in contrast with the ESCA and STAD samples in the cluster (ESCA-STAD-COAD-CHOL-Cluster4) containing the MSI-H colon samples62.

  Moreover, TFs that are universally highly expressed but exhibit high centrality only in specific clusters emphasize the importance of using centrality measures to guide the identification of key regulatory TFs. For example, JUND (a member of the AP-1 family) is universally highly expressed but exhibits high betweenness centrality only in KIRP-KIRC-Cluster7 (renal) and SKCM-ACC-Cluster20 (adrenocortical cancers) leading to a notably higher TF-score in both (Supplementary Figure 6 with additional examples in Supplementary Figure 4). Prior work has demonstrated the importance of AP-1 member TFs in pro-invasiveness phenotypes in both renal and a subset of adrenocortical cancers supporting our results63.

  Our methodology based on TF scores can capture integrated features of the chromatin and transcriptomic landscape and helps in identification of key regulatory TFs and master regulators of each cluster. It allows grouping of patients based on similarity of the key regulators, providing an ideal framework to identify potential drug targets. A comparison of our predicted key regulatory TFs with DepMap and other approaches is shown in Supplementary Tables 5, 6 and Supplementary Figure 7 64–66.


### Cancer Regulatory Networks and Susceptibilities (CaRNetS): Computational framework to identify drug candidates for each cluster
  We used the clusters based on TF scores to identify new potential drug targets in a novel approach called Cancer Regulatory Networks and Susceptibilities (CaRNetS). Recent studies have shown that besides the key regulatory TFs, the proteins that interact with them can be promising drug targets due to their ability to dysregulate the downstream TF targets, for example the FOXM1:CENPF regulon in prostate cancer, TWIST1/SNAI1:TGFβ and RUNX1/ETS1:CBFβ in metastasis in multiple cancers 18,67–69. Thus, to identify the proteins that co-operate with the key regulatory TFs in the appropriate biological context, we used the co-dependency scores of genes from DepMap64. To do this we first need to identify cell lines that best model patient clusters and identify the targetable genes that are highly correlated with cluster-specific key regulatory TFs. We used the nearest-template prediction approach using RNA-Seq data from the patient clusters and cell lines from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) (Figure 3A) (Methods)70,71. We applied this approach to 496 cell lines belonging to similar tissue types as the cancer types in our patient cohort and with gene dependency data; 335 of which we can robustly assign to a patient cluster (Figure 3B. Supplementary Table 7).

  The majority of cell lines (64%) are assigned to clusters containing their specific cancer type though there is notable variation across cancer types with a median of 78% cell lines of a given cancer type showing concordance with the cancer type of its assigned cluster (Figure 3C). Prominent examples of divergence from expected cancer types include the cell lines FU97, NCI-H226, SW-1710 and JHH1, which have previously been associated with STAD, LUSC, BLCA and LIHC assigned as LIHC, MESO, KIRC/KIRP and UCEC respectively, an observation supported by other comprehensive approaches72. The cancer type with the most divergent assignments from expectation is LUAD. However, we find 37% (32/86) LUAD cell lines predicted to represent non-LUAD clusters are assigned to clusters containing squamous-like cancers and an additional 38% (33/86) are predicted to represent neural/neuroendocrine-like cancer types, with 94% of those (31/33) having been annotated as small-cell/neuroendocrine-like. This potentially indicates that some LUAD cell lines are more neuroendocrine than their labels suggest. We also explored whether there were obvious biases against accurate prediction for cell lines taken from metastatic sites given that most TCGA samples are from primary cancer sites. We do not find any evidence for differences in prediction accuracy by tumor sampling site (Supplementary Figure 8). Although the assignment of some cell lines to clusters containing other cancer subtypes may be related to the accuracy of the approach (Methods), overall this indicates that this RNA-seq based assignment of cell lines may reflect the underlying biology better than the original cancer subtype assigned to the cell line.

  The assignment of cell lines to patient clusters allowed us to use the data from DepMap to identify proteins that likely co-operate with the key regulatory TFs in each cluster24. The DepMap dataset represents the effects of knockdown of over 18,000 genes on the growth/viability of over 800 cell lines. Negative dependency scores indicate that the knockdown of the gene had deleterious effects on a cell line whereas positive scores indicate that knockdown promoted cell line growth. To identify genes highly correlated with our key regulatory TFs in a cluster-specific fashion that are druggable by existing compounds, we computed correlation of gene dependency scores in a cluster-specific fashion. Next, to identify therapeutically relevant genes, we utilized the Drug Interaction Database to identify those genes with known interactions with an available and/or published compound73. We further prioritized TF-Gene pairs using an estimate of drug toxicity of compounds that interact with the gene member of the pair and TF-score from our regulatory networks (Figure 4A) (Methods). We curated a list of Cluster-Gene pairs that represent druggable genes currently used in the clinic or have entered clinical trials in their respective cancers (Supplementary Table 8) 2–4,14,74–79. These include ESR1 in ER+ breast cancer, KRAS in colon and lung cancers, EGFR in lung cancer, and AR in prostate cancer. We used these pairs (highlighted in Supplementary Table 8) with substantial evidence and a long history of importance for treatment as well as a well-studied relationship with one or more key TFs identified for that cancer in our regulatory networks to optimally integrate the features to compute a CaRNetS score for each TF-Gene pair.74,79–85


### Drug targets identified using CaRNetS
  Our approach identified a set of target genes and corresponding drugs as candidates for each cluster (Figure 4B, Supplementary Table 9). The CaRNetS scores are able to identify candidates known to be drivers of cancers or targets of inhibitors, which were not used for optimization, providing validation to the approach (Supplementary Table 8,2–4,14,74–78). Indeed, we find that 12 out of 18 genes for which inhibitors are currently used in the clinic or are under trials rank at 90% or higher in at least one cluster (Figure 4C and 4D). Importantly our approach reveals other potential targetable genes for each cluster, which is especially promising for those with few or no targeted therapies such as neuroendocrine cancers, Testicular and germ cell tumors (TGCT) and mesothelioma (MESO) (Figure 4C). Even for cancers with known targets, resistance to targeted therapy is widespread and thus the candidates identified by CaRNetS may offer promising opportunities for combination and/or second-line therapies5,6.

  In addition to the above, we identified EP300 as a top candidate in ER+ BRCA and in PRAD. EP300 is itself a histone acetyltransferase and is frequently found at active enhancer regions. Our result is supported by a recent study that demonstrated the benefits of EP300/CBP inhibitors in AR-positive BRCA and AR-positive PRAD cell lines with negligible effects on IC50’s for AR-negative cancers86. We also identified FGFR4 in LIHC, which is supported by recent studies showing efficacy of an inhibitor, Roblitinib, in vitro and in vivo87. We find other chromatin modulators such as BRD7 in low grade glioma (LGG), BRD9 in Liver/Hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC) and KDM5A in TGCT, KDM1A, KDM4A, KMT5B and KDM5C in PRAD as high priority druggable genes.

  In addition to the above candidates with some evidence from other studies supporting their prioritization (FGFR4 and EP30086,87), we identified several candidate genes in cancers with few effective targeted therapies, namely BRD9 in LIHC, MDM2 in renal cancer (KIRC), and NAMPT and PPAT in neuroendocrine cancers. To validate these predictions, we tested inhibitors of BRD9, MDM2, NAMPT and PPAT in HepG2 (LIHC-Clust15), A498 (KIRP-KIRC-Clust7) and DMS53 (LUAD-PCPG-STAD-Cluster21) cell lines respectively. We observed statistically significant effects on cell growth for BRD9, MDM2, NAMPT and PPAT inhibitors as single agents compared to HCC1143 (BRCA-Clust10) control across all 4 genes as well as A498 and HepG2 controls in select experiments, as measured by cell viability assays (Figure 5 A-D and Supplementary Figure 9), and an inability to identify IC50s for these inhibitors in the control cell line HCC1143. Encouragingly, the observed effect of the NAMPT inhibitor, Teglarinad Chloride, on DMS53 cell lines is concordant with its effect in GOT1 cells, a gut neuroendocrine cell line, both in vitro as well as in xenograft models88.

  Mercaptopurine hydrate is identified as one of the two drugs targeting the protein PPAT and is highly correlated with both INSM1 and ASCL1, key regulatory TFs in neuroendocrine/small cell tumors. The drug has a primary indication in hematological malignancies with occasional use in Crohn’s disease89–91. This is consistent with the observation of Balanis et al. of shared susceptibilities between hematological malignancies and neuroendocrine/small-cell tumors11. We identified MDM2 as a priority target and utilized RG7112, which is a highly selective inhibitor of MDM2 for validation. MDM2 is an oncogenic ubiquitin ligase, responsible for ubiquitination and degradation of TP53 and other tumor suppressor proteins92–94. MDM2 is highly correlated with PAX2 in renal cell lines, a gene implicated in other renal pathologies95,96. Our networks indicate that PAX2 is regulated by NR2F2, another key regulatory TF in renal cancers. Teglarinad Chloride was identified as the only drug that targets Nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase (NAMPT), a key member of the NAD+ salvage pathway. Moreover, NAMPT is a direct regulatory target of ASCL1 in our regulatory networks 97,98. TP-472 was one of several highly selective inhibitors of BRD9, identified as a top drug candidate in hepatocellular cancer. BRD9 is a bromodomain protein and has been characterized as part of non-canonical BAF (SWI/SNF) complexes that function as ATP-dependent chromatin remodelers99. In our analysis it is highly correlated with HNF4A, which has the highest TF-score in hepatocellular cancers and importantly, BRD9 is both highly expressed in LIHC and associated with worse outcomes100.

  Importantly, all four drugs validated here are candidates in cancer types that have dismal outcomes even with the use of existing treatment options (Figure 5). In LIHC, the patients with the best outcomes can expect a 5-year survival of 50–70% with a recurrence rate of up to 80% at 5 years depending on initial treatment modality101–103. Similarly, neuroendocrine lung cancers have dismal outcomes with ~32% 5-year survival rates104–106. This underscores the dire need for effective first line therapies for these cancers and the potential utility of CaRNetS in identifying these therapies.

  While high-throughput validation of all drugs predicted by CaRNetS is not feasible, we compared our results with the PRISM dataset10. Although the PRISM dataset has some major limitations, such as the treatment of cells with only one (2.5 uM) dose of most compounds of interest and reading out the effect of this compound on cell line growth as measured at one time point (120 hours post treatment), it provides the results of a high-throughput assay against which the computational predictions can be compared. In an AUPRC-based comparison we find CaRNetS performs better than dependency from DepMap in 14/19 clusters (Supplementary Figure 10). Details can be found under Methods section ‘Comparison of predicted key regulatory TFs with other approaches’.


## Discussion:
  We present a novel approach that leverages a high-quality set of enhancer-gene predictions in a large patient cohort to reconstruct directed TF-Gene networks for 371 primary cancer patients across 22 cancer types. We compute scores for all TFs that reflect their importance at the integrated levels of chromatin accessibility and transcription and allow clustering of patient samples. While some of these TFs can be potentially targeted by drugs, in general, the DNA binding activity of TFs is hard to target. Thus, we develop a framework which relies on the interactions of TFs with other targetable proteins in the appropriate biological context. Importantly, our approach, CaRNetS, identifies known key genes used for targeted therapy, such as BRAF in skin cancers, CTNNB1 (B-Catenin) in colon cancers and ERBB2 (Her2) in Lung cancers. With these known positive cases as a reference point, we sought to validate the top candidates in cancers with limited effective targeted therapies. Validation was performed on 4 gene candidates across 3 disease types. Our approach found that inhibition of all 4 candidate genes significantly affected cell growth in cell lines representing renal, liver, and neuroendocrine cancer types as compared to controls.

  We note that although the essentiality scores of genes in these cell lines also indicate molecular dependencies and the sole use of cell line dependency data can also help identify drug candidates, a major limitation is the volume of genes identified as essential for a given cell line with the average cell line dependent on the presence of ~2150 genes. High throughput screens are an incredible tool that enable broader testing and identification of key pathways previously unexplored in many cancers. However, there exists the potential for false negatives due to issues with dosing amounts and scheduling (chronic treatment vs. single treatments) while seemingly positive drugs may be highly toxic in vivo. We intentionally consider drug toxicity as an important feature in CaRNetS, a feature typically ignored by most gene dependency-based approaches. CaRNetS uses an approach that relies on the molecular features of tumors and may be used in integration with CRISPR-based approaches to identify the most promising candidates. The TFs identified represent key contributors to the cancer type/cell lineage and co-dependent genes are typically members of the same signaling cascades and pathways, are protein-protein interactors, and/or regulatory targets. Overall, the strong predictive capabilities of CaRNets are based on (a) construction of gene regulatory networks with edge directionality between TFs and their target genes, which is missing in networks based solely on RNA-seq15, (b) modeling the joint contribution of multiple ATAC-seq peaks to gene expression resulting in more accurate patient-specific enhancer to target gene connections, and (c) incorporation of druggable proteins that co-bind/interact with lineage determining TFs. CaRNets is available online at GitHub and a docker image has been made available for download.

  The approach used in this study is one example for determining the importance of each TF and we acknowledge that the combination could be optimized in future studies. Furthermore, since the TFs are predicted to be important for lineage, future studies may be used to validate the impact of TF knockdown on chromatin accessibility, transcriptomes, morphology, and viability. Integration with genomic level events, such as single nucleotide variations and structural variations, may also provide explanations for some clusters observed using the TF scores. If uniformly generated and processed ATAC-seq data becomes available for cell lines in CCLE, its integration with RNA-seq data and subsequent computation of TF scores to assign cell lines to patient clusters may remove some discrepancies of cell line–patient cancer type observed. We note that since there are only 21 known gene-drug candidates which can be used as ground truth to obtain the scores of gene-drug pairs, the use of machine learning approaches was not feasible, but this may change in future with the availability of more data. Moreover, intratumor heterogeneity is a major issue for tumor treatment, and our network inference method can be potentially developed further to deal with single-cell data. Additionally, while we used three control cell lines from three different clusters to validate the four drugs, future experiments with cell lines representative of all clusters which are not the predicted targets of the inhibitor in question will further demonstrate its specificity.

  We envision that with the ease of performing ATAC-seq assays on patient tissue, tremendous amounts of ATAC-seq data will be generated from cancer samples in the future, including for metastatic cancers. CaRNets will enable the use of these datasets to answer the most pressing questions in cancer biology and identify drug targets.

## Experimental Model and Subject details

### Data processing
  ATAC-Seq of 400 primary cancer patients was from Corces et al.15 and RNA-seq data were extracted from NCI Genomic Data Commons (GDC)91 with matching sample submitter_id. The ATAC-seq counts and RNA-seq counts used in this study were pre-processed by the above studies; Log2 of Transcripts Per Million (TPM) was adopted for RNA-seq counts, while log2 counts are used for ATAC-seq counts.


### Cell Culture
  MCF7 (HTB-22), MDA-MB-231 (HTB-26) and T-47D (HTB-133), A498 (HTB-44), DMS 53 (CRL-2062), HCC-1143 (CRL-2321), Hep G2 (HB-8065) cell lines were obtained from ATCC. MCF7 and MDA-MB-231 were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM); A498 and HepG2 were cultured in Eagle’s Minimum Essential Medium (EMEM); DMS 53 was cultured in Waymouth’s MB752/1 Medium; HCC-1143 and T-47D were cultured in RPMI Medium 1640. All media were obtained from Gibco and were supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (Omega Scientific), 1% Sodium Pyruvate (Gibco), 1% Pen Strep (Gibco), 1% HEPES Buffer (Corning). T-47D media was additionally supplemented with 0.2 units/mL Bovine Insulin (Sigma Aldrich, IO516). Cells were regularly passaged and tested for presence of mycoplasma contamination (Mycoplasma PCR Detection Kit, Applied Biological Materials).

  These cell lines were chosen because they have additional datasets that can be used for training the models and for validation, such as HiChIP, ChIA-PET, ChIP-seq of many TFs, and gene perturbation paired with gene expression characterization.32 Moreover, they have the added benefit of representing different lineages of a cancer in the same organ (breast), which aligns with the framework of our study.


## Method Details

### RNA-seq and ATAC-seq data processing
  RNA-seq and ATAC-seq were generated for 3 cell lines (MCF-7, T-47D and MDA-MB-231). Cells were grown to near confluence, pelleted and frozen at −80°C.

  RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Plus Mini kit (74134, Qiagen) and sent to the Weill Cornell Medicine Genomics Core facility, where an Illumina TruSeq stranded mRNA Library prep was made using HiSeq 2500 and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 S1 Flow Cell, PE100 cycles. RNA-seq data were aligned to hg38 using STAR, counts was calculated using HTSeq and TPM was calculated as described by Wagner et al.

  For ATACseq, nuclei were isolated from >95% viable cells in log phase growth and processed at the Weill Cornell Medicine Epigenomics Core using the OMIN-ATAC protocol as detailed in and sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq 6000, SP Flow Cell PE50 cycles. ATAC-seq data was mapped to hg38 and processed using ENCODE ATAC-seq pipeline (https://www.encodeproject.org/atac-seq/).


### Identifying targets of regulatory elements (DGTAC):
  The first step uses ElasticNet regression to calculate the coefficients for all peak-gene connections within the +/−0.5 Mbp distance of the TSS of every gene across 371 patient samples. The collected coefficients are used as peaks’ weight to the target gene expression. After filtering out peaks with zero coefficient, for each peak of each gene, we calculate a residual I term representing the contribution of the single peak to the target gene expression for each patient sample. In the second step, we construct a feature matrix consisting of peak heights, peak residual (e), distance to TSS, copy number alteration of presumed gene target and gene expression and train a random forest model to predict gene expression using the aforementioned features. We use ChIA-PET enriched for CTCF/cohesin loops in MCF7 cells as true interacting pairs. Only the peak-gene pairs with non-zero coefficients from ElasticNet regression are kept in the second step (all pairs with zero coefficient will be discarded and not considered). Then from all pairs with non-zero coefficients, the positive set will be pairs within the CTCF/cohesin loops, and the negative set are pairs outside loops. The predicted peak-gene links represent the targets of regulatory elements25. Then, cross-validation was carried out in two ways: (i) 10-fold cross-validation in which we divided the data into 10 parts, taking 9 parts for training and 1 part for testing and repeated 10 times with a different part selected for testing each time. The mean AUROC = 0.9113 ± 0.0192. (ii) To avoid information leakage, we also conducted cross-validation with chromosome dropout. From chromosomes 1 to 22, we keep peak-gene pairs from one chromosome as testing, and use the data from the rest of the chromosomes for training. This process was carried out 22 times, each time using a different chromosome. The mean AUROC = 0.8945 ± 0.0294.


### Validating patient-specific peak-gene edges
  To validate the peak-gene edges for each patient, we used H3K27ac HiChIP datasets that provide the physical contacts between enhancers and promoters and can serve as gold standard for our model. However, since H3K27ac HiChIP is only available for a few cell lines, we instead compared the patient-specific prediction of our model trained using CTCF/cohesin ChIA-PET data with the patient-specific prediction of the model trained using H3K27ac HiChIP data. Thus, we trained a model for a breast cancer cell line (MDA-MB-231) for which H3K27ac HiChIP contacts are available. To compare the output from models trained on CTCF ChIA-PET and H3K27ac HiChIP data, we evaluated the predictions for each patient based on ChIA-PET model using HiChIP-model-based predictions as the reference. We observe high precision (average = 0.880, Supplementary Figure 11) and recall (average = 0.807, Supplementary Figure 11) values for all patients indicating the results of models trained with H3K27ac HiChIP are similar to the results of the model trained with CTCF/cohesin ChIA-PET.


### Connecting TF-peak edges
  For each patient, we connected the TF to peak links by using a footprint-based method, PIQ 29.

  Likely TF binding events are identified for 1764 motifs corresponding to 850 TFs and TF families on a per sample basis. In cases where a TF possesses multiple motifs, we use the union of those motifs as the set of binding events for that TF. Binding events are then subset to those with high (>=0.7) estimated positive predictive values (PPV). This final set of binding events is considered as the true set and intersected with known regulatory-elements and their target genes to create an edge list of TFs-peaks-genes for each patient.


### Validating TF-peak edges
  To validate TF-Peak edges, TF footprints are intersected with the patient-specific peak-to-gene links for MCF7 then compared to ChIP-Seq binding events for the same TFs in MCF7 within the same regulatory regions. Over the range of lower accessibility peak to higher accessibility peaks, we calculate precision and recall for each TF. True positives (TP) are defined as peaks predicted to be bound by both ChIP-Seq and PIQ. False positives (FP) are defined as peaks predicted to be bound by PIQ but not ChIP-Seq. False negatives (FN) are defined as peaks predicted to be bound by ChIP-Seq but not PIQ. These predictions are compared to the predicted TF binding sites using a motif search performed using motifmatchr in the same regions and for the same range of accessibility107. Our evaluation indicated that PIQ yields higher precision in the peaks used to construct the regulatory networks than motifmatchr (Supplementary Figure 2C). Given our goal was the construction of the most accurate patient networks possible, we used PIQ for downstream analysis.


### Validating Regulatory targets of TFs
  RNA-Seq data from T47D siRNA mediated knockdown of ESR1 was collected and log2 fold-change (log2FC) was calculated relative to a non-targeting control. True targets of ESR1 are genes with a log2FC <=−0.5 after siESR1 K/D31. We compare DGTAC to a correlation-based method derived from the same patient dataset. To identify TF-binding events we use a footprinting approach to find ESR1 binding events in all open chromatin in an ER+ BRCA cell line (MCF7)27. We subsequently intersect these binding events with predicted peak-gene connections for DGTAC and the correlation-based method separately to identify the predicted target genes of ESR1. We use a Fisher’s exact test to quantify the magnitude and statistical significance of correctly identifying ESR1 target genes in the siRNA experiment using predicted TF binding events and regulatory targets of enhancer regions (Supplementary Table 3). Additional validation of DGTAC can be found in other references. 25

  We also validated the TF targets for MCF-7 using the LINCS dataset32,33. We used the level 5 z-score as the expression changes comparing to the appropriate controls as suggested by LINCS. Among the top 5 key regulatory TFs, 4 TFs are present in the LINCS dataset: ESR1, TFAP2A, ZFX, and MYB. We checked for enrichment of our predicted targets among the genes that show differential expression upon knockdown. LINCS provides data at two points, 96 hours and 144 hours. We find statistically significant enrichment of our predicted targets among the genes differentially expressed upon knockdown for all 4 TFs, although the time point where the enrichment is observed varies for TFs likely indicating the time it takes for the knockdown effect to propagate through the network (Supplementary Table 3).

### Identifying critical TFs for patient network clusters
  Edgelists for each patient consisting of TF-gene edges are converted to graphs and each node evaluated for outdegree and betweenness centrality. Betweenness and outdegree are separately converted to within-sample ranks with the highest value for each metric being ranked 1st. We calculate TF-score as the within-sample, per TF, average of the rank of betweenness centrality, outdegree and gene expression.

  Samples are projected onto a 2-dimensional space using an R implementation of t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (tSNE) on the top 15 principal components of the TF-score matrix of all TFs for 371 samples with a perplexity of 30 being used for tSNE. We subsequently cluster samples using an implementation of Density-based spatial clustering of applications with noise (DBSCAN) in R38,39. We specify a minimum cluster size of 4 samples and an epsilon that corresponds to the largest changes in the slope of the K-distance curve. Curves used for epsilon selection can be found in Supplementary Figure 12.

  Critical TFs for patient clusters were identified using in-cluster vs. out-of-cluster comparisons of TF-score for every TF using a Wilcoxon rank sum test. Candidates are ordered by the ratio of the means of the two populations and filtered for those candidate TFs meeting statistical significance with p-values adjusted for FDR using the Benjamini-Hochberg method.


### Assigning cell lines to patient clusters
  Cell lines are assigned to patient clusters by using a nearest template prediction (NTP) approach using RNA-seq data for the cell lines and the patients. We define a set of template genes for each cluster as the set of genes that are highly expressed in a given cluster when compared to all other patient clusters (Z-Score > 1.4 & Top 1000 genes expressed by cluster average). The number of genes in the ‘template’ set ranges from 104 to 664 genes. We use these template genes to calculate the cosine distance (1-cosine similarity) of a given cell line to each cluster based on expression. For each cosine distance computed, its significance value is computed by comparing to the cosine distances from 1000 randomly sampled genesets of the same length as the template set. Cell lines are then assigned to the cluster with the lowest, statistically significant (FDR <=0.1) cosine distance. Similar approaches have been used by previous studies for assignment of samples/cell lines to cancer subtypes71,108–110.

  To assess the accuracy of this approach, we generated a cell-line specific ground truth by clustering cell lines based on their RNA-seq data and using a density-based clustering approach as with our patient data. In our analysis of expression data from 828 cell lines profiled as part of the CCLE, we identified 37 clusters of cell lines sharing similar expression profiles. Using the NTP based approach, we identified a set of genes representative of each patient cluster, then used that same set of genes to identify the cluster that each cell line belongs to. We find that 695/828 cell lines are assigned to their clusters resulting in an overall accuracy of 83.9%. The median accuracy on a per cluster basis is 91% (Supplementary Figure 13). We find that reducing the number of template genes to match that of the cluster with the shortest template length (104 genes) did not substantially change the prediction accuracy with 694/828 cell lines still accurately assigned to their correct clusters (83.8%).

  We also used ATAC-Seq data for CCLE cell lines from the ATAC-DB cohort111 performed footprinting and in conjunction with RNA-Seq data for matching cell lines from CCLE, constructed networks for 92 unique samples representing 11 unique cell lines across 6 cancer types. We performed NTP to assign cell lines to clusters with TF-Score and matched cell lines to clusters containing their cancer types in 61% of the cell lines in this validation approach. Despite the samples largely clustering together on the basis of cell line and cancer type as shown below, we observe that the same cell line can be assigned to different patient clusters (Supplementary Figure 15), likely owing to differences in experimental protocol and sequencing depth across the cell lines in comparison to the patient samples while also acknowledging that further optimization of this approach is possible. With this in mind, we kept the assignment of cell lines to patient clusters using expression only.


### Comparison of predicted key regulatory TFs with other approaches

#### DepMap:
  While the DepMap dataset represents a tremendous resource, it has its own limitations. Examples of some pitfalls include an inability to target many genes that are identified as important, single timepoint for evaluation of knockdown efficacy, incomplete penetrance of the knockout phenotype, confounders introduced by copy number of the target gene and culture conditions112–116. Thus, DepMap data has many false positives and false negatives. Moreover, while cell lines are a great tool for understanding biology, they do not capture the full diversity and biology of patient data owing to the rarity of patient cancer types and/or difficulty in culturing specific cancer subtypes without transformation117–119. Despite these limitations, this dataset is a tremendous resource to identify important genes in cell lines and we have used it to benchmark our TF-Score based approach. Using the nearest template prediction method, we identified a set of 335 cell lines in the DepMap dataset that we could assign to 19 clusters. Using our most confident cell line assignments (Q-value <=0.05, Cosine distance <= 0.175), we subsequently identified the TFs with the greatest average (mean) dependency scores in cell lines assigned to a cluster relative to all other cell lines assigned to other clusters. This results in a set of ~34 TFs per cluster (top 5% by a combination of relative and absolute dependency). Using this set as the reference, we computed the precision and recall for our top TF predictions using our approach. TFs belonging to the same family were grouped together. We find high F1-scores for most clusters (Supplementary Table 5) with 13/18 clusters showing scores >0.7. This shows that TFs ranked high by our approach are indeed also ranked high by DepMap for the cell lines that match the clusters.


#### Comparison with individual components of the TF-Score:
  Using the cluster identities from TF-Score to ensure a consistent sample set and cell line assignments using the NTP-based approach, we identified key regulatory TFs using each of the 3 major components of TF-Score separately and compared those to the TFs identified using DepMap dependencies (Supplementary Table 10). We find that individually, expression results in overall higher overlap with DepMap data in 8 clusters while the remaining 10 clusters show similar or better overlap with TF-Score than with expression. Similarly, betweenness shows higher overlap than TF-Score in 7 clusters while outdegree shows higher overlap in 1 cluster. Overall, the clusters that show higher overlap with expression or betweenness individually may be distinct.


### CaCTS:
  The CaCTS algorithm uses RNA-seq data and an entropy-based measure to identify TFs highly expressed in a given tumor type relative to 65. One to one comparison between our approach and CaCTS is not possible since CaCTS clusters are based on tumor type (even subtypes are grouped together) while our clusters are based on lineage and may contain multiple cancer types. However, upon comparing the precision of our cluster candidates with union of those cancer types from CaCTS, we do find reasonable agreement between the top TFs identified using our approach and using CaCTS with more than half of the top 5% TFs identified by TF score being in the top 5% TFs by CaCTS (Supplementary Table 6). Encouragingly the top 10 ranked TFs by TF-Score are almost always among the top 5% TFs obtained from CaCTS (median=0.7, min=0.1, max=1.0). We performed a similar all to all comparison of key regulatory TFs from the 22 cancer types provided in the CaCTS approach that are present in the TF-Score approach. We find that the key regulatory TFs in 20/22 cancer types from CaCTS overlap best with those containing a matching cancer type from TF-Score with the two exceptions being cholangiosarcoma best overlapping with LIHC in TF-Score and LUSC best overlapping a cluster containing ESCA, HNSC and CESC cancers. In both cases, there are similarities in either site of origin or underlying biology of the cancer types.


### Identifying druggable gene candidates and prioritizing drugs
  Correlation of essentiality is calculated across all 18,133 gene pairs using the rcorr function in the Hmisc package for cell lines that match each of the patient clusters separately. Cell line cluster identity is identified using the previously described methodology. Gene pairs are then filtered so that 1 member of the pair is one of the key regulatory TFs in that cluster using TF-score in patient data. We use spearman correlation to lower the frequency of spurious or weak gene essentiality correlations then use a Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-value of 0.2 as an initial filter before working with the top 10% of pairs that pass that filter. Pairs are scored individually using the formula below:  Scorei=Di*(abs(Rix)−abs(Ei)−EDi−ESi)*(1+1/Ux)*(1+1/Ix)*(1−Ti)

  Rix represents the absolute value of the correlation between TFx and Genei.

  Di is a Boolean indicating whether Genei is targetable by known drugs.

  Ei is the median essentiality of Genei across all cell lines. Essential genes have lower (negative) values. Genes with high negative Ei values are essential in many clusters and those with high positive Ei values would lead to an increase in cell growth upon knockdown. abs(Ei) is used to downweigh such genes.

  ESi is the median essentiality of Genei in cell lines matching that cluster

  EDi is the differential median essentiality of Genei between cell lines matching that cluster and all cell lines. Median essentiality was used since it identifies the key candidates.

  Ux is a measure of TFx ubiquity across all patient clusters.

  Ix is a measure of TFx importance in patient cluster matching cell lines under consideration, this is a ranked variable with higher rank representing less importance.

  Ti is a measure of how many drugs that target Genei also affect genes that are broad-spectrum essential across all cell lines.

  Drug candidates are prioritized using the following formula:  Drug_Scorec=Ic+Ec+Sc+Tc

  Ic represents the nature of the interaction between compoundc and genei. This is a categorical variable converted to an integer with antagonists receiving positive values, agonists and activators receiving negative values.

  Ec represents the evidence supporting the interaction between compoundc and genei.

  This is a categorical variable converted to an integer. Interactions sourced from clinical trials and/or the FDA are given higher ranks (lower values of 1−Ec) while interactions from single publications and less curated datasets receive lower ranks.

  Sc represents the specificity of compoundc compared to all other compounds in dataset. Sc is defined as:

  1−(#ofgenestargetedbycompoundc)/median(#ofgenestargetedbyallcompoundsinthedataset).

  Higher values of Sc are desirable.

  Tc represents the median inferred toxicity of compoundc based on all predicted targets of compoundc. The toxicity is inferred from the median gene dependency of all genes targeted by that compound across all cell lines. Lower (more negative) values of Tc imply a more toxic compound. The score of all known druggable genes and drug pairs either improves or stays the same upon inclusion of all these terms (Supplementary Figure 14). While inclusion of Ti does not improve the score, we think this is because the known drugs have some undesirable off-target effects since they also target other genes, and ideally one would want to prioritize others that do not have these effects. Thus, we decided to include Ti term. Moreover, since Ux and Ix can have numerical values (ranging from 1 to 34), we use (1+1/Ux or 1+1/Ix). We found that using just 1/Ux or 1/Ix minimizes the contribution of these terms and does not optimize the score of known drugs. Ti is a fractional value hence the use of 1−Ti.


### Validating drug candidates in vitro:
  Cells were seeded in 96 well plate at a density of 3000 cells/well. Multiple control cell lines were used: HCC1143 (ER− breast cancer), DMS53 (lung cancer), and A498 (kidney cancer). These controls were chosen due to the availability of cells in sufficient quantities to allow for the experiments and because they do not belong to the predicted target cluster of the drugs being evaluated. The use of control cell lines is just to show that the small molecule inhibitors used won’t just kill any cells but rather show effects in specific cell lines. The expression of the target gene is not necessarily higher in the target cell line relative to the controls, and thus expression-only analysis would not be able to identify them as potential target genes (Supplementary Figure 9).

  A498 (ATCC-HTB-44) cells were treated with half log dilutions of RG-7112 (Selleckchem S7030). Cells were assessed using Cell Titer Glo (Promega-G9242) after 6h. DMS53 (ATCC-CRL-2062) cells were treated with log dilutions of Mercaptopurine Hydrate (Cayman Chemicals 23675) and half log dilutions of Teglarinad Chloride (MedChemExpress – HY- 10080). Cells were assessed with Cell Titer Glo at 6h and 48h respectively. HEPG2 cells were treated with doubling dilutions of TP-472 (Tocris-6000) and were assessed after 48h on treatment. All assays were performed with 3 biological replicates consisting of 3 technical replicates each at every drug concentration. To minimize plate variation, the control cell line was added to the same 96 well. Internal controls also included wells incubated with vehicle and medium alone.
