# Conjugative transfer of the IncN plasmid pKM101 is mediated by dynamic interactions between the TraK accessory factor and TraI relaxase


## Abstract
  Conjugative dissemination of mobile genetic elements (MGEs) among bacteria is initiated by assembly of the relaxosome at the MGE’s origin-of-transfer (oriT) sequence. A critical but poorly defined step of relaxosome assembly involves recruitment of the catalytic relaxase to its DNA strand-specific nicking site within oriT. Here, we present evidence by AlphaFold modeling, affinity pulldowns, and in vivo site-directed photocrosslinking that the TraK Ribbon-Helix-Helix DNA-binding protein recruits TraI to oriT through a dynamic interaction in which TraI’s C-terminal unstructured domain (TraICTD) wraps around TraK’s C-proximal tetramerization domain. Upon relaxosome assembly, conformational changes disrupt this contact, and TraICTD instead self-associates as a prerequisite for relaxase catalytic functions or substrate engagement with the transfer channel. These findings delineate key early-stage processing reactions required for conjugative dissemination of a model MGE.


## Introduction
  Conjugative transfer of plasmids and other mobile genetic elements (MGEs) is an enormous medical problem because these elements often code for resistance to multiple antibiotics [1–3]. Conjugation requires the functions of two sets of proteins, the DNA transfer and replication (Dtr) proteins that initiate processing of MGEs for transfer and the transfer (Tra) proteins that assemble as the transenvelope type IV secretion system (T4SS) or mating channel [4–6]. In sequential reactions, the Dtr proteins assemble at the origin-of-transfer (oriT) sequence of the MGE, forming the relaxosome. The relaxase component then cleaves the DNA strand destined for transfer (T-strand) at the nic site and remains covalently bound to the 5’ end of the T-strand [7]. The relaxase and other Dtr factors confer recognition of the DNA substrate by the VirD4-like substrate receptor, also termed the type IV coupling protein (T4CP) [8]. Upon substrate engagement, the T4CP coordinates with one or two other ATPases generically termed VirB4 and VirB11 to dissociate Dtr accessory factors, unfold the relaxase, and deliver the processed substrate into the translocation channel [9–12]. Relaxase-mediated nicking of the DNA substrate is catalyzed by a transesterase domain, and presently there are several available crystal structures of these domains, both with and without DNA bound [13–21]. There is also a structure available of almost full-length TraI relaxase encoded by the F plasmid [22]. While the oriT cleavage reaction is well-defined enzymatically and structurally (see [20,23,24]), comparatively little is known concerning the initiating steps of relaxosome assembly, or subsequent reactions involving relaxosome engagement with the T4CP relaxosome - T4CP docking reaction or processing of the DNA substrate for entrance into the mating channel. Further definition of the early steps of DNA transfer is critical because each reaction represents a viable target for development of inhibitory strategies aimed at suppressing the proliferation of antibiotic resistance.

  The IncN plasmid pKM101 encodes a model conjugation system of the sub-type of T4SSs designated as ‘minimized’; these systems are built from a minimal set of 12 subunits homologous to the VirB1-VirB11 and VirD4 components of the archetypal Agrobacterium tumefaciens VirB/VirD4 system [6,25]. pKM101 encodes one Dtr accessory factor, TraK, which is a member of the Ribbon-Helix-Helix (RHH) family of DNA binding proteins, and TraI, which is a multi-domain relaxase [26]. TraK’s RHH domain resides at the N terminus and is implicated in binding a specific DNA target sequence within pKM101’s oriT. TraK’s C-proximal region, which is predominantly α-helical and thus termed αHR, promotes formation of TraK homotetramers and also binds TraI [26]. Through RHH binding of oriT and αHR binding of TraI, TraK is proposed to direct assembly of the relaxosome to initiate pKM101 processing for transfer.

  Remarkably, one or both of TraK’s domains also bind each of the three ATPases, VirD4-like TraJ, VirB4-like TraB, and VirB11-like TraG, that are required for DNA transfer through the pKM101-encoded T4SS (hereafter T4SSKM) [26]. Through this network of interactions, TraK directs formation of a stable TraJ - TraB complex as well as the docking of TraI with VirB4. Independently of its role in pKM101 processing, therefore, TraK physically couples the DNA substrate with the T4SSKM channel to initiate transfer.

  In this study, we further explored the nature of the TraK - TraI interaction required for relaxosome assembly. TraI has an N-proximal transesterase domain that catalyzes oriT nicking and a C-proximal helicase domain responsible for unwinding the T-strand from its template strand [21]. We previously reported that TraK binds TraI’s helicase domain [26], but in fact this C-proximal region of TraI consists not only of the helicase fold but also a ~130-residue linker domain (hereafter LD) between the transesterase and helicase domains and a ~167-residue C-terminal domain (hereafter TraICTD) [21]. Here, we present an AlphaFold model depicting a specific TraK - TraI interaction characterized by wrapping of the largely unstructured TraICTD around TraK’s αHR homotetramerization domain. We provide experimental support for this predicted interaction, for its dissociation in the presence of the oriT sequence, and for the alternative self-association of the TraICTD in the absence of TraK. Our findings support a dynamic model in which TraK recruits TraI to the oriT sequence through binding the unstructured TraICTD. Upon formation of the relaxosome, conformational changes trigger release of the TraICTD from TraK, which in turn enables TraICTD self-association as a possible prerequisite for TraI-mediated oriT catalytic activities or substrate engagement with the T4CP or T4SSKM channel.

### Conjugation assay.
  Donor and recipient cells were grown overnight with antibiotic selections, diluted 100-fold in LB without antibiotics, and incubated for 1.5 h at 37 °C with shaking. Arabinose (0.2% final concentration) was added in the donor cultures for the induction of gene expression, and cells were incubated for an additional 1 h at 37 °C with shaking. Donor and recipient cells were mixed in a 1:1 ratio. For solid-surface conjugation, 30 μl of the mating mix was added on sterile nitrocellulose filters on LB agar plates supplemented with 0.2% arabinose. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h, and filters were resuspended in 1 ml fresh LB. For liquid conjugation, 100 μl of the mating mix was inoculated into 1 ml LB and incubated at 37 °C for 0.5 h. Mating mixtures were vortexed for 1 min and maintained on ice to terminate conjugation. Mating mixtures were serially diluted and spread on LB agar plates with antibiotics selective for donors (D) or transconjugants (Tcs). Transfer frequency was reported as Tcs/D. Conjugation experiments were performed at least three times, and three representative datapoints were reported together with the average transfer frequencies with standard deviations. P-values between indicated data sets were calculated by the homoscedastic Student’s t-test. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.001. For detection of TraI variants, TraJStrep, and TraKHA, the donor cell culture (1 ml) was centrifuged at 16,000 x g for 2 min, cells were resuspended and lysed in 100 μl of Laemmli buffer with boiling for 5 min, and total cell extracts were electrophoresed through SDS-polyacrylamide gels for analysis by western blotting.


### His affinity pull-down assay.
  MC4100 cells carrying the indicated plasmids were grown overnight with antibiotic selections, diluted 100-fold in 30 ml of fresh LB without antibiotics, and incubated for 1.5 h at 37 °C with shaking. Arabinose (0.2% final concentration) was added for the induction of gene expression, and cells were incubated for an additional 1 h at 37 °C with shaking. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 5,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and samples were kept on ice for remaining steps. Cells were resuspended in 1 ml of ice-cold PBS and disrupted 5 times by bead-beating. Samples were then centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and supernatants were subjected to pulldowns with His-pur Ni-NTA magnetic beads (Thermo Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Eluates were mixed with same volume of 2x Laemmli buffer, boiled for 5 min, and electrophoresed through SDS-polyacrylamide gels for analysis by western blotting.


### Bacterial two-hybrid assay.
  Detection of protein interactions with the bacterial two-hybrid assay (BACTH) was carried out with the BTH System Kit (EUROMEDEX)] [28]. Briefly, pairs of proteins to be tested were fused to the two catalytic domains, T18 and T25, of the Bordetella pertussis adenylate cyclase. Plasmids producing the two fusion proteins plus or minus a third traK expression plasmid were introduced into the BTH101 reporter strain. The resulting strains were grown overnight with antibiotic selections. Cells were then spotted on LB agar plates supplemented with antibiotic, IPTG (0.5 mM final concentration), arabinose (0.2% final concentration) and X-gal (40 μg/ml) and incubated overnight at 30 °C.


### Site-specific UV crosslinking.
  MC4100(pSUP) [29] cells carrying the indicated plasmids were grown overnight with antibiotic selections, diluted 100-fold in 10 ml of fresh LB without antibiotics, and incubated for 1.5 h at 37 °C with shaking. Arabinose (0.2% final concentration) was added for the induction of gene expression and p-benzoyl-l-phenylalanine (pBPA, 1 mM final concentration, CHEM-IMPEX-INT’L) was added for the synthesis of the unnatural amino acid incorporated TraI variants. Cells were incubated for an additional 1 h at 37 °C with shaking. Production of the pBPA-incorporated TraIHis variants was assessed by pelleting a 1 ml aliquot of cells by centrifugation at 16,000 × g for 2 min, resuspension of the cell pellet in 100 μl of Laemmli buffer with boiling for 5 min, and electrophoresis of the resulting protein extracts through SDS-polyacrylamide gels for analysis by western blotting. The remaining 9 ml of cell culture was pelleted by centrifugation at 5,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and cells were resuspended in 2 ml of ice-cold PBS. Cells were transferred to a 24-well plate and irradiated with UV (365 nm) at 3–4 cm from the bottom of the 24-well plate for 15 min on ice. Every 5 min, the UV irradiation was stopped for 5 min to prevent heat accumulation. Cells were then collected into an Eppendorf tube and pelleted by centrifugation at 16,000 × g for 2 min at 4 °C. Cells were resuspended in 0.5 ml fresh ice-cold PBS and disrupted 5 times by bead-beating. Sample was then centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C and 50 μl of the supernatant was mixed with same volume of 2x Laemmli buffer, boiled for 5 min, and the resulting cell extracts were electrophoresed through SDS-polyacrylamide gels for analysis by western blotting.


### Protein detection by western blotting.
  Proteins from cell lysates or pull-down assays were electrophoresed through SDS-polyacrylamide gels, transferred to nitrocellulose membranes and detected by immunostaining using primary antibodies against Strep (Genescript), His (Sigma), or HA (Invitrogen) epitope tags. Proteins were analyzed on a per cell-equivalent basis using the β-subunit of RNA polymerase (BioLegend) as a loading control. Primary antibodies were reacted with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies followed by detection by chemiluminescence.


### AlphaFold modeling.
  AlphaFold2 models were generated using ColabFold v1.5.5 [30]. For monomers, model type alphafold2_ptm was used with 3 recycles, and for the prediction of oligomers and protein-protein interactions alphafold2_multimer_v3 was used with 20 recycles [31,32]. AlphaFold3 models were generated via alphafoldserver.com using standard settings [33]. Models were visualized using PyMOL v2.5.5.


## Results

### TraI’s C terminus contributes to protein stability and function.
  An AlphaFold2 model of TraI predicts the structure of the transesterase, linker and helicase domains with high confidence (Fig. 1A, S1A). An indicator of this is that the previously solved structure of the N-terminal transesterase domain with bound oriT DNA [21] has an RMSD to the model of 0.94 Å (over 234 residues). What is not well predicted is the 167 residue, largely unstructured TraICTD.

  TraI with His-tags inserted at residue 430 following the LD or at the C terminus functioned equivalently to untagged TraI in mediating transfer of poriT (Fig. 1B, C); this plasmid carries the pKM101 oriT sequence and is efficiently translocated through the T4SSKM channel [26]. The C-terminally tagged protein was readily detected by immunostaining, but the internally tagged protein was only faintly detected possibly because the tag is buried in a protein fold and inaccessible to the α-His antibodies (Fig. 1C). A deletion of the LD strongly attenuated TraI function as assessed by mating assays, and the ΔLD mutant protein was abundantly detected by immunostaining (Fig. 1B, C). A ΔCTD mutation also strongly attenuated TraI function, but the mutant protein was only faintly detectable regardless of whether the His-tag was internally or C-terminally located. We also deleted the last 30 residues of TraI (residues 1048–1078); this deletion also strongly attenuated TraI function but the mutant protein accumulated at levels comparable to the full-length protein (Fig. 1C).


### The TraICTD is predicted to wrap around the TraK αHR tetramerization domain.
  In our previous study, we presented crosslinking data suggesting that TraK assembles as a homotetramer [26], which now can be modelled using AlphaFold (Fig. 2A, S1B). The N-proximal region of the modelled TraK tetramer consists of two RHH domains assembled from two pairs of TraK dimers. These RHH domains presumably bind adjacent target sequences within the oriT region, reminiscent of DNA binding activities of the equivalent domains associated with F plasmid-encoded TraM [34]. The αHR domain consists of two sub-domains, a 95 Å long four-stranded helical bundle (here termed 4HB) in which the helices are interrupted by hinge regions that might allow for flexibility and bending. The 4HB connects to a C-terminal sub-domain (TraKCTD) built by folding of α-helices of each monomer back against the neighboring helix of the central helical bundle. In the tetrameric model, AlphaFold2 predicts the TraKCTD with high confidence (ipTM = 0.82), whereas the 4HB and RHH domains have lower confidence (Fig. S1B), indicating that the CTD forms the anchor for the tetramer. The remaining protein is likely more flexible, which might allow for independent binding of the RHH domains to their target sequences within oriT.

  Next, we generated an AlphaFold2 model of the TraK tetramer in a complex with TraI. The folded region of TraI is not predicted to interact with TraK, and the confidence of the resulting complex is very low. While the model positions TraI’s relaxase and helicase domains and TraK’s RHH domains in juxtaposition and possibly on the same plane for binding oriT target sequences, due to the low model confidence in this region, this positioning is likely mostly by chance (Fig. 2B, S1C). By contrast, modeling indicated that TraI’s CTD potentially wraps around TraK’s αHR (Fig. 2B, C). Most notably, the four AlphaFold2 models with the highest confidence show that α-helices near the C terminus of TraI form close contacts with TraK at the junction between the 4HB and CTD (Fig. 2D, S1D). The modelling was redone upon the release of AlphaFold3 [33], which predicts the same TraICTD - TraK interaction.


### TraI’s CTD interacts with TraK and self-associates in the absence but not presence of TraK.
  Using pull-down assays, we previously showed that C-terminally His-tagged TraI (TraIHis) binds HA-tagged TraK (TraKHA), as does a similarly-tagged TraI truncation variant composed of TraI’s linker, helicase and C-terminal domains (residues 317–1078)[26]. By contrast, the transesterase domain alone (residues 1–317) fails to detectably interact with TraK [26]. We extended these findings to distinguish whether TraI’s helicase or C-terminal domains, or both domains, mediate the TraK interaction. Truncation variants deleted of the TraICTD (1–911, 317–911) failed to retain TraK at detectable levels, however, the TraICTD alone (residues 912–1078) retained TraK in the pulldowns. TraI’s CTD thus appears to mediate TraK - TraI complex formation independently of a contribution from the helicase domain.

  Previously, a His-tagged TraI variant deleted of the transesterase domain (TraIΔTE; residues 317–1078) was detected at low levels by immunostaining with anti-His antibodies[26], suggesting that the C-proximal region of the protein is intrinsically unstable or that the C-terminal His-tag is inaccessible to anti-His antibodies upon immunostaining. Similarly, here we found that C-terminally His-tagged variant proteins composed only of the helicase domain (317–911) or CTD (912–1078) were detected at low levels, despite the capacity of the TraICTD fragment to retain TraK in the pulldowns (Fig. 3A). We attempted to detect the truncated proteins by appending His-tags to the N termini of these TraI fragments or fusing a GST moiety to their C-termini. Full-length TraI bearing a C-terminal GST/His-tag accumulated at abundant levels in whole cell extracts and retained TraKHA despite apparent proteolytic degradation during the pulldowns (Fig. S2). The helicase fragment (317–911) with an N-terminal His-tag was abundantly detected yet failed to retain TraKHA in the pulldowns (Fig. S2). The TraICTD (912–1078) bearing a C-terminal GST/His-tag was faintly detected in whole cell extracts, but highly enriched in the affinity pulldowns. Curiously, this form of the protein has a predicted molecular size of 45 kDa but migrated as two species of ~55 and 70 kDa possibly reflecting higher-order oligomeric states (see below). Nevertheless, this variant retained TraKHA in pulldowns, strengthening a conclusion that the TraICTD alone binds TraK (Fig. S2).

  Although full-length TraI as well as the transesterase and helicase domains behave as monomers in vitro, results of two-hybrid assays suggest that TraI forms homomultimers in vivo [26]. We asked whether distinct domains of TraI self-associate in vivo and, if so, whether production of TraK impacts TraI homomultimerization. Intriguingly, each of TraI’s domains self-associated, but with different requirements with respect to TraK production (Fig. 3B). TraI’s helicase domain (residues 430–911) multimerized independently of TraK, as also observed for full-length TraI. However, TraI’s transesterase domain self-associated only in a strain engineered to co-produce TraK. Conversely, the TraICTD self-associated in the absence but not the presence of TraK. Thus far, we have failed to gain evidence for formation of a stable TraK - TraI transesterase domain interaction, suggesting that TraK exerts its effect on transesterase self-association indirectly or through transient binding. By contrast, results of our pull-down (Figs. 3A, S2) and in vivo crosslinking (see below) assays firmly establish that TraK binds TraI’s CTD, suggesting that TraK directly impedes TraICTD homomultimerization.


### TraICTD - TraK contacts are detected by in vivo site-directed photocrosslinking.
  We mapped the TraICTD - TraK interface by introducing amber mutations along the length of TraI’s CTD for genetic incorporation of UV-crosslinkable p-benzoyl-L-phenylalanine (pBPA) (Fig. 4A) [29]. All TraI variants efficiently incorporated pBPA, as evidenced by detection of C-terminally His-tagged full-length proteins by immunostaining (Fig. 4B). Upon photoactivation of strains co-producing the pBPA-incorporated TraI variants and TraK, TraI - TraK crosslinked complexes were detected when pBPA was substituted at positions D1006, A1010, S1022, Q1024, Y1029, and F1040 (Fig. 4C). Remarkably, these were positions that AlphaFold2 predicted to be in close contact with TraK in the TraICTD - TraK complexes (Fig. 4D). TraI variants with pBPA substituted for residues in the extreme N- or C-proximal regions of the CTD failed to or only weakly crosslinked with TraK. Two variants, with pBPA substituted for residues H1011 and Y1045, also failed to crosslink with TraK (Fig. 4C), even though these residues are within the region of TraI predicted to wrap around TraK (Fig. 4D). Interestingly, however, both substitutions placed pBPA within predicted short α-helical segments; an outward orientation of pBPA in the helix relative to TraK could account for inefficient crosslinking.

  In the above experiments, the pBPA-substituted TraI variants were produced with TraK in the absence of the pKM101 plasmid substrate. We repeated these experiments with isogenic strains carrying pKM101 and found that the photoactivatable TraI - TraK crosslinks observed in the absence of the plasmid were no longer detected in its presence (Fig. S3A). This was most evident for TraI variants with pBPA substituted for S1022, Q1024, Y1029, and F1040. Variants with substitutions at D1006 and A1010 formed low-abundance complexes regardless of the presence of pKM101. These findings suggest that in the presence of pKM101 the TraI - TraK complex undergoes a conformational change(s) that disrupts the TraK - TraICTD interaction. Such a conformational change could accompany the binding of one or both proteins to the plasmid’s oriT sequence or to another pKM101-encoded protein(s).

  We again repeated the crosslinking experiments using a strain carrying poriT, which possesses pKM101’s oriT sequence but no pKM101 coding sequences. The presence of poriT conferred changes similar to pKM101 in crosslinking patterns for TraI variants bearing pBPA substitutions at residues S1022, Q1024, and Y1029 (Fig. S3B). These findings suggest that binding of TraK or TraI to oriT disrupts the TraICTD - TraK interface. However, we also noted that the presence of pKM101 - but not poriT - abolished formation of low-abundance TraK complexes by the TraI F1040pBPA substitution mutant (Fig. S3B). Binding of TraK or TraI to another pKM101-encoded protein, e.g., a T4SSKM channel component(s), thus might also structurally alter the TraICTD - TraK interface.


## Discussion
  TraK is a member of a large subfamily of RHH DNA-binding proteins that promote assembly of relaxosomes at oriT sequences to initiate conjugative transfer of MGEs [7,26,35]. Results of our previous chemical and photoactivatable site-directed crosslinking studies supplied evidence that TraK assembles as a homotetramer in vivo [26]. Here, by modeling, we gained evidence that the architecture of the TraK homotetramer has an overall resemblance to the F plasmid-encoded RHH factor TraM [34]. Like TraM, TraK’s N-proximal region consists of two distinct RHH domains that are tethered together via a C-proximal tetrameric bundle, although TraK’s tetrameric bundle is considerably longer than that of TraM (see Fig. S4). Other accessory factor proteins share this overall structure but have varying degrees of α-helical stalk lengths, oligomerization motifs and even oligomeric states (Fig. S4) [34–36]. Based on the solved TraM-DNA complexes [34,37], it is reasonable to propose that TraK’s dimeric RHH folds bind specific subsites within the pKM101 oriT sequence as an initiating step in relaxosome assembly.

  Upon binding of its target sequences (designated sbmA, sbmB, sbmC), TraM induces a conformational change in the DNA that results in localized bending and unwinding of the dsDNA. This allows a second copy of the TraM homotetramer to bind newly-accessible sbm sites and, possibly, recruitment of F-encoded TraI (TraIF) relaxase to the exposed single-stranded nic site [34,37,38]. Other RHH factors, such as the Enterococcus faecalis pCF10-encoded PcfF, also induce bending within oriT sequences with a similar outcome of dsDNA unwinding and, presumptively, relaxase recruitment to its nic site [35]. Besides localized dsDNA unwinding, RHH factors have been proposed to recruit relaxases to cognate oriT sequences through direct protein - protein interactions. Indeed, a few RHH factor - relaxase interactions have been demonstrated, including PcfF with the PcfG relaxase from the E. faecalis pCF10 system and MbeC with the MbeA relaxase from the ColE1 system [35,39]. In some systems, RHH factor - relaxase interactions have been inferred from observed stimulatory effects on relaxase catalytic activities in vitro, such as reported for R388-encoded RHH factor TrwA on TrwC [40,41] and F-encoded TraM on TraIF [38,42]. Importantly, in the F system, TraM was shown to stimulate the nicking activity of full-length TraIF, but not a mutant deleted of a ~300 residue unstructured C-terminal domain (CTD), prompting the authors to propose that TraM exerts its stimulatory effect through binding of TraIF’s CTD [38].

  It is not yet known whether TraK induces bending of the pKM101 oriT sequence, but here we have presented modeling and experimental support for direct binding of TraI by wrapping of the relaxase’s unstructured CTD around TraK’s αHR. All four of the highest-confidence models predicted a similar wrapping configuration and remarkably similar structural interfaces between the TraICTD and TraK specifically at the junction between 4HB and CTD subregions (Fig. 2D). Results of pull-down assays confirmed that TraK binds the TraICTD, but not detectably to the transesterase or helicase domains (Fig. 3A, S2). These findings are in line with modeling, which generated low-confidence predictions concerning possible contacts between TraK and either of these TraI domains, and also with our prior inability to demonstrate such contacts by affinity pull-down or two-hybrid assays, or by site-directed in vivo crosslinking [26]. We suspect that such interactions - if they exist - are transient or weak or occur only in the context of the assembled relaxosome.

  In line with experimental evidence suggesting that the CTD of TraIF interacts with its cognate RHH factor, TraM [38,42], TraIF’s CTD adopts a novel structural fold that includes a coiled-coil domain of functional importance [43]. As coiled-coil domains are known to mediate protein - protein interactions especially within multimeric protein complexes [44,45], we asked whether AlphaFold predicts a TraIF CTD - TraM contact, but the confidence level was too low to support such a model. We also attempted to model interactions between the PcfF, MbeC, and TrwA RHH factors and cognate relaxases, but invariably the interface predicted template modeling (ipTM) scores were below 0.4, indicating that AlphaFold also cannot model these complexes (ipTM values < 0.6 indicate a failed prediction) [33]. This is in contrast to the TraK - TraI model, which despite overall low confidence had some regions where the confidence was higher, including the TraK - TraICTD interface that we experimentally validated. It should be noted that, despite recent structural evidence that conjugation-associated RHH factors characteristically have N-proximal RHH DNA-binding domains and C-proximal α-helical rich tetramerization domains, their primary sequences are highly variable. Similarly, while many relaxases particularly of the MobF sub-family have C-terminal extensions beyond their helicase domains, these CTDs characteristically vary extensively both in length and primary sequence. In view of these observations, and the lack of predictive power of structural modeling, further empirical investigations are needed to determine the extent to which our present findings and those reported for the F system are generalizable to other conjugation systems.

  Our truncation analyses confirmed that the TraI’s CTD is essential for transfer of a pKM101 substrate through the T4SSKM channel. Previously, by use of the Cre Recombinase Assay for Translocation (CRAfT), we determined that TraK is required for translocation of the TraI protein independently of co-transferred pKM101 DNA [26]. Moreover, by CRAfT, we determined that TraI’s transesterase domain does not translocate at detectable levels, whereas the C-proximal region composed of the helicase and C-terminal domains is efficiently translocated only by donor cells also producing TraK. Insofar as we detect TraK binding to the TraICTD but not the helicase domain, we conclude that TraK promotes transfer of TraI’s C-proximal region through direct binding of the CTD. We also observed that the TraIΔCTD truncation mutant accumulates at low levels, suggestive of a role of the TraICTD in stabilization of the full-length protein (Fig. 2A). While it is appealing to suggest that the TraK - TraICTD contact promotes TraI stability, we previously determined that production of TraI from a nonnative promoter on a multicopy ColE1 plasmid yields comparable amounts of the relaxase in the absence and presence of TraK [26]. Nevertheless, it remains to be tested whether TraK confers stabilizing effects on TraI when both proteins are produced stoichiometrically from their native promoter(s).

  Besides the transesterase, helicase, and C-terminal domains, many relaxases characteristically possess linker regions or domains of unknown function between the transesterase and helicase domains [24]. Results of our truncation analyses firmly established that the 130-residue LD of pKM101-encoded TraI is essential for DNA transfer through the T4SSKM channel. Based on the AlphaFold model, we predict that TraI’s LD mediates contacts between the transesterase and helicase domains, possibly coordinating oriT nicking and DNA-unwinding activities. Interestingly, the corresponding region of F-encoded TraI was shown to carry a requisite translocation signal (TSA) implicated in binding of the F-encoded TraD T4CP or another T4SS machine component [22,46]. It is therefore possible that the LD of pKM101-encoded TraI mediates contacts with components of the T4SSKM channel previously shown to bind TraI, including the VirD4-like TraJ T4CP or the VirB11-like TraG or VirB4-like TraC ATPases [26].

  Finally, two lines of study supplied evidence that assembly of the pKM101 relaxosome induces conformational changes in the TraK - TraI ternary complex. First, in the BACTH screen, we detected opposing effects of TraK production on self-interaction of TraI’s transesterase and C-terminal domains. Second, we detected loss of TraK - TraICTD crosslinkable contacts in cells carrying the pKM101 oriT sequence. We reconcile these findings by proposing a dynamic model in which TraK recruits TraI to the oriT sequence through binding of TraI’s CTD (see Fig. S5). Upon binding of one or both proteins to respective target sequences in the oriT, the ternary complex undergoes a conformational change resulting in dissociation of the TraK-TraICTD interaction. In turn, the TraICTD dimerizes, possibly stimulating dimerization and catalytic activity of the transesterase domain or the productive engagement of the relaxosome with VirD4-like TraJ. Although purified TraI as well as its transesterase and helicase domains behave as monomers in vitro [21], possible stimulatory effects of TraK on TraI dimerization and its catalytic activities have not been investigated. In principle, monomeric forms of relaxases are capable of catalyzing nicking and religation reactions if they have two catalytic Tyr residues, as do the pKM101- and F-encoded TraI relaxases. However, evidence for dimerization of other relaxases in solution has been reported [47,48], and in the F system two molecules of TraIF were shown to bind to the oriT sequence, one on each side of the nic site [22]. Interestingly, TraIF bound 5’ of the nic site exists in an open “transesterase” active conformation while that bound 3’ of the nic site in a closed “helicase” active conformation. TraIF bound 5’ of nic is proposed to initiate processing in response to a recipient-generated signal, resulting in formation of a single-stranded bubble. This in turn enables binding of the second copy of TraIF, which is proposed carry out the helicase activity [22]. Here, we propose a similar model for pKM101-encoded TraI, in which the observed dimerizing activity of TraI’s CTD serves to orchestrate binding of both copies of the relaxase on the pKM101 substrate (Fig. S5). It is enticing to suggest that CTDs associated with TraIKM and TraIF and other relaxases of the MobF family function generally to coordinate assembly of relaxosomes (consisting minimally of an RHH factor and two copies of the relaxase) to orchestrate transesterase/rejoining and helicase activities, and possibly also DNA substrate - T4CP docking reactions.
