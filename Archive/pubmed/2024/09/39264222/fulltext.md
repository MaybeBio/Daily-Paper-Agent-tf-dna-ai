# In Silico Screening, Molecular Dynamics Simulation and Binding Free Energy identify single point mutations that destabilize p53 and reduce binding to DNA


## Abstract
  Considering p53’s pivotal role as a tumor suppressor protein, proactive identification, and characterization of potentially harmful p53 mutations are crucial before they appear in the population. To address this, four computational prediction tools—SIFT, Polyphen-2, PhD-SNP, and MutPred2—utilizing sequence-based and machine learning algorithms, were employed to identify potentially deleterious p53 nsSNPs (non-synonymous single nucleotide polymorphisms) that may impact p53 structure, dynamics, and binding with DNA. These computational methods identified three variants, namely C141Y, C238S, and L265P, as detrimental to p53 stability. Furthermore, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations revealed that all three variants exhibited heightened structural flexibility compared to the native protein, especially the C141Y and L265P mutations. Consequently, due to the altered structure of mutant p53, the DNA binding affinity of all three variants decreased by approximately 1.8 to 9.7 times compared to wildtype p53 binding with DNA (14 μM). Notably, the L265P mutation exhibited an approximately ten-fold greater reduction in binding affinity. Consequently, the presence of the L265P mutation in p53 could pose a substantial risk to humans. Given that p53 regulates abnormal tumor growth, this research carries significant implications for surveillance efforts and the development of anticancer therapies.


## Introduction
  The World Health Organization (WHO) has ranked cancer as the primary or secondary cause of death in 112 countries before the age of 70, and as the third leading cause in 23 other countries worldwide.(1) In 2030, the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) projects 2,281,658 new cancer diagnoses in the United States, equating to 6251 new cases per day, with 612,390 anticipated deaths, approximately 1678 daily.(2) GLOBOCAN 2020 forecasts 16.3 million cancer deaths and 30.2 million new cases globally by 2040.(2) These statistics underscore the alarming global burden of cancer. Cancer arises from uncontrolled cell growth, which disrupts the normal process of cell division and replacement. Normally, cells divide, and regenerate as required, with damaged or aging cells undergoing programmed cell death and being replaced. However, when this process is disrupted due to faulty DNA, abnormal cell growth and division occur. Such dysregulation is often associated with alterations in multiple regulatory genes,(3) particularly Tp53, which codes for the p53 protein, a pivotal tumor suppressor protein.

  The normal cell cycle includes phases such as G1, S, G2, and M which governs the growth, replication, and division of cells. In response to various cellular stresses such as DNA damage and oncogene activation, p53 is activated to halt the cell cycle, allowing time for DNA repair or inducing programmed cell death (apoptosis) if the damage is irreparable (Figure 1). The p53 protein act as a transcription factor to express various regulatory proteins that play crucial roles in maintaining cellular integrity. The p53 protein induces the expression of cell cycle regulators like P21 (CDKN1A) that inhibits cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs), leading to cell cycle arrest, particularly at the G1/S checkpoint, giving cells time to repair DNA damage.(4) This checkpoint serves to maintain genomic integrity and prevent the proliferation of potentially cancerous cells. p53 also acts to reduce the expression of PLK4 (polo-like kinase 4), thereby inhibiting prolonged PLK4 activity and the subsequent amplification of centrosomes. XPC (Xeroderma Pigmentosum, Complementation Group C) and POLK (DNA Polymerase Kappa) are both proteins involved in DNA repair process. XPC repairs bulky DNA lesions caused by factors like UV radiation and chemical mutagens, while POLK enzyme is involved in translesion DNA synthesis. If the DNA damage is irreparable, p53 can initiate apoptosis by activating BAX (Bcl-2-associated X) and PUMA (p53 upregulated modulator of apoptosis) proteins, which are involved in the apoptotic pathway. This ensures that cells with significant DNA damage do not proliferate. Certain p53 mutations can disrupt its DNA binding, hindering the expression of regulatory proteins and leading to overactivation of TERT (Telomerase Reverse Transcriptase) and c-MYC (Cellular myelocytomatosis) proteins. TERT, a key component of telomerase, maintains telomere length, facilitating cell proliferation upon upregulation. Likewise, c-MYC drives cell cycle progression by activating cyclins and CDKs while inhibiting cell cycle inhibitors. Approximately 75% of the p53 mutations are missense mutations stemming from non-synonymous single nucleotide polymorphisms (nsSNPs), and 97% are localized in the DNA-binding region.(5,6)

  The human Tp53 tumor suppressor gene (17p13.1 locus)(7) that encodes for the p53 protein consists of 393 amino acids.(8) The p53 forms a tetramer with the DNA (Figure 2A). The full length p53 protein contains transactivation domains (TAD1, TAD2), a proline-rich domain (PRD), a DNA-binding domain (DBD consisting of β strands S1 to S10, helices H1, H2 and loops L1 to L3), a tetramerization domain (TMD) and a regulatory domain (RGD). TAD, PRD, DBD, TMD, and RGD domains range from residues 1–63, residues 63–97, residues 102–292, residues 323–356, and residues 363–393, respectively (Figure 2B).(9–11) Tetramerization enhances its DNA binding affinity by 1000-fold compared to its isolated core DNA binding domain under in vitro conditions.(12) A recent joint molecular dynamics and experimental study has shown that mutation such as R337H in the tetramerization domain can affect p53 structural stability and disrupt the DNA binding site.(13) The p53 tetramer binds cooperatively to double-stranded DNA with the consensus sequence RRRCWWGYYY (where R = A, G; W = A, T; Y = C, T), and the binding sites comprise two decameric (10-basse pair) motifs (half-sites) separated by 0–13 base pairs.(14,15) This sequence is typically found in the promoter region of target genes, allowing p53 to activate the expression of the regulatory proteins.(16–18)

  Mutations in p53 can lead to abnormal growth of tumor; however, not all mutations affect the function of p53 in a similar manner. The effect of a mutation on tumor progression varies based on the specific residue and the gene region carrying the mutation.(19) Mutant p53 suppresses and interferes with other proteins, such as p73, the MRE11-Rad51-NSB complex, and SP-1, to produce chemoresistance, genomic imbalance, or proliferation.(6,20,21) In addition, mutant p53 may stimulate the functionality of other proteins, such as VDR, SREBP, ETS2, NF-Y, or NRF2, leading to increased multiplication, the buildup of reactive oxygen species, cholesterol production, and higher cell survival.(22–24) In vivo studies with Tp53 knock-in mice revealed that the R172H mutation, equivalent to the human R175H hotspot, introduced at the germ-line level, leads to a tumor phenotype with significantly increased metastasis.(25) Another mutation, R248Q, found in Li-Fraumeni patients, has been linked to a higher tumor count, earlier onset, and shorter tumor-free survival compared to those with the G245S mutation in P53.(26) A separate study identified the R248Q, R273H, Y163H, and R175 mutations as likely harmful to the structure of P53, disrupting DNA binding and being associated with an elevated risk of cancer progression.(27)

  TP53 gene mutations are significantly more prevalent in African American breast cancer patients (42.9%) compared to their Caucasian counterparts (27.6%).(28) This disparity may contribute to the increased incidence and rapid progression of various cancers, such as triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), gastric cancer, and lung cancer, within the African American population. In a study involving 595 mutations across 119 gastric adenocarcinoma patients, half of the mutations were found in TP53, with African American patients exhibiting a significantly higher rate of TP53 mutations (89%) compared to other ethnic groups.(29) Among Asians, Hispanics, and Caucasians, the rates of TP53 mutations were reported as 56%, 43%, and 40%, respectively. Mutations in the Tp53 gene are prevalent across various molecular subtypes of breast cancer. For instance, basal-like tumors like TNBC exhibit the highest frequency of p53 mutations, reaching 88%.(29) Additionally, p53 mutations correlate with reduced overall survival rates in metastatic breast cancer across different subtypes. Furthermore, these mutations are more prevalent in patients with advanced-stage and aggressive tumor types.(30,31) Mutated and inactivated p53 proteins are detected in approximately 80% of TNBC cases, contributing to accelerated tumor growth and metastasis.(32)

  Therefore, understanding the impact of mutations on the structure and function of p53 is crucial for understanding cancer progression.(33) Additionally, the presence of mutant p53s in cancer cells underscores their appeal as potential therapeutic targets.(34) While no FDA-approved medications specifically target p53, numerous therapeutics that target p53 are currently undergoing preclinical and clinical trials.(35) These therapies primarily focus on enhancing p53 activity in cancer, restoring the functionality of the p53 pathway in p53-mutated cancers, targeting p53 in immunotherapy, and employing combination therapies that target p53, p53 checkpoints, or mutant p53 for cancer treatment.(35) A deep understanding of the mutant p53 structures and their molecular pathways of interactions holds immense potential for advancing targeted cancer therapy.

  Present study explores and identifies possible deleterious and disease related p53 mutations using several in silico machine learning and sequence-based algorithms such as SIFT(36,37), Polyphen-2 (38), PhD-SNP(39), and MutPred2(40), aligning with recommendations from the American College of Medical Genetics & Genomics to employ multiple methods for identifying disease-causing mutations.(41) Several researcher have employed these approaches previously with considerable success to predict deleterious non-synonymous single nucleotide polymorphisms (nsSNPs) for protein function.(42,43) The Uniprot database(8), a widely utilized and centralized repository containing comprehensive protein sequence and function information, was employed to identify nsSNPs occurring in p53 (Figure 1C). A total of 58 nsSNPs, previously documented in literature as natural variants of p53, were selected for analysis (Figure 2C). Mutations identified as deleterious and disease-causing by these computational tools underwent further examination through molecular dynamic (MD) simulations. Crystallographic structures of p53 DNA-binding domain in complex with DNA (PDB ID 1TUP)(44) served as the basis for MD simulations, aiming to assess the impact of mutations on protein structure, dynamics, stability, and the binding free energy with DNA. MD simulations were performed using both the p53 protein only system as well as p53-DNA complex systems for wildtype and the variants. Free energy calculations using the molecular mechanics generalized Born solvent accessibility (MM/GBSA) approach were performed to evaluate the binding free energy between DNA and the wildtype/mutated forms of p53, elucidating whether mutations altered their binding affinity. The computationally obtained binding affinities of the mutant p53 proteins were compared with experimentally obtained values for cancer causing mutations G245S, R249, and R273H. Pairwise energy decomposition analysis was conducted to identify the residues in the p53 protein and DNA that contributed most significantly to the overall free energy. Additionally, Rosetta Flex-ddg, another computational tool, was incorporated in this study to identify mutations, the hotspots, in p53 protein that contribute more significantly to binding affinity than other mutations.(45)


## Methodology

### In silico methods to predict impact of pathogenic mutations on protein structure
  The Universal Protein Resource (UniProt) database(8) was employed to obtain various naturally occurring nsSNPs of p53. While databases like NCI-TCGA, Ensembl, and Cosmic have identified some of these mutations as pathogenic, influence on the structural stability of the protein remains unaddressed for many mutations. Here we reassessed the pathogenicity and structural stability of the mutations through analysis with SIFT(36,37,46,47), PolyPhen-2(38,48), PhD-SNP(49–51) and MutPred2(40,52) (See Figure S1 in supporting information). This approach aligns with the recommendation of the American College of Medical Genetics & Genomics suggests, which advocated for the utilization of multiple methods to identify disease-causing mutations.(41) These methods are also important for structural stability prediction as they rely of sequence homology and structural evolutionary information.

  The SIFT algorithm functions by using sequence homology, and it presumes that important amino acids will be conserved in the protein family, so changes at well-conserved positions tend to be predicted as deleterious.(46) SIFT scores a mutation of a protein sequence from zero to one. A score below 0.05 is anticipated to impact protein function; hence, if the score is zero, the mutation is likely to be more harmful.(46,47) PolyPhen-2 (Polymorphism Phenotyping v2) predicts the possible impact of amino acid substitutions on the stability and function of human proteins using structural and comparative evolutionary information.(48) PolyPhen-2 achieves its accuracy in predictions due to the utilization of a machine-learning technique to train its probabilistic classifier.(38,48). PolyPhen-2 shows over 68% accuracy in predicting disease-causing nsSNPs.(38) It gives a confidence score that ranges from 0 to 1 like SIFT but here the interpretation is different. Scores less than 0.15 are predicted to be as benign SNPs and scores ranges 0.15 to 1 are possibly damaging SNPs. However, SNPs are considered highly damaging for the protein structure if PolyPhen-2 scores between 0.85 and 1.

  Based on the prediction scores from SIFT (score 0) and PolyPhen-2 (score 1), selected SNPs were further analyzed using PhD-SNP and MutPred2 (Figure 2B). PhD-SNP is a support vector machine (SVM) classifier trained using a dataset comprising more than one million amino acid polymorphisms through supervised machine learning techniques to predict variant pathogenicity and it is reported to be 74% accurate in predicting human diseases causing SNPs.(49–51) MutPred2 is a machine learning algorithm designed to assess the pathogenicity of amino acid substitutions. It elucidates their impact on the phenotype by modeling a diverse range of structural and functional alterations derived from the amino acid sequence.(52) The model not only facilitate the interpretation of pathogenicity and molecular alteration scores, but also provide a framework to rigorously rank the underlying mechanisms.(40) For a given amino acid sequence and substitution, MutPred2 extracts six categories of features. The original and mutated sequences are passed through different sequence-based protein property predictors. Next, all categories of features are presented to an ensemble of neural networks trained to distinguish between pathogenic and benign variants.(52) Finally, MutPred2 returns two outputs, the general score (g score) and the property score (p score). The g score determines the probability that the nsSNP is related to disease, and the p-score indicates whether there are certain structural or functional properties that are disrupted by incorporating the mutation.(40,52) By combing these two scores the prediction hypothesis can be interpreted as follows, actionable hypothesis for g > 0.5 and p < 0.05, confident hypothesis for g > 0.75 and p < 0.05, and very confident hypothesis for g > 0.75 and P < 0.01.


### Molecular dynamics simulations
  Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed by utilizing the AMBER20 software suite.(53) The p53 wildtype and mutant systems were constructed using the crystal structure (1TUP)(44) derived from the protein data bank (PDB).(54) and using the web-based simulation builder CHARMM-gui.org.(55) The additive CHARMM36m forcefield for proteins and nucleic acids were used throughout the construction of these systems.56 To accommodate all four systems, a rectangular water box was constructed with a radius of 10Å from the edge of the complex’s surface, and a series of TIP3P water molecules were used to solvate the box. Using the Monte-Carlo approach for ion placement, required K+ and Cl− ions were added to make the solution concentration of 0.15 M to mimic physiological condition. The systems underwent a steepest descent energy minimization for a total of 2500 cycles. After the 2500-cycle minimization, an additional 5000 cycles were performed using a conjugate gradient algorithm. The systems underwent a 2 ns equilibration period under NVT conditions, where the volume (V), number of particles (N), and temperature (T) remained constant. During the equilibration phase, each system was held in place by a positional restriction of 1 kcal/mol. The p53 systems (without DNA) underwent 450 ns production MD simulations under NPT conditions, resulting in a cumulative MD simulation time of 1.8 μs for all four systems (wildtype and three mutants). The pressure of the simulation was kept at 1 atmosphere, and the temperature was held constant at 303.15 K using the Langevin dynamics.(58) The Langevin thermostat used a coefficient of friction (γ) of 1.0 ps−1. The Monte-Carlo barostat maintained a constant pressure, and the isotropic position scaling method was utilized to develop the dynamics of maintaining a constant pressure. Integration was performed using a leap-frog algorithm with a time step of 2 fs, and coordinates were recorded every 5000 steps in the trajectory. The SHAKE algorithm was applied to maintain constraints on all bonds involving hydrogen atoms.(59)


### Binding Free Energy Calculations
  In MM/GBSA calculations, the binding free energy (ΔGbind) between the DNA and p53 (wildtype and mutants) are calculated as  (1) ΔGbind=ΔH−TΔS≈ΔEMM,gas+ΔGsol−TΔS  where ΔEMM,gas, ΔGsol and -TΔS are the changes of the gas phase molecular mechanics energy, the solvation free energy, and the conformational entropy upon binding, respectively. The ΔEMM,gas is the sum of the internal energy (ΔEinternal) arising from bond, angle and dihedral interactions, non-bonded electrostatic energy (ΔEELE) and Van der Waals energy (ΔEVDW).

  In MM/GBSA calculations, the ΔEint is cancelled since the complex, receptor, and ligand parameter files are created from the same trajectory. The solvation free energy (ΔGsol) is calculated from the sum of the polar solvation energy (ΔGGB) using the Generalized Born model, and the non-polar energy is calculated based on the solvent-accessible surface area (ΔGsurf) according to the LCPO algorithm.(58)  (3) ΔGsol=ΔGGB+ΔGsurf

  The conformational entropy changes TΔS is usually computed by normal-mode analysis on a set of conformational snapshots taken from MD simulations.

  Three independent 200ns MD simulation for each of the wildtype and mutation protein systems in complex with DNA (p53-DNA complex) were performed for the MM/GBSA calculation. The additive CHARMM36m forcefield for proteins and nucleic acids were used to construct these systems.(56,57) All remaining MD simulation parameters were maintained identical to those used in the MD simulation of p53 systems. MM/GBSA binding free energies were calculated from 50 independent frames. The Ambertools20(60) ante-MMPBSA python script(58) was used to create the complex, receptor, and ligand parameter files from the solvated parameter files used in the MD simulations and in the script we set radii mbondi2, igb = 5 and the salt concentration = 0.15 M. Additionally, the dielectric constant of solvent and dielectric constant of solute were set to 78.5 and 1.0, respectively, which are Amber default and recommended values. The solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) was calculated using γ = 0.0072 kcal/mol/Å2 and β = 0.0 kcal/mol, respectively. The conformational entropy change is usually computed by normal-mode analysis on a set of conformational snapshots taken from MD simulations. In this case, contribution from entropy is neglected because of its large computational cost and low prediction accuracy. The binding enthalpy was calculated for all four 200 ns MD replicates and averaged.


### Rosetta Flex ddG
  Rosetta Flex-ddG(45) was performed by following the steps in the flex-ddG tutorial available in the Github repository (https://github.com/Kortemme-Lab/flex_ddG_tutorial) and using the recommended parameters. At first, the input complex structure was minimized by adding harmonic restraint to all the alpha carbon pairs within 9 Å using a harmonic score potential with a standard deviation set to 0.5 Å. The backrub method was used to prepare an ensemble of 35 different models moving on a randomly chosen protein segment consisting of 3–12 residues residing within 8 Å of any mutated position. Residues with a Cβ atom within 8 Å of any mutant position and the adjacent N and C-terminal residues are defined as neighborhoods where the backrub move is performed.(45) It is recommended to make an ensemble of 35 structures to make the average calculation more consistent and reproducible. A total of 35,000 backrub steps were performed for each mutation. Optimization of the side chain conformations for both wildtype and mutant structures were done using Rosetta Packer”.(61) All the 35 models were further minimized to optimize side chain and backbone torsions applying same restrain parameters. The wild- type and mutant complexes, along with the unbound partners, were scored individually for each ensemble, and the ddG values were calculated from the average of the 35 ensembles. Rosetta Talaris all-atom energy function was used for scoring and calculating ddG values.(62) To improve the correlation with experimental values ddG values are also calculated by incorporating generalized additive model (GAM).(63)


## Results and Discussion

### Impact of pathogenic mutations on protein structure
  To find out the most deleterious mutations that may affect the protein’s structural stability, a total of 58 natural variants of p53 protein were sourced from the Universal Protein Resource (UniProt)(64) database. Firstly, SIFT (sorting intolerant from tolerant) predicts 52 SNPs (SIFT score < 0.05) to be deleterious among the 58 SNPs (See Table S1 in the supporting information). Among the 52 SNPs, 43 of them have a SIFT score of 0, indicating highly deleterious according to SIFT prediction. PolyPhen-2 (Polymorphism Phenotyping v2)(38) predicts if the mutations are damaging to the protein structure. PolyPhen scores less than 0.15 are considered benign SNPs, and scores ranging from 0.15 to 1 are predicted to be possibly damaging. However, SNPs with a PolyPhen score between 0.85 and 1 are considered damaging for the protein structure with high confidence. Among the 58 variants, PolyPhen2 predicts that 49 of them are damaging to the protein structure with high confidence (See Table S1 in supporting information). 23 of the SNPs have a SIFT score of 0 and a Polyphen2 score of 1 which suggest that these mutations are deleterious as well as damaging structural integrity of p53. These 23 mutations were further analyzed with PhD-SNP and Mutpred2.

  Due to the limitations of relying solely on a single in silico method such as SIFT for predicting mutation pathogenicity, PhD-SNP was utilized to validate the pathogenicity of the selected twenty-three SNPs. When both SIFT and PhD-SNP concur on pathogenicity, computational reliability is significantly enhanced. Unlike SIFT, PhD-SNP employs Support Vector Machines (SVMs) trained on diverse sequences and evolutionary data to forecast SNP pathogenicity, offering broad applicability in predicting variants responsible for human diseases.(49) Like SIFT, PhD-SNP also predicts these 23 mutations to be deleterious and disease causing (Table 1). Similarly, MutPred2 was employed because relying solely on one in silico method like PolyPhen-2 is insufficient to determine if the 23 pathogenic mutations will affect the structural integrity of p53.(52) MutPred2, a machine learning software tool, integrates genetic and molecular data to predict the pathogenicity of amino acid substitutions within a protein sequence. MutPred2 provides two outputs: the general score (g score) indicating the likelihood that the nsSNP is disease-related, and the property score (p score) indicating whether specific structural or functional properties are disrupted by the mutation. The prediction hypotheses are considered actionable (g > 0.5 and p < 0.05), confident (g > 0.75 and p < 0.05) and very confident (g > 0.75 and P < 0.01) based on the g and p scores. Analysis of the g and p scores from Mutpred2 reveals that the C141Y, C238S, and L265P mutations can be reliably categorized as detrimental to the structural integrity of p53, potentially resulting in disease (Table 1). Mutpred2 predicts that the C141Y mutation potentially induces the gain of a ß strand, the C238S mutation results in the loss of disulfide coordination with a Zn ion and the L265P mutation may lead to a gain of intrinsic disorder due to the substitution of leucine with proline. The analysis of these three mutations is extended through MD simulation to validate the predictions and explore their effects on protein-DNA binding post-mutation.


### Impact of C141Y, C238S, and L265P mutations on protein dynamics.

#### Largest deviations observed in C141Y and L265P.
  The 450ns molecular dynamics simulations (MD) of wild-type and C141Y, C238S, and L265P mutant p53 protein structures revealed that the root mean square deviation (RMSD) values for C141Y and L265P mutants diverged more from those of the wild-type proteins. The average RMSD of the wild-type protein remained stable at 5.17 Å throughout the simulation, whereas C141Y and L265P mutants showed notably higher average RMSD values of 7.29Å and 8.94Å, respectively, indicating significant deviations from their original configurations. In contrast, C238S exhibited lower fluctuations with an average RMSD of 5.655Å (Figure 3A). The RMSD of the L265P mutant protein experiences a notable increase, reaching its peak fluctuation (<12.0 Å) between 300 and 350ns into the simulation. For the C141Y mutant, there is an initial intense fluctuation during the early stages of the MD simulation (0–85 ns), followed by a stabilization period with minor variations between 90 and 300 ns, culminating in its highest RMSD value of 11.22 Å. In contrast, the C238S mutant maintains relatively stable conformational characteristics for most of the simulation time (RMSD 5.655 Å), indicating fewer conformational changes compared to the wild type (RMSD 6.17 Å). The elevated RMSD observed for these p53 variants is consistent with findings from MutPred2 and PolyPhen-2, which also identified these mutations as significantly impacting the structural integrity of p53 in comparison to the other 55 mutations analyzed in this study.

  The root-mean-square-fluctuation (RMSF) of the Cα atoms for each residue was computed to assess local fluctuations over time relative to the wild type (see Figure 3B). Compared to the wild type, mutants C141Y and L265P exhibited higher residual fluctuations. Conversely, both the wild-type p53 and C238S mutant demonstrated minimal fluctuations. Notably, residues 96 to 102, 166 to 192, 210 to 216, and 243 to 255 of the p53 protein displayed increased fluctuation, predominantly located within loop regions (Figure 3B).

  The Radius of Gyration (Rg) is a crucial measure in evaluating the spatial arrangement of atoms within a protein, reflecting its degree of folding and overall structural stability. Throughout the MD simulation, the wild-type p53 consistently displays low Rg values, suggesting a tightly folded and compact structure (Figure 3C). Similarly, the C238S mutant exhibits minimal deviation from the wild type. In contrast, both the C141Y and L265P mutants exhibit Rg values higher than those of the wild type, indicating that these mutant structures are less compact and more flexible. Although the mutant structures remain relatively stable up to 150ns, significant conformational changes occur thereafter. Particularly noteworthy is that the C141Y and L265P mutants display the highest Rg values of 19.56 Å and 19.95 Å, respectively, compared to their initial Rg values of 16.5 Å at the beginning of the MD simulation, indicating pronounced instability (Figure 3C).


#### Principal component analysis (PCA) reveals largest correlated modes of movement for C141Y and L265P.
  We further incorporated PCA to find the correlated modes of movements of the proteins during simulations along their first two eigenvectors. The projections of the crucial subspace of PC1 against PC2 revealed that the wildtype (Figure 4) exhibited a distinct compact mobility characteristic than the mutant systems. This study revealed that the C141Y and L265P mutant proteins occupied more space along the first two major eigenvectors than the wildtype protein. In this case, L265P demonstrated a larger range of cluster distribution and mobility in comparison to the other systems. The C141Y mutant has a moderate value along both the first and second eigenvectors (Figure 4) suggesting the alteration of the structural conformation of the protein. On the other hand, the C238S mutation in the protein showed a cluster distribution comparable with the wildtype protein structure indicating this mutation does not affect the structural conformation as much as the C141Y or the L265P mutation.


#### Regions Exhibiting Extensive Changes in Protein Structure.
  Certain regions of the protein may undergo more extensive changes compared to others. If these regions are located within the DNA-binding interfaces, they may affect p53’s ability to bind to DNA, thereby hampering the expression of essential regulatory proteins involved in cell growth arrest, DNA repair, and apoptosis. This disruption can lead to the overactivation of TERT and c-MYC proteins, resulting in abnormal cell growth. Twenty frames were extracted at equal intervals from the beginning to the end of the entire MD simulation (Figure 5).

  These frames were evenly distributed across the simulation time, with red-colored structures representing the initial phase, white-colored structures from the middle stages, and blue-colored structures from the final segment of the simulation. All 20 structures were superimposed for each system to visualize the extent of structural fluctuations in both the wild-type and mutant proteins. Overall, the DNA binding regions (highlighted in orange boxes) and the non-binding regions of the wild-type protein exhibited lower fluctuation over time (Figure 5A). In contrast, both regions were found to be flexible in the mutant protein structures. Among the three mutant structures, C238S (Figure 5C) demonstrated greater stability, whereas the C141Y (Figure 5B) and L265P (Figure 5D) mutant systems displayed higher flexibility during the MD simulation compared to the wild-type system. This suggests C141Y and L265P may have reduced binding affinity with the DNA. Importantly, these observations align with the results obtained from RMSD, RMSF, Rg (Figure 3), and PCA (Figure 4) analyses. Note that these calculations were performed using simulations that included only the protein, without incorporating the DNA.


#### Examining mutational impact through intramolecular hydrogen bonding with other residues.
  To delve deeper into the impact of C141Y, C238S, and L265P mutations on the stability of the tumor suppressor protein p53, intramolecular hydrogen bonds (excluding those involving successive residues) were analyzed between CYS141, CYS238, LEU265, and all residues within the wild-type protein, as well as between the mutant residues TYR141, SER238, PRO265, and all residues within the mutant structures. These calculations provided insights into how these mutations influence the hydrogen bonding network in p53 (Figure 6 and supporting information Table S2). In the wild-type protein, CYS141 predominantly forms two hydrogen bonds with TYR234: TYR234O/CYS141HN and CYS141O/TYR234HN, with frequencies of 97.8% and 78.9%, respectively, throughout the simulation. In the C141Y variant, these hydrogen bond frequencies decrease to 97.5% and 69.3% for TYR234O/TYR141HN and TYR141O/TYR234HN, respectively. Additionally, new hydrogen bonds are formed: GLN136O/TYR141HH, LEU137O/TYR141HH and ALA138o/TYR141HH, with frequencies of 8.7%, 7.5%, and 6.7, respectively. Prior to the mutation, CYS238 exhibited a single hydrogen bond with a frequency of 23.29% between TYR236O and CYS238HN. After the mutation, the frequency of this hydrogen bond decrease to 2.6% but new hydrogen bonds are formed: SER238O/CYS176HN ASN239O/SER238HG1, with frequencies of 62.7%, and 9.7%, respectively. In the wild-type protein, the LEU265 residue forms one hydrogen bond, LEU265HN/LEU257O, with a frequency of 94.74%. However, the L265P mutant protein demonstrate a very weak hydrogen bond, PRO265O/SER106HN, with frequency of 1%. Native contact analysis also reveals important residues that surrounds that the wildtype and mutant residues (See supporting information Table S3).

  The total number of hydrogen bonds formed between the native residues in positions 141, 238, and 265 with the remaining residues of the protein and between the mutated residues with all the other residues can further explain the effects of the mutations in protein structure. The total number of hydrogen bonds with neighboring residues was determined from the MD simulation, and these values were used to analyze the distribution of the relative frequency of intramolecular hydrogen bonds between the wildtype/mutant residues and all other protein residues.

  In the native p53, a higher frequency of hydrogen bonds is observed between wildtype Leu265 and the remaining residues compared to the mutant Pro265 and the residues within the L265P mutant structure (Figure 7). However, the total number of hydrogen bonds remains similar for Cys141 and Cys238 when compared to the wildtype (see Figure S2 in supporting information). The decrease in hydrogen bonds in L265P indicates a decrease in protein stability when this mutation occurs in p53. This aligns with the findings of the RMSD, RMSF, Rg, and PCA analyses, which showed that L265P exhibited greater flexibility compared to the wildtype protein.


#### Examining mutational impact through intermolecular hydrogen bonding with solvent.
  To explore the impact of solvent on the structure and dynamics of wild type and mutant proteins (C141Y, C238S, and L265P), an analysis of intermolecular hydrogen bonds was conducted between residues 141, 238, and 265 with the water molecules (see supporting information Table 4S). The findings reveal that C141 in the wildtype doesn’t form any hydrogen bonds with water as this residue is not exposed to water and mostly buried inside the protein. However, Y141 in the C141Y variant exhibits a greater number of hydrogen bonds with the surrounding water molecules as the larger tyrosine exposed the protein to interact with water. Similarly, the analysis shows that the CYS 238 amino acid in the wild-type protein interacts with only a few hydrogen bonds with the surrounding water molecules, whereas the SER 238 amino acid in the C238S mutant protein forms more hydrogen bonds with the solvent. Additionally, the bond lengths observed in S238 of the mutant are shorter compared to those in C238 of the native protein. Additionally, the analysis revealed a minimal number of intermolecular hydrogen bonds between the Leu-265 residue and water molecules, as well as between the mutant Pro-265 and the solvent, likely due to the nonpolar nature of both amino acids.


### Binding free energy confirms reduced binding of p53 mutations with DNA
  The p53 functions as a sequence-specific DNA-binding protein which targets specific regions in DNA strand to activate regulatory genes that control cell proliferation (Figure 8A). When p53 fails to activate these regulatory components, the body loses control over the cell cycle, potentially leading to tumorigenesis. Single-point mutations in p53 can diminish its ability to bind to DNA. Therefore, calculating the binding free energy between p53 and DNA in wildtype and mutant forms can offer valuable insights into the stability and function of p53.


#### MM/GBSA binding free energies confirm reduced binding of mutant proteins to DNA.
  MM/GBSA binding energies of C141Y, C238S, and L265P mutant p53 protein-DNA complexes were compared with those of the wild-type protein-DNA complexes (Table 2 and Figure 8B). These binding free energies were computed following three independent MD simulations of each wild-type and mutant systems, with the results subsequently averaged (for all individual free energy calculations, see supporting information Table S5).

  The calculated binding free energy between DNA and the wildtype p53 protein was −59.2 kcal/mol, whereas −41.8 kcal/mol, −47.20 kcal/mol and −36.91 kcal/mol for the C141Y, C238S, and L265P mutant proteins, respectively. The reason for the reduction in binding free energy could be explained by the contribution of the Van der Waals interaction energy terms. Van der Waals interaction energy contribution to the total binding affinity is higher (−78.2 kcal/mol) in wildtype compared to the mutant systems, where the values were −66.9 kcal/mol, −65.3 kcal/mol, and −57.4 kcal/mol for C141Y, C238S, and L265P, respectively. The effect of total electrostatic and polar solvation energy contribution to the total binding affinity is similar in wildtype (−3109.1 + 3139.4 = 30.4 kcal/mol), as well as for the C141Y (−3018.8 + 3053.5 = 34.7 kcal/mol), C238S (−2890.7 + 2918.1 = 27.3 kcal/mol), and L265P (−2792.7 + 2821.3 = 28.6 kcal/mol). The non-polar contribution to the total energy is again found higher in wild type (−11.4 kcal/mol) compared to the C141Y (−9.6 kcal/mol), C238S (−9.2 kcal/mol), and L265P (−8.1 kcal/mol). Of the three mutations, L265P exhibits the lowest binding free energy compared to the other systems (Table 2), aligning with our earlier structural analyses that indicated significant structural disruption caused by this mutation.


#### Pairwise decomposition analysis reveals interactions critical for binding with DNA.
  A pairwise decomposition analysis was conducted using the MM/GBSA method to determine the interaction energy between all residues of the protein and the bases of the DNA fragment (supporting information, Table S6). The analysis unveiled that the LYS120-ADE1104, LYS120-THY1105, SER121-THY1106, and Arg248-THY1011 pairs are the primary contributors to the DNA binding affinity of the p53 protein (Figure 8C). These residues play a crucial role in stabilizing DNA binding through hydrogen bonds. Particularly, the LYS120-ADE1104 and LYS120-THY1105 pairs contributed over 20 kcal/mol of energy, while the SER121-THY1106 and ARG248-THY1011 pairs contributed approximately 5 kcal/mol and 15 kcal/mol, respectively, to the binding of wild-type p53 with DNA. All three mutations (C141Y, C238S, and L265P) substantially reduced the interactions of the LYS120-ADE1104, LYS120-THY1105, and SER121-THY1106 pairs by at least 9, 4, and 2 kcal/mol, respectively. Conversely, their interaction with the ARG248-THY1011 pair increased by about 8 kcal/mol. The mutated proteins exhibit a loss in their binding interactions with the ADE1104, THY1105, and THY1106 bases of DNA. This phenomenon may elucidate the mechanism by which the p53 loses its functional activity as a transcription factor for genes.


#### Comparison of Equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) with experiment.
  Ang et al.(65) reported the equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) between DNA and the DNA binding domain of wildtype p53, as well as its variants G245S, R249S, and R273H (Table 3).

  It’s worth noting that the KD values were determined after introducing mutations (M133L/V203A/N239Y/N268D) for the sake of thermal stability. While the KD value for the wild-type p53 (T-p53, where T represents thermally stable) was found to be 10 μM, all thermally stable variants—G245S, R249S, and R273H—exhibited higher KD values of 35 μM, 56 μM, and 51 μM, respectively, indicating a reduction in binding affinity by about 3.5 to 5 times.(65) It’s also important to acknowledge that the reported values are for the monomeric DNB p53 domain, whereas p53 binds as a tetramer with a single DNA double helix (Figure 2A), implying that the actual binding affinity would be reduced by about 150 (3.54) to 675 (5.14) times.(65) The corresponding binding free energies for the G245S (−5.77 kcal/mol), R249S (−5.51 kcal/mol), and R273H (−5.56 kcal/mol) variants were also lower compared to those of DNB-T-p53 (−6.48 kcal/mol) and DNB-p53 (−6.29 kcal/mol) (Figure 8D).

  While MM/GBSA computes relative binding free energies for similar proteins, these values are adjusted to match experimental results using equation  (4) χNV=χEW×χMVχMW+X  where χMW and χMV represent the MM/GBSA binding free energies of DNA with wildtype p53 and p53 variant, respectively, while χEW and χNV represent the experimental binding free energies of DNA with wildtype p53 and the corrected binding free energy of DNA with the variant. X denotes an arbitrary correction factor, set to 1.22 in this study. The corrected binding free energies for C141Y, C238S, and L265P are −5.95 kcal/mol, −6.16 kcal/mol, and −5.01 kcal/mol, respectively. The corresponding calculated KD values for C141Y, C238S, and L265P are 25.4 μM, 17.5 μM and 135.2 μM, respectively, and they indicate a reduction in binding affinity by about 1.8 to 9.7 times when compared to wildtype p53 binding with DNA (14 μM). Therefore, all p53 mutations with structural alterations demonstrate a decreased binding affinity with DNA. Particularly, the L265P mutation exhibits a more pronounced reduction in binding affinity (9.7 times), along with increased changes in RMSD, RMSF, Rg, PCA, etc. Consequently, the L265P mutation could pose a significant risk to humans.


#### Rosetta Flex-ddG indicates reduced binding capacity in mutations compared to wildtype.
  An additional computational approach, Rosetta Flex-ddG,(45) has been employed to delve deeper into the effects of mutation. Tailored for predicting alterations in protein-protein binding affinities post-mutation, this method proves to be an asset in this study. A total of 35,000 Monte Carlo steps were executed, generating an ensemble of 35 structures for each mutant system. To assess convergence, four checkpoints were employed with 10, 20, 30, and the final 35 structures. The convergence analysis confirmed stability with the full ensemble of 35 structures, as depicted in Figure S1. The scores were computed using the Rosetta talaris energy function (Talaris 2014).(62) To assess DNA binding capability following mutation, the Rosetta Flex-ddG energy values were compared between the ensemble of wild-type protein structures (wt_dG) and that of mutant protein structures (mut_dG). Higher negative values in wt_dG compared to mut_dG suggest reduced DNA binding after mutation. Additionally, a generalized additive model (GAM) within Flex-ddG calculates energy differences, ddG_Gam, enhancing its predictive capabilities.(63) A positive ddG_Gam value signifies less favorable DNA binding. Rosetta Flex-ddg scores for wt_dG and mut_dG as well as the ddG_Gam obtained from all 35 ensemble structures are provided in Table 4 and are graphically represented in Figure 8E (Detail convergence of the Flex-ddg data is also provided in supporting information, Table S7).

  In all three mutations—C141Y, C238S, and L265P—positive ddG_Gam values were observed, indicating a reduced capacity for binding compared to the wild type. Once again, the L265P mutation exhibited the highest ddG_Gam value (1.64 REU). This suggests that despite the 265 positions being distal from the DNA binding site, its mutation affects ensemble structures and sidechains, resulting in diminished binding.


## Conclusion
  Computational analyses can unveil structural features and elucidate the underlying causes of protein structure destabilization due to mutations. In this investigation, our focus was on the pivotal human tumor suppressor protein, p53, known for its role in tumor suppression as well as progression. Considering that p53 is the most important tumor suppressor protein, it is important to proactively characterize disease causing p53 mutations before they become prevalent in the population. To address this concern, we employed four distinct computational prediction tools, SIFT, Polyphen-2, PhD-SNP, and MutPred2. These analyses identified that among 58 natural variants of p53, the mutations C141Y, C238S, and L265P are likely to be the most damaging to the structural integrity of p53. The variants identified through these in silico methods were then investigated using molecular dynamic simulations to grasp the repercussions of these nsSNPs on the protein’s structure and its binding with DNA. Analyses including RMSD, RMSF, Rg, and PCA underscored that the mutations can lead to a reduction in protein stability. Moreover, both intramolecular and intermolecular hydrogen bonds were observed to be fewer in the mutants compared to the wild-type protein. Furthermore, MM/GBSA binding free energy calculations for wild-type and mutant p53 proteins with DNA unveiled a decrease in binding affinity for all three mutant proteins in comparison to the wild-type counterpart. Our findings are in alignment with the experimentally determined values of three other cancer-causing mutations of p53, further bolstering the robustness of our observations. Additionally, Flex-ddG scores corroborated these findings, with mutant scores being lower compared to the wild-type, indicating that the mutations and the ensemble of structures are less likely to bind with DNA. Moreover, pairwise decomposition analysis revealed critical residues in p53 and bases in DNA crucial for their binding, shedding light on how mutations impact their interaction energies. Specifically, C141Y, C238S, and L265P notably reduced the interactions of LYS120-ADE1104, LYS120-THY1105, and SER121-THY1106 pairs, pivotal for the robust binding of the p53-DNA complex. Our results showed a tenfold decrease in DNA binding affinity for L265P, highlighting a significant risk for patients carrying this mutation. This study carries profound implications for surveillance endeavors andoffers valuable insights for the strategic development of future inhibitors targeting p53 variants with increased susceptibility to drug resistance.
