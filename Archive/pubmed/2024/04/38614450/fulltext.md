# Illuminating ligand-induced dynamics in nuclear receptors through MD simulations


## Abstract
  Nuclear receptors (NRs) regulate gene expression in critical physiological processes, with their functionality finely tuned by ligand-induced conformational changes. While NRs may sometimes undergo significant conformational motions in response to ligand-binding, these effects are more commonly subtle and challenging to study by traditional structural or biophysical methods. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations are a powerful tool to bridge the gap between static protein-ligand structures and dynamical changes that govern NR function. Here, we summarize a handful of recent studies that apply MD simulations to study NRs. We present diverse methodologies for analyzing simulation data with a detailed examination of the information each method can yield. By delving into the strengths, limitations and unique contributions of these tools, this review provides guidance for extracting meaningful data from MD simulations to advance the goal of understanding the intricate mechanisms by which ligands orchestrate a range of functional outcomes in NRs.


## Introduction
  NRs are a family of transcription factors that regulate genes controlling crucial physiological processes, such as development, metabolism, reproduction, and inflammation [1,2]. Classified as ligand-regulated transcription factors, a wide range of endogenous, exogenous and synthetic molecules can control the expression of these genes upon binding to the ligand-binding domain (LBD) [3]. Depending on their shape and structure, ligands can drive diverse functional outcomes in NRs by inducing conformational changes [4]. This ability to be functionally toggled by small molecules confers high value on NRs as drug development targets. It motivates intense study to decipher the relationship between ligand structure and transcriptional output. A variety of structural and biophysical studies have generally agreed that NR dynamics mediate a critical linkage between ligand structure and transcriptional output [5-7]. We liken the relationship between a ligand and the functional outcome induced upon NR binding, to that of a paintbrush and the artwork it creates (Fig. 1). Without accounting for the dynamic involvement of the artist, we are left with an incomplete perspective of how the brush produces the picture. Likewise, to fully understand how ligands achieve functional modulation of NRs, we must consider the dynamic events accompanying ligand-binding, particularly conformational changes that propagate across the receptor, and initiate a series of molecular events.

  Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations have been described as a ‘computational microscope’ [8], due to their availability to provide an atomic-level view of proteins, revealing fluctuations not readily observable in static structures. Simulations can unveil the temporal evolution of protein-ligand complexes, illuminating the dynamic interplay between the two and identifying motions and interactions that influence binding affinity, stability, and ultimately determine function. Here, we review recent studies that have used MD simulations to gain insight into NR function by studying ligand-induced dynamics in the LBD (Table 1). The NR LBD is comprised of 11–12 α-helices that form a 3-layer sandwich that encloses a hydrophobic ligand binding pocket (LBP). The C-terminal helix, also known as helix 12 (H12) or activationfunction helix (AF-H) forms the activation function 2 (AF-2) surface, the site of coregulator recruitment (Fig. 2). We also discuss results showing how addition of a bound coregulator peptide fragment influences LBD dynamics. All coregulators discussed in this review refer only to peptide fragments.

  As the site of both ligand-binding and coregulator recruitment, the LBD plays a crucial role in converting ligand-binding signals to various cellular responses. We outline several common MD analysis techniques that give mechanistic insight into fluctuations, dynamics, protein-ligand interactions, structural perturbations, and allosteric communication. Each section of the review describes a different analysis technique, with schematic figures (not representative of real data) provided to illustrate the information emerging from the various analysis methods. The majority of studies use a minimum of 500 ns long simulations, with a few performing 3 or 4 replicate trajectories. Given that NR LBDs contain an average of 250 amino acids, these timescales are sufficient to monitor local ligand-induced conformational changes throughout the domain.


## RMSD and RMSF: assessing structural deviations and flexibility
  NRs share a conserved LBD structure, comprising 10–12 helices with beta sheets (Fig. 2), a shared feature found in type 1 receptors (steroid receptors) and some type 2 receptors [9,10]. Assessing the flexibility of these regions can report on the functional identity and characteristics of bound ligands. Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF) and Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD) calculations are useful means to discern how ligands influence conformational dynamics of NRs (Fig. 3).

  RMSD reports on the global deviation in structure compared to a reference state [11]. Changes in RMSD upon ligand-binding are evidence of a global structural change that usually requires finer analyses for interpretation (Fig. 3a). The structural deviations induced by agonists often correlate with their efficacy, as observed in studies of glucocorticoid receptor (GR). Shi et al. demonstrated a correlation between higher efficacy agonists, like dexamethasone and cortisol, and a slightly lower RMSD [12]. In a ternary farnesoid X receptor (FXR)-coregulator-ligand complex, the agonist (tropifexor) had a lower RMSD compared to both partial agonist-bound (DM-175) and unliganded states [13]. However, the opposite was observed in estrogen-related receptor gamma (ERRγ) where binding of inverse agonist 4-OHT produced a smaller change in RMSD compared to agonist GSK4716 [14]. Generally, ligands stabilize the receptor and produce smaller deviations compared to the unliganded form, reflected in lower RMSD and RMSF, a trend observed across FXR [15,16] and ERRγ [14].

  RMSF calculations reveal structural deviations on a per-residue basis, reporting on the flexibility of protein segments. These calculations are helpful for identifying functionally important regions of proteins, as ligands with diverging functional profiles may modulate the stability of distinct areas within a receptor (Fig. 3b). While loops are inherently more flexible than structured regions [17], fluctuations in helices often pinpoint ligand-induced dynamics. The LBD binding pocket is bordered by H3, H5, H6 and H10/11, regions that are particularly susceptible to differential perturbations induced by ligandbinding. Azhagiya Singam et al. highlighted distinctions in androgen receptor (AR) agonists, antagonists and selective modulators through fluctuations in H3 and H12 [18]. Similar trends are observed in FXR [19] and ERRγ [14], where specific ligands reduce fluctuations in certain helices or rigidify loop regions, emphasizing their distinct effects on receptor dynamics.

  RMSF calculations also provide a unique metric for comparing homologous receptors, such as used by Slominski et al. to compare liver X receptor alpha (LXRα) and beta (LXRβ) [17]. While these sister receptors have 78 % identity in the 31 identical amino acids bordering the binding pocket [20], variations in stability were observed between the isoforms when interacting with a family of Vitamin (D3) and lumisterol (L3) derivatives. Additionally, RMSF revealed the effects of coregulator binding on the receptors’ flexibility. Coactivators had stabilizing effects on FXR [19] and AR [21], primarily in H12 and associated regions. Coactivator-induced flexibility could serve as a discriminative factor between active and inactive ligands. Mays et al. indicated that upon binding of NR coactivator-2 (Tif2) to liver receptor homolog-1 (LRH-1) [22], increased fluctuations were evident in regions of H12, the loop preceding H12, and an alternate activation surface (AF-B) located on H6.


## Quantifying and dissecting receptor-ligand binding
  Binding free energy calculations are a valuable tool for revealing differences between active and inactive ligands that bind to NRs. Employing methods such as MM-PBSA/GBSA in MD simulations [23], these calculations permit a decomposition of energy values, identifying energetic components (e.g. van der Waals, electrostatics) that are most important for NR-ligand interactions.

  While a relationship often exists between agonist potency and binding affinity, several studies illustrate that inactive ligands can also exhibit tighter binding. As experimental measurements of binding for a large set of ligands can be tedious, computational methods provide a valuable alternative for estimating binding strength. Notably, energy calculations concerning L3 and D3 derivatives in LXRα and LXRβ showed remarkable agreement with experimental binding assays [17].

  In AR, agonists were predicted from simulations to display stronger binding energies (~14–16 kcal/mol) than antagonists and selective modulators (~8–12 kcal/mol) [18]. Decomposition revealed that despite similar van der Waals interactions contributions, electrostatics played a more substantial role in binding of agonists and selective modulators. Conversely, in ERRγ, hydrophobic interactions predominantly contributed to the enhanced binding affinity for an inverse agonist over an agonist [14]. In FXR, the binding of an imatinib analog (P16) at an allosteric site modulated the affinity for FXR agonist CDCA in the binding pocket. Chen et al. used the MM-GBSA method to show that when bound near H2, H5, and H6, P16 enhanced CDCA affinity by strengthening electrostatic interactions and polarization [15].

  Other studies explore the influence of coregulator peptides on NR-ligand binding. In FXR, Kumari et al. used free energy calculations to predict that FXR affinity for agonists is increased over inverse agonists in the presence of a nuclear receptor coactivator-2 (NCoA-2) coregulator peptide [13]. Their subsequent analysis with an agonist, OCA, reaffirmed FXR’s stronger association with the steroid receptor coactivator-1 (SRC-1) peptide in the presence of an agonist [19], supporting the flexible nature of the AF-2 surface and its ability to switch conformations for selective coregulator recruitment. Similar studies allowed Jin et al. to develop a mechanistic understanding of AR’s affinity for agonist DHT and antagonist HFT in complex with the steroid receptor coactivator-3 (SRC3) coregulator peptide [21]. They observed increased affinity for DHT but weaker interaction with HFT when SRC3 was bound. While electrostatics were similar, the increase in van der Waals energies revealed that the presence of SRC3 coregulator prompted additional interactions between DHT and hydrophobic pocket residues. Conversely, HFT binding is weakened by SRC3 due to a loss of non-polar interactions, confirming the essential roles of crucial hydrophobic residues in AR-ligand recognition.


## Hydrogen bonds: patterns and persistence
  Hydrogen bonds, crucial for protein stability and ligand-binding, play a pivotal role in diverse ligand activities. Investigating the presence and strength of hydrogen bonds formed between NRs and their ligands may illuminate mechanisms of ligand selectivity and transcriptional activity. MD simulations possess a unique advantage over experimental methods such as crystallography and NMR for reporting on the stability of hydrogen bonds. For instance, structural differences between ligands are expected to influence interactions within the LBP. Slominski et al. illustrated this with that D3 and L3 derivatives bound LXRα and LXRβ distinctly, observing differing hydrogen bonding occupancies with specific residues [17], which could indicate that D3 and L3-derived ligands activate LXRs by distinct mechanisms.

  Distinct hydrogen bonding patterns can provide a way to discern different ligand classes. As highlighted by Azhagiya Singam et al., potent AR agonists such as DHT, 17β-trenbolone, and methyltrienolone form hydrogen bonds with T877 at higher occupancies compared to antagonists [18]. Similarly, Sasidharan et al. noted that agonist GSK4716 forms more hydrogen bonds with ERRγ compared to the inverse agonist 4-OHT [14]. In LRH-1, stable hydrogen bonds were observed more frequently with active ligands than inactive ligands in the LBP [22].

  Coregulators can also impact NR-ligand hydrogen bond patterns. Jin et al. found consistent hydrogen bonding between AR and DHT regardless of the presence of the SRC3 coregulator [21]. However, Kumari et al. demonstrated that the coregulator altered hydrogen bond interactions of OCA with specific residues in the FXR LBP [19]. These findings underscore the diverse nature of hydrogen bonding across receptors and even between ligands for the same receptor, offering deeper insight into the functional behavior of NRs.


## Dynamic allosteric networks to describe communities and connectivity
  Community analysis provides dynamic groupings of protein residues, revealing amino acids whose motions are highly correlated [24] (Fig. 4a). These ‘communities’, defined based on intrinsic dynamics of the receptor, can identify informational hubs and illuminate structural communication within NRs. For instance, the presence or absence of specific communities can indicate impaired communication, suggesting functional differences. Shi et al. showed that GR ligands induced distinct communication patterns, partitioning the receptor into unique communities [12]. Weaker agonists induce fewer communities than more potent agonists such as dexamethasone. One of the missing communities was an information hub, resulting in impaired connectivity across distant regions of GR in the weaker agonist complexes.

  Community analysis can delineate differences in motions between active and inactive ligands, which can illuminate how ligands modulate NRs to achieve activation. Mays et al. demonstrated that active ligands of LRH-1 were significantly more likely to be in community with H7, unlike inactive ligands which led to connectivity with AF-B, unveiling distinct dynamical behavior based of LRH-1 ligands [22]. Kumari et al. highlighted distinctive community arrangements between an agonist and partial agonist of FXR [13].

  Furthermore, the dynamical organization can be significantly altered by binding to different coregulators. As demonstrated by La Sala et al., the LBP of GR divides into two communities in the presence of Tif2 coregulator peptide [25]. However, upon binding to PRGC-1 coregulator, LBP is formed exclusively by one community. PRGC-1 appears to promote interconnection among the residues of the LBP, ultimately enhancing overall stability.

  Suboptimal path analysis [26] assesses the strength of communication between two distant sites in a protein complex, quantifying the correlated motions of residues along the paths linking two distant points (Fig. 4b). The complex is conceptualized as a network, where each Cα is a node, and the connection between each node is an edge. Nodes exhibiting higher correlation possess stronger edges, and the cumulative strength of edges for nodes along paths connecting two points signifies the level of communication. Within these paths, the strongest or “optimal” path and a subset of “suboptimal” paths are believed to convey the most information between two points.

  This analysis approach yields insight into ligand activity and mechanisms. Mays et al. demonstrated that LRH-1 complexes bound to inactive ligands exhibit increased communication paths between the ligand and AF-2, compared to active ligands [22]. This suggests that the inactive ligands promote stronger communication with the AF-2 surface. Examining the direction of suboptimal paths helped to distinguish mechanisms, revealing that inactive ligands predominantly traverse H10 as their primary route of communication, while active ligands induce broader connectivity with various regions of the receptor.

  Shi et al. performed suboptimal path analysis between two specific communities in GR, showing that strong agonists displayed shorter optimal pathways compared to weak agonists [12]. To study allosteric communication between H5 and H12 of GR, Bruno et al. identified the top 1000 suboptimal paths between Arg614 (H5) and Glu755 (H12) for several GR agonists [27]. Path lengths were either weakened or unperturbed relative to apo GR, correlating with structural features of ligands. Directions of paths also revealed conformational effects of ligand substitutions in GR.

  To produce an unbiased identification of sites within LRH-1 driving ligand-specific activity, Cato et al. utilized a unique approach of determining all suboptimal paths between the LBD and bound Tif2 coregulator peptide [28]. Some regions were connected to the coregulator peptide by significantly higher numbers of paths than other residues, identifying them as potential novel allosteric regions. By quantifying and comparing suboptimal paths between various LRH-1 complexes, these studies also illuminated how structural modifications in synthetic LRH-1 ligands altered communication across the LBD. The same method also allowed the group to compare homologous NRs steroidogenic factor 1 (SF-1) and LRH-1 [29] and determine why, in spite of their high (61 %) sequence conservation in the LBD, synthetic ligands display selectivity for the receptors.

  Additionally, Liu et al. employed suboptimal paths analysis to show that GR possesses an allosteric network mediated by the conserved Asn564 (human GR numbering) which is uniquely modulated by vamorolone to achieve both transactivation and transrepression [30]. Longer path lengths as well as reduced utilization of Asn564 in suboptimal paths supported vamorolone-induced weakening of allosteric communication between LBP and AFS.


## The ‘most important’ motions
  Principal component analysis (PCA) of an MD trajectory uncovers essential motions within a complex, potentially revealing distinct dynamic behavior of a receptor when bound to different ligands [31-33] (Fig. 5). Sasidharan et al. noted that the principal components (PCs) obtained from the apo-form of ERRγ indicated a wider range of motion and larger conformational change [14]. These changes were observed in loops which demonstrate larger flexibility in the apo receptor but become stabilized upon ligand binding. Because of the inherent flexibility of loop regions, their motions can often dominate in RMSF analyses [34]. PCA can separate loop motions, revealing subtle but essential dynamics in helices and other structured regions. Slominski et al. found distinct variation in the H12/AF-2 region of LXRs with different D3 and L3 derivatives [17].

  Between two and ten of the top PCs are usually analyzed to identify the most important motions in the protein. In FXR complexes, Kumari et al. report that the initial ten PCs captured anywhere from 43 % to 72 % of the total fluctuations in the complex [13,19] with the top two PCs usually capturing the significant variations between systems. These analyses unveiled allosteric changes induced by coregulator binding away from H12. Chen et al. analyzed the top three PCs in P16-bound FXR and showed that distant loops were allosterically stabilized [15].

  Moreover, PCA reveals regions of receptors with coupled motions or selective alterations based on ligand functional profiles. Mays et al. analyzed the combined fluctuations of the first four PCs of LRH-1 complexes, showing that ligand and coregulator binding differentially affects dynamics of critical LRH-1 regions, including AF-H and AF-B [22]. In AR, the inclusion of the SRC3 peptide in simulations induced different overall motions in agonist versus antagonist AR complexes [21].


## Collective motions that sense subtle changes
  Examining residues whose motions are correlated in a simulation may unveil functional coupling, allosterically-linked regions and intraprotein communication. Thus, changes in correlation can reveal subtle dynamic alterations even in the absence of a large conformational change. Covariances calculated between residue pairs reveal whether motions are correlated, anti-correlated, or not correlated [35] (Fig. 6). Correlation between H3 and H4–H5 was different in various GR-bound ligand complexes, suggesting different strengths of communication [12]. The observation of enhanced correlation between H3 and Loop 11–12 was an indication of the importance of both agonist and coactivator, as their combined presence achieves unique stabilization required for FXR function. In many cases, anticorrelated motions are also interpreted as an indicator of functional coupling between sites, e.g. in FXR-coregulator complexes [13].

  Communication between the LBP and AF-2/H12 is often analyzed using covariance analyses, e.g. in FXR [13] and AR [21], where correlation between LBP and AF-2 was altered in a ternary complex with ligand and coactivator, compared to coactivator alone. Jin et al. also showed that the addition of SRC3 peptide altered correlation patterns between LBP and AF-2/H12. Such alterations in correlation can be used to identify a rationale for experimental observations, e.g. differential NR-coregulator affinity in the presence of different ligands. Finally, in GR, weakened correlative movements between the LBP and coregulator binding site were interpreted as ligand-induced impairment in signal propagation between the two sites [25].


## Secondary, tertiary and beyond: diving into the nuances of protein conformation
  Changes in protein secondary structure can be used to predict how ligands might influence receptor structure, function, and stability. The DSSP (Defined Secondary Structure of the Protein) algorithm (Fig. 7a) was used by Slominski et al. to investigate structural changes in LXRα and LXRβ induced by D3 and L3 derivatives [17]. While no significant changes emerged, subtle variations in the α-helix occupancy were observed in H10-H12, induced by structural modifications on ligands. Further, Kumari et al. highlighted the capacity of secondary structure changes to discriminate between agonists and partial agonists [13]. Partial agonists induced conformational changes within L11/H12 which were not observed in the agonists. Coregulator binding reduced the extent of perturbations and thus stabilized several loops.

  Monitoring radius of gyration (Rg) may reveal how ligands influence compactness over the course of a simulation [36] (Fig. 7b). Kumari et al. showed only minor Rg changes when coregulators are added to FXR in apo, agonist or partial agonist bound states [13]. A similar observation was reported by Sasidharan et al., where agonist and inverse agonist complexes of ERRγ retained stable Rg values over the simulation time, compared to the apo-form which undergoes a small change [14]. Similarly, subtle changes in Rg were reported by Jose et al. for FXR bound to agonists alvimopan and montelukast compared to apo FXR [16]. Because these studies mostly report small Rg changes on the order of 0.2 Å or less, it remains uncertain whether the observed changes hold significance. However, it is possible that larger changes in Rg would only be expected if the domain undergoes unfolding. Additional work would be required to determine whether ligands can induce larger Rg changes in NR LBDs.

  Solvent accessible surface area (SASA) analysis is used in many cases as another metric for assessing overall conformational stability and global changes in the receptor induced by ligand and coregulator binding (Fig. 7c). Jose et al. reported comparable SASA values among montelukast-FXR and alvimopan-FXR complexes, as well as the apo-form [16]. However, Sasidharan et al. noted a smaller SASA in apo-form of ERRγ when compared to the ligand-bound forms, suggesting that the ligand binding induces subtle structural changes [14].

  Perhaps a more informative use of SASA is to compare NR binding pockets in various functional states. Kumari et al. showed that the SASA distribution plot of the binding site of agonist and partial agonist to FXR were similar [13]. In a second study, Kumari et al. revealed that the presence of coactivator enhanced pocket stability, resulting in a lower SASA value compared to OCA binding to FXR alone [19]. These differences illustrate how ligands or coregulators can change the environment and solvent accessibility of certain amino acids, explaining functional effects observed in experiments.


## Summary and future outlook
  Small alterations to the structure of an NR ligand can profoundly alter its effect on transcription, underscoring the critical need for investigative methods capable of discerning subtle dynamic differences induced by each ligand. Collectively, the studies reviewed here highlight the broad applicability of MD simulations for monitoring both large and nuanced conformational shifts in NR-ligand complexes. This review serves as a resource of various simulation methodologies, offering an overview of their applications in illuminating structural dynamics, ligand-binding mechanisms, and allosteric modulations essential to the functionality of NRs. Based on the studies reviewed here which focus on the NR LBDs, 500 ns is an optimal simulation length for observing ligand-induced changes. Of note, larger conformational shifts cannot be captured in 500 ns of classical simulations, which is our focus in this review. This is particularly relevant for H12 of the LBD, which is dynamic and adopts both docked and undocked conformations where it separates from the rest of the LBD [37,38]. These motions occur on the microsecond – millisecond timescales [39] and would be enabled by enhanced sampling MD methods, such as accelerated MD [40,41], metadynamics [42] and replica-exchange MD [43,44]. While outside the scope of this review, these methods have also been applied to study nuclear receptors [38,45-47]. Enhanced sampling approaches are also useful for increasing conformational sampling to gain a more thorough picture of protein conformational landscapes, as classical MD is sometimes unable to escape from an initial energy well [48].

  A challenge with MD analyses is determining whether differences observed between multiple simulation states are significant and reporting on biologically relevant changes. This can be particularly difficult when observed changes are subtle. In the absence of complementary experiments, one way to increase confidence in MD data is to perform replicate simulations. While only 6 of these 14 studies employ replicate trajectories, it has been recognized that conducting replicate simulations is crucial [49] and should be adopted as a standard practice in MD studies. With the progression of technology, the expansion of computational power, and the refinement of algorithms, MD simulations will permit more accurate and realistic simulations of biological processes. Thus, for the goals of understanding the dynamic behavior of NR-ligand interactions and gaining insight for rational drug design, the utility of MD simulations will only continue to grow.
