## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text (main text, methods, results, discussion) without supplementary figures/tables content
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific soundness, methodological validity, claims vs. evidence, clarity, and significance of the reported results
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors present SPREd, a neural network tool for gene regulatory network (GRN) reconstruction that directly predicts regulatory edges from expression data, trained on synthetic data generated by the SERGIO simulator. They claim superior or comparable performance to state-of-the-art methods (GENIE3, ENNET, PORTIA, TIGRESS) on synthetic benchmarks with high TF co-expression and on real yeast datasets.
- **Visible evidence base** Main text figures (Figs 1–4), Tables 1–2, and references to supplementary figures/tables (S1–S8, Tables S1–S6) whose content is not fully visible
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Supplementary figures and tables (S1–S8, Tables S1–S6) are referenced but not provided; full details of hyperparameter settings, training/validation splits, and statistical significance tests are not fully described in the main text

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** The manuscript presents a conceptually interesting shift in GRN reconstruction paradigm, moving from expression prediction to direct edge prediction, and leverages synthetic data to overcome the lack of gold-standard training sets. The idea is timely and the results on synthetic benchmarks are encouraging. However, several technical aspects require clarification and additional validation before the claims of superiority or competitiveness on real data can be fully established. The reliance on a single simulator for training and the limited real-data evaluation (one organism, three gold standards) raise questions about generalizability. The manuscript is clearly written and accessible, but the evidence base is incomplete without the supplementary material.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Computational biologists and bioinformaticians working on gene regulatory network inference, particularly those interested in supervised learning approaches and the use of simulation for training data. The work may also appeal to researchers developing machine learning methods for biological network reconstruction and those interested in benchmarking methodologies.
- **Major strengths** 1) The core idea of directly predicting edges rather than expression is a meaningful departure from dominant approaches and addresses the small-sample problem. 2) The use of a biophysics-inspired simulator (SERGIO) for generating training data is principled and potentially scalable. 3) The evaluation includes both synthetic benchmarks with controlled parameters and real yeast data, which is commendable. 4) The manuscript is well-structured and the writing is clear for a broad audience.
- **Major Concerns** See detailed items below.
- **Minor Comments** See detailed items below.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** R1-M1 (training/test overlap), R1-M2 (real-data evaluation scope), R1-M3 (baseline tuning and statistical rigor)
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** **Originality**: High. The direct edge-prediction paradigm with simulation-supervised training is a novel contribution. **Scientific importance**: Moderate to high. If validated, the approach could offer a new avenue for GRN inference, but the current evidence does not yet demonstrate a decisive advantage over existing methods in realistic settings. **Interdisciplinary readership**: Moderate. The topic is of interest to computational biologists and bioinformaticians, but the methodological focus may limit broader appeal. **Technical soundness**: Moderate. The approach is reasonable, but several technical details and validations are missing or unclear. **Readability for nonspecialists**: Good. The manuscript is generally accessible, with clear explanations of the problem and approach.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The core idea is promising and the results are suggestive, but the current evidence base is insufficient to fully establish the claims of superiority or broad competitiveness.

### Major Concerns

- **Concern ID** R1-M1
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** Yes
- **Axis** Technical soundness / Experimental design
- **Claim pointer** The authors claim SPREd outperforms state-of-the-art methods on synthetic benchmarks with high TF co-expression (Section 3.2, Fig. 2C–D, Table 1).
- **Evidence pointer** Section 3.2, Fig. 2, Table 1, Supplementary Tables S1–S3
- **Concern** The synthetic benchmark used for evaluation appears to be generated with the same simulation parameters as the training data (Section 3.2: "We trained SPREd on a data set of nGRN=250 GRNs generated with the same simulation parameters as the test set"). This raises a critical concern about potential train-test overlap or at least distributional similarity that may not reflect real-world conditions. While the authors later test on heterogeneous benchmarks (Section 3.3), the primary claim of superiority is based on this matched setting. The extent to which the test GRNs are independent from training GRNs (e.g., different random seeds, different underlying GRN structures) is not clearly stated.
- **Why it matters** If the test set is drawn from the same distribution as the training set, the comparison may be biased in favor of SPREd, which has seen this distribution during training, whereas the other methods have not. This would inflate the reported performance advantage and undermine the claim of general superiority.
- **Resolution test** The authors should clarify the exact relationship between training and test GRNs (e.g., whether they are disjoint in terms of GRN structure, parameter values, or random seeds). Ideally, they should report performance on a test set that is explicitly held out and generated with different parameters or from a different simulator to demonstrate genuine generalization.

- **Concern ID** R1-M2
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** Yes
- **Axis** Scientific importance / Generalizability
- **Claim pointer** The authors claim SPREd performs "significantly better than or comparably to existing methods" on real yeast data (Section 3.4, Table 2).
- **Evidence pointer** Section 3.4, Table 2, Supplementary Figs S6–S7, Supplementary Table S6
- **Concern** The real-data evaluation is limited to a single organism (yeast) and three gold-standard GRNs derived from the same underlying data sources. While this is a recognized benchmark, it is unclear how representative these results are for other organisms or data types (e.g., human, plant, or single-cell data). The authors acknowledge this limitation in the Discussion but do not provide any additional evidence of generalizability. Furthermore, the absolute performance values are low (AP in the 5% range), and the improvement over random baseline, while statistically significant, is modest.
- **Why it matters** The claim of broad utility for GRN reconstruction requires evidence across diverse biological contexts. A single-organism evaluation, especially with low absolute performance, is insufficient to establish that SPREd will be useful in practice for researchers studying other systems.
- **Resolution test** The authors should either (a) provide additional real-data evaluations on at least one other organism (e.g., E. coli, mouse, or human) or (b) temper their claims to be specific to yeast and clearly state the limitations of generalizability. Additionally, they should report performance metrics that are more interpretable for practitioners, such as precision at a fixed recall level or the number of known regulators recovered in the top-k predictions.

- **Concern ID** R1-M3
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** Yes
- **Axis** Technical soundness / Statistical rigor
- **Claim pointer** The authors claim SPREd outperforms ENNET, GENIE3, TIGRESS, and PORTIA on synthetic benchmarks (Section 3.2, Table 1).
- **Evidence pointer** Section 3.2, Table 1, Supplementary Tables S1–S3
- **Concern** The comparison with baseline methods may not be entirely fair. The authors do not report whether hyperparameters for the baseline methods were tuned on the same validation sets or whether default parameters were used. Additionally, the statistical significance of the performance differences is only reported in supplementary tables, and the main text does not provide confidence intervals or effect sizes. The choice of AP over AUPRC is justified, but the authors should also report AUPRC for completeness, as it is a more commonly used metric in the field.
- **Why it matters** Unfair comparisons or lack of statistical rigor can lead to overclaimed performance advantages. The community relies on reproducible and fair benchmarks to assess new methods, and any perceived bias in the evaluation will undermine confidence in the results.
- **Resolution test** The authors should describe the hyperparameter tuning procedure for all methods, including baselines, and report performance with confidence intervals or standard errors across multiple runs. They should also provide AUPRC values alongside AP and AUROC to facilitate comparison with other studies.

### Minor Comments

- **Concern ID** R1-m1
- **Severity** Minor
- **Axis** Clarity / Reproducibility
- **Affected element** Methods, Section 2.4 and 2.5
- **Evidence pointer** Section 2.4, 2.5, Supplementary Fig. S8
- **Issue** The description of the neural network architectures is brief, and the exact number of layers, kernel sizes, and activation functions for the convolutional layers are not fully specified in the main text. The reader is referred to supplementary figures, but the level of detail is insufficient for reproduction without the supplementary material.
- **Required correction** Provide a complete architectural description in the main text or a table, including layer dimensions, kernel sizes, padding, stride, and the number of parameters. This is essential for reproducibility.

- **Concern ID** R1-m2
- **Severity** Minor
- **Axis** Clarity / Presentation
- **Affected element** Results, Section 3.2
- **Evidence pointer** Section 3.2, Fig. 2
- **Issue** The head-to-head comparison in Fig. 2E is described as showing "a noticeable degree of complementarity between SPREd-SP and PORTIA," but the figure is not visible in the provided text. It is unclear what specific pattern in the figure supports this interpretation.
- **Required correction** Clarify the basis for the complementarity claim, either by describing the pattern in the text or by providing a quantitative measure of complementarity (e.g., the fraction of cases where one method succeeds and the other fails).

- **Concern ID** R1-m3
- **Severity** Minor
- **Axis** Technical soundness / Feature analysis
- **Affected element** Results, Section 3.2, Fig. 2F
- **Evidence pointer** Section 3.2, Fig. 2F, Supplementary Fig. S2
- **Issue** The ablation analysis shows that the precision feature is the most important, but the authors do not discuss why this might be the case or what biological interpretation can be drawn. This is a missed opportunity to provide insight into the model's behavior.
- **Required correction** Add a brief discussion of the biological or statistical rationale for the importance of the precision feature, and consider whether this finding suggests simplifications to the feature set.

- **Concern ID** R1-m4
- **Severity** Minor
- **Axis** Clarity / Scope
- **Affected element** Discussion, Section 4
- **Evidence pointer** Section 4
- **Issue** The Discussion contains some repetition, particularly regarding future directions (e.g., hyperparameter tuning and feature engineering are mentioned twice in slightly different forms). This could be streamlined.
- **Required correction** Consolidate the future directions into a single, focused paragraph to improve readability.

- **Concern ID** R1-m5
- **Severity** Minor
- **Axis** Reproducibility / Data availability
- **Affected element** Availability and implementation
- **Evidence pointer** Availability and implementation section
- **Issue** The availability statement provides a GitHub link but does not specify the license, the version of the code used for the reported results, or whether the synthetic data generation scripts are included. This limits the reproducibility of the work.
- **Required correction** Specify the software license, provide a versioned release or DOI, and confirm that all scripts for generating synthetic data and running the benchmarks are included in the repository.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that SPREd "outperforms" state-of-the-art methods on synthetic benchmarks is not fully supported without clarification of the train-test relationship (R1-M1).
- The claim of "significantly better than or comparable to" existing methods on real data is based on a single organism and may not generalize (R1-M2).
- The claim that the precision feature is uniquely important (Fig. 2F) is based on an ablation that is not fully described; the statistical significance of the drop is not reported.
- The claim of "complementarity" between SPREd-SP and PORTIA (Fig. 2E) is not substantiated in the visible text.
- The generalizability of the approach to other simulators or real datasets with different characteristics is asserted but not demonstrated.
