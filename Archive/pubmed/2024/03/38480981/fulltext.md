# Functional genomic screens with death-rate analyses reveal mechanisms of drug action


## Abstract
  A common approach for understanding how drugs induce their therapeutic effects is to identify the genetic determinants of drug sensitivity. Because “chemo-genetic profiles” are performed in a pooled format, inference of gene function is subject to several confounding influences related to variation in growth rates between clones. We developed Method for Evaluating Death Using a Simulation-assisted Approach (MEDUSA), which uses time-resolved measurements, along with model-driven constraints, to reveal the combination of growth and death rates that generated the observed drug response. MEDUSA is uniquely effective at identifying death-regulatory genes. We apply MEDUSA to characterize DNA damage-induced lethality in the presence and absence of p53. Loss of p53 switches the mechanism of DNA damage-induced death from apoptosis to a non-apoptotic death that requires high respiration. These findings demonstrate the utility of MEDUSA, both for determining the genetic dependencies of lethality, and for revealing opportunities to potentiate chemo-efficacy in a cancer-specific manner.


## INTRODUCTION
  Understanding mechanisms of drug action is important for identifying settings in which drugs will be effective, and for predicting potential mechanisms of drug resistance. For anti-cancer drugs, a key aspect is to understand mechanism(s) by which drugs promote cell death. A central issue is that more than a dozen forms of regulated cell death have been identified1-4. For many subtypes, the mechanisms of activation/regulation are not clear, compromising our ability to identify which pathways are activated and how to improve responses. The most well-studied form of death is apoptosis, and our understanding of this process has led to new drugs that activate apoptotic death5,6. Similarly, a detailed understanding of non-apoptotic death, and the factors that dictate choice between death subtypes, will likely reveal new therapeutic options.

  Functional genetics have become a powerful means to understand the function of all genes and/or gene regulatory elements7. When applied in the context of drug treatment (i.e., “chemo-genetic profiling”8), these methods identify genetic determinants of drug sensitivity, which, in theory, should include the mechanisms of drug-induced death. In practice, however, because these experiments are performed in a “pooled” format, inferring the death-regulatory function of each gene is confounded by growth rate variation between clones. In chemo-genetic profiling, gene function is inferred based on how the relative abundance of each clone changes in the context of treatment. Because clonal abundance is a function of both growth and death, from the perspective of cell death, growth rate variation represents a confounding influence.

  Analytical methods exist for resolving the confounding effects of growth rate variation in the context of in vitro drug sensitivity and in functional genetics9,10. However, a second issue that has not been deeply considered is variation in the coordination between growth and death. In other words, to what extent is a drug-treated population proliferating while cells die? The existing growth rate normalization methods are focused on neutralizing biases created by growth rate variations in the untreated population (i.e., differences in the expected population size in the absence of therapy); growth/death coordination, on the other hand, refers to biases created by growth rate variations in the treated population (i.e., the relative contributions of growth inhibition vs. death activation in creating the observed population size). We previously created an analytical method, called Growth Rate Adjusted DEath (GRADE), for characterizing drug-induced growth/death coordination11. Our prior studies reveal that growth/death coordination varies dramatically across drug classes. It remains unclear to what extent genetic perturbations would alter growth/death coordination, and this feature has not been addressed in the context of chemo-genetic profiling.

  To address these issues, we created a Method for Evaluating Death Using a Simulation-assisted Approach (MEDUSA). Rather than scoring clonal abundance, MEDUSA deconvolves these data with computational simulations that explicitly model clonal dynamics to reveal how genetic perturbations affect drug-induced growth and death rates. Using MEDUSA, we characterize the genetic determinants of DNA damage-induced death in the presence and absence of p53. Contrary to prior studies, we find that loss of p53 switches the dominant mechanism of cell death from apoptosis, to a non-apoptotic death that depends on high mitochondrial respiration. Our findings demonstrate the accuracy of MEDUSA for identifying death-regulatory genes, and our study reveals new strategies for sensitizing the lethality of DNA-damaging chemotherapies in p53-deficient cancer cells.


## RESULTS

### Varied growth/death coordination masks non-apoptotic death
  To explore how the interpretation of cell death is affected by variation in drug-induced growth/death coordination, we evaluated responses to DNA damage in the presence and absence of p53. Following DNA damage, p53 is required for activation of downstream cell fates, including G1/S cell cycle arrest and apoptotic death (Fig. 1a)12-15. Thus, we reasoned that loss of p53 should change how growth and death are coordinated following DNA damage. We began by profiling sensitivity to nine DNA-damaging chemotherapeutics. Each drug was tested in wildtype or p53-null cells (Supplementary Table 1). We used an assay that enables quantification of both live and dead cells, and how these populations change over time (FLICK)16,17. These data enable an evaluation of conventional response measurements that conflate growth inhibitory and death activating phenotypes, such as “relative viability” (RV; i.e., number of live cells, relative to untreated control). However, these data can also be used to calculate death-specific measurements, such as lethal fraction (LF; i.e., percentage of the population that is dead, following drug exposure), or its inverse, fractional viability (FV; Extended Data Fig. 1a). Consistent with prior studies, when using conventional metrics, we found that p53-null cells are less sensitive than cells with functional p53 to DNA damaging chemotherapies, such as the topoisomerase II inhibitor, Etoposide (Fig. 1b)18,19.

  The increased viability in p53-null cells may be driven by increased proliferation, decreased death, or a combination of these features; however, conventional response metrics do not distinguish between these possibilities. To more accurately score how p53 affects DNA damage sensitivity, we used the GRADE framework11. The GRADE approach derives from the comparison of two complementary measurements: the normalized growth rate inhibition value (GR9) and the drug-induced lethal fraction (LF20). Comprehensive simulations of all combinations of drug-induced growth/death rates reveal a finite area of possible GR/LF relationships; drug-induced changes to cell proliferation, death, and/or growth/death coordination can then be calculated from the position of the observed drug response within a GR/LF plot (Fig. 1c)11. In p53-wildtype cells, we observed a bi-phasic response, with low doses of DNA damage exclusively inhibiting proliferation, and higher doses activating cell death. Importantly, the lethal doses of DNA damage project along the left boundary of the theoretical GR/LF area (Fig. 1d and Extended Data Fig. 1b). The only possible way to get those particular pairs of LF and GR values is if the population has stopped growing nearly instantly, with the observed fraction of the cells subsequently dying. Thus, DNA damage-induced cell death in p53-proficient cells occurs exclusively in a population of growth-arrested cells. In contrast, lethal doses of DNA damage in p53-null cells project through the middle of the theoretical GR/LF area, revealing that these cells continue to proliferate following exposure to DNA damage, but simultaneously die at high rates, even at low doses (Fig. 1d and Extended Data Fig. 1b). Thus, as expected, loss of p53 changes growth/death coordination following DNA damage.

  To characterize how altered growth/death coordination affects our understanding of cell death, we next explored GRADE-inferred growth and death rates for p53-wildtype and null cells (Fig. 1d and Extended Data Fig. 2a). Surprisingly, death rates in p53-null cells were generally higher than in p53-wildtype (Fig. 1d). This was unexpected, given prior literature demonstrating that p53 is required for DNA damage-induced apoptosis14,18. Nonetheless, using p53-targeted siRNAs, we validated that p53 depletion does not substantially alter the level of DNA damage-induced lethality (Fig. 1e). Thus, the loss of DNA damage sensitivity observed in p53-null cells is not caused by decreased activation of cell death.

  We next determined if an increased DNA damage-induced growth rate was sufficient to explain the drug insensitivity of p53-null cells. DNA damage inhibits proliferation through multiple cell cycle checkpoints. Thus, we characterized the p53-specific contribution to growth arrest by knocking out p53 and evaluating DNA damage-induced growth in isogenic cells (Fig. 1f). We used U2OS, a p53-wildtype cell line. CRISPR-mediated knockout of TP53 did not substantially alter levels of DNA damage accumulation, or the duration of DNA damage signaling (Extended Data Fig. 2b-c). Furthermore, U2OS and U2OSp53KO cells grow at similar rates in the absence of drug, but only U2OSp53KO cells continue to proliferate in the presence of DNA damage, due to loss of DNA damage-induced G1/S arrest (Extended Data Fig. 2d-e). These data are consistent with GRADE-based observations in cells that are naturally p53-null. Using our experimentally measured rates, we simulated relative viability, finding that the DNA damage insensitivity observed for p53-null cells is driven entirely by increased proliferation (Extended Data Fig. 2f-i).

  Notably, altered growth/death coordination cannot completely explain why these phenotypes were not previously observed. Most prior studies complement relative viability with direct measurements of dead cells, typically monitoring hallmarks of apoptosis. Thus, we reasoned that DNA damage-induced death must switch from being predominantly apoptotic in p53-wildtype cells to being non-apoptotic in p53-null cells. For DNA damage, the preponderance of evidence suggests that p53 is not required for the activation of cell death, per se, but specifically required for apoptosis14. Because cell death has typically been evaluated using markers that are specific to apoptotic cells, non-apoptotic death can occur and go unnoticed. Using measurements that are death-specific, but agnostic to the mechanism of death, we found that Etoposide induced death at similar levels in U2OS and U2OSp53KO cells (Fig. 1g); however, clear differences were observed in the kinetics of cell death. In particular, the death onset time was significantly later in U2OSp53KO than in U2OS, but also death occurred at a faster overall rate once initiated (Fig. 1h). Similar differences were observed for p53-null cell lines, compared to those with wildtype p53, and these kinetic differences were also observed across a panel of mechanistically distinct DNA-damaging agents (Fig. 1d and Extended Data Fig. 3a). Faster population death rates are an indication of increased death synchrony, a common feature of inflammatory non-apoptotic death20-23.

  In U2OS, Etoposide-induced death was associated with the conventional hallmarks of apoptosis: sensitivity to the caspase inhibitor zVAD, activation of the apoptotic executioner, caspase-3, and acquisition of “membrane blebbing” morphology (Fig. 1i-k and Extended Data Fig. 3b). These hallmarks were absent in U2OSp53KO cells (Fig. 1i-k). Furthermore, death in U2OS was immunologically silent, whereas conditioned media taken from Etoposide-treated U2OSp53KO cells induced inflammatory responses (Extended Data Fig. 3c-e). Importantly, although p53 controls expression/activity of several pro-apoptotic genes24-26, these differences did not result from a lack of apoptotic capacity in U2OSp53KO cells: using BH3 profiling, we found that U2OS and U2OSp53KO have similar levels of apoptotic priming and both cell types have similar sensitivities to the BH3 mimetic compound ABT-199 (Extended Data Fig. 4a-c). Taken together, these data demonstrate that altered growth/death coordination, combined with a switching from apoptotic to non-apoptotic death, has obscured the death activated by DNA damage in p53-null cells.


### Chemo-genetic profiles mischaracterize death regulation
  Having found that DNA damage activates a non-apoptotic cell death in p53-null cells, we next aimed to resolve how this form of death is regulated. Chemo-genetic profiling is an established method for resolving drug mechanisms of action by systematically evaluating the genetic dependencies of the drug response. Chemo-genetic profiles generally score changes in the relative abundance of clones in the drug-treated versus untreated conditions7,8,27. This is precisely the same as relative viability, which we found is intrinsically insensitive to death regulation, particularly at the long drug exposure times that are typically profiled (Extended Data Fig. 2h-i). Thus, we reasoned that conventional analysis methods used in chemo-genetic profiling studies may also fail to identify death-regulatory genes. To explore this logic, we performed a chemo-genetic profiling study to identify the genetic determinants of Etoposide sensitivity (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Table 2). We used the GeCKOv2 sgRNA library to generate single gene knockouts, in Cas9-expressing U2OS or U2OSp53KO cells28. In prior studies, drugs have been screened at doses that confer a 20-50% reduction in population size over 2-4 weeks (e.g., “ED20” dose)29. These doses of Etoposide, however, fail to induce any cell death (Extended Data Fig. 5a-b). Thus, to optimize our screen for evaluation of cell death, we increased the dose to induce substantial lethality, and shortened the assay time, such that evolution of the drug-treated population would be driven by variations in the degree of death, rather than by proliferation. Treatment with 5 μM Etoposide for 4 days resulted in ~50% lethal fraction in both cell types, without causing a population “bottleneck,” which would deteriorate assay sensitivity (Fig. 2b-c and Supplementary Table 2). Preliminary analyses revealed a high correlation among replicates and that essential genes were dropping out over time, suggesting that data quality was not compromised by the atypical assay conditions that we used (Fig. 2d and Extended Data Fig. 5c-e).

  As a first pass, we used a conventional analysis to identify the genetic determinants of Etoposide sensitivity in U2OS cells (Extended Data Fig. 5c and Supplementary Table 3). This analysis identified more than 800 genes that were significantly enriched/depleted in Etoposide-treated versus untreated cells. Importantly, although Etoposide induced apoptosis in U2OS cells, our chemo-genetic profile failed to identify apoptotic regulatory genes, instead identifying genes that regulate proliferation (Fig. 2e). Similar trends were observed upon re-analysis of 74 published chemo-genetic profiles of apoptotic drugs (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Table 4). Further inspection also revealed clear evidence of confounded insights due to growth rate variation among knockout clones. For example, loss of DNA repair should phenocopy higher doses of Etoposide, and thus should increase drug sensitivity (negative L2RV). However, DNA repair genes generally scored with increased relative abundance, commonly interpreted as promoting drug resistance (Fig. 2g). A similar phenotype is observed for essential genes, which drop out over time due to low growth rates (Fig. 2g)30. Thus, conventional analysis methods used in chemo-genetic profiling fail to identify death-regulatory genes due to the confounding effects of variations in growth and/or growth/death coordination.


### Development of a method to infer drug-induced death rates
  We reasoned that accurately identifying death-regulatory genes using chemo-genetic profiling would require calculating the drug-induced death rate, rather than relative population size or growth fitness. Generally, calculating the death rate requires counting both live and dead cells11,20. While recovery of dead cells is possible in the context of apoptotic death, due to the relative stability of the apoptotic corpse31,32, recovering intact dead cells may not be possible in the context of non-apoptotic death due to cell rupture. Thus, we devised a new strategy for inferring the death rate from the data that is typically available in chemo-genetic profiling studies. We call this approach: Method for Evaluating Death Using a Simulation-assisted Approach (MEDUSA). The general principle underlying MEDUSA is that the drug-induced death rate can be inferred from a combination of 3 insights: 1) the relative abundance of each sgRNA in treated versus untreated groups (i.e., the conventional chemo-genetic profiling score), 2) the observed growth rate defect for each sgRNA in untreated vs. T0 comparison (i.e., gene essentiality score), and 3) simulations of population dynamics, parameterized using experimentally measured growth rates, death rates, and growth/death coordination (Fig. 3a).

  The doses of DNA damage that we screened project along the left boundary in a GRADE plot, suggesting that at the tested doses, death occurred in a growth-arrested population (Fig. 3b). This feature simplifies the inference of death rate, by limiting the possible ways in which growth and death could integrate to generate an observed population size. Using MEDUSA, we visualize the relationship between growth, death, and population size using a phase diagram which reports, in color, the relative clonal abundance at each combination of growth and death rates (Fig. 3c).

  To use MEDUSA for characterizing the death-regulatory function of each gene, we first identify the compendium of growth/death combinations that could have resulted in the observed relative abundance for each clone (Fig. 3d(i)). To infer the specific combination of growth and death, we used the experimentally observed gene fitness score to determine how each gene knockout affected the proliferation rate (Fig. 3d(ii)). These two features – observed relative abundance and observed growth rate defect – can then be used to infer the relative death rate for each knockout clone (Fig. 3d(iii)).

  Using MEDUSA, we computed gene-level growth and death rates for each gene knockout (Fig. 3e). Importantly, MEDUSA reveals that common analysis methods fail because most genes fall within a region of the phase diagram that is associated with a Type I or Type II error, or if the growth defect is strong enough, even a Type III error (inverted inference, Fig. 3f). For instance, the inverted inference for DNA repair genes is caused by the slow growth rate of these cells in the untreated condition, which obscures the high death rates of these cells when exposed to DNA damage (Fig. 3e and Extended Data Fig. 6a). We also identified 533 “false negatives” that could be rescued by MEDUSA (Supplementary Table 5). One notable example is TDP2, a DNA repair protein that repairs adducts created by Etoposide. Knocking out TDP2 increases Etoposide potency29,33. At the dose/time of our experiment, however, a conventional analysis scores TDP2 at ~0, erroneously suggesting that knocking out TDP2 has no effect on Etoposide sensitivity. MEDUSA reveals that the neutral score results instead from a combination of increased death in the drug-treated condition and decreased growth in the untreated condition. We validated these inferences using TDP2-targeting sgRNAs, finding as predicted by MEDUSA, that knockout of TDP2 resulted in a slower growth rate in the absence of exogenous DNA damage, and a faster death rate in the presence of Etoposide (Fig. 3g and Extended Data Fig. 6b). Notably, in addition to the 533 false negatives, MEDUSA identifies that 763 false positive hits, which appear to erroneously score in conventional analyses as regulating drug sensitivity due to slow growth in the untreated condition (Supplementary Table 5). Thus, for inferring gene function from chemo-genetic profiling data, MEDUSA improves sensitivity and accuracy of death-regulatory insights by resolving confounding effects caused by varied proliferation rates.


### MEDUSA accurately characterizes death-regulatory genes
  The MEDUSA method is widely applicable for evaluating any drug, but depends on an explicitly modeled growth/death coordination. In the context of the DNA-damaging drugs that we studied, the underlying model assumes that death occurs in a non-growing population. While this model is experimentally validated for wild-type cells, it may not always be true in the context of knockouts, which could alter growth/death coordination. Thus, we performed a large-scale validation of our death rate inferences for different classes of predictions (Fig. 4a and Extended Data Fig. 6b-c). We focused on the top 20 genes predicted to increase or decrease the death rate. For comparison, we also evaluated the top 20 genes predicted to cause drug sensitization/resistance, based on a conventional analysis (Supplementary Table 6). Overall, MEDUSA-inferred death rates were strongly predictive of the death-regulatory function of each gene (Fig. 4b).

  A conventional chemo-genetic profiling analysis was not significant for predicting death-regulatory function (Fig. 4c). The data, however, were clearly non-random (OR = 6.6) and may have been significant if a larger set of genes were tested. Of note, these two analysis methods only produce conflicting predictions for knockouts that cause reduced growth rates. Thus, to directly compare MEDUSA and conventional analyses, we focused on the subset of genes whose deletion caused a reduced growth rate. Regardless of the growth rate observed, MEDUSA continued to accurately predict the death-regulatory function of each gene (OR = 18; Fig. 4d and Extended Data Fig. 6d,e). Importantly, for gene knockouts with a reduced growth rate, conventional analyses produced predictions of death-regulatory function that were approximately twenty times worse than random predictions (OR = 0.06; Fig. 4d and Extended Data Fig. 6d,f).


### MEDUSA identifies a respiration-dependent form of death
  Given that MEDUSA accurately identifies death-regulatory genes, we next used MEDUSA to characterize the genetic dependencies of DNA damage-induced lethality in the p53-deficient context. MEDUSA identified 632 genes that modulate the Etoposide-induced death rate when deleted in U2OS, and 965 genes that modulate death in U2OSp53KO (798 of 965 genes were unique to U2OSp53KO; Extended Data Fig. 6g and Supplementary Table 5). Importantly, in U2OS, knockouts that alter the death rate were significantly enriched for known apoptotic regulatory genes, and these effects were not observed in U2OSp53KO cells (Fig. 5a). Furthermore, the distinction between p53-proficient and p53-deficient cells was not observed when we used a conventional analysis (Fig. 5a). Thus, a MEDUSA-based analysis further confirms our observation that apoptosis is not activated following DNA damage in p53-null cells (Fig. 1). Furthermore, when compared to conventional analysis methods used in chemo-genetic profiling, these data highlight the unique sensitivity of MEDUSA for characterizing death-regulatory function.

  MEDUSA also revealed a unique dependency in U2OSp53KO cells for genes involved in oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) (Fig. 5b). Deletion of OXPHOS regulatory genes rescued viability in Etoposide-treated U2OSp53KO; however, knocking out these genes did not significantly alter death in U2OS cells (Fig. 5b-c). To validate this, we focused on panel of OXPHOS genes that promote lethality in p53 KO but not WT U2OS cells. Consistent with MEDUSA-derived predictions, knocking out any of these genes reduced Etoposide-induced lethality in U2OSp53KO, but did not affect cell death in U2OS (Fig. 5d and Extended Data Fig. 7a-b).

  The OXPHOS genes that we validated encode components of Complex I and V of the electron transport chain (ETC). To further investigate differences in the use of OXPHOS in U2OS and U2OSp53KO, we measured the oxygen consumption rate, using the Seahorse Extracellular Flux assay. Drug exposure induced a modest decrease in the spare respiratory capacity of both cell types. However, we observed – independent of drug therapy – that U2OS and U2OSp53KO have strikingly different respiratory profiles, with U2OSp53KO cells having increased essentially all aspects of respiration (Fig. 5e and Extended Data Fig. 7c). Increased respiration did not result from increased mitochondrial abundance (Extended Data Fig. 7d-e). We considered whether the altered respiration was caused by dysregulated p53-MDM2 signaling, as MDM2 is known to regulate Complex I34,35; however, MDM2 knockout did not alter drug sensitivity in either cell type (Extended Data Fig. 7f-g). The increased respiration observed in p53KO cells may have been caused by increased ETC “supercomplex” formation, which can increase respiratory efficiency (Extended Data Fig. 7h-i)36. To directly evaluate if high respiration contributes to Etoposide-induced lethality in U2OSp53KO cells, we measured Etoposide sensitivity in the presence or absence of Rotenone or Oligomycin, which inhibit ETC Complexes I and V, respectively. For both inhibitors, we observed no effect in U2OS; however, both Rotenone and Oligomycin inhibited death in U2OSp53KO cells (Fig. 5f-g and Extended Data Fig. 7j).

  Critically, if our chemo-genetic profiles were analyzed using conventional methods, OXPHOS would score as promoting “resistance” in both p53-proficient and -deficient settings. However, MEDUSA clarifies the cause of this specious drug resistance phenotype: while OXPHOS genes have similar effects on growth, loss of OXPHOS genes leads to different effects on death in the presence and absence of p53 (Fig. 5c). Thus, critical differences in mechanisms of lethality in the presence and absence of p53 would have been missed using conventional approaches.


### Cell death in p53-deficient cells depends on high NAD+
  Because OXPHOS activity drives the activation of different forms of death in p53-proficient/deficient cells, we used metabolite profiling to further resolve why DNA damage activates non-apoptotic death in p53-deficient cells. We performed mass spectrometry-based metabolite profiling, both at steady-state and following Etoposide exposure. We quantified the abundance of 463 metabolites (Supplementary Table 7). The steady-state metabolite profiles were very similar for these isogenic cells, with significant differences observed for only 12 metabolites (Extended Data Fig. 8a). More pronounced differences were observed following Etoposide exposure, with 118 metabolites significantly enriched or depleted in U2OS and/or U2OSp53KO cells (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Table 7). The metabolites altered following Etoposide exposure were enriched within 10 metabolic pathways; however, only 3 closely related pathways were uniquely altered in the U2OSp53KO background: Glycolysis, Pentose Phosphate Pathway (PPP), and TCA Cycle (Fig. 6b). Metabolites within these 3 pathways were depleted in U2OSp53KO cells following drug exposure; similar changes were not observed in U2OS (Fig. 6c and Extended Data Fig. 8b).

  Decreased glycolytic, PPP, and/or TCA metabolites following drug exposure could occur due to drug-induced changes in the use of these pathways. Alternatively, these decreases may result from “survivor bias”, caused by preferential death of cells with high levels of particular metabolites. To distinguish between these possibilities, we used isotope tracing with labeled glucose (Extended Data Fig. 9a and Supplementary Table 8). The degree to which an intermediate metabolite is derived from the activity of a given pathway can be represented as the ratio of the labelled downstream metabolite relative to its labelled upstream precursor (e.g., fumerate+2/citrate+2)37. Based on this analysis, Etoposide did not induce changes in the use of glycolysis (Extended Data Fig. 9b). Etoposide altered the use of PPP and TCA; however, the observed changes were nearly identical in U2OS and U2OSp53KO (Extended Data Fig. 9b). Thus, the Etoposide-induced depletion of metabolites within glycolysis, PPP, and TCA does not appear to result from differences in the use of these pathways in the presence/absence of p53.

  Given these results, we next tested whether high levels of TCA, PPP, and/or glycolytic metabolites sensitize p53-deficient cells to DNA damage. Although very few metabolites differed in untreated conditions between U2OS and U2OSp53KO, we observed a striking difference for NAD+ (Extended Data Fig. 8b). NAD+ is a cofactor used in multiple metabolic reactions within TCA, PPP, and glycolysis (Fig. 6c). Prior to Etoposide exposure, NAD+ is higher in U2OSp53KO compared to U2OS (Fig. 6d). However, at long time points following Etoposide exposure (i.e., longer than the death onset time), NAD+ is significantly decreased in U2OSp53KO, but remains unchanged in U2OS cells (Fig. 6d). This pattern is consistent with survivor bias, in which high levels of NAD+ sensitize cells to DNA damage, only in the p53-deficient context. To directly test this model, we exposed cells to the NAMPT inhibitor, FK866. NAMPT is the rate-limiting enzyme in the NAD salvage pathway, which promotes production of NAD+, and MEDUSA-based inferences find that NAMPT contributes to DNA damage-induced lethality only in p53KO cells (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Table 5)38,39. We observed that FK866 inhibits the lethality of Etoposide only in p53-deficient cells (Fig. 6f). Similar effects were observed for other DNA-damaging drugs, and in a panel of genetically diverse p53-proficient and p53-deficient cancer cells (Fig. 6g and Extended Data Fig. 10a).

  OXPHOS is an NADH-consuming process that generates NAD+. Thus, we investigated the extent to which OXPHOS activity in p53-deficient cells affects the levels of NAD+, and conversely, if modulation of NAD+ affects respiration. Consistent with expectations, blocking OXPHOS using Rotenone increases NADH and decreases NAD+ (Extended Data Fig. 10b-c). NAD+ was decreased to a similar magnitude following OXPHOS inhibition and NAMPT inhibition, and both inhibit the lethality of DNA damage to a similar extent (Fig. 5f-g, 6f, and Extended Data Fig. 10b). In contrast, depleting NAD+ using FK866 did not alter respiration (Extended Data Fig. 10d). Taken together these data suggest that hyperactive respiration in p53-deficient cells promotes the lethality of DNA damage due to production of excess NAD+.


## DISCUSSION
  In this study, we describe a method for scoring the death-regulatory function of each gene in chemo-genetic profiling data. These experiments typically evaluate how genes affect growth fitness, which fails to identify death-regulatory genes, due to the confounding effects of growth rate variation between clones. To address these issues, we developed MEDUSA, a new method for inferring the drug-induced death rate for each knockout with a pool. The model we used was specific for the growth/death coordination observed for DNA damage; however, with appropriate modifications to this model, MEDUSA should be effective in any drug context. Moreover, other methods, such as sequencing of dead cells following sorting, are only effective if dead cells remain intact, as in the context of apoptosis. In contrast, MEDUSA does not require recovery/sequencing of dead cells; thus, MEDUSA is likely to be the best method for identifying the genetic dependencies of non-apoptotic death subtypes.

  To validate the accuracy of MEDUSA, we explored mechanisms of DNA damage-induced death in p53-deficient cells. p53 is generally required for activation of apoptosis following DNA damage14,18,19,40. We found situations, however, in which loss of p53 does not affect the level of cell death, but instead changes the type of death activated following DNA damage. Several prior studies have characterized alternative mechanisms for p53-independent activation of apoptosis following DNA damage41,42. Additionally, p53 is also known to positively and/or negatively regulate both apoptotic and non-apoptotic processes in other contexts43-45. To our knowledge, however, our study is the first to demonstrate that DNA damage activates high levels of non-apoptotic death in the absence of p53.

  Notably, MEDUSA also clearly identified this p53-dependent switch between apoptotic and non-apoptotic death following DNA damage (Extended Data Fig. 6g). Thus, MEDUSA-based inferences could have been used to identify this phenotype, instead of the series of cellular, biochemical, and phenotypic assays used in this study. Furthermore, using MEDUSA, we further clarified that DNA damage induces a mitochondrial respiration and NAD+-dependent form of non-apoptotic death. Future studies should address the extent to which these findings are conserved in other tissue or disease contexts particularly for p53-deficient settings not tested in this study, as well as address which subtype of non-apoptotic death is activated by DNA damage in p53-deficient cells, and why this mechanism requires NAD+.

  Taken together, our findings reveal that methods used throughout the community for evaluating drug sensitivity have generated a systematic bias, limiting our understanding of non-apoptotic cell death. In the context of p53-deficient cancers, the high levels of non-apoptotic death that we see were likely missed in prior studies, because these cells rupture and are difficult to recover, and because the corpses lack the conventional hallmarks of apoptotic cells. Thus, these phenotypes would have been previously attributed to lack of proliferation. Importantly, in this study, we evaluated death using methods that do not require the collection, handling, or separation of dead cells, which alleviates a bias against cells that rupture following non-apoptotic death16,17. Furthermore, our analytical methods explicitly score proliferation and death rates, rather than population sizes or growth fitness.

  Using these refined methods, we find that the genetic dependencies for apoptotic and non-apoptotic death are almost entirely distinct, even when death is activated by a common stimulus in isogenic cells. Thus, efforts to personalize lethal drug therapies will likely benefit from understanding the shared versus death pathway-specific genetic dependencies for these drugs. The extent to which targeting non-apoptotic death will improve therapeutic responses remains to be determined. Nonetheless, our study reveals the genetic dependencies of a respiration-dependent form of non-apoptotic which is prominently induced by DNA damage in p53-deficient cancers.


## METHODS

### Cell lines and reagents
  A431, A549, BT549, HCC1143, HCC1806, HT-29, MCF7, MCF10A, MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-436, MDA-MB-453, MDA-MB-468, SK-BR-3, and U2OS cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). A375, HT-1080, Malme-3M, NCI-H460, and UACC62 cells were acquired from the Green lab (UMass Chan Medical School). Caki-1, cells were acquired from the Kim lab (UMass Chan Medical School). Following expansion, all cells were authenticated using STR profiling with their identity confirmed. HT-1080 cells were grown in EMEM (ATCC, #30-2003). ### Assays to measure drug-induced cell death
  The FLICK assay was performed as described previously16,17. Briefly, cells were seeded at a density of 2,000-5,000 cells per well in black 96-well plates (Greiner, #655090), plated in 90 μL of media, and incubated overnight. Cells were drugged with the indicated doses of drug or vehicle, along with 5 μM SYTOX green, in 10 μL media. ### Simulation of population-level responses to DNA damage
  The response of cells to DNA damage was modeled as a biphasic response. For untreated cells, growth is modeled simply as an exponential growth equation (equation I). For cells treated with DNA damage, the change in population size is modeled with exponential equations for growth and death (equation II).

  To simulate a dose curve of treated p53 WT cells, the modeled growth rate was set to 0, and the drug-induced death rate was parameterized around experimentally observed values. To simulate loss of cell cycle arrest, the growth rate of treated cells was parameterized using the experimentally observed growth rate in p53 KO cells treated with DNA damage, while maintaining the experimentally observed p53 WT death rates. ### Chemo-genetic profiling of responses to DNA damage
  Whole-genome CRISPR screens were performed using the GeCKOv2 two-vector system (Addgene, #1000000049). The two pooled DNA half-libraries (A and B) were combined to generate a library of 123,411 sgRNAs. This library was amplified according to the Zhang lab’s protocol (available on Addgene) and virus was generated using 293T cells. U2OS-Cas9 and U2OSp53KO-Cas9 cells were generated using viral transduction of the lentiCas9-Blast plasmid (Addgene, #52962), followed by a 5-day selection with 5 μg/mL blasticidin. U2OS-Cas9 and U2OSp53KO-Cas9 cells were transduced with the GeCKOv2 viral library using “spinfection” of >200 x106 cells per genotype, divided into 12-well plates (final volume 2 mL per well), with 2x106 cells, 300 μL of virus, and 0.8 μL/mL polybrene (Millipore, #TR1003G) in each well. Each 12-well plate was centrifuged at 37°C for 2 hours at 830 x g. Following media replacement and overnight recovery, cells were replated into 8-layer flasks (Greiner Bio-one, # an678108), incubated overnight, treated with 1 μg/mL puromycin for 3 days, replated into 8-layer flasks, and further expanded for 3 days. 50-100 x106 cells were plated in triplicate for each experimental condition and for the T0 controls. On day 0, the treated conditions were drugged with 5 μM Etoposide. Untreated cells were passaged on days 1 and 3 (maintaining 400x coverage), and treated cells were passaged on day 3. Live cells from the treated and untreated conditions were all collected and frozen on day 4. Genomic DNA was isolated from the cell pellets using a phenol-chloroform–based extraction method, and sgRNA sequences were extracted from each genome by PCR (forward: 5’CGATTTCTTGGCTTTATATATCTTGTGG’3 and reverse: 5’CTCTGCTGTCCCTGTAATAAACC’3). A second round of PCR added multiplexing barcodes, and each gel-purified library was sequenced on a HiSeq4000 at 300x coverage.


### Conventional analysis of chemo-genetic profiles (L2RV)
  Read quality was verified using FastQC, and the libraries were de-multiplexed using the barcode splitter function in the FASTX tool kit. The FASTX trimmer function was used to remove the non-variable sgRNA regions from each sequence. Reads were mapped to the GeCKOv2 library using Bowtie2, allowing for a single mismatch. Guides with low counts (bottom 5%) were removed from the library. miRNA sequences were also removed. Sequencing depth was normalized using the distribution of the 1000 non-targeting guides. For each comparison of interest, the log2 relative viability (L2RV) was calculated using a parametric fit in DESeq2. Guide-level scores were collapsed to a single gene-level L2RV by taking the mean. The non-targeting sgRNAs were randomized and assigned to 6-guide non-targeting “genes.” Each fold-change value was z-scored based on the distribution of L2RV scores for the non-targeting genes. An empiric p-value was determined for each gene, and this score was FDR corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (Extended Data Fig. 5c).


### MEDUSA-based analysis of chemo-genetic profiles
  Conventional methods for analyzing whole-genome CRISPR screens compare the relative population size of treated and untreated conditions, which is highly sensitive to changes in growth and insensitive to changes in death. We developed MEDUSA, which infers the death rate from a combination of three insights: 1) the population size at assay end point, 2) the experimentally measured growth rate for each clone, and 3) the coordination of growth and death from GRADE-based analysis.

  Implementation of MEDUSA begins by developing a model of population dynamics in the presence and absence of drug. Growth and death rates for our model were parameterized based on drug titration data used to optimize our screen. Using this model, we simulated all possible ways in which a gene knockout could affect growth and drug-induced death rates, treating these features as independent variables. From the simulated data, we computed the relative size of the treated and untreated populations and a conventional L2RV. Under the conditions tested in our chemo-genetic screen, DNA damage-induced death occurs only after cells have stopped proliferating. The lack of growth during the death phase of the response significantly simplifies the possible ways that perturbations to growth and death integrate to create a given number of cells. Thus, the varied combinations of growth and death rates that yield the same population size create a continuous non-linear “manifold” at each level of L2RV. The implication of this simple structure is that the drug-induced death rate for each single gene knockout can be clearly inferred from a combination of the relative population size (e.g., L2RV) and the relative growth rate in the absence of drug (e.g., the gene fitness score). The MEDUSA-inferred sgRNA-level death rates were collapsed to the gene-level by taking the mean. Gene-level growth and death rates were z-scored based on the distribution of rates for the non-targeting genes. Bootstrapping was used to determine empiric p-values, which were FDR corrected. We define death-regulatory genes using an FDR threshold of < 0.05.


### MEDUSA validation
  MEDUSA validation included the top and bottom 10 genes from the L2RV-based analysis, the top and bottom 10 genes from the MEDUSA-inferred death rate analysis, and 4 genes involved in the canonical repair of Etoposide-induced DNA damage. MEDUSA predicts that: 20 gene knockouts will increase death rate, 10 will decrease death rate, and 10 will not affect the death rate. For these same 40 genes, L2RV-based analysis predicts that: 12 genes will induce drug sensitization when knocked out, whereas 21 will induce drug resistance, and 7 will not affect death.

  For each gene, the highest-scoring sgRNA was selected from the GeCKOv2 library. Each guide was cloned into the pX330-puro plasmid using the single-step digestion-ligation protocol from the Zhang lab (available on the Zhang lab Addgene page). To validate each guide, U2OS cells were plated in 6-well plates at a density of 200,000 cells per well. Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #L3000008) was used to transiently transfect each sgRNA-pX330-puro plasmid into a separate well. Cells were treated with 1 μg/mL puromycin for 3 days after transfection. Cells were replated after selection and allowed to recover for 2 days. Post-recovery, samples were plated at 2,000 cells per well on black 96-well plates. Changes in drug sensitivity and growth rate were measured using the FLICK assay. Relative drug sensitivity was determined by comparing the LF of each sgRNA to that of a non-targeting sgRNA control (ΔLF) at 96 hours. For measurement of growth rate, plates of untreated cells were lysed at 24, 48, and 72 hours with 0.1% Triton X-100. The total cell number at each timepoint was fit to an exponential model to determine growth rates.

### Metabolite profiling

#### Metabolite isolation and LC-MS:
  Cells were seeded on 6-well plates at 200,000 cells per well or on 10 cm plates at 1.5x106 cells per plate. After adherence overnight, cells were treated with DMSO, 1 μM FK866, 1 μM Rotenone, 5 μM Etoposide, or 31.6 μM Etoposide. Samples were collected in parallel for metabolite extraction or protein quantification. For total protein quantification, cells were trypsinized and pelleted, lysed using SDS lysis buffer, and quantified by BCA assay. For extraction of metabolites, media was removed, and cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS. With the 6-well plate on dry ice, cells were submerged in 500 μL (for 6-well plates) or 2 mL (for 10 cm dishes) 80% MeOH. Samples were incubated at −80°C for 15 minutes. Cell scrapers were used to harvest each sample, and sample wells were washed with an additional 300 μL of 80% MeOH. Samples were vortexed at 4°C for 10 minutes and centrifuged at top speed for 10 minutes at 4°C. Supernatant was transferred to a new tube and samples were dried using a speed vac. Data were collected using a QExactive Plus Quadrupole Orbitrap Mass Spectrometer equipped with a HESI II probe and quantified by integrating peaks in TraceFinder 5.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Mass tolerance was set to 5 ppm and expected retention times were benchmarked using in-house chemical standards. Raw ion counts were normalized to total protein. Fold-change and significance was determined using a custom MATLAB script. Enriched metabolic signatures were identified using Pathway Analysis in MetaboAnalyst 5.0.


#### Isotope tracing using 13C6-Glucose:
  Isotope tracing experiments were performed using heavy D-13C6-Glucose (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, #CLM-1396). Cells were seeded in 6-well plates, incubated overnight, and treated with drug. Eight hours prior to the collection of each timepoint, the growth medium was swapped with glucose-free DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #11966025) supplemented with 10 mM D-13C6-Glucose. Metabolites were extracted from cells using 80% methanol as above, and metabolite abundance was quantified using LC-MS and TraceFinder. Natural abundance of heavy isotopes was corrected using IsoCorrectoR (Bioconductor), and samples were normalized to total protein.


### Data analysis and statistics
  Unless otherwise noted, data analysis was performed in MATLAB (version R2019b) using built-in functions. Violin and beeswarm-style plots were generated in GraphPad Prism 9 (version 9.5.0). Pair-wise statistical comparisons were made using a two-sample t-test unless otherwise noted. For the MEDUSA-based analysis, growth rate, and death rate values from the chemo-genetic screens, significance was determined empirically by bootstrapping a p-value and performing FDR correction with the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. GSEA analyses were performed using the GSEA 4.1.0 package, and the associated data was plotted in MATLAB. ImageStudio 3.1.4 was used to analyze western blots. Flow cytometry analysis was performed using FlowJo version 10.8.1. Image analysis was performed using Fiji (ImageJ2, version 2.3.0).
