# Integration of chromosome locations and functional aspects of enhancers and topologically associating domains in knowledge graphs enables versatile queries about gene regulation


## Abstract
  Knowledge about transcription factor binding and regulation, target genes, cis-regulatory modules and topologically associating domains is not only defined by functional associations like biological processes or diseases but also has a determinative genome location aspect. Here, we exploit these location and functional aspects together to develop new strategies to enable advanced data querying. Many databases have been developed to provide information about enhancers, but a schema that allows the standardized representation of data, securing interoperability between resources, has been lacking. In this work, we use knowledge graphs for the standardized representation of enhancers and topologically associating domains, together with data about their target genes, transcription factors, location on the human genome, and functional data about diseases and gene ontology annotations. We used this schema to integrate twenty-five enhancer datasets and two domain datasets, creating the most powerful integrative resource in this field to date. The knowledge graphs have been implemented using the Resource Description Framework and integrated within the open-access BioGateway knowledge network, generating a resource that contains an interoperable set of knowledge graphs (enhancers, TADs, genes, proteins, diseases, GO terms, and interactions between domains). We show how advanced queries, which combine functional and location restrictions, can be used to develop new hypotheses about functional aspects of gene expression regulation.


## Introduction
  The regulation of transcription of a gene involves the binding and correct positioning of DNA binding transcription factors (dbTFs) relative to the RNA polymerase complex. The dbTFs, in complexes together with transcription co-factors and other proteins, play a key role in the transcription activity of specific target genes. These protein complexes converge on transcription factor binding sites (TFBS): the specific DNA sequence recognized by a dbTF. TFBS can be located at various positions relative to the target genes, constituting cis-regulatory modules (CRMs) (1–4), the enhancers being the most studied distal cis-regulatory modules (5). The topology of the chromatin around a gene, including local accessibility of CRMs and their co-location with a gene in a topologically associating domain (TAD) also plays an important role in defining the level of gene transcription (6). The interactions between enhancer and promoter regulatory complexes inside the domain are more frequent (intra-TAD interactions) (2,4,6–9), so the TAD itself can be considered a regulatory domain. The knowledge about dbTF binding and regulation, their target genes, CRMs, and TADs is not only captured by functional associations like their involvement in biological processes or diseases, but it also has a determinative genome location component (10).

  Although there is still some controversy (11), enhancers are typically defined as sequences able to increase the expression of target genes independent of their orientation (5,12). Multiple mechanisms have been proposed and observed through which these sequences can increase the transcription of target genes (4,13–16). Although this is very much in development and attracts a huge amount of attention, we can currently characterize these mechanisms as the recruitment of molecular elements and/or the production of eRNAs that collaborate, in some way, in the transcription process of target genes when enhancer and promoter sequences are sufficiently close in space due to DNA loops or the conformation of molecular condensates/hubs (15). Therefore, enhancer activity does not depend on the sequences themselves, but on the specific dbTFs and cofactors that bind to it, and on the activity of the produced eRNAs. Moreover, enhancers are sequences that have very specific biological roles (5,17–19), and their alteration has been implicated in a wide range of pathologies (20–23), making them candidates for therapeutic intervention. The existence of multiple TFBS in enhancers means that they can accommodate combinatorial binding of dbTFs, with composite mechanisms of action, which increases the variability that these regulatory sequences can have depending on the biological context (11,18).

  When we collected enhancer data from web resources (March 2023) for Homo sapiens, we found about thirty databases dedicated to regulatory sequences with, to some extent, complementary information. The coverage of enhancers in databases has recently been analyzed (24), showing that currently there is no reference database for querying information. There is little connection between the different sources, and the databases identify sequences and create connections between data following different strategies, resulting in different data. The types of annotations between databases also varies according to their focus, and the lack of consensus in a formal representation of data and standardization of terminology is reflected in different representations and data formats (24). In addition, the query interfaces of these databases are often relatively simple, which limits the type of queries that can be performed (Supplementary material Table S1). The lack of interoperability between sources also limits the development of federated queries. Therefore currently, a user is forced to perform multiple queries that can involve multiple databases, to subsequently process the results due to the different formats obtained, and connect the data with other related data domains if one desires to perform advanced queries. When including TADs in a query, the problems are similar. But, in addition, these domains are under-represented in biological databases, probably because of their more recent discovery, which makes their use in query design still somewhat limited.

  Knowledge graphs (KG) (25) have been proposed as a technological solution for complex scenarios that involve integrating, managing and extracting value from large, heterogeneous data sources. Also, KGs respond to the need to develop more powerful and versatile queries and enable the representation of both the data and the domain knowledge. They have been recently used for developing integrative resources for supporting the prediction of drug-target interactions (26) or the identification of drivers of resistance in lung cancer (27). Their development requires the application of best practices that allow resolving major current challenges such as interoperability between databases, a formal representation of knowledge, a standardization of terminology, a data format that is understandable both by humans and machines, and the development of advanced federated queries (28–31).

  In this work, we have implemented knowledge graphs using Semantic Web technologies, including Resource Description Framework (RDF), RDF Schema (RDFS), Web Ontology Language (OWL) and SPARQL Protocol and RDF Query Language (SPARQL) for querying, since they constitute an effective technological solution for exploiting the vast amounts of information provided by life sciences technologies.

  Various efforts have been undertaken in the field of RDF knowledge graphs for life sciences (32–36), among which BioGateway (BGW) is the most relevant for the work presented here. BGW is a graph-based, integrative systems biology knowledge resource built from RDF triples (34). The BioGateway core network contains data for the 20 most thoroughly investigated species and includes UniProt annotations for their corresponding reference proteomes (37), the associated Gene Ontology annotations (38), protein-protein interactions from IntAct (39), and orthology relationships from OrthoDB (40). BGW is further augmented with human transcription factor - target gene interactions from two sources (TFLink and CollecTRI) (41,42), protein-protein regulatory relations (43), the Molecular Interactions vocabulary, the Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (44), and the NCBI taxonomy (45). BGW can be accessed through its SPARQL endpoint (http://ssb4.nt.ntnu.no:23122/sparql), through a dedicated Cytoscape application (the BioGateway App) (46), and the newly developed web tool INTUITION (https://semantics.inf.um.es/intuition/). Both tools launch SPARQL queries from a user-friendly interface to make the knowledge network easily accessible. BGW contains graphs related to biological concepts like genes, proteins and phenotype, as well as graphs about interactions between these different entities, for instance, a graph of interactions between transcription factors and target genes, or genes and phenotypes. However, it so far did not offer graphs containing information about regulatory sequences associated with gene expression, or the specific location of genomic entities along chromosomes.

  The graphs based on RDF triples are especially useful in models that aim to represent semantic and linked data on the web following FAIR principles recommending the standardization and interoperability of information. However, it is not the only existing approach to knowledge graphs (47–49). The GenomicKB model (50) uses property graphs to integrate, store, and share genomic information. Property graphs structure their data into nodes and relations that may have associated properties as key-value pairs. This representation system can simplify some graphs and make queries simpler but requires further efforts to deliver the objectives of standardization and interoperability (47,48). GenomicKB provides an extensive compiled knowledge network with a user-friendly query app. However, the GenomicKB model has been designed with features of aggregate models rather than of integrative ones, what limits the standardization and interoperability of the knowledge network, and make it fall short of fully meeting all FAIR principles (51). A more detailed analysis of the two models and examples of queries that highlight the differences between them has been included in Supplementary material Section 4.

  To use RDF effectively for the integration of the vast and diverse set of human enhancer sequences and TADs into the BioGateway core network, including their genome locations, we propose a novel graph-based scheme. The graphs (see Figures 1 and 2) have been used to integrate the content of 25 enhancer databases and two TAD databases, together with the BGW core network, the result being shown in Figure 3 and available through the open-access endpoint and the user-friendly web application INTUITION. We demonstrate its use in a set of advanced queries (three use cases) that until now were not efficiently possible. Furthermore, we have connected the resources of our schema with the Biolink Model (52) and added this model as a dedicated graph to BGW. Biolink has been recently proposed as a universal schema for knowledge graphs in clinical, biomedical, and translational science to address different challenges that obstruct interoperability between different knowledge graphs available on the Semantic Web. For the technical development, we built on principles implemented in BioGateway: the use of semantic graphs, the use of unique identification of resources through Uniform Resource Identifiers (URIs), and the use of existing ontologies to provide an integrated and richly annotated description of biological networks. All the generated code, a SPARQL tutorial to facilitate the use of the BGW knowledge network, as well as the use case queries and the results obtained are available in a repository generated for this purpose (https://github.com/juan-mulero/cisreg).

  The new BGW provides an integrated dataset that allows for advanced queries that can still be quickly executed, while the BGW core knowledge network has been augmented with information that increases the range of queries to include aspects of gene regulation and operations that are delimited by genomic location (genomic coordinates of enhancers, TADs, and genes). The volume of enhancer and TAD data incorporated from 27 disparate resources now makes BGW the most powerful tool for querying integrated information in this domain. In the use cases, we employ this new network to connect different knowledge domains and exploit functional and location aspects to illustrate advanced queries. The use cases explain how to formulate biological questions, design queries and analyze results about regulatory networks associated with different phenotypes, as well as the possible impacts of mutations on regulatory sequences. Therefore, BGW offers a tool that will allow users to expand the breadth of biological questions related to the field of gene regulation and regulatory systems analysis.

  This article contributes to the development of the Gene Regulation Knowledge Commons as proposed by the Gene Regulation Consortium (GRECO, https://thegreco.org/) and targeted by the GREEKC COST Action (https://greekc.org/). The GRECO focus is on the generation, curation and analysis of data and knowledge about gene regulation processes (53). Our work on schematizing enhancer data and TADs provides the semantic formalization for another crucial area of the gene regulation domain, namely enhancing the interoperability of gene regulation data with the addition of content from twenty-five enhancer databases and two TAD resources. We propose BGW as the next important step towards achieving interoperability of gene regulation data.


## Materials and methods

### The KG schema
  We can define a KG schema as a structured representation of information that uses a graph model to describe, organize, and relate entities, which are objects or concepts that are represented by nodes in the network, and connected by edges.

  In this section, we describe the schema developed for the representation and integration of data about enhancers, TAD domains, and their relations with other entities, in the BGW knowledge network for Homo sapiens. The incorporation of genome location coordinates also required an extension of the gene graph, to include genomic coordinates for genes. Figures 1, 2 and 4 illustrate the modeling details of enhancers, TADs and the extension of the gene schema, while Figure 3 summarizes the graph structure of the generated network. The rest of the graphs included in the model were previously described (https://biogateway.eu/) (34,54). However, to explain how the different graphs can be used as one integrated knowledge network, we have included details in the repository of the relations existing in each graph in BGW.

  The schema is based on RDF (https://www.w3.org/TR/rdf11-concepts), which is a standard model for data interchange on the web, based on the identification of entities as resources through Uniform Resource Identifiers (URIs), which enable their unique and unequivocal identification on the web.

  The BGW knowledge network is structured in individual graphs, which represent different knowledge domains. Each representation of information can be viewed as a statement that is composed of three elements (hence, a triple) that can be seen as part of a sentence: <subject> <predicate> <object>, and express a directional relationship between a subject and an object, or more general: two resources. Two types of triples can be distinguished in each generated RDF file: (1) triples that define classes and properties; and (2) triples that define the instances of the classes (55). We have also used RDF Schema (RDFS), which supports the definition of these classes, properties, and instances (56). The classes define entities or concepts (for example, an enhancer with unique coordinates), the instances represent individuals of classes (e.g. the enhancer sequence according to Ensembl, or this same enhancer sequence according to RefSeq), and properties express directional relations/relationships between classes (Tables 1 and 2). The relations between entities are realized whenever the association is supported by a database.

  Since BGW integrates information from different sources, each class constitutes a node linked both to the data defining that concept (data common to all instances) and to the instances that include the specific metadata from each of the integrated sources. In this way, classes act as collecting points and integration units of information, so they are useful for representing and querying integrated information between sources. In contrast, instances or individuals are mainly used to model each entity according to individual database sources, so the instances are linked to the specific data of each individual, which specifies the differences between entities. In this way, the instances reflect the original source and its metadata, and useful for querying metadata from these sources and contrasting information between databases (more details are available in the Supplementary material Figures S2–S7).

  RDF graph data can be queried with the standardized query language SPARQL, which makes it possible to execute federated queries by combining information from multiple RDF graphs available from different endpoints.

  In BGW, enhancers and TAD sequences are defined in the crm and tad graphs by their coordinates and the specific genome build to which these coordinates apply, so each sequence with unique coordinates constitutes a distinct class entity. To define genomic coordinates, each entity has three edges, with chromosome number, start position, and end position. In the case of genes, they also have an edge to denote the strand. The genes were previously defined in BioGateway (34,46), as were the dbTFs (54) and other entities that we reused (proteins, diseases, GO terms and interactions between domains) (34). Other properties, like biosamples and methodologies, which link enhancers and TADs to additional resources, are represented by their corresponding URIs in different ontologies. These are detailed in Figure 1 and Table 2.

  Enhancers can be experimentally observed to be involved in the regulation of target genes, so the dedicated graph (crm2gene) models the relation between these two entities (Figure 2A). However, enhancers can also be associated with genes by way of proximity (57,58). The annotation of DNA sequences with genomic coordinates allows to connect different entities by queries specifying the allowed distance between them as desired by the user. It can also constrain a query for entities to specific TADs, by co-location, enabling the detection of possible regulatory modules (examples can be seen in the use case queries).

  Because the activity of enhancers derives from their ability to bind dbTFs (5,18), the association between enhancers and dbTFs, defined as BioGateway proteins, is also covered by the crm2tfac graph (Figure 2B).

  The focus on the contribution of enhancers in gene regulation and their broad availability in databases also stems from the fact that aberrations in these sequence elements play an important role in many diseases and constitute potential therapeutic targets (20,59). Therefore, we also include associations between enhancers and diseases through the crm2phen graph (Figure 2C).

#### Enhancers and TADs
  The schema design used for enhancers and TADs is similar. Each enhancer and TAD with unique coordinates is identified as a class, with a unique URI, and as a subclass of an entity defined by Sequence Ontology (SO) (32). In the current version of SO (March 2023), the different types of cis-regulatory modules (CRM) are identified as children of the ‘cis-regulatory module’ or CRM class (SO:0000727), which identifies regulatory elements with the ability to modulate gene transcription in cis (61) (Supplementary material Figure S1). Therefore, each enhancer is defined as a subclass of the CRM class of SO (see Supplementary material Section 2). In turn, each TAD is defined as a subclass of the ‘topologically associating domain’ class (SO:0002304).

  Each enhancer and TAD class always has a set of statements (start and end position, chromosome, genome assembly, taxon, label, and description). Each database includes specific annotations that describe and enrich the sequences and may differ according to the focus of the database. The main annotations that have been included as statements in the enhancer and TADs instances correspond to relationships of these entities with identification methods, biological samples used in the identification, reference articles and databases, and cross-references (URLs) to the records in their corresponding databases (Figure 1 and Supplementary material Figures S2, S3 and S5).

  Since the relations between enhancers and other entities modeled in BGW, such as genes, dbTFs, and phenotypes, can also contain metadata of interest, e.g. the specific cell type, these connections have been modeled in separate graphs (crm2gene, crm2tfac and crm2phen; Figure 2 and Supplementary material Figures S6–S7). In these graphs the scheme is similar, each association between two different entities constitutes a new class whose instances include the metadata specific to each database.

  While classes represent concepts (each sequence with unique coordinates), instances represent individuals of those concepts (the sequence in each database). Thus, one sequence can have one instance when it is present in a single repository, or several instances when it is hosted in different repositories. It also provides provenance as it keeps a registry of the origin of individual pieces of information, which allows, for example, to contrast information between sources.

  The Biolink Model v3.5.4 does not define specific DNA sequences that regulate gene expression like CRMs or TADs, but it provides more general classes like RegulatoryRegion (cis/trans genomic region that affect the transcription of genes) and NucleicAcidEntity (nucleotide-based sequences) that are useful for the purpose. Therefore, to connect the enhancers and TADs of the model to Biolink we defined the CRM and TAD classes of SO as subclasses of biolink:RegulatoryRegion and biolink:NucleicAcidEntity, respectively (more details in Supplementary material Section 3). The crm—phenotype relations are also defined as biolink:EntitytoDiseaseAssociationMixin, but the crm—target gene and crm - dbTF relations were not linked to the Biolink schema due to missing appropriate classes.


#### Genes
  Human genes were previously modeled and implemented in a specific BioGateway knowledge graph (34), where each gene corresponds to a subclass of the class ‘Gene’, defined in the Semanticscience Integrated Ontology (60) (http://semanticscience.org/resource/SIO_010035).

  To perform queries related to genome location, distance and sequence overlap, the gene graph was extended to include the corresponding genomic coordinates (reference assembly hg38). Enhancer-promoter interactions are largely dependent on proximity and higher-order genome organization. Some estimates suggest that in approximately 40% of these interactions of an enhancer, the nearest gene is involved (57). On the other hand, enhancers can also cooperate with other enhancers at distances of 1 kb to 10 Mb (58). In addition, the chromosome segmentation into TADs is also highly relevant for gene regulation, because TAD boundary sequences organize and segment the genome into regulatory domains that group self-interacting elements (6,62). Therefore, the inclusion of genomic coordinates allows to connect the enhancer, gene, and TAD graphs and exploit the location aspect to open new possibilities to query the content of the RDF knowledge graph (examples in the queries). In addition, as indicated above, the genes are connected to the enhancers by a specific property, whenever there is a database to support this relationship (Figure 4 and Table 2). Each gene was also linked to the biolink:Gene class (see Supplementary material Section 3).


#### Reuse of content from existing ontologies
  To avoid the duplication of efforts and to achieve interoperability with other knowledge domains, we reused established BioGateway resources, existing bio-ontologies, and the Biolink Model (v3.5.4) (52) to represent data in the model. These resources are summarized in Table 1, with a few examples of IDs, and the URLs of the prefixes used are shown in Supplementary material Table S2. More details about how the resources are linked to Biolink are shown in Figure 1-4 and Supplementary material Section S3. In Supplementary material Tables S3– S5 a summary of the correspondence between BioGateway and Biolink classes and properties is given.

  RDF is based on the representation of information as web resources, but biological databases do not identify their information by way of URIs, instead, they use labels to identify the information. In addition, those labels do not follow a standard data format. Therefore, in the representation of data in the schema, if a database uses a a term from a reference ontology as an identifier, we linked the term ID to its corresponding path to build the full URI (for example, OMIM:114500 to http://purl.bioontology.org/ontology/OMIM/114500). If the value instead is a label, we manually map it into reference vocabularies included in Table 1 to replace the string label by an appropriate URI. When these vocabularies do not contain the required resource, we used the URIs of the nearest parent class.

### The data integration method
  The next step was to retrieve data about enhancers and TADs from biological databases, representing them using the new schema and integrating the data in BioGateway. We found 29 operative repositories (March 2023) with information about these sequences in humans, which is the best-represented organism in biological databases. In this work, we selected 27 databases: twenty-five covering enhancers and two dedicated to TAD domains (see Supplementary material Section 2.4). We selected databases with different types of annotations to construct a schema that covers a wide range of annotations.

  The data processing method included four steps: data collection, data pre-processing, RDF file generation, file loading, and graph generation (Figure 5); which are explained in the next subsections.


#### Data collection
  We used the following twenty-five databases with regulatory elements annotated as enhancer sequences: dbSUPER (75), ENdb (76), EnDisease (77), EnhancerDB (78), Ensembl Regulatory Build v109(79), FANTOM5 (57), RAEdb (80), RefSeq 110 (81), VISTA Enhancer (82), CancerEnD (83), JEME (84), FOCS (85), HACER (86), EnhancerAtlas 2.0 (87), ChromHMM (88), SEA 3.0 (89), GenoSTAN (90), SEdb 2.0 (91), scEnhancer (92), EnhFFL (93), GeneHancer 4.8 (94), Roadmap (15-states and DNaseI) (95), TiED (96), SCREEN V3 (97) and DiseaseEnhancer (98). For TAD we used data provided by two databases: 3D Genome Browser (99) and TADKB (100). Detailed information about the databases is shown in Table 3. From these repositories, we downloaded the txt, tsv, and bed files available in the Downloads section or their corresponding FTP portals. The links are provided in Supplementary material Table S6. We also downloaded the gene annotation of the hg38 reference genome. For this purpose, we used RefSeq (81) (Supplementary material Table S6), which was also used as a source of human enhancers.


#### Data pre-processing
  To automate the generation of RDF triples, the files were first pre-processed to generate standardized tsv files, which were then fed to a script that generated the RDF files (format of the files is shown in Supplementary material Section 1). These standardized tsv files compile the essential data from each database, including sequence coordinates, genome assembly, identification methods, bibliographic references, linked biosamples, target genes, transcription factors, and associated diseases.

  Pre-processing was performed using code in R v4.1 (https://github.com/juan-mulero/cisreg), aided by the availability of Bioconductor packages for the required tasks. The tasks carried out are detailed in Supplementary material Section 2.5. Briefly, they involve filtering and merging data, standardization of formats, acquisition, and mapping of coordinates to hg38 assembly, updating obsolete gene symbols, and enrichment with data derived from the web portals, original articles, and their supplementary materials.

  Table 3 shows that most of the sequences collected could be used for the development of the integrative resource because they could be remapped to the hg38 reference genome with a minimum ratio of 0.95 in reassigned bases. In addition to sequences that could not be remapped, we discarded sequences that did not correspond to NCBI standard reference chromosomes (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/assembly/GCF_000001405.26/).


#### Generation of RDF files
  The generation of files with a standardized format facilitates the conversion of these files into RDF files through an automated and uniform way (code in Go language available at https://github.com/juan-mulero/cisreg). For each database, we generated RDF files with the N-Triples format (https://www.w3.org/TR/n-triples/) which are later used for the generation of knowledge graphs. The number of sequences and triples generated from each of these files can be found in Table 3 and Supplementary material Table S7. To illustrate the content of these files, a fragment of an RDF file from each graph is available in the GitHub repository.


#### Loading and graph generation
  Finally, for generating the network and to be able to perform queries, the generated RDF files were loaded into an appropriate triple store database to build the knowledge graphs. We used the Open-Source Edition of the Virtuoso server (https://github.com/openlink/virtuoso-opensource), provided by OpenLink Software, which has shown to be the best query system when operating with a native RDF database system (101). To produce the new BGW knowledge network (Figure 3) we added to the BGW core network the following graphs: crm, tad, crm2gene, crm2tfac and crm2phen graphs (Figures 1 and 2), the extended gene graph (Figure 4) and the Biolink schema (34,52).

## Results

### The knowledge graphs
  The data processing and reformatting of the data from 27 resources resulted in five knowledge graphs (Figures 1 and 2): one for enhancer sequences (crm graph), three for relations between enhancers and other entities modeled in BioGateway (crm - target genes, crm2gene graph; crm - dbTFs, crm2tfac; crm—phenotypes, crm2phen graph) and one for TADs (tad graph).

  The CRM graph is composed of 683 145 879 triples, while the TAD graph has 3 878 459 triples. The number of enhancers with unique coordinates (classes of enhancer sequences) is 29 090 002, while the number of unique instances is 29 343 588 (Table 4). In the case of TAD sequences, the graph contains 169 878 domains with unique coordinates (169 883 unique instances). Therefore, although there are shared sequences between the databases used, most of them are unique. However, it is important to realize that two sequences are declared unique if their boundary coordinates are not the same, meaning that they still can share a large overlap. The distribution of the sequences per database is shown in Table 3, whereas the distribution per chromosome is available in Supplementary material Table S8. The general statistics corresponding to the relation graphs crm2gene, crm2tfac, and crm2phen are also included in Table 4.

  Considering the other annotated data extracted from the 25 enhancer resources, the knowledge graphs contain links with 19,977 different protein-coding target genes modeled in BGW, 253 TFs, 429 disease entities (162 from DO, 148 from MeSH and 119 from OMIM), 785 reference articles, 443 cell lines (CLO), 330 cell types (CL) and 278 anatomical structures (UBERON). The graphs from the TAD domains are supported by two reference articles, two databases, 17 cell lines (CLO), 27 cell types (CL), and 24 anatomical structures (UBERON). Finally, the BGW gene graph (http://rdf.biogateway.eu/graph/gene) with genomic coordinates contains 419,453 triples for human data and involves 20,605 human genes (Figure 4).

  The remaining BGW graphs (prot, go, mi, omim, taxon, gene2phen, prot2prot, prot2cc, prot2bp, prot2mf, tfac2gene, ortho and reg2targ) have kept their original design, but have been updated to accommodate the latest version of the source databases. Statistics about each graph have been included in the repository to give a more complete overview of the network. With this, BGW (Figure 3) contains functionally annotated information about genes, their proteins, protein binding partners and regulatory interactions, protein orthology relations, GO annotations (molecular functions, biological processes, and cellular components), transcription factors regulating these genes, associations with diseases, and finally the new enhancers and TADs, amenable to complex queries including searches for co-localization in the genome.

## Discussion
  The continued advancements in technologies available to study gene regulation at a global scale are giving rise to a huge increase in the number and content of biological databases hosting the results (24). However, exploiting such a breadth and depth of data in an integrative way remains difficult due a the lack of standardization. Although the research community is increasingly aware of the need for a standardized and formal representation of knowledge, as demonstrated by initiatives such as the COST Action GREEKC, the Monarch Initiative, or ELIXIR, the lack of interoperability continues to limit and complicate the tasks of querying, integration of results and drawing conclusions.

  As one possible solution, we have constructed semantically integrated knowledge graphs that provide interoperability between sources of enhancer data, TAD data and other available functionally annotated data including the BioGateway knowledge network, the Biolink Model, and several core ontologies (32–34,52), together available through the BGW semantic knowledge base. The generated knowledge network offers new opportunities to explore and use the wealth of existing data on cis-regulatory sequences and domains, as we have demonstrated through a set of use cases. As the knowledge graphs constitute a structured and computer-readable model, they are also open to the application of other computer algorithms such as machine learning algorithms, which can help to find patterns with biological relevance in the data, possibly enabling new knowledge discovery and the development of hypotheses. The use of Large Language Models could also help to develop natural language interfaces for querying BGW, as well as assisting in KG life cycle activities, for example, helping to incorporate the content of additional databases.

  To abide by accepted standards for scientific data management and administration, we have followed RDF best practices and BGW design principles. We have reused BGW and high-level ontologies as our semantic resources, to avoid duplication of efforts and redundancies. In that vein, we have also linked our new knowledge network to the Biolink Model. The Biolink initiative, which aims to provide a common framework to harmonize knowledge graphs for advancing the generation of new knowledge (52), alleviates some of the problems plaguing the current ecosystem of bio-ontologies. However, while today it does not model some necessary classes, including the regulatory sequences that we cover in this work, Biolink is in continuous development and may address this in their future releases. Whereas the OBO Foundry promotes the development of an orthogonal collection of bio-ontologies (33), multiple ontologies still model the same entities in different ways, generating duplications that lead to fragmentation and loss of interoperability between resources that choose different ontology versions. In addition to redundancy problems, there are also gaps in the content of existing ontologies. This problem has become evident in our work, for example, when we mapped the methodologies that provide the evidence for the data, and the biosamples. There is a multitude of concepts that are not covered by the chosen ontologies. As a consequence, an automatic mapping proved not to be effective for connecting evidence and biosamples and a manual mapping had to be carried out. The hierarchically closest parent concept was used when the exact concept was not available. These problems of entity mapping caused by duplications and gaps between ontologies is an issue that the Biomedical Data Translator Consortium is addressing, but the alignment of different ontologies/vocabularies is out of the scope of this paper.

  The design of the schema and the algorithms used facilitate the inclusion of new relationships, so the schema presented is open to future extensions. The schema has been developed for Homo sapiens. However, the inclusion of other organisms could easily follow the same design and algorithmic approach. Other genome assemblies could also be included by remapping the coordinates of the sequences and creating position nodes. In this way, the model can also provide a historical record of the evolution of the sequences, as well as facilitate the work of users who use other assemblies. A similar approach could be applied for other types of sequences and structures (61). The topological diversity of enhancer sequences observed (24) is also an issue that can be covered in future extensions of the schema.

  In the results obtained after the generation of triples, we have observed that there are few enhancers and TAD that have the same genomic coordinates between the different databases used. However, multiple enhancers and TADs overlap with each other. Therefore, this resource can be exploited to search for consensus sequences to reduce the volume of existing sequences that have a high level of overlap and vary by the boundaries of the sequences. These sequences can differ both according to the biological sample used and the method of detection. As such, our combined knowledge graph may serve as a source for regulatory sequence curation.

  The proposed graphs standardize and integrate information from different databases hosting sequences involved in gene regulation. To facilitate the exploitation of the query possibilities that BGW offers, we have provided several examples of use cases and made their queries available. We also provide a tutorial for the development of SPARQL queries in BGW and in the INTUITION tool (Supplementary material and repository). The new knowledge graph design and content offers technical advantages in comparison to the original databases and other previous knowledge networks (50), some of which are characteristic of graph-based design and the use of the SPARQL query language (109):

  From a biological point of view, these advanced query possibilities provide the user with access to an integrated knowledge network where they can generate answers to a new field of biological questions. The query results can provide the basis for formulating hypotheses or testing results with information that would otherwise only be available in multiple disparate databases. The knowledge domains that can be exploited are multiple, as well as the relationships between them, thanks to the graph-based modeling of the network (Figure 3). The queries to BGW can handle genes, proteins, enhancers, TADs, phenotypes, GO terms and different types of relations between biological entities like protein-protein interactions, regulatory and orthology relations, links between dbTF-target gene, genes-phenotypes, enhancer-target genes, enhancers-diseases, dbTFs linked to enhancers, or connections between proteins and their molecular function, biological process and cellular component annotations. In this network we can exploit available and directly observed information, but we can also exploit other strategies, like sequence co-location aspects, to generate new hypotheses. The use cases shown are examples that illustrate the potential of the network with a focus on the study of gene expression regulation.

  It is also important to note that BGW is not the only knowledge network that models biological information. GenomicKB was recently proposed (50), providing a knowledge base that pursues interoperability between sources to explore and investigate entities of the human genome. GenomicKB opted for a property graph (49,110,111), while in BGW we have developed an RDF graph because this facilitates the provision of semantics and the compliance of FAIR principles (51) (more details in Supplementary material Section 4). GenomicKB provides a highly practical network because it offers a wide range of information domains and a user-friendly application to compose and launch queries through its web portal, without knowledge of a query language. However, we believe that our BGW resource offers several advantages because of design decisions underlying the GenomicKB framework, as detailed in Supplementary material Section 4:

  In contrast, the BGW network is (i) an integrative model; (ii) it reuses pre-existing URIs to represent the information in the network; (iii) it is available through the web app and the open access endpoint, which allows federated queries enabled by the SPARQL language and (iv) it is computationally accessible through its endpoint, with all the query potential offered by the SPARQL, so an application does not limit the type of queries. But it does require basic skills in SPARQL, and for that reason, we have included a SPARQL tutorial both in the GitHub repository and the Supplementary material Section 8 with more use case examples applied to the BGW network. In addition, we provided a user-friendly web application, INTUITION (https://semantics.inf.um.es/intuition/), which allows to launch SPARQL queries without the limitations previously discussed and detailed in Supplementary material- Section 4.

  It should be noted that both knowledge bases are complementary, because they have a different scope and goal. GenomicKB has a wider scope, whereas GBW provides content centered on gene regulation. Therefore, both use different source databases but share the capability of exploiting location aspects to develop new query strategies that other knowledge bases do not support. They also exhibit differences related to the graph technology used. BGW uses RDF graphs, a technology that pays much attention to the standardization and interoperability of the information, and allows for the assembly of distributed queries. GenomicKB uses an LPG graph, which is a technology that provides a flexible graph model and focuses on local queries. In addition, RDF triple stores and property graph stores are currently not directly interoperable owing to the differences in their underlying structures, but the ultimate goal is that both approaches can interact and benefit mutually, a reason why we also link the new entities of BGW to the Biolink model, which also accommodates a property graph model. Therefore, both models enrich different approaches from which the users can choose.

  The limitations of our work include that only the human genome is covered and that other functional elements of the genome are lacking, such as silencers, transcription factor binding sites, insulators, or chromatin features (accessibility, methylation status, etc). Therefore, BGW is open to further extensions and we hope that our work serves as a primer that highlights the relevance of integration and interoperability between sources and that it will inspire the community working in the domains of gene expression regulation, knowledge network building, semantic web tools, and FAIR principles to generate rich and complementary sets of federated resources.

  In addition to this, it is important to note that most of the sequences in the databases correspond to predictions obtained through different experimental and computational approaches. Each of these approaches or strategies provides different levels of evidence for the detected sequences, but also a different set of sequences (112). This is because regulatory sequences are often detected through indirect measurements that capture features correlated with the sequences. However, regulatory sequences have shown diverse chromatin profiles that make it difficult to identify sequences and their activity (104,113–115). Therefore, most of the detected sequences are predictions that need further experimental validation and should be treated as such (11,112). Furthermore, the modularity of the sequences (18) and their biological context-dependent regulatory activity (5,104) make the interpretation of results difficult, even in direct measures of enhancer activity (11,112). Consequently, a negative result in a direct measure, such as reporter assays or gene editing, does not exclude completely the possible functionality of an enhancer sequence (116).


## Conclusion
  Current databases about gene regulation usually lack standards for interoperable data representation, and such lack can be overcome with the help of reformatting the data and converting them to knowledge graphs. In this work, we have shown how RDF knowledge graphs can contribute to the development of an interoperable representation of data about enhancers and TADs, which has enabled the integration of the content of 27 databases into the BioGateway resource. The data integration and homogenization approach is based on best practices and international recommendations for achieving interoperability and provides users with a possibility to address new questions to the wealth of gene regulation information and represents another step forward towards the knowledge commons for gene regulation.
