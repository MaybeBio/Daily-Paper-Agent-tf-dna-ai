# TDP-43 Promotes Amyloid-beta Toxicity by Delaying Fibril Maturation via Direct Molecular Interaction


## Abstract
  Amyloid-β (Aβ) is a peptide that undergoes self-assembly into amyloid fibrils, which compose the hallmark plaques observed in Alzheimer’s disease (AD). TAR DNA-binding protein 43 (TDP-43) is a protein with mislocalization and aggregation implicated in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and other neurodegenerative diseases. Recent work suggests that TDP-43 may interact with Aβ, inhibiting the formation of amyloid fibrils and worsening AD pathology, but the molecular details of their interaction remain unknown. Using all-atom discrete molecular dynamics simulations, we systematically investigated the direct molecular interaction between Aβ and TDP-43. We found that Aβ monomers were able to bind near the flexible nuclear localization sequence of the N-terminal domain (NTD) of TDP-43, adopting β-sheet rich conformations that were promoted by the interaction. Furthermore, Aβ associated with the nucleic acid binding interface of the tandem RNA recognition motifs of TDP-43 via electrostatic interactions. Using the computational peptide array method, we found the strongest C-terminal domain interaction with Aβ to be within the amyloidogenic core region of TDP-43. With experimental evidence suggesting the NTD is necessary for inhibiting Aβ fibril growth, we also simulated the NTD with an Aβ40 fibril seed. We found that the NTD was able to strongly bind the elongation surface of the fibril seed via extensive hydrogen bonding and could also diffuse along the lateral surface via electrostatic interactions. Our results suggest that TDP-43 binding to the elongation surface, thereby sterically blocking Aβ monomer addition, is responsible for the experimentally observed inhibition of fibril growth. We conclude that TDP-43 may promote Aβ toxicity by stabilizing the oligomeric state and kinetically delaying fibril maturation.


## Introduction
  Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the world’s most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder, responsible for approximately 60% of dementia cases, and is characterized by symptoms including memory loss and cognitive decline.1 Neuropathologically, AD patient brains show the presence of lesions formed by neurofibrillary tangles and senile plaques, composed of tau and amyloid-β (Aβ), respectively.2 Considered to be central to the pathophysiology of AD,3,4 Aβ is an approximately 4 kDa peptide produced via proteolytic cleavage of amyloid precursor protein (APP) by beta and gamma secretases.5 Isoforms of the Aβ peptide of varying lengths are produced by secretase action,6 with the most abundant isoforms containing 40 and 42 residues,7 known as Aβ40 and Aβ42, respectively. While Aβ40 is more abundant in the brain,8 Aβ42 is more amyloidogenic in vitro9 and shows greater toxicity in vivo.10 Aβ peptides can exist as soluble monomers and oligomers prior to maturation into insoluble amyloid fibrils3. While great effort has been expended pursuing therapeutics that target amyloid fibrils,11 recent studies suggest that oligomers, rather than fibrils, are the most toxic species in the pathogenesis of AD.12–14 Although the precise mechanisms of amyloid toxicity remain unclear, membrane disruption by oligomeric species is strongly implicated as a toxic event in the pathophysiology of AD.12,15–17 With the population of oligomers being correlated with cytotoxicity,13,18 accelerated fibrillization kinetics can produce an attenuating effect on Aβ toxicity,19–21 though means of mitigating amyloid toxicity remain the focus of intensive research.

  TAR DNA-binding protein 43 (TDP-43) is a 414-amino-acid nuclear RNA/DNA-binding protein implicated in several neurodegenerative diseases including amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and frontotemporal lobar degeneration with TDP-43 pathology (FTLD-TDP).22–25 Sharing a common feature with neurodegenerative amyloid-associated diseases, ALS and FTLD-TDP, among other TDP-43 proteinopathies, feature the neurotoxic cytoplasmic mislocalization and subsequent aggregation of the implicated protein.26,27 Structurally, TDP-43 consists of a well-folded N-terminal domain (NTD), two highly conserved RNA-recognition motifs (RRM1–2), and a disordered C-terminal domain (CTD).28 Structural studies have revealed that the NTD functions in the dimerization of TDP-43, which appears essential for its physiological function in RNA splicing.29 Downstream of the folded NTD is the flexible nuclear localization sequence (NLS), which is rich in lysine and arginine residues and is recognized by importin α1/β for nuclear translocation.30 The role of the tandem RRM1 and RRM2 in disease remains unclear, with some studies pointing towards possible relevance to the aberrant self-assembly of TDP-43,31 and others suggesting ALS-linked mutations interfere with proper RNA recruitment.32 The vast majority of neurodegeneration-linked mutations are located in the disordered CTD,33 which mediates liquid-liquid phase separation important for stress granule formation and pathological liquid-to-solid phase separation.34,35 Deposits composed of C-terminal fragments of TDP-43 are considered neuropathological hallmarks in the brains of people with ALS and FTLD-TDP, though the precise role of C-terminal fragments in disease etiology remains unclear.36

  Though typically associated with the aforementioned diseases, TDP-43 pathology is also observed in the brains of up to 57% of AD patients.37 Interestingly, AD patients with Aβ and TDP-43 copathology tend to have more severe disease than those with Aβ pathology alone.38,39 These observations have generated interest in a possible cross-interaction between Aβ and TDP-43, and two experimental studies have investigated their interaction. The first was not dedicated specifically to understanding Aβ cross interaction with TDP-43, but did include in vitro experiments that suggested TDP-43 oligomers could suppress Aβ fibrillization and cross-seed Aβ to form amyloid oligomers.40 The second experimental study confirmed the substoichiometric inhibition of Aβ fibrillization by TDP-43, specifically by NTD-containing TDP-43 variants, through a series of in vitro experiments. Furthermore, ThT fluorescence assays with Aβ prepared in soluble form revealed that TDP-43 was able to more potently inhibit the fibrillization of Aβ40 versus Aβ42. To assess the effect of interaction with TDP-43 on Aβ-mediated toxicity, the authors performed in vivo experiments in which mice were injected with either the product of Aβ or TDP-43 incubation alone or Aβ cross-seeded by TDP-43 at 100:1 molar ratio, and it was found that cross-seeded Aβ more significantly impaired spatial memory and hippocampus long-term potentiation in mice than did either Aβ or TDP-43 alone.41 Furthermore, immunostaining demonstrated that TDP-43 oligomers colocalized with Aβ plaques and intraneuronal Aβ in transgenic mice.41 Given the substoichiometric inhibition of Aβ by TDP-43 in vitro as well as modified Aβ oligomerization dynamics in the presence of TDP-43, a direct interaction between the two proteins capable of enhancing Aβ pathogenicity seems apparent. Despite this, the available experimental evidence studies are lacking in mechanistic insights on their interaction and the molecular rationalization of the observed pathological relationship between Aβ and TDP-43 remains unknown.

  To better understand the observed pathogenic relationship between Aβ and TDP-43, we computationally investigated the direct molecular interaction between the two proteins. We performed implicit solvent, all-atom discrete molecular dynamics (DMD) simulations42 to systematically study the interaction between monomeric Aβ and TDP-43 as well as TDP-43 with an Aβ40 fibril seed, accumulating ~612 μs of atomistic simulation time studying the cross-interaction across all systems. For monomeric form Aβ simulations, we considered both Aβ40 and Aβ42, seeking to rationalize the stronger interaction observed for TDP-43 with Aβ40 in vitro. Considering the computational burden of simulating full-length TDP-43, we utilized a divide-and-conquer approach to study Aβ interaction with each TDP-43 domain. Our results revealed that Aβ preferred to bind the flexible NLS in simulations with the NTD. Furthermore, simulations with RRM1–2 demonstrated an electrostatic-driven interaction, with Aβ transiently binding the positively charged nucleic acid binding interface. Using a computational peptide-array method, we identified the hotspot of interaction with the CTD to be within the amyloidogenic core region of TDP-43. Simulations of the TDP-43 NTD with an Aβ40 fibril seed revealed that the NTD could strongly bind the elongation surface of the fibril seed through extensive intermolecular hydrogen bonding. Our results suggested that the positioning of the NTD could sterically block incoming Aβ monomers from incorporating into the fibril seed, thereby accounting for the experimentally observed inhibition effect,40,41 which may also be rationalized in part by transient Aβ monomer binding to the various domains of TDP-43. Overall, our study provides novel molecular insights on the pathological interaction between Aβ and TDP-43, which is of great importance towards an improved mechanistic understanding of the crosstalk influenced pathophysiology of AD and other neurodegenerative diseases. The findings of our study additionally highlight the importance of addressing neurotoxic protein-protein cross-interactions in future therapeutic developments for AD as well as the need for experimental studies to further understand the relevant timescales and cellular contexts of aberrant cross-interactions with clinical significance.


## Results and Discussion

### TDP-43 interacted strongly with Aβ in silico
  While the two previously described experimental studies40,41 have suggested an interaction between TDP-43 and Aβ that inhibits fibrillization and promotes oligomers, no computational studies to-date have further investigated this interaction. Applying a divide-and-conquer approach similar to that used experimentally, we systematically studied the interaction between TDP-43 and Aβ in silico using DMD simulations and observed a strong interaction between the two. The AlphaFold43 predicted structure of full-length TDP-43 is shown in Figure 1a, and is divided according to our simulation scheme. TDP-43 contains two folded domains, the NTD and RRM1–2, and a largely unstructured CTD. We found that monomeric Aβ was able to interact with each of these domains, but with differing strength. Overall Aβ-TDP-43 contact frequencies were calculated for each non-CTD system by dividing the number of snapshots with at least one intermolecular residue contact by the total number of snapshots in final 400 ns of simulation, and the displayed mean corresponds to the averaged value across the 48 independent trajectories performed for each system. Comparing the computed contact frequencies between systems, Aβ had a greater mean contact frequency with the NTD than with RRM1–2 (Figure 1b). For both folded domains, the NTD and RRM1–2, the interaction was stronger with Aβ40 than with Aβ42, in agreement with experimental evidence.41 The highest contact frequency among all tested systems occurred between the NTD and a short Aβ40 fibril seed. Here, no dissociation events in the final 400 ns of simulation time were observed in any of the 48 trajectories, resulting in a mean contact frequency equal to one and standard error of the mean equal to zero.

  While experimental work has demonstrated the importance of the NTD for TDP-43 interaction with Aβ,41 the importance of the CTD and its ability to interact with Aβ remains unclear. Due to the significant length of the CTD, which we defined as residues 270–414, and its largely disordered nature, we elected to screen the CTD for hotspots of interaction with Aβ using a computational peptide array method. Briefly, the 145-residue sequence corresponding to the CTD as defined was split into a series of 15-residue fragments, each shifted by five residues along the CTD sequence. The resulting 27 fragments were each independently simulated with a full-length Aβ40 or Aβ42 monomer, with 20 DMD simulations being performed for each fragment-Aβ system. Contact analysis revealed a single hotspot of cross-interaction located within the amyloidogenic core region44 (ACR) of TDP-43, with both Aβ40 and Aβ42 binding with highest frequency to TDP-43 residues F316 and I318 (Figure 1c). The amyloidogenicity of this region may increase its propensity to interact with Aβ, and a cross-seeding effect could be possible whereby Aβ, which is more amyloidogenic than the TDP-43 ACR, could nucleate pathogenic conformational changes within the region and thereby contribute to aberrant aggregation of TDP-43 in a co-oligomerization process. Indeed, such co-oligomerization has been suggested to take place by previous experimental work.40 Our results, however, provide only limited characterization of the interaction between Aβ and the CTD due to the computational peptide array method utilizing short 15-residue fragments of TDP-43, thus not capturing the amyloidogenic region in its entirety. Nonetheless, the identification of an Aβ cross-interaction hotspot located within the most highly amyloidogenic region of TDP-43 remains an intriguing finding that inspires more detailed future study.


### Monomeric Aβ interacted strongly with the flexible TDP-43 nuclear localization sequence within the NTD
  To investigate the interaction between Aβ and the TDP-43 NTD, which was suggested experimentally to be crucial for the inhibition of Aβ fibrillization,41 we separately simulated Aβ40 and Aβ42 monomers with a monomeric form of the NTD containing TDP-43 residues 1–102. For the sake of convention, we refer to this entire sequence as the “NTD” while distinction is drawn between the folded domain and flexible NLS region when necessary. We elected to use the NMR-derived solution structure of the NTD with the PDB ID 5MRG as an input structure due to its inclusion of the NLS, which was included in the experimentally tested variant containing residues 1–100.41 The NTD structure was constrained as described in Methods and Materials. During simulation, the N-terminal ~78 residues (“folded domain”) of the NTD maintained their native, well-characterized folded structure, while the C-terminal ~24 residues, which were not subject to the same Gō constraint as the folded domain, primarily assumed a random coil conformation that was transiently interrupted by helix formation. The flexibility of the NLS region allowed for binding with Aβ, which behaves as an intrinsically disordered protein in aqueous solution45 and our implicit solvent simulations.

  As evidenced by the contact map and accompanying residue-wise binding plots computed from the final 400 ns of simulation, interaction with the NLS region was the dominant feature of NTD-Aβ binding (Figure 2a). The NLS region primarily interacted with the hydrophobic cores of Aβ, which are in-common regions to both isoforms that poses the greatest amyloidogenicity, namely an N-terminal hydrophobic core located at approximately residues 17–21 and a C-terminal hydrophobic core extending from approximately residue 30 to the C-terminus of the Aβ peptide. Due to the stronger interaction with Aβ40, as evidenced by the higher mean overall contact frequency (Figure 1b), the contact map for the NTD with this isoform is shown. NTD-Aβ42 binding produced similar patterns, though interaction with the NLS region was notably preferred at the N-terminal Aβ42 hydrophobic core (Figure S2) compared to the C-terminal core, which was near-equally favored in the case of Aβ40. Four patterns are readily visible in the contact map, corresponding to parallel and anti-parallel intermolecular β-sheet formation between the C-terminal NLS region of the NTD and both the N-terminal and C-terminal amyloidogenic core regions of Aβ40. As shown by the Aβ40 residue-wise binding plot with TDP-43, the two Aβ40 hydrophobic core sequence regions coincided with the peaks of residue-wise contact frequency with the NTD. More pronouncedly, the TDP-43 residue-wise binding Aβ dramatically peaked with within the NLS region, with several residues achieving a mean contact frequency greater than 0.70 with the Aβ40 monomer (Figure 2a). Antiparallel β-sheet formation between the C-terminal residues of the NTD and the C-terminal Aβ40 amyloidogenic core was favored over parallel β-sheet formation, while the opposite was true for intermolecular β-sheet formation with the N-terminal amyloidogenic core of Aβ40 (Figure 2a). Examples of each of the four dominant contact patterns are shown with participating residues indicated in Figure 2b. Aβ40 was able to occasionally form β-sheets with the NLS region bilaterally (frame IV) but was more typically bound to the NLS region unilaterally while itself forming an intramolecular β-sheet (frame I). Parallel intermolecular β-sheet formation between the C-terminal (frame II) and N-terminal (frame III) amyloidogenic cores of Aβ40 featured positioning of basic NLS residue side chains towards the implicit solvent, and hence charge-charge interactions between basic NLS residues and acidic Aβ residues were not necessary for binding. Our simulation results suggested that the most important feature of the NLS region responsible for its enhanced interaction with Aβ was its flexible backbone conformation, which enabled Aβ to readily bind and form backbone hydrogen bonds.

  Comparison of the contact maps for Aβ40 (Figure 2a) versus Aβ42 (Figure S2) with the NTD revealed commonalities as well as differences in the specific residues that facilitated binding. For example, both isoforms formed parallel and anti-parallel β-sheets between Aβ residues 10YEVHHQKLVFF20 and TDP-43 residues 92SAVKVKRAVQK102. However, a smaller subset of these residues was especially important for Aβ42-NTD binding, namely Aβ42 15QKLVF19 and TDP-43 95KVKRA99. Several residue pairs from these sequences were key for facilitating binding via backbone hydrogen bonding and possessed the greatest pairwise residue contact frequencies present in the contact map. Specifically, the three highest pairwise contact frequencies belonged to TDP-4397K---L17Aβ42, TDP-4397K---V18Aβ42, and TDP-4398R---V18Aβ42. Contrastingly, pairwise residue interaction between hydrophobic C-terminal Aβ42 residues and TDP-43 NTD residues was much weaker and contributed less to Aβ42-NTD binding (Figure S2). On the other hand, the residue pairs with greatest contact frequencies in the case of Aβ40 and the NTD were TDP-4397K---L34Aβ40, TDP-4397K---G33Aβ40, and TDP-4396V---L34Aβ40, indicating that C-terminal Aβ40 hydrophobic residues were most important for facilitating binding. However, the Aβ40-NTD contact map (Figure 2a) shows that the difference in binding strength between N-terminal and C-terminal residues with the NTD was not as dramatic as in the case of Aβ42. Rather, both hydrophobic regions formed well-defined intermolecular β-sheet patterns with nearly equal propensity in both the parallel and anti-parallel directions with the NLS region. The more equal distribution of contacts partly rationalizes the greater overall contact frequency observed for Aβ40 with the NTD, as the well-defined contact patterns present between the NLS region of the NTD and both the N-terminal and C-terminal hydrophobic regions of Aβ40 increased the overall likelihood of successful binding whereas Aβ42-NTD binding was more restrictive due to greater residue-specificity and localized binding to the N-terminal hydrophobic region (Figure S2).

  For both isoforms, simulation systems generally evolved to feature 7–12 intermolecular hydrogen bonds between Aβ and the NTD (Figure 3d), but with notably more trajectories achieving this relatively stable condition for Aβ40 than for Aβ42. Dissociation of Aβ and the NTD occurred for both isoforms, but more frequently resulted in sustained non-contact in the case of Aβ42 (Figure 3c). For relative energy comparison, a two-dimensional potential of mean force (2D PMF) as a function of the number of intermolecular hydrogen bonds and residue contacts between the NTD and Aβ was computed for each isoform. While both PMFs featured a similar central basin corresponding to around eight hydrogen bonds and approximately 70 residue contacts, the central basin markedly extended into conformational space corresponding to significantly more intermolecular hydrogen bonds and residue contacts for Aβ40 (Figure 2c) but not for Aβ42 (Figure 2d). Low-contact conformations were possible due to the propensity of Aβ to form extensive intramolecular contacts while not requiring as many intermolecular hydrogen bonds (Figure 2, conformations a, α). However, increased hydrogen-bonding significantly stabilized the NTD-Aβ complex, resulting in highly populated intermediate-contact conformations, which featured both Aβ intramolecular backbone hydrogen bonding in addition to intermolecular β-sheet formation with the NLS region of the NTD (Figure 2, conformations b, β). Especially high-contact conformations were enabled by bilateral backbone hydrogen-bonding between Aβ and the NLS region (Figure 2, conformations c, γ).

  Generally, our results indicated a strong interaction between Aβ and the NTD of TDP-43, especially so for Aβ40. Binding with the NTD is significant due to experimental evidence that the NTD is crucial for the TDP-43 inhibition Aβ fibrillization, an effect that was noted to be stronger for Aβ40 than Aβ42.41 According to our monomer simulation results, the NTD may achieve this effect in-part by sequestering Aβ to the flexible NLS region for which Aβ has high affinity. Aβ monomers stably interacting with TDP-43 in this manner will not be available for incorporating into a growing Aβ fibril, resulting in slower fibrillization as observed experimentally. While binding was greatly favored at the C-terminal NLS region of the NTD, contact was also possible at sites on the folded domain portion of the NTD (Figure 2a), though these potential monomer-binding sites are probably less-important for the inhibition effect due to the lesser strength of the interaction. While computational cost considerations demanded that only one Aβ monomer was included in each system, it is conceivable based on the observed conformations that multiple Aβ monomers could form an oligomeric assembly near the NTD, with one Aβ bound to TDP-43 at the NLS region and serving as an anchor for the other peptides, thereby preventing multiple monomers from assuming the fibrillar state. Alternatively, since bilateral binding was observed, it is conceivable that multiple Aβ monomers could bind simultaneously to the NLS region and nearly saturating its exposed hydrogen bond donors and acceptors. In either hypothetical case, the NTD is capable of delaying the incorporation of multiple Aβ peptides into a growing fibril, which may partially explain the experimentally observed substoichiometry of the inhibition of Aβ fibrillization by TDP-43.41


### TDP-43 interacted more strongly with Aβ40 than Aβ42 due to differences in intramolecular hydrogen bonding and intrinsic secondary structure
  Our simulation results revealed that for both the TDP-43 NTD and RRM1–2, interaction was significantly stronger with Aβ40 than with Aβ42 (Figure 1b) as gauged by overall contact frequency, in agreement with experimental results that demonstrated a greater fibrillization inhibition effect by TDP-43 on Aβ40.41 Visualization of the time evolution of intermolecular residue contacts between each Aβ isoform and the NTD revealed that sustained contact was more likely with Aβ40 than with Aβ42 (Figure 3a). This was corroborated by the probability density function of the number of intermolecular residue contacts, which showed peaks for both isoforms at approximately 70 contacts, but with a significantly greater corresponding probability for Aβ40 (Figure 3b). To illustrate specific examples of the general features that distinguished NTD interaction with Aβ40 vs. Aβ42, we analyzed single trajectory intermolecular residue contacts and inter-backbone hydrogen bond time evolution to gain more detailed insights into the conformational dynamics of each system. Trajectories with sustained contact, exemplified by Figure 3e, were more common for Aβ40 (Figure S3a–d) and featured contact highly correlated with intermolecular hydrogen bonding. On the other hand, Aβ42 more frequently dissociated as the NTD-Aβ42 complex was not as extensively stabilized by intermolecular hydrogen bonding (Figure 3f), resulting in a more transient nature of interaction (Figure S3e–h). Trajectories that failed to achieve sustained contact were generally those that did not feature an extended intermolecular β-sheet between the NLS region of the NTD and Aβ, visually exemplified by the snapshots shown in Figure 3e. The differing interaction lifetime observed in silico rationalizes the more efficient inhibition of Aβ40 fibrillization by TDP-43 as in-part due to increased monomer affinity, resulting in a more significant effect on lag time kinetics.

  To examine the difference in overall contact frequency (Figure 1b) between TDP-43 and either Aβ40 or Aβ42, we computed one 2D PMF as a function of the number of intramolecular (Aβ-Aβ) and intermolecular (NTD-Aβ) residue contacts and another a function of the number of intramolecular (Aβ-Aβ) and intermolecular (NTD-Aβ) hydrogen, and computed each of these for both Aβ40 and Aβ42 with the NTD. We found that for residue contacts, both isoforms resulted in a central basin corresponding to similar numbers of inter- and intramolecular residue contacts. However, this basin was slightly narrower for Aβ42 with respect to both inter- and intramolecular residue contacts (Figure 3i), indicating that stable NTD-Aβ42 complexes were more restricted in conformational space than those with Aβ40 (Figure 3g). Additionally, the basin corresponding to the unbound state was deeper for Aβ42 and shifted towards a greater number of intramolecular residue contacts relative to Aβ40. Comparing inter- and intramolecular hydrogen bonding, we found that the central basin was shifted towards more intramolecular Aβ hydrogen bonds for Aβ42 (Figure 3j) relative to Aβ40 (Figure 3h). However, an arm of the central basin did extend towards greater numbers of intramolecular hydrogen bonds in the case of Aβ40, likely due to its ability to form an extended intramolecular β-sheet, which was supported by interaction with the NTD. Similar to residue contacts, the unbound state Aβ42 featured more intramolecular hydrogen bonds than did the unbound state of Aβ40, suggesting that Aβ42 was more extensively stabilized by intramolecular hydrogen bonding when not bound to the NTD, affording significant potential energy minimization that reduced the relative favorability of binding.

  Secondary structure analysis of control Aβ monomers revealed additional distinctions that further rationalized the greater overall contact frequency between TDP-43 and Aβ40. These differences included elevated residue-wise β-sheet propensity at the C-terminus of Aβ42 relative to Aβ40 (Figure S4a), which was enriched in unstructured coil and bend propensity at these residues (Figure S4c). Most importantly, turn content between residues 36–38 was greatly enriched in Aβ42, which had a nearly four-fold higher turn propensity at residue G37 compared to Aβ40 (Figure S4d), suggesting higher-propensity formation of a hairpin within the C-terminal hydrophobic region of Aβ42. The formation of this hairpin facilitated an intramolecular β-sheet capped by a short β-strand composed of Aβ42 residues 38/39–41. The short length of this β-strand allowed for only a small number of hydrogen bonds to be formed between the C-terminus of Aβ42 and the NTD, weakening the interaction and reducing the overall likelihood of sustained contact between the C-terminal hydrophobic region of Aβ42 and the NTD. The same was not true for Aβ40, which often contained its C-terminal residues in an extended intramolecular β-sheet, exemplified by the snapshots shown in Figure 3e. In the extended conformation, binding was stronger between the NTD and Aβ due to the increased number of hydrogen bonds. With this conformation being much less-populated for Aβ42 due to C-terminal hairpin formation, the overall likelihood of successful binding was reduced compared to Aβ40. Collectively, our analysis suggested that the greater interaction strength of TDP-43 with Aβ40 was due to the propensity of Aβ42 to form intramolecular residue contacts and hydrogen bonds more extensively than Aβ40 as a result of its two additional C-terminal hydrophobic residues, thus enthalpically reducing the relative favorability of interaction with TDP-43, in addition to C-terminal Aβ42 hairpin formation that limited NTD-binding competent conformational space. Consequently, TDP-43 likely exerts a weaker effect on Aβ42 fibrillization41 in vitro due to the greater population of unbound monomers available for incorporation into a fibril, which rapidly self-assemble in accordance with the known higher amyloidogenicity of Aβ42 versus Aβ40.46


### Aβ binding with RRM1–2 was driven by electrostatic interactions
  Experimentally, it was shown that TDP-43 RRM1–2 could bind Aβ but alone could not inhibit the fibrillization of Aβ.41 To understand how Aβ interacts specifically with RRM1–2, we simulated either an Aβ40 or Aβ42 monomer with residues 102–269 of TDP-43, using the NMR-solved structure of RRM1–2 in tandem (PDB: 4BS2, see Methods and Materials), which is the only existing experimentally solved structure of TDP-43 containing both RRMs, hence its selection for study. Contact map analysis of the interaction between Aβ and RRM1–2 in our simulations revealed several distinct “bands” of contact, corresponding to TDP-43 residues that had particularly high contact frequency with most residues of Aβ (Figure 4a). These contact bands were particularly strong for Aβ40, which barely contacted RRM1–2 outside of the sequence vicinity of the residues responsible for these bands. Namely, TDP-43 residues K145 and R227 accounted for all 10 of the intermolecular residue pairs with the highest contact frequencies present in the contact map. In the structure of RRM1–2, K145 is positioned such that its butylammonium sidechain is solvent exposed and can easily interact with an incoming Aβ monomer. Consequently, K145 had the highest contact frequency with Aβ40 among all RRM1–2 residues. Specific important interactions included attractive electrostatic interactions between K145 and Aβ40 E22 or D23 (Figure 4b), two acidic residues that helped anchor Aβ40 to RRM1–2 via their charge-charge interactions with the basic TDP-43 residues normally involved in nucleic acid binding. Similarly, the guanidinium sidechain of R227 is freely accessible and was frequently contacted by Aβ40, specifically by Aβ40 residues E22 and the nearby F20 (Figure 4b). As the binding of nucleic acids is a primary function of RRM1–2, our results appear sensible given that Aβ is a negatively charged peptide with high conformational flexibility. The relative uniformity of the Aβ40 residue-wise contact frequency with RRM1–2 and lack of any discernible contact peaks corresponding to the amyloidogenic core regions of Aβ (Figure 4a) suggest that hydrophobic interaction and hydrogen bonding were less important for binding compared to electrostatic interactions that anchored the Aβ peptide, which tended to remain highly dynamic. Interaction between RRM1–2 and Aβ42 resulted in a similar contact map (Figure S5), but with a lower overall binding frequency (Figure 1). Hydrogen bonding did occur between Aβ and RRM1–2 for both isoforms but was seldom sustained for extended periods of simulation time (Figure 4e). Furthermore, the distribution of intermolecular hydrogen bonds showed that there was no secondary non-zero peak, and the probability density function decreased with increasing number of hydrogen bonds (Figure 4f).

  Individual trajectory dynamics were less distinguishable between Aβ40 and Aβ42 with RRM1–2 than in the case of NTD-Aβ interaction, with frequent dissociation events being observed for both isoforms (Figure 4c), contributing to the overall heterogeneity of system evolution suggested by the averaged time evolution of contacts and hydrogen bonds (Figure S7). The probability of peak residue contacts between Aβ and RRM1–2 was indeed higher for Aβ40, but only modestly (Figure 4d). Given that the two isoforms possess identical charged residues and seeing as though electrostatic interaction was most important for Aβ binding with RRM1–2, the additional two C-terminal hydrophobic residues in Aβ42 were not expected to greatly affect binding. The observed difference likely resulted from the greater propensity of Aβ42 to form intramolecular contacts that can stabilize the peptide as a free monomer, decreasing the favorability of interaction with RRM1–2 non-specifically. Single trajectory analysis revealed that both Aβ isoforms intermittently contacted RRM1–2, primarily interacting via anchor points that were most frequently basic residues with highly accessible side chains. Aβ was most commonly found in contact with only one RRM at a time, but occasionally formed contacts with both RRMs simultaneously (Figure 4g). These more extensive complexes did not necessarily possess greater stability, and few trajectories achieved sustained contact between Aβ and RRM1–2 (Figure 4c). In contrast with our NTD-Aβ results, RRM1–2 interaction with Aβ was highly dynamic, with a single trajectory accumulating several distinct conformations as a result of successive binding, dissociation, and diffusion to another binding site (Figure 4h). Hydrogen bonding sometimes accompanied residue contacts but did not necessarily drive periods of high contact (Figure S6), suggesting that contributions from hydrophobicity and electrostatic interactions were often more important for binding. The structure of Aβ was also highly dynamic during interaction with RRM1–2, occasionally becoming completely disordered (Figure 4h). Generally, however, Aβ formed an intramolecular β-sheet that was maintained during binding. Despite the relatively transient nature of their interaction, our simulation results nonetheless demonstrated significant binding between Aβ and RRM1–2 powered by electrostatic interactions, in agreement with experimental results.41 While RRM1–2 could, in principle, sequester Aβ via monomer binding and thereby inhibit fibrillization, our simulation results demonstrated that the complex formed by RRM1–2 and Aβ lacks the stability necessary for sustained capture of Aβ, thus rationalizing why RRM1–2 alone was not able to effectively inhibit Aβ fibrillization in vitro.41


### TDP-43 domains distinctly influenced Aβ secondary structure upon binding
  To understand how interaction with TDP-43 promoted Aβ toxicity in vivo,41 we sought to determine the specific conformations favored by Aβ upon binding with TDP-43 during DMD simulations and the implications of the structures adopted. Thus, we computed the secondary structure of Aβ in simulation and emphasized the changes in structure content induced by interaction with TDP-43. For simplicity, we used a common cutoff of one residue contact to distinguish between bound and unbound states and calculated the secondary structure content of Aβ from the bound states, which were acquired from the entirety of simulation time. To represent the unbound state, we calculated the equilibrium secondary structure content of a free Aβ monomer in a negative control simulation, which was then used as a reference to determine the changes in secondary structure induced via binding with TDP-43 domains. We found that the NTD, RRM1–2, and specific regions of the CTD exerted distinct influences on Aβ secondary structure upon contact. Given the generally stronger interaction with Aβ40 and greater observed changes in secondary structure, the results shown in Figure 5 refer exclusively to Aβ40 containing systems. Identically organized secondary structure results Aβ42 are shown in Figure S9. Most notably, interaction with the NTD did not as greatly affect C-terminal β-sheet content in Aβ42, nor was helix content as significantly reduced. Generally speaking, however, interaction with the NTD and RRM1–2 resulted in similar patterns in secondary structure change for both Aβ40 and Aβ42.

  We found that interaction with the NTD increased β-sheet propensity for residues 7–40 of Aβ40 to varying degrees, with several residues demonstrating significant increases. Namely, Aβ residues H14, H15, Q16, D23, V24, L34, and G38 all showed an increase in β-sheet propensity greater than 100% (Figure 5a), reflecting the formation of extended intramolecular β-sheets that were favored during interaction with the NTD. Increases in β-sheet content were present but less significant for residues within the N-terminal hydrophobic core of Aβ, namely the well-characterized sequence 17LVFFA21, which intrinsically possesses high β-sheet propensity and thus was less affected by interaction with TDP-43. Notably, interaction with the NTD stabilized β-sheet formation by the C-terminal residues of Aβ40 (Figure 5a), which are also important for fibril formation.6 Interaction with the NTD, therefore, stabilized conformations of Aβ known to be amyloidogenic that could favor nucleation of Aβ oligomers by lowering the free energy barrier to extended β-sheet conformations, thereby promoting Aβ toxicity. By contrast, interaction with RRM1–2 minimally impacted the β-sheet content of Aβ, consistent with the previous observation that hydrogen bonding was essential for binding. Interestingly, interaction with RRM1–2 increased helix propensity for every Aβ40 residue, with particularly significant changes observed for residues 10YEVH13 and 29GAIIGLM35 (Figure 5b). Interaction with the NTD, on the other hand, more-or-less uniformly decreased helix propensity for Aβ40 residues 10–40 (Figure 5b). Interaction with RRM1–2 minimally impacted Aβ40 coil and bend content (Figure 5c) and sporadically affected turn content (Figure 5d), but with relatively small changes in residue-wise propensity noted. By contrast, interaction with the NTD significantly reduced coil and bend content (Figure 5c) as well as turn content (Figure 5d), with these changes being distinctly clustered near the N-terminal and C-terminal hydrophobic cores of Aβ40 and thus coupled to increases in β-sheet propensity.

  Nominally, the most significant feature of Aβ secondary structure during interaction with TDP-43 was the markedly higher total β-sheet content induced by NTD binding (Figure 5e). Viewing the results as percent changes in secondary structure content, Aβ40 helix content was reduced by a similar magnitude as β-sheet content was increased due to interaction with the NTD (Figure 5f). However, helices represented the lowest propensity secondary structure in Aβ, and the increase in β-sheet content was enabled primarily by the adoption of this structure by residues that typically exist as part of a coil or bend. Although interaction with RRM1–2 greatly increased helix content for specific residues, the aggregate effect of RRM1–2 on Aβ secondary structure was minimal (Figure 5f), and Aβ effectively behaved structurally as a free monomer even when contacting RRM1–2. Whereas Aβ secondary structure rapidly equilibrated in the presence of RRM1–2 (Figure S8c–d), β-sheet content gradually increased due to NTD interaction (Figure S8a–b). Ultimately, interaction with the NTD significantly impacted Aβ total secondary structure and promoted pathogenic, β-sheet rich structures (Figure 5f). Thus, our simulation results rationalize the experimentally observed increased toxicity of TDP-43 interaction induced Aβ species, demonstrating that these are likely to be β-sheet rich and predisposed to structural conformations known to support the nucleation of toxic oligomers.47

  We also investigated the effect of each TDP-43 fragment used in the computational peptide array on total Aβ helix and β-sheet content to detect any potentially amyloidogenic interactions. As expected, secondary structure changes were generally coupled and fragments that reduced Aβ helix content generally increased β-sheet content and vice versa. Notably, the fragment containing TDP-43 residues 310–324 resulted in the greatest increase in total β-sheet content, which approached the magnitude of relative increase seen for interaction with the NTD and was accompanied by a likewise significant decrease in relative helix content (Figure 5g). Meanwhile, the fragment containing TDP-43 residues 320–334, which have been shown experimentally to take part in α-helix formation,48 significantly promoted Aβ40 helix content but only slightly reduced β-sheet content (Figure 5g). Interestingly, this fragment did not have the same effect on Aβ42, for which helix content was instead increased by several C-terminal TDP-43 fragments that did not similarly affect Aβ40 (Figure S9). We also computed the residue-wise secondary structure of the TDP-43 CTD and found that outside of helices formed between residues 320 and 347, the CTD was largely unstructured, as predicted due to its low complexity. The secondary structure of the CTD was extremely similar in the presence of Aβ40 (Figure 5h) and Aβ42 (Figure S9), reflecting the near-identical contact frequency that many CTD residues had with each isoform (Figure 1c). CTD β-sheet content was maximized for residues 311–321, which coincided with the region determined to have the highest contact frequency with Aβ by the computational peptide array method. Thus, an intermolecular β-sheet between Aβ and these residues within the TDP-43 amyloidogenic core was formed. However, further study is required to understand the potentially pathogenic interaction between Aβ and the TDP-43 CTD.


### The TDP-43 NTD strongly interacted with a short Aβ40 fibril seed and capped its elongation surface
  One of the critical early steps in amyloid fibril formation is the nucleation of short fibril seeds, which can then grow axially via monomer addition. The fibril seed represents a structural transition point between metastable oligomers and stable fibrils, and the growth of fibril seeds and ensuing secondary nucleation corresponds to the rapid elongation phase that is part of classical amyloid aggregation kinetics.49 By blocking the growth of fibril seeds, the lag phase can be extended, and fibril maturation delayed, and thus interaction with fibril seeds is a well-known mechanism of inhibiting fibrillization.50–53 To further investigate how TDP-43 was capable of potently inhibiting Aβ fibrillization under substoichiometric conditions in vitro,41 we elected to simulate the NTD of TDP-43 with a short fibril seed. We chose to test the NTD for interaction with the fibril seed as only NTD-containing TDP-43 variants, including the NTD alone, were capable of inhibiting Aβ fibrillization in vitro.41 Since the effect on Aβ fibrillization was more potent for Aβ40 in vitro, we chose to test the interaction using an Aβ40 fibril seed. To model the fibril seed, we selected the most recently solved structure of an Aβ40 fibril derived from human AD cortical tissue. It is worth noting that the selected structure is missing the N-terminal 9 residues of Aβ40, most likely due to high conformational flexibility. However, the monomer NTD contact map with Aβ40 (Figure 2a) revealed very little interaction with these residues, and thus we predicted that their absence would have little effect on the results of the fibril simulation.

  The interaction between the NTD and the fibril seed featured the strongest binding between TDP-43 and Aβ of any tested system. The NTD achieved a contact frequency of 1.0 with the fibril seed in the final 400 ns of simulation (Figure 1b), with zero dissociation events recorded across any of the 48 independent trajectories during this period. Individual Aβ residues possessing the greatest contact frequency with the NTD were found on the elongation surfaces of the fibril seed, and binding was asymmetric in magnitude between the two (Figure 6a). Residues on the lateral surface of the fibril seed also contacted the NTD, though asymmetrically across each lateral face (Figure 6a). To assess the relative favorability of elongation versus lateral surface binding and its evolution over the course of the simulation time, we computed a 2D PMF as a function of elongation surface contacts and lateral surface contacts for two time periods, one corresponding to “early simulation” and defined as 0 to 400 ns and the other corresponding to “late simulation” and defined as 800–1200 ns. Contacts were at the elongation surface if made with one of the four exposed chains found at the ends of the fibril seed with unsaturated hydrogen bond donors and acceptors. Lateral surface contacts were defined as those between the NTD and one of four interior chains of the fibril seed that lie between the elongation surface chains. The early simulation effective free energy landscape showed a prominent basin, labeled b, corresponding to an intermediate number of elongation contacts and lateral contacts with a nearby extension of the basin, labeled c, which was characterized by more elongation surface contacts but significantly fewer lateral surface contacts (Figure 6b). Naturally, a basin corresponding to the unbound state, labeled a, was present in the early simulation landscape, but this disappeared as the system evolved and was absent in the late simulation landscape. The late simulation effective free energy landscape also contained a central basin and secondary high-contact basin, and these were significantly deepened by system evolution. The basin corresponding to greater numbers of elongation surface contacts, labeled γ, matured and extended significantly towards greater elongation surface contact relative to the basin present in the early simulation landscape (Figure 6c). Meanwhile, the central basin, labeled β, was consistent with b in the number of contacts with each surface but was significantly deepened, indicating that his basin corresponded to a highly favorable conformation. System evolution also resulted in the definition of a shallow basin, labeled α, corresponding to a relatively low number of both elongation surface and lateral surface contacts, representing a more weakly bound state.

  Comparing the early simulation snapshots to those from the later time period, it is evident that the NTD initially contacted both fibril seed surfaces and that conformations matured to feature more stable configurations in which the NLS region of the NTD was more completely bound to the elongation surface, while folded domain contacted either the lateral surface or the elongation surface. Captured as basins b and β, a common configuration of NTD binding featured the folded domain interacting with the lateral surface while the NLS region stably interacted with the elongation surface, forming backbone hydrogen bonds with the exposed Aβ donors and acceptors. Binding of the folded domain with the lateral surface was either static (Figure 6d) or dynamic (Figure 6e), while NLS region binding at the elongation surface tended to be much more stable and was the dominant anchoring force that drove the interaction between the NTD and the fibril. TDP-43 residues within the NLS region contributed most towards fibril binding as evidenced by C-terminal residues possessing much greater contact frequencies with the fibril when represented as an average of all its constituent chains (Figure S12c). Folded domain binding to the lateral surface was favored at the extended β-sheets formed by the N-terminal residues of Aβ in the fibril seed (Figure S12b). Several electrostatic interactions were identified that contributed to the stability of this binding, including TDP-43D13---K16Aβ, which possessed the highest contact frequency of residue pair (Figure S12c) and was a conserved interaction across all lateral surface chains (Figure S11), TDP-43R42---E11Aβ, and TDP-43E9---R83TDP-43, an intramolecular interaction that stabilized the relative positioning of the folded domain and the NLS region (Figure 6f, detail view of 600 ns snapshot shown in Figure 6d). Another crucial structural feature of the interaction was the positioning of the basic side chains that protrude from the NLS region, as these must avoid any steric clashes for the binding to be stable. Detailed analysis revealed that side chains such as those of R83, K84, K97, and K102 were freely exposed to the implicit solvent (Figure 6g, detail view of VI snapshot shown in Figure 6j). Meanwhile, attractive electrostatic interactions such as TDP-43K82---E11Aβ and TDP-43K95---E22Aβ further stabilized the elongation surface interaction. Interestingly, the guanidinium group of R98 was able to be buried between the two protofilaments composing the fibril seed (Figure 6g). Collectively, these interactions contributed to the high stability of binding that enabled all 48 independent trajectories to maintain a bound state for the final 400 ns of simulation, and in many cases longer (Figure 7a).

  As previously noted, binding was asymmetric between the two elongation surfaces of the fibril seed. Contact maps were generated with each chain to explore the various contact patterns possible at the elongation surface. One chain (Figure 6j) markedly possessed the highest pairwise residue contact frequencies because of its strong preference to form parallel and anti-parallel intermolecular β-sheets between the NLS region of the NTD and specifically the N-terminal Aβ residues, which form the longest β-strand present in the fibril structure. Contrastingly, the other chain at this surface displayed lower-probability parallel and anti-parallel β-sheet contact patterns, which appeared as faint diagonal bands in the contact map (Figure 6k). However, despite its relatively weaker interaction with the NLS, this chain proved to have the highest overall contact frequency with the NTD, seemingly because of its higher propensity to interact with the folded domain. (Figure S10d) At the other end of the fibril seed, the two elongation surface chains displayed more similar contact patterns, with both featuring parallel and anti-parallel β-sheet contact patterns with a clear preference for binding between the NLS region of the NTD and the N-terminal hydrophobic core region of Aβ (Figures 6h and 6i). Indeed, averaging the four elongation surface contact maps, which are shown in-full in Figure S10, revealed an overall preference for these N-terminal Aβ amyloidogenic core residues, which formed predominantly anti-parallel intermolecular β-sheets with the NLS region of the NTD (Figure S12a). Notably, the clear cross-β pattern observed in the monomer contact map (Figure 2a) formed between the NLS region and the C-terminal Aβ amyloidogenic core was missing from the fibril elongation surface averaged contact map, perhaps because of the local curvature present in the fibril structure at these residues preventing the formation of rigid intermolecular β-sheets. Examining binding with each elongation surface chain allowed for a variety of conformations to be visualized. For example, the folded domain and NLS region forming an intermolecular β-sheet were frequently bound to opposing ends of the same elongation surface (Figure 6, conformations I, III, V), while in other cases the folded domain and intermolecular β-sheet were found on the same side of the same elongation surface (Figure 6, conformations IV, VI). The folded domain was also found anchored near the center of the elongation fibril, with the NLS forming β-sheet contacts on either side in close proximity (Figure 6, conformations II and VIII). Rarely, the NLS region formed β-sheet contacts with both elongation surfaces simultaneously (Figure 6, state VII).

  Assessment of the dynamics of the system revealed that binding between the NTD and the short Aβ40 fibril seed represented a much more stable interaction than seen for any of the monomer binding systems. Most independent trajectories achieved more than 50 residue contacts for a sustained period of simulation time and similarly had a stable number of intermolecular hydrogen bonds (Figure 7a). On average, the number of residue contacts steadily increased as trajectories adopted more stable conformations featuring a greater number of hydrogen bonds, leading to the eventual convergence of the relative number of residue contacts and hydrogen bonds between the NTD and the fibril seed (Figure 7b). Trajectories were classified as high-, medium-, or low-contact based on the distribution of intermolecular residue contacts, which showed three peak-like features (Figure S13) corresponding to common conformations with distinctive qualitative features. High-contact trajectories included those responsible for the right-most peak and tail in the distribution (Figure S13), and were qualitatively characterized by an “extended” conformation of the NTD in which the NLS was fully extended, and the folded domain anchored itself to the opposite end of the fibril via hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions (Figure 7c). Such trajectories were highly stable and the NTD remained in practically the same conformation for nearly the entire length of the simulation time once bound. Medium-contact trajectories accounted for the central peak of the distribution (Figure S13) and were also characterized by intermolecular β-sheet formation between the NLS region and the elongation surface, but with the folded domain bound to either the lateral or elongation surface and positioned in closer proximity to this β-sheet (Figure 7d). Thus, the resulting conformation was not maximally extended, and less fibril surface area was covered. Low-contact trajectories accounted for the left-most peak and tail of the distribution (Figure S13) and featured an interaction driven mainly by folded domain binding, with less extensive contacts formed by the NLS region (Figure 7e). To assess the system evolution, we computed a 2D PMF as a function of the number of intermolecular hydrogen bonds and residue contacts for four distinct time periods. The results demonstrated that system evolution produced the diagonal broadening of a central basin accompanied by the narrowing of the deepest contour of the basin, corresponding to a conformation with ~7 hydrogen bonds and ~120 residue contacts (Figure 7f).

  Our results immediately suggest a mechanism by which the NTD could potently inhibit the fibrillization of Aβ as demonstrated experimentally. The underlying structural feature of the interaction that was common to nearly every trajectory was contact between the NTD and the elongation surface of the fibril seed, whether by contact with the NLS region, folded domain, or both as was frequently observed. In principle, binding of NTD to the fibril seed elongation surface could sterically block incoming Aβ monomers from incorporating into the fibril, especially with the folded domain positioned prominently on the elongation surface in addition to the NLS providing significant stability of binding. Capping of fibril seeds has been previously demonstrated as a mechanism of inhibiting fibril growth,53,54 and our results in the present study provide a striking example of this effect, characterized by both highly stable binding as well as significant steric hindrance posed by the folded domain in the case of TDP-43 interaction with an Aβ40 fibril seed. There are several considerations with respect to experimental evidence that are worth noting. First, as has been stated before, TDP-43 more potently inhibited the fibrillization of Aβ40 than Aβ42 in vitro.41 In addition to weaker monomer affinity, this can be additionally explained by our simulation results as possibly related to the specific structural arrangement of the Aβ40 fibril used in this study, which is significantly more elongated than Aβ42 fibrils derived from human brains.55 Seeing as though the NLS region of the NTD preferred to bind the N-terminal residues of the Aβ fibril seed, which form a nearly linear β-strand, versus the C-terminal residues that feature greater local curvature, it is possible that binding with the NTD is favored by more linear fibrillar arrangements, which are uncommon for Aβ42. Second, previous experimental work found that addition of TDP-43 was unable to reverse the seeding reaction.41 Our simulation results demonstrated that although elongation surface binding was favored in the case of the short fibril seed used in our system, strong lateral surface binding was also possible. For a longer fibril, such as those present in the seeding reaction that was studied experimentally, this lateral surface binding would be favored due to the much greater surface area, and thus the elongation surface of the fibril would have a much lower probability of being capped by the NTD. Synthesizing our novel computational results and previous experimental results, the inhibitory effect of the NTD on Aβ fibrillization appears to be strongest during the early stages of aggregation, at which point fibrils have not yet grown to lengths that would favor lateral surface binding and thus enable growth at the elongation surfaces. In any case, our in-silico results proved to be in strong agreement with the experimentally observed capacity of the NTD alone to inhibit fibril growth and suggested that the NTD is able to achieve this effect via a conformationally diverse fibril seed capping mechanism. Importantly, this mechanism allows for the inhibition of fibrillization at substoichiometric ratios, as one TDP-43 monomer can block the growth of a fibril seed that would normally serve as a nucleus for the fibrillization of many Aβ monomers.


## Conclusion
  In this study, we systematically studied the direct molecular interaction between TDP-43 and Aβ using atomistic DMD simulations. Using a combination of approaches, we managed to study the interaction between all 414 residues of TDP-43 with the two most abundant Aβ isoforms. Simulating the NTD of TDP-43 with an Aβ monomer, we observed a strong interaction that was largely localized to the NLS region of the NTD, which interacted with the hydrophobic core regions of Aβ. Interaction with the NTD strongly promoted the adoption of β-sheet rich conformations by Aβ, which were accompanied by decreases in helix and coil content. We also found that Aβ could bind RRM1–2 through electrostatic interactions with the basic TDP-43 residues normally involved in nucleic acid binding. In contrast to interaction with the NTD, RRM1–2 binding only minimally impacted Aβ secondary structure, and bound conformations resembled those of a free monomer. Within the CTD, our computational peptide array method resolved a hotspot of interaction between Aβ and a specific region within the amyloidogenic core of TDP-43. Most strikingly, simulation of the NTD with a short fibril seed revealed strong binding, which was more stable than that observed in any other system. The NTD tended to form extensive contacts at the fibril seed elongation surface, with most conformations posing a significant steric hindrance at the elongation surface that would block an incoming Aβ monomer from incorporating into the fibril. Collectively, our results provide a detailed understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying the pathogenic interaction between TDP-43 and Aβ. The β-sheet rich conformations of an Aβ monomer promoted by interaction with the NTD could support the nucleation of toxic oligomers, and monomer binding by both the NTD and RRM1–2, which featured several monomer binding sites, could delay fibrillization. Importantly, binding of fibril seeds by the NTD could block the transition from nucleation to elongation, thereby extending the lag phase and kinetically delaying fibril maturation as observed experimentally. Consequently, oligomers may become more highly populated in the setting of Aβ-TDP-43 interaction, resulting in worsened toxicity as oligomeric species are known to be the most neurotoxic species in the aggregation pathway. Our computational study synthesizes this understanding with molecular detail, providing important insights on the specific mechanisms and features of clinically relevant pathogenic protein cross-interaction in neurodegenerative disease.


## Methods and Materials

### Discrete Molecular Dynamics Simulations
  DMD is a modified version of traditional molecular dynamics (MD) in which continuous potentials governing atomic interactions are replaced by step-wise potential functions.56 By only solving for the trajectories of colliding atoms rather than continuously integrating forces and velocities, DMD achieves enhanced sampling efficiency compared to traditional MD and has been widely used by our group57–59 and others60–62 to study protein folding and biomolecular interactions. DMD utilizes collision-driven dynamics, with a collision occurring when two atoms encounter an energy step according to discretized potentials. Upon collision, their momenta are updated according to conservation laws. We used the extensively benchmarked Medusa force field in our simulations, with Van der Waals parameters based on CHARMM1963 and an implicit solvation model computed according to the Lazaridis-Karplus effective energy function, EEF1.64 Hydrogen bond formation and distance-dependent interactions were modeled using a reaction-like algorithm.42 Screened electrostatic interactions were evaluated using the Debye-Hückel approximation with a Debye length of 1 nm, dielectric constant of 80, and monovalent ion concentration of ~100 mM. For all systems containing charged peptides, counterbalance ions (Na+ or Cl−) were added to maintain net zero charge. The Andersen thermostat was used to maintain a constant simulation temperature of 300K for all systems.65 The units of mass, time, length, and energy were 1 Dalton, ~50 fs, 0.1 nm, and 1 kcal/mol, respectively.


### NTD & Aβ Molecular Systems
  The amino acids sequences of Aβ40, Aβ42, and the NTD (TDP-43 residues 1–102) are shown in Figure S1. An NMR-solved structure of the NTD in monomeric form (PDB ID: 5MRG66) was selected for the input NTD structure due to its inclusion of the NLS region, which was present in previous experiments.41 For the initial Aβ42 structure, atomic coordinates were taken from a solution structure solved by NMR (PDB ID: 1Z0Q67). To model Aβ40, this structure was truncated via the deletion of the C-terminal isoleucine and alanine residues. An Aβ peptide (either Aβ40 or Aβ42) and the NTD were placed randomly in a 15 nm cubic box with periodic boundary condition, with a minimum initial intermolecular separation of 2.0 nm. There were 48 independent trajectories containing Aβ40 and the NTD as well as an additional 48 independent trajectories containing Aβ42 and the NTD, and Aβ40 and Aβ42 systems were analyzed separately. Each of the 48 independent trajectories for each system received different random starting positions and velocities. The well-folded N-terminal 76 residues of the NTD were subject to a Gō constraint, which enforces a maximum separation distance between certain α-carbons, thus providing flexibility while maintaining secondary and tertiary structure. To prevent improbable conformations (due to steric clash with RRM1–2), a constraint was introduced to prevent the end of the flexible NLS from contacting the folded domain. Each independent trajectory lasted 1200 ns.


### RRM1–2 & Aβ Molecular Systems
  The amino acid sequence of RRM1–2 (TDP-43 residues 102–269) is shown in Figure S1. The initial RRM1–2 structure was an NMR-solved structure containing both RRMs in tandem (PDB ID: 4BS268). Monomer Aβ simulations with RRM1–2 were also performed using a 15 nm cubic box with periodic boundary condition and initial intermolecular separation of 2.0 nm. To reduce the computational cost, the RRM1–2 structure, consisting of two well-folded motifs in tandem, was held static, trading off dynamical resolution for longer timescale simulations. As with the NTD, 48 independent trajectories contained Aβ40 and RRM1–2 while 48 others contained Aβ42 and RRM1–2. All trajectories were initialized with different random starting positions and velocities. Each independent trajectory lasted 1200 ns.


### NTD & Aβ fibril seed Molecular System
  For simulation of the NTD with an Aβ fibril seed, a recently solved Aβ40 fibril structure (PDB ID: 6W0O69), the morphology of which was determined to be abundant in human AD cortical tissue, was selected for use. We selected this particular structure because of its apparent clinical relevance and its inclusion of all residues that were important for interaction with TDP-43 in monomer simulations. Fibrillar Aβ40 was chosen rather than Aβ42 to better complement experimental results, which suggested a stronger fibrillization inhibition effect for Aβ40.41 Within a 15 nm cubic box with periodic boundary condition, the NTD was randomly positioned away from the fibril seed with a minimum initial intermolecular separation of 2.0 nm. To reduce computational cost, the fibril seed was held static. The NTD was able to move and was subject to the same Gō constraint as applied in the monomeric Aβ simulation. The same NTD structure was used for simulation with the Aβ fibril seed as with the Aβ monomers. Each with a unique initial random positioning of the NTD away from the fibril seed, 48 independent trajectories lasted 1200 ns.


### Computational Peptide Array Method
  Due to the large size of the flexible and disordered TDP-43 CTD (residues 270–414), the computational peptide array method70,71 was used to probe for interaction hotspots with an Aβ monomer. The CTD sequence, which is shown in Figure S1, was divided into a series of overlapping 15-residue fragments, each shifted by five residues. The fragments were initially modelled as random coils, consistent with the disordered and low-complexity character of the CTD. For each fragment, an Aβ peptide and the fragment were randomly placed in a 10 nm cubic box with periodic boundary condition and an initial minimum intermolecular separation of 2.0 nm. For each fragment system, 20 independent DMD simulations with unique randomized initial positions and velocities at were performed, each lasting 600 ns.


### Computational Analysis
  Secondary structures were computed according to the DSSP algorithm.72 Two atoms were in contact if their distance was less than the 0.65 nm cutoff. Two residues formed a contact if they had at least one atomic contact between them. The formation of a backbone hydrogen bond between two residues was recognized if the N···O distance was less than 0.35 nm and the N−H···O angle was greater than 150°. The two-dimensional potential of mean force was defined as PMF = −KBTlnP(x, y) where P(x, y) was the probability of finding a conformation with parameters x and y during the simulation time selected for analysis. Unless otherwise indicated, the final 400 ns of each independent simulation with the folded TDP-43 domains were selected for analysis. In the case of the CTD peptide array, the latter 300 ns of each independent simulation were selected for analysis. All time-evolution plots for single trajectories or averages across all trajectories were shown as the moving average of every 100 data points, equating to 0.5 ns.
