# Blood DNA methylation in post-acute sequelae of COVID-19 (PASC): a prospective cohort study


## Background
  DNA methylation integrates environmental signals with transcriptional programs. COVID-19 infection induces changes in the host methylome. While post-acute sequelae of COVID-19 (PASC) is a long-term complication of acute illness, its association with DNA methylation is unknown. No universal blood marker of PASC, superseding single organ dysfunctions, has yet been identified.


## Methods
  In this single centre prospective cohort study, PASC, post-COVID without PASC, and healthy participants were enrolled to investigate their symptoms association with peripheral blood DNA methylation data generated with state-of-the-art whole genome sequencing. PASC-induced quality-of-life deterioration was scored with a validated instrument, SF-36. Analyses were conducted to identify potential functional roles of differentially methylated loci, and machine learning algorithms were used to resolve PASC severity.


## Findings
  103 patients with PASC (22.3% male, 77.7% female), 15 patients with previous COVID-19 infection but no PASC (40.0% male, 60.0% female), and 27 healthy volunteers (48.1% male, 51.9% female) were enrolled. Whole genome methylation sequencing revealed 39 differentially methylated regions (DMRs) specific to PASC, each harbouring an average of 15 consecutive positions, that differentiate patients with PASC from the two control groups. Motif analyses of PASC-regulated DMRs identify binding domains for transcription factors regulating circadian rhythm and others. Some DMRs annotated to protein coding genes were associated with changes of RNA expression. Machine learning support vector algorithm and random forest hierarchical clustering reveal 28 unique differentially methylated positions (DMPs) in the genome discriminating patients with better and worse quality of life.


## Interpretation
  Blood DNA methylation levels identify PASC, stratify PASC severity, and suggest that DNA motifs are targeted by circadian rhythm-regulating pathways in PASC.

## Introduction
  Although significant progress has been made on the understanding of COVID-19 pathophysiology and treatment options, millions of surviving patients still develop post-acute sequelae of COVID-19 (PASC).1, 2, 3 PASC involves many chronic deficits that undermine individuals’ quality of life, reinsertion to work, and other relevant outcomes.4, 5, 6 Given the diversity of PASC-associated deficits, this entity has been extraordinarily difficult to characterize accurately.7,8 Indeed, biomarkers of PASC are nonspecific or relevant to single-organ dysfunctions.9 There are currently no global PASC biomarkers encompassing multi-organ deficits. The identification of global PASC molecular signatures could facilitate diagnosis and severity scoring; and provide important insights into the process pathophysiology.

  Methylation of cytosine-phospho-guanine (CpG) dinucleotide positions is an epigenetic modification induced by environmental and other cues, which results in altered gene expression and cellular phenotype.10 Blood DNA methylation profiles covary with organ deficits displayed by patients with PASC: pulmonary deterioration,11,12 cognitive dysfunction,13,14 chronic fatigue,15 sleep disturbances,16 and postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS).17 COVID-19 infection is a powerful regulator of DNA methylation.18,19 DNA methylation can persist long after the resolution of the inciting insult, conditioning protracted perturbations to the cellular phenotype.20 Indeed, COVID-19-induced DNA methylation changes in circulating leukocytes remain abnormal at least one year after infection resolution.21 Thus, DNA methylation is a plausible mechanism causing the lingering effects of COVID-19 and underpinning the pathophysiology of PASC, at least as it relates to the immune system. Whole genome methylation sequencing (WGMS), which interrogates all possible CpG dinucleotides in each patient (>25 million unique CpGs), provides a substantially higher resolution (∼30-fold) to capture persistent COVID-19-induced DNA methylation changes in comparison with microarray technologies.22 We postulate that the use of WGMS to quantify DNA methylation levels would have a greater chance of identifying specific CpGs that discriminate PASC from healthy individuals. Moreover, even though patients with PASC uniformly complain of worsening quality of life,23 no PASC molecular signature has been associated with quality-of-life measurements. Thus, we hypothesized that if the large amount of data generated through WGMS could be correlated with a validated score of quality of life, then a fundamental PASC signature, superseding unique organ or system dysfunctions, could be elucidated. To test these hypotheses, we conducted a single-centre prospective cohort study of 103 ambulatory patients with self-reported PASC and determined their DNA methylation profiles. We scored these patients’ disease burden by using a validated quality-of-life instrument, the Medical Outcomes Study 36-Item Short-Form Health Survey (SF-36).24, 25, 26 This instrument has been extensively used to score deterioration of quality of life after acute illness.27, 28, 29


## Methods

### Human subject enrolment ethics
  In compliance with our approval from the Albany Medical College Committee on Research Involving Human Subjects (AMC IRB Study No. 5670), we conducted a single-centre observational study. Informed consent was obtained from all participants in this study. Self-referred adult patients coming to the Outpatient Pulmonary Clinic at Albany Medical Center in Albany, NY complaining of symptoms of PASC were considered for enrolment. While the clinic operated at the Pulmonary Medicine outpatient's facility, we offered global and comprehensive clinical evaluation to patients who sought advice regarding diverse symptoms associated with PASC, even those exceeding pulmonary conditions. Definition of PASC was ongoing, relapsing, or new symptoms or conditions present 30 or more days after COVID infection.30

  Enrolment took place between February 15, 2021, and June 22, 2021. Patients were eligible for enrolment if they were older than 18 years, had previously tested positive for COVID-19, and reported lingering symptoms after disease recovery. Exclusion criteria were presence of a known comorbidity with symptoms that could be attributable to PASC, including chronic pulmonary diseases, depression, fibromyalgia, and others; cancer; recent infection other than COVID-19; current or recent pregnancy; intake of immunosuppressive medications including mycophenolate, corticosteroids, and others; or inability to provide consent. Informed consent was obtained from the patient directly for every patient in this study. All patients expressed variable chronic disease burdens including fatigue, sleep disturbances, cognitive dysfunction, and reduced general heath scores as measured by the Short Form Health Survey (SF-36) compared to healthy volunteers. This instrument, that has been extensively used to score deterioration of quality of life after acute illness,27, 28, 29 consist of nine categorical domains that each contribute a maximum of 100 points, with higher scores indicating better health status; and a score of 900 indicating no quality-of-life limitation.26 The questionnaire was administered by a dedicated research coordinator. The patient was provided the time to read and answer the questions, and after completion, if there was a question that remained unanswered, the coordinator requested the patient to complete that. Twenty-seven fully vaccinated age matched healthy volunteers were enrolled for use as a reference of baseline DNA methylation levels. These patients expressed a lack of symptoms compromising quality of life, including the specific domains present in the SF-36 score. Control participants attested that, at the moment of their enrolment and since the start of the pandemic, they had never contracted COVID-19 or suffered from a flu-like syndrome that may have been an undiagnosed COVID-19 infection. From all the participants, whole blood DNA was obtained following standard protocols.18,21,22 The primary outcome measure was PASC in comparison with healthy participants (categorical outcome). The secondary outcome measure was the SF-36 as a continuous variable (within the PASC cohort). Subject sex was self-reported by study participants.


### Blood collection, DNA isolation, and whole-genome methylation sequencing
  After subject enrolment, blood was collected using a K2 EDTA coated BD Vacutainer® (BD, 366643). Within 30 min from collection, blood was aliquoted and stored frozen at −80 °C until further processing. DNA was isolated from 250 μL of fresh-frozen whole blood using the GeneJET whole blood kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, K0782) following manufacturer's protocols. The isolated DNA was contributed chiefly by circulating leukocytes as they are normally the only nucleated cells in circulation. DNA concentration was determined using a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and normalized to 20 ng/μL for sequencing. Two hundred nanograms of high molecular weight genomic DNA was forwarded to the University of Illinois at Urbana–Champaign Roy J. Carver Biotechnology Center for DNA sequence library construction, using the NEBNext Enzymatic Methyl-seq (EM-seq™) kit (New England Biolabs, #E7120L), and whole genome sequencing on a Next-Generation sequencer (Illumina NovaSeq6000). This approach yields ∼500 million 150 bp-sequence reads for each library, thereby providing the methylation status of >25 million candidate CpG sites in the linear DNA sequence of bases along its 5’ → 3′ direction when a cytosine nucleotide is followed by a guanine nucleotide (i.e., CpG sites), with a coverage depth of ∼40 sequence reads.


### Sequencing quality control and mapping
  Raw FASTQ files were independently assessed for quality assurance using FastQC (v0.11.8) to evaluate per base sequence quality, per base sequence content, GC content, and duplication levels. Following quality assessment, no samples were discarded due to low quality sequencing. Paired-end FASTQ files were trimmed for quality and adapter contamination using trim_galore (v0.6.7) in paired-end mode using a quality score cutoff of 30 and minimum read length of 30 base pairs (bp). Trimmed reads were aligned to the hg38 version of the human genome with gemBS (v3.5.5) which was run in paired-end mode with all other parameters set to default. Following alignment, methylated and unmethylated read counts were collapsed across strands for each patient. As blood-based biospecimens comprise a composite of different white blood cell types, to adjust for cell-type heterogeneity in patients, cell-type proportions were estimated using the reference-free deconvolution of subpopulations existing in methylation data (DXM) algorithm.31 CpG read counts were imported into R environment (v4.3.1). CpGs were kept for further analysis if all patients (N = 130) had at minimum 5x coverage and were associated to autosomal chromosomes (N = 19,971,143 CpGs). Principal components were calculated for each sample using the top 5% of most variable CpGs.


### Differential methylation analysis
  For both the groupwise comparison of patients with PASC (long-COVID, LC; N = 103) versus healthy control patients (H; N = 27), and of patients with PASC (long-COVID, LC; N = 103) versus patients post-COVID infection but without PASC (PC; N = 15), R package dmrseq (v1.20.1) was used to identify differentially methylated regions (DMRs).32 For DMR identification, dmrseq was run with the following parameters: methylation cutoff >5% between groups, minimum number of CpGs in each candidate region set to 5, number of permutations set to 20, and adjusting for estimated cell type proportions, the first two principal components, sex of patients, age of patients, and BMI of patients. Adjusting for principal components facilitates accounting for latent sources of variance that are not captured in other covariates, such as batch effects, samples procurements and others; that are not directly accounted for in models that are fit to the data. Tested candidate regions were determined to be DMRs if their q-value following permutation met a significance cutoff of 0.05, identifying 39 DMRs. For the analysis of methylation levels and SF-36 score, only patients with PASC (long-COVID, LC) were kept for further analysis (N = 103). R package DSS (v2.48.0) was used for the identification of differentially methylated positions, or CpGs (DMPs).33 Methylation values were smoothed over a span of 150bp followed by fitting a beta-binomial model for each CpG, adjusting for estimated cell type proportions, the first two principal components, sex of patients, age of patients, and BMI of patients. The empirical distribution of P-values resulting from the theoretical null (standard normal distribution) for the test statistics of the CpGs deviated from the expected uniform distribution with an enrichment around small P-values. Accordingly, under-dispersed theoretical null test statistics were used to model the empirical null and calculate local false discovery rates (LFDRs) with fdrtool (v1.2.17).34 LFDRs were used to account for multiple testing and to aid interpretability. A CpG locus with a LFDR <0.05 was identified as a differentially methylated CpG (DMP). DMRs and DMPs were annotated to standard genomic structures and genes using R package ChIPseeker (v1.36.0).35 Code used to analyse data will be available on GitHub (https://github.com/andymadrid/PASC_WGMS_Analysis.git).


### Annotation of DMRs
  Coordinates from DMRs were converted to granges objects using R package GenomicRanges (v1.48.0). DMRs were annotated to genes using R packages ChIPseeker (v1.36.0), TxDb.Hsapiens.UCSC.hg38. knownGene (v.3.15.0), and org. Hs.eg.db (v3.15.0) using a span of −3000bp upstream of the transcription start site as the promoter region. Sequence data has been deposited at the database of Genotypes and Phenotypes (dbGaP) with accession number phs003658.


### HOMER motif analysis
  Identified DMRs were analysed for known motifs using the HOMER (Hypergeometric Optimization of Motif EnRichment) software (Version 4.11). Analyses were performed with the hg38 genome as background, along with Function ‘findMotifsGenome.pl’.36 Using sequencing data generated for a previously published study,22 new comparisons were made and identified DMRs were analysed by HOMER as above.


### Machine learning prediction of SF-36 score
  Patients with PASC (n = 103) were divided into low and high SF-36 groups based on the median value of SF-36 scores calculated from all participants (median SF-36 score = 333.5). Two machine learning algorithms were implemented to identify the minimal CpGs required for classifying participants as low or high SF-36. The support vector machine algorithm was utilized from the R package sigFeature (v1.18.0).37 The random forest algorithm was utilized from the R package Boruta (v8.0.0).38 CpGs found to show statistically significant associations between SF-36 scores, and DNA methylation levels (n = 403) were used in machine learning models. Both machine learning algorithms were used to build binary classification models and rank CpGs by importance for the feature selection analyses. Minimal CpGs were selected as those that were simultaneously identified in the top 10% of both lists (n = 28).


### Leukocyte messenger RNA (mRNA) expression determination
  Whole blood samples simultaneously collected from all participants were freshly filtered, and leukocytes were isolated using LeukoLOCK filters (Cat. No. AM1923; Thermo Fisher). RNA was then extracted from the filters following the manufacturer's instructions and as previously reported.39 Five hundred nanograms of total RNA were used to prepare cDNA using Qiagen RT Master Mix at 42 °C (Cat. No. 210215; Qiagen) following manufacturer's instructions. After RT reaction, 2 μL of cDNA was used per qPCR reaction. qPCR was performed on a CFX Connect (Bio-Rad) instrument using SYBR green-based universal iTaq supermix (Cat. No. 1725125; Bio-Rad) and 2 pmol primers (IDT). Fold induction was calculated using the ΔΔCt method using GAPDH as the reference gene. Each sample was assayed in triplicate, and a negative control was included in each experiment. Primers were selected for all RNA-transcripts that corresponded to known protein coding genes and were annotated to the identified PASC-specific DMRs. Primer sequences can be found in Supplementary Table S4.


### Statistical analyses
  In addition to the bioinformatics analysis, statistical comparisons were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10.2.3.

### Blood DNA methylation levels are altered in patients with PASC
  To test the hypothesis that PASC is associated with persistent changes in blood DNA methylation levels, we compared patients with PASC global and site-specific differential methylation with healthy controls profiles, and with previously COVID-19-infected individuals who did not develop PASC (post-COVID). Mean DNA methylation levels between groups were similar, suggesting that PASC is not associated with global changes in blood DNA methylation (Fig. 2-A). We then analysed differential DNA methylation at the locus-specific level. To achieve that goal, we compared groups of consecutive positions (CpGs) that are similarly and significantly hypo- or hypermethylated (differentially methylated regions (DMRs)). This conservative multi-position threshold to accept significance ensures rigor in identified associations.18 We found 39 DMRs that significantly discriminate PASC versus healthy groups; and 476 DMRs that significantly discriminate PASC versus post-COVID patients. These DMRs were constituted by an average of 15 similarly disrupted CpGs per region (i.e., all hypo- or all hypermethylated) and were distributed as shown in Fig. 2-B and Supplementary File S2 and S3, with about 20% located at promoter and exonic regions, and 80% located at intronic and intergenic regions. There was no chromosome-specific distribution of DMRs, with 34 DMRs being hypermethylated and 5 hypomethylated in PASC versus healthy participants, and 283 regions being hypermethylated and 193 regions being hypomethylated in PASC versus post-COVID patients (Fig. 2-C and D).Fig. 2Blood DNA methylation levels are altered in Patients with PASC. a, Global DNA methylation levels in healthy, Post-COVID, and PASC groups. b, Distribution of differentially methylated regions (DMRs) in genomic structures in PASC (N = 103) versus Healthy (N = 27) and PASC (N = 103) versus Post-COVID (N = 15) comparisons. c, Manhattan plot indicating the chromosomes that harbour the DMRs differentiating PASC versus healthy individuals. Significantly changing DMRs with q values less than 0.05 are indicated as solid dots above or below the dotted black lines. d, Manhattan plot indicating the chromosomes that harbour the DMRs differentiating PASC versus Post-COVID, non-PASC. Significantly changing DMRs with q values less than 0.05 are indicated as solid dots above or below the dotted black lines.


### PASC-associated DMRs are different from previously COVID-19-infected (no PASC) patients
  To ascertain the specificity of DMRs present in patients with PASC, we compared DMRs identified from PASC versus post-COVID patients who were asymptomatic at the time of DNA sequencing (did not suffer from PASC) to those identified from PASC versus healthy volunteers. The 39 regions that were found differentially methylated in PASC versus healthy participants were also part of those regions that made PASC different from post-COVID patients, suggesting that changes evoked by PASC are specific to this condition and not found in healthy volunteers or asymptomatic individuals (Fig. 3-A). We noticed that the number of identified DMRs in PASC versus patients without PASC (non-PASC) was substantially larger than those found versus healthy volunteers. We speculate that these differences were due to the fact that patients without PASC (non-PASC) had originally been hospitalized during their acute infection, and thus suffered from more severe COVID-19 disease burden leading to more pronounced changes in their methylome.22Fig. 3PASC induces specific DNA methylation of transcription factors motifs. a, Comparative analysis was conducted to identify the DMRs that were present in both the PASC versus Healthy and PASC versus Post-COVID comparisons. b, HOMER motif analysis of the 39 overlapping PASC-specific DMRs identified several overrepresented motifs including BMAL1, NPAS, and others. c, Chromosomal distribution of the identified 39 DMRs across the genome shows concentration to certain chromosomes.

  DNA methylation at the promoter and shore regions is canonically associated with transcriptional repression.10 However, methylation of distant regulatory elements such as enhancers and super enhancers, located far away from the transcriptional initiation site, are critical regulators of gene expression.40,41 To determine if PASC-associated DMRs were located at genomic areas targeted by known transcription factors, we conducted a HOMER motif analysis. This analysis identified multiple motifs overrepresented in the patients with PASC and not in either healthy participants or post-COVID control groups, including areas targeted by members of the basic helix–loop–helix-PER-ARNT-SIM (bHLH–PAS) family of transcription factors such as brain and muscle arnt-like protein 1 (BMAL1), NPAS and others (Fig. 3-B and C). These data suggests that locus-specific blood DNA methylation levels may discriminate patients with and without PASC based on the presence of specific DMRs.


### Transcriptional impact of DNA methylation in PASC
  To test whether DNA methylation changes impact gene expression, we conducted qPCR of RNA samples obtained simultaneously with the DNA samples used for the previous analyses. Within the 39 identified PASC-specific DMRs, we found that 7 protein-coding genes had known annotation to these regions (Supplementary Table S4). Out of these 7 genes, 3 were differently expressed between healthy, post-COVID (no PASC) and individuals with PASC (Fig. 4). ### PASC-induced quality-of-life deterioration co-varies with blood DNA methylation levels
  As blood DNA methylation profiles from patients with PASC differ from the healthy participants’ cohorts, we hypothesized that these epigenetic marks represent complex readouts of patient health status, and that their perturbations could associate with PASC severity. Thus, we reasoned that the magnitude of quality-of-life deterioration induced by PASC would significantly co-vary with DNA methylation profiles. To test that hypothesis, we conducted a regression analysis of CpG methylation levels of individuals with PASC against their SF-36 score as a continuous variable. We found that the DNA methylation levels at 403 CpGs significantly correlated with SF-36 score, which comprised 273 CpGs that have decreasing methylation levels with decreasing SF-36 (i.e., relatively hypomethylation with worse quality of life, lower SF-36 score), and 130 CpGs that have increasing methylation levels with decreasing SF-36 (i.e., relatively hypermethylated with quality-of-life, lower SF-36 score). These differentially methylated CpGs were distributed along the entire genome without chromosome-specific bias (Fig. 5-A and Supplementary Table S5). To better appreciate contrasting effects of DNA methylation on PASC, we divided the PASC cohort based on participants SF-36 scores higher and lower than the median value. The demographic and other characteristics of the two groups are presented in Table 2, Table 3, Table 4 Except for preexisting pulmonary disease and aggregated Charlson comorbidity index, there were no significant differences between groups including previous COVID-19 hospitalization, body-mass index (BMI), ethnicity, reason for consultation, or pulmonary physiology. All the disaggregated domains of the SF-36 were statistically different between groups, suggesting that there were no biases toward a specific component of quality-of-life deterioration that was driving the score difference. Female patients were significantly more represented in the low SF-36 score (worse quality of life), which is consistent with previous literature,42 and median income per household according to US census data indicated that worse quality of life significantly associated with lower incomes. ## Discussion
  In this prospective, single centre cohort study, we show that patients with PASC display a blood DNA methylation profile that is different from age-matched healthy participants. The enrolled patients with PASC had come to the outpatient clinic with diverse complaints, and PASC burden was quantified with a quality-of-life instrument. Our data suggests that there is a common biological process that underpins PASC that supersedes single organ dysfunctions. Indeed, we found no individual domains of quality of life, such as physical and social functioning, mental status, or others, driving the effect of PASC on the main or secondary outcomes. Because these analyses were conducted with peripheral blood and involved bulk (not sorted or single cell) sequencing, the translational potential of our findings is substantial.44

  Blood DNA methylation signatures are mainly driven by epigenetic changes occurring in immune and inflammatory cells, which recently have been attributed to the pathophysiology of PASC.45 We chose a conservative threshold to define DNA methylation changes in the primary outcome analyses: the presence of similarly hyper or hypomethylated positions located consecutively, which makes random associations less likely.18 Importantly, while most of the differentially methylated regions were located at intronic and intergenic areas, accumulating evidence indicates that these locations often harbour critical regulatory elements such as enhancers and super enhancers controlling gene expression.40 For instance, motif analysis identified potential targets of regulatory mechanisms including members of the basic helix–loop-helix-PER-ARNT-SIM (bHLH-PAS) family of transcription factors: brain and muscle arnt-like protein 1 (BMAL1), NPAS and others. Interestingly, these transcription factors regulate circadian rhythm-related programs.46,47 Indeed, bHLH family is constituted of over 100 members binding to an E-box motif with broad functional roles.48 The most enriched E-box is the preferred motif for the circadian regulator, BMAL1.49 Notably, binding of BMAL1 and other members of the bHLH family are sensitive to DNA methylation, and circadian rhythm per se is associated with alterations in DNA methylation.50, 51, 52 Abnormal circadian rhythm has been associated with perturbed inflammatory response,53,54 and more recently, with PASC.55, 56, 57 Interestingly, while recent evidence indicates that reduced intestinal absorption of the serotonin precursor tryptophan could be related to the neurocognitive deficits of PASC,58 alterations of the intestinal microbiome leading to disruption of absorptive mechanisms have been attributed to circadian rhythm dysregulation as well.59,60 PASC is significantly associated with sleep disturbances including somnolence, insomnia, and others.61,62 Because our data suggests immune dysregulation of mechanisms dependent on circadian rhythm integrity, it is possible that some sleep-associated disturbances induced by PASC affect the methylome and become perpetuated in the immune compartment, which may not be captured by regular sleep-related screening tools. Indeed, sleep disturbances with and without circadian rhythm dysregulation are manifested by chronic fatigue, which is a dominant symptom of PASC.23 Another important motif identified in this analysis is MITF, which has been previously reported as part of the macrophage top transcriptional programs defining healthy-to-severe COVID-19 phenotype transition.63 A previous study of host-viral infection maps also identified MITF as one of the up-regulated genes.64 MITF regulates innate immunity suppression65 and has been recently associated with COVID-19 exposure during pregnancy.66

  In order to define the PASC specificity of these changes, we compared our data with DNA methylation analyses conducted on previously COVID-19-infected patients who were longitudinally followed up one year later.22 These patients referred no lingering symptoms after resolution of acute infection and specifically lack of symptomatic burden after one month post discharge, and were sequenced with an identical platform to the one used in this study.22 We found that the 39 DMRs discriminating healthy versus patients with PASC were also differentiating PASC versus non-PASC, previously COVID-19-infected individuals, strongly suggesting that PASC, not solely previous COVID-19 infection, exerts a unique regulation of the leukocyte methylome. Importantly, the number of CpGs and directionality of methylation changes (hyper versus hypomethylation) were consistent in both comparisons.

  We found that some DMRs present in genes that regulate protein products are associated with significant transcript changes in patients with PASC, including the antiapoptotic protein FANK1.67 As we conducted the RNA expression analysis using bulk samples, cell-specific transcripts could also have been differentially expressed between patients with PASC and patients without PASC (non-PASC). Future analyses using sorted and/or single cell RNA sequencing data could test this possibility.44

  While regulation of chromatin accessibility has been described in mature immune cells 1–3 months following COVID-19,68 this is an early study that investigates DNA methylation in association with PASC. Even though previous research indicates that blood DNA methylation changes induced by COVID-19 can persist long after acute infection resolution,21,22 we provide evidence suggesting that DNA methylation signatures are correlated with PASC diagnosis and disease burden. We deliberately chose to use bulk DNA sequencing from peripheral blood, an approach with higher translational potential given its relative simplicity compared to sorted or single cell technologies.44 However, it remains to be determined which cell type/s drive persistent DNA methylation marks. For instance, granulocytes, M1 macrophages, and activated dendritic cells are terminally differentiated and do not proliferate in the periphery.69 Thus, if any of these cells were found to drive the DNA methylation signatures observed in PASC, that would implicate lingering epigenetic marks that remain in the bone marrow precursors or other compartment. Also, the harbouring of DNA methylation marks in replicative progenitor cells would indicate the heritability of these epigenetic changes into daughter cells, which is generally regulated by DNMT1.70 It is also possible that a remaining population of memory cells, such as T cells,69 which are present in the circulation for longer periods, maintain the persistent DNA methylation signature we found in our patients. Future studies will clarify these intriguing points.

  We found increased PASC burden to be significantly associated with reduced median income of the participant's residential area as determined by ZIP Code™. This finding, obtained with a standard public health measure of socioeconomic status,71 has been recently described in the COVID literature.72,73 Indeed, there is growing interest in the possible epigenetic effects of lower socioeconomic status, which could operate as a biological surrogate of social disadvantage by conditioning the expression of genes critical to cope with acute and chronic stressors.74 Future research focused on the interaction between PASC, DNA methylation and socioeconomic disadvantage could elucidate locus-specific epigenetic targets of poverty.

  Our study has some limitations. First, this is a single centre study and as such it might be influenced by multiple biases that make the presented data not fully replicable in other settings. For instance, there is an ancestry bias caused by predominant white race presence in this cohort.75 Future validation studies will clarify this point. Second, as this is a cross-sectional study, we were unable to determine if the quality of life and DNA methylation associations continue upon symptomatic improvement. Third, we do not have DNA methylation data from the patients with PASC before their COVID-19 infection, and therefore it is not possible to state if the aberrant epigenetic signatures occurred de novo or instead were preexisting in the PASC-affected individuals, thereby creating an epigenetic susceptibility. Fourth, while we specifically interrogated by qPCR genes identified with known annotation to PASC-specific DMRs, bulk or single-cell sequencing of RNA from isolated leukocytes would provide a more informative assessment of the transcriptional consequences of these altered methylation profiles. Moreover, due to the consistently reported lack of correlation between leukocyte transcript expression and soluble (plasma) protein expression,39,44,76, 77, 78 future work involving proteomic analysis of isolated leukocytes from patients with PASC will potentially reveal the translational effect of the gene expression profiles associated with differential methylation of these genes. Additionally, studies with animal models could illuminate the full trajectory of DNA methylation changes starting before COVID-19 infection. Fourth, this study was conducted in a window of time that involved alpha and beta COVID-19 strains (>90% of cases).79 While infections during periods of Omicron variant predominance decreases the risk of severe forms of PASC,80,81 it remains unknown if epigenetic regulation in PASC is strain dependent. Also, while all patients in the non-PASC group were originally hospitalized at the time of their COVID infection, most of the PASC group had COVID that did not need hospitalization, suggesting a milder disease course. There is also an obvious quantitative imbalance between PASC and healthy volunteer cohorts, which is due to the fact that at the moment of the enrolment, the vast majority of volunteers had already contracted COVID in the past, rendering them ineligible. This imbalance likely contributed to the cohort underpowering. These differences, along with the fact that time from COVID diagnosis was shorter in the PASC group, need to be considered as limitations.

  Moreover, due to differences in vaccines availability at the time of enrolment of the multiple compared groups, there might have been biases caused by this inconsistency.82 Factors found to be associated with DNA methylation, such as body-mass index,83, 84, 85 could have influenced the analyses, although they were added to the multivariable models to adjust for the persistent effects of differential methylation over the investigated outcome. Finally, while the Pulmonary and Critical Care Medicine specialty has been historically associated with acute COVID infection, PASC symptoms supersede single organ dysfunctions. While the enrolled patients came to the visit complaining of symptoms that superseded respiratory complaints, we cannot exclude that the fact that the enrolment took place in a clinic run by pulmonary physicians introduced some type of bias to the cohort, emphasizing the need for future external corroboration of our data.

  Overall, we present evidence that a global signature underpinning PASC can be identified in the peripheral blood methylome. This signature appears to be independent of the organ dysfunction driving the symptoms and involves a limited number of differentially methylated regions and positions. Moreover, this signature identification does not require single cell separation, making it an instrument with actionable implications for the diagnosis, prediction, and treatment monitoring of patients with PASC.


### Conclusion
  Blood DNA methylation levels identify PASC, stratify PASC severity, and suggests that DNA motifs are targeted by circadian rhythm-regulating pathways in PASC.
