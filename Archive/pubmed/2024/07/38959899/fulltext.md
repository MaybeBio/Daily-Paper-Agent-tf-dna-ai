# Major-groove sequence-specific RNA recognition by LoaP, a paralog of transcription elongation factor NusG


## SUMMARY
  LoaP is a member of the universal NusG protein family. Previously, we reported that unlike other characterized homologs, LoaP binds RNA sequence-specifically, recognizing a stem-loop in the 5’-untranslated region of operons it regulates. To elucidate how this NusG homolog acquired this ability, we now determined the co-crystal structure of Thermoanaerobacter pseudethanolicus LoaP bound to its cognate 26-nucleotide dfn RNA element. Our structure reveals that the LoaP C-terminal KOW domain recognizes the helical portion of the RNA by docking into a broadened major groove, while a protruding β-hairpin of the N-terminal NusG-like domain binds the UNCG tetraloop capping the stem-loop. Major-groove RNA recognition is unusual and is made possible by conserved features of the dfn hairpin. Superposition with structures of other NusG proteins implies that LoaP can bind concurrently to the dfn RNA and the transcription elongation complex, suggesting a new level of co-transcriptional regulation by proteins of this conserved family.


## INTRODUCTION
  NusG is a transcription factor with orthologs in all domains of life1. It has distinct N- and C-terminal (NTD, CTD) domains. The NTD associates with the transcription elongation complex (TEC), binding to RNA polymerase (RNAP) adjacent to both the upstream duplex DNA and the single-stranded nontemplate DNA2. Bound to TEC, the NusG NTD suppresses backtracking, promotes processivity and generally reduces pausing2,3. The CTD functions as a hub for protein-protein interactions, associating with ribosomal protein S10 to facilitate interactions between the TEC and the leading ribosome3–5. Upon encountering a stop codon, the NusG CTD:S10 interaction is disrupted, and the TEC uncouples from the ribosome; this frees the CTD to associate with ρ protein, promoting transcription termination6,7. All bacteria encode NusG, but many also produce specialized paralogs, such as RfaH, LoaP, ActX, TaA, and UpxY, to regulate targeted operons1,8,9. Of these proteins, only RfaH has been investigated in detail. RfaH is specifically recruited to the TEC by a nontemplate DNA sequence10 called ops. Similar to NusG, the RfaH CTD binds tightly to S1011. Yet, the RfaH CTD does not bind ρ, and thereby prevents transcription termination12.

  In many Actinobacteria, Firmicutes and Spirochaetes, the LoaP gene lies near long operons responsible for synthesis of polysaccharides and secondary metabolites13. Bacillus velezensis LoaP controls the difficidin and macrolactin polyketide biosynthesis operons by promoting readthrough of intrinsic terminators. Each of those operons features a long 5’ leader region that contains a small RNA hairpin required for antitermination14. While E. coli NusG did not bind this dfn RNA element, LoaP from B. velezensis, Thermoanaerobacter pseudethanolicus, and Slackia heliotrinireducens recognized it14 with Kd 47–160 nM. LoaP is the first NusG family protein with demonstrated sequence-specific RNA-binding.

  To determine the molecular basis for the sequence-specific RNA-binding activity of LoaP, we have now determined the co-crystal structure of T. pseudethanolicus LoaP bound to the dfn RNA hairpin. This reveals NTD and CTD highly similar to E. coli NusG and RfaH. Unexpectedly, the crystal structure and structure-guided mutagenesis indicate that both NTD and CTD participate in sequence-specific RNA recognition. Furthermore, superposition of LoaP with structures of TEC-bound NusG and RfaH suggests that molecular surfaces used by LoaP to bind the dfn RNA and the TEC are distinct. Therefore, our work suggests that LoaP may simultaneously bind to its cognate dfn RNA element and the TEC. Thus, LoaP is likely to employ a unique antitermination mechanism, fundamentally different than the antitermination complexes that have been described for regulation of phage λ gene expression, for the RfaH regulon, or for regulation of the E. coli rrn operons.


## RESULTS

### Overall structure of the LoaP-RNA complex
  The 1:1 complex formed between selenomethionyl T. pseudethanolicus LoaP and a 26-nucleotide RNA derived from the B. velezensis dfn 5’ leader region was purified by size-exclusion chromatography and co-crystallized. The structure was determined by the single-wavelength anomalous dispersion (SAD) method, and refined at 2.7 Å resolution (Fig. 1, Methods, Table S1). The asymmetric unit of our cocrystals is comprised of three independent LoaP-RNA complexes. The three protein chains superimpose closely, except for partially disordered segments of one, with pairwise root-mean-square differences (r.m.s.d.) of 1.2 – 2.6 Å (1.7 ± 0.8.; n=3 for 138 or 161 Cα pairs) (Fig. S1A–C). The RNA folds into a stem-loop, comprised of an underwound stem and, as predicted14, a terminal UNCG tetraloop. LoaP has distinct N- and C-terminal domains, both of which participate in forming the protein-RNA interface. LoaP binding buries 832 ± 17 Å2 (n=3) of the solvent-accessible surface area of the RNA.


### An RNA stem-loop with a broad major groove
  The 5’ and 3’ ends of the dfn hairpin RNA associate to form five canonical Watson-Crick base pairs. These are followed by a central underwound segment that is comprised of two Watson-Crick base pairs flanked on either side by two single nucleotide bulges (U6 and C9, respectively), and culminates with three canonical Watson-Crick base pairs located just below the apical tetraloop (Fig. 1,2). The solvent-exposed conformation of U6 appears to be stabilized through formation of a hydrogen bond between the 2’-OH of A5 and the pro-Sp non-bridging phosphate oxygen (NBPO) of C7 (Fig. S2A). The two Watson-Crick base pairs adjacent to U6 (C7•G21 and G8•C20) are unusual because their helical axes are displaced such that G8 makes a cross-strand stack on G21. This, and the two bulged pyrimidines produce a local, pronounced broadening of the major groove. Thus, for the five A-form distal base pairs, the C1’-C1’ distance is 10.57 ± 0.15 Å (mean ± s.d.; n = 15), while the U6 to G21 and C9 to A19 C1’-C1’ distances are 19.48 ± 0.13 Å (n = 3) and 18.62 ± 0.06 Å (n = 3), respectively (Fig S2A). The apex of the RNA adopts the canonical UNCG tetraloop fold15,16 comprising a Z-turn. Thus, the closing pair features a hydrogen bond between O2 of U13 and N1 of the syn conformation G16, as well as a water-mediated hydrogen bond between the N3 of U13 and the N2 of G16; U14 at the second position is extruded into the minor groove side; C15 in the third position lies in the major groove side, its base stacking atop U13 and its ribose positioned above the base of G16. Other canonical features of UNCG tetraloops present in the dfn RNA hairpin include a hydrogen bond between N4 of C15 and the pro-Sp NBPO of U14, as well as C2’-endo sugar pucker conformations for U14 and C15 (Fig S2B).


### Bipartite RNA recognition by LoaP
  The N-terminal portion of LoaP adopts the NGN domain17 fold, characteristic of NusG homologs, which consists of a three-stranded anti-parallel β-sheet flanked by three helices. For LoaP, a long β hairpin (β2’ and β3’) extends the core β2 and β3 strands, protruding toward the tetraloop of the bound RNA (Fig. 1, Fig. S1C). The LoaP C-terminal portion folds into a KOW domain, a five-stranded β-barrel (Fig. 1, Fig. S1C). The LoaP NTD and CTD are closely apposed, with an interfacial solvent inaccessible surface area of 2277 ± 97 Å2 (n = 3). Mapping of the electrostatic potential of LoaP onto its molecular surface shows the pronounced electropositive character of its RNA-binding surfaces (Fig 2A). This is likely a common feature of LoaP orthologs, given the high phylogenetic conservation of the RNA-protein interface (Fig S1C).

  The CTD contributes 52.6% ± 1.3% (n=3) of the LoaP solvent-inaccessible RNA interface and makes two sets of contacts with the central section of the RNA (Fig. S2C, S3A). First, it recognizes the solvent-extruded U6 using an electropositive pocket. Lys140 anchors the domain to the pro-Sp NBPO of G8, and the backbone amide of Ile135 hydrogen bonds to the N1 of the U6 base, onto which the guanidinium of Arg120 stacks. In addition, the ribose O4’ of U6 is within hydrogen bonding distance of the Ile138 backbone amide. Thus, LoaP directly recognizes several features of the 5’ bulged pyrimidine (Fig. 1D, 2C). Second, the broad major groove of the RNA allows LoaP to use residues in the loop connecting its β6 and β7 strands to make direct hydrogen bonds with the Hoogsteen edges of nucleotides. Lys142 hydrogen bonds with O6 of RNA residue G17, while Arg141 adopts two alternate conformations. In one, the guanidinium of Arg141 makes a bidentate interaction with both the N7 and O6 of the G11 nucleobase. In the other, it simultaneously hydrogen bonds to the pro-Rp NBPOs of U10 and G11 (Fig. 2D).

  LoaP uses both its domains to recognize the UNCG tetraloop (Fig. 1D, 2E). The majority of tetraloop interactions are made by residues from the protuberant β2’-β3’ hairpin of the NGN domain, including Arg36, Lys51, Glu40 and Arg41. The guanidinium of Arg36 recognizes both NBPOs of G16. In the best ordered complex in our co-crystals (Fig. S1B) Lys51 makes a water-mediated interaction with the pro-Rp NBPO of C15. The Nζ of Lys51 also makes a water-mediated hydrogen bond to the sidechain of Glu40, which stacks directly on top of the C15 nucleobase (the third position of the tetraloop) and also hydrogen bonds to its 2’-OH. The C15 nucleobase in turn makes a direct interaction with the main-chain carbonyl of Arg41, and a water-mediated interaction with the amide of the same residue. From the CTD, Arg144 makes three hydrogen bonding interactions with the tetraloop: to the O2 of the C15 nucleobase, to the 2’-OH of C15, and to the pro-Rp NBPO of G16. Thus, LoaP recognizes the conformation of the ribose-phosphate backbone of the tetraloop and the identity of C15 using conserved residues from its two domains that are structurally conserved across NusG family proteins (Fig. 1D, S1C, S3A).


### Structure-based mutagenic analysis of RNA recognition
  We examined the functional importance of LoaP-RNA interactions present in our co-crystal structure by generating site-directed mutations in the protein and evaluating their effect on binding affinity. Similarly, we also tested RNA ligands that contained substitutions at key protein-interacting functional groups (Fig 3, Fig. S3).

  To test the relative importance of protein-RNA interactions in the central region of the duplex stem (Fig. 2C), we mutated Arg120, which stacks on the nucleobase of the bulged U6. This mutation had only a modest detrimental effect (~1.8 fold) on affinity. However, the combination of the removal of the A5 2’-OH with a phosphorothioate substitution at C7, which should perturb their intramolecular interaction, resulted in a more severe loss of binding affinity. Similarly, a phosphorothioate substitution at the bulged U6 resulted in significant loss of binding. An even greater loss of binding activity was observed when the U6 phosphorothioate substitution was combined with N3 methylation. This methylation would be expected to result in steric blockage, given that the U6 N3 hydrogen bonds to the backbone carbonyl oxygen of Ile135 of the CTD. A phosphorothioate substitution at G8, which sits below the bulged C9 residue and is hydrogen bonds with Lys140, also resulted in a significant decrease in binding affinity. Arg141 adopts two alternative conformations that interact with the backbone and base of G11 (Fig. 2D). Mutation of Arg141 resulted in a significant (>7-fold) decrease in binding affinity. The combined mutation of Arg141 and Lys142 resulted in an even greater (>10-fold) decrease in binding. Together, these data demonstrate that the center region of the RNA hairpin, including the lower bulged pyrimidine, contains important recognition features that require multiple protein contacts.

  Mutation of LoaP residues involved in tetraloop interactions (Fig. 1D,2E) generally resulted in significantly decreased RNA-binding activity. For example, alanine substitution of Glu40, which stacks on the base of C15, decreased binding by >15-fold. The importance of the stacking interaction is also supported by the Glu40His mutation (where the mutant residue is expected to preserve the ability to stack on the base, and possibly the hydrogen bond to the 2’-OH of C15). This mutation decreased binding only by 3.5-fold. Likewise, mutation of two arginine residues that make direct contacts with the phosphate backbone, Arg36Ser and Arg144Ser, reduced binding by 25.5-fold and 24.3-fold, respectively. Furthermore, a double mutation carrying both arginine mutations showed a further loss of binding activity (Fig 3, S3).

  The functional importance of interactions with the UNCG tetraloop is also supported by alterations of the RNA (Fig 3, S3). The most deleterious was substitution of C15 with 4-thiouridine. This substitution is predicted to interfere with two of the three hydrogen bonds formed by the native base. Phosphorothioate substitution of NBPO at G16, which may sterically interfere with salt bridge formation with Arg36 and Arg144, significantly reduced binding. A double alteration of residue C15, containing both phosphorothioate and deoxyribose substitutions, is predicted to interfere with interactions with Glu40, Lys51 and Arg144; this RNA demonstrated severely reduced binding activity. Overall, these experiments demonstrate that recognition of the UNCG tetraloop, particularly C15 at the third position of the loop, is critical for LoaP binding to the dfn RNA hairpin.


### Structural model of LoaP interaction with RNAP
  Cryo-electron microscopy structures of NusG or RfaH bound to the TEC have previously shown2,4 that these two proteins bridge the β and β’ subunits of RNAP using a conserved surface of their respective NGN domains; once bound, they appear to chaperone the upstream DNA duplex. To evaluate how LoaP compares to these TEC-associated NusG family proteins, we superimposed our co-crystal structure on these previously reported structures (Fig. 4A,B). The LoaP NGN domain superimposes closely on that of TEC-bound RfaH (Fig. S4A), suggesting that our LoaP structure is likely to be directly relevant to its TEC-bound state. In this homology model, the only clash is between the clamp helices of the β’ subunit of RNAP and LoaP β7, β8, and the loop connecting them (in the LoaP CTD) (Fig. 4C). Notably, the dfn RNA-binding surface of LoaP is mostly distinct from the residues that, by homology, it would use to bind to RNAP (Fig. 4D). Although we cannot rule out that the TEC-bound state may exhibit modest differences from the LoaP-RNA co-crystal structure, it is also possible that the structure of the Bacillus TEC simply differs in this region, offering no steric clash with the LoaP-RNA complex. Alternatively, it is also possible that the LoaP-RNA complex triggers structural changes in the TEC near the apex of the clamp helices, which in turn might lead to structural changes within the RNA exit tunnel as this is where the other end of the helix terminates. When complexed with the TEC, the RfaH NTD directly recognizes the ops hairpin located within the nontemplate DNA. The equivalent region of the LoaP NTD is structurally conserved and is not involved in dfn RNA binding. While the rest of the NTD is nearly identical to E. coli NusG and RfaH, this particular portion of LoaP exhibits moderate structural differences (Fig. S4A); therefore, we speculate that LoaP may, in principle, recognize yet-to-be-identified DNA elements in the nontemplate DNA strand in addition to, or rather than ops. Overall, the LoaP-TEC complex model derived herein is consistent with the conformations of TEC-bound, NusG-like proteins that have been published previously.


## DISCUSSION
  Our work demonstrates that LoaP combines the distinct strategies used by DNA- and RNA-binding proteins to recognize its cognate dfn hairpin RNA. Sequence-specific DNA-binding proteins typically recognize the exposed base edges in the major groove of the B-form duplex. This is because the patterns of nucleobase functional groups in this groove (but not in the minor groove) uniquely specify the sequence of the DNA, and because the wide major groove can accommodate protein structural elements such as α-helices and β-hairpins18,19. Steric blockage by the 2’-OH precludes duplex RNA from adopting the B-form conformation. RNA instead adopts the A-form helix, characterized by a deep major groove whose narrowness precludes direct access by folded polypeptides20. In consequence, proteins that bind double-stranded RNA are usually not sequence-specific, and proteins that recognize specific RNA sequences typically bind to non-duplex nucleotides, such as those in bulges and loops, or to the broadened termini of duplexes21–23. The concurrent recognition of the sequences of the duplex and of the loop of its target using structured polypeptides by LoaP (Fig. 1) is unusual among RNA-binding proteins.

  Previous mutagenesis of the dfn RNA had shown that LoaP binding is abolished both in vitro and in vivo when the two bulged nucleotides of the stem of the dfn hairpin are deleted, but the deleterious effect was less pronounced when their identity was changed, or only one was deleted14. Consistent with this, our structure shows that LoaP recognizes the unique backbone conformation of the bulged U6, and engages in a combination of hydrogen bonding to and stacking on its base (Fig. 2C). The second bulged nucleotide (C9) is not recognized by LoaP, and its sole function appears to facilitate broadening of the major groove (Fig. S2A), which in turn allows the CTD, docked on the RNA backbone near the bulged U6, to access the major groove (Fig. 2D). There, Arg141 and Lys142, extending from the loop connecting β6 and β7, hydrogen bond to the major groove edges of G11 and G17, which are part of canonical Watson-Crick pairs. Our mutagenesis demonstrates that Arg141, which participates in a canonical bidentate interaction with G11, is critical for LoaP binding affinity. Thus, the CTD recognizes a combination of backbone and base features in the helical portion of the dfn RNA hairpin. This mode of RNA binding by a KOW domain is novel, as previous structural studies have shown KOW domains using other faces of their fold to bind to terminal loops (Fig S4B) or nucleotide bulges but not major groove intromission24,25.

  Although UNCG tetraloops are widespread26 in RNAs, the co-crystal structure of LoaP bound to its cognate RNA reveals only the third known structural strategy16 employed by proteins to recognize this universal architectural element of RNA. Previously, the structures of UNCG tetraloops bound to the nucleocapsid (NC) protein of the Rous sarcoma virus27, and of the U2 snRNP constituent SF3A1-UBL have been reported28. The former employs a zinc finger to recognize a guanine base in the second position of a UGCG tetraloop. It approaches from the minor groove side and sandwiches the base between two tyrosine residues. SF3A1-UBL employs three lysines and one phenylalanine from its compact ubiquitin-like fold to recognize the closing U•G pair and the third-position cytosine of a UUCG tetraloop from the major groove side. LoaP recognition is also from the major groove side, but relies on an extended β-hairpin recognizing both the backbone and the third-position C of the UNCG tetraloop. Unlike NC and SF3A1-UBL, LoaP uses a glutamate, and not an aromatic amino acid, to stack on a solvent-exposed base of the tetraloop. Major groove binding by a β-hairpin is reminiscent of sequence-specific DNA binding proteins, such as the E. coli Met repressor29 and the Smad MH1 domain30, but is unprecedented for sequence-specific RNA binding. While the CTD of LoaP also contributes one residue to tetraloop recognition, the extensive use of the NGN-domain β2’-β3’ hairpin in specific recognition of the dfn RNA is unexpected, as previous analyses14 had suggested that the CTD was exclusively responsible for RNA binding by LoaP. The LoaP β2’-β3’ hairpin is conserved across NusG-family proteins (Fig. S4A), even though it is partially disordered in several crystal structures. Thus, this element may undergo RNA-binding induced folding in other NusG family proteins and endow them with the ability to bind sequence-specifically to their yet-to-be-described cognate RNA elements.

  The structure of some NusG family proteins may reorganize when they bind to the TEC. For example, RfaH switches from an autoinhibited state to an active conformation in which its NGN binds to a paused TEC thorough a characteristic nontemplate DNA sequence, and that can associate tightly with ribosomal protein S10 using its CTD11,31–34 (Fig. 4?). Bioinformatics analyses have predicted that structure switching may be a general feature35,36 of many NusG-like proteins, perhaps including LoaP. However, the domain organization of LoaP in complex with the dfn RNA element is more consistent with the active forms of NusG-like proteins (Fig. S4A, C). Since the structure of unbound LoaP is unknown, it remains possible that RNA binding induces or selects the domain organization that we have determined. Regardless, the LoaP structure presented herein appears to be directly relevant to how it may associate with the TEC because its NGN domain superimposes closely with other NusG-like proteins that have been previously structurally analyzed, including but not limited to E. coli NusG, Thermotoga maritima NusG, and RfaH (Fig. 4), and its CTD superimposes closely with the CTD of these proteins. The most functionally relevant context for the LoaP structure is that in the TEC. Several prior studies have reported structures2,37 of either NusG or RfaH bound to TEC. Moreover, recent structures have been determined for E. coli antitermination complexes that control bacteriophage λ gene regulation or that govern expression of ribosomal RNA operons38,39. Our model (Fig 4) shows that the LoaP-dfn RNA complex can occupy the homologous locus on the TEC with no substantial steric clash. Thus, the LoaP conformation present in our co-crystal structure is mostly compatible with LoaP simultaneously binding to RNAP the upstream DNA duplex, and the dfn RNA. Our analysis also suggests that other NusG family proteins may be able to recognize RNA elements sequence-specifically, and do so even while engaged with the TEC. This would represent a hitherto unrecognized mechanism of transcription elongation regulation, which could be widespread, given the universality of NusG proteins.


## METHODS

### LoaP expression and purification
  DNA encoding LoaP from Thermoanaerobacter pseudethanolicus was purchased (IDT) and subcloned in-frame to an IPTG-inducible, N-terminal decahistidine tag that was translationally fused to a SUMO tag. The plasmid (pAmr57) was transformed into E. coli BL21 (T7 Express Competent E. coli; New England BioLabs) for protein overexpression. Site-directed LoaP mutants were constructed using synthetic DNA primers (IDT) and KLD Enzyme Mix (New England Biolabs).

  E. coli were grown in 2xYT at 37 °C to OD600nm = 0.6, induced with 1 mM IPTG, and incubated for 21 additional hours at room temperature. After harvesting, cell pellets were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C. Thawed cells were resuspended in 50 mM KH2PO4 (pH 7.2), 1 M KCl, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM EDTA, and 5% (v/v) glycerol. After addition of 0.5 g/l lysozyme (lyophilized powder, Sigma-Aldrich), the suspension was incubated on ice for 15 minutes. Then, after addition of PMSF to 1 mM, cells were lysed by microfluidization. The lysate was treated with polyethyleneimine (pH 7.2) to a final concentration of 0.5% (v/v), incubated on ice for 15 minutes, and clarified by centrifugation. The supernatant was fractionated with ammonium sulfate (45% w/v). The precipitated protein was resuspended in 50 mM KH2PO4 (pH 7.2), 300 mM KCl, 0.5 mM DTT, 0.1 mM EDTA, 40 mM imidazole, 5% glycerol (v/v) and loaded on a nickel affinity column (HisTrap NP Ni Sepahrose, Cytiva), which was washed and eluted with an imidazole gradient to 400 mM. The eluted proteins were treated with SUMO protease (Winkler laboratory, unpublished) for 30 minutes on ice and dialyzed at 4 °C against 10 mM KH2PO4 (pH 7.2), 100 mM KCl, 1 mM DTT, 0.1 mM EDTA, 5% (v/v) glycerol. LoaP was then purified by cation exchange chromatography (HiTrap SP HP, Cytiva) with a linear gradient to 600 mM KCl. LoaP was concentrated to ~ 6.3 g/L by ultrafiltration (10 kDa MWCO Amicon Ultra; Millipore) and further fractionated by size-exclusion chromatography (HiLoad Superdex 150; Cytiva) in 25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.2, 100 mM KCl, 1 mM DTT, 0.1 mM EDTA, 10% glycerol. Site-directed mutant proteins were purified in the same manner. Purified LoaP proteins were concentrated to 10 g/l, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C.

  For selenomethionyl protein production, transformed E. coli cells (from starter cultures in LB media) were grown in M9 minimal supplemented with 1 mM MgSO4, 10 g/l thiamine, 10 mM CaCl2, 1.0 g/l NH4Cl, and 4.0 g/l glucose at 37 °C until OD600nm of 0.5, whereupon 100 mg/L each of L-threonine, L-lysine, L-phenylalanine, DL-selenomethionine, and 50 mg/L each of L-leucine, L-isoleucine, L-valine were added. After a further 20 minutes at 37 °C, IPTG was added to 1 mM, and then cultures were grown at room temperature for 21 hours. Selenomethionyl LoaP was purified as described above, except that KCl was replaced with NaCl in the storage buffer, and DTT concentration was increased to 5 mM in all buffers. Electrospray ionization mass spectrometry confirmed the masses of purified sulfur and selenomethionyl LoaP proteins (measured, 21007.7 Da and 21242.2 Da, respectively; calculated, 21006.9 Da and 21241.4 Da, respectively.)


### RNA synthesis and purification
  The dfn RNA hairpin [corresponding to that of the LoaP associated regulon of Bacillus velezensis (FZB42)], preceded by a self-cleaving hammerhead ribozyme, was transcribed in vitro from templates prepared by PCR amplification of plasmid pAmr1 as described46. RNA was purified by electrophoresis on 12% polyacrylamide (19:1 acrylamide:bis-acrylamide), 1x TBE, 8 M urea gels, electroeluted from gel slices, washed twice with 1 M NaCl, desalted by ultrafiltration (3 kDa MWCO, Millipore), filtered and stored in Milli-Q water at −20°C. RNA binding experiments were performed synthetic RNA (Dharmacon) that was deprotected following manufacturer’s instructions and employed without further purification.


### Crystallization and structure determination
  RNA was thawed on ice, diluted to 300 μM final concentration in binding buffer containing 10 mM Tris HCl (pH 7.2), 100 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, heated at 92 °C for 3 min and then immediately cooled on ice for > 15 minutes. Selenomethionyl LoaP was diluted in the same buffer, mixed at a 1:1 molar ratio with the RNA (100 μM final concentration) and then incubated at room temperature for 45 min. Co-crystals were grown by the vapor diffusion method at 21 °C. Sitting drops prepared by mixing 0.4 μL each of the LoaP-RNA complex and a reservoir solution containing 0.25 M sodium acetate (pH 5.2), and 12% (w/v) polyethylene glycol (PEG) 3350 were equilibrated against 50 μL of reservoir solution. Rhomboid shaped crystals with maximum dimensions 50 × 100 × 150 μm3 grew in 5–6 days. After adding 2 μL of 30% (v/v) PEG 400 directly to the drops and incubating for 5 minutes at room temperature, crystals were mounted in nylon loops and plunged into liquid nitrogen.

  Diffraction data were collected at 100K at beamline 5.0.1 of the Advanced Light Source (ALS) and integrated and scaled using XDS. Heavy atom sites from SHELXD were refined using AutoSol yielding a mean overall figure of merit of 0.229. Density modification using Resolve resulted in an electron density map (Fig. S1B) in which three copies of protein-RNA complex could be built manually using Coot. Iterative rounds of manual building and simulated annealing, energy minimization, and B-factor refinement in phenix.refine produced the current model (Table S1). All structural figures were prepared with PyMol and are representations of the atomic coordinates of protein chain A and RNA chain D (complex A), unless otherwise noted.


### Equilibrium binding measurements
  RNA-binding experiments were performed using purified LoaP mutants and RNA oligos purchased from Dharmacon encoding for the same sequence of RNA used in the crystallographic studies with an additional GCC overhang at the 5′ end to improve radiolabeling efficiency. RNA oligos were 5′-radiolabeled with [γ-32P] ATP using T4 Polynucleotide Kinase (T4 PNK; New England Biolabs), purified using Zymo RNA Concentrator and Clean-up kit, and stored in Milli-Q H2O at −20°C. To set up the binding reactions, 5′ 32P-labeled RNA was diluted in 10 mM KH2PO4 (pH 7.2), 100 mM KCl, 0.1 mM EDTA at 300 cpm/μL, heated at 92 °C for 3 min and rapidly cooled in an ice bath for a minimum of 15 min. Yeast RNA (Invitrogen) was added to the 5′ 32P-labeled RNA solution at 25 μg/mL final concentration then the mixture was titrated into 96-well plates containing an increasing concentration gradient of LoaP proteins (0.1 nM – 50 μM) in 10 mM KH2PO4 (pH 7.2), 100 mM KCl, 0.1 mM EDTA. Binding reactions were incubated at room temperature for 60 min. 2 μL aliquots from each binding reaction were spotted onto dry Amersham Protran 0.45 NC Nitrocellulose membrane (Cytiva) using a multi-blot fixed replicator (V&P Scientific, Inc.). After adsorption, membranes were allowed to dry in air for 20 min and then exposed to an Amersham phosphor imaging screen for 16 h. Phosphor screens were imaged on an Amersham Typhoon Imager at 100 μm/pixel. Data were quantified and analyzed using ImageJ Software and Graphpad Prism Software.
