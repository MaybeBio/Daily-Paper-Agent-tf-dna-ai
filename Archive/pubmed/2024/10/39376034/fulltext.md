# MultiSC: a deep learning pipeline for analyzing multiomics single-cell data


## Abstract
  Single-cell technologies enable researchers to investigate cell functions at an individual cell level and study cellular processes with higher resolution. Several multi-omics single-cell sequencing techniques have been developed to explore various aspects of cellular behavior. Using NEAT-seq as an example, this method simultaneously obtains three kinds of omics data for each cell: gene expression, chromatin accessibility, and protein expression of transcription factors (TFs). Consequently, NEAT-seq offers a more comprehensive understanding of cellular activities in multiple modalities. However, there is a lack of tools available for effectively integrating the three types of omics data. To address this gap, we propose a novel pipeline called MultiSC for the analysis of MULTIomic Single-Cell data. Our pipeline leverages a multimodal constraint autoencoder (single-cell hierarchical constraint autoencoder) to integrate the multi-omics data during the clustering process and a matrix factorization–based model (scMF) to predict target genes regulated by a TF. Moreover, we utilize multivariate linear regression models to predict gene regulatory networks from the multi-omics data. Additional functionalities, including differential expression, mediation analysis, and causal inference, are also incorporated into the MultiSC pipeline. Extensive experiments were conducted to evaluate the performance of MultiSC. The results demonstrate that our pipeline enables researchers to gain a comprehensive view of cell activities and gene regulatory networks by fully leveraging the potential of multiomics single-cell data. By employing MultiSC, researchers can effectively integrate and analyze diverse omics data types, enhancing their understanding of cellular processes.


## Introduction
  Single-cell technologies offer a unique opportunity to profile complex biological systems at a high resolution. Building upon the foundation of traditional single-cell messenger RNA (mRNA) sequencing technology, numerous advancements have emerged to measure cellular status and variations across multiple modalities. These modalities include protein expression, DNA copy number, DNA methylation, DNA mutation, and chromatin accessibility [1]. By incorporating these diverse measurements, researchers can obtain a profound and comprehensive understanding of biological systems.

  Among the diverse modalities, the protein abundance, which is directly related to the cellular functions and fates, can be quantified by counting the antibody-derived tags (ADTs) [2]. The data of protein abundance are strong evidence for dividing various cell types in a single-cell dataset. On the other hand, the level of chromatin accessibility can be measured by the assay for the transposase-accessible chromatin sequencing at the single cell level (scATAC-seq) [3]. This technology uses the hyperactive Tn5 transposase, which simultaneously tags and fragments DNA sequences in open chromatin regions [4]. The data obtained through scATAC-seq presents an invaluable opportunity to delve into gene regulatory networks at the single-cell level.

  By analyzing the accessibility of chromatin regions, scATAC-seq enables us to uncover the intricate interactions and regulatory relationships among genes within an individual cell. This approach provides a deeper understanding of the mechanisms governing gene expression and cellular behavior, thereby advancing our knowledge of complex biological processes.

  More recently, multi-omics single-cell methods have been developed to jointly profile multiple modalities within the same cell [1]. To name a few, SNARE-seq and 10× Single-Cell Multiome assay for transposase-accessible chromatin (ATAC) + Gene Expression are designed to measure the chromatin accessibility and gene expression simultaneously [5, 6], cellular indexing of transcriptomes and epitopes (CITE-seq) enables to jointly profile the mRNA expression and surface protein levels [2], ASAP-seq characterizes epigenomic change and protein expression [7], and scMT-seq measures the mRNA expression and DNA methylation [8]. These paired measurements enable us to explore both the cell properties and the cell-to-cell interactions in a multidimensional view. NEAT-seq is a cutting-edge technology that allows the simultaneous profiling of three omics data at the cellular level: gene expression, chromatin accessibility, and intranuclear protein expression. This powerful approach offers a more comprehensive view of regulatory pathways, bridging the gap between transcription factor abundance and target gene expression by leveraging the strength of chromatin accessibility. By integrating these multiple layers of information, NEAT-seq enables researchers to gain deeper insights into the intricate mechanisms underlying gene regulation and regulatory networks within cells.

  Many computational technologies have been developed to analyze data from multi-omics single-cell technologies. To name a few, TotalVI is designed to learn a joint latent representation from mRNA and ADT expression data by employing a conditional autoencoder model [9]. CiteFuse and BREM-SC are developed for clustering CITE-seq data by integrating the cell-to-cell similarities from two sources and fitting the data into two Dirichlet distributions, respectively [10, 11]. Seurat V3 is also developed to cluster CITE-seq data. It builds a weighted nearest neighbor graph by leveraging the neighbor information from different sources [12]. Cobolt is developed to learn a joint latent representation of single-cell mRNA and ATAC data by employing a variational autoencoder model [13]. Multi-Omics Factor Analysis (MOFA) integrates multiple modalities using factor analysis, and the learned factors can represent the variation across modalities [14]. However, to the best of our knowledge, there is currently no existing method or pipeline that effectively integrates the information from all three omics data types and enables comprehensive downstream analyses, specifically tailored for data generated by technologies like NEAT-seq. This lack of integration limits our ability to fully explore the wealth of information provided by these multi-omic datasets. As a result, there is a pressing need to develop a novel approach that seamlessly combines the three omics data and offers a comprehensive suite of downstream analyses, empowering researchers to unlock the full potential of NEAT-seq and similar multi-omic technologies.

  In this study, we propose a pipeline, MultiSC, for analyzing the multi-omics single-cell data. MultiSC has three parts, a single-cell hierarchical constraint autoencoder (scHCAE) for clustering cells, a matrix factorization–based model (scMF) for predicting gene regulatory networks, and several multivariate linear regression (MLR) analyses to explore the regulatory relationship between TFs and target genes. Besides these main functions, MultiSC can also implement differential analysis, mediation analysis, and causal inference analysis for the multi-omics data. Our research findings demonstrate that MultiSC is a valuable tool for harnessing the hidden information within NEAT-seq and similar single-cell multi-omic datasets. By effectively integrating and analyzing the three omics data types, MultiSC enables researchers to uncover valuable insights that were previously inaccessible. We conducted comprehensive experiments including benchmarking, sensitivity tests, ablation study, and case study on real multiomics single-cell datasets. These results establish MultiSC as a promising and essential resource for studying and exploring the complexities of single-cell multi-omic data.

### Competing methods
  Seurat (https://github.com/satijalab/seurat) [23], Cobolt, and MOFA are used as competing methods for clustering analysis. Seurat receives the raw count of each data source as inputs. We tune the resolution parameter in the function “FindClusters” until it estimates a similar number of clusters with that in the true labels. Preprocessed count matrices for RNA, ATAC, and ADT are fed into Cobolt and MOFA models to generate embeddings and predictions. To get the desired number of clusters, we adjust the resolution in the clustering function of Cobolt and set the number of clusters k in the cluster_sample function of MOFA.


#### scMF for predicting transcription factor to gene regulatory network
  The second model is designed to predict the TF to the gene regulatory network by using matrix factorization (Fig. 2). Specifically, the gene-to-cell matrix (Y) can be factorized into two matrices, an ADT-to-cell matrix (B) and a gene-to-ADT matrix (W):

  Since Y and B are profiled in NEAT-seq, the aim of this model is to learn W, which can be considered as the learnable weights. We initialized the weights by overlapping the predicted binding sites of the TFs and the peak regions in scATAC-seq from NEAT-seq data. If there is a detected peak in the binding site of a TF and the peak is in a gene region (−20 000 to +5000 bp), we consider this gene as the candidate target gene of the TF (see details in the Supplementary Note 1). The gene-to-ADT matrix (W) is learned by minimizing the distance between the factorized ADT-to-cell matrix and the profiled ADT-to-cell matrix in NEAT-seq. For training in batches, we add a layer of batch normalization before calculating the loss function. It is noted that the batch normalization layer is not learnable to keep the interpretability of the weights (gene-to-ADT matrix).


#### Finding target genes of transcription factors in a specific cell type
  After doing the clustering analysis, we can select a subset of cells in a specific cell type (cluster). Since the clustering process is guided by the ADT expression, we can find one or more clusters that are enriched with the ADT(s) and then compare them to other clusters. It is noted that most ADTs measured in the NEAT-seq data are TFs. After selecting two clusters of cells, we do the differential expression (DE) analysis between them for both mRNA and ATAC data by using the one-sided Wilcoxon test. Genes that are highly expressed in both mRNA and ATAC data from the TF-enriched group are considered the confident target genes for that TF.

## Results

### TF-guided cell clustering analysis
  We first cluster the NEAT-seq data by scHCAE (Fig. 1). For exploring the regulatory mechanism of the TFs, scHCAE allows the users to detect the TF-regulated cells during the clustering process by applying the constraints. Besides, by employing a multimodal autoencoder, scHCAE integrates the information from mRNA and ATAC for a more comprehensive clustering. Using GATA3 as an example, we use the GATA3 ADT to build the constraints in scHCAE. Specifically, we put the cells with the same expression levels of GATA3 ADT (the top and bottom 25%) to be in the same cluster and pull the cells with different levels of GATA3 ADT to be in the different clusters. Figure 3a shows the clustering results of scHCAE and the expression of GATA3 ADT and mRNA. We group the expression level of ADT into three levels: high, medium, and low. Since the mRNA data are zero-inflated, we only group it into two levels: high and medium. In the clustering results of scHCAE, we detect two clusters, Clusters 0 and 5, mostly composed of the GATA3 (ADT)-enriched and -absent cells, respectively (Fig. 3a middle and Fig. 3c bottom). The expression of GATA3 mRNA shows similar patterns (Fig. 3a right and Fig. 3c top). The difference between the expression of mRNA and ADT reveals the post-transcriptional and post-translational effects [26, 27]. The comparison, such as the DE analysis, of these two clusters will be very helpful in exploring the regulatory functions of GATA3 (see next section). To demonstrate the advantages of scHCAE, we also conduct the clustering analysis by using Seurat on the mRNA and ATAC data, respectively (Fig. 3d). The numbers of cells in the clusters from Seurat are in a similar pattern to that of scHCAE (Fig. 3b). Both methods detect a few large clusters and some small clusters. However, scHCAE can better separate different clusters by integrating the mRNA and ATAC data (Fig. 3a and d). Besides, all the GATA3 (ADT and mRNA)-enriched and -absent cells are mixed in different clusters from Seurat (Fig. 3d). Based on these results, we cannot explore the regulatory mechanisms of the GATA3-mediated cells. In summary, these results demonstrate that scHCAE can take the best advantage of NEAT-seq data in the clustering analysis. The same analyses are conducted using the NEAT-seq data in the second lane, and the results and figures are in Supplementary Note 7 (Fig. S3–S7).

  We use Hopkins statistics [28] and Silhouette coefficients [29] to evaluate the results from scHCAE and other competing methods. scHCAE outperforms other methods in both metrics across two real datasets. The Hopkins statistic is used to determine the clustering tendency of a dataset, measuring whether the data points are uniformly distributed or exhibit significant clustering. As shown in Fig. 4a, the higher Hopkins statistics of scHCAE compared to the competing methods indicate a stronger clustering tendency and better performance in capturing the inherent structure of the data. The Silhouette coefficient measures the quality of clustering by assessing how similar each data point is to its own cluster compared to other clusters. A higher Silhouette coefficient indicates better-defined and more separated clusters (Fig. 4b). The consistent outperformance of scHCAE in both Hopkins statistics and Silhouette coefficients across these datasets highlights its effectiveness and reliability in multi-omics data integration and clustering. We also conduct a detailed case study using a real dataset from TEA-seq (Fig. S8 and S9), a sensitivity test for parameter selections (Fig. S10), and an ablation test (Table S6 and S7, Fig. S11 and S12) comparing the original model to the model without ADT constraints (see Supplementary Notes 10–12).


#### Target gene prediction
  We develop a matrix factorization–based model (scMF) to predict the target genes of TFs by using the multimodal data from NEAT-seq. The trainable TF-to-gene matrix is initialized using the predicted target genes of the five TFs (measured by NEAT-seq) from ATAC-seq data (also measured in NEAT-seq). Figure 5 summarizes the results of scMF. Compared to the initial target genes, scMF detects 1000–2000 new target genes for the five TFs (Fig. 5b). On the other hand, scMF also rejects 1000–2000 initialized target genes from ATAC-seq (Fig. 5a). To test the accuracy of the prediction, we show the regulatory weights learned from scMF of the five TFs to six marker genes of Treg cells (Fig. 5c). We find that the marker genes are highly regulated by FOXP3, which is a well-known marker of Treg cells [30]. In addition, we downloaded the proved target genes of GATA3 and FOXP3 in the CHEA Transcription Factor Targets dataset [31] and compared them with the detected targets from scMF. We find that more than two-thirds of proved target genes are covered by the results of scMF (Fig. 5e). It is noted that the proved target genes that are not in the input cell-to-genes matrix of scMF are not considered in this calculation. Figure 3f shows the common and unique target genes from GATA3, FOXP3, and Tbet. Only the new detected targets are shown here. We find that GATA3 and Tbet have many common target genes. This is supported by many previous studies. For example, Kanhere et al. demonstrated that T-bet and GATA3 bind to multiple distal sites at immune regulatory genes in T cells [32]. Besides, GATA3 occupies T-bet binding sites at Th1 genes, and T-bet induces GATA3 binding at these sites. Figure 3d shows the top 20 target genes of GATA3 (rows) and the regulations from other TFs (columns). Many hub genes are listed here. For example, CDK1 plays a key role in the control of the cell cycle [33]; SIX4 is also a TF that is involved in cell differentiation, cell migration, and cell survival [34]; POLR2A, which encodes DNA-dependent RNA polymerase, catalyzes the transcription of DNA into RNA [35]. These results demonstrate that scMF can achieve a superior performance on target gene prediction by integrating the mRNA, ATAC, and ADT data.


#### Transcription factor-based differential expression
  We then perform the DE analysis from the clustering results above (Fig. 6). Our DE analyses are conducted on both mRNA and ATAC data. We consider the genes that are significantly upregulated in the GATA3-enriched cells from both mRNA and ATAC data as the confident target genes of GATA3 (Fig. 6a). Figure 5c shows the DE results of mRNA (left) and ATAC (right). We find that the gene GATA3 has a high expression in the GATA3 ADT–enriched cells, which is consistent with our expectation. Some other genes, such as CD44, with high expression in GATA3-enriched cells, have been proven in previous studies (Fig. 6b left and Fig. 6c left) [36]. On the other hand, in the DE results of ATAC data, we also find many important DE genes. For example, ETS1, another TF, is upregulated in the GATA3-enriched cells, which has been shown to be associated with GATA3 in many previous studies (Fig. 6b right and Fig. 6c right) [37, 38]. Using the 311 confident target genes of GATA3, we perform enrichment analysis on the Reactome gene set. We find two significant pathways of RHO GTPase that are demonstrated as a key regulator of T lymphocytes. See Supplementary Table S1 and Note 2 for the confident target cells of GATA3.


#### Multivariate linear regression of transcription factor and ATAC
  We then perform an MLR to investigate the target gene for each TF (Fig. 7). Our MLR model uses the ADT of a TF (ADT), the ATAC of a gene (ATAC), and their interaction (ADT * ATAC) as the independent variable and the expression of that gene (mRNA) as the response variable (Fig. 7a). Our assumption is that a gene can be considered as a target of a TF if and only if ADT * ATAC is significant in the MLR model since TFs work on the target genes through the bindings on the chromatin/DNA. Using GATA3 as the example, Fig. 7b shows the beta coefficients of the top 20 significant target genes (Pval of ADT * ATAC <0.05). We denote the significant genes as the confident target genes. We also calculate the potential target gene by building the simpler regression model: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $mRNA\sim ATAC$\end{document}. We denote the significant gene from this model as the potential target genes since their chromatin accessibility is associated with their gene expression. Then we calculate the ratio of the number of confident target genes over the number of potential target genes for each TF (Fig. 7c). We find that only one-third to one-half of the potential target genes are the confident target genes for a given TF. The rest of the accessible chromatin regions are probably bound by other TFs. Figure 7d shows the GSEA of the Reactome gene set from the confident target genes of GATA3. We find that two ACYL chain remodeling pathways are downregulated in the GATA3-enriched cells. This is supported by a current study in which GATA3 downregulated the promoter activity of elovl5 in a large yellow croaker [36]. In addition, we extract the peaks in the region of gene NDUFB3 (−20 000 to +5000) and use these peaks as the ATAC part to run the MLR analysis again. NDUFB3 encodes an accessory subunit of the mitochondrial membrane respiratory chain [37]. We select this gene since it has the highest beta coefficient in the MLR analysis (Fig. 7b). We find that only one out of four peaks have a significant contribution to the expression of NDUFB3 (Fig. 7e). Thus, this peak performs a core function in connecting the binding of TF (GATA3) and the expression of target gene (NDUFB3).


#### Multivariate linear regression of two transcription factors
  The last MLR model is to investigate the coregulation of two TFs (Fig. 8). Therefore, the independent variables in this model are two TFs (ADT1 + ADT2) and their interaction (ADT1 * ADT2), and the response variable is the expression of a gene (Fig. 8a). For a more profound analysis, ATAC can also be added to this model. We only consider the gene that has a significant interaction of two TFs (ADT1 * ADT2) as the cotarget gene of the two TFs. Figure 8b shows the correlation between GATA3 ADT and FOXP3 ADT. We find a strong correlation between these two TFs. This indicates that they may be involved in some common regulatory pathways [38]. Figure 8c shows the top 20 significant cotarget genes of FOXP3 and GATA3. In this gene list, the gene with the highest estimate is LYN, which is an src family nonreceptor tyrosine-protein kinase that plays an important role in the regulation of innate and adaptive immune responses. The interactions between GATA3, FOXP3, and LYN have been demonstrated in some previous studies [39, 40]. Figure 8d shows the GSEA of the Reactome gene set from the cotarget genes of GATA3 and FOXP3. We find two pathways about eukaryotic translation (initiation and elongation). The relationship between GATA3 and eukaryotic translation has been demonstrated in many previous studies [41–43].


#### Mediation analysis and causal inference
  MultiSC can also implement two types of mediation analyses to detect (i) the mediating effect of chromatin accessibility (ATAC) between TF and target genes and (ii) the mediating effect from a TF to the target genes of another TF. See the demonstration of the results in Supplementary Notes 3 and 4. Briefly, we find that ATAC has weak mediating effects between TFs and target genes (Table S3). This is caused by the weak dependency from ATAC to ADT. In addition, we transplant a causal inference method [44] to MultiSC for detecting the cause between two groups of variables. We adjust this method for NEAT-seq data (Fig. S1); therefore, the aim of it becomes finding the cause of the regulation of target genes between a group of TFs and the chromatin accessibility (ATAC) data of them. The results are provided in Supplementary Note 5. Briefly, we found that TFs are always the triggers of the regulation of target genes (Table S5).


## Discussion
  In this study, we introduce MultiSC, a pipeline designed for the comprehensive analysis of multi-omic single-cell datasets, with a specific focus on NEAT-seq data. MultiSC encompasses four key analyses to extract valuable insights from the data. The first analysis in MultiSC is clustering, where we propose a novel method called scHCAE; the second analysis involves predicting the target genes of TFs using the scMF (matrix factorization) model; the third analysis employs an MLR model to explore target genes regulated by a specific TF; and the fourth analysis also utilizes an MLR model, but it focuses on investigating cotarget genes of two TFs. It is important to note that TF-based DE and MLR of TFs and ATAC analyses can be performed on the entire cell population or on specific cell types, such as clusters identified by scHCAE in the first analysis. Furthermore, MultiSC allows for additional downstream analyses beyond the four presented in this study. For instance, trajectory analysis of TF-enriched cells and motif analysis of TFs and target genes can provide further insights. Users can choose state-of-the-art methods for these downstream analyses based on the results generated by the MultiSC pipeline.

  Based on our proposed results, we anticipate that MultiSC will be widely embraced in the field of multimodal single-cell analyses. Its versatility and powerful analytical capabilities make it an indispensable tool for researchers aiming to harness the full potential of multi-omics single-cell datasets. We evaluate MultiSC against several competing methods, including MOFA, Cobolt, and Seurat, which are established tools for multi-omics data integration and analysis. Our evaluations demonstrate that scHCAE outperforms these methods in terms of both clustering accuracy and consistency, as evidenced by higher Hopkins statistics and Silhouette scores across two real datasets. Sensitivity and ablation studies are also conducted, demonstrating the robustness of the performance of our model. However, it is important to acknowledge certain limitations of MultiSC that should be considered in future research endeavors. One limitation is that MultiSC treats the gene expression data in some special technologies (such as NEAT-seq) as regular scRNA-seq data. However, it is worth noting that the gene expression measured by NEAT-seq represents only intranuclear mRNA. While previous analyses have shown that intranuclear mRNA in PBMC can serve as a representative of the overall cellular mRNA, this may not hold true for all tissues. Further investigation is necessary to determine the generalizability of this assumption. Moreover, for analyses requiring raw feature dimensions, such as scMF and MLR, MultiSC does not currently support the integration of different batches. Aligning data from different batches while preserving the scales across different omics poses a challenge. Addressing this issue will be a focus of our future studies. Additionally, in scHCAE, MLR, mediation, and causal inference analyses, MultiSC utilizes gene-level ATAC data as input, potentially resulting in the loss of peak-level information. Overcoming this limitation and incorporating peak-level information will be a priority for our future research. Besides, our current models are only designed for single-batch data and cannot be utilized for multibatch data analyses. A potential way is to add a conditional autoencoder (CAE) module in the network to integrate the data from different batches. Our previous work has demonstrated that CAE can successfully remove batch effects on multibatch multi-omics single-cell data [45]. By acknowledging these potential limitations, we aim to enhance and refine MultiSC in future studies. Our goal is to address these challenges and develop solutions that enable the seamless integration and analysis of multiomics single-cell data, thereby further advancing the field of single-cell multiomics research.


## Conclusion
  We have presented MultiSC, a comprehensive pipeline for analyzing multi-omics single-cell data. MultiSC integrates three key components: a single-cell hierarchical constraint autoencoder (scHCAE) for cell clustering, a matrix factorization model (scMF) for predicting gene regulatory networks, and multivariate linear regression (MLR) analyses to investigate regulatory relationships between transcription factors and target genes. Beyond these core functionalities, MultiSC also supports differential analysis, mediation analysis, and causal inference for multi-omics data. Our extensive experiments, including benchmarking, sensitivity tests, ablation studies, and real-world case studies, demonstrate the robustness and utility of MultiSC in extracting valuable insights from NEAT-seq and similar single-cell multi-omics datasets. These results establish MultiSC as a crucial tool for advancing the study of single-cell multi-omic data, offering researchers new opportunities to explore and understand complex biological systems.
