# ZBP1 as a prognostic biomarker correlated with cell proliferation in clear cell renal cell carcinoma


## Background
  Z-DNA-binding protein 1 (ZBP1) is a critical Z-DNA- and Z-RNA-binding protein. However, its diagnostic and prognostic significance, as well as its functions in clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), are not well understood.


## Materials and methods
  Paired and unpaired differential expression analyses of ZBP1 were performed in pan-cancer cells. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and survival analyses were used to evaluate the clinical significance of ZBP1. Functional enrichment and immune infiltration analyses were used to explore the mechanisms underlying ZBP1 expression. Western blotting, qRT-PCR, CCK-8, and colony formation assays were used to investigate the potential function of ZBP1 in ccRCC cells. Molecular docking was utilized to identify drugs targeting ZBP1.


## Results
  ZBP1 was highly expressed in ccRCC, demonstrating significant diagnostic and prognostic value. ZBP1 mRNA expression correlated with TNM stage, pathologic stage, and histologic grade. Functional enrichment analyses indicated that ZBP1 is involved in multiple immune processes. Moreover, ZBP1 mRNA expression was positively correlated with the infiltrating levels of T cells and cytotoxic cells, and negatively correlated with the infiltrating levels of mast cells and Th17 cells in ccRCC. Biological experiments confirmed that ZBP1 promotes ccRCC cell proliferation. Molecular docking studies identified rucaparib as a potential drug targeting ZBP1.


## Conclusion
  Our research findings suggest that targeting ZBP1 could represent a novel therapeutic approach to inhibit the progression of ccRCC.

## Introduction
  The incidence rate of renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is the third highest among urinary system tumors worldwide, and the mortality rate is the highest among urinary system tumors [1]. With advancements in diagnostic technology and improvements in healthcare practices, RCC is increasingly being diagnosed at an early stage in many countries, which may contribute to a stabilization of the mortality rate for RCC [2,3]. Surgery is the gold standard for treating localized RCC; however, one-third of patients still experience metastasis and/or may experience recurrence after surgery [4]. Combination therapies involving tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are recommended as first-line therapies for advanced RCC [5]. However, many patients do not respond to these targeted therapies and subsequently experience disease progression [6]. Therefore, it is necessary to identify new therapeutic targets for progressive RCC [7].

  Z-DNA-binding protein 1 (ZBP1) functions as a double-stranded DNA and RNA helical sensor with an unusual left-handed Z structure [8,9]. Recent researches have demonstrated that ZBP1 is a key upstream regulatory factor in cell death and pro-inflammatory signaling [10,11]. Upon activation by Z-DNA/RNA, ZBP1 induces apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis, which are three regulated forms of cell death and activate inflammatory responses [11,12]. Necrosis is believed to limit tumor growth and enhance adaptive immune responses by releasing danger signals and new antigens [13,14]. Therefore, ZBP1 is involved in a range of diseases, ranging from infections and inflammation to cancer, making it a potenial target of ongoing research [15,16].

  In this study, we performed a pan-cancer analysis to investigate the correlation between ZBP1 expression and clinical features in patients with clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC). We also explored the potential molecular mechanisms and the relationship between ZBP1 and tumor-infiltrating immune cells. Additionally, experiments in vivo and in vitro validated the results of the bioinformatic analysis. Collectively, our findings suggest a significant role for ZBP1 in ccRCC.


## Material and methods

### Data sets sources
  RNA-sequencing expression profiles (level 3) and associated pan-cancer clinical information were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov). Additional normal tissue data were downloaded from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) database (https://commonfund.nih.gov/gtex).


### Survival analysis
  The proportional hazards assumption and fitting survival regression were assessed using the survival package. The survminer and ggplot2 packages were used to visualize the results. The optimal cut-off value was determined using the surv_cutpoint function from the survminer package, and the best grouping method was selected [17].


### Differential gene expression analysis
  Patients with ccRCC were divided into two groups based on ZBP1 expression levels (high or low using the median value as the cutoff). Differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis was conducted using the DESeq2 software package to compare the two groups [18]. A |log2-fold-change (FC)| > 1 and an adjusted p value < 0.05 were set as the thresholds for identifying DEGs. The results were visualized using the ggplot2 package.


### Functional enrichment analysis
  Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and gene set enrichment analyses (GSEA) were conducted using the clusterProfiler package. The results of enrichment analysis were visualized using the ggplot2 and igraph packages. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis was conducted using the STRING database.


### Immune infiltration analysis
  According to the ssGSEA algorithm provided in the R package “GSVA” [1.46.0]. [19], the immune cell markers specified in the study were used to calculate immune cell infiltration in the corresponding datasets [20]. The ggplot2 package was used to visualize the results. The correlation between the expression of the indicated genes and immune cells was assessed using Spearman's correlation analysis.


### Tissue samples
  ccRCC and normal tissue samples were collected at the Department of Urology, Shanghai Pudong Hospital, Fudan University Pudong Medical Center, Shanghai, China. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Shanghai Pudong Hospital Ethics Committee. All samples were stored in a −80 °C refrigerator.


### Immunohistochemistry of human tissues or transplanted tumor in vivo
  Immunohistochemistry was performed using an anti-ZBP1 antibody (1:100, 13285-1-AP, Proteintech, China) and Ki-67 antibody (1:500, Abcam, ab92742) as previously described [21]. Staining intensity was scored as 0 (negative), 1 (weak), 2 (moderate), and 3 (strong). Scores representing the proportion of positively stained tumor cells were graded as 0 (0–5 %), 1 (6–25 %), 2 (26–50 %), 3 (51–75 %), and 4 (>75 %). The IHC-score was calculated as the product of staining intensity × the percentage of positive tumor cells. The results of immunohistochemistry were reviewed by two pathologists.


### Cell culture and siRNA transfection
  The human ccRCC cell lines A498, ACHN, 769-P, and 786-O, along with the human normal renal tubular epithelial cell line HK-2 were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Briefly, 786-O and 769-P cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, New York, NY, USA). A498, ACHN, and HK-2 cells were cultured in MEM (NEAA) medium (Gibco). All culture media were supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin (P/S; Gibco). Cells were cultured in standard cell culture incubators at 37 °C with 5 % CO₂. For transient knockdown of ccRCC cells, small interfering RNA (siRNA, IBSBIO, Shanghai, China) was transfected into RCC cells using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) at a final concentration of 50 nM. The cells were harvested 24 or 48 h after transfection.


### RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)
  Total RNA was extracted by TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA), and 1 μg total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (TaKaRa, Japan). qRT-PCR was performed using TB Green Fast qPCR Mix (TaKaRa, Japan). mRNA levels were normalized to those of GAPDH. Data were analyzed using 2−ΔΔCT method. The primer sequences used were as follows: GAPDH forward primer, GTCTCCTCTGACTTCAACAGCG; GAPDH reverse primer, ACCACCCTGTTGCTGTAGCCAA; ZBP1 forward primer, GCAAACTCCGAAGCCATCCAGA; ZBP1 reverse primer, CCAAGTTGAGGAATCACCTGGTG.


### Western blot (WB)
  Total protein was extracted using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer, and 30 μg of total protein was loaded onto a polyacrylamide gel for electrophoresis. After transferring the proteins to nitrocellulose filter membranes and blocking nonspecific binding sites, the membrane was incubated overnight with anti-ZBP1 (1:1000, 13285-1-AP, Proteintech, China) and anti-GAPDH (1:1000, ab8245, Abcam, UK) antibodies at 4 °C, followed by incubation with a secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, the membrane was washed with Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST) and developed using ECL reagent.


### CCK-8 and colony formation assays
  Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; YEASEN, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Birefly, cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 800 cells/well and cultured for the indicated time periods. After the designated incubation period, CCK-8 solution was added to wells and incubated for 2 h. Optical density (OD) value was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. In the colony formation assays, cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 800 cells/well. Then the cells were cultured for 12 d, after which colonies were fixed and stained with 0.1 % crystal violet.


### Cell line-derived xenograft (CDX)
  shRNA based on siRNA (si-ZBP1-3, Sense 5′–GCAAAGUCAGCCUCAAUUAUU–3, Anti-sense 5′–UAAUUGAGGCUGACUUUGCUC–3′) sequence was packaged into lentivirus. ACHN cells were infected with lentivirus followed by puromycin (1 μg/ml) selection. Nude BALB/c mice were purchased from GemPharmatech LLC. (Nanjing, China). Briefly, 4-week-old mice were subcutaneously injected with ZBP1 knockdown or control ACHN cells. Tumor volumes were measured weekly, and the mice were euthanized after 5 weeks. This animal study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Shanghai Pudong Hospital.


### Molecular docking
  The ligands and proteins required for molecular docking were prepared by AutoDock Vina software (http://vina.scripps.edu/). The crystal structure of the target protein was preprocessed; this included removing hydrogen atoms, modifying amino acids, optimizing energy, and adjusting the force field parameters to achieve the low-energy conformation of the ligand structure. Then the target structure was molecularly docked with the active ingredient structure using the Vina module in PyRx software. The affinity (kcal/mol) value represents the binding ability between the ligand and protein. PyMOL software and Discovery Studio 2020 Client were used for visual analysis.


### Statistical analysis
  Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and R (version 4.2.1; R Core Team, http://www.r-project.org/). The statistical significance of ZBP1 expression in non-paired or paired samples was analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Survival analyses were performed using the Kaplan-Meier method. Other statistical analyses were performed using Student's t-test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.


## Results

### ZBP1 expression is significantly elevated in ccRCC
  To characterize ZBP1 expression in cancer, ZBP1 mRNA expression was analyzed across 33 cancer types using TCGA and GTEx databases. The results showed that ZBP1 mRNA expression was significantly higher in 18 types of cancer tissues (compared to unpaired normal tissues). Notably, elevated ZBP1 levels were observed in breast cancer (BRCA), colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), and kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC) (Fig. 1A). Conversely, ZBP1 expression was lower in nine types of cancer tissues (compared to unpaired normal tissues). Further comparison of ZBP1 expression in cancer tissues and paired normal tissues from TCGA database revealed that ZBP1 was upregulated in only eight cancers (compared to paired normal tissues). These cancers included BRCA, cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL), esophageal carcinoma (ESCA), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), KIRC, kidney papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), and stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD) (Fig. 1B). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were then obtained for these eight cancers, with KIRC showing the highest area under the ROC curve (AUC) (AUC = 0.930, CI: 0.902–0.957), suggesting that ZBP1 may serve as a biomarker for distinguishing ccRCC tissues from normal tissues (Fig. 1C–J). ### ZBP1 expression as a potential prognostic biomarker in ccRCC
  Survival analysis was used to evaluate the correlation between ZBP1 expression and the survival of patients with ccRCC. The results indicated that high expression was associated with poor OS, DSS, and PFI (Fig. 3A–C). Further subgroup analysis showed that high ZBP1 expression was associated with poor OS in various subgroups, including patients with T3 and T4 stages, M1 stage, stage III and IV, grade 3 and grade 4, and those older than 60 years (Fig. 3D–H). Nevertheless, ZBP1 expression was not associated with OS in N1 patients (Fig. 3I). A time-dependent ROC curve was plotted, and the results revealed that the AUC values for the specified time points in patients with ccRCC based on ZBP1 expression were greater than 0.5 (Fig. 3J). A nomogram containing pathologic T stage, pathologic stage, histologic grade, and ZBP1 expression levels as parameters was generated. This nomogram exhibited clinical value in predicting the 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year survival probabilities of patients with ccRCC (Fig. 3K). ### ZBP1 expression is associated with immune infiltration
  Functional analyses indicated that ZBP1 expression levels were associated with immune infiltration. Therefore, we investigated the relation between ZBP1 levels and tumor infiltration of different immune cells in ccRCC (Fig. 5A). The results revealed that ZBP1 expression was positively correlated with the infiltrating levels of T cells (R = 0.704, p < 0.001) and cytotoxic cells (R = 0.613, p < 0.001) (Fig. 5B and C). In contrast, ZBP1 expression was negatively correlated with the infiltration levels of mast cells (R = −0.166, p < 0.001) and Th17 cells (R = −0.264, p < 0.001) (Fig. 5D and E). Interestingly, ZBP1 expression positively correlated with multiple immune checkpoint genes, including LAG3, TIGIT, CTLA-4, HAVCR2, SIGLEC15, CD274, PDCD1, and PDCD1LG2 (Fig. 5F).


### Inhibition of ZBP1 impedes the proliferation of ccRCC cells

Immunohistochemistry and western blotting showed that the expression of ZBP1 in ccRCC tissues was higher than that in normal tissues (Fig. 6A and B, Figure S1). We also tested ZBP1 expression in ccRCC cell lines, and the results indicated that ZBP1 expression was elevated in ccRCC cell lines compared to that in normal control (HK-2) (Fig. 6C and Fig. S2). To investigate the function of ZBP1, we utilized three siRNAs to deplete ZBP1, with si-ZBP1-3, which exhibited the highest knockdown efficiency, being used in subsequent experiments (Fig. 6D–F, Fig. S3). CCK-8 and colony formation assays demonstrated that ZBP1 inhibited the proliferation of ccRCC cells (Fig. 6G–I). Furthermore, a tumor-forming experiment in nude mice was conducted to evaluate the growth of ACHN cells in vivo (Fig. 6J). As anticipated, ZBP1 knockdown in ACHN cells inhibited tumor growth in vivo, as evidenced by tumor volume and weight (Fig. 6K and L). ### Screening of small molecules targeting ZBP1
  Currently, no clinically viable small-molecule inhibitors specific for ZBP1 are available. The Connectivity Map (CMap), a database with the largest perturbation-driven gene expression dataset, was used to screen the drugs. We put upregulated genes that from DEG analysis in CMap and the top 50 valid genes, including ZBP1, made up the expression profile in the compound screen. A heatmap of the candidate compounds is shown in Fig. 7A. Based on the relevance score, we listed the top 10 small molecules that were negatively correlated with the expression profiles of these 50 genes (Fig. 7B). The closer the value is to −1, the more likely the compound is to act as an inhibitor of ZBP1.Fig. 7Screening small molecules targeting ZBP1.(A) Heatmap of Candidate compounds screened by CMap. (B) The top 10 small molecules that were negatively correlated with expression profiles of 50 genes. (C) Chemical structure of rucaparib. (D) 3D diagram of rucaparib and ZBP1 docking.   Next, molecular docking was used to calculate the binding energies between ZBP1 and the candidate compounds. Generally, a docking energy value less than −5.0 kcal/mol indicates good binding activity, while a value less than −7.0 kcal/mol indicates strong binding activity [22]. The results demonstrated good binding activity of SJ-172550, actarit, nitrazepam, fillalbin, and rucaparib with ZBP1(Fig. 7B). SJ-172550 is an MDM4 inhibitor that is unstable in aqueous buffers, leading to the formation of side products with unknown biological activity [23]. Actarit is an orally active immunomodulatory agent used to treat rheumatoid arthritis. However, its suppressive effect on immunity may not be beneficial as an anti-tumor agent [24]. Nitrazepam is a benzodiazepine with sedative and hypnotic properties. However, the long-term oral administration of nitrazepam may cause cancers [25]. Fillalbin is a natural compound that has not previously been reported in tumor-related studies. Rucaparib is a PARP inhibitor with significant potential for cancer therapy, particularly for prostate and ovarian cancers [26,27]. Therefore, rucaparib is the most promising ZBP1-targeted drug for renal cancer treatment. The rucaparib's chemical structure is shown in Fig. 7C. 3D diagram of rucaparib and ZBP1 docking is shown in Fig. 7D. Based on the binding mode of molecular docking, rucaparib forms a conventional hydrogen bond with SER149 of ZBP1, a carbon-hydrogen bond with ARG146 of ZBP1, a pi-pi stacked interaction with TYR145 of ZBP1, and a pi-alkyl hydrophobic interaction with ARG142 of ZBP1, as shown in the 2D diagram (Fig. 7E).


## Discussion
  ccRCC is the most common type of RCC and is prone to metastasis, leading to a poor prognosis [28]. Although treatment methods are diverse, their effectiveness in advanced patients remains unsatisfactory [29]. The development of targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors accounts for the major recent treatment advances [30]. This underscores the urgent need for new drug targets to develop more effective RCC-targeted therapies. ZBP1 has recently been shown to be related to malignant diseases; however, its specific mechanism in ccRCC remains controversial. This research aims to determine the diagnostic and prognostic value of ZBP1 expression, explore the association between ZBP1 and immune infiltrates, and explore the potential functions of ZBP1 in ccRCC.

  Although pathological diagnosis and prognostic scoring could provide direction for ccRCC treatment, biomarkers for early diagnosis, patient prognosis, and treatment response remain an unmet need [31]. ZBP1 has been found to be highly expressed in human hepatocellular carcinoma and melanoma [32,33]. In breast cancer, ZBP1 is expressed during tumorigenesis; however, its expression is repressed in metastatic breast cancer cell lines [34]. In addition, ZBP1 is selectively expressed during late B cell development in both murine and human cells [33]. However, there have been no reports on the expression of ZBP1 in ccRCC. In this research, we found that ZBP1 was highly expressed in ccRCC, and its high expression was correlated with a high pathologic T stage, high pathologic stage, high histologic grade, and poor overall survival in pan-cancer analysis. Moreover, ZBP1 demonstrated significant diagnostic value for ccRCC, with an AUC of 0.930 in the ROC analyses. We also constructed a nomogram to predict survival probability. These results suggest that ZBP1 is a potential predictive survival marker for ccRCC.

  Immunotherapy has shown remarkable clinical efficacy in ccRCC [35]. Cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) kill both infected and cancerous cells. In particular, ZBP1 can be used as a genetic adjuvant for DNA vaccines to promote effective anti-tumor CTL immunity [36]. In breast cancer, ZBP1 increases immune cell infiltration and may be a potential target for predicting and treating triple-negative breast cancer with metastases [37]. In ccRCC, ZBP1-associated DEGs were enriched in immune-related processes, including the CTLA-4 pathway, cancer immunotherapy by PD-1 blockade, and T cell-associated processes. In addition, ZBP1 expression was positively associated with the number of infiltrating T cells and cytotoxic cells. Using the STRING database, we identified multiple proteins that may interact with ZBP1. Previous studies have reported that when activated, ZBP1 induces the recruitment of TBK1 and IRF3 to its C-terminal region, activating downstream interferon regulatory factors (IRFs) and NF-kappa B transcription factors, leading to IFN-I responses [8]. In addition, ZBP1 can recruit RIPK1 and RIPK3 through RHIM-RHIM homotypic interactions, driving inflammation and programmed cell death [38]. Interestingly, IKBKE has not been reported to interact with ZBP1, and this will be the focus of future studies. Furthermore, ZBP1 expression was positively associated with the expression of CTLA-4, PD-L1, PD-1, and other immune checkpoint genes. Previous studies have demonstrated that ccRCC is a highly immune-infiltrated tumor [[39], [40], [41]]. In contrast, high levels of tumor CD8+ T cell infiltration are associated with a worse prognosis in patients with ccRCC [42]. Notably, high ZBP1 expression in melanoma correlates with tumor infiltration by lymphocytes and better prognosis [33]. Both previous research and our study suggest a significant and close connection between ZBP1, tumor infiltration, and patient prognosis in ccRCC, which may inform the development of new strategies to enhance immunotherapy for ccRCC.

  ZBP1 is a cytosolic RNA and DNA sensor [8], but its molecular function in ccRCC remains unclear. In breast cancer cells, ZBP1 and beta-catenin form a feedback loop [43]. Disruption of this loop represses ZBP1 expression, leading to the increased proliferation and migration of metastatic breast cancer cells [44]. However, a recent research found that ZBP1 deletion does not influence the proliferation of MVT-1 cells (mouse breast cancer cells) or MVT-1 tumor growth [45]. Additionally, the interaction of ZBP1 with TBK1 and IRF3 promotes the proliferation of myeloma plasma cells [33]. Our in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrate that ZBP1 inhibition suppresses ccRCC cell proliferation. These results indicate that the functions of ZBP1 may vary across different cancer types and biological processes.

  Although in-depth mechanistic studies of ZBP1 are warranted, our study extends the findings on ZBP1 in ccRCC. The limitations of our study include the small sample size and the absence of a validation cohort. Additionally, we did not investigate other potential functions of ZBP1 in ccRCC. Further in vitro and in vivo experiments are needed to validate the role of ZBP1 in ccRCC. Currently, there are no clinically available ZBP1 inhibitors. However, through a combination of molecular docking and screening, rucaparib could be a potential ZBP1 inhibitor for ccRCC therapy; however, this requires further verification.

  In summary, our study highlights the diagnostic and prognostic value of ZBP1 in ccRCC. We uncovered a close association between ZBP1, tumor infiltration, and patient prognosis in ccRCC, as well as the role of ZBP1 in promoting ccRCC cellular proliferation. Given the function of ZBP1 in ccRCC and other diseases, future studies on ZBP1 are crucial for developing targeted therapies or immunotherapies for ccRCC.
