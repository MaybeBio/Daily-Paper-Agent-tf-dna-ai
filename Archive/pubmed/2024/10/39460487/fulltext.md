# Profiling Breast Tumor Heterogeneity and Identifying Breast Cancer Subtypes Through Tumor‐Associated Immune Cell Signatures and Immuno Nano Sensors


## Abstract
  Breast cancer is a complex and heterogeneous disease with varying cellular, genetic, epigenetic, and molecular expressions. The detection of intratumor heterogeneity in breast cancer poses significant challenges due to its complex multifaceted characteristics, yet its identification is crucial for guiding effective treatment decisions and understanding the disease progression. Currently, there exists no method capable of capturing the full extent of breast tumor heterogeneity. In this study, the aim is to identify and characterize metabolic heterogeneity in breast tumors using immune cells and an ultrafast laser‐fabricated Immuno Nano Sensor. Combining spectral markers from both Natural Killer (NK) and T cells, a machine‐learning approach is implemented to distinguish cancer from healthy samples, identify primary versus metastatic tumors, and determine estrogen receptor (ER)/progesterone receptor (PR) status at the single‐cell level. The platform successfully distinguished heterogeneous breast cancer samples from healthy individuals, achieving 97.8% sensitivity and 92.2% specificity, and accurately identified primary tumors from metastatic tumors. Characteristic spectral signatures allow for discrimination between ER/PR‐positive and negative tumors with 97.5% sensitivity. This study demonstrates the potential of immune cell‐based metabolic profiling in providing a comprehensive assessment of breast tumor heterogeneity and paving the way for minimally invasive liquid biopsy approaches in breast cancer diagnosis and management.


## Introduction
  Breast cancer is a significant health concern affecting women worldwide, accounting for 12.5% of all newly diagnosed cancers annually.[  1  ] Breast cancer is highly heterogeneous with various cancer cells within the same tumor existing in diverse metabolic states due to variations in cellular, genetic, epigenetic, and molecular expression.[  2 ,  3 ,  4  ] These molecular expressions are not uniformly constant and can dynamically evolve over time as the tumor progresses to a different location.[  5 ,  6  ] For example, the estrogen receptor (ER) expression is inconsistent between primary and metastatic breast cancer samples and even within the primary sample, there can be non‐uniform expression.[  7 ,  8 ,  9  ] The inter‐tumor heterogeneity and intra‐tumor metabolic transitions occur at the single‐cell level, presenting challenges in diagnosis. Liquid biopsy methods have emerged as promising tools for detecting cancer, but they focus on specific genetic or molecular alterations associated with cancer, limiting their ability to provide a comprehensive view of tumor heterogeneity. Currently, there exists no method capable of capturing the full extent of breast tumor heterogeneity.

  Immune cells exert selective pressure on tumors controlling tumor heterogeneity. Both Natural Killer cells (NK) and T cells employ distinct methods of tumor recognition and eliminate specific cancer cell subpopulations.[  10 ,  11 ,  12  ] However, few cancer cells develop immune evasion abilities and circumvent immune response and continue to grow. Over time, this selective pressure from the immune system contributes to the development of a more diverse tumor population. The surviving cancer cells are those that have acquired cellular, genetic, epigenetic, and molecular alterations that make them less susceptible to immune response. As a result, the tumor becomes more heterogeneous, consisting of multiple clones with different characteristics and responses to treatments. One of the mechanisms that tumor cells employ involves inducing metabolic reprogramming in immune cells, which alters their energy production, nutrient utilization, and signaling pathways.[  10 ,  11 ,  12 ,  13  ] The complexity of immune cell metabolic reprogramming is directly proportional to the heterogeneity of the tumor population. The unique metabolic tumor‐associated signatures thus acquired can provide a comprehensive view of tumor heterogeneity. In this study, we postulate that by harnessing the complementary functions of T cells and NK cells, a comprehensive understanding of breast cancer heterogeneity can be achieved which can provide valuable information about the presence of breast cancer and the molecular subtypes.

  The metabolic changes happening within the immune cells on interaction with heterogenous population of breast cancer cells are complex and cannot be identified solely by the presence or absence of single marker. Therefore, in this study, we designed an Immuno Nano Sensor platform utilizing surface enhanced Raman spectroscopy as the detection methodology which is capable of identifying the molecular heterogeneity in entirety without cellular isolation. The collective metabolic changes are captured as changes in protein, lipid, and nucleic acid composition resulting from metabolic adaptations upon interaction with tumors. These metabolic changes are not random, but highly specific and can be reliably detected, making them valuable biomarkers for distinguishing between healthy and tumor‐interacted immune cells. The superior signal enhancement with Immuno Nano Sensor was due to reduction in the size of the probe to less than 5 nm achieved through multiphoton ionization. This reduction in size increased the ratio of surface area to volume, thereby enhancing the sensor's sensitivity to immune cell signals. In addition, the size of the probes was tuned to match that of the analyte molecules, resulting in more efficient binding and amplified signals. The incorporation of sharp edges proved instrumental in enhancing the local electromagnetic field, thereby generating a stronger plasmonic response. Additionally, the presence of oxygen‐containing functional groups facilitated the generation of a hydrophilic surface, promoting cell adhesion with the sensor.

  In this study, we successfully identified and characterized metabolic heterogeneity within breast tumors through the utilization of breast tumor‐associated immune cells and nano immuno sensor. The observed heterogeneity encompassed variations in cell origin (primary and metastatic) and cell‐specific estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR) expression.[  14  ] Immune cells play a crucial role in discerning these heterogeneous features, with NK cells enabling the characterization of metastatic[  15 ,  16  ] and ER‐positive tumors, while T cells facilitated the profiling of primary and ER‐negative populations.[  17 ,  18 ,  19  ] Using the spectral markers from both NK and T cells, we implemented a machine learning approach to sequentially identify cancer from healthy samples, followed by the distinction between primary and metastatic tumors, and eventually determining the ER/PR status at the single‐cell level. First, to establish comprehensive signatures for this analysis, we developed an in vitro model using MDA MB231, MCF‐7, and BT breast cancer cell lines and primary T cell, primary NK cell and NK‐92 cell lines, effectively mimicking the tumor ‐immune interactions and facilitating the generation of Breast Tumor‐Associated T cells (B‐TAT) and NK (B‐TANK) signatures. The metabolic signatures of B‐TAT and B‐TANK were then compared to their normal counterparts from healthy cells. We found that both immune cells captured distinct metabolic characteristics of the tumor, which may be due to the fact that T and NK cells recognise and eliminate tumors in distinct ways, resulting in unique metabolic reprogramming. Next, in addition to the distinct differences in the spectra of healthy and tumor‐interacted NK and T cells, we observed distinct metabolic reprogramming in immune cells when interacting with tumor cells in primary and metastatic sites. This leads to the identification of unique metabolic signatures and molecular characteristics associated with location‐specific heterogeneity which was primarily due to variations in protein, lipid, and nucleic acids. Third, the distinct metabolic demands experienced by immune cells within the tumor microenvironment contributed to the observed cell‐type‐specific alterations in immune metabolism, resulting in cell‐specific metabolic alterations. Exploiting these unique metabolic alterations in breast tumor‐associated T and NK cell profiles allowed for the distinction of ER/PR‐negative tumors with remarkable sensitivity (97.5%) and specificity (78.5%). Finally, we validated our findings using preclinical real‐time patient samples. With 5 µL of peripheral blood, our Immuno Nano Sensor successfully distinguished 104 heterogeneous breast cancer blood samples from 20 healthy individuals. The machine learning classifier, employing random forest methodology, achieved impressive sensitivity (97.8%) and specificity (92.2%) in identifying breast cancer. Moreover, we accurately identified primary tumors with a sensitivity of 93%. Additionally, we discerned characteristic spectral signatures for ER/PR‐positive and ‐negative lesions with 97.5% sensitivity and 78.5% specificity, indicating the potential of our approach in identifying specific hormone receptor status. The Immuno Nano Sensor's capability to identify and analyze tumor‐associated immune cells without relying on the release of tumor biomarkers enabled us to achieve higher levels of sensitivity and signal‐to‐noise ratio. Overall, our findings highlight the immense clinical potential of the Immuno Nano Sensor as a minimally invasive liquid biopsy tool for comprehensive breast cancer detection.


## Results and Discussion

### Immuno Nano Sensor for Comprehensive Breast Cancer Diagnosis – Study Objectives, Design, Approach and Rationale
  The primary goal of this study was to determine the diagnostic potential of Immuno Nano Sensor for breast cancer diagnosis based on the signature of breast tumor‐associated T cell (B‐TAT) and breast tumor‐associated Natural Killer cells (B‐TANK) in circulation. Blood samples were obtained from the Ontario Tumor Bank (OTB) and brain tumor biobank (St. Michael's Hospital, Department of Surgery) using strict inclusion and exclusion criteria. Patients with an age of 18 or older were eligible to participate in the study. The clinical stage of the disease was determined using the TNM staging method, and the tumor was graded using the WHO system.

  The Immuno Nano Sensor incorporates nickel nano probes that efficiently interact with immune cells, facilitating the detection of metabolic reprogramming in these cells. To establish a robust classification framework based on Raman spectra signatures, we collected healthy NK and T cell signatures as controls and mapped the Raman spectra of T and NK cells (B‐TAT and B‐TANK) that had interacted with breast tumors and their subtypes (Figure  1 , left panel). To classify the samples based on their Raman spectra signatures, we employed a machine learning technique utilizing the Random Forest classifier. Through this approach, we successfully developed an algorithm, named ImmunoSign, which identified important spectral features and patterns that effectively distinguish between healthy and cancerous samples, primary and metastatic tumors, as well as differentiate between ER+ and PR+ subtypes (estrogen receptor‐positive and progesterone receptor ‐positive). The algorithm was trained on a comprehensive set of Raman spectra data from T and NK cells and rigorously validated using an independent dataset (Figure 1, right panel). This approach allowed for the identification of important spectral features that distinguish between healthy and cancerous immune cells, even in the presence of potential interferences from other components in the blood. Immuno Nano Sensor utilizes only a minimal amount of peripheral blood (5 µL), making it a minimally invasive approach capturing real‐time tumor information and detecting tumor‐specific alterations. This approach holds significant promise for immune‐based tri‐class breast cancer diagnosis, addressing the current limitations of conventional techniques and enhancing the accuracy and reliability of breast cancer diagnosis (Figure 1).


### Synthesis and Characterization of Immuno Nano Sensor
  The Nickel‐metal targets were irradiated with a high‐intensity ultrafast laser beam. When the incident energy surpasses the ionization threshold of the material, Ni ions are ejected out of the target surface. The ejected ions, free ions, and free radicals generate a plasma plume, as shown in the schematic in Figure  2A  The Ni ions interact with the oxygen ions in the ambient atmosphere to form NiO particles that are self‐assembled on the target surface upon condensation. A schematic showing the deposited particles is depicted in Figure 2B The as‐formed particles were analyzed using Scanning Transmission electron microscopy to study the particle morphology. The particles had a polygonal structure. The average particle size was estimated by employing a size distribution analysis. A normal distribution fit was performed to estimate the average size of the particles to be 3.6 ± 0.9 nm. The average particle size was calculated based on the TEM images (Figure S1, Supporting Information). Multiple particles were interconnected to form a twork of nanoscale sensors, as evidenced by the STEM image in Figure 2C.

  The sensor was characterized using Raman spectroscopy to analyze the chemical composition (Figure 2D). The Raman band ≈500 cm−1 corresponds to nanocrystalline NiO. The 2LO band appears ≈1090 cm−1, and a two‐magnon mode was observed at 1500 cm−1.[  20  ] The Raman characterization confirmed the presence of NiO particles.

  To capture the trace amounts of tumor‐associated immune signatures from the sample (B‐TAT and B‐TANK), an ultrasensitive sensor was required. The laser‐synthesized Immuno Nano Sensor was self‐functionalized with SERS capabilities. The signals from an immune cell, when it is adsorbed by the Immuno Nano Sensor, were compared to the immune signals obtained when the sample was placed on a bare Ni substrate. The immune signals obtained by utilizing the substrate and the sensor surface is represented in Figure 2G. It can be observed that the immune signals are substantially enhanced by the Immuno Nano Sensor. The obtained Raman peak intensities were compared for the five significant peaks. We obtained a maximum enhancement of ≈700‐fold, calculated as ratio of the Raman signal intensity with the enhancing substrate (I) to the signal intensity without the substrate (I0) (I/I0). The excellent signal‐to‐noise ratio offered by the Immuno Nano Sensor favors the detection of immune signals from the sample. The enhancement factor provided by the immunosensor was calculated for the analyte Rhodamine 6G at millimolar concentration. Figure S2 (Supporting Information) shows the SERS spectra of R6G with and without Immune Nano Sensor. The Raman intensity of 1513 cm−1 peak generated on the substrate and the sensor was employed for the calculation. An enhancement factor of 9.408 × 1011 was obtained for the immunosensor, indicating substantial Raman enhancement.

  The high SERS enhancement offered by the Immuno Nano Sensor can be attributed to multiple reasons. First, the nano size of the sensor spanning sub‐10 nm scale particles that forms a network can produce highly localized enhanced electric fields due to surface plasmon resonance. Figure S3 (Supporting Information) shows FDTD simulation of Ni probes demonstrating the presence of localized surface plasmon resonance. The electrical field enhancement at the apex of cubical probe calculated to be 1.91 eV at 785 nm. The simulation is showed for single particle and when multiple nanoparticles come together, the enhancement of the hotspot is significantly increased. Besides, they also provide a high surface‐to‐volume ratio for better adsorption of the analyte samples on the sensor surface. The network structure would have facilitated the efficient capture of the immune cells. Additionally, in Surface Enhanced Raman Scattering (SERS), the analyte molecules are physically adsorbed on the sensor surface, which facilitates chemical enhancement of the Raman signals. This enhancement is primarily due to a charge‐transfer process between the sensor and the analyte, leading to an increase in Raman signal intensity. Importantly, this process does not involve any chemical reaction between the sensor and the analyte during spectral acquisition. Consequently, NiO particles used in the sensor exhibit minimal or no interference with the Raman spectra of the analytes (Figure 2G).


### Breast Tumor‐Interacted T Cells and NK Cells Capture Unique Aspects of Breast Tumor Heterogeneity
  Breast cancer is a complex and heterogeneous disease and are known to exhibit considerable phenotypic plasticity with varying receptor expression patterns.[  19 ,  20 ,  21  ] For example, a single cancer cell can express estrogen receptor‐positive (ER+) and/or progesterone receptor‐positive (PR+) markers or transform into a cancer stem cell, making it challenging to detect disease using conventional approaches. To address the challenge of detecting the dynamic heterogeneity of breast cancer, it is essential to identify a robust biomarker that can capture the various facets of the disease. Immune cells are a highly promising candidate due to their exceptional capacity to recognize and provide a comprehensive, dynamic and multifaceted assessment of the tumor that conventional techniques are unable to match. In order to identify the most suitable immune cell type for capturing the heterogeneity of breast cancer, we conducted a comprehensive search to find highly abundant immune cells in the tumor microenvironment. We used TIMER (Tumor Immune Estimation Resource) database to analyze the immune infiltrates in breast cancers.[  22  ] One of the features of TIMER is the ability to visualize the correlation between gene expression and immune cell infiltration. In this case, the BRCA expression level versus CD8+ T cell infiltration and the BRCA expression level versus NK cell infiltration graphs, shows the correlation between the expression level of the BRCA gene and the infiltration of immune cells in breast cancer. A correlation analysis revealed that both T and NK cells were significantly infiltrated breast tumors. T cells are central to the adaptive immune response, which provides a tailored and long‐lasting defense against specific cancer cells. Tumor cells often express unique antigens (tumor‐associated antigens or neoantigens) that can be recognized by T cells.[  23  ] This specificity allows T cells to distinguish between normal and cancerous cells, making them valuable for cancer detection. Natural Killer cells form the first line of defense against cancer. NK cells do not require additional “training” or previous sensitization to identify and kill cancer cells.[  24 ,  25  ] NK cells are able to target and eliminate quiescent and non‐proliferating cells such as cancer stem cells responsible for metastasis and invasion. This preferential cytotoxicity toward CSCs is valuable for early cancer diagnosis.[  26 ,  27  ] The antigen recognition capabilities of T cells and the preferential targeting of cancer stem cells by NK cells together enable these immune cells to detect and target various cancer cell types and the complex heterogeneity of the tumor microenvironment. Therefore, we chose to focus on these two immune cell types for our study. In response to signals from breast cancer, both T cells and NK cells get reprogrammed to recognize and attack cancer cells. This editing by tumor cells induces metabolic changes in T and NK cells, resulting in altered proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids within the cell. These altered metabolic profile can provide us diagnostic information.

  Following the selection of T and NK cells, we studied their cellular metabolic expression using Raman spectroscopy after co‐culturing T cells (Human peripheral blood pan – T cells, stem cell technologies) and NK cells (primary NK cells – Stem cell technologies, NK‐92, ATCC) with breast cancer cells (MDAMB231) using transwell inserts for 24 h. T and NK cells have distinct abilities to identify and kill cancer cells,[  23 ,  24 ,  25 ,  26  ] and consequently their Raman spectra reflect their metabolic interaction. The Raman spectrum of a cell comprises the vibrational modes of various molecular species, including proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, and other biomolecules. These molecules are not uniformly distributed within the cell, leading to distinct Raman signatures in different regions. This spatial heterogeneity arises from the complex and varied biochemical environment within different cellular compartments such as the nucleus, cytoplasm, and organelles. While SERS spectra of two cells can show variability due to differences in cellular composition and microenvironment, this heterogeneity is minimal among the same group of cells. However, variability can be more pronounced when comparing healthy cells and cancer cells, given their distinct biochemical profiles, which helps in identifying cancer and its subtypes. Figure  3D,E shows Raman spectra of Breast tumor associated T cell and Breast tumor associated NK cell. Characteristic peaks were seen at 854 cm−1, 1072, 1128, and 1400 cm−1 corresponding to ring breathing tyrosine (proteins), Triglycerides (fatty acids), C‐O stretching (carbohydrates) and Cholesterol, fatty acid band d(CH2) (lipids) for breast tumor associated T cell and at 1000, 1465, 1589, and 1679 cm−1 corresponding to phenylalanine (protein), lipids, hydroxyproline (protein) and Amide I (b‐sheet) for breast tumor associated NK cell.[  28 ,  29 ,  30 ,  31  ] We found that both immune cells captured various metabolic characteristics of the tumor, which could be due to the fact that when T cells and NK cells engage with tumors, they interact differently, resulting to different metabolic reprogramming. Intercellular heterogeneity within the same group of cells (e.g., healthy cells) was minimized by acquiring multiple spectra for each cell and averaging them. This approach reduced the impact of outliers and random variations. The spectra obtained were representative of the cell population by sampling multiple cells within the specified area. High‐resolution Raman mapping of cells was performed to capture spatial variations and build a comprehensive profile of the cell's Raman signature. This method involved collecting spectra at numerous points across the cell, providing detailed spatial information about molecular distributions.

  Furthermore, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of the obtained Raman spectra by employing Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to visualize the metabolic profile of the cells. Lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids were investigated using PCA score plots, where PC1 and PC2 accounted for the majority of the variance. Score plot from PC1 showing maximum variance is shown in Figure 3F. As shown in Figure 3G, two separate clusters were observed showing that breast tumor‐associated T cell and breast tumor‐associated NK cell have different metabolic properties.


### Distinctive Metabolic Spectral Profiles of Naïve Immune Cells and Tumor‐Associated Immune Cells at Single Cell Level
  After recognizing the ability of NK and T cells in capturing the heterogeneity nature of the breast tumor microenvironment, we conducted a comparative analysis of the Raman spectra involving examining the spectra of immune cells that had interacted with the breast tumor versus those that were healthy and had not interacted with the breast tumor (Figure  4A,B). By doing this, we aimed to utilize Raman spectroscopy for diagnostic purposes. These spectral differences can potentially serve as diagnostic biomarkers to distinguish between tumor‐altered immune cells and healthy immune cells.

  The Raman spectra of the healthy, un‐interacted NK and T cells displayed classical cellular metabolites, such as proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids. In the spectra of tumor un‐interacted T cell and NK cell, the most prominent T cell peaks include 852, 1131, and 1297 cm−1 corresponding to tyrosine, fatty acid, and cytosine and the most prominent peaks for NK cells include phenylalanine at 1000 cm−1, Amide III at 1250 cm−1, C‐H deformation at 1450 cm−1 and amide I at 1600 cm−1. Further, the results of the statistical analysis revealed a significant difference between the healthy population and the cancer population for four specific Raman spectral peaks: 1114, 1150, 1346, and 1476 cm−1, corresponding to the Saccharide band, Glycogen, Glucose, and Amide II, respectively. The two‐tailed t‐test showed a statistically significant distinction between the two groups, indicating significant variations in the molecular composition and metabolic profile associated with these biomolecules (Figure 4F).

  Next, in our analysis of Raman data, we employed principal component analysis (PCA) to reduce the multidimensional nature of the data into two principal components. By doing so, we were able to analyze all the peaks together and identify any significant differences between healthy and tumor‐interacted immune cells. The resulting PCA plot (Figure 4C) showed two separate clusters, indicating that the biochemical makeup of these cells varies significantly. To further analyze the data, we used a loading plot (Figure 4D) to identify the peaks that contributed the most to the variance. These peaks were then used to create a heat map (Figure 4E), which clearly demonstrated the spectral variation between healthy and tumor‐associated immune cells. The heat map was plotted where each row represents a Raman marker (loading) and each column represents a sample. Each cell in the heat map is color‐coded according to the values of that cell from the spectral data, visually representing the variation and distribution of Raman markers between the different samples. Overall, our use of PCA and subsequent analysis techniques allowed us to gain valuable insights into the biochemical differences between these cell types.

  In order to further validate the differential SERS expression seen in naïve (healthy) and breast tumor‐associated T cells (B‐TAT) and breast tumor‐associated NK (B‐TANK) cells, we performed Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA). The expression levels of CD69 were analyzed through ELISA. CD69 encodes a protein in the C‐type lectin family that serves as an early activation marker for T cells and NK cells.[  32 ,  33  ] Its expression increases rapidly upon tumor interaction and activation, making it an excellent indicator of immune cell activation status.[  34  ] However, healthy immune cells show significantly lower levels of CD69 due to the lack of such activation stimuli in the normal physiological state. Upon activation, CD69+ T cells increase their reliance on glycolysis to sustain rapid proliferation and cytokine production.[  35  ] Activated T cells also boost the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) to generate nucleotides and NADPH for cell growth and reactive oxygen species (ROS) regulation. This metabolic reprogramming supports the differentiation into effector T cells with enhanced cytotoxic and helper functions.[  36  ] In NK cells, activation signaled by CD69 upregulation is accompanied by a metabolic switch to glycolysis, which fuels the energy‐intensive processes of degranulation, cytokine secretion (IFN‐γ), and increased cytotoxicity.[  37  ]

  Figure S4A (Supporting Information) shows optimal standard curve generated with standard control based on OD at 450 nm. The results of the ELISA, analyzed using t‐tests, indicate that the expression of CD69 was significantly upregulated in breast tumor‐associated T cells (B‐TAT) compared to control T cells (**** p < 0.0001) and breast tumor‐associated NK cells (B‐TANK) compared to control NK cells (****p < 0.0001). This substantial difference in CD69 expression levels in B‐TAT and B‐TANK cells versus control T and NK cells suggests that breast tumor‐associated T and NK cells are phenotypically distinct from their healthy counterparts. These findings imply that immune cells undergo metabolic reprogramming upon interaction with breast tumor cells, which is consistent with the results obtained from SERS, highlighting the alteration and metabolic shifts occurring in tumor‐associated lymphocytes.


### Distinguishing Primary and Metastatic Cancer through location specific metabolic reprogramming in immune cells
  We differentiated healthy and cancer samples based on spectral variations and proceeded to compare primary tumor interactions with metastatic tumor interactions. Raman spectra were obtained from T and NK cells interacting with primary and metastatic breast tumors (Figure  5A,B). We observed distinctive spectral variations between primary and metastatic tumor interactions. Statistical analysis using Student's t‐test revealed significant variations at specific Raman peaks, including 998, 1005, 1304, and 1434 cm−1 (Figure 5F) Principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated clear clustering patterns, distinguishing primary tumor interactions from metastatic tumor interactions (Figure 5C) Peaks contributing significantly to the observed spectral variation were identified using the loading plot generated from PCA (Figure 5D). These specific Raman features were selected for further analysis and a heatmap was constructed to visualize the differences between primary tumor interactions and metastatic tumor interactions, providing a comprehensive comparison of spectral profiles (Figure 5E). The results indicate distinct metabolic profiles associated with primary and metastatic cancers, highlighting the potential of our approach to differentiate between these tumor types based on immune cell interactions.


### Tumor‐Associated Immune Cell Signatures Enable Non‐Invasive Detection of Breast Cancer
  Figure  6A depicts the schematic diagram outlining the process of comprehensive breast cancer diagnosis using the Immuno Nano Sensor. The training data was generated using in vitro cell culture models and peripheral blood samples. For in vitro cell culture model, T and NK cells were co‐cultured with cancer cells and for peripheral blood samples, T and NK cells were isolated using magnetic bead separation. This ensured that the training data consisted of tumor‐associated T and NK cells, free from other cell types for the development of a robust classification model. The buffy coat sample derived from patients are dropped on the immune nanosensor for testing and B‐TAT and B‐TANK signals are analysed using machine learning techniques, especially Random Forest classifier. The Random Forest classifier is a robust algorithm that employs an ensemble of decision trees. By constructing numerous decision trees using random subsets of features and merges them to improve accuracy and controls overfitting. Random Forest can effectively manage datasets with a large number of features relative to the number of samples. It does this by selecting a random subset of features for each tree, which helps in reducing the risk of overfitting and allows the model to focus on the most informative features. Random forest classifier can effectively handle high‐dimensional data and capture intricate relationships present in the immune cell signatures. Each decision tree individually categorizes the input data, and the ultimate classification is determined by the majority vote from all the trees. This classification method is particularly suitable for our study as it enables us to address the complexity of the immune cell signatures and make accurate predictions based on their collective analysis. In addition, cross‐validation was performed to ensure that the models were not only trained on the given dataset but also validated on separate data. Through this approach, we were able to address the limitation of small sample size. Student t‐test showing significant variation in the signals captured by B‐TAT and B‐TANK. (**** = p <0.001) at 1076 and 1323 cm−1 corresponding to lipid C─C and guanine respectively. This confirms the distinct metabolic alterations induced by the immune cell‐tumor interactions which serve as valuable biomarkers for breast cancer diagnosis. However, to avoid random variations in individually metabolites to be correlated to a cancer marker, we collectively took the entire spectra to train the ImmunoSign algorithm for the classification of breast cancer from healthy and breast cancer patients. The algorithm was able to differentiate between healthy and breast cancer based on the immune cell signals captured by B‐TAT and B‐TANK (middle panel – scatter plot). ### Tumor‐Associated Immune Cell Signatures Enable Location‐Specific and Cell Type‐Specific Classification of Heterogenous Breast Cancer
  Immuno Nano Sensor exhibited the ability to not only distinguish between healthy and cancer samples but also provide insights into the origin of the tumor. Specifically, due to the affinity of NK cells to interact with cancer stem cells (CSCs),[  27 ,  38 ,  39  ] the signals captured by B‐TAT and B‐TANK encompassed the characteristics of CSCs responsible for metastasis. Leveraging this unique feature, we conducted another round of analysis using the ImmunoSign algorithm to differentiate between primary and metastatic cancers (Figure 7A). Encouragingly, the classifier achieved a sensitivity of 93.1% and specificity of 100%, indicating its effectiveness in accurately classifying primary and metastatic cancers.(Figure 7B)

  Furthermore, by incorporating additional spectral markers obtained from the loading plot analysis of ER PR‐positive and ER PR‐negative tumors, our immunoSign algorithm successfully differentiated between all primary and metastatic tumors in terms of their ER PR status which can provide information on targeted therapy decisions(Figure 7C).[  40 ,  41 ,  42 ,  43  ] We obtained a sensitivity of 97.5% and a specificity of 78.9%, highlighting the algorithm's ability to accurately classify tumors based on their ER PR expression. Taken together, the results suggest that the Immuno Nano Sensor, in combination with the ImmunoSign algorithm, holds promise as a comprehensive diagnostic tool for breast cancer.


## Conclusion
  This study highlights the significant potential of the Immuno Nano Sensor in utilizing tumor‐associated immune cell signatures for the early detection and characterization of breast cancer. Our machine learning approach utilizing SERS and immuno nano sensor, successfully classified cancerous samples from healthy controls, differentiated between primary and metastatic cancers, and determined the ER/PR status of tumors. We found that both immune cell types exhibited distinct metabolic properties influenced by the tumor microenvironment, leading to differential metabolic reprogramming. We observed that both immune cells captured distinct metabolic properties of the tumor, resulting in differential metabolic reprogramming. Utilizing these specific metabolic changes in breast tumor‐associated T and NK cell profiles enabled the differentiation between cancerous and healthy cells. Using random forest machine learning classifier, we achieved high sensitivity (97.8%) and specificity (92.2%) in diagnosing breast cancer. Further, primary tumors were identified from metastatic tumors with 93% sensitivity and ER/PR‐positive and negative status with 97.5% sensitivity and 78.5% specificity, demonstrating the promise of our method for identifying and classifying breast tumors for diagnostic and therapeutic approaches. Overall, our results demonstrate the tremendous clinical potential of the Immuno Nano Sensor as a minimally invasive liquid biopsy technique for comprehensive breast cancer detection.


## Experimental Section

### Synthesis of Immuno Nano Sensor
  The Immuno Nano Sensor was synthesized using ultrashort pulsed laser ablation technique. A femtosecond laser of Clark‐MXR Impulse series with a central wavelength of 1030 nm was employed for fabricating the sensor. Nickel substrates were purchased with 99% purity. The substrates were ultrasonicated in distilled water and acetone and mounted on an XYZ translation stage before ablation. The ablation pattern was generated using EzCAD software. The fabrication was carried out at 25 MHz repetition rate and 1 mm s−1 scanning speed. We employed nitrogen atmosphere at 2 bar pressure surrounding the ablation plume.


### Immuno Nano Sensor Characterization
  The as‐synthesized sensor was characterized using electron microscopy technique to analyze the particle size and morphology. The STEM image was acquired using an HF3300 environmental CFE TEM. The particle size distribution was estimated using ImageJ software. The Raman characterization was carried out using Renishaw InVia Confocal Raman microscope with an incident wavelength of 785 nm.

### Cell Culture
  Human peripheral blood pan – T cells were purchased from stem cell technologies. The T cells were activated and expanded in complete Immunocult‐XF T cell expansion medium. The media is prepared by adding cytokines (Human Recombinant IL‐2) to Immunocult‐XF T cell expansion medium. Human T cells are seeded at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells mL−1. To activate T cells, 25 µL mL−1 of ImmunoCult Human CD3/CD28 T Cell Activator was added to the cell suspension. Cells were incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO₂. Fresh complete medium was added every 2–3 days. T cells were harvested on Day 10 from the start of culture.

  Primary NK cells were purchased from stem cell technologies, NK‐92 cells were purchased from ATCC and were maintained in Alpha minimum essential medium w/o nucleosides. In addition, 0.2 mm myo‐inositol, 0.1 mm 2‐mercaptoethanol, 0.02 mm folic acid, 12.5% horse serum, and 12.5% fetal bovine serum were added to the culture medium. To maintain adequate proliferation, IL‐2 was added at 150 IU/mL. All cells were cultured at 37 degree Celsius at 5% CO2 atmosphere.

  The primary breast cancer population was cultured from BT‐474 cell line purchased from ATCC (American Type Cell Culture). The metastatic breast cancer population was cultured using MDA‐MB‐231 cell line grown in Leibovitz's L‐15 Medium with 10% FBS. All cell culture was carried out according to the protocol given by ATCC.

  Co‐culture of immune cells and patient‐derived tumor cells was carried out in trans‐well plates of 0.4 µm pore size purchased from Corning. Immune cells (T/NK) were seeded in the insert of trans‐well plate. The stem‐cell enriched tumor population of breast cancer were seeded in the lower chamber. After 24 h of co‐culture, the immune cell subset was centrifuged and isolated for further analysis.


### Patient Sample Collection
  All buffy coat samples employed in this study were obtained after taking informed consent from patients. The primary breast cancer samples were obtained from Ontario Tumor Bank and the metastatic breast cancer samples were obtained from Brain Tumor Biobank at St. Michael's Hospital, Toronto, ON. The study was approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Board (REB 2021–129).


### SERS Data Acquisition
  The SERS spectra obtained in this study was acquired using a 785 nm incident wavelength laser utilizing Renishaw InVia Raman microscope. For cell culture experiments, spectra were collected from T cell and NK cell control, tumor‐interacted NK cell, and tumor‐interacted T cell. For each individual cell, we acquired spectra at 10 different points within the same cell, with each point's spectra taken in triplicates using a 10 s acquisition time and averaged. The representative spectrum shown is an average of 30 individual measurements (3 replicates × 10 points) for each cell.

  For patient samples, we analyzed 10 healthy individuals and 20 breast cancer patients (10 primary, 10 metastatic). During area mapping of a 25 µm × 25 µm region on the sensor surface, we typically observed an average of 5 cells. The Raman spectra for each cell were taken as described above and then analyzed to represent each sample. The laser power was set to 5 mW, with data acquired over three accumulations with a 10 s exposure time for each acquisition using a 50x objective lens. The working volume was set to 5 µL for all samples.


### Statistical Data Analysis
  Where applicable, both multivariate and univariate analyses were conducted. Origin 2022 was utilized for multivariate statistics including principal component analysis (PCA), while GraphPad Prism 9.0 was utilized for univariate analysis (t‐test). All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Student's t‐test is utilized for comparing singular data across two groups. Random Forest was used to differentiate categorical variables using R. From the samples, eighty percent of the original data were used as training data and twenty percent as prediction data. The calibration model was cross‐validated for calibration error (training data) and cross‐validation error (test data). Cross‐validation data were used to evaluate the model's predictive performance. With sensitivity, specificity, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, and area under the curve (AUC) for training, cross‐validation, and test sets, the model's performance was validated.
