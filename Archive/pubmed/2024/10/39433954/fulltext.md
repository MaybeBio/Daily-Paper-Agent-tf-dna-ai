# H3K9me3 demethylation by JMJD2B is regulated by pirfenidone resulting in improved NASH


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-75458-2.


## Introduction
  Nonalcoholic steatohepatitis -NASH-, lately known as MASH -Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis- is the most severe form of MASLD -metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease-, and currently one of the most prevalent chronic liver diseases1. First-line therapy remains lifestyle and dietary modification, nevertheless patients have low adherence to these therapeutic interventions and just recently the first drug to treat MASLD has been approved2,3. Therefore, there is an uprising need for pharmacological options4. In this context, Pirfenidone (PFD) has been recognized to decrease NASH features5,6, but previous studies lack focus on epigenetic pathways, specifically histone methylation processes. This is significant since methylation of histone 3 lysine 9 (H3K9) plays a key role in NASH onset and development7. According to recent findings, Histone H3 lysine 9 methylation mainly represses transcription, and these repressive marks (di-, or trimethylated lysine) can be -reversibly- removed by histone demethylases, switching them in mono-methylated lysine and promoting gene expression8. Histone H3 lysine 4 demethylation occurs due to specific demethylases like the Jumonji C (JmjC) domain containing protein family. Histone demethylase JMJD2B/KDM4B overexpression was shown in the liver of mice fed a high-fat diet and in HepG2 cells exposed to palmitate, correlating with upregulated expression of Pparg2, Fabp and CD36 along with accumulation of hepatic lipids and progression to NASH in experimental models7,8. Thus, the decrease in JMJD2B protein inhibits the progression of NASH in murine models9,10. Pirfenidone (5-methyl-1-phenyl-2(1 H)-pyridone) is an antifibrogenic molecule approved for the treatment of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis by FDA and to treat liver fibrosis in Mexico by COFEPRIS. In NASH, PDF has demonstrated total steatosis reduction by regulating molecules such as Ppara and Sirt1, improving liver health in experimental models4,11,12 and fibrosis reduction in clinical studies13. The aim of this study was to investigate the effect of PFD as epigenetic regulator, specifically, in H3K9 demethylation processes by JMJD2B and how this can affect insulin resistance, hepatic lipid accumulation, and histological changes, as well as to corroborate the key molecular mechanisms involved in PA-exposed HepG2 cells.


## Methods

### Chemicals
  Pirfenidone was obtained from Tecoland Corporation. All other chemicals used were of reagent grade.


### Animals and diet
  Four-week-old male C57BL/6J mice weighing 20–25 g were fed standard diet and water before the experiment. After one week-acclimatization, mice were randomly allocated in 3 groups and fed for 8-wk either with standard diet (Envigo T.2018 S.15, 3.1 Kcal/g, 18% lipids, 58% carbohydrates, 24% proteins) and water (NormoDiet (ND), n = 5) or high-fat diet (Envigo TD.06414, 5.1 Kcal/g, 60.3% lipids, 21.4% carbohydrates, 18.3% proteins) and high-carbohydrate beverage (2.31% fructose, 1.89% sucrose) (HF). At week 8th, HF animals were divided in two subgroups (n = 5): HF-group continued with HF-diet until 16wk to induce NASH as control group, while another 5 cognate animals (HFP group) received ∼300 mg/kg body weight/day PFD mixed with HF food from week 8th to week 16th. Mice were fed ad libitum and had free access to beverage and received care and management according to Official Mexican Norm NOM-062-ZOO-1999 and guidelines of University of Guadalajara Animal Facility. Research Committee at the University Center for Health Sciences of the University of Guadalajara approved this study (protocol number: CI-00518). Animals were sacrificed under anesthesia with Zoletil 100® (tiletamine/zolazepam) after 4 h of fasting. All methods carried out in animals were in accordance with the guidelines of University of Guadalajara Animal Facility. Results involving animal experiments are reported according to the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines. Mice were purchased from Cinvestav Animal Facility (Mexico City).


### Cell culture
  HepG2 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Corning Inc., Oneonta, NY, USA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Biowest, Riverside, MO, USA) and an antibiotic/antimycotic mixture at 1× (Antibiotic/Antimycotic 100×, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 95% air / 5% CO2. After reaching 80% confluence, cells were subcultured in six-well plates at 6.5 × 104 cells/well. Prior to performing assays, cells were serum-starved for 24 h. Palmitic acid (PA, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was used to generate intracellular lipid accumulation and lipotoxicity. A 100 mM PA stock solution was prepared by dissolving PA in sterile water by alternating vortexing and heating at 70 °C. A 5 mM PA solution was prepared by adding 200 µL from the stock PA solution to 3.8 mL of serum-free DMEM with 5% NEFA-free BSA. The 5 mM PA-NEFA free BSA solution was shaken at 140 rpm at 40 °C for 1 h. After this time, this solution was diluted in serum-free DMEM 5% NEFA-free BSA to achieve a PA concentration of 0.5 mM and immediately added to cells. Twenty-four hours after PA exposure, medium was replaced, and cells were keep serum starved. Pirfenidone was diluted in 0.1% DMSO and added to the medium to reach a final concentration of 500µM. Cells were incubated 24 h with pirfenidone. Control cells were treated with 5% NEFA-free BSA and 0.1% DMSO was used as vehicle. Cell cultured experiments were performed in triplicate at least three times. HepG2 cells were obtained from ATCC (HB-8065; 41106514 H87).


### Oil red staining
  Cells were fixed with 10% formaldehyde for 5 min, washed with PBS and incubated 5 min with 60% isopropanol. Oil Red O solution (3 mg/ml) was added to stain for 5 min (ORO; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Subsequently, cells were washed to be co-stained with hematoxylin for 30 s and washed. 10 Microphotographs/well were taken. Also, cellular lipid content was measured extracting intracellular lipid droplets stained with ORO using 100% isopropanol. ORO absorbance was measured at 510 nm using a microplate reader. Lipid content was calculated using: % stain = (sample absorvance/ control absorvance) × 100.


### Energy intake, weight, fasting glucose, and insulin sensitivity
  Food and beverage intake was measured systematically three times per week between 9 and 10 am, and Kcal ingestion was calculated. Animal weight was recorded weekly during the study. At the end of the 16 weeks, 48 h before sacrifice; mice were short-fasted for 4 h to analyze insulin tolerance test (ITT) after an intraperitoneal injection of human-recombinant short-acting insulin at standardized dose of 0.025 U/mice. Tail vein blood obtained by puncture with a needle was used to measure glucose levels using a blood glucose meter (One Touch, LifeScan Inc. Malvern, PA, USA) and Select test stripts at 0, 30, 60, and 90 min after insulin injection. Finally, the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated.


### Serum biomarkers and liver histological analysis
  Blood was collected from ocular vein and centrifuged to obtain serum. Biochemical serum tests were analyzed using a VITROS350 analyzer (Ortho Clinical Diagnostics, Raritan, NJ, USA). Representative fragments of tissues were fixed in 10% buffered formalin solution for subsequent histological analysis. In 5 μm liver-sections, morphology and extracellular matrix deposition were examined using H&E and Masson´s staining. Two blind-to-study pathologists performed histologic analysis. Inflammation nodules, the number of fibrotic bridges, steatosis, and centrilobulilllar and periportal fibrosis, were scrutinized in at least 10 microscopic fields at 40X in three main liver lobules of each animal.


### Transcriptome analysis
  Total RNA was extracted from liver tissue with Trizol (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and mRNAs were labeled. Liver transcriptome was scanned using Mus musculus genome -version with 22,000 genes- dual channel microarray. Z score values of > 1.5 and < 1.5 were considered significant. Venn diagrams showed superimposed genes that were upregulated and downregulated between ND vs. HF, and between HF vs. HFP. In addition, heatmaps were built to visualize differential gene expression using Complex Heatmap package in the R environment. StringDB (Version 11.5) was used to visualize gene interactions in enriched pathways. Raw data from microarrays were generated at Unidad de Microarreglos de la Universidad Nacional Autonoma de México. Raw and processed data from microarrays are deposited in GEO under the following accession numbers: GPL34822 and GSE275069.


### Inmmunoblotting
  Nuclear protein extraction from liver tissue or cell homogenates was performed using explicit buffers to separate nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins. Protein quantification was carried out using Bradford reagent. Proteins were separated in 15% SDS-PAGE gels, transferred to PVDF membranes, and immunoblotted overnight at 4 °C with specific antibodies (H3K9me, H3K9me3, JMJD2B, Suppl Table 1). Bands were revealed using anti-Mouse-IgG-POD/anti-Rabbit-IgG-POD (1:10000) and detected using ChemiDoc™ XRS + equipment. Band quantification was made with Image Lab 5.0 software. Lamina b was used as loading control. Stripping and reprobing of western blotting membranes was made maximum 3 times.


### Molecular docking and structure viewing
  PyMOL Molecular Graphics System software was used to visualize molecular structures and to predict ligand binding14. Crystal structure of human JMJD2B-ligand binding domain was obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) with accession code 4LXL. Pyridine-2,4-dicarboxylic acid and pirfenidone 3D structures were obtained from the PubChem database with the accession number 10,365 and 40,632, respectively.


### Chromatin immunoprecipitation
  ChIP analyses were performed as follows. Liver tissue was sliced in liquid nitrogen. Formaldehyde 1.5% and glycine 0.125 M were added for 5 min to quench the crosslinking reaction. Chromatin was extracted using lysis buffer (50mM Tris pH 8.1, 10mM EDTA, 1% SDS). Lysates were sonicated and cleared by centrifugation at 10,000 g for 25 min at 4 °C. Five hundred µl of sample were immunoprecipitated overnight at 4 °C with 5 mg of the corresponding antibody (Suppl Table 2). Samples were washed and boiled to revert crosslinking, and DNA was recovered for qPCR. Fold Enrichment method was used for ChIP-qPCR analysis. The Delta Ct = Ct (gene of interest) - Ct (constitutive gene), was calculated. Data are given as fold change over control.


### Statistical analysis
  Continuous quantitative variables are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Shapiro-Wilk test was applied to determine normal distribution. ANOVA was conducted to identify differences between groups, followed by Tukey post hoc test, suitable for continuous variables with a normal distribution. For continuous variables that did not follow a normal distribution, the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test was used, along with the Dunnett’s multiple comparison test. A p < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant. Analyses were performed using R Studio statistical software, and Prism 9 was used for creating graphs.


## Results

### Pirfenidone improves biochemical and bodily characteristics in a NASH model
  As observed in Fig. 1A and B, body weight in animals fed with normal diet was 27.98 g ± 0.47, while HF group showed a 76.0% increase (49.25 g ± 2.36) and PFD group had a 44.3% increase (40.33 g ± 2.82) in weight compared to the ND group. HFP mice showed 18.11% decrement in body weight compared to HF group (p < 0.05). In Fig. 1C liver weight at sacrifice presented a significant difference (p < 0.05) between ND and HF group, although there was no difference between HF and HFP animals. Finally, in Fig. 1D, a significant difference in weight of epididymal adipose tissue between HFP and HF was observed (p < 0.001). Figure 1E displays representative photographs of mice prior to sacrifice. Also, liver and lipid biochemical parameters were analyzed. Biochemical data revealed a restored liver function, where serum AST and ALT were significantly lower in HFP (p < 0.0001) compared to HF (Fig. 2A and B). Likewise, in the lipid profile, pirfenidone treatment reduced total cholesterol, triglycerides and VLDL compared to HF (p < 0.01, 0.0001 and 0.05) respectively; (Fig. 2C, D and E). Figure 2F illustrates fasting glucose levels prior to sacrifice, highlighting the statistical difference found between HF and HFP (p < 0.05). HFP animals preserved insulin sensitivity evaluated through ITT, reaching similar serum glucose levels than ND animals as depicted in Fig. 2G. The AUC in Fig. 2H showed that ND had the lowest values and HF had the highest, and when comparing HFP with HF, pirfenidone group showed a significant decreased value (p < 0.01), indicating insulin responsiveness.

### Hepatic histological structures in NASH are preserved after pirfenidone treatment
  Histological analysis in HF group showed substantial tissue damage, characterized by steatosis, fibrosis, and the consequent changes in inflammation. In Fig. 3A, H&E staining indicated a significant reduction in inflammatory nodules (p < 0.001) after pirfenidone treatment. In addition, there is a decrease in the number of fibrotic bridges between the periportal and centrilobular areas in HFP animals against HF mice (p < 0.05). Steatosis had a significant reduction in PFD- group compared to HF group (p < 0.001). Also, a substantial decrease was demonstrated in macrosteatosis (p < 0.0001; Fig. 3B). Fibrosis development was mainly observable in the periportal and centrilobular areas, and as depicted in Fig. 3C fibrosis was greater in HF group vs. HFP group, in both areas, (p < 0.01 and p < 0.05, respectively). Also, Table 3 S demonstrates that caloric ingestion between HF and HFP groups was similar, while ND animals have a reduced intake. This clearly supports that the benefits observed in PFD animals regarding body weight, histology and biochemical parameters is due to pirfenidone treatment. Also, solid differences between ND and HFP animals in caloric intake are showed, besides the clear histological damage in HF model indicates model was undoubtedly stablished.

### Pirfenidone binds to Jmjd2b and could modified its activity
  Figure 6 shows the molecular docking analysis between Jmjd2b and Lutidinic acid, as well as pirfenidone. Pirfenidone is a 2-pyridone with phenyl and methyl groups at position 1 and 5, respectively (Fig. 6A). Lutidinic acid is a 2,4-dicarboxylic acid, being a JMJD2B inhibitor since it competes with the co-substrate alpha-ketoglutarate (Fig. 6A). Figure 6B represents Protein Data Bank (PDB) crystal structure of JMJD2B. In the 3D reconstruction, 12 alpha-helixes and 12 beta-sheets can be seen. The JMJD2B catalytic site (JmjC) is found from amino acids 143 to 309 as depicted in 6B upper panel. In Fig. 6C, ten possible interactions between JMJD2B catalytic site and pyridinedicarboxylic acid are shown. In addition, 15 simulations were performed with pirfenidone and the complete JMJD2B molecule; and another 10 simulations were run selecting only the demethylation-implicated JmjC-domain. For each possible protein-ligand interaction, affinity strength was obtained using Autodock Vina 1.1.2 win32, as Gibbs energy (Supplemental Fig. 1). Results showed a great strength of negative affinity for pyridinedicarboxylic acid affinity (kcal/mol): -7.3 RMSD l.b. and the RMSD u.b.: 0.000. Affinity of pirfenidone with the whole JMJD2B molecule was in similar levels (kcal/mol): -7.4 RMSD l.b. and the RMSD u.b.: 0.000. Pirfenidone binding with JmjC catalytic site indicated affinity of (kcal/mol): -8.0 RMSD l.b. and the RMSD u.b.: 0.000. Based on this analysis, the thermodynamic affinity of pirfenidone for JMJD2B could be demonstrated. Using 3D molecular visualization system PyMOL (PyMOL2), Fig. 6C showed the binding of the pyridinedicarboxylic acid ligand to JmjC domain, through six amino acids: Lys 207, His 277, His 189, Lys 242, Tyr 178, and Tyr 173. Figure 6D revealed the affinity binding of pirfenidone to JmjC domain at Lys 242, when using the complete-JMJD2B molecule. Figure 6E demonstrated pirfenidone interaction to JmjC domain through Tyr 178, when running interactions with this specific domain. These models demonstrated, structurally and thermodynamically, that PFD could interact with JMJD2B JmjC-domain, and therefore, inhibit its demethylation activity.

### Pirfenidone reduce lipotoxicity and downregulates the expression of JMJD2B in HepG2 cells
  We corroborate PFD impact in JMJD2B-expression in a lipotoxicity model using cultured HepG2 cells exposed to PA. As depicted in Fig. 7A, intracellular lipid vacuole accumulation was notable reduced after PFD treatment within HepG2 cells (p > 0.0001). Immunoblotting analysis showed that also in HepG2 cells, PFD effectively decreased JMJD2B protein versus an overexpression in PA control group as presented in Fig. 7B (p > 0.05). Increase of H3K9me3 and diminution of H3K9me was observed in nuclear extracts of PFD treated cells (Fig. 7B, p > 0.01). These findings provide compelling evidence that PFD exhibits an antagonistic behavior towards JMJD2B and effectively counteracts fat accumulation in HepG2 cells. Patter of H3K9 metilation and JMJD2B expression are the same in mice and cell cultures, where lipid damage overexpresses JMJD2B and increases monometilation mark in H3K9. This configuration is reversed by PFD in both models, indicating is not casualty and adding evidence to this possible epigenetic mechanism of PR-PFD.

## Discussion
  Epigenetic processes that regulate histone methylation patterns play a key role in the progression of NASH7,10,15; and certain molecules involved are considered therapeutic targets. Specific epigenetic marks are associated to the deregulation of lipid and glucose metabolism, insulin resistance, cell proliferation and apoptosis, all these processes play a key role in NASH progression16. Histone methylation is important for repression or activation of chromatin17 and histone 3 N-terminal tail has 4 Lysine residues that can be mono, di or tri-methylated. Is it known that H3 trimethylation can activate chromatin, as in H3K4 and H3K36 or, inactivate gene expression, as in H3K9 and H3K2718. The importance of histone methylation status is that mono, di or tri-methylations fulfill precise roles or actions in specific biological processes19,20. Modifications in histone methylation are due to certain enzymes capable to transfer or remove methyl groups18. Histone demethylases have been identified as contributing factors in the development of several diseases, including NASH, in particular the iron- and alpha-ketoglutarate dependent JmJC-domain containing proteins. The JmjC domain is present in more than 100 enzymes18,21 and is responsible for the demethylation of tri-methylated and di-methylated substrates, achieved through hydroxylation reactions. Jumonji-type demethylases belong to the cupin superfamily of oxygenases and contain an ion in their catalytic domain and use alpha-ketoglutarate as co-substrate; consists of JMJD2A, JMJD2B, JMJD2C and JMJD2D. JMJD2D efficiently demethylates di- and trimethyl H3K9 (H3K9me2/3), promoting gene transcription18,21. Here, we tested in silico binding of PFD to JMJD2B through JmjC-domain, which could explain a decreased activity of JMJD2B, and the observed augmentation of H3K9me3 in the liver of obese mice and nuclear extracts of PA-exposed HepG2 cells treated with pirfenidone. This result, correlates with hepatic transcriptome analysis where Fasn, Srebp1, Pparg Acaca, Sd1, and Cd36 genes, among others, were downregulated after PFD treatment as compared to HF animals. Specifically in the liver, JMJD2B overexpression is associated to hepatocellular carcinoma progression and to NASH development22,23. JMJD2B expression is increased in hepatosteatosis cell and animal models, indicating that JMJD2B overexpression is associated to steatosis establishment. JMJD2B overexpression has been correlated to Pparg2 upregulation promoting lipogenic gene expression, and to upregulated expression of LXR-dependent lipogenic genes through the removal of me2 and me3 marks in H3K97,8. This data correlates with ours where JMJD2B is increased in NASH mice and in PA-exposed HepG2 cells where adipogenic genes were upregulated. In contrast, Cheng et al. using KO-JMJD2B mice found that after 8w with HFD or 7months with ND animals developed obesity, insulin resistance, and lipid-serum panel increase. This fact might seem controversial with our result; however, and interestingly, liver specific deletion of JMJD2B produce mice of normal weight and body composition, on both chow and HFD24. Indicating that decrease of hepatic JMJD2B, as in our study, maintains homeostasis in energy expenditure and lipid metabolism. Similarly, to Cheng and cols.; Kang et al. showed that is explicitly the specific inactivation of JMJD2B in adipocytes, the responsible for obesity induction due to alterations in adipocyte-energy expenditure25. It is important to highlight that complete KO-JMJD2B mice had a lower birth and surviving rate, indicating the importance of JMJD2B in the whole organism24. Then, the observed not covalent bind of PFD to the lysine 178 in JmjC domain of JMJD2B (2.2°A distance); would allow a reversible union, without a complete remotion of the protein in the rest of the organism, that could affect other essential JMJD2B functions26. Importantly, pirfenidone shows molecular mimicry with pyridine-2,4-dicarboxylic acid, a potent inhibitor of JMJD2E and the enzyme JmjC domain-family27,28. Hence, it can be hypothesized that similarly, pirfenidone might function as an epigenetic regulator influencing JMJD2B expression and consequently H3K9me3 status, which reflects improvement of liver function, hepatic histological analysis and decrease in epididymal adipose tissue while restoring AST, ALT, cholesterol, triglycerides and VLDL. On the other hand, PPARG signaling pathway has been widely studied in NASH, and in addition, Fasn and Srebp1 have been considered as potential therapeutic targets29–33. Here, we demonstrated that Fasn, Srebp1 and Pparg are enriched in H3K9me3/me2 chromatin, clarifying the pathway involved in these gene regulation. In this context, pirfenidone have also previously exhibited a PPARA agonist/ligand activity, incrementing lipid oxidative pathways in the liver causing hepatic improvement in a NASH mice model. Likewise, PFD increased hepatic SIRT-1 levels, a histone deacetylase, and a key molecule for Ppara expression5. As well, other epigenetic regulators like hepatic miRNAs, have been described to be modified by PFD administration in a NASH model improving liver condition and reversing key lipogenic processes. PFD induced differential expression of miR-21a-5p, miR-34a-5p, miR-122-5p and miR-103-3p, all involved in NASH pathophysiology6. In accordance with this evidence, it could be suggested that PFD function as an epigenetic regulator, modulating hepatic gene expression.

  It is important to mention that, previously Chen et al. found that pirfenidone can reverse insulin resistance in steatohepatitis, as well as liver inflammation and fibrosis; pondering it as a potential treatment for NASH34. In our results, PFD successfully increased insulin sensitivity. Basal glucose levels were similar between the HF and HFP groups; however, after administration of insulin, animals treated with PFD demonstrated higher insulin sensitivity, bringing glucose serum levels down to normal levels. Insulin resistance is a key factor for homeostasis of lipogenic processes, then its alterations are key for NASH development. DAG can phosphorylate PKC, and in turn phosphorylate the insulin receptor, causing its activation and inhibiting insulin binding. Since DAGs are subproducts during TAG storage in NASH. In addition, JNK increases its activity during NAFLD/NASH development, promoting inflammation and liver tissue damage. JNK directly phosphorylates the insulin receptor, increasing hyperinsulinemia and hyperglycemia4,35.

  Furthermore, Chikara Komiya et al. demonstrated that pirfenidone is capable of reversing NASH-related fibrosis in MC4R-OK mice fed with a Western diet. They also emphasize the reduction of apoptotic processes in liver cells12. As known, periportal area (zone 1) consists of hepatocytes that perform mainly oxidative functions, while hepatocytes neighboring the central vein (zone 3) are specialized in anabolic mechanisms, such as lipogenesis36. Thus, during NASH process, deregulation in zone 1, causes decrease in oxidative processes, while lipogenic processes increase5. In our animals, after treatment with PFD, steatosis almost disappeared in zone 1, indicating that lipid oxidative mechanisms prevailed. In addition, after PFD treatment inflammatory nodules in liver parenchyma are almost completely vanished. Likewise, an incipient fibrosis is only observed near hepatic veins, while fibrotic bridges between zone 1 and zone 3 -noticeable in the HF group- are missing in PFD treated animals. Notably, the dose of PFD used in this study is equivalent to a 1459 mg/day dose in humans using the body surface area (BSA) normalization method; dosing that falls between the therapeutic dose range (1200-1800 mg) used in a number of clinical trial, including ours13,36,37. Further evidence about pirfenidone reversing these processes was demonstrated also in patients with residual fibrosis from hepatitis C infection, and in cirrhosis animal models13,11,38, considering in silico analysis also had demonstrated the importance of identifying key genes39,40.

  In conclusion as depicted in graphical abstract, Fig. 7C, it could be proposed that PFD acts as epigenetic modulator restoring hepatic function in NASH mice and cell models through downregulation of JMJD2B and H3K9 methylation alteration. Also, PFD modifies expression of molecules that promote an increase in lipid oxidation in hepatocytes. Consequently, PFD might be studied in clinical trials to corroborate these findings in MASLD patients.
