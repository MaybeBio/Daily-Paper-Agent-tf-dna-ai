# Novel Antioxidant
Peptides from Fermented Whey Protein
by Lactobacillus rhamnosus B2-1: Separation and Identification
by in Vitro and in Silico Approaches


## Abstract
  

  Whey is a byproduct of the dairy industry and is rich in protein. To enhance the significance of such byproducts and find efficacious antioxidants for combating oxidative stress, this study reported on the preparation, purification, and identification of novel peptides with antioxidant activities from whey protein metabolites following fermentation by Lactobacillus rhamnosus B2-1. The isolation and identification processes involved macroporous adsorption resin column chromatography, gel filtration column chromatography, and liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry. Therein, three novel antioxidant peptides (PKYPVEPF, LEASPEVI, and YPFPGPIHNS) were selected to be synthesized, and they demonstrated remarkable antioxidant activities in vitro chemical assays. PKYPVEPF, LEASPEVI, and YPFPGPIHNS (100 μg/mL) displayed a notable cytoprotective impact on HepG2 cells under oxidative stress induced by H2O2, increasing the cell viability from 49.02 ± 3.05% to 88.59 ± 10.49%, 82.38 ± 19.16%, and 85.15 ± 7.19%, respectively. Moreover, the peptides boosted the activities of catalase and superoxide dismutase in damaged cells and reduced reactive oxygen species levels. The molecular docking studies highlighted that these antioxidant peptides efficiently bound to key amino acids in the Kelch domain of Keap1, thereby preventing the interaction between Keap1 and Nrf2. In conclusion, PKYPVEPF, LEASPEVI, and YPFPGPIHNS demonstrated substantial antioxidant activity, suggesting their potential for widespread application as functional food additives and ingredients.


## Introduction
  Reactive oxygen species (ROS), encompassing superoxide anion radicals (O2•–), hydroxyl radicals (•OH), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and nitric oxide free radicals (NO•), are byproducts of normal cellular metabolism. While a certain level of ROS is essential for physiological processes, their excessive accumulation can contribute to oxidative stress, inducing damage to the human body.1 Oxidative stress can lead to detrimental effects such as protein cross-linking, lipid peroxidation, and damage to RNA and DNA, which may result in the development of chronic diseases.2 Antioxidant agents are substances that can capture and neutralize ROS, thereby mitigating their harmful effects on the human body. Therefore, supplementing with antioxidant agents in food and drug processes is a preferred strategy to combat the damage caused by excessive ROS. Given the adverse health effects associated with artificial antioxidants, such as carcinogenesis and high costs, there is an increasing inclination toward exploring natural, efficient, and nontoxic alternatives. The development and application of these natural antioxidants hold promising prospects as substitutes for synthetic ones.3 In addition to widely recognized antioxidants like phenolic and flavonoid compounds, there is increasing evidence in the scientific literature focusing on the evaluation and characterization of antioxidant peptides sourced from food and its processing byproducts. These peptides have revealed antioxidant effects both in vivo and in vitro.4,5 Various food sources, including agrocybe aegerita,6 tilapia,7 mushrooms,8 rice bran,9 and monkfish,10 have been explored for the extraction of antioxidant peptides due to their notable abilities to scavenge ROS, provide cytoprotective effects, and protect DNA from oxidative damage. Furthermore, hydrolysates derived from diverse food-processing byproducts, such as porcine liver,11 Baijiu vinasse,12 and broken rice,13 have been highlighted for their beneficial antioxidant activity. This highlights the potential applications of these sources as replacements for synthetic antioxidants in the food industry.

  Whey protein, constituting approximately 20% of the total milk protein, contains a rich array of physiologically active molecules. Through fermentation, whey protein is broken down into smaller peptides that exhibit biological activity across various physiological systems. In addition, peptides released by fermentation are believed to be safer and healthier, because they are obtained from edible food proteins. This method is more cost-effective than enzymatic hydrolysis since commercial enzymes are expensive, but the culture costs are considerably lower.14 Fermentation of whey by microbial strains offers the potential to reduce its high lactose content, contributing to the production of lactic acid and metabolites, among which are bioactive peptides with antioxidant properties. Moreover, the enzymatic action of microbial strains, particularly their proteolytic activity, can significantly increase the yield of bioactive peptides. Substantial evidence supports this finding.15,16

  Antioxidant enzymes, encompassing catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), are critically involved in the body’s defense against oxidative stress. Research indicates that the Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1)-nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) pathway modulates the antioxidant enzyme defense system.17 Furthermore, research has demonstrated that antioxidant peptides competitively bind to Keap1, thereby inhibiting the development of the Keap1-Nrf2 complex.18 In these cases, numerous researchers have suggested that disrupting the Keap1-Nrf2 interaction and preserving the integrity of Nrf2 are key steps in reducing free radical damage. Certain researchers have documented that peptides can disrupt the binding between Keap1 and Nrf2, leading to increased levels of Nrf2 in the nucleus.19,20 This process allows peptides to exert their antioxidant effects by binding to the site of Nrf2 within the Keap1 pocket. Hence, the identification of peptides capable of binding to Keap1 is crucial for the identification of natural antioxidant peptides. This plays a vital role in elucidating the mechanism of action of antioxidant peptides.

  Indeed, our prior investigations have indicated the potent antioxidant properties of the byproducts generated from whey protein fermented by Lactobacillus rhamnosus B2-1 (L. rhamnosus B2-1), indicating their promise as antioxidants.21 However, the exact sequences of antioxidant peptides and their mechanisms of action remain unconfirmed. Based on these considerations, this study conducted the preparation, purification, and detection of novel antioxidant peptides obtained from whey protein hydrolysate by L. rhamnosus B2-1 fermentation. The synthesis of three antioxidant peptides was followed by an evaluation of their antioxidant properties using chemical assays and an assessment of their cytoprotective impact on H2O2-triggered oxidative damage in HepG2 cells. The mechanisms underlying their antioxidant activity and cytoprotective effects were elucidated through molecular docking analyses. This research serves to establish a comprehensive theoretical foundation for the utilization of antioxidant protein resources. Additionally, it supports the development and advancement of high-activity natural antioxidants, contributing to both the scientific understanding and practical applications in this field.


## Materials and Methods

### Chemicals and Reagents
  L. rhamnosus B2-1 was acquired from the Microecological Engineering and Technology Research Center of Qiqihar Medical University. Demineralized whey powder D70 (total protein, 12.0%, lactose, 78–84%) was purchased from Alpavit (Lauben, Germany). HepG2 cell lines were obtained from the Cell Resource Center, Peking Union Medical College. The following reagents were procured from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA): 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT), 2,2′-azinobis(3-ethylbenzthiazoline-6-sulfonate) (ABTS), 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA), Trolox, and fetal bovine serum (FBS). Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM) was sourced from Gibco BRL Life Technology (Gaithersburg, USA). Kits for SOD and CAT were acquired from Nanjing Jiancheng (Nanjing, China). Macroporous resin D101 was obtained from Amicogen Biopharm Co., Ltd. (Jining, China), and Sephadex G-50 and G-15 were acquired from GE Healthcare Bio-Sciences AB Co., Ltd. (Germany). Analytical-grade chemicals and reagents were used.


### Peptide Synthesis
  The peptides KVLI (AP1), VALALARE (AP2), EVF (AP3), VFR (AP4), KFL (AP7), PKYPVEPF (AP8), LEASPEVI (AP9), and YPFPGPIHNS (AP12) (each purity ≥95%) were synthesized by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).


### Preparation of Whey Protein
Hydrolysates
  L. rhamnosus B2-1 was cultured in 1 mL of whey protein-MRS medium comprising beef extract (10 g), desalted whey powder (10 g), glucose (20 g), Tween 80 (1 mL), yeast extract (5 g), sodium acetate (5 g), magnesium sulfate (0.2 g), dipotassium hydrogen phosphate (2 g), sodium citrate (2 g), manganese sulfate (0.05 g), and water to make up to 1 L. The medium was sterilized at 121 °C for 15 min and was then subjected to anaerobic incubation at 37 °C for 48 h. After two successive expansions, the culture was transferred to a fresh whey protein-MRS medium and incubated for an additional 48 h. Subsequently, the culture was centrifugated for 10 min at 6000 r/min, and the supernatant was lyophilized.21


### Extraction and Purification of Antioxidant Peptides

#### Macroporous
Adsorption Resin Column Chromatography
  For the initial separation, the fermentation broths underwent macroporous adsorption resin column chromatography using a D101 resin. The broths were loaded on a D101 resin column (26 × 300 mm) and sequentially eluted with deionized water, 50% ethanol, and 100% ethanol solutions at a flow rate of 2.5 mL/min. The absorbance reading of the eluent at 214 and 280 nm was noted, and three fractions were collected, followed by rotary evaporation and freeze-drying, and then assessed in vitro at 2.0 mg/mL.


#### Gel Filtration Column Chromatography
  After the initial separation, the fraction exhibiting the greatest antioxidant activity underwent additional separation and purification. Sephadex G-50 and G-15 chromatography columns, each measuring 16 mm × 100 cm, were selected for this study. Before use, these columns were pre-equilibrated with deionized water to ensure optimal performance. The column was eluted with deionized water at a rate of 0.7 mL/min per tube, with absorbance readings at 214 and 280 nm. Various fractions were collected, subjected to rotary evaporation, freeze-dried, and subsequently assessed in vitro at 1.0 and 0.5 mg/mL, respectively.

### Detection of Antioxidant Peptides with Liquid
Chromatography
with Tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS/MS)
  After purification with Sephadex G-15 chromatography, the fraction exhibiting the greatest antioxidant activity (F3-3) underwent additional identification using LC-MS/MS. The elution conditions were as follows: solution A comprised 0.1% formic acid in water, while solution B comprised 0.1% acetonitrile formic acid (84% acetonitrile). Initially, an RP-C18 liquid chromatography column (with dimensions of 0.15 mm × 150 mm, manufactured by Column Technology Inc.) was primed by equilibration with a solution consisting of 95% solution A. Subsequently, samples were loaded from the autosampler onto Zorbax 300SB-C18 peptide traps (Agilent Technologies, Wilmington, DE), followed by separation through the column. The liquid phase gradient was set as follows: 0–50 min, 4–50% solution B; 50–54 min, 50–100% solution B; 54–60 min, 100% solution B. The products underwent separation using capillary HPLC and were examined by mass spectrometry utilizing a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher) over a 60 min period. The raw mass spectra files (Raw File) underwent processing utilizing MaxQuant version 1.5.5.1, which aided in a series of calculations to score peptide sequences with high confidence.


## Screening of Antioxidant
Peptides Using in Silico Methods
  The peptides identified were aligned with known antioxidative peptides in DFBP (http://www.cqudfbp.net/) to identify potential new antioxidative peptides.22 PeptideRanker (http://distilldeep.ucd.ie/PeptideRanker/) was utilized to score the potentially unknown antioxidant peptides.13 The screening criterion is that the score of the peptide is higher than 0.5. The solubility of the peptides was determined using the peptide property calculator (http://www.innovagen.com/proteomics-tools).23 Moreover, ToxicPred was utilized for predicting the toxicity of peptides (https://webs.iiitd.edu.in/raghava/toxinpred/).13


### Synthesis of Antioxidant Peptides
  Synthesis of antioxidant peptides was executed via the solid-phase synthesis method at Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China).


### Determination of in Vitro Biological Antioxidant
Activity

#### Cytotoxicity Assay
  HepG2 cells were grown in DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), ampicillin sodium, and streptomycin sulfate. The cells were kept at 37 °C in a humid environment with 5% CO2. The cytotoxicity of antioxidant peptides on HepG2 cells was examined using the MTT assay, following a previously reported method with slight modifications.24 Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were collected and plated at a density of 7 × 103 cells/well in 96-well plates. After the cells were allowed to grow overnight, the medium was replaced with peptide solutions of varying concentrations for 24 h. Upon completion of the incubation period, the plates underwent PBS washing followed by treatment with 20 μL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL) for 3 h. Subsequently, the supernatant was aspirated and subjected to dissolution in 150 μL of DMSO. The absorbance at 490 nm was subsequently measured utilizing a microplate reader to examine cell viability (Spark, TECAN, Switzerland). The formula used to calculate cell viability is as follows:4


#### Development of a HepG2 Cell Model of Oxidative Damage Triggered
by H2O2
  H2O2 was used for establishing the oxidative damage model of HepG2 cells according to the previous method with slight modifications.25 HepG2 cells were inoculated at a density of 7 × 103 cells/well into 96-well plates. Following 24 h, each well received 100 μL of H2O2 solution (0.1, 0.5, 1, 1.5, and 2 mM) or 100 μL of DMEM medium as a control group and was then incubated for 3 h. Cell viability was subsequently determined using the MTT assay.


#### Cytoprotective Impact of
Peptides on HepG2 Cells Triggered by
H2O2
  To develop an oxidative damage model of HepG2 cells, a concentration of 1 mM H2O2 was utilized. HepG2 cells were inoculated in 96-well plates at a density of 7 × 103 cells/well and underwent incubation for 24 h. The damaged and control groups received culture medium (100 μL) for 24 h, whereas the protective group was treated with 100 μL of peptide solution (25 and 100 μg/mL) for the same duration. Following this incubation, 50 μL of H2O2 solution was applied to the damaged and protective groups for 3 h. Cell viability was subsequently measured by using the MTT assay.


#### Impact of Peptides on Intracellular ROS Production
in H2O2-Induced HepG2 Cells
  Intracellular ROS generation was measured following a modified version of a previously reported method.23 The experimental setup mirrored that used for evaluating the cytoprotective impact of peptides on HepG2 cells exposed to H2O2. The cells were exposed to a 10 μM DCFH-DA solution and maintained at 37 °C for a duration of 30 min. Following this incubation period, the extracellular DCFH-DA was removed, and the cells were rinsed three times using PBS. The fluorescence intensity was recorded by using a microplate reader (Spark, TECAN, Switzerland). Emission was detected at 528 nm, followed by excitation at 485 nm.


#### Impact of Peptides on the Activity of Antioxidant
Enzymes in
H2O2-Induced HepG2 Cells
  The assay was conducted following previous methods.10 HepG2 cells were inoculated into six-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells/well in 2 mL of medium and grouped as outlined in the methodology for assessing the cytoprotective impact of peptides on H2O2-triggered HepG2 cells. The protective group received peptide solutions at 25 and 100 μg/mL. The damaged and protective groups were then exposed to H2O2. The cells were collected by using 0.25% trypsin, and the digestion was halted with the addition of a culture medium. The cells were rinsed twice with PBS, and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 10 min. This process was followed by the collection of cells from each well with subsequent resuspension in 0.5 mL of PBS. The SOD and CAT activities were then detected by utilizing commercial kits. The cell suspensions were then examined concerning their protein concentration using the BCA method to normalize SOD and CAT levels, which were presented as U/mg of prot.


### Molecular Docking
  Molecular docking analysis was executed utilizing the AutoDock Vina program to explore the potential binding modes of the antioxidant peptide with the human Keap1 receptor protein as potential inhibitors.26 This analysis examined the binding configuration between the antioxidant peptide and the Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (PDB code: 4L7B). ChemDraw software was utilized to draw the structural formula of the substrate peptide, while Chem3D software was employed to optimize its energy and save it in the PDB format. AutoDock Tools 1.5.6 was then used to add charges and perform other processing steps on the small peptide’s PDB file, ultimately saving it in PDBQT format. Using the AutoDock Tools 1.5.6 graphical interface, polar hydrogen atoms and charges were introduced to the proteins, and the receptor PDB format file was converted to PDBQT format. The search space for the protein molecules was defined, and an appropriate grid was constructed in the x-, y-, and z-axis directions with an exhaustiveness setting of 8. The molecular docking process was then carried out following standard procedures. The docking results were represented by binding energy, with a lower energy value indicating a stronger binding affinity between the ligand and the protein.


### Statistical Analysis
  All experiments were repeated in triplicate, and statistical analysis was performed using the software SPSS 26.0 and Origin 8.0. Experimental data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and significant differences with a level of p < 0.05 were evaluated using the one-way ANOVA Duncan post hoc test.


## Results and Discussion

### Extraction
of Antioxidant Peptides
  The resulting mixture, after hydrolysis, known as protein hydrolysate, comprises partially hydrolyzed proteins, free amino acids, and peptides with diverse hydrophobicity, chain lengths, and net charges. To isolate specific peptides from these hydrolysates, additional purification steps are required. Consequently, the released antioxidant peptides undergo several purification processes before their characteristics are evaluated.

  The freeze-dried powder derived from the fermentation broth containing whey protein metabolites produced by L. rhamnosus B2-1 was subjected to separation through macroporous adsorption resin column chromatography using D101 resin. This process resulted in the successive separation of three distinct peaks, designated as D1, D2, and D3 (Figure 1A). Their antioxidant activities were assessed using three different in vitro assays, each based on distinct mechanisms: •OH scavenging activity, ABTS•+ scavenging activity, and oxygen radical absorbance capacity. The results demonstrated that D2 exhibited the highest antioxidant activities, with an ABTS•+ scavenging activity of 92.98 ± 0.07% (p < 0.05), •OH scavenging activity of 41.74 ± 0.24%, and the relative ORAC value of 1079.95 ± 4.79 μM Trolox equivalent/g (p < 0.05) at 2.0 mg/mL (Figure 1B). These findings indicate that D2 exhibited the highest antioxidant activity, suggesting that it is likely enriched with more potent antioxidant peptides. In summary, D2 was chosen for further purification using Sephadex G-50 and G-15 chromatography. As depicted in Figure 1C, three fractions (F1–F3) were separated by Sephadex G-50 chromatography, and their antioxidant activities were evaluated. Both F2 and F3 demonstrated significant ABTS•+ scavenging abilities. The scavenging activity of F2 (92.83 ± 0.07%) was slightly higher than that of F3 (92.35 ± 0.18%), with no significant differences observed between F2 and F3 at 1.0 mg/mL. However, the results of the other two experiments highlighted that F3 had the highest •OH scavenging activity (39.69 ± 1.47%) (p < 0.05) and the relative ORAC values (1170.03 ± 24.07 μM Trolox equivalent/g) (p < 0.05) at 1.0 mg/mL. Based on these results, F3 was subjected to further separation into three subfractions (F3-1–F3-3) using Sephadex G-15 chromatography. The antioxidant activity of each subfraction at a concentration of 0.5 mg/mL is illustrated in Figure 1F. The antioxidant activities of F3-3 were elevated compared with those of separations, with an ABTS•+ scavenging activity of 92.61 ± 0.75%, a •OH scavenging activity of 40.38 ± 2.83%, and the relative ORAC value of 1534.62 ± 8.82 μM Trolox equivalent/g (p < 0.05).

  The antioxidative capacities of bioactive peptides in vitro have been assessed by using a combination of chemical methods and advanced technologies. Nevertheless, a single technique is insufficient to fully characterize the antioxidant activity of the peptides. Therefore, multiple antioxidant activity measurement methods should be used to provide a comprehensive assessment of the antioxidant potential of bioactive peptides. Patel et al.27 evaluated the antioxidative activities of fermentation peptides through •OH and ABTS•+ scavenging assays. In earlier studies, researchers hydrolyzed a pecan protein isolate to acquire an antioxidant peptide. The purified peptide demonstrated robust scavenging activity against ABTS•+, 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), and •OH, effectively slowing down oxidation reactions.28 Gu et al.29 applied DPPH radical scavenging assays and ORAC assays to assess the antioxidant capacity of peptides identified from the frog H. guentheri.

  The antioxidative mechanisms and findings examined by various antioxidant tests can vary significantly, making it challenging to compare one method directly with another. Additionally, the diverse structural characteristics of antioxidant peptides suggest that their activity fails to be accurately inferred from a single test model. The assays used to measure antioxidant activity are classified based on the principle of the chemical reaction involved, which primarily occurs through hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) and electronic transfer (ET) pathways. The ORAC method is mainly based on the HAT mechanism and quantifies the ability of antioxidants to decompose free radicals. Based on the mixed-mode assays (HAT/ET), the ABTS assay measures the extent of ABTS free radical neutralization by antioxidants, where the presence of antioxidants results in reduced absorption at 734 nm, leading to a lighter-color solution.30 The determination of •OH scavenging activity assay is mainly through the Fenton reaction, and the change in the redox state of the solution, as well as the antioxidant activity of the substance tested, can be indirectly reflected according to the change of absorbance.31 Therefore, this study used various methods to examine the antioxidant properties comprehensively, with the aim of better understanding their potential protective impacts. Based on the above experimental results, F3-3 was chosen for further sequence analysis.


### Screening and Identification of Antioxidant Peptides
  Following several purification steps, F3-3 underwent analysis by LC-MS/MS to determine the peptide composition and amino acid sequences. A total of 23 peptides (designated as AP1 to AP23) were identified, typically comprising 3 to 10 amino acids with molecular weights (MWs) varying from 359.21 to 1240.61 Da (Table 1). In numerous studies, the possible mechanisms behind the antioxidant activity of peptides have been examined, with the active antioxidant potential primarily linked to their MWs, amino acid composition, and certain functional properties such as hydrophobicity.32 Previous studies have suggested that smaller antioxidant peptides, typically with MWs ranging between 500 and 1500 Da, may contribute more to antioxidant activity relative to larger peptides.33 Regarding nutritional bioactivity, smaller antioxidant peptides are also known to traverse intestinal epithelial cells more readily and are absorbed more efficiently by the body. Compared with other hydrophilic amino acids, peptides exhibiting high antioxidant activity have been recorded to contain an elevated proportion of hydrophobic amino acids. This characteristic is considered a critical factor enabling these peptides to exhibit their antioxidant capacity. Proline (Pro), alanine (Ala), leucine (Leu), valine (Val), isoleucine (Ile), methionine (Met), tryptophan (Trp), and phenylalanine (Phe) are classified as hydrophobic amino acids.34 The inclusion of hydrophobic amino acids is pivotal for the scavenging of free radicals, enhancing the peptide solubility in lipidic food systems. Furthermore, the presence of hydrophobic amino acids has the potential to enhance the interaction between antioxidant peptides and fatty acids, thus mitigating lipid peroxidation.35 Additionally, the biological activity of these peptides, initially identified, was assessed based on their ranker score. Peptides with a ranking value exceeding 0.5 demonstrate a heightened probability of exhibiting bioactivity. Finally, a comparison of the peptide sequences was conducted against the DFBP database, and previously identified antioxidant peptides were removed. Among them, AP5, AP6, and AP10 had been previously reported in the literature, confirming the feasibility of our experimental approach.36,37

  Utilizing these criteria, this study identified eight antioxidant peptides with low MW, rich in hydrophobic amino acids, or high predicted bioactivity scores through peptide synthesis, and their antioxidant activities were extensively investigated in subsequent analyses. The MWs and hydrophobic amino acid proportions of the peptides are as follows: KVLI (AP1, 471.34 Da), VALALARE (AP2, 841.50 Da), EVF (AP3, 393.19 Da), VFR (AP4, 420.25 Da), KFL (AP7, 406.26 Da), PKYPVEPF (AP8, 975.51 Da), LEASPEVI (AP9, 856.45 Da), and YPFPGPIHNS (AP12, 1127.54 Da). Among these peptides, all exhibited good water solubility, except for AP12, which exhibited low water solubility. Additionally, all peptides were determined to be nontoxic. The MS/MS spectra of the eight antioxidant peptides are depicted in Figure 2 and Figure S1. This observation aligns with the understanding that antioxidant peptides typically comprise 2–20 amino acid residues with a MW < 6000 Da.38

  As indicated in Table 2, AP9 and AP12 exhibited notable scavenging activity against •OH, with respective half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of 1.869 and 2.299 mg/mL, respectively. Particularly noteworthy was the superior ABTS•+ scavenging ability of AP12 with IC50 values of 0.031 mg/mL, followed by AP8 (0.034 mg/mL), AP9 (0.0423 mg/mL), and AP2 (1.345 mg/mL). Additionally, AP12 demonstrated higher antioxidant efficacy than other peptides regarding oxygen radical absorbance capacity, with relative ORAC values of 6449.45 ± 186.51 μM Trolox equivalent/g, followed by AP8 (5454.93 ± 11.66 μM Trolox equivalent/g), AP9 (3818.35 ± 187.94 μM Trolox equivalent/g), and AP2 (3781.55 ± 129.91 μM Trolox equivalent/g).

  In general, aromatic amino acids such as Tyr feature a phenolic hydroxyl group, allowing them to directly capture free radicals by serving as hydrogen donors, thereby fulfilling the role of scavenging free radicals.39 The ABTS•+ readily interacts with any hydroxylated aromatic compound. Consequently, the phenolic −OH group present in Tyr serves as a free radical scavenger by directly providing hydrogen to ABTS•+, resulting in the stabilization of ABTS•+ as a molecule. Furthermore, the ORAC method relies on the mechanism of HAT. Amino acids like Tyr, with phenolic and indole groups, exhibit the capacity to donate hydrogen; thus, peptides containing these amino acids demonstrate elevated ORAC values. Hence, only AP8 and AP12, which contain a Tyr residue in this sequence, exhibited potent ABTS•+ radical scavenging activity and higher the relative ORAC values, relative to other peptides. This finding aligns with previous research, indicating that peptides such as YLVN and TFY, which harbor Tyr residues, exhibit notable ABTS•+ scavenging activities and elevate ORAC values, likely attributed to the presence of Tyr residues.

  Polar amino acid residues, particularly acidic residues such as Glu and Arg, are pivotal for the scavenging of •OH and the chelation of metal ions by antioxidant peptides. This is due to the presence of carboxyl and amino groups in their side chains. Moreover, Pro serves as a heterocyclic amino acid capable of inhibiting ROS generation and apoptosis in mammalian cells. Mammalian cell experiments suggest that Pro acts as a broad-spectrum antioxidant and a proficient scavenger of •OH, offering crucial cellular protection. Additionally, the presence of Pro not only enhances antioxidant activity but also facilitates peptide interaction with lipid bilayer membranes, facilitating them to reach target organs effectively.40 Furthermore, another study suggested that Ser, Pro, Ala, and Val residues have a propensity to form hydrogen bonds with the receptor. Notably, Pro and Val amino acid residues are inclined to engage in hydrophobic interactions with the receptor. This observation may offer valuable insights into the structure–activity relationship of antioxidant peptides.24 Similar outcomes were documented by Sheng et al.,41 suggesting that Pro, Ser, Val, Ile, and Leu within the sequence of PSVSLT contribute to scavenging excess •OH, thereby inhibiting oxidative stress in cells and biological tissues. Likewise, the antioxidant peptides EDIVCW and MEPVW, purified from the hydrolysate of monkfish, exhibited high scavenging activities against •OH attributed to the presence of Glu, Pro, and Val residues.10 Hence, peptides such as AP3, AP9, and AP12, containing the amino acid residues mentioned earlier, exhibited more potent •OH scavenging activity compared with other peptides. Generally, peptides can stabilize free radicals by either donating electrons to them or absorbing electrons from them. In both scenarios, the reactivity of the free radicals is significantly diminished.

  The antioxidant capacity of peptides is influenced by various factors, with the amino acid compositions of the N-terminal and C-terminal regions being a crucial determinant. The presence of hydrophobic amino acids in these regions can enhance the antioxidant activity of the peptides. In this study, all of the selected APs contained hydrophobic amino acids at either the N-terminal or C-terminal region, thus indicating that the presence of hydrophobic amino acids in these regions was associated with higher antioxidant activity.

  Variations in the antioxidant activities of peptides may be caused by their MW, as smaller peptides are more advantageous in penetrating cells and tissues and exerting their functions. In this study, the MWs of the selected APs ranged from 393.1900 to 1127.5400 Da, facilitating their accessibility to target sites and efficient scavenging of excess free radicals. Nevertheless, despite AP1 containing the highest proportion of hydrophobic amino acids, its ability to scavenge •OH and ABTS•+ was deemed unsatisfactory. Research suggests that the positioning of active amino acids also significantly influences the antioxidant activity of peptides.42 To summarize, the link observed between the structure and functionality of the identified peptides indicates that AP2, AP3, AP8, AP9, and AP12 hold promise for exhibiting remarkable antioxidant efficacy. Therefore, these five novel peptides were chosen for further investigation.


### Cytotoxicity Assay of Antioxidant Peptides
  HepG2 cells, derived from human hepatoma, are highly differentiated and free of virus, closely resembling human primary hepatocytes in terms of metabolism and biotransformation activities. Consequently, HepG2 cells are widely used in assays. Ren et al.,43 Hu et al.,44 and Wen et al.45 have evaluated the antioxidant properties of the peptides using HepG2 cells. Therefore, the cytotoxic effects of the five selected peptides were determined in HepG2 cells by the MTT assay. As shown in Figure 3A, the peptides did not exhibit significant toxicity and even promoted cell proliferation at a concentration of 0.5–500 μg/mL relative to the control group. Based on these outcomes, the five antioxidant peptides can be chosen for further cellular antioxidant experiments within this concentration range.


### Cytoprotective Impact of Antioxidant Peptides on Oxidative Damage
in HepG2 Cells

#### Development of a Model Demonstrating Oxidative
Damage in HepG2
Cells Triggered by H2O2
  H2O2, generated as a byproduct of oxygen metabolism, occurs ubiquitously within cells and can function as a Fenton reactant, triggering oxidative stress in vivo. The extent and intensity of cell damage are contingent upon the duration and concentration of H2O2 exposure, ultimately leading to reduced cell viability. H2O2 treatment is a traditional method for creating an oxidatively damaged cell model. Achieving a cell viability of 50–60% relative to untreated cells is considered ideal for studying the effects of antioxidants on injured cells.23,46 As illustrated in Figure 3B, when establishing an H2O2-triggered oxidative damage model, the viability of HepG2 cells declined with increasing concentrations of H2O2, with 1 mM H2O2 reducing cell viability by approximately 50%. This reduction is likely due to the ability of H2O2 to easily penetrate cell membranes and oxidize unsaturated fatty acids, inducing oxidative stress and triggering a lipid peroxidation chain reaction, ultimately resulting in apoptosis.47 Therefore, to develop the oxidative damage model, HepG2 cells were exposed to 1 mM H2O2 for 3 h, resulting in cell viability of 49.91 ± 5.66%, satisfying the modeling requirements. This moderate level of oxidative damage provides an optimal condition for conducting subsequent experiments on damage protection, enabling a precise evaluation of the impact of the sample on the model.


#### Cytoprotective
Impact of Antioxidant Peptides against Cellular
Damage
  The cytoprotective impact of the antioxidant peptides against cells is illustrated in Figure 3C. In comparison to the control group, the cell viability in the damaged group notably decreased to 49.02 ± 3.05% (p < 0.05), confirming the successful and stable establishment of the cell damage model. As shown in Figure 3C, treatment with the five antioxidant peptides contributed to varying degrees of improvement in the cell viability of the experimental group relative to the damaged group. These outcomes demonstrate that the five antioxidant peptides have a significant cytoprotective impact against H2O2-induced oxidative damage in HepG2 cells. When treated with 25 μg/mL of the antioxidant peptides, the viability of HepG2 cells increased as follows: in the case of AP2: from 49.02 ± 3.05% to 65.18 ± 3.03%, AP3: to 64.01 ± 3.60%, AP8: to 65.53 ± 4.98%, AP9: to 66.23 ± 4.70%, and AP12: to 58.41 ± 7.39%. When the concentration of the peptides was increased to 100 μg/mL, there was a notable enhancement in HepG2 cell viability. Specifically, cell viability increased from 49.02 ± 3.05% to 71.62 ± 6.69% with AP2, 72.79 ± 15.36% with AP3, 88.59 ± 10.49% with AP8, 82.38 ± 19.16% with AP9, and 85.15 ± 7.19% with AP12. Among these, the antioxidant peptides AP8, AP9, and AP12 demonstrated notably enhanced cell viability relative to AP2 and AP3. To further explore the impact of the three antioxidant peptides (AP8, AP9, and AP12) on intracellular antioxidant enzymes CAT and SOD in HepG2 cells, additional tests were conducted. Comparable findings were observed in a study by Hu et al.,10 where antioxidant peptides derived from monkfish muscle protein hydrolysate demonstrated a significant protective impact against H2O2-triggered oxidative damage HepG2 cells, particularly at high concentrations.


#### Effects of Antioxidant
Peptides on Intracellular ROS Production
  ROS are byproducts of cellular metabolism and can cause oxidative stress, leading to apoptosis and cell death at moderate to high concentrations. Antioxidant peptides mitigate ROS effects by donating hydrogen or electrons, thus neutralizing these reactive molecules. Tang et al.48 investigated the impact of antioxidant peptides isolated from Hydrilla verticillata (Linn. f.) Royle (HVR) on ROS levels in H2O2-triggered HepG2 cells. Additionally, accumulating evidence supports the significant role of antioxidant peptides in mitigating ROS. For example, Li et al.49 identified a novel antioxidant peptide, EDEQKFWGK, from porcine plasma hydrolysate (PPH). This peptide exhibited good antioxidant properties attributed to the presence of hydrophobic amino acids, aromatic amino acids, and acidic amino acids, which effectively donate hydrogen atoms or electrons to neutralize ROS. DCFH-DA, being nonfluorescent, easily permeates the cell membrane. Upon entering the cell, intracellular ROS oxidizes DCFH-DA to DCF, which subsequently emits fluorescence. This mechanism allows for the quantitative and qualitative detection of dynamic changes in intracellular ROS levels.

  The impact of the three antioxidant peptides on ROS generation is depicted in Figure 3D. When HepG2 cells were exposed to H2O2, the level of ROS observed was 332.02 ± 31.85%. This increase is extremely significant, implying that H2O2 greatly elevated ROS levels compared with the control group. Conversely, exposure to varying concentrations of the three antioxidant peptides resulted in considerably lower ROS levels relative to the damaged group (p < 0.05), with a more pronounced effect observed at higher concentrations. Specifically, at 100 μg/mL, AP8, AP9, and AP12 decreased ROS levels from 332.02 ± 31.85% to 128.63 ± 34.26%, 166.23 ± 17.46%, and 129.51 ± 27.92%, respectively. These outcomes indicate that the three antioxidant peptides effectively suppress the generation and accumulation of intracellular ROS, thereby safeguarding cells from oxidative damage.


#### Impact of Antioxidant Peptides
on Cellular Antioxidant Enzyme
Activity
  Within cells, pivotal antioxidant enzymes responsible for scavenging radicals include SOD and CAT, playing integral roles in maintaining the body’s antioxidant defense mechanisms crucial for physiological functions. These enzymes help regulate intracellular ROS levels and assist cells in resisting the oxidative damage induced by H2O2. Among them, SOD catalyzes the dismutation of superoxide radicals into H2O2 and O2, while CAT catalyzes the decomposition of H2O2 into H2O and O2, thereby mitigating oxidative damage within the body. Consequently, alterations in the activities of these antioxidant enzymes are indicative of the cellular antioxidant response.

  The protective capacity of the three antioxidant peptides on HepG2 cells subjected to H2O2-triggered oxidative damage was verified by measuring the cellular enzymatic activities after preincubation with the peptide solution. Figure 4 illustrates the impacts of the three antioxidant peptides on the activities of SOD and CAT in HepG2 cells experiencing H2O2-triggered damage. As depicted in Figure 4, exposure to 1 mM H2O2 for 3 h significantly inhibited SOD and CAT activities in the damaged group relative to the control group (p < 0.05). This finding aligns with relevant reports, indicating that oxidative damage by H2O2 leads to reduced activities of these key antioxidant enzymes in HepG2 cells.10

  In the damaged group, the SOD level decreased from 138.72 ± 1.84 to 33.86 ± 0.54 U/mg prot and the CAT level dropped from 11.07 ± 1.03 to 2.82 ± 0.73 U/mg prot relative to the control group. Pretreatment with the three antioxidant peptides at 25 and 100 μg/mL led to a dose-dependent enhancement in enzyme activity in H2O2-stressed cells, with a more pronounced effect noted in the high-dose group. Relative to the H2O2-treated group, the SOD activities in cells preincubated with the three antioxidant peptides (AP8, AP9, and AP12) at 100 μg/mL increased from 33.86 ± 0.54 U/mg prot to 109.37 ± 4.91, 127.28 ± 2.11, and 115.53 ± 10.76 U/mg prot, respectively. Similarly, CAT activities rose from 2.82 ± 0.73 U/mg prot to 7.46 ± 2.12, 6.68 ± 0.48, and 8.77 ± 2.35 U/mg prot, respectively. The outcomes confirmed that the cytoprotective impact of the three antioxidant peptides against cellular oxidative damage was linked to an enhancement in the activity of cellular antioxidant enzymes. Comparable findings have been reported for antioxidant peptides derived from yak (Bos grunniens) bone hydrolysates by Sun et al.,50 who reported that, after pretreatment with GFPGADGVA, the activities of SOD and CAT increased significantly. These findings are similar to the report of Wen et al.,45 who highlighted that pretreatment with watermelon seed protein hydrolysates significantly increased of the antioxidant enzymes activities in H2O2-induced oxidant damage cells.


#### Molecular
Docking of Antioxidant Peptides with Keap1
  The molecular docking technique predicts the interaction mode and action site between a target active substance and a receptor protein, providing detailed insights into molecular interactions. The Keap1-Nrf2 signaling pathway serves as a vital defense mechanism in the body, aiding in resistance against oxidative damage. Typically, Keap1 binds to Nrf2, facilitating its degradation and suppressing the antioxidant stress response. Nevertheless, antioxidant molecules disrupt this process by binding to the Kelch domain of Keap1. This interference inhibits the Keap1-Nrf2 interaction, thereby effectively activating the antioxidant pathway. The Keap1-Nrf2 signaling pathway not only modulates the expression of antioxidant enzymes like SOD and CAT but also represents a critical target for preventing chronic ailments, including cancer, diabetes, respiratory diseases, and neurodegenerative disorders.24 This study employed AutoDock to analyze the interactions between peptides and the Keap1 Kelch domain, providing a deeper understanding of the binding affinity and activity mechanisms of these peptides with Keap1.

  Figure S2 depicts the structural formulas of the substrate peptides, while Figure 5 depicts the three-dimensional (3D) and two-dimensional (2D) structure diagrams along with the predicted interaction modes of the peptides binding to Keap1. Notably, the images reveal that all three antioxidant peptides are adeptly inserted into the pocket of Keap1. The binding energies between Keap1 and AP8, AP9, and AP12 were calculated to be −8.299, −7.327, and −8.221 kcal/mol, respectively. AP8 exhibited the highest affinity for the hydrophobic position of Keap1, establishing hydrogen bonds with VAL467 and alkyl interactions with VAL561, VAL514, and LEU468. Additionally, it formed π–cation interactions with ARG326, while its van der Waals forces involved ILE559, VAL418, VAL608, and VAL512 residues of Keap1. On the other hand, AP9 interacted with VAL514, ARG326, and VAL512 residues of Keap1 through hydrogen bonds, while its alkyl interactions involved VAL467, VAL561, ALA607, and CYS368 residues of Keap1. Moreover, it interacted via van der Waals forces with ILE559, VAL369, VAL418, VAL608, VAL463, GLY509, LEU365, ALA466, and others. Lastly, AP12 formed hydrogen bonds with ARG380, ASN387, and ARG415 and displayed alkyl interactions with ALA558. A π–π T-type interaction was observed between a benzene ring of AP12 and the benzene ring of TYR334, along with a π–π stacking interaction with TYR525. Notably, the action sites, including VAL512, VAL604, LEU557, and LEU365, were consistent with the Keap1 binding sites reported in related studies.51 Additionally, Wang et al.52 highlighted the potential of antioxidant peptides from cottonseed protein hydrolysate to bind to Keap1, attributing it to residues such as TYR334, TYR525, ARG380, and ARG415. Wei et al.53 similarly reported that the hams-derived antioxidant peptide AR14 disrupts the Keap1-Nrf2 interaction by binding to the Nrf2 binding sites within the Keap1-Kelch structural domain. This interaction involves amino acid residues, such as ARG326, VAL369, VAL418, VAL608, VAL463, and VAL467, which align with the binding sites identified in our study.

  It has been highlighted in prior research that the Keap1-Nrf2 pathway can be modulated by antioxidant peptides, exerting their effects by interacting with key amino acid residues in Keap1.13 In conclusion, the docking results demonstrated that the three antioxidant peptides (AP8, AP9, and AP12) effectively occupied parts of the Nrf2 binding sites, thereby potentially hindering the interaction between Keap1 and Nrf2. Considering the outcomes of cell experiments, it is hypothesized that elevated Nrf2 levels in cells are sustained by these three antioxidant peptides binding to the Nrf2 binding site on Keap1, which consequently stimulates the expression of antioxidant and cytoprotective protein genes (such as CAT and SOD). The antioxidant stress response is activated by elevated Nrf2 levels in cells, thereby enhancing the scavenging of free radicals.

  In summary, eight peptides were identified from whey protein fermented by L. rhamnosus B2-1 following purification via macroporous adsorption resin column chromatography and gel filtration column chromatography. Among them, the three peptides were identified as PKYPVEPF (AP8), LEASPEVI (AP9), and YPFPGPIHNS (AP12). These peptides demonstrated high ABTS•+ scavenging, •OH scavenging, and oxygen radical absorbance capacity during the in vitro study. The studies found that molecular weight, amino acid composition, and sequence were crucial structural properties of these peptidic antioxidants that define their radical scavenging activity. In this perspective, antioxidant peptides with smaller molecular weights can contribute more to antioxidant activity when compared to the bigger peptides, because of their enhanced ability to interact with ROS and terminate free radical chain reactions. In terms of nutritional bioactivity, the smaller antioxidant peptides are also absorbed more effectively in the body. In this study, the molecular weight range of the selected APs (AP8, AP9, and AP12) was 856.45–1127.54 Da. Therefore, the screening of an appropriate molecular weight is one of the keys to optimizing the antioxidant activity of the peptides. Furthermore, the presence of hydrophobic amino acids may enhance the affinity and reactivity of peptides to the cell membrane, thereby increasing the accessibility of peptides to lipid-soluble ROS and contributing to the termination of lipid peroxidation. If their positions are in the N-terminal or C-terminal regions of the peptides, the antioxidant activity can also be improved. Therefore, the antioxidant peptides we selected (AP8, AP9, and AP12) all had high antioxidant activity. Crucially, the peptides demonstrated protective impacts on H2O2-triggered oxidative stress in HepG2 cells, with AP8, AP9, and AP12 showing particularly promising results in enhancing the oxidative defense system by reducing ROS levels. Moreover, the molecular docking results further supported the binding affinity of these three antioxidant peptides to Keap1, indicating potential interactions through hydrogen bonding, van der Waals force, alkyl interactions, and π–π interactions. The findings suggested that AP8, AP9, and AP12 may enhance cellular resistance to H2O2-triggered oxidative damage by the activation of the Keap1-Nrf2 pathway, leading to enhanced activity of antioxidant enzymes, including SOD and CAT, thus exerting antioxidant effects. Interestingly, AP8, which had the highest affinity and formed stable hydrogen bonds and alkyl interactions with the amino acid residues of Keap1, did not have relatively high CAT and SOD activities. The possible cause lies in the fact that in molecular docking, due to inherent issues such as the restricted sampling of ligand and receptor conformations and the employment of approximate scoring functions, the outcomes are not entirely consistent with those from laboratory results.54 However, molecular docking can still narrow down a large number of candidate peptides to a small number, facilitating a more targeted lab research strategy in the future, which not only saves a great deal of lab time but also allows for more efficient use of limited research resources. In addition, the role of active peptides in the body may be affected by gastrointestinal digestion. Considering the possible changes in the structure of these antioxidant peptides during digestion and metabolic transformation after absorption, future investigations should focus on exploring the in vivo potential and other potential bioactivities of these peptides.
