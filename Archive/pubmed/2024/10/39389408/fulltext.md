# Prophylactic (R,S)-ketamine and (2S,6S)-HNK decrease fear expression by differentially modulating fear neural ensembles


## BACKGROUND:
  We previously reported that a single injection of (R,S)-ketamine or its metabolite (2S,6S)-hydroxynorketamine (HNK) prior to stress attenuates learned fear. However, whether these drugs attenuate learned fear through divergent or convergent effects on neural activity remains to be determined.


## METHODS:
  129S6/SvEv male mice were injected with saline, (R,S)-ketamine, or (2S,6S)-HNK one week before a 3-shock contextual fear conditioning (CFC) paradigm. Five days later, mice were re-exposed to the aversive context, and euthanized one hour later to quantify active cells. Brains were processed for c-fos immunoreactivity, and neural networks were built with a novel, wide-scale imaging pipeline.


## RESULTS:
  We found that (R,S)-ketamine and (2S,6S)-HNK attenuate learned fear. Fear-related neural activity was altered in: dorsal CA3 following (2S,6S)-HNK; ventral CA3 and CA1, infralimbic (IL) and prelimbic (PL) regions, insular cortex (IC), retrosplenial cortex (RSP), piriform cortex (PIR), nucleus reuniens (RE), and periaqueductal grey (PAG) following both (R,S)-ketamine and (2S,6S)-HNK; and in the paraventricular nucleus of thalamus (PVT) following (R,S)-ketamine. Dorsal CA3 and ventral hippocampus activation correlated with freezing in the (R,S)-ketamine group, and RSP activation correlated with freezing in both (R,S)-ketamine and (2S,6S)-HNK groups. (R,S)-ketamine increased connectivity between cortical and subcortical regions while (2S,6S)-HNK increased connectivity within these regions.


## CONCLUSIONS:
  This work identifies novel nodes in fear networks, involving the RE, PIR, IC, PAG and RSP, that can be targeted with neuromodulatory strategies or pharmaceutical compounds to treat fear-induced disorders. This approach could be used to optimize target engagement and dosing strategies of existing medications.


## INTRODUCTION
  (R,S)-ketamine, an N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist initially developed as an anesthetic, has emerged as an efficacious rapid-acting antidepressant (1,2). (R,S)-ketamine acts within 2 hours following a single dose (2,3) in patients with major depressive disorder (MDD) and has sustained effects for approximately 1–2 weeks (1). Interestingly, we have previously reported that in addition to its antidepressant property, (R,S)-ketamine can also be a prophylactic against stress (4–9). In particular, we found that a single injection of (R,S)-ketamine administered 1 week before stress protects against stress-induced behavioral despair and attenuates learned fear in adult mice (4–9). (R,S)-ketamine’s prophylactic effect has been replicated by other laboratories (10,11) and has been shown to be effective in the clinic (12, 13), demonstrating its promising potential as a preventative treatment.

  (R,S)-ketamine is stereoselectively metabolized into various metabolites, including (R,S)-norketamine and (2R,6R;2S,6S)-hydroxynorketamine ((2R,6R;2S,6S)-HNK) (14,15). Recent studies have shown that the metabolites of (R,S)-ketamine may also exert antidepressant and prophylactic effects (7, 14). Several HNK metabolites have been found in the brain and plasma following (R,S)-ketamine infusion, with (2R,6R;2S,6S)-HNK being the predominant circulating metabolites in plasma (15,16). Although the S(+)-enantiomers (esketamine) have higher NMDAR affinity than the R(−)-enantiomers and have been recently approved by FDA for treatment-resistant depression (TRD) (17), other recent reports suggest that (R)-ketamine and (2R,6R)-HNK are more effective antidepressants and have less psychotomimetic side effects (18, 19). In line with these studies, we have recently found that (R,S)-ketamine and its metabolite (2R,6R)-HNK, but not (2S,6S)-HNK, reduce behavioral despair (7). Interestingly, we found that prophylactic administration of (2S,6S)-HNK, but not (2R,6R)-HNK, attenuates learned fear in male mice (7). At an electrophysiological level, (R,S)-ketamine and (2R,6R)-HNK have similar effects as they alters both NMDA and AMPA currents in hippocampus CA3, while (2S,6S)-HNK administration only impacts NMDA current in the same hippocampus subregion. However, whether and how systemic administration of (R,S)-ketamine or (2S,6S)-HNK affects neural activity in specific brain regions during fear retrieval is still unknown.

  Previous studies in humans (20,21) have used wide-scale imaging techniques, such as magnetoencephalography (MEG) (18) or functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) (19) to investigate how antidepressant drugs change activity throughout the brain. However, these techniques do not allow for single-cell level resolution. In contrast, a recent mouse study employed wide-scale microscopy to map cell-specific, region-specific activity by using c-fos expression as a proxy for neural activity following an acute injection of (R,S)-ketamine or the psychedelic psilocybin (22). This approach produced an unbiased list of brain regions activated by the two drugs, highlighting potential convergent targets for therapeutic effects. Nevertheless, inter-regional cross correlations between brain regions or functional network changes following drug administration were not investigated.

  In this study, we use a custom, wide-scale imaging pipeline developed in our laboratory (23) to investigate how fear related activity and functional networks change following (R,S)-ketamine and (2S,6S)-HNK administration. We show that (R,S)-ketamine and (2S,6S)-HNK differentially alter fear ensembles activity across numerous brain regions. (R,S)-ketamine increases the connectivity between cortical and subcortical regions, while (2S,6S)-HNK administration decreases the overall connectivity but increases the connectivity within cortical and subcortical regions. This work contributes to understanding neuronal activity changes following prophylactic drugs at the level of both single regions and functional networks. Understanding the circuit mechanisms of fear prophylaxis and how prophylactic drugs, such as (R,S)-ketamine and (2S,6S)-HNK alter brain activity will help development of new targeted therapies to alleviate fear-induced psychiatric disorders and improve targeting and dosing optimization with existing medications.


## METHODS AND MATERIALS
  For a full description of Methods and Materials, please refer to the Supplemental Methods in Supplement 1.


### Mice
  Male 129S6/SvEvTac mice were purchased from Taconic (Hudson, NY) at 7–8 weeks of age. Mice were housed 4–5 per cage in a 12-h (06:00–18:00) light-dark colony room at 22°C. Food and water were provided ad libitum. Behavioral testing was performed during the light phase. All mice were approximately 9–12 weeks of age. All experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the New York State Psychiatric Institute (NYSPI).


### Drugs

#### (R,S)-ketamine and (2S,6S)-HNK
  A single injection of saline (0.9% NaCl), (R,S)-ketamine (30 mg/kg) (Ketaset III, Ketamine HCl injection, Fort Dodge Animal Health, Fort Dodge, IA), (2S,6S)-HNK (synthesized by the Organic Chemistry Collaborative Center (OCCC) at Columbia University) (0.075 mg/kg), or dizocilpine (0.1mg/kg) (MK-801, Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) was administered 1 week before the start of CFC. Doses were chosen based on dose titration data from our and other studies (4–9, 14, 24).


## RESULTS

### (R,S)-ketamine and (2S,6S)-HNK attenuate learned fear but differentially change activity in ventral CA3 and CA1
  We first sought to replicate our previously published effect of (R,S)-ketamine and (2S,6S)-HNK on buffering fear behavior (7). Dosing was based on this prior study (7). Mice were injected with saline, (R,S)-ketamine (30 mg/kg), or (2S,6S)-HNK (0.075 mg/kg), and one week later were administered a 3-shock CFC paradigm (Figure 1A). Five days later they were re-exposed to the CFC context to assess fear expression. One hour following CFC re-exposure, mice were euthanized, and brains were processed for c-fos immunoreactivity. (R,S)-ketamine and (2S,6S)-HNK-injected mice froze significantly less than the saline-injected mice during CFC re-exposure (Figure 1B-C). In addition, to determine whether other NMDAR antagonists could attenuate learned fear, we injected saline or dizocilpine (0.1 mg/kg) in a separate cohort of mice (Figure S1A). In contrast to (R,S)-ketamine, dizocilpine did impact fear behavior (Figure S1B-S1C), suggesting that not all NMDAR antagonists act prophylactically against fear.

  We next sought to analyze the neural activity changes underlying this behavioral change. We selected regions known to be affected by (R,S)-ketamine, and/or participate in fear memory a priori. This precluded us from correcting for multiple comparisons (25, 26), since we tested for individual regions and their relation (27) (Table S1-2). However, we also performed a multi factor ANOVA with appropriate post-hoc tests, which revealed significant effects of Drug and Brain Region and a significant interaction of Drug x Brain Region (Table S3). We first focused on the hippocampus (HPC), an area involved in fear expression and (R,S)-ketamine effects (6, 28–30). We quantified two immediate early genes (IEGs), c-fos and Arc, as proxy for neural activity in the dorsal and ventral HPC utilizing our recently developed wide-scale imaging pipeline (23) (Figure 1D-L). In the dorsal dentate gyrus (dDG), IEG expression did not differ between groups (Figures 1G). In the dorsal CA3 (dCA3), (2S,6S)-HNK administration, but not (R,S)-ketamine decreased c-fos, but not Arc expression (Figure 1H). In dorsal CA1 (dCA1) and ventral DG (vDG), IEG expression did not differ between groups (Figures 1I, 1J, S2). In ventral CA3 (vCA3) and ventral CA1 (vCA1), (R,S)-ketamine, increased c-fos, but not Arc expression, while (2S,6S)-HNK decreased c-fos, only in vCA3 (Figure 1K-1L). The lack of drug effect on Arc expression might be due to its role in long-lasting rather than just recent information storage (31) like c-fos (22,23,32), and suggests that c-fos might be a better marker for drug-induced activation.

  To determine whether the neural activity in these brain regions correlated to freezing behavior, we performed a regression analysis to calculate the correlations between freezing during CFC re-exposure and c-fos expression. In dCA3, vDG, and vCA3, freezing (%) positively correlated with c-fos expression only in the (R,S)-ketamine group (Figure 1M-1Q). In vCA1, freezing (%) negatively correlated with c-fos expression in the (R,S)-ketamine group (Figure 1R). These data suggest that (R,S)-ketamine and (2S,6S)-HNK differentially affect neural activity in the HPC and that high dCA3, vDG and vCA3 activity or low vCA1 activity is associated with increased fear expression following (R,S)-ketamine, but not (2S,6S)-HNK administration.


### (R,S)-ketamine, but not (2S,6S)-HNK, increases neural activity in ILA and PL
  Next, we investigated whether (R,S)-ketamine or (2S,6S)-HNK changed neural activity in the prefrontal cortex (PFC) (Figure 2A) and amygdala (AMY) (Figure 2B), two regions also involved in fear expression and known to be affected by (R,S)-ketamine (33–35). In the PFC, there was a significant effect of Drug on c-fos expression (Figure 2C). In the anterior cingulate cortex (ACA), there were no differences in c-fos expression between groups. However, in the ILA and PL cortex, there was significantly higher c-fos expression in (R,S)-ketamine-, but not (2S,6S)-HNK-administered mice relative to saline (Figure 2C). In the basolateral amygdala (BLA), there was no difference between the groups (Figure 2D). In all other subregions of the AMY [(i.e., basomedial (BMA), central (CEA), and lateral (LA) amygdala], c-fos expression was comparable between all groups. In all PFC subregions and in the BLA, c-fos expression did not correlate with freezing (%) (Figure 2E-2H). However, in the BMA, c-fos expression did correlate with freezing (%) in (R,S)-ketamine-administered mice, with less c-fos expression being associated with decreased freezing (Figure 2I). In the CEA (Figure 2J) and LA (Figure 2K), c-fos expression did not correlate with freezing (%) (Figure 2H). These data indicate that neural activity in the ILA and PL is impacted by (R,S)-ketamine, but not (2S,6S)-HNK. Moreover, decreased activation of the BMA is associated with reduced fear expression in the (R,S)-ketamine group, but not (2S,6S)-HNK group.


### (R,S)-ketamine and (2S,6S)-HNK differentially modulate neural activity across multiple brain regions
  Then, we expanded c-fos expression analysis to other brain regions that have been implicated in mediating the effects of (R,S)-ketamine (36–43). Both, (R,S)-ketamine and (2S,6S)-HNK increased c-fos expression in the retrosplenial cortex (RSP) (Figure 3A, 3E), piriform cortex (PIR) (Figure 3B, 3F), nucleus reuniens (RE) (Figure 3C, 3G), and periaqueductal grey (PAG) (Figure 3D, 3H). Of note, in the RSP, c-fos expression significantly correlated with freezing behavior in both the (R,S)-ketamine and (2S,6S)-HNK groups, but not in the saline group (Figure 3I) suggesting that the RSP might be a convergent node for the prophylactic effects of these drugs. In the PIR (Figure 3J), RE (Figure 3K), and PAG (Figure 3L), c-fos expression was not correlated with freezing behavior in any group.

  Conversely, in the PVT, (R,S)-ketamine, but not (2S,6S)-HNK administration resulted in increased c-fos expression relative to saline (Figure 3M, 3P). Interestingly, in the insular cortex (IC), (R,S)-ketamine administration increased, while (2S,6S)-HNK decreased c-fos expression when compared to saline administration (Figure 3N, 3Q). In the nucleus accumbens (ACB), c-fos expression was comparable between all groups (Figure 3O, 3R). c-fos expression did not correlate with freezing behavior in any groups in the PVT (Figure 3S), IC (Figure 3T), or ACB (Figure 3U), suggesting that these regions might not be mediating the prophylactic effects of (R,S)-ketamine and (2S,6S)-HNK on fear.


### (R,S)-ketamine and (2S,6S)-HNK differentially change coordinated activity between multiple brain regions
  We next sought to determine whether these prophylactic compounds alter brain network activity to contribute to their fear buffering effects. We examined differences in correlated activity by calculating the regional pairwise cross correlations between c-fos expression in each brain region for each of the 3 groups. Relative to saline (Figure 4A), (R,S)-ketamine administration resulted in an overall increase in positive correlations between cortical and subcortical regions (Figure 4B), while (2S,6S)-HNK administration resulted in an overall decrease in positive correlations between these regions (Figure 4C). Specifically, in the (R,S)-ketamine group, there were numerous positive correlations among various thalamic nuclei; and between the HPC, ACB, AMY, and cortical regions. These patterns were weakened or absent in the (2S,6S)-HNK group, which showed negative correlations between AMY and PFC, and between vHPC and ACB.

  Next, we constructed brain networks of the 3 groups using the pairwise correlations, including only significant correlations (p < 0.05) in order to understand how (R,S)-ketamine and (2S,6S)-HNK affect the network structure (Figure 4D-4F). Relative to saline (Figure 4D), the (R,S)-ketamine network was much sparser and showed only positive correlations (Figure 4E). This pruning suggests higher organization which is consistent with the increased coordinated activity observed in the correlation matrices. Additionally, the (R,S)-ketamine network was focused around positive connections involving emotion-regulation cortices, particularly the IC and PFC (ACA, ILA, LA), as well as between AMY (LA, BLA, BMA, and CEA) and thalamic regions, with numerous connections involving the vDG and dCA3. In contrast, the (2S,6S)-HNK network showed high positive connections within the AMY (LA, CEA, BMA, and BLA) and exhibited strong negative connections between ACB, vCA3, dCA3, ACA, LA and CEA (Figure 4F). Overall, these data suggest that (R,S)-ketamine and (2S,6S)-HNK differentially alter the correlated activity of these brain regions and act through different mechanisms to attenuate learned fear.

  We further examined the differences between the correlations in the 3 groups using a permutation analysis. There were 16 inter-regional correlations where the difference was both large (R difference >= 1) and significant (p < 0.005) between saline vs. (R,S)-ketamine (Figure 4G), 19 inter-regional correlations between saline vs. (2S,6S)-HNK (Figure 4H), and 7 between (R,S)-ketamine vs. (2S,6S)-HNK (Figure 4I). In particular, we found that (R,S)-ketamine increases the correlations between regions such as the PAG and HPC regions or between PVT, PFC, and AMY regions (Figure 4J). In contrast, (2S,6S)-HNK increases the correlations within HPC regions, or between PVT with PFC regions, and decreases the correlations between AMY and PFC regions, or between IC and ACB (Figure 4K). Lastly, correlations between AMY region LA with PL, ACB and vCA1, between AMY region BLA with ACA and PVT, between PAG and dDG, or between PIR and PL inverted and decreased in the (2S,6S)-HNK group compared to the (R,S)-ketamine group (Figure 4L). These data suggest that following (2S,6S)-HNK administration, most functional connections between cortical and subcortical regions, such as AMY and PFC decrease, compared to (R,S)-ketamine administration.

  To better understand the structure and properties of the networks, we computed several network measures (Figure 4Q-S, S3). The saline network showed a trending increase in the average degree of network (i.e., how many nodes each node is connected to) (Figure 4M) compared to the (R,S)-ketamine and (2S,6S)-HNK networks, suggesting that these drugs might attenuate fear by weakening the connections between some nodes. However, all networks showed similar global efficiency (i.e., the number of connections from any one node to any other node) (Figure 4N), and clustering coefficient (i.e., the number of each node’s neighbors that are also connected to one another) (Figure 4O). The (R,S)-ketamine and (2S,6S)-HNK networks showed a trending increase in betweenness centrality (i.e., the number of shortest paths that pass through a given node) relative to saline (Figure 4P-S), suggesting that the saline network has an higher number of direct connections between regions (Figure 4D). This might prevent one particular region from controlling the flow of information, which is what might take place in the (R,S)-ketamine and (2S,6S)-HNK networks instead. To highlight individual regions that were primarily controlling the flow of information, we examined the betweenness of individual nodes in the 3 networks. We found that nodes such as IC, RSP, ACB, and CEA showed the highest betweenness centrality in the saline network (Figure 4Q). Nodes such as vDG, IC, BLA, and PVT showed the highest betweenness centrality in the (R,S)-ketamine network (Figure 4R), while nodes such as ACA, CEA, RE, and LA showed the highest betweenness centrality in the (2S,6S)-HNK network (Figure 4S). Taken together, these findings suggest that while (R,S)-ketamine is associated with a predominant centrality of vHPC, cortical, AMY and thalamic nuclei, (2S,6S)-HNK is associated with higher centrality of cortical, amygdala and thalamic nuclei and decentralization of vHPC compared to the (R,S)-ketamine network.

  We next calculated the coefficient of variation (cv, ratio of standard deviation to the mean) (Figure 5A) and the connectivity percentage (cp, percentage of neighbors with significant correlations (p < 0.05)) (Figure 5B) in the 3 groups for all 20 brain regions included in the analysis. Cv was <1 for all brain regions, indicating low data variability in all groups. Regions such as vCA3 and PL showed greater variability in c-fos expression in (R,S)-ketamine group relative to saline, which is consistent with increased c-fos expression in the (R,S)-ketamine group in these regions. We found heterogenous effects of saline, (R,S)-ketamine, and (2S,6S)-HNK on connectivity percentage on a region-by-region basis (Figure 5B). IC and PIR had the highest cp in the saline group, in line with high betweenness of these regions in this group. CEA and dCA1 cp was significantly different between (R,S)-ketamine vs. (2S,6S)-HNK groups in line with the opposite regulation induced by these drugs on CEA at a connectivity and network levels. ACA showed higher connectivity percentage in the (2S,6S)-HNK group compared to saline and (R,S)-ketamine groups in line with its high betweenness centrality in this group. This suggests that ACA might play a central role in the (2S,6S)-HNK prophylactic effects.


## DISCUSSION
  Here, we investigated the effects of (R,S)-ketamine and its metabolite (2S,6S)-HNK on fear neural ensembles in several brain regions. We used a recently developed wide-scale imaging pipeline (23) to quantify fear ensemble activity across 20 anatomically defined brain regions. We found that (R,S)-ketamine and (2S,6S)-HNK attenuate learned fear, in accord with our previous studies (5–7). Furthermore, we replicated our previous HPC results (6,7). We found that (2S,6S)-HNK, but not (R,S)-ketamine decreases dCA3 activity and that (R,S)-ketamine, but not (2S,6S)-HNK, increases vCA3 and vCA1 activity. vDG, dCA3 and vCA3 and vCA1 activation significantly correlated with freezing in the (R,S)-ketamine-, but not (2S,6S)-HNK-administered mice, suggesting that these regions might be key regions mediating prophylactic efficacy of (R,S)-ketamine, but they may not be the primary regions mediating prophylactic efficacy of (2S,6S)-HNK.

  By utilizing our recently developed wide-scale imaging pipeline (23), we expanded our analysis outside of the HPC to several regions of interest. In the PFC, we found that (R,S)-ketamine, but not (2S,6S)-HNK increased ILA and PL, but not ACA activation consistently with previous findings from our laboratory (6) and other groups (30, 44). However, previous work showed that ILA and PL play an opposite role in fear expression (45) and are differentially regulated by (R,S)-ketamine (34). Nevertheless, the use of a different type of stressor and timeline might explain why we do not observe a differential activation of PFC regions following (R,S)-ketamine.

  We also found increased activity in the RSP following both (R,S)-ketamine and (2S,6S)-HNK, in line with the role of RSP in emotional processing in humans (46) and mice (37). Moreover, the RSP is connected to regions activated by (R,S)-ketamine, such as HPC, TH, and PFC (46), and is involved in (R,S)-ketamine’s effect (37, 47) and fear responses (48). Importantly, we found that RSP activation significantly correlated with freezing in both (R,S)-ketamine and (2S,6S)-HNK groups, suggesting RSP as a novel node to target with neuromodulatory techniques or prophylactic drugs to alleviate fear disorders.

  Likewise, we found an effect of both (R,S)-ketamine and (2S,6S)-HNK on PIR and RE activation. The PIR is involved in (R,S)-ketamine effects (36, 49), and PIR-AMY connections are necessary for fear memory (50). The RE modulate memory by driving transient synchronized beta in the prefrontal-hippocampal system (51) and is involved in (R,S)-ketamine effects (40), and fear memory attenuation (52), consistent with our results. Similarly, in the PAG, activity was increased following both (R,S)-ketamine and (2S,6S)-HNK, in line with PAG’s role in fear memory (53). In particular, PAG cells in the AMY-PAG pathway show increased activity during fear retrieval (54) and are involved in the effects of (R,S)-ketamine and its metabolites on behavior (38, 39). Moreover, PFC-PAG projections are involved in stress resilience (55), suggesting that PAG is an optimal candidate to target with prophylactic drugs.

  As previously shown (22), we found (R,S)-ketamine-induced PVT activation, which is in agreement with the role of PVT-PFC connections in controlling mood stability in mice (56). Interestingly, we found that the IC, a region involved in cognitive processing and emotions (57), was oppositely regulated by (R,S)-ketamine and (2S,6S)-HNK. While (R,S)-ketamine increased IC activity as published (22, 58, 59), (2S,6S)-HNK decreased IC activation, which is also associated with antidepressant efficacy (60). Further studies characterizing the specific pathways regulating IC activation will be needed to address these contrasting results.

  Our findings are novel as they not only reveal new nodes that could be targeted with prophylactic drugs, but also how the connectivity between these nodes change following these drugs. Relative to saline, (R,S)-ketamine decreased overall connectivity between regions which relates to human fMRI studies showing that increased overall functional connectivity (61) and decreased connectivity in the cingulo-insular salience network (21) are associated with psychosis symptoms, while (R,S)-ketamine-induced reduction in cortical connectivity improves behavior (62). Conversely, work in humans showed that depression is associated with disconnection between subcortical and cortical regions, especially PFC (63,64), and (R,S)-ketamine might be therapeutic by restoring the connectivity between cortical regions and the rest of the brain up to 24 hours following administration (64, 65). Therefore, we might not be able to observe a similar increase in connectivity because of our later timeline (1 week following administration). Relative to (R,S)-ketamine, (2S,6S)-HNK decreased overall connectivity between regions, but increased connectivity within subcortical and cortical regions. In particular, (2S,6S)-HNK network showed negative correlations between LA and CEA with ACA, which might reflect an inhibition of the AMY by the PFC. This could be associated with (2S,6S)-HNK prophylactic efficacy as depressed patients lack this inhibition (66). Moreover, (2S,6S)-HNK network shows negative correlations between vCA3 and vDG with ACB which are protective against stress (67), and between PVT and CEA, which regulate fear responses (68).

  By using the permutation analysis, we found that (R,S)-ketamine increases the correlations among the PVT and RE, AMY, PFC and ACB. These data are in agreement with the role of PVT in modulating stress responses through its connection with ACB and AMY (68, 69). Moreover, PVT activity is affected by (R,S)-ketamine (70). Lastly, increased PVT connectivity with PFC and IC following (R,S)-ketamine administration is consistent with the role of this circuit in mood stabilization (59, 71). Overall, these findings are consistent with the high betweenness centrality of PVT in the (R,S)-ketamine group. The connectivity between PAG with BLA, RE, dDG and vDG, vCA1 with dCA3, vCA3 with dCA1, PIR with vCA1, and vCA1 with IC were also strengthened following (R,S)-ketamine, consistently with the role of these circuits in fear attenuation and emotions (6, 54, 72).

  Similar to (R,S)-ketamine, (2S,6S)-HNK increased connectivity between PVT and PFC, compared to the saline. However, relative to the saline, (2S,6S)-HNK administration decreased connectivity between the AMY with ACB and PFC and between the PFC and PIR, which is associated with antidepressant effects (73). Interestingly, some regions that had decreased connectivity (e.g., ACA, CEA, and LA), showed high betweenness centrality, suggesting that even if they are small hubs (with reduced connections), they are still central nodes for drug efficacy and represent optimal targets for research. Connectivity between AMY with ACB, PFC, and vCA1, PAG with dDG and PVT with AMY were decreased in the (2S,6S)-HNK group compared to the (R,S)-ketamine group, as shown in the correlation analysis data.

  Although we found regional activation differences following (R,S)-ketamine or (2S,6S)-HNK, the mechanisms behind these differences remain to be determined. (R,S)-ketamine is rapidly metabolized into neuroactive metabolites, such as HNKs, contributing to its therapeutic effects (74,75). In addition to NMDARs activated by both (R,S)-ketamine and (2S,6S)-HNK in CA3 (7), there are other receptors involved in the drug-specific effects. For example, (R,S)-ketamine and (2S,6S)-HNK activate the brain-derived neurotrophic factor receptor, TrkB, in the HPC (76) and the PFC (77), respectively. Moreover, (R,S)-ketamine, but not (2S,6S)-HNK activates AMPARs (7), group 2 metabotropic glutamate receptors (mGLUR2/3) (78), and opioid receptors in several regions (79). In addition to these receptors, (R,S)-ketamine, but not (2S,6S)-HNK, alters neurotransmitters like gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) (80), serotonin, norepinephrine and dopamine in the PFC (81). Taken together, these data indicate that (R,S)-ketamine’s effects are attributable to its metabolites, but some molecular mechanisms are specific for (R,S)-ketamine vs. HNKs.

  In summary, our results confirm the involvement of known regions in prophylactic efficacy, as well as point to new areas of interest, such as the RE, PIR, IC, PAG and RSP. Future studies could readily apply the same approach to study other drugs and other behaviors, with the goal of finding nodes in the network that can be targeted with prophylactic drugs to alleviate fear disorders.
