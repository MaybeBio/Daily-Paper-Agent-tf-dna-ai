# Probing Hot Spots of Protein-Protein Interactions Mediated by the Safety-Belt Region of REV7


## SUMMARY
  REV7 is a HORMA family adaptor protein best known as an accessory subunit of the translesion synthesis (TLS) DNA polymerase ζ (Polζ). In this role, REV7 binds REV3, the catalytic subunit of Polζ, by locking REV7-binding motifs (RBMs) in REV3 underneath the REV7 safety-belt loop. The same mechanism is used by REV7 to interact with RBMs from other proteins in DNA damage response (DDR) and mitosis. Because of the importance of REV7 for TLS and other DDR pathways, targeting REV7:RBM protein-protein interactions (PPIs) with small molecules has emerged as a strategy to enhance cancer response to genotoxic chemotherapy. To identify druggable pockets at the REV7:RBM interface, we performed computational analyses of REV7 complexed with several RBM partners. The contributions of different interface regions to REV7:RBM stabilization were corroborated experimentally. These studies provide insights into key intermolecular interactions and establish targetable regions of REV7 for the design of REV7:RBM PPI inhibitors.


## INTRODUCTION
  REV7 (MAD2L2/MAD2B/FANCV) is an accessory subunit of the B-family translesion synthesis (TLS) DNA polymerase ζ (POLζ),1–5 which plays a central role in the bypass replication over a wide range of DNA lesions and the post-replicational filling of lesion-containing single-stranded DNA gaps.6–7 In TLS, POLζ functions as an ‘extender’ DNA polymerase, which extends the lesion-distorted DNA primer-template after nucleotide incorporation across from the lesion by an ‘inserter’ Y-family TLS polymerase (one of POLη, POLɩ, POLκ).8–10 The ‘inserter’ and ‘extender’ TLS polymerases form a heteroprotein complex via protein-protein interactions (PPIs) with the TLS scaffolding proteins, the mono-ubiquitinated sliding clamp PCNA and the DNA polymerase REV1.5 Within the 4-subunit POLζ assembly (REV3/REV7/POLD2/POLD3 complex),4–5,11–12 REV7 interacts with the two REV7-binding motifs (RBM1 and RBM2) of the catalytic subunit REV32,13–15 and forms a homodimer, which bridges REV3 with the accessory subunits POLD2/POLD3.2,16–17 Within the multiprotein TLS complex, REV7 is essential for recruiting POLζ to damaged DNA through interaction with the C-terminal domain of the scaffolding polymerase REV1 (REV1-CT).15,18–20

  Beyond TLS, REV7 is involved in a range of cellular processes, including other DNA damage response (DDR) pathways, cell cycle regulation, and gene transcription.1–2,21–26 In DDR, REV7 functions as a subunit of the shieldin (SHLD1/SHLD2/SHLD3/REV7) complex, which mediates the pathway choice for DNA double-stranded break (DSB) repair by suppressing DNA end resection required for homologous recombination (HR) and promoting non-homologous end joining (NHEJ).26–30 As a subunit of POLζ, REV7 also plays a role in DNA interstrand cross-link (ICL) repair and it has been included in the Fanconi Anemia (FA) gene family as FANCV.31–34 In cell cycle regulation, REV7 prevents the premature mitotic exit by sequestering the anaphase promoting complex/cyclosome (APC/C) activator CDH1.21,35 In addition, REV7 interacts with multiple proteins involved in mitotic progression, including Ras-related nuclear protein (RAN) GTPase, clathrin light chain A (CLTA), and chromosome alignment-maintaining phosphoprotein (CAMP).36–39 In regulation of gene expression, REV7 interacts with the transcription factors ELK-1 and TCF4.24–25

  REV7 is a HORMA (Hop1, Rev7, Mad2) family adaptor protein,40–41 which binds the majority of its partners via a conserved mechanism whereby the REV7 ‘safety-belt’ loop closes on the top of a REV7-binding motif (RBM) centered around the Pxxx(A/P)P sequence.13–14,42 Several crystal structures of human REV7 complexed with RBMs from different proteins have been reported, including REV3 (RBM1 and RBM2), SHLD3, CAMP, RAN GTPase, and Shigella Invasin,15,18,30,37,39,42–43 These structures revealed an extensive intermolecular interface with the buried surface area (BSA) up to 2000 Å2 comprised of several regions that mediate the REV7:RBM PPI. The region preceding the RBM proline core is locked underneath the ‘safety belt’ loop and forms a β-strand extending the REV7 β-sheet, while the region following the RBM proline core may form an α-helix that further extends the REV7:RBM interface (Figure 1). Previous studies revealed a variability in RBM sequences recognized by REV713–14,42 and structural diversity among the REV7:RBM complexes whereby a part of the REV7 safety-belt loop called the “dynamic adaptor region” (DAR) can adjust its conformation when bound to different RBM partners.37

  A detailed knowledge of contributions from different interface regions that stabilize the REV7:RBM PPI would enhance our understanding of the ability of REV7 to bind diverse RBM partners via the ‘safety-belt’ mechanism. Because TLS plays a central role in mediating cancer resistance to genotoxic first-line chemotherapy,44–46 targeting PPIs within the heteroprotein TLS complex has recently emerged as a promising strategy to enhance the efficacy of first-line anti-cancer drugs.47–50 Specifically, the two RBMs in human REV3 are necessary to confer cell resistance to cisplatin and prevent spontaneous chromosome breaks,14 making the REV7:REV3 PPI an attractive target for the development of adjuvant anti-cancer therapeutics.51 The detailed understanding of the sequence and structural determinants of the REV7:RBM interaction will open new opportunities for developing small molecule TLS inhibitors disrupting the REV7/REV3 PPI. Here we performed molecular dynamics simulations and binding free energy calculations to probe the key intermolecular interactions between REV7 and several representative RBMs and corroborated the results of computational studies by fluorescence polarization (FP) displacement assays with truncated RBM peptides. These studies characterized hot spots on the REV7:RBM interface contributing the majority of binding free energy, which can be targeted with small molecules to disrupt REV7 PPIs in TLS and other DDR pathways. In addition, these residues can be altered to probe their functional significance for REV7 PPIs in cell. Our findings provide new insights into the REV7 recognition of RBM ligands via the ‘safety-belt’ mechanism and offer guidelines for designing inhibitors of REV7 PPIs holding promise as anti-cancer agents.


## RESULTS

### Structural diversity of REV7:RBM PPIs.
  The minimal core sequence (MCS) of an RBM that is necessary and sufficient for binding human REV7 was originally defined as φφxPxxxpP, where φ is a hydrophobic residue, P is proline, p is proline or another residue, and x is any residue (Figure 1A).13 Subsequent structural studies of REV7 complexes with RBMs from different proteins revealed more details of the REV7:RBM PPI.15,30,37,39,43,52 These structures demonstrate that an RBM is threaded through REV7 in a closed conformation characteristic of HORMA proteins (Figure 1B–D),40–41 which led to a slight revision of the RBM core sequence to φφψPxxxpP (where ψ is a hydrophobic or charged/polar residue with long aliphatic side chain).42 The N-terminal region of REV7 (residues 1–156) includes three α-helices αA, αB, and αC docked against an antiparallel core β-sheet comprised of strands β4, β5, β6 (Figure 1B–D). The C-terminus of REV7 includes a safety belt region (residues 156–183) that closes on the top of an RBM ligand, and a β8’-β8” hairpin (residues 184–207) that extends the β4-β6 β-sheet locking the ligand underneath the REV7’s safety-belt (Figure 1B–D). The N-terminal φφψP fragment of the RBM forms a β-strand, which pairs with the β6-strand of the REV7 β-sheet and a short β7-strand (residues 171–173) within the REV7 safety-belt. The C-terminal region of RBMs following the MCS (φφψPxxxpP) may form an amphipathic α-helix (nψxxφxxφφ, where n is an N-capping residue),42–43,53 which contributes additional intermolecular contacts (in REV7 complexes with REV3RBM1, REV3RBM2, and SHLD3RBM).15,43,52 However, this helix is missing in REV7 complexes with CAMPRBM, RANRBM, and IPARBM.37,39 When bound to different RBMs, a part of the REV7 safety-belt called the “dynamic adaptor region” (DAR, residues 156–173) may alter its conformation.39 While the β7 strand within this region (residues 171–173), which pairs with the RBM β-strand, is preserved in all REV7:RBM complexes, the variable portion (residues 156–166) adopts a 310 helix (when bound to RANRBM or IPARBM) and a β-strand that pairs with β7 (when complexed with REV3RBM2). This electron density in this region is poorly defined in crystal structures when bound to REV3RBM1 or CAMPRBM (Figure 1B).

  The strongest REV7 complexes are with the REV3 and SHLD3 RBMs (KD ~ 10 nM range), which exhibit extensive BSA (up to 2000 Å2).52 The PPI is stabilized by several RBM regions that, for the purpose of this work, are defined as (i) the N-terminal β-strand (βRBM; φφψP), (ii) the proline core (xxxpP), and (iii) the C-terminal amphipathic α-helix (αRBM; nψxxφxxφφ) (shown in yellow, green, and blue in Figure 1A, respectively). The RBM proline residues are considered essential for the REV7 binding;13 however, double proline mutations of the SHLD3 RBM attenuated, but did not abolish the PPI.52 Consistent with the C-terminal α-helix missing in several REV7:RBM complexes,37,39 the α-helix truncation mutant of SHLD3 RBM also retained the ability to bind REV7.52

  Considering the complexity and structural diversity of REV7:RBM PPIs, we sought to determine the relative contributions of different regions of the interaction interface to stabilization of the complexes and identify interaction hot spots contributing the majority of binding free energy that can be targeted with small molecule PPI inhibitors.54–55 For our computational analysis, we selected the two REV7 complexes with REV3RBM1 and REV3RBM2, which possess both the N-terminal β-strand and the C-terminal α-helix while inducing different conformations of the REV7 DAR (310 helix vs β-strand) (Figure 1B).15,43 We also analyzed REV7 complexes with RANRBM and IPARBM, which lack the C-terminal α-helix.56 Specifically, we evaluated the closed form of dimerization deficient REV7 R124A mutant bound to REV3RBM1 (REV3 residues 1875–1896, PDB ID 3VU7),18 REV3RBM2 (REV3 residues 1991–2012, PDB ID 6BC8),43 RANRBM (RAN residues 166–187, PDB ID 6NIF),38 and IPARBM (IPA residues 60–72 PDB ID 6KEA).37 For each of these complexes, we explored specific intermolecular contacts that govern the REV7:RBM PPIs using a combination of molecular dynamics (MD) simulations and binding energy analysis by the molecular mechanics/Poisson Boltzmann surface area (MM/PBSA) approach.57–58


### Molecular Dynamics Simulations.
  MD trajectories (50ns, 300K) for the REV7 complexes with REV3RBM1, REV3RBM2, RANRBM, and IPARBM used for the MM/PBSA of binding energy analysis were generated as described in the Methods. The root mean square deviations (RMSD) from the initial structures calculated for the backbone atoms of REV7 (1.85 Å to 2.25 Å) and RBM peptides (2.01 Å, 1.97 Å, 3.42 Å and 1.76 Å for REV3RBM1, REV3RBM2, RANRBM, IPARBM, respectively) indicate that the overall structures of the complexes were well-maintained throughout the MD simulations (Table 1, Figures S1–S4). Interestingly, the βRBM region was the most stable RBM region for the REV7 complexes with REV3RBM1, REV3RBM2, and IPARBM, while the proline core was the most stable for the REV7:RANRBM complex (Table 1). The isotropic temperature factors (B-factors) calculated from the MD trajectories for each complex, while being lower than the reported crystallographic B-factors, correctly reproduced the rigid and dynamic protein regions, suggesting that our simulations correctly captured the overall protein flexibility (Figures S1–S4).


### Binding energy analysis for the REV7:RBM complexes.
  To gain a more detailed understanding of intermolecular interactions that govern the REV7:RBM binding, we performed MM/PBSA binding energy analysis for each of the four complexes evaluated in our MD simulations. This method has been successfully used for energy decomposition of protein-protein, protein-DNA, and protein-small molecule interactions.59–62 For each of the four REV7:RBM complexes, the interaction energies were averaged over snapshots taken from the 50 ns MD trajectories, as described previously by our groups.61 In our analysis, we have only evaluated the enthalpic contribution (ΔH) to the binding free energy (ΔG) of the complexes, as the entropy of the system was significantly biased by fluctuations of the RBM regions that were not directly interacting with REV7. As shown in Table 2, the total binding enthalpy (ΔH) correlated with the buried surface area (BSA) of the RBM peptide in each of the REV7:RBM complexes. In agreement with experimentally determined binding affinities,30,37,43 REV3 RBMs that interact with REV7 via both the N-terminal the βRBM region and the α-helix region demonstrated enhanced binding energies as compared to RANRBM and IPARBM, which lack the C-terminal α-helix.


### Pairwise binding energy decompositions for the REV7:RBM PPIs:
  To evaluate contributions from different regions of the binding interface to stabilization of the REV7:RBM complexes, we performed pairwise energy decompositions for interacting residues in REV7 and RBM motifs (Tables S1–S4). The analysis of binding energy contributions from the βRBM, proline core, and α-helix binding regions of the RBM peptides reveals that the βRBM region contributes the majority of binding energy to each complex, while the αRBM region and the proline core, respectively, are less important for REV3RBM1 and REV3RBM2 binding (Table 3). This trend is also conserved for the REV7 complexes with RANRBM and IPARBM, which lack the C-terminal αRBM region. As noted above, the conformation of the variable portion of the DAR in the REV7 complexes with RANRBM and IPARBM (310 helix) differs from that in the REV7 complexes with REV3RBM1 (poorly defined) and REV3RBM2 (β-strand) (Figure 1B–D). Therefore, the four REV7:RBM complexes considered in our analysis (Table 3) cover the whole range of conformations adapted by REV7 (310 helix vs β-strand DAR) and RBM (α-helix vs extended C-terminus) upon the complex formation. To further assess key intermolecular interactions for each complex, we have performed per-residue pairwise energy decompositions described below.


### Binding energy contributions from RBM residues:
  To evaluate the importance of different RBM positions to stabilization of REV7:RBM PPIs, we used pairwise interaction energies derived from the MM/PBSA analysis (Tables S1–S4) to calculate per-residue binding energy contributions for different RBM positions (Figure 2). As expected, the most significant contributors to stabilization of all of the considered REV7:RBM complexes were βRBM residues (φφψ) preceding the first proline of the RBM MCS (φφψPxxxpP). These residues form a β-strand stabilized by a network of the backbone hydrogen bonds with the REV7 β6-strand and a short β7-strand from the REV7 safety belt. The most significant contributions to the binding energy were from the φψ pair preceding the first RBM proline, with ψ (K1879 in REV3RBM1, M1995 in REV3RBM2, I64 in IPARBM, M179 in RANRBM) forming side-chain contacts with the REV7 residue I172 and φ (L1878 in REV3RBM1, I1994 in REV3RBM2, L63 in IPARBM, A178 in RANRBM) contributing side-chain contacts with REV7 residue V150 in addition to the backbone hydrogen bonds (Figures 2 and 3). Note that in REV3RBM2, K1991 and I1992 preceding the φφψ sequence further extend the N-terminal βRBM-strand and form additional contacts with resides from the REV7 β6-strand. Proline residues of the φφψPxxxpP region, although interacting with W171, P170, Y63 (the first proline) and a pocket formed by Y63, Y37, F146, L60 (the pP pair), contribute less binding energy and likely primarily serve in a structural role to define the conformation of the RBM peptide (Figures 2 and 3). Note that among the considered RBMs, REV3RBM1 and RANRBM have proline (P1883 and P184) in the first position of the pP pair, while in REV3RBM2 and IPARBM, this position is occupied by alanine. The residues from αRBM region (nψxxφxxφφ), which is missing in IPARBM in RANRBM, are the least important for stabilization of the complex (Figure 2). The most noticeable contributors to the binding energy from this region are R1880 in REV3RBM1 and R1887 in REV3RBM2 (ψ immediately following the N-capping residue), which interact with the REV7 E35-P38 region, and I1890 in REV3RBM1 and V2006 in REV3RBM2 (φ in the second turn of the αRBM helix). In addition, W2009 and L2010 in REV3RBM2 (φφ pair in the third turn of the αRBM helix) interact with REV7 residues H57, P58, and I41 (Figures 2 and 3). Peripheral contacts of W2009 and L2010 in the REV3RBM2 α-helix and K1990, K1991, and I1992 in the REV3RBM2 β-strand extend the interaction interface (Table 2) and contribute additional binding energy to the REV7:REV3RBM2 PPI (Figure 2).

  To test the importance of peripheral interactions mediated by the REV3RBM2 α-helix, we replaced the W2009 residue with an alanine and performed an additional simulation with this mutant system. While the energy of any individual pi-pi stacking interaction involving histidine is on the order of –3 to –4 kcal/mol,AAA our simulations predicted a decrease in binding energy of ~7 kcal/mol (Table 2), which highlights the importance of the W2009/H57 intermolecular pi-pi interaction for the stabilization of the REV7/REV3RBM2 structure. Consistent with the simulation results, our experimental FP displacement assay revealed a reduced ability of the RBM3RBM2 W2009A peptide to disrupt the REV7:RBM PPI (see below; Table 4, peptide 11).


### Identification of key residues on the REV7:RBM interface:
  Next, we sought to characterize the regions of REV7 important for stabilization of REV7:RBM complexes. As described above for RBM sequences, we used the MM/PBSA derived pairwise interaction energies (Tables S1–S4) to calculate per-residue binding energy contributions for REV7 residues (Figure 3).


#### REV7:REV3RBM1 PPI:
  Residues L1878 and K1879 of the REV3RBM1 βRBM region (φψ pair of the φφψPxxxpP MCS) form an anti-parallel β-sheet with β6/β7 of REV7 (Figure 2 and 3, Figure S5) stabilized by the backbone hydrogen bonds with V150 (β6) and I172 (β7). In addition to the backbone hydrogen bonds, the positively charged side chain of K1879 (βRBM) forms dynamic salt bridges with D175 and D178 (electrostatic contributions to pairwise energies of −49.3 and −36.0 kcal/mol, respectively; Table S1) and interacts with I172. The aliphatic side chains of I1877 and L1878 (φφ pair in βRBM) are encaged by H151, A174, V179 (I1877) and V150, T152, M160, I163, W171, L173 (L1878), while the side chain of P1880 binds the pocket formed by W171, P170, Y63, F169, V148, and T67. Overall, REV7 residues W171 (β7), I172 (β7), V150 (β6) that form the backbone hydrogen bonds and accommodate side chains of the φφψP βRBM region, as well as D175 and D178 that interact with K1879 in REV3RBM1 contribute the majority of binding energy to this PPI (Figure 3).

  The proline core of REV3RBM1 primarily interacts with REV7 through P1884 and P1885 (pP pair of φφψPxxxpP MCS) that bind a hydrophobic pocket formed by Y63, Y37, F146, L60 of REV7 (Figure 3). In addition, L1881 and M1882 of REV3RBM1 form strong van der Waals contacts with P170 and Y63, respectively (Table S1). The αRBM region of REV3RBM1 interacts with REV7 via R1887 and I1890 in the first and second turns of the RBM α-helix (ψ and the first φ of nψxxφxxφφ sequence), whereby R1887 binds E35-P38 of REV7, and I1890 enters a hydrophobic cavity formed by H57, Y37, and I41. Overall, REV7 residues that bind βRBM and αRBM regions provide modest contributions to the total energy of the REV7/REV3RBM1 PPI (Figure 3).


#### REV7:REV3RBM2 PPI:
  As noted above, the variable portion of the REV7 DAR (residues 156–166) forms a short β-strand (residues 163–166) when complexed with REV3RBM2 or a 310 helix when bound to IPARBM and RANRBM, or is poorly defined when bound to REV3RBM1 (Figures 2 and 3, Figure S8). In all four structures; however, the variable part of the DAR (residues 156–166) does not form direct contacts with the RBM and contributes little to stabilization of the REV7:RBM complexes (Tables S1–S4). Despite the minor alternation in the REV7 structure, key interactions of REV3RBM2 closely mimic those of the REV7:REV3RBM1 complex. Because of the additional peripheral contacts of W2009 and L2010 in the REV3RBM2 α-helix and K1990, K1991 and I1992 in the REV3RBM2 β-strand, the total BSA for the REV7:REV3RBM2 complex is the highest of all four structures (2060 Å2).

  The φφψP βRBM region of REV3RBM2 is preceded by K1990 and K1991 that form transient salt bridges with side chains of E176 and E81 in REV7 (Figure 3; Table S2), while the following REV3RBM2 residue I1992 extends the REV7 βRBM-strand. Similar to all other structures, the φψ residues of βRBM, I1994 and M1995, form a network of hydrogen bonds with V150 (β6) and I172 (β7) of REV7. In addition to hydrogen bonds between βRBM and β6/β7 of REV7, the side chains of I1992 and I1994 on one side of βRBM are encaged by E154, T152 (β6), V150 (β6) of REV7, while V1993 and M1995 on the other side of βRBM interact with REV7 residues V150, H151, W171, and I172. The side chain of P1996 forms stabilizing van der Waals contacts with W171 (Figure 3). Similar to the REV7:REV3RBM1 PPI, W171 (β7) and V150 (β6) were the most significant contributors to binding energy of the REV7:REV3RBM2 complex (Figure 3).

  The proline core region of REV3RBM2 only moderately contributes to the binding energy (Figure 2) with the pP pair (A2000/P2001) interacting with the pocket formed by Y37 and Y63 of REV7 (Figure 3). The αRBM region of REV3RBM2 (nψxxφxxφφ; residues 2002–2010) forms a 3-turn α-helix with the residues facing REV7 from each turn of the helix contributing to the binding energy (Figure 2). In addition to interactions of R2003 (ψ) and V2006 (first φ) that are similar to those of R1887 and I1890 in REV3RBM1 (Figure 3), the W2009/L2010 pair (last φφ) binds the pocket formed by H57, P58 and I41 of REV7 (Figure 3) with the indole of W2009 forming pi-pi stacking interactions with the imidazole of H57.


#### REV7:RBMIPA and REV7:RANRBM PPIs:
  Key pairwise interactions of REV7 residues with IPARBM and RANRBM closely resemble those in the REV7/REV3RBM1 and REV7/REV3RBM2 complexes with the exception of contacts mediated by the αRBM region, which is absent in IPARBM and RANRBM. Like REV3 RBMs, RANRBM and IPARBM interact with REV7 through a short βRBM-strand (φφψP; residues 62–65 in IPA and 177–180 in IPA) that forms an antiparallel β-sheet with the β6 and β7 strands of REV7 through a conserved set of the backbone hydrogen bonds and side-chain interactions (Figures 2 and 3; Figures S6 and S7; Tables S3 and S4). The IPARBM peptide is the shortest analyzed RBM with only two residues preceding the βRBM sequence, while RANRBM includes a more extensive region N-terminal to βRBM (residues 166–176). The residues preceding βRBM, however, contribute little binding energy with the exception of N61 (IPA) and F176 (RAN) that pair with the REV7 residue T152 (Figures 2 and 3). In all complexes, the most significant contributions to the total binding energy were from W171 (β7), I172 (β7), V150 (β6) of REV7 that interact with the βRBM region.

  In the proline core region, A69/P70 (IPA) and P184/P185 (RAN) pP pairs fit into the hydrophobic pocket formed by Y63, Y37, Y63, F146, and L60 of REV7, closely resembling interactions of the P1884/P1885 and A2000/P2001 pairs in REV3RBM1 and REV3RBM2 (Figure 3). Generally, the first position of the pP pair contributes less to stabilization of the REV7:RBM complexes than the second, with the alanine in the first position being less favorable than the proline. The IPARBM and RANRBM peptides considered in this work are truncated following the proline core; therefore, no interactions mediated by the αRBM region are present.


### Hydrogen bonding at the REV7:RBM interface:
  To probe more specific interactions at the REV7:RBM interface, we traced all the hydrogen bonds present during our simulation time across each system. The total number of distinct hydrogen bonds across each system are as follows: REV7/REV3RBM1, 45; REV7/REV3RBM2, 46; REV7:RBMIPA, 56; and REV7:RANRBM, 59. More hydrogen bonds were identified in the IPA and RAN simulations; however, there were significantly fewer bonds in these systems that were stable (>25% occupancy) across the entire simulation time compared to the two REV3 systems. There were three REV7 residues that formed stable hydrogen bonds in each system. The side chain phenol of REV7 residue Y37 formed a hydrogen bond with the backbone amide of the final proline residue of the proline core. The side chain phenol of residue Y63 formed a hydrogen bond with the backbone amide of a central residue in the proline core three residues preceding the final proline. Finally, the backbone amide of REV7 residue I172 formed a hydrogen bond with the backbone amide of the RBM residue immediately preceding the proline found at the end of the βRBM region. These hydrogen bonds formed between I172 and the RBM residue immediately preceding the RBM proline have the highest occupancies in each system: REV7/REV3RBM1, 89.3%; REV7/REV3RBM2, 79.5%; REV7:RBMIPA, 83.5%; and REV7:RANRBM, 88.9% (Table S5).


### Fluorescence polarization displacement assay for the REV7/FAM-REV3RBM1 complex:
  We sought to validate our computational results by probing the minimal RBM peptide sequence required to disrupt the REV7:RBM PPI. To accomplish this goal, we developed a fluorescence polarization (FP) assay in which a FAM label was covalently linked to the N-terminus of a human REV3RBM1 peptide (residues 1872–1898). The FAM-REV3RBM1 peptide forms a stable 1:1 complex with REV7. To determine the optimal concentration of the complex for the assay, we evaluated the ability of both unlabeled REV3RBM1 and REV3RBM2 to disrupt the complex across a broad range of concentrations. A robust, concentration dependent displacement of the FAM-REV3RBM1 by both unlabeled peptides was observed with a complex concentration of 100 nM (Figure 4A and 4B). We also evaluated the ability of RANRBM and IPARBM to displace FAM-REV3RBM1 from its complex with REV7 (Figure 4C and 4D), which were less effective at disrupting the PPI than to the two REV3 RBMs consistent with RBMs derived from RAN and IPA lacking the C-terminal αRBM binding region.

  Our computational studies suggest that the βRBM and proline core regions of the RBMs are sufficient for REV7 recognition, whereas the α-helix region provides additional contact surface for enhanced affinity, which is consistent with previous studies of the REV7:RBM PPIs.13–15,30,39,43,52 Our next step was to experimentally validate these results by evaluating several truncated and modified peptides derived from either REV3RBM1 or REV3RBM2 for their ability to disrupt the REV7/FAM-REV3RBM1 complex (Table 4; Figure S9). Here we utilized the REV3 RBMs because these peptides contain each of the three regions that mediate the high affinity REV7 binding (βRBM, the proline core, αRBM), and because REV7 complexed with REV3RBM1 or REV3RBM2 adopts alternative conformations of the DAR region (310 helix vs β-strand).37 The specific truncated peptides (Table 4, Figures S9 and S10) were designed to explore the significance of different RBM regions (and/or RBM residues) for REV7 binding. It is important to note that these studies probe disruption of an established complex using a binding competition assay rather than explore a direct binding to REV7; however, the FP-based displacement assay provides a rapid method to evaluate a larger number of peptides and identify residues essential for binding.

  The first two peptides we evaluated contained only the central PxxxpPSR region of either REV3RBM1 (4) or REV3RBM2 (9). Neither of these truncated peptides was able to disrupt the REV7:REV3RBM1 PPI at concentrations up to 200 μM. This is consistent with the notion that the full-length REV3RBM1 peptide forms essential intermolecular interactions via the βRBM, proline core and αRBM regions that the PxxxpPSR sequence cannot replicate. Mutation of the first proline of the pP pair to alanine in the REV3RBM1 peptide (P1884A, peptide 5) led to only an approximately twofold reduction in activity compared to WT REV3RBM1 (1) (IC50 values = 8.0 μM and 3.4 μM, respectively) suggesting this position (which is replaced with alanine in REV3RBM2) is not essential for high-affinity binding. These results are consistent with the results of recent studies of SHLD3RBM, which tested the effect of double P to A mutations in the PxxxpP sequence on SHLD3 binding affinity.52 The truncated peptides derived from each REV3RBM that contained the βRBM and proline core regions (2 and 7) led to a moderate disruption of the complex (IC50 17.8 μM and 32.8 μM, respectively). Conversely, the truncated peptides containing the proline core and αRBM regions (3, IC50 = 9.1 μM and 8, IC50 = 8.9 μM) led to a stronger disruption of the complex relative to the βRBM and proline core peptides. These results indicate that the αRBM region in each REV3RBM contributes more significantly to an improved interaction with REV7 than is suggested by our computational analysis. The α-helix formation in the αRBM region sequesters residues with aliphatic sidechains on one side of the helix, which forms an extensive interface with a hydrophobic patch on REV7 (Figure 3). The dehydration of this hydrophobic interface is favorable from an entropic standpoint. Thus, while the contributions to binding enthalpies from the βRBM and proline core regions may be overall higher relative to αRBM (Figure 2), this entropic effect may increase the significance of αRBM for stabilization of the REV7:RBM PPI. Indeed, the REV3RBM2 peptide with W2009 mutated to alanine (11) resulted in only weak disruption of the complex that cannot be quantified in our experiment. Although located in the last turn of the αRBM helix, W2009 forms key contacts with REV7 residues H57, P58 and I41 (Figure 3). Loss of this residue destabilizes the complex similar to truncation of the whole αRBM region, highlighting the significance of peripheral contacts formed by the REV3RBM2 α-helix. Conversely, the REV3RBM2 peptide (10) with mutations of peripheral residues that extend the βRBM region (K1991A and I1992A) was still capable of disrupting the complex to a degree comparable to the full-length WT REV3RBM2 peptide (8) (IC50 values = 1.9 μM vs. 1.5 μM, respectively). In addition, the REV3RBM1 peptide (3) with the β-strand region removed was also able to disrupt the complex, albeit slightly weaker than the full-length WT REV3RBM1 peptide (1). Taken together, these results suggest a relatively higher contribution of the αRBM region in comparison with the βRBM region to stabilization of the REV7:RBM PPI. This may also explain the weaker complex disruption by RANRBM and IPARBM peptides (IC50 values = 83 μM and 147 μM respectively), which both lack the residues necessary to form the α-helical region. Taken together, our results underscore the importance of residues peripheral to the proline core in the REV7:RBM interaction. In particular, we find based on our FP assays that the presence of the αRBM region significantly improves the affinity of the REV7:RBM PPI, while these interactions appear to be less sensitive to changes in the RBM β-strand.


## DISCUSSION
  We have utilized MD simulations in combination with the MM/PBSA approach to identify key amino acid residues at the REV7:RBM PPI interface that constitute interaction hot spots contributing the majority of free-energy to high affinity binding. These studies characterized three RBM regions that contribute to REV7:RBM binding interactions: βRBM, proline core, and αRBM. With respect to specific binding energy contributions, our computational studies revealed that the βRBM and proline core regions are likely sufficient for REV7 recognition and binding, while the αRBM region contributes additional intermolecular interactions to improve overall REV7:RBM affinity (Figure 2). REV7 hot spot residues with the most pronounced binding energy contributions were (i) V150, H151 from β6 and W171, I172 from β7 strands that interact with the φψ pair of the φφψP βRBM region, (ii) a hydrophobic patch formed by Y63, Y37, F146 and L60 that accommodates the pP pair of the RBM proline core region, and (iii) a pocket formed by H57, Y37, and I41 that encages ψ and the first φ residues of the nψxxφxxφφ αRBM region (Figure 3). It is likely that effective small molecule inhibitors of the REV7:RBM PPI will target a combination of these REV7 sites. To validate our computational results, we developed an FP displacement assay that was used to evaluate both WT and truncated/mutated RBMs for their ability to disrupt the REV7:FAM-REV3RBM1 PPI. Overall, these experimental results supported our computational findings while also highlighting a more significant role of the αRBM region in stabilization of the REV7:RBM PPI than suggested by our computational studies. Truncated REV3RBM peptides in which the α-helical region was removed were significantly less active when compared to the full-length RBMs. Taken together, these data provide insight into regions of the RBM binding interface on REV7 that can potentially be targeted with small molecules as well as establish an assay that will be essential for the evaluation of potential small molecule inhibitors of the REV7:RBM PPI. In addition, our analysis provides guidelines for designing REV7 mutations that modulate its interactions with various RBM substrates to assess the functional significance of REV7 PPIs in cells.

### METHOD DETAILS

#### Molecular dynamics simulations
  The crystal structures of REV7 complex with REV3RBM1 (PDB: 3UV7), REV3RBM2 (PDB: 6BC8), RANRBM (PDB: 6NIF) and IPARBM (PDB: 6KEA)18,37,43 were used as starting structures for molecular dynamics (MD) simulations performed in AMBER18.64 The Amber14SB was used for the parameterization of the protein and peptide molecules.65 Prior to simulations, all crystallographic waters were removed, and each of the complexes was embedded in a box filled with the explicit TIP3P-type water molecules and the appropriate number of ions. The energy of each solvated complex was minimized using the SANDER program in AMBER14.0. First, energy minimization was performed for water molecules and counter ions using the steepest descent (SD, 1,000 steps) and conjugate gradient (CG, 1,000 steps) algorithms while keeping coordinates of the protein-peptide complex fixed. The solvated system was further energy minimized with SD (10,000 steps) and CG (10,000 steps) with positional restraints removed, followed by heating of the system from 0 to 300 K for 100 ps with the Langevin thermostat, and density equilibration for 500 ps. During these steps, protein and peptide backbones were restrained using a harmonic potential with the force constant of 5.0 kcal/mol/Å2. In the next 2 ns, restraints were gradually removed by reducing the force constant from 5 to 1 kcal/mol/Å2. Three well-equilibrated systems were generated, and final unrestrained MD trajectories were performed for 50 ns using a 2 fs time step.


#### Binding energy calculations
  The Molecular Mechanics/Poisson–Boltzmann (Generalized Born) Surface Area (MM/PB(GB)SA) method was used to calculate binding free energy from the snapshots of MD trajectories.59,62 The binding energies for each of the REV7:RBMs complexes were evaluated for 200 snapshots taken from 10 to 20 ns of the MD trajectories at a 50 ps interval. The total binding free energies, ΔGbinding, were calculated as follows:  ΔGbinding=ΔH+TΔS≈ΔEmm+ΔGsol–TΔS   ΔEmm=ΔEint+ΔEele+ΔEvdw   ΔGsol=ΔGGB+ΔGSA   ΔGSA=0.0072kcalmolA2ΔSASAA2  where ΔH is the enthalpy change in the system, T is the absolute temperature in K, ΔS is the entropy change in the system, ΔEmm is the changes of the gas phase molecular mechanism energy and ΔGsol is the solvation free energy. ΔEmm is the sum of the changes of the internal energy ΔEint (bond, angle, and dihedral energies), the electrostatic energy ΔEele and the van der Waals energy (ΔEvdw).ΔGsol is composed of two components: the electrostatic solvation energy ΔGGB (polar contribution) and the nonpolar contribution ΔGSA. The polar contribution is calculated by the GB model,66 and the nonpolar contribution is estimated based on the solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) determined with the LCPO method.67 The entropic change –TΔS is neglected in this work because the calculations are performed for RBMs from the same chemical series.


#### Preparation of the REV7/FAM-REV3RBM1 complex
  The dimerization deficient human REV7R124A mutant (hereby referred to as REV7) was overexpressed in E. Coli (BL21) cells transformed with a pETDuet-1 (Novagen) plasmid with the gene encoding REV7 subcloned in the multiple cloning site 1 (MCS-1) and the empty multiple cloning site (MCS-2).15 Following transformation, cells were grown overnight in 50 mL of LB medium at 32°C. The overnight culture was then used to inoculate 1L of LB medium and bacteria were grown at 37 °C to an OD600 of 1.0, followed by induction of protein expression by 1 mM IPTG at 20°C overnight. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, resuspended in lysis buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate, 300 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, 1 mM PMSF, pH 8.5), and lysed by sonication. The lysate was centrifuged and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 μM PVDF membrane, loaded onto a TALON cobalt resin column and incubated for 0.5 hr at 4 °C. After thorough washing with loading buffer, the protein was eluted with the elution buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate, 300 mM NaCl, 300 mM imidazole at pH 8.5). To form the complex, REV7R124A was preincubated with an excess of custom synthetized (GenScript) REV3RBM1 peptide (residues 1872–1898) modified with an N-terminal fluorescein amidite label (FAM-REV3RBM1). The REV7/FAM-REV3RBM1 complex was purified by size-exclusion chromatography on a HiLoad 16/600 Supderdex 75 pg column in a buffer containing 20 mM sodium phosphate, 100 mM NaCl, pH 7.4. The purified complex was concentrated as necessary using the 10K MWCO AMICON Ultra Centrifugal filters.


#### Fluorescence polarization displacement assay
  The displacement of fluorescently labeled FAM-REV3RBM1 peptide from its complex with REV7 by increasing concentrations of the unlabeled RBM peptides (Table 4) was evaluated using the fluorescence polarization (FP) based assay. In this assay, the REV7:FAM-REV3RBM1 complex (10 μL, 0.2 μM) diluted in a phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was added to a black 384-well plate (ProxiPlate-384 F Plus, PerkinElmer). An unlabeled RBM peptide (10 μL, varying concentrations) was added to the wells with REV7:FAM-REV3RBM1 solution to give a final unlabeled peptide concentration of 100 μM − 1 nM in a total 20 μL/well volume. In each plate, a 20 μL PBS buffer well was used as the negative control, and a 10 μL complex / 10 μL PBS buffer well was used as the positive control. The plate was incubated at room temperature for 1 hour with gentle mixing. Fluorescence polarization (FP) was measured at 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 minutes in millipolarization (mP) units on a Synergy H1 Hybrid multimode microplate reader (Biotek, excitation 485 nM, emission 528 nM).


### QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS
  The IC50 values (the unlabeled peptide concentration required for 50% displacement) for the displacement studies were determined by nonlinear regression of the log concentration vs response data in a custom script in MATLAB 2022b.
