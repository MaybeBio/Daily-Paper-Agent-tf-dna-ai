# A novel molecule ZYZ329 targeting histone methyltransferase SMYD3 suppresses pathological angiogenesis via the driven mitochondrial ROS/HIF-1α/VEGFA axis


## Background
  The histone methyltransferase SMYD3 (SET and MYND domain containing 3) is critical for vascular homeostasis and may be implicated in pathological angiogenesis. However, its mechanism remains elusive, and targeted inhibitors are in early-stage development. We aim to clarify whether SMYD3 regulates angiogenesis and develop novel molecules targeting SMYD3.


## Methods
  Expression profile of SMYD3 under pro-angiogenic conditions was characterized by bioinformatics analysis and endothelial cell (EC) validation. The effects of SMYD3 knockdown, knockdown-overexpression, and overexpression on angiogenesis were validated, including Matrigel neovascularization, aortic ring sprouting, and EC tube formation. Regarding molecule development, virtual screening, structural modifications, molecular docking, inhibition screening of EC proliferation, and SMYD3 enzyme activity and cellular thermal shift assays were employed. A novel molecule ZYZ329 was identified and evaluated in angiogenesis. Mechanistic studies involved genetic approaches and the mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mROS) scavenger MitoQ (Mitoquinone mesylate). Finally, a murine hindlimb ischemia model and rat skin healing model were established to evaluate ZYZ329 on pathological and physiological angiogenesis.


## Results
  SMYD3 was upregulated in endothelial cells under ischemic, hypoxic, and VEGF-stimulated conditions. And SMYD3 gene knockdown, overexpression after knockdown, and overexpression regulated the angiogenesis capacity of endothelial cells. After virtual screening and structural modifications, the novel molecule ZYZ329 was developed for dual inhibition of EC proliferation (IC50 = 6.147 μM) and SMYD3 enzymatic function (IC50 = 0.419 μM). The ZYZ329 engaged SMYD3 in cells, and exhibited a certain degree of selectivity for SMYD3. Moreover, ZYZ329 significantly attenuated pathological angiogenesis in a dose-dependent manner. Mechanistically, genetic or pharmacological inhibition of SMYD3 impaired HIF-1α stabilization and downstream VEGFA production. SMYD3 genetic perturbation modulated mROS without affecting apoptosis in hypoxic endothelial cell. MitoQ intervention confirmed that moderate SMYD3-driven mROS elevation regulates the HIF-1α/VEGFA axis in angiogenesis. In hindlimb ischemia model, ZYZ329 significantly impaired blood flow recovery and post-ischemic angiogenesis, demonstrating superior efficacy to EPZ031686. However, ZYZ329 had no significant effect on physiological angiogenesis.


## Conclusions
  SMYD3 drives angiogenesis partially via the mROS/HIF-1α/VEGFA axis. We developed a novel molecule ZYZ329, which potently suppresses pathological angiogenesis. This offers potential therapeutic target and lead structures for treating angiogenesis-related diseases.

## Introduction
  Cardiovascular disease represents a critical global public health challenge, threatening human health and longevity [1]. Arteries, as the primary conduits of systemic blood circulation, play a pivotal role in maintaining vascular homeostasis [2]. Angiogenesis is the process of new blood vessels formation from pre-existing ones [3, 4]. However, in pathological conditions, such as tumors, diabetic retinopathy, and rheumatoid arthritis, angiogenesis persists aberrantly, manifesting as disorganized vascular structure, excessive and aberrant branching, and heightened vascular permeability. Consequently, these structurally aberrant vascular networks fail to establish functional microcirculatory perfusion [5]. In contrast to tightly regulated physiological angiogenesis (such as wound healing), pathological angiogenesis is driven by overexpression of pro-angiogenic factors (such as Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor A, VEGFA) and coupled with insufficient counter-regulation by anti-angiogenic factors [6]. In recent years, mounting evidence has revealed the relationship between pathological angiogenesis and endothelial cells, with epigenetic regulation emerging as a pivotal research focus in this field.

  Pathological angiogenesis is primarily initiated by sustained hypoxia, ischemia, or dysregulation of angiogenic factors (particularly VEGFA), disrupting the physiological balance between pro- and anti-angiogenic signaling [7, 8]. Under normal oxygen concentrations, HIF-1α (hypoxia inducible factor-1α) is hydroxylated by prolyl hydroxylases (PHDs) and HIF inhibitory factors (FIH), leading to its degradation [9]. When cells are exposed to hypoxia (typically O2 < 5%), the hydroxylation of HIF-1α is inhibited, allowing it to escape degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome system [10]. Stabilized HIF-1α rapidly accumulates in the cytoplasm and translocates into the nucleus, where it recognizes and binds to the hypoxia response element (HRE) with HIF-1β subunit, thereby initiating angiogenesis signaling cascades [11]. Notably, the VEGFA promoter contains a functional HRE, making it a direct transcriptional target of HIF-1 [12, 13]. Beyond hydroxylation, modifications such as methylation, demethylation, acetylation, and phosphorylation also participate in regulating HIF-1α stability and transcriptional activity at various levels [14]. However, the methylation modification of HIF-1α has not been fully elucidated, and it is still unclear how methylation modification affects its generation, activity, or degradation pathways. The precise upstream regulatory mechanisms of this pathway, particularly how it becomes persistently and aberrantly activated under pathological conditions, still require further elucidation.

  Epigenetic regulation has increasingly been recognized as a key modulator of hypoxia-responsive transcription, shaping both the intensity and duration of HIF-1α–driven programs, including VEGFA expression [15, 16]. Among chromatin-modifying enzymes, the histone methyltransferase SMYD3 warrants special attention due to its capacity to deposit activating histone methylation marks and its frequent overexpression in a variety of cancers, where it promotes oncogenic transcriptional networks associated with proliferation and invasion [17, 18]. Histone methyltransferase SMYD3 is a protein containing SET, MYND, and CTD domains [19]. This multi-domain structure enables SMYD3 to methylate histone and non-histone substrates, thereby regulating diverse physiological and pathological processes including transcriptional regulation, metabolic reprogramming, and cellular differentiation [20, 21]. In zebrafish studies, knockdown of SMYD3 in embryos led to pericardial edema and structural abnormalities in the trunk vasculature, suggesting its critical regulatory function in cardiac morphogenesis and vascular development [22]. Beyond its indispensable role in embryogenesis, SMYD3 is markedly overexpressed in diverse malignancies, including small cell lung cancer [23], colorectal cancer [24], gastric cancer [25], breast cancer [26], pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [27], and chronic lymphocytic leukemia [28]. SMYD3 overexpression in multiple malignancies was significantly associated with reduced overall survival and progression-free survival, serving as an independent prognostic factor for adverse outcomes [29, 30]. These studies indicate that SMYD3 plays a critical regulatory role in the proliferation, differentiation, and migration of various cell types, positioning it as a potential therapeutic target for cancer and vascular pathologies. SMYD3 may be involved in pathological angiogenesis, but its precise regulatory mechanisms remain elusive.

  The development of SMYD3 inhibitors has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy. However, the current small-molecule inhibitors remain limited. Key compounds include BCI-121 [31], BAY-6035 [32], GSK2807 [33], EPZ031686 [34], and newer agents like the covalent inhibitor EM127 [35], autophagy inducer 7 r [36] and novel molecule ZYZ384 [37]. Nevertheless, the limitations of these existing compounds in terms of potency, selectivity, and cellular activity hinder both the precise mechanistic investigation of SMYD3 function and its validation as a therapeutic target. This creates a dual challenge: a poorly understood biological role coupled with a lack of adequate chemical tools.

  Therefore, a significant knowledge gap exists regarding the precise function and molecular mechanism of SMYD3 in pathological angiogenesis. It remains unknown whether SMYD3 directly regulates the pivotal HIF-1α/VEGFA signaling axis. To address these gaps, this study was designed to elucidate the role of SMYD3 in pathological angiogenesis and to discover a novel, potent, and selective SMYD3 inhibitor. By leveraging both genetic tools and this novel molecule, we aimed to mechanistically dissect SMYD3 function and validate its potential as a therapeutic target for angiogenesis-related diseases. This study not only provided a valuable chemical tool for functional study of the SMYD3 enzyme, but also offered potential therapeutic targets and drug lead compounds for treating angiogenesis-related diseases including diabetic retinopathy, rheumatoid arthritis, and tumor.


## Materials and methods

### Bioinformatics analysis
  In the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, links: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gds) of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), we searched for in vivo animal models and in vitro cell models related to pathological angiogenesis using the keywords “Ischemia”, “Hypoxia”, and “VEGF”. The gene expression datasets GSE26887 and GSE1041 related to pathological angiogenesis were retrieved. The datasets were accessed via the following links: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE26887 and https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE1041 (accessed on 2 November 2023). The GSE26887 dataset, containing RNA-seq data from human left ventricular biopsies, was used to compare post-ischemic heart failure samples (n = 19) with non-infarcted control samples (n = 5). The GSE1041 dataset, consisting of RNA-seq data from primary HUVEC, was used to compare cells under hypoxia versus a normoxic control. Data cleansing, grouping, and differential expression analysis were performed on the GEO data using R (ver. 4.4.2) in conjunction with bioinformatics analysis packages such as “ggplot2,” “DESeq2,” “limma,” “dplyr,” and “tinyarray.” In addition, the expression of SMYD3 and VEGFA in human vascular tissue was analyzed using the Genotype-Tissue Expression Project (GTEx, links: https://www.genome.gov/Funded-Programs-Projects/Genotype-Tissue-Expression-Project) database (accessed on 10 February 2024) [38].


### Human samples
  All human samples were obtained from patients undergoing liver resection at Shanghai General Hospital between February and October 2025. Hepatocellular carcinoma specimens and control tissues from patients with benign hepatic lesions (e.g., hepatic hemangioma) were subjected to IHC analysis. All procedures involving human liver samples were approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai General Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment in the study.


### Cell culture and treatment
  Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC) were purchased from ScienCell (#8000) and cultured in complete endothelial cell medium (ECM, ScienCell) containing 1% ECGS (Endothelial Cell Growth Supplement, ScienCell), 1% penicillin/streptomycin solution (P/S, ScienCell), and 5% FBS (Fetal Bovine Serum, ScienCell). The cells were cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Human microvascular endothelial cells (HMEC) were purchased from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures and cultured in MCDB 131 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS and 10 ng/mL human epidermal growth factor (hEGF, Sigma). Cells were passaged when they reached 90% confluence, and experiments were performed using cells that had been expanded no more than 12 times. To induce hypoxia, HUVEC were cultured in a hypoxic chamber with a gas concentration of 1% O2 and 5% CO2. For cell treatment, cultured HUVEC were synchronized with low serum (0.5% FBS) for 12 hours and then treated with human VEGFA recombinant protein (PeproTech) at the indicated concentrations or times. For protein degradation experiments, cells were treated with cycloheximide (CHX, Sigma-Aldrich) at a final concentration of 40 μg/mL for the indicated times. For inhibitor screening, a series of small molecule compounds were added at a final concentration of 10 μM for 24, 48, or 72 hours. For MitoQ treatment, 24 hours after virus transduction, cells were treated with 1 μM MitoQ (MedChemExpress) for 48 hours.


### RNA isolation and qRT-PCR
  After treatment, cells were quickly washed with PBS and lysed with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). RNA was then extracted with chloroform, precipitated with isopropanol, and washed with 75% ethanol. After drying, the precipitate was dissolved in nuclease-free water, and the concentration was measured using a NanoDrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific) before being stored in a −80 °C freezer.

  The obtained RNA was diluted to the same concentration and reverse transcribed into cDNA using RT Master Mix for qPCR reagent (Abclonal). Subsequently, qPCR reactions were performed using SYBR Green Fast qPCR Mix (Abclonal) on the CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad) as described previously [39]. Gene expression was calculated based on the cycle threshold (Ct value). The primers used were synthesized by GenScript, and their sequences are shown in Table S1.


### Western blot (WB)
  After cell treatment, proteins were extracted using RIPA strong lysis buffer (ShareBio) containing protease inhibitor cocktail (ShareBio) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (ShareBio), and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (ShareBio). Equal amounts of protein were loaded, separated by 10% SDS-PAGE (Epizyme), and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore). After blocking with nonfat dry milk, the membranes were incubated overnight with primary antibodies, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit (1:10000, Abclonal) or anti-mouse secondary antibodies (1:10000, Abclonal). Images were acquired using Bio-Rad ChemiDoc Imaging Systems, and semi-quantitative analysis was performed using Image J software based on grayscale values. The ratio of the target protein to the internal control protein was calculated. The primary antibodies and dilutions used are as follows: rabbit anti-SMYD3 (1:5000, ab187149, Abcam), rabbit anti-HIF-1α (1:1000, 36169S, Cell Signaling Technology), rabbit anti-H3K4me3 (1:5000, A22146, Abcam), rabbit anti-Total H3 (1:10000, 17168-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit anti-VEGFA (1:5000, ER30607, Huabio), rabbit anti-BCL-2 (1:1000, 3498S, Cell Signaling Technology), rabbit anti-BAX (1:4000, 50599-2-Ig, Proteintech), rabbit anti-SMYD2 (1:3000, 21290-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit anti-EZH2 (1:1000, R24813, Zenbio), rabbit anti-EHMT2 (1:1000, R40038, Zenbio), rabbit anti-H3K36me2 (1:1000, R40006, Zenbio), rabbit anti-H3K27me3 (1:1000, R26242, Zenbio), rabbit anti-H3K9me2 (1:1000, R502359, Zenbio), rabbit anti-T-VEGFR2 (1:5000, 26415-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit anti-P-VEGFR2 (1:3000, AP1494, Abclonal), mouse anti-GAPDH (1:10000, 60004-1-Ig, Proteintech) and mouse anti-β-Tubulin (1:5000, AC010, Abclonal).


### Lentivirus, adenovirus, and small interfering RNA (SiRNA) transfection
  The SMYD3 knockdown lentivirus Sh-SMYD3, and the vector control virus Sh-Ctrl were commissioned to Bio-lifespan (Shanghai, China) for construction. For lentiviral transfection, HUVEC were seeded into 6-well plates. When the cells reached approximately 30% confluency, 5 μg/mL polybrene (Yeasen) was added and Sh-SMYD3 or Sh-Ctrl lentiviruses were added simultaneously. Expression of the target protein and internal control was measured 48 or 72 hours later. The sequences were 5’-AACATCTACCAGCTGAAGGTG-3’ for SMYD3 ShRNA 1, and 5’-CAGCCTGATTGAAGATTTGAT-3’ for SMYD3 ShRNA 2.

  For SiRNA transfection, HUVEC were seeded into 6-well plates. After approximately 18 hours, when the cells reached 70%-90% confluency, a mixture of Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) and Si HIF-1α or the nonsense control Si Ctrl was added according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The medium was replaced with fresh ECM every other day, and the expression of target protein or gene was detected 48 or 72 hours after transfection. The sequences were 5’- CUCCCUAUAUCCCAAUGGATT-3’ for Si HIF-1α, 5’-GGAAAUCUCUUGCAAGCUATT-3’ for si-KDR, and 5’- UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3’ for Si Ctrl (Scramble-siRNA). The SiRNA were purchased from Bio-lifespan, Shanghai, China.

  The pCMV-SMYD3, SMYD2, EZH2, and EHMT2/G9a plasmids were obtained from MiaoLing Plasmid, China. The Ad-SMYD3 adenovirus was generated by BIORUN, Wuhan, China. The lentivirus carrying the synonymous SMYD3-Res mutation was generated by Bio-lifespan, Shanghai, China.


### Matrigel plug assay
  The Matrigel plug assay is a commonly used experimental method for evaluating angiogenesis in vivo and provides an important tool for research in the fields of vascular biology, oncology, and drug development [40]. Matrigel is a basement membrane preparation extracted from mouse sarcoma that contains a variety of extracellular matrix components and growth factors [41]. As described previously [42], Matrigel (Corning) was thawed on ice, and lentivirus Sh-SMYD3, control virus Sh-Ctrl, ZYZ329 (10 µM final concentration), EPZ031686 (10 µM final concentration), or an equal amount of DMSO were added to 500 ul of Matrigel. The mixture was then injected subcutaneously into the flanks of C57BL/6 mice. After 14 days, plugs were excised, photographed for gross morphology, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. For morphological assessment, paraffin embedded plugs were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and imaged using an Olympus SLIDEVIEW VS200 for qualitative assessment of cellular infiltration. For quantitative analysis, plugs were embedded in O.C.T. compound, and 6 µm sections were prepared from the central portion of each plug. We systematically selected five non-consecutive sections per plug for immunofluorescent staining with an anti-CD31 antibody (1:400, 28083-1-AP, Proteintech) and DAPI. Images were captured on an Olympus SpinSR10 confocal microscope using a 20× objective. For each section, three non-overlapping fields of view were randomly imaged by an investigator blinded to the treatment groups. Angiogenesis was quantified using ImageJ. Vessel area was calculated as the percentage of the total area occupied by CD31-positive pixels.


### Mouse aortic ring sprouting assay
  Male mice aged 6–8 weeks were randomly selected and perfused with PBS via the left ventricle. The thoracic aorta was excised and placed in Opti-MEM medium containing 1% P/S. Under a dissecting microscope, attached connective and adipose tissue, as well as branching vessels, were carefully removed. The aorta was then cut into approximately 0.5 mm loops, taking care not to damage the endothelial cells. Aortic rings were placed in a 48-well plate containing Matrigel, covered with another layer of Matrigel, and ECM culture medium containing lentivirus Sh-SMYD3, control virus Sh-Ctrl, ZYZ329 (10 µM final concentration), EPZ031686 (10 µM final concentration), or an equal amount of DMSO was added. The culture medium was changed every other day, and photos were taken during 4–7 days.


### Tube formation assay
  The tube formation assay is a rapid quantitative method widely used to assess in vitro angiogenesis, screen for genes, signaling pathways, or drugs that regulate angiogenesis, and to evaluate the tube-forming ability of endothelial cells [43]. Matrigel was thawed on ice and added to a 96-well cell culture plate as previously described. Cells were then detached with 0.25% trypsin (Gibco) and seeded at 1–2 × 104 cells per well. Complete ECM medium, along with the test lentivirus, compound, and corresponding controls, was added. After incubation for 4–16 hours, cells were observed under a microscope and representative images of the tubular network were captured. Morphological parameters, including total tubular length, number of tubules, and branch points, were calculated using the “Angiogenesis Analyzer” plugin in ImageJ software.


### Chick chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) assay
  The chick chorioallantoic membrane assay utilizes the chorioallantoic membrane during development to investigate the effects of drugs or test substances on promoting or inhibiting angiogenesis [44]. 9–10 day-old chick embryos are used, and the CAM is exposed by windowing. The eggshell, which covers the air chamber, is removed, and the inner shell membrane is moistened with saline, then carefully peeled off to ensure the integrity of the CAM. The test substance is then applied, and after a certain period of time, the angiogenesis and vascular density of the CAM are recorded using a camera.


### Wound healing assay
  HUVEC in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 6-well plates and treated with SMYD3 knockdown lentivirus, a lentiviral control, a SMYD3 inhibitor, or a DMSO control. Subsequently, the cells were starved for 24 hours in a reduced-serum medium supplemented with 0.5% FBS. Using a sterile 200 µL pipette tip, the wells were evenly scratched perpendicularly to the plate, ensuring uniform scratch width. Dislodged cell debris was removed by washing with PBS. Following treatment with 100 ng/mL VEGF or a PBS control, the cells were observed under a microscope at appropriate time points, and the area of HUVEC migration was quantified using ImageJ software.


### NO measurement
  Nitrogen oxides (NOx), namely NO2−, NO3−, and nitrosothiols, were measured as NO production in HUVEC by chemiluminescence as described previously [42]. Briefly, the instrument voltage reading was adjusted to a baseline level, and a standard curve was generated using a series of sodium nitrite concentrations. The treated HUVEC culture supernatant was added to a chemiluminescent NOx detector (Sievers 280, Zysense). The NOx content in the sample was calculated using the area under the peak. Each sample was measured at least four times, and the average value was calculated.


### Structure modification based on lead compound 1
  Based on the indolin-2-one scaffold of the lead compound 1, a series of derivatives were designed by incorporating a Schiff base (hydrazone) moiety. The Schiff base (C = N) framework is an exceptionally adaptable drug-like pharmacophore widely utilized in the development of cancer treatments or cellular apoptosis inducers. The structures containing this bioactive moiety showed remarkable anticancer activities via the inhibition of numerous kinds of enzymes, proteins, and/or receptors that play essential roles in cell growth and survival. Consequently, Schiff base-fused chemical compounds, because of their wide scope of biological activities and synthetic applications, have been developed as a target heterocyclic framework in the research and development of medicinal chemistry. The ZYZ329 series synthesis method (general procedure) is as follows:

  A mixture of isatin and hydrazine hydrate in methanol was refluxed for 3 h and cooled to room temperature to yield the isatin monohydrazone intermediate. Then, corresponding aromatic aldehyde in methanol was added to the reaction. The reaction progress was monitored by thin-layer chromatography. After completion, the mixture was cooled to room temperature. If precipitate formed, the reaction solution was allowed to stand for 1 h, followed by filtration and recrystallization from methanol. If no precipitate formed, it was extracted with ethyl acetate and water. The organic layer was collected, dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, concentrated under reduced pressure, and purified by silica gel column chromatography.


### Molecular docking studies
  To determine binding affinity, the optimal binding mode and interaction of ZYZ329 with SMYD3 protein were assessed by molecular docking techniques using the CDOCKER module of Discovery Studio version 4.5 (Dassault Systemes BIOVIA) software. The structure of ZYZ329 was produced using Chem Draw software, and docking runs were performed and analyzed. A ranked list of the docked molecule to protein target was assessed. The ZYZ329-protein complex that best represented the docked complex was utilized for further analysis using the PyMol (Version 2.5.2) molecular visualization platform.


### Molecular dynamic simulation study
  The stability of the ZYZ329 interaction and binding to the SMYD3 active site was determined by MD simulation with the GROMACS package version 2019.3. The amber99sb-ildn force field was applied to protein parameters. Multiwfn was used to calculate ZYZ329 electrostatic potential, and the ligand was parameterized with the General Amber Force Field (GAFF) to allocate atom types and bonding parameters. Two sodium ions were added to neutralize the charge of the system, and the long-range electrostatic interactions were calculated using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method. Proteins were solvated in a 12-layer cubic box with periodic boundary conditions using the TIP3P water model with a minimum distance of 1.0 Å from solute to box boundary. Prior to simulation, the two systems were first balanced through 0.1 ns in the normal volume and temperature (NVT), with normal pressure and temperature (NPT) gathering at 300 K and 1 atmosphere, using periodic boundary conditions to apply position constraints on proteins and small molecules. Subsequently, a MD production run was performed in the NPT assemble using a Berendsen thermostat-barostat. The periodic boundary condition was used in the system, the motion equation was used together with the leap-frog algorithm, and the time step was 2 fs. Various geometric properties of the system, such as RMSD and RMSF, were determined and graphed using the Xmgrace tool. The g_mmpbsa method was applied to calculate binding free energy and energy decomposition.


### The ADMET prediction of ZYZ329
  The ADMET (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion and toxicity) profile of ZYZ329 was predicted using the ADMET Descriptors module in Discovery Studio 4.5 with default parameter settings. Key parameters evaluated included aqueous solubility at 25 °C, blood brain barrier (BBB) penetration, cytochrome P4502D6 inhibition, hepatotoxicity, human intestinal absorption (HIA), and plasma protein binding. In addition, the chemical structure of ZYZ329 was uploaded as SMILES to ADMETLab 3.0 platform [45] (https://admetlab3.scbdd.com/, accessed on 1 November 2025) and then the bioavailability of ZYZ329 was predicted.


### In vitro histone methyltransferase activity assay
  Plasmids expressing Flag-tagged SMYD3 (pCMV-SMYD3(human)-3×FLAG), SMYD2 (pLV3-CMV-SMYD2 (human)-3×FLAG), EZH2 (pCMV-EZH2 (human)-3×FLAG), and G9a (pCMV-EHMT2 (human)-3×FLAG), along with a control plasmid, were transfected into 293T cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). Flag-tagged proteins were purified using Anti-Flag magnetic beads (ShareBio), eluted via competitive peptide elution, and quantified prior to use. The in vitro histone methyltransferase (HMTase) activity assay was performed as previously described [46]. Briefly, immunoprecipitated protein (2.0 μg) was incubated with a histone mixture (2 μg, Sigma) and unlabeled SAM (20 µM, Sigma), and varying concentrations of ZYZ329 (or DMSO control) in 40 µl of HMTase buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.5; 100 mM NaCl; 10 mM dithiothreitol) at 30 °C for 1 hour. Levels of H3K4me3, H3K36me2, H3K27me3, H3K9me2, as well as the target proteins SMYD3, SMYD2, EZH2, EHMT2/G9a, and histone H3 were analyzed by Western blot.


### SMYD3 homogeneous assay
  The commercial SMYD3 homogeneous assay kit was obtained from BPS Bioscience (Cat. #52063). According to the manufacturer’s instructions, various concentrations of ZYZ329 (0–100 μM) were incubated with the SMYD3/HSP90 complex and a GST-tagged SMYD3 substrate at 30 °C for 4 hours. Acceptor beads and the primary antibody were then added, followed by the addition of donor beads. The α signal was measured using a Varioskan LUX Multimode Microplate Reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The α count is directly proportional to SMYD3 enzymatic activity.


### Cellular Thermal Shift Assay (CETSA)
  The Cellular Thermal Shift Assay (CETSA) was performed as previously described [47]. 293T cells were transfected with plasmids encoding SMYD3 or other target proteins and subsequently treated with 20 μM ZYZ329 or an equivalent volume of DMSO for 24 hours. After harvesting and washing, the cells were aliquoted into PCR tubes and heated in a thermal cycler (T100, Bio-Rad) at a temperature gradient of 37, 40, 43, 46, 49, 52, 55, 58, 61, and 64 °C for 3 minutes to denature protein. The cells were then resuspended in NP-40 buffer and subjected to three freeze-thaw cycles using liquid nitrogen. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was collected, and the levels of SMYD3, SMYD2, EZH2, EHMT2/G9a, and β-tubulin were analyzed by Western blot.


### Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay
  HUVEC were seeded in a 96-well plate, with approximately 5,000 cells per well. The next day, different concentrations or types of SMYD3 inhibitors were added along with the same volume of DMSO solution. Six replicate wells were set up for each group to minimize intra-group variation. Blank wells (no cell culture medium) and control wells (untreated cells only) were also set up. After the incubation period, a final volume of 10% CCK-8 solution (Yeasen) was added at the appropriate time points and mixed thoroughly, taking care to avoid bubbles that could affect absorbance measurement. The plates were then incubated in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator for 1 hour. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured and analyzed using a microplate reader (Varioskan Flash, Thermo Fisher Scientific).


### Enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
  According to the manufacturer’s instructions, the supernatant after cell treatment was collected and the concentration of VEGFA was calculated using the Human VEGF-A ELISA Kit (Invitrogen, BMS277-2) based on the standard curve.


### Immunofluorescence staining
  Slides were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), permeabilized with 0.3% Triton-X 100, and blocked with 3% BSA. The slides were then incubated overnight with primary antibody: rabbit anti-CD31 (1:400, 28083-1-AP, Proteintech). Secondary antibodies used were: Alexa Fluor 555 conjugate anti-rabbit IgG (1:1000, 4413S, Cell Signaling Technology) or Alexa Fluor 488 conjugate anti-rabbit IgG (1:1000, 4412S, Cell Signaling Technology). After mounting in antifade mounting medium with DAPI (Beyotime), the slides were observed and photographed under a confocal microscope (SpinSR10, Olympus).


### Mitochondrial ROS levels and membrane potential measurement
  Mitochondrial ROS levels were measured using MitoSOX Red (Invitrogen). HUVEC were seeded in confocal dishes and incubated with 10 μM MitoSOX Red for 30 minutes at 37 °C. After washing, the cells were incubated with 10 μg/mL Hoechst 33342 (Invitrogen) for 10 minutes. Finally, the cells were observed under a confocal microscope.

  Mitochondrial membrane potential was detected using a Mitochondrial membrane potential assay kit with JC-1 (Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and images were captured under a confocal microscope.


### Hindlimb ischemia model
  Male C57BL/6 mice, 6–8 weeks old, weighing 20.0 ± 2.0 g, were purchased from Shanghai Bikai Laboratory Animal Company and maintained in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) environment at the Laboratory Animal Center Fudan University. All mice had free access to adequate water and food and were housed in an environment with a moderate temperature and relative humidity.

  As described previously, the hindlimb ischemia model was established [48]. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane mixed with oxygen, and were placed in the supine position on a constant-temperature heating pad. The hair of the mouse’s hindlimbs was first shaved with an electric shaver and then completely removed with a depilatory cream. The skin of the mouse’s hindlimb was disinfected with 75% alcohol and iodine, and the superficial skin was incised. Under a dissecting microscope, the neurovascular bundle was exposed, and the femoral artery, femoral vein, and nerve were carefully isolated. The femoral artery was ligated distally near the knee and proximally near the popliteal artery. The femoral artery was severed between the proximal and distal surgical knots using ophthalmic scissors. The surgical incisions were sutured layer by layer. The mice were then placed on a heating pad and monitored continuously until awake. After the mice recovered for 1 hour, blood flow in both hindlimbs was monitored using laser speckle imaging (RFLSI ZW Laser Speckle Contrast Imaging System, RWD Life Science).

  Following the establishment of the hindlimb ischemia model, mice were randomly assigned to four groups: (1) Sham, (2) Vehicle, (3) ZYZ329 (25 mg/kg), and (4) EPZ031686 (25 mg/kg). The sham group underwent the same surgical procedure except for the ligation and excision of the femoral artery. A sample size of n = 8 mice per group was used for this study. And blood flow was recorded before surgery and at 0, 3, 7, and 14 days after surgery. The perfusion ratio between the ligated and non-ligated hindlimbs was calculated using the non-ligated hindlimb as a reference.

  To measure collateral blood flow in the ischemic hindlimb, the left ventricle was perfused with saline containing heparin and then with angiographic contrast agent (MV-120, Microfill Injection Compounds, Flow tech). ### Statistical analysis
  All statistical results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Data were assessed for normal distribution and homogeneity of variance using the Shapiro-Wilk normality test and the Levene method, respectively. For normally distributed data with homogeneous variance, comparisons between two groups were performed using the unpaired Student’s t test, and comparisons between three or more groups were performed using One-way ANOVA analysis of variance with a Bonferroni post hoc test. Two-way ANOVA analysis of variance with a Bonferroni post hoc test was used to compare differences between two factors at multiple levels. For data with unequal variance or non-normal distribution, pairwise comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test, and comparisons between three or more groups were performed using the nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis test, with Tukey’s post hoc test to correct for multiple comparisons. All results were plotted and analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10 (version 10.4.0). A p value of less than 0.05 was considered significant. Each experiment was repeated at least three times.

### SMYD3 was upregulated under pathological angiogenesis stimulation
  To elucidate the role of SMYD3 in pathological angiogenesis, we investigated the expression dynamics of SMYD3 under different pathological conditions, such as ischemia or hypoxia stimulation. Ischemic heart failure drives pathological angiogenesis within the myocardium [49]. Consistent with this, GSE26887 dataset [50] showed that SMYD3 expression was elevated in the left ventricle of patients with post-ischemic heart failure (Fig. 1A). Moreover, the expression changes of SMYD family genes in HUVEC under hypoxia in GSE1041 dataset were analyzed [51]. Transcript profiling of the SMYD family in HUVEC exposed to hypoxia revealed a selective and obvious induction of SMYD3 (Fig. 1B), whereas the expression levels of SMYD1, SMYD2, SMYD4, and SMYD5 remained statistically unchanged (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, we characterized the alterations in SMYD3 expression within VEGFA-stimulated endothelial cells and ischemic gastrocnemius muscle tissue. In HUVEC, a well-established model isolated from large vessels, VEGFA stimulation significantly increased both SMYD3 mRNA and protein levels in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 1D, F). Furthermore, stimulation with varying concentrations of VEGFA also revealed a concentration-dependent increase in SMYD3 mRNA and protein expression (Fig. 1E, G). In a murine model of hindlimb ischemia induced by femoral artery ligation, SMYD3 protein expression exhibited a 2.4-fold elevation in the gastrocnemius muscle of the ligated limb compared to the non-ligated control (Fig. 1H). Similarly, in human microvascular endothelial cells (HMECs), which are of microvascular origin, SMYD3 protein levels were also upregulated by VEGFA stimulation. This induction exhibited distinct time- and concentration-dependent dynamics (Fig. 1I, J). To further investigate the clinical relevance of SMYD3 in a pathology closely associated with aberrant angiogenesis, we examined its expression in patient-derived hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) samples. Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis revealed that SMYD3 expression was significantly upregulated in HCC tissues compared with control tissues (Fig. 1K). Both lentiviral ShRNA1 and ShRNA2 constructs markedly reduced the expression of SMYD3 and its downstream histone modification product H3K4me3 (trimethylation of lysine 4 on histone H3 protein subunit). Lentiviral ShRNA2 demonstrated superior efficacy, achieving approximately 65% SMYD3 knockdown, as confirmed by Western blot (Fig. 2A). Subsequent studies utilized lentiviral ShRNA2 (Sh-SMYD3) for SMYD3 knockdown. Processes such as neovascularization, sprouting, and tubule formation have been shown to play an important role in angiogenesis [52]. Therefore, we conducted in vivo Matrigel plug assay, ex vivo aortic ring sprouting assay, and in vitro endothelial tube formation in Matrigel to comprehensively confirm the functional impact of SMYD3 on angiogenic processes. ### Stable binding of ZYZ329 to SMYD3
  Molecular docking was performed to predict the binding mode and interaction of ZYZ329 with SMYD3 protein. The binding energy of ZYZ329 and SMYD3 was calculated to be −39.56 kcal/mol with a reasonable binding pocket; this pocket contributes to maintaining a stable conformation of ZYZ329 and SMYD3 (Fig. 5A, B). The binding region involved multiple amino acid residues, including PHE183, THR184, MET190, ILE214, TYR257, HIS366, PRO367, ASN181, SER182, CYS186, SER202, PRO210, CYS212, SER213, VAL215, PHE216, ILE237, CYS238, TYR239, GLN256, and VAL368 (Fig. 5C). The binding interface involved multiple intermolecular forces, including hydrogen bonds, van der Waals interactions, π-π stacking, and hydrophobic contacts. Specifically, ZYZ329 was predicted to form three strong hydrogen bonds with SMYD3: one between the hydrazone group and THR184, another involving the benzodioxole moiety and THR184, and a third between the oxygen atom of the benzodioxole and TYR257. In addition, a π–σ interaction was observed between the benzodioxole aromatic ring and PHE183. This interaction, occurring between an aromatic system and an aliphatic C–H bond, is recognized in drug design as a subtle yet important structural feature for optimizing ligand binding affinity and selectivity. Additionally, ZYZ329 formed conjugated interactions with PHE183, HIS366, MET190, ILE214, and PRO367 of SMYD3 protein. Collectively, these interactions likely contribute to stabilizing the predicted ZYZ329-SMYD3 complex.Fig. 5ZYZ329 showed favorable binding activity with SMYD3 protein.   The root mean square deviation (RMSD) was used to quantify structural deviations of the SMYD3-ligand complex from the initial conformation over time. In general, a stable RMSD plateau with small fluctuations suggests that the protein-ligand complex has reached an equilibrated and structurally stable state, whereas large or continuously increasing RMSD values may indicate substantial conformational drift. According to the RMSD analyses, the complex system trajectories were comparatively stable from 40 ns, with RMSD values fluctuating within 0.1 nm (Fig. 5D). This result indicated that the systems had reached convergence. During the final simulation phase (180-200 ns), the complex maintained a low RMSD plateau (about 0.5 nm), indicating sustained structural stability of the ZYZ329–SMYD3 complex. The root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) was calculated from the movement of each residue along the trajectory around the mean position, revealing the flexibility of specific protein regions during MD simulations. Higher RMSF values correspond to regions with greater flexibility. Notably, residues located in or near the predicted binding pocket (such as PHE183, THR184, MET190, ILE214, TYR257, HIS366, and PRO367) showed moderate fluctuations, consistent with a relatively stable binding mode inferred from molecular docking (Fig. 5E).

  The binding free energy of ZYZ329 to SMYD3 was calculated to be −22.56 ± 3.35 kcal/mol. This total binding energy was composed of van der Waals energy (−34.70 ± 2.078 kcal/mol), electrostatic energy (−6.06 ± 3.78 kcal/mol), polar solvation energy (22.70 ± 2.52 kcal/mol), and solvent accessible surface area (SASA) nonpolar solvation energy (−4.4 ± 0.25 kcal/mol). We subsequently calculated the energetic contribution of the potential key residues involved in the interactions of ZYZ329 and SMYD3. The results indicated that the following residues contribute favorably to ZYZ329 binding: residues PHE183 (−1.565 kcal/mol), THR184 (−2.745 kcal/mol), MET190 (−2.451 kcal/mol), ILE214 (−2.193 kcal/mol), TYR257 (−1.423 kcal/mol), PRO367 (−1.132 kcal/mol), SER182 (−1.77 kcal/mol), and VAL368 (−1.211 kcal/mol). These residues appear to be beneficial and critical binding sites for ZYZ329 and may be important for mediating its potential physiological effects on SMYD3. In contrast, interactions with TYR239 and GLN256 appeared unfavorable in this model (Fig. 5F). In addition, the energy decomposition of key residues was performed to identify the dominant interaction types (Fig. 5G). Analysis of the discrete energy terms revealed van der Waals interactions and electrostatic energy were the primary driving forces for binding in the complex, whereas polar solvation energy contributed unfavorably to the total interaction. Finally, the nonpolar solvation energy provided a minor favorable contribution to total binding free energy.

  To date, we have designed, synthesized, and screened the SMYD3 activity molecule ZYZ329 and predicted its potential binding mode with SMYD3.

  Furthermore, to elucidate the concentration-dependent biological effects of ZYZ329 targeting SMYD3, we evaluated HIF-1α and VEGFA expression under treatment with varying concentrations of ZYZ329. Hypoxia markedly induced the expression of HIF-1α and VEGFA in HUVEC and HMEC. In HMEC, treatment with ZYZ329 significantly attenuated the hypoxia-induced upregulation of HIF-1α and VEGFA in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. S5A). Consistently, VEGFA secretion in the conditioned medium decreased progressively with increasing ZYZ329 concentrations (Fig. S5C). Notably, similar results were observed in HUVEC, where ZYZ329 effectively inhibited hypoxia-induced HIF-1α and VEGFA expression (Fig. S5B) as well as VEGFA secretion (Fig. S5D) in a dose-dependent fashion. Considering that anti-VEGF therapies often encounter resistance and that ZYZ329 also modulates VEGFA, we employed the VEGFA-neutralizing antibody bevacizumab to investigate the potential combinatorial effects of ZYZ329 and anti-VEGF therapy. Our results demonstrated that both ZYZ329 and bevacizumab, when applied individually, significantly suppressed VEGF-induced endothelial cell tube formation (Fig. S5E) and proliferation (Fig. S5F). Notably, the combination of ZYZ329 and bevacizumab produced a more pronounced inhibition of both tube formation and cell proliferation compared to either treatment alone, suggesting that ZYZ329 may enhance the therapeutic efficacy of current anti-VEGF strategies.

  Since VEGFA is a pro-angiogenic factor downstream of HIF-1α, and we also found in bioinformatics analysis that HIF-1α is a potential downstream target of SMYD3 on angiogenesis (Fig. S6), we hypothesized that SMYD3-controlled angiogenesis is mediated by HIF-1α. To test this, we used siRNA (Si HIF-1α) to deplete HIF-1α expression. As expected, knockdown of HIF-1α led to a significant reduction in VEGFA levels (Fig. 7G), consistent with its canonical role as a direct transcriptional activator. Following HIF-1α knockdown, there was no significant difference in VEGFA expression between the Sh-SMYD3 knockdown group and the Sh-Ctrl group (Fig. 7G). Furthermore, the difference in tube formation between the two groups was abolished after HIF-1α depletion (Fig. 7H, I). To further investigate the mechanism by which SMYD3 regulated HIF-1α, we added cycloheximide (CHX) to inhibit protein synthesis and measure the kinetics of HIF-1α degradation. HIF-1α degradation was accelerated in Sh-SMYD3 cells compared with Sh-Ctrl cells (Fig. 7J). Similarly, when SMYD3 was inhibited by ZYZ329, HIF-1α degradation was significantly more rapid than in the DMSO group (Fig. 7K). These findings suggest that both genetic knockdown and pharmacological inhibition of SMYD3 reduce HIF-1α protein stability, thereby attenuating VEGFA production and angiogenesis. Critically, HIF-1α serves as an essential mediator of SMYD3-controlled angiogenic regulation, as evidenced by the abolition of anti-angiogenic effects upon HIF-1α depletion.


### SMYD3 regulated HIF-1α/VEGF axis partially via mitochondrial ROS
  Previous studies have shown that mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mROS) stabilize HIF-1α protein and activate its downstream signaling, and contribute to malignant progression [54]. To clarify the mechanism by which SMYD3 regulates pathological angiogenesis, we determined whether SMYD3 regulates the HIF-1α/VEGFA signaling pathway through mROS. Under hypoxic conditions, knockdown of SMYD3 significantly attenuated mROS levels in endothelial cells (Fig. 8A). However, overexpression of SMYD3 using adenovirus conversely elevated mROS levels (Fig. 8B). To validate mROS as the functional mediator, we employed the mitochondrial-targeted antioxidant MitoQ to scavenge mROS. MitoQ treatment significantly attenuated hypoxia-induced mROS accumulation. Notably, MitoQ abolished SMYD3 overexpression-induced mROS elevation, eliminating the difference between Ad-SMYD3 and Ad-Ctrl groups in hypoxic HUVEC (Fig. 8C). Accumulation of mROS triggers excessive oxidative stress, which in turn causes a loss of mitochondrial membrane potential and promotes the release of apoptotic proteins, thereby impairing angiogenesis [55]. Therefore, we examined whether the mROS elevation caused by SMYD3 overexpression is tolerated by endothelial cells and whether it induces apoptosis in HUVECs under hypoxia. Hoechst 33342 nuclear staining confirmed intact nuclear morphology without nuclear fragmentation or condensation (Fig. 8C). Mitochondrial membrane potential dissipation is a hallmark of early apoptosis [56]. In hypoxic HUVEC, JC-1 staining results showed no significant changes in mitochondrial membrane potential levels following SMYD3 overexpression or MitoQ treatment, indicating sustained mitochondrial homeostasis under pathological stress (Fig. 8D). In addition, Western blot analysis of apoptosis regulators revealed that neither SMYD3 overexpression nor MitoQ treatment altered the expression of pro-apoptotic BAX or the BAX/BCL-2 ratio, consistent with maintained mitochondrial integrity (Fig. 8E). These results demonstrated that SMYD3 overexpression induces a moderate elevation of mROS within physiologically tolerable thresholds, sustaining mitochondrial homeostasis in hypoxic endothelial cells. We further investigated whether this SMYD3-dependent mROS modulation regulates angiogenic signaling. In hypoxic HUVEC, SMYD3 overexpression significantly upregulated HIF-1α and VEGFA expression. However, MitoQ treatment abolished this SMYD3-mediated upregulation, eliminating differences in HIF-1α and VEGFA expression between Ad-SMYD3 and Ad-Ctrl groups (Fig. 8F). In terms of tube formation assay, SMYD3 overexpression significantly increased tube length, but this pro-angiogenic effect was abolished by MitoQ treatment (Fig. 8G). Collectively, these results suggest that SMYD3 regulates the HIF-1α/VEGFA axis partially via mROS-dependent signaling in pathological angiogenesis. In HUVEC, knockdown of SMYD3 significantly attenuated VEGFA-induced activation of VEGFR2, as evidenced by a marked reduction in phosphorylation at the Tyr1175 residue (Fig. S7A). Tyr1175 is a key phosphorylation site known to promote pro-angiogenic signaling [50]. Furthermore, treatment with increasing concentrations of ZYZ329 progressively suppressed VEGFA-stimulated phosphorylation of VEGFR2 at Tyr1175 in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. S7B). Collectively, these results indicate that beyond reducing VEGFA expression, genetic or pharmacological inhibition of SMYD3 may also partially impair VEGFR2 signal transduction.

  Building on the elucidated mechanism of SMYD3-mediated angiogenesis regulation partially via the mROS/HIF-1α/VEGFA axis, we assessed the in vivo anti-angiogenic efficacy of the novel SMYD3 inhibitor ZYZ329 using a murine hindlimb ischemia model. Hindlimb perfusion recovery was quantitatively assessed using laser speckle imaging preoperatively and at postoperative days 0, 3, 7, and 14. Laser speckle imaging analysis confirmed that severe impairment of hindlimb perfusion on the ligated side after femoral artery ligation, with residual perfusion ratio reduced to approximately 15% of the contralateral control, thus validating the successful establishment of the model (Fig. 9B, C). Subsequently, the ischemic/non-ischemic hindlimb perfusion ratio progressively recovered in the Vehicle group. In contrast, treatment with EPZ031686 or ZYZ329 significantly impaired this recovery, with the ZYZ329 group showing the most pronounced suppression (Fig. 9B, C).   We performed an additional in vivo efficacy study using a dose-escalation regimen of ZYZ329 in the hindlimb ischemia model. Mice were treated with low, medium, or high doses of ZYZ329 (10, 25, and 40 mg/kg/day, respectively) via intraperitoneal injection. The rats in the Vehicle control group received a solvent, while the treatment group was administered ZYZ329 via intraperitoneal injection at a dosage of 25 mg/kg/day. ## Discussion
  Pathological angiogenesis, characterized by persistent and dysregulated vessel formation, drives disease progression in conditions such as solid tumors, diabetic retinopathy, atherosclerotic plaque instability, and rheumatoid arthritis [5]. Targeting pathological angiogenesis represents a promising therapeutic strategy. Our research has shown that SMYD3 expression in endothelial cells is upregulated in response to pro-angiogenic stimuli, including ischemia, hypoxia, and VEGF exposure. Both genetic knockdown and pharmacological inhibition of SMYD3 potently suppressed angiogenic processes. Mechanistically, SMYD3 regulated the HIF-1α/VEGFA signaling axis by modulating mitochondrial ROS production, thereby controlling angiogenesis in endothelial cells. Towards translational application, we developed ZYZ329, a novel small molecule targeting SMYD3, through an integrated drug discovery approach combining computational screening, medicinal chemistry optimization, and pharmacological validation. ZYZ329 exhibited potent anti-angiogenic efficacy in both in vitro and in vivo models.

  In the in vitro studies, we selected HUVEC derived from macrovessels and HMEC derived from microvessels to validate our key findings. HUVEC were selected because they constitute the benchmark model for angiogenesis research, characterized by high experimental reproducibility and extensive documentation in the literature. Complementarily, HMEC were utilized to model the biological properties of microvessels, which serve as the predominant locus of pathological angiogenesis. The combined use of these endothelial cell types strengthens the rigor and translational relevance of our conclusions. In the in vivo experiments, a murine hindlimb ischemia model was used to evaluate pathological angiogenesis, whereas a rat skin wound-healing model was employed to characterize physiological angiogenic responses. The hindlimb ischemia model primarily reflects a process of therapeutic or reparative angiogenesis, which can differ from the dysregulated pathological angiogenesis seen in diseases like cancer. To bridge this gap and enhance clinical relevance, we examined SMYD3 expression in patient-derived liver cancer tissues. The marked upregulation of SMYD3 within the tumor vasculature (Fig. 1K) provides compelling evidence that the proposed mechanism is operative within the tumor microenvironment. Nonetheless, future studies investigating the role of SMYD3 in other angiogenesis-dependent disease models, such as diabetic retinopathy or orthotopic tumor models, would be highly valuable. Moreover, exploring the cell-type-specific functions of SMYD3 in other components of the tumor microenvironment, such as pericytes or immune cells, could reveal further layers of regulation.

  Our data indicate that VEGF and hypoxia induce SMYD3 protein expression, but knockdown of HIF-1α did not significantly alter SMYD3 expression. Interestingly, however, knockdown of VEGFR2 resulted in a statistically significant downregulation of SMYD3 expression (Fig. S9). This suggests that SMYD3 may be a downstream target of VEGFR2 and may participate in a positive feedback loop with HIF-1α. We propose that the VEGF/VEGFR2 signaling pathway and the SMYD3/HIF-1α pathway may form a positive feedback loop: VEGF upregulates SMYD3 via VEGFR2; SMYD3, in turn, stabilizes HIF-1α; and HIF-1α can then transcriptionally activate more VEGF, thereby potentially amplifying the hypoxic signaling pathway. However, further studies are still needed to elucidate how VEGFR2 influences the regulation of SMYD3. In addition, according to literature review, VEGF may also regulate SMYD3 expression through other pathways. Existing studies point to at least two plausible mechanisms involving the transcription factors E2F-1 and the β-catenin/TCF4 complex. Both E2F-1 and the β-catenin/TCF4 complex have been independently shown to directly bind to and activate the SMYD3 promoter [25, 29]. Critically, VEGF signaling is known to modulate the activity of both pathways, enhancing E2F-1 promoter occupancy and β-catenin activity [59, 60]. This suggests that VEGF likely induces SMYD3 transcription by co-opting a network of these transcription factors. This positions SMYD3 as a key convergence point or “hub”, integrating signals from both hypoxia and growth factors. Dissecting the relative contribution of each transcription factor in VEGF-mediated SMYD3 induction via promoter assays and ChIP-seq remains an important direction for future research.

  Previous studies have indicated that SMYD3 is associated with tumor growth and metastasis [61]. However, the role of SMYD3 in regulating pathological angiogenesis remains undefined. Here, we identify aberrant SMYD3 upregulation as a pivotal regulator of angiogenesis. It has been reported that VEGF stimulation or hypoxic exposure induces high oxidative stress accompanied by intracellular ROS accumulation [62, 63]. This oxidative burden drives endothelial dysfunction and aberrant angiogenesis [64]. Our data extend this paradigm by demonstrating that SMYD3 and mROS levels were elevated in hypoxic HUVECs, and that knockdown or overexpression of SMYD3 modulated intracellular mROS levels. This is consistent with previous reports. In zebrafish, SMYD3 activated the hypoxia signaling pathway and induced the accumulation of ROS [65]. In pancreatic cancer and lung adenocarcinoma, overexpression of SMYD3 is associated with excessive activation of the RAS signaling pathway and elevated ROS levels [27]. Therefore, we hypothesize that elevated SMYD3 expression in endothelial cells promotes pathological angiogenesis by driving mitochondrial ROS generation. To delineate the functional role of SMYD3 in angiogenesis, a lentivirus was constructed to effectively knock down SMYD3. As expected, knockdown of SMYD3 reduced pathological angiogenesis both in vivo and in vitro. It is worth noting that elevated ROS levels exhibit a dual role in angiogenesis regulation, and there are also reports that ROS inhibit angiogenesis by promoting cell apoptosis [66]. In physiological angiogenesis, excessive ROS impairs the function of vascular endothelial cells and reduces blood flow recovery in the hindlimbs of mice [67]. In pathological angiogenesis, a moderate level of ROS induces the activation of proteins such as HIF-1α and NF-κB, which are known to promote pathological angiogenesis [68]. This difference is likely related to the concentration of ROS. Whereas supraphysiological ROS promotes endothelial apoptosis and impairs angiogenesis, moderate ROS elevation supports pro-angiogenic signaling within a tolerable range [69]. In non-small cell lung cancer cells, SMYD3 knockdown significantly upregulated pro-apoptotic proteins and downregulated anti-apoptotic proteins [70]. Therefore, it is possible that upregulation of SMYD3 may antagonize excessive ROS-mediated apoptosis, thereby maintaining endothelial cell viability and promoting angiogenesis.

  Furthermore, we observed that either knockdown or pharmacological inhibition of SMYD3 significantly decreased the intracellular levels of NO and ROS in endothelial cells. This perturbation may disrupt the delicate redox equilibrium between NO and ROS, thereby potentially contributing to endothelial dysfunction. However, in the context of pathological angiogenesis, the endothelial environment is often characterized by a state of high oxidative stress, where excessive ROS production itself leads to NO scavenging and eNOS uncoupling, ultimately causing dysfunction [5, 71]. We hypothesize that the primary effect of SMYD3 inhibition is to normalize this initial state of excessive oxidative stress. The subsequent reduction in NO may reflect a return to a more quiescent endothelial state, rather than a primary detrimental effect. This study confirms that inhibiting SMYD3 reduces mROS levels, and that scavenging mROS reverses the upregulation of HIF-1α induced by SMYD3 overexpression, indicating that mROS is a critical component of the SMYD3 regulatory pathway. Moreover, previous studies have shown that SMYD3 can also directly bind to and stabilize HIF-1α; therefore, SMYD3 may finely regulate HIF-1α stability through multiple mechanisms [65]. Nevertheless, the precise interplay between SMYD3, ROS, and NO signaling under different physiological and pathological conditions is intricate and warrants further systematic investigation.

  In cancer, mROS has been shown to promote the activation of HIF-1α signaling by stabilizing HIF-1α protein, thereby promoting cancer progression [72]. HIF-1α is also a known pro-angiogenic signal [73]. Based on our previous research and literature reports, we demonstrated that SMYD3 modulates mROS levels to stabilize HIF-1α protein. This SMYD3-mROS-HIF-1α axis subsequently activates VEGFA production, thereby regulating angiogenesis. The precise mechanistic cascade by which SMYD3 regulates the HIF-1α/VEGFA axis warrants further discussion. While SMYD3 is a histone methyltransferase capable of direct transcriptional activation, several lines of evidence from our work and others suggest that its control over HIF-1α and VEGFA in our models is likely indirect and predominantly mediated at the post-translational level. Firstly, our previous genome-wide analysis (ChIP-seq and RNA-seq) in AngII-stimulated rat aortic endothelial cells revealed no direct regulation of the VEGFA or HIF1A genes by SMYD3, nor did it significantly alter their transcription levels, arguing against a direct transcriptional mechanism [74]. This finding is consistent with established literature demonstrating that SMYD3 can directly bind to and stabilize the HIF-1α protein in a catalytic-activity-independent manner, thereby preventing its ubiquitin-mediated degradation [65]. This protein-stabilizing effect provides a plausible explanation for the observed accumulation of HIF-1α and subsequent upregulation of its target gene, VEGFA. Furthermore, our current data implicate mitochondrial ROS as a downstream effector, as scavenging mROS abrogated the SMYD3-mediated upregulation of HIF-1α and VEGFA. However, this does not rule out parallel, non-transcriptional mechanisms. Indeed, literature suggests that SMYD3 can methylate non-histone proteins, including key angiogenic signaling molecules like VEGFR1 and AKT1 [75, 76]. Therefore, it is plausible that SMYD3 orchestrates angiogenesis on multiple levels. Future studies employing ChIP-seq in hypoxic endothelial cells will be invaluable to conclusively map the direct genomic targets of SMYD3 and fully elucidate the transcriptional versus non-transcriptional arms of its regulatory network.

  VEGFA is a master driver of pathological angiogenesis. After binding to VEGFR2, VEGFA activates VEGFR2 tyrosine kinase activity and initiates multiple downstream signaling cascades, thereby inducing endothelial cell proliferation, migration, tube formation, and other angiogenic responses [77, 78]. Currently, there are many targeted therapies against VEGFA/VEGF signaling, such as bevacizumab and aflibercept, which are widely used in the treatment of pathological angiogenesis-related diseases [79]. Although VEGFA-targeted therapy has made significant progress in many areas, it still faces challenges such as drug resistance and long-term medication requirements [80]. The mechanistic profile of ZYZ329 suggests a complementary role alongside anti-VEGF therapy, potentially mitigating treatment resistance. Notably, we observed that ZYZ329 enhances the anti-angiogenic effects of bevacizumab in endothelial cells. This finding supports the development of combination therapies that incorporate ZYZ329 with anti-VEGF agents to potentially overcome resistance and improve efficacy. Similarly, recent studies showed that the bifunctional compound DYB‑03 inhibits tumor angiogenesis and exerts significant anti‑tumor activity by simultaneously targeting HIF-1α and EZH2 [81]. In an analogous strategy, co-targeting SMYD3 and EZH2 may disrupt compensatory epigenetic mechanisms and yield enhanced anti-angiogenic outcomes. This study supports SMYD3 inhibition as a potential alternative or adjunctive therapeutic strategy to reduce the treatment burden and improve efficacy.

  The existing SMYD3 inhibitors consist primarily of chemical tools used for target validation. BCI-121 was the first small-molecule inhibitor of SMYD3 to be described [31]. It demonstrated efficacy in preclinical models, including reducing tumor growth in xenografts [82, 83]. However, the pharmacological profile of this compound remains inadequately characterized [19]. Furthermore, reported inconsistencies regarding its effect on intracellular H3K4 methylation levels warrant cautious interpretation of biological data derived from its application [84]. BAY-6035 is another SMYD3 inhibitor and has been characterized as a chemical probe targeting SMYD3, implying its primary use for mechanistic studies rather than direct therapeutic application. However, detailed pharmacological data and broad accessibility for this compound are still limited [32]. Similarly, other inhibitors like GSK2807 have been referenced, yet publicly available information on their profiling and application remains scarce [33]. EPZ031686, a sulfonamide compound, is arguably the most well-characterized non-covalent SMYD3 inhibitor to date and is often used as a reference inhibitor in research [34, 85]. Similar to BCI-121, and perhaps most tellingly, to our knowledge there is no evidence of EPZ031686 advancing into clinical trials. More recent efforts have explored alternative mechanisms of inhibition, leading to novel agents that further highlight both the potential of targeting SMYD3 and the remaining room for improvement. EM127 represents a significant advancement as a second-generation, site-specific covalent inhibitor of SMYD3 [35, 86]. While advantageous for target residence time, covalent inhibitors carry inherent risks, including the potential for off-target modification and an increase the risk of immunogenicity. In conclusion, the journey of SMYD3 inhibitor development is far from over. The limitations of the current agents are not minor; they represent fundamental barriers to clinical translation.

  Regarding ZYZ329, in both the hindlimb ischemia animal model and functional assays using endothelial cells, ZYZ329 demonstrated superior efficacy compared to EPZ031686. Although ZYZ329 showed lower potency in the SMYD3 enzymatic assay at the molecular level, its moderate activity (IC50 = 0.419 μM), along with supportive molecular docking results, is consistent with its in vivo potency. We hypothesize that the relatively high molecular weight of EPZ031686 (590 Da) may limit its drug-likeness due to a violation of Lipinski’s Rule of Five, a widely recognized guideline for assessing a compound’s drug-likeness [87]. This rule suggests that a molecule is less likely to exhibit good drug-likeness if it violates more than one of the following criteria: molecular weight > 500, LogP > 5, hydrogen bond donors > 5, or hydrogen bond acceptors > 10. In contrast, ZYZ329 has a molecular weight of 329 Da, which falls well within the acceptable range defined by these rules, potentially facilitating better cellular uptake and tissue distribution. In terms of pharmacokinetics and toxicity, in silico pharmacokinetic (PK) predictions from ADMETLab 3.0 suggest that ZYZ329 exhibits favorable predicted human liver microsome (HLM) stability (> 30 min) and low clearance (3.333 mL/min/kg). In contrast, EPZ031686 shows relatively poorer PK properties, with HLM stability below 30 minutes and clearance exceeding 7.372 mL/min/kg, which may partly contribute to its comparatively weaker in vivo performance. Additionally, ZYZ329 demonstrates a predicted bioavailability of over 50%, further supporting its drug-like profile. In terms of toxicity, ZYZ329 also shows a favorable predicted hERG safety profile, with a probability of 0.183 for being a hERG inhibitor, which is significantly lower than that of EPZ031686 (0.993). Overall, these favorable properties of ZYZ329 may enhance its translational potential. Furthermore, the high cost of EPZ031686 may present a practical barrier. Conversely, ZYZ329 emerges as a compelling candidate for further development due to its potent SMYD3 targeting activity, a straightforward synthesis (completed in two steps) using economical and readily available starting materials, and favorable predicted bioavailability and pharmacokinetic profiles.

  A critical aspect in evaluating any new SMYD3 inhibitor is its target selectivity. Beyond molecular docking, the selectivity of ZYZ329 and its cellular target engagement with the SMYD3 enzyme were rigorously assessed through in vitro methyltransferase activity assays and the Cellular Thermal Shift Assay (CETSA). Crucially, ZYZ329 exhibited minimal cross-reactivity against other key methyltransferases, including the closely related family member SMYD2, as well as EZH2 and EHMT2. This confirms its utility as a selective inhibitor over the tested methyltransferases for dissecting SMYD3-specific biological functions. Furthermore, while our current data demonstrate selectivity against these key methyltransferases, a broader, unbiased screening across a larger panel (e.g., a commercial kinase/methyltransferase panel) would be necessary to fully map the off-target profile of ZYZ329. Future medicinal chemistry efforts will focus on optimizing the ZYZ329 scaffold to enhance its potency into the nanomolar range and improve its drug-like properties, guided by structure-activity relationship studies.

  A more critical concern is whether targeting a fundamental process like angiogenesis could impair beneficial physiological events. To investigate this, we tested ZYZ329 in a rat skin wound healing model, a classic process reliant on physiological angiogenesis. Interestingly, no statistically significant difference in wound healing was observed between the ZYZ329-treated group and the vehicle control group. While this outcome could be partially attributed to pharmacokinetic factors, it is also consistent with previous studies showing that genetic inactivation of SMYD3 under physiological conditions does not result in detectable abnormalities or developmental defects in mice, suggesting that SMYD3 inhibitors may possess a favorable safety profile [88]. Consistent with this, SMYD3 expression is markedly upregulated in pathological settings, such as tumors and chronic ischemia, while remaining relatively low during normal tissue repair. Consequently, ZYZ329 may exert substantial effects primarily in contexts where SMYD3 signaling is aberrantly activated. Nevertheless, these findings also remind us that systemic and sustained administration of potent SMYD3 inhibitors may elicit unintended side effects, including the exacerbation of myocardial ischemia or impairment of ovarian function. A comprehensive evaluation of these possible toxicities in appropriate preclinical models will be an essential next step in the development of SMYD3-targeted therapies. Looking ahead, a critical research trajectory will involve conducting definitive in vivo pharmacokinetic studies to fully characterize the absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) profile of ZYZ329. Concurrently, comprehensive assessments of its long-term chronic toxicity and potential off-target effects are critical for defining its safety window. In parallel, future medicinal chemistry campaigns will be dedicated to systematically optimizing the ZYZ329 molecular scaffold. Guided by robust structure-activity relationship (SAR) insights, these efforts will aim to enhance its potency into the nanomolar range and further refine its drug-like properties, such as solubility and metabolic stability.


## Conclusion
  In summary, this study demonstrated that SMYD3 is upregulated in endothelial cells under pro-angiogenic conditions. Both genetic and pharmacological inhibition of SMYD3 markedly attenuated angiogenesis. SMYD3 regulated pathological angiogenesis partially through the mROS/HIF-1α/VEGFA axis. A novel SMYD3-targeting molecule ZYZ329 was developed and exhibited potent anti-angiogenic efficacy. These findings identify ZYZ329 as a promising therapeutic candidate for treating angiogenesis-driven diseases such as metastatic cancers and diabetic retinopathy.
