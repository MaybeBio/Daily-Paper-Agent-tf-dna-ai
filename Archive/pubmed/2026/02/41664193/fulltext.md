# Integrative machine learning identifies a TEAD4-driven endothelial program shaping drug sensitivity and microvascular invasion in HCC


## Aim
  Microvascular invasion (MVI) drives early recurrence and poor survival in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). While tumor cell invasiveness has been well explored, the contribution of endothelial cells (ECs) to MVI and treatment response, including mechanisms of drug resistance, remains unclear. This study aimed to characterize endothelial programs linked to MVI and to develop a machine learning model for prognostic and therapeutic prediction.


## Methods
  We integrated single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics from HCC samples with different MVI status. Endothelial heterogeneity was analyzed using pseudotime trajectories, transcription factor networks, and cell–cell communication. Spatial mapping with cell2location and Spotlight localized endothelial subsets and defined their distribution around tumor nests and invasive fronts. A machine learning prognostic model based on Hippo–YAP pathway regulators was constructed, and drug-response patterns were assessed using CTRP and PRISM datasets. Key regulators were validated through tube formation and proliferation assays.


## Results
  We identified an endothelial subset with strong Hippo–YAP activation that was enriched in MVI⁺ tumors and positioned at the peritumoral invasive front. These cells showed stem-like features and marked angiogenic potential. TEAD4 emerged as the dominant downstream transcription factor, driving APLN, ANGPT2, and VEGFA expression. TEAD4 inhibition reduced tube formation and limited tumor cell proliferation. The Hippo–YAP–based prognostic model, generated through integrative machine learning, outperformed clinical variables and existing signatures across multiple cohorts. High-risk scores correlated with immunosuppressive features and heightened sensitivity to microtubule- and cell-cycle–targeting drugs, including paclitaxel, irinotecan, and ispinesib.


## Conclusion
  This study reveals a TEAD4-centered endothelial program that promotes angiogenesis and MVI in HCC. The machine learning–based prognostic model provides a robust tool for risk assessment and may help guide future therapeutic strategies targeting endothelial signaling.

## Introduce
  HCC is one of the most prevalent malignancies of the digestive system worldwide, with high incidence and mortality rates [1]. Although surgical resection and liver transplantation represent the primary curative options for HCC, postoperative recurrence remains common and long-term survival remains limited, posing a significant clinical challenge [2]. Numerous studies indicate that MVI is a critical pathological determinant of HCC recurrence and long-term prognosis, and has been extensively employed for postoperative risk stratification and prognostic evaluation [3, 4]. However, MVI does not manifest in all HCC patients, and its underlying mechanisms exhibit substantial heterogeneity, which remain incompletely understood.

  MVI is typically characterized by tumor cells penetrating the stromal barrier to invade the adjacent vascular microenvironment, engaging in direct interactions with vascular endothelial cells [4]. This process not only reflects the high invasiveness of tumor cells but also indicates that the tumor vascular microenvironment plays a pivotal role. Vascular endothelial cells not only serve as conduits for tumor nutrients and oxygen but also directly contribute to MVI through vascular remodeling and secretion of pro-angiogenic factors. Emerging evidence further suggests that abnormal vascular structure and endothelial dysfunction contribute to therapeutic resistance in HCC by limiting drug delivery, inducing hypoxic niches, and shaping an immunosuppressive microenvironment [5]. Pro-angiogenic endothelial states have also been associated with reduced sensitivity to tyrosine kinase inhibitors and impaired immune cell infiltration, underscoring the broader impact of endothelial programs on both MVI formation and treatment response [6].

  The Hippo-YAP pathway has recently emerged as a central focus in cancer research, regulating cell proliferation, apoptosis, stemness, and angiogenesis [7–9]. Under physiological conditions, the Hippo pathway maintains tissue homeostasis by restricting nuclear translocation of YAP/TAZ, whereas in tumors, YAP/TAZ activation often correlates with enhanced invasiveness and poor patient prognosis [10]. Evidence indicates that YAP/TAZ can interact with TEAD family transcription factors to promote angiogenesis and metastasis in various cancers [11–13]. However, the specific role of Hippo-YAP signaling in HCC vascular endothelial cells, particularly its contribution to MVI, remains poorly characterized.

  Clinically, anti-angiogenic therapies, such as bevacizumab and multi-target tyrosine kinase inhibitors, have become standard treatments for advanced HCC, yet their efficacy in suppressing MVI remains suboptimal. This suggests that MVI-associated angiogenesis may be driven by factors beyond the classical VEGF signaling pathway. Elucidating the molecular characteristics of vascular endothelial cells in MVI and identifying novel regulatory pathways and therapeutic targets represents a critical challenge and potential avenue for advancing anti-MVI strategies.

  In this study, we employed integrated single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses to comprehensively delineate the transcriptional landscape of HCC vascular endothelial cells and to investigate their molecular mechanisms underlying MVI. We further focused on the Hippo-YAP pathway and its key downstream regulators, aiming to elucidate their potential roles in angiogenesis and MVI. This provides a rationale for developing novel anti-MVI therapeutic strategies.


## Methods

### Single-cell quality control and annotation
  Single-cell RNA-seq data GSE24288912 [14] were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database and integrated. DoubletFinder was used to remove potential doublets, and decontX was applied to correct for ambient RNA contamination. The percentage of mitochondrial, ribosomal, and erythrocyte genes was calculated using the PercentageFeatureSet function. Cells expressing more than 300 genes, with mitochondrial gene expression < 20% and erythrocyte gene proportion < 1%, were retained for downstream analysis. The integrated dataset was normalized, and the top 2000 highly variable genes were identified using FindVariableFeature. All genes were scaled using ScaleData, and principal component analysis (PCA) was performed via RunPCA. Batch effects were corrected using the Harmony algorithm. Clustering was conducted with FindNeighbors and FindClusters (resolution = 0.8), followed by dimensionality reduction using UMAP. Marker genes for each cluster were identified using FindAllMarkers, and cell types were annotated based on literature references [14] and the CellMarker 2.0 database [15]. For validation, survival-associated LIHC and pan-cancer transcriptomic data were retrieved from the UCSC database. External datasets GSE116174, HCCDB18, E-TABM-36, GSE10141, GSE144269, GSE14520, GSE27150, and GSE76427 were employed for model verification. Differential expression between tumor and normal groups was calculated using the limma package [16] with a threshold of p < 0.05.Cell-type deconvolution was performed using BayesPrism [17], with single-cell data serving as the prior to infer tumor microenvironment composition and gene expression from bulk RNA-seq. Samples were stratified into high and low expression groups based on optimal cutoffs for subsequent survival analysis using the survival package.


### Copy number variation analysis
  For epithelial cells, copy number variations (CNVs) were inferred using the inferCNV algorithm (infercnvpy v0.4.0), with the average profile of non-malignant immune cells as the reference. Large-scale CNV patterns at the single-cell level were used to distinguish malignant from non-malignant epithelial cells.


### Tissue enrichment of cell types
  Tissue-specific enrichment of cell populations was analyzed using STARTRAC-distribution (Ro/e) [18]. The ratio of observed to expected cell counts, calculated via chi-square tests, was used to identify enrichment patterns. Clusters with Ro/e > 1.5 were considered tissue-enriched, whereas clusters with Ro/e < 0.5 were considered underrepresented. Fisher’s exact test was further applied to validate cell-type distributions across tissues.


### Gene set and functional scoring
  Hippo-YAP gene sets were obtained from the MSigDB database (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb), including core kinases, transcriptional cofactors, interacting proteins, and reported downstream targets. GSVA was performed to evaluate pathway activity in both single-cell and spatial transcriptomic datasets.

  Five scoring methods—AUCell, UCell [19], singscore [20], ssGSEA [21], and AddModuleScore [22]—were applied to single-cell data. Scores were normalized and merged, and samples were stratified into high- and low-expression groups based on the median score.


### SCENIC analysis
  Regulatory networks for transcription factors (TFs) in different cell states were inferred using SCENIC [23] via pySCENIC (Python 3.10). Enrichment analysis was conducted using the RcisTarget database to identify TF-target relationships near transcription start sites (TSS).


### Cell-cell communication and pseudotime analysis
  CellChat [24] was used to analyze intercellular communication based on the CellChatDB.human reference, including 32 signaling pathways. Key ligand-receptor interactions were further validated using NicheNet [25]. Pseudotime trajectories were constructed using Monocle 2 [26] to infer cellular differentiation pathways. CellCall [27] was used to validate intercellular signaling networks.


### Spatial transcriptomics
  Paired spatial transcriptomic slides (HCC-2T, HCC2T, HCC-2 L) [28] were analyzed. Tumor regions were delineated using Cottrazm, integrating CNV features to identify malignant core spots. Microregions were annotated according to dominant cell-type proportions. AUCell was used to quantify gene set enrichment per spot, and Spearman correlation analysis assessed associations between cell-type composition and gene set AUC scores. Spatial deconvolution was performed using Spotlight and cell2location [29], with default parameters. Co-localization analysis was conducted using CARD [30].


### Prognostic model construction and validation
  Prognostic genes were used to develop a consensus IRLS by integrating 10 machine learning algorithms and 101 combinations, including random survival forest (RSF), elastic net (Enet), Lasso, Ridge, stepwise Cox, CoxBoost, plsRcox, SuperPC, GBM, and survival-SVM. The workflow included: (1) Identification of intersecting differential genes between high- and low-score endothelial cells and Apelin/APJ gene set. (2) Model fitting with 101 algorithm combinations using leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) in the TCGA-LIHC cohort. 3.Validation in independent datasets (GSE116174, HCCDB18). 4.Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) was calculated for all models; the model with the highest average C-index was selected.Samples were stratified based on optimal cutoffs, and survival analysis was performed using the survival package. Meta-analysis across multiple datasets was performed using the Meta package, with log(HR) as the primary measure. HR < 1 indicated tumor-suppressive effect, whereas HR > 1 indicated oncogenic effect.


### Immune infiltration and immunotherapy prediction
  The ESTIMATE algorithm [31] was used to calculate stromal and immune scores. CIBERSORT [32], EPIC [33], MCPCounter [34], TIMER [35], xCell [36] (via IOBR) quantified immune cell composition differences between prognostic groups. Submap [37] predicted immunotherapy responsiveness, and TIP evaluated T-cell functional differences. GSEA analysis was conducted with the GseaVis package. Z-score-based correlations were calculated between model genes and key immune pathways [38]. Single-gene analyses used ESTIMATE [31] and MCP-counter [34], with 28 immune-cell gene sets from PMID:28052254 [39] evaluated via ssGSEA.


### Drug sensitivity prediction
  Expression data for human cancer cell lines (CCLs) were obtained from the CCLE [40]. Drug sensitivity data were retrieved from CTRP v2.0 and PRISM Repurposing datasets. Area under the dose-response curve (AUC) values were calculated for high- and low-risk groups to assess potential drug sensitivity.


### Tissue samples and hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining
  Tissue specimens were approved by the Ethics Committee of Anhui University of Chinese Medicine (Approval No. 2024MCZQ09). Samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and processed strictly according to the laboratory’s standard operating procedures (SOP), including trimming, dehydration, paraffin embedding, sectioning, and hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining. After mounting, sections were reviewed by a pathologist to ensure morphologic quality.


### Immunofluorescence
  Paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (95 °C, 15 min). After cooling, sections were blocked with 5% goat serum for 30 min, followed by incubation with YAP1 antibody (Proteintech, Cat. 13584-1-AP) and CD34 antibody (Abcam, Cat. ab81289) overnight at 4 °C. On the next day, slides were washed with PBS and incubated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, Alexa Fluor series) at room temperature for 1 h in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and slides were mounted using anti-fade medium. Images were acquired with a confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany). Colocalization of YAP1 and CD34 was quantified using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 (Media Cybernetics, USA).


### Cell culture and co-culture assay
  Human hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines ECDHCC-1 (BNCC, Beijing, China) and Huh7 (ATCC, USA) were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. For co-culture assays, Transwell chambers with 0.4 μm pores (Corning) were used. ECDHCC-1 cells were seeded in the upper chamber, while Huh7 cells were plated in the lower chamber. After 48 h of co-culture, ECDHCC-1 cells were collected for Western blotting and tube formation assays, whereas Huh7 cells were subjected to EdU proliferation assays.


### Western blotting
  ECDHCC-1 cells were collected from control or MGH-CP1 (a TEAD4 inhibitor, SelleckChem, USA) treatment groups. Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors (Beyotime, China) on ice for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay (Thermo Fisher, USA). Equal amounts of protein (20–50 µg) were resolved on 10–15% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk in TBST for 1 h and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were visualized using ECL reagents (Thermo Fisher, USA) and imaged on a ChemiDoc system (Bio-Rad, USA). GAPDH served as the loading control. Primary antibodies included: Apelin polyclonal antibody (Invitrogen, Cat. PA5-114860), Angiopoietin-2 recombinant antibody (Proteintech, Cat. 83816-1-RR), VEGFA recombinant antibody (Proteintech, Cat. 81323-2-RR), and GAPDH monoclonal antibody (Proteintech, Cat. 60004-1-Ig).


### EdU proliferation assay
  Cell proliferation was assessed using an EdU DNA synthesis assay kit (Abcam, #ab287859). Huh7 cells were incubated with 10 µM EdU working solution for 2 h, washed with PBS, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. After permeabilization with 1% saponin solution, cells were stained with the Click-iT reaction cocktail according to the manufacturer’s instructions and incubated in the dark for 30 min at room temperature. Fluorescent signals were analyzed by flow cytometry (BD LSRFortessa™, USA), and data were processed with FlowJo software.


### Tube formation assay
  Matrigel (Corning, USA) was thawed at 4 °C overnight, and 50 µL was added to each well of a 96-well plate, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 30 min to solidify. ECDHCC-1 cells were digested, resuspended, and seeded at a density of 2 × 10⁵/mL (100 µL per well). Tube formation was observed at 3, 6, and 12 h under an inverted microscope, and images were captured from five random fields per well. Branching points were quantified using ImageJ, with three replicates per group.


## Results

### Single-cell landscape of hepatocellular carcinoma
  Annotation of single-cell transcriptomic data identified 14 distinct cell subpopulations, including T cells (CD3D), NK cells (NKG7), endothelial cells (PECAM1), fibroblasts (ACTA2), hepatocytes (ALB), monocytes (FCN1), macrophages (CD68), neutrophils (FCGR3B), B cells (CD79A), mast cells (TPSB2), plasma cells (SDC1), dendritic cells (CD1C), proliferating cells (MKI67), and hepatic progenitor cells (HPCs, CK19) (Fig. 1A). The top three markers for each cluster highlighted the major differentially expressed genes (Fig. 1B), and feature plots confirmed the spatial distribution of representative markers in the UMAP embedding (Fig. 1C). Functional enrichment analysis of cluster-specific marker genes further validated the biological roles of these cell populations and supported the accuracy of cell-type annotation (Fig. 1D). Moreover, inferCNV analysis was used to distinguish malignant hepatocytes, which revealed extensive copy number variations in hepatocytes derived from tumor samples, thereby defining them as tumor cells (Supplementary Fig. 1A).

### The Hippo–YAP signaling pathway as a key driver of microvascular invasion in HCC
  In MVI⁺ tumor samples, HE staining revealed extensive vascular structures tightly surrounded by tumor tissue, with intravascular tumor thrombi observed in some vessels, indicative of classic morphological features of MVI. By contrast, in MVI⁻ samples, tumor cells were observed surrounding the vasculature, but no intraluminal tumor emboli were detected, and no definitive evidence of MVI was present (Fig. 2A). GSVA was applied to compare signaling pathways in vascular endothelial cells between MVI⁺ and MVI⁻ samples, revealing a significant increase in Hippo–YAP pathway component scores in MVI⁺ regions (Fig. 2B). Dual immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that, in MVI⁺ tumors, the endothelial marker CD34 (green) formed dense vascular networks, while YAP (red) was strongly expressed in endothelial cells and showed clear colocalization with CD34, suggesting YAP activation within the MVI-associated vascular microenvironment. In contrast, MVI⁻ tumors exhibited fewer and more sparsely distributed vessels, lower YAP expression, and minimal colocalization with CD34 (Fig. 2C). We observed variable expression of Hippo–YAP pathway genes across tumors (Supplementary Fig. 1B). To further quantify pathway enrichment, five independent scoring methods were applied, consistently demonstrating the highest Hippo scores in endothelial cells and fibroblasts (Fig. 2D). Comparative analysis showed that endothelial cells in MVI⁺ tumors had significantly higher Hippo scores than those in MVI⁻ tumors (p < 0.05), whereas fibroblasts displayed only a non-significant upward trend (p > 0.05) (Fig. 2E). At the whole-tumor level, Hippo scores were higher in MVI⁺ samples, with enrichment particularly evident in endothelial cell subpopulations (Fig. 2F), whereas fibroblasts showed no significant differences (Fig. 2G).

### Pseudotime and functional profiling of Hippo-related endothelial cells
  We further annotated endothelial cells identified in HCC samples into arterial, venous, capillary, and tip-like subtypes. Ro/e analysis revealed that endothelial cells with elevated Hippo activity (high-Hippo ECs) were predominantly enriched in arterial, capillary, and tip-like populations (Fig. 3A). CellCall analysis suggested that high-Hippo endothelial cells (ECs) exhibit stronger predicted communication with malignant hepatocytes compared with low-Hippo ECs. These interactions were mainly enriched in Hippo–YAP–related signaling modules, indicating a potential association between Hippo activity in ECs and tumor–endothelial communication (Fig. 3B). Pseudotime trajectory analysis indicated that high-Hippo ECs resided at the early stages of endothelial differentiation, with Hippo scores gradually declining along the differentiation trajectory (Fig. 3C). Consistently, CytoTRACE confirmed that high-Hippo ECs possessed greater stemness potential (Supplementary Fig. 2A). SCENIC analysis revealed that high-Hippo ECs were characterized by the activation of differentiation-associated transcription factor HOXB2, as well as angiogenesis-related regulators including ETS1 and ELF1. In contrast, low-Hippo ECs were enriched for inflammation-associated factors such as STAT1 (Fig. 3D). Clinical correlation analysis demonstrated that a higher proportion of high-Hippo ECs was significantly associated with poorer prognosis in both the TCGA and HCCDB18 cohorts (Fig. 3E). Differential pathway enrichment analysis further indicated that high-Hippo ECs displayed upregulation of angiogenesis-related pathways, such as Focal adhesion and the VEGF signaling pathway, whereas no significant enrichment was observed in low-Hippo ECs (Fig. 3F–G). Moreover, the Hippo-YAP downstream effector TEAD4 was markedly upregulated in high-Hippo ECs (Supplementary Fig. 2B), together with pro-angiogenic factors APLN and ANGPT2, which were also strongly expressed in this subset (Supplementary Fig. 2C–D).

### Spatial transcriptomic analysis of endothelial cells based on Hippo scores
  To further elucidate the role of Hippo–YAP signaling within the tumor microenvironment (TME), we employed spatial transcriptomics to map the localization of endothelial cells. We first localized epithelial and endothelial compartments using the marker genes EPCAM and PECAM1, respectively. EPCAM was predominantly enriched within tumor nests, whereas PECAM1 exhibited a distribution pattern along vascular structures. We next visualized the spatial expression patterns of YAP1, a central node of the Hippo–YAP pathway, together with its downstream effector TEAD4 (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, deconvolution analysis using the Spotlight algorithm across three tissue sections revealed a clear clustering of hepatocellular carcinoma cells within tumor nests. High-Hippo endothelial cells (high Hippo Endo) were predominantly distributed around the nests, whereas low-Hippo endothelial cells (low Hippo Endo) exhibited a less distinct boundary-associated distribution. We speculate that this spatial pattern of low Hippo Endo may be attributable to inflammation-induced angiogenesis within the tumor core (Fig. 4B). In addition, Hippo–YAP pathway scores derived from spatial transcriptomic analysis further corroborated these distributional patterns (Fig. 4C). To further examine cellular co-localization, we applied CARD analysis, which revealed significant spatial co-localization between high Hippo Endo and tumor cells (Fig. 4D). This observation was independently validated using cell2location (Supplementary Fig. 2A–C).

### Intercellular communication analysis
  To explore potential interactions between high- and low-Hippo endothelial subpopulations and other cell types, we performed expression-based communication analyses using NicheNet and CellChat. NicheNet analysis indicated that, in MVI⁺ tumors, when tumor cells were treated as ligands and endothelial cells as receivers, several ligand–receptor pairs showed higher predicted activity, including APELA-APLNR, ANGPTL3-(ITGA5/ITGAV), SEMA3F-(NRP1/2), as well as ANGPT2, APLN, and SEMA related signals (Fig. 5A–B). CellChat analysis further revealed that tumors with high Hippo scores were enriched for vascular-associated signaling pathways, such as ANGPT, PDGF, IGFBP, and PROC (Fig. 5C). When endothelial cells were modeled as signal senders, IGFBP3-TMEM219 interactions were preferentially detected in high-Hippo tumors. In addition, HBEGF-EGFR interactions between endothelial and tumor cells and CXCL-related interactions between endothelial cells and fibroblasts showed higher inferred activity (Fig. 5D).When endothelial cells were modeled as signal receivers, PROC-related receptor complexes, including PROC-(PROCR+HSP90AA1) and PROC-(PROCR+F2R), were predicted to be associated with angiogenesis-related programs.

### Machine learning–based construction of a Hippo-YAP–Related prognostic model
  DEGs between high- and low-Hippo Endo subpopulations were identified using the FindMarkers function. The intersection of these DEGs with Hippo-YAP pathway genes yielded 17 key differential genes. We then employed an integrative machine learning framework to develop a consensus Hippo-YAP–related prognostic model. Within the TCGA-LIHC, HCCDB18, and GSE116174 cohorts, we fitted 101 predictive models and evaluated each using the concordance index (C-index) in validation datasets. Considering both C-index scores and gene numbers, the Lasso combined with Random Survival Forest (RSF) model was identified as optimal (Fig. 6A). Subsequently, SHAP analysis was performed to interpret the contribution of individual genes to the model (Fig. 6B). Patients with high Hippo-related gene scores (HRGs) exhibited significantly worse prognosis in both training and validation cohorts (p < 0.05; Fig. 6C–E). We further compared the prognostic performance of HRGs with other clinical variables. HRGs outperformed all clinical parameters in the TCGA-LIHC and HCCDB18 cohorts (Fig. 6F–G) and demonstrated superior performance relative to most clinical variables in the GSE116174 cohort (Fig. 6H). We validated the HRG model using independent hepatocellular carcinoma transcriptomic cohorts. Meta-analysis confirmed that HRGs were significant risk factors (Fig. 6I). Comparison with previously published models in TCGA-LIHC demonstrated a distinct advantage of HRGs (Fig. 6J). Calibration curves further indicated high predictive accuracy of the model (Fig. 6K). Decision curve analysis (DCA) showed that the clinical utility of the 1-, 2-, and 3-year prognostic models was superior to other indicators (Fig. 6L–N). Survival proportional hazard analysis further confirmed that HRGs were significant risk factors (p < 0.001; Fig. 6O).

### Immune infiltration and immunotherapy prediction
  Enrichment analysis indicated that HRGs were positively associated with tumor-promoting pathways such as cell cycle and DNA damage repair, whereas they were negatively correlated with immune-related pathways, including IFN-γ signaling and antigen processing and presentation (APM). Additionally, HRGs exhibited positive correlations with immunosuppressive cell types such as regulatory T cells (Tregs) and helper T cells (Th), but were negatively correlated with effector immune cells, including CD8⁺ T cells and plasma cells (Fig. 7A).Tumor Immune Phenotype (TIP) analysis revealed that the high-HRG group exhibited enhanced anti-tumor immune activity, particularly during the infiltration of immune cells into tumors (Step 5), compared to the low-HRG group (Fig. 7B). ESTIMATE analysis showed that the high-HRG group had significantly lower Stromal, Immune, and ESTIMATE scores (p < 0.05), but higher tumor purity (p < 0.01) compared to the low-HRG group (Fig. 7C–F). These findings suggest that tumors in the high-risk group are more cell-dense with reduced stromal content, which may facilitate tumor proliferation and metastasis.Submap analysis indicated that the high-HRG group exhibited a higher proportion of complete/partial response (CR/PR) to PD-1 blockade therapy (p < 0.001; Fig. 7G), suggesting potential feasibility of HRG-based immunotherapy. Subsequent Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) of HRG-associated DEGs demonstrated significant enrichment in WNT signaling, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), oxidative stress, and Notch pathways (Fig. 7H).

### HRG programs sensitize tumors to anti-mitotic therapy and limit resistance
  Drug sensitivity analysis showed that tumors with high HRG expression had clearly lower predicted AUC values for several drugs, including paclitaxel, irinotecan, ispinesib, and GSK461364 (Figure. 8A–B). Both the correlation results and the AUC boxplots pointed to the same trend: the high-HRG group responded better to microtubule inhibitors and other agents that target cell-cycle processes. These findings suggest that HRG-related gene programs may make high-HRG tumors more vulnerable to treatments that interfere with mitosis or DNA replication.

  Fig. 8Drug sensitivity analysis of HRG-related subgroups. A. Correlation analysis revealed that higher HRG expression was associated with lower predicted AUC values for several compounds in the CTRP dataset, including paclitaxel, CAY10618, GSK461364, leptomycin B, and SB-743,921, indicating increased drug sensitivity in the high-HRG subgroup. Corresponding boxplots further confirmed significantly reduced AUC values in high-HRG samples. B. PRISM-based analysis showed similar patterns, with the high-PPS group exhibiting enhanced sensitivity to irinotecan, ispinesib, LY2606368, vinblastine, and YM-155, as reflected by negative correlation coefficients and lower AUC distributions. *Asterisks indicate statistical significance (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, **P < 0.001)

  HCC progression is closely associated with WNT, EMT, and Notch signaling pathways. To investigate the relationship between HRGs and these malignant features, we quantified the HRG-mediated activation of WNT, EMT, and Notch pathways using a z-score-based method. We observed significant positive correlations between HRGs and EMT, WNT, and Notch pathways (p < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. 4A), which were consistently observed across multiple TCGA tumor types (Supplementary Fig. 4B–D). Moreover, TEAD4, one of the model genes, was positively correlated with most cytotoxic immune cells and immune-related pathways, including inflammatory response and TNF-α signaling, as well as oncogenic pathways such as PI3K-AKT, WNT, and Notch (Supplementary Fig. 5A–B). Further immune enrichment analysis demonstrated that TEAD4 was positively associated with IFN-related pathways, JAK-STAT signaling, and TGF-β pathways across various tumors, suggesting a potential role in modulating the tumor immune microenvironment (Supplementary Fig. 6B).

## Discussion
  MVI represents a critical pathological feature in HCC and serves as a key determinant of prognosis and recurrence risk. Previous studies have largely focused on tumor cell invasiveness to explain MVI formation, emphasizing the ability of tumor cells to breach extracellular matrices and vascular walls [41, 42]. However, our study provides a novel perspective, revealing the central role of vascular endothelial cells in MVI formation through single-cell and spatial transcriptomics analyses.Our findings demonstrate that Hippo-YAP signaling is significantly activated in MVI-associated endothelial cells. This observation aligns with previous reports linking YAP activation to poor prognosis [43, 44] but further specifies cell-type specificity: Hippo activation is most pronounced in endothelial cells, whereas fibroblasts exhibit only a mild trend. These results suggest that MVI is not solely driven by tumor cells but also results from aberrant vascular endothelial remodeling and functional alterations. Pseudotime analysis further revealed that Hippo-high endothelial cells reside at the early stages of differentiation trajectories, exhibiting higher stemness and enhanced pro-angiogenic potential, which may explain their driving role in MVI formation.

  Under physiological conditions, the Hippo pathway regulates YAP/TAZ phosphorylation via upstream kinases such as MST1/2 and LATS1/2, thereby preventing nuclear translocation and interaction with TEAD family transcription factors [45]. This regulatory mechanism is crucial for maintaining tissue homeostasis and restricting abnormal angiogenesis. In gastrointestinal tumors, mutations, copy number variations, or loss of tumor suppressors such as NF2 or RASSF1 can inactivate upstream Hippo signaling, releasing YAP/TAZ inhibition and leading to aberrant activation of TEAD family members [46–48]. This “loss-of-inhibition” shift likely underlies the pro-angiogenic phenotype observed in tumor endothelial cells.

  Our study identifies a disease-relevant endothelial state driven by strong activation of the Hippo–YAP–TEAD4 axis. By integrating upstream kinases, signaling intermediates, and TEAD transcription factors, the Hippo score captured global pathway activity and highlighted TEAD4 as the principal downstream regulator. Spatial mapping showed that Hippo-high endothelial cells cluster along the tumor–vascular interface, whereas Hippo-low endothelial cells reside within tumor interiors with inflammatory features, underscoring the distinction between MVI-related and inflammation-driven angiogenesis. Hippo-high endothelial cells upregulated APLN, ANGPT2, and VEGF, exhibited arterial/tip-cell characteristics, and were enriched at the invasive boundary, consistent with a direct role in vascular remodeling and MVI formation. TEAD4 inhibition markedly impaired endothelial tube formation and tumor cell proliferation, supporting a vascular program mechanistically distinct from classical VEGF-driven angiogenesis. Importantly, this endothelial phenotype also intersected with therapeutic resistance: aberrant vasculature can restrict drug penetration, sustain hypoxia, and limit immune trafficking, and the YAP/TEAD-driven state we observed mirrors transcriptional programs associated with reduced TKI sensitivity, immune exclusion, and adaptive angiogenic escape. Moreover, pro-angiogenic endothelial plasticity marked by TEAD4–APLN induction may enable rapid vascular compensation under treatment pressure, contributing to both intrinsic and acquired resistance. Consistent with these biological features, pharmacogenomic analyses revealed that Hippo–YAP–high tumors exhibited selective vulnerability to microtubule-targeting agents such as paclitaxel, irinotecan, and ispinesib, suggesting that endothelial reprogramming may expose therapeutic windows despite broader resistance patterns.

  To translate these insights, we developed a machine-learning prognostic model integrating upstream Hippo regulators, TEAD transcriptional targets, and endothelial functional markers. Importantly, the model was derived from transcriptional programs specific to MVI-associated endothelial states rather than from general angiogenesis-related genes, thereby capturing endothelial reprogramming linked to vascular invasion. The model consistently outperformed conventional clinical variables and previously reported signatures across multiple cohorts, demonstrating that endothelial-derived transcriptional programs provide robust biomarkers for recurrence risk, vascular invasiveness, and therapeutic susceptibility. Beyond prognosis, incorporating endothelial signaling states into predictive frameworks offers a path toward stratifying patients by drug response and guiding combination strategies aimed at overcoming endothelial-driven resistance. Together, these findings position the TEAD4-centered endothelial program as both a mechanistic driver of MVI and a clinically actionable axis that links endothelial reprogramming, vascular invasion, and treatment response in HCC.

  Despite these strengths, several limitations remain. Prospective multicenter cohorts are needed to validate the robustness of the machine-learning model and its applicability across clinical settings. In addition, the interactions between Hippo–YAP–TEAD4 signaling, endothelial plasticity, and immune modulation remain incompletely understood and warrant further study. In summary, our work identifies a TEAD4-centered endothelial program that drives vascular remodeling and MVI in HCC, linking endothelial reprogramming to both vascular invasion and treatment response. These insights support the development of therapies targeting endothelial-specific Hippo–YAP–TEAD4 activity, potentially combined with agents that overcome vascular-mediated resistance, and provide a foundation for precision strategies in MVI-related risk stratification and treatment.


## Conclusion
  This study systematically delineates the driving role of TEAD4 in HCC-associated MVI, emphasizing the central role of endothelial cell-specific YAP activation in tumor vascular remodeling. Targeting TEAD4 represents a promising anti-angiogenic strategy. Our findings extend the current understanding of the HCC vascular ecosystem and provide a theoretical basis and potential tool for precise identification and intervention in high-risk MVI patients. Future work should focus on preclinical development of TEAD4-targeted therapies and explore their combination with immunotherapy or existing anti-angiogenic agents to achieve breakthroughs in HCC treatment.
