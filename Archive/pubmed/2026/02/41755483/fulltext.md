# Genomic and structural insights into TATA-Binding protein from cestodes


## ABSTRACT
  The TATA-binding protein (TBP) is an essential component of the eukaryotic transcriptional machinery, yet little is known about the structure, evolution, and functional divergence of TBP paralogs in parasitic flatworms. Here, we performed a comprehensive comparative analysis of TBP1 and TBP2 across cestode species of medical and veterinary relevance. Using genomic annotation, multiple sequence alignment, homology modeling, phylogenetics, electrostatic potential mapping, and protein – DNA docking complemented with molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, we reveal that both paralogs maintain a highly conserved genomic organization and the canonical TBP α/β saddle architecture. TBP1 exhibits moderate variability in sequence and electrostatic in peripheral regions, whereas TBP2 is more conserved, suggesting distinct evolutionary constraints. Despite paralog divergence, all cestode TBPs retain the key aromatic and basic residues required for minor-groove recognition of the TATA box and interaction with general transcription factors. Docking and MD simulations confirm a conserved pattern of TATA-binding across species, with TBP2 displaying exceptionally uniform interaction networks. Together, these findings provide genomic and structural data for TBPs in cestodes, suggesting the existence of paralog-specific regulatory roles and offering new insights into transcriptional control in parasitic flatworms.


## Introduction
  Transcription initiation in eukaryotes is a highly coordinated process that requires the ordered assembly of the pre-initiation complex (PIC) on promoter DNA [1,2]. In RNA polymerase II (Pol II) transcription, a central component of this machinery is the TATA-binding protein (TBP), a highly conserved factor that functions primarily as a subunit of the general transcription factor TFIID [2]. In metazoans, TFIID is composed of TBP and at least thirteen TBP-associated factors (TAFs) and serves as the main promoter recognition complex for Pol II, acting at both TATA-containing and non-canonical promoters [3,4]. As part of TFIID, TBP binds the minor groove of DNA, induces a pronounced bend in the promoter, and nucleates the recruitment of additional general transcription factors, including TFIIA, TFIIB, TFIIE, TFIIF, and TFIIH, ultimately enabling Pol II loading and transcription initiation [5].

  Although TBP was originally characterized through its affinity for the TATA box, the usage and distribution of TATA elements vary substantially among eukaryotes and promoter classes. In yeast, both SAGA- and TFIID-dominated promoters contain functional TATA sequences, albeit with distinct architectures and regulatory properties, demonstrating that the absence of a canonical TATA box is not a universal feature of eukaryotic promoters [2]. In metazoans, many promoters lack a clearly defined consensus TATA sequence and instead rely on alternative core promoter elements such as the Initiator (Inr), downstream promoter element (DPE), or motif ten element (MTE) [6], as well as protein – protein interactions within TFIID and with transcriptional co-activators, to position TBP accurately at the transcription start site [7,8]. These mechanisms highlight the evolutionary conservation of TBP function together with substantial flexibility in promoter recognition strategies [9].

  Beyond its role in Pol II transcription, TBP is also an essential component of the transcriptional machinery of RNA polymerases I and III. In Pol I transcription, TBP forms part of the SL1 (also known as TIF-IB) complex together with Pol I-specific TAFs and is required for transcription of ribosomal RNA genes [10,11]. In Pol III transcription, TBP is a core subunit of the TFIIIB complex, where it associates with Brf1 or Brf2 and the B subunit to direct transcription of tRNA genes, 5S rRNA, and other small RNAs [12,13]. Thus, TBP represents a central and multifunctional hub coordinating transcription by all three nuclear RNA polymerases [14].

  In metazoans, the TBP family has diversified through gene duplication events, giving rise to a set of TBP-related factors (TRFs) or TBP-like proteins (TBPLs) that share homology within the conserved C-terminal DNA-binding core domain (CTD) [15]. In addition to the canonical TBP, which is present in all eukaryotes, three related factors have been described: TRF1, which is specific to Drosophila [16]; TBPL1 (also known as TRF2), which is conserved across metazoans [17]; and TBPL2 (also referred to as TBP2 or TRF3), which is restricted to vertebrates [18]. While these paralogs retain the overall structural framework of TBP, they exhibit marked divergence in their N-terminal domains (NTD), interacting partners, and regulatory functions, reflecting functional specialization rather than redundancy [19].

  Functional studies have shown that TBP paralogs participate in distinct transcriptional programs linked to development and cell-type specificity. TBPL1 has been implicated in alternative Pol II transcription systems and can form complexes that are distinct from canonical TFIID [20], whereas TBPL2 plays a critical role during vertebrate oogenesis and early embryogenesis, where it can partially replace TBP in Pol II transcription [21]. These observations support the view that expansion of the TBP family provided metazoans with an evolutionarily flexible toolkit to fine-tune transcriptional regulation across developmental stages and tissues [22].

  The NTD is one of the most variable regions of the transcriptional machinery across eukaryotes. Unlike the highly conserved CTD responsible for TATA-box binding and recruitment of general transcription factors, the NTD region differs markedly in length, sequence composition, and predicted structural disorder among species [23,24]. This variability has been linked to differences in regulatory complexity, developmental strategies, and genome organization across lineages. Insertions, deletions, and low-complexity segments accumulate rapidly in this region, suggesting that it evolves under relaxed selective pressure compared with the DNA-binding domain. Previous studies have demonstrated that deletion of the NTD of mouse TBP results in only subtle phenotypic changes [25,26]. As a result, the NTD may contribute to species-specific modulation of TBP function while preserving the essential architecture required for transcription initiation [26,27].

  In non-model eukaryotes such as cestodes, however, the composition and functional organization of TBP-dependent transcription complexes remain largely unexplored. It is currently unknown whether cestodes possess a canonical TFIID complex composed of TBP and Pol II-specific TAFs, or whether they encode functional counterparts of the SL1 and TFIIIB complexes required for Pol I and Pol III transcription, respectively. Moreover, the extent to which cestode TBPs participate in transcription by multiple RNA polymerases has not been established. In addition, the presence and evolutionary conservation of more divergent TBP family members, such as TBPL1/TRF2 or other TBP-related factors, have not been systematically assessed in cestode genomes.

  Earlier work identified two TBP paralogs in Taenia solium (TsTBP1 and TsTBP2) [28–30], but their evolutionary conservation, genomic architecture, and structural properties across cestode lineages have not been systematically analyzed. This represents a critical knowledge gap, because TBP and its paralogs occupy pivotal positions at the top of the transcriptional hierarchy, understanding their conservation and divergence could illuminate how cestodes regulate developmental switches, host adaptation, and stage-specific gene expression. In metazoans, differences between TBPs have been associated with promoter selectivity, chromatin context, and interaction with specific transcription factors. Addressing these questions is essential for understanding the evolution of transcriptional regulation in parasitic flatworms and for placing cestode transcriptional mechanisms within the broader context of eukaryotic diversity.

  In this study, we combine genomic analysis, evolutionary comparison, structural modeling, electrostatic profiling, and protein – DNA interaction predictions to generate an integrative characterization of TBP paralogs in cestodes. By defining both shared and lineage-specific features of TBP1 and TBP2, our work establishes a comprehensive basis for interpreting their roles in promoter recognition and provides foundational insights for future functional and regulatory studies in parasitic flatworms.


## Materials and methods

### Data sources and retrieval of TBP homologs
  Protein and gene sequences for TBP homologs were retrieved from WormBase ParaSite (https://parasite.wormbase.org/) and GenBank (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genbank/). Previously characterized T. solium TBP1 and TBP2 [28,29] sequences were used as BLASTP and TBLASTN queries to identify putative orthologs in other cestode genomes. For each species, the highest-scoring hits with complete open reading frames (ORFs) were selected. All candidate sequences were manually curated to correct annotation errors, including incomplete exon boundaries, mispredicted start or stop codons, or incorrect intron/exon assignments.


### Multiple sequence alignment and secondary structure prediction and phylogenetic analysis
  Full-length TBP1 and TBP2 amino acid sequences were aligned using ClustalW (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/jdispatcher/msa/clustalo). Alignments were manually inspected and adjusted. Conserved residues involved in DNA binding and transcription factor interactions were annotated based on previous structural studies. Secondary structure prediction for all TBP sequences was performed using JPred4 (https://www.compbio.dundee.ac.uk/jpred/), which integrates neural network – based algorithms to identify α-helices, β-strands, and coil regions. Predicted secondary structural elements were mapped onto the ClustalW alignment for comparison across species. Phylogenetic reconstruction of TBP1 and TBP2 orthologs was performed using MEGA X. The best-fit substitution model was selected using the model testing module in MEGA, and maximum-likelihood trees were generated. The analysis also includes human TBP (HsTBP, Uniprot ID: P20226), mouse TBP (MmTBP, Uniprot ID: P29037), yeast TBP (ScTBP Uniprot ID: P13393), human TBPL1 (HsTBPL1, Uniprot ID: P62380), mouse TBPL1 (MmTBPL1 Uniprot ID: P62340), human TBPL2 (HsTBPL2 Uniprot ID: Q6SJ96), mouse TBPL2 (MmTBPL2, Uniprot ID: Q6SJ95) and Drosophila TRF (DmTRF, Uniprot ID: Q27896) in order to identify the TBP family member most closely related to cestode homologs.


### Homology modeling of TBP1 and TBP2 structures
  Three-dimensional structural models of HsTBP, MmTBP, HsTBPL2, MmTBPL2, and cestodes TBP1 and TBP2 were generated using AlphaFold2 (https://alphafoldserver.com) to ensure high-confidence predictions. Templates included high-resolution TBP CTD structures from human, yeast, and Drosophila (PDB IDs 1CDW, 1YTB, 1NVP). Top models were selected based on DOPE score and Ramachandran plot statistics (PROCHECK). Protein superpositions were performed using PyMOL v2.6. Root-mean-square deviations (RMSDs) were calculated for all pairwise model comparisons. ChimeraX was used for visualization.


### Electrostatic surface potential calculations
  Electrostatic potentials were calculated using the APBS plugin in PyMOL. Partial charges were assigned using the PDB2PQR server; electrostatic maps were computed at ± 5 kT/e, and surfaces were visualized using a blue-white-red gradient (positive to negative). Electrostatic patterns were compared across species and between TBPs, focusing on the concave DNA-binding surface and convex transcription factor-binding surfaces. ChimeraX was used for visualization.


### Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations
  Protein – DNA docking for HsTBP, MmTBP, ScTBP, HsTBPL2, MmTBPL2, and cestodes TBP1 and TBP2 was performed using the HDOCK web server. Homology-modeled TBP structures were docked against the TATA-box of AdML promoter sequences (TATAAAAG), and the top-ranked poses with the lowest docking energies were selected for simulation. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were carried out in YASARA Structure, using an AMBER14 force field and periodic boundary condition. Protein – DNA interaction residues were visualized using DNAproDB (https://dnaprodb.usc.edu/index.html).


## Results

### Identification of TBPs in cestode genomes
  We conducted a bioinformatic search across multiple databases to identify TBPs in diverse cestode species. We identified seven TsTBP1-like sequences, such as in Taenia saginata (Tsatbp1; PRJNA71493, TSAs00013g02840), T. asiatica (Tatbp1; PRJEB532, TASK_0000175301), Echinococcus granulosus (Egtbp1; PRJEB121, EgrG_000972300), E. multilocularis (Emtbp1; PRJEB122, EmuJ_000972300), Hydatigera taeniformis (Httbp1; PRJEB534, TTAC_0000978001), Hymenolepis diminuta (Hdtbp1; PRJEB30942, WMSIL1_LOCUS14480) and H. nana (Hntbp1; PRJEB508, HNAJ_0000219201). We also identified six sequences homologous to TsTBP2, such as in T. saginata (Tsatbp2; PRJNA71493, TSAs00133g09167), T. asiatica (Tatbp2; PRJEB532, TASK_0000929501), T. multiceps (Tmtbp2; PRJNA30764, Tm4G010429), E. granulosus (Egtbp2; PRJEB121, EgrG_000419500), E. multilocularis (Emtbp2; PRJEB122, EmuJ_000419500) and H. diminuta (Hdtbp2; PRJEB507, HDID_0000507901). Several retrieved sequences contained annotation errors; therefore, we manually inspected and corrected these annotations.   Across TBP1 genes, coding regions ranged from 717 bp to 822 bp and typically contained four introns positioned within conserved regions of the core TBP fold. Introns varied substantially in length, from as short as 44–76 bp in Hymenolepis species to over 130 bp in Echinococcus spp. Despite this variation, exon counts were highly conserved, with five exons in most species and only three exons in H. diminuta and H. nana. The encoded proteins ranged from 238 to 243 amino acids, with predicted molecular weights near 26.5–27.4 kDa and isoelectric points around pI 10.0–10.6. All sequences contained the hallmark N-terminal and C-terminal TBP signature motifs, and the exon – intron boundaries consistently mapped to equivalent structural landmarks (QLQ52–53, GFD133–134, FTT/T T168–170, VLT/LTG199–201).

  Noteworthy, TBP2 genes exhibited a similarly conserved organization but were generally shorter, with coding sequences of 648 bp. All TBP2 orthologs encoded proteins of 215 amino acids, with predicted molecular weights of ~24.1–24.2 kDa and isoelectric points between pI 9.7 and 10.0. Gene structures were composed of four introns, with the longest introns located at the MSQ158–159 and IIT189–190 junctions. Exon composition was highly uniform, with five exons of fixed length across all Taenia and Echinococcus species analyzed. The main differences among TBP2 genes were restricted to minor variations in intron length within the third and fourth introns.

  The exon – intron structure of both TBP1 and TBP2 genes is remarkably conserved across cestodes, with variation primarily arising from differences in intron length in Echinococcus and Hymenolepis species. The high conservation of exon boundaries aligns with the strong structural constraints of the TBP DNA-binding domain, while the divergence in intron size likely reflects species-specific genome architecture.

  Key residues previously implicated in interaction with some TFs were found to be conserved across all sequences, such as TFIIA binding, amino acids involved in DNA minor-groove recognition, residues associated with TFIIB interaction and some other TBP-interacting TFs (Figure 1(A), highlighted in blue, red, green and black, respectively). A vertical reference line was included to delineate the CTD, which is the most conserved region of TBP and encompasses the majority of functional interaction sites. As expected, this region displayed near-complete sequence identity within each TBP family and only minor divergence between TBP1 and TBP2 proteins. In contrast, the NTD segments, preceding β1, exhibited higher variability in length and composition, including species-specific insertions in Hymenolepis and Echinococcus.

  Pairwise identity analyses further supported the distinct but tightly constrained evolutionary trajectories of cestode TBP1 and TBP2 proteins (Figure 1(B)). Within each group, sequences displayed very high conservation, with intra-family pairwise identities ranging from 78–92% for TBP1 and 74–89% for TBP2, consistent with their preserved structural cores and shared functional roles. In contrast, comparisons between TBP1 and TBP2 sequences revealed substantially lower identities (38–47%), reflecting the well-known divergence of these paralogs following ancient gene duplication. Notably, most of this inter-family divergence localized to the NTD region and to surface-exposed residues outside the DNA- and TFIIA-binding interfaces, whereas the CTD, encompassing the key architectural and functional determinants, retained markedly higher identity (up to 60–65%). These patterns highlight the functional specialization of the TBP1 and TBP2 families while underscoring the strong selective pressure maintaining the integrity of the core TBP fold. The alignment reveals that cestode TBPs retain the canonical structural scaffold and preserve all residues essential for TFIIA and DNA binding, as well as most determinants for interaction with general transcription factors. The strong conservation across diverse species further supports the functional equivalence of these proteins and highlights the selective pressure acting on the CTD.


### Phylogenetic relationships and evolutionary divergence of cestode TBP1 and TBP2 sequences
  Phylogenetic reconstruction based on the full-length TBP amino acid sequences robustly resolved the TBP1 and TBP2 groups into two well-supported clades, consistent with their distinct evolutionary histories (Figure 2). All cestodes TBP2 sequences clustered together, forming a monophyletic group clearly separated from the cestodes TBP1 lineage by a long internal branch (branch length ≈0.38), reflecting their deep divergence following the ancestral duplication event. Within the TBP2 clade, H. diminuta TBP2 branched basally, whereas the two Echinococcus sequences (EgTBP2 and EmTBP2) formed an extremely tight cluster (branch length < 0.003), indicating near-identical sequences. The Taenia species formed a second well-supported subcluster, where TaTBP2, TsaTBP2, TmTBP2, and TsoTBP2 grouped together with minimal divergence (branch lengths <0.012), suggesting strong sequence conservation across this genus. Figure 2.Phylogenetic analysis of TBPs in cestodes. Phylogenetic tree inferred from the amino acid sequences of cestode TBP1 and TBP2 proteins. The analysis also includes HsTBP, MmTBP, ScTBP, HsTBPL1, MmTBPL1, HsTBPL2, MmTBPL2 and DmTRF for comparative purposes.

  The TBP1 clade exhibited a similar topology, with short internal branches indicating the high conservation previously observed in the identity analyses. Echinococcus TBP1 sequences (EmTBP1 and EgTBP1) again grouped tightly (branch length ≈0.0007–0.0035). Within Taeniidae, TaTBP1 and TsaTBP1 were identical at the sequence level (branch length = 0.0000), and together formed a cluster with TsoTBP1 and TtTBP1, reflecting their close evolutionary relationship. H. nana and H. diminuta TBP1 sequences branched basally within the TBP1 lineage, consistent with their taxonomic positions and the slightly higher divergence observed relative to Taenia and Echinococcus.

  The dendrogram revealed clear separation of TBP1 and TBP2 families, extremely tight clustering within genera, and the expected taxonomic structure of Taeniidae and Hymenolepididae, supporting the accuracy of the annotations and the conserved evolutionary trajectories of TBPs across cestodes.

  On the other hand, analysis shows that cestode TBP1 proteins cluster within the canonical TBP clade, including ScTBP, MmTBP and HsTBP, indicating that cestode TBP1 is most closely related to the ubiquitously expressed canonical TBP rather than to TBP-related factors. In contrast, cestode TBP2 form a distinct monophyletic group that is clearly separated from the cestode TBP1 clade and instead cluster with TBPL2, rather than with TBPL1 or DmTRF1. Thus, while cestode TBP1 shows the closest phylogenetic relationship to canonical TBP, cestode TBP2 is most closely related to TBPL2, supporting the presence of two evolutionarily distinct TBP lineages in cestodes.


### Structural conservation and functional surface mapping of the C-terminal domain of cestode TBPs
  To investigate the extent of structural conservation within cestode TBPs, we generated a consensus three-dimensional model of the canonical CTD using all high-confidence TBP1 and TBP2 sequences identified across the taxonomic range analyzed (Figure 3). The resulting structural ensemble revealed a highly conserved TBP core fold, consisting of the characteristic saddle-shaped architecture with two pseudo-symmetrical repeats forming the concave DNA-binding surface and the convex protein-interaction interface. No major conformational deviations were observed between TBP1- and TBP2-derived models, consistent with the strong criteria selection inferred from sequence conservation analyses. Figure 3.Consensus structural model of the C-terminal domain of cestode TBPs highlighting key residues involved in transcription factor and DNA recognition. Consensus three-dimensional structure of the conserved CTD of cestode TBPs, shown in two opposite orientations (180° rotation). The TBP Fold is rendered in gray, and key residues known to mediate essential molecular contacts are highlighted according to their functional regions: residues involved in TFIIA interaction (green), TFIIB interaction (blue), DNA minor-groove binding (red), and positions implicated in contacts with additional transcription factors (black).

  Mapping of functionally critical positions onto the consensus structure recapitulated the spatial organization of canonical TBP interaction surfaces. Aromatic residues involved in minor-groove DNA recognition formed a contiguous patch along the inner concave face of the protein, preserving the classical arrangement required for TATA-box engagement. Residues that mediate interaction with TFIIA localized to the lateral region of the saddle, whereas positively charged residues associated with TFIIB binding occupied the opposite flank, creating a well-defined electrostatic surface. A third cluster of conserved basic residues positioned on the convex face corresponded to the general transcription factor (GTF) contact region, previously implicated in interaction with additional components of the PIC. The non-overlapping distribution of these patches highlights the functional compartmentalization of the CTD, which appears to be strictly preserved across all cestode species examined.


### Electrostatic isosurface properties of cestode TBPs
  To investigate whether subtle physicochemical variations accompany the otherwise highly conserved CTD TBP fold, we computed the electrostatic potential distribution for each TBP1 and projected it onto the molecular surface. All proteins exhibited the canonical negatively charged concave saddle associated with minor-groove recognition of the TATA element, together with a predominantly positive distribution on the convex face (Figure 4). This global arrangement resembled that of TBPs from other eukaryotes, confirming the conservation of key electrostatic determinants required for DNA binding. Ribbon representations (left panels) and electrostatic potential isosurface (right panels) are shown for TBP from human (HsTBP), TBP from mouse (MmTBP), TBP1 from E. granulosus (EgTBP1), E. multilocularis (EmTBP1), H. diminuta (HdTBP1), H. nana (HnTBP1), T. crassiceps (TcTBP1), T. saginata (TsaTBP1), and T. taeniaeformis (TtTBP1). All structures display the characteristic saddle-shaped DNA-binding architecture typical of TBPs; negatively and positively charged regions are indicated in red and blue, respectively. A structural superposition highlights the high degree of conservation in the C-terminal core among species.

  Despite this overall similarity, species-specific differences were observed in localized charge patches at both ends of the saddle. In H. diminuta and H. nana, the DNA-facing groove exhibited a particularly strong negative potential, suggestive of an enhanced electrostatic complementarity with the phosphate backbone. Conversely, Taenia TBP1 proteins displayed a more neutral or slightly less polarized electrostatic profile in these regions, consistent with moderate sequence divergence in residues contributing to surface potential. At the periphery of the convex face, where interactions with GTFs occur, variation was also detected, though all proteins retained at least one positively charged tract compatible with TFIIB docking.

  Structural superposition of all TBP1 models revealed a near-perfect alignment of the β-sheet core and α-helices, with only minor deviations in loop regions (Figure 4, bottom-right panel). These topological differences correspond to the variable electrostatic patches described above, suggesting that species-specific substitutions modulate local charge without altering the conserved TBP architecture. Collectively, these data indicate that the DNA-binding potential of cestode TBP1 proteins is preserved, while peripheral electrostatic features might fine-tune interactions with transcriptional partners in a species-dependent manner.

  Homology models of TBP2 from seven cestode species revealed a highly conserved overall fold consistent with the canonical TBP α/β saddle architecture (Figure 5). All TBP2 proteins displayed the characteristic pair of pseudo-symmetrical lobes formed by β-sheets flanked by α-helices, generating the concave DNA-binding surface expected for TATA element recognition. Despite minor variations in loop orientation, the superposition of all models showed an RMSD within a narrow range, indicative of strong structural conservation across cestode TBP2 orthologs.   Electrostatic surface analyses further demonstrated that all TBP2 homologs share a predominantly basic concave surface, compatible with their expected DNA-binding function. Although subtle differences in charge distribution were observed, particularly along the outer edges of the β-sheet platform and the N-terminal helices, no drastic shifts in electrostatic potential were identified. These variations are consistent with previously reported lineage-specific adaptations in TBP family proteins and may contribute to fine tuning of promoter selectivity among cestodes.

  Pairwise sequence identity among the TBP2 proteins ranged from 74–89%, confirming that TBP2 is more conserved than TBP1 within the cestode lineage. The highest identities were observed between closely related Taenia species, whereas the lowest values were found in comparisons involving H. diminuta, the most distantly related species in the dataset. Despite this moderate sequence divergence, all residues known to participate in TATA-box binding in other metazoans were retained or conservatively substituted, supporting the strong evolutionary constraint imposed on TBP2 structural integrity.


### Minor-groove recognition and conserved contact networks in DNA binding
  To investigate the DNA-binding properties of cestode TBP1 proteins, we modeled their interaction with the canonical TATA element from the AdML promoter (TATAAAAG). The interaction revealed a highly conserved binding architecture across all TBP1 orthologs analyzed (Figure 6). Consistent with the structural constraints of the TBP fold, all proteins established their major stabilizing contacts within the minor groove, primarily through residues located in the S1–H2 and S4–H5 regions, which correspond to the well-established DNA-binding surfaces in eukaryotic TBPs.   Despite the strong conservation of the global interaction pattern, species-specific differences were observed at the 3' end of the TATA sequence, where variations in loop and β-strand residues gave rise to subtle differences in base and sugar contacts. Notably, residues equivalent to L189, V155 and N53 in the Echinococcus TBP1 orthologs showed slight positional shifts in Taenia and Hymenolepis species, leading to minor differences in phosphate backbone interactions. Nevertheless, these variations did not alter the overall geometry of the protein – DNA interface.

  Similar to TBP1, all TBP2 orthologs bound the TATA sequence through a conserved set of residues positioned within the characteristic S1–H2 and S4–H5 motifs, forming a stable network of minor-groove contacts (Figure 7). These interactions included multiple hydrophobic insertions and hydrogen bonds that contribute to TATA bending, consistent with the canonical TBP – DNA binding mechanism described in other eukaryotes.   Unlike TBP1, TBP2 proteins displayed even greater uniformity in the number, position and identity of contacting residues. Most contacts were concentrated along residues equivalent to F164, I187, V135 and T98 in Echinococcus TBP2 proteins, with nearly identical patterns observed across Taenia, Hymenolepis and Hydatigena sequences. Species-specific variability was minimal and restricted to residues contacting the central adenines and thymidines of the TATA-box, where slight differences in loop orientation resulted in small shifts in phosphate and sugar interactions.

  Despite these minor variations, the overall protein – DNA interface remained highly conserved among TBP2 orthologs. All structures preserved the hallmark minor-groove insertion geometry, and no TBP2 model exhibited atypical or noncanonical base contacts. These results support the conclusion that TBP2 paralogs across cestodes maintain a functionally constrained TATA-binding interface, potentially reflecting a more specialized or conserved role in transcription initiation relative to TBP1.


## Discussion
  The present study provides a comprehensive comparative analysis of TBP1 and TBP2 orthologs across of cestode species, integrating genomic organization, protein structural modeling, evolutionary relationships, electrostatic analyses, and protein – DNA interaction predictions. Our findings consolidate the view that both paralogs are highly conserved at the structural and functional levels, yet exhibit meaningful divergence patterns that likely reflect distinct regulatory roles within cestode transcriptional machinery. This duality mirrors the well-established functional partitioning of TBP paralogs in other metazoan lineages [27].

  Phylogenetic analyses revealed that TBP1 and TBP2 form two deeply divergent monophyletic clades in cestodes, consistent with a duplication event early in platyhelminth evolution. Cestode TBP1 proteins cluster robustly within the canonical TBP clade together with fungal and vertebrate TBPs, indicating that they represent true orthologs of the ubiquitously expressed eukaryotic TBP. In contrast, cestode TBP2 proteins are clearly separated from the cestodes TBP1 clade and instead group with TBPL2, defining a distinct TBP2-like lineage in cestodes. The strong clustering within genera and the extremely short branch lengths observed for Echinococcus and Taenia species underscore the high degree of purifying selection acting on TBP sequences, particularly within the conserved C-terminal DNA-binding domain. Similar patterns of divergence and conservation have been reported for TBP paralogs in vertebrates, insects, and nematodes, where TBPL2 factors have undergone substantial evolutionary differentiation while retaining the core structural features required for DNA binding [18,26,31].

  The deep divergence between TBP1 and TBP2 in cestodes (38–47% identity), combined with the high conservation within each paralogous group, suggests that each paralog plays a nonredundant role. This pattern mirrors observations in vertebrates, in which TBP2 is essential during oocyte development, whereas TBP1 functions more broadly in somatic transcription [21,32,33]. Although equivalent developmental specialization has not yet been explored in cestodes, the strong structural conservation but clear sequence divergence raises the possibility of lineage-specific functional partitioning.

  The overall exon – intron structures of TBP1 and TBP2 genes were remarkably conserved, with fixed intron positions mapping precisely onto conserved structural landmarks of the TBP fold. Such conservation aligns with previous findings highlighting the extreme functional constraint on TBP architecture required for TATA-box recognition [34]. Variation in intron length, especially in Hymenolepis and Echinococcus species, likely reflects broader genome architectural differences among cestodes rather than functional differences, since exon boundaries and encoded domains remained unchanged.

  Structural modeling confirmed that all cestode orthologs retain the canonical saddle-shaped α/β fold characteristic of the TBP CTD [35,36]. Superposition analyses demonstrated near-identity within each paralogous group, with deviations limited primarily to flexible loops. This mirrors structural data from yeast, human, and Drosophila TBPs showing that even minor alterations in loop geometry can modulate promoter occupancy or interaction with general transcription factors (GTFs) [15,37].

  Electrostatic isosurface analyses revealed a shared signature across all TBP1 and TBP2 orthologs. A strongly negative concave surface conducive to minor-groove insertion, and a predominantly positive convex surface favorable for interaction with TFIIA, TFIIB, and additional TFs. These features are consistent with experimentally resolved electrostatic landscapes of TBP in yeast and humans, which have been shown to be essential for promoter selectivity and transcription initiation kinetics [38–40].

  Subtle species-specific differences were observed, particularly in Hymenolepis and Taenia species, where edge regions of the saddle showed charge variation. Although these shifts did not disrupt the global TBP electrostatic pattern, they may modulate the affinity for specific promoter subsets or influence cooperative binding with GTFs [41]. Comparable fine-tuning of electrostatic potential has been reported as a mechanism for species-specific promoter usage in eukaryotic parasites such as Plasmodium falciparum [42,43].

  Despite sequence divergence among cestode species, TBP1 and TBP2 exhibited a highly conserved pattern of minor-groove contacts with the AdML TATA-box. The key aromatic and hydrophobic residues characteristic of canonical TBP – TATA interaction (e.g., phenylalanine’s mediating DNA bending) were fully preserved, mirroring biochemical data from yeast and metazoan TBPs. Interestingly, TBP2 showed slightly less variability than TBP1 across species, suggesting a more constrained functional role.

  The consistent pattern of interactions across species implies that TATA-dependent transcription initiation is likely conserved in cestodes [29]. Minor shifts in loop contacts at the 3' end of the TATA-box, particularly in Taenia and Hymenolepis TBP1 orthologs, may reflect adaptations to species-specific promoter architectures. Such differential accommodation of DNA contacts is a recognized mechanism for tuning transcriptional output and developmental timing in other organisms [27].

  The integrative analyses presented here demonstrate that cestode TBP1 and TBP2 paralogs retain the hallmark features of eukaryotic TBPs while exhibiting paralog-specific and lineage-specific variations. These findings suggest the existence of transcriptional specialization analogous to that observed in other metazoans, where TBP paralogs support distinct transcriptional programs [19]. In cestodes, such specialization may relate to developmental transitions between hosts, regulation of strobilation, or adaptation to parasitic lifestyles.

  Future work should aim to clarify the functional differences between TBP1 and TBP2 in cestodes, including promoter occupancy studies, expression profiling across developmental stages, Localization in tissue, and biochemical assays to determine affinities for GTFs and promoter elements. These investigations will be essential for understanding the broader transcriptional landscape underpinning cestode development and parasitism.
