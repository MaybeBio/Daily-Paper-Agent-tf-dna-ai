# One-Pot Gateway to
Quinazoline–Thiohydantoin
Fused Scaffolds and Discovery of Their Antileukemic Activity


## Abstract
  In drug discovery, fusing bioactive heterocyclic frameworks leads to new analogs with improved properties. Quinazoline and thiohydantoin are prominent heterocycles, recognized for their diverse pharmacological profiles. Although quinazolines fused with various heterocycles are present in numerous clinical drugs, quinazoline–thiohydantoin fused frameworks, along with their synthetic approaches, remain unreported. Here, we introduce a transition metal-free, one-pot approach that enables the efficient and high-yielding synthesis (up to 92% yield) of this previously unexplored class of heterocyclic compounds. A streamlined three-step domino reaction, enabling the formation of three C–N bonds in a single operation, followed by dehydrogenation step, results in the desired quinazoline–thiohydantoin fused scaffolds, which exhibit potent and selective cytotoxicity against multidrug-resistant P-glycoprotein-overexpressing CEM/ADR5000 leukemia cells. The compounds, achieving IC50 values as low as 0.13 ± 0.02 μM, are over 290-fold more active in vitro than the clinical standard doxorubicin (IC50 = 38.17 ± 13.32 μM). Screening of 474 kinases, in silico docking (binding energy: −7.38 ± 0.02 kcal/mol; pKi: 3.9 ± 0.12 μM), and dose-kinetic assays identified MAPK14 (p38α) as a primary target (IC50 = 131 nM), consistent with its role in oncogenic stress signaling. Additionally, these compounds reduce nuclear localization of myocardin-related transcription factor A (MRTF-A), a key driver of tumor resistance. This new study lays the groundwork for developing quinazoline–thiohydantoin-based drugs that can overcome multidrug-resistance mechanisms.


## Introduction
  Heterocycles are among the most prevalent structural motifs in approved pharmaceuticals, serving as key components in a wide range of therapeutics. −   Quinazolines, in particular, represent a privileged class of nitrogen-containing heterocycles, −     known for their incorporation into antiviral, antimalarial, anticancer, and anti-inflammatory agents. Examples of such medically valuable quinazolines include Gefitinib and Febrifugine (Figure  a). Their synthetic accessibility and biological versatility have made them central to drug discovery. Recent efforts have focused on diversifying quinazoline frameworks through ring fusion, a strategy that enhances molecular complexity and pharmacological profiles. Notable examples of such fused systems include the clinical agents Anagrelide, Evodiamine, and Quazinone (Figure  b).

  In parallel, the thiohydantoin scaffold has gained recognition for its medicinal value, appearing in compounds with antituberculosis, hypolipidemic, ,  antiviral, antimutagenic, and anticancer −      activities. Thiohydantoin-based molecules have also been identified as kinase inhibitors and modulators of key signaling pathways, underscoring their utility in targeting cancer diseases. ,  Medically valuable thiohydantoins include Enzalutamide and Apalutamide (Figure  a). ,  Despite the individual importance of quinazolines and thiohydantoins, a fused architecture combining both remains unknown (Figure  c). The absence of such structures reflects a lack of synthetic strategies capable of efficiently accessing these fused heterocycles. Here, we report the first synthesis of quinazoline–thiohydantoin fused frameworks via a transition metal-free one-pot sequence (Figure  d). This streamlined protocol features the formation of three C–N bonds in a single operation via a domino process, followed by oxidative dehydrogenation, yielding the target compounds with high efficiency and excellent atom economy. Beyond synthetic accessibility, these novel fused heterocycles exhibit potent activity against sensitive wild-type CCRF-CEM and multidrug-resistant P-glycoprotein-overexpressing CEM/ADR5000 cell lines, outperforming the clinically approved treatment. In silico analyses, kinome profiling, and dose-kinetic studies identified selective inhibition of MAPK14 (p38α) as a key mechanism of action (IC50 = 131 nM). These results position the quinazoline–thiohydantoin scaffold as a promising platform for the development of new anticancer agents targeting resistant malignancies.


## Results and Discussion

### Chemistry: Reaction Development
  To develop a combined process of the three-step domino reaction and a dehydrogenation step, we investigated the conditions for these two reactions in a one-pot process (Table  ). Since 1a can be obtained easily and fast starting from commercially available substrates on a large scale, it was the optimal substrate to optimize reaction conditions stepwise, beginning with the three-step domino reaction, carrying out the subsequent dehydrogenation step without variation of reaction conditions (entries 1 and 2).

  The three-step domino reaction in dichloromethane (DCM) as solvent was catalyzed with trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) (10 mol %). The subsequent dehydrogenation step in entries 1 and 2 (Table  ) was carried out at room temperature (r.t.) for 1 h in the presence of DDQ (2,3-dichloro-5,6-dicyano-1,4-benzoquinone) as an oxidizing agent.

  The initial three-step domino reaction is around 10-times faster by elevating the temperature from r.t. to 40 °C (entry 1 vs. entry 2, Table  ). In addition, the yield of product 3a increased from 54% to 92%. With the optimized conditions for the three-step domino reaction, we further focused on optimizing the dehydrogenation step. The use of MnO2 as an oxidizing reagent in CHCl3 at 40 °C for 3 h (entry 3) led to the decomposition of substrate 1a. As a green and renewable reagent, oxygen from ambient air mediated dehydrogenation at r.t. with a good yield of 74% (entry 4). The yield was further increased to 87% using pressurized oxygen from a balloon (entry 5).

  The accompanying drawbacklong reaction times (72–100 h)could be overcome by a combination of urea hydrogen peroxide (UHP) and iodine in EtOAc/EtOH solvent mixture (reaction time of 5 h, entry 6). Nonetheless, the best result concerning reaction time (1 h) and observed product yield (92%) was obtained by using DDQ (entry 2). Therefore, DDQ was chosen as the oxidizing reagent for the developed one-pot process.

  The formation of a fused heterocycle 3a likely follows the reaction sequence shown in Table  . It includes the formation of three C–N bonds in a single operation via a three-step domino process and a subsequent dehydrogenation reaction performed in a one-pot manner. In the first step of the three-step domino reaction, the amine group of aniline 1a reacts with the aldehyde function of ethyl glyoxylate 2 to form an imine. In the second step, the amine of the thiourea moiety undergoes an intramolecular attack on imine via an aza-Mannich-type reaction to form a tetrahydroquinazoline heterocycle. To generate the thiohydantoin moiety, the secondary amine and the ester function undergo an intramolecular amide formation, and the released domino product can be used further without its isolation. The desired quinazoline–thiohydantoin framework 3a is obtained via a subsequent dehydrogenation reaction. Using the optimized conditions (entry 2, Table  ), we next demonstrated the generality of the four-step one-pot process by employing a variety of aniline-thioureas with achiral subunits R1 to form products 3a–3s (Figure  ).

  Substrates containing electron-withdrawing and electron-donating groups on the aromatic rings (R1) adjacent to the thiourea moiety were compatible. We obtained yields ranging from 49% to 92% for all substrates with achiral aromatic/heteroaromatic subunits (products 3a–3l, Figure  ). Substrates, containing different neutral and functionalized aliphatic moieties (R1) were also tolerated in this reaction, resulting in products 3m–3s with good yields (53%–88%) over four steps. We next focused on the synthesis of quinazoline–thiohydantoin fused heterocycles with chiral R1-subunits (Figure  ). First, easily accessible (R)-1-phenylethyl-based aniline-thioureas were used as substrates to gain chiral products 5a–5m (Figure  ). The process exhibits good tolerance to various substituents (R) on the aniline moiety, including electron-withdrawing (F, Cl, Br, NO2) and electron-donating (OMe, Me) groups, and different substitution patterns. Reaction products 5a–5c, 5e–5m were isolated in fair to good yields (32%–63%). Only the reaction toward 5d showed merely low conversion. A possible explanation for the low yield (<10%) might be the presence of Cl-substituent in the ortho position of aniline subunit, which can interfere with the imine bond formation step in the domino reaction (Table  ).

  Subsequently, different chiral quinazoline–thiohydantoin fused heterocycles 5n–5w with an unsubstituted quinazoline backbone were obtained with good yields over four steps (50%–77%). To obtain the corresponding enantiomeric pairs 5n/5o and 5 q /5r for their subsequent biological investigations, varied absolute configurations in R1 substituents were implemented. Since even the substitution pattern on the chiral R1 moiety is expected to potentially influence the bioactivity of products, we also prepared compound 5s (77% yield) for a planned structure–activity relationship (SAR) study and direct comparison with a regioisomer 5r (Figure  ).

  The developed one-pot synthetic method is general and allows even the easy formation of a hybrid of quinazoline–thiohydantoin and artemisinin (structure 5w). Artemisinin is a natural antimalarial compound extracted from the Chinese medicinal plant Artemisia annua L. There are also known artemisinin-derived agents with anticancer properties, such as artesunic acid, which has been shown to induce cell death in several types of cancer cells, including pancreatic cancer, T leukemia cells, breast cancer cells, and myeloid leukemia cells through the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and modulation of multiple signaling pathways. −    The efficacy of hybrid drugs and their potential to overcome even drug resistance has been widely proven. −    Encouraged by our previous results and experiences with artemisinin-based hybrids and their high antileukemic activities, −     we selected an artemisinin derivative as a hybridization partner. By linking the artemisinin moiety to the quinazoline–thiohydantoin scaffold, we aimed to combine the distinct pharmacophores to achieve potential synergistic effectsenhancing cytotoxicity toward cancer cells while retaining selectivity. Indeed, artemisinin was tolerated in this one-pot reaction, and the corresponding product 5w was formed in 69% yield, demonstrating that this method is well-suited for the facile synthesis of bioactive hybrid compounds.


### Antileukemic Activity
  The worldwide numbers regarding leukemia published by the American Cancer Society for 2018 are sobering: 437,033 new cases and 309,006 deaths caused by leukemia worldwide. Chemotherapy is still the most promising option for cancer treatment. However, 90% of failures in chemotherapy result from metastasis of cancers related to drug resistance. −      Multidrug resistance (MDR), which is the ability of drug-resistant tumors to exhibit simultaneous resistance to several structurally and functionally unrelated chemotherapeutic agents, is considered a crucial obstacle for effective clinical cancer chemotherapy. A substantial challenge worldwide is emergent drug resistance in leukemia cells against approved drugs, such as doxorubicin. To address this fundamental issue, research on new antileukemia agents is urgently needed.

  Quinazoline and (thio)­hydantoin-based compounds showed relatively high activity against cancer, including leukemia, in the past. ,  We, therefore, decided to evaluate the activities of our novel quinazoline–thiohydantoin fused heterocycles against leukemia cells, anticipating promising results. In order to investigate the antileukemia activity of quinazoline–thiohydantoin fused heterocycles, we employed the synthesized heterocycles against sensitive wild-type CCRF-CEM and multidrug-resistant P-glycoprotein-overexpressing CEM/ADR5000 cells (Table  ). Interestingly, while investigated achiral compounds 3a, 3j–3l and 3n–3p (series 3, Figure  ) showed no significant activity against the CCRF-CEM and CEM/ADR5000 cell lines, all chiral heterocycles containing branched arylethyl moieties attached to the nitrogen atom of the thiohydantoin unit, 5a–5c, 5f, 5g, 5j, 5k, 5n–5s (series 5, Figure  ) showed high antileukemia effect (Table  ). Therefore, we focused on the more promising compound series 5 for further investigations. As a main result, compounds in series 5 exerted a stronger activity against multidrug-resistant P-glycoprotein-overexpressing CEM/ADR5000 cells (the range of IC50 values was between 0.13 ± 0.02 μM and 1.33 ± 0.25 μM) than the reference drug doxorubicin (IC50 = 38.17 ± 13.32 μM). To further investigate the activity spectrum of this new class of fused heterocycles, we have tested selected compounds on four additional leukemia cell lines (RPMI-8226, K562, HL-60, and MOLT-4) at one dose (10 μM). The IC50 values obtained (Figure S1, Supporting Information) revealed that the novel heterocyclic compounds exhibit lower activity against these cell lines, suggesting a preferential targeting of multidrug-resistant, P-glycoprotein-overexpressing CEM/ADR5000 leukemia cells.

  To estimate the selectivity of our quinazoline–thiohydantoin fused heterocycles, we have also investigated them against healthy human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). Remarkably, all tested compounds were significantly less active against healthy human PBMCs, than against sensitive and multidrug-resistant leukemia cells. The range of IC50 values in PBMCs was between 1.50 ± 0.02 μM and >100 μM. Novel heterocycles 5a–5c, 5f, 5g, 5j, 5n–5s and 5w showed the highest activity against multidrug-resistant P-glycoprotein-overexpressing CEM/ADR5000 cells, with IC50 values between 0.13 ± 0.02 μM and 0.53 ± 0.09 μM, up to 300-fold lower than doxorubicin (IC50 = 38.17 ± 13.32 μM). This outstanding result suggests the great potential of these unprecedented quinazoline–thiohydantoin fused heterocycles as lead structures of prospective antileukemia agents to bypass multidrug resistance in leukemia cells. Since even minor modification of the drug structure can impact the observed activity, we next compared the activities of naphthalene-derived compounds 5q, 5r and 5s. Whereas regioisomers 5r (IC50 = 0.32 ± 0.09 μM) and 5s (IC50 = 0.24 ± 0.02 μM) showed similar antileukemia behavior, the activity of 5q (IC50 = 0.53 ± 0.03 μM) was about half of its enantiomer 5r. Interestingly, however, the activities of enantiomeric compounds 5n (IC50 = 0.49 ± 0.02 μM) and 5o (IC50 = 0.46 ± 0.01 μM) were quite similar, indicating that the absolute configuration at the chiral center and the chirality itself might not play a crucial role in the antileukemic activity of these compounds. In silico target prediction and molecular docking studies (see the chapter below) confirmed that the presence of a branched arylethyl group attached to the nitrogen atom of the thiohydantoin unit and not the chirality of this group is important for the high antileukemic activity of fused heterocycles. Indeed, chiral enantiopure compounds 5t, 5u, and 5v lacking a branched arylethyl group were much less or not active against the investigated leukemia cell lines (Table  ).

  The hybrid compound 5w showed high activity against both leukemia cell lines (IC50 (CCRF-CEM) = 0.17 ± 0.02 μM and IC50 (CEM/ADR5000) = 0.18 ± 0.03 μM) and no cytotoxic effect on healthy cells (IC50 (PBMCs) > 10 μM). By comparing these bioactivities with the low activity of parent artemisinin (IC50 (CCRF-CEM) = 36.90 ± 6.90 μM and IC50 (CEM/ADR5000) = 26.90 ± 4.40 μM) and quinazoline–thiohydantoin fused heterocycle 3o (IC50 (CCRF-CEM) = 33.80 ± 10.75 μM and IC50 (CEM/ADR5000) = 63.90 ± 30.30 μM), the power of the hybridization concept is revealed.

  Among the most active compounds against the multidrug-resistant P-glycoprotein-overexpressing CEM/ADR500 cells were 5a, 5c, 5j, and 5w, all exhibiting similar IC50 values of 0.13–0.18 μM. Compound 5a (IC50 = 0.15 μM) was selected as a model drug for detailed mechanistic studies primarily because it features a canonical quinazoline–thiohydantoin scaffold with well-defined structural properties and because it was available in larger quantities than the comparably potent 5c and 5j, which facilitated its immediate use in extensive biological and mechanistic assays (see Kinome Profiling and Target Validation section).

  Notably, although the hybrid compound 5w, which contains an artemisinin subunit linked to the quinazoline–thiohydantoin scaffold, showed excellent potency and selectivity, it was not pursued further for mechanistic studies in this work, since the superior bioactivity and selectivity profile observed for 5w may arise from the complementary mechanisms of the two pharmacophores. Hybrid compound 5w includes an artemisinin subunit, suggesting a different mechanism of action that may involve additional targets or ROS-mediated pathways and represents an interesting avenue for future investigation.

  Thus, we focused on compound 5a to establish a clear link between the canonical quinazoline–thiohydantoin scaffold and its bioactivity. To elucidate the biological mode of action of 5a and understand how its cellular effects translate into overcoming drug resistance, we next investigated whether its activity involves modulation of key oncogenic signaling pathways. Serum Response Factor (SRF) and its transcriptional coactivators MRTF-A and -B have been shown to trigger tumor growth. ,  Nuclear localization of MRTF-A and -B prevails in hepatocellular and mammary carcinoma cells as well as in leukemia cells lacking the tumor suppressor deleted in liver cancer 1 (DLC1). ,  Recent evidence implicated active nuclear MRTF-A as a dominant tumor resistance driver and a biomarker to predict tumor responsiveness to MRTF inhibitors. We, therefore, tested whether selected quinazoline–thiohydantoin fused heterocycle 5a can inhibit MRTF-A nuclear localization to bypass resistance (Figure  ).

  We first analyzed the localization of MRTF-A in a human leukemia cell line HAP1. Treatment with 5a efficiently increased cytoplasmic MRTF-A localization (Figure  ). Since it was recently demonstrated that inhibition of MRTF-A nuclear localization has antitumor effects by inducing oncogene-induced senescence, we also tested whether 5a can activate cellular senescence. Indeed, 5a provoked a senescence response mediated by the oncogene Ras and downstream MAPK/pRb pathways (Figure S2, Supporting Information). These data further support that 5a and derivatives are promising antileukemia agents for combatting multidrug-resistant leukemia.


### In Silico Target Prediction and Molecular Docking
  To predict potential target candidates for the selected compound 5a, we used two web servers PharmMapper and SwissTargetPredictionknown reliable tools for potential drug target identification using pharmacophore mapping approach. Interestingly, several kinase targets were identified in both PharmMapper  and SwissTargetPrediction  analyses (see Supporting Information). Among these, p38α MAPK (mitogen-activated protein kinase) emerged as a main candidate for further docking studies. Notably, this protein was predicted as a top target by SwissTargetPrediction tools and kinase profiling data (see below) but was not identified in the PharmMapper results. Molecular docking revealed that compound 5a exhibited promising binding at the ATP-binding site of p38α MAP kinase (Figure  ), with a binding energy of −7.38 ± 0.02 kcal/mol and a predicted inhibition constant (pKi) of 3.9 ± 0.12 μM. These findings suggest that the p38α MAPK, might be a primary target of compound 5a, potentially explaining its high anticancer activity.

  Among the most active antileukemic compounds identified in this study are structures containing branched arylethyl moieties attached to the nitrogen atom of the thiohydantoin unit, such as compounds 5a–5s (Figure   and Table  ). The presence of an aryl group is essential for achieving high activity against multidrug-resistant P-glycoprotein-overexpressing CEM/ADR5000 cells. This is evident when comparing the activities of the N-isopropyl-substituted compound 3n (IC50 = 31.4 ± 7.7 μM) with the phenylethyl-substituted chiral derivative 5n (IC50 = 0.49 ± 0.02 μM).

  Notably, replacing the phenylethyl group with a methylethyl (isopropyl) moiety (5n vs 3n) results in a significant reduction in activity. Incorporating linear alkyl chains further diminishes the activity (e.g., 3o, IC50 = 63.9 ± 30.3 μM; 3p, inactive). Therefore, the R1 moiety should ideally be branched and include an aryl group. A comparison of IC50 values between sets of chiral compounds 5a–5s (containing chiral arylethyl moieties) and 5t–5v (containing chiral aliphatic moieties) indicates that the presence of a chiral center in the N-substituent is not crucial for activity (Figure   and Table  ). Instead, the key determinant is the presence of an aryl group, as confirmed also by docking experiments. Docking studies revealed that the phenyl substituent of compound 5a forms a stabilizing π–π interaction with an amino acid PHE169 at the ATP-binding site of p38α MAP kinase (Figure  ). Notably, similar activities were observed for the enantiomeric pairs 5n/5o and 5q/5r (Figure   and Table  ), confirming that chirality does not significantly influence the observed activity.

  Unlike 5a, hybrid 5w, containing an artemisinin subunit, may not rely on π–π interactions within the ATP-binding site of p38α MAP kinase, and its bioactivity could involve additional/alternative targets and/or reactive oxygen species–mediated pathways associated with the artemisinin moiety. Detailed target identification for hybrid 5w, which is likely to confer a distinct mechanism of action compared with 5a, will be conducted in a follow-up study and reported elsewhere.


### Kinome Profiling and Target Validation
  In silico target prediction suggested that compound 5a could be a potential multikinase inhibitor. To validate this observation, we have performed kinome inhibition screening for 474 different kinases in response to compound 5a (1.5 μM). The obtained results demonstrated that 24 out of the 474 kinases were inhibited by 5a (Figure  ). From these, we selected the top five kinases (those with ≥85% inhibition) for subsequent dose–response validation. Dose–kinetic studies were performed for MAPK14 (p38α), JAK3, FER, NEK1, and GRK4, and IC50 values were calculated. The IC50 values ranged from 100 to 500 nM, with MAPK14 emerging as the top target for 5a, showing an IC50 of 131 nM (Figure  ). MAPK14, also known as p38α, is a member of the p38 MAPK family and plays a pivotal role in initiating different disease states such as inflammatory disorders, and cancer. Studies indicate that MAPK14 plays a crucial role in orchestrating the DNA damage response and maintaining chromosomal stability during cancer progression. Moreover, reduced levels of MAPK14 have been linked to increased DNA damage and chromosomal instability in cancer cells, ultimately leading to cancer cell apoptosis and tumor regression.

  Specifically, p38α MAP kinase is known to regulate actin dynamics and SRF/MRTF signaling through downstream phosphorylation events. ,  Inhibition of p38α can diminish actin polymerization and thereby reduce the nuclear accumulation of MRTF-A, ultimately suppressing transcription of MRTF-dependent genes linked to multidrug resistance. In addition to impairing RhoA/actin signaling, modulation of MRTF-A phosphorylation may further contribute to impairment of MRTF-A nuclear localization, because previous studies have shown that phosphorylation by ERK2 serves as a downregulatory mechanism that promotes nuclear export of MRTF-A. The observation that compound 5a strongly suppresses MRTF-A nuclear localization (Figure  ) is therefore consistent with its identified p38α inhibitory activity.


## Conclusions
  We developed a facile transition metal-free one-pot process, which joins a three-step domino reaction, enabling the formation of three C–N bonds in a single operation, followed by a dehydrogenation step toward the first quinazoline–thiohydantoin fused frameworks. In this operationally simple and expeditious four-step sequence with only a single workup procedure, yields of up to 92% were obtained. We evaluated the antileukemia activity and substantiated the results with investigations on the changed MRTF-A nuclear localization affected by quinazoline–thiohydantoin fused heterocycles. In particular, the new fused heterocycles exerted pronounced activities against multidrug-resistant leukemia cells (IC50 (CEM/ADR5000) down to 0.13 ± 0.02 μM) and high selectivity (IC50 (PBMCs) up to >100 μM), thus presenting a very promising drug developmental basis compared to the approved drug doxorubicin (IC50 (CEM/ADR5000) 38.17 ± 13.32 μM) and (IC50 (PBMCs) 1.8 μM). Furthermore, the presence of a selected quinazoline–thiohydantoin fused heterocycle 5a led to a reduced localization of MRTF-A in the nucleus and resulted in oncogene-induced senescence. Since the correlation between MRTF-A nuclear localization and drug resistance in leukemia cells is well established, we identified the new quinazoline–thiohydantoins as promising agents to overcome the tremendous resistance problem in leukemia treatment.

  In silico analyses identified several kinase targets for compound 5a, with p38α MAPK emerging as the primary candidate, supported by docking studies showing strong binding at its ATP-binding site (binding energy: −7.38 ± 0.02 kcal/mol; pKi: 3.9 ± 0.12 μM). Structures containing branched arylethyl moieties at the nitrogen atom of the thiohydantoin unit, such as compound 5a and derivatives, demonstrate the highest antileukemic activity against multidrug-resistant CEM/ADR5000 cells, with the aryl group being essential for activity through stabilizing π–π interactions at the ATP-binding site of p38α MAP kinase.

  In closing, the straightforward one-pot synthetic approach and target validation studies enable the easy and direct formation and further optimization of model drugs to overcome multidrug resistance in leukemia cells.
