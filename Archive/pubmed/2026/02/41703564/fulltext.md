# Growth differentiation factor 15 promotes the malignant progression of multiple myeloma via activation of PI3K/Akt/NF-κB signaling pathway


## Objective
  Extramedullary disease (EMD) in multiple myeloma (MM) is associated with poor prognosis and presents considerable treatment challenges. However, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood. This study aimed to identify prognostic biomarkers through bioinformatics analysis and investigate the mechanisms governing proliferation and metastasis in MM.


## Methods
  Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between MM and control samples were identified from datasets GSE146649 and GSE24870 through differential expression analysis. Prognostic biomarkers associated with MM were subsequently screened using weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), support vector machine recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE), and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). The biological effects and underlying mechanisms of growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15) in MM were further evaluated using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), CCK-8 assay, flow cytometry, cell adhesion assay, transwell migration assay, Western blotting, and co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP).


## Results
  Four prognostic biomarkers—BIRC3, PRDX1, BCAP31, and GDF15 were identified as being involved in MM proliferation and metatasis. GDF15 was selected as the key gene for further investigation. Serum GDF15 levels were significantly elevated in MM patients with EMD compared to those without EMD and healthy controls. Inhibition of GFRAL, the specific receptor for GDF15, reversed the activation of the PI3K/Akt/NF-κB signaling pathway and attenuated upregulation of CXC chemokine receptors 4 (CXCR4) and matrix metalloproteinases 9 (MMP9). Treatment with uprosertib, a Akt inhibitor, abrogated the GDF15-induced increases in CXCR4 and MMP9 expression.


## Conclusion
  Upregulation of GDF15 is positively correlated with MM progression. Moreover, GDF15 enhances the migratory and invasive capacities of MM cells via the PI3K/Akt/NF-κB signaling pathway, mediated by CXCR4 and MMP9. Thus, GDF15 may represent a potential therapeutic target for EMD in multiple myeloma.

## Introduction
  Multiple myeloma (MM) is a plasma cell neoplasm characterized by malignant proliferation of monoclonal plasma cells and excessive secretion of monoclonal immunoglobulin [1]. According to data from the Global Cancer Observatory (gco.iarc.fr), 30,300 new MM cases and 18,662 MM-related deaths were reported in China in 2022. MM ranks as the second most prevalent hematologic malignancy, accounting for 13% of all hematological cancers and 1% of all cancer cases globally [2]. MM cells primarily reside within the bone marrow microenvironment; however, they can disseminate from primary sites via hematogenous spread to form extramedullary plasmacytomas in distant tissues and organs, a condition referred to as extramedullary disease (EMD). The incidence of EMD ranges from approximately 7% at initial diagnosis to 6%–20% in patients with relapsed or refractory MM [3]. The presence of EMD is associated with an adverse prognosis. While novel therapeutic agents—such as proteasome inhibitors, immunomodulatory drugs, and anti-CD38 monoclonal antibodies—have demonstrated efficacy in MM patients without extramedullary involvement, their effectiveness is markedly reduced in those with EMD [4]. Therefore, identifying reliable biomarkers and elucidating the underlying molecular mechanisms of EMD pathogenesis are crucial for developing more effective treatment strategies [5].

  This study utilized bioinformatics approaches to identify potential molecular biomarkers for early diagnosis and prognosis prediction of MM. Two gene expression datasets (GSE146649 and GSE24870) were obtained from public repositories and subjected to differential expression analysis. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), support vector machine-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE), and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis were applied to integrated to candidate MM-associated biomarkers. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted to explore the biological pathways involved. Additionally, immune infiltration analysis was performed to investigate the potential roles of these biomarkers within the tumor immune microenvironment, providing a theoretical basis for therapies aimed at modulating their expression. Four candidate biomarkers were identified: BIRC3, PRDX1, BCAP31, and GDF15. Based on comprehensive literature review, growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15) has been implicated in the progression of various solid tumors [6, 7]. Consequently, GDF15 and its associated PI3K-Akt signaling pathway were selected as the focus for further investigations.

  GDF15, a distant member of the transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) superfamily, is regulated by p53 and classified as a secretory cytokine [8, 9]. Its expression is typically low in normal tissues but markably elevated under conditions such as hypoxia, inflammation, and acute injury [9]. GDF15 has been closely associated with the malignant behavior of multiple myeloma (MM) cells [10]. Specifically, GDF15 can directly promote MM cells proliferation and the self-renewal of tumor stem cells [11, 12]. Aberrant GDF15 expression has been implicated in epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) [13] and cancer metastasis [14]. The interaction between GDF15 and its receptor, glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor family receptor alpha-like (GFRAL), not only alters the cell structure, cell adhesion, actin cytoskeleton organization, and focal adhesion kinase (FAK) phosphorylation [15], but also modulates the expression of CXCR4 and MMP9, thereby promoting tumor proliferation and metastasis in some kinds of cancer [6]. Mechanistic studies suggest that the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway may regulate the expression of MMP9 and CXCR4 [16–18]. However, the expression level of GDF15 in extramedullary disease (EMD) and its role in MM progression remain unclear. We hypothesize that upregulated GDF15 expression in MM and EMD may activate the Akt signaling pathway through binding to GFRAL on the surface of MM cells, subsequently regulating the expression of MMPs, CXCR4, and E-cadherin, thus promoting MM progression (Fig. 1). ## Materials and methods

### Sources of data
  The datasets GSE146649 and GSE24870 were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). GSE146649 includes gene expression profiles of mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) isolated from bone marrow (BM) at successive stages of MM (n = 31) and from healthy donors (HDs) (n = 10), generated using the Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 Array (HG-U133_Plus_2). GSE24870, based on the GPL571 [HG-U133A_2] Affymetrix Human Genome U133A 2.0 Array, was used for model development and validation, and included gene expression data from CD34 + cell subsets of 7 patients with multiple myeloma and 5 healthy volunteers.


### Differential expression analysis
  Following acquisition of the GSE146649 dataset, log2(x + 1) transformation was applied for normalization to ensure accuracy and reliability in downstream analyses. Subsequently, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using stringent criteria: adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| > 1, to accurately detect genes significantly altered between disease and control groups [19]. ### WGCNA
  Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed on the entire training set using MM status as the trait of interest. The analysis was conducted using the R package “WGCNA” [20] on the GSE146649 dataset. Samples were initially clustered to identify and remove outliers, and sample dendrograms were constructed for visualization. Genes with average expression levels below 1 were filtered out. Non-compliant genes and samples were excluded using the goodSamplesGenes function. Hierarchical clustering (hclust) was then performed on disease samples, and outliers were removed based on cluster analysis to ensure analytical accuracy.

  A soft-thresholding power was selected to achieve a scale-free topology with an R² value approaching 0.80. A co-expression similarity matrix was constructed using all detected genes from GSE146649, followed by module detection through hierarchical clustering based on topological overlap dissimilarity.

  Using the dynamic hybrid tree-cutting algorithm, modules containing at least 200 genes were identified (MEDissThres = 0.25 for merging similar modules, p < 0.05) and subjected to correlation analysis with sample groups (MM vs. control). The key module most strongly associated with MM was selected for further analysis. The brief statistical checklist for WGCNA is shown in Table 1.


### Acquisition and analysis of candidate genes
  To identify candidate genes, the overlapping genes between differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in multiple myeloma (MM) and key module genes identified by weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) were determined using the R package “ggvenn.” The intersecting genes were visualized via a Venn diagram. Chromosomal localization and correlation analyses of the candidate genes were performed, and Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were conducted with a significance threshold of *p* < 0.05 to identify functionally relevant pathways.


### PPI network establishment and modular analysis
  Protein–protein interaction (PPI) information for DEG-encoded proteins was retrieved from the STRING database (available online: http://string-db.org). Topological analysis of the PPI network was performed using five centrality measures: maximal clique centrality (MCC), Degree, density of maximum neighborhood component (DMNC), edge percolated component (EPC), and Closeness. Modules were screened based on these scores and node counts to identify the most significant module. The intersection of the top 120 genes ranked by each of the five algorithms was calculated and visualized using the R package “VennDiagram”.


### Screening for critical genes via machine learning methods
  To further identify potential diagnostic biomarkers for MM, machine learning algorithms were applied. Based on the candidate gene list, support vector machine-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) were employed to construct predictive models for the prioritization of critical genes. Using the R package “caret,” SVM-RFE analysis was performed with feature selection guided by cross-validation (“cv” algorithm).

  XGBoost is an efficient gradient boosting framework that enhances model performance through sequential construction of decision trees and iterative optimization. It incorporates regularization to reduce overfitting and generates feature importance scores to highlight genes most associated with classification outcomes. Owing to its robustness in handling high-dimensional genomic data, XGBoost enables effective feature selection, thereby improving classification accuracy and facilitating the identification of critical genes.

  The intersection of genes selected by SVM-RFE and those identified by XGBoost was determined and visualized using the R package “VennDiagram.”


### Analysis of critical genes expression profiles and assessment of biomarkers’diagnostic value
  To examine the expression patterns of critical genes in MM patients compared to controls, the GSE146649 dataset was utilized. Differential expression analysis between MM and normal control samples in GSE146649 was conducted using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with statistical significance set at *p* < 0.05. Results were presented as boxplots generated with the “ggplot2” package.

  Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed using the pROC package to assess the ability of critical genes to discriminate MM from control samples in GSE146649 [21]. ROC curves are plotted across varying dichotomous thresholds, with sensitivity (true-positive rate) on the y-axis and 1-specificity (false-positive rate) on the x-axis. The area under the curve (AUC) reflects diagnostic accuracy: an AUC approaching 1 indicates excellent discriminatory power; an AUC between 0.5 and 0.7 suggests limited discrimination, 0.7–0.9 indicates acceptable to good performance, and > 0.9 indicates high accuracy. An AUC of 0.5 implies no discriminative ability. Given the purpose of the dataset in predicting disease risk, an AUC threshold of 0.7 was adopted; critical genes with AUC values exceeding 0.7 in GSE146649 were considered potential diagnostic biomarkers.


### Assessment of the diagnostic model
  SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) method was employed to provide both local and global interpretability by quantifying the contribution of each feature to the prediction outcomes, thereby enhancing model transparency and interpretability. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was conducted to assess the net benefit (NB) of the models across various threshold probabilities using the R package “ggDCA”. Subsequently, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed for the key genes utilizing the R package “pROC”.

  To evaluate the predictive performance of key genes regarding the occurrence of MM, a nomogram model was developed based on the expression levels of these genes, with disease status (present/absent) as the outcome variable. The model was constructed using logistic regression analysis with the R package “rms” in the development cohort (GSE146649) and then validated in an independent validation cohort (GSE24870).

  The Hosmer–Lemeshow test and assessment of predictive performance were carried out for both the development set (GSE146649) and validation set (GSE24870) using the R packages “ResourceSelection” and “pROC”. A p-value greater than 0.05 indicates that the model passes the Hosmer–Lemeshow (HL) test, suggesting no significant discrepancy between predicted and observed probabilities, thus reflecting adequate calibration. Conversely, a p-value less than 0.05 indicates failure to pass the HL test, implying a lack of fit between predicted and observed values.


### Immune infiltrative analysis
  To investigate the role of biomarkers in the immune microenvironment of MM, immune infiltration analysis was performed using the GSE146649 dataset. The infiltration levels of immune cell types were assessed via the ssGSEA (single-sample gene set enrichment analysis) algorithm [22], and differences in immune cell abundances between the disease and control groups were evaluated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Spearman correlation analysis was subsequently applied to explore the associations between key genes and immune cell populations in the development cohort (p < 0.05, |r| > 0.3). The brief statistical checklist for ssGSEA is shown in Table 1.


### Chromosomal and subcellular localization of critical genes
  Chromosomal localization of genes is crucial for understanding gene structure, function, and genomic interactions. To determine the genomic positions of critical genes, gene location data were obtained from the ENSEMBL database (gencode.v19.annotation.gtf.gz) and visualized using the “Circos” package in R to generate a chromosomal distribution map.

  FASTA sequences of the critical genes were retrieved from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). These sequences were then submitted to the mRNALocate database (previously referred to as mRNALocater) to predict subcellular localization probabilities for each key gene. Finally, a stacked bar plot was generated using the “ggplot2” package for visualization.


### GSEA and GSVA analysis
  To identify potential regulatory pathways and biological functions associated with the expression of target genes, GSEA enrichment analysis was performed. In all samples from the GSE146649 dataset, Spearman correlation coefficients between each critical gene and all other expressed genes were calculated (p < 0.05 considered statistically significant). All genes were ranked in descending order according to their correlation coefficients to create a ranked gene list for each key gene. GSEA was then conducted using the R package “clusterProfiler”, with background gene sets sourced from the MSigDB database (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb), including KEGG pathways (c2.cp.kegg.v2023.1.Hs.symbols.gmt) and GO biological processes (c5.go.bp.v2023.2.Hs.symbols.gmt). An adjusted p-value (p.adj) < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for pathway enrichment. The top five enriched pathways, selected based on |NES| > 1, are presented.

  To investigate pathway alterations between multiple myeloma (MM) and control groups in the MM dataset, the background gene set “h.all.v2023.1.Hs.symbols.gmt”, comprising 50 Hallmark pathways, was downloaded from the MSigDB database (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb). Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) was performed on all genes from both MM and normal control samples in the GSE146649 dataset using the R package “GSVA”. Differential analysis of GSVA scores was subsequently conducted between the two groups (MM vs. Control), with a significance threshold set at *p* < 0.05. The brief statistical checklist for GSEA and GSVA analysis is shown in Table 1.


### Construction of regulatory networks
  To identify transcription factors (TFs) potentially regulating the critical genes, the ChEA3 database (https://maayanlab.cloud/chea3) was used to predict TFs targeting these genes [23].

  To understand the genes associated with biomarkers and the functional pathways in which they are involved, GeneMANIA analysis was conducted [24]. Subsequently, the “multiMiR” package was employed to predict miRNAs targeting the critical genes based on the Microcosm database (http://mirtoolsgallery.tech/mirtoolsgallery/), and the results were visualized using Cytoscape. Additionally, the StarBase database (https://rnasysu.com/encori/) was utilized to predict lncRNAs associated with the identified miRNAs [25]. A Sankey diagram was generated using the “ggalluvial” package.


### Prediction and molecular Docking analysis of potential therapeutic agents
  Gene-based drug prediction enables the identification of drugs associated with gene expression profiles, thereby facilitating the discovery of novel therapeutics. By leveraging drug-gene interaction databases, promising compounds for MM treatment were explored. To identify potential MM-targeting compounds, the DGIdb database (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/databasecommons/database/id/) [26] was used to predict drugs targeting the critical genes, followed by construction of a drug–gene interaction network using Cytoscape.

  To assess the binding affinity between key target proteins and candidate compounds, molecular docking was performed using the CB-Dock2 platform (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/php/index.php) [27]. Compounds with high predicted docking scores and available three-dimensional (3D) structures were selected for analysis. Drug structures were retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) [28] and energy-minimized using Chem3D [29]. For the critical target genes, nucleotide sequences were submitted to the AlphaFold server (https://alphafoldserver.com/) to predict corresponding protein 3D structures, followed by identification of potential ligand-binding pockets. Molecular docking was carried out via CB-Dock2, with a docking score ≤ -5 kcal/mol considered indicative of strong binding affinity.


### CTD analysis
  The Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD, https://ctdbase.org/) is a publicly available resource that curates comprehensive gene–chemical–disease interaction data, aiming to elucidate molecular mechanisms underlying the relationships between environmental exposures and human diseases [30]. In this study, critical genes were systematically queried in the CTD database to retrieve the top 10 most significantly associated diseases for each gene. The results were visualized using radar charts generated by the R package “fmsb” to provide an intuitive representation of the disease association patterns of the critical genes.


### Correlation analysis among critical genes
  To evaluate correlations among critical genes, Spearman correlation analysis was performed using the “psych” package to assess pairwise relationships between the critical genes across dataset samples. A *p*-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Results were visualized using ggplot2.


### Cell lines and cell culture
  The MM cell lines ARP-1 and U266 were obtained from the Cancer Research Institute of Central South University (Changsha, Hunan province, China). Cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (10-040-CV, Corning, Shanghai, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (35-082-CV, Corning, New Zealand) under a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37 °C.


### Analysis of cell viability and apoptosis
  MM cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well and treated with GDF15 (GD5-H5149, ACRO Biosystems, Beijing, China) for 24, 48, or 72 h. At designated time points, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (MA0218, Meilunbio, Dalian, Liaoning, China) was added to each well, and cells were incubated under a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37 °C for 2 h. Cell was assessed by measuring absorbance at 450 nm using an ELX-800 microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA).

  For apoptosis detection, MM cells were treated with 1 mL of 200-ng/mL GDF15 for 72 h, followed by incubated with 1 µL of 1-µg/mL bortezomib (BTZ) (QILU PHARMACEUTICAL, Shandong, China) for 12 h. Cells were then washed twice with pre-cooled phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (BKM-PBS-500, Biochem, Shengzhen, China) via centrifugation at 2000 g and 4 °C for 5 minutes. The cell pellet was resuspended to a final concentration of 5 × 105cells/mL. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, 5 µL of annexin V-FITC and 5 µL of propidium iodide (PI) were added to 100 µL of cell suspension from the Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (MK1028, Boster Bio, Wuhan, Hubei, China), mixed gently, and incubated in the dark for 10 min. Apoptotic cells were analyzed using a fluorescence microscope and a flow cytometer (CytoFLEX S, Beckman Coulter, California, USA). The percentage of apoptotic cells was calculated as the sum of the populations of cells in the upper right (UR) and lower right (LR) quadrants.


### Patient sample and protein detection in human serum
  Blood samples were collected from multiple myeloma (MM) patients with extramedullary disease (EMD) and without EMD (non-EMD) at the First Affiliated Hospital of Jishou University, in accordance with the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Clinical Practice Guidelines in Oncology for Multiple Myeloma (Version 1.2024). Based on imaging findings (MRI, CT, and PET/CT) and clinical diagnosis, patients were classified into two groups: MM without EMD (non-EMD, n = 40) and MM with EMD (EMD, n = 40). Patients in the EMD group exhibited plasma cell tumors outside the bone marrow. Individuals with mild thrombocytopenia, normal bone marrow morphology, and no history of allergic or autoimmune diseases, recent infection, or glucocorticoid use were included as controls. Blood samples were collected in EDTA-coated tubes before and/or after surgery. After centrifugation at 2500 × g for 5 min, supernatants were collected and stored as serum at − 20 °C until analysis. Serum concentrations of GDF15 were determined using a human GDF15 ELISA kit (CHE0112, 4 A Biotech, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.


### Cell transfection with GFRAL-targeted SiRNAs
  GFRAL-specific small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) were designed and synthesized by Ribobio (Guangzhou, Guangdong, China). For transfection, 65 nM GFRAL-targeted siRNA and 6.5 µL of Lipo6000 transfection reagent (C0526, Beyotime, Foshan, Guangdong, China) were diluted in 260 µL of cold, serum-free RPMI 1640 medium and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. When MM cells cultured in 6-well plates reached approximately 50% confluence, 260 µL of the transfection mixture was combined with 1740 µL of cold, serum-free RPMI 1640 medium and added to each well. After 6 h of incubation, the medium was replaced with fresh culture medium containing 10% FBS, and cells were maintained under standard conditions (humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂ at 37 °C). At 36 and 48 h post-transfection, mRNA and protein expression levels of GFRAL were evaluated by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) and Western blotting, respectively. A non-silencing siRNA (Ribobio) was used as a negative control to validate transfection efficiency. The sequences of the GFRAL-targeted siRNAs are as follows:

### RNA isolation and real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction
  Total RNA was isolated using the BeyoMag™ Animal RNA Isolation Kit with Magnetic Beads (R0077, Beyotime, Foshan, Guangdong, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA concentration was determined spectrophotometrically using a NanoDrop 2000 instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). Genomic DNA contamination was eliminated and first-strand cDNA was synthesized using NovoScript® Plus All-in-one 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (E047, Novoprotein, Suzhou, Jiangsu, China) under the following reaction conditions: 50°C for 15 min and 85°C for 5 s. Real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using the NovoStart® SYBR qPCR SuperMix Plus kit (E096, Novoprotein, Suzhou, Jiangsu, China) on an Eco™ Real-Time PCR System (Illumina, California, USA) with the following thermal cycling parameters: initial denaturation at 95°C for 1 min followed by 35 cycles of 95°C for 20 s and 65°C for 1 min. The relative mRNA expression levels of target genes were normalized to GAPDH (Ribobio, Guangzhou, Guangdong, China) and calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method (Methods 25: 402–408, 2001). The primer sequences used were as follows: GFRAL-forward, 5’-AGCAGCCATACGGTTCTTCTATC-3’; GFRAL-reverse, 5’-CATGTCTTGCTGTGAAGAGCTTC-3’ (designed and synthesized by Ribobio, Guangzhou, Guangdong, China).


### Assessment of adherence to fibronectin
  Adhesion of multiple myeloma (MM) cells to fibronectin (FN) was evaluated after 48 h of treatment with GDF15. Briefly, 96-well plates were coated with 50 µg/mL purified human FN (F8180, Solarbio, Beijing, China) and incubated at 4 °C for 18 h. Non-specific binding sites were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (Sigma Chemicals) for 2 h at room temperature. ARP-1 cells were seeded at a density of 8 × 10³ cells/well, while U266 cells were seeded at 1.5 × 10⁴ cells/well due to their lower baseline adhesion capacity. Cells were allowed to adhere for 30, 60, or 120 min at 37 °C. After washing with PBS, adherent cells were fixed and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 10 min, followed by PBS washes. Cell adhesion was assessed microscopically (DMi3000 B; Leica Microsystems GmbH), and the number of cells in five randomly selected fields was counted.


### Transwell assay
  Cell migration and invasion were evaluated using Transwell assays. For migration, the lower chamber was coated with 40 µL of 50 ng/mL FN and air-dried for 1 h at room temperature. For invasion assays, Transwell inserts were pre-coated with 30 µL Matrigel, air-dried naturally, and then treated with FN. Multiple myeloma cells were treated with 500 ng/mL GDF15 for 48 h, collected by centrifugation (1000 × g for 5 min), and resuspended in serum-free RPMI 1640 medium at concentrations of 3 × 10⁵ cells/mL (ARP-1) and 5 × 10⁵ cells/mL (U266). A volume of 100 µL of cell suspension was added to the upper chamber, and 500 µL of RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and SDF-1α (#300–28 A-2UG, Peprotech, Neobioscience, Shenzhen, Guangdong, China) at a final concentration of 50 ng/mL was added to the lower chamber to stimulate chemotactic migration. After incubation at 37 °C for 24 h (migration) or 48 h (invasion), non-migrated or non-invaded cells were removed from the upper surface. Cells that migrated to the lower chamber or invaded through the membrane were washed three times with PBS, fixed in methanol at room temperature for 30 min, stained with crystal violet for 10 min, and visualized under a microscope. The number of migrated or invaded cells in five random fields was quantified.


### Western blotting
  Western blotting was performed to assess the protein expression levels of GFRAL, BAX, BCL-2, caspase-9, E-cadherin, CXCR4, PI3K, Akt, phosphorylated (p)-Akt (Ser473), NF-κB, and MMP9. Total proteins were extracted using a total protein extraction kit (EX1100, Solarbio, Beijing, China) and quantified with a BCA protein assay kit (MA0082, Meilunbio, Dalian, Liaoning, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Proteins were separated by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (10% gel, 20 µg protein per lane) and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (0.45 μm). Membranes were blocked with QuickBlock™ Western Blocking Reagent (P0252, Beyotime, Foshan, Guangdong, China) for 15 min at room temperature, followed by incubation with the following primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight: anti-E-cadherin (1:1000; 28731-1-AP), anti-CXCR4 (1:1000; 11073-2-AP), anti-Akt (1:1000; 10176-2-AP), anti-p-Akt (1:1000; 28731-1-AP), anti-MMP9 (1:1000; 10375-2-AP), anti-GAPDH (1:1000; 10494-1-AP), anti-BAX (1:1000; 50599-2-Ig), anti-BCL-2 (1:1000; 12789-1-AP), anti-caspase-9 (1:1000; 10380-1-AP), anti-PI3K (1:1000; 67121-1-Ig) (all from Proteintech, Wuhan, Hubei, China), anti-NF-κB p65 (1:1000; WL01273b, Wanleibio, Shenyang, Liaoning, China), and anti-GFRAL (1:1500; CSB-PA751020LA01HU, CUSABIO, Wuhan, Hubei, China). Following primary antibody incubation, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated AffiniPure goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:6000; BA1054; Boster Bio, Wuhan, Hubei, China) for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using a BeyoECL Star kit (P0018AS, Beyotime, Foshan, Guangdong, China), and images were acquired using a gel imaging system (MiniChemi420, Sagecreation, Beijing, China). Densitometric analysis was conducted using ImageJ software, and target protein expression levels were normalized to GAPDH.


### Co-immunoprecipitation assay
  A co-immunoprecipitation assay was carried out using an immunoprecipitation kit containing protein A + G magnetic beads (P2179, Beyotime, Foshan, Guangdong, China) to investigate potential interactions between GDF15, GFRAL, and CXCR4. Multiple myeloma (MM) cells (ARP-1, U266) were treated with 200 ng/mL recombinant GDF15 for 24 h and collected by centrifugation at 1000 × g for 5 min at room temperature. Cells were washed three times with PBS, residual liquid was removed, and cell pellets were resuspended by gently scraping the tube bottom. Subsequently, cells (5 × 10⁵–1 × 10⁶) were lysed in lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (100–200 µL), followed by centrifugation at 10,000–14,000 × g at 4 °C to obtain total protein extracts. An aliquot of the lysate was retained as input/total control.

  Given that CXCR4 is a transmembrane receptor expressed on MM cells, it was used as the bait protein to pull down GDF15 and GFRAL. Protein A + G magnetic beads were mixed with 500 µL of anti-CXCR4 antibody or control IgG antibody working solution (diluted in 1× TBS to a final concentration of 750 µg/mL) and incubated on a rotator for 1 h at room temperature. The antibody-bound beads were resuspended in 500 µL of TBS and subjected to magnetic separation for 10 s, followed by three washes to remove unbound components. Thereafter, 500 µL of total protein lysate was incubated with 20 µL of anti-CXCR4-conjugated or IgG-conjugated magnetic beads on a shaker at 4 °C overnight. The next day, protein–antibody complexes were isolated via magnetic separation for 10 s. The beads were then washed three times with lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors (500 µL per wash). For each 20 µL of original magnetic bead suspension, 100 µL of 1× SDS-PAGE sample loading buffer was added. The mixture was heated at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by magnetic separation for 10 s. The supernatant was collected for subsequent Western blot analysis.


### Statistical analysis
  The statistical analysis and data visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0; GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Correlation analysis was employed to evaluate the association between GDF15 protein expression and the clinical characteristics of patients with multiple myeloma (MM) and extramedullary disease (EMD). All experiments were conducted in triplicate. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used for comparisons among three or more groups. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), while other data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (****, P < 0.0001; ***, P < 0.001; **, P < 0.01; *, P < 0.05; ns, P ≥ 0.05).


## Results

### Identification of DEGs
  A total of 1,576 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified between MM and control samples from the GSE146649 dataset. The top 10 upregulated and downregulated genes were visualized in volcano plots (Fig. 3A) and heatmaps (Fig. 3B), respectively.

  Subsequently, the R package “WGCNA” was applied to assess sample clustering outliers. The results indicated good overall sample clustering in GSE146649 (Fig. 3C). Samples located below the red line were selected for subsequent WGCNA analysis. A soft-thresholding power of 20 was chosen, as the scale-free topology fit index (R²) approached 0.80 at this value (Fig. 3D). Ultimately, five merged modules were identified (Fig. 3E). The module most strongly correlated with MM was designated as the key module. The grey module showed the highest correlation magnitude (|cor| > 0.3, P < 0.05) and contained 14,924 genes (Fig. 3F). These genes were defined as key module genes.

  To identify candidate genes, an intersection was performed between the 1,576 significantly differentially expressed genes in MM and the 14,924 genes within the key module derived from WGCNA, yielding 1,072 overlapping genes (Fig. 3G).

  Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) revealed nine pathways with differential activity between the two groups, including seven significantly upregulated and two significantly downregulated functional pathways. Heatmap visualization was generated using the “heatmap” package (Fig. 3H and I). In the MM group, pancreatic β-cell function, coagulation, angiogenesis, allograft rejection, KRAS signaling (both up- and downregulated), inflammatory response, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition were enriched compared to the control group. These findings suggest that the pathophysiology of MM may involve immune activation, coagulation abnormalities, dysregulation of signal transduction, and alterations in metabolic processes.

  Enrichment analysis identified the top five most significantly enriched gene functions, ranked by the number of associated genes. In terms of biological processes, candidate genes were primarily involved in wound healing, muscle tissue development, and morphogenesis of a branching structure. With respect to cellular components, candidate genes were predominantly enriched in cell-substrate junctions, focal adhesions, and collagen-containing extracellular matrices. For molecular functions, candidate genes were mainly associated with actin binding, cadherin binding, and structural constituents of the extracellular matrix (Fig. 3J).

  KEGG pathway enrichment analysis identified six significantly enriched pathways. Candidate genes were primarily implicated in proteoglycans in cancer, the AGE–RAGE signaling pathway in diabetic complications, small cell lung cancer, the TGF-β signaling pathway, *Vibrio cholerae* infection, and the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway (Fig. 3K).

  Through the construction of a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network involving 1,072 candidate genes and subsequent evaluation using five algorithms (MCC, Degree, DMNC, EPC, and Closeness) in the CytoHubba plugin, the top 120 genes were identified and intersected. A total of 25 overlapping genes (ANGPT1, HYOU1, COL4A2, SPARC, VEGFC, PDIA4, COL4A1, CYR61, FGF1, ENG, BIRC3, MMP14, GDF15, PDIA3, TGFB2, CD24, BCAP31, POSTN, BCL2L11, LOX, COL3A1, TIMP1, BCL2L1, COL18A1, PRDX1) were obtained and visualized. These genes were subsequently regarded as candidate genes for further analysis(Fig. 3L and Q).


### Four biomarkers were identified
  To identify key genes, we modeled the 25 candidate genes using SVM-RFE and XGBoost. The model achieved its highest predictive accuracy with 19 or 20 characteristic genes (Fig. 4A and B). SVM screening selected all 20 genes that had prediction scores exceeding zero (Fig. 4C). The error curve decreased monotonically, showing that the model’s validation error declined progressively during training and eventually stabilized around 0.1, which reflects its favorable predictive performance (Fig. 4D). XGBoost predicted 12 genes (Fig. 4E), and the intersection of these with the 20 from SVM-RFE yielded 11 overlapping genes (Fig. 4F).

  A Wilcoxon rank-sum test in dataset GSE146649 compared the expression levels of these 11 genes between the MM group and normal controls, revealing statistically significant differential expression for only 9 genes (p < 0.05, Fig. 4G). Evaluation of their predictive value in MM showed that, among these 9, only four—BIRC3, PRDX1, BCAP31, and GDF15—displayed high predictive accuracy (AUC > 0.7) in the dataset. These four genes were therefore selected as key genes for subsequent analysis (Fig. 4H).

  SHAP and DCA were applied to multiple myeloma datasets GSE146649 and GSE24870, and ROC analysis of the key genes BIRC3, PRDX1, BCAP31, and GDF15 yielded an AUC of 0.994, indicating high diagnostic value (Fig. 4I). All key genes showed strong diagnostic efficacy in MM (Fig. 4J and L). The decision curve analysis (DCA) results indicate that within the probability range of 0–1 risk threshold, the model’s curve consistently exceeds the “all” line (assuming all patients have received treatment) and the “none” line (assuming no patients have received treatment), demonstrating the clinical practicality of the model (Fig. 4M). As shown in Fig. 4N, the expression levels of BIRC3, PRDX1, BCAP31, and GDF15 in the samples were used to calculate individual scores (indicated by red points in the “Points” section). The sum of these scores produces a total points value, and in all cases, the predicted probabilities exceeds 0.5. In the development set (p = 0.908) or validation set (p = 0.497), there was no significant deviation between the predicted results and the observed results, with both p-values > 0.05, indicating good model calibration (Fig. 4O). The predictive performance of the nomogram was evaluated using the AUC. In the development set (GSE146649), the nomogram achieved an AUC of 0.719, while the validation set (GSE24870) had an AUC of 0.875, confirming its strong disease prediction ability (Fig. 4P).

The results showed significant enrichment of activated CD8 + T cells, T follicular helper (Tfh) cells, and CD56 < sup > dim</sup> natural killer (NK) cells in patients with multiple myeloma (MM), whereas central memory CD4 + T cells were predominantly enriched in control samples (Fig. 5A). In contrast, BIRC3 exhibited a significant negative correlation with CD56 < sup > dim</sup > NK cells (Fig. 5C and D). Distinct genes demonstrated varying degrees of association with multiple immune cell populations, including activated CD8 + T cells, monocytes, and NK cells, with BCAP31 and PRDX1 showing strong positive correlations with specific immune cell subsets. These correlation patterns suggest potential functional roles of these genes in immune cell infiltration and immune responses, particularly in the recruitment of activated CD8 + T cells and monocytes (Fig. 5E and H). Significant differential correlations were observed between key genes and the infiltration levels of activated CD8 + T cells and CD56 < sup > dim</sup > NK cells (Fig. 5I and O).

(H) Western blotting showed that uprosertib, an Akt inhibitor, suppressed the expression of p-Akt, MMP9, and CXCR4 in MM cells (****P < 0.0001 versus control group; ****P < 0.0001 versus GDF15 + uprosertib group).   Chemokine-receptor interactions can trigger tumor cell detachment from the primary site, facilitating directional migration along chemokine gradients and subsequent tissue invasion [32, 33]. To examine this mechanism in MM cells, SDF-1α was added to the lower chamber during the Transwell assay. Compared to the GDF15 treatment group, the SDF-1α + GDF15 group exhibited significantly increased migration (ARP-1: **, P = 0.0085; U266: *, P = 0.0395). However, no significant difference in invasive capacity was observed in GDF15-treated MM cells in the presence of SDF-1α (ARP-1: P = 0.3884; U266: P = 0.2644; Fig. 10B). These results suggest that GDF15 contributes to MM cell migration and metastatic progression.

  To further validate whether the GDF15/GFRAL axis promotes migration and invasion of MM cells via the AKT/pAKT/NF‑κB signaling pathway, we silenced GFRAL using siRNA and subsequently reactivated the pathway with the AKT activator (SC79; AbMole, HOU, USA). Results showed that, compared with the siRNC group, SC79 modestly enhanced the migration of ARP‑1 cells and restored migration in U266 cells (ARP-1: ***, P < 0.001; U266: ns). Moreover, in invasion assays, SC79 significantly promoted the invasive ability of both ARP‑1 and U266 cells(ARP-1: *, P < 0.05; U266: *, P < 0.05). These findings indicate that reactivation of the AKT signaling pathway can rescue the migratory and invasive capacities of MM cells (Fig. 10C).

  Western blot analysis demonstrated that GDF15 significantly upregulated protein expression of MMP9 (ARP-1: ****, P < 0.0001; U266: ****, P < 0.0001) and CXCR4 (ARP-1: ****, P < 0.0001; U266: ****, P < 0.0001) in MM cells. Consistently, previous experiments showed a dose-dependent increase in CXCR4 expression following treatment with 100, 500, and 1000 ng/mL GDF15 (Supplementary Fig. 1). In contrast, transfection with GFRAL siRNA1 significantly reduced the protein levels of both MMP9 (ARP-1: ****, P < 0.0001; U266: ****, P < 0.0001) and CXCR4 (ARP-1: ****, P < 0.0001; U266: ****, P < 0.0001) in MM cells (Fig. 10D). These data indicate that the GDF15/GFRAL axis promotes the migratory and invasive potential of MM cells by regulating the expression of CXCR4 and MMP9.

  Based on the results mentioned above, we investigated the mechanisms through which GDF15/GFRAL regulates CXCR4 and MMP9 in multiple myeloma (MM). Previous studies have demonstrated that GDF15 enhances the survival of patients with MM by activating the Akt signaling pathway [12] and promotes tumor growth and metastasis via the Akt/GSK-3β/β-catenin signaling pathway [34]. However, the positional relationship of signaling molecules activated by GDF15/GFRAL—whether upstream or downstream of Akt—in MM cells remains unclear. Therefore, we examined the effects of GDF15/GFRAL on the PI3K/Akt/NF-κB signaling pathway in MM cell lines. Western blot analysis revealed that GDF15 upregulated p-Akt expression and the p-Akt/Akt ratio in ARP-1 (P < 0.0001) and U266 (P < 0.0001) cells in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 10E and Supplementary Fig. 2). Furthermore, GDF15 significantly increased the expression levels of PI3K and NF-κB in both ARP-1 and U266 cell lines (P < 0.0001 for all comparisons). Prior to siRNA interference, we also examined GFRAL expression in ARP‑1 and U266 cells using qPCR and Western blot assays. Detectable expression of GFRAL was confirmed at both the mRNA and protein levels (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4). GDF15-treated MM cells transfected with GFRAL siRNA1 exhibited significantly reduced expression of PI3K, p-Akt, and NF-κB (ARP-1: **** P < 0.0001; U266: ****, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 10G). These findings suggest that GDF15/GFRAL may activate the PI3K/Akt/NF-κB signaling pathway in MM cells.We also examined the effect of the SC79 on the expression of p-AKT and NF-κB proteins in the context of GFRAL knockdown. The results demonstrated that, compared with the siRNC group, SC79 further enhanced the expression of p-AKT (ARP-1: **, P < 0.01; U266: ****, P < 0.0001) and NF-κB (ARP-1: ****, P < 0.0001; U266: ****, P < 0.0001). These findings support the conclusion that the GDF15/GFRAL axis likely regulates the migration and invasion of MM cells through the AKT/p-AKT/NF-κB signaling pathway.

  To determine whether GDF15 regulates CXCR4 and MMP9 expression in MM cells via the Akt signaling pathway, we treated MM cells with uprosertib, a selective Akt inhibitor. Western blot results following treatment with uprosertib and GDF15 are shown in Fig. 10H. GDF15 significantly enhanced the protein expression of p-Akt, CXCR4, and MMP9 in both ARP-1 (****, P < 0.0001) and U266 (****, P < 0.0001) cell lines. However, uprosertib markedly attenuated the expression of p-Akt, CXCR4, and MMP9 in GDF15-treated ARP-1 (****, P < 0.0001) and U266 (**** P < 0.0001) cells. These results indicate that GDF15/GFRAL may regulate the expression of CXCR4 and MMP9 through the PI3K/Akt/NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby promoting MM metastasis.

  GDF15, which modulates the biological behavior of MM cells, is primarily derived from the tumor microenvironment and exerts its effects via cell surface receptors on tumor cells [12]. Given that GDF15 may promote migration and invasion of MM cells by upregulating CXCR4 expression, we sought to determine whether GDF15 or its receptor GFRAL directly interacts with CXCR4 in MM cells. To this end, co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays were performed using CXCR4, a transmembrane receptor protein, as the bait. Subsequent western blotting was used to assess the protein expression of CXCR4, GDF15, and GFRAL. A clear band corresponding to CXCR4 was detected, but no bands for GDF15 or GFRAL were observed (Fig. 10F), indicating that CXCR4 does not directly bind to GDF15. This suggests that GDF15 may indirectly regulate CXCR4 expression through other, yet unidentified, molecular mediators.


## Discussion
  MM is an incurable plasma cell neoplasm and one of the most common hematologic malignancies. Although therapeutic strategies for MM have advanced significantly over the past two decades, the majority of patients, particularly those with EMD, eventually experience relapse, highlighting the urgent need for novel biomarkers and therapeutic approaches in MM management.

  This study was designed to achieve two primary objectives: (1) to identify blood-based molecular biomarkers associated with MM, and (2) to investigate the mechanistic link between the candidate biomarker GDF15 and MM progression. Using bioinformatics approaches, we analyzed gene expression profiles related to MM and performed functional pathway enrichment analysis. Machine learning techniques were subsequently employed to screen potential diagnostic biomarkers, which were then validated experimentally using clinical blood samples and MM cell lines.Bioinformatics analysis identified four key genes associated with MM: GDF15, BCAP31, BIRC3, and PRDX1. A diagnostic prediction model was constructed based on these genes and demonstrated strong predictive performance.Functional enrichment analysis indicated that these four biomarkers are involved in critical biological processes underlying MM pathophysiology and progression. Immune infiltration analysis suggested that GDF15, BCAP31, BIRC3, and PRDX1 may influence MM progression through modulation of natural killer (NK) cells activity. Subsequently, we focused on GDF15 and the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway to further explore their molecular regulatory mechanisms.

  Extensive literature indicates that elevated GDF15 expression is a marker of disease progression in various malignancies. Notably, serum and tissue levels of GDF15 are significantly higher in patients with metastatic melanoma [35] and prostate cancers [15] compared to those without metastasis and healthy controls. Increased GDF15 expression has been linked to liver metastasis in colorectal cancer [36] and lung metastasis in osteosarcoma [37]. Clinical evidence also demonstrates higher GDF15 expression in patients with prostate, breast, lung, and colorectal cancers who have bone metastases [38]. Similarly, serum GDF15 expression is associated with disease progression in MM [8, 10]. Importantly, MM patients exhibiting high GDF15 expression are often ineligible for autologous stem cell transplantation [39]. Plasma soluble GDF15 concentration positively correlates with serum β2-microglobulin (β2-MG) and vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR) levels in MM [8, 10]. Moreover, elevated serum GDF15 reflects tumor burden, progression, and response to therapy in MM. However, the association between GDF15 and EMD in MM remains unclear. Therefore, we compared GDF15 expression levels between MM patients with EMD and those without EMD. Our results showed that serum GDF15 levels were significantly elevated in MM patients compared to healthy individuals, regardless of EMD status. Notably, GDF15 expression was significantly higher in MM patients with EMD than in those without EMD. These findings support a strong association between elevated serum GDF15 and metastatic progression in MM. Furthermore, we observed that GDF15 significantly promoted MM cell proliferation, consistent with previous reports [11]. Bmi-1 has been shown to contribute to BTZ resistance in MM by upregulating BCL-2 and downregulating BAX [40, 41]. In line with these findings, our study demonstrated that GDF15 inhibits apoptosis and enhances BTZ resistance in MM cells by modulating the BCL-2/BAX ratio and caspase-9 expression, indicating a pro-tumorigenic role for GDF15 in MM progression. Thus, further investigation into the role of GDF15 in MM metastasis may provide insights into the pathogenesis of EMD.

  The interaction between cytokines and MM cells within the BM microenvironment regulates the chemotactic activity and adhesive capacity of these cells, thereby mediating tumor metastasis [42–44]. Chemokines modulate MM cell metastasis through positive or negative regulation of various signaling pathways. When MM cells egress from the BM and circulate as clonal circulating plasma cells (cPCs), reduced expression of CXCR4 may impair cPC homing and subsequent development of EMD [4, 45]. In contrast, CXCR4 facilitates tumor cell migration along stromal cell-derived factor-1α (SDF-1α) gradients and enhances invasive potential [32, 33]. Compared with BM-tropic cells, EMD-tropic cells exhibit significantly higher CXCR4 expression. Thus, CXCR4 may play a critical role in promoting EMT in MM cells, driving their transition to an invasive phenotype. The SDF-1α–CXCR4 axis has been demonstrated to contribute importantly to MM progression and EMD development [46]. Additionally, chemokine receptor-mediated regulation of adhesion molecules represents another key mechanism underlying MM metastasis. For instance, loss of p53 suppresses E-cadherin-mediated adhesion and promotes EMT via the miR-19a/CXCR5 pathway, thereby facilitating extramedullary dissemination of MM cells [47]. Notably, upregulation of CXCR4 induces expression of Twist, Slug, and Snail while suppressing E-cadherin expression [48]. Ultimately, increased CXCR4 expression diminishes cellular adhesion and enhances transport capacity of MM cells [42, 46], facilitating migration to secondary myeloma microenvironments [42, 43], and promoting an enhanced EMT phenotype [46]. The SDF-1α–CXCR4 axis also activates nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB), which indirectly stimulates secretion of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) from bone marrow stromal cells (BMSCs), thereby supporting MM cell growth, survival, and migration; it further mediates matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) synthesis, promoting hematogenous tumor dissemination [32, 48, 49]. Therefore, further investigation into upstream and downstream regulatory pathways linked to CXCR4 in MM metastasis may enhance understanding of the pathophysiology of EMD.

  Glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor family receptor alpha-like (GFRAL), the specific receptor for growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15), activates downstream signaling pathways including protein kinase B (PKB/Akt), extracellular signal-regulated kinases (ERK), and phosphoinositide-specific phospholipase C (PI-PLC) [9]. The GDF15/GFRAL axis is implicated in the regulation of cardiovascular diseases, inflammatory responses, autoimmune disorders, and energy metabolism [9, 50]. Activation of the Akt signaling pathway contributes to tumor metastasis by modulating the expression of CXCR4 [51] and MMP9 [16]. GDF15 increases p-Akt levels in MM [12], suggesting that it promotes cellular metastasis through regulation of CXCR4 and MMP9. Furthermore, the transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β)–Smad2/3 pathway may play a significant role in tumor metastasis and positively regulate CXCR4 expression. GDF15 activates the TGF-β–Smad2/3 signaling cascade, thereby promoting metastasis in pancreatic cancer [52], and osteosarcoma [37]. Cisatracurium, an anti-neoplastic agent, has been shown to reverse EMT and downregulate CXCR4 expression by inhibiting the TGF-β–Smad2/3 pathway in colon cancer [33]. Therefore, elevated GDF15 levels [10, 12] may regulate CXCR4 and MMP9 expression via the TGF-β/Smad pathway, consequently promoting MM cell metastasis.

  Whether GDF15 directly induces (EMD remains unclear. However, it may promote MM metastasis through the following mechanisms: (Ⅰ) GDF15 promotes MM metastasis by activating CXCR4 and MMP9 via the Akt signaling pathway. Additionally, GDF15 activates Akt and enhances the expression of the transcription factor SOX2 in MM [12]. Upregulated SOX2 positively regulates Bmi-1 and activates cellular mesenchymal–epithelial transition factor (c-MET), thereby promoting MM metastasis [53]. In turn, Bmi-1 negatively regulates P53 expression at the transcriptional level [54], thus maintaining continuous cancer cell renewal and promoting EMT and extramedullary spread [47]. Similarly, activation of the Akt signaling pathway has been shown to upregulate CXCR4 expression, facilitating metastasis in lung cancer [51]. Studies indicate that the PI3K/AKT/mTORC1 signaling pathway regulates MMP9 expression by modulating MMP9 promoter activity through NF-κB (p65) in mammary epithelial cells [16]. Therefore, GDF15/GFRAL may promote MM metastasis by regulating CXCR4 and MMP9 through the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. Notably, it has also been reported that the CXCR family can upregulate MMP9 expression via the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, thereby enhancing cancer cell invasion and metastasis [55]. It is reasonable to hypothesize a positive feedback loop between the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway and CXCR4. (Ⅱ) GDF15 can bind to the TGF-β receptor and activate the Smad signaling pathway [37, 56], which promotes the expression of CXCR4 and MMP2/9 [33, 56]. This contributes to MM metastasis by inducing homing or altering tumor cell surface protein conformation to enhance EMT. Previous studies have confirmed that GDF15 promotes tumor metastasis via activation of the TGF-β–Smad2/3 signaling pathway [37, 52], which also promotes CXCR4 expression [33]. However, upregulation of CXCR4 inhibits E-cadherin expression in MM, enhancing the migratory ability of MM cells [42, 43]. Several studies showed that CXCR4 promotes the proliferation, migration, and invasion in tumors through PI3K/AKT signal pathway [57, 58]. In our study, we found that GDF15 likely does not directly bind to CXCR4 but instead interacts with GFRAL to regulate CXCR4 and MMP9 through the PI3K/Akt/NF-κB pathway. Given that GDF15 regulates metastatic markers such as CXCR4 and MMP9 via the TGF-β signaling pathway, whether it mediates TGF-β to promote MM metastasis warrants further investigation. Of interest, the CXCR4/TGF-β1 axis has been implicated in colorectal cancer (CRC) liver metastasis by mediating hepatic stellate cell (HSC) differentiation into cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) [59]., suggesting a potential positive feedback loop between CXCR4 and the TGF-β signaling pathway.

  This study has several limitations. First, the analysis was based on bioinformatics evaluation of transcriptomic data from public databases. Although microarray data may harbor inherent biases, the datasets used underwent rigorous preprocessing and filtering. Second, the sample size was limited; therefore, to strengthen validation, we utilized multiple independent datasets and experimentally verified the target gene GDF15. Third, although the current model demonstrates predictive performance as indicated by its AUC value, further validation in larger cohorts is necessary to confirm its clinical applicability. Furthermore, ssGSEA analysis also has certain limitations. For instance, ssGSEA calculates the relative enrichment of gene sets within a sample, which makes the results highly dependent on the distribution of gene expression within the sample. Although enrichment scores can be compared across different samples, their biological significance is relative. ssGSEA is sensitive to intra-sample expression sequencing and prone to technical bias. In large-scale samples, inter-sample technical heterogeneity may be amplified, which can be mitigated through strict batch correction and quality control but cannot be completely eliminated. ssGSEA is a “single-sample” method, with each sample calculated independently without relying on an external sample set as a background. While this provides sample-specific signals, it also means the lack of a global background distribution as a reference, potentially reducing the detection of weak signals. When the sample size is extremely large, subtle biological differences may become statistically significant, but their effect size may be small, requiring caution in interpretation. Despite these limitations, we still adopted ssGSEA in our study because it has advantages in capturing sample-specific pathway activity and is suitable for our research objectives. To mitigate these limitations, we implemented the following measures: combining multiple enrichment analysis methods (e.g., GSEA) for cross-validation, rigorous data preprocessing including batch correction, normalization, and filtering of abnormal samples, and focusing on trends rather than absolute values when interpreting results. We primarily focused on the relative differences in enrichment scores between sample groups rather than the absolute scores of individual samples. Future studies will expand the cohort size, incorporate additional stratifications, and include experimental validations such as protein-level assays and gene-editing approaches to improve the model’s robustness and generalizability. Likewise, future clinical investigations will explore how the three additional genes (BCAP31, BIRC3, and PRDX1) influence MM disease progression through their regulatory effects on downstream genes. These genes may serve as novel biomarkers in MM and provide deeper insights into the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying EMD.

  MM is a plasma cell malignancy frequently associated with skeletal comorbidities. Vitamin D (1,25(OH)₂D), the biologically active form of vitamin D, plays a critical role in maintaining skeletal homeostasis [60]. Calcitriol, the hormonally active metabolite of vitamin D, serves as an essential regulator of hematopoiesis and immune function. A randomized clinical trial has demonstrated that calcitriol enhances absolute lymphocyte count (ALC) recovery and improves relapse-free survival (RFS), indicating its safety and potential benefit following autologous peripheral blood hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in patients with multiple myeloma [61]. Nanomolar concentrations of 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D₃ have been shown to significantly inhibit the proliferation of the human myeloma cell line RPMI 8226, while simultaneously inducing phenotypic changes, including increased expression of butyrate esterase, CD20 (B1), CD15 (Leu-M1) antigens, and lambda light chains, as well as decreased expression of the PC1 antigen, as assessed by microfluorometric analysis [62]. Based on the core MM-related gene GDF15, we previously predicted via the DGIdb database that calcitriol may serve as a potential therapeutic agent for MM, and hypothesized that its anti-myeloma effects might be mediated through interaction with GDF15. However, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated.


## Conclusion
  This study systematically identified genes and their regulatory networks associated with MM through comprehensive bioinformatics analyses. Four key genes were determined as potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets, and a diagnostic model with high predictive accuracy was established. Serum levels of GDF15 is remarkably high in MM patients with EMD. Furthermore, GDF15/GFRAL enhances the migratory and invasive capacities of MM cells by modulating the expression of CXCR4 and MMP9 through activation of the PI3K/Akt/NF-κB signaling pathway. In conclusion, GDF15 represents a promising therapeutic target for MM with EMD, and further investigation into GDF15-targeted therapies is warranted.
