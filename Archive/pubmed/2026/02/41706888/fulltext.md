# Oncogenic function and transcriptional dynamics of MYCN in liver tumorigenesis


## Significance
  Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains a lethal cancer due to late diagnosis and frequent recurrence. Although MYCN is recognized as a stemness-associated oncogene, its role and spatial dynamics in liver tumorigenesis remain unclear. Here, we show that MYCN actively drives HCC development in combination with AKT activation and marks a spatially restricted microenvironment enriched for epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and Wnt/β-catenin signaling features that expand during tumor progression. Integrating spatial transcriptomics with machine learning, we develop a MYCN niche score that predicts recurrence risk and identifies precancerous microenvironments in nontumor liver tissue. This approach provides a potential strategy for early detection of high-risk patients before malignant transformation.


## Results

### MYCN Synergizes with AKT Activation to Promote Liver Tumorigenesis.
  A transposon-based expression system delivered via HTVi was used to overexpress MYCN in the livers of mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). First, we evaluated the transfection efficiency of a GFP-labeled MYCN vector at 24 h post-HTVi (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). Whole-liver section staining revealed that HTVi with a volume of 2 mL, but not 1 mL, effectively delivered the GFP-labeled MYCN vector into mouse livers, indicating the importance of pressure-mediated membrane permeability in enhancing gene transfer into liver cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). Consistent with the retrograde pressure mechanism in the central vein (CV) during HTVi delivery, coimmunofluorescence staining demonstrated that GFP-labeled MYCN was exclusively expressed in glutamine synthetase (GS)-positive hepatocytes adjacent to the CV, but not in E-Cadherin (E-Cad)-positive hepatocytes near the portal vein (PV) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D). Serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT), a marker of liver injury, was transiently elevated 24 h after HTVi, but returned to baseline by day 3, indicating that the injury was acute and self-limited (SI Appendix, Fig. S1E).

  Next, we used this HTVi-based transposon system to introduce the GFP-labeled MYCN into the mouse liver alone or in combination with HA-labeled myristoylated AKT, a constitutively active form of AKT known to promote lipogenesis (24) and MYC-driven liver tumorigenesis (25) (Fig. 1A). Notably, only the MYCN+AKT group exhibited severe mortality, with a 50% survival rate at 50 d post-HTVi (Fig. 1B). Macroscopic (Fig. 1C and SI Appendix, Fig. S2A) and microscopic (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B) analyses of the liver revealed tumor development exclusively in the MYCN+AKT group, showing a 72% tumor incidence rate at 50 d post-HTVi (Fig. 1D). Although the body weights of the MYCN alone and MYCN+AKT groups showed statistically significant increases compared with those in controls, the actual biological difference was minimal (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C). Moreover, serum glucose levels were not significantly different between control and MYCN+AKT mice at 50 d post-HTVi, indicating a lack of systemic metabolic alterations (SI Appendix, Fig. S2D). Time-series monitoring showed that serum ALT levels (SI Appendix, Fig. S2E) and the liver-to-body weight ratio (SI Appendix, Fig. S2F), which are indicators of hepatomegaly and proliferative liver growth, remained comparable with those of the control group at 3 and 14 d in all experimental groups. By day 50, only tumor-bearing mice exhibited a dramatic increase in both parameters. Additionally, serum lactate dehydrogenase levels, a marker of tumor burden and tissue damage, were significantly elevated in tumor-bearing mice, whereas no significant changes were observed in nontumorous mice (Fig. 1E). PCR analysis and immunostaining of bulk liver tissues of tumor-bearing mice showed significantly increased expression of HCC markers Afp (Fig. 1F), Gpc3 (glypican-3; Fig. 1G), Prom1 (CD133; Fig. 1H), and collagen IV (26) (SI Appendix, Fig. S2G). Histological examination exclusively revealed classical moderately to poorly differentiated HCC regions, exhibiting trabecular and nest-like growth patterns composed of large polygonal cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, along with only minor (<1%) pseudoglandular areas (Fig. 1I). Coimmunofluorescence staining demonstrated that GFP-labeled MYCN was selectively expressed in GPC3-positive cells of the liver of tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 1J) but not in pan-cytokeratin (pan-CK)-positive cells (Fig. 1K). Although previous studies reported that MYC overexpression induces hepatoblastoma (HB) formation (27), our histological analyses confirmed the absence of HB-like rosette structures. Immunostaining showed that MYCN+AKT tumors were negative for GS (SI Appendix, Fig. S2H), which are typically elevated in HBs. Collectively, these data support a synergistic role of MYCN and AKT activation in aggressive liver tumorigenesis with the distinct histological features of HCC.


### Transcriptional Landscape of MYCN-Driven Liver Tumors.
  Gene and protein expression analysis of bulk liver tissues revealed that MYCN was detectable at 3 d post-HTVi, but was largely absent by 50 d in the MYCN alone group (Fig. 1L). In contrast, in the MYCN+AKT group, MYCN expression persisted within the nuclear fraction for 50 d, but only in the livers of tumor-bearing mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). HA staining confirmed that HA-labeled myristoylated AKT remained detectable at 50 d post-HTVi in the AKT alone group and was strongly induced in MYCN+AKT tumors (Fig. 1L). A ductal structure in the GS-positive CV regions was also observed in the AKT alone group (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B), consistent with previous studies showing that AKT activation promotes heterogeneous HCC and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) development over prolonged periods (25). At 50 d post-HTVi, baseline AKT expression was evident in nontumor tissues, whereas tumor tissues displayed two distinct protein bands corresponding to endogenous and exogenous (HA-tagged) AKT, along with AKT phosphorylated at Ser473, indicating active AKT signaling (Fig. 1L). Immunofluorescence staining revealed that MYCN overexpression induced pronounced apoptosis in pericentral hepatocytes at 3 d post-HTVi, as evidenced by the colocalization with apoptosis markers cleaved caspase-3 and phosphorylated histone H2A.X (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C) and formation of apoptotic bodies (Fig. 1M and SI Appendix, Fig. S3D). These findings suggest that the absence of tumors in the MYCN alone group most likely reflects the rapid elimination of MYCN-overexpressing cells rather than an inherent inability of MYCN to drive oncogenesis. In contrast, AKT coexpression stabilizes MYCN-positive hepatocytes, enabling their survival, persistence, and eventual expansion. Notably, a small number of MYCN-positive cells remained detectable in the MYCN alone group after 50 d (SI Appendix, Fig. S3D), and thus we cannot exclude the possibility that a prolonged observation could reveal late-onset tumorigenesis driven by MYCN alone.

  We investigated the molecular mechanisms underlying MYCN-driven liver tumorigenesis using RNA-seq analysis (Dataset S1). To better capture MYCN-related tumor progression, we included previously established AKT alone tumors (25). Principal component analysis (PCA) of the global transcriptional profiles (Fig. 1N) revealed distinct molecular patterns separating AKT alone tumors from MYCN+AKT tumors. Compared with MYCN+AKT nontumors or controls, 1,105 up-regulated genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S3E) and 734 down-regulated genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S3F) were specifically enriched in MYCN+AKT tumors but not in AKT alone tumors. Gene ontology (GO) analysis indicated that these MYCN+AKT-specific genes are associated with activation of translation (SI Appendix, Fig. S3G) and suppression of metabolic pathways (SI Appendix, Fig. S3H). Next, we examined the relevance of MYCN-driven tumors in human liver cancer based on their transcriptional profiles. Comparison with The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) datasets and a HB RNA-seq dataset (28) showed that the transcriptomic profiles of MYCN+AKT tumors more closely resembled HCC (TCGA-LIHC) than ICC (TCGA-CHOL) and HB (Fig. 1O). To determine the specific HCC subtype associated with MYCN-driven tumors, we applied a uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) combined with the Louvain clustering method (29) (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). This analysis identified three distinct transcriptional clusters in human HCC, with MYCN+AKT tumor signatures predominantly represented in cluster 2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). Notably, patients in cluster 2 showed a reduced disease-free survival probability, reflecting a higher risk of recurrence and potential therapeutic resistance (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C). Clinical feature analysis indicated that cluster 2 had a higher proportion of male patients and markedly lower tumor break load (SI Appendix, Table S1 and Fig. S4D). GO analysis revealed distinct biological features of each cluster (SI Appendix, Fig. S4E). Cluster 0 was enriched in metabolic pathways, consistent with a well-differentiated, metabolically active HCC subtype. Cluster 1 was enriched in developmental pathways such as appendage and limb morphogenesis, suggesting a dedifferentiated stem-like tumor state. In contrast, cluster 2 was enriched in stress-related signaling pathways, indicating a stress-adapted, metabolically reprogrammed, and potentially therapy-resistant tumor phenotype. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that MYCN-driven liver tumors in mice closely resemble a specific subset of human HCC characterized by a stress-adaptive transcriptional state.


### Transcriptional Programs Regulated by MYCN.
  To further explore the direct effect of MYCN on hepatocytes, we overexpressed GFP-labeled MYCN in the normal hepatocyte Hc cell line using a transposon-based expression system (Fig. 2A). HC cells lack endogenous MYCN expression (20) but exhibit high basal AKT activity (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A), making them a useful model that partially recapitulates the MYCN+AKT context observed in vivo. Consistent with its oncogenic role in mouse models, MYCN overexpression significantly upregulated the expression of cell cycle-related genes, including CCNB1 (Cyclin B1; Fig. 2B), and promoted colony proliferation (Fig. 2C). We performed RNA-seq analysis to explore the underlying transcriptional mechanisms (Dataset S2). PCA clearly separated the two independent MYCN-overexpressing clones from the wild-type (WT) cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). Differential expression analysis identified 176 upregulated and 330 downregulated genes in the MYCN overexpression clones (SI Appendix, Fig. S5C). GO analysis revealed that the upregulated genes were significantly enriched in developmental pathways, whereas the downregulated genes were strongly enriched in EMT and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling-related pathways (SI Appendix, Fig. S5D). Morphologically, time-lapse imaging demonstrated that MYCN-overexpressing cells exhibited increased spreading of daughter cells from the spheroid clusters (SI Appendix, Fig. S5E). Furthermore, we validated the expression of key EMT markers and found that MYCN overexpression significantly downregulated CDH1 (E-Cad, an epithelial marker) expression and upregulated fibronectin (FN1) (a mesenchymal marker) (SI Appendix, Fig. S5F) expression.

  We then applied CUT&RUN-seq analysis and integrated the data with the RNA-seq data to investigate the transcriptional effect of MYCN. Compared with input controls, 24,072 MYCN-binding peaks identified in MYCN-overexpression HC cells (Dataset S3). Peaks were annotated based on their genomic location within ±2 kb of the transcription start site (TSS) (Fig. 2D) and transcription end sites (TES), as recent studies suggest that these regions can substantially influence gene expression regulated by MYC family members (30). De novo motif analysis of MYCN CUT&RUN peaks using HOMER identified a highly significant enrichment of a motif closely matching the canonical MYCN E-box binding sequence (P = 1 × 10−60; SI Appendix, Fig. S5G). Most peaks localized upstream or within gene bodies (73.1%), whereas 6,488 peaks (26.9%) were located downstream of TES (Fig. 2E). In comparison, MYC ChIP-seq data from HepG2 cells (ENCODE) showed TES-proximal binding at a lower proportion (10.4%; Fig. 2E). Peak overlap analysis revealed 570 shared peaks between MYCN and MYC, of which 520 MYCN peaks mapped to promoters, indicating substantial functional similarity between these TF (Fig. 2F and Dataset S4). Integration of human MYCN/MYC peaks with RNA-seq data showed that, among 1,130 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in MYCN-overexpressing HC cells, 465 genes (41.1%) were bound by MYCN, 65 genes (5.7%) by MYC, and 38 genes by both MYCN and MYC (Fig. 2G and Dataset S5). GO analysis of the 465 direct MYCN target genes revealed that upregulated genes were enriched for development-related processes, including regulation of small GTPase signaling, limbic and eye development, and connective tissue formation, whereas downregulated genes were enriched for ECM organization and xenobiotic metabolism (Fig. 2H). These results suggest that MYCN directly reshapes transcriptional programs to drive cytoskeletal remodeling, tissue architecture changes, and metabolic adaptation.


### Spatiotemporal Remodeling in Liver Tumorigenesis.
  Having established that MYCN functions as an oncogenic driver in the HTVi model, we investigated how MYCN becomes transcriptionally activated in physiologically relevant disease settings. We performed spatial transcriptomics using the 10× Genomics Visium platform in a metabolic HCC mouse model, STAM (Fig. 3A), which mimics MASH and type I diabetes (31). This model recapitulates the key metabolic and inflammatory features of human MASH-driven HCC and exhibits strong transcriptional similarity to human HCC (32). To capture the dynamic spatial transcriptional reprogramming during tumor progression, we analyzed liver tissues at the early (16 wk; STAM16w) and late (20 wk; STAM20w) stages (Fig. 3 B and C). Spatial transcriptomic profiling yielded 4,977 captured spots across all conditions, with a median of 3,136 genes detected per spot (SI Appendix, Table S2). Spatial UMAP clustering of combined spots from STAM16w and STAM20w samples by Harmony (33) revealed 12 distinct transcriptional clusters (namely “STAM clusters”) (Fig. 3D). These clusters clearly distinguished the two timepoints (Fig. 3E). In STAM16w, most spots grouped into two major clusters (STAM clusters 0 and 2) (Fig. 3F and SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). A defining feature of the liver is its spatial zonation, which governs metabolic and functional heterogeneity across the lobule and is tightly regulated by Wnt/β-catenin signaling (34). To assess whether zonation was preserved, we examined the expression of zonation markers Cyp2f2 (periportal) and Cyp2e1 (pericentral). In STAM16w, the two major clusters showed mutually exclusive expression of Cyp2f2 and Cyp2e1, indicating preservation of lobular architecture (Fig. 3G). In contrast, STAM20w spots displayed markedly increased transcriptional heterogeneity (Fig. 3F and SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). The STAM cluster 9, which emerged exclusively in STAM20w mice, was characterized by Cyp2f2 expression and likely corresponded to periportal-like regions. Although multiple clusters in STAM20w expressed Cyp2e1, the pericentral organization appeared to be disrupted, with less spatial distinction compared to STAM16w. To quantitatively assess the zonal dominance, we calculated the Cyp2e1/Cyp2f2 expression ratio per spot (Fig. 3H). STAM20w showed a shift toward pericentral gene expression compared with STAM16w. Consistently, immunofluorescence staining demonstrated expanded GS-positive CV regions in the livers of STAM20w mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S6B) and striking accumulation of F4/80-positive macrophages in GS-positive CV regions (Fig. 3I). These results suggest that the increased heterogeneity in STAM20w arises primarily from disruption and expansion of CV regions. This is not surprising, as increased steatosis severity is often accompanied by lobular inflammation and pericentral fibrosis (35). Notably, although clusters corresponding to CV- and PV-dominant zones were present at both time points, they were transcriptionally distinct, reflecting temporal rewiring of zonation programs (Fig. 3G). GO biological process analysis revealed that upregulated genes in the CV region of STAM20w were significantly enriched in translation-related pathways, whereas downregulated genes were enriched in metabolic pathways (SI Appendix, Fig. S6C). Interestingly, a recent spatial transcriptomic study of aged mouse livers also reported reduced metabolic activity in the pericentral regions (36), suggesting that zonation-associated metabolic dysregulation may play a pathological role in liver aging and tumorigenesis.

  To further explore the transcriptional features that underlie tissue architecture and spatial interactions, we examined DEGs across spatial clusters (SI Appendix, Fig. S6D). Statistical analysis revealed Mycn was significantly upregulated in STAM cluster 10 (P = 7.85e–09) and to a lesser extent in STAM cluster 8 (P = 0.041). Notably, STAM cluster 10 was exclusively detected in STAM20w samples and marked by high expression of genes associated with ECM remodeling, such as Ankrd1, Dcdc2a, and Lce6a. STAM cluster 8 expressed canonical fibrotic markers, including Acta2 (α-SMA), Col1a1, and Mfap4, indicating a stromal niche enriched for activated hepatic stellate or fibrogenic cells. Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) using hallmark gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database revealed significant enrichment of the “EMT” pathway in both clusters 8 and 10 (SI Appendix, Fig. S6E). Together, STAM clusters 8 and 10 represented EMT-prone stromal or transitional cell states, which is consistent with our findings linking MYCN to EMT activation and invasive phenotypes. At the tissue level, EMT scores were also significantly higher in the STAM20w group than those in the STAM16w group, indicating progressive activation of EMT programs during MASH progression (SI Appendix, Fig. S6F). Next, we explored spatial proximity between “EMT spots” and “malignant spots” with the EMT GSVA scores and Afp expression levels. The top 5% spots for each metric were classified as high-EMT or high-Afp spots. Both spots expanded from STAM16w to STAM20w (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 G–L). Interestingly, double-positive (high-EMT + high-Afp) spots were restricted to STAM clusters 7, 8, and 10 (SI Appendix, Fig. S6M). Spatially, EMT-high regions were adjacent to but did not overlap with Afp-high regions, suggesting a physical interaction between the EMT-prone niche and liver tumorigenesis. Among these clusters, we focused on STAM cluster 8, which showed a temporal increase in proportion from STAM16w to STAM20w, indicating its potential role as a progressive tumor-promoting niche. GO analysis of the upregulated DEGs within STAM cluster 8 revealed strong translation-related pathways between STAM20w and STAM16w (Dataset S6 and SI Appendix, Fig. S6N). In contrast, downregulated DEGs were significantly enriched in metabolic and immune-related functions (SI Appendix, Fig. S6O).

  To further validate the robustness of MYCN-associated microenvironmental remodeling in liver tumorigenesis, we performed spatial transcriptomics in another HCC mouse model, DEN+CCL4, which mimics DNA damage-driven, fibrosis-promoting tumorigenesis (37) (SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). Similar to the STAM model, we analyzed liver tissues from the DEN+CCL4 model at early (22 wk; DEN22w) and late (30 wk; DEN30w) stages (SI Appendix, Fig. S7B). Spatial UMAP clustering of combined spots using Harmony clearly distinguished the two mouse models, whereas the DEN22w and DEN30w samples showed no obvious separation (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 C and D). However, analysis of cluster distribution across all four conditions revealed that the proportion of combined cluster 9 increased from early to late stages in both models (SI Appendix, Fig. S7E). Notably, Mycn was significantly upregulated and EMT was the most enriched pathway in this cluster (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 F and G). These findings provide additional support for a functional link between MYCN-driven transcriptional dynamics and EMT-associated reprogramming.


### Spatial Transcriptional Features of MYCN Niche in Liver Tumorigenesis.
  To investigate the in situ transcriptional regulation of MYCN within its native microenvironment, we focused on spatial transcriptomic profiling of intact liver sections from STAM20w mice. Graph-based UMAP clustering revealed marked spatial heterogeneity and segregated the tissues into 10 distinct clusters (Fig. 4A). Among these, one cluster showed significantly elevated Mycn expression when compared with all other clusters (Fig. 4B), which we defined as the “MYCN niche.” Differential gene expression analysis identified 167 signature genes enriched in the MYCN niche (Dataset S7). GO analysis revealed significant enrichment of pathways related to epithelial development, cell adhesion, and migration (Fig. 4C). Notably, canonical malignant hepatocyte markers, such as Afp and Myc were absent from this cluster, suggesting that the MYCN niche may represent an early, tumor-promoting microenvironment prior to malignant transformation. To determine whether this niche is regulated by specific TFs, we performed an upstream regulator analysis using the causal analytics algorithm implemented in the IPA platform. The analysis identified Wnt/β-catenin-associated transcriptional regulators, including SMARCA4, TCF7L2, and CTNNB1, as significantly activated in the MYCN niche (Fig. 4D). Additionally, SP1, a direct transcriptional activator of MYCN (38), was predicted to be activated, further supporting the biological role of this niche. Regulators, such as SREBF1 (a master regulator of lipid metabolism) and SMAD3 (a mediator of TGF-β signaling), were also enriched, indicating links between MYCN activity, lipid dysregulation, and EMT-associated signaling. Given the critical role of Wnt/β-catenin signaling in liver tumorigenesis, we focused on TCF7L2, a DNA-binding TF positively correlated with MYCN expression in the TCGA-LIHC cohort (Fig. 4E). Using the JASPAR database (39), we identified a conserved TCF7L2 binding motif (GAGCTTCAAGCGC) within the MYCN promoter region (−14 to −1 bp upstream of TSS) (Fig. 4 F, Left). To validate this interaction, we performed TCF7L2 CUT&RUN-PCR in the MYCN-high HCC cell line JHH7 (23). We observed higher enrichment in the motif-containing region (Primer 1) than that in the control region (Primer 2) (Fig. 4 F, Right). To functionally assess whether TCF7L2 regulates MYCN transcription, we conducted loss- and gain-of-function experiments. siRNA-mediated knockdown of TCF7L2 in JHH7 cells resulted in a significant decrease in MYCN promoter activity (Fig. 4G) and endogenous MYCN mRNA expression (Fig. 4H). Conversely, the overexpression of TCF7L2 in HEK293 cells (which express low levels of MYCN) using transposon-based expression vectors significantly increased MYCN expression (Fig. 4I). Collectively, these results identify a spatially defined MYCN niche in liver tumorigenesis as a premalignant microenvironment governed by activated Wnt/β-catenin signaling.

  Next, to investigate how the MYCN niche interacts with its surrounding microenvironment, we manually selected spots corresponding to the MYCN niche and adjacent two layers of surrounding spots, hereafter referred to as the “Broad region,” using the Loupe Browser. Given the bulk resolution of Visium technology (55 to 100 µm spot diameter), this broader region captures the transcriptome of 10 to 20 cells. Seventy-nine MYCN niche spots and 86 Broad region spots were selected for comparative analysis (Fig. 4J and Dataset S8). GO analysis revealed that genes upregulated in the Broad region were enriched in lipid metabolism pathways, whereas genes upregulated in the MYCN niche were significantly associated with translation-related pathways (Fig. 4K). To further examine cell–cell communication, we applied CellChat analysis (40) to infer potential ligand–receptor interactions. This revealed active communication through the osteopontin (SPP1) and fibroblast growth factor signaling pathways (Fig. 4L). Notably, SPP1-CD44 signaling was strongly enriched as a directional signal from the MYCN niche to the Broad region. Recently, SPP1+ macrophages were implicated in establishing an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and mediating resistance to immunotherapy in liver cancer (17). However, we observed significantly higher expression of the macrophage marker Adgre1 in the Broad region compared to the MYCN niche (Fig. 4M). These data suggest that MYCN niche cells may function as upstream modulators, secreting SPP1 to recruit or activate macrophages, rather than being populated by SPP1-expressing macrophages. Moreover, the enrichment of lipid metabolism pathways in the Broad region (Fig. 4K) was consistent with our earlier observation of macrophage accumulation in the GS-positive CV regions (Fig. 3I), where lipid metabolism is highly active. To directly evaluate whether macrophage activation influences MYCN expression in hepatocytes, we performed a conditioned medium (CM) experiment. When human hepatocyte Hc cells were treated with CM derived from lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-activated RAW264.7 macrophages, expression of both MYCN and its upstream regulator TCF7L2 was significantly increased compared with treatment with CM from unstimulated macrophages (Fig. 4N). Interestingly, when the CM from LPS-activated macrophages was applied to MYCN-overexpressing hepatocytes, these cells showed a blunted inflammatory response, with significantly lower IL-6 expression than that in WT cells (Fig. 4O). This suggests that MYCN overexpression suppresses inflammatory response, reflecting enhanced stress adaptation or immune evasion in transformed hepatocytes. Collectively, our study uncovers a regulatory circuit (termed the “MYCN loop”) in liver tumorigenesis (Fig. 4P). In this paradigm, chronic liver injury, and inflammation, such as that in lipid-rich microenvironments, induce MYCN expression, which in turn downregulates hepatocyte inflammatory responses. Repeated cycles of this MYCN loop may drive irreversible oncogenic transformation during liver tumorigenesis.


### Machine Learning-Based MYCN Niche Score in Nontumor Tissues Predicts HCC Prognosis.
  To explore the clinical translational potential of our findings, we developed a machine learning-based model that captures the transcriptional characteristics of the MYCN niche and predicts liver cancer risk and prognosis. We used spatial transcriptomic data from STAM16w and STAM20w mice, separating 189 spots from STAM clusters 8 and 10 as MYCN niche spots and the remaining 4,750 as other cluster spots. Differential expression analysis identified 3,288 DEGs (fold change > 2; adjusted P < 0.05) between these groups. Of these, 2,418 genes with one-to-one human orthologs were used as the training dataset for Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression to distinguish the MYCN niche spots (Fig. 5A and SI Appendix, Fig. S8A). Based on the minimum deviance criterion, LASSO modeling selected 40 key feature genes (SI Appendix, Table S3), achieving excellent predictive performance with an area under the curve of 0.931 (Fig. 5B). Notably, Mycn was one of the selected feature genes. Gsn (Gelsolin), Cryab (Alpha-crystallin B chain), and Col1a2 (Collagen alpha-2(I) chain) ranked as the top three most weighted genes in the LASSO model. Data mining of the human Single-cell Atlas in Liver Cancer (14) revealed that these genes were predominantly expressed in CAFs and a previously uncharacterized nonmalignant cell population (SI Appendix, Fig. S8B). In parallel, we trained a random forest (RF) model. Feature importance was ranked by mean decrease in Gini impurity, and 25 of the top 40 RF-ranked genes overlapped with the LASSO-selected genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S8C). Because of its interpretability, we used the LASSO model to compute a MYCN score as a weighted sum of the feature genes. We calculated the MYCN score for each Visium spot and observed its spatial proximity to Afp-high malignant spots (SI Appendix, Fig. S8D). To further characterize the spatial structure of MYCN niches, we quantified distance-dependent expression gradients centered on niche spots (top 5% of LASSO scores). Afp expression showed steep decay from niche cores at 20 wk, indicating increased malignant infiltration (SI Appendix, Fig. S8E). The relative spatial pattern shifted from peripheral/intermediate enrichment in early niches to core-dominated gradients in advanced niches (SI Appendix, Fig. S8F), supporting a model in which early establishment of a MYCN-driven premalignant niche that facilitates later tumor expansion.

  Next, we applied this MYCN score to TCGA-LIHC cohort and stratified the patients based on median score. High MYCN-score patients showed significantly worse overall survival in both HCC tumors (P = 0.031; Fig. 5C) and adjacent nontumor tissues (P = 0.0013; Fig. 5D). Multivariable Cox regression analysis showed that, after adjusting for gender, race, age, and pathological stage, the MYCN score in nontumor tissues remained strongly associated with improved survival (P = 0.0096; SI Appendix, Fig. S8G). Moreover, nontumors with high MYCN scores exhibited significantly elevated GSVA EMT scores (Fig. 5E). Conversely, nontumors with low MYCN scores were enriched in bile acid and xenobiotic metabolism pathways (SI Appendix, Fig. S8H), suggesting preservation of liver function. Differential expression analysis revealed that nontumors with high MYCN scores exhibited transcriptional activation (298 upregulated vs. 8 downregulated genes; SI Appendix, Fig. S8I), and LASSO genes were among the upregulated genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S8J). GO analysis indicated enrichment of immune- and stress-related pathways (Fig. 5F). To further characterize the tumor microenvironment, we used xCell (41) to deconvolute cell types in the TCGA bulk RNA-seq data. The nontumors with high MYCN scores showed increased immune and stromal cell infiltration, including a higher immune score, microenvironment score, CD4+ T cells, macrophages, and fibroblasts, and lower neutrophils and basophils (Fig. 5 G and H). Finally, we validated the MYCN score in an independent HCC cohort, GSE14520 (221 tumors vs. 210 nontumors) (42). Of 40 LASSO features, 37 genes were conserved in the dataset (SI Appendix, Fig. S9A), and a modified MYCN score was calculated. No significant differences were observed between tumor and nontumor tissues (SI Appendix, Fig. S9B). MYCN scores in nontumors were associated with clinical features of liver injury (e.g., cirrhosis and elevated serum ALT), but not with tumor-specific parameters such as tumor size or stage (SI Appendix, Fig. S9C). Consistent with the TCGA results, a higher MYCN score predicted worse overall survival in both tumors (P = 0.049; Fig. 5I) and nontumors (P = 0.036; Fig. 5J). Importantly, the MYCN score was significantly associated with recurrence risk in nontumor tissues (P = 0.0055; Fig. 5L), but not in tumors (P = 0.12; Fig. 5K). In a multivariable Cox regression model, MYCN score in nontumors tissues remained an independent predictor of recurrence-free survival (P = 0.021) and showed a protective trend for overall survival (P = 0.074; SI Appendix, Fig. S9D). Collectively, tumor MYCN scores show minimal separation while nontumor scores exhibit superior performance, underscoring its value as a precancer risk predictor but not a tumor-intrinsic biomarker. Notably, the GSE14520 dataset was derived from an Asian HBV-related cohort, whereas TCGA-LIHC predominantly includes non-Asian populations (SI Appendix, Fig. S9E), suggesting that the MYCN score may reflect a shared mechanism across different HCC etiologies and clinical backgrounds. In addition, the MYCN score showed superior performance in predicting survival compared with the previously reported TLS-50 gene signature of tertiary lymphoid structures localized at the leading edge of the nontumor region (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 F and G) (16) and bulk MYCN expression (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 H and I). Together, our findings validate the MYCN score as a proof-of-concept biomarker that integrates spatial transcriptomics and machine learning to capture early microenvironmental alterations predictive of HCC prognosis and recurrence risk.


### Comparison of MYCN with MYC Family Members.
  RNA-seq and western blot analyses showed that the gene and protein expression levels of Myc and Mycn were increased in MYCN+AKT tumors (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 A and B). Next, we examined the relationship between MYCN- and MYC-associated HCC signatures. No difference in transcriptional similarity was observed between MYCN+AKT tumors with either the MYC-high or MYC-low groups in TCGA-LIHC (SI Appendix, Fig. S10C). MYCN and MYC expression frequently display a reciprocal pattern in neuroblastoma (43). Consistently, the TARGET neuroblastoma dataset demonstrated a strong negative correlation between MYCN and MYC expression (SI Appendix, Fig. S10D). In contrast, no significant correlation was detected between the two genes in the TCGA-LIHC dataset (SI Appendix, Fig. S10E), nor in our previously reported CAGE-based transcriptomic dataset from a European HCC cohort (SI Appendix, Fig. S10F) (20). Transcriptomic analysis of human cancer cell lines from the CCLE database further confirmed a strong negative correlation in neuroblastoma cell lines (SI Appendix, Fig. S10G), but not in HCC cell lines (SI Appendix, Fig. S10H). At the protein level, we previously reported that MYCN protein expression is significantly increased in HCC tumors compared with paired nontumor tissues (SI Appendix, Fig. S10I) (21). We examined c-Myc (SI Appendix, Fig. S10J) and L-Myc (SI Appendix, Fig. S10K) protein expression in the same paired samples. MYCN protein expression did not correlate with c-Myc (SI Appendix, Fig. S10L), but showed a significant positive correlation with L-Myc protein (SI Appendix, Fig. S10M). Together, these results demonstrate that MYCN and MYC family members exhibit distinct expression patterns in HCC, suggesting different upstream transcriptional regulatory mechanisms. To elucidate these mechanisms, we performed JASPAR-based prediction of TF binding in the MYC and MYCN promoter regions. We identified 75 TFs or TF–dimer complexes predicted to selectively bind the MYCN promoter but not the MYC promoter (SI Appendix, Fig. S10N and Dataset S9). Among these, 31 TFs such as LEF1, NR2F1, and HNF4G were significantly upregulated in MYCN-high TCGA-LIHC patients (SI Appendix, Fig. S10O and Dataset S10). Finally, we evaluated the clinical relevance of MYCN and MYC expression in TCGA-LIHC tumor tissues. Median-based stratification showed that MYC expression did not predict overall survival (SI Appendix, Fig. S10P), whereas higher MYCN expression showed a trend toward worse prognosis (P = 0.088; SI Appendix, Fig. S10Q), consistent with our previous findings (21). When patients were divided into four groups (MYC-high/MYCN-high, MYC-high/MYCN-low, MYC-low/MYCN-high, MYC-low/MYCN-low), MYCN provided stronger prognostic discrimination than MYC, with the MYC-low/MYCN-high group showing the worst survival (SI Appendix, Fig. S10R).


## Discussion
  Understanding how early microenvironmental cues initiate and affect liver tumorigenesis in HCC remains a critical challenge. This difficulty arises not only from the low natural incidence of liver cancer and long latency of disease progression but also from the complex interplay of diverse cell populations within the tumor-promoting microenvironment. The updated hallmarks of cancer emphasize the importance of early intervention and prevention, noting that genetic mutations alone are insufficient to explain cancer development because identical mutations can be present in normal tissues without leading to malignancy (44, 45). This highlights the crucial role of nonmutational mechanisms, such as transcriptional reprogramming driven by the microenvironment (46), in shaping tumor initiation and progression and supports the utility of transcriptomic dynamics as early predictive biomarkers. By gain-of-function modeling and prior knowledge-guided spatiotemporal transcriptomic profiling in mouse models, our study offers insights into how transcriptional plasticity and tissue remodeling converge to form a tumor-permissive ecosystem. We identified MYCN as an oncogenic driver and revealed a dynamic MYCN-enriched niche characterized by EMT and Wnt/β-catenin signaling, which expands during tumor progression and localizes near transformed malignant cells. Importantly, our machine learning-based MYCN niche score demonstrated strong predictive value for recurrence risk, particularly in nontumor liver tissues, highlighting its translational potential for identifying precancerous niches predisposed to de novo tumorigenesis.

  MYCN shares a highly conserved sequence with its paralog MYC and can functionally replace each other in various biological contexts (47). MYC is a well-established oncogene involved in many cancer types, including HCC (48). Previous studies have shown that MYC overexpression alone, either in adeno-associated virus mediated CAG-Myc transgenic mice (27) or in doxycycline-inducible tetO-MYC transgenic mice (49), is sufficient to induce liver tumors. In contrast, in our HTVi model, MYCN overexpression alone was insufficient to drive tumor formation. This finding is supported by a previous study that showed that MYC overexpression using the same HTVi approach does not induce liver tumors, even after prolonged periods exceeding 1 y (25). In our study, MYCN expression was nearly undetectable at the experimental endpoint in the MYCN alone group, suggesting apoptosis-mediated loss of hepatocytes following high-dose MYCN overexpression. Oncogene-induced proliferative stress triggers apoptosis, senescence, and cellular elimination, acting as a cell-autonomous tumor-suppressive mechanism (50). Therefore, this model does not represent “MYCN overexpression without oncogenic transformation,” but rather reflects the elimination of hepatocytes with excessively high MYCN levels. Moreover, a recent study demonstrated that MYC overexpression under the endogenous ROSA26 promoter had limited ability to induce HCC, even with p53 depletion (29). These findings indicate that the tumorigenic potential of MYC is highly dependent on expression dose and experimental context, which is an important and often underappreciated aspect of MYC biology. In addition, TF–independent cytoplasmic functions have been reported for MYC in other contexts, including antinecroptotic activity mediated by binding to receptor-interacting protein kinase 3 (51) and regulation of cytoskeletal remodeling by truncated MYC (52). In our MYCN overexpression experiments, GFP was expressed under a separate CMV promoter and was not fused to MYCN; thus, the observed cytoplasmic GFP signal does not reflect MYCN localization. Consistent with this, nuclear–cytoplasmic fractionation of liver tumors and de novo motif analysis of MYCN CUT&RUN peaks in HC cells did not provide evidence supporting a cytoplasmic, non-TF function of MYCN in our HCC models.

  Collectively, our findings suggest MYCN as a functional driver and spatial marker of tumor-promoting microenvironments in liver tumorigenesis, and propose a clinically actionable strategy to identify high-risk patients through transcriptomic profiling of nontumor liver tissue. By integrating spatial transcriptomics, molecular validation, and machine learning, we presented a translational framework for decoding early oncogenic ecosystems and deriving predictive biomarkers. Future studies are warranted to investigate lipidomic reprogramming in the MYCN niche and its potential role in driving EMT and tumor initiation. Importantly, both the current study and our previous work (25) are based on HTVi–mediated transposon expression, a system that is accompanied by liver injury and supraphysiological transgene expression. Previous studies have shown that experiment-dependent liver injury and microenvironmental context can influence lineage commitment during liver tumorigenesis, even in the presence of identical genetic drivers (53). Therefore, further validation of the oncogenic role of MYCN in additional experimental settings, including genetically engineered mouse models, will be required. Although our LASSO-derived biomarkers hold promise as clinical tools, a key challenge remains in determining whether genes, such as GSN, CRYAB, and COL1A2, merely correlate with tumorigenesis or represent functional drivers amenable to therapeutic targeting. Previous studies have explored the role of GSN in HCC cell lines (54); however, our spatial and single-cell analyses implicated CAFs and a previously uncharacterized nonmalignant cell population as key sources of GSN expression in the tumor microenvironment. Elucidating the cellular functions associated with these biomarkers, particularly within the identified niche, is crucial for understanding the pathological mechanisms underlying HCC and advancing targeted therapeutic strategies.
