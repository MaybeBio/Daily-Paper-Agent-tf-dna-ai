# Evaluating the evolutionary relationship of TATA binding protein (TBP) with various folding patterns of protein domains using support vector machine (SVM)


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-38883-z.


## Introduction
  Eukaryotic transcription initiation system is robust, accurate and well-regulated to provide transcriptional yield1 and involves numerous protein factors2,3. The eukaryotic RNA polymerases (pol I, II and III) transcribe nuclear genes4 and require supplementary proteins (called general initiation factors) to initiate transcription from the corresponding promoters5. The TATA box binding polypeptide (TBP or TFIIDг) was initially identified as a constituent of the class II initiation factor (TFIID), but is now recognized to be requisite for transcription by all the RNA polymerases6–9. TBP thus is a universal transcription factor and resembles the essential subunits common to every RNA polymerase10. During transcription initiation and regulation by pol II, TBP associates with other polypeptides (called TBP-associated factors (TAFs))11 to assemble a multi-protein complex called TFIID12.

  Transcription factor II D (TFIID) is a ubiquitous transcription factor that regulates transcription in eukaryotes by RNA polymerase II13. TFIID mediate both activation and inhibition of transcription, can recognize core promoter sequences on DNA and assemble a huge pre-initiation complex (PIC)13. TFIID can bind to all protein gene promoters, specific activators, repressors and cofactors to regulate protein gene expression and modify chromatin in the active areas of the genome14–16. TFIID is thus a > 1 MDa sizeable assembly to integrate diverse regulatory signals into transcriptional output via TATA-binding proteins (TBP) and 13/14 diverse TAFs17–19. The complex structure formed by residues 11–77 of the N-terminal region of TAFII-230 (the TFIID 230 kDa subunit) and TBP reveal that dTAF(II)230 controls the TATA box-binding activity negatively within the TFIID complex20. In the absence of native TFIID, TBP can’t mediate activator-dependent transcription21,22, but can assemble PIC23 and mediate basal (core promoter) transcription together with other general class II factors. TBP can thus interact directly with carboxy-terminus of pol II’s large subunit of, TFIIA and TFIIB, various negative co-factors like NC1, NC2, DRl to prevent PIC formation and an initiator binding factor (TFII-1) for TATA-less promoters24,25.

  TBP was initially purified and described from yeast8 and later on complementary DNAs encoding TBP were cloned from a range of eukaryotes21,22,26–29. 180 amino acids in the C-terminal of TBP are phylogenetically conserved and contain two repeats of 66–67 amino acids, with 40% identity, flanking a highly basic segment identified as the basic repeat21. This basic repeat of TBP binds to the TATA consensus sequence (TATAa/tAa/t)30,31 with high affinity32, interacts with the minor groove33,34 and promotes DNA bending35. In different organisms, the N-terminus of TBP differ in length and have miniscule or no conservation and is not essential for transcription in certain yeast strains6,7.

  The rapid expansion of sequence and structural databases has led to a significant improvement in protein and genome sequencing data, creating a pressing need to identify the evolutionarily-related proteins, folds and domains. Proteins with related or diverse structure can exhibit the same, similar or entirely different folds and functions36. Interestingly, identical protein function can be accomplished by different folds37–39, although all such folds are evolutionarily related. Protein homologs and analogs with similar backbone structures are believed to share a common ancestor origin, which defines a protein fold family40. Studies on the evolution of functional proteins primarily focus on structural aspect and often disregard protein function41,42. Thus, domains similar in structure and sequence reveal their derivation from a core ancestral complement, highlighting the necessity to identify evolutionarily-related proteins.

  The evolution of protein folds presents intriguing possibilities, including the convergence of diverse, independently evolving protein families into the same fold43. Conversely, a single protein family with a highly conserved fold can diverge into a multiple distinct families sharing that fold44. This raises important question about what defines highly designable folds45 and whether computational methods and bioinformatics analysis can identify these sequences capable of forming such folds. Sequence search programs can routinely find related proteins with different folds and facilitate the comparison of three-dimensional (3D) structure of a protein with structurally similar neighbors from a databases46. Dali and TOP searches are online-based web-servers used for comparing protein structures (structural alignment) in 3D47,48.

  Sequence analysis tools for comparing the amino-acid sequences of dissimilar proteins to identify the similarities and differences. Programs like BLAST and PSI-BLAST are widely used for identify similar proteins, which helps to identify the evolutionary relationship between proteins49. Beyond sequence analysis, protein structures provide critical insights into function and evolutionary relationships. Computational methods, such as homology modeling and protein threading, can also be employed to predict protein structures based on known homologous structures50. These approaches collectively bridge the gap between sequence, structure, and function, advancing our knowledge of protein evolution and biology.

  Proteins are often composed of multiple functional and/or structural units called domains. Domain analysis involves identifying these domains within protein sequences and structures, providing insights into their functional and evolutionary roles. Domains can be identified using various tools and databases, such as Pfam, SMART, and InterPro, which classify proteins into families based on shared domains and motifs51.

  By comparing protein sequences and structures across various organisms, one can infer evolutionary relationships and trace the evolutionary history of protein domains and folds. Phylogenetic analysis, which constructs evolutionary trees based on sequence or structural similarities, is a widely used for this purpose52.

  Understanding the evolutionary relationships between proteins can provide insights into their functions. Functional annotation involves predicting the biological roles and activities of proteins based on their evolutionary relationships, sequence motifs, and structural features. To further our understanding of protein structure and function, the integration of structural and sequential analysis is often essential in clarifying the evolutionary links, functional characteristics, and classification of protein domains and folds. Machine learning (ML) approaches, such as support vector machine (SVM) based analysis can further enhance our understanding of the relationship between protein function and evolutionary history. Many ML methods are available for the prediction and classification of proteins. Most ML methods are capable of identifying proteins effectively. SVM, in particular, has been widely used for various biological applications. By combining these approaches, researches can get deeper insights into protein biology, enabling more accurate predictions and classification of protein function and evolution. Therefore, a method utilizing SVM was developed in this study.

  In this study, 1tba protein and its structurally related proteins were analyzed based on the machine learning approach. SVM, a widely used approach in computational biology for protein prediction and analysis53, was employed to investigate protein/s that share structurally similarities with 1tba proteins, but exhibit different functions. The structural alignment program such as DALI and TOP search was used to identify the structurally-related proteins. In this study, two methods were developed based on their sequence profiles, one using the complete sequence (whole chain) and the other using aligned sequences, both integrated with various SVM approaches. These developed methods were rigorously tested using blind datasets, sequences from DALI and TOP-search, and known function protein sequences. The results of this comprehensive analysis, along with detailed findings are described in this article, providing insights into the structural and functional relationships of 1tba and its related proteins.

  Based on this study, all data and models have been made publicly available on GitHub, an online software development platform. These resources are intended to assist researchers in identifying structurally related proteins, including TATA-box binding protein-like (1tba, B), Glutamine synthetase/guanido kinase (3ju6,B), LuxS, MPP, ThrRS/AlaRS common domain (3w3s, A), Thioesterase/thiol ester dehydrase-isomerase-like (4v12,A) and Tetrahydrobiopterin biosynthesis enzymes-like (5k95,B1). Additionally, detailed insights into the structural and sequence similarities of the TATA-binding proteins (TBP) domain across a range of protein fold types with diverse functional capabilities are provided. This repository serves as a significant tool for exploring evolutionary relationships, functional annotations, and structural classifications of these proteins, facilitating further research in this field.


## Methods

### Structure similarity search
  In this study, the 1tba, B protein chain was used as a query in both DALI and TOP search, to identify structurally similar protein chains. The search results primarily revealed protein chains with similar functional domain folds, most of which belong to the same functional group. To identify distinct folds, we manually curated the results using the Evolutionary Classification of Protein Domains (ECOD)54,55. While many proteins shared structural similarity with the 1tba protein, a few were found to have different functions, including 1tba, B1, 3ju6,B2, 3ju5-B2, 4rf6-A1, 4rf7-A1, 4rf8-B2, 4rf9-B4, 3m10-B2, 4am1-A2, 1bg0-A2, 4bg4-B2, 1m15-A2, 4gvy-A2, 1p50-A2, 1p52-A2, 3w3s, A2, 3h0r-H2, 2f2a-B2, 2df4-B2. For the final analysis, 1tba, B, 3ju6,B, 3w3s, A, 4v12-A, 5k95,B1 proteins were selected. High-scoring hits were identified and classified based on fold classification in ECOD. Different folds were collected, visually examined and superimposed with 1tba protein folds by defining equivalent regions using TopMatch tools and PyMOL visualization software.


### Sequence similarity search
  The sequence similarity for 1tba was searched using PSI-BLAST program (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) against the non-redundant database of NCBI to retrieve similar sequences. The default cut-off E-value threshold of 0.001 was used. Initially, proteins were selected from the DALI and TOP search, including 1tba, B1, 3ju6,B2, 3ju5-B2, 4rf6-A1, 4rf7-A1, 4rf8-B2, 4rf9-B4, 3m10-B2, 4am1-A2, 1bg0-A2, 4bg4-B2, 1m15-A2, 4gvy-A2, 1p50-A2, 1p52-A2, 3w3s, A2, 3h0r-H2, 2f2a-B2 and 2df4-B2. A simple BLAST search was performed against non-redundant database, aligned sequences were collected and SVM models were constructed using different amino acid profiles.

  Secondly, SVM models were developed using the PSI-BLAST for the complete sequences of 1tba, B, 3ju6,B, 5k95,B, 4v12,A and 3w3s, A. Thirdly, the aligned sequences of 1tba, B, 3ju6,B, 5k95,B, 4v12,A and 3w3s, A proteins from PSI-BLAST data were utilized to construct additional models.


### Support vector machine (SVM) for finding the protein function
  SVMs are a category of supervised machine learning algorithm commonly used for classification and regression tasks. The SVM_light package, downloaded from https://www.cs.cornell.edu/people/tj/svm_light/ was used for model construction. When working with SVMs, labeled datasets are required, where each data point is associated with a specific class or target value. SVMs can be effectively applied to various protein prediction tasks, including tasks such as protein classification, Protein Structure, Protein Function, Protein-Protein Interaction, Protein Localization, Protein Binding Site Prediction and Protein Fold Recognition, amongst others56–63. For instance, SVMs can be employed to predict the function of a protein based on its amino acid sequence or other relevant features, enabling the classification of proteins into functional categories such as enzyme, transporter, receptor etc.

  SVM software enables users to define a number of parameters as well as inbuilt kernel functions such as linear kernel, radial basis function and polynomial kernel (of a given degree). In this study, we used the polynomial kernel of a given degree. We performed a parameter optimization for the RBF kernel. The values tested were: γ from 0.1 to 500, C from 0.1 to 2000, and j from 1 to 5. The best parameters were chosen according to the highest accuracy.


### Random forest
  Random forest (RF) is a machine learning technique that can be used for making prediction based on a set of decision trees. It is a versatile and widely used approach applicable to both classification and regression tasks. In this study, RF was employed using the Waikato Environment for Knowledge Analysis (WEKA) program to construct a prediction model. We used amino acid composition (AAC), dipeptide composition (DPC), and hybrid composition (AAC + DPC) data as input for all approaches in the random forest analysis. Initially, a decision tree was constructed using training datasets, and the classes of the unknown sample were allocated using the default parameter of RF (Random Forest –P 100 –l 100 –num –slots 1 -K 0 -M 1.0 -V 0.001 -S 1) in WEKA packages. The J48 algorithm was also utilized with the default parameter J48 –C 0.25 –M 2 in order to compare the RF outcomes64.


### Datasets for SVM
  The positive set sequences were retrieved by running PSI-BLAST on the NCBI database for up to 5 iterations (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi). In the complete sequence datasets, 500 sequences were used for each protein class. In the aligned protein studies, the following sequences were used, 1tba, B-500, 3ju6,B-501, 5k95,B-500, 4v12-A-875 and 3w3s, A-500. Initially, redundancy filtering was attempted using the CD-HIT tool with a 90% sequence identity cut-off. However, this resulted in a very limited number of sequences. Consequently, all available sequences were used to construct the models. The negative dataset was generated using the SWISS-PROT database (https://www.uniprot.org/). All sequences were downloaded, and a random selection of these sequences was used to create the negative dataset, which comprises a mixture of all protein sequences. Sequence ID uniqueness was validated using the IFERROR function in Microsoft Excel, ensuring no duplicate identifiers were present in the dataset, and confirmed that no positive sequences were present in the negative dataset. The blind dataset was retrieved from Dali (http://ekhidna2.biocenter.helsinki.fi/dali/) and a TOP search (https://topsearch.services.came.sbg.ac.at/) engine, with outputs of 972 and 587 respectively.

### Hybrid approach
  A hybrid approach was developed to predict the sequence accurately. This approach combines both AAC and DPC profiles. In this approach, a vector length 420 was used, comprising 20 from AAC and 400 from DPC profiles. The col_add program of GPSR 1.0 was used to merge the AAC and DPC profiles, resulting in a hybrid profile for SVM execution.


### Evaluation and performance
  For the performance evaluation, a five-fold cross validation strategy was employed. The 1tba-positive and negative dataset were our starting points. Both positive and negative datasets were randomly divided into five equal-sized subsets. The cross-validation process was then performed iteratively five times. In each iteration, four subsets were used to train the SVM classifier and the remaining subset was used to test the classifier68,69. After each iteration, the performance of the classifier was evaluated using four common metrics, Sensitivity (True Positive Rate), Specificity (True Negative Rate), Accuracy (ACC) and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC). The performance metrics obtained from all five iterations were averaged to compute the final performance of the classifier. These metrics provide a comprehensive evaluation of the classifier’s ability to correctly classify both positive and negative instances. ## Results
  Several computational methods are available to predict the proteins, protein-protein and protein-drug interactions based on their function using machine learning approach, mainly using the SVM-based approaches57,70,71. This study also deals with the prediction of the 1tba protein and its evolutionarily related proteins. The protein domain with PDB ID 1tba was selected as a query template because it is classified under a single superfamily in the SCOP database (Structural Classification of Proteins; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/pdbe/scop/). Domains within a single superfamily often share significant structural similarity despite having divergent functions. Consequently, searches using this template primarily retrieve domains that are both structurally and functionally similar. However, these searches also rarely identify distant homologs—domains with different functions but similar structures. These structurally similar yet functionally divergent domains are particularly valuable for studying protein evolution and evolutionary relationships.

  In the analysis of 1tba protein, structurally similar proteins with diverse functions were identified. After extensive structural and SVM-based analysis, five proteins were selected for further study, which are 1tba, B, 3ju6,B, 5k95,B, 4v12,A and 3w3s, A. Two prediction methods were developed, the first method was developed with the full-length (complete) sequences of the protein to build predictive models, and the second method focused on aligned (partial) sequences, capturing the conserved regions.

  Both structural and sequence-based analyses were conducted, and the results were integrated with the SVM models to classify and predict the protein functions. The DALI and TOP-search structural alignment tools were used to identify structurally related proteins and the developed SVM models were applied to validate these findings. This comprehensive approach helped to explore the evolutionary and functional relationships among the selected proteins, providing valuable insights into their structural and functional diversity.


### Protein structural alignment analysis
  In this study, the structural similarity of the TBP-like TATA-box binding protein-like-1tba was evaluated using DALI and TOP search online tools. All structurally similar proteins were collected and categorized based on their functions using the Evolutionary Classification of Protein Domains (ECOD). Initially, 19 proteins with top scores were selected including 1tba, B1, 3ju6,B2, 3ju5-B2, 4rf6-A1, 4rf7-A1, 4rf8-B2, 4rf9-B4, 3m10-B2, 4am1-A2, 1bg0-A2, 4bg4-B2, 1m15-A2, 4gvy-A2, 1p50-A2, 1p52-A2, 3w3s, A2, 3h0r-H2, 2f2a-B2 and 2df4-B2. The analysis was conducted under the ‘one protein, one function’ framework. Although 19 proteins were initially identified, only a subset was analyzed to ensure each protein represented a unique function, preventing over-representation of any single functional category. Based on the SVM performance for one protein one function, the proteins 1tba, B, 3ju6,B, 5k95,B, 4v12,A and 3w3s, A were selected. Visual examination of various protein domain folds was performed and these folds were superimposed onto the 1tba domain fold using top-match tools and the PyMOL visualization software (Figure-1). This allowed us to define equivalent regions and analyze structural conservation. According to ECOD’s X-group classification, the protein functions are: 3ju6,B - Glutamine synthetase-like; 5k95,B - Tetrahydrobiopterin biosynthesis enzymes-like, 4v12,A - Thioesterase/thiol ester dehydrase-isomerase-like and 3w3s, A - LuxS, MPP, ThrRS/AlaRS common domain.

  Both proteins are tryptophan synthase from the bacterium Salmonella typhimurium. 3w3s is the wild-type enzyme, while 4v12 is a mutant. The key difference is at position 305 of the beta (β) subunit: 3w3s contains a negatively charged Aspartic acid (Asp, D), whereas in 4v12, this residue has been replaced via site-directed mutagenesis with Alanine (Ala, A), which has a small, neutral methyl group side chain. This detailed analysis highlights the structural and functional diversity of proteins related to the TATA-box binding proteins family, providing insights into their evolutionary relationships and functional roles.

The high ACC and MCC show good prediction performance, with the aligned sequence model achieving slightly higher accuracy for some proteins, such as 4v12. ### Performance of dipeptide composition (DPC)-SVM based modules
  In the DPC analysis, the performance of the models was evaluated, the maximum accuracy achieved (ACC) and MCC in the complete sequence profile model were 87.72%, 0.88; 91.16%, 0.92; 93.60%, 0.94; 91.08%, 0.91; 88.01%, 0.88 for 1tba, 3ju6, 3w3s, 4v12 and 5k95 respectively (Table 1). In the aligned sequence profile, the ACC and MCC were 88.86%, 0.89; 92.56%, 0.93; 93.81%, 0.92, 87.73%, 0.88 for 3ju6, 3w3s, 4v12 and 5k95 respectively (Table-1). Notably, the 1tba protein did not make the models using the aligned sequence profile. This may be attributed to its shorter sequence length and the presence of highly similar sequences in the dataset, which could have limited the model’s ability to generalize effectively (Table-1). When using SVM with shorter and highly similar sequences, the model faces difficulties in distinguishing between classes. Short sequences contain limited information, resulting in fewer features (e.g., dipeptide composition). This reduces the discriminatory gap between data points, making it challenging for the SVM to learn an effective decision boundary.

  In most protein machine learning tasks, DPC provides a more significant contribution to model performance than AAC. This is because DPC captures local sequence-order information, while AAC only provides a global, order-agnostic summary. Typically, DPC tends to show higher predictive power than AAC, because it captures local sequence order information. AAC only provides global composition, losing all information about the context and neighbors of each amino acid. An ablation analysis will often show a significant performance drop when DPC is removed, underscoring its importance.

  Overall, the DPC-based models demonstrated strong predictive performance, particularly for 3w3s and 4v12, which achieved the highest ACC and MCC values. These results highlight the utility of dipeptide composition as a feature for protein classification and functional prediction, though sequence length and diversity remain important considerations for model development.


### Performance of hybrid (AAC + DPC)-SVM based modules
  The hybrid approach, which combines AAC and DPC, generates a feature vector of length 420 (20 from AAC + 400 from DPC). This increased dimensionality provides more information for the SVM to analyze, leading to better performance than either AAC or DPC alone. Particularly for shorter sequences, the enhanced feature representation of the hybrid method significantly improves predictive accuracy compared to the simpler AAC approach.

  In the hybrid approach, the models achieved the maximum accuracy with MCC in complete sequence profile model as 89.57%, 0.89; 96.07%, 0.96; 97.64%, 0.97; 95.88%, 0.96; 93.92%, 0.94 for 1tba, 3ju6, 3w3s, 4v12 and 5k95 respectively (Table-1). In the aligned sequence profile, the ACC and MCC were 90.96%, 0.91; 96.16%, 0.96; 95.90%, 0.96; 97.05%, 0.96; 95.31%, 0.95 for 1tba, 3ju6, 3w3s, 4v12 and 5k95 respectively (Table-1). The hybrid approach outperformed than the individual AAC or DPC models, achieving higher ACC and MCC values across all proteins.

  Notably, 3w3s and 4v12 consistently performed well, with ACC values exceeding 95% in both complete and aligned sequence profiles and the results are summarized in Table-1. Both these proteins are different in function. The 3w3s, A protein is a type-2 serine tRNA ligase, and the 4v12 protein is a dehydratase from Myobacterium smegmatis. This higher prediction accuracy of the SVM model is mostly due to its ability to model complex, non-linear relationships, its strong generalization ability, and the use of sophisticated feature extraction methods like AAC, DPC, and hybrid approaches.

  The reason to choose SVM over RF is that SVMs are uniquely equipped to handle the high-dimensional, non-linear feature vectors derived from amino acid sequences. Their ability to use specialized string kernels allows them to efficiently and powerfully model functional relationships based on sequence similarity, all while maintaining strong theoretical guarantees against over-fitting. While Random Forests are excellent, interpretable classifiers, they often become the tool of choice for more tabular data related to proteins rather than for making predictions directly from the raw sequence data itself.

## Discussion
  In this study, the structural homology of the 1tba protein was investigated using the DALI and TOP search tools. During the structural search, several protein domains exhibiting structural similarity to the 1tba protein fold were identified. Interestingly, some of these protein domains shared high structural similarities with 1tba protein but belonged to different protein folds, indicating functional diversity despite structural conservation. The PSI-BLAST was performed for all proteins, including the 1tba, and similar proteins were selected for further analysis.

  In an effort to reduce redundancy, we applied CD-HIT with a 90 similarity threshold. This process yielded a substantially reduced number of sequences. In this analysis we have received the output results for aligned sequences that 2, 4, 235, 267 and 19 as 1tba, B, 3ju6,B, 3w3s, A, 4v12,A and 5k95 respectively. In the whole sequence, we received the output results as 151, 77, 220, 133 and 22 as 1tba, B, 3ju6,B, 3w3s, A, 4v12,A and 5k95 respectively. Due to the less number of output after running CD-HIT for 1tba, B, 3ju6,B, and 5k95 in the aligned sequences, we used the whole (all) sequences for all proteins. However, the developed models are often susceptible to over-fitting. To evaluate the performance of models developed using redundant datasets, they should be tested on both known (training/validation) and unknown (blind test) datasets. We applied this approach by testing our models on independent sets of known and unknown sequences. The models predicted all sequences correctly, resulting in zero false negatives and zero false positives. This indicates that the developed models are robust and accurate for predicting the target proteins.

  Initially, we employed a multi-class prediction approach (one-vs-rest). However, to identify related proteins across different functional groups, a one-vs-common-negative scheme was determined to be more appropriate for constructing the prediction model.

  SVM is widely used in the fields of basic and clinical research for clinical data analysis, clinical trials and detection of various diseases77–79. In this study, an accurate method was developed for identifying the various functional proteins, including TATA-box binding protein-like (1tba, B), Glutamine synthetase/guanido kinase (3ju6,B), LuxS, MPP, ThrRS/AlaRS common domain (3w3s, A), Thioesterase/thiol ester dehydrase-isomerase-like (4v12,A) and Tetrahydrobiopterin biosynthesis enzymes-like (5k95,B1). This was achieved using SVM_light package with different amino acid approaches. Notably, this study was not limited to the identification of complete proteins, but was extended to include functional analysis using aligned sequences.

  To determine the optimal method for sequence prediction, we compared two strategies: prediction using whole sequences and prediction using only the aligned portions of sequences (partial sequences). Our results indicate that the whole-sequence-based method outperforms the aligned-sequence-based method.

  Furthermore, within the aligned-sequence-based approach, the Amino Acid Composition (AAC) encoding method proved more effective than the Dipeptide Composition (DPC) and hybrid approaches. This is likely because the aligned sequences are significantly shorter. Due to this reduced length, the DPC and hybrid methods cannot generate their required 400- and 420-dimensional feature vectors, respectively, leading to inferior performance. The AAC method, with its fixed 20-dimensional vector, is less affected by sequence length and was therefore more robust.

  As a result, the developed complete protein and aligned protein models accurately identified their own or related functional proteins. The complete models successfully identified their respective classes of protein sequences in all approaches. For aligned sequences, the DPC and Hybrid models predominantly recognized the sequences, while some sequences of 3w3s were identified by the 1tba, 3ju6, and 4v12 models in the AAC approach. Notably, the 5k95 hybrid model predicted 103 sequences of 4v12 proteins.

  In the aligned sequence models, the DPC and Hybrid approaches successfully recognized sequences of their respective classes. However, in the AAC approach, the 1tba and 3ju6 models predicted 3w3s sequences, and the 3w3s models also predicted 1tba and 3ju6 sequences. When evaluating aligned sequence models on complete sequences, most sequences of 3ju6, 4v12 and 5k95 were recognized by their respective models across all approaches. However, the 1tba AAC model failed to recognize its own sequences, and the 3w3s models recognized less of their own sequences as 387, 38 and 81 in AAC, DPC and Hybrid approaches respectively. Additionally, the 1tba was unable to make a model in DPC approach for all provided parameters; it may be due to the short length of the sequences. The 1tba hybrid model predicted only 383 sequences out of 500.

  In addition, high-scoring aligned sequences from the DALI and TOP searches, representing proteins with diverse functions, were tested on the developed models. Both the complete and aligned 3ju6 models successfully recognized the related proteins in all approaches. The complete DALI and TOP search sequences, along with 1tba outputs result sequences, were evaluated using the developed models. All models were able to recognize these sequences (Table-2), with the 3w3s protein AAC and Hybrid models predicting a large number of sequences. Furthermore, it was demonstrated that the same sequences were predicted across all classes.

  For external validation, no independent blind dataset was available for each protein. Therefore, the known data was treated as positive and negative sequences, while the unknown sequences from DALI and TopSearch were used as the blind dataset. The model’s performance was validated using both a known (positive) dataset and blind dataset.

  The 1tba, 3ju6 complete and aligned sequence models consistently predicted the same functional proteins. In contrast, the 3w3s models predicted different functional proteins. Structural alignment was also performed with the predicted proteins, which aligned well with 1tba and 3w3s protein (Figure-5, Figure-6 a, and b).

  We also compared the performance with the other machine learning techniques such as Random Forest (RF) and J48 algorithms for classifying the 1tba related proteins. The performance of our AAC method was slightly better than both RF and J48, primarily in terms of the MCC. For all other approaches, the results were broadly similar, though RF and J48 were marginally better in terms of accuracy.

  Glutamine synthetase (GS) and the guanido kinase family share a common structural fold in their C-terminal catalytic domains, which consists of two β-α-β2-α repeats80. GS is a prime enzyme in the metabolic pathway that produces glutamine from glutamate and ATP81. There are three different classes of GS82–84. Class I enzymes (GSI) are found in prokaryotes, class II enzymes (GSII) in eukaryotes and bacteria, and class III enzymes (GSIII) in Bacteroides fragilis. GSI are oligomers of 12 identical subunits, GSII of 8 identical subunits and GSIII of six identical subunits. Although the three types of GS are evidently structurally related, their sequence similarities are not so prevalent.

  The functional importance of GS in several pathogens makes it a viable drug target85,86. Biochemical and structural studies had shown its probable target as anti-leishmanial drug87. Since only GS can synthesize glutamine de novo, its deficiency can cause embryonic lethality, as embryo cannot detoxify ammonia88. GS deficiency in humans is uncommon; however congenital human glutamine synthetase deficiency can lead to failure of multiple organs and death of a newborn89.

  A two-layer core α/β structure: α-β(2)-α-β(2) is a presumed editing domain and is found in N-terminal of threonyl-tRNA synthetase (ThrRS), C-terminal of alanyl-tRNA synthetase (AlaRS), and a hypothetical protein, PH0574 from the archaea Pyrococcus horikoshii90; a probable circular permutation of LuxS91–93. ThrRS acts as an autoantigen in autoimmune diseases like dermatomyositis and polymyositis94, regulates MUC1 biosynthesis to promote pancreatic cancer cell migration95 and correlates with angiogenesis in epithelial ovarian cancer96. AlaRS is vital for protein synthesis and its defective activity can lead to neurodegeneration97. AlaRS also catalyzes protein lactylation and promotes gastric cancer cell proliferation98.

  The HotDog superfamily embodies a domain called the ‘hot dog’ fold and is present in thioesterases, thiol ester dehydratase-isomerases and Escherichia coli beta-hydroxydecanoyl thiol ester dehydrase (FabA)99. This fold has a ‘bun’ of seven-stranded antiparallel β-sheets, wrapping a five-turn α-helical ‘sausage’. Numerous prokaryotic, archaeal and eukaryotic proteins contain this domain and have related yet dissimilar catalytic roles in fatty acid metabolism and, degradation of phenylacetic acid and an environmental pollutant, 4-chlorobenzoate. FapR also has this fold and thus is a non-catalytic bacterial homologue, regulating fatty acid biosynthesis100. More than sixty proteins have the ‘hot dog’ fold domain and this fold has a robust association with transcriptional regulation of fatty acid biosynthesis and metabolism101. Proteins with this fold are chiefly coenzyme A-binding enzymes with diverse substrates positioned at their active sites and thus are central to pathogenesis of numerous human diseases102.

  Tetrahydrobiopterin (BH(4)) is an essential cofactor present in possibly every cell of higher organisms as a general neuroprotectant factor103. Restricted BH(4) availability can cause neurological diseases, like Dopa-responsive dystonia, Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s disease, autism and depression103. Thus BH4 metabolism is a promising metabolic and pharmacological target for neurodegenerative diseases, chronic metabolic syndromes and primary mitochondriopathies104.

  Thus, our findings provide understanding of clinically and functionally important proteins through evolutionary viewpoint and this will advance the mechanistic understanding of these proteins in various human diseases. Identification of novel related folds and domains in these proteins will help identify novel drugs and combat drug resistance encountered during current molecular therapeutics.


## Conclusion
  The study of evolutionarily related protein domains, their folds, architecture, and classification through structural and sequential analysis is a crucial aspect of bioinformatics and molecular biology. Our study aimed to develop a method for identifying the 1tba TATA-box binding protein-like fold and its evolutionary related protein folds. The performance of the AAC, DPC, and Hybrid models was evaluated on both known and unknown datasets. All models correctly predicted their respective class datasets, a result further supported by the data in the accompanying confusion matrix tables. This process represents a cross-validation technique, which is a standard method for validating model performance by testing its predictive accuracy on both known and unknown (blind-dataset) data. Our results suggest that the Glutamine synthetase/guanido kinase (3ju6,B) and LuxS, MPP, ThrRS/AlaRS common domain (3w3s, A) are closely related to 1tba protein fold. Additionally, the ThrRS/AlaRS common domain (3w3s, A) was closely related to 3Q6A_1-TBP-like, 3MGJ_1, 1W2I_1-Alpha-beta plaits, as identified by the complete sequence models. Furthermore, the 2EJX_1, 3W9K_1, 5FMF_21-TBP-like protein folds were also closely related to the 3w3s protein folds and were identified by all the aligned sequence models. The findings imply that the proteins may have diverged from a common ancestor, retaining similar structural motifs despite potential functional differences.

  These findings provide valuable insights into the structural and sequence relationships among different protein folds. Understanding these relationships is crucial for unraveling the evolutionary connections and functional implications of these proteins, especially in various human diseases. This type of insilico analysis contributes to the broader field of bioinformatics and molecular biology by providing a robust computational framework for studying protein evolution and function.
