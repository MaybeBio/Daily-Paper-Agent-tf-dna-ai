# Identification and verification of hub genes related to mitochondrial dysfunction in epilepsy based on bioinformatics analysis


## Background
  Epilepsy is a neurological disorder characterized by recurrent seizures, and understanding its underlying molecular mechanisms remains a significant challenge. The objective of this study was to investigate the role of mitochondrial energy metabolism-related differentially expressed genes (MRDEGs) in epilepsy and explore diagnostic models based on these genes.


## Methods
  Datasets were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. A differential expression analysis was conducted to identify MRDEGs. Diagnostic models were developed using logistic regression, support vector machine (SVM), and random forest (RF) algorithms. LASSO regression was employed to mitigate overfitting. The diagnostic value of the models was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed on the hub genes. Protein‒protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed and visualized using Cytoscape software. Additionally, mRNA‒miRNA and mRNA‒transcription factor (TF) interaction networks were constructed.


## Results
  In the GSE143272 dataset, logistic regression analysis revealed 26 statistically significant MRDEGs. The SVM model achieved the highest accuracy, with 22 MRDEGs. The RF algorithm identified 11 important MRDEGs for which IncNodePurity > 0.80. LASSO regression yielded a diagnostic model comprising five hub genes: ACAA1, ALDH3B1, DLST, GCDH, and NDUFB9. The ROC curves showed high accuracy for DLST (AUC > 0.9). GO and KEGG analyses revealed significant enrichment in processes such as mitochondrial ATP synthesis coupled with electron transport. PPI networks illustrated the interactions between hub genes.


## Conclusions
  In conclusion, we elucidated the critical role of MRDEGs in the pathogenesis of epilepsy and developed a robust diagnostic model with potential clinical applications.


## Introduction
  Approximately 60 million individuals have epilepsy globally, and more than 4.9 million new cases of epilepsy are reported each year [1]. Although the impact of the disease decreased from 1990 to 2016, epilepsy remains a major cause of disability and death [2]. Despite advancements in medical treatments, approximately 30% of individuals with epilepsy are still resistant to antiepileptic drugs, which imposes a substantial burden on the healthcare system and significantly affects quality of life [3]. Currently, the diagnosis of epilepsy primarily depends on clinical assessments and electroencephalograms. Although these methods are valuable, they often fail to provide a comprehensive understanding of the underlying molecular mechanisms of the disease [4]. Given that numerous underlying disease mechanisms contribute to the development of epilepsy, the aetiology of the disease remains elusive in most patients [5]. Thus, the exploration of the pathogenesis, diagnostic biomarkers, and therapeutic targets of epilepsy holds immense significance for assessing and ameliorating the condition of patients.

  Genetic factors play a pivotal role in the aetiology of epilepsy. Recent studies have identified a multitude of epilepsy-associated genes and genetic variants that contribute to the intricate nature of the disease [6, 7]. For example, compared with other types of epilepsy, genetic generalized epilepsy has been shown to carry a greater burden of copy number variations [8]. These genetic insights have laid the foundation for precision medicine strategies that seek to customize treatments according to individual genetic profiles [9]. Mitochondrion-related genes are associated with multiple neurodegenerative disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease, indicating their potential involvement in epilepsy as well [10–12]. This association is further corroborated by the finding that specific instances of mitochondrial dysfunction (MD) are frequently accompanied by epileptic seizures, underscoring the importance of mitochondria in preserving neuronal stability [13]. Therefore, the crucial genes involved in mitochondrial energy metabolism are likely to impact the pathogenesis of epilepsy and serve as suitable therapeutic targets. Nonetheless, the specific mitochondrial energy metabolism-related differentially expressed genes (MRDEGs) associated with epilepsy and their diagnostic utility remain unknown. Given our limited understanding of the underlying mechanisms of epilepsy, novel and dependable screening approaches must be developed to improve diagnostic precision and therapeutic efficacy, ultimately contributing to the improvement of patient outcomes.

  In this study, we utilized datasets obtained from three Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) repositories. Through a series of bioinformatics analyses, we identified significant MRDEGs with the potential to serve as biomarkers for the diagnosis of epilepsy. Furthermore, functional enrichment analyses provided insights into the biological processes and pathways associated with these genes. The construction of protein‒protein interaction (PPI) networks and the assessment of immune cell infiltration highlighted the complex interplay between MRDEGs and immune cells, suggesting potential therapeutic targets for further exploration.


## Materials and methods

### Data acquisition and processing
  The flowchart of the methodology used in the present investigation is depicted in Fig. 1. A total of three epilepsy gene expression microarray datasets (GSE143272 [14] https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE143272, GSE4290 [15] https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE4290, and GSE32534 [16] https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE32534) were retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information Gene Expression Omnibus (NCBI GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The samples in GSE143272, GSE4290, and GSE32534 were obtained from Homo sapiens, with blood, brain, and the neocortex as tissue sources, respectively. The grouping information of “Epilepsy” and “Control” samples in the three datasets GSE143272, GSE4290, and GSE32534 are strictly defined in accordance with the official annotations of the GEO database to ensure the consistency of the grouping standards. A total of 57 samples from patients with different responses to single antiepileptic drug treatment were excluded from the GSE143272 dataset to focus on the population that did not receive monotherapy. In addition, 34 samples from epilepsy patients who did not receive monotherapy and 51 corresponding control samples were included. A total of 157 tumour samples were excluded from the GSE4290 dataset, and 23 brain tissue samples from patients with nontumor-related epilepsy patients were selected. The GSE32534 dataset contains 5 samples from epilepsy patients and 5 corresponding control samples, as annotated in the GEO dataset. Samples with missing clinical annotations or poor data quality (as reported by submitters) were excluded. No label recoding or changes were made during the data preprocessing process, ensuring the consistency and comparability of the labels between the various datasets. Only the blood samples in GSE143272 were used to construct the model, while the brain tissue (GSE4290) and neocortex (GSE32534) datasets were used for external validation. Direct integration analysis of the datasets was not conducted to avoid systematic bias caused by differences in tissue sources. Instead, models were built using a single source and the robustness of the models was verified across sources. The preprocessing process of the GEO dataset GSE143272 was performed as follows: all samples or genes with missing values were completely removed. For genes represented by multiple probes, the average expression level of all corresponding probes was calculated to obtain a single value for each gene. The expression data were then normalized using the normalizeBetweenArrays function in the R package “limma 3.64.1”, with quantile normalization adopted by default to ensure comparability across samples and reduce technical variation [17]. No additional filtering was performed for genes with low expression or low variance in this study. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified based on the thresholds of |logFC| > 0 and p.adj < 0.05. Data splitting of the training and internal test subsets was not performed; the model was developed using GSE143272 and passed GSE4290 and the external validation GSE32534. Comparisons of the expression matrices prior to and following normalization were conducted by categorizing samples into control and epilepsy groups, and principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to evaluate the normalization efficacy [18]. We searched the PubMed website (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) using “mitochondrial energy metabolism” as a keyword to identify published studies related to mitochondrial energy metabolism-related gene (MRG) sets [19]. A total of 188 MRGs were identified, and detailed information is provided in Table 1.

### MRDEGs related to epilepsy
  The R package “limma” was used to analyse the differences in gene expression between the control and epilepsy cohorts [20, 21]. A threshold of |logFC| > 0 and q value (p value correction for Benjamini–Hochberg) < 0.05 was established to select the differentially expressed genes (DEGs). MRDEGs were obtained by performing a variance analysis of the GSE143272 dataset. Intersectional MRGs were identified in the GSE4290 and GSE32534 datasets, leading to the mapping of MRDEGs. A plot comparing expression between groups was generated based on the expression levels of the MRDEGs to further examine the changes in their expression within the GSE14327 dataset. Volcano plots and heatmaps of the DEGs were created using the R packages “ggplot2 3.5.2” and “pheatmap 1.0.13”, respectively.


### Gene set enrichment analysis
  The genes in the GSE143272 dataset were ranked based on their logFC values. The R package “clusterProfiler 4.16.0” was subsequently used to conduct gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) on all genes within the GSE143272 dataset. The gene sets “c2.cp.kegg.v6.2.symbols” and “c2.all.v7.2.symbols” were retrieved from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) for GSEA [22]. The criteria for GSEA screening were p.adj < 0.05 and an FDR value (q value) < 0.05, and the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) method was used to correct the p values.


### Gene set variation analysis
  All genes in the GSE143272 dataset were subjected to gene set variation analysis (GSVA) 2.2.0, which is designed to quantify differences in enriched functions across different groups. The significance threshold for GSVA was determined as p.adj < 0.05, and the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) was used to correct the p values.


### Construction and validation of the diagnostic model for epilepsy
  The dependent variable of the model was the binary variable of “Epilepsy” and “Control”. The outcome variable was defined as a binary classification label (Epilepsy = 1, Control = 0), and the label definition was consistent in the training set (GSE143272) and all the external validation sets (GSE4290 and GSE32534). A logistic regression analysis was conducted to formulate a diagnostic model for epilepsy utilizing MRDEGs from the GSE143272 dataset. MRDEGs with a p value < 0.05 were used as the criterion for screening, and a logistic regression model was subsequently constructed.

  Afterwards, the SVM algorithm was employed to construct the SVM model [23]. The parameters were set as follows: k = 5 (the number of features removed in each iteration), half.above = 100 (half the features were removed each time when the number of features exceeded 100), and 10-fold cross-validation. By systematically evaluating the classification error rate for different feature subset sizes, the MRDEGs were further filtered based on the number of genes that achieved the highest accuracy and the lowest error rate. The expression levels of the MRDEGs in the expression matrix of the GSE143272 dataset were utilized to construct the model using the “RF 4.7–1.2” package with the parameters set. The random seed was set to 1000, an integrated model containing 1000 decision trees was constructed, and the feature importance was evaluated through the increase in node purity (IncNodePurity).   The R package rms 8.0–0 was used to construct a nomogram (nomogram) based on the results of LASSO regression analysis to determine the relationships among the DEGs included in the MRDEG diagnostic model [26]. Additionally, the R package “Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) 1.1” was used to create DCA plots based on the hub genes in the GSE143272 dataset, with the purpose of assessing the accuracy and discriminative ability of the diagnostic model [27].

  We utilized the “pROC” package in R to generate receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for the hub genes of the MRDEG diagnostic model in the GSE143272 dataset [28]. The area under the curve (AUC) was subsequently calculated to assess the diagnostic potential of hub gene expression in patients with epilepsy. All the AUC values were calculated based on the predicted probabilities of this binary classification model. Moreover, the risk coefficients obtained from the LASSO regression analysis were used to compute the corresponding risk scores in the GSE4290 and GSE32534 datasets. The “pROC” package was then used to plot ROC curves for the hub genes within these datasets, and the AUC was calculated to validate the diagnostic value of hub gene expression in patients with epilepsy.


### Gene ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of genes and genomes enrichment analyses
  The R package “clusterProfiler” was used to conduct Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses of the hub genes [29]. The screening criteria for statistically significant results were set as p.adj < 0.05 and an FDR value (q value) < 0.05, and the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) method was used to correct the p values.


### Protein‒protein interaction network
  The STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) was used to analyse the hub genes, with a minimum required interaction score greater than 0.400 (medium confidence) serving as the criterion for constructing the hub gene-related protein‒protein interaction (PPI) network [30]. Cytoscape software (version 3.9.1) was used to visualize the network [31].


### Construction of mRNA‒miRNA and mRNA‒TF interaction networks
  The microRNAs (miRNAs) related to the hub genes were retrieved from the miRDB database (https://mirdb.org/) to explore the interactions between hub genes and miRNAs [32]. The mRNA‒miRNA regulatory network was visualized using Cytoscape software [31]. The transcription factors (TFs) were retrieved from the ChIPBase database (http://rna.sysu.edu.cn/chipbase/) to analyse their regulatory effects on the hub genes [33]. Cytoscape software was used to visualize the mRNA–TF regulatory network.


### Analysis of immune cell infiltration
  The first step involved annotating each type of infiltrating immune cell. Next, the enrichment scores generated by single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) were used to determine the relative abundance of infiltrating immune cells in each sample, thereby establishing an immune cell infiltration matrix for the GSE143272 dataset [34]. After retaining the results with a p value < 0.05, the R package “ggplot2” was employed to generate plots comparing the groups to illustrate the differences in the infiltration of immune cells between the control and epilepsy groups in the GSE143272 dataset. Additionally, another R package was used to create a correlation heatmap that displayed the results of the correlation analysis among the immune cells themselves.

  The CIBERSORT algorithm, in conjunction with the LM22 signature gene matrix, was employed to filter the data with immune cell enrichment scores greater than zero [35]. Then, the specific results for the immune cell infiltration matrix for the GSE143272 dataset were obtained, and a proportion bar chart was generated for visualization. The R package was used to create a heatmap that presented the results of the correlation analysis among the immune cells themselves. After retaining the results with a p value < 0.05, the “ggplot2” package in R was used to construct a bubble plot of the correlations, which represented the outcomes of the correlation analysis between the hub genes and immune cells.


### Statistical analysis
  All data processing and analyses in this study were conducted using R software (version 4.2.2). The statistical significance of differences in normally distributed continuous variables between two groups was evaluated using an independent Student’s t test, unless indicated otherwise. The Mann‒Whitney U test (Wilcoxon rank sum test) was employed to analyse the differences between variables that did not follow a normal distribution. Spearman’s correlation analysis was performed to calculate the correlation coefficients between various molecules. Unless noted otherwise, a p value threshold of less than 0.05 was established for statistical significance, and all p values were considered two-tailed. Except for the analyses involving multiple tests, the original P values were used for the remaining single comparisons (such as t tests between two groups and correlation analyses).

### Construction and validation of the diagnostic model for epilepsy
  We conducted a logistic regression analysis utilizing these 26 most relevant MRDEGs. A logistic regression model was constructed, and the outcomes were visually presented in a forest plot (Fig. 5A). All 26 MRDEGs were significantly different according to the logistic regression model (p < 0.05). We developed an SVM model based on the 26 MRDEGs using the SVM algorithm. We determined the number of genes associated with the lowest error rate (Fig. 5B) and the highest accuracy rate (Fig. 5C).

  The SVM model achieved the highest accuracy when 22 genes were included. These 22 MRDEGs were ALDH18A1, MDH2, ACAA1, ALDH3B1, COX6C, NDUFB9, IDH1, PAAF1, PGM2, NDUFS3, OXCT1, PDHB, UQCRFS1, GCDH, COX10, NDUFA13, DLST, HADHB, NDUFB7, HADH, PPA2, and NDUFS4. Next, we analysed the expression levels of the 26 MRDEGs in the GSE143272 dataset using the RF algorithm (Fig. 5D). IncNodePurity represents the increase in node purity. A higher node purity signifies fewer impurities (i.e., a smaller Gini coefficient). We employed IncNodePurity > 0.80 as the criterion to filter the results of the specific analysis. Eleven MRDEGs, namely, DLST, ACAA1, NDUFV1, NDUFB9, NDUFA13, ALDH3B1, NDUFB7, ECHS1, OXCT1, GCDH, and IDH1, were identified (Fig. 5E).

  We performed an intersection analysis between the genes with p < 0.05 from the logistic regression analysis and the MRDEGs identified using the SVM and RF models. This analysis yielded a total of 9 intersecting genes, for which a Venn diagram was constructed (Fig. 6A). The 9 intersecting genes were ACAA1, ALDH3B1, DLST, GCDH, IDH1, NDUFA13, NDUFB7, NDUFB9 and OXCT1. Next, we generated diagrams of the LASSO regression model and LASSO variable trajectory for visualization (Fig. 6B–C). The results indicated that five MRDEGs incorporated into the LASSO regression model, namely, ACAA1, ALDH3B1, DLST, GCDH and NDUFB9, were identified as hub genes. We developed a nomogram based on the five hub genes within the diagnostic model to further validate the diagnostic value of the MRDEG model, illustrating the interrelationships of these hub genes in the GSE143272 dataset (Fig. 6D). The findings revealed that the utility of the expression of the hub gene NDUFB9 for the MRDEG diagnostic model was significantly greater than that of the other variables, whereas the utility of the ACAA1 expression level was significantly lower. The performance of the model in predicting the actual outcomes was assessed by comparing the fitting of the optimal theoretical probability (solid line) and the model-predicted probability (dashed line) under the various scenarios depicted in Fig. 6E. Within a certain range, the line of the model consistently remained above both the epilepsy-positive line and the epilepsy-negative line, as shown in Fig. 6F, indicating greater net benefit and superior model performance.

  Furthermore, the R package pROC 1.18.5 was employed to generate ROC curves based on the hub genes in the GSE143272 dataset (Fig. 7A–B). The ROC curve analysis revealed that the expression level of the hub gene DLST in the GSE143272 dataset was highly accurate (AUC > 0.9) in distinguishing between the high-risk and low-risk groups within the MRDEG diagnostic model. ## Discussion
  Despite the availability of numerous pharmacological interventions for epilepsy, a significant proportion of individuals still suffer from persistent seizures, underscoring the urgent need for improved therapeutic strategies [36]. In the present study, we found that key MRDEGs in epilepsy, such as DLST, ACAA1, ALDH3B1, GCDH, and NDUFB9, are significantly involved in pathways associated with mitochondrial ATP synthesis and that their altered expression patterns are closely related to the pathogenesis of epilepsy. Abnormalities in mitochondrial ATP synthesis and electron transport pathways can lead to a limited energy supply and impaired oxidative phosphorylation in neurons. An insufficient energy supply weakens the function of Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, causing membrane depolarization and abnormal discharge. Dysfunction of complex I (such as downregulation of NDUFB9) leads to ROS accumulation and Ca²⁺ overload, further increasing synaptic excitability and neural network synchronization. These results provide valuable insights into mitochondrial gene-based therapeutic approaches for epilepsy [37, 38]. Several primary mitochondrial diseases can manifest with epilepsy, including mitochondrial encephalopathy, lactic acidosis, myoclonic epilepsy with ragged red fibres, and Leigh syndrome (LS) [39, 40]. Wolfgang et al. demonstrated that MD represents a pivotal mechanism underlying epileptic seizures and contributes to their occurrence [41]. Metabolism-based treatments, such as high-fat ketogenic diets, have the potential to restore metabolic homeostasis and achieve seizure control [42]. In this study, we focused on identifying MRDEGs in epilepsy patients and constructing a diagnostic model to improve disease management. This approach shows promise by identifying novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets for early diagnosis, thereby enhancing the clinical management of patients with epilepsy and alleviating their disease burden.

  Our study revealed that key MRDEGs in epilepsy, including DLST, ACAA1, ALDH3B1, GCDH, and NDUFB9, are significantly involved in pathways associated with mitochondrial ATP synthesis. DLST serves as a crucial component of the alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complex, which plays a vital role in the tricarboxylic acid cycle and ATP production [43, 44]. The reduced expression of DLST in patients with epilepsy suggests that MD contributes to the progression of the disease. ACAA1 encodes human peroxisomal 3-oxoacyl-CoA thiolase, an enzyme involved in the beta-oxidation pathway of fatty acids, a critical process for energy production in cells [45]. ALDH3B1 encodes a protein involved in the detoxification of aldehydes, which can impair mitochondrial function [46, 47]. Its role in epilepsy was underscored in a bioinformatics analysis that revealed key genes associated with the disease [48], which is consistent with our findings. GCDH participates in the catabolism of lysine, hydroxylysine, and tryptophan, linking amino acid metabolism to mitochondrial energy production [49]. Posset et al. reported that deficiencies in GCDH are known to cause glutaric aciduria type I, a metabolic disorder that leads to neurological symptoms, including seizures [50]. NDUFB9 is a component of Complex I in the electron transport chain and is essential for initiating electron transfer and proton pumping [51]. Mutations or altered expression of NDUFB9 can impair mitochondrial function, leading to reduced ATP availability and increased oxidative stress, both of which are implicated in epilepsy. Our study shows that NDUFB9 expression is decreased in samples from patients with epilepsy, potentially because it reduces ATP availability and thereby promotes epilepsy progression. DLST and ACAA1 are the first MRDEGs identified in epilepsy patients in our study. These genes may serve as potential biomarkers for epilepsy diagnosis and targets for therapeutic intervention.

  Based on the results of the GO and KEGG enrichment analyses, we identified several pathways in which MRDEGs were significantly enriched in patients with epilepsy. These pathways involve mitochondrial ATP synthesis-coupled electron transport, electron transfer activity, and small-molecule metabolic processes. These processes are essential for maintaining cellular energy homeostasis, and their dysregulation has been associated with a variety of neurological disorders, including epilepsy [52]. Verweij et al. observed that MD in both animal and human brain tissues is affected by epilepsy, which is characterized by impaired oxidative phosphorylation and ATP production [53]. Furthermore, the role of small-molecule metabolic processes, such as the regulation of polyamines, has been extensively explored in the context of epilepsy. Polyamines are small, positively charged alkylamines that modulate ion channels and ionotropic glutamate receptors, and their dysregulation has been closely linked to the pathogenesis of epilepsy [54]. These pathways are critical for sustaining neuronal function and integrity, especially under the metabolic stress conditions frequently observed in patients with epilepsy [55]. The high diagnostic accuracy of the model based on these hub genes, including ACAA1, ALDH3B1, DLST, GCDH and NDUFB9, is evidenced by the ROC curve analysis, highlighting their potential as diagnostic biomarkers for epilepsy.

  Consistent with the findings of Tsuchiya et al. [56], we observed alterations in immune cell infiltration, suggesting a link between MD and the immune response in epilepsy patients, which warrants further investigation. The characteristics of infiltrating immune cells in patients with epilepsy are significantly correlated with mitochondrial gene expression. The results of our analysis revealed that the expression of mitochondrial energy metabolism-related genes (DLST, GCDH, and NDUFB9) tended to decrease in the epilepsy group and was significantly negatively correlated with the levels of infiltrating immune cells such as activated CD8 + T cells, macrophages, and neutrophils. The upregulation of ACAA1 and ALDH3B1 expression is positively correlated with the infiltration of various immune cells. For instance, a recent study utilizing Mendelian randomization confirmed a causal relationship between certain immune cell characteristics and the epilepsy risk, reinforcing the notion that immune cells contribute to the onset and progression of the disease [57]. Luo et al. also reported changes in the expression of immune-related genes and the presence of inflammatory markers in patients with epilepsy [58]. Furthermore, significant differences in immune cell phenotypes, such as activated CD8 + T cells and activated dendritic cells, were detected between the two groups, suggesting their potential roles in disease mechanisms [59]. After being primed in brain-draining lymph nodes, CD8 + T cells specifically attack neurons, leading to the apoptotic degeneration of neurons and compromising hippocampal network function, which results in fundamental hyperexcitability and impaired memory skills, thereby inducing an epileptogenic process [60]. Additionally, Tröscher et al. discovered that the number of T cells, particularly CD8 + cytotoxic T cells, was significantly elevated in the hippocampus of patients with medial temporal lobe epilepsy [61]. However, the role of dendritic cells in epilepsy remains poorly understood. One might hypothesize that epilepsy is related to the high excitability of dendrites, but it is still unclear whether the changes in dendritic cells associated with epilepsy are a consequence of repeated seizures. We anticipate that advancements in the technology for assessing dendritic excitability will facilitate a better understanding of epilepsy. The involvement of the immune system in epilepsy is further corroborated by findings that immune-related pathways, including the JAK–STAT pathway, are involved [62, 63].

  Increasing evidence indicates that neuroinflammation and metabolism play crucial roles in the pathophysiology of epilepsy. Metabolic disorders and neuroinflammation intensify each other, creating a vicious cycle [64–67]. Our results revealed a significant correlation between the infiltration levels of different immune cells and the expression of genes related to mitochondrial energy metabolism, and significant negative correlations were generally observed between hub genes (NDUFB9, GCDH, and DLST) and immune cells (p < 0.05). Significant positive correlations were observed between hub genes (ALDH3B1 and ACAA1) and immune cells (p < 0.05), suggesting that mitochondrial dysfunction may regulate immune activation through metabolic signals and that the enhanced immune response can in turn affect energy metabolism, thereby leading to an imbalance in immune–metabolic coupling. This process may play important roles in the inflammatory response and neuronal overexcitation in epilepsy.

  Additionally, research has demonstrated that immune cells, such as mast cells and natural killer cells, are differentially regulated in patients with epilepsy, with notable correlations observed between these cell types and specific genes, such as epidermal growth factor receptors [68]. These findings underscore the complexity of immune system involvement in epilepsy, in which different immune cell subsets play distinct roles at various disease stages. For example, the expression signatures of Th1 and Th2 cells differ between normal and epileptic tissues, suggesting a shift in immune response profiles in patients with epilepsy [69]. Overall, our findings regarding differences in immune cells in patients with epilepsy reinforce the potential of targeting immune-related pathways and cells for the development of novel therapeutic strategies for epilepsy.


## Limitations
  Despite these encouraging findings, this study has several limitations. First, this study relies primarily on bioinformatics analysis without incorporating laboratory experiments, and the results still need experimental and clinical verification to support subsequent research. Second, the sample sizes derived from the datasets (GSE143272, GSE4290, and GSE32534) are relatively limited, which limits the generalizability of the results. Third, the absence of clinical validation implies that the diagnostic models and identified biomarkers have not yet been evaluated in a real-world clinical context. Furthermore, the utilization of multiple datasets introduces the potential for batch effects, which could influence the results, despite our normalization efforts.


## Conclusions
  In conclusion, we successfully identified 26 MRGs in patients with epilepsy from GEO datasets and constructed diagnostic models. The integration of various bioinformatics tools provided comprehensive insights into the underlying genetic mechanisms of epilepsy. These findings highlight potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for epilepsy, which are beneficial for future research. However, further validation through laboratory experiments and clinical studies is essential to confirm these results and fully realize their potential in clinical applications.
