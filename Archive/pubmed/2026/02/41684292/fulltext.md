# Amentoflavone, a Preferentially TGF‐β Receptor 1 Inhibitor, Alleviates Cardiac Remodeling and Dysfunction Through Modulating the TGF‐β/Smad and MAPK Signaling Pathways


## ABSTRACT
  Chronic cardiac stress induces cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis, thereby promoting pathological heart remodeling, which is the core pathological process of heart failure. Amentoflavone (AMF) has beneficial therapeutic effects against several cardiovascular diseases; however, its role and underlying mechanisms in pathological cardiac remodeling remain unclear. Here, we investigated the protective role of AMF against pathological cardiac remodeling in transverse aortic constriction (TAC)‐induced overloaded hearts. Mice with pressure overload caused by TAC were treated with AMF, and the effects of AMF on cardiac function, cardiac hypertrophy, and fibrosis were analyzed. RNA‐seq, molecular docking, surface plasmon resonance (SPR), and co‐IP were used to identify the downstream targets of AMF. Angiotensin II (Ang II) and transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGF‐β1) were used to characterize the role of AMF and its downstream targets in cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis in vitro. Sustained AMF administration significantly suppressed TAC‐induced cardiac remodeling. Compared with those in TAC model mice, the heart/body weight ratio, cardiomyocyte cross‐sectional area, and hypertrophic molecular markers were significantly lower in AMF‐treated TAC model mice. Moreover, TAC‐induced myofibroblast generation and extracellular matrix deposition were effectively reversed following AMF administration. Similar results were obtained after AMF treatment for cardiomyocyte hypertrophy and cardiac fibrosis in vitro. Mechanistically, by directly binding to TGF‐β receptor 1, AMF blocked the regulatory effect of TGF‐β1 and inhibited the TGF‐β/Smad and MAPK signaling pathways. These results suggest that AMF, through binding to TGF‐β receptor 1, may be a promising therapeutic option for pathological cardiac remodeling.


## Introduction
  Cardiac remodeling, a response of the myocardium to chronic cardiac stress conditions, is characterized by cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis and ultimately causes heart failure (Cohn et al. 2000). Despite the availability of multiple pharmaceutical approaches, such as classic drugs, including β‐blockers, angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs), and angiotensin receptor‐neprilysin inhibitors (ARNIs), which are used to suppress pathological ventricular remodeling, the complex mechanisms and pathological processes involved in irreversible maladaptation during remodeling and heart failure remain among the leading causes of death worldwide (Baman and Ahmad 2020). These findings indicate an urgent need for novel therapies. Many active ingredients in traditional Chinese medicines that exert cardiovascular protective effects, such as ginsenosides Rd (Wan et al. 2023) and celastrol (Ye et al. 2020), have been shown to reduce cardiac remodeling and ameliorate heart failure caused by transverse aortic constriction (TAC) or angiotensin II (Ang II) perfusion in mice. However, the low bioavailability and narrow therapeutic window make the clinical translation of these active ingredients challenging.

  Amentoflavone (AMF), a natural biflavonoid that has attracted attention due to its abundant content in  Ginkgo biloba  biflavonoid preparations (Šamec et al. 2022), possesses beneficial biological activities, such as anti‐inflammatory, antioxidant, hypoglycemic, and vasodilating effects (Xiong et al. 2021). Notably, recent studies have revealed that AMF exhibits excellent cardioprotective potential by attenuating cardiomyocyte apoptosis, pyrodeath, and oxidative stress in mouse models of cardiac ischemia–reperfusion injury (Li et al. 2021) and doxorubicin‐induced acute cardiotoxicity (Alherz et al. 2022; Fang et al. 2023). However, the function and underlying mechanisms of action of AMF in cardiac remodeling in vitro and in vivo have not been identified.

  Numerous studies indicate that transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGF‐β1) plays a pivotal role in the development of cardiac hypertrophy, fibrosis, and heart failure (Heger et al. 2016). Transgenic mice overexpressing TGF‐β1 consistently exhibit cardiac hypertrophy characterized by the hypertrophic growth of cardiomyocytes and interstitial fibrosis, and the targeted deletion of TGF‐β1 or blockade of TGF‐β1 with neutralizing antibodies significantly alleviates pathological cardiac remodeling and improves cardiac function (Rosenkranz et al. 2002). However, excessive TGF‐β signaling inhibition increases inflammation and leads to increased mortality in patients (Engebretsen et al. 2014). More specific targeted drugs that inhibit harmful TGF‐β pathways have important implications for the prevention and treatment of heart failure.

  In this study, we investigated the effects of AMF on TAC‐induced cardiac remodeling. The effects of AMF on cardiomyocyte hypertrophy and cardiac fibrosis in vitro were also examined in primary cardiomyocytes and cardiac fibroblasts. The results of our study verified the protective role of AMF against cardiac remodeling in the TAC model. Mechanistically, AMF directly acted on TGF‐β receptor 1 (TβR1), inhibited TGF‐β1‐induced myocardial damage, and alleviated cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis in vivo and in vitro, indicating that the “AMF‐TβR1” pathway is a promising target for the treatment of cardiac remodeling and heart failure.


## Materials and Methods

### Animal Model
  Eight‐week‐old C57BL/6J mice (female and male, 1:1) were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of the Third Military Medical University. The mice were housed under a 12 h light/dark cycle (6 a.m. to 6 p.m.) in a facility with 40%–50% humidity and had ad libitum access to food and water. TAC was performed as follows. Mice of the same age and sex were anaesthetized with 2.5% isoflurane in oxygen. The mice were intubated to ensure unobstructed breathing, and the hair on the chest was removed using hair removal cream. The skin was subsequently incised, and the chest was opened along the sternal stalk to expose the aortic arch. A 26G needle was placed between the left and right common carotid arteries, and the aortic arch was fully constricted with nonabsorbable sutures. The needle was immediately removed to restore the blood supply. Postoperatively, the chest muscles and skin were sutured. Mice in the sham group underwent an identical procedure without aortic constriction. The mice were allowed to recover consciousness spontaneously on a constant‐temperature heating table before the ventilator was removed. The mice were then fed a standard diet and monitored for 6 weeks. AMF (HY‐N0662, purity 99.81%, MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) or vehicle was administered via daily intraperitoneal injections for 6 weeks after TAC. To determine the optimal therapeutic dose, three concentrations (10, 25, and 50 mg/kg) were initially tested. The experimental mice were randomly grouped and received daily intraperitoneal injections. The optimal dose was determined to be 25 mg/kg, which was used for subsequent experiments. Captopril (20 mg/kg/day) was intraperitoneally injected as a positive control. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Third Military Medical University (ethics approval number: AMUWEC20247113) and adhered to the guidelines in the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.


### Echocardiography
  Mice were anaesthetized with a mixture of oxygen and 2.5% isoflurane, and the hair on the chest area was removed using Veet hair removal cream. The paws of the mice were taped to an electrode pad. With stable body temperature and respiratory rate maintained, cardiac ultrasound was performed using a Vevo2100 high‐resolution imaging system (Visual Sonics, Canada) with a 30 MHz probe, acquiring intraventricular M‐mode and B‐mode images from the parasternal short‐axis view at the papillary muscle level. Left ventricular ejection fraction, fractional shortening, and diastolic/systolic left ventricular wall thickness were calculated to assess left ventricular systolic function.


### Serum Levels of Metabolic Factors
  Whole trunk blood was collected from sacrificed mice and then centrifuged at 8000 × g and 4°C for 10 min, after which the supernatant (serum) was transferred to a new tube. Serum ALT (C009‐3‐1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China), AST (C010‐3‐1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China), triglyceride (A110‐1‐1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China), and cholesterol (A111‐1‐1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) levels were measured by spectrophotometry using a ThermoScientific Multiskan GO spectrophotometer, following the specifications provided by the respective kit manufacturers. Blood glucose levels following the trial period were measured using universal glucose test strips (based on the glucose oxidase method) and a Roche Vitality Blood Glucose Meter (Roche Diabetes Care GmbH; Roche Diagnostic Products (Shanghai) Co. Ltd., Shanghai, China).


### Hematoxylin–Eosin (H&E) Staining
  Staining was performed using a HE staining kit (G1120, Solarbio, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, tissues were sectioned (4 μm thick), dried, and deparaffinized. The sections were sequentially stained with HE, dehydrated through a graded alcohol series, and mounted with neutral resin. The sections were visualized under a light microscope to assess morphological changes.


### Sirius Red Staining
  Staining was carried out using a Picrosirius Red Stain Kit (ab150681, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, 4‐μm‐thick cardiac sections were stained with saturated picric acid Sirius red staining solution at room temperature for 1 h. The cardiac sections were rinsed with acidic ethanol for several minutes. Collagen volume was calculated from 8 to 12 random fields per heart, with n = 8 hearts per experimental group. The collagen volume fraction was calculated, in a blinded manner, as the percentage of picrosirius red‐stained area relative to the total cardiac section area using ImageJ software.


### Myocyte Cross‐Sectional Area
  Cardiac cross‐sectional area was assessed using wheat germ agglutinin (WGA). Cardiac sections were subjected to antigen retrieval in sodium citrate‐EDTA buffer (P0086, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) in boiling water for 30 min. The sections were then washed with PBS buffer, covered with WGA (W11261, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA), and incubated in a water bath at 37°C for 30 min. Cell nuclei were stained with DAPI (C1005, Beyotime). The WGA‐stained cardiac area was calculated for 8–12 random fields per heart, with n = 8 hearts per experimental group.


### Cell Culture
  Neonatal rat ventricular myocytes (NRVMs) and neonatal rat cardiac fibroblasts (NRCFs) were isolated from 1‐ to 3‐day‐old Sprague–Dawley rats. Briefly, the chest skin was disinfected with 75% alcohol, and the skin was cut with sterile scissors to expose and excise the heart. The isolated hearts were washed with precooled 1× PBS (P1020, Solarbio) to remove residual blood and then minced into < 1 mm3 pieces. The tissue pieces were digested with preheated 0.25% trypsin (T1300, Solarbio) and collagenase I (C8140, Solarbio). The cell suspension was subsequently centrifuged (1000 × g, 5 min), the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM, Gibco, New York, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), 100 U/mL streptomycin, 100 μg/mL penicillin, and amphotericin B (P7630, Solarbio). Next, the cells were cultured for 1–1.5 h. Cardiomyocytes and fibroblasts were separated on the basis of differential adhesion. NRVMs and NRCFs were cultured in 5% FBS at 37°C with 5% CO2 for another 24 h. The cells were treated with Ang II (A9847, purity ≥ 98%, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA), AMF (HY‐N0662, purity 99.81%, MedChemExpress), or recombinant human TGF‐β1 (GF346, purity > 98%, Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA).


### Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8) Analysis
  NRVMs and NRCFs were seeded in 96‐well plates, and the cells were cultured in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37°C for 24 h. Then, 100 μL of complete medium containing 10 μL of CCK‐8 (HY‐K0301, MCE) was added to each well. After 1 h of incubation, the absorbance values at a wavelength of 450 nm were measured using a microplate reader (CMax Plus, Molecular Devices, USA).


### RNA Extraction and Quantitative Real‐Time PCR
  Total RNA was isolated from tissues or cells using a total RNA extraction kit (R1200, Solarbio, Beijing, China). Reverse transcription was then performed using a PrimeScript RT Kit (RR036A, Takara, China) in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol. SYBR Green fluorescent dye (CN830S, Takara, China) and a 7300 real‐time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, USA) were used for real‐time quantitative PCR analyses following the manufacturers' instructions. The data were analyzed via the 2−ΔΔCt  method. Target mRNA expression was normalized to GAPDH expression. The primers used for PCR were provided by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), and their detailed sequences are shown in Table S1.


### 5‐Ethynyl‐2′‐Deoxyuridine (EdU) Fluorescence Staining
  EdU fluorescence staining was used to detect newly synthesized DNA in cardiac fibroblasts after the indicated treatments. Staining was performed using a Click‐iT EdU cell proliferation kit (C10338, Thermo Fisher) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. Newly synthesized DNA was dyed red, and nuclei were dyed blue.


### Immunofluorescence Staining
  Cultured cells were washed with precooled PBS buffer and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 15 min. Next, the membranes of cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X‐100 (P0096, Beyotime) at room temperature for 10 min, after which nonspecific binding sites were blocked with blocking solution (P0260, Beyotime) at room temperature for 10 min. The cells were incubated with primary antibodies (anti‐cTNT, 1:200, MA5‐12960, Invitrogen, CA, USA; anti‐α‐SMA, 1:200, 14‐9760‐82, Invitrogen, CA, USA; and anti‐vimentin, 1:200, 5741S, Cell Signaling Technology, Boston, USA) overnight at 4°C on an orbital shaker. The cells were then incubated with goat anti‐mouse IgG (Alexa Fluor 488, 1:400, ab150113, Abcam) or goat anti‐rabbit IgG (Alexa Fluor 594, 1:400, ab150080, Abcam) on a shaker in the dark for 2 h at 37°C. Cell nuclei were stained with DAPI (C1005, Beyotime). Finally, the cells were covered with an anti‐fluorescence quenching agent (P0128S, Beyotime), and fluorescence signals were detected via fluorescence microscopy (Olympus, Japan). A quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity was conducted in a blinded manner using ImageJ software.


### Protein Extraction and Western Blot Analysis
  Cardiac tissue or cells were lysed using strong lysis buffer (R0010, Solarbio) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (P1260, Solarbio). Protein concentrations were measured using a BCA protein assay (P0011, Beyotime). The proteins were then separated via 12% or 10% SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. After being blocked with 5% skim milk for 2 h, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies (listed in Table S1). At room temperature, the membrane was incubated with IRDye 800CW secondary antibodies (926‐32213, LI‐COR; 926‐32212, LI‐COR) for 1 h. Excess antibodies were removed by washing with 1× TBST. The optical density integral values were read using Odyssey, and protein expression was analyzed using ImageJ software. GAPDH was used as an internal control for statistical analysis.


### RNA‐Seq Analysis
  Cardiac tissue samples from the TAC + AMF group and TAC + vehicle group (five samples per group) were sequenced by LC‐Bio Technology (Hangzhou, China). Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher, 15596018) following the manufacturer's procedure, and RNA quantity and purity were analyzed using a Bioanalyzer 2100 and RNA 6000 Nano LabChip Kit (Agilent, CA, USA, 5067‐1511). High‐quality RNA samples with an RIN > 7.0 were used to construct a sequencing library. cDNA was synthesized from the fragmented RNA using Invitrogen SuperScript II Reverse Transcriptase (cat. 1896649, CA, USA). RNA sequencing was conducted utilizing the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (LC Bio Technology, China). Subsequently, differential gene expression analysis was carried out using the DESeq2 package (fold change ≥ 2 and q < 0.05). Bioinformatic analysis was performed using OmicStudio tools accessible at https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool. Visual representations, such as volcano plots, were generated utilizing the R programming language on the OmicStudio platform (https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool).


### Molecular Docking
  For protein–protein molecular docking experiments, first, the target protein amino acid sequences were retrieved from the UniProt database. These sequences were subsequently sent to the AlphaFold3 server to predict five interaction models, and the top‐ranked model was selected for predicted interaction site analysis. Specific interacting amino acid residues were visualized using PyMOL 2.6. For small molecule–protein docking, protein sequences were obtained from UniProt; 3D structures were predicted using AlphaFold3, and target small molecule files were downloaded from the PubChem database. AutoDock tools were subsequently used for structural preparation: for proteins, water molecules were removed, hydrogen atoms were added, nonpolar hydrogen atoms were processed, Gasteiger charges were calculated, and the results were saved in PDBQT format; for ligands, 3D structures were generated, hydrogen atoms were added, nonpolar hydrogen atoms were processed, Gasteiger charges were assigned, rotatable bonds were detected, and the results were saved in PDBQT format. Finally, the docking box parameters were defined, and semiflexible docking was performed using AutoDock Vina; the result with the highest binding affinity was selected for PyMOL visualization.


### Molecular Dynamics Simulations
  GROMACS software (version 2022.4, https://www.gromacs.org/) was used for the molecular dynamics simulations. The parameters and topologies of the protein (TβR1) and ligand (AMF) were generated using the Amber14sb‐parmbsc1 force field and general Amber force field (GAFF), respectively. The system was solvated with atomistic TIP3P water in an octahedral water box and neutralized by adding 0.150 M chloride and sodium ions. The particle mesh Ewald (PME) method was used to calculate the long‐range electrostatic interactions. The molecular dynamics simulation duration was set to 100 ns. After the molecular dynamics simulations, the trajectory was generated using the GROMACS trjconv command. The values of the root‐mean‐square deviation (RMSD) and radius of gyration (Rg) of the TβR1‐AMF complex were obtained.


### Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR)
  The equilibrium‐binding constant (KD) of AMF and recombinant human TGF‐beta RI protein (Cat#: 10459‐H02H, SinoBiological, China) was determined by SPR conducted using a Biacore T200 system (Cytiva) at 25°C. Briefly, the TβR1 protein was immobilized onto Series S CM5 sensor chips (Cytiva) by amine‐coupling chemistry to achieve an immobilization level of 10,000 resonance units (RU). Then, the compound was diluted to 100 μM with running buffer and injected into the chip. In each cycle, a 100 μL sample was flowed through the chip for 60 s at a constant flow rate of 30 μL/min. The compound was diluted to several different concentrations (12.5, 25, 50, 100, and 200 μM) with 5% PBS running buffer and injected into the chip from low to high concentrations. In each cycle, a 200 μL sample was prepared at the indicated concentrations by dilution into running buffer with a 120 s contact time and 180 s dissociation time. Data analysis was performed using Biacore T200 Evaluation Software Version 3.0. After double subtraction on the surface‐bound page, data fitting was conducted using the 1:1 steady‐state affinity model.


### Statistical Analysis
  Three or more biological replicates were included for all the experiments. The data were analyzed with GraphPad Prism 9.0, and all the results are expressed as means ± SEMs. First, all the data sets were tested for normality using the D'Agostino and Pearson test (n ≥ 8) and the Shapiro–Wilk test (n < 8). For normally distributed data, Student's t‐test (unpaired two‐tailed) was subsequently used to compare two groups; one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett's multiple comparisons test was used to compare multiple groups. For nonnormally distributed data, the Mann–Whitney U‐test (unpaired two‐tailed) was used to compare two groups. p < 0.05 was considered significant, and statistically significant differences are shown with asterisks (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001).


## Results

### AMF Suppresses Pathological Cardiac Remodeling In Vivo
  To investigate the role of AMF in cardiac remodeling, we performed TAC on 8‐week‐old mice followed by daily intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of AMF (10, 25, and 50 mg/kg) (Figure S1A–G). Intraperitoneal injections of captopril (20 mg/kg/day) were used as a positive control and significantly suppressed cardiac dysfunction (Figure S1A–G). AMF treatment attenuated TAC‐induced cardiac dysfunction in a dose‐dependent manner, with cardiac protection peaking at 25 mg/kg (Figure S1A–G). The administration of AMF at 25 mg/kg had no adverse effects on body weight, blood glucose, or metabolic parameters (ALT, AST, TG, and TC), and no hepatorenal toxicity was observed (Figure S2A–J). Therefore, as the most cardioprotective dose, 25 mg/kg was selected for subsequent experiments (Figure 1A). Consistent with other reports (Wan et al. 2023), echocardiography revealed that the TAC load significantly decreased the LVEF (Figure 1B) and LVFS (Figure 1C), whereas the systolic anterior left ventricular wall (LVAW) thickness increased (Figure 1D), indicating severe cardiac dysfunction. However, the mice that underwent TAC surgery and received AMF injections exhibited significant improvements in cardiac function, with notably greater LVEF and LVFS and lower ventricular wall thickness (Figure 1B–D). Additionally, the results showed that AMF administration significantly reduced TAC‐induced changes in heart size, the HW/BW ratio, and the HW/TL ratio (Figure 1E–G). These findings were confirmed by the reduced levels of hypertrophic markers, such as Nppa, Nppb, and Myh7 (Figure 1H). In addition, HE and WGA staining revealed that AMF treatment inhibited the TAC‐induced expansion of cardiomyocytes (Figure 1I).

  Cardiac fibrosis is one of the main phenotypes that occurs in the late stage of pressure overload, leading to cardiac enlargement, stiffening, and ventricular dysfunction (Frangogiannis 2021). The degree of cardiac interstitial fibrosis and perivascular fibrosis was lower in the AMF‐treated mice than in the vehicle‐treated mice (Figure 1J1); the corresponding statistical results are shown in Figure 1J2. AMF treatment effectively inhibited collagen production and reduced the generation of myofibroblasts in the TAC group, as demonstrated by the collagen 1 and α‐SMA protein levels (Figure 1K). Additionally, the expression of cardiac fibrosis markers, including FN1, Col1α1, Col3α1, α‐SMA, and Mmp9, which were increased in TAC mice, significantly decreased after AMF treatment (Figure 1L).


### AMF Inhibits Cardiomyocyte Hypertrophy In Vitro
  The adaptive enlargement of cardiomyocytes plays a key role in pathological cardiac remodeling. To investigate whether AMF effectively inhibits cardiomyocyte hypertrophy in vitro, the viability of NRVMs treated with different doses of AMF for 24 h was assessed using CCK‐8 assays. Consistent with previous studies (Fang et al. 2023), 25 μM AMF did not affect cell viability; therefore, this dose was selected as the working concentration (Figure 2A). AMF significantly attenuated Ang II‐induced cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, as evidenced by a decrease in the area of cardiomyocytes (Figure 2B). Moreover, Ang II increased the protein and mRNA expression levels of Nppa, Nppb, and Myh7 in NRVMs; however, these effects were reversed after AMF treatment (Figure 2C,D). Together, AMF prevents pathological cardiomyocyte hypertrophy in vitro.


### AMF Inhibits Pathological Cardiac Fibrosis In Vitro
  To explore the protective effect of AMF against TGF‐β1‐induced cardiac fibrosis in vitro, the cytotoxicity of AMF at its optimal dose (25 μM) to NRCFs was first determined using CCK‐8 assays (Figure 3A). TGF‐β1 upregulated the mRNA expression of fibrotic markers, such as FN1, Col1α1, Col3α1, α‐SMA, and Mmp9 (Figure 3B), and induced the expression of the fibrotic proteins Collagen 1 and α‐SMA (Figure 3C); however, these effects were partially inhibited by AMF (Figure 3B,C). In addition, immunofluorescence staining revealed that the presence of AMF diminished the TGF‐β1‐induced transition of fibroblasts into α‐SMA‐positive myofibroblasts, although the results for the AMF group were still higher than those for the vehicle group (Figure 3D). TGF‐β1 increased the proliferation and viability of cardiac fibroblasts, but AMF inhibited this effect, as evidenced by the number of EdU‐positive cells (Figure 3E) and CCK‐8 assay results (Figure 3F). In conclusion, these findings suggested that AMF treatment effectively reduced cardiac fibrosis in vitro.


### AMF Inhibits the TGF‐β Signaling Pathway During Pathological Cardiac Remodeling
  To elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying the protective effects of AMF against TAC‐induced cardiac remodeling, RNA sequencing was carried out using murine hearts from two groups: TAC and TAC combined with AMF treatment (25 mg/kg). The differentially expressed genes (DEGs; q < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1) for the indicated comparison groups are presented in Figure 4A. A total of 1679 DEGs were identified in the TAC combined with AMF treatment group versus the TAC group, 384 of which were upregulated and 1395 of which were downregulated (Figure 4B). Among the top 20 most significant GO signaling pathways, major cardiac remodeling pathways, including “collagen‐containing extracellular matrix,” “extracellular matrix,” and “inflammatory response,” were suppressed by AMF, supporting the close relationship between cardiac remodeling and AMF (Figure 4C). As revealed by the KEGG analysis, the TGF‐β signaling pathway was the top‐ranked enriched pathway among the DEGs (Figure 4D). Additionally, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) confirmed that the TGF‐β signaling pathway was suppressed by AMF (Figure 4E). Furthermore, AMF treatment significantly diminished the TAC‐induced enrichment of TGF‐β‐related DEGs (Figure 4F). These findings suggest that AMF may confer protection against cardiac remodeling by inhibiting the TGF‐β signaling pathway.


### AMF Negatively Regulates the TGF‐β/Smad and MAPK Signaling Pathways
  We subsequently investigated the role of AMF in regulating TGF‐β signaling. TβR1, which is activated by TGF‐β1, is phosphorylated to activate downstream Smad2/3 signaling to promote cardiac hypertrophy (Watkins et al. 2006). The western blot results revealed that AMF inhibited the phosphorylation of TβR1 and Smad2/3 in hypertrophic cardiac tissue (Figure 5A). Moreover, the expression of Smad7, a negative regulator of TGF‐β1 signaling, was decreased in the TAC group but significantly increased after AMF treatment (Figure 5A). Activated TβR1 signaling could act on downstream targets, such as the MAPK pathway (Zhang 2017), and the results revealed the significant upregulation of phosphorylated JNK, ERK and P38 expression in hypertrophic cardiac tissue; however, these effects were inhibited by AMF treatment (Figure 5B). Ang II activates TGF‐β1 signaling in cardiomyocytes (Rosenkranz 2004; Zhang, Lin, et al. 2021). As shown in Figure 5C, western blot analyses revealed high TβR1 and Smad2/3 phosphorylation and lower Smad7 levels in NRVMs under Ang II stimulation, similar to previous results; however, these effects were significantly inhibited by AMF treatment. High p‐JNK, p‐ERK, and p‐P38 protein expression following Ang II stimulation was also suppressed by AMF (Figure 5D). These results indicated that the effect of AMF was dependent on repressing the phosphorylation of TβR1, which subsequently inhibited the canonical TGF‐β/Smad signaling pathway and noncanonical MAPK pathway.


### TGF‐β1 Antagonizes the Effects of AMF on Cardiomyocyte Hypertrophy, While High Concentrations of AMF Inhibit the Effects of TGF‐β1 In Vitro
  We introduced exogenous TGF‐β1 as a competitor to investigate the effect of AMF on Ang II‐induced cardiomyocyte hypertrophy in vitro. Notably, exogenous TGF‐β1 impaired the AMF‐mediated inhibition of the TGF‐β/Smad and MAPK signaling pathways in Ang II‐induced cardiomyocyte hypertrophy (Figure 6A), as evidenced by the increased expression of p‐TβR1, p‐Smad2/3, p‐JNK, p‐P38, and p‐ERK and decreased expression of Smad7 in the TGF‐β1 intervention group (Figure S3A). Real‐time quantitative PCR demonstrated that TGF‐β1 diminished the protective effects of AMF on cardiac hypertrophy indicators, including Nppa, Nppb, and Myh7 (Figure 6B). Furthermore, the AMF‐mediated amelioration of the Ang II‐induced increase in cardiomyocyte size was inhibited by TGF‐β1 overexpression (Figure 6C). Additionally, TGF‐β1 overexpression reversed the AMF‐induced decrease in ANP protein levels (Figure S3B). Exogenous TGF‐β1 antagonized the cardioprotective effects of AMF against cardiomyocyte hypertrophy in vitro, suggesting that the inhibition of TGF‐β signaling by AMF is crucial for its cardioprotective effect. Moreover, western blot analysis revealed that different doses of AMF significantly inhibited p‐TβR1 and p‐Smad2/3 while promoting the upregulation of the expression of the fibrotic inhibitory signal Smad7 in NRCFs under TGF‐β1 stimulation (Figure 6D). Additionally, the expression of the MAPK signaling components p‐JNK, p‐ERK, and p‐P38 was significantly inhibited, which was closely associated with the therapeutic dose of AMF (Figure 6E).


### TβR1 Is a Direct AMF‐Binding Protein
  We hypothesized that AMF interacts directly with TβR1 to block the regulatory effect of TGF‐β1, thereby inhibiting the TGF‐β/Smad and MAPK signaling pathways. To elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying the AMF‐mediated modulation of this process, we conducted molecular docking simulations to identify potential binding sites within the TβR1–TGF‐β1 dimer complex (Figure 7A). We subsequently performed ternary complex modeling of AMF, TβR1, and TGF‐β1 to characterize their interactive functions. The computed binding energy of the TβR1‐AMF complex, at −11.4 kcal/mol, indicated a robust interaction, suggesting high binding affinity (Figure 7B). Two‐dimensional pharmacophore analysis of the TβR1‐AMF‐TGF‐β1 ternary complex revealed that AMF forms a critical hydrogen bond with the ASP63 residue in the TβR1 binding pocket (Figure 7C). Notably, molecular docking also predicted interactions between AMF and the PRO317/CYS318 residues of TGF‐β1; additional contacts introduce spatial constraints that further destabilize the TGF‐β1–TβR1 binding interface (Figure 7B,C). These interactions not only sterically impede TGF‐β1 binding but also disrupt key interprotein amino acid linkages essential for the dimer stabilization of TGF‐β1–TβR1. To further evaluate the structural stability of the AMF‐TβR1 complex, we performed molecular dynamics simulations of TβR1‐AMF, with a steady protein backbone, and the time evolution of the weighted RMSDs indicated stable equilibrium of the complex (Figure 7D). As evidenced by the Rg measurements, AMF bound tightly to TβR1 (Figure 7E). SPR data confirmed that AMF binds to the TβR1 pocket, forming a stable, relatively high‐affinity complex (Figure 7F,G). Coimmunoprecipitation (co‐IP) assays ultimately demonstrated that AMF inhibited the binding of TGF‐β1 to TβR1 in hypertrophic cardiac tissue (Figure 7H). These findings suggest that AMF directly targets TβR1, induces steric hindrance within the TGF‐β1–TβR1 interaction domain, and antagonizes TGF‐β1‐driven cardiac hypertrophy and ECM remodeling by modulating the TGF‐β/Smad and MAPK signaling pathways in cardiomyocytes and cardiac fibroblasts (Figure 8).


## Discussion
  Adverse cardiac remodeling characterized by pathological cardiac hypertrophy and interstitial fibrosis due to cardiac overloading eventually progresses to heart failure (Shimizu and Minamino 2016; González et al. 2018). Notably, clinical pharmacological agents for the treatment of adverse cardiac remodeling are still far from satisfactory, and both the morbidity and mortality of HF are increasing (Baman and Ahmad 2020; van Berlo et al. 2013). In this study, we showed that AMF, a natural biflavonoid structurally distinct from TβR1 antagonists, protected cardiac function against TGF‐β1–TβR1‐induced cardiac remodeling under pressure overload.

  Naturally occurring bioactive compounds such as celastrol and ginsenoside Rd. have been shown to be associated with cardiac injury repair and improved cardiac function in mice (Wan et al. 2023; Ye et al. 2020). However, owing to their poor bioavailability (< 1%) (Zhang, Zhang, et al. 2021), unclear mechanism, and potential side effects (Ye et al. 2020), large‐scale clinical application has not been realized. AMF, a bioactive compound that is abundant in  G. biloba , achieves up to 80% bioavailability following intraperitoneal injection, a half‐life of 5 h, and 90% elimination within 24 h after administration (Liao et al. 2015). The 25 mg/kg dose was ultimately chosen because it improved cardiac function without cardiotoxicity and had no adverse effects on body weight, blood glucose, or metabolic parameters (ALT, AST, TC, and TG), with no observed hepatorenal toxicity. AMF (1–300 μM) was tested on NRVMs/NRCFs for 24 h in vitro, and CCK‐8 assays revealed significant viability impairment at 150/300 μM; thus, 25 μM was selected because it had no effect on normal viability, proliferation, or morphology. Owing to its potent anti‐inflammatory, antioxidant, hypoglycemic, and vasodilatory properties, AMF has been shown to ameliorate myocardial ischemia–reperfusion injury (Li et al. 2021), prevent cardiovascular and hepatic dysfunction in metabolic syndrome models (Qin et al. 2018), and alleviate doxorubicin‐induced myocardial toxicity (Alherz et al. 2022; Fang et al. 2023). Nevertheless, the role of AMF in TAC‐induced cardiac remodeling has not been investigated, and its therapeutic mechanisms of action require further elucidation. Our results showed that AMF attenuated cardiac dysfunction, hypertrophy, and fibrosis in TAC‐induced pressure overload hearts without causing systemic toxicity.

  TGF‐β1 signaling is critical for the regulation of the phenotype and function of cardiomyocytes and cardiac fibroblasts involved in cardiac remodeling (Frangogiannis 2022; Meng et al. 2016). TβR1 is a key node in TGF‐β1 signal transduction, and cardiac overloading activates the binding of TGF‐β1 to TβR1 in heart cells (Frangogiannis 2022). Upon TGF‐β1 binding, the ligand‐bound TβRII dimer recruits the TβRI dimer and phosphorylates the serine and threonine residues in the GS domain of TβRI to activate TβRI (Wrana et al. 1994; Wieser et al. 1995). Alternatively, activated TβRI can transduce both Smad‐dependent and non‐Smad signaling pathways (e.g., MAPK, Rho GTPase, and PI3K/AKT) (Zhang 2017; Zeglinski et al. 2016). In this study, AMF inhibited TβR1 phosphorylation and the downstream Smad/MAPK signaling pathway, thereby attenuating TGF‐β1‐mediated cardiac damage. However, when TGF‐β1 was overexpressed, the protective effects of AMF disappeared. In NRCFs, AMF dose‐dependently blocks TGF‐β1‐driven fibrotic responses, including myofibroblast transdifferentiation and ECM deposition (Khalil et al. 2017). Molecular modeling and experimental validation demonstrated that AMF disrupted TGF‐β1–TβR1 binding through steric hindrance by contacting TβR1 while potentially engaging TGF‐β1 to impose structural constraints that destabilize their interface. These findings provide a mechanistic basis for the direct modulation of TβR1 by AMF, highlighting its dual mode of action by concurrently targeting both the receptor and ligand to block their interaction.

  The conditional genetic deletion of TβR1/2 in fibroblasts dramatically attenuates pressure overload–induced cardiac hypertrophy and improves ventricular function (Rosenkranz et al. 2002). The administration of SM16, a specific TβR1 inhibitor, increases patient mortality rates, an effect that is attributed to increased left ventricular dilatation and inflammation (Engebretsen et al. 2014). In our study, AMF demonstrated cardioprotective effects comparable to those of captopril (positive control) in mice. As a potential TβR1 inhibitor, AMF antagonizes the effects of activated TβR1 in cardiomyocytes and cardiac fibroblasts, especially at therapeutic doses, without causing cardiac, hepatic, or renal toxicity. Although its short half‐life necessitates formulation optimization (e.g., sustained‐release preparations), AMF shows strong clinical potential pending further efficacy and safety validation in human trials. These findings suggest that AMF may be a promising therapeutic option to mitigate cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis and prevent the development of heart failure.

  There are several limitations in this study. Considering the postoperative weakness of mice after TAC and the short half‐life of AMF, we selected continuous intraperitoneal injection for 42 days to observe the therapeutic effect of AMF. Although our study revealed that continuous therapeutic doses of AMF had no systemic toxicity, optimizing the formulation and biodistribution profiles of AMF, such as prolonging its half‐life, increasing its AUC, increasing its cardiac accumulation, and reducing its administration frequency, are the goals of our future studies.

  In summary, our study demonstrated that AMF inhibits cardiac hypertrophy and cardiac fibrosis by directly targeting TβR1, thereby alleviating cardiac remodeling. These findings offer compelling evidence for the therapeutic potential of AMF in heart failure.
