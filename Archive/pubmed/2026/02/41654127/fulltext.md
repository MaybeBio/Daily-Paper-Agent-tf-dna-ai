# HPV18E6 and CDK5 virus-host interaction is a prospective therapeutic target for HPV-positive cervical cancer


## Abstract
  The E6 oncoprotein encoded by the cancer-causing human papillomavirus (HPV) is vital for maintaining the ability of the virus to promote cell proliferation and cancer progression. This study identified that cyclin-dependent kinase 5 (CDK5) is a previously unidentified host target of E6 encoded by cancer-causing HPV genotypes. Although E6 may not be a phosphorylation substrate of CDK5, interaction with CDK5 nonetheless increases the steady-state level of E6. To further elucidate the significance of CDK5-E6 interaction, we adopted pharmacological inhibition and silencing approaches. Leveraging the computer-aided molecular docking and in vitro screening approaches, we identified CP681301, a potent CDK5 inhibitor, which can inhibit CDK5-E6 complex formation. More intriguingly, this study underlines that CP681301 can inhibit the E6-E6AP-p53 axis, conferring inhibition on cancer phenotypes of HPV-positive cancer cells, including the ability of cells to proliferate, transform, migrate and invade through Matrigel. Our study highlights that CDK5-E6 is a promising drug target for the design of next-generation targeted therapeutics for HPV-associated diseases.


## Introduction
  Cervical cancer is the fourth most common cancer in women, mainly caused by persistent infection by the cancer-causing types of human papillomavirus (HPV) [1,2]. Even though cervical cancer can be effectively prevented through regular screening and early surgical interventions, the overall burden of the disease, especially in Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs), has not decreased significantly [[3], [4], [5]]. Moreover, the implementation of national HPV vaccination programs in most places remains non-gender neutral. In LMICs, due to a lack of established infrastructure and financial support [6,7], some of them may rely on support from non-profit organizations, like the World Health Organisation (WHO) and Gavi (the Vaccine Alliance) (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32706907/). Among over 400 HPV genotypes identified, 13 are classified as the so-called high-risk types. HPV16 and 18 are the most prevalent types contributing to over 70% of invasive cervical cancers [4]. In case of low-grade lesions, full viral genome expression can be detected in the uterine cervix. As the lesion develops, the HPV genome can integrate into the host cells, resulting in the continuous overexpression of the E6 and E7 oncoproteins [8] and loss of other viral early genes. The oncogenic properties of E6 and E7 are well described by their ability to degrade host tumour suppressors. E6 interacts and degrades the tumour suppressor protein p53 through the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. The interaction between E6 and p53 is mediated by the formation of a complex involving cellular proteins, such as E6-associated protein (E6AP) and Mouse Double Minute 2 homolog (MDM2). E6 recruits E6AP, which acts as an E3 ubiquitin ligase, leading to the ubiquitination and subsequent degradation of p53 [[9], [10], [11]], leading to dysregulation of apoptosis, cell cycle, and cell polarity. Meanwhile, E7 degrades pRB [12,13], resulting in the perturbation of E2F-directed transcriptional transactivation, which finally affects the cell cycle and promotes cell proliferation [[14], [15], [16]]. E6 and E7 activities combined can activate the DNA damage response [17]. E6 protein also shows a vital role in the life cycle of host cells, such as, in the E6-expressing keratinocytes, a steady-state increase in levels of G1- and G2-related cyclins/Cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) was observed [18,19], and an increase in genome instability [20,21].

  CDKs are protein kinases, including CDK1, CDK2, CDK4, CDK5, CDK6, and CDK7, which belong to the serine/threonine kinase class that participate in many crucial cellular processes [22]. CDK5, a multifunctional kinase, that plays vital roles in the development, function, and survival of neurons [23]. Recently, numerous studies have elucidated that the roles of CDK5 have been described beyond the central nervous system (CNS). CDK5 plays a central role in DNA damage response, immune response, apoptosis, cell migration, invasion, and angiogenesis [[24], [25], [26]]. Increasing evidence has identified that the upregulation of CDK5 promotes cancer progression and aggression of pancreatic cancer, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), glioblastoma, colorectal cancer and cervical cancer [[27], [28], [29]]. Due to the significant role of CDK5 in cancers, it has been suggested as an attractive target for anti-tumour therapeutic development. In the case of cervical cancer, the association between CDK5 and HPV has not been elucidated.

  In view of this, this study focused on the association between E6 encoded by high-risk HPV types, including HPV16 and HPV18, and CDK5. We demonstrated that CDK5 can bind with 18E6 and 16E6. Therefore, we performed a series of in vitro and cell-based assays to investigate the binding between E6 and CDK5. Furthermore, through molecular docking and screenings, we discovered that CP681301 can disrupt the CDK5-E6 complex formation and revert HPV-mediated carcinogenesis.

### CP681301 exerted a selective inhibitory effect on HPV-positive cervical cancer cells

Then, we performed the CCK-8 assay, we found that CP681301 exhibited significantly more activity against cell proliferation on HPV positive cell line when compared with HPV-negative cells: HeLa (HPV18 positive) with an IC50 of 2 μM and CaSki (HPV16 positive) with an IC50 of 3.7 μM; C33A (HPV-negative cervical cancer cell line) with an IC50 of more than 10 μM (Fig. 2B and C). However, CP681301 showed no specific effect on HPV positive or HPV-negative SCC cancer cell lines, SCC090, and SAS, with the IC50 of 3 μM, 2 μM, respectively. Moreover, we further investigated the inhibition selectivity of CP681301 on HPV positive cancer cells over HPV negative cancer cells.   We next examined the effect of CP681301 on cell invasion ability, another cancer hallmark, of HeLa, CaSki, and C33A cells. After 24 h treatment, HeLa cells treated with 0.6 μM of CP681301 showed a significantly (230.00 ± 66.73, P = 0.0105) lower ability to invade than DMSO-treated HeLa. A dose-dependent inhibitory effect was observed when HeLa cells were treated with a higher concentration (65.00 ± 41.82, P = 0.004) of CP681301 (Fig. 3C (i)-(ii)). At the same time, we also observed a similar dose-dependent inhibition phenotype when CaSki cells were treated with low and high concentrations of CP681301 (Figure S3 B(i)-(ii)). To distinguish invasion-specific effects from general cytotoxic activity, proliferation of C33A cells was assessed under identical conditions (Fig. 2C). No significant inhibition of proliferation was observed, indicating that the differential invasion phenotype is not secondary to a global suppression of cell growth in the HPV-negative model. Meanwhile, in the invasion assay, CP681301 treatment did not affect the invasive ability of C33A cells when compared with the vehicle control treatment (Fig. 3D (i)-(ii)). This illustrated that the invasion ability of HeLa and CaSki cells was inhibited by CP681301 treatment, and this occurs in a treatment dose- and HPV-dependent manner.

  This result indicates that inhibition of CDK5 using CP681301 effectively weakens the oncogenic potential of HPV oncoprotein, resulting in dampening of HPV-driven cell migration, growth in an anchorage-independent manner and invasion through extracellular matrix.

  To further validate the intracellular CDK5-E6 complex formation, we performed an immunofluorescence assay, which allows us to examine the subcellular colocalization of the protein complex. We transfected the His-tagged CDK5, HA-tagged HPV16E6 and HPV18E6 into an HPV-null cell line, U-2 OS cells. An overexpression approach was adopted because the CDK5 protein level is controlled and normally exists at a low level; the U-2 OS cells were used as it is an immortalised HPV-negative epithelial cell line, with a clear nuclear: cytoplasmic ratio, allowing us to observe a clear subcellular localization of the 2 proteins. The cells were probed with antibodies specifically against His (for CDK5) and HA (for E6), respectively. As shown in Fig. 4C, we observed HPV16 and 18E6s were present in the nucleus and cytoplasm. We observed that CDK5 was predominantly co-localised with HPV16E6 and 18E6 in the nucleus of U-2 OS cells, indicating that the CDK5-HPV16E6 and 18E6 complex were formed in the nucleus and that E6s do not appear to affect the subcellular localization of CDK5. In the subsequent assays, we focused on elucidating the association between E6 and CDK5.

Collectively, our results indicate that CP681301 exerts a specific effect on HPV18-positive HeLa cells, particularly in inhibiting the CDK5-18E6 protein complex and the proliferation.


### CP681301 treatment destabilized E6, reduced E6AP and rescued p53

## Discussion
  The cancer-causing HPV genotypes can establish a persistent infection, and this may lead to the integration of the viral genome into the host genome and the subsequent constitutive high levels of E6 and E7 oncoproteins. The HPV-encoded E6 and E7 proteins can interact with and degrade tumour suppression proteins, p53 and pRB, respectively, in a proteasome-dependent manner. E6 and E7, collaboratively, induced an upregulation of cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs). Among the CDKs, although the role of Cyclin-dependent kinase 5 (CDK5) has been implicated in cancers [24,25,28,29], the association of with HPV has not been well-defined.

  This study provides evidence showing that elevation of CDK5 occurs concurrently with E6 constitutive high levels in HPV-positive cancer cells. We first studied the CDK5-HPV in cancer formation, then further delve into CDK5-HPV interaction at the molecular level.

  Studying the expression profile of genes of interest at public meta-databases, like GEPIA, is indeed comprehensive and informative. Nonetheless, these databases often do not differentiate between HPV-associated and non-HPV-associated cancers. To address our study interests, we studied the CDK5 protein levels in normal, HPV-negative and HPV-positive cancer cell lines, which indicate that in a homogenous cell background, a high HPV oncoprotein level could have a functional role contributing to increased CDK5 protein, and potentially CDK5 function.

  Using ectopic overexpression in HPV-null or HPV-positive cervical cancer cells, coupled with HPV oncogenes depletion approaches and HPV18 full genome expression in normal keratinocytes, we consistently demonstrated that E6 established a mutual relationship with CDK5. This virus-host interaction orchestrated the role of E6 in maintaining cancer phenotypes, including cell proliferation, transformation, migration and invasion.

  We leveraged a computer-aided tool to identify small molecules that can disrupt CDK5-E6 association, and identified CP681301 as a potent inhibitor of HPV18 HeLa cells. CP681301 not only halted the HPV-mediated cancer phenotypes, but the inhibitor also binds directly and dissociates the CDK5-E6 interaction, resulting in reduced E6 steady-state and E6AP levels. Both of these molecular events restore the ability of p53 to function as a tumour suppressor. However, a reduced half-life of HPV 18E6 was observed in the presence of CP681301, and this coincides with a decrease in 18E6 transcription in cells treated with the compound. Therefore, the shortened E6 half-life may be attributed to the reduction in E6 transcription following CP681301 treatment. Through this study, we were intrigued to reveal, for the first time, that CP681301 can reduce E6AP levels in HPV-positive cancer cells. Based on the current knowledge, CP681301 may act directly on CDK5 and not on p53. CDK5 is an upstream protein of p53, which CDK5 phosphorylates, stabilizes and activates p53 [32]. In the HPV context, p53 is effectively degraded by E6; therefore, CDK5 may not play a significant role on p53 functions. While in HPV-containing cells, E6 leverages the binding with CDK5 to maintain E6 stability, allowing it to exert its oncogenic activities. Although CDK5 is a kinase, in our hands, CDK5 does not maintain E6 stability through phosphorylation, and E6 may interact with CDK5 at a region outside of the CDK5 kinase domain. Hence, E6 is not a phosphorylation substrate of CDK5. Nonetheless, the effect of CP681301 treatment on transcriptomic profiles, including E6AP and p53 transcripts, should be studied further.

  While a downregulation of E6AP has been noted upon downregulation of E6/E7 expression or treatment with CP681301, it may not provide sufficient evidence to fully account for the observed phenotypic changes. Notably, E6 enhances the turnover of E6AP protein, leading to a reduced half-life of E6AP in HPV-positive cells compared to HPV-negative cells. Previous studies also showed that E6AP stability increases in the absence of HPV oncoproteins, further complicating the relationship between E6AP levels and phenotypic variations [33]. A similar inhibitory effect on E6AP was observed in HPV-negative cancer cells, indicating that CP681301 may exert a broad inhibitory effect on E6AP independent of HPV status, which may not directly correlate with p53 stabilisation.

  Despite showing somewhat similar oncogenic potential, the protein structures of HPV16E6 and HPV18E6 vary, leading to potential differences in how CP681301 binds to and interacts with these proteins. Specifically, CP681301 may bind more readily to certain structural regions of the HPV18E6 protein, effectively inhibiting its activity. However, its binding to the HPV16E6 protein may be weaker or ineffective, resulting in a relatively weaker inhibitory effect on HPV16E6. Furthermore, the mechanism by which CP681301 inhibits the HPV18E6 protein may differ from that of HPV16E6, contributing to differential binding affinity to or disrupting the HPV16E6-CDK5 interaction as effectively as it does with HPV18E6.

  However, there are still some drawbacks to this study. As we observed that CDK5 demonstrated a higher binding affinity to 18E6 protein, we therefore focused on HPV-associated cervical cancer. In our experiments, we utilized C33A as a control for HPV-negative cervical cancer cell lines, but this choice may not encompass the full diversity of HPV-negative cervical cancer types. We acknowledge that another HPV-negative cervical cancer cells, other than C33A, should be used as a parallel comparison. In the target verification phase, a significant limitation of our study was the lack of stable knockdown cell lines for CDK5 and E6 in HeLa cells. Without these stable knockdown models, we were unable to effectively verify the long-term impact of disrupting the CDK5-E6 protein-protein interaction on carcinoma progression. Additionally, the long-term effects and potential off-target effects of this inhibitor remain to be thoroughly investigated. Lastly, the generalizability of these findings to other HPV-associated cancers originated from other anatomical sites, such as vulva, head and neck and anus, requires further validation.

  In our hands, we also observed that CP681301 is potentially toxic, and indiscriminately inhibiting cancer cells disregarding of HPV status, although HPV-positive cervical cancer cells may be more susceptible to the CP681301 inhibitory effect than HPV-negative cervical cancer cells. We also noted that treatment with CP681301 affected the E6AP protein. The regulatory mechanisms of E6AP may differ depending upon cell types and HPV status. For instance, HPV-negative and -positive cancer cells are genetically distinct, and they harbour different genetic mutations. Some of the well-known examples are TP53 and PIK3 mutations are different in these 2 cancer types. These differences contribute to how cells response to treatment clinically. HPV-negative head and neck cancer cells are more resistant to platinum-based chemotherapeutics [34], whilst HPV-positive cervical cancers are more susceptible to platinum chemotherapeutics [35].

  To conclude, the current study is the first to identify the effect of CDK5 on the HPV18E6 and to examine the mechanisms of CDK5 and HPV18E6 in the carcinoma process, leading to subsequent HPV-mediated downstream oncogenic events. Based on our current and previous studies, we provide a hypothetical rationale for how CDK5 could be proposed as a new therapeutic target for HPV-mediated cancers. Upon expression of CDK5, it binds to HPV18 E6 in the nucleus, leading to the stabilisation of HPV18E6. This then promotes HPV-induced carcinogenesis, including increased cell proliferation, transformation, migration and invasion. Although CP681301 may exert a broad cytotoxic effect, it can disrupt the CDK5-E6 complex formation, resulting in dampening cell proliferation, transformation, migration and invasion of HPV-positive cervical cancer cells. CDK5-E6 is a valuable drug target. Novel therapeutic molecules specific towards CDK5-E6 can be designed, as targeted therapeutic for HPV-associated cancers.


## Conclusions
  In summary, our study shows, for the first time, CDK5 is a genuine and previously unrecognised target of 18E6 encoded by cancer-causing HPVs. We also found that CDK5 can stabilise the E6 protein. Therefore, the CDK5-E6 complex can as a novel target for the treatment of HPV positive cervical cancer. More importantly, we also screened a compound, CP681301, whichcan disturb the formation of the CDK5-E6 complex and then lead to cell proliferation, transformation, migration and invasion.


## Materials and methods

### Plasmids
  HPV-E6 expression constructs, namely, pcDNA3.1: HA-HPV-16 and -18 E6, pGEX2T:HPV-16, -18 E6, -16E7 and -18E7 (encoding GST-HPV16, 18 conjugated proteins, respectively), Lacz were generously gifted by Dr. Lawrence Banks, ICGEB. These expression constructs were used in in vitro binding, co-immunoprecipitation, and transient expression assays. The pcDNA3.1: His-CDK5 was generously provided by Prof. Wang Yun, School of Basic Medical Sciences, Peking University, pGEX2T: CDK5 was generously provided by Prof. Robert Zhong QI, The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology. These plasmids were used in co-immunoprecipitation, immunofluorescence, and transient expression assays.


### Cell lines and transfection
  HeLa (HPV18 positive cervical cancer cells), CaSki (HPV16 positive cervical cancer cells), human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293 (HPV-null epithelial cells), C33A (HPV-null cervical cancer cells), U-2 OS (HPV-null human osteosarcorma cells), SAS (HPV-null human head and neck squamous cell carcinoma cell line), and SCC090 (HPV16-positive human HNSCC cell line) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, GIBCO) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. The HGK12 cell line is an immortalised cell line derived from human gingival keratinocytes sourced from oral cavity tissue, created through the introduction of specific viral oncogenes, such as those from human papillomavirus (HPV), was kindly gifts from Prof. Lui, School of Biomedical Sciences, The Chinese University of Hong Kong. Identities of these human cell lines were validated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling using the AmpFlSTR Identifiler Plus PCR Amplification Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with the Applied Biosystems 3500 Series Genetic Analyzer, and analyzed by GeneMapper Software 5 (Applied Biosystems). The STR profile of all cells lines showed >88% concordance with their reference profiles in the ATCC database.


### Stable cell line construction
  The full-genome plasmid of HPV18 and the pcDNA3.1 plasmid vector were electroporated into HGK12 cells. At 24 h post-electroporation, the culture medium was supplemented with G418 (800 μg/mL) and CaCl2 (500 ng/mL) for selection. After 30 passages, a stable HGK12 cell line expressing HPV18 was successfully established.


### GST fusion protein purification and In vitro binding assay
  The purification of GST-tagged fusion proteins and pcDNA3.1: His-CDK5 protein was performed as previously described [36]. Transformants carrying the pGEX2T:HPV16E6, HPV18E6, HPV16E7, and HPV18E7 constructs were cultured overnight, then inoculated into fresh medium and incubated for 1 h. Recombinant protein expression was induced by adding isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG, Sigma) to a final concentration of 1 mM, followed by a 3-h incubation. The bacteria were harvested, lysed in cold 1 × PBS containing 1% Triton X-100, and sonicated three times, each for 15 s. The supernatants were collected and incubated with glutathione-conjugated agarose resin on a rotating wheel at 4 °C for 4 h. After extensive washing, the bound GST fusion proteins were analyzed by SDS-PAGE and stained with Coomassie Blue Stain Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

  For CDK5 protein preparation, the CDK5 plasmid was transfected into 293 cells for 24 h, after which cell lysates were collected using E1A buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 100 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 1% Triton X-100, 1% PMSF, protease inhibitor cocktail). The CDK5 protein was then incubated with GST fusion proteins for 2 h. After extensive washing, 10 μL of 2 × SDS loading buffer was added to the beads, and the mixture was incubated at 95 °C for 5 min. Binding interactions were analyzed by SDS-PAGE.

  CDK5 protein was synthesized in vitro using the TNT® Coupled Reticulocyte Lysate System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. Equal amounts of in vitro-translated CDK5 were mixed with purified GST fusion proteins and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. The mixtures were then washed three times with PBS containing 0.1% Tween 20. The bound proteins were analyzed by SDS-PAGE and western blotting to detect CDK5.


### Cell proliferation assay
  Cell proliferation of HeLa, CaSki, C33A, SCC090, and SAS cells was assessed using the WST-8 assay with the Cell Counting Kit-8 (MedChem Express). HeLa, CaSki, SCC090, C33A and SAS cells were seeded into 96-well plates, with 100 μL of DMEM containing 10% FBS. The day before CP681301 treatment, and DMSO was added as a negative control. Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator for 24 h. After 24 h, Cell Counting Kit-8 solution was added to the medium at a 1:10 ratio, followed by a 2-h incubation at 37 °C. The optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured using a LEDETECT 96 (Labexim Products) plate reader. This procedure was repeated on days 2 (48 h), 3 (72 h), and 4 (96 h). Cell viability curves were generated and analyzed using GraphPad™ Prism 8 software.


### Cycloheximide chase assay (CHX)
  The human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293 (HPV-null epithelial cells) cell line was transfected with HA-tagged HPV16E6, HPV18E6, and His-CDK5 plasmids, as well as lacZ-expressing plasmids, using a calcium phosphate transfection reagent. The pcDNA 3.1 plasmid was used as a negative control. After 24 h of transfection, HEK293 cells were treated with cycloheximide (CHX) at a final concentration of 10 μg/mL. Cells were harvested at 0, 30, 60, and 120 min post-CHX treatment and subjected to Western blot analysis. The half-life of HPV16E6 and HPV18E6 proteins was determined using the one-phase exponential decay model in GraphPad Prism 8 software.


### Immunoprecipitation and western blotting
  Immunoprecipitation samples or total cellular proteins were collected by lysing cells directly in 2 × SDS sample buffer, followed by incubation at 95 °C for 5 min. The protein samples were then separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (BIO-RAD). The transferred membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: monoclonal rabbit anti-HA (Cell Signaling Technology), monoclonal mouse anti-p53 (Santa Cruz), monoclonal mouse anti-HPV18E6 (Santa Cruz), monoclonal mouse anti-CDK5 (Santa Cruz), and monoclonal mouse anti-β-actin (Santa Cruz). After primary antibody incubation, the membranes were washed and incubated with diluted secondary antibodies—polyclonal swine anti-rabbit IgG HRP or polyclonal rabbit anti-mouse IgG HRP (Dako)—for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoblots were visualized using the Azure 200 Chemiluminescent Imaging System (Azure Biosystems).


### Wound healing
  Cell migration abilities of HeLa (HPV18-positive cervical cancer cell line), CaSki (HPV16-positive cervical cancer cell line), and SAS (HPV-negative head and neck cancer cell line) were assessed using a wound healing assay. Cells were seeded into each well of a 6-well plate at a density of 5 × 10^5 cells/well. Once the cell monolayer reached full confluence, a wound was introduced by scraping the monolayer with a 200 μL pipette tip. The cells were then washed three times with PBS and treated with either 0.6 μM (LC) or 1.5 μM (HC) CP681301, or with DMSO as a control. Cell migration was monitored by time-lapse microscopy (Nikon) for 24 h, with images captured every 6 h. The percentage of wound closure was calculated by measuring the area of the wound at 6-, 12-, and 24-h post-scratching, and comparing it to the wound area at 0 h.


### Cell invasion assay
  HPV 18-positive HeLa cells, HPV 16-positive CaSki cells, and HPV-negative C33A cells, along with the non-invasive breast cancer MCF7 cell line as a negative control, were used for the invasion assay. Matrigel matrix (Corning) was aliquoted in serum-free DMEM at a concentration of 200 μg/mL and kept on ice, then added to the Corning BioCoat™ Matrigel® invasion chamber (Corning, USA) and allowed to solidify for 2 h. Subsequently, HeLa, CaSki, C33A, and MCF7 cells were seeded into the upper chamber containing serum-free DMEM. DMEM medium with 20% FBS was added to the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. After 24 h of incubation, the upper chamber was washed with PBS, and non-invading cells were removed with autoclaved cotton swabs. The invading cells on the lower surface of the membrane were fixed with 500 μL absolute methanol, and the upper section of the membrane was treated with two drops of methanol for 5 min. The fixed cells were stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 60 min, then washed with double-distilled water (ddH2O). The chamber was inverted and allowed to air dry overnight. The stained membrane was excised, immersed in xylene, and mounted on a glass slide with ProLong Diamond Antifade Mountant (Invitrogen, USA). The slides were examined under a light microscope (Leica, Germany) at × 100 magnification. Ten random fields were captured, and the number of invading cells was quantified using ImageJ software.


### Immunofluorescence assay (IF)
  This assay was performed as previously described [31]. Approximately 2 × 10^5 U-2 OS cells were seeded onto coverslips and transfected with HA-tagged HPV16 E6, HA-tagged HPV18 E6, and His-tagged CDK5. After transfection, the cells were fixed with ice-cold absolute methanol at −20 °C for 30 min and then incubated with primary antibodies against HA (Roche) and His (Santa Cruz Biotechnology). This was followed by staining with Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibody and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-mouse secondary antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and counterstaining with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). The subcellular localization of HPV16 E6, HPV18 E6, and CDK5 was analyzed using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon).


### Compounds screening assay
  To identify potent CDK5-18E6 inhibitors, we generated a comprehensive chemical library composed of 1000 diverse compounds sourced from reputable databases: PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), Drugbank (https://go.drugbank.com/), ZINC (https://zinc15.docking.org/), and ChemSpider (https://www.chemspider.com/). This library was carefully curated to include a variety of molecular structures and pharmacological properties. The screening process was executed in three distinct stages to systematically narrow down the 1000 candidate compounds to two final candidates. Initially, we conducted virtual screening using molecular docking techniques using Autodock vina, assessing the binding affinity of each compound to the CDK5-18E6. Throughout the process, we employed stringent criteria for selection, including the inhibition percentages observed in preliminary assays and structure-activity relationships (SAR) that guided our choices.

  After these initial screenings, the top 10 candidates were subjected to the CCK-8 assay to determine their effectiveness in inhibiting cell proliferation. Our results indicated that CP681301 displayed significantly higher activity against HPV-positive cell lines compared to HPV-negative ones. This selectivity underscores the potential of CP681301 as a promising therapeutic option targeting CDK5 in HPV-related malignancies. Additional methodological details regarding the screening and selection processes will be incorporated in the revised manuscript to address reviewer concerns and provide clarity on our methodology.


### Microscale thermophoresis assay (MST)
  As described previously [37], the equilibrium dissociation constant (Kd) values were determined using the Monolith NT.115 instrument (NanoTemper Technologies). GST-CDK5, GST-16E6, GST-18E6, and the negative control GST proteins were fluorescently labeled according to the manufacturer's protocol. CP681301 was prepared at concentrations ranging from 5 nmol/L to 1 mmol/L and incubated with 12.5 nmol of purified, labeled GST-CDK5, GST-16E6, GST-18E6, and the negative control GST protein for 10 min. The samples were then loaded into NanoTemper glass capillaries, and microthermophoresis was performed using 40% LED power. The Kd values were calculated using the mass action equation in the NanoTemper software, based on measurements from three independent experiments.


### ADP-Glo kinase assay
  The kinase reaction was performed by incubating approximately 1 μg of purified GST-AurA and GST-CDK5 with equal amounts of purified 16E6/18E6 GST fusion proteins in 30 μL of kinase reaction buffer. CDK5 and AurA kinase activities were assessed using the ADP-Glo Kinase Assay (Promega), following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, the kinase reaction was mixed with ADP-Glo Reagent at a 1:1 ratio and incubated in a white-base 384-well plate for 1 h. Then, an equal volume of kinase detection reagent was added, and the mixture was incubated for an additional hour at 25 °C in the dark. The luminescence generated by ADP production was measured using a VICTOR Multilabel Plate Reader (PerkinElmer).


### Transient transfection and transfection of specific siRNA against 18E6/E7
  HeLa cells were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, approximately 3 × 10^5 cells were seeded in a 6-well plate. The following day, around 2 μg of DNA, along with P3000 and Lipofectamine 3000, were diluted in DMEM with reduced serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The mixture was then added to the cells and incubated for 5 h at 37 °C. After incubation, the medium was replaced with fresh DMEM containing 10% FBS, and the cells were incubated overnight.

  HEK 293 cells were transfected using the calcium phosphate precipitation method [38]. Approximately 3 × 10^5 HEK293 cells were seeded in a 6-well plate and incubated for 24 h. A DNA mixture was then prepared and added dropwise to 100 μL of 2 × HEPES-Buffered Saline (HBS) (280 mM NaCl, 50 mM HEPES, and 1.5 mM sodium phosphate, pH 7.1–7.2), and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. The resulting mixture was then added to the cells and incubated overnight.

  HeLa cells were transfected using Lipofectamine LTX transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with 133 nM specific siRNA targeting 18E6/E7 (Cell Signaling Technology, USA), diluted in serum-free Opti-MEM reduced serum media. The mixture was added to the cells and incubated for 5 h at 37 °C. Afterward, the medium was replaced with fresh DMEM containing 10% FBS, and the cells were incubated for 48 h.


### Soft agar colony formation assay
  The transforming abilities of HeLa and CaSki cell lines were assessed using a soft agar colony formation assay. Briefly, a base agar layer was prepared by mixing 1.2% agarose with 2 × DMEM containing 20% FBS at a 1:1 ratio and poured into 12-well plates to solidify. The top agar layer was prepared by mixing 0.7% agarose with 2 × DMEM containing 20% FBS at a 1:1 ratio, along with cells and CP681301 for each well. The top agar was then overlaid on the solidified base agar and allowed to solidify. The plates were incubated in a humidified incubator at 37 °C for 14 days to allow cell colony formation. To prevent dehydration of the top agar, 200 μL of DMEM with 10% FBS was added to each well every two days. After 3 weeks of incubation, the agar layers were stained with 0.05% crystal violet diluted in 10% ethanol. The stained agar was photographed, and the number of colonies was counted using ImageJ software.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed in duplicate using 10 μM primers and Luna® Universal qPCR Mastermix (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA) in a real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Bedford, MA, USA).
