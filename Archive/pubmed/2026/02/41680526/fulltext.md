# Skipjack tuna bone derived biocalcium ameliorates C2C12 myotube atrophy through microRNA29b regulation


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-39977-4.


## Introduction
  Sarcopenia is a progressive loss of skeletal muscle mass and strength, leading to compromised mobility, higher risks of falls, fractures, and disabilities, and loss of independence1,2. Dietary supplements are among various preventive strategies for sarcopenia. Growing evidence highlights the importance of calcium in skeletal muscle health and its potential role in preventing sarcopenia. Calcium serves not only as a structural element but also as a key regulator of myogenesis through the modulation of calpains—calcium-dependent cysteine proteases that control muscle differentiation3,4. Epidemiological studies have reported the lower calcium intake of individuals with sarcopenia compared with that of healthy adults, while higher daily intake has been associated with greater muscle mass and reduced risk of sarcopenia3–5. Hence, calcium may be able to treat or prevent sarcopenia.

  Beyond conventional supplements, marine-derived calcium—specifically biocalcium (Bio) produced from the bones of skipjack tuna (Katsuwonus pelamis)—is emerging as a superior alternative source. The tuna processing industry generates large volumes of bone frames as by-product waste; developing Bio from this calcium-rich resource crucially enhances sustainability while creating a high-value nutritional product6–8. Specifically, skipjack tuna bones constitute approximately 10–15% of the total biomass in the canning industry, and their repurposing through this valorization strategy significantly mitigates the environmental burden associated with industrial seafood waste disposal6,8. From a production efficiency standpoint, the sequential enzymatic and chemical treatment of these bone frames yields a high-purity hydroxyapatite complex with a whiteness index (L*-value) of 88.67, effectively removing volatile odor compounds while maintaining an optimal calcium-to-phosphorus ratio comparable to natural bone8. Skipjack tuna bone-derived Bio—unlike inorganic calcium salts—is an organic complex that retains an associated matrix of collagen and bioactive peptides6,8. Compositional analysis of Bio prepared through sequential cleaning, defatting, and bleaching demonstrates high concentrations of essential minerals, specifically calcium at 23.02% and phosphorus at 10.14%. Furthermore, amino acid profiling confirms that Bio retains a significant proteinaceous component, primarily collagen, as indicated by a high glycine content (315 residues/1000 residues) and a total imino acid content (proline and hydroxyproline) of 179 residues/1000 residues6. This unique chemical profile is critical for its functional advantage: by chelating calcium ions, these peptides significantly prevent the precipitation and crystallization of insoluble calcium phosphate salts in the gastrointestinal tract8. Consequently, skipjack tuna bone-derived Bio, compared to traditional inorganic calcium carbonate, demonstrates superior bioavailability (approximately 13% compared to approximately 10% of bioavailability in traditional synthetic calcium carbonate) and absorption and biological efficiency7. Furthermore, preclinical studies using rats with ovariectomy-induced osteoporosis have already confirmed its beneficial effects on bone health, including increased bone mineral density and improved trabecular architecture7, providing a compelling rationale to investigate its potential in mitigating the progression of sarcopenia.

  In investigating the molecular mechanisms underlying sarcopenia, mouse C2C12 myoblasts are skeletal muscle cells widely used as an in vitro model, as they display stable transcriptional and protein expression profiles during myogenic differentiation1. Cellular senescence, impaired myogenic differentiation, inflammaging, and an imbalance between protein synthesis and degradation are hallmarks of sarcopenia progression. In particular, senescent muscle cells secrete proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-6, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, and IL-1β, which activate the canonical nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) signaling pathway. This pathway induces inflammatory gene expression and stimulates muscle ring finger 1 (MuRF1), thereby promoting protein degradation1,2. Moreover, inhibition of the Akt/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway suppresses protein synthesis. By activating the transcription factor forkhead box O (FoxO), including FoxO3a, the suppression of Akt/mTOR signaling pathway also promotes protein degradation, leading to the upregulation of muscle atrophy-induced ubiquitin ligases MuRF1 and atrogin-11,2,9. The p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway contributes to muscle cell senescence and, in concert with the activation of the canonical NF-κB signaling pathway, enhances the nitric oxide (NO) production accompanying protein degradation1,2. Dexamethasone (Dex), a synthetic glucocorticoid widely used to induce muscle atrophy in C2C12 myotubes, suppresses protein synthesis through inhibition of the Akt/mTOR signaling pathway, increases MuRF1 and atrogin-1 expression, reduces expression of myogenic transcription factors myogenin and myoblast determination protein (MyoD), and ultimately decreases myotube diameter1.

  MicroRNA-29b (miR-29b) emerges as a critical regulator in sarcopenia pathogenesis through its multifaceted effects on skeletal muscle homeostasis and atrophy pathways9–11. Unlike many miRNAs that exhibit condition-specific expression, miR-29b has been identified as a universal driver of muscle atrophy across diverse pathological contexts, including aging, denervation, fasting, and cancer cachexia. In the broader context of sarcopenia, miR-29b acts as a molecular switch that shifts the balance of muscle proteostasis toward catabolism9. miR-29b, by directly targeting key anabolic signaling components, including insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) and the regulatory subunit of phosphoinositide 3-kinase [PI3K (p85α)], contributes to multiple types of muscle atrophy by suppressing the IGF-1/PI3K/Akt signaling pathway essential for balancing muscle protein synthesis and degradation9,11. This microRNA also specifically targets Akt3, reducing myoblast proliferation and also facilitating differentiation into myotubes during skeletal muscle development, which may compromise the regenerative capacity of aged muscle12. The NF-κB–Yin Yang 1 (YY1)–miR-29b regulatory axis plays a pivotal role in myogenic differentiation and muscle atrophy. Under physiological conditions, NF-κB-dependent YY1 activity suppresses miR-29b expression to maintain myoblasts in an undifferentiated state, whereas attenuation of this pathway during differentiation permits miR-29b upregulation, which in turn downregulates YY1 and contributes to myotube formation. Dysregulation of this circuit under atrophic conditions contributes to sarcopenia progression11,13,14. Of critical importance, elevated miR-29b expression promotes the upregulation of MuRF1 and atrogin-1 through FoxO3a activation, thereby directly linking this microRNA to the muscle atrophy machinery characteristic of sarcopenia9. This multifaceted regulatory role suggests that elevated miR-29b levels in aged muscle may not only accelerate protein degradation but also impair the regenerative capacity of muscle progenitor cells through the induction of cellular senescence9. Consequently, targeting miR-29b represents a promising therapeutic strategy for mitigating muscle wasting. While Chen et al. have utilized engineered circular RNA to inhibit miR-29b and accordingly attenuates muscle atrophy across multiple experimental models15, the potential of natural nutraceuticals—specifically skipjack tuna bone-derived Bio—to modulate this regulatory microRNA and preserve muscle integrity remains to be explored.

  Despite the growing interest in calcium supplementation and the promising potential of skipjack tuna bone-derived Bio for bone health, its direct molecular impact on sarcopenia pathogenesis remains poorly understood. Specifically, no study has investigated or demonstrated the modulation by skipjack tuna bone-derived Bio of the crucial molecular mechanisms that drive muscle atrophy in vitro—including protein synthesis/degradation imbalance, protein signaling pathways, and miR-29b regulation. Shoring up this knowledge gap is an essential step in validating the potential of Bio as a sustainable nutritional intervention strategy for sarcopenia. We therefore aimed to investigate the protective effects of skipjack tuna bone-derived Bio on C2C12 myotubes undergoing Dex-induced atrophy—specifically, its influence on inflammatory responses and key pathways related to muscle protein turnover. Furthermore, building on the critical role of miR-29b in regulating muscle atrophy, to computationally support these findings, we employed molecular docking simulations by characterizing the binding interactions between miR-29b and myogenin and MyoD proteins.


## Results

### Bio rescues atrophy and reduces oxidative stress in C2C12 myotubes
  The MTT assay (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Tables S1–S2) showed that Dex-treated C2C12 myotubes had no significant proportion of cell viability compared with C2C12 myotubes not treated with Dex and Bio (p-value = 0.0829). There was no significant change in viability in C2C12 myotubes treated with Dex and those treated with Dex and incremental 1–20 µg/mL Bio (p-values: Bio1 > 0.99, Bio2.5 > 0.99, Bio5 > 0.99, Bio10 > 0.99, and Bio20 > 0.99). However, at 30 µg/mL, Bio suppressed the cell viability of Dex-treated C2C12 myotubes significantly (p-value = 9.324 × 10− 4). Consistent with the MTT results, the lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assay (Fig. 1b) showed Dex-treated C2C12 myotubes showed a slight but non-significant increase in LDH release compared to Dex-untreated C2C12 myotubes (p-value = 0.4816). Bio at concentrations of 1–20 µg/mL did not induce significant LDH release in Dex-treated C2C12 myotubes compared to those treated with Dex alone (p-values: Bio1 > 0.99, Bio2.5 > 0.99, Bio5 > 0.99, Bio10 > 0.99, and Bio20 = 0.1115). However, treatment with 30 µg/mL Bio resulted in a significant increase in LDH activity (p-value = 3.3312 × 10− 5), confirming the cytotoxic effect observed at this higher concentration. Because these results indicate that the Bio concentration range of 1–20 µg/mL was non-cytotoxic, we used it in subsequent experiments. Due to the absence of prior data on Bio’s effects on C2C12 myotubes, the selected concentration range was empirically determined through initial cell viability and cytotoxicity assays. These findings serve as a preliminary foundation for establishing the therapeutic window of Bio in skeletal muscle models.

  Microscopic examination (Fig. 1c) and myotube diameters (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Tables S3–S5) demonstrated that Dex-induced atrophic morphology in C2C12 myotubes (decreased diameter, p-value = 0.0022) and it was rescued by 5, 10, and 20 µg/mL Bio (p-values compared with Dex-induced cell samples: Bio5 = 0.1462, Bio10 = 0.1029, and Bio20 = 9.7748 × 10− 4).

  We used nitrite concentration as an indicator for production of NO—a marker associated with oxidative stress and inflammation—in C2C12 myotubes treated with Dex and Bio. After the treatment of C2C12 myotubes with Dex, NO production significantly increased (p-value = 0.001082) (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Tables S6–S7). In Dex-treated C2C12 myotubes, however, Bio significantly and dose-dependently decreased NO production (p-values: Bio1, Bio5, and Bio10 = 0.004328, and Bio20 = 0.009996).


### Bio suppresses inflammation in C2C12 myotubes
  Using qRT-PCR to examine proinflammatory cytokines (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Tables S8–S16), we demonstrated that Bio dose-dependently reduced IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β expression in Dex-treated C2C12 myotubes (p-values of IL-6: Bio1 = 0.0779, Bio5 = 0.0233, Bio10 = 0.0136, and Bio20 = 0.0090; p-values of TNF-α: Bio1 = 0.0826, Bio5 = 0.0322, Bio10 = 0.0094, and Bio20 = 0.0081; p-values of IL-1β: Bio1 = 0.0262, Bio5 = 0.0111, Bio10 = 0.0087, and Bio20 = 0.0025). Furthermore, with ELISA we showed that Dex significantly increased IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β concentrations in C2C12 myotubes (p-values: IL-6 = 1.3376 × 10− 5, TNF-α = 3.7027 × 10− 8, and IL-1β = 9.3790 × 10− 6). By contrast, Bio significantly and dose-dependently reduced IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β concentrations in Dex-treated C2C12 myotubes (p-values of IL-6: Bio1 = 0.0107, Bio5 = 0.0014, Bio10 = 1.444 × 10− 4, and Bio20 = 9.1031 × 10− 6; p-values of TNF-α: Bio1 = 0.0024, Bio5 = 1.6711 × 10− 4, Bio10 = 2.6064 × 10− 5, and Bio20 = 2.1336 × 10− 7 ; p-values of IL-1β: Bio1 = 0.0155, Bio5 = 8.2332 × 10− 4, Bio10 = 1.3855 × 10− 4, and Bio20 = 6.6402 × 10− 5) (Figs. 1g–i and Supplementary Tables S17–S25).


### Bio restores protein turnover balance in C2C12 myotubes
  Using qRT-PCR to examine myogenic differentiation markers (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Tables S26–S34), we demonstrated that Bio significantly and dose-dependently reduced MuRF1 and atrogin-1 expression in Dex-treated C2C12 myotubes, except for MuRF1 and atrogin-1 expression after the treatment with Bio1 (p-values of MuRF1: Bio1 = 0.0691, Bio5 = 0.0166, Bio10 = 4.2862 × 10− 4, and Bio20 = 5.8282 × 10− 4; p-values of atrogin-1: Bio1 = 0.2330, Bio5 = 0.0219, Bio10 = 0.0232, and Bio20 = 0.0050). In contrast, Bio significantly and dose-dependently promoted MTOR expression in Dex-treated C2C12 myotubes, except after the treatment with Bio20 (p-values: Bio1 = 3.8959 × 10− 4, Bio5 = 4.6343 × 10− 4, Bio10 = 0.0226, and Bio20 = 0.1207).

  Using Western blot analysis we showed that Dex treatment increased the expression of total NF-κB p65, p38 MAPK, and FoxO3a proteins in C2C12 myotubes but decreased the expression of Akt (p-values: total NF-κB p65 = 0.1397, p38 MAPK = 0.0543, FoxO3a = 0.0104, and Akt = 0.2526) (Figs. 2b–f, Supplementary Tables S35–S46, and Supplementary Figs. S1–S15). On the contrary, Bio downregulated the expression of total NF-κB p65, p38 MAPK—except for p38 MAPK after the treatment with Bio1—and FoxO3a proteins in Dex-treated C2C12 myotubes (p-values of total NF-κB p65: Bio1 = 0.8109, Bio5 = 0.5601, Bio10 = 0.5333, and Bio20 = 0.4052; p-values of p38 MAPK: Bio1 > 0.99, Bio5 = 0.6306, Bio10 = 0.5337, and Bio20 = 0.6166; p-values of FoxO3a: Bio1 = 0.0107, Bio5 = 0.0149, Bio10 = 9.1021 × 10− 4, and Bio20 = 0.0172). Similarly, Bio upregulated Akt expression (p-values: Bio1 > 0.99, Bio5 > 0.99, Bio10 = 0.5162, and Bio20 = 0.7604). Crucially, among the investigated proteins, only FoxO3a expression exhibited a statistically significant change (p < 0.05) both upon Dex induction and after treatment with Bio at all concentrations. Furthermore, dose-dependent changes were evident for total NF-κB p65 (downregulation) and Akt (upregulation) across the entire range of Bio concentrations (1–20 µg/mL), while p38 MAPK expression showed a clear dose-dependent reduction from 5 µg/mL to 20 µg/mL. However, the present Western blot analysis was limited in that the phosphorylated forms of NF-κB p65, p38 MAPK, FoxO3a, and Akt were not clearly detected in Bio- and Dex-treated C2C12 myotubes.


### Bio modulates miR-29b and myogenic gene expression
  We measured the expression of primary (pri)-, precursor (pre)-, and mature-miR-29b in Dex-treated C2C12 myotubes treated with 0–20 µg/mL Bio. All three forms of miR-29b showed dose-dependent downregulation (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Tables S47–S55; p-values of pri-miR-29b: Bio1 = 0.0457, Bio5 = 0.0476, Bio10 = 0.0100, and Bio20 = 2.3373 × 10− 4; p-values of pre-miR-29b: Bio1 > 0.99, Bio5 = 0.0815, Bio10 = 0.0104, and Bio20 = 0.0124; and p-values of mature-miR-29b: Bio1 = 0.0758, Bio5 = 0.0183, Bio10 = 0.0031, and Bio20 = 0.0011). We then measured the relative expression of myogenin and MyoD in Dex-treated C2C12 myotubes treated with Bio and transfected with a miR-29b mimic and miR-29b inhibitor. After transfection for 48 h, we confirmed the transfection efficiency using qRT-PCR as reported16,17. Transfection of miR-29b mimic decreased myogenin and MyoD expression and dose-dependently across all Bio treatment conditions (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Tables S56–S61; p-values of myogenin: Bio1 > 0.99, Bio5 = 0.0540, Bio10 = 0.0238, and Bio20 = 0.0078; p-values of MyoD: Bio1 > 0.99, Bio5 = 0.0641, Bio10 = 0.0027, and Bio20 = 0.0094). Conversely, transfection of miR-29b inhibitor increased myogenin and MyoD expression dose-dependently across all Bio treatment conditions (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Tables S62–S67; p-values of myogenin: Bio1 = 0.4901, Bio5 = 0.1073, Bio10 = 0.0597, and Bio20 = 0.0183; p-values of MyoD: Bio1 = 0.2620, Bio5 = 0.1705, Bio10 = 0.0093, and Bio20 = 0.0302).

The docking scores, representing the predicted binding affinity, indicated a strong potential for both proteins to interact with both mouse (mmu-) and human (hsa-) miR-29b.

  For the interactions between myogenin and miR-29b, we observed the strongest predicted binding affinity with the mature hsa-miR-29b-2-5p, which yielded the most negative docking score of − 293.32, along with the highest confidence score of 0.9462. Among the stem-loop forms, we observed the strongest binding affinity with both the mmu- and hsa-mir-29b-2 stem-loops (docking score = − 288.02).

  The overall binding affinities for the interactions between MyoD and miR-29b were generally stronger than those observed with myogenin. The most negative docking score was recorded for the stem-loop hsa-mir-29b-1 at − 312.14, accompanied by the highest confidence score of 0.9624. Among the mature forms, the mmu-mir-29b-3p and hsa-mir-29b-3p showed the strongest affinity for MyoD (docking score of both miRNAs = − 290.44), suggesting a significant role for the 3p strand in binding MyoD. Collectively, these results demonstrate a high theoretical likelihood that discrete mature and precursor forms of miR-29b can directly interact with key myogenic transcription factors, particularly MyoD and myogenin, suggesting their regulatory role in muscle molecular mechanisms.

## Discussion
  Here we demonstrate that skipjack tuna bone-derived Bio robustly counteracts Dex-induced atrophy in C2C12 myotubes. Dex treatment produced the expected atrophy phenotype—elevated expression of MuRF1 and atrogin-1 and suppressed myogenin and MyoD—consistent with inflammation-induced catabolism via FoxO3 and canonical NF-κB signaling pathways18,19. Co‑treatment with Bio countered these changes by rescuing decreased myotube diameter, reversing MuRF1/atrogin-1 upregulation, and restoring the mTOR signaling pathway. These findings align with known mechanisms whereby Dex inhibits the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway and activates FoxO3 expression, thereby inducing atrophy-related genes18,19. The ability of Bio to reactivate total Akt expression and suppress FoxO3a expression suggests a shift back toward anabolism. As widely documented, Akt phosphorylation sequesters FoxO transcription factors in the cytosol and prevents atrophy-related gene induction18,19. In our in vitro findings, Bio reversed the Dex-mediated suppression of Akt signaling (demonstrated by dose-dependently increased total Akt expression) and prevented FoxO3a expression, thereby rescuing downstream protein synthesis. Support for protein synthesis rescue is further provided by evidence that stimulating PI3K/Akt can counteract Dex‑induced FoxO3a activation and muscle wasting19.

  Notably, by modulating markers of atrophy-promoting inflammation in skeletal muscle, Bio exerted significant anti‑inflammatory effects in the Dex model20. Dex increased pro‑inflammatory mediators (NO, IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β) and may also activate canonical NF-κB and p38 MAPK signaling pathways. Bio markedly suppressed these inflammatory markers and signaling molecules. Elevated pro‑inflammatory mediators activate NF-κB and drive muscle catabolism, while compounds that inhibit NF-κB or oxidative stress can ameliorate muscle atrophy20. For instance, the dipeptide carnosine was recently shown to protect C2C12 myotubes from Dex by reducing reactive oxygen species and FoxO3a–mediated atrophy-related gene expression21. Similarly, Bio’s dose-dependent downregulation of total NF-κB p65 and p38 MAPK is the likely foundation for the reduced proinflammatory cytokine output and myotube preservation we observed. Thus, Bio appears to break the cycle of Dex‑triggered inflammation and proteolysis, reminiscent of other nutraceuticals targeting inflammatory pathways to maintain muscle integrity20,21.

  At the molecular level, Bio modulated key regulatory axes of protein turnover: Bio-treated myotubes showed decreased expression of MuRF1 and atrogin-1 and increased activity of MTOR, indicating a shift toward protein synthesis and away from degradation. This aligns with Bio’s effect of maintaining IGF‑1/PI3K/Akt signaling pathways: by restoring total Akt expression, Bio would inactivate FoxO3a, preventing it from transcribing atrophy-related genes19. Akt-mediated phosphorylation of FoxO is known to sequester FoxO in the cytosol, thereby blocking muscle wasting genes18. By the same token, Bio’s inhibition of total p38 MAPK (a stress-activated kinase) likely contributes, since p38 has been implicated in oxidative stress-induced atrophy-related gene expression22. Together, these shifts suggest that Bio relieves the Dex-driven suppression of anabolic signaling, thereby re-establishing proteostatic balance. We acknowledge that a technical constraint in our Western blot analysis was the inability to clearly detect the phosphorylated forms of NF-κB p65, p38 MAPK, and Akt on the membranes. Consequently, the interpretation of Bio’s involvement in these signaling pathways remains preliminary, as definitive conclusions regarding pathway activation typically require phosphorylation-specific evidence. To address this, future studies are planned to incorporate phosphorylation-specific Western blotting to conclusively delineate the activation kinetics of these pathways. However, measuring total protein levels still provides a valuable overview of the regulatory trends in canonical signaling. Although the changes in total NF-κB p65, p38 MAPK, and Akt did not consistently reach statistical significance, these non-significant findings suggest that Bio’s protective effects may stem from modulating protein activity or phosphorylation status rather than altering total protein abundance. This interpretation is supported by the dose-dependent downregulation of downstream atrophic markers, such as MuRF1 and atrogin-1, and the suppression of proinflammatory cytokines (IL-6 and TNF-α), which are well-documented targets of these signaling pathways. Thus, while the total protein data are indicative of potential trends, the biological significance of Bio’s action is confirmed by the robust modulation of these downstream functional outcomes.

  Importantly, Bio acted on the microRNA landscape to enhance myogenic capacity by reducing pri-, pre-, and mature-miR-29b levels in Dex-treated C2C12 myotubes. This modulation is of high clinical relevance, as miR-29b is now recognized as a pivotal therapeutic target for sarcopenia and frailty23. In elderly Chinese patients with sarcopenia and high cardiovascular risk, miR-29b expression is significantly correlated with appendicular skeletal muscle mass24,25. MiR-29b is a well-recognized mediator of muscle atrophy, promoting degradation by targeting IGF‑1 and PI3K (thereby inhibiting the Akt pathway) and through its inhibition, alleviating Dex- and cytokine-induced atrophy9. Li et al. showed that miR-29b overexpression drives atrophy, whereas its blocking rescues myotubes from Dex and TNF-α by preserving the IGF1–PI3K/Akt signaling pathway9. Consistent with this, recent CRISPR studies demonstrated that miR-29b suppression activates Akt/mTOR and protects against diverse atrophic stimuli26. The well documented NF-κB–YY1–miR-29b regulatory circuit has illuminated the molecular connection between inflammation and miR-29b13. The finding that Bio simultaneously suppresses total NF-κB p65 expression and miR-29b biogenesis suggests a coordinated mechanism whereby attenuation of inflammatory pathway disrupts transcriptional programs promoting miR-29b expression under atrophic stress. Together, these findings suggest that Bio downregulates miR-29b indirectly through suppression of inflammatory NF-κB–dependent transcriptional programs, rather than through a direct interaction with the miRNA itself.

  To further explore whether the Bio-mediated suppression of miR-29b could relieve post-transcriptional repression of myogenic regulators, molecular docking simulations were employed as a hypothesis-generating approach to assess potential interactions between miR-29b and myogenin or MyoD. The predicted docking scores and binding affinities were consistently similar across both mouse and human sequences, suggesting that this regulatory relationship may be evolutionarily conserved and supporting the translational relevance of Bio’s action. Direct experimental validation of these predicted interactions, such as RNA immunoprecipitation or luciferase reporter assays, will be required in future studies. Nevertheless, the concordance between docking predictions, miR-29b gain- and loss-of-function experiments, and the restoration of MyoD and myogenin expression by Bio supports the biological plausibility of this regulatory axis. By suppressing miR-29b, Bio could thus relieve a post-transcriptional block on these factors, as seen when a miR-29b inhibitor restored myogenin and MyoD expression in Dex-treated C2C12 myotubes. Conversely, the introduction of an miR-29b mimic markedly suppressed the expression of these myogenic markers, confirming that miR-29b exerts a direct inhibitory effect on the myogenic differentiation under atrophic conditions. Bio’s downregulation of miR-29b is likely to contribute to its effects via both the Akt/FoxO axis and direct enhancement of myogenic factor expression9,26.

  These multiple mechanisms reflect Bio’s unique composition and highlight its translational potential. Unlike conventional calcium supplements such as calcium carbonate and calcined bone powder, which also contain calcium and phosphorus, skipjack tuna bone-derived Bio also contains a rich supply of collagen and bioactive peptides6–8. The superiority of Bio over traditional inorganic sources is supported by previous comparative studies demonstrating that skipjack tuna bone-derived biocalcium exhibits higher bioavailability and absorption than inorganic calcium carbonate and calcined bone, likely due to the presence of peptides that keep calcium in a soluble, absorbable form within the gastrointestinal tract7,8. Furthermore, while inorganic calcium salts primarily provide structural minerals, the organic-mineral complex in Bio may exert direct anti-atrophic effects through its peptide components, a synergy not found in conventional supplements. Dakhovnik et al. demonstrated that supplementation with a specific ratio of collagen-derived amino acids (glycine, proline, and hydroxyproline) significantly improved muscle strength and physical function in vivo27. Given that Bio is rich in these specific amino acids—with glycine and imino acids comprising a substantial portion of its organic matrix6—it is highly likely that these components work synergistically with calcium to promote muscle growth27. This further distinguishes Bio from inorganic calcium salts, which lack the bioactive amino acid profile necessary to elicit such comprehensive proteostatic benefits. Characterization using Scanning Electron Microscopy with Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy reveals that Bio possesses a more intense distribution of calcium and phosphorus on its surface compared to original bone, following the effective removal of non-collagenous organic matter6. Additionally, the treatment process significantly enhances the physical quality of the supplement, resulting in a marked increase in whiteness and a near-total reduction in volatile compounds associated with fishy odors, such as heptanal and 1-octen-3-ol6. By retaining an associated matrix that chelates calcium ions, this organic-mineral complex shields them from binding with phosphates and prevents the crystallization of insoluble salts in the small intestine, thereby maintaining calcium in a soluble state for a longer duration and significantly enhancing its absorption efficiency compared with calcium carbonate8. Calcium itself is critical in muscle health—driving excitation–contraction coupling and acting as a second messenger in mTOR and Ca2+/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase signaling to regulate protein metabolism, and as a critical requirement for calpain proteases involved in myogenesis and protein turnover28. Bio may promote muscle maintenance through both its mineral content and its bioactive organic components, making it a functional nutraceutical. Chronic inflammation, inadequate nutrition, and aging all contribute to sarcopenia20, and Bio’s ability to mitigate inflammation, restore Akt/mTOR signaling, and suppress muscle-degrading miRNAs positions it as a promising preventive strategy against sarcopenia, borne out by consistent epidemiological evidence linking higher serum calcium with reduced sarcopenia risk28, and further supporting the protective role of calcium-based supplements in muscle mass preservation. Bio’s superior effects on musculoskeletal health are likely driven by the synergistic action between chelated calcium and intrinsic peptides29. The dose-dependent protective effects of Bio were observed predominantly within the 5–20 µg/mL range. While we observed consistent improvements in myotube atrophy as the dose increased, we identified 20 µg/mL as the practical upper limit for this effect. Beyond this concentration, specifically at 30 µg/mL, the onset of significant cytotoxicity—evidenced by reduced metabolic activity in the MTT assay and increased LDH leakage—precluded further evaluation of dose-dependent Bio’s effects.

  The sustainable production of Bio from skipjack tuna bone frames—a high-volume by-product of the tuna industry—adds environmental value through seafood waste valorization. However, it is important to acknowledge the inherent limitations in establishing definitive causality based on these in vitro observations. While our data demonstrate that Bio treatment consistently aligns with the amelioration of dexamethasone-induced atrophy and the suppression of inflammatory signaling, these outcomes represent strong correlations within a simplified cellular environment. While myotube diameter served as a quantitative indicator for assessing atrophy rescue in the current study, we recognize that a more systematic characterization of cellular morphology—including the quantification of cell fusion indices and alignment patterns—would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of Bio’s protective role. These additional morphological parameters will be prioritized in our future investigations to more deeply characterize the structural preservation of muscle fibers under atrophic stress. Furthermore, the multi-factorial nature of sarcopenia—involving systemic crosstalk and endocrine regulation—cannot be fully replicated in a myotube model. Therefore, while the concordance between miR-29b modulation and the rescue of myogenic markers is compelling, these mechanistic links should be interpreted as potential pathways that require further validation through targeted loss-of-function studies to establish direct causal relationships. Future research should utilize rodent models of aging-related (e.g., SAMP8 mice30or dexamethasone-induced sarcopenia to evaluate muscle mass/body weight ratios and fiber cross-sectional area within a complex physiological environment. These studies will allow us to assess whether Bio can preserve muscle contraction force and normalize systemic miR-29b regulation across different muscle groups. From a therapeutic perspective, the ability of Bio to suppress miR-29b suggests a novel intervention for sarcopenia. Beyond its role as a structural mineral source, Bio acts as a molecular modulator that could potentially be used to restore muscle proteostasis in elderly patients. Transitioning to human subjects, pilot randomized controlled trials involving elderly individuals with early-stage sarcopenia are warranted to assess the long-term safety, optimal dosage, and efficacy of Bio as a nutritional intervention31. These clinical approaches would evaluate functional outcomes such as grip strength, gait speed (via the short physical performance battery), and imaging-based assessments of muscle mass using Dual-energy X-ray Absorptiometry32–34. Such studies will address key limitations of the current cell-based system, including the absence of systemic inflammation, endocrine regulation, gastrointestinal nutrient absorption, and organ-system crosstalk that significantly influence muscle homeostasis in animal models and humans.


## Conclusions
  Skipjack tuna bone‑derived Bio effectively rescues C2C12 myotubes from Dex‑induced atrophy, as evidenced by the dose-dependent restoration of myotube diameter. This protective effect is achieved by reducing inflammation, suppressing protein degradation (canonical NF-κB, p38 MAPK, and FoxO3a signaling pathways and MuRF1 and atrogin-1 expression), and enhancing protein synthesis pathways (Akt and MTOR expression). Crucially, Bio acts by lowering miR-29b levels; the functional significance of this was validated by miR-29b inhibitor and mimic transfections, which respectively promoted and suppressed myogenin and MyoD expression. These combined actions enhance myogenic differentiation, supported by reactivated MyoD and myogenin expression (Fig. 6). This study underscores Bio’s promise as a functional food supplement for preserving muscle health and preventing sarcopenia.

## Methods

### Materials
  We purchased mouse (Mus musculus) C2C12 myoblasts from the ATCC Cell Bank (Biomedia, Bangkok, Thailand). We purchased Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) and supplements (10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, 2% inactivated horse serum, and 100× antibiotic–antimycotic) from Gibco Co. (Bangkok, Thailand). We purchased an RNeasy Kit for RNA Purification from Qiagen (Valencia, CA, USA). We purchased the other reagents from Sigma Chemical Co. (Bangkok, Thailand).


### Preparation of skipjack tuna bone-derived Bio
  We prepared skipjack tuna bone-derived Bio as reported6,8. Pre-cooked bones of skipjack tuna (average size 6.5–7.5 kg/fish) were obtained from a local cannery (Songkla Canning Public Co., Ltd., Songkhla, Thailand). Approximately 20 kg of bones were transported within 1 h to the Department of Food Technology, Prince of Songkla University, Hat Yai, in polyethylene bags and stored at − 20 °C until further use (not longer than 1 month). For pretreatment, bones were cut longitudinally into halves using an electric saw (W210E, Union Kitchen & Service, Bangkok, Thailand) and cleaned with a high-pressure water jet (Andaman, Zinsano, Bangkok, Thailand; 120 bar, 360 L/h) for 1–2 min to remove adhering flesh and other residues.

  To remove residual non-collagenous proteins as reported6, the cleaned bones were soaked in 2 M NaOH at a bone-to-solution ratio of 1:10 (w/v) at 50 °C for 30 min under continuous stirring (150 rpm) with an overhead stirrer (RW 20.n, IKA-Werke GmbH & Co. KG, Staufen, Germany). The alkaline solution was discarded, and the bones were rinsed thoroughly with running water until the effluent reached a neutral or slightly alkaline pH. The treated samples were briefly neutralized with 1.25 mM HCl, washed again, and subsequently dried in a rotary tray dryer (50 °C, 2 h; air velocity 1.5 m/s). The dried material was crushed with a milling machine (YCM-1.1E, Yor Yong Hah Heng, Bangkok, Thailand) to yield particles of approximately 3–4 mm.

  For lipid removal, the ground bone samples were extracted twice with hexane at a ratio of 1:10 (w/v) at 25 °C for 60 min with continuous stirring. The solvent was replaced once, and the samples were left at room temperature until completely dry and free of solvent odor.

  Benjakul et al. reported that bleaching using both sodium hypochlorite and hydrogen peroxide increases the whiteness of the defatted bones and decreases redness and yellowness6. Accordingly, the defatted bones were bleached in 2.5% (v/v) sodium hypochlorite for 30 min at room temperature with gentle stirring, followed by thorough rinsing. A second bleaching step was carried out using 2.5% (v/v) hydrogen peroxide for 60 min under similar conditions. After washing with running water, the samples were dried in a rotary tray dryer at 50 °C for 5 h.

  Finally, the dried bone material (named Bio) was finely ground in a planetary ball mill (PM 100, Retsch GmbH, Haan, Germany) with 25 stainless steel balls (20 mm diameter) at 200 rpm for 2.5 h. The powder was sieved to obtain a particle size below 75 μm and stored in airtight containers for further analysis.


### Cell culture
  We cultured C2C12 myoblasts in DMEM. Before use, we added 10% fetal bovine serum and antibiotic–antimycotic to DMEM. We incubated all cell lines in a humidified chamber (5% CO2 and 37 °C). We cultured semiconfluent C2C12 myoblasts on 6-well plates containing DMEM with 2% horse serum and antibiotic–antimycotic solution for 1 d to induce differentiation into C2C12 myotubes. We replenished the culture medium every 2 d. We then treated C2C12 myotubes with 1 µM Dex for 24 h to mimic muscle atrophy35–38. We treated Dex-treated C2C12 myotubes with Bio for further experiments. The Dex-only treated C2C12 myotubes served as the positive control, while Dex-untreated C2C12 myotubes were utilized as the negative control.


### Cell viability and cytotoxicity analyses
  For cell viability analysis, we seeded 3 × 103 cells/mL of Dex-treated C2C12 myotubes in 96-well plates for 1 d. We then added 10 µL of 1 mg/mL MTT solution to each sample well and incubated the cells in a humidified chamber for 4 h. After the incubation period, we discarded the supernatant and added 100 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide to each sample well. We shook the 96-well plates for 10 min and measured absorbance using a microplate spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Multiskan FC, Pittsburgh PA, USA) at 570 nm.

  For cell cytotoxicity analysis, LDH release was measured using the CytoTox 96 Non-Radioactive Cytotoxicity Assay kit (Promega, Bangkok, Thailand) according to the manufacturer’s protocol and as reported39,40. Briefly, Dex-treated C2C12 myotubes were seeded at 3 × 10³ cells/mL in 96-well plates for 1 d. Then, the cell culture supernatant was collected and incubated with the LDH substrate. The absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Multiskan FC, Pittsburgh, PA, USA).


### Myotube morphology examination
  The structural integrity and diameter of C2C12 myotubes were evaluated using an EVOS M5000 inverted microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Pittsburgh PA, USA). Microscopy images were analyzed using ImageJ software version 1.54g (https://imagej.net/ij/). Myotube diameters were measured at the points where they intersected two orthogonal axes drawn through the center of each image. To minimize bias, an independent researcher performed the analysis in a blinded manner. For each experimental condition, a minimum of 50 myotubes were analyzed in triplicate, and the average values were used for subsequent statistical comparisons.


### Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)
  We seeded C2C12 myotubes in each condition in six-well plates (5 × 103 cells/well) and then used RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) to extract total RNA. The procedure of RNA extraction was performed as reported by Cheng et al.39. We used ReverTra Ace qPCR Kit (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan) to convert the extracted RNA into complementary DNA and used Fast-Start SYBR Green Master Mix (Roche Diagnostic, Mannheim, Germany) to measure the expression levels of IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, MuRF-1, atrogin-1, MTOR, myogenin, and MyoD. We normalized the expression of each target gene to that of β-actin. We used the following specific primer sequences for qRT-PCR amplification: IL-6 forward 5′-GGCGGATCGGATGTTGTGAT-3′, reverse 5′-GGACCCCAGACAATCGGTTG-3′; TNF-α forward 5′-GGAACACGTCGTGGGATAATG-3′, reverse 5′-GGCAGACTTTGGATGCTTCTT-3′; IL-1β forward 5′-CCACCTTTTGACAGTGATGAG-3′, reverse 5′-CCAGGTCAAAGGTTTGGAAGC-3′; MuRF-1 forward 5′-GGAACCTGCTGGTGGAAAACATC-3′, reverse 5′-CGTCTTCGTGTTCCTTGCACATC-3′; atrogin-1 forward 5′-GCAGAGAGTCGGCAAGTC-3′, reverse 5′-CAGGTCGGTGATCGTGAG-3′; MTOR forward 5′-ACCGGCACACATTTGAAGAAG-3′, reverse 5′-CTCGTTGAGGATCAGCAAGG-3′; myogenin forward 5′- CAACCAGGAGGAGCGCGATCTCCG-3′, reverse 5′- AGGCGCTGTGGGAGTT GCATTCACT-3′; MyoD forward 5′-CCCCGGCGGCAGAATGGCTACG-3′, reverse 5′- GGTCTGGGTTCCCTGTTCTGTGT-3′; β-actin forward 5′-TCGTGCGTGACATCAAAGA-3′, reverse 5′-CATACCCAAGAAGGAAGGCT-3′.

  We measured miR-29b expression as reported40–42. We extracted miRNA-enriched total RNA using an miRNeasy kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA). We synthesized first-strand complementary DNA from 1 µg RNA using miScript II RT Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA). We performed qRT-PCR analysis of pri-, pre-, and mature-miR-29b using miScript primer assays (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA), a SYBR Green polymerase chain reaction kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA), and ABI Prism 7500 HT sequence detection system (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA). U6 was used as reference gene (forward: 5′-CTCGCTTCGGCAGCACA-3′, reverse: 5′-AACGCTTCACGAATTTGCGT-3′). The relative expression of miR-29b was calculated using 2−ΔΔCt method41,42.


### Detection of cytokines using ELISA
  We detected IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β using a modified protocol based on Jantarawong et al.43. We cultured C2C12 myotubes on 6-well plates (1 × 105 cells/well), incubated them overnight, and treated them with Bio for 1 h and then with Dex for 6 h. We collected the cell supernatant by centrifugation. We measured IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β concentrations in the supernatants of each sample using mouse IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β ELISA MAX Deluxe sets (BioLegend, Advanced Medical Science, Bangkok, Thailand), respectively.


### Measurement of NO production
  We used a colorimetric assay to measure the concentration of nitrite, a soluble oxidation product of NO, released from the cells. We seeded Dex- and Bio-treated C2C12 myotubes (5 × 105 cells/mL) on a 96-well plate overnight. Nitrite concentrations were determined using a Griess reagent kit (Abcam, Biomed Diagnostics, Bangkok, Thailand) through a colorimetric assay based on the Griess reaction. The absorbance of the collected cell supernatants was read at 540 nm with a spectrophotometer (Multiskan FC, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Pittsburgh, PA, USA).


### Western blot analysis
  We seeded the cells in each condition in six-well plates (4 × 105 cells/well). We then extracted proteins from cell lysates using a RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Washington DC, USA). We centrifuged the cell lysates at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C before collecting the supernatant. We employed SDS-PAGE to separate the proteins on a 12% acrylamide gel (at 100 V for 90 min). The proteins were then transferred to polyvinyl difluoride membranes (100 V, 60 min). The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBS-Tween at room temperature for 4 h and incubated at 4 °C overnight with anti-total NF-κB p65, anti-p38 MAPK, anti-FoxO3a, anti-Akt, and anti-β-actin (Cell Signaling Technology, Beverly, MA, USA) diluted 1:1,000 in blocking buffer. Then, the membranes were washed with TBS-Tween three times for 10 min each, incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology, Beverly, MA, USA; diluted 1:10,000 in blocking buffer) at room temperature for 2 h, and washed with TBS-Tween three times for 10 min each. Protein bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence substrate and visualized using the Chemiluminescence Vilber (Fusion Solo S) digital imaging system (Vilber, France).


### MiRNA transfection
  The miRNA transfection was performed as reported by Jantarawong et al.44. We transfected an miR-29b mimic and miR-29b inhibitor (Invitrogen, NY, USA) into Dex-treated C2C12 myotubes in OptiMEM medium (Invitrogen, NY, USA) using HiPerfect transfection reagent (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA), as described40,45. After 48 h, we analyzed myogenin and MyoD expression in the transfected cells.


### Ethics declarations
  We confirm that no live vertebrates were used in this study. The Bio was derived from skipjack tuna bone by-products obtained from a commercial cannery, and the study utilized in vitro cell line models.


### Statistical analysis
  We present all data as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and derived them from experiments conducted in triplicate or with 6 to 8 replicates, depending on the assay. We calculated effect sizes using Cohen’s d to assess the magnitude of differences between experimental and control groups. Absolute values of Cohen’s d of more than 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 were interpreted as small, medium, and large effects, respectively. We evaluated the normality of data distribution within each group using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For datasets where all groups followed a normal distribution, we assessed differences between two groups (for instance, treated vs. control) using a one-tailed Student’s t-test. If at least one group in a given dataset did not meet the assumption of normality, we made group comparisons using the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test (one-tailed). The use of one-tailed tests was justified by our directional hypothesis that Bio treatment would specifically improve markers in C2C12 myotubes compared to the Dex-induced atrophy group. We defined statistical significance as p < 0.05, which served as the primary threshold for identifying biologically relevant changes, and indicated significant differences with an asterisk (*) or hash (#) in all relevant bar graphs. To account for multiple comparisons among the different Bio treatment groups and the Dex-induced atrophy group, p-values were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction. Statistical significance was defined as an adjusted p < 0.05, ensuring the reliability of our findings across multiple experimental conditions.

We visualized molecular interactions using the BIOVIA discovery studio visualizer (version 21.1.0.20298; https://discover.3ds.com/discovery-studio-visualizer-download/, accessed on October 24, 2025).
