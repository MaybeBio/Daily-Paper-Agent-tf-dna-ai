# Tardigrade Dsup: Interactions with DNA and protection of cells from oxidative stress


## Abstract
  The remarkable capability of Tardigrade to survive under extreme conditions has been partially attributed to Dsup, an intrinsically disordered, highly positively charged protein. Dsup has been shown to bind to DNA in vitro, a property that has been associated with the capability of Dsup to exhibit stress-protective effects when expressed in mammalian cells. However, DNA binding of Dsup has not been visualized in living cells and expression of Dsup in different cell types was associated with either protective or detrimental effects. In addition, the effect of Dsup expression has not been clearly demonstrated at the organism level. Here we combined molecular dynamics (MD) simulations and fluorescence lifetime imaging microscopy (FLIM)-Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) to interrogate Dsup-DNA interactions and demonstrate Dsup binding to DNA in live mammalian cells. Furthermore, Dsup expression in both HEK293T cells and yeast enhanced cell survival in the presence of hydrogen peroxide, suggesting that the presence of Dsup allows both mammalian and yeast cells to better cope with oxidative stress. This study provides a better understanding of the property and functional role of Dsup and lays a foundation to explore new approaches to enhance stress resistance.


## INTRODUCTION
  Tardigrades are a phylum of microorganisms that exhibit incredible resistance to stress. They possess the ability to survive under extreme conditions such as intense heat and pressure and have been described as “practically unkillable”. They can survive in a wide variety of environments from scorching deserts to outer space, placing them in a category of animals known as extremophiles, a group of organisms known for their extreme survivability. This incredible resistive capability can be attributed to a combination of the damage suppressor protein Dsup and cryptobiosis (1). Dsup has been found in the tardigrade Ramazzottius varieornatus and is partially responsible for the tardigrade’s resistive qualities.

  Dsup is a 445-amino acid protein that is characterized by being largely unstructured and highly charged. Charged residues, predominantly basic ones including 59 lysines and 12 arginines together with 29 aspartates and 19 glutamates, account for 26.7% of the total amino acid composition. A computational study suggests that the intrinsically disordered nature of the protein enables Dsup to adopt different conformations to fit the shape of DNA (2). The electrostatic interactions facilitated by the charged residues and high protein flexibility lead to the formation of “a molecular aggregate” in which Dsup shields DNA. Dsup has been reported to localize in the nucleus and bind to chromatin when expressed in yeast (3). In vitro studies demonstrate that Dsup directly binds to free DNA in a seemingly nonspecific manner (4). Moreover, Dsup can also associate with nucleosomes, suggesting an additional layer of chromatin protection (5). Functionally, Dsup has been demonstrated to protect DNA from damage. For example, Dsup expressed in cultured HEK293T cells suppressed X-ray induced DNA damage by 40% (4); local and transient expression of Dsup reduced radiation-induced DNA damage in healthy tissue surrounding oral cancer in mice (6). However, in some cases, directly expressing Dsup in cortical neurons resulted in the formation of DNA double-stranded breaks, thereby promoting DNA damage (7). Thus, neither Dsup’s interactions with DNA nor its functional effects have been well understood.

  Oxygen, a crucial component for the survival of many organisms, has the tendency to produce reactive oxygen species (ROS). Common types of reactive oxygen species are the hydroxyl radical, the superoxide radical, and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). These types of ROS are typically due to errors in the electron transport chain during cellular respiration (8), and can also be induced by environmental stimuli such as exposure to ultraviolet radiation and toxic heavy metals such as cadmium and lead (9). ROS causes oxidative stress to cells, contributes to the aging process, and is linked to the onset and progression of various diseases such as cancer, Parkinson’s, Alzheimer’s, and cardiovascular diseases (10). ROS can cause many different forms of damage to DNA, including strand breaks, base substitution, and protein-DNA cross-links. These damages result in either programmed cell death or compromised DNA functionality, which could lead to the development of cancer (8). The effects of Dsup on protecting cultured cells and whole organisms other than Tardigrades from ROS-mediated DNA damages await further investigation.

  In this study, we applied MD simulations to probe Dsup-DNA interactions in silico and used Fluorescence Lifetime Imaging Microscopy with Forster Resonance Energy Transfer (FLIM-FRET) to directly visualize these interactions in live mammalian cells. Furthermore, using HEK293T cells as well as a yeast strain expressing Dsup under the control of doxycycline, we tested the effect of Dusp on cell survival upon exposure to H2O2. Our study sheds light on the damage suppressor protein’s ability to protect DNA in different living systems, bringing us one step closer to a possible solution for oxidative stress linked diseases such as cancer and other age-related diseases.


## MATERIALS AND METHODS

### Cell culture and transfection
  HEK293T (ATCC, CRL-3216) cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) (Gibco) containing 4.5 g l−1 glucose, 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Sigma), and 1% (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin (Pen-Strep) (Sigma-Aldrich). All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. For imaging experiments, cells were plated onto sterile 35-mm glass-bottomed dishes and grown to 50–70% confluence. Transient transfection was performed using PolyJet following manufacturer’s protocols, after which cells were cultured for an additional 24 h.


### HEK293T stable cell line generation
  HEK293T stable cell lines were generated using lentiviral plasmid vectors (plenti-TetO-Dsup-EGFP and plenti-rtTA) by dual-infection. To package the lentivirus containing TetO-Dsup-EGFP and rtTA, each pLenti-construct was mixed with psPAX2 and pMD2.G-VSV-g (3:2:1) and co-transfected into HEK293T cells. The medium containing packaged lentivirus was collected after 24 h and directly added to HEK293T in 6 cm cultured dish to generate rtTA + TetO-Dsup-EGFP dual-stable cells. After 24 h, cells were diluted and plated in 10 cm cell culture dishes and then treated with Puromycin (1 μg mL−1) and Blasticidin (10 μg mL−1) to select transduced cells. For cell maintenance, cells were cultured in the medium with Puromycin (0.5 μg mL−1) and Blasticidin (5 μg mL−1). Doxycycline will induce the protein expression only when both rtTA and TetO-Dsup-EGFP integrated to cellular genome.


### CCK-8 colorimetric assay
  Cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 15k cells per well in 100 μL of culture medium and incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 24 hours. Various concentrations of H2O2 and 12.5 ng mL−1 of Doxycycline were added to the cells for 48 hours. Following this, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the plate was incubated for an additional 1 hr. After gently mixing the plate on an orbital shaker, the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.


### Yeast cell culture
  Standard methods for the growth, maintenance and transformation of yeast and bacteria and for manipulation of DNA were used throughout. The yeast strains used in this study were generated from BY4741 (MATa his3D1 leu2D0 met15D0 ura3D0) strain background. A plasmid with yeast codon-optimized Dsup driven by doxycycline-inducible Tet-on promoter system (NHB1054: untagged Dsup, or NHB1086: N-terminus tagged Dsup) was digested by NotI and integrated to the Leu2D0 locus of the background strain.


### Yeast viability experiments
  Wild type and Dsup cultures were incubated overnight to early exponential phase (OD ~0.6 with our OD meter), and then diluted and separated into 8 tubes. 4 of the tubes are the Dsup experiment group and cells were induced with 500 nM doxycycline. The other 4 tubes are the control group and have no doxycycline induction. The cells were incubated for 3 hours and grew to OD ~0.6. The Dsup experiment groups were then centrifuged down and resuspended in SD media without doxycycline and allowed to recover for 1 hour. 2 mM hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) was then added to each tube. At the designated time points after addition of H2O2, cells were collected by centrifugation before staining with Propidium Iodide (PI) dye (final concentration 6 μg mL−1) at room temperature for 2 minutes. Then 3 μL of each cell sample was transferred onto a glass slide and imaged with fluorescence microscopy.


### Fluorescence imaging
  Microscopy experiments were performed using a Nikon Ti-E inverted fluorescence microscope with Perfect Focus, coupled with an EMCCD camera (Andor iXon X3 DU897). The light source is a spectra X LED system. Images were taken using a CFI plan Apochromat Lambda DM 60× oil immersion objective (NA 1.40 WD 0.13MM). The exposure and intensity setting for each channel were set as follows: Phase 50 ms, iRFP 300 ms at 10% lamp intensity with an EM Gain of 50, mRuby 50 ms at 10% lamp intensity with an EM Gain of 200. The EM Gain settings are within the linear range.


### FLIM-FRET imaging and analysis
  HEK293T cells were imaged using a Leica SP8 FALCON confocal microscope equipped with a 63×/1.4 NA oil-immersion objective, a white-light laser (WLL), and a HyD SMD single molecule detector (Leica). For fluorescence imaging, EGFP was excited at 488 nm and collected with a 505–530 nm band-pass filter. SYTO™ 59 fluorescence was exited at 610 nm and collected with a 630–670 nm band-pass filter. For FLIM imaging, EGFP was excited at 488 nm by using the WLL at a repetition rate of 40 MHz. Images were acquired, collecting maximal 1,000 photons per pixel. A region of interest (ROI) corresponding to the cell nucleus was selected per cell. Fast FLIM maps (512×512 pixels), fitting of fluorescence decay curves, and amplitude-weighted mean fluorescence lifetimes were calculated in LAS X (Lecia) software by fitting a bi-exponential decay model to the decay of each ROI. Graphs were plotted using Prism 8 (GraphPad).


### MD simulations
  The sequence of wild-type (WT) Dsup residues 361–445 (Dsup for short) was from Uniprot entry P0DOW4: 361-PPRRSSRLTS SGTGAGSAPA AAKGGAKRAA SSSSTPSNAK KQATGGAGKA AATKATAAKS AASKAPQNGA GAKKKGGKAG GRKRK-445. The sequence of the 20E mutant (Dsup-20E for short) was generated by replacing all R and K residues with E, resulting in 361-PPEESSELTS SGTGAGSAPA AAEGGAEEAA SSSSTPSNAE EQATGGAGEA AATEATAAES AASEAPQNGA GAEEEGGEAG GEEEE-445. Disordered conformations of WT Dsup were generated using the TraDES method (13); three conformations with radii of gyration close to that expected (14) for an IDP with 85 residues (Rg = 2.54 N0.522 = 25.8 Å for N = 85) were selected for three Dsup chains. Similar to our previous study (15), a 35-base pair DNA was placed along the diagonal of a cubic box with a 120 Å side length; the three Dsup chains were randomly added to the box. Lastly, the system was solvated with water molecules, neutralized with eight K+ ions, and brought to a physiological salt concentration of 0.145 M by additional K+ and Cl− ions. The DNA, protein, water, and ion force fields were Amber OL21 (16), ff14SB (17), OPC (18), and the Joung and Cheatham ion parameters (19). The total number of atoms was 218,565.

  The system was energy minimized for 5000 steps (half in steepest descent and half in conjugate gradient) using the sander module of AMBER22 (20), followed by a 250 ps equilibration at constant NVT with a 1 fs time step, where the temperature was ramped from 0 to 300 K for the initial 100 ps and maintained at 300 K for the remaining 150 ps. Positional restraints were imposed on the Dsup chains and DNA with a force constant of 5 kcal mol–1 Å−2. Further equilibration was conducted without restraints for 1 ns with a 2-fs time step at constant NPT (300 K and 1 bar). Lastly, the simulations were continued in four replicates at constant NPT for 1 μs each with a 2-fs time step. All simulations were run on GPUs using pmemd.cuda (21). The cutoff for nonbonded interactions was 12 Å. Long-range electrostatic interactions were treated by the particle mesh Ewald method (22). The SHAKE algorithm (23) was used to restrain bonds involving hydrogens. The temperature was maintained at 300 K by the Langevin thermostat with a damping constant of 1 ps–1, and the pressure was maintained at 1 bar by the Berendsen barostat (24). The mutant system was prepared and simulated using the same protocol as described for the WT protein, except that charge neutralization was achieved by introducing 128 K+ ions. For DNA melting in the absence of Dsup, the 35-base pair DNA solvated by water and ions (68 K+ ions for charge neutralization and additional K+ and Cl− ions for 0.145 M salt concentration) was energy minimized for 5000 steps and then equilibrated for 250 ps at constant NVT with a 1 fs time step, where the temperature was ramped from 300 to 500 K for the initial 100 ps and maintained at 500 K for the remaining 150 ps. The simulations were further continued in four replicates at constant NVT (500 K) for 200 ns. The simulations of DNA melting in the presence of WT Dsup or the 20E mutant were very similar, except that the initial snapshot was taken from the simulations at 300 K and energy minimization was skipped.

  Snapshots were saved at 10 ps intervals, and analyses were done on the last 500 ns for the simulations at 300 K and for the entire trajectories for the simulations at 500 K. CPPTRAJ (25) and in-house python and tcl scripts were used to calculate contacts within 3.5 Å between heavy atoms of Dsup and DNA. A base pair was considered melted when any hydrogen bond was broken (donor–acceptor distance > 3 Å).


### Statistics and reproducibility
  All experiments were independently repeated as noted in the figure legends. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8. For Gaussian data, Student’s t test was used for pairwise comparisons. For comparing three or more sets of data, ordinary one-way ANOVA followed by multiple comparisons test was performed. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 with a 95% confidence interval. The number of cells analyzed (n cells) and number of independent experiments are reported in all figure legends.


## RESULTS

### MD simulations of Dsup-DNA interactions
  The DNA-binding region of Dsup was isolated to the C-terminal ~90 residues (5), which are rich in basic residues. To characterize the Dsup-DNA interactions in atomic details, we performed MD simulations of three copies of the C-terminal region (residues 361–445; 6 Arg, 14 Lys, no Asp or Glu) in complex with a 35-base pair DNA. The simulations were run in four replicates, each for 1000 ns. Similar to an arginine-rich intrinsically disordered protein protamine (15), the Dsup chains quickly collapsed around the DNA (Fig. 1a). The initial interactions were dominated by salt bridges between Dsup Arg and Lys residues and DNA backbone phosphates (snapshot at 53 ns). Soon after, these residues formed hydrogen bonds with nucleobases (snapshot at 110 ns). In the last 500 ns, all three Dsup chains stably bound the DNA (Fig. 1b). When averaged over the four replicate simulations, 34.3 residues of the Dsup chains interacted with phosphates and 8.2 residues interacted with bases; each Dsup-interacting phosphate engaged with 1.4 Dsup residues while each base-interacting residue engaged with 1.9 bases (Fig. 1c). We further broke down the bases into GC or AT types, and found a very modest preference of GC over AT (at a 1.1-to-1 ratio) when forming residue-base interactions.

  To highlight the roles of Arg and Lys residues in mediating the interactions of Dsup with DNA, we mutated all these residues into Glu. In the corresponding simulations with the 20E mutant, the interactions with the DNA backbone phosphates all but disappeared, and the number of Dsup-interacting nucleobases was reduced by nearly 90%.

  To demonstrate the protective effect of Dsup against duplex separation, we melted the DNA in four replicate simulations at 500 K in the absence or presence of Dsup. Without Dsup, the DNA melted completely at 110 ns (Fig. 1d). In contrast, with the three Dsup chains initially bound, the DNA melted much more slowly; 4.8 base pairs remained intact at 110 ns of simulations and 2–3 base pairs remained intact even at 200 ns. Figure 2 presents representative snapshots at 0, 20, and 132 ns of one simulation to illustrate the melting process. Base pair fraying started at the DNA termini, but the three Dsup chains maintained their contacts with the DNA (snapshot at 20 ns). As melting proceeded toward the middle section of the DNA, the three Dsup chains stayed bound, apparently forming a protective layer that slowed strand separation (snapshot at 132 ns). In particular, four base pairs (sequence numbering 15-GGGC-18 in the first strand) remained intact and the downstream base pair was still partially intact, thanks to interactions with numerous Arg and Lys residues (inset at 132 ns). In stark contrast, in the presence of the 20E mutant, complete melting of the DNA occurred quickly, at 86 ns. A simple salt like KCl at 0.145 M stabilizes DNA against melting by neutralizing backbone phosphates (melting completed at 92 ns without KCl), but Dsup has a much greater stabilizing effect by also engaging with nucleobases (Fig. 2). Conversely, the negative charges of the 20E mutant repel the DNA backbone and interfere with the stabilizing effect of KCl, thereby accelerating DNA melting. In short, the Arg and Lys residues of Dsup interact with both the backbone phosphates and nucleobases and thereby protect DNA against melting.


### FLIM-FRET demonstrates Dsup binding to DNA in living mammalian cells
  To directly probe Dsup-DNA interactions in live mammalian cells, we developed an assay based on FRET, a well-known spectroscopic ruler for monitoring molecular interactions in live cells. In combination with FLIM, FRET can be quantitatively read out through measuring the fluorescence lifetime changes of donor fluorophore. FLIM-FRET has been used as a reliable tool to detect in situ protein-DNA interactions in the cell nucleus (26). To establish a FLIM-FRET assay for Dsup-DNA interaction, we selected enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) as the FRET donor to fuse to the C-terminal of Dsup protein and SYTO™ 59 Red Fluorescent Nucleic Acid Stain as the FRET acceptor to stain DNA. When Dsup binds to DNA, the EGFP tag is expected to be in close proximity to SYTO™ 59 on DNA, leading to a shortened fluorescence lifetime of EGFP.

  To first establish the DNA-protein assay, we performed a positive control experiment using histone 2B (H2B)-EGFP which is known to be tightly wrapped by chromatin DNA. HEK293T cells transiently expressing H2B-EGFP were stained with SYTO™ 59, followed by FLIM-FRET imaging. Upon binding to DNA, the red fluorescence from SYTO™ 59 was mainly detected in the nuclear area. H2B-EGFP was co-localized in the nuclei with SYTO™ 59 stained DNA (Fig. 3a), and significantly short lifetime from H2B-EGFP was observed (τ = 1.78 ± 0.02 ns, n = 26 cells) compared to H2B-EGFP only cells (τ = 2.48 ± 0.02 ns, n = 17 cells) (Fig. 3b). Next, we stained HEK293T cells transiently expressing Dsup-EGFP with SYTO™ 59. As shown in Fig. 3a, we observed green fluorescence in the nuclei indicating the subcellular location of Dsup. We then performed FLIM-FRET measurements and observed significantly shorter Dsup-EGFP fluorescence lifetime within nuclei stained with SYTO™ 59 (τ = 1.79 ± 0.02 ns, n = 24 cells) compared with Dsup-EGFP cells without SYTO™ 59 staining (τ = 2.51 ± 0.01 ns, n = 19 cells) (Fig. 3c). To prove that the C-terminal region of Dsup is the main region where Dsup binds to DNA, we generated two mutant Dsup constructs: the C-terminal deletion of Dsup (Dsup-ΔC) and the 20E mutant within the C-terminal region of Dsup (Dsup-20E). To counter the possibility that the absence of Dsup-C or high negative charges in it might alter the localization of these Dsup mutants in the nucleus, we fused EGFP-NLS to both of them. After staining and imaging HEK293T cells expressing Dsup-ΔC-EGFP-NLS and Dsup-20E-EGFP-NLS, we observed no significant differences in lifetime when comparing Dsup-ΔC-EGFP-NLS (τ = 2.48 ± 0.01 ns, n = 45 cells) and Dsup-20E-EGFP-NLS (τ = 2.48 ± 0.01 ns, n = 37 cells) to non-stained cells (Dsup-ΔC, τ = 2.49 ± 0.01 ns, n = 60 cells; Dsup-20E, τ = 2.50 ± 0.01 ns, n = 48 cells) (Fig. 3d,e). To further rule out the possibility of non-specific intermolecular FRET, we performed a negative control experiment with EGFP-NLS expressed in the nucleus. From images, EGFP-NLS was largely directed to the nuclear plasm co-localizing with SYTO™ 59 stained DNA (Fig. 3a). As expected, EGFP-NLS expressing cells showed no difference in fluorescence lifetime with (τ = 2.45 ± 0.01 ns, n = 29 cells) or without SYTO™ 59 staining (τ = 2.45 ± 0.01 ns, n = 24 cells) (Fig. 3f). Together, these results provide direct evidence for association between Dsup and DNA within the nuclei of live mammalian cells.


### Dsup expression enhances cell survival in the presence of hydrogen peroxide
  High levels of hydrogen peroxide are known to induce oxidative stress and cause cell death through a variety of mechanisms including hydrogen peroxide-induced DNA damage (27, 28). We investigated the effect of Dsup expression on HEK293T cell survival exposed to H2O2. Overexpression of Dsup over an extended time has been reported to increase cell death (7); we observed a similar adverse effect in cell growth experiments using HEK293T cells (Fig. 4a). We thus generated a HEK293T stable cell line expressing Dsup-EGFP, regulated by a Tet-on promoter and doxycycline (29). As demonstrated in Fig. 4b, live-cell imaging revealed the nuclear localization of Dsup-EGFP upon doxycycline (Dox, 25 nM) induction, verifying the correct subcellular localization of this protein. Dox-induced Dsup expression was observed starting at 2 hour and became robust after 4 hours (Fig. 4c), thus we performed the following experiments with pre-inducing cells with Dox for 4 hours.

  To explore Dsup’s role in protecting genomic DNA against H2O2, we employed the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay, a widely used colorimetric technique for assessing cell proliferation. Cells were exposed to varying concentrations of H2O2 for 24 hours and dehydrogenase activities in cells were measured and compared. Notably, Dsup expression increased cell viability compared to no Dsup expression control at 0.25 mM and 0.5 mM H2O2, while no significant differences were observed at either lower or higher H2O2 concentrations (Fig. 4d). After a 48-hour treatment with H2O2, Dsup expression increased cell viability compared to no Dsup expression control under conditions from 0.125 mM to 0.5 mM H2O2 (Fig. 4e). The null effect at lower H2O2 can be explained by a lack of H2O2 damage, whereas that at higher H2O2 can be attributed to an overwhelming level of H2O2 damage. To further demonstrate that Dsup protects DNA damage from oxidative stress primarily through the C-terminal region binding to DNA, cells stably expressing mutant Dsup variants Dsup-ΔC-EGFP-NLS or Dsup-20E-EGFP-NLS were treated with H2O2 for 48 hours, followed by cell survival assay. Compared to both mutants, cells expressing WT Dsup showed increased cell viability (Fig. 4f). These findings suggest that Dsup expression in mammalian cells improves cell survival under oxidative stress, particularly in the presence of reactive oxygen species like hydrogen peroxide.


### Effects of Tardigrade Dsup on stress resistance of yeast
  To further evaluate the effects of Dsup on another organism, we turned to the budding yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae and generated a genetically engineered budding yeast strain where Dsup expression is under doxycycline control. Doxycycline was used to control the Tet-on promoter and express the Dsup tagged on the N-terminus by a red fluorescent protein mRuby (30) to track the expression level of Dsup. As shown in Fig. 5a, we observed red fluorescence in the nuclei, which colocalized with the Nhp4a-iRFP nuclear marker. We analyzed the expression of Dsup by tracking the red fluorescence intensity as a function of time in yeast cells with Dsup expression induced by 500 nM doxycycline, compared with no doxycycline control. The expression of Dsup in cells increased with time in the presence of doxycycline, whereas there was generally no Dsup expression in the absence of doxycycline, showing low leakage of the dox-inducible promoter system (Fig. 5b).

  We then treated yeast cells with H2O2 in liquid culture and used propidium iodide (PI) staining to identify severely damaged cells. In order to avoid adverse effects of fluorescent protein tagging on Dsup’s function, we used a yeast strain containing untagged, dox-inducible Dsup. As shown in Fig. 5c, five hours after H2O2 (2 mM) addition, cells with Dsup induction exhibited 11.6 ± 2.4% of cell death, compared to 41.8 ± 1.7% death by those without Dsup induction (Fig. 5c). This is due to more cells being severely damaged and stained by PI in non-induction cells. These results indicate that after five hours of exposure to hydrogen peroxide, the Dsup-expressing cells exhibited statistically significantly lower cell death than cells without Dsup. Thus, the expression of Dsup in yeast is beneficial for its survival under oxidative stresses caused by hydrogen peroxide.


## DISCUSSION
  Buildup of excess ROS places oxidative stress on cells, altering base pairs and causing double-stranded breaks, leading to impaired DNA functionality among other detrimental effects. Oxidative stress and the resulting damage can drive the aging process as well as cause the development of multiple diseases. Identification of factors that confer resistance to oxidative stress induced damage could aid in the slowing of the aging process and the prevention of serious diseases. The investigation of stress-resistance is therefore important for aging research and for addressing unmet needs of people affected by aging related disorders. Tardigrades, otherwise known as water bears, are near-microscopic animals well known for their incredible resistive capabilities under extreme conditions such as intense radiation and in outer space. The tardigrade Dsup protein was suggested to protect DNA from various stresses and damages. In this study, we investigated the interactions between Dsup and DNA both in silico and in live cells and probed the role of Dsup in protecting mammalian and yeast cells from H2O2-induced toxicity.

  To provide a mechanistic understanding of the protective effects of Dsup, we performed MD simulations of DNA binding with the C-terminal disordered region of Dsup. The simulations showed that Dsup chains wrap around DNA, with Arg and Lys interacting with the DNA backbone phosphates and nucleobases via salt bridges and hydrogen bonds, respectively. Through these interactions, Dsup may provide a physical barrier to prevent ROS-induced double-stranded breaks and damage on DNA base pairs. Additionally, Dsup may keep DNA from structural changes that are susceptible to damage. Indeed, our simulations at 500 K revealed that Dsup slows down the melting of DNA. Although we caution against drawing a direct link between slowing down in DNA melting (Fig. 1c) and reduction in cell death (Fig. 5c), it stands to reason that strand separation or other more subtle and transient structural changes can expose nucleobases and thereby make them more vulnerable to ROS-induced reactions.

  Dsup was shown to bind to both free DNA and nucleosome in vitro, with enhanced binding towards nucleosome (4, 5). Our data clearly demonstrates direct Dsup-DNA interactions in the nuclei of living mammalian cells, which complements the in vitro studies. Dsup-expressing mammalian cells can be readily stained by the red fluorescent nucleic acid dye, suggesting that the small molecule binding to DNA and Dsup binding to DNA are not interfering with each other. The FLIM-FRET-based imaging method for studying biomolecular interactions offers quantitative and sensitive readouts. Although widely used as a method for detecting protein-protein interactions, there were fewer examples of live-cell FLIM-FRET studies on interactions between DNA and DNA-binding proteins. Our study demonstrated that the GFP-SYTO based FLIM-FRET assay is an efficient method for detecting Dsup-DNA interactions in situ and should be readily extensible to other systems.

  We further showed that Dsup expression protects HEK293T cells from oxidative stress, consistent with the findings reported by Hashimoto et al. (4). However, Dsup expression in cortical neurons was reported to result in the formation of DNA double-stranded breaks and condensation of chromatin (7). Consistent with this report, we observed that HEK293T cells with high expression of Dsup grew more slowly than cells without Dsup (Fig. 4a). These results suggest that when overexpressed, Dsup’s interactions with DNA may impede the access of replication, transcription, and DNA repair machinery, thereby contributing to the adverse effects observed in neurons and other cell types.

  Budding yeast is a commonly used model organism due to its genetic tractability. Importantly, budding yeast retains many of the core eukaryotic cellular processes including elements of programmed cell death (PCD) such as apoptosis. Similar to mammalian cells, ROS plays a major role in PCD pathways in yeast (8). Furthermore, yeast is also a popular model organism to study the mechanisms of aging due to its short lifetime and genetic tractability (31). Prior to our study, the effects of Dsup in protecting yeast from oxidative stress have been reported (3). In agreement with that work, our study, by measuring the survival of yeast cells in liquid cultures, showed Dsup expression could protect yeast from hydrogen peroxide-induced stress and damage at millimolar concentration level. Together, these studies suggest that yeast could serve as a valuable model for investigating the mechanisms and potential applications of Dsup in slowing aging or mitigating age-related diseases. For instance, yeast aging is driven by genomic instability and mitochondrial dysfunction (32–35), both of which are influenced, at least in part, by ROS. It would be interesting to examine how Dsup impacts the progression of these key aging hallmarks and to identify strategies for optimizing its protective effects.

  Finally, we acknowledge that our study showed that Dsup’s protective function against oxidative stress is conserved in the two eukaryotic models tested here. However, its effects may be organism- or cell-type dependent, which warrants further investigation.
