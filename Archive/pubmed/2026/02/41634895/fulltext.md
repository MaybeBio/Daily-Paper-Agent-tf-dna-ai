# Multi-omics analysis and experiments validate the tumor-suppressive role of mitochondrial lipid metabolism gene ACSM5 in lung adenocarcinoma and its impact on the immune microenvironment


## Background
  Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most prevalent subtype of non-small cell lung cancer, characterized by high incidence and poor prognosis. Mitochondrial lipid metabolism (MTLM) contributes to tumor proliferation and drug resistance by regulating lipid breakdown and energy supply and is strongly linked to prognosis in multiple cancers. Nevertheless, its impact on LUAD has yet to be clarified.


## Methods
  MTLM-associated genes were extracted from the MitoCarta 3.0 database and relevant publications. A four-gene prognostic model was formulated using Cox regression analysis, with individual gene contributions clarified through Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) analysis. The model was verified using The Cancer Genome Atlas and Gene Expression Omnibus datasets, and a nomogram incorporating clinical features was developed. Immunomicroenvironment characteristics and drug sensitivity were evaluated, leading to the identification of potential therapeutic agents. A competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNA) and transcription factor (TF) regulatory network was constructed for the core genes. Drug components targeting these core genes were identified through reverse pharmacology and molecular docking. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was carried out to analyze the expression of core genes across cellular subpopulations. Finally, the core gene ACSM5 was investigated for its prognostic value and immune regulatory role in LUAD, with its function further validated through clonogenic assays and Transwell experiments.


## Results
  A MTLM-related prognostic model incorporating ACSL4, ACSS3, CHPT1, and ACSM5 was established, with ACSM5 contributing most significantly to the model’s prediction. Patients in the high-risk group had notably shorter survival than those in the low-risk group, and the risk score was identified as an independent prognostic factor. The nomogram, combining clinical features and risk scores, showed an AUC value of 0.712. Notable differences were detected between distinct risk groups in terms of immune microenvironment and drug sensitivity. The ceRNA network and TF regulatory network revealed upstream and downstream targets of the core genes. Reverse pharmacological analysis identified drugs and components affecting the CYP27A1 gene. Molecular docking results showed strong binding between CHPT1 and 3-methylcholanthrene and aztreonam. ScRNA-seq revealed the expression patterns of core genes at the single-cell level. High ACSM5 expression in LUAD was positively linked to prognosis, and ACSM5 was determined to be an independent prognostic factor for LUAD. Experimental results demonstrated that ACSM5 inhibits LUAD cell proliferation, invasion, and migration.


## Conclusion
  The MTLM-based four-gene prognostic model developed in this study effectively predicts LUAD patient outcomes, with ACSM5 serving as the core prognostic gene. This model provides new theoretical support for personalized treatment and precise prognosis assessment in LUAD.

## Introduction
  Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most prevalent subtype of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), making up about 40% of all lung cancer cases [1–3]. However, many patients are diagnosed at locally advanced or metastatic stages, with a 5-year survival rate of only 15%−20% [4–6]. Although emerging therapies, such as targeted therapy and immunotherapy, have led to significant survival improvements in some patients [7, 8], clinically effective molecular tools for risk stratification and treatment decision-making are still lacking. Many patients experience high recurrence rates and suboptimal treatment outcomes due to inadequate prognostic assessment or imprecise therapeutic strategies. Therefore, identifying biomarkers strongly linked to LUAD progression and prognosis, as well as establishing reliable prognostic prediction systems, is essential for personalized treatment.

  Mitochondria serve as the central hub of cellular energy metabolism, and their dysfunction is closely associated with tumorigenesis and progression [9–11]. The mitochondrial membrane has a distinct lipid composition that is essential for maintaining the organelle’s normal morphology and function [12–14]. Mitochondrial lipid metabolism (MTLM), a key branch of the mitochondrial metabolic network, directly participates in tumor progression by regulating lipolysis, energy supply, and the synthesis of signaling molecules [15, 16]. For example, low-density lipoprotein promotes the progression of triple-negative breast cancer cells by reprogramming mitochondrial metabolism [17]; LACTB exerts anticancer effects by enhancing MTLM [18]; and elevated expression of the mitochondrial citrate transporter SLC25A1 in colorectal cancer promotes energy metabolism reprogramming, driving tumor growth and enhancing energy stress tolerance [19]. However, in LUAD, the expression patterns of MTLM-associated genes, their prognostic mechanisms, and their regulation of the tumor immune microenvironment (TME), as well as their influence on treatment response, remain understudied and poorly understood.

  This study focuses on MTLM-related genes in LUAD and develops a novel prognostic model. Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) analysis was employed to elucidate the contribution of each gene within the model. The model’s validity and independence were confirmed using multiple datasets, and a nomogram was constructed incorporating clinical features. Functional enrichment analysis subsequently revealed the connection between immune cell infiltration and risk scores. Drug sensitivity analysis facilitated the identification of potential therapeutic agents. Through competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNA) and transcription factor (TF) regulatory networks, upstream mechanisms and downstream targets of core genes were uncovered. High-affinity drug compounds were screened via reverse network pharmacology and molecular docking. Additionally, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was utilized to precisely map the expression patterns of core genes across distinct cellular subpopulations. Finally, the core gene ACSM5 was examined in detail, with its prognostic role in LUAD and its impact on the immune microenvironment supported by experimental validation. This study aims to provide a precise prognostic assessment tool for LUAD patients and offer new theoretical foundations for developing personalized treatment strategies.


## Materials and methods

### Data acquisition and preprocessing
  Data for this study were obtained from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/), including gene expression, somatic mutations, copy number variations (CNVs), and clinical data for LUAD patients [20]. The dataset comprises 59 normal samples and 541 tumor samples. For external validation, the GSE72094 dataset, which contains 442 LUAD samples, was selected from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) [21]. We also summarized the specific sample sizes and basic demographic information for both datasets (Table S1, S2). LUAD transcriptome matrices from TCGA and GEO were extracted using Perl scripts, and gene annotation was performed using the R package rtracklayer [22]. After converting Ensembl gene IDs to official gene symbols, the median expression value was chosen. Prognostic-associated gene expression was validated using the scRNA-seq dataset GSE149655, and the expression levels of prognostic genes were also assessed through the HPA database. Genomic analysis of model genes was performed using cBioPortal for Cancer Genomics (http://www.cbioportal.org, version v3.2.11) [23].

  The screening of MTLM genes was based on multiple sources. Initially, 123 genes were selected from the MitoCarta 3.0 database [24], which provides mitochondrial localization information for genes within cells. Additionally, 21 genes related to MTLM were identified from the literature [25]. After eliminating duplicates, a total of 140 MTLM-related genes were compiled, providing the data foundation for this study (Table S3). The workflow is illustrated in Figure S1.


### Development of the prognostic model
  The “limma” package in R was first used to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the normal and tumor groups, with a threshold of (|Log2 FC| > 1, adjusted p-value < 0.05). Univariate Cox regression analysis was then performed to identify genes associated with prognosis (p < 0.05). Genes related to survival were identified using the “survival” package for Cox regression analysis. Next, the “glmnet” package was employed to construct a Cox prognostic model and perform survival analysis. The “kernelshap” and “shapviz” packages were then applied for SHAP analysis. This approach enabled the construction and evaluation of biomarker-based prognostic models, providing deeper insights into the contributions of individual variables through SHAP analysis.

  Dimensionality reduction analysis was subsequently executed on the data from the risk documents, including Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP), t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE), and Principal Component Analysis (PCA) analyses. PCA was conducted using the “prcomp” function, UMAP analysis was carried out with the “umap” package, and tSNE analysis was performed using the “Rtsne” package. Each dimensionality reduction method generated corresponding visualizations (PCA plot, UMAP plot, and tSNE plot), which provided an intuitive illustration of the differences between distinct risk groups.


### Validation of the prognostic model
  The risk file was first read, and samples were sorted based on their risk scores. A risk curve was then plotted to distinguish different risk groups, with the critical cutoff point clearly marked. Next, a survival status plot was created to display patient survival times, using distinct colors to differentiate between survival and death states. A risk heatmap was generated to show each sample’s risk score alongside relevant clinical characteristics. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated using the “survminer” package.

  Subsequently, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated using the “survival” and “timeROC” packages, with area under the curve (AUC) values calculated to validate the model. Survival analysis was also undertaken to assess the association between clinical characteristics and risk scores. The survdiff function was used for difference testing, and survival curves were fitted using survfit, with survival patterns visualized using ggsurvplot. The coxph function from the “survival” package was employed to determine hazard ratios (HR) for each clinical variable. Custom bioForest functions were used to generate forest plots for Cox regression analyses.


### Development and validation of the nomogram
  First, the risk score was integrated with clinical data, and Cox regression analysis was executed using the coxph function from the “survival” package [26] to generate a regression model. Next, a risk score plot was generated using the regplot function from the “regplot” package to visualize the connection between the risk score and clinical features. The “rms” package was then utilized to generate calibration curves to assess the predictive performance of the risk score plot. The “timeROC” package was employed to evaluate the model’s performance in 5-year predictions and to calculate the AUC value. Additionally, ROC curves were plotted for each individual clinical feature, and their respective AUC values were computed. Finally, an independent prognostic analysis was conducted, which included both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses.


### Enrichment analysis
  First, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was undertaken on DEGs using the “clusterProfiler” [27, 28] package to identify significant gene sets. Visualizations illustrating GO categories and gene enrichment patterns were generated. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis [29] was then executed using the enrichKEGG function from the “clusterProfiler” package to identify significant pathways. Next, we performed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) to compare gene expression differences between different risk groups. GSEA was based on the genome-wide ranked gene sets.

  We investigated the connection between ACSM5 gene expression and immune infiltration. Expression data were merged with tumor microenvironment scores to ensure data consistency. Using the “reshape2” package, the data were formatted for ggplot2 plotting, and a violin plot was generated. Immune infiltration analysis was then performed by selecting tumor samples, grouping them by gene expression, and screening and normalizing the immune cell infiltration data. Correlations between immune checkpoint genes and ACSM5 were examined using Pearson’s correlation test, and significantly correlated genes (p < 0.001) were identified. Finally, the connection between ACSM5 expression and tumor mutational burden (TMB) was examined.


### Immune infiltration analysis and drug sensitivity analysis
  First, differential analysis of immune cell infiltration was carried out. Data were preprocessed using the “reshape2” and “ggpubr” packages, with immune cell types filtered based on p-values < 0.05. Next, ssGSEA analysis of immune function was conducted using “GSVA,” calculating immune scores and normalizing them with a custom “normalize” function. Chi-square tests were then performed to assess differences in immune profiling between different risk groups. The relationship between TIDE scores and risk groups was examined, with comparison plots generated for different risk cohorts. Finally, the connection between risk scores and stem cell scores (RNAss) was explored, with a scatter plot illustrating their relationship.

  For drug sensitivity analysis, the “oncoPredict,” [30] “limma,” [31] and “parallel” packages were utilized. Gene expression data were read and normalized, with normal samples removed and batch corrections applied. Drug sensitivity data were loaded, and sensitivity was calculated using the “calcPhenotype” function. Predictive analysis was performed on training data, low-variance genes were filtered out, and a minimum sample size was established.


### The CeRNA regulatory network, TF regulatory network, and reverse network Pharmacology analysis
  MiRNAs associated with prognostic genes were identified by integrating data from four databases: “miRanda.tsv,” “miRTarBase.tsv,” “miRDB.tsv,” and “TargetScan.tsv.” First, miRNA-prognostic gene association data were extracted from these databases. MiRNAs showing associations across all four databases were selected to ensure the reliability and consistency of the results. Simultaneously, by analyzing the “spongeScan.tsv” database, lncRNAs interacting with target miRNAs were identified, and relevant lncRNA data were extracted. After integration, a list of lncRNAs interacting with miRNAs was constructed, forming the basis for building the ceRNA network. Additionally, TF-related data were obtained from the GRNpedia website (https://www.grnpedia.org/trrust/).

  Furthermore, reverse network pharmacology analysis was performed. First, drug-gene connection data were downloaded from the CTD database (https://ctdbase.org/) [32], and relevant drug components were extracted by integrating this data with the core gene list file. The filtered drug component results were saved as a CSV file. Next, drug component names were extracted, and drug enrichment analysis was executed using the “enricher” function to identify significantly enriched drugs. A relationship network among drugs, components, and targets was constructed, and the corresponding regulatory network diagram was visualized using CytoScape software (https://cytoscape.org/).


### Molecular docking analysis
  To assess the sensitivity of prognostic genes to drugs, molecular docking analysis was conducted. First, correlations between prognostic genes and drugs were sourced from the DSigDB database (https://dsigdb.tanlab.org/) [33]. Drug structural files for molecular docking were then downloaded from the PubChem website (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) [34], and corresponding protein structural files were obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/) [35]. Molecular docking analysis was performed using the CB-DOCK2 platform (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/index.php) to explore binding patterns between candidate drugs and prognostic genes. Among the six prognostic genes, only the CHPT1 gene data were utilized for molecular docking analysis.


### The scRNA-seq data analysis
  The following detailed normalization steps were incorporated for single-cell analysis: First, expression matrices in 10X Genomics format were read, and cells with fewer than 50 gene expression counts and mitochondrial gene expression exceeding 15% were filtered out. The data were then normalized using the NormalizeData function. The LogNormalize method divided each cell’s gene expression values by its total count and multiplied by a constant (10,000) to account for sequencing depth variations between cells, ensuring comparability. Subsequently, the ScaleData function was used to standardize the data, adjusting all gene expression values to a mean of zero and a variance of one, preparing the data for PCA. PCA was performed using the RunPCA function for dimensionality reduction, with batch effects adjusted using the harmony package to mitigate experimental batch effects. Cell clustering and visualization were then performed using the FindNeighbors, FindClusters, and RunTSNE functions (clustering resolution set to 0.6). DEGs were identified using the FindAllMarkers function (|Log2 FC| > 1, adjusted p-value < 0.05) to determine marker genes for each cell subpopulation. Finally, cell annotation was performed using the SingleR package combined with seven reference databases (including “HumanPrimaryCellAtlas” and “BlueprintEncode”) and visualized through tSNE plots.

  The expression patterns of prognostic genes in single-cell data were then explored. Data processing and correction were carried out using R packages such as “Seurat”, “SingleR”, “clustree”, “harmony”, “assertthat”, and “SCpubr”. After loading the Seurat object, genes corresponding to the cell data were extracted from the feature gene file. The expression of feature genes across different cell populations was then visualized using scatter plots, violin plots, and dot plots generated by the Seurat functions “FeaturePlot”, “VlnPlot”, and “DotPlot”, respectively.


### Cell culture and transfection
  The BEAS-2, PC9, HCC827, and A549 cell lines were procured from ProCell (Wuhan, China). To explore the function of ACSM5, a human ACSM5 overexpression plasmid, developed by Hanbio Co. Ltd (Shanghai, China), was employed. This plasmid was inserted into the pcDNA 3.1(+) vector (Hanbio Co. Ltd, Shanghai, China). For cell transfection, Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) was employed, according to the manufacturer’s protocol.


### Real-time quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)
  Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA synthesis was executed with the PrimeScript RT Kit (Takara). qRT-PCR was conducted using SYBR Green dye (Takara), and data were analyzed using the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method. β-actin was used as the internal reference gene to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the results. The primer sequences used were as follows: ACSM5-F: 5’-GGACAGGGACTGTGATGATTCC-3’; ACSM5-R: 5’-CCTTGGAGCTAGGGAGTCAC-3’; β-actin-F: 5’-GAGAAAATCTGGCACCACACC-3’; β-actin-R: 5’-GGATAGCACAGCCTGGATAGCAA-3’.


### Western blot
  Intracellular proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer containing 1% protease inhibitor (P1005, Thermo Fisher, China). The proteins were separated by 4%−20% gradient SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes for immunodetection. Membranes were incubated with 5% nonfat milk solution at room temperature for 1 h, followed by overnight incubation at 4℃ with diluted primary antibody. After incubation, membranes were treated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using chemiluminescence and imaged with a Chemidoc (Bio-Rad) imaging system. The primary antibodies used were ACSM5 (1:1000; ImmunoWay, YN7849) and α-tubulin (1:1000; Proteintech, 11224-1-AP).


### Colony formation assays
  To determine cell proliferation, 500 cells were inoculated into a 6-well plate and cultured for 10 days. The medium was changed regularly, and cell growth was monitored throughout the culture period. Afterward, cells were fixed with formaldehyde and stained with crystal violet to observe changes in cell number and morphology. Cell counting was executed using an inverted microscope, and images were captured to document growth patterns. Each experiment was repeated three times to ensure result reliability.


### Transwell assays
  In cell migration assays, 2 × 10⁴ suspended cells were seeded into the upper chamber of Transwell devices, and 200 µL of serum-free medium was added. For invasion assays, 50 µL of Matrigel (diluted 1:8) was applied to the bottom of the lower chamber and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. Subsequently, 5 × 10⁴ cells suspended in 200 µL serum-free medium were added to the upper chamber. To promote migration and proliferation, 600 µL of medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum was added to the lower chamber. After 24 h of incubation, cells that migrated or invaded into the lower chamber were fixed and stained. Images of five random fields of view were captured using an inverted microscope, and cell numbers were counted. Triplicate replicates were performed for each experimental condition to ensure result reliability.


### Statistical analysis
  Data analysis, processing, and visualization were conducted using R software (version 4.5.1) and GraphPad Prism 9. Chi-square tests were used to compare clinical and molecular characteristics between subtypes. ANOVA was employed for multi-group comparisons, followed by Bonferroni and Dunnett’s multiple comparisons tests. Two-group comparisons were performed using t-tests. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Where FDR adjustment was required, adjusted p-values < 0.05 were used.


## Results

### Confirmation and enrichment analysis of MTLM-related DEGs
  Differential analysis of 140 MTLM-related genes identified 34 DEGs (Fig. 1A, Table S4). Subsequent univariate Cox regression revealed 6 genes significantly linked to prognosis (Fig. 1B). GO enrichment of the 34 DEGs indicated involvement in fatty acid metabolic process, lipid modification, and lipid catabolic process (biological process); enrichment in the mitochondrial outer membrane, outer membrane, and organelle outer membrane (cellular component); and association with transferring groups other than amino–acyl groups activity, ligase activity and acyltransferase (molecular function) (Fig. 1C). KEGG pathway analysis further revealed significant enrichment in the fatty acid metabolism, PPAR signaling pathway, and fatty acid degradation (Fig. 1D).

### Prognostic model development and SHAP analysis
  The prognostic model was carried out using the Cox proportional hazards regression method. The risk score formula is as follows: Risk Score = (0.2394) × ACSL4 + (−0.2490) × ACSS3 + (0.1683) × CHPT1 + (−0.4857) × ACSM5. SHAP analysis was then performed to assess the contribution of each gene to the model’s prediction. Bar charts and swarm plots illustrated the average SHAP values for each gene, with ACSM5 showing the highest contribution (0.228), indicating its significant impact on model prediction (Fig. 2A, B). Waterfall plots and individual sample plots presented the prediction results for single samples, where yellow indicates that the gene assigned the sample to the high-risk group. The composite prediction score of 1.25 for this sample exceeded the benchmark risk threshold of 0.617, confirming its classification as high-risk (Fig. 2C, D). Figure 2E presents dimensionality reduction plots (PCA, tSNE, and UMAP), which clearly distinguish the distinct risk groups. These analyses highlight the pivotal role of MTLM-associated genes in lipid metabolism.

A Bar chart showing the average SHAP values for different genes; B Swarm plot illustrating the distribution of SHAP values; C, D Waterfall plot and individual sample force plot showing the model prediction results for individual samples; E Dimensionality reduction plots (PCA, tSNE, and UMAP) distinguishing high-risk (orange) and low-risk (blue) groups


### Prognostic model evaluation
  The model’s capability was validated by calculating each patient’s risk score based on the four-gene model and categorizing patients into high-risk and low-risk cohorts categorized by the median score. The results displayed a significant association between high-risk status and poorer survival prognosis (Fig. 3A). Kaplan-Meier survival curves illustrated that survival durations in the high-risk group were markedly shorter than those in the low-risk group in both the TCGA and GEO datasets (TCGA: p < 0.001; GEO: p = 0.023) (Fig. 3B). Figure 3C shows the ROC curves for 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year survival, with AUC values of 0.677, 0.657, and 0.645, respectively, indicating that the model has valuable predictive capability. Additionally, Kaplan-Meier survival curves demonstrated that patients aged ≥ 60 years (p < 0.001), females (p = 0.015), males (p = 0.002), those with M0 (p < 0.001), N0 (p = 0.004), and stage I-II (p = 0.003) disease in the high-risk group had significantly lower survival probabilities compared to those in the low-risk group (Figure S2). Figure 3D further confirmed the risk model’s applicability across clinical subgroups, revealing considerable differences in overall stage, T stage, and N stage between distinct risk groups. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses validated the risk score as an independent prognostic factor (Fig. 3E). Overall, this four-gene risk model demonstrates reliable predictive ability across various datasets and clinical subgroups, while confirming its independent prognostic value in clinical practice.

### Nomogram creation and assessment
  A nomogram was constructed to calculate prognostic scores, thereby predicting survival probabilities at various time points (Fig. 4A). The nomogram’s calibration curve indicated high concordance between the predicted and actual survival rates (Fig. 4B). ROC curve analysis revealed the nomogram’s highest AUC value of 0.712, demonstrating its strong accuracy in predicting survival duration (Fig. 4C). Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses further validated that the nomogram is an independent prognostic factor (Fig. 4D). This nomogram serves as an effective and reliable tool for survival prediction and independent prognostic assessment.

### Enrichment analysis, immune microenvironment profiling, and drug sensitivity analysis
  Next, enrichment analysis and immune microenvironment evaluation were performed. GSEA GO analysis (Fig. 5A) revealed that the high-risk group was markedly enriched in functions related to the cornified envelope, ribosomal subunit, cytosolic ribosome, ribosome, and structural constituents of the ribosome. In contrast, the low-risk group demonstrated enrichment in functions such as ciliary plasma, cytoplasmic region, plasma membrane signaling receptor complex, immunoglobulin complex, and T cell receptor complex. GSEA KEGG analysis (Fig. 5B) displayed that the high-risk group was enriched in pathways such as ascorbate and aldarate metabolism, cell cycle, ribosome, proteasome, and pentose and glucuronate interconversions, while the low-risk group was enriched in pathways like allograft rejection, asthma, intestinal immune network for IgA production, viral myocarditis, and cell adhesion molecules. Immune cell infiltration analysis (Fig. 5C) showed higher expression of resting mast cells in the low-risk group, whereas the high-risk group had increased expression of activated dendritic cells, eosinophils, and neutrophils. Immune function analysis (Fig. 5D) demonstrated that most immune functions were elevated in the low-risk group. The heatmap (Fig. 5E) illustrates the association between model genes, risk scores, and immune cells, revealing that risk scores positively correlate with neutrophils, resting NK cells, and activated dendritic cells, while negatively correlating with regulatory T cells, resting mast cells, M2 macrophages, and resting dendritic cells. Immune subtyping analysis (Fig. 5F) demonstrated significant differences in the distribution of subtypes across risk groups (p = 0.001). The low-risk group exhibited significantly higher immune activity, as shown by higher ImmuneScore, StromalScore, and ESTIMATEScore (Fig. 5G). Moreover, the low-risk group had higher TIDE scores (Fig. 5H). Correlation analysis (Fig. 5I) displayed a strong positive connection between risk scores and RNAss (r = 0.2, p = 8.6e-06).

  Drug sensitivity analysis exhibited notable differences between risk groups. In the high-risk group, lower IC50 values for drugs such as irinotecan, gemcitabine, erlotinib, crizotinib, savolitinib, 5-fluorouracil, trametinib, buparlisib, talazoparib, and pevonedistat indicated increased sensitivity to these agents. In contrast, the low-risk group showed lower IC50 values for axitinib and ABT737, suggesting higher sensitivity to these drugs (Figure S3).


### Regulatory network analysis, drug enrichment analysis, and molecular docking of prognostic genes
  The regulatory network analysis was performed on six prognostic genes. Figure 6A illustrates the ceRNA regulatory network interactions between ACSL4, ACSS3, and AGPAT5 with miRNAs and lncRNAs. The TF regulatory network in Fig. 6B shows that the CYP27A1 gene is regulated by transcription factors such as HNF4A, NR0B2, SP3, and SP1. Reverse network pharmacology analysis (Fig. 6C) indicates that Niuhuang may influence the CYP27A1 gene via its component, deoxycholic acid, while Kuandonghua may affect CYP27A1 through its component, senkirkine, thus impacting the prognosis of LUAD. The drug enrichment analysis of six prognostic genes revealed significant associations with periodate-oxidized adenosine, 3-methylcholanthrene, cerebrosterol, 2-arachidonoylglycerol, 7-ketocholesterol, 25-hydroxycholesterol, aztreonam, and 27-hydroxycholesterol (Fig. 7A). Additionally, molecular docking analysis demonstrated strong binding interactions between CHPT1 and 3-methylcholanthrene (vina score: −9.5) as well as aztreonam (vina score: −8.0), highlighting their respective protein-binding sites (Fig. 7B). These results offer potential avenues for further investigation into the interactions between drugs and prognostic genes. This study uncovered potential interactions between prognostic genes, drugs, and regulatory factors through regulatory network and molecular docking analyses, offering new insights and directions for future research.

### ScRNA-seq data analysis of prognostic genes
  The GEO database was screened to obtain the scRNA-seq dataset GSE149655 [1], associated with LUAD. This dataset includes LUAD lesion samples GSM4506699 and GSM4506701, as well as distal normal lung tissue samples GSM4506698 and GSM4506700. Through t-SNE dimensionality reduction analysis, 19 cell subpopulations were identified (Fig. 8A). Cell annotation results revealed the distribution of various cell types in the scRNA-seq data, including epithelial cells, T cells, endothelial cells, adipocytes, macrophages, monocytes, and fibroblasts (Fig. 8B). Bubble and violin plots displayed the expression levels of six prognostic genes in distinct cell types. CYP27A1 showed the highest expression in macrophages, AGPAT5 was most abundant in T cells, ACSL4 exhibited elevated expression in both macrophages and monocytes, ACSS3 demonstrated strong expression in macrophages, CHPT1 was most prominent in epithelial cells, and ACSM5 was most abundant in macrophages (Fig. 8C, D). Figure 8E further illustrates the expression distribution of these six prognostic genes in the t-SNE dimensionality reduction plot. Overall, these single-cell analysis results reveal differential expression of prognostic genes across distinct cell types, suggesting their potential critical roles in the immune microenvironment of LUAD. ScRNA-seq analysis indicated the expression patterns of prognostic genes in LUAD across various cell types, offering valuable insights for further investigation of their roles within the TME.

### The genomic analysis of the four model genes and GSEA enrichment analysis
  The genomics of the four model genes were further explored, and a GSEA analysis was conducted using the expression levels of all genes in the genome as background. First, the cBioPortal website was used to display the structural variations, gene mutations, and CNVs of the four model genes (ACSL4, ACSS3, CHPT1, and ACSM5) in the TCGA database. Figure S4A illustrates the protein structure and mutation sites, highlighting specific mutation loci and the number of mutated patients. The bar chart in Figure S4B shows the frequency of different mutation types in each gene, revealing the distribution and frequency differences across the genes. Next, co-expressed genes most strongly correlated with the model genes were identified (Figure S4C). To further assess the impact of the model genes against the background of the entire genome’s expression levels, GSEA analysis was performed (Figure S4D, S4E). The GSEA-GO enrichment analysis revealed that ACSL4 was positively correlated with detection of chemical stimulus, sensory perception of smell, and olfactory receptor activity, and negatively correlated with immunoglobulin complex and hormone activity. ACSS3 was negatively correlated with regulation of cellular amino acid metabolic process, cytosolic large ribosomal subunit, organellar ribosome, respiratory chain complex, and small ribosomal subunit. CHPT1 was positively correlated with alcohol metabolic process, hormone metabolic process, response to xenobiotic stimulus, steroid metabolic process, and monooxygenase activity. The GSEA-KEGG enrichment analysis showed that ACSL4 was positively correlated with the olfactory transduction pathway and negatively correlated with maturity onset diabetes of the young and ribosome pathways. ACSS3 was positively correlated with the olfactory transduction pathway and negatively correlated with aminoacyl tRNA biosynthesis, galactose metabolism, ribosome, and RNA polymerase pathways. CHPT1 was positively correlated with drug metabolism cytochrome P450, metabolism of xenobiotics by cytochrome P450, pentose and glucuronate interconversions, retinol metabolism, and steroid hormone biosynthesis pathways.


### ACSM5 expression and prognostic analysis
  ACSM5 is the most significant gene contributing to the predictive model among the model genes, and its role in LUAD has not been extensively explored in previous studies. Therefore, we further investigated its function in LUAD. First, pan-cancer analysis revealed that ACSM5 is expressed at low levels across 18 tumor types, including UCEC, CESC, BRCA, COAD, CHOL, ESCA, KICH, HNSC, KIRC, LUAD, LIHC, LUSC, PRAD, PAAD, READ, THCA, STAD, and BLCA (Fig. 10A, B). Paired comparisons between normal and tumor tissues further displayed the significant reduction of ACSM5 expression in tumor tissues (Fig. 10C). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis displayed that the high-expression group had a longer overall survival compared to the low-expression group (p = 0.0021) (Fig. 10D). Additionally, significant differences were observed in age, overall stage, and T stage between the different expression groups, with the high-expression group having a higher proportion of patients aged ≥ 65 years, in stage I, and with T1 stage (Fig. 10E). Based on ACSM5 expression and clinical characteristics, a nomogram was constructed (Fig. 10F). Calibration curve validation indicated that the predicted survival rates closely matched the observed values (Fig. 10G). Furthermore, both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses validated that ACSM5 is an independent prognostic factor (Fig. 10H). Low ACSM5 expression is strongly linked to poorer prognosis in patients with LUAD, offering a novel biomarker for risk assessment and clinical management in LUAD.

### Enrichment analysis of ACSM5 different expression groups
  A total of 1152 DEGs were determined between the ACSM5 distinct expression groups (|Log2 FC| > 1, adjusted p-value < 0.05) (Table S5). Of these, 944 genes were upregulated in the high-expression group, while 208 genes were downregulated in the low-expression group. A heatmap illustrating the top 50 DEGs in each group is presented in Fig. 11A. GSEA GO enrichment analysis of these DEGs highlighted notable enrichment in functional pathways, including antigen receptor-mediated signaling, external side of the plasma membrane, regulation of T cell activation, plasma membrane signaling receptor complex, and T cell receptor complex (Fig. 11B). GO enrichment analysis results indicated that, at the Biological Process level, these DEGs were primarily associated with leukocyte proliferation and regulation of immune effector processes. At the Cellular Component level, they were mainly enriched in the T cell receptor complex and plasma membrane signaling receptor complex. At the Molecular Function level, they were primarily involved in immune receptor activity, carbohydrate binding, and peptide binding (Fig. 11D). ACSM5 is notably underexpressed in LUAD and is strongly associated with patient survival and clinical characteristics, providing a novel direction for prognostic assessment in this disease.

## Immune infiltration analysis across ACSM5 expression groups
  Next, immune infiltration analysis was executed on the DEGs. Immune score results showed that the ACSM5 high-expression group significantly exceeded the low-expression group in StromalScore, ImmuneScore, and ESTIMATEScore (Fig. 12A). Immune infiltration analysis revealed increased proportions of plasma cells, resting NK cells, activated dendritic cells, and activated mast cells in the ACSM5 low-expression group. In contrast, the ACSM5 high-expression group exhibited elevated proportions of resting CD4 memory T cells, monocytes, M2 macrophages, M1 macrophages, resting dendritic cells, and resting mast cells (Fig. 12B). Correlation analysis and lollipop plots revealed that ACSM5 expression was strongly linked to macrophages M2 (r = 0.32, p < 0.001), resting mast cells (r = 0.81, p < 0.001), resting dendritic cells (r = 0.25, p < 0.001), CD4 memory resting T cells (r = 0.19, p < 0.001), monocytes (r = 0.16, p < 0.001), and macrophages M1 (r = 0.11, p = 0.020), and negatively correlated with resting NK cells (r = −0.13, p = 0.007), activated dendritic cells (r = −0.14, p = 0.004), activated mast cells (r = −0.19, p < 0.001), and plasma cells (r = −0.25, p < 0.001) (Fig. 12C, E). Additionally, ACSM5 expression was inversely linked to TMB (r = −0.22, p = 5.4e-07) (Fig. 12D). Further analysis indicated notable correlations between ACSM5 expression and 31 immune checkpoint (ICP) genes (Fig. 12F). Finally, analysis of immunotherapy response exhibited that patients with high ACSM5 expression demonstrated more pronounced responses to programmed death-1 (PD-1) inhibitors, cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) inhibitors, and their combination therapy (Fig. 12G).

### Functional experimental validation
  Functional experiments were carried out to further validate the role of ACSM5 in LUAD. First, the mRNA and protein expression levels of ACSM5 were measured in the LUAD cell lines A549, PC9, and HCC827 and the normal lung cell line BEAS-2B. The results indicated that ACSM5 expression was significantly downregulated in the cancer cell lines compared to the normal cell line (Fig. 13A). A549 and PC9 cells were then transfected with either the overexpression plasmid (OE-ACSM5) or the control plasmid (Vector). Transfection efficiency was verified through qRT-PCR and Western blot analysis (Fig. 13B). Further functional assays revealed that ACSM5 overexpression significantly inhibited the proliferation of A549 and PC9 cells, as shown by the colony formation assay (Fig. 13C, D). Additionally, Transwell assays demonstrated that ACSM5 overexpression markedly reduced the migration and invasion capabilities of both A549 and PC9 cells (Fig. 13E, F). The experimental findings further supported the tumor-suppressive role of ACSM5 in LUAD.

## Discussion
  LUAD, a major subtype of NSCLC, is frequently diagnosed at an advanced stage with limited treatment options and poor prognosis, underscoring the urgent need for new prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets [36–38]. Abnormal lipid metabolism is recognized as a crucial element in carcinogenesis [39–41]. Research shows that cancer cells meet their rapid growth demands by increasing fatty acid uptake and synthesis [42]. While the endoplasmic reticulum serves as the primary site for cellular lipid synthesis [43, 44], mitochondria also play a pivotal role in lipid metabolism, particularly in the synthesis of phosphatidylethanolamine, cardiolipin, and phosphatidylglycerol [45, 46]. In recent years, there has been growing focus on the role of MTLM in tumors. For instance, the glycolytic enzyme PFKL promotes the fusion of lipid droplets with mitochondria during energy stress in tumor cells, activating fatty acid β-oxidation to support tumor proliferation. This highlights PFKL as a novel target for lipid metabolism-based interventions [47]. Additionally, studies have shown that dietary lipids influence cancer progression by regulating mitochondrial function, providing a theoretical foundation for dietary interventions [48]. Moreover, MTLM is integral to the immune evasion and escape processes in tumors [25].

  This study established a four-gene prognostic model comprising ACSL4, ACSS3, CHPT1, and ACSM5. The model demonstrated stable prognostic discriminatory ability in both the TCGA and GEO independent datasets, with significantly reduced survival times observed in the high-risk group. The AUC values of the ROC curves at 1, 3, and 5 years all exceeded 0.65. Cox regression analyses further validated that the risk score is an independent prognostic factor. Even among patients with identical T stages (T1-2 or T3-4) or N0, M0 stages, the model effectively distinguished survival differences, providing molecular evidence for the refined management of clinical subgroups. The AUC value of the nomogram, built on the risk score and clinical features, increased to 0.712. Furthermore, the predicted survival rates at 1 to 5 years showed high concordance with actual survival rates, further enhancing the prediction’s practicality and accuracy. Notably, SHAP analysis identified ACSM5 as the gene most critical to model prediction, directing research focus toward this understudied LUAD-associated gene. Pan-cancer analysis revealed that ACSM5 is broadly underexpressed across 18 tumor types, with notably prolonged overall survival observed in LUAD patients exhibiting high ACSM5 expression. This suggests its potential role as a pan-cancer tumor suppressor gene.

  Compared to other established prognostic frameworks for LUAD [49–51], our model is distinguished by its innovative focus on MTLM pathways, providing a novel biological perspective for prognosis research. In terms of clinical applicability, the model integrates gene expression data with clinical variables, such as age and tumor stage, and is validated across multiple datasets, including TCGA and GEO, thereby significantly improving its clinical utility and generalizability. Regarding predictive performance, the model quantifies forecasting efficacy through clear AUC values, with multi-dataset validation further enhancing the reliability and operational feasibility of the results. In summary, our model offers distinct advantages in mechanism, clinical application, and predictive capability, making it a promising tool for precision prognostic assessment in LUAD.

  As a member of the acyl-CoA synthase family, ACSM5 plays a pivotal role in fatty acid metabolism and is strongly linked to various malignant tumors [52–54]. Studies have shown that DEGs in the ACSM5-high expression group are significantly enriched in immune pathways such as antigen receptor signaling and cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions, suggesting that ACSM5 may influence LUAD progression by regulating immune cell function. Functional experiments further validated ACSM5’s inhibitory effect on LUAD progression. Immune checkpoints, including PD-1 and CTLA-4, are crucial in maintaining immune tolerance and regulating antitumor responses [55, 56]. Notably, this study found significant correlations between ACSM5 expression levels and the abundance of multiple immune checkpoint genes. High ACSM5 expression was associated with enhanced responsiveness to PD-1 and CTLA-4 inhibitors, suggesting that ACSM5 may serve as a key biomarker for predicting patient sensitivity to immune checkpoint blockade therapy. In addition to ACSM5, the ACSS3 gene warrants attention. ACSS3 catalyzes the conversion of acetic acid to acetyl-CoA, a substrate for various biosynthetic processes, including lipid synthesis and histone acetylation [57]. Our analysis revealed that ACSS3 expression negatively correlates with LUAD prognosis, suggesting that it may influence LUAD development through metabolic reprogramming. Furthermore, regulatory network analysis provides new therapeutic directions. The ceRNA network suggests that ACSS3, ACSL4, and AGPAT5 may be regulated by lncRNA/miRNA. Molecular docking analysis demonstrated that CHPT1 has a strong binding affinity with small molecules such as 3-methylcholanthrene and aztreonam, which may impact lipid metabolism by inhibiting CHPT1 activity. Reverse network pharmacology analysis identified deoxycholic acid (from Niuhuang) and senkirkine (from Kuandonghua) as potential modulators of CYP27A1 expression. These findings suggest that active components from traditional Chinese medicine could serve as potential targets for MTLM-directed therapies, providing new insights into integrated Chinese-Western approaches to LUAD treatment.

  The immune microenvironment plays an essential role in the progression and prognosis of LUAD [58]. This study systematically reveals, for the first time, the relationship between MTLM-associated genes and the LUAD immune microenvironment, providing a novel perspective for precision screening in immunotherapy. Regarding immune cell infiltration patterns, the low-risk group exhibited an “immune-enriched” phenotype, characterized by increased infiltration of quiescent mast cells, M2 macrophages, and quiescent CD4 memory T cells. Concurrently, ImmuneScore, StromalScore, and ESTIMATEScore were markedly elevated, indicating robust immune function. In contrast, the high-risk group exhibited increased infiltration of activated dendritic cells, eosinophils, and neutrophils. Drug sensitivity analysis demonstrated significant differences in response to multiple chemotherapeutic agents between the different risk groups. Notably, the high-risk group exhibited lower IC50 values for irinotecan, gemcitabine, erlotinib, crizotinib, and others, indicating heightened sensitivity to these agents. The drug sensitivity profile of the high-risk group may offer novel therapeutic avenues for patients with aggressive disease characteristics. Conversely, the low-risk group exhibited lower IC50 values for axitinib and ABT737, suggesting heightened sensitivity to these agents. Axitinib, a potent vascular endothelial growth factor receptor inhibitor, is already used in renal cell carcinoma treatment and may offer therapeutic benefits in LUAD by inhibiting tumor angiogenesis [59–61]. The Bcl-2 family inhibitor ABT737 has shown potential in overcoming cancer cell anti-apoptotic mechanisms, indicating that low-risk group patients may benefit from its targeted therapy [62–64]. Collectively, this study provides new insights into the connection between the LUAD immune microenvironment and drug sensitivity, offering potential foundations for precision medicine.

  This study has several limitations. First, despite the powerful analytical capabilities of bioinformatics methods, the datasets used have inherent limitations that may introduce bias. While computational prediction models provide valuable insights, their results may not always accurately reflect biological significance, necessitating further experimental validation. Second, although multiple external datasets were used for validation, the current sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, certain potential confounding factors, such as comorbidities, treatment modalities, and genetic mutations, were not considered. To address these limitations, future research should integrate multi-omics approaches, conduct prospective clinical studies to further validate the prognostic value of the identified genes, explore the mechanisms related to MTLM or the immune microenvironment, and validate the model’s generalizability in larger, independent cohorts.


## Conclusion
  This study proposed a four-gene (ACSL4, ACSS3, CHPT1, ACSM5) prognostic model associated with MTLM. Considerable differences were detected between the distinct risk groups in terms of prognosis, immune infiltration, and drug sensitivity. Overexpression of the core gene ACSM5 was found to correlate with a favorable prognosis and to inhibit LUAD cell proliferation, invasion, and migration. This study provides novel theoretical support for precision prognosis assessment and targeted therapy in LUAD.
