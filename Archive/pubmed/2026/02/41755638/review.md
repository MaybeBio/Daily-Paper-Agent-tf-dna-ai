## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text including abstract, introduction, materials and methods, results, discussion, and supplementary figure/table references
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific validity of claims, experimental support, methodological soundness, and interpretation of results as presented in the provided manuscript text
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors report that the intrinsically disordered transactivation domain (TAD) of N-myc interacts directly with the DNA-binding domain (DBD) of TFIIIC5, a subunit of the RNA Polymerase III assembly factor TFIIIC. They map two interaction regions in N-myc (around residues 16-38, containing the conserved Myc box 0, and residues 61-89, containing a region of helical propensity), identify the TFIIIC5 DBD as the binding surface, show that the C-terminal acidic plug of TFIIIC5 inhibits N-myc binding, and provide AlphaFold models of the interaction. They further demonstrate that N-myc competes with DNA for TFIIIC5 binding and validate the interaction in cells using co-immunoprecipitation with a F28A-Y29A N-myc variant.
- **Visible evidence base** Results sections describing pull-down assays (His-tag based), NMR titrations (1H-15N HSQC), hydrogen-deuterium exchange mass spectrometry, isothermal titration calorimetry, fluorescence polarization assays, X-ray crystallography (TFIIIC5 DBD ΔΔ structure at 2.6 Å), AlphaFold2/AlphaFold3 modelling, electrophoretic mobility shift assays, thermal response index calibration assays, and co-immunoprecipitation experiments in SH-EP neuroblastoma cells. Supplementary tables referenced for plasmid details, HDMS-MS summary, and crystallography statistics.
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Actual figures and supplementary figures not provided; crystallographic data collection and refinement statistics table not provided; HDX-MS summary table not provided; raw NMR spectra not shown; AlphaFold model coordinate files not independently assessable; plasmid sequences not shown; uncropped gel/blot images not shown; statistical analysis details for co-immunoprecipitation replicates not provided

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript presents a comprehensive and methodologically diverse characterization of the interaction between the N-myc TAD and the TFIIIC5 DBD. The authors employ an impressive array of orthogonal techniques including NMR, HDX-MS, ITC, FP, X-ray crystallography, and AlphaFold modelling to map the interaction interface. The finding that N-myc binds to the DNA-binding surface of TFIIIC5 and competes with DNA is potentially significant for understanding the non-canonical functions of Myc family proteins in Pol III transcription and mRNA quality control. However, several concerns temper my enthusiasm: the AlphaFold models are presented with confidence that may exceed their reliability, the functional significance of the interaction in cells is only weakly validated, and the claim of competition with DNA is based on indirect evidence. The manuscript would be strengthened by additional experimental validation of the structural models and clearer demonstration of the biological relevance of the interaction.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers studying Myc family oncoproteins and their interaction networks, particularly those interested in non-canonical functions of Myc beyond Pol II transcription. The work will also appeal to structural biologists studying transcription factor complexes, specifically those investigating TFIIIC architecture and function. Additionally, the methodological approach combining NMR, HDX-MS, and AlphaFold will interest biochemists studying intrinsically disordered protein regions and their interactions with folded domains. The potential link to mRNA quality control mechanisms may attract researchers studying transcriptional surveillance and co-transcriptional processes.
- **Major strengths** 1) The use of multiple orthogonal biophysical techniques (NMR, HDX-MS, ITC, FP) to map the interaction provides converging evidence for the binding interface. 2) The identification of two distinct N-myc regions involved in binding, with different affinities, adds mechanistic depth. 3) The demonstration that the acidic plug of TFIIIC5 regulates N-myc binding provides a clear regulatory mechanism. 4) The X-ray crystal structure of the TFIIIC5 DBD ΔΔ provides a solid experimental foundation for interpreting the interaction data. 5) The validation in cells using co-immunoprecipitation with a variant N-myc protein bridges the biochemical and cellular contexts.
- **Major Concerns** See detailed items below.
- **Minor Comments** See detailed items below.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** R1-M1 (AlphaFold model reliability), R1-M2 (DNA competition evidence), R1-M3 (cellular validation strength), R1-M4 (binding affinity discrepancy)
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** **Originality**: The finding that a Myc family TAD binds to the DNA-binding surface of a TFIIIC subunit and competes with DNA is novel and extends the known interaction repertoire of Myc proteins. However, the conceptual framework of acidic TADs binding to positively charged surfaces is well established, and the authors themselves note similarities to other Myc interactions. **Scientific importance**: The work has potential importance for understanding Myc function in Pol III transcription and mRNA quality control, but the functional consequences of the interaction are not directly demonstrated, limiting the immediate impact. **Interdisciplinary readership**: The work bridges structural biology, biochemistry, and cancer biology, and would interest readers across these fields. The methodological diversity is a strength in this regard. **Technical soundness**: The experimental techniques are generally well-executed and appropriate, but the reliance on AlphaFold models without experimental structural validation for the complex, and the indirect nature of the DNA competition experiments, weaken the technical foundation. **Readability for nonspecialists**: The manuscript is clearly written and the logic is easy to follow, though some sections (particularly the NMR and HDX-MS descriptions) assume specialist knowledge. The figures are referenced appropriately but could not be assessed directly.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved, particularly regarding the reliability of the AlphaFold models, the strength of the DNA competition evidence, and the cellular validation. The core finding of N-myc binding to the TFIIIC5 DBD is well-supported by the biochemical data, but the broader claims about competition with DNA and biological relevance require additional experimental support.

### Major Concerns

- **Concern ID** R1-M1
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** Yes
- **Axis** Structural model reliability
- **Claim pointer** The authors state that "AlphaFold modelling predicts with high-confidence a binding mode for the higher affinity N-myc motif that overlaps with the predicted intramolecular binding site of the C-terminal acidic plug of TFIIIC5" and use these models to draw conclusions about the molecular basis of the interaction.
- **Evidence pointer** Results section "Modelling the N-myc:TFIIIC5 interaction"; Figure 8
- **Concern** The manuscript relies heavily on AlphaFold2-Multimer and AlphaFold3 predictions to propose a detailed binding model, yet the authors themselves acknowledge that "X-ray crystal structures are being published which contradict previously published AlphaFold models." The ipTM scores reported (0.717 and 0.651 for N-myc 19-38 with full-length and ΔΔ DBD, respectively; 0.737 and 0.516 for N-myc 75-89) are only moderate, and the authors note that the N-myc 75-89 predictions showed multiple binding modes. Despite this, the manuscript presents the AlphaFold models as a central component of the proposed mechanism, including specific residue-level interactions (e.g., F28 packing against Y404, F21 packing into a hydrophobic pocket). The validation of these models is indirect, relying on HDX data that show only partial agreement. The authors do include a cautionary statement, but the overall presentation gives the models more weight than is warranted given their acknowledged limitations.
- **Why it matters** The proposed mechanism of interaction, including the competition with the acidic plug and the specific residue contacts, forms the basis for understanding how N-myc competes with DNA and how the interaction is regulated. If the AlphaFold models are incorrect in their details, the mechanistic conclusions drawn from them would be compromised. The authors' own acknowledgment of AlphaFold's unreliability in some cases undermines confidence in the specific binding mode proposed.
- **Resolution test** The authors should either provide experimental validation of the proposed binding mode (e.g., mutagenesis of predicted interface residues with binding assays, or an experimental structure of the complex) or substantially temper the claims made based on the AlphaFold models. At minimum, the manuscript should clearly distinguish between experimentally validated aspects of the interaction and those that are purely computational predictions, and the limitations of the models should be discussed more prominently.

- **Concern ID** R1-M2
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** Yes
- **Axis** Functional evidence for DNA competition
- **Claim pointer** The authors state that "N-myc competes with DNA for TFIIIC5 binding" and that this competition "provides a basis for their mutual regulation."
- **Evidence pointer** Results section "N-myc competes with DNA for TFIIIC5 binding"; Figure 7
- **Concern** The evidence for DNA competition is indirect. The EMSA experiments show that TFIIIC5 DBD ΔΔ binds DNA, and the TRIC experiments show that N-myc reduces DNA binding. However, the EMSA data are described as "quite variable" with KD estimates ranging from 127 nM to 446 nM across six replicates. The TRIC experiments used a different DNA substrate (a hairpin rather than the A-box-containing duplex used in EMSA) and the effect of N-myc was only estimated as a 10-100-fold decrease in affinity without a determined KD. The manuscript does not demonstrate that the N-myc binding site and the DNA binding site on TFIIIC5 DBD directly overlap, nor does it show that the competition is specific (e.g., a control protein or peptide should not compete). The claim of mutual regulation between N-myc and DNA for TFIIIC5 binding is therefore not firmly established.
- **Why it matters** The competition between N-myc and DNA for TFIIIC5 binding is a central claim of the manuscript and is presented as a key mechanistic insight. If this competition is not robustly demonstrated, the broader implications for how N-myc regulates TFIIIC5 function are weakened. The variability in the EMSA data and the indirect nature of the TRIC experiments leave room for alternative explanations (e.g., non-specific effects of N-myc on DNA binding, or allosteric effects).
- **Resolution test** The authors should provide more rigorous evidence for direct competition, such as: (1) a competition assay with a control protein or peptide that does not bind TFIIIC5; (2) demonstration that the N-myc binding site and DNA binding site overlap (e.g., via mutagenesis of the predicted DNA-binding residues and testing N-myc binding); (3) a direct binding assay showing that N-myc and DNA cannot bind simultaneously to TFIIIC5 DBD; (4) improved EMSA data with tighter replicates and a determined KD for the competition.

- **Concern ID** R1-M3
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** No
- **Axis** Cellular validation
- **Claim pointer** The authors state that "Mutation in MB0 reduces the TFIIIC5 interaction with N-myc in cells" based on co-immunoprecipitation experiments in SH-EP neuroblastoma cells.
- **Evidence pointer** Results section "Mutation in MB0 reduces the TFIIIC5 interaction with N-myc in cells"; Figure 9
- **Concern** The cellular validation is limited to a single co-immunoprecipitation experiment with a F28A-Y29A variant. The observed reduction is described as "small but statistically robust" (P = 0.0054) with approximately 50% reduction. However, the manuscript does not provide details on the number of biological replicates, the statistical test used, or whether the experiment was performed with appropriate controls (e.g., verification of equal expression levels of WT and variant N-myc, confirmation that the mutation does not affect N-myc stability or folding). The F28A-Y29A mutation is in the MB0 region, but the manuscript does not demonstrate that this mutation specifically disrupts the TFIIIC5 interaction without affecting other N-myc functions or interactions. The connection between the in vitro binding data and the cellular phenotype is therefore not fully established.
- **Why it matters** The cellular validation is important for establishing the biological relevance of the interaction. If the F28A-Y29A mutation has pleiotropic effects on N-myc function, the observed reduction in TFIIIC5 co-immunoprecipitation may not be specifically due to disruption of the MB0-TFIIIC5 interaction. The manuscript's claim that this validates the molecular basis of the interaction in cells is therefore not fully supported.
- **Resolution test** The authors should provide additional details on the co-immunoprecipitation experiments, including the number of replicates, statistical methods, and controls for equal protein expression. They should also consider testing additional mutations in the MB0 region or performing rescue experiments to confirm the specificity of the effect. Ideally, the cellular experiments should be extended to assess the functional consequences of disrupting the N-myc-TFIIIC5 interaction (e.g., effects on Pol III transcription or mRNA quality control).

- **Concern ID** R1-M4
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** No
- **Axis** Binding affinity discrepancy
- **Claim pointer** The authors report that MB0 binds TFIIIC5 DBD ΔΔ with a KD of 0.060 μM by FP and approximately 150 nM by ITC, while the 61-89 peptide binds with a KD of 1.1 μM by FP.
- **Evidence pointer** Results section "N-myc TAD binds to TFIIIC5 DBD ΔΔ with high affinity"; Figure 4; Supplementary Figure S2
- **Concern** The manuscript reports a significant discrepancy between the FP and ITC measurements for the MB0 peptide (60 nM vs. 150 nM, approximately 2.5-fold difference). While the authors note that technical differences (temperature) may account for this, the discrepancy is not fully explained. More concerning is the fact that the ITC experiment at 37°C resulted in protein precipitation, and the experiment was only successfully performed at 10°C. The endothermic heat signature observed is unusual for protein-protein interactions and may complicate the interpretation of the thermodynamic parameters. The manuscript does not report the full thermodynamic parameters (ΔH, ΔS) from the ITC experiment, which would help in understanding the binding mechanism. Additionally, the FP experiments used chemically synthesized peptides, which may not fully recapitulate the behavior of the full-length N-myc TAD in the context of the intact protein.
- **Why it matters** The binding affinities are central to the claim that the MB0 interaction is the dominant, high-affinity interaction between N-myc and TFIIIC5. If the affinity measurements are not reliable, the conclusion that MB0 is the primary binding site is weakened. The unusual thermodynamics and the technical difficulties with the ITC experiment raise questions about the robustness of the affinity measurements.
- **Resolution test** The authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the ITC data, including the full thermodynamic parameters and a discussion of the endothermic signature. They should also consider performing additional binding experiments with the full-length N-myc TAD (not just peptides) to confirm the affinities. If possible, the ITC experiment should be repeated under conditions where protein precipitation does not occur, or alternative methods (e.g., surface plasmon resonance) should be used to corroborate the affinities.

### Minor Comments

- **Concern ID** R1-m1
- **Severity** Minor
- **Axis** Experimental detail
- **Affected element** NMR titration methodology
- **Evidence pointer** Results section "N-myc uses two linear peptide sequences to bind TFIIIC5 DBD ΔΔ"; Figure 2
- **Issue** The NMR titration experiments are described as monitoring "peak intensity changes" rather than chemical shift perturbations, and the authors attribute this to intermediate exchange or formation of a "fuzzy" complex. However, the manuscript does not provide details on how the intensity ratios were calculated, which residues were excluded due to overlap, or how the threshold for significant intensity changes was determined. The statement that "many of these intensity changes were observed early in the titration" is qualitative and would benefit from a more quantitative analysis.
- **Required correction** The authors should provide a more detailed description of the NMR data analysis, including the criteria for identifying significantly affected residues, the number of residues analyzed, and a representative plot or table showing the intensity ratios for all assigned residues. This would strengthen the conclusion that specific regions of N-myc are involved in binding.

- **Concern ID** R1-m2
- **Severity** Minor
- **Axis** Data presentation
- **Affected element** HDX-MS results
- **Evidence pointer** Results section "Hydrogen-deuterium exchange mass spectrometry suggests two binding hotspots on TFIIIC5"; Figure 3; Supplementary Table S3
- **Issue** The HDX-MS data are described as showing "two binding hotspots" on TFIIIC5, but the manuscript does not provide a clear quantitative measure of the protection levels or the statistical significance of the differences between the apo and bound states. The statement that "five N-myc peptides covering five different sequences were significantly protected" would benefit from a definition of "significant" in this context.
- **Required correction** The authors should provide a more detailed presentation of the HDX-MS data, including the magnitude of protection for each peptide, the statistical significance thresholds used, and a clear visualization (e.g., Woods plot or heatmap) showing the protected regions on both N-myc and TFIIIC5. This would allow readers to assess the strength of the evidence for the proposed binding interface.

- **Concern ID** R1-m3
- **Severity** Minor
- **Axis** Interpretation
- **Affected element** AlphaFold model discussion
- **Evidence pointer** Results section "Modelling the N-myc:TFIIIC5 interaction"; Figure 8
- **Issue** The authors state that "the N-myc 19-38 peptide is more likely to be correct due to the consistency of the prediction" but do not provide a clear metric for this consistency. The manuscript would benefit from a more detailed comparison of the different models generated, including the structural variability across the 20 models and the confidence metrics (e.g., pLDDT, PAE) for the interface residues.
- **Required correction** The authors should provide a quantitative assessment of the model confidence, including the distribution of ipTM scores across the 20 models, the structural superposition of the top models, and the pLDDT values for the interface residues. This would help readers evaluate the reliability of the proposed binding mode.

- **Concern ID** R1-m4
- **Severity** Minor
- **Axis** Clarity
- **Affected element** Discussion of biological relevance
- **Evidence pointer** Discussion section
- **Issue** The discussion of the biological relevance of the N-myc:TFIIIC5 interaction is largely speculative, with the authors offering several possible functions (e.g., recycling factor, avidity trap, recruitment of other proteins) without experimental evidence to distinguish between them. While this is appropriate for a discussion section, the manuscript would benefit from a clearer statement of which aspects of the proposed model are supported by data and which are speculative.
- **Required correction** The authors should clearly delineate between conclusions that are directly supported by their experimental data and those that are speculative. This could be achieved by adding a paragraph that explicitly states the limitations of the current study and the specific experiments needed to test the proposed model.

- **Concern ID** R1-m5
- **Severity** Minor
- **Axis** Methodology
- **Affected element** EMSA quantification
- **Evidence pointer** Results section "N-myc competes with DNA for TFIIIC5 binding"; Figure 7
- **Issue** The EMSA data are described as "quite variable" with KD estimates ranging from 127 nM to 446 nM across six independent replicates. The manuscript does not provide an explanation for this variability or a discussion of how it affects the reliability of the conclusions drawn from these experiments.
- **Required correction** The authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the EMSA data, including the source of variability (e.g., protein batch, DNA substrate, gel conditions) and a discussion of how the variability affects the interpretation of the results. If the variability cannot be reduced, the authors should consider using a more reliable assay for DNA binding (e.g., fluorescence anisotropy with a labeled DNA substrate).

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that N-myc competes with DNA for TFIIIC5 binding is based on indirect evidence (TRIC experiments with a single N-myc concentration) and is not fully supported by the data presented.
- The specific binding mode proposed for the N-myc 19-38 peptide with TFIIIC5 DBD, including the displacement of the acidic plug, is based on AlphaFold models that the authors themselves acknowledge may be unreliable.
- The claim that the MB0 interaction is the "most biologically relevant" is not directly tested, as the cellular experiments only examine a single mutation and do not assess functional consequences.
- The statement that the N-myc:TFIIIC5 interaction "contributes to quality control in mRNA transcription" is presented as established from prior work but is not directly tested in this manuscript.
- The assertion that the interaction between N-myc and TFIIIC5 "competes with DNA and other interactions, providing a basis for their mutual regulation" extends beyond the experimental evidence, which only demonstrates binding and competition in vitro.
