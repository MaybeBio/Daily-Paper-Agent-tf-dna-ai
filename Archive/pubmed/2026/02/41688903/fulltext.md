# Dehydrocorydaline treatment suppresses Th17 differentiation via the mTOR-mediated STAT3-RORγt signaling in systemic lupus erythematosus


## Background
  Recent studies have demonstrated the pivotal role of pathogenic Th17 cells during the target organ damage in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). Therefore, identifying Th17-related precision therapeutic targets is essential for developing effective treatments.


## Methods
  Potential targets of a novel alkaloid compound, Dehydrocorydaline (DHC), in SLE were investigated using integrated network pharmacology and molecular docking. The therapeutic efficacy of DHC was evaluated in vivo using a lupus-prone mouse model and in vitro via pathogenic Th17 polarization assays. Mechanistic studies were conducted using transcriptional analysis, cell thermal shift assay (CETSA), microscale thermophoresis (MST), and chromatin immunoprecipitation-quantitative PCR (ChIP-qPCR).


## Results
  This study demonstrates the therapeutic potential of DHC in SLE. Network pharmacology analysis identified mTOR signaling and Th17 cell polarization as key potential targets and pathways of DHC. In vivo, DHC treatment selectively reduced the frequencies of Th17 cells, inhibited serum IL-17A levels, restored glomerular filtration rate (GFR), and attenuated renal damage in lupus mice. Molecular docking, CETSA, and MST results suggested a direct interaction between DHC and mTOR. In vitro, DHC inhibited the phosphorylation of mTOR and STAT3 during pathogenic Th17 differentiation. Pharmacological activation of mTOR reversed the inhibitory effects of DHC on STAT3 activation and Th17 differentiation. Mechanistically, DHC blocked the mTOR-dependent STAT3 nuclear translocation and Rorc transcription during Th17 polarization.


## Conclusion
  DHC attenuates renal damage in SLE by suppressing the Th17 response via the mTOR/STAT3/RORγt axis. This finding represents a novel therapeutic strategy for addressing the unmet clinical needs in SLE.

## Introduction
  Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic autoimmune disease characterized by multiple-organ damage. Lupus nephritis (LN), one of the most severe renal manifestations, affects approximately half of SLE patients and represents a leading cause of morbidity and mortality. Despite therapeutic advances in SLE, approximately 5–20% of LN patients progress to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) within 15 years of diagnosis (Anders et al. 2020). Currently, single-cell RNA sequencing with kidney biopsy samples from LN patients identified the expression of interleukin-17 receptor (IL-17RA) in the majority of renal cells from LN patients, indicating the contribution of IL-17 signaling in LN pathogenesis (Arazi et al. 2019). To date, effective early prevention and intervention strategies for LN remain limited. Thus, further exploration of precision treatment targets is essential to improve the diagnosis and clinical management of LN.

  IL-17-producing helper T (Th17) cells serve as a proinflammatory T helper subset, characterized by their secretion of IL-17 and expression of the transcription factor RORγt (Annunziato et al. 2007). Our previous work has established the critical role of Th17 cells in both initiating the pathogenic process of SLE (Tang et al. 2025; Ma et al. 2021). We have also demonstrated that Th17 cells are crucial for maintaining the survival of pathogenic plasma cells in human and murine lupus (Ma et al. 2021). Consequently, Th17 and IL-17 represent promising therapeutic targets for SLE. The mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) is essential for the Th17 response and SLE pathogenesis (He et al. 2020). The mTOR polymorphism rs2295080 is associated with an increased risk of SLE (He et al. 2020). Inhibition of mTOR with rapamycin has been shown to alleviaterenal damage in both animal models and SLE patients by suppressing the proliferation of pro-inflammatory T subsets, including T helper 1 (Th1) cells and Th17 (Song et al. 2021). Furthermore, recent studies indicate that rapamycin blocks Th17 polarization and mitigates the pathogenic process of Th17 in experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) (Hou et al. 2017).

  Dehydrocorydaline (DHC) is an active alkaloid derived from Corydalis yanhusuo W. T. Wang (C. yanhusuo). Current evidence suggests that DHC exhibits therapeutic potential in various diseases, including myocardial ischemia–reperfusion injury, atherosclerosis, chronic unpredictable mild stress, pulmonary fibrosis, and gastric ulcer (He et al. 2025;Li et al. 2025; Li et al. 2021; Wen et al. 2022). Mechanistic studies indicate that DHC may promote M2 macrophage polarization, inhibit fibroblast activation, and modulate smooth muscle cell phenotype (Dang et al. 2025). However, the therapeutic effects of DHC on SLE and LN, as well as its underlying mechanisms, remain largely unexplored.

  In the present study, we demonstrate that DHC attenuates SLE progression and suppresses the Th17 response in lupus-prone mice. Through integrated analysis using bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), network pharmacology, molecular docking, cell thermal shift assay (CETSA), and microscale thermophoresis (MST), we reveal that DHC directly inhibits the Th17 response via the mTOR-STAT3 signaling axis. Importantly, DHC may function as an mTOR antagonist, reducing STAT3 phosphorylation and thereby suppressing the transcriptional activity of RORγt during Th17 differentiation. Our findings identify DHC as a novel therapeutic candidate for the treatment of SLE.


## Materials and methods

### Network pharmacology
  Major components of DHC were initially obtained using three databases–Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology Database and Analysis Platform (TCMSP, http://tcmspw.com/tcmsp.php), Swiss Target Prediction (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/), and Similarity Ensemble Approach (SEA, http://sea.bkslab.org/)-to predict potential targets. Following data integration and duplicate removal, 117 valid targets of DHC were obtained. Subsequently, a search for SLE-related targets was performed using GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/), yielding 8,775 potential targets. Following application of a standard screening method, 836 valid targets with relevance scores exceeding three times the median were selected. By intersecting the 117 DHC targets with the 836 SLE targets, 29 common targets were identified. These targets were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO, http://geneontology.org/) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG, https://www.kegg.jp/) enrichment analysis using the Metascape platform (http://metascape.org/), and the results were visualized via the bioinformatics website (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/). Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis was performed using the STRING database (https://string-db.org/), and the network was reconstructed using Cytoscape 3.10.2 for topological analysis.


### Chemicals and reagents
  DHC (HY-N0674) and MHY1485 (HY-B0795) were purchased from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) and dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; MP Biomedicals, Solon, OH, USA). The solubilization formulation of DHC for intraperitoneal injection consisted of 2% DMSO, 40% PEG300 (Selleckchem, Houston, TX, USA), 5% Tween 80 (Solarbio, Beijing, China), and 53% saline. The chemical structure of DHC was depicted via the ChemDraw software.


### Animal model
  Female 6-week-old C57BL/6J mice were obtained from WuXi AppTec Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and housed in the Specific Pathogen-Free (SPF) facility of the Laboratory Animal Center, Sun Yat-sen University, Shenzhen Campus. Splenic lymphocytes from female C57BL/6J mice were stimulated with 5 mg/mL concanavalin A (GLPBIO, Montclair, CA, USA). Apoptotic chromatin was extracted from activated lymphocytes and adjusted to a final concentration of 2 μg/μL. On days 0, 14, and 28, C57BL/6 mice were immunized subcutaneously with an emulsion containing 2 μg/μL chromatin and Complete Freund’s Adjuvant (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) mixed at a 1:1 ratio in a total volume of 200 µL. On day 42, mice were injected subcutaneously in the inguinal region with a 1:1 emulsion of 2 μg/μL apoptotic chromatin and Quick CTL Adjuvant (Biodragon, Beijing, China) as we reported previously (Ma et al. 2023; Li et al. 2004).


### Cell culture
  T cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Procell, Wuhan, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Excell, Wuhan, China), 2 mM L-glutamine (Procell), 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA), and 55 μM β-mercaptoethanol (Gibco). Cells were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2. CD4+ naïve T cells (Tn) were isolated from the spleens of female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) by magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) using the Mouse CD4+ T Cell Isolation Kit (RWD Life Science, Shenzhen, China), LarSep Columns (RWD Life Science), and MACS® MultiStand (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, DEU) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Purified CD4+ Tn (1 × 106 cells/well) were seeded into 6-well plates (NEST, Wuxi, China) pre-coated with 2 μg/mL purified anti-mouse CD3ε antibody (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) and 2 μg/mL purified anti-mouse CD28 antibody (BioLegend). For Th17 polarization, cells were cultured in complete RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 2 ng/mL recombinant mouse TGF-β1 (BioLegend), 20 ng/mL recombinant mouse IL-6 (BioLegend), 25 ng/mL recombinant mouse IL-23 (BioLegend), 20 ng/mL mouse IL-1β recombinant protein (PeproTech, Cranbury, NJ, USA), 5 μg/mL purified anti-mouse IL-4 antibody (BioLegend), 5 μg/mL purified anti-mouse IFN-γ antibody (BioLegend) at 37 °C/5% CO2 for 72 h.


### Microscale thermophoresis (MST)
  The pCMV3-mTOR-green fluorescent protein (GFP) expression plasmid was constructed by replacing the C-terminal OFPSpark® tag in the commercial pCMV3-C-OFPSpark-mTOR plasmid (Sino Biological, Beijing, China) with GFP, using restriction cloning. HEK293T cells transfected with the mTOR-GFP plasmid were harvested and lysed 48 h post-transfection. Transfection efficiency was assessed by detecting GFP fluorescence signals using an Invitrogen EVOS M5000 fluorescence microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Serially diluted DHC solutions were mixed with an equal volume of cell lysate and loaded into Monolith Capillaries, and binding was measured using the NanoTemper Monolith NT.115 instrument (NanoTemper Technologies, Munich, Germany). Data were collected with MO. Control 2 software and binding curves were fitted using MO. Affinity Analysis v3 software (NanoTemper Technologies).


### Cell thermal shift assay (CETSA)
  The lysates of HEK293T cells with overexpression of mTOR were incubated with or without 50 µM DHC and heated at temperatures ranging from 40 to 65 °C for 5 min, followed by cooling at room temperature for 5 min. Subsequently, the samples were centrifuged for western blot analysis.


### Statistical analysis
  Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA). P-values were determined by unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

  Detailed methods were uploaded in the Supplementary Materials and Tab.S1.


## Results

### DHC attenuates renal damage in lupus mice
  DHC is a natural alkaloid. To further explore the effect of DHC on SLE, lupus mice were treated with either DHC or solvent control for a successive 12 weeks, as shown in Fig. 1A. We injected DHC (0.5 mg/kg) at a safe dosage with no impairment of hepatic or renal function (He et al. 2025). Spleens of each group exhibited significantly reduced spleen/body weight ratios in DHC-treated lupus mice (Fig. 1B). However, comparable body weights were observed between vehicle and DHC-treated lupus mice (Fig. S1). Importantly, the transdermal glomerular filtration rate (GFR) was measured in lupus mice using the miniaturized fluorescence monitor to assess real-time renal function with high sensitivity (Friedemann et al. 2016). As shown in Fig. 1C, DHC-treated lupus mice exhibited much faster fluorescence decay when compared with vehicle-treated control lupus mice. Indicating improved GFR (in mL/min/100 g b.w.) based on the accelerated decay of fluorescence intensity over time (plasma half-life of FITC-sinistrin). Moreover, histological analysis also showed significantly reduced glomerular infiltration and immune-complex deposition (IgG and IgM) in lupus mice with DHC treatment (Fig. 1D). Statistical analysis confirmed the dramatically restored GFR values and ameliorated glomerular damage in DHC-treated lupus mice (Fig. 1E). Interestingly, DHC showed no significant effects on serum anti-dsDNA IgG and anti-chromatin IgG levels in lupus mice (Fig. S2). Indicating the therapeutic effects of DHC may through an autoantibody-independent manner. ### DHC selectively inhibits the Th17 response in lupus mice
  For the validation of the DHC-targeted pathway in the treatment of SLE, single-cell suspensions were prepared from the spleens and kidneys of lupus mice for flow cytometry analysis. We then distinguished the CD4+ T cells, CD4+CD62L+CD44− Tn, CD4+CD62L+CD44+ central memory T cells (Tcm), and CD4+CD62L−CD44+ effector T cells (Teff). No significant changes were observed in the splenic CD4+ Tn, Tcm, and Teff subsets in lupus mice with or without DHC treatment in vivo. Moreover, by intracellular cytokine staining, we also detected the IFN-γ-secreting Th1 and IL-17A-secreting Th17 in lupus mice. We observed a dramatic reduction in both frequencies and absolute numbers of Th17 in the spleens of lupus mice with DHC administration (Fig. 3A and B). We detected markedly decreased frequencies of CD4+ T cells and Th17 in the kidneys of DHC-treated lupus mice as well (Fig. 3C and D). The frequencies of Th1 subsets remained comparable in mice with or without DHC intervention in vivo (Fig. 3A-D). Furthermore, the imbalance of proinflammatory Th17 and inhibitory regulatory T cells (Tregs) has been implicated in the pathogenesis of lupus (Yang et al. 2009). Herein, we also detected the frequencies of CD4+CD25+Foxp3+ Tregs. Interestingly, we found that there were no significant changes in the frequencies of Treg in the spleen and kidney of lupus mice with DHC treatment (Fig. S3 A and B).   The potential core targets of DHC were then screened using the TCMSP, Swiss Target Prediction, and the SEA. ### DHC inhibits the Th17 response in vitro

By flow cytometric analysis, we observed the dose-dependent inhibition in the frequencies of IL-17-secreting cells in the Th17 polarization system (Fig. 6A).   For the signal cascade analysis, we detected the effects of DHC on mTOR, JAK1/2, STAT3, and p65 activation in the Th17 polarization system as indicated by Western blotting (Chen et al. 2007).   To verify the interaction between DHC and mTOR, we first transfected 293T cells with the pCMV3-mTOR-GFP plasmid to overexpress mTOR with the GFP label. The transfection efficacy was identified by GFP fluorescence (Fig. 7C). We then performed CETSA and MST to confirm the engagement between DHC and mTOR using the cell lysis from 293T cells with mTOR-GFP expression. The CETSA results exhibited that DHC treatment significantly enhanced the thermal stability of mTOR at higher temperatures, when compared to the DMSO-treated vehicle group (Fig. 7D and E). Consistently, results of the MST assay showed a higher binding affinity for DHC and mTOR, and the KD value was 5.85 μM (Fig. 7F). Moreover, the mTOR activator also significantly reversed the DHC-induced reduction in mRNA and protein levels of RORγt in the Th17 polarization system (Fig. 8B and C). For the signal cascade analysis, we observed the markedly rescued mTOR phosphorylation and STAT3 phosphorylation by MHY1485 in DHC-treated Th17 (Fig. 8D). However, no significant changes in protein levels of total mTOR but reversed STAT3 protein expression were observed by MHY1485 in DHC-treated Th17 (Fig. S8A).   IRF4 and BATF are two transcription factors related to Th17 polarization (Wang et al. 2025). Here, we observed no significant changes in the protein levels of IRF4 and BATF by either DHC or MHY1485 treatment (Fig. S9). ### DHC limits the mTOR-dependent nuclear trans-localization of STAT3 during Th17 differentiation in vitro
  As the canonical transcriptional activity of STAT3 needs to be performed in the nuclear region, to demonstrate the underlying mechanism of the mTOR/STAT3/RORγt axis modulated by DHC, we analyzed the nuclear-cytoplasmic translocation of STAT3 with or without the DHC treatment and MHY1485 preincubation. As shown in Fig. 8E and F, we isolated the cytoplasmic and nuclear proteins, respectively, to determine the subcellular localization of p-STAT3 in Th17. As expected, the Th17 in the control group showed strong nuclear localization of p-STAT3. DHC treatment significantly reduced the nucleus p-STAT3, and the preincubation of MHY1485 could restore the nuclear entry of p-STAT3 in Th17 by DHC. In contrast, the cytoplasmic p-STAT3 showed no significant changes during DHC and MHY1485 treatment. To further address the transcriptional activity of STAT3, we performed the ChIP-qPCR assay with the anti-STAT3 antibody. Consistently, DHC treatment alone significantly reduced the binding of STAT3 to the promoter region of Rorc, and preincubation of MHY1485 could rescue the binding of STAT3 (Fig. 8G). Our findings indicate the central role of DHC in the treatment of SLE by inhibiting the mTOR/STAT3/RORγt axis during Th17 differentiation.


## Discussion
  SLE is a multisystem autoimmune disorder characterized by dysregulated immune responses and chronic inflammation. Conventional therapies, such as glucocorticoids and immunosuppressants, are limited by severe side effects and high relapse rates (Mok et al. 2023). In this study, we employed the apoptotic chromatin-immunized lupus mouse model, which closely represents key features of human SLE, including natural anti-dsDNA IgG production and immune complex deposition-induced renal injury (Li et al. 2004; Ma et al. 2018). Notably, serum autoantibody levels of anti-dsDNA IgG and anti-chromatin IgG were not significantly altered by DHC treatment, indicating that the therapeutic effects of DHC are likely mediated through an autoantibody-independent mechanism.

  The dissociation between autoantibody production and renal injury has been well documented in lupus-prone (NZB × NZW) F1 mice. In Ig μ heavy chain transgenic mice, high titers of autoantibodies are observed despite markedly ameliorated glomerulonephritis (Wellmann et al. 2001). Similarly, STAT6-deficient (NZB × NZW) F1 mice exhibit significant protection from renal damage in the presence of persistently elevated anti-dsDNA antibody levels (Jacob et al. 2003). In addition, we have previously reported the cardiovascular protective effects of DHC, which may further contribute to its therapeutic benefits in lupus (Wen et al. 2022). Collectively, these findings support the concept that the uncoupling of autoantibody levels and renal pathology observed in DHC-treated lupus mice likely reflects the potent anti-inflammatory and tissue-protective effects of DHC, rather than direct suppression of autoantibody production.

  Network pharmacology has emerged as a transformative approach for SLE drug discovery, particularly for traditional medicines (Li et al. 2013). Using an integrated approach involving target identification and validation, network construction, transcriptomic integration, and pathway enrichment, we identified the impact of DHC on the Th17 differentiation pathway during lupus pathogenesis. Further screening of bioactive compounds via network pharmacology may uncover additional effective natural components for SLE treatment.

  DHC is a quaternary ammonium alkaloid extracted from the tubers of C. yanhusuo (Li et al. 2014). Clinical findings indicate that Yanhusuo exhibits significant therapeutic efficacy in diseases such as coronary heart disease, acute myocardial infarction, and atherosclerosis (Wen et al. 2022; Li et al. 2025). Importantly, SLE patients also showed a higher occurrence of cardiovascular diseases (CVD), such as ischemic heart disease and cardiac arrest (Schoenfeld et al. 2013). The underlying pathophysiology is mainly driven by chronic inflammation and immune dysregulation in SLE. Consequently, the cardiovascular protective effects of DHC in both lupus patients and animal models require further validation.

  We report for the first time the anti-Th17 response of DHC in SLE. However, the imbalance of Th1, Th17, and Treg cells has been reported in SLE patients (Talaat et al. 2015). Furthermore, the pathogenic effects of Th1 mediated by IFN-γ secretion have been reported in lupus mice through the IFNGR1/2-pSTAT1-TBX21 axis, and a cohort study further indicated a positive correlation between serum IFN-γ and the SLEDAI scores (Chen et al. 2022; Theofilopoulos et al. 2001). Here, we observed no significant changes in Th1 and serum IFN-γ levels in DHC-treated lupus mice. Similarly, no marked changes in the frequencies of Treg cells were observed in DHC-treated lupus mice. These results suggest that DHC selectively suppresses the Th17 response without substantially affecting Tregs or other Th cell populations in lupus mice. Targeting mTOR has shown promising therapeutic effects in SLE patients according to a meta-analysis. Notably, the mTOR inhibitor, sirolimus, effectively promoted the remission of nephritis development in SLE patients as well (Ji et al. 2020). Mechanistically, the mTOR forms two distinct complexes, mTORC1 and mTORC2, based on the molecular components of the complex (Sarbassov et al. 2005). Rapamycin is a selective mTOR inhibitor primarily via blocking mTORC1 activity (Li et al. 2014). The mTORC1 serves as a crucial link between nutrition and metabolism, which could sense nutrients, growth factors, and environmental stresses. Previous studies showed that mTORC1 is located in the lysosome membrane and is activated by the Rheb GTPase (Yang et al. 2017). In our study, we detected that DHC inhibits mTOR phosphorylation and suppresses STAT3 nuclear translocation during Th17 polarization.

  Prior work has shown that protein kinase C-θ (PKC-θ) upregulates STAT3 in Th17 cells (Kwon et al. 2012). Additionally, non-canonical STAT3 activity regulates TCR-induced mitochondrial membrane potential and Ca2+ flux, thereby influencing the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-17 (Poholek et al. 2020). The presence of STAT3 in mitochondria also participates in Th17 differentiation (Lee et al. 2025). Here, we observed the upregulation of total protein levels of STAT3 by DHC. The underlying mechanisms require further investigation.


## Conclusions
  In conclusion, our study on Th17 and lupus mice suggests that DHC reduces renal damage and inflammation by inhibiting the activation of mTOR-STAT3 and the transcription of Rorc during Th17 polarization. Thus, natural product DHC may be a potential candidate in the treatment of SLE and its associated target organ damage.
