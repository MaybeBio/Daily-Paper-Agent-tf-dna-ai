# Identification and validation of paraptosis-related prognostic biomarkers in lung adenocarcinoma: An observational study based on transcriptomics and clinical outcomes


## Abstract
  Paraptosis plays a critical role in mediating anti-tumor effects by inducing cell death in cancer cells. However, its specific involvement in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) remains inadequately understood. This study aims to systematically investigate the prognostic significance and underlying mechanisms of paraptosis-related genes (PRGs) in LUAD. Differentially expressed genes were identified between LUAD and control samples from the training set and cross-referenced with PRGs to generate candidate genes (CGs). Prognostic genes were selected from CGs using regression analysis, leading to the development of a LUAD risk model, which was validated in an independent validation set. Clinical characteristics were analyzed to identify independent prognostic factors for constructing a nomogram. Functional and immune infiltration analyses were performed on high-/low-risk cohorts from the training set. Drug predictions related to prognostic genes were made and subsequently validated through molecular docking. Polymerase chain reaction was performed to validate the expression of prognostic genes. Four prognostic genes (CDKN3, PEBP1, TNFRSF19, and PHB) were identified from 27 CGs through regression analysis. The prognostic risk model demonstrated robust predictive capacity for LUAD prognosis and exhibited generalizability. Significant associations were observed between risk scores and clinical features, including age, TNM.stage, T-stage, and N-stage (P < .05). These risk scores served as independent prognostic factors for the nomogram model, offering strong predictive power for LUAD. Vorinostat and raloxifene exhibited notable binding affinity for PEBP1. Elevated CDKN3 expression was observed in LUAD, while PEBP1 and TNFRSF19 expressions were reduced. This study highlights the prognostic value of PRGs, specifically CDKN3, PEBP1, TNFRSF19, and PHB. CDKN3 and PHB emerged as risk factors for LUAD prognosis, whereas PEBP1 and TNFRSF19 did not. In-depth analysis of the tumor microenvironment revealed the distribution and correlations of immune cell types influenced by PRGs and risk score. Furthermore, an independent prognostic model for LUAD was developed, enhancing our understanding of high-/low-risk cohorts’ functional pathways. Drug prediction results provided valuable insights into potential therapeutic strategies for LUAD, warranting further investigation.


## 1. Introduction
  Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, with its incidence and mortality rates rising sharply in recent years.[1,2] According to the World Health Organization Classification of Thoracic Cancers 2021, lung cancer (LC) classification primarily relies on morphological assessment, followed by immunohistochemistry and molecular biology techniques.[3] Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), comprising approximately 85% of all LC cases, is the most common form, with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) being its most prevalent subtype.[4–6] The incidence of LUAD continues to increase. LUAD is characterized by invasive growth, transitioning from a tumor that preserves alveolar structure to one that remodels and invades lung tissue.[7] Recent advances in understanding LUAD’s molecular pathogenesis have led to the development of several precision therapies.[8] While the prognostic significance of genes like NPM1 and EMG1 has been demonstrated,[2,9] considerable knowledge gaps persist. LUAD is highly heterogeneous, with its pathogenesis driven by numerous genetic mutations, epigenetic changes, and the aberrant activation of signaling pathways.[10] However, the full scope of these complex regulatory networks remains poorly understood. Additionally, despite advances in diagnostics and treatments, the 5-year survival rate for LUAD remains dismally low at 10% to 20%, highlighting the urgent need for novel biomarkers beyond current therapeutic targets.[11] Furthermore, the role of the tumor microenvironment (TME) in LUAD, particularly in disease progression and resistance to treatment, remains incompletely elucidated, with a focus on the interactions between immune cells and tumor cells. Finally, although paraptosis has demonstrated anti-tumor effects in various cancers, its specific mechanisms in LUAD and the function of paraptosis-related genes (PRGs) have yet to be fully explored, necessitating further research for therapeutic applications.

  Programmed cell death (PCD) is a fundamental regulatory mechanism in living systems, playing a critical role in maintaining intracellular stability both in health and disease.[12] Apoptosis, one of the key forms of PCD, is vital to various diseases, including cancer.[13] Paraptosis, another significant form of PCD, is characterized by cytoplasmic vacuolization and differs from apoptosis in several crucial aspects.[14] Paraptosis represents a therapeutically exploitable death mechanism, induced in cancers by natural compounds and metal complexes.[11] Members of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) family have been shown to be essential for the induction of paraptosis.[15] Additionally, studies suggest that the activation of the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway facilitates LUAD proliferation and metastasis.[16] Both paraptosis and LUAD are linked to the MAPK pathway. Paraptosis signaling is also involved in other cancers, including colorectal cancer, glioblastoma, and pancreatic cancer,[17–19] and may similarly impact LUAD. A recent study identified 24 PRGs, including CDKN3, TP53, and PHB, that are highly up-regulated in tumor tissues and play critical roles in LUAD treatment and prognosis.[20] Despite this, the expression patterns, prognostic significance, and molecular functions (MFs) of PRGs in LUAD remain underexplored, and the associated molecular mechanisms are not fully understood. Consequently, the development of therapeutic strategies, early diagnosis, and prognosis for patients with LUAD relies heavily on the identification of novel and more reliable biomarkers.

  This study conducted a comprehensive investigation into the prognostic significance of PRGs and their potential mechanisms in LUAD through bioinformatic analysis and experimental validation. Stratifying patients with LUAD by predictive risk scores will provide insights into their immunological status, TME, and drug responses, offering new therapeutic options for clinical management.


## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1. Data source
  The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)-LUAD dataset, comprising 511 LUAD cases and 59 normal lung tissue samples, was obtained from the TCGA database (http://cancergenome.nih.gov/) for use as the training set. This dataset included raw data for patients with LUAD, such as RNA-seq data, clinical details, and survival information. The validation set, GSE13213 (platform: GPL6480), was sourced from the Gene Expression Omnibus (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), containing 117 lung tissue samples from patients with LUAD along with clinical information, including age, gender, pathological stage, and survival data. Additionally, 66 PRGs were compiled from existing literature.[21]


### 2.2. Differential expression analysis
  For differential gene expression analysis, the DESeq2 package (version 1.38.0)[22] was employed to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between LUAD and control groups in the training set. The thresholds were set to |log2 fold change (FC)| > 0.5 and P.adj < .05. Volcano and heat maps of DEGs were generated using the “ggplot2” (version 3.4.1)[23] and pheatmap (version 1.0.12, https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=pheatmap) packages, respectively, with the top 10 up- and down-regulated DEGs labeled.


### 2.3. Acquisition and function of candidate genes (CGs)
  To identify DEGs related to PRGs in LUAD, the intersection of DEGs and PRGs was obtained using the ‘ggvenn’ package (version 0.1.9, https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=ggvenn) to generate the CGs. To explore the biological functions and signaling pathways associated with CGs, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed using the “ClusterProfiler” package (version 4.7.1.3).[24] GO analysis encompassed biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and MF. The analyses were performed with a P-value threshold of <.05, and the top 15 most significant enriched functions were visualized in dendrograms.


### 2.4. Screening for prognostic genes
  Univariate Cox regression analysis (P < .05) was conducted on CGs in LUAD samples from the training set using the coxph function in the ‘survival’ package (version 3.5.3, https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=survival) to identify genes with prognostic value. These genes were further tested for the proportional hazards (PH) assumption (P > .05). Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression, implemented via the “glmnet” package (version 4.1.4),[25] was used to perform feature selection and control model complexity through L1 regularization. LASSO is particularly effective for handling high-dimensional data and constructing concise models, especially when key variables must be clearly defined. The optimal model was determined when lambda was minimized. Prognostic genes were selected from the LASSO model for constructing the LUAD risk model: risk score=∑ni=1(βgene×coefi).


### 2.5. Evaluation of prognostic risk model
  A risk model was constructed based on prognostic genes, with risk scores calculated for each patient with LUAD in the training set using the aforementioned formula. LUAD samples were then divided into high- and low-risk cohorts based on the median risk score. The distribution of risk scores and survival status for patients with LUAD were visualized. The difference in overall survival (OS) between the 2 risk cohorts was assessed using the log-rank test (P < .05) and displayed with Kaplan–Meier (K–M) curves, generated via the ‘survminer’ package (version 0.4.9, https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=survminer). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted using the ‘survivalROC’ package (version 1.0.3.1, https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=survivalROC) to predict OS in patients with LUAD at 1, 3, and 5 years, with the area under the curve (AUC) calculated to indicate predictive accuracy (AUC > 0.6).


### 2.6. Validation of prognostic risk model
  To validate the generalizability of the model, the risk model was applied to LUAD data from the validation set. Similar high- and low-risk cohorts were established, and the distribution of risk scores and patient survival status were displayed. ROC curve analysis further assessed the model’s accuracy.


### 2.7. Clinical characteristics analysis
  To verify the reliability of the risk model, LUAD samples from the training set were grouped based on clinical characteristics such as age (≤60 years, >60 years), gender (male, female), TNM.stage (stage I, stage II, stage III, and stage IV), T-stage (T1–4), N-stage (N0–2), and M-stage (M1, M2). Survival analysis was conducted between high- and low-risk cohorts within each clinical subgroup, with the results presented via K–M curves (P < .05). The distribution of risk scores across these clinical characteristics was examined using the Wilcoxon test (for 2 groups) and Kruskal–Wallis test (for multiple groups) (P < .05).


### 2.8. Independent prognostic analysis
  To investigate whether the risk score serves as an independent prognostic factor for LUAD, univariate Cox regression analysis was performed on the significantly different clinical characteristics and risk score from the training set. Potential independent prognostic factors with hazard ratios (HR) ≠ 1 and P < .05 were selected. These factors were further tested for PH assumption (P > .05). Factors that passed the PH test were then analyzed using multivariate regression analysis through the “survival” package to determine final independent prognostic factors (P < .05). A nomogram was developed using the ‘rms’ package (version 6.2-0, https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=rms), with each factor scored according to its impact on LUAD prognosis (i.e., regression coefficient). The total points were calculated to predict 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival probabilities for patients with LUAD. To assess the accuracy of the nomogram, a calibration curve was plotted, with results closer to the reference line (true probability) indicating greater reliability. Finally, ROC curve analysis was performed using the “survivalROC” package, with an AUC > 0.6 validating the independent prognostic factors.


### 2.9. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA)
  To understand the biological processes underlying the significant enrichment observed in high- and low-risk patients with LUAD, GSEA was performed on the 2 risk cohorts in the training set. Initially, differences between the 2 cohorts (high-risk vs low-risk) were analyzed using the “DESeq2” package, with log2FC values calculated and ranked by magnitude. GSEA was subsequently executed using the “ClusterProfiler” package, with reference gene sets taken from the Molecular Signatures Database: “c2.cp.v2023.2.Hs.symbols.gmt” and “c5.all.v2023.2.Hs.symbols.gmt.” A threshold of P.adjust < .05 and |normalized enrichment score (NES)| > 1 was applied. The top 3 positively and negatively enriched KEGG pathways and GO functions were displayed based on NES rankings.


### 2.10. Immune infiltration analysis
  To investigate immune infiltration in LUAD, the xCell algorithm[26] was used to calculate immune cell infiltration scores for 64 immune cell types across the high- and low-risk cohorts in the training set. The distribution of immune cell proportions was visualized. The Wilcoxon test was then applied to identify immune cells with significantly different infiltration scores between the 2 cohorts (P < .05). Correlation analyses were performed to assess the relationships between immune cell infiltration, risk score, and prognostic genes. This analysis was conducted in the LUAD training set using the ‘psych’ package (version 2.2.9, https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=psych), with correlation coefficients (|r|) > 0.3 and P < .05 considered significant.


### 2.11. Drug prediction and molecular docking
  To identify potential drugs targeting the prognostic genes, the CellMiner database was queried. Drug–gene pairs with |r| > 0.4 and P < .05 were retained for further analysis. The drug–prognostic gene interaction network was visualized using the “ggalluvial” package (version 0.12.5).[27] To assess the binding potential of the drugs with the prognostic genes, molecular docking simulations were conducted using CB-Dock and visualized with Pymol software. First, the protein structures of prognostic genes were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank. Next, top-ranked drugs correlated with the prognostic genes were obtained from the PubChem database. Finally, molecular docking was performed for key drugs and their respective prognostic genes, with binding energy values of <−1.2 kcal/mol considered significant.


### 2.12. Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)
  The expression levels of prognostic genes were validated in 5 LUAD and control samples via RT-qPCR. The clinical samples of this study were collected from Shenzhen Hospital, Southern Medical University with written informed consent obtained from all participants. The study protocol received ethics approval (Approval No.: NYSZYYEC2024K072R001) from the Institutional Review Board of Shenzhen Hospital, Southern Medical University. Total RNA was isolated from the 10 samples with TRIzol reagent (Ambion, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA concentration was measured using a NanoPhotometer N50. cDNA was generated via reverse transcription with the SureScript-First-strand-cDNA-synthesis-kit, and the reverse transcription process was conducted using an S1000TM Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad, USA). qPCR experiments were executed with a CFX Connect real-time quantitative fluorescence PCR instrument (Bio-Rad, USA) under the following conditions: initial denaturation for 1 minute at 95°C, followed by 40 cycles of 20 seconds at 95°C, 20 seconds at 55°C, and 30 seconds at 72°C. GAPDH was used as the reference gene, and relative expression levels of prognostic genes were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCT method.


### 2.13. Statistical analysis
  All statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria, http://www.r-project.org/). Differences between groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon test, with significance set at P < .05.


## 3. Results

### 3.1. The 27 CGs came from 8857 DEGs and 66 PRGs
  Differential expression analysis identified a total of 8857 DEGs, including 5639 up-regulated and 3218 down-regulated DEGs in the LUAD group (Fig. 1A and B). The intersection of these 8857 DEGs and 66 PRGs resulted in 27 CGs (Fig. 1C). For these 27 CGs, 307 GO biological functions were enriched, including 243 BPs, 28 MFs, and 36 CCs. The most significantly enriched GO functions included “BP: ER overload response,” “MF: calcium-release channel activity,” and “CC: smooth endoplasmic reticulum” (Fig. 1D). Additionally, the CGs were significantly enriched for 21 KEGG pathways, with the highest enrichment observed for the “MAPK signaling pathway,” “small cell LC,” and “P53 signaling pathway” (Fig. 1E).


### 3.2. Risk model could effectively act as a prognostic model for LUAD
  Univariate Cox regression analysis of the 27 CGs revealed 6 genes with prognostic value (HR ≠ 1, P < .05), all of which passed the PH assumption test (P > .05) (Fig. 2A and Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R386). Subsequently, LASSO analysis was performed on these 6 prognostic genes using LUAD samples from the training set. The optimal model, identified when lambda.min = 0.01504096, included 4 prognostic genes: CDKN3, PEBP1, TNFRSF19, and PHB (Fig. 2B). The resulting risk score formula was as follows: risk score = (0.1835 × CDKN3) + (−0.2506 × PEBP1) + (−0.1042 × TNFRSF19) + (0.0746 × PHB). The LUAD samples in the training set were divided into high- and low-risk cohorts based on the median risk score, resulting in 255 high-risk and 256 low-risk samples. The distribution of risk scores and patient survival status for the 2 cohorts were displayed (Fig. 2C and D). K–M curves demonstrated that low-risk patients with LUAD had significantly longer survival compared to high-risk patients (P < .0001) (Fig. 2E). The ROC curve showed AUC values of 0.63, 0.65, and 0.65 at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively, confirming that the constructed risk model effectively serves as a prognostic tool for LUAD (Fig. 2F).

  In the validation set, risk scores for each patient with LUAD were calculated based on the prognostic risk model, and the patients were divided into high- and low-risk cohorts, with 58 high-risk and 59 low-risk patients (Fig. 3A and B). K–M curves again indicated a significant survival difference between the 2 cohorts (P = .00074) (Fig. 3C). In the ROC curves of OS on in LUAD patients, the AUCs were all above 0.6, indicating that the constructed prognostic risk model is effective (Fig. 3D). However, it did not exceed 0.7, and external validation in a clinical setting was still required.


### 3.3. Risk cohorts and risk score differed significantly in some clinical characteristics
  Clinical characteristic analysis revealed significant survival differences (P < .05) between high- and low-risk cohorts across various groups, including age (>60 years), gender (male and female), T-stage (T2 and T3), N-stage (N0 and N1), and M-stage (M0) and TNM.stage (stage II) (Fig. 4, Figure S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R386). Further exploration of the relationship between risk score and clinical characteristics identified notable differences in risk scores across age, TNM.stage, T-stage, and N-stage (P < .05) (Fig. 5).


### 3.4. Nomogram was a great predictor of survival probability in patients with LUAD
  Univariate Cox analysis, combining age, TNM.stage, T-stage, N-stage, and risk score using LUAD data from the training set, showed that all factors met the criteria for prognostic significance (HR ≠ 1, P < .05) (Fig. 6A). All 4 factors also satisfied the PH assumption (P > .05). Multivariate Cox analysis confirmed their independent prognostic value, with P < .05 for all 3 factors (Fig. 6B). A nomogram was developed incorporating these independent prognostic factors, with higher total points correlating to increased survival probability for patients with LUAD (Fig. 6C). Calibration curves indicated excellent agreement between the predicted 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival probabilities and the reference line, demonstrating the nomogram’s high predictive accuracy (Fig. 6D). Additionally, the ROC curve showed AUCs >0.6 at 1, 3, and 5 years, validating risk score, TNM.stage, and T-stage, and N-stage as independent prognostic factors (Fig. 6E).


### 3.5. Functional differences between risk cohorts
  GSEA results revealed significant enrichment in 541 GO functions and 368 KEGG pathways for the high- and low-risk cohorts in the training set (Fig. 7A and B). Among the top findings, the most significantly enriched KEGG pathways and GO functions, ranked by positive and negative NES scores, included “spindle elongation” and the “medicus reference cenpe interaction with NDC80 complex,” both of which were notably active in the high-risk cohort. These pathways and functions may therefore contribute to the higher risk of LUAD by influencing disease progression.


### 3.6. There were strong correlations between risk score and prognostic genes with most of differential immune cells
  To explore the immune characteristics during the development of LUAD, the xCell algorithm was 1st used to calculate the infiltration scores of 64 types of immune cells in LUAD patients in the high/low-risk groups, and the distribution proportions of different immune cell types were observed (Fig. 8A). A comparison of infiltration scores between the 2 risk cohorts revealed 40 immune cell types with significant differences, including adipocytes, astrocytes, and CD4+ memory T cells (Figure S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R386). Correlation analysis showed strong associations between risk score, prognostic genes (CDKN3, TNFRSF19, PHB, and PEBP1), and most differential immune cells (Fig. 8B). Notably, both risk score and CDKN3 were strongly negatively correlated with hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) and positively correlated with Th2 cells. TNFRSF19 exhibited the strongest negative correlation with Th1 cells and the strongest positive correlation with neurons. PHB showed the strongest negative correlation with HSC and the strongest positive correlation with megakaryocyte–erythroid progenitors (MEP). PEBP1 had a robust negative correlation with keratinocytes and a strong positive correlation with neurons. We speculated that these prognostic genes might exert relevant functions through aforementioned immune cells.


### 3.7. Vorinosta and raloxifene might be key drugs in LUAD
  Drug analysis of the 4 prognostic genes revealed no predicted drugs for CDKN3, 8 for PEBP1, 3 for TNFRSF19, and 3 for PHB. These results were integrated into a drug–prognostic gene interaction network (Fig. 9A). Since TNFRSF19 and PHB lacked available protein structures, molecular docking was performed using the PEBP1 protein structure. The top-ranked drugs, vorinostat and raloxifene, were selected for docking. Molecular docking results showed that PEBP1 bound to vorinostat with a docking score of −7.3 kcal/mol and to raloxifene with a score of −9.0 kcal/mol, with both interactions involving hydrogen bonds (Fig. 9B and C). These findings suggest strong binding affinities between PEBP1 and both drugs, indicating potential molecular interactions. However, these in silico results require experimental validation to confirm their therapeutic efficacy in LUAD.


### 3.8. The results of RT-qPCR
  Expression analysis revealed a significant increase in CDKN3 expression in patients with LUAD compared to controls, with a P-value of .0260 (Fig. 10A). PEBP1 showed a significant decrease in expression in patients with LUAD (P-value = .0004) (Fig. 10B). TNFRSF19 also exhibited significantly lower expression in patients with LUAD (P-value < .0001) (Fig. 10C). However, PHB showed no significant difference between patients with LUAD and controls (P-value = .7799) (Fig. 10D). H-GAPDH was used as the internal reference for PEBP1 and TNFRSF19, while H-GAPDH2 served as the reference for CDKN3 and PHB. The results presented in Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R386.


## 4. Discussion
  NSCLC accounts for approximately 85% of all LC cases, with LUAD being the most common subtype, representing approximately 50% of all LCs. Consequently, LUAD is a leading cause of cancer-related deaths globally.[3] The complex pathophysiology of LUAD involves genetic mutations, epigenetic alterations, and other factors.[10] Despite advances in targeted therapy and immunotherapy, the prognosis for LUAD remains poor, with a 5-year survival rate of approximately 10% to 20%.[11] Consequently, there is an urgent need to identify novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets to improve patient outcomes.

  Paraptosis, an unconventional form of PCD, is characterized by cytoplasmic vacuolization, often accompanied by endoplasmic reticulum and/or mitochondrial enlargement.[14] Unlike apoptosis, paraptosis induces cell death by disrupting protein, redox, and ion homeostasis, and does not rely on cysteine caspase activation.[28] While paraptosis has demonstrated anti-tumor effects in several cancers, its role in LUAD remains unclear.[29] The 4 prognostic genes (CDKN3, PEBP1, TNFRSF19, and PHB) identified in this study were all selected from PRGs. Their expression and prognostic value in LUAD are potentially linked to paraptosis mechanisms. Regarding core features of paraptosis, it depends on MAPK pathway activation and is accompanied by cytoplasmic vacuolation.[15,16] Consistently, KEGG enrichment analysis in this study revealed that these CGs were significantly enriched in the “MAPK signaling pathway,” suggesting they may influence LUAD progression by regulating this key proptosis pathway.

  Given that the mechanistic contribution of PRGs to tumorigenesis is poorly understood, exploring their role in LUAD may reveal new therapeutic strategies. For instance, Zhuo and Song employed bioinformatics analysis to delineate the prognostic characteristics of 2 distinct molecular subtypes in LUAD and validated the prognostic biomarker CDKN3.[20] However, that study involved fewer prognostic markers and did not encompass predictive analysis of therapeutic agents. In contrast, the present study 1st identified 27 DEGs in LUAD using bioinformatic approaches and further refined these to obtain 4 prognostic genes (PEBP1, TNFRSF19, CDKN3, and PHB) with enhanced clinical specificity. Subsequently, an independent prognostic model was constructed to enable risk stratification. Finally, significant associations among risk scores, the prognostic genes, and differentially expressed immune cells were established, and potential drug targets were screened (e.g., vorinostat and raloxifene demonstrated high binding affinity for PEBP1). Furthermore, this study systematically explored the potential mechanistic roles of these 4 genes in LUAD progression and therapeutic targeting.

  CDKN3, located at the 14q22 locus, plays a significant role in cancer development and progression by encoding the CDKN3 protein.[30] Identified as a tumor suppressor in several malignancies,[31] CDKN3 has emerged as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target in LUAD.[10] Its expression is up-regulated during LUAD progression and is involved in critical biological processes, including the cell cycle, cell division, and cell proliferation.[32,33] Previous studies have used CDKN3 as a metabolism-related gene to develop predictive models, demonstrating that high CDKN3 expression is associated with poor prognosis in patients with LUAD.[34,35] Additionally, CDKN3 knockdown significantly reduces tumor cell invasion, migration, and proliferation, as supported by previous studies.[36]

  This study confirmed significant CDKN3 overexpression in LUAD (HR = 1.3, univariate Cox regression), identifying it as a prognostic risk factor. Among 40 immune cell types analyzed in the LUAD microenvironment, CDKN3 was found to correlate with 34, showing the strongest negative association with HSC and the strongest positive association with Th2 cells. It has been reported that HSC expression is decreased in the high-risk LUAD group.[37] Moreover, glycolysis in patients with LUAD has been strongly linked to poor prognosis and resistance to immunotherapy, potentially driven by Th2 cell infiltration,[38] which aligns with our findings. These results suggest that CDKN3 plays a biologically significant role in LUAD therapy and warrants further investigation.

  PEBP1, also known as Raf kinase inhibitory protein, inhibits cell metastasis in NSCLC by blocking the Raf/ERK/STAT3 signaling pathway.[39] In NSCLC, PEBP1 mRNA expression is significantly down-regulated, and lower expression levels are associated with poorer differentiation and more advanced clinical TNM staging in patients.[40] PEBP1 can also inhibit paracellular apoptosis,[41] and in LUAD, PEBP1 regulates iron mutation in LUAD cells. The transcription factor NFYA inhibits this mutation by transcriptionally down-regulating PEBP1 expression,[42] highlighting PEBP1’s significant impact on LUAD initiation and progression, making it a promising therapeutic target.[43] In immune infiltration studies, PEBP1 expression was positively correlated with resting mast cells, potentially linked to PEBP1’s negative regulation of mast cell activation.[44]

  Univariate Cox regression analysis revealed that PEBP1 serves as a protective factor for LUAD prognosis, with an HR of 0.71. PEBP1 was associated with 21 out of 40 immune cell types in the LUAD immune microenvironment. These immune cells showed the strongest positive correlation with neuronal cells and the strongest negative correlation with keratinocytes. The role of neuronal cells in LUAD, as well as the impact of PEBP1 on keratinocytes, warrants further investigation to understand their contribution to LUAD onset and progression. Additionally, the high-affinity binding of vorinostat and raloxifene to PEBP1 suggests their potential use in LUAD treatment.

  TNFRSF19, also known as TAJ/TROY, is an orphan member of the TNF receptor family primarily expressed in skin and lung tissue cells. It plays a pivotal role in maintaining the correct phenotype of postnatal human lung tissue.[45] TNFRSF19’s distinct structural domain can induce apoptosis without caspase involvement,[46] and overexpression of this protein results in non-apoptotic cell death with paraptosis-like morphology.[47] In LC studies, TNFRSF19 expression was found to be significantly lower in tumor tissues compared to paraneoplastic tissues. Its expression level was negatively correlated with survival time and tumor stage, suggesting that TNFRSF19 serves as a key tumor suppressor gene in lung tissue.[48,49] Furthermore, TNFRSF19 has been closely linked to LUAD prognosis,[50] and its inclusion in risk scoring and prognostic models for patients with LUAD has been explored.[51,52]

  Given that TNFRSF19 is underexpressed in LUAD, it is considered a protective factor for LUAD prognosis. Analysis of 40 immune cell types in the LUAD immune microenvironment revealed that TNFRSF19 was associated with 32 of these cells, with the strongest negative correlation to Th1 cells and the strongest positive correlation to neuronal cells, mirroring the association seen with PEBP1. TNFRSF19 has been identified as a component of the Nogo receptor complex, with its expression elevated in human glioma tissues and cells. Inhibition of TNFRSF19 expression in vivo and in vitro slows glioma progression,[53] raising the possibility that this finding may be relevant to its positive correlation with neuronal cells in LUAD. Further research is needed to explore this relationship in the context of LUAD.

  PHB encodes prohibitin, a protein primarily localized to mitochondria, where it plays a critical role in maintaining mitochondrial function and preventing cellular aging. It is highly conserved and functions as a scaffolding protein essential for cancer cell proliferation and survival.[54,55] PHB interacts with the MAPK signaling pathway to promote cancer cell proliferation and tumor formation, particularly in colorectal cancer cells.[56] PHB1 has been found to be widely expressed in NSCLC, correlating with a low survival rate and chemoresistance, making PHB1 inhibition a promising therapeutic approach for kras-mediated NSCLC.[57,58]

  In this study, PHB was significantly expressed in LUAD, consistent with earlier findings.[20] Univariate Cox regression analysis revealed an HR of 1.3 for PHB, identifying it as a prognostic risk factor for both LUAD and CDKN3. In an analysis of 40 immune cell types in the LUAD immune microenvironment, PHB was found to correlate with 28 of the 40 cell types, showing the strongest negative correlation with HSC, similar to CDKN3, and the strongest positive correlation with MEP. The significant relationship between PHB, HSC, and MEP in LUAD suggests that further investigation is needed to understand how PHB mediates changes in HSC and MEP levels in LUAD, and to elucidate PHB’s specific role in these processes. Additionally, the functional divergence or convergence of PHB-CDKN3 antagonism in HSCs and its mechanistic basis warrant further exploration.

  Although this study established a prognostic risk model using TCGA-LUAD data and successfully identified 4 genes (CDKN3, PEBP1, TNFRSF19, and PHB) with significant prognostic value, laying a foundation for LUAD treatment, several limitations remain. First, the sample size used for RT-qPCR validation was relatively small. While the results were consistent with TCGA/Gene Expression Omnibus analyses, this limited size may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Second, despite constructing the prognostic model, the specific biological functions and clinical applicability of these PRGs in LUAD require further investigation. Third, although the model’s AUC values all exceeded 0.6, indicating some predictive capability, they did not reach the commonly used threshold of 0.7, necessitating further research to enhance accuracy. Fourth, this study only examined a subset of PRGs; future studies are needed to identify other relevant genes and pathways in LUAD. Furthermore, the predicted potential drugs, vorinostat and raloxifene, currently lack supporting clinical trial data or pharmacological studies confirming their efficacy in LC, rendering their therapeutic potential for LUAD speculative. Finally, the latest (9th edition) Lung Cancer TNM (LCTNM) staging system subdivides N2 LC into N2a and N2b; however, our dataset lacked this granular information, necessitating analysis of more detailed data. Future work will incorporate additional genes, clinical variables, and more detailed staging criteria to refine the model. Validation will be strengthened by performing RT-qPCR on larger, multi-center clinical cohorts. Concurrently, in vitro and in vivo experimental techniques (e.g., gene knockout, overexpression) will be employed to verify these findings and elucidate the precise regulatory mechanisms of the prognostic genes in LUAD. Finally, pharmacological studies on vorinostat and raloxifene will be pursued. This will involve assessing efficacy through post-treatment cell viability assays and therapeutic experiments in animal disease models, along with mechanistic exploration, prior to evaluating their clinical potential.


## 5. Conclusions
  This study successfully identified 4 genes with significant prognostic value (CDKN3, PEBP1, TNFRSF19, and PHB) and developed a prognostic risk model by extensively analyzing the role of PRGs in LUAD. RT-qPCR analysis of 5 LUAD samples and matched controls provided preliminary validation of the prognostic gene expression. The model demonstrated strong predictive capability in both the training and validation sets, offering a novel tool for assessing LUAD prognosis. Additionally, by identifying paraptosis-associated genes and mapping immune cell distributions within the TME, this study highlighted potential therapeutic agents that may target these genes, paving the way for new treatment strategies in LUAD.
