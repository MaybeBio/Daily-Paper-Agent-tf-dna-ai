# A DNABERT based deep learning framework for predicting transcription factor binding sites


## Abstract
  Transcription factors are proteins that regulate the expression of genes by binding to specific genomic regions known as Transcription Factor Binding Sites (TFBSs), typically located in the promoter regions of those genes. Accurate prediction of these binding sites is essential for understanding the complex gene regulatory networks underlying various cellular functions. In this regard, many deep learning models have been developed for such prediction, but there is still scope of improvement. In this work, we have developed a deep learning model which uses pre-trained DNABERT, a convolutional neural network (CNN) module, a modified convolutional block attention module (MCBAM), a multi-scale convolutions with attention (MSCA) module and an output module. The pre-trained DNABERT is used for sequence embedding, thereby capturing the long-term dependencies in the DNA sequences while the CNN, MCBAM and MSCA modules are useful in extracting higher-order local features. The proposed model TFBS-Finder is trained and tested on 165 ENCODE ChIP-seq datasets. We have also performed ablation studies as well as cross-cell line validations and comparisons with other models. The experimental results show the superiority of the proposed method in predicting TFBSs compared to the existing methodologies. Furthermore, we have performed visual analysis to show the interpretability of the prediction results. The source code of the proposed model is available at https://github.com/NimishaGhosh/TFBS-Finder.


## Introduction
  Transcription factors (TFs) are proteins that play an important role in the regulation of genetic transcriptions by binding to DNA regulatory sequences known as Transcription Factor Binding Sites (TFBSs) (typically of size 4-30 bp)1–3. Accurate prediction of TFBSs provides crucial information on regulatory networks that control cellular functions4,5, as well as identifying disease-associated variants and regulatory regions, enabling early detection and targeted therapies6. Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq)7 can be used to identify DNA fragments that interact with TFs. However, such method is costly, paving the way for more inexpensive but effective computational approaches to predict TFBSs.

  In recent years, researchers have proposed several machine learning and deep learning approaches to identify TFBSs which include Hidden Markov Models (HMM)8, Support Vector Machine (SVM)9, Random Forest (RF)10,11 models etc. Although traditional machine learning algorithms provide competitive results, they are dependent on other methods for feature extraction and they also do not work well with large-scale datasets. To address these challenges, recently many deep learning algorithms have been proposed, which show immense potential in predicting TFBSs when working with huge amount of experimental data such as ChIP-seq. In this regard, DeepSEA12 and DeepBind13 which use convolutional neural networks (CNNs) showcase good performance for the prediction of TFBSs. However, CNNs fail to capture long term dependencies between different positions in a DNA sequence. To overcome this, DanQ14 proposed by Quang et al. and DeepSite15 proposed by Zhang et al., combine CNN and bidirectional long-short term memory (BiLSTM) to predict TFBSs. Other models such as DSAC16, D-SSCA17, DeepSTF18, SAResNet19 and MultiTF20 use different attention mechanisms for TFBS prediction.

  The evolutionary deep learning model Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT)21 effectively learns contextual information in natural language processing. Since its proposal in21, BERT has also been used for pre-training human DNA and protein sequences resulting in DNABERT22 and ProteinBERT23 respectively. DNABERT is an effective pre-trained model for embedding DNA sequences and subsequently other deep learning models may be used for classifying TFBSs. In this regard, Ghosh et al.24 have used DNABERT for embedding the DNA sequences, while CNN, BiLSTM and capsule network have been used as the subsequent prediction layers. Luo et al.25 have proposed TFBert which uses DNABERT model for pre-training and then uses fine-tuning for the final prediction. On the other hand, BERT-TFBS6 has DNABERT-2, a CNN module, a convolution block attention module (CBAM) and an output module for TFBS prediction on 165 ChIP-seq datasets. All these works have shown promising results in predicting TFBSs.

  Taking cue from the literature, in this work we propose TFBS-Finder that utilises DNABERT, a CNN module, a modified convolution block attention module (MCBAM), a multi-scale convolutions with attention (MSCA) module and an output module to predict TFBSs. Similarly to6, we have trained and tested TFBS-Finder on 165 ChIP-Seq datasets, encompassing different cell-lines and TFs. The results show the superiority of TFBS-Finder when compared to the state-of-the-art predictors.

  To summarise, the main contributions of the work are as follows:We propose a novel DNABERT-based deep learning model (TFBS-Finder) for predicting TFBSs. The model utilises DNABERT for embedding while CNN, MCBAM, MSCA and output modules are used for subsequent prediction.We have performed ablation study to showcase the importance of each module of TFBS-Finder.To show the generalisability and robustness of TFBS-Finder, cross-cell line validations are conducted to predict TFBSs.For interpretability of the prediction results, we have performed detailed visualisation analysis.All codes along with a sample dataset and a trained model are provided at https://github.com/NimishaGhosh/TFBS-Finder/.

Initially, the analysis is performed to check if TFBS-Finder is able to differentiate sequences containing TFBS from shuffled sequences. Subsequently, we have performed comparison studies with existing state-of-the-art predictors to show the superiority of TFBS-Finder.

### Cross-cell line validation
  Cross-cell line validation is performed in order to show the generalisability and robustness of TFBS-Finder to identify the binding sites of a particular TF, in this case CTCF, occurring in different cell lines. In this regard, we have considered four cell lines Gm12878, Helas3, Hepg2 and K562 which have many TFs in common (as shown in Fig. 2) and also have a large number of CTCF sequences.Fig. 2Venn Diagram to show the common TFs between (a) Gm12878 and Helas3, (b) Gm12878 and Hepg2, (c) Gm12878 and K562, (d) Hepg2 and Helas3, (e) Hepg2 and K562 and (f) Helas3 and K562.

  To prepare the training dataset for cross-cell validation, the training sequences bounded by CTCF are considered as positive while the rest of the sequences bounded by other TFs are considered as negative. This resulted in 71761 positive and 147870 negative sequences for Gm12878. Similarly, for Helas3, these statistics are 84901 and 166311 while for Hepg2 such numbers of positive and negative sequences are 35857 and 381333 and for K562, they are 82297 and 406752. As can be observed from the statistics, the number of negative sequences is quite high as compared to the positive sequences which is quite obvious given the fact that only the sequences bounded by CTCF forms the positive sequences while those bounded by the other TFs together constitute the negative sequences. As the motivation is to identify the binding sites of CTCF, to balance the dataset we have randomly selected a number of negative sequences equal to the number of positive ones while ensuring that the negative sequences have candidates from all the other TFs (71761, 84901, 35857 and 82297 for Gm12878, Helas3, Hepg2 and K562 respectively). The test dataset is also prepared in a similar manner, where the sequences bounded by CTCF are considered as positive and those bounded by other TFs are negative. For example, for CTCF pertaining to Gm12878 (for wgEncodeAwgTfbsBroadGm12878CtcfUniPk), the number of positive sequences in test set is 8644 indicating that there are 8644 such sequences in the dataset wgEncodeAwgTfbsBroadGm12878CtcfUniPk with TFBSs for CTCF while 37098 negative sequences constitute the other 15 TFs. For the other TFs, the number of positive sequences includes the binding sequences of CTCF in wgEncodeAwgTfbsBroadGm12878CtcfUniPk and wgEncodeAwgTfbsUtaGm12878CtcfUniPk, while the negative sequences are their own binding sites. ### Visual analysis
  To understand which DNA regions TFBS-Finder prioritises during prediction, we have performed a detailed visualisation analysis focusing on transcription factors CEBPB and GATA3. We have selected CEBPB and GATA3 because their binding motifs are well-characterised and consistently represented across major motif databases like HOCOMOCO. This allows robust cross-database validation of the motifs recovered by TFBS-Finder.

  For the correctly predicted TFBS sequences, we have computed per-base saliency scores, which highlight the nucleotides that most strongly influence the model’s decision. These saliency maps are exported as JSON files and processed using TF-MoDISco (Transcription Factor Motif Discovery from Importance Scores)31, which identifies recurring sequence patterns (seqlets) and aggregates them into Position Weight Matrices (PWMs). The resulting PWMs have been compared against known TF motifs in the HOCOMOCO database using the MEME Suite (TOMTOM32). TOMTOM provides statistical significance measures, including both p-values and q-values, indicating the likelihood that the discovered motif corresponds to a known TF binding motif. The analysis reveals that TF-MoDISco-derived CEBPB motif significantly matches the corresponding motif in HOCOMOCO database with p-value of 2.78E-07 and q-value of 2.20E-04. Similarly, the motif extracted for GATA3 has p- and q-values as 7.97E-08 and 2.12E-05 respectively. These results confirm that TFBS-Finder successfully recovers the canonical binding patterns of both TFs. ## Materials and methods
  In this section, data preparation is provided followed by a discussion on the pipeline of the work.


### Data preparation
  In this work, we have considered the benchmark 165 ChIP-seq datasets from Enclyopedia of DNA elements (ENCODE)34, consisting of 29 different TFs from 32 cell lines. The dataset is taken from Zeng et al.35 where each positive sequence in the dataset is a 101 bp DNA sequence length containing TFBSs while the negative samples are obtained by shuffling the positive sequences conserving dinucleotide frequencies. Detailed descriptions of the datasets used in this work are given in Supplementary Table S1.


### Pipeline of the work
  The pipeline for the proposed work is given in Fig. 7. The proposed model consists of five modules encompassing a DNABERT module, a Convolutional Neural (CNN) module, a modified CBAM (MCBAM) module, a multi-scale convolutions with attention module (MSCA) and an output module.DNABERT: DNABERT is pre-trained on DNA sequences to extract long-term dependencies within such sequences. Here, the sequences are divided into tokens of size k.CNN: DNABERT generates embeddings for k-mer tokens in DNA sequences, which capture context-aware representations of the input. The CNN module is used for extracting high-order local features from such embeddings by utilising a convolutional layer.MCBAM: This module applies spatial and channel attention mechanisms to selectively enhance important local features extracted by the previous module.MSCA: This module further enhances the local features captured by the feature extraction module. This is achieved by introducing multi-scale convolutions with an attention mechanism. Here, the attention is a form of feature-wise attention which is applied in the context of convolutional feature extraction.Output module: Using the relevant features captured by MCBAM and MSCA, this module uses such features and applies a series of convolutions, pooling and multi-layer perceptrons to provide the final prediction of the presence or absence of TFBSs.Fig. 7Pipeline of the work.


#### DNABERT

### Performance metrics
  The different metrics used in this work to showcase the prediction performance of TFBS-Finder are accuracy, PR-AUC and ROC-AUC. Accuracy represents the proportion of correctly predicted samples. However, accuracy can turn out to be a biased metric when the data is imbalanced. In this regard, PR-AUC and ROC-AUC are some other parameters to judge prediction performance. PR-AUC is the area under the precision-recall curve and represents the model’s overall performance. Its value lies between 0 and 1 where a higher value of PR-AUC depicts better prediction performance. For imbalance class distributions, PR-AUC is a better metric than accuracy. Another metric which is equally suitable for evaluating predictor performance is ROC-AUC or Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve, which is a graphical representation of the performance of a binary classification model at various classification thresholds. In order to evaluate the performance of TFBS-Finder, all the three metrics have been used in this work.


## Conclusion
  In this work, we propose TFBS-Finder which utilises DNABERT for global context while CNN, MCBAM and MSCA performs feature extraction and capture local contexts to predict TFBSs for DNA sequences. TFBS-Finder is trained and tested on 165 ChIP-seq datasets where the experimental studies show that TFBS-Finder outperforms the existing predictors in terms of average accuracy, PR-AUC and ROC-AUC. We have also performed ablation studies to understand the importance of local and global contexts to identify TFBSs. Furthermore, cross-cell line validations are also performed to show the generalisability and robustness of TFBS-Finder. We have also carried out visual analysis for better interpretation of the predicted results. Although, the proposed model shows very inspiring results, as a future work, we would additionally like to incorporate DNA structure information along with DNA sequences to see the effect on the prediction capacity of TFBS-Finder.

  It is to be noted that setting negatives as shuffled di-nucleotide sequences can introduce a bias in the analysis, since they are not actual DNA sequences but artificial sequences. Thus, it can be easier for a model to separate positives from those negatives. On the other hand, they allow to conserve the nucleotide composition that can highly impact the learning process. However, all the existing methodologies with which we have compared TFBS-Finder have considered the same Chip-Seq experiments. Thus, when we compare our results with the existing methods, we have to assume the same negative sets. Moreover, the choice of alternative negative sequences is not at all trivial. Keeping in mind that the closer the nucleotide composition the more fair the analysis is, the best choice would be the promoter regions but not all Chip-Seq sequences come from promoter regions. Also, in this case there can be a bias introduced by the kind of chosen genes. We have scheduled this analysis as a further future work where we aim to study the impact of different negative choices on results of TFBS-Finder as well as of existing models.
